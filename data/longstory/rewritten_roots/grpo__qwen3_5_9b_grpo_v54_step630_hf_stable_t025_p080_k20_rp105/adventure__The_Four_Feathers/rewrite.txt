The first guest to arrive at Broad Place was Lieutenant Sutch. It was mid-June, and the afternoon sun bathed the old red-brick house, perched on a southern slope of the Surrey hills, in a warmth that made it glow like a rare jewel emerging from the dark forest of pines below. Sutch limped across the hall, where the portraits of the Fevershams ascended one above another toward the ceiling, and stepped out onto the stone-flagged terrace at the rear. There he found his host sitting upright as a boy, gazing southward toward the Sussex Downs.

"How's the leg?" General Feversham asked, rising briskly from his chair. He was a small, wiry man; despite his white hair, he possessed an alertness that was purely physical. His face was bony, with a high, narrow forehead and steel-blue eyes that were inexpressive, suggesting a barrenness of mind.

"It gave me trouble during the winter," Sutch replied. "But that was to be expected." Feversham nodded, and for a moment both men fell silent. From the terrace, the ground dropped steeply to a wide plain of brown earth and emerald fields, dotted with dark clumps of trees. Voices rose through the sunshine, small but distinct. Far away toward Horsham, a coil of white smoke from a train snaked rapidly among the trees, and on the horizon, the Downs rose, patched with white chalk.

"I thought I should find you here," Sutch said.

"It was my wife's favorite corner," Feversham answered in a voice devoid of emotion. "She would sit here by the hour. She had a strange liking for wide and empty spaces."

"Yes," Sutch agreed. "She had imagination. Her thoughts could people them."

Feversham glanced at his companion as though he barely understood. But he asked no questions. What he did not comprehend, he habitually dismissed as unworthy of his attention. He spoke immediately of a different topic.

"There will be a leaf from our table tonight."

"Yes. Collins, Barberton, and Vaughan went this winter. Well, we are all permanently shelved on the world's half-pay list as it is. The obituary column is merely the final formality that gazettes us out of the service altogether," Sutch said, stretching to ease his crippled leg, which fourteen years prior had been crushed and twisted in the fall of a scaling ladder.

"I am glad you came before the others," Feversham continued. "I wish to take your opinion. This day means more to me than the anniversary of our attack on the Redan. At the very moment we stood under arms in the dark—"

"To the west of the quarries; I remember," Sutch interrupted, taking a deep breath. "How could one forget?"

"At that very moment Harry was born in this house. I thought, therefore, that if you did not object, he might join us tonight. He is at home. He will, of course, enter the service, and he might learn something, perhaps, which will prove useful later—one never knows."

"By all means," Sutch said eagerly. Since his visits to General Feversham were limited to these anniversary dinners, he had never yet seen Harry Feversham.

For many years, Sutch had been puzzled by the qualities in General Feversham that had attracted Muriel Graham, a woman remarkable for both the refinement of her intellect and the beauty of her person. He could never find an explanation. He was content only with the knowledge that for some mysterious reason she had married a man so much older than herself and so unlike her in character. Personal courage and an indomitable self-confidence were the only qualities that shone forth in General Feversham. Sutch drifted back in thought over twenty years, to a time before he had participated, as an officer of the Naval Brigade, in that unsuccessful assault on the Redan. He remembered a season in London, fresh from the China station, and felt a curiosity to see Harry Feversham. He did not admit that it was more than the natural curiosity of a man disabled in comparative youth who had made the study of human nature a hobby. He was simply interested to see whether the lad took after his mother or his father—that was all.

That night, Harry Feversham took a place at the dinner table and listened to the stories his elders told, while Lieutenant Sutch watched him. The tales were all of that dark winter in the Crimea, and a fresh story was always being told before the previous one ended. They were stories of death, hazardous exploits, the pinch of famine, and the chill of snow. Yet they were recounted in clipped words and with a matter-of-fact tone, as though the men relating them viewed these events only as distant occurrences. There was seldom a comment more pronounced than a mere "That's curious," or an exclamation more significant than a laugh.

But Harry Feversham sat listening as though the incidents thus carelessly narrated were happening actually at that moment within the walls of the room. His dark eyes—the eyes of his mother—turned with each story from speaker to speaker, wide open and fixed, waiting until the last word was spoken. He listened fascinated and enthralled. So vividly did the changes of expression shoot and quiver across his face that it seemed to Sutch the lad must actually hear the drone of bullets in the air, actually resist the stunning shock of a charge, actually ride down into the thick of a squadron where guns screeched out a tongue of flame from a fog. Once a major of artillery spoke of the suspense of the hours between the parading of the troops before a battle and the first command to advance; and Harry's shoulders worked under the intolerable strain of those lagging minutes.

But he did more than tense his shoulders. He threw a single, furtive, wavering glance backward. Lieutenant Sutch was startled, indeed more than startled—he was pained. For this was, after all, Muriel Graham's boy.

The look was too familiar to Sutch. He had seen it on the faces of recruits during their first experience of battle too often to misunderstand it. A particular picture rose before his mind: an advancing square at Inkermann, and a tall, big soldier rushing forward from the line in the eagerness of his attack, then stopping suddenly as though he suddenly realized he was alone and had to meet alone the charge of a mounted Cossack. Sutch remembered very clearly the fatal wavering glance the big soldier had thrown backward toward his companions—a glance accompanied by a queer, sickly smile. He remembered, with equal vividness, its consequence. Though the soldier carried a loaded musket with a bayonet locked to the muzzle, he had, without an effort of self-defense, received the Cossack's lance-thrust in his throat.

Sutch glanced hurriedly around the table, afraid that General Feversham or one of his guests had remarked on the same look and the same smile on Harry's face. But no one had eyes for the lad; each visitor was waiting too eagerly for an opportunity to tell a story of their own. Sutch drew a breath of relief and turned to Harry. But the boy was sitting with his elbows on the cloth and his head propped between his hands, lost to the glare of the room and its glitter of silver, constructing again out of the swift succession of anecdotes a world of cries and wounds, maddened riderless chargers, and men writhing in a fog of cannon-smoke. Even the curtest, least graphic description of the biting days and nights in the trenches set the lad shivering. His face grew pinched, as though the iron frost of that winter was actually eating into his bones. Sutch touched him lightly on the elbow.

"You renew those days for me," he said. "Though the heat is dripping down the windows, I feel the chill of the Crimea."

Harry roused himself from his absorption.

"The stories renew them," he said.

"No. It is you listening to the stories."

Before Harry could reply, General Feversham's voice broke sharply from the head of the table: "Harry, look at the clock!"

At once all eyes turned upon the lad. The hands of the clock made the acutest of angles. It was close upon midnight; and from eight, without so much as a word or a question, he had sat at the dinner table listening. Yet even now he rose with reluctance.

"Must I go, father?" he asked, and the general's guests intervened in a chorus. The conversation was clear gain to the lad, a first taste of powder which might stand him in good stead afterwards.

"Besides, it's the boy's birthday," added the major of artillery. "He wants to stay; that's plain. You wouldn't find a youngster of fourteen sit all these hours without a kick of the foot against the table-leg unless the conversation entertained him. Let him stay, Feversham!"

For once, General Feversham relaxed the iron discipline under which the boy lived.

"Very well," he said. "Harry shall have an hour's furlough from his bed. A single hour won't make much difference."

Harry's eyes turned toward his father, and for a moment rested upon his face with a curious, steady gaze. It seemed to Sutch that they uttered a question, and, rightly or wrongly, he interpreted the question into words: "Are you blind?"

But General Feversham was already talking to his neighbors, and Harry quietly sat down again, propping his chin upon his hands, listening with all his soul. Yet he was not entertained; rather, he was enthralled. He sat quiet under the compulsion of a spell. His face became unnaturally white, his eyes unnaturally large, while the flames of the candles shone ever redder and more blurred through a blue haze of tobacco smoke, and the level of the wine grew steadily lower in the decanters.

Thus, half of that one hour's furlough passed. Then General Feversham, himself jogged by the unlucky mention of a name, suddenly blurted out in his jerky fashion: "Lord Wilmington. One of the best names in England, if you please. Did you ever see his house in Warwickshire? Every inch of the ground you would think would have a voice to bid him play the man, if only in remembrance of his fathers.... It seemed incredible and mere camp rumor, but the rumor grew. If it was whispered at the Alma, it was spoken aloud at Inkermann, it was shouted at Balaclava. Before Sebastopol the hideous thing was proved. Wilmington was acting as galloper to his general. I believe upon my soul the general chose him for the duty, so that the fellow might set himself right. There were three hundred yards of bullet-swept flat ground, and a message to be carried across them. Had Wilmington toppled off his horse on the way, why, there were the whispers silenced forever. Had he ridden through alive he earned distinction besides. But he didn't dare; he refused! Imagine it if you can! He sat shaking on his horse and declined. You should have seen the general. His face turned the color of that Burgundy. 'No doubt you have a previous engagement,' he said, in the politest voice you ever heard—just that, not a word of abuse. A previous engagement on the battlefield! For the life of me, I could hardly help laughing. But it was a tragic business for Wilmington. He was broken, of course, and slunk back to London. Every house was closed to him; he dropped out of his circle like a lead bullet you let slip out of your hand into the sea. The very women in Piccadilly spat if he spoke to them; and he blew his brains out in a back bedroom off the Haymarket. Curious that, eh? He hadn't the pluck to face the bullets when his name was at stake, yet he could blow his own brains out afterwards."

Lieutenant Sutch chanced to look at the clock as the story came to an end. It was now a quarter to one. Harry Feversham still had a quarter of an hour's furlough, which was occupied by a retired surgeon-general with a great wagging beard, sitting nearly opposite the boy.

"I can tell you an incident still more curious," he said. "The man in this case had never been under fire before, but he was of my own profession. Life and death were part of his business. Nor was he really in any particular danger. The affair happened during a hill campaign in India. We were encamped in a valley, and a few Pathans used to lie out on the hillside at night and take long shots into the camp. A bullet ripped through the canvas of the hospital tent—that was all. The surgeon crept out to his own quarters, and his orderly discovered him half-an-hour afterward lying in his blood, stone-dead."

"Hit?" exclaimed the major.

"Not a bit of it," said the surgeon. "He had quietly opened his instrument-case in the dark, taken out a lancet, and severed his femoral artery. Sheer panic, do you see, at the whistle of a bullet."

Even upon these men, case-hardened to horrors, the incident related in its bald simplicity wrought its effect. From some there broke a half-uttered exclamation of disbelief; others moved restlessly in their chairs with a sort of physical discomfort, because a man had sunk so far below humanity. Here an officer gulped his wine, there a second shook his shoulders as though to shake the knowledge off as a dog shakes water. There was only one in all that company who sat perfectly still in the silence which followed the story. That one was the boy, Harry Feversham.

He sat with his hands now clenched upon his knees and leaning forward a little across the table toward the surgeon, his cheeks white as paper, his eyes burning, burning with ferocity. He had the look of a dangerous animal in the trap. His body was gathered, his muscles taut. Sutch had a fear that the lad meant to leap across the table and strike with all his strength in the savagery of despair. He had indeed reached out a restraining hand when General Feversham's matter-of-fact voice intervened, and the boy's attitude suddenly relaxed.

"Queer incomprehensible things happen. Here are two of them. You can only say they are the truth and pray God you may forget 'em.

Thirteen years had rolled on, yet the calendar remained fixed upon June. The air was cool, deceptive for the season, carrying the crisp chill of early summer rather than the heat of mid-summer. High above the bustling heart of Westminster, in a block of buildings that loomed like a silent fortress against the night sky, a small gathering took place. To a stranger crossing St. James's Park beneath the suspension bridge, the tiers of lighted windows rising to the tenth floor might have seemed the dwelling of mountain gnomes, hidden away from the world below.

Inside the dining room of this flat, the atmosphere was dark and restful, warmed by a comfortable fire that belied the calendar. Four men sat around the table, the smoke of their cigars mingling with the scent of dinner.

Harry Feversham sat unchanged in spirit, though time had carved a fair moustache upon his dark hair and broadened his frame into that of a middle-aged athlete. His features, however, retained the sharp scrutiny of the night Lieutenant Sutch had once examined them so closely. Beside him sat Captain Trench, a small, balding man with a sharp, resourceful face and black eyes that darted with remarkable activity. Opposite him was Lieutenant Willoughby, a man of a different stamp entirely. With a round forehead, a thick snub nose, and vacant, protruding eyes, he possessed an aspect of invincible stupidity. He spoke rarely, and when he did, his words drifted toward points long forgotten, twisting a moustache whose ends curled up toward his eyes with ridiculous ferocity. Stubborn as well as slow, Willoughby was a man who clung to his own crippled thoughts, refusing to admit harm unless he had laboriously convinced himself of it.

The fourth at the table was Durrance, a lieutenant of the East Surrey Regiment and Feversham's oldest friend, who had arrived in answer to a telegram.

The year was 1882. The thoughts of civilians in London were heavy with anxiety regarding Egypt, while soldiers felt an eager anticipation. News from the East painted a grim picture: Arabi Pasha was strengthening the fortifications of Alexandria, and further south, a danger was swelling like a thundercloud. Just a year prior, a young Dongolawi named Mohammed Ahmed had marched through the villages of the White Nile, preaching with the fire of a zealot the coming of a Saviour. The passionate victims of Turkish taxation had listened, finding holy names whispered in the wind and imprinted upon the eggs they gathered. Now, Mohammed had declared himself that Saviour, and his first battles against the Turks were already being won.

"There will be trouble," said Trench, and the sentence hung in the air as the text upon which three of the four men focused their minds.

Yet, in a rare interval, Harry Feversham spoke of a different subject.

"I am very glad you were all able to dine with me tonight," he explained to Durrance. "I telegraphed to Castleton as well, an officer of ours, but he was dining with a big man in the War Office and leaves for Scotland afterwards, so he could not come. I have news of a sort."

The three men leaned forward, their minds still occupied by the specter of war, but Feversham's news was personal.

"I only reached London this morning from Dublin," he said, a shade of embarrassment coloring his voice. "I have been some weeks there."

Durrance lifted his eyes from the tablecloth and looked quietly at his friend. "Yes?" he asked steadily.

"I have come back engaged to be married."

Durrance lifted his glass to his lips. "Well, here's luck to you, Harry," he said. The wish was curt, yet to Feversham's ears, it carried the weight of true affection. Between these two men, affectionate phrases were unnecessary. Their friendship was a secure instrument, forged for hard, lifelong use, valuable enough to demand sacrifice without ever needing to be spoken of. They knew that such bonds entailed obligations, and if sacrifices became needful, they would be made in silence.

"Thank you, Jack!" said Feversham. "I am glad of your good wishes. It was you who introduced me to Ethne; I cannot forget it."

Durrance set his glass down without haste. A moment of silence followed, during which he sat with his eyes upon the tablecloth and his hands resting on the edge.

"Yes," he said in a level voice. "I did you a good turn then."

He seemed poised to say more, unsure how to phrase it, until Captain Trench's sharp, practical voice intervened.

"Will this make any difference?" Trench asked.

Feversham replaced his cigar between his lips. "You mean, shall I leave the service?" he asked slowly. "I don't know."

Durrance seized the opportunity to rise and cross to the window, standing with his back to his companions. Feversham took the abrupt movement for a reproach and addressed his friend's back rather than Trench.

"I don't know," he repeated. "It will need thought. There is much to be said. On the one side, of course, there's my father, my career, such as it is. On the other hand, there is her father, Dermod Eustace."

"He wishes you to chuck your commission?" asked Willoughby.

"He has no doubt the Irishman's objection to constituted authority," said Trench, laughing. "But need you subscribe to it, Feversham?"

"It is not merely that." Feversham continued, carefully weighing his words, still looking across the shoulders of his companions at his friend. "Dermod is old, his estates are going to ruin, and there are other things. You know, Jack?"

The direct appeal hung in the air, yet Durrance answered absently. "Yes, I know," he added, quoting a catch-word. "If you want any whiskey, rap twice on the floor with your foot. The servants understand."

"Precisely," said Feversham. He continued, "Besides, there is Ethne herself. Dermod for once did an appropriate thing when he gave her that name. For she is of her country, and more, of her county. She has the love of it in her bones. I do not think that she could be quite happy in India, or indeed in any place which was not within reach of Donegal, the smell of its peat, its streams, and the brown friendliness of its hills. One has to consider that."

He waited for an answer, receiving none, and went on. Durrance, however, had no thought of reproach in his mind. He knew Feversham was speaking, wishing deeply that his friend would continue, yet he paid no heed to the words. He stood looking steadfastly out the window. Over against him was the glare from Pall Mall striking upward to the sky, the chains of light banked one above another as the town rose northward, accompanied by the rumble of a million carriages. At his feet, very far below, lay St. James's Park, silent and black—a quiet pool of darkness amidst the glitter and noise. Durrance had a great desire to escape into that secrecy, but he could not do so without remark. Therefore, he kept his back turned, leaning his forehead against the window, hoping his friend would continue talking. He was facing one of the sacrifices that must not be mentioned, and no sign must betray it.

Feversham did continue, and though Durrance did not listen, Captain Trench gave him his closest attention. It was evident that Harry was giving reasons seriously considered, not making mere excuses. In the end, Trench was satisfied.

"Well, I drink to you, Feversham," he said, "with all the proper sentiments."

"I too, old man," said Willoughby, obediently following his senior's lead.

Thus they drank their comrade's health. As their empty glasses rattled on the table, there came a knock upon the door.

The two officers looked up. Durrance turned from the window. Feversham said, "Come in," and his servant brought in a telegram.

Feversham tore open the envelope carelessly, read through the message with equal carelessness, and then sat very still. His eyes were fixed upon the slip of pink paper, his face grown at once extremely grave. He sat thus for an appreciable time, not stunned so much as thoughtful. A complete silence fell upon the room. Feversham's three guests averted their eyes. Durrance turned again to his window; Willoughby twisted his moustache and gazed intently upward at the ceiling; Captain Trench shifted his chair round and stared into the glowing fire. Each man's attitude expressed a certain suspense. It seemed that sharp upon the heels of Feversham's good news, calamity had come knocking at the door.

"There is no answer," said Harry, and fell to silence again. Once he raised his head and looked at Trench as though he had a mind to speak, but he thought the better of it and dropped again to the consideration of the message.

In a moment or two, the silence was sharply interrupted, but not by any of the expectant men within the room. The interruption came from without. From the parade ground of Wellington Barracks, the drums and fifes sounding the tattoo shrilled through the open window with a startling clearness, like a sharp summons, before diminishing as the band marched away across the gravel and growing loud again.

Feversham did not change his attitude, but the look upon his face was now that of a man listening thoughtfully, just as he had read thoughtfully. In the years that followed, that moment was to recur again and again to the recollection of each of Harry's three guests. The lighted room, the bright homely fire, the open window overlooking the myriad lamps of London, Harry Feversham seated with the telegram spread before him, the drums and fifes calling loudly and then dwindling to music very small and pretty—music which beckoned where a moment ago it had commanded—all these details made up a picture whose colors were not to fade by any lapse of time, although its significance was not apprehended now.

It was remembered that Feversham rose abruptly from his chair just before the tattoo ceased. He crumpled the telegram loosely in his hands, tossed it into the fire, and then, leaning his back against the chimney-piece and upon one side of the fireplace, said again:

"I don't know;" as though he had thrust that message, whatever it might be, from his mind, and was summing up in this indefinite way the argument which had gone before.

Thus the long silence was broken, and a spell was lifted. The fire took hold upon the telegram and shook it, so that it moved like a thing alive and in pain. It twisted, and part of it unrolled, and for a second lay open and smooth of creases, lit up by the flame and as yet untouched. Two or three words sprang, as it were, out of a yellow glare of fire and were legible. Then the flame seized upon that smooth part too, and in a moment shrivelled it into black tatters. Captain Trench had been staring into the fire all this while.

"You return to Dublin, I suppose?" said Durrance. He had moved back into the room. Like his companions, he was conscious of an unexplained relief.

"To Dublin? No; I go to Donegal in three weeks' time. There is to be a dance. It is hoped you will come."

"I am not sure that I can manage it. There is just a chance, I believe, should trouble come in the East, that I may go out on the staff."

The talk thus came round again to the chances of peace and war, holding in that quarter until the boom of the Westminster clock told that the hour was eleven. Captain Trench rose from his seat on the last stroke; Willoughby and Durrance followed his example.

"I shall see you tomorrow," said Durrance to Feversham.

"As usual," replied Harry.

His three guests descended from his rooms and walked across the Park together. At the corner of Pall Mall, however, they parted company; Durrance mounted St. James's Street, while Trench and Willoughby crossed the road into St. James's Square. There Trench slipped his arm through Willoughby's, to Willoughby's surprise, for Trench was an undemonstrative man.

"You know Castleton's address?" he asked.

"Albemarle Street," Willoughby answered, adding the number.

"He leaves Euston at twelve o'clock. It is now ten minutes past eleven. Are you curious, Willoughby? I confess to curiosity. I am an inquisitive methodical person, and when a man gets a telegram bidding him tell Trench something and he tells Trench nothing, I am curious as a philosopher to know what that something is! Castleton is the only other officer of our regiment in London. It is likely, therefore, that the telegram came from Castleton. Castleton, too, was dining with a big man from the War Office. I think that if we take a hansom to Albemarle Street, we shall just catch Castleton upon his door-step."

Mr. Willoughby, who understood very little of Trench's meaning, nevertheless cordially agreed to the proposal.

"I think it would be prudent," said he, and he hailed a passing cab. A moment later the two men were driving to Albemarle Street.

Durrance walked alone to his lodging, his mind drifting back two years to a peculiar whim of old Dermod Eustace that had dragged him unwillingly to the house by the Lennon River in Donegal. There, amidst his surprise, he had met Dermod's daughter, Ethne. She possessed a quality that stunned everyone who first spoke with her. Durrance had spent a night in the house, and throughout that evening, she had played her violin. Seated with her back to her audience, as was her custom, she shielded her performance from any distracting glance or gesture, guarding the purity of her concentration. The melodies she produced still rang in Durrance's ears. The girl held a rare gift; the strings of her violin answered the questions of her bow with startling clarity.

There was a particular piece, the *Melusine* overture, that seemed to hold the very sob of the waves. Durrance had listened in wonder that night, for the violin spoke to him of things the girl could not possibly know. It sang of long, perilous journeys and the faces of strange lands; of silver paths across moonlit seas and beckoning voices rising from the desert's edge. Its tone grew deeper, more mysterious, telling of great joys unattainable and griefs eternal, elevated by their magnitude into something noble. It spoke of longings beyond words, yet never a single note of mere complaint. That night, watching Ethne's face turned away, Durrance had drawn a lesson he struggled to keep clear in his mind: true music does not complain.

And so, when he rode into the Row the next morning, his blue eyes looked out upon the world from his bronzed face with no less of his usual friendliness. He waited at half-past nine by the clump of lilacs and laburnums at the end of the sand, but Harry Feversham did not join him. Nor did he for the next three weeks. Since graduating from Oxford, it had been their custom to meet at this specific spot and hour whenever both happened to be in town. Durrance felt a profound sense of loss, as if he had lost his friend entirely.

Yet, the rumors of war swelled into certainty. Finally, when Feversham kept the tryst, Durrance had news to share.

"I told you luck might look my way. Well, she has. I'm going out to Egypt on General Graham's staff. There's talk we may run down the Red Sea to Suakin afterward."

The exhilaration in his voice brought an unmistakable envy to Feversham's eyes. It struck Durrance as strange, even pleasant in that moment of good fortune, that Harry Feversham should envy him. But he interpreted the look through the lens of his own ambitions.

"It is rough on you," Durrance said sympathetically, "that your regiment has to stay behind."

Feversham rode by his friend's side in silence. As they reached the chairs beneath the trees, he spoke.

"That was expected. The day you dined with me, I sent in my papers."

"That night?" Durrance asked, turning in his saddle. "After we had gone?"

"Yes," Feversham replied, accepting the correction. He wondered if it had been intended, but Durrance rode on silently. Once again, Harry sensed a reproach in his friend's silence, and once again, he was mistaken. For Durrance suddenly spoke heartily, laughing aloud.

"I remember. You gave us your reasons that night. But for the life of me, I can't help wishing we were going out together. When do you leave for Ireland?"

"To-night."

"So soon?"

They turned their horses and rode westward again down the alley of trees. The morning air was still fresh. The limes and chestnuts retained their early green, and since May was late that year, their blossoms still hung delicately white like snow upon the branches, shining red against the dark rhododendrons. The park shimmered in a haze of sunlight, and the distant roar of the streets sounded like the tumbling of river water.

"It is a long time since we bathed in Sandford Lasher," Durrance said.

"Or froze in the Easter vacations in the big snow-gully on Great End," Feversham returned. Both men felt that on this morning, a volume in their book of life had ended. Since the volume had been pleasant to read, and they did not know if its successors would sustain its promise, they looked backward through the leaves before putting it finally away.

"You must stay with us, Jack, when you come back," Feversham said.

Durrance had schooled himself not to wince, and he did not, even at the anticipatory "us." If his left hand tightened upon the reins, the sign was undetectable to his friend.

"If I come back," Durrance said. "You know my creed. I could never pity a man who died on active service. I would very much like to come by that end myself."

It was a simple creed, consistent with the simplicity of the man who spoke it. It amounted to nothing more than this: that to die decently was worth many years of life. He uttered it without melancholy or foreboding. Still, he feared his friend might interpret the words differently, so he looked quickly into Feversham's face. He saw only that puzzling look of envy again.

"You see, there are worse things that can happen," Durrance continued. "Disablement, for instance. Clever men might manage to put up with it. But what in the world should I do if I had to sit in a chair all my days? It makes me shiver to think of it," he added, shaking his broad shoulders to shake off the fear. "Well, this is the last ride. Let us gallop," and he urged his horse forward.

Feversham followed suit, and side by side they raced down the sand. At the bottom of the Row, they stopped, shook hands, and with the curtest of nods parted. Feversham rode out of the park; Durrance turned back, walking his horse up toward the seats beneath the trees.

Even as a boy in his home at Southpool in Devonshire, upon a wooded creek of the Salcombe estuary, Durrance had always felt a certain restlessness—a desire to sail down that creek and out over the sea levels, dreaming of queer, outlandish countries and peoples beyond the dark, familiar woods. This restlessness had grown with him, so that "Guessens," even after inheriting its farms and lands, remained in his thoughts merely as a place to return to, not an estate to occupy a life. He purposely exaggerated this restlessness now, setting it against the words Feversham had spoken, which he knew to be true. Ethne Eustace would hardly be happy outside her county of Donegal. Therefore, even had things fallen out differently, as he phrased it, there might have been a clash. Perhaps it was best that Harry Feversham was to marry Ethne—and no one else.

Thus, he argued as he rode, until the riders vanished from his sight, leaving the ladies in their colored frocks beneath the cool of the trees. The trees themselves dwindled to ragged mimosas; the brown sand at his feet spread out in a widening circle, taking on the bright color of honey; and upon the empty sand, black stones began to heap themselves shapelessly like coal, flashing in the sun like mirrors. Deep in his anticipations of the Soudan, he heard his name called softly in a woman's voice. Looking up, he found himself close by the rails.

"How do you do, Mrs. Adair?" he said, stopping his horse. Mrs. Adair gave him her hand across the rails. She was Durrance's neighbor at Southpool, a year or two his elder—a tall woman remarkable for the many shades of her thick brown hair and the peculiar pallor of her face. But at this moment, her face had brightened; there was a hint of color in her cheeks.

"I have news for you," Durrance said. "Two special items. One, Harry Feversham is to be married."

"To whom?" the lady asked eagerly.

"You should know. It was in your house in Hill Street that Harry first met her; and I introduced him. He has been improving the acquaintance in Dublin."

But Mrs. Adair already understood; it was plain the news was welcome.

"Ethne Eustace!" she cried. "They will be married soon?"

"There is nothing to prevent it."

"I am glad," the lady sighed, as though with relief. "What is your second item?"

"As good as the first. I go out on General Graham's staff."

Mrs. Adair fell silent. A look of anxiety entered her eyes, and the color drained from her face.

"You are very glad, I suppose," she said slowly.

Durrance's voice left her in no doubt.

"I should think I was. I go soon, too, and the sooner the better. I will come and dine some night, if I may, before I go."

"My husband will be pleased to see you," Mrs. Adair said, rather coldly. Durrance did not notice the coldness. He had his own reasons for making the most of the opportunity that had come his way; he urged his enthusiasm, laying it bare in words more for his own benefit than for hers. Indeed, he had always had a vague impression of the lady. She was handsome in a queer, foreign way, not uncommon along the coasts of Devonshire and Cornwall, with good hair and always well-dressed. Moreover, she was friendly. At that point, Durrance's knowledge of her ended. Perhaps her chief merit in his eyes was that she had made friends with Ethne Eustace. But he was to become better acquainted with Mrs. Adair. He rode away from the park with the old regret in his mind that the fortunes of himself and his friend were finally severed that morning. In truth, he had that morning set the strands of a new rope a-weaving, destined to bring them together again in a strange and terrible relationship. Mrs. Adair followed him out of the park and walked home very thoughtfully.

Durrance had exactly one week to provide his equipment and arrange his estate in Devonshire. It passed in a continuous hurry of preparation, so that his newspaper lay each day unfolded in his rooms. The general was to travel overland to Brindisi; and so, on an evening of wind and rain toward the end of July, Durrance stepped from the Dover pier into the mail-boat for Calais. In spite of the rain and the gloomy night, a small crowd had gathered to give the general a send-off. As the ropes were cast off, a feeble cheer was raised. Before the cheer had ended, Durrance found himself beset by a strange illusion. Leaning upon the bulwarks, he idly wondered if this was his last view of England, wishing that one of his friends had come down to see him go, when it seemed suddenly that his wish was answered. He caught a glimpse of a man standing beneath a gas-lamp, a man of the stature and wearing the likeness of Harry Feversham.

Durrance rubbed his eyes and looked again. But the wind made the tongue of light flicker uncertainly within the glass; the rain blurred the quay. He could only be certain that a man stood there, vaguely distinguishing beneath the lamp the whiteness of a face. "It is an illusion," he told himself. "Harry Feversham is at that moment most likely listening to Ethne playing the violin under a clear sky in a high garden of Donegal."

But even as he turned from the bulwarks, there came a lull in the wind. The lights burned bright and steady on the pier, and the face leaped from the shadows, distinct in feature and expression. Durrance leaned out over the side of the boat.

"Harry!" he shouted, at the top of a wondering voice.

But the figure beneath the lamp never stirred. The wind blew the lights again this way and that; the paddles churned the water; the mail-boat passed beyond the pier. "It is an illusion," he repeated. "It is a coincidence. It is the face of a stranger very like to Harry Feversham's. It could not be Feversham's, because the face Durrance had seen so distinctly for a moment was a haggard, wistful face—a face stamped with extraordinary misery; the face of a man cast out from among his fellows."

Durrance had been very busy all that week. He had completely forgotten the arrival of that telegram and the suspense caused by its long perusal. His newspaper had lain unfolded in his rooms. But his friend Harry Feversham had come to see him off.

The journey to Dublin had been a blur of night mail and dawn, a hurried escape from the shadow of Durrance's presence in the Row. By the forenoon, Feversham stood on the quay at Ramelton, the air thick with the scent of the Lennon River. Waiting for him was Ethne, seated in a dog-cart that seemed too small for her commanding figure. She offered him a hand and a smile that transcended mere camaraderie, carrying the warmth of a shared secret.

"You are surprised to see me," she observed, reading the tension in his gaze.

"I always am," Feversham replied, his voice rough. "For always you exceed my thoughts of you."

As the traps were packed, Ethne spoke softly. "I shall drive slowly. I brought no groom on purpose. Guests arrive tomorrow; we have only today."

They set off along the wide causeway, turning up the steep, narrow street of Ramelton. Feversham sat in silence, absorbing the scene with a pride that felt like ownership. He noted the dark thicket of trees climbing the riverbank, the old grey bridge, and the drowsy quiet of the town. It was Ethne's world, and for the first time, he felt he belonged to it.

Ethne was twenty-one, tall and strong, with shoulders squared and a grace that moved like a deer. Her dark brown hair was coiled tightly at the nape of her neck, framing cheeks flushed with health and eyes of clear grey that met his with frank honesty. She possessed a straightforward simplicity, a strength that excluded violence but embraced gentleness. Feversham recalled the local legend of her courage—the story of how she had once leaped from a runaway dog-cart on a steep hill, balancing along the shafts to grab the reins and stop the horse ten yards from a sharp curve. He admired her, unaware yet of the flaws that shadowed her virtues.

For the first part of the drive, Ethne was as silent as her companion, her mind elsewhere. Only when they left the town and hit the straight road to Letterkenny did she turn to him, her tone steady and level, stating a fact as simple as the weather.

"I saw this morning that your regiment was ordered from India to Egypt. You could have gone with it, had I not come in your way. There would have been chances of distinction. I have hindered you, and I am very sorry. Of course, you could not know that there was any possibility of your regiment going, but I can understand it is very hard for you to be left behind. I blame myself."

Feversham stared ahead, his voice suddenly hoarse. "You need not."

"How can I help it?" she pressed, her eyes fixed on his face. "I blame myself the more because I do not see things quite like other women. Supposing you had gone to Egypt, and the worst had happened... I should have felt very lonely, of course, all my days. But I should have known quite surely that when those days were over, you and I would see much of one another."

She spoke without hesitation, as if describing a trivial occurrence. Feversham caught his breath, a man in pain, yet he remained still, unable to trust himself to answer. He kept his lips closed, and Ethne continued, her voice softening.

"You see, I can put up with the absence of the people I care about better than most. I do not feel that I have lost them at all." She paused, searching for the right words. "You know how things happen. One goes along in a dull sort of way, and then suddenly a face springs out from the crowd of one's acquaintances, and you know it at once and certainly for the face of a friend. It is almost as though you had come upon someone long looked for and now gladly recovered. Well, such friends—there are few, no doubt, but after all, only the few really count. Such friends one does not lose, whether they are absent, or even—dead."

"Unless," said Feversham, slowly, "one has made a mistake. Suppose the face in the crowd is a mask, what then? One may make mistakes."

Ethne shook her head decidedly. "Of that kind, no. One may seem to have made mistakes, and perhaps for a long while. But in the end, one would be proved not to have made them."

Her implicit faith took hold of him, torturing him until silence became impossible. "Ethne," he cried, "you don't know—"

At that moment, Ethne reined in her horse, laughed, and pointed her whip. They had reached the top of a hill a couple of miles from Ramelton. The road ran between stone walls enclosing open fields on the left and a wood of oaks and beeches on the right. Built into the left-hand wall was a scarlet letter-box.

"I wanted to show you that," she interrupted. "It was there I used to post my letters to you during the anxious times."

Feversham let slip his opportunity of speech. "The letter-box is very convenient," he said.

"Yes," Ethne replied, driving on until they reached a crumbling section of the park wall. "That's where I used to climb over to post the letters. There's a tree on the other side of the wall as convenient as the letter-box. I used to run down the half-mile of avenue at night."

"There might have been thieves," Feversham exclaimed.

"There were thorns," Ethne corrected, turning through the gates to drive up to the porch of the long, irregular grey house. "Well, we have still a day before the dance."

"I suppose the whole countryside is coming," Feversham noted.

"It daren't do anything else," Ethne laughed. "My father would send the police to fetch them if they stayed away, just as he fetched your friend Mr. Durrance here. By the way, Mr. Durrance has sent me a present—a Guarnerius violin."

The door opened, and a thin, lank old man emerged onto the steps. With a fierce peaked face like a bird of prey, he softened instantly into friendliness upon seeing Feversham. A stranger might have thought he winked, though his left eyelid continually drooped over the eye.

"How do you do?" he said. "Glad to see you. Must make yourself at home. If you want any whiskey, stamp twice on the floor with your foot. The servants understand." With that, he retreated inside.

The biographer of Dermod Eustace would need a wary mind to work. Though the old master of Lennon House had not lain in his grave for twenty years, he had already swollen into a legendary character. Anecdotes had grown upon his memory like barnacles. He practiced an ancient, tyrannous hospitality, forcing bed and board upon strangers, and was a man of another century who glowered at a topsy-turvy world, reconciled to it only after much alcohol. He was an intoxicated Coriolanus, believing people should be shepherded with a stick, yet mindful of his manners even to the lowliest. The townsfolk of Ramelton took pride in the fact that even at his worst, galloping down the steep cobbled streets on a big white mare with his inseparable collie dog, he had never ridden down anyone except a man. He was rather afraid of his daughter, who wisely kept him doubtful of her displeasure, and he had conceived a great liking for Harry Feversham.

Harry saw little of him that day. Dermod retired to his office while Feversham and Ethne spent the afternoon fishing for salmon in the Lennon River. It was an afternoon restful as a Sabbath, the birds still. From the house, the lawns fell steeply, shaded by trees and dappled by sunlight, to a valley where the river flowed swift and black under overarching boughs. There was a fall where the water slid over rocks with a smoothness so unbroken it looked solid, except at one point where a spur stood sharply up, and the river broke back upon itself in an amber wave through which the sun shone. Opposite this spur, they sat for a long while, talking at times, but mostly listening to the roar of the water and watching its perpetual flow.

At last, the sunset came, casting long shadows. They stood up, looked at each other with a smile, and walked slowly back to the house. It was an afternoon Feversham was long to remember, for the next night was the night of the dance.

As the band struck up the opening bars of the fourth waltz, Ethne left her position at the drawing-room door. Taking Feversham's arm, she passed out into the hall. The hall was empty, the front door standing open to the cool of the summer night. From the ballroom came the swaying lilt of the music and the beat of the dancers' feet. Ethne drew a breath of relief at her reprieve from her duties, then dropped her partner's arm and crossed to a side table.

"The post is in," she said. "There are letters, one, two, three, for you, and a little box."

She held the box out to him—a little white jeweller's cardboard box—and was struck by its absence of weight. "It must be empty," she said.

Yet it was most carefully sealed and tied. Feversham broke the seals and unfastened the string. He looked at the address. The box had been forwarded from his lodgings, and he was not familiar with the handwriting.

"There is some mistake," he said as he shook the lid open, and then he stopped abruptly. Three white feathers fluttered out of the box, swayed and rocked for a moment in the air, and then, one after another, settled gently down upon the floor. They lay like flakes of snow upon the dark polished boards. But they were not whiter than Harry Feversham's cheeks. He stood and stared at the feathers until he felt a light touch upon his arm. He looked and saw Ethne's gloved hand upon his sleeve.

"What does it mean?" she asked. There was some perplexity in her voice, but nothing more. The smile upon her face and the loyal confidence in her eyes showed she had never a doubt that his first word would lift it from her. "What does it mean?"

"That there are things which cannot be hid, I suppose," said Feversham.

For a little while Ethne did not speak. The languorous music floated into the hall, and the trees whispered from the garden through the open door. Then she shook his arm gently, uttered a breathless little laugh, and spoke as though she were pleading with a child.

"I don't think you understand, Harry. Here are three white feathers. They were sent to you in jest? Oh, of course in jest. But it is a cruel kind of jest—"

"They were sent in deadly earnest."

He spoke now, looking her straight in the eyes. Ethne dropped her hand from his sleeve.

"Who sent them?" she asked.

Feversham had not given a thought to that matter. The message was all in all; the men who had sent it were unimportant. But Ethne reached out her hand and took the box from him. There were three visiting cards lying at the bottom, and she took them out and read them aloud.

"Captain Trench, Mr. Castleton, Mr. Willoughby. Do you know these men?"

"All three are officers of my old regiment."

The girl was dazed. She knelt down upon the floor and gathered the feathers into her hand with a vague thought that merely to touch them would help her to comprehension. They lay upon the palm of her white glove, and she blew gently upon them, and they swam up into the air and hung fluttering and rocking. As they floated downward she caught them again, and so she slowly felt her way to another question.

"Were they justly sent?" she asked.

"Yes," said Harry Feversham.

He had no thought of denial or evasion. He was only aware that the dreadful thing for so many years dreadfully anticipated had at last befallen him. He was known for a coward. The word which had long blazed upon the wall of his thoughts in letters of fire was now written large in the public places. He stood as he had once stood before the portraits of his fathers, mutely accepting condemnation. It was the girl who denied, as she still kneeled upon the floor.

"I do not believe that is true," she said. "You could not look me in the face so steadily were it true. Your eyes would seek the floor, not mine."

"Yet it is true."

"Three little white feathers," she said slowly; and then, with a sob in her throat, "This afternoon we were under the elms down by the Lennon River—do you remember, Harry?—just you and I. And then come three little white feathers, and the world's at an end."

"Oh, don't!" cried Harry, and his voice broke upon the word. Up till now he had spoken with a steadiness matching the steadiness of his eyes. But these last words of hers, the picture which they evoked in his memories, the pathetic simplicity of her utterance, caught him by the heart. But Ethne seemed not to hear the appeal. She was listening with her face turned toward the ballroom. The chatter and laughter of the voices there grew louder and nearer. She understood that the music had ceased. She rose quickly to her feet, clenching the feathers in her hand, and opened a door. It was the door of her sitting room.

"Come," she said.

Harry followed her into the room, and she closed the door, shutting out the noise.

"Now," she said, "will you tell me, if you please, why the feathers have been sent?"

She stood quietly before him; her face was pale, but Feversham could not gather from her expression any feeling which she might have beyond a desire and a determination to get at the truth. She spoke, too, with the same quietude. He answered, as he had answered before, directly and to the point, without any attempt at mitigation.

Habit was a heavy cloak they both slipped on without thinking. The careless chatter of the ballroom spilled from their lips automatically; the mask of enjoyment plastered itself onto their faces so perfectly that no soul in Lennon House that night suspected a swift, violent severance had torn them apart. Harry Feversham watched Ethne laugh and talk as if the world owed her nothing, perpetually surprised by her own performance, failing to notice he wore the same gaunt disguise. When she swung past him, the light, rhythmic thud of her feet nearly convinced him her heart was still in the dance. It seemed she could command the color on her cheeks with a mere thought. Thus, they both wore brave faces, exactly as she had commanded. They even danced together.

Yet, all the while, Ethne carried a great, crushing load of pain and humiliation that threatened to collapse her at any moment, while Feversham felt those four feathers burning hot against his breast. It was a marvel to him that the entire company remained ignorant of their disgrace. He never approached a partner without the terrifying notion that she would turn upon him, spitting the contemptuous name that was now his on her tongue. Yet, strangely, he felt no fear. He would not have cared if the word had been spoken aloud. He had already lost Ethne. He watched her, scanning her circle of guests in vain, searching for a fit comparison to measure himself against. There were women—pretty, graceful, even beautiful—but Ethne stood apart, defined by the particular architecture of her beauty. The broad forehead, the perfect arch of her eyebrows, the great steady, clear grey eyes, the full red lips capable of dimpling into tenderness or shutting level with resolution, and the royal grace of her carriage marked her out to Feversham's thinking, a beacon in any crowd. He watched her with a despairing amazement that he had ever possessed a chance of owning her.

Only once did her endurance crack, and then only for a fleeting second. She was dancing with Feversham, and as she glanced toward the windows, she saw the daylight beginning to bleed through the blinds, pale and cold.

"Look!" she said, and suddenly Feversham felt her entire weight press against his arms. Her face drained of color, turning tired and very grey. Her eyes squeezed shut tight, then fluttered open again. He feared she would faint.

"The morning at last!" she exclaimed, and then, in a voice worn as thin as her complexion, "I wonder whether it is right that one should suffer so much pain."

"Hush!" whispered Feversham. "Courage! A few minutes more—only a very few!" He stopped, standing firm before her until her strength returned.

"Thank you!" she said gratefully, and the bright wheel of the dance caught them in its spokes again.

It was strange that he should be exhorting her to courage while she thanked him for support; but the irony of this queer, momentary reversal of their positions escaped both of them. Ethne was too exhausted by the strain of those final hours, and Feversham, having learned from that single failure of her endurance, from the drawn aspect of her face and the depths of agony in her eyes, understood how deeply he had wounded her. He no longer thought, "I have lost her"; he no longer considered his loss at all. He heard her words, "I wonder whether it is right that one should suffer so much pain," ringing down the world with him, persistent in his ears, spoken with the very accent of her voice. He was certain he would hear them at the end, above the voices of any who should stand around him when he died, hearing in them his condemnation. For it was not right.

The ball finished shortly thereafter. The last carriage drove away, and those staying in the house sought the smoking-room or retired upstairs to bed, segregated by sex. Feversham, however, lingered in the hall with Ethne. She understood why.

"There is no need," she said, standing with her back to him as she lit a candle, "I have told my father. I told him everything."

Feversham bowed his head in acquiescence.

"Still, I must wait and see him," he said.

Ethne did not object, but she turned and looked at him quickly, her brows drawn in a frown of perplexity. To wait for her father under such circumstances seemed to argue a certain courage. Indeed, she felt a flicker of apprehension as she heard the door of the study open and Dermod's footsteps echo on the floor. Dermod walked straight up to Harry Feversham, looking for once truly what he was—a very old man—and stood there staring into Feversham's face with a muddled, bewildered expression. Twice he opened his mouth to speak, but no words came. In the end, he turned to the table, lit his candle and Harry Feversham's, and then turned back toward Feversham. He moved rather quickly, so that Ethne took a step forward as if to interpose herself, but he did nothing more than stare at Feversham again for a long, agonizing time. Finally, he took up his candle.

"Well—" he said, and stopped. He snuffed the wick with the scissors and began again. "Well—" he said, and stopped once more. Apparently, his candle had offered no suitable expressions. He stared into the flame now instead of into Feversham's face, for an equal length of time. He could think of nothing whatever to say, and yet he was conscious that something must be said. In the end, he said lamely:

"If you want any whiskey, stamp twice on the floor with your foot. The servants understand."

Thereupon, he walked heavily up the stairs. The old man's forbearance was perhaps not the least part of Harry Feversham's punishment.

It was broad daylight when Ethne was finally alone within her room. She drew up the blinds and threw the windows wide. The cool, fresh air of the morning struck her like a draught of spring water. She looked out upon a world as yet unillumined by colors, finding therein an image of her days to come. The dark, tall trees looked black; the winding paths, a singular dead white; the very lawns were dull and grey, though the dew lay upon them like a network of frost. It was a noisy world, however, for all its aspect of quiet. For the blackbirds were calling from the branches and the grass, and down beneath the overhanging trees, the Lennon flowed in music between its banks. Ethne drew back from the window. She had much to do that morning before she slept. For she designed, with her natural thoroughness, to make an end at once of all her associations with Harry Feversham. She wished that from the moment she next woke, she might never encounter a single thing that could recall him to her memory. And with a sort of stubborn persistence, she went about the work.

But she changed her mind. In the very process of collecting together the gifts he had given her, she altered her course. For each gift she looked upon held its history, and the days before this miserable night, which had darkened her happiness, came back to her one by one slowly as she gazed at them. She determined to keep one thing that had belonged to Harry Feversham—a small thing, a thing of no value. At first, she chose a penknife he had once lent her and she had forgotten to return. But the next instant she dropped it and hurried away. For she was after all an Irish girl, and though she did not believe in superstitions, where superstitions were concerned, she preferred to be on the safe side. She selected his photograph in the end and locked it away in a drawer.

She gathered the rest of his presents together, packed them carefully in a box, fastened it, addressed it, and carried it down to the hall so the servants might dispatch it in the morning. Then, returning to her room, she took his letters, made a little pile of them on the hearth, and set them alight. They took some time to consume, but she waited, sitting upright in her armchair while the flame crept from sheet to sheet, discolouring the paper, blackening the writing like a stream of ink, and leaving in the end only flakes of ashes like feathers, and white flakes like white feathers. The last sparks were barely extinguished when she heard a cautious step on the gravel beneath her window.

It was broad daylight, but her candle was still burning on the table at her side, and with a quick, instinctive movement, she reached out her arm and put the light out. Then she sat very still and rigid, listening. For a while, she heard only the blackbirds calling from the trees in the garden and the throbbing music of the river. Afterward, she heard the footsteps again, cautiously retreating; and in spite of her will, in spite of her formal disposal of the letters and the presents, she was mastered all at once—not by pain or humiliation, but by an overpowering sense of loneliness. She seemed to be seated high on an empty world of ruins. She rose quickly from her chair, and her eyes fell upon a violin case. With a sigh of relief, she opened it, and a little while after, one or two of the guests sleeping in the house chanced to wake up and heard floating down the corridors the music of a violin played very lovingly and low. Ethne was not aware that the violin she held was the Guarnerius that Durrance had sent to her. She only understood that she had a companion to share her loneliness.

The night of August 30th arrived, a month having elapsed since the festivities at Lennon House. Yet the quiet countryside of Donegal remained abuzz with the sensational topic of Harry Feversham's vanishing. In the winding streets of the town and around the dinner tables of the gentry, gossip flourished with a fervor that bordered on the manic. The townsfolk insisted they had spotted Harry on the very morning following the dance, precisely at five minutes to six—though Mrs. Brien O'Brien, with her characteristic precision, corrected them to ten minutes past—clad in his formal attire and bearing the pallid visage of a man facing suicide, hastening along the causeway beside Lennon Bridge. A drag-net was proposed as the sole solution to the riddle. Mr. Dennis Rafferty, residing on the road to Rathmullen, even refused a plate of salmon, declaring himself no cannibal, a sentiment that quickly became the toast of the town. Yet their speculations remained as distant from the truth as ever. Only two individuals possessed the reality of the situation, and they conducted their daily lives with a steady, almost defiant normalcy. Perhaps Ethne had softened slightly, and Dermod grown more irritable, but these were the only shifts. Gossip reigned supreme, unaware of the truth.

In stark contrast, Harry Feversham was in London. Lieutenant Sutch confirmed this fact on the night of the 30th. Throughout the day, the city had trembled under the weight of rumors regarding a massive engagement at Kassassin in the desert east of Ismailia. Messengers darted through the streets, heralding alternating victories and disasters. There had been a moonlit charge by General Drury-Lowe's Cavalry Brigade, which reportedly rolled up Arabi's left flank and seized his artillery. Rumors swirled of an English general slain and the York and Lancaster Regiment decimated. London hung in a state of anxious suspense. By eleven o'clock, a vast, silent crowd had gathered beneath the gas-lamps of Pall Mall, their pale faces turned upward toward the illuminated blinds of the War Office. The atmosphere was heavy with stillness. Only when a shadow briefly crossed the glass did a ripple of anticipation pass through the throng, causing them to sway in a unified, expectant motion.

Lieutenant Sutch, mindful of his injured leg, stood near the edge of the crowd, his back against the parapet of the Junior Carlton Club, when a hand touched his arm. He turned to find Harry Feversham beside him. Feversham's face was agitated, unnaturally pale, his eyes burning with the intensity of a fever. For a fleeting moment, Sutch was unsure if he recognized the man speaking to him.

"I could have been out there in Egypt tonight," Harry said, his voice quick and troubled. "Think of it! I could have been sitting by a campfire in the desert, debating the battle with Jack Durrance; or perhaps dead. What difference would it have made? I could have been in Egypt tonight!"

Feversham's sudden appearance, coupled with his erratic speech, signaled to Sutch that his friend's fortunes had taken a disastrous turn. Questions swarmed in Sutch's mind, yet he voiced none. He took Feversham's arm and guided him away from the press.

"I saw you in the crowd," Feversham continued. "I intended to speak to you because—do you recall, some time ago, you gave me your card? I have kept it, fearing I might need it. You said that in times of trouble, reaching out could help."

Sutch halted his companion.

"We shall go inside. There is a quiet corner in the upper smoking-room," he suggested. Harry looked up, realizing they were at the steps of the Army and Navy Club.

"Good heavens, not there!" he cried in a sharp whisper, moving swiftly into the shadow of the roadway where the lights did not fall directly upon him. Sutch limped after him. "Not tonight. It is late. Tomorrow, if you will, in some secluded place, after dark. I do not venture out in daylight."

Once more, Lieutenant Sutch asked no questions.

"I know a quiet restaurant," Sutch offered. "If we dine there at nine, we shall encounter no acquaintances. I shall meet you just before nine tomorrow night at the corner of Swallow Street."

They met as planned the following evening at a table in the corner of the Criterion grill-room. As Feversham entered, he scanned the room with nervous glances.

"I frequent this place often when in town," Sutch remarked. "Listen!" The rhythmic thrumming of the engines powering the electric lights was distinct, their vibrations palpable.

"It reminds me of a ship," Sutch smiled. "I can almost imagine myself back in the gun-room. Let us eat. Then you may tell me your story."

"Have you heard nothing of it?" Feversham asked, suspicion coloring his tone.

"Not a word," Sutch replied. Feversham exhaled in relief; he had feared every passerby knew.

Sutch appeared more concerned this evening than the night before. In the full light, Harry Feversham looked gaunt and haggard, his eyes sunken in dark hollows. He breathed and moved with a restless, feverish agitation, his nerves seemingly stretched to their breaking point. Twice between courses, he attempted to begin his tale, but Sutch waited until the cloth was removed.

"Now," he said, offering his cigar-case. "Take your time, Harry."

Feversham recounted the entire truth, neither exaggerating nor omitting details. He forced himself into a slow, deliberate, matter-of-fact delivery, creating an illusion that he was merely recounting a stranger's tale to pass the time. He began with the Crimean night at Broad Place and concluded with the ball at Lennon House.

"I returned across Lough Swilly early that morning," he said finally. "I traveled immediately to London. Since then, I have remained in my rooms all day, listening to the bugles calling in the barrack-yard beneath my windows. At night, I wander the streets or lie in bed waiting for the Westminster clock to strike each quarter. On foggy nights, I can hear the steam-sirens on the river. Do you know when the ducks begin to quack in St. James's Park?" he asked with a dry laugh. "Precisely at two o'clock."

Sutch listened without interruption. However, midway through the narrative, his demeanor shifted significantly. Until Harry mentioned his concealment of the telegram, Sutch had sat with his arms on the table, his gaze fixed on his companion. From that point onward, he raised a hand to his forehead, shielding his face while the remainder of the story unfolded. Feversham understood the reason instantly. Lieutenant Sutch wished to hide the scorn he felt, unable to trust his facial muscles. Yet Feversham mitigated nothing; he continued steadily and truthfully to the end. "It is yours," she said. "You were in Egypt. I could not bear to send it back to you there."

"I have hoped lately, since I learned of it," Durrance replied, "that you might accept it."

"You see I have," Ethne said, meeting his gaze directly. "I accepted it some time ago. There was a time when I needed assurance that I had sure friends. Something tangible helped. I was very glad to have it."

Durrance lifted the instrument from the table, handling it with the delicacy due to a sacred vessel.

"You have played upon it? Perhaps the Musoline overture?" he asked.

"Do you remember that?" she returned, laughing. "Yes, I have played it, but only recently. For a long time I kept it away. It spoke to me too intimately of many things I wished to forget," and she spoke these words without hesitation or a lowering of her eyes.

Durrance collected his luggage from Rathmullen the following day and remained at the farm for a week. Yet, until the final hour of his visit, neither Ethne nor Durrance mentioned Harry Feversham, despite spending much time together. Dermod was even more broken than Mrs. Adair's descriptions had suggested. His speech had shrunk to monosyllables; his frame had wasted away, his clothes hanging loosely on his limbs, his stature diminished. Even the anger had vanished from his eyes. He had become a recline, dozing most of the day by a fire, even in the heat of July. His longest excursion was to the small grey church standing naked on a mound a quarter-mile away, a journey that taxed his remaining strength. He was an old man fallen into decrepitude, barely recognizable, so that his gestures and the rare tones of his voice struck Durrance as painfully artificial, like the mimicry of a dead man. His collie dog seemed to age in his company; seeing them side by side, one might have said they shared a mutual sympathy.

Consequently, Durrance and Ethne were thrown much together. During the day, whether the weather was wet or fine, they tramped the hills. She, her face glowing with color and her eyes burning with jealous eagerness, showed him her country and demanded his admiration. In the evenings, she would take her violin and, sitting as of old with an averted face, bid the strings speak of heights and depths. Durrance sat watching the sweep of her arm and the absorption of her face, silently calculating his chances. He had not arrived at Glenalla with Lieutenant Sutch's expectation that he would succeed. The shadow of Harry Feversham might well stand between them. Furthermore, Durrance knew that poverty would weigh more heavily on him than on most women. He had proof of this: though the Lennon House was ruined and its lands gone, Ethne remained among her own people. They still awaited her visits; she was still the princess of that countryside.

On the other hand, she took a frank pleasure in his company and led him to speak of his three years of service in the East. No detail was too insignificant for her inquiries. As he spoke, her eyes constantly scanned him, and the smile on her lips continually approved. Durrance did not understand what she sought. Possibly, no one could have understood unless they knew what had passed between Harry Feversham and Ethne. Durrance wore the likeness of a man, and she was anxious to ensure that the spirit of a man informed it. He was a dark lantern to her; there might be a flame burning within, or there might be only vacancy and darkness. She was pushing back the slide to be sure.

She brought up Egypt on the last day of his visit. They sat on the hillside at the edge of a stream that leaped from ledge to ledge down a miniature gorge of rock, flowing over deep pools between the ledges as a torrent of clear black water.

"I traveled once for four days amongst the mirages," he said. "Lagoons, still as a mirror and fringed with misty trees. You could almost walk your camel up to its knees in them before the lagoon receded and the sand glared at you. One cannot imagine that glare. Every stone within view dances and shakes like a heliograph; you can see—yes, actually see—the heat flow breast-high across the desert swift as this stream here, only pellucid. So until the sun sets ahead of you, level with your eyes! Imagine the nights that follow—nights of infinite silence, with a cool, friendly wind blowing from horizon to horizon—and your bed spread for you under the great dome of stars. Oh," he cried, drawing a deep breath, "but that country grows on you. It's like the Southern Cross—four overrated stars when first you see them, but in a week you begin to look for them, and you miss them when you travel north again." He raised himself on his elbow and turned suddenly toward her. "Do you know—I can only speak for myself—but I never feel alone in those empty spaces. On the contrary, I always feel very close to the things I care about, and to the few people I care about too."

Her eyes shone brightly upon him, her lips parted in a smile. He moved nearer to her on the grass, sitting with his feet gathered under him on one side and leaning on his arm.

"I used to imagine you out there," he said. "You would have loved it—from the start before daybreak in the dark to the campfire at night. You would have been at home. I used to think so as I lay awake wondering how the world went with my friends."

"And you go back there?" she asked.

Durrance did not answer immediately. The roar of the torrent throbbed around them. When he finally spoke, all enthusiasm had drained from his voice. He gazed into the stream.

"To Wadi Halfa. For two years. I suppose so."

Ethne knelt on the grass at his side.

"I shall miss you," she said.

She knelt just behind him as he sat on the ground, and again a silence fell between them.

"Of what are you thinking?"

"That you need not miss me," he said, aware that she drew back and sank down on her heels. "My appointment at Halfa—I might shorten its term. I might perhaps avoid it altogether. I have still half my furlough."

She did not answer, nor did she change her posture. She remained very still, and Durrance was alarmed. All his hopes sank. He knew that a stillness of attitude was with her as definite an expression of distress as a cry of pain with another woman. He turned toward her. Her head was bent, but she raised it as he turned, and though her lips smiled, there was a look of great trouble in her eyes. Durrance was a man like another. His first thought was whether there was not some obstacle hindering her compliance, even though she herself were willing.

"There is your father," he said.

"Yes," she answered. "There is my father too. I could not leave him."

"Nor need you," he said quickly. "That difficulty can be surmounted. To tell the truth, I was not thinking of your father at the moment."

"Nor was I," she said.

Durrance turned away and sat for a while staring down the rocks into a wrinkled pool of water just beneath. It was, after all, the shadow of Feversham that stretched between himself and her.

"I know, of course," he said, "that you would never feel trouble, as so many do, with half your heart. You would neither easily care nor lightly forget."

"I remember enough," she returned in a low voice, "to make your words rather a pain to me. Some day perhaps I may bring myself to tell everything that happened at that ball three years ago, and then you will be better able to understand why I am a little distressed. All that I can tell you now is this: I have a great fear that I was to some degree the cause of another man's ruin. I do not mean that I was to blame for it. But if I had not been known to him, his career might perhaps never have come to such an abrupt end. I am not sure, but I am afraid. I asked whether it was so, and I was told 'no,' but I think very likely that generosity dictated that answer. And the fear stays. I am much distressed by it. I lie awake with it at night. And then you come, whom I greatly value, and you say quietly, 'Will you please spoil my career too?'" She struck one hand sharply into the other and cried, "But that I will not do."

And again he answered:

"There is no need that you should. Wadi Halfa is not the only place where a soldier can find work to his hand."

His voice had taken on a new hopefulness. For he had listened intently to her words and construed them by the dictionary of his desires. She had not said that friendship bounded all her thoughts of him. Therefore, he need not believe it. Women were given to a hinting modesty of speech, at all events the best of them. A man might read a little more emphasis into their tones, underline their words, and still be short of their meaning, as he argued. A subtle delicacy graced them in nature. Durrance was near to Benedick's mood. "One whom I value"; "I shall miss you"; there might be a double meaning in the phrases. When she said that she needed to be assured that she had sure friends, did she not mean that she needed their companionship? But the argument, had he been acute enough to see it, proved how deep he was sunk in error. For what this girl spoke, she habitually meant, and she habitually meant no more. Moreover, on this occasion, she had particularly weighed her words.

"No doubt," she said, "a soldier can. But can this soldier find work so suitable? Listen, please, till I have done. I was so very glad to hear all that you have told me about your work and your journeys. I was still more glad because of the satisfaction with which you told it. For it seemed to me, as I listened and as I watched, that you had found the one true straight channel along which your life could run swift and smoothly and unharassed. And so few do that—so very few!" She wrung her hands and cried, "And now you spoil it all."

Durrance suddenly faced her. He ceased arguing; he cried in a voice of passion:

"I am for you, Ethne! There's the true straight channel, and upon my word I believe you are for me. I thought—I admit it—at one time I would spend my life out there in the East, and the thought contented me. But I had schooled myself into contentment, for I believed you married." Ethne ever so slightly flinched, and he himself recognized that he had spoken in a voice overloud, so that it had something almost of brutality.

"Do I hurt you?" he continued. "I am sorry. But let me speak the whole truth out; I cannot afford reticence. I want you to know the first and last of it. I say now that I love you. Yes, but I could have said it with equal truth five years ago. It is five years since your father arrested me at the ferry down there on Lough Swilly, because I wished to press on to Letterkenny and not delay a night by stopping with a stranger. Five years since I first saw you, first heard the language of your violin. I remember how you sat with your back towards me. The light shone on your hair; I could just see your eyelashes and the color of your cheeks. I remember the sweep of your arm.... My dear, you are for me; I am for you."

But she drew back from his outstretched hands.

"No," she said very gently, but with a decision he could not mistake. She saw more clearly into his mind than he did himself. The restlessness of the born traveler, the craving for the large and lonely spaces in the outlandish corners of the world, the incurable intermittent fever to be moving,

The month of May brought a restless energy to Durrance, whose gaze lifted from the dusty horizon of Wadi Halfa, eager to turn toward home. Yet, unaware of the gravity unfolding five hundred miles to the south, across the vast Nubian expanse and the treacherous Belly of Stones, a fate far more critical to his own destiny was being woven in silence.

On the abandoned track between Berber and Suakin, the Wells of Obak lay buried deep within shifting dunes. To the east, a fringe of trees marked the boundary between the sands and a stony plain crowned with granite hills; to the west, the desert stretched fifty-eight miles of unbroken waterlessness toward Mahobey and the Nile. It was a landscape of deceptive flatness, where a mere tuft of grass could be mistaken for a tree promising shade, and a small pile of stones could loom like a mountain. In this particular May, no place could be more desolate. The sun hammered the earth from dawn until dusk with unbearable intensity, while all night long a biting wind swept across the sands, sculpting pyramids of sand only to level them, carving valleys and filling slopes, ensuring the face of the country was in constant flux. Vultures and sand-grouse held dominion over this wasteland, their presence undisturbed. Scattered among the dunes lay the bleached skeletons of camels, silent testimony to caravans long vanished, alongside the rotting remains of a branch-and-hoop house where Arabs once herded goats. Here, the sun rose and set over a hollow circle of honey-colored earth. Silence hung heavy, like night over water, turning the absolute stillness into a place of mystery and waiting.

Yet, in this month of May, one man sojourned secretly at the wells. Each morning at sunrise, he drove two camels—swift riding-mares of the pure Bisharin breed—from the shelter of the trees. He watered them, then sat by the well-mouth for three hours, watching the dunes and the distant town of Berber. Afterward, he drove them back into the trees, feeding them delicately on *dhoura* spread upon a cloth, and vanished for the rest of the day. For five mornings, he repeated this ritual, unseen and unapproached.

On the sixth morning, just as he prepared to return to his hiding place, figures appeared against the sky on a sand crest: a man driving a donkey. The Arab sitting by the well glanced at the animal, noting its grey coat, and half-risen, he paused. His gaze shifted to the driver, whose *jellab* was pulled tight over his face against the glare, revealing bare legs of ebony blackness against the pale sand. The driver was a negro. The Arab sat back down, feigning the most complete indifference, waiting for the stranger to approach. He did not flinch when he heard the soft tread of feet behind him.

"Salam aleikum," the negro said, stopping. He carried a long spear, a short one, and a hide shield, which he laid on the ground before sitting beside the Arab.

The Arab bowed his head. "Aleikum es salam," he replied, waiting.

"It is Abou Fatma?" the negro asked.

The Arab nodded.

"Two days ago," the negro continued, "a man of the Bisharin, Moussa Fedil, stopped me in the market-place of Berber. Seeing I was hungry, he gave me food. When I had eaten, he charged me to drive this donkey to Abou Fatma at the Wells of Obak."

Abou Fatma glanced at the donkey with careless detachment, as if noticing it for the first time. "Tayeeb," he said equally casually. "The donkey is mine." He sat motionless, signaling that the business was concluded and the stranger might depart.

But the negro held his ground. "I am to meet Moussa Fedil again on the third morning from now, in the market-place of Berber. Give me a token to carry back, so he knows I have fulfilled the charge and can reward me."

Abou Fatma drew his knife from his small of the back, picked up a stick from the ground, and notched it thrice at each end. "This shall be a sign to Moussa Fedil," he said, handing the stick to his companion. The negro secured it in the corner of his wrap, loosened the water-skin from the donkey's back, filled it at the well, and slung it over his shoulders. Picking up his spears and shield, he began the laborious climb up the loose sand slope. Abou Fatma watched him disappear over the crest, then rose hastily in his turn. When Harry Feversham had departed from Obak six days prior to cross the barren desert to the Nile, this grey donkey had carried his water-skins and provisions.

Abou Fatma drove the donkey down among the trees and fastened it to a stem. Examining the animal's shoulders, he found a tiny incision in the left shoulder, neatly stitched with fine thread. He cut the stitches, pressed the wound open, and extracted a tiny package no larger than a postage stamp. It was a goat's bladder containing a note written in Arabic and folded small. Abou Fatma had not served as Gordon's body-servant in vain; he had learned to read during his tenure. Unfolding the note, he read:

*"The houses which were once Berber are destroyed, and a new town of wide streets is building. There is no longer any sign by which I may know the ruins of Yusef's house from the ruins of a hundred houses; nor does Yusef any longer sell rock-salt in the bazaar. Yet wait for me another week."*

The author of this letter was Harry Feversham. Clad in the patched *jubbeh* of the Dervishes over his stained skin, his hair frizzed on his crown and falling in matted, seaweed-like locks down his neck, he wandered the wide, pit-ridden streets of New Berber in search of Yusef. To the south, separated by a mile of desert, lay the old town where Abou Fatma had once slept and hidden letters—a warren of ruined houses facing narrow alleys. Front walls had been pulled down, roofs carried away, leaving only bare inner walls standing. As Feversham wandered among them at night, the place seemed a labyrinth of long, crumbling courts, indistinguishable from one another save by degrees of decay. Foxes already dug their burrows beneath the foundations.

He had calculated that one night should suffice for his stay in Berber. His plan was to creep through the gate at dusk and face the journey to Obak before the stars faded. Now, however, he must move steadily among the crowds like a man with urgent business, dreading conversation lest his tongue betray him, listening intently for the name Yusef to strike his ears. Despair visited him at times, fear always. Yet from these sharp pangs arose a kind of madness, a frenzy of obstinacy, a fanatical belief that he could not fail and that the universe held no injustice great enough to burden the one man least fitted to bear it, only to destroy him callously for his effort.

Fear gripped him on that morning, three days after leaving Abou Fatma, as he crested a slope and saw the sand stretch like a lagoon to the dark brown walls of the town, with the overshadowing foliage of great date palms rising on the Nile bank beyond. Within those walls crowded the Dervishes. It seemed sheer madness to imagine escaping detection there, even for an hour. Was it right, he wondered, even to try? The longer he stood, the more insistent the question became. The low mud walls grew sinister; the welcoming green of the palms, after so many arid days of sun and stone, became an ironic invitation to death. He began to wonder if he had already done enough for honor by venturing so near.

The sun beat down upon him; his strength ebbed as though his veins were opened. If he were caught—as surely he would be—he saw the fanatical faces crowding fiercely about him. Were mutilations practiced here? He looked about, shivering in the strong heat, the great loneliness of the place striking him so hard his knees shook. He turned and began to run, leaping in panic across the naked desert under the sun, while feeble, inarticulate cries broke from his throat.

He ran only a few yards before the very violence of his flight stopped him. Were four years of anticipation nothing? Had he schooled himself in the tongue, lived in the bazaars, for no end? Was he still the craven who had surrendered his papers? The quiet confidence with which he had revealed his plan to Lieutenant Sutch in the Criterion grill-room was merely the vainglory of a man deceiving himself? And Ethne?

He dropped to the ground, drawing his coat over his head until he was a brown spot indistinguishable from the sand, an irregularity in the great waste. He shut out the prospect, and over thousands of miles of continent and sea, he summoned Ethne's face to his mind. Soon, he was back in Donegal. The summer night whispered through the open doorway of the hall; in a nearby room, people danced to music. He saw the three feathers flutter to the floor; he read the growing trouble in Ethne's face. If he could accomplish this thing, and the even harder task lying beyond, perhaps someday he might see that face cleared of its sorrow. Significant words echoed in his ears: *"I should have no doubt that you and I would see much of one another afterwards."*

As the sun began to set, he rose from the ground and walked down toward Berber, passing between the gates.

A month had passed since the dust of the Sudan settled on Durrance's boots, and he stood once more in the bustling heart of London. His club awaited him, but it was a letter from Ethne that truly summoned him. The envelope bore a stark, black border—a somber frame around the news inside. She wrote simply that she was staying with Mrs. Adair and requested his presence, yet the darkness of the border spoke volumes of the sorrow within.

The next afternoon, Durrance found Ethne alone in the quiet drawing room of Hill Street. The air felt heavier than usual, charged with a grief that had not yet found its voice.

"I did not write to Wadi Halfa," she explained immediately, her gaze steady but distant. "I believed you would be returning home before my words could reach you. My father died last month, towards the end of May."

Durrance felt the familiar ache in his chest. "I feared this when I received your letter," he said softly. "I am deeply sorry. You will miss him."

"More than I can express," she replied, her voice carrying a quiet, profound weight. "He died one morning early—I believe I should tell you the circumstances if you are willing to listen."

She recounted the passing of Dermod, a narrative where the chill of the morning was merely the setting, not the cause. It was a slow dissolution, a fading rather than a sudden strike. Yet, in his final hours, Dermod had reclaimed a fragment of his old, masterful spirit.

"We knew he was dying," Ethne said, her eyes fixed on him. "He knew it too. At seven o'clock, the afternoon after our conversation, he called his dog by name. The beast sprang instantly onto the bed, despite his voice being no more than a whisper. Crouching close, it pressed its muzzle beneath my father's hand with a soft whine. Then he commanded me to leave him and the dog entirely alone. I was to shut the door upon us. The dog would signal when I might open it again."

She paused, her composure unbroken. "I obeyed. I waited outside until one o'clock. Then, a loud, sudden howl moaned through the house." She stopped, the only crack in her armor, before continuing with simple, unadorned honesty. "It was the agony of waiting in that passage as the afternoon bled into night. Long before the end, the room would have been dark. No lamp remained. One could imagine him lying still in the great four-poster bed, his face turned toward the hills, as the light faded. The room slipping into shadow, the windows looming larger in the gloom, the dog by his side, and no one else. He demanded it be so, but it was a cruelty I could not bear."

Durrance said nothing, offering instead the deep comfort of his silence. He pictured those six hours of twilight and darkness, her standing by the door, her hand pressed to her heart to stifle its frantic rhythm. There was something harsh in Dermod's resolve to face the end in solitude.

"It was before he told me to leave him," Ethne said, breaking the heavy quiet. "He spoke to someone. Of whom do you think he spoke?"

She looked directly at Durrance. There was no tremor in her eyes, no wavering in her tone, yet a sudden, electric thrum of hope seized Durrance's breath.

"Tell me," he urged, leaning forward, suspended in a tense anticipation.

"Of Mr. Feversham," she answered.

Durrance recoiled, pulling back sharply. It was not the name he had expected. He cast his eyes downward to the carpet, hiding the turmoil on his face.

"My father was always very fond of him," Ethne continued gently. "I would like to know if you have any knowledge of what he is doing, or where he is."

Durrance remained silent, his face averted. He reflected on the strange, enduring grip Harry Feversham held over the hearts of those who had known him. Even the man he had wronged, the father who had suffered so much at his hands, remembered him with kindness on his deathbed. A bitter taste filled Durrance's mouth, a reflection he feared might betray itself in his expression or voice. Compelled by Ethne's insistence, he finally spoke.

"You have never encountered him, I suppose?" she asked.

Durrance rose and walked to the window, staring out into the street to conceal his features. Yet, a surge of deep anger colored his words, escaping his control.

"No," he said. "I never have." Suddenly, the anger took the reins. "And I never wish to." He turned back to the center of the room, his voice abrupt. "He was my friend, I know. But I cannot remember that friendship now. I can only think that if he had been the true man we took him for, you would not have waited alone in that dark passage during those six hours."

"You are going back to Glenalla?" he asked.

"Yes."

"You will live there alone?"

"Yes."

Silence stretched between them, thick and unyielding. Then Durrance moved behind her chair.

"You once said you might tell me why your engagement was broken off."

"But you know," she replied. "What you said at the window proved you knew."

"No, I do not. A few words your father dropped. He asked me for news of Feversham the last time we spoke. But I know nothing definite. I should like you to tell me."

Ethne shook her head, leaning forward with her elbows on her knees. "Not now," she said. Silence returned, heavy once more. Durrance broke it again.

"I have only one more year at Halfa. It would be wise to leave Egypt then, I think. I do not expect much will be done in the Soudan for some little while. I do not think I will stay there—in any case. I mean, even if you decide to remain alone at Glenalla."

Ethne made no pretense of ignoring the implication. "We are neither of us children," she said. "You have all your life to think of. We should be prudent."

"Yes," Durrance snapped, a sudden exasperation rising in him. "But the right kind of prudence. The prudence which knows that it is worth while to dare a good deal."

Ethne did not move. Her back was to him, her face hidden, and for a long while she remained still and silent. Finally, she asked a question in a very low, gentle voice. "Do you want me so very much?"

Before he could answer, she turned quickly toward him. "Try not to," she exclaimed earnestly. "For this one year, try not to. You have much to occupy your thoughts. Try to forget me altogether." There was just enough regret in her tone, a sorrow at the prospect of losing a valued friend, to soften the sting of her words. It confirmed Durrance's delusion that but for her fear of ruining his career, she would have answered differently. But before he could reply, Mrs. Adair entered the room, and Durrance departed carrying that illusion with him.

That evening, he dined at his club and afterward sat smoking his cigar beneath the great tree where he had sat a year prior, vainly seeking news of Harry Feversham. The night was clear and starry, the air possessing the pleasant languor of a summer June. Lights flashed from windows and doorways, giving the leaves a fresh, spring-like green, while carriages rolled along the roadway with a thunderous hum like the sea.

On this night, too, a man entered the courtyard who knew Durrance. But unlike the previous visitor, Lieutenant Sutch, who had hesitated, this man approached without pause. He sat directly beside Durrance. Durrance dropped the paper he was reading and extended his hand.

"How do you do?" he asked. This was Captain Mather.

"I was wondering if I should meet you when I read the evening paper," Mather said. "I knew it was about the time one might expect to find you in London. You have seen, I suppose?"

"What?" Durrance asked.

"Then you haven't," Mather replied, picking up the dropped newspaper and flipping through the pages to locate the specific news. "You remember that last reconnaissance we made from Suakin?"

"Very well."

"We halted by the Sinkat fort at midday. There was an Arab hiding in the trees at the back of the glacis."

"Yes."

"Have you forgotten the yarn he told you?"

"About Gordon's letters and the wall of a house in Berber? No, I have not forgotten."

"Then here is something that will interest you," Mather said, folding the paper and handing it to Durrance, pointing to a paragraph. It was brief, a mere summary, devoid of detail. Durrance read it between puffs of his cigar.

"The fellow must have gone back to Berber after all," Mather remarked. "A risky business. Abou Fatma—that was the man's name."

The paragraph made no mention of Abou Fatma, nor of any man except Captain Willoughby, the Deputy-Governor of Suakin. It merely announced that certain letters the Mahdi had sent to Gordon, summoning him to surrender Khartum and inviting conversion to the Mahdist religion, along with copies of Gordon's curt replies, had been recovered from a wall in Berber and brought safely to Captain Willoughby.

"They were hardly worth risking a life for," Mather said.

"Perhaps not," Durrance replied, with a touch of doubt. "But after all, one is glad they have been recovered. Perhaps the copies are in Gordon's own hand. They are, at all events, of historic interest."

"In a way, no doubt," Mather agreed. "But even so, their recovery throws no light upon the history of the siege. It can make no real difference to anyone, not even to the historian."

"That is true," Durrance conceded, and there was nothing more untrue. In the very spot where he had sought news of Feversham, news had now come to him—though he did not know it. He was in the dark, unable to appreciate that here was information which, however insignificant to the historian, touched the springs of his own life. He dismissed the paragraph from his mind and sat pondering the conversation of that afternoon with Ethne, undeterred.

Ethne had indeed mentioned Harry Feversham, asking for news of him. But she might have been—nay, she probably had been—moved to ask because her father's last words had referred to him. She had spoken his name in a perfectly steady voice; indeed, the mere fact that she had spoken it at all might signify that it no longer held power over her. There was something hopeful in her request that he try, during this one year, to omit her from his thoughts. It seemed almost to imply that if he could not, she might, at the end of it, give him the answer he longed for.

He allowed a few days to pass before calling again at Mrs. Adair's house. But he found only Mrs. Adair. Ethne had left London and returned to Donegal. She had departed rather suddenly, Mrs. Adair told him, with no certain knowledge of the reason.

Durrance, however, had no doubt as to the cause. Ethne was putting into practice the policy she had commended to his thoughts. He was to try to forget her, and she would aid his success by her absence from his sight. In attributing this reason to her, Durrance was right. But one thing Ethne had forgotten. She had not asked him to cease writing to her. Accordingly, in the autumn of that year, the letters began again to arrive from the Soudan.

She was frankly glad to receive them, yet simultaneously troubled. For in spite of their careful reticence, phrases would occasionally leap out—a repetition of some trivial sentence she had spoken long ago and forgotten. She could not help but see that, in spite of her prayers, she lived perpetually in his thoughts. There was also a strain of hopefulness, as though he moved in a world painted with new colors and suddenly grown musical.

Ethne had never freed herself from the haunting fear that one man's life had been spoiled because of her; she had never faltered in her determination that this should not happen to a second. Only with Durrance's letters before her could she not evade a new and perplexing question. By what means was that possibility to be avoided? There were two ways. By choosing which of them could she fulfill her determination? She was no longer so sure as she had been the year before that his career was all in all. The question recurred to her again and again. She took it out with her on the hillside, accompanied by the letters, pondering and puzzling over it, gaining never an inch nearer to a solution. Even her violin failed her in this strait.

The moon hung heavy and full over Wadi Halfa, washing the night in a pale, pearlescent glow that stripped the stars of their fire. On a grassy knoll overlooking the sluggish Nile, three officers sat in silence, their cigars glowing like embers against the vast, white expanse of the desert below. The river mirrored the sky, a burnished sheet of water separating the garrison from the infinite, jewel-studded plain.

Among them sat Colonel Dawson, a man of middle years whose face was etched with the worry of command. Beside him, Lieutenant Calder, young and grey-haired, smoked with a gloom that matched his friend's anxiety. The third, Major Walters, a tall Major of the Army Service Corps, offered the only cheer in the room.

"He's three weeks overdue," Dawson muttered, tugging at his moustache. "Methodical men don't vanish without a trace."

"Give Durrance a campfire and a couple of sheikhs," Walters countered, gesturing to the empty desert. "He'd buck to them for a month. He's not lost, Colonel. He's just taking his time."

But the silence between them was thick with a different kind of fear. It wasn't just the missing man; it was the memory of the night before he left.

"I remember that night," Calder said, his voice dropping. "He didn't go to his quarters. He walked down to Tewfikieh."

Calder described the scene: the noisy frontier town where dialects clashed and footsteps sank into the sand. He recounted how Durrance had arrived at ten, restless and agitated, expecting a visitor who never came. When Calder checked on him at four the next morning, Durrance was still sitting on the verandah, head bowed, refusing to speak even as he mounted his camel. He had ridden out into the desert, head sunk in his breast, as if fleeing a ghost.

"And who was this visitor?" Dawson asked, leaning forward.

"I don't know," Calder admitted, his eyes darting around the table. "But I saw something that night. Durrance stopped at a café where a Greek musician was playing for his supper. They talked. Just for a moment. That's the only conversation I witnessed."

As he spoke, Calder felt a chill. He looked up and froze. Standing in the doorway of the mess-room, dusting the red brick color from his sunburned face, was Darrance himself.

The room erupted.

"When did you get back?" Major Walters cried, springing up.

Durrance stepped onto the verandah, his movements stiff, his eyes fixed on nothing in particular. "The delay was unavoidable," he said, his voice flat.

Calder watched him closely. Durrance sat, but he didn't reach for the food. When the waiter brought a brandy-and-soda, Durrance didn't take it; he simply held out his hand, letting the waiter pour it into his palm. It was a small gesture, but to Calder, it was a confession.

"You're taking life easily this morning," Durrance said abruptly, breaking the tension.

"I have eight weeks' arrears of letters to clear," Calder replied with a nervous laugh.

Durrance's face clouded. "True. I forgot." He rose and walked into the mess-room, heading straight for his office. Calder followed, his heart hammering. He knew what waited inside: letters from a woman, written in a hand he recognized from rare, whispered confidences.

Inside the office, Durrance collapsed into his chair, burying his face in his arms. Calder entered, slamming the door shut.

"I have a paper requiring your signature," Calder said, trying to steady his voice.

Durrance didn't move. "Very well. Bring it."

Calder stepped forward, holding out his pipe. Durrance reached out, his hand trembling, and grasped the stem. The hot bowl burned his skin, and he jerked his arm back, dropping the broken pipe to the floor.

"How did it happen?" Calder asked softly, putting an arm around his friend's shaking shoulders.

Durrance buried his face in his hands. "A high wind took my helmet. I ran after it... I went down like a log."

He told the story of the fall, of the confusion between day and night, and finally, the realization that he could see nothing. He had gone blind.

Calder fetched the Syrian doctor, who confirmed the worst: the optic nerve was destroyed. There was no cure. The silence that followed was heavy, broken only by the rustle of papers.

"There are two letters here," Calder said gently, picking up the envelopes from the desk. "From a woman. An Irish postmark."

"No," Durrance exclaimed, grabbing Calder's arm. "Don't open them."

"But you've been away eight weeks," Calder insisted. "You need to know."

Durrance hesitated, then relented. "Read them. Please."

Calder opened the letters. They were simple, filled with descriptions of the Donegal countryside, yet radiating a warmth and a fierce protectiveness. The writer, Ethne Eustace, spoke of Durrance's career as if it were her own, her love evident in every line. Reading them, Calder felt a strange mixture of pity and hope.

Afterward, Durrance laughed, a harsh, brittle sound. "Think! Suppose I had been engaged to her! She would never have let me break it off once I went blind. What an escape!"

"An escape?" Calder asked, confused.

"Yes. I knew a man who went blind. A good fellow. A year later, he had narrowed down into the most selfish, egotistical beast imaginable. He couldn't cross a road without his wife at his elbow, and he became a monster of jealousy. I'm glad she refused me. For that, I am most grateful."

Calder felt a pang of sorrow. "She refused you?"

"Twice," Durrance said. "What an escape! I shall be more trouble than he was. I can't sit in a chair and think. I've lived out of doors, and that's the only life for me."

Calder tried to comfort him, but Durrance's despair was absolute. "You won't want my company in a year's time," he said, laughing again.

"Stop that," Calder urged. "Let me read the rest."

He read the remaining letters, his mind drifting to the two from Ethne. He wondered if there was more beneath the surface, a deeper emotion hidden in the simplicity of her words. He thought of the girl in Ireland, wondering if she understood the magnitude of the calamity that had befallen her friend.

Finally, Durrance decided to write back. He dictated a letter to Ethne, explaining that a sunstroke had blinded him. Calder took the letter to the hospital to consult the doctor again.

"Can a sunstroke destroy the optic nerve?" Calder asked the Syrian.

"A mere sunstroke? No," the doctor replied. "But it may be the occasion. You must look deeper. To the brain."

Calder felt a wave of horror. "Then this calamity is not all. There will be more to follow—death or..."

"No," the doctor reassured him. "That does not follow."

Back at the mess-room, Calder held Durrance's letter, torn between sending it and destroying it. He realized how much blindness meant to a man like Durrance, a man who found his home in the wild places. Would Ethne understand?

He walked to the post-office and sent the letter. Somewhere in the Mediterranean, it crossed paths with a reply from Ethne. Two weeks later, at Cairo, Calder read it aloud to Durrance.

Ethne wrote that she had considered everything he had said. She understood that his thoughts of her had never changed. She went back on her old argument that marriage would be an injury to him. She would marry him upon his return to England.

"That's rough luck, isn't it?" Durrance said, his voice trembling. "For here's the one thing I have always wished for, and it comes when I can no longer take it."

"I think you will find it very difficult to refuse," Calder said. "I don't know Miss Eustace, but from her letters, I can guess. I don't think she is a woman who will say 'yes' one day and 'no' the next because bad times come. If she cares for you, and you for her, you are doing her an insult if you imagine she cannot marry you and still be happy."

Durrance thought over this. Marriage with a blind man. It might be possible, if there was love on both sides. And there was the letter from Ethne to prove it. Besides, there were compensations. She could live in her own country, clear the debts of the Lennon estate.

"Besides," Calder added, "there is always a possibility of a cure."

"There is no such possibility," Durrance said with startling decision. "You know that as well as I do."

"You needn't start so guiltily," Calder said, glancing at the letter in his hand. "I haven't overheard a word."

"Then what makes you think there's no chance?" Durrance asked.

"The voice of every doctor who encourages hope gives the lie to their words," Durrance said. "If one cannot see, one can at least hear."

Calder looked at his friend, surprised by this sudden acuteness. Then Durrance asked, "When was that letter written?"

Immediately, he asked another question: "What makes you jump?"

Calder laughed nervously. "I was looking at the letter when you asked. It was written on the fifteenth of May."

"Ah," Durrance said. "The day I returned to Wadi Halfa blind."

Calder sat frozen, gazing anxiously at his companion. It was a queer coincidence. Durrance laughed it off with a careless chuckle, but Calder knew his friend was listening with the utmost intentness, waiting for some sign of relief that never came. The desert wind blew, but inside the mess-room, the silence was absolute.

Ethne stood rigid before the drawing-room window of the Hill Street house, her breath held in a suspended tension. Beside her, Mrs. Adair sat before her tea-table, both women acting as sentinels, ears straining against the afternoon air for a specific signal. The sash was thrown wide, a deliberate invitation for sound to breach the sanctuary of the room. It was half-past five; June had returned with its golden fervor, turning London into a glittering tapestry of pleasure and verdant shade. Across the way, windows bloomed with floral gaiety, and below, carriages glided smoothly toward the Park. Then, a sudden jingle of bells cut through the hum, swelling into a crescendo. Mrs. Adair lifted her head sharply.

"That's a cab," she whispered.

"Yes."

Ethne leaned forward, peering down. "But it isn't stopping," she murmured, as the metallic clatter faded into the distance.

Mrs. Adair glanced at the clock, her expression shifting from anticipation to a dawning unease. "Colonel Durrance is late," she said, turning a curious gaze toward Ethne. To her, Ethne's affirmative had carried a weight of dread rather than eagerness; her posture at the window suggested apprehension. Yet, as the carriage passed without halting, Mrs. Adair sensed a flicker of relief in Ethne's stance. "Why didn't you go to the station to meet him?" she asked slowly.

The reply came instantly.

"He might have assumed I came because I viewed him as helpless, and I cannot allow him to think that. He has his servant with him." Ethne watched the street again, pausing briefly as if to gather courage before speaking. "You recall the telegram I showed you?"

"From Lieutenant Calder, stating that Colonel Durrance had gone blind?"

"Yes. I require a promise from you: never to mention it. I do not want him to know I ever received it."

Mrs. Adair was perplexed, a sensation she detested. She had seen the telegram, but she had not been privy to Ethne's subsequent letter to Durrance, a pledge made the moment the news arrived. When Ethne had first displayed the telegram, she had simply declared, "I am engaged to him." Mrs. Adair had accepted this as a settled fact, allowing herself to remain in that comfortable delusion.

"You will promise?" Ethne pressed.

"Certainly, my dear, if you wish," Mrs. Adair replied, her shoulders lifting in an ungracious shrug. "But there must be a reason. I fail to comprehend why you demand such secrecy."

"Two lives must not be ruined because of me."

Mrs. Adair suspected Ethne feared the blind man she was betrothed to, and she was right. It had been exactly one year since Ethne had bid Durrance attempt to forget her in this very room, on a sunlit afternoon just like this one. Every letter since had proven futile; he had not forgotten. Even the last missive, three weeks prior—a childish scrawl from Wadi Halfa with uneven, straggling letters—where he detailed his calamity and renounced his suit, confirmed it more surely than any hopeful correspondence before it. As she waited, Ethne realized with crushing clarity that the burden of forgetting now rested entirely on her shoulders. If she could not erase the memory, she must ensure that no careless word betrayed her own lingering attachment.

"No," she said firmly. "Two lives shall not be spoiled because of me," and she turned to her friend.

"Are you certain, Ethne," Mrs. Adair asked, "that this path of yours—the path of marriage—will not ultimately destroy both of you? Do you not fear that you will eventually come to see Colonel Durrance as a hindrance, a drag? Might he not feel the same?"

"No," Ethne answered with decisive conviction. "I shall not feel it, and he must not."

In Mrs. Adair's mind, the two lives at stake were those of Durrance and Harry Feversham. She was mistaken; the true stakes were Durrance and Ethne herself. Ethne did not correct her, nor did she dispute the point. Instead, she was secretly relieved that her friend held this erroneous belief, allowing Mrs. Adair to maintain her comforting illusion.

Ethne resumed her vigil at the window, mapping out her future with military precision, anticipating every potential ambush. The task was arduous, and fear began to coil within her chest as the minutes ticked by, lest she falter. Yet, the goal justified the peril. Durrance had lost everything that gave life meaning the moment his sight failed—everything, save one thing. "What should I do if I were crippled?" he had asked Harry Feversham that morning on the river bank. "A clever man might endure it. But what if I were condemned to sit in a chair for the rest of my days?" Ethne had not heard those words, yet she understood the man perfectly without them. By birth, he was heir to vast estates and a legacy of exploration; he had lost his heritage. The long journeys, the exotic faces of foreign lands, the crackle of the campfire, the red spark against the black desert silence, the sleep beneath bright stars, the cool night winds, and the work of governance—all were gone. Only one thing remained: herself, and only as long as he could believe she desired him.

Just as she solidified her resolve, the cab arrived at the door, unnoticed until the servant's bell rang.

"He has come!" she gasped, starting.

Durrance was not naturally inquisitive; he accepted friends as they were, refusing to dissect them under a microscope. Ethne reasoned that quieting his suspicions would be easy, especially now. There was no cause for alarm. Yet, despite her logic, she braced herself for the encounter rather than rushing to welcome him.

Mrs. Adair slipped away, leaving Ethne alone as Durrance entered. She did not move immediately, holding her pose, expecting the change in him to strike her with force. Instead, she was surprised by the absence of the changes she feared. His face was weathered, his hair streaked with grey, but there was no air of helplessness or uncertainty—the very trait she dreaded most for his sake. He walked into the room with the confidence of sight; his memory seemed to chart the exact position of every piece of furniture. At most, he reached out a hand once or twice where he expected a chair.

Ethne retreated silently to the window, unsure of how to greet him, but he smiled and approached her directly.

"Ethne," he said.

"It isn't true, then," she exclaimed, the words forced out by the ease of his movement. "You have recovered."

"It is quite true, and I have not recovered," he answered calmly. "But you shifted at the window, and so I knew you were there."

"How did you know? I made no noise."

"No, but the window was open. The street noise suddenly intensified, so I knew someone had moved aside. I guessed it was you."

Their exchange lacked the customary formality of an engaged couple meeting after a separation, yet it successfully bypassed embarrassment. Ethne avoided any perfunctory expressions of regret, sensing there was no need, and Durrance showed no desire to hear them. They shook hands, and Ethne stepped back into the room.

"I shall prepare some tea," she offered. "Then we can speak."

"Yes, we must have a talk, mustn't we?" Durrance replied seriously. He soon shed this gravity, chatting with good humor about his journey home. He even found amusement in recounting his initial days of blindness, and Ethne's anxieties began to dissolve. They had nearly vanished when a shift in his demeanor brought them crashing back.

"I wrote to you from Wadi Halfa," he said. "Do you suppose you could read the letter?"

"Quite well," Ethne replied.

"I had a friend of mine hold the paper and tell me when I was writing on it versus the blotting-pad," he continued, laughing lightly. "Calder—of the Sappers—but you do not know him."

He dropped the name quickly, and it struck Ethne like a blow; he had set a trap. The stillness of his face suggested he was listening with intense scrutiny for a reaction, a startled exclamation that would reveal her knowledge of Calder. Did he suspect? Did he know of the telegram? Did he guess her letter was born of pity? She searched his face, finding nothing but alertness. In the past, a year ago, the expression of his eyes would have answered her definitively, even if he held his tongue.

"I could read the letter without difficulty," she answered softly. "It was the letter you would have written. But I had written to you before, and of course your news could make no difference. I retract no word of what I wrote."

Durrance sat with his hands on his knees, leaning forward slightly. Ethne was lost again. She could not discern from his manner or face whether he accepted or questioned her response. She realized painfully that a year ago, with his sight, she would have known the truth instantly.

"Yes, I know you. You would take nothing back," he said finally. "But there is my point of view."

Ethne looked at him, apprehension tightening her chest.

"Yes?" she replied, striving for an air of unconcern. "Will you tell me?"

Durrance agreed, beginning to speak in the deliberate tone of a man who had weighed his thoughts, memorized his argument, and decided on the most lucid order of words.

"I know what blindness means to all men—a growing, narrowing egotism unless one guards oneself perpetually. And will one guard oneself perpetually? Blindness means that to all men," he repeated emphatically. "But it must mean more to me, who am deprived of every occupation. If I were a writer, I could dictate. If I were a businessman, I could manage my affairs. But I am a soldier, and not a clever one. Jealousy, a continual and irritable curiosity—there is no Paul Pry like a blind man—a querulous claim upon your attention—these are my special dangers." Ethne laughed gently, contradicting his gloom.

"Well, perhaps one may hold them off," he acknowledged. "But they must be considered. I have considered them. I am not speaking without thought. I have pondered and puzzled over this whole matter night after night since receiving your letter, wondering what I should do. You know how gladly, with what gratitude, I would have answered, 'Yes, let the marriage proceed,' if I dared. If I dared! But I think—don't you?—that great trouble often clears one's wits. I used to lie awake in Cairo thinking; the trivialities gradually fell away, and a few straight, simple truths stood out vividly. One feels compelled to cling to them with all one's might, because nothing else remains."

"Yes, I understand that," Ethne replied in a low voice. She had undergone precisely such an experience herself. It might have been she, not Durrance, speaking. She looked up at him, and for the first time, she began to understand that they shared much in common. She repeated to herself with renewed firmness, "Two lives shall not be spoiled because of me."

"Well?" she asked.

"Well, here is one of those straight truths. Marriage between a man crippled like myself, whose life is effectively ended, and a woman like you, active and young, whose life is in full bloom, would be quite wrong unless each brings to it far more than friendship. It would be quite wrong if it implied a sacrifice for you."

"It implies no sacrifice," she answered firmly.

Durrance nodded. The answer satisfied him, and Ethne felt it was the intonation he valued more than the words. His concentrated attitude confirmed this. She began to wonder if quieting his suspicions would be easier now that he was blind, or if the very condition made it more difficult.

"Then do you bring more than friendship?" he asked suddenly. "You will be very honest, I know. Tell me."

Ethne was in a quandary. She knew she must answer, immediately and without ambiguity, and she must answer honestly.

"There is nothing," she replied, as firmly as before, "nothing in the world which I wish for so earnestly as that you and I should marry."

It was an honest wish, spoken honestly. She knew nothing of the conversation between Harry Feversham and Lieutenant Sutch in the Criterion Grill Room; she knew nothing of Harry's plans; she had not heard of the Gordon letters recovered from the ruins on the Nile. Harry Feversham had, as far as she knew and intended, vanished completely from her life. Therefore, her wish was pure. But it was not an exact answer to Durrance's question, and she hoped he would listen to the tone rather than the words. He seemed content with it.

"Thank you, Ethne," he said, taking her hand and shaking it. His face smiled at her. He asked no further questions. There was no doubt, she thought; his suspicions were quieted; he was entirely content. Then Mrs. Adair entered with discretion.

She possessed the tact to greet Durrance as a man suffering no disadvantage, speaking as though she had seen him only the week prior.

"I suppose Ethne has told you of our plan," she said, taking her tea from Ethne's hand.

"No, not yet," Ethne answered.

"What plan?" Durrance asked.

"It is all arranged," Mrs. Adair said. "You will wish to return home to Guessens in Devonshire. I am your neighbor—only a couple of fields separate us. So Ethne will stay with me during the interval before your marriage."

"That is very kind of you, Mrs. Adair," Durrance exclaimed; "for, of course, there will be an interval."

"A short one, no doubt," Mrs. Adair replied.

"Well, it is this way. If there is a chance I may recover my sight, it would be better to seize it immediately. Time counts for much in these cases."

The heat of July and August pressed down upon the Salcombe estuary, but it was not the sun that warmed Ethne's apprehensions; they had grown heavy and cold within her chest. One morning, standing at the open window of Mrs. Adair's cottage, she voiced the fear that had been eating at her.

"I am afraid," she said, her voice trembling slightly in the bright air.

Mrs. Adair, seated in the cool shadow of the room behind her, did not look up from her book. She offered only a quiet, knowing smile.

"Of what? That some accident befell Colonel Durrance in London yesterday?"

"No," Ethne replied slowly, turning her gaze toward the stone-flagged terrace below. "Not of that. For he is crossing the lawn towards us now."

Mrs. Adair's smile deepened, though her eyes remained fixed on the printed page. "I thought so," she murmured, her voice barely audible.

Ethne ignored the comfort of the words, her mind racing with a sharper, darker question. "Do you think he has any hope at all of recovering his sight?"

Mrs. Adair set the book aside, her curiosity piqued by the girl's intensity. "Would a man travel to London repeatedly to consult an oculist if he believed the cure impossible? Of course he hopes."

"I am afraid," Ethne insisted, stepping out onto the terrace. "Laura, haven't you noticed how quickly he has changed? How fast he learns to read your silences? He infers your unspoken thoughts, completes your sentences, and interprets your gestures as commentary. At times, I feel he sees into the very corners of my mind. He reads me like a child's lesson book."

"You are at a disadvantage," Mrs. Adair observed calmly. "You no longer have your face to shield your thoughts."

"And his eyes no longer tell me anything," Ethne added, her voice dropping.

The truth lay in both statements. Once, Durrance's blindness had forced him to rely on her expressions, but now, stripped of her visual cues, he possessed a terrifying detachment. Conversely, Ethne, who had never doubted his intentions, now found herself constantly in the dark. The blindness had reversed their dynamic, leaving her vulnerable.

Mrs. Adair, however, was less concerned with the psychological shift and more intrigued by Ethne's sudden confession. The girl, once so frank, was now agitated, shifting her weight, hiding things.

"There is something you must conceal from him?" Mrs. Adair asked softly.

"Yes."

"Something important?"

"Something I must conceal at all costs," Ethne exclaimed, though a shadow of doubt crossed her mind—had Durrance already guessed?

She walked to the edge of the terrace, looking out over the lawn toward the creek. Beyond the hedge, the fields rolled gently, and in the distance, among the trees, she saw the smoke rising from Colonel Durrance's house. A sailing-boat tacked slowly against the wind further down the water. Ethne composed herself, summoning the strength to face the man approaching steadily across the grass. His cane tapped rhythmically, switching at the blades of grass with practiced habit, his feet finding the path without sight.

As Ethne descended the steps, Mrs. Adair dropped her book and moved to the window, hidden by the curtains. Her face was drawn, her eyes burning with a fierce, hungry light. She watched the meeting with a mixture of pity and triumph.

"Something she must conceal," Mrs. Adair whispered to herself, her tone laced with contempt for Ethne's fear of a blind husband. "For my part, I am glad," she thought, relieved that blindness had disabled Durrance. If her chance ever came, this dependency would be her ultimate victory. She watched Ethne and Durrance walk side by side toward the creek, sensing the plea in the younger woman's posture.

"You saw your oculist yesterday?" Ethne asked quickly as they met.

Durrance shrugged. "One must wait. Only time can show if a cure is possible."

"But must *we* wait?" Ethne pressed.

"Surely," he replied wisely. "It would be wiser on all counts." Then, unexpectedly, he asked, "Was it Mrs. Adair who proposed this plan for me to return to Guessens while you stayed here?"

Ethne, startled, explained that she had come to London in distress and that Laura had suggested the arrangement. "I welcomed it with all my heart," she said, her voice pleading for confirmation.

"I know that very well, Ethne," Durrance said gently. "But it is hard on you, who wishes to be back in your own country. I remember a sentence Harry Feversham spoke long ago in London, just before I left for Egypt. He said, 'She is of her country and more of her county. I do not think she could be happy in any place which was not within reach of Donegal.' To keep you here, at such cost to you, seems selfish."

"I was not thinking of that," Ethne interrupted, her anxiety flaring. "That makes me out selfish. I was merely wondering why you insist on waiting. For, of course, we pray for your sight, but the fact of a cure makes no difference."

She repeated the phrase with emphasis: "It can make no difference."

Durrance started as if roused from a trance. "I beg your pardon, Ethne. I was thinking of Harry Feversham. There is something I want you to tell me. You said a long time ago at Glenalla that you might one day tell me what happened. I would like to know now. What took place that night at Lennon House that broke off your engagement and sent him away an outcast?"

Ethne fell silent. "I would rather not. It is all over and done with. I do not want you to ask me ever."

Durrance accepted her refusal cheerfully. "Very well. It might hurt you to answer, and I do not wish to cause you pain."

"It is not because I fear pain," Ethne explained earnestly. "Mr. Feversham is a man I knew well, and now he is dead."

As they walked toward the gap in the trees along the creek bank, Ethne raised her eyes. The small boat she had noticed earlier had grounded itself on the shore. The sail was lowered, and standing on the bank, staring vaguely toward the house, was a man.

"A stranger has landed," Ethne said, seizing the interruption as a lifeline. "He looks lost. I will go and help him."

She ran forward, needing the distraction. The stranger approached, a man of middle height with a short snub nose, vacant brown eyes, and a ferocious moustache. He wore a hat that he removed to reveal a balding forehead.

"I have sailed down from Kingsbridge," he said. "Can you tell me if this house is called The Pool?"

"Yes; you will find Mrs. Adair if you go up the steps," Ethne replied.

"I came to see Miss Eustace."

Ethne turned, surprised. "I am Miss Eustace."

The stranger studied her in silence. "So I thought." He twisted a moustache before speaking again. "I have had trouble finding you, Miss Eustace. I went all the way to Glenalla—for nothing. Hard on a man whose leave is short!"

"I am very sorry," Ethne smiled, though uneasily. "Why have you been put to this trouble?"

"You have forgotten my name, no doubt."

"I do not think I have ever heard it."

"Oh, yes, you have. You heard it five years ago. I am Captain Willoughby."

Ethne recoiled. Color drained from her cheeks, her lips set hard, and her eyes grew cold. She glared at him silently.

Willoughby was unfazed, speaking with the air of a man forgiving a minor breach of manners. "I can understand that you do not welcome me, Miss Eustace, but none of us foresaw that you would be present when the three white feathers came into Feversham's hands."

Ethne swept the explanation aside. "How do you know I was present?"

"Feversham told me."

"You have seen him?" The cry burst from her lips, a raw throb of the heart that startled both of them. She had tried to erase Harry Feversham from her mind, to treat him as dead, yet the reality of his existence crashed over her.

"You have actually seen him?" she repeated, her voice trembling with wonder. "You have spoken to him? When?"

"A year ago, at Suakin. Else why should I be here?"

The question struck Ethne like a blow. She did not dare speculate on the answer.

"Yes," she said slowly, reluctantly. "After all, why are you here?"

Willoughby reached into his breast pocket, withdrew a case, and with deliberate slowness produced a tiny, soiled white feather. He held it out to her.

"I have come to give you this."

Ethne did not take it; she shrank from it.

"Why?" she asked unsteadily.

"Three white feathers, three separate accusations of cowardice, were sent to Feversham by three separate men. This is one of those feathers, forwarded from his lodgings to Ramelton five years ago. I am one of the three men who sent them. I have come to tell you that I withdraw my accusation. I take my feather back."

"And you bring it to me?"

"He asked me to."

Ethne finally took the feather. It was light and fragile, yet it felt momentous in her palm. Suddenly, the trees and the garden began to whirl around her. Willoughby's voice grew distant and thin, though she desperately wanted to hear every word. She felt a profound coldness despite the August sun. Yet, the presence of Captain Willoughby, one of the three men she could never forgive, gave her the strength to command herself. She refused to show weakness before any of the detested trio. With an effort, she steadied herself.

"Come," she said. "I will hear your story. Your news was a shock. Even now, I do not quite understand."

She led him to a secluded plot of grass above the creek, enclosed by thick hedges, with a garden seat in the center.

"Now," she said, gesturing for him to sit beside her. "Take your time. Forget nothing. Even his words, if you remember them! I shall thank you for his words."

She clenched the white feather in her hand. Somehow, Harry Feversham had redeemed his honor; somehow, she had been unjust to him. She was eager to learn how. Memories sternly repressed for five years flooded her mind—the Devonshire meadows, the salt of the sea, and the spring in Dublin before the feathers arrived at Ramelton.

Willoughby began his story, and almost immediately, the English countryside dissolved into a shifting mist. In its place rose the strange, wide country of the East that Durrance had described so often. The red cliffs of the coast vanished, replaced by stunted mimosas of the desert; the blue sea became vast stretches of yellow sand; the green hills turned to sharp-peaked, pyramidal black rocks rising abruptly from the dunes.

It was the bitter irony of her position that, while physically standing in the English countryside, she was mentally transported to the trials of her lover in the East, understanding his suffering only through the confidences of the man who had once accused him.

"I will not interrupt you," Ethne said, as Willoughby settled beside her. He had spoken barely twenty words before she broke her promise.

"I am Deputy-Governor of Suakin," he began. "My chief was on leave in May. You are fortunate not to know Suakin, Miss Eustace, particularly in May. No white woman can live there. It possesses a sodden, intolerable heat peculiar to itself. The air hangs heavy with brine; you cannot sleep at night for its oppression. Well, I was sitting on the verandah of the palace, first floor, about ten o'clock one night, gazing out over the harbour and the distillation works, wondering if sleep were worth the effort, when a servant informed me that a man wishing to see me had arrived. He refused to give his name. It was Feversham. Only a single lamp burned in the verandah, and the night was so dark I did not recognize him until he stood close beside me."

At once Ethne interrupted. "How did he look?"

Willoughby frowned, his eyes widening in uncertainty. "Honestly, I do not know," he said doubtfully. "Much like other men who have spent a year or two in the Sudan, perhaps a trifle overtrained and that sort of thing."

"Never mind," Ethne sighed, her expression one of disappointed resignation. For five years she had heard nothing of Harry Feversham. She hungered for news of him, for the cadence of his habitual phrases, for the description of his familiar gestures. She possessed the woman's anxious need to know his physical state, to discern if his face or figure had altered, and if so, how. But glancing at the obtuse, unobservant countenance of Captain Willoughby, she realized that however much she craved these details, she must go without them.

"I beg your pardon," she said. "Please, go on."

Willoughby resumed. "I asked him what he wanted and why he had not sent in his name. 'You would not have seen me if I had,' he replied, and he drew a packet of letters from his pocket. Now, those letters, Miss Eustace, had been written a long while ago by General Gordon in Khartum. They had been carried down the Nile as far as Berber. But the day after they reached Berber, that town surrendered to the Mahdists. Abou Fatma, the messenger who carried them, hid them in the wall of the house of an Arab named Yusef, who sold rock-salt in the market-place. Abou was then thrown into prison on suspicion and escaped to Suakin. The letters remained hidden in that wall until Feversham recovered them. I examined them and saw they were of no value, so I asked Feversham bluntly why, a man who had not dared to accompany his regiment on active service, had risked death and torture to retrieve them."

Standing upon that verandah, with the quiet pool of water before him, Feversham had told his story quietly and without exaggeration. He had related how he met Abou Fatma at Suakin, how they planned the recovery, how they traveled together to Obak, and since Abou dared not go further, how Feversham drove his grey donkey alone to Berber. He had not concealed the access of panic that loosened his joints when he first saw the low brown walls of the town and the towering date palms behind on the Nile bank; the terror that had set him running and leaping across the empty desert in the sunlight, a marrowless thing of fear. However, he made one omission. He said nothing of the hours he spent crouching on the hot sand, his coat drawn over his head, while he drew a woman's face toward him across continents and seas, nerving himself to endure by the look of sorrow it wore.

"He went down into Berber at the setting of the sun," Captain Willoughby said, and that was all he had to say. It was enough, however, for Ethne Eustace. She drew a deep breath of relief, her face softening, a light entering her grey eyes, a smile touching her lips.

"He went down into Berber," she repeated softly.

"And found that the old town had been destroyed by the orders of the Emir, and that a new one was building upon its southern confines," Willoughby continued. "All the landmarks by which Feversham was to know the house where the letters were hidden had vanished. The roofs were torn off, the houses dismantled, the front walls carried away. Narrow alleys of crumbling five-courts—so Feversham described the place—crossed this way and that, gaping to the stars. Here and there perhaps a broken tower rose, the remnant of a rich man's house. But of any sign that could tell a man where the hut of Yusef, who once sold rock-salt, had stood, there was no hope in those acres of crumbling mud. The foxes had already made their burrows there."

The smile faded from Ethne's face, but she looked again at the white feather lying in her palm, and she laughed with a great contentment. It was yellow with desert dust. It was proof that in this story there was to be no word of failure.

"Go on," she said.

Willoughby related the dispatch of the negro with the donkey to Abou Fatma at the Wells of Obak.

"Feversham stayed for a fortnight in Berber," Willoughby continued. "A week during which he came every morning to the well and waited for the return of his negro from Obak, and a week during which that negro searched for Yusef, who once sold rock-salt in the market-place. I doubt, Miss Eustace, if you can realize, however hard you try, what that fortnight must have meant to Feversham—the anxiety, the danger, the continued expectation that a voice would bid him halt and a hand fall upon his shoulder, the urgent knowledge that if the hand fell, death would be the least part of his penalty. I imagine the town—a town of low houses and broad streets of sand, dug here and there into pits for mud wherewith to build the houses, and overhead the blistering sun and a hot, shadowless sky. In no corner was there any darkness or concealment. And all day a crowd jostled and shouted up and down these streets—for that is the Mahdist policy to crowd the towns so that all may be watched and every man his neighbor's spy. Feversham dared not seek the shelter of a roof at night, for he dared not trust his tongue. He bought his food each day at the booths but feared any conversation. He slept at night in some corner of the old deserted town, in the acres of the ruined five-courts. For the same reason he must not slink in the by-ways by day lest anyone question him about his business; nor listen on the chance of hearing Yusef's name in public places lest other loiterers joke with him and draw him into their talk. Nor dare he in the daylight prowl about those crumbled ruins. From sunrise to sunset he must go quickly up and down the streets of the town like a man bent upon urgent business which permits of no delay. And that continued for a fortnight, Miss Eustace! A weary, trying life, don't you think? I wish I could tell you of it as vividly as he told me that night upon the balcony of the palace at Suakin."

Ethne wished it too with all her heart. Harry Feversham had made his story very real to Captain Willoughby; so that even after the lapse of fifteen months, this unimaginative creature was sensible of a contrast and a deficiency in his manner of narration.

"In front of us was the quiet harbour and the Red Sea, above us the African stars. Feversham spoke in the quietest manner possible, but with a peculiar deliberation and with his eyes fixed upon my face, as though he was forcing me to feel with him and to understand. Even when he lit his cigar he did not avert his eyes. By this time I had given him a cigar and offered him a chair. I had really, I assure you, Miss Eustace. It was the first time in four years that he had sat with one of his equals, or indeed with any of his countrymen on a footing of equality. He told me so. I wish I could remember all that he told me." Willoughby stopped and cudgelled his brains helplessly. He gave up the effort in the end.

"Well," he resumed, "after Feversham had skulked for a fortnight in Berber, the negro discovered Yusef, no longer selling salt, but tending a small plantation of dhurra on the river's edge. From Yusef, Feversham obtained particulars enough to guide him to the house where the letters were concealed in the inner wall. But Yusef was no longer to be trusted. Possibly Feversham's accent betrayed him. The more likely conjecture is that Yusef took Feversham for a spy and thought it wise to confess to Mohammed-el-Kheir, the Emir, his own share in the concealment of the letters. That, however, is a mere conjecture. The important fact is this. On the same night Feversham went alone to old Berber."

"Alone!" said Ethne. "Yes?"

"He found the house fronting a narrow alley, and the sixth of the row. The front wall was destroyed, but the two side walls and the back wall still stood. Three feet from the floor and two feet from the right-hand corner the letters were hidden in that inner wall. Feversham dug into the mud bricks with his knife; he made a hole wherein he could slip his hand. The wall was thick; he dug deep, stopping now and again to feel for the packet. At last his fingers clasped and drew it out; as he hid it in a fold of his jibbeh, the light of a lantern shone upon him from behind."

Ethne started as though she had been trapped herself. Those acres of roofless five-courts, with here and there a tower showing up against the sky, the lonely alleys, the dead silence here beneath the stars, the cries and the beating of drums and the glare of lights from the new town—Harry Feversham alone with the letters, with, in a word, some portion of his honour redeemed, and finally, the lantern flashing upon him in that solitary place—the scene itself and the progress of the incidents were so visible to Ethne at that moment that even with the feather in her open palm she could hardly bring herself to believe that Harry Feversham had escaped.

"Well, well?" she asked.

"He was standing with his face to the wall; the light came from the alley behind him. He did not turn, but out of the corner of his eye he could see a fold of a white robe hanging motionless. He carefully secured the package, with a care indeed and a composure which astonished him even at that moment. The shock had strung him to a concentration and lucidity of thought unknown to him till then. His fingers were trembling, he remarked, as he tied the knots, but it was with excitement, and an excitement which did not flurry. His mind worked rapidly, but quite coolly, quite deliberately. He came to a perfectly definite conclusion as to what he must do. Every faculty which he possessed was extraordinarily clear, and at the same time extraordinarily still. He had his knife in his hand, he faced about suddenly and ran. There were two men waiting. Feversham ran at the man who held the lantern. He was aware of the point of a spear, he ducked and beat it aside with his left arm, he leaped forward and struck with his right. The Arab fell at his feet; the lantern was extinguished. Feversham sprang across the white-robed body and ran eastward, toward the open desert. But in no panic; he had never been so collected. He was followed by the second soldier. He had foreseen that he would be followed. If he was to escape, it was indeed necessary that he should be. He turned a corner, crouched behind a wall, and as the Arab came running by he leaped out upon his shoulders. And again as he leaped he struck."

Captain Willoughby stopped at this point of his story and turned towards Ethne. He had something to say which perplexed and at the same time impressed him, and he spoke with a desire for an explanation.

"The strangest feature of those few fierce, short minutes," he said, "was that Feversham felt no fear. I don't understand that, do you? From the first moment when the lantern shone upon him from behind, to the last when he turned his feet eastward and ran through the ruined alleys and broken walls toward the desert and the Wells of Obak, he felt no fear."

This was the most mysterious part of Harry Feversham's story to Captain Willoughby. Here was a man who so shrank from the possibilities of battle that he must actually send in his papers rather than confront them; yet when he stood in dire and immediate peril he felt no fear. Captain Willoughby might well turn to Ethne for an explanation.

There had been no mystery in it to Harry Feversham, but a great bitterness of spirit. He had sat on the verandah at Suakin, whittling away at the edge of Captain Willoughby's table with the very knife which he had used in Berber to dig out the letters, and which had proved so handy a weapon when the lantern shone out behind him—the one glimmering point of light in that vast acreage of ruin. Harry Feversham had kept it carefully uncleansed of blood; he had treasured it all through his flight across the two hundred and forty odd miles of desert into Suakin; it was, next to the white feathers, the thing which he held most precious of his possessions, and not merely because it would serve as a corroboration of his story to Captain Willoughby, but because the weapon enabled him to believe and realize it himself. Ethne had completely erased Colonel Durrance from her mind. Since the moment Captain Willoughby placed the soiled, yellowed feather into her palm, her world had narrowed to a single focus: Harry Feversham. She had dragged Willoughby into the secluded enclosure, and his tale had consumed her, filling the gaps in his narrative with vivid recollections of Harry's gestures, voice, and gaze. For a fleeting holiday, she had been swept away from the August garden, forgetting the five weary years she had spent holding her head high against the world. Those years of silent endurance vanished, blotted out by the shock of seeing Durrance emerge from the hedge.

"Hush!" she whispered to Willoughby. Her face drained of color, her eyes squeezing shut in a spasm of agony. Yet, there was no time for grief. Their brief conversation was over; the holiday ended. She must immediately resume the heavy burden of forgetting. She knew she never truly could forget, so she resolved to do the next best thing: offer no sign that she hadn't. Durrance must continue to believe she loved him with a devotion deeper than friendship.

Durrance stood at the hedge's entrance, then stepped inside. He was too quick to suspect anything; he should not have met Willoughby or known of his arrival. Ethne scanned the area for an escape route, knowing it was futile. The creek lay ahead, and three high, thick hedges enclosed them behind and to the sides. There was only one exit, and Durrance blocked it.

"Stay still," she murmured. "Do you know him?"

"Of course. We served together at Suakin for three years. I heard he'd gone blind. I'm relieved to see he hasn't." He noted the ease of Durrance's stride.

"Speak lower," Ethne hissed. "It is true. He *is* blind."

"One would never guess. Consolation feels pointless. What can I say to him?"

"Say nothing!"

Durrance remained just inside the enclosure, seemingly staring directly at the two figures on the bench.

"Ethne," he said, using a tone so calm and certain it created the illusion of perfect sight.

"It's impossible he can see," Willoughby protested. "He's looking right at us."

"He sees nothing."

Durrance called out again, "Ethne!"—this time with a note of doubt in his voice.

"Do you hear? He's unsure," Ethne whispered urgently. "Stay perfectly still."

"Why?"

"He must not know you are here." She clamped her hand tightly around Willoughby's arm to prevent movement. Willoughby fell silent, compelled by her tense demeanor. She sat rigid, holding her breath, her face strained forward, her eyes wide with terror. Willoughby felt a rising excitement, not from his own fear, but from her palpable apprehension.

"He's coming toward us," he whispered.

"Not a word. Not a move."

"Ethne!" Durrance cried again, advancing further into the clearing. Ethne and Willoughby sat frozen, watching him. He walked past the bench and stopped, facing them directly. Willoughby thought surely he was being seen. Durrance stood relaxed, ready to speak. Even Ethne, certain of his blindness, began to doubt her own knowledge.

"Ethne!" he said once more in that quiet, authoritative voice. When no answer came, he shrugged and turned toward the creek. His back was to them now, yet Ethne, trained to read the subtle shifts in his bearing, knew he was listening intently. The way he held his head, the casual swing of his stick—all signaled he was straining to hear. Her grip on Willoughby's arm tightened. They held this pose for a minute before Durrance spun around and took a sudden step toward the seat. Ethne, anticipating his tricks, did not flinch. There was not even the rustle of her skirt. She held Willoughby fast.

"I wonder where in the world she can be," Durrance muttered aloud, then walked back out of the enclosure. Ethne did not release Willoughby's arm until Durrance was gone.

"That was close," Willoughby breathed when he could speak. "Suppose Durrance had sat right on top of us?"

"Why suppose, since he didn't?" Ethne asked calmly. "Did you tell me everything?"

"So far as I remember."

"And all of this happened in the spring?"

"The spring of last year," Willoughby confirmed.

"Yes. I have a question. Why didn't you bring this feather to me last summer?"

"Last year my leave was short. I spent it hunting ibex in the hills north of Suakin."

"I see," Ethne said quietly. "I hope you had good sport."

"It wasn't bad."

Last summer, Ethne had been free. If Willoughby had returned with news of Harry instead of hunting ibex on Jebel Araft, her life would have changed entirely. The cry "Why didn't you come?" echoed loudly in her heart, but outwardly she showed no sign of the damage Willoughby's delay had caused. Possessing the self-command of a woman hardened by trial, she spoke with such unconcern that Willoughby assumed her questions were born of mere curiosity.

"You might have written," she suggested.

"Feversham didn't think there was any hurry. Explaining in a letter would have been difficult. He asked me to come to you when I had the chance, and I had none until now. To be honest, I thought it likely he might have returned before me."

"Oh, no," Ethne replied. "There could be no possibility of that. The other two feathers still remain to be redeemed before he will ask me to take back mine."

Willoughby shook his head. "Feversham can never persuade Castleton and Trench to cancel their accusations as he persuaded me."

"Why not?"

"Major Castleton was killed when the square broke at Tamai."

"Killed?" Ethne cried, laughing briefly and satisfactorily. Willoughby stared at her, disbelief warring with the evidence of her ears. Her face, however, revealed she was thoroughly pleased. Ethne was a Celt; she felt death was not a significant matter. She could hate, and she could be as hard as iron to those she despised. These three men she hated with every fiber of her being. True, she had given the fourth feather to Harry merely to show agreement, but she cared little for that formality. She was delighted to hear Major Castleton was dead and done with.

"And Colonel Trench?" she asked.

"No," Willoughby answered. "You're disappointed? He is worse off. He was captured during a reconnaissance. He is now a prisoner in Omdurman."

"Ah!" Ethne said.

"I don't think you realize," Willoughby said sternly, "what captivity in Omdurman implies. If you did, even if you disliked the captive, you would feel pity."

"Not I," Ethne said stubbornly.

"I will tell you what it implies."

"No. I don't wish to hear about Colonel Trench. Besides, you must go. Before you leave, tell me one thing: what became of Mr. Feversham after he gave you that feather?"

"I told him he had done everything reasonably expected, and he accepted my advice. He boarded the first steamer to Suez and left the Sudan."

"I must find out where he is. He must return. Did he need money?"

"No. He still draws his allowance from his father. He said he had more than enough."

"I am glad of that," Ethne said. She dismissed Willoughby to wait in the enclosure while she went out alone to ensure the path was clear. The garden was empty. Durrance had vanished, and the great stone terrace and the house, with its striped sunblinds, looked like a sleeping entity. It was nearly one o'clock; the birds were silent. The profound quiet of the garden struck Ethne as strange. Only bees hummed drowsily among the flowerbeds, and a boy's voice called from the meadow slopes across the creek. She returned to Willoughby.

"You can go now," she said. "I cannot pretend friendship for you, Captain Willoughby, but it was kind of you to find me and tell your story. You are returning to Kingsbridge immediately? I hope so. I do not want Colonel Durrance to know of your visit or what you told me."

"Durrance was Feversham's great friend," Willoughby objected.

"He is unaware any feathers were sent to Mr. Feversham, so there is no need to inform him one has been returned," Ethne answered. "He doesn't know why our engagement was broken. I do not wish him to know. Your story would enlighten him, and he must remain ignorant."

"Why?" Willoughby asked. Stubborn by nature, he demanded the reason before promising silence. Ethne gave it simply.

"I am engaged to Colonel Durrance." She feared he already suspected her feelings were limited to friendship. If he learned that the fault breaking her engagement to Harry had been bravely atoned for, he would insist on stripping her of the one thing left to her. She was bound to him by honor, and she was determined he would not win her by sacrificing her own happiness because Harry had redeemed his.

Captain Willoughby pursed his lips and whistled. "Engaged to Jack Durrance!" he exclaimed. "Then I seem to have wasted my time bringing you that feather," pointing to it. She held it in her open hand and drew it back sharply, as if fearing he would snatch it.

"I am most grateful for it," she returned.

"It's a bit of a muddle, isn't it?" Willoughby remarked. "It seems rough on Feversham, and on Jack Durrance too." He looked at Ethne, noting her careful handling of the feather, the glow in her eyes as she listened, and the eager tone of her questions. "I shouldn't wonder if it's rough on you too, Miss Eustace."

Ethne said nothing. They walked out of the enclosure toward where Willoughby had moored his boat. She hurried him down the bank to the water's edge, intent on having him sail away unseen.

But Ethne had not counted on Mrs. Adair, who had observed much of Ethne's movements that morning. From the drawing-room window, she had watched Ethne meet Durrance at the terrace steps, seen them walk toward the estuary, noticed Willoughby's boat grounding in the gap between the trees, and seen a man disembark while Ethne went to meet him. Mrs. Adair was not a woman to abandon her post of observation. From behind the curtains, she watched with the curiosity of a villager lowering blinds to get a better view of a stranger passing down the street. Ethne and the man turned away, disappearing among the trees, leaving Durrance forgotten and alone. Mrs. Adair immediately thought of the enclosure at the water's edge. The conversation had lasted some time; since the couple did not reappear quickly, a question flashed into her mind: "Could the stranger be Harry Feversham?" Ethne had no friends in this part of the world. The question pressed on her, and she longed for an answer, specifically one that would convict Ethne Eustace of duplicity. Her interest turned to excitement when she saw Durrance, tired of waiting, follow Ethne's steps. But what followed interested her even more.

Durrance reappeared, surprisingly alone, and walked straight to the house, up the terrace, and into the drawing-room.

"Have you seen Ethne?" he asked.

"Is she not in the little garden by the water?" Mrs. Adair asked.

"No. I went in and called to her. It was empty."

"Indeed?" Mrs. Adair said. "Then I don't know where she is. Are you going?"

"Yes, home."

Mrs. Adair made no effort to detain him then.

"It was a natural plan to propose as soon as I heard of your ill-luck."

Mrs. Adair possessed a mind that was as winding as a labyrinth, preferring to peer through a keyhole rather than walk boldly through a door. To her, truth earned through subtle maneuvering held a far greater value than facts handed over freely by a simple question. She avoided direct inquiries on a twisted principle, finding a day well-spent only if she could coax a trivial piece of news from a companion's lips, something a straightforward request would have secured instantly.

Thus, though she was deeply baffled by the significance Ethne placed on a single white feather, and puzzled by the good tidings regarding Harry Feversham that Captain Willoughby had delivered, Mrs. Adair betrayed not a flicker of confusion during lunch. Ethne laughed, admitting her guilt, and retreated to her room immediately after the meal. There, in the solitude of the afternoon, she replayed Willoughby's story in her mind. Her heart swelled with a rhythm like divine music. The regret that Harry had not returned a year prior, when she was free to receive him, paled in comparison to the sheer joy of the revelation itself. That joy had erased the sting of his past rejection at Lennon House. Her pride, shattered once before, was now fully restored—not by a stranger, but by the very man who had wounded her. She felt a new vitality coursing through her veins, a return of her youth under the August sun.

From her dressing-case, she retrieved the only portrait of Harry Feversham she had kept. It was an image of someone dead to her in terms of romance, yet alive as a friend. She rejoiced in keeping it, for Harry had required no feigned loyalty or encouragement from her to sustain his high ideals. Placing the white feather beside the portrait, she locked them together in the drawer, sealing her renewed hope.

Returning to the window, she watched the shadows dance on the lawn as the breeze stirred the trees. Yet, her heart yearned for Donegal. It was August; the heather would soon bloom on the hillsides. She wished the news had arrived there, amidst the brown mountains and granite outcrops. Great sorrows and great joys alike pulled her homeward, for there, endurance was easier and gladness more complete.

Her only living tether to that land was Dermod's old collie, now her inseparable companion. To the dog, she poured her confidences; if her voice broke into tears, the animal would remain silent. Through him, she understood truths Willoughby had omitted and Harry had never voiced. She visualized those three years of hiding in the cramped city of Suakin, watching troops march to battle while Harry slipped away to avoid recognition by old friends like Durrance or Willoughby. As these images formed, she asked herself, *"Where was I then? What was I doing?"*

She sat in a golden haze until the lights shifted on the creek and the rooks wheeled noisily into the night, signaling evening.

That dinner, Ethne brought a buoyancy of spirit that surprised everyone. Mrs. Adair noted how brightly Ethne's eyes shone and how fresh her complexion appeared. "You will play on your violin tonight, I think," he said with a smile as they rose.

"Yes," she answered, "I will—with all my heart."

Durrance laughed and held the door open. The violin had remained locked in its case for two months, treated by Durrance as a barometer of Ethne's soul. If the world was kind, the case opened; if cruel, it stayed shut to prevent old wounds from bleeding before others. Tonight, however, it was to be unlocked.

Mrs. Adair lingered until Ethne was out of earshot. "You have noticed the change in her tonight?" she whispered.

"Yes," Durrance replied. "We waited for it, hoped for it, and nearly despaired. Are you so glad of the change?"

"Do you wonder?" Mrs. Adair asked, a look of pity softening her face before she withdrew.

Durrance took the weight of Ethne's transformation entirely to himself. He followed her to the drawing-room, where she sat by the open window, tightening the strings of her violin. He took a chair behind her, sinking into the shadows.

"What shall I play to you?" she asked.

"The *Musoline Overture*," he answered. "You played it the first evening I came to Ramelton. I remember it clearly. Play it again tonight. I wish to compare."

"I have played it since."

He listened with an intensity he had not shown that evening, applying what he believed was a final test to decide the course of their lives. As Ethne began to play, she forgot Durrance's presence. The air outside was still and warm; the moonlight lay like a solid silver floor on the creek; the trees dreamed under the stars. *"If he could only hear!"* she thought. *"If he could only wake and know this is a message of friendship!"*

Carrying this fantasy, she played with a skill she had never before displayed, turning her violin into a voice of profound sympathy. The music became a bridge swinging through the air, allowing her and Harry to meet and shake hands for just a few minutes before parting on their separate paths. These moments would sustain them both. The chords rang out against the silence, declaring the pride she had reclaimed that day. Her fancy hardened into belief. It was no longer *"If he should hear,"* but *"He must hear!"*

Struck by this fervor, a strange hope enthralled her. *"If he could answer!"*

She lingered on the final bars, waiting for a response. When the music faded into silence, she sat with the violin on her knees, gazing eagerly across the moonlit garden.

An answer did come, but not across the lawn. It emerged from the dark shadows of the room behind her, spoken through Durrance's voice.

Harry Feversham stood under the trees, guarded by four Ansar soldiers. His clothes had been stripped from him; he wore only a torn, ragged jibbeh and a twist of cotton on his head to shield him from the sun. His bare shoulders and arms were scorched and blistered. His ankles were fettered, his wrists bound with a rope of palm fiber, and an iron collar was locked about his neck, to which a chain was attached, held by one of the soldiers. He stood and smiled at the mocking crowd, seeming well pleased, like a lunatic.

That was the character he had assumed. If he could sustain it, baffling his captors so they were unsure whether he was truly daft or an agent with promises of help and arms to the disaffected tribes of Kordofan, then there was a chance they might fear to dispose of him themselves and send him forward to Omdurman. But it was hard work. Inside the house, the Emir and his counsellors debated his destiny; on the riverbank and within his view, a high gallows stood out black and sinister against the yellow sand. Harry Feversham was very glad of the chain about his neck and the fetters on his legs. They helped him betray no panic, assuring him of its futility.

These hours of waiting, while the sun rose higher and no one came from the gateway, were the worst he had ever endured. All through that fortnight in Berber, a hope of escape had sustained him, and when that lantern shone upon him from behind in the ruined acres, what had to be done must be done quickly, leaving no time for fear or thought. Here, there was time, and too much of it.

He had time to anticipate and foresee. He felt his heart sinking till he was faint, just as in those distant days when he had heard hounds scuffling in a covert and sat shaking upon his horse. He glanced furtively toward the gallows and foresaw vultures perched upon his shoulders, fluttering about his eyes. But the man had grown during his years of probation. The fear of physical suffering was not uppermost in his mind, nor even the fear that he would walk unmanfully to the high gallows. A greater dread haunted him: if he died now, here at Dongola, Ethne would never take back that fourth feather, and his strong hope of the "afterwards" would never come to its fulfillment. He was very glad of the collar and the fetters. He summoned his wits together and, standing there alone without a companion to share his miseries, he laughed, scraped, and grimaced at his tormentors.

An old hag danced and gesticulated before him, singing a monotonous song. Her gestures were pantomimic, menacing him with abominable mutilations; her words described in simple, unexpurgated language the grievous death agonies awaiting him and the eternity of torture in hell. Feversham understood and inwardly shuddered, but he only imitated her gestures, nodding and mowing at her as though she sang to him of Paradise. Others took their war-trumpets, placed the mouths against the prisoner's ears, and blew with all their might.

"Do you hear, Kaffir?" cried a child, dancing with delight before him. "Do you hear our ombeyehs? Blow louder! Blow louder!"

But the prisoner only clapped his hands and cried out that the music was good.

Finally, a tall warrior with a long, heavy spear approached. A cry was raised, and a space was cleared. He stood before the captive, poised his spear, swinging it backward and forward to make his arm supple before he thrust, like a bowler before delivering a ball. Feversham glanced wildly about, seeing no escape, and suddenly flung out his breast to meet the blow. But the spear never reached him. As the warrior lunged from the shoulder, one of the four guards jerked the neck chain violently from behind, and the prisoner was flung, half-throttled, onto his back. Three times, and each time to a roar of delight, this pastime was repeated. Then a soldier appeared in the gateway of Nejoumi's house.

"Bring him in!" he cried. Followed by the curses and threats of the crowd, the prisoner was dragged under the arch across a courtyard into a dark room.

For a few moments Feversham could see nothing. Then his eyes began to adapt to the gloom, and he distinguished a tall, bearded man sitting upon an angareb, the native bedstead of the Sudan, and two others squatting beside him on the ground. The man on the angareb was the Emir.

"You are a spy of the Government from Wadi Halfa," he said.

"No, I am a musician," returned the prisoner, laughing happily, like a man who has made a jest.

Nejoumi made a sign, and an instrument with many broken strings was handed to the captive. Feversham seated himself upon the ground. With slow, fumbling fingers, breathing hard as he bent over the zither, he began to elicit a wavering melody. It was the melody Durrance had listened to in the street of Tewfikieh on the eve of his last journey into the desert; and which Ethne Eustace had played only the night before in the quiet drawing-room at Southpool. It was the only melody Feversham knew. When he finished, Nejoumi began again.

"You are a spy."

"I have told you the truth," answered Feversham, stubbornly. Nejoumi took a different tone. He called for food, and the raw liver of a camel, covered with salt and red pepper, was placed before Feversham. Seldom had a man had smaller inclination to eat, but Feversham ate, none the less, knowing well that reluctance would be construed as fear, and that the signs of fear might condemn him to death. While he ate, Nejoumi questioned him, in the silkiest voice, about the fortifications of Cairo, the strength of the garrison at Assouan, and the rumors of dissension between the Khedive and the Sirdar.

But to each question Feversham replied: "How should a Greek know of these matters?"

Nejoumi rose from his angareb and roughly gave an order. The soldiers seized Feversham and dragged him out again into the sunlight. They poured water upon the palm-rope binding his wrists, so that the thongs swelled and bit into his flesh.

"Speak, Kaffir. You carry promises to Kordofan."

Feversham was silent. He clung doggedly to the plan over which he had so long and carefully pondered. He could not improve upon it by any alteration suggested by fear, at a moment when he could not think clearly. A rope was flung about his neck, and he was pushed and driven beneath the gallows.

"Speak, Kaffir," said Nejoumi; "so shall you escape death."

Feversham smiled and grimaced, and shook his head loosely from side to side. It was astonishing to him that he could do it, that he did not fall down upon his knees and beg for mercy. It was still more astonishing to him that he felt no temptation so to demean himself. He wondered whether the oft-repeated story was true, that criminals in English prisons went quietly and with dignity to the scaffold because they had been drugged. For without drugs, he seemed to be behaving with no less dignity himself. His heart was beating very fast, but with a sort of excitement. He did not even think of Ethne at that moment; certainly the great dread that his strong hope would never be fulfilled did not trouble him at all. He had his allotted part to play, and he just played it; and that was all.

Nejoumi looked at him sourly for a moment. He turned to the men who stood ready to draw away the angareb on which Feversham was placed.

"To-morrow," said he, "the Kaffir shall go to Omdurman."

Feversham began to feel then that the rope of palm fiber tortured his wrists.

Mrs. Adair watched the scene with a growing knot of anxiety in her stomach. She had spilled secrets to Durrance, and now she waited, trembling, to see how he would handle them. Part of her feared he would strip away the illusion, telling Ethne plainly that he had seen through her feigned affection for him to the true depth of her love for Feversham. If he did, he would fail to hide her role in the discovery, and Ethne would be forced to confront him, her eyes searching his face for the truth. It was a terrifying prospect. So, initially, she breathed a sigh of relief when nothing changed between Ethne and Durrance. They moved through the days as if the day Willoughby arrived and the evening Ethne played the *Musoline Overture* had never happened. But as the initial panic faded and the futility of her interference settled in, confusion gave way to anger and disappointment.

Durrance, however, had already decided on a course of action. Ethne wanted him to remain ignorant; in her distress, she found comfort in believing she had brought happiness to the one man she truly respected. Durrance saw no reason to shatter this fragile peace yet. He needed absolute certainty regarding the rift between Ethne and Feversham, and he needed to know Harry's fate before acting. So, he played the part of the oblivious friend. He dropped his scrutiny, feigned contentment, and made light of his own blindness, claiming Ethne's company was a sufficient compensation.

"You see," he told her, "one gets used to blindness and accepts it as natural. But one never gets used to you, Ethne. Every meeting reveals something new and delightful. Besides, there is always the possibility of a cure."

The strategy worked. Ethne sensed his suspicions had vanished, allowing her to mask her misery with his boundless cheerfulness. Yet her grief was profound. For a brief moment, she had felt the lightness of heart she possessed before the three white feathers arrived at Ramelton; now, she felt the heavy, dull ache that followed. There was a difference, though. Her pride was restored, and Durrance's words sparked a faint hope that Harry might yet be saved. But she knew the reality: the sleepless nights, the burning heat in her head as she waited for dawn. She could no longer pretend Harry was merely a dead friend. He was alive, trapped in unknown straits, and she thirsted to know his condition, her patience fraying at the edges.

"I suppose escape is possible from Omdurman," she said one day, forcing her voice to sound indifferent.

"Possible? Yes, I think so," Durrance replied cheerfully. "It is difficult and takes time. Attempts have been made to get Trench and others out, but they haven't succeeded yet. The problem is the intermediary."

Ethne looked sharply at him. "The intermediary?" she asked, then stopped herself abruptly. "I think I begin to understand. You mean the Arab who can move between Omdurman and the Egyptian frontier?"

"Yes," Durrance confirmed. "Usually a Dervish pedlar or merchant trading with the Sudanese tribes. He slips into Wadi Halfa or Assouan or Suakin to arrange the exchange. The risk is immense; he would face swift execution if caught. So, it's no wonder he shirks the danger at the last moment. Often, he is a rogue. You make arrangements in Egypt, pay him, and six months or a year later, he returns alone with excuses. 'Summer was unfavorable,' or 'the guards were tighter,' or 'I was suspected.' He asks for more money. His story may be true, you pay again, and he returns empty-handed once more."

Ethne nodded slowly. "Exactly."

In that moment, Durrance had explained a crucial detail she had missed. She realized Harry Feversham must have planned to use himself as the intermediary. She couldn't determine how his capture served that aim, but now she understood: he intended to be his own go-between. Her hopes surged. He had planned with care; he would be desperate for the second feather to return to her, and if he could rescue Trench, he wouldn't stay behind.

They sat on the terrace as the sunset painted the creek water red.

"Life would not be easy in the prison of Omdurman," Ethne murmured, striving for indifference.

"Easy!" Durrance exclaimed. "No, it would be terrible. A hovel crowded with Arabs, no light, no air, the roof perhaps two feet above your head. Locked from sundown to morning. Likely, the prisoners would stand all night in that foul den, packed so tightly. Imagine it here in England on a night like this! Think what it would be on an August night in the Sudan! Especially if you had memories of a place like this to make the torture worse."

Ethne gazed across the cool garden. Right now, Harry Feversham might be gasping for breath in that dark, stinking cell, dry-throated and feverish, with visions of Ramelton's grassy slopes and the liquid music of the Lennon River playing in his ears.

"One would pray for death," Ethne said slowly, "unless..." She paused, about to add, "unless one went there deliberately with a purpose," but cut herself off. Durrance finished the thought.

"Unless there was a chance of escape," he said. "And there is a chance—if Feversham is in Omdurman."

He feared he had revealed too much about the horrors of the prison, so he added, "Of course, what I've described is hearsay, not proven fact. Prisoners may not have such a hard time," and dropped the subject. Ethne didn't pursue it. She wondered how Durrance knew about her sudden disappearance from the drawing room that night, but he never mentioned it. The noticeable shift in his demeanor, the lack of his usual caution, soothed her fears. It seemed he had found a simple explanation for himself. She also questioned why he had told her about the meeting in Wadi Halfa and Feversham's journey south, but she found a strange, satisfying answer: she believed the news was a message delivered by a higher power, meant solely for her understanding.

For the month of September, they maintained the pretense. Every morning, while Durrance was in Devonshire, he would cross the fields to meet Ethne at The Pool. Mrs. Adair, watching them laugh and talk without a trace of estrangement, grew increasingly furious, finding it harder to maintain her silence. It was a month of strain for all three. Everyone felt a wave of relief when Durrance announced visits to his oculist in London. These trips increased in frequency and duration. Even Ethne was grateful; she could drop the mask, feel exhaustion, and find solitude to gather strength for her high spirits when Durrance returned. There were hours when despair seized her. "Shall I be able to keep up the pretense when we are married, when we are always together?" she asked herself. But she pushed the question away, daring not to look ahead lest her strength fail.

After the third visit, Durrance said, "Do you remember that famous oculist at Wiesbaden? It seems advisable I go to him."

"You are recommended to go?"

"Yes, and to go alone."

Ethne looked up with a sharp glance. "You think I'd be dull at Wiesbaden? No fear of that. I can find a relative to accompany me."

"No; it is for my own account," Durrance answered. "I may have to enter a home. It is better to be quiet and see no one for a time."

"You are sure?" Ethne asked. "It would hurt me if you proposed this because you felt I'd be happier at Glenalla."

"No, that isn't the reason," Durrance replied truthfully. He felt it necessary for both of them to separate. Like Ethne, he suffered under the tyranny of constant simulation. Only because he knew how deeply she valued keeping her resolve intact—that two lives shouldn't be ruined by her—could he hold back from shouting the truth.

"I am returning to London next week," he added. "When I come back, I'll be in a position to tell you if I'm going to Wiesbaden or not."

Durrance was glad he had mentioned this plan before Calder's telegram from Wadi Halfa arrived. Ethne couldn't link his departure to any news about Feversham. The telegram came one afternoon; Durrance took it to The Pool that evening and handed it to her. There were only four words:

"Feversham imprisoned at Omdurman."

With a newfound delicacy born of his suffering, Durrance stepped away as soon as he gave her the paper and joined Mrs. Adair in the drawing room. He folded the telegram so that by the time Ethne unfolded it, she was alone on the terrace. She recalled his words about the prison, and her imagination expanded upon them. The quiet of a September evening hung over the fields; a light mist rose from the creek and crept over the garden bank. Already, the prison doors were shut in that hot land where the Niles met. "He is to pay for his fault ten times over," she cried in revolt against the disproportion. "And the fault was his father's and mine too more than his own. For neither of us understood."

She blamed herself for giving him the fourth feather. Leaning on the stone balustrade with her eyes closed, she wondered if Harry would survive the night, if he was still alive to endure it. The coolness of the stones beneath her hands became the bitterest reproach.

"Something can now be done."

Durrance emerged from the drawing room window, warning her of his approach. "He was and is my friend; I cannot leave him there. I shall write tonight to Calder. Money will not be spared. He is my friend, Ethne. You will see. From Suakin or Assouan, something will be done."

He credited all potential aid to his own friendship, ensuring Ethne believed he had no further personal stake in Harry Feversham's fate.

She turned to him suddenly, interrupting him. "Major Castleton is dead?"

"Castleton?" he exclaimed. "There was a Castleton in Feversham's regiment. Is that the man?"

"Yes. He is dead?"

"He was killed at Tamai."

"You are sure—quite sure?"

"He was within the square of the Second Brigade on the edge of the great gulley when Osman Digna's men sprang out of the earth and broke through. I was in that square, too. I saw Castleton killed."

"I am glad," Ethne said.

She spoke simply and distinctly. The first feather had been brought by Captain Willoughby. It was possible Colonel Trench might bring back the second. Harry Feversham had succeeded once under great difficulty and peril. The peril was greater now, the obstacles more arduous, but she understood that. She took that one success as an omen that another might follow. Feversham had planned with care; he had the money to execute his plans. Besides, she was a woman of strong faith. She was relieved to know the sender of the third feather could never be approached. Moreover, she hated him, and that ended the matter.

Durrance was startled. He was a soldier of a type rarer than war stories suggest: Hector of Troy was his ancestor. He was neither hysterical nor vindictive, not an elderly schoolboy with a taste for loud talk, but a quiet man who did his work without noise, stern when needed, yet gentle and compassionate by nature. This barbaric utterance from Ethne Eustace baffled him.

"You disliked Major Castleton so much?" he exclaimed.

"I never knew him."

"Yet you are glad that he is dead?"

"I am quite glad," Ethne said stubbornly.

She made another slip speaking thus of Major Castleton, and Durrance did not miss it. He remembered it, pondering it in his gun-room at Guessens. It added weight to the explanation he was building regarding Harry Feversham's disgrace and disappearance. The story was becoming clear to his sharpened wits. Captain Willoughby's visit and the token he brought had given him the clue. A white feather meant nothing but an accusation of cowardice. Durrance couldn't recall detecting any sign of cowardice in Harry Feversham; the charge perpetually struck him as incredible.

But the fact remained. Something had happened on the night of the ball at Lennon House, and from that date, Harry had been an outcast. Suppose a white feather had been forwarded to Lennon House and opened in Ethne's presence? Or perhaps more than one? Ethne had returned from her long talk with Willoughby clutching that white feather as though it were the most precious thing in the world.

So much Mrs. Adair had told him.

It followed, then, that the cowardice had been atoned for, or at least in one particular instance. Ethne's recapture of her youth pointed inevitably to that conclusion. She treasured the feather because it was no longer a symbol of cowardice, but a symbol of cowardice atoned.

But Harry Feversham had not returned; he still slunk in the world's by-ways. Willoughby was not the only one who had brought the accusation; there were two others. Durrance had long since identified one of them. When Durrance suggested Harry might be taken to Omdurman, Ethne had immediately replied, "Colonel Trench is in Omdurman." She needed no explanation for Harry's disappearance from Wadi Halfa into the southern Sudan. It was deliberate; he had gone out to be captured, to be taken to Omdurman. Moreover, Ethne had spoken of the untrustworthiness of the go-between, which again aided Durrance's conjectures. There was some obligation upon Feversham to come to Trench's help. Suppose Feversham had laid his plans for rescue and ventured into the desert to be his own go-between? It followed that a second feather had been sent to Ramelton, and that Trench had sent it.

The smoke in the club's smoking-room hung thick and yellow, a fog that seemed to settle permanently around Captain Willoughby. To the regulars, he was a fixture of irritation, a man whose presence acted like a steam-roller on conversation, flattening any spark of wit into dull ash. He was a relentless raconteur, spinning tales of strangers with a vacant, amicable smile, utterly deaf to the polite snubs that usually forced him out. Yet, Willoughby was irresistible in his own way; his sheer, unadulterated boredom was a trap. Those who didn't know him would eventually flee, but those who did, like the members of his club, simply rose and scattered, leaving him with a large, empty corner of the room.

It was early October when the waiter approached this solitary island of silence, handing Willoughby a card. Willoughby took it eagerly; he craved company, and his usual circle had vanished into urgent engagements. But as he scanned the name, his expression darkened. "Colonel Durrance!" he muttered, scratching his head. Durrance was a man who had never visited him with anything resembling friendship. Why would he come now? It felt as though Durrance had somehow tracked him down to Kingsbridge.

"Does Colonel Durrance know I'm here?" Willoughby asked the waiter.

"Yes, sir."

"Show him in."

Willoughby braced himself. Diplomacy would be required, a delicate dance to deflect questions about Miss Ethne Eustace. He wanted Feversham and Durrance to fight their own battles, not his. As Durrance entered, looking calm and composed, Willoughby watched him with suspicion. To his surprise, Durrance offered no immediate inquiries. Instead, Willoughby rose and guided his visitor to the deserted corner, offering a cigarette with a nervous check.

"Oh, I'll smoke," Durrance replied smoothly. "A man can't truly enjoy tobacco unless he sees the smoke. If I let my cigar go out, I'd know at once. But don't worry, I won't." He lit his cigar with deliberate care and leaned back.

"I'm lucky to find you, Willoughby," Durrance said. "I'm only in town today, coming up from Devonshire to see my oculist. Mather told me you were away in the country on my last visit. You remember Mather? We were together in Suakin."

"Of course," Willoughby said, his tone instantly warmer. Talking about Mather was a safe harbor; perhaps it would distract Durrance from the matter that clearly troubled him.

"We're both curious about one thing," Durrance continued. "Did you ever encounter an Arab named Abou Fatma?"

"Abou Fatma," Willoughby repeated slowly. "One of the Hadendoas?"

"No, a Kabbabish."

"Abou Fatma?" Willoughby feigned ignorance, stopping abruptly. It wasn't a natural pause; he expected a follow-up question. Instead, he was internally panicking, wondering how Durrance knew of a name he himself had only heard once a year prior on the verandah of the Palace at Suakin. He had told the truth; he had never met Abou Fatma, though Feversham had.

"That makes me even more curious," Durrance pressed. "Mather and I were on the last reconnaissance in '84. We found Abou Fatma hiding near Sinkat fort. He spoke of the Gordon letters hidden in Berber. Ah, you remember the name now."

"I was just retrieving my pipe," Willoughby lied, pulling it from his pocket. "But the name rings a bell now that you mention the letters."

"They were brought to you in Suakin some fifteen months ago," Durrance noted. "Mather showed me the report in the *Evening Standard*. I'm curious if Abou Fatma returned to Berber and retrieved them. But since you've never seen him, it follows he wasn't the man."

Willoughby felt a pang of regret for his hasty denial. "No, it wasn't Abou Fatma," he admitted with an awkward hesitation, dreading the next question. He filled his pipe, searching for an answer, but Durrance offered none.

"I wondered," Durrance mused. "I thought Abou Fatma wouldn't return to Berber. After all, whoever took that job did so at the risk of his life, and with Gordon dead, for no obvious reason."

"Quite so," Willoughby breathed, feeling a wave of relief. "Since Gordon was dead, for no reason."

"For no obvious reason, I think I said," Durrance corrected calmly. Willoughby glanced at him suspiciously, wondering if Durrance knew about his trip to Kingsbridge. Durrance, however, seemed satisfied, leaning back with his face tilted toward the ceiling, having lost interest in the Gordon letters. He put no further questions to the trap.

Thinking of how Harry Feversham might redeem himself, Durrance realized the only path was the recovery of those letters from the ruined wall in Berber. There had been no danger in Suakin since that last reconnaissance; the great troop-ships had sailed safely to Suez. Willoughby had risked only his health in that white palace on the Red Sea. There was no moment where Feversham could claim, "Your life was forfeit but for me." Durrance, reviewing the gossip from Wadi Halfa and his furloughs, was convinced Feversham had repeated his story at Suakin within Willoughby's hearing. Willoughby's reticence confirmed it. Durrance, a master of observation, had seen the wariness in Willoughby's voice—the same caution Ethne Eustace often used. He had set traps, specifically the comment about "no obvious reason," and Willoughby had stumbled into them.

Durrance had no desire for Willoughby to warn Ethne. He needed to guard against that possibility.

"I want to tell you why I sought you out," Durrance said. "It was because of Harry Feversham."

Willoughby, congratulating himself on escaping the Abou Fatma interrogation, jumped in his seat. Durrance ignored the agitation. "Something happened to Feversham. Five years ago. He did something, or left something undone—the secret has been kept—and he disappeared. Now, you're returning to the Sudan, Willoughby?"

"Yes, in a week."

"Well, Harry Feversham is in the Sudan," Durrance leaned in. "I met him this spring at Wadi Halfa. He had fallen rather low. It's strange, isn't it? A man known well going under in a second, disappearing as if down an oubliette in an old French castle. I want you to look out for him. Let me know if you find him. Harry Feversham was a friend—one of my few real friends."

"All right," Willoughby said cheerfully, his suspicion momentarily quieted. "I'll look out for Feversham. I remember he was a great friend of yours."

He reached for a match. Durrance heard the scrape of phosphorus and the flare. Willoughby lit his pipe, a well-seasoned briar that bubbled as he sucked on the stem.

"Yes, a great friend," Durrance said. "You and I dined with him in his flat above St. James's Park just before we left England."

At that casual sentence, Willoughby's pipe ceased bubbling. A moment of silence passed, then Willoughby swore violently and stamped his foot on the carpet. Durrance's imagination sparked. He pictured the scene: himself smoking, a round table between them, and Willoughby frozen mid-light, staring suspiciously at the blind man until the match burned his fingers, causing the curse and the stamp. Durrance had never given that dinner a thought until now. It deserved scrutiny.

"There were you, me, and Feversham," Durrance continued. "Feversham asked us there to announce his engagement to Miss Eustace. He had just returned from Dublin. That was almost the last we saw of him." He took a drag and added, "By the way, there was a third man present."

"Was there?" Willoughby asked, feigning forgetfulness. "It's so long ago."

"Yes—Trench."

"To be sure," Willoughby said, his voice carefully careless. "We won't dine with poor old Trench again soon."

"And we shall never dine with Castleton again," Durrance said slowly.

"Castleton wasn't there," Willoughby exclaimed, the slip betraying that the memory was vivid despite the years.

"No, but he was expected," Durrance countered.

"No, not even expected," Willoughby corrected quickly. "He was dining elsewhere. He sent the telegram, you remember."

"Ah, yes, a telegram came," Durrance acknowledged.

That dinner party was the key. Willoughby, Trench, and Castleton were the architects of Harry Feversham's disgrace. Durrance tried to recall the details; he remembered leaning against the window above St. James's Park, hearing the tattoo from Wellington Barracks, and receiving the telegram. He visualized another scene: Feversham at the table, reading and re-reading the message, while Trench and Willoughby waited silently, expectant, and Durrance staring out into the cool park, indifferent.

"Castleton was dining with a big man from the War Office that night," Durrance said. A slight movement at his side warned him he was getting too close. He sat a while longer discussing the Sudan's prospects before rising.

"Well, I can rely on you, Willoughby, to help Feversham if you find him. Draw on me for money."

"I will do my best," Willoughby said. "You're going? I could have won a bet off you this afternoon."

"How?"

"You said you never let your cigars go out. This one is stone cold."

"I forgot; I was thinking of Feversham. Good-bye."

Durrance took a cab and drove away. Willoughby was glad to see him go, satisfied with his diplomatic performance. He had hoodwinked the old colonel, he thought, and returned to the smoking-room for a whiskey and potass.

But Durrance had not been hoodwinked. The final puzzle piece had clicked into place. He recalled that no one at the dinner had identified the sender of the telegram. Feversham had read it, crumpled it, and tossed it into the fire without a word. But now Willoughby claimed it came from Castleton, who was dining with a high War Office official. The act of cowardice that earned the three white feathers at Ramelton became clear. Almost immediately after the telegram arrived, Feversham had resigned his commission. Durrance knew he hadn't resigned when the news came. The telegram must have announced that Feversham's regiment was ordered on active service. The more Durrance reflected, the more certain he became. Nothing was more natural than for Castleton to share good news in confidence.

He now had the complete sequence of facts. Why Feversham had panicked, why he had played the coward the moment after his engagement to Ethne Eustace—a time when every manly quality should have been at its peak—remained an inexplicable mystery. But Durrance set that question aside, dismissing it as trivial. The simple, true reality emerged clear: Harry Feversham was atoning for his single act of cowardice with a full and overflowing measure of penance.

"I shall astonish old Sutch," Durrance chuckled to himself. He took the night mail to Devonshire that same evening, reaching home before midday.

The carriage waited at the steps of Guessens, its roof laden with Durrance's luggage, his servant poised at the door. Within the drawing-room, Durrance bid Ethne farewell with a hurried efficiency, arranging their parting to be brief. Yet, as they stepped onto the terrace, Mrs. Adair stood at the top of the stone flight, intercepting the moment.

"I must speak to Colonel Durrance before he departs," she said.

Ethne nodded, turning to her lover. "Then we shall say goodbye here," she told Durrance, her voice tight with the weight of recent months. "Write from Wiesbaden? Soon, please!"

"The instant I arrive," Durrance promised.

He descended the steps with Mrs. Adair, leaving Ethne alone on the terrace. The theater of pretense had finally closed; the long vigil of surveillance was over, and both men and women breathed freely. Durrance moved across the lawn with brisk energy, eager to leave the house behind. Mrs. Adair, however, trailed in his wake, her step heavy.

"So you are going," she murmured, her voice dripping with desolation. "In two days, you will be in Wiesbaden, and Ethne at Glenalla. We shall be scattered. This place will feel terribly empty."

She had achieved her goal: separation. The sight of them together, the sound of their voices, was no longer a torture. Yet, strangely, her victory tasted like ash. "The house will feel very hollow after you are all gone," she added, turning to walk beside him into the garden.

"We shall return, no doubt," Durrance offered, trying to soothe her.

Mrs. Adair scanned the grounds. The summer blooms were gone, the sunlight dimmed by gathering clouds. The grass beneath their feet was a dull green, the stream running grey through the trees, and autumn leaves—russet and yellow—drifted across the lawns.

"How long will you stay in Wiesbaden?" she asked.

"I cannot say. As long as it is advisable," he replied.

"That tells me nothing," she snapped. "I suppose that was the point."

Durrance said nothing, and her silence bred resentment. She knew nothing of his plans, unaware if he intended to honor his engagement to Ethne or sever it entirely. Curvature gnawed at her. The prospect of a long separation filled her with dread.

"Do you distrust me?" she demanded, her voice rising with anger.

Durrance answered gently. "Have I no cause to distrust you? Why did you reveal Captain Willoughby's arrival? Why did you interfere?"

"I thought you ought to know."

"But Ethne wished that secret kept. I am glad to know, very glad. Yet, after all, you told me, and you were Ethne's friend."

"Yours, too, I hope," Mrs. Adair retorted, her composure cracking. "How could I maintain silence? Don't you understand?"

"No."

Durrance might have understood, but he had never truly considered her, and she felt the sting of that indifference. His simple denial rankled.

"I spoke brutally, didn't I?" she said. "I told you the truth as harshly as I could. Does that not help you understand?"

Again, Durrance said only "No." The monosyllable shattered her restraint. Suddenly, she began to speak incoherently, pouring out thoughts she had long suppressed. Standing outside herself, she watched her own madness unfold, yet she could not stop.

"I told you the truth brutally on purpose," she confessed, her voice trembling. "I was stung because you refused to see what was plain to anyone else. I wanted to hurt you. I am a bad, bad woman, I suppose. There you and she were in the room, talking in the dark; there I was alone on the terrace. It was the same today. You and Ethne inside, I outside. I wonder if it will always be so. But you will not say—you will not say." She struck her hands together in despair, but Durrance walked silently toward the stile, quickening his pace until she had to hurry to keep up. It was a silent rebuke, but it failed to deter her.

"I would not have minded so much," she continued, her scorn sharpening, "if Ethne had truly cared for you. But she never did more than a friend cares. And what is friendship worth?"

"Something, surely," Durrance said.

"It does not prevent Ethne from shrinking from her friend!" Mrs. Adair cried. "She shrinks from you. Shall I tell you why? Because you are blind. She is afraid. While I... while I will tell you the truth—I am glad. When the news came from Wadi Halfa that you were blind, I was glad; when I saw you in Hill Street, I was glad; ever since, I have been glad—quite glad. Because I saw her shrink. From the beginning, she shrank, fearing her life would be hampered and fettered," her voice dropping to a whisper, thick with venom. "I am not afraid," she repeated passionately. "I am not afraid. I am not afraid."

To Durrance, this outburst from Ethne's friend seemed more horrific than anything he had ever witnessed.

"Ethne wrote to you at Wadi Halfa out of pity," Mrs. Adair insisted. "That was all. She wrote out of pity; then, terrified of what she had done, she lacked the courage to admit her fear. You would not have blamed her if she had been frank; you would have remained her friend. But she had no courage."

Durrance knew there was another explanation, the true one, for Ethne's timidity. He also knew that arguing against Mrs. Adair's slanders now would be futile. Tomorrow he would leave the Salcombe estuary, and Ethne would head for the Irish Channel. He merely said quietly, "You are not just to Ethne."

At those words, Mrs. Adair's madness evaporated. She recognized the futility of her boasts and her detractions. Her words might be true, but they achieved nothing. Durrance was always in the room with Ethne, never on the terrace with her. Realizing her degradation, she fell back into excuses.

"I am a bad woman, I suppose," she whispered, sounding pitiful even to herself. "But after all, I have not had the happiest of lives. Perhaps there is something to be said for me."

They reached the stile, and Durrance turned to face her. He saw the sternness fade from his own expression, replaced by a quiet readiness to listen. He recalled the dignity and reticence she once possessed, and the violence of the contrast made him willing to hear her side.

"Will you tell me?" he asked gently.

"I was married almost straight from school," she began, her voice simple and unadorned. "I was a mere girl, knowing nothing, married to a man I knew nothing of. It was my mother's doing. She thought she was acting for the best, securing me a position, comfort, and safety from poverty. I accepted blindly, ignorantly. My mother was imperious, and I was accustomed to obedience. I married dutifully the man she chose. The case is common, no doubt, but its frequency makes it no easier to endure."

"But Mr. Adair?" Durrance interjected. "After all, I knew him. He was older, certainly, but kind. I think he cared for you."

"Yes. He was kindness itself, and he cared for me. Both are true. That knowledge was the only link. At first, I was content. I had a house in town and another here. But it was dull," she gestured with her arms, spreading them wide. "Oh, how dull it was! Do you know the little back streets of a manufacturing town? Rows of identical houses, ugly and regular, with the same windows, the same doors, the same steps. Overhead, a drift of smoke; below, every green thing black and dirty. It is the sort of street where any charlatan promising color to grey lives can gather followers. When I thought of my life, one of those streets always came to mind. There are women, countless women, for whom managing a large house, the London season, and the round of visits are enough. I, alas, was not one of them. Dull! You, with your hundred thousand duties, cannot conceive how oppressively dull my life was. And that was not all!" She hesitated, unable to stop midway.

"I married, knowing nothing of the important things. I believed at first that this was the allotted life of all women. But soon doubts arose. I learned that there was something more to be won from existence than mere dullness; at least, something more for others, though not for me. One cannot help learning this. One passes a man and woman riding together, glances into the woman's face, or speaks briefly to her alone, and from the happiness in her eyes and voice, one knows with absolute certainty that there is ever so much more. Only the chance of that 'ever so much more' my mother had denied to me."

All sternness had vanished from Durrance's face. Mrs. Adair spoke with great simplicity, the violence of her earlier outburst gone. She did not appeal for pity; she simply told her story.

"And then you came," she continued. "I met you, and met you again. You went away on your duties and returned; and I learned now, not that there was 'ever so much more,' but exactly what that meant. But it was still denied to me. Yet, in spite of that, I felt happier. I thought I would be content to have you as a friend, to watch your progress, and to feel pride in it. But you see—Ethne came, too, and you turned to her. At once—oh, at once! If you had only been a little slower to turn to her! In a very short while, I was sad and sorry that you had ever entered my life."

"I knew nothing of this," Durrance said. "I never suspected. I am sorry."

"I took care you should not suspect," Mrs. Adair replied. "But I tried to keep you; with all my wits I tried. No matchmaker in the world ever worked harder to bring two people together than I did to unite Ethne and Mr. Feversham, and I succeeded."

The statement struck Durrance like a blow. He leaned back against the stile, almost laughing. Here was the origin of the entire tragedy. From such small beginnings it had grown! It was a trite reflection, but personally applied, it stole the breath. Durrance thought backward to those days when he walked his own path, heedless of the people he touched, never dreaming they were influencing his life right up to his dying day. Feversham's disgrace, Ethne's years of unhappiness, the wearying pretenses of the last few months—all stemmed from years ago when Mrs. Adair, to keep Durrance for herself, had thrown Feversham and Ethne into each other's company.

"I succeeded," Mrs. Adair continued. "You told me one morning in the Row that I had succeeded. How glad I was! You did not notice, I am sure. The next moment, you took all my joy by telling me you were starting for the Soudan. You were away three years. They were not happy years for me. You came back. My husband was dead, but Ethne was free. Ethne refused you, but you went blind, and she claimed you. You can see what ups and downs have fallen to me. But these months here have been the worst."

"I am very sorry," Durrance said. Mrs. Adair was right; there was indeed something to be said for her. The world had been cruel to her. He realized her suffering, for he suffered in much the same way himself. It was intelligible why she had betrayed Ethne's secret that night on the terrace, and he could not help but be gentle.

"I am very sorry, Mrs. Adair," he repeated lamely. There was nothing more to say, and he held out his hand.

"Good-bye," she said. Durrance climbed over the stile and crossed the fields to his house.

Mrs. Adair stood by the stile for a long time after he had gone. She had fired her bolt, hitting no one but herself and the man she cared for. She realized this distinctly. She looked forward slightly and understood that if Durrance did not keep Ethne to her promise and marry her, he would return to Guessens. That reflection revealed the folly of her outcry even more clearly. If she had kept silent, she would have had a true and constant friend for her neighbor, and that would have been something. It would have been a great deal. But since she had spoken, they could never meet without embarrassment. No matter how cordially they practiced, there would always remain in their minds the recollection of what she had said and what he had listened to on the afternoon he left for Wiesbaden.

The river cut through a landscape of indifference, a callous country inhabited by a callous race, observed Calder as he drifted downstream from Wadi Halfa toward Assouan on his three-month leave. Leaning against the railing of the upper deck, he watched the barge lashed tightly to the steamer's side. Below, amidst the native passengers, sat an *angareb*—a rough matting bedstead—bearing a motionless figure swathed in a black veil. Two Arabs had hauled this burden aboard at Korosko that morning; now, seated in the stern, they laughed and chattered, paying no heed to the weight they carried. To the casual eye, the occupant might have been a dead man or woman, so utterly neglected was the form. Calder scanned the horizon, where glaring sands and barren rocks, sculpted into the hard, silent shapes of pyramids, dominated the view. The only sign of life was a narrow, meagre strip of green clinging to the water's edge, the sole response of the Sudan to the relentless heat.

Calder's gaze returned to the *angareb*. Beneath the black cloth, the outline of a nose, hollow eyes, and a mouth was visible, but the veil concealed whether the lips bore a moustache or the chin a beard. The slanting sunlight crept closer, burning the yellow light over the black recumbent form. The natives nearby sought the shade of the upper deck, yet no one moved the *angareb*, and the two men in the stern remained oblivious to their charge. As the sun blazed pitilessly upon the face, the living creature beneath did not stir. The veil did not flutter, the legs remained rigid, and the arms lay pressed against the side.

"Move the *angareb* into the shadow," Calder shouted to the men in the stern. "And be quick!"

The Arabs rose reluctantly and complied.

"Is it a man or a woman?" Calder asked.

"A man," one replied. "We are taking him to the hospital in Assouan, but we do not think he will live. He fell from a palm tree three weeks ago."

"You give him nothing to eat or drink?"

"He is too ill."

It was a familiar tale, the logical conclusion of a fatalistic belief that life and death were pre-written scripts. The man lying quietly beneath the black covering had likely suffered nothing more than a bruise at the start, a wound curable within a week by simple remedies. Instead, he had been left to the mercy of the sun and flies, unwashed, unfed, and parched. The bruise had festered into a sore, the sore had gangrened, and when all hope was lost, the Egyptian Mudir of Korosko had discovered the accident and dispatched the man downriver. Yet, despite the familiarity of the story, Calder could not shake the fascination of the man's immobility. When a strong wind sprang up at sunset, blowing against the current, he felt a strange comfort knowing the sick man would find some relief.

That evening, as his neighbor at the dinner table spoke with a German accent, an impulse seized Calder.

"You are not a doctor by any chance?"

"Not a doctor," the man replied, "but a student of medicine at Bonn. I came from Cairo to see the Second Cataract, but was not allowed to go farther than Wadi Halfa."

Calder interrupted immediately. "Then I will trespass upon your holiday and claim your professional assistance."

"For yourself? With pleasure, though I should never have guessed you were ill," the student smiled, adjusting his eyeglasses.

"Nor am I. It is an Arab for whom I ask your help."

"The man on the bedstead?"

"Yes, if you will be so good. I must warn you—he was hurt three weeks ago, and I know these people. No one has touched him since. The sight will not be pretty. This is not a nice country for untended wounds."

The German student shrugged. "All experience is good," he said, and the two men rose to join him on the upper deck.

The wind had freshened during dinner, raising waves that tossed the steamer and its barge, breaking the water against the hull.

"He was below there," the student noted, leaning over the rail to peer into the darkness of the lower deck. Night had fallen, and save for a single lamp swinging from the center of the upper deck, the area was swallowed by blackness. Beyond the circle of uncertain light, only the bow remained visible, where the breaking water flung a white sheet of spray like driven snow.

"He has been moved," the student observed. "There is no one in the bows."

Calder stared into the gloom. "I believe the *angareb* is there. I believe it is."

Following the student, he hurried down the stairway to the lower deck and stepped onto the side. Now he could be certain. The *angareb* stood in a wash of water exactly where it had been moved that morning. Upon it, the figure beneath the black covering lay as motionless as ever, expressionless, though the cold spray broke continually upon its face.

"I thought it would be so," Calder said. He retrieved a lantern and, with the German student, climbed across the bulwarks onto the barge. He summoned the two Arabs.

"Move the *angareb* from the bows," he commanded. Once they had obeyed, he added, "Now take that covering off. I wish my friend, who is a doctor, to see the wound."

The two men hesitated. One, with an air of insolence, objected. "There are doctors in Assouan, whither we are taking him."

Calder raised the lantern and drew the veil away from the wounded man himself. "Now, if you please," he told his companion. The German student examined the wounded thigh while Calder held the lantern high. As predicted, it was a grim business; the wound crawled with infection. The student was glad to cover it up again.

"I can do nothing," he said. "Perhaps in a hospital, with baths and dressings... relief will be given at all events, but more? I do not know. Here, I could not even begin." He glanced at the Arabs. "Do these two men understand English?"

"No," Calder answered.

"Then I can tell you something. He did not get the hurt by falling out of a palm tree. That is a lie. The injury was done by the blade of a spear or some similar weapon."

"Are you sure?"

"Yes."

Calder bent suddenly toward the Arab on the *angareb*. Though the man never moved, he was conscious. Calder had watched him steadily and saw that his eyes followed the spoken words.

"You understand English?" Calder asked.

The Arab could not answer with his lips, but a look of comprehension flashed in his face.

"Where do you come from?"

The lips tried to move, but no whisper escaped. Yet his eyes spoke, vainly, with a great eagerness to answer. Calder dropped to his knees beside the man's head, holding the lantern close, and enunciated the names of towns.

"From Dongola?"

No gleam in the Arab's eyes responded.

"From Metemneh? From Berber? From Omdurman? Ah!"

The Arab answered to that word; he closed his eyelids. Calder pressed on eagerly.

"You were wounded there? No. Where then? At Berber? Yes. You were in prison at Omdurman and escaped? No. Yet you were wounded."

Calder sank back on his knees, his reflection sparking with excitement. He leaned closer to the Arab's ear, speaking in a lower key.

"You were helping someone to escape? Yes. Who? El Kaimakam Trench? No." He listed the names of other white captives from Omdurman, and to each, the Arab's eyes answered "No." "It was Effendi Feversham, then?" he said, and the eyes assented as clearly as if lips had spoken.

But this was all the information Calder could secure. "I too am pledged to help Effendi Feversham," he said, but in vain. The Arab could not speak, could not even tell his name, and his companions refused to meddle. Clinging to a story that absolved them of responsibility, they insisted they were merely following orders from kinsmen in Korosko to transport a stranger to Assouan. Calder obtained the news Durrance waited for—the confirmation of Harry Feversham's fate—but it remained hidden in a locked book within the Arab's mind. He stood beside the helpless man on the *angareb*. There he was, eager enough to speak, but the extremity of weakness laid a finger upon his lips. All Calder could do was ensure he reached the hospital in Assouan safely.

"Will he recover?" Calder asked, and the doctors shook their heads in doubt. There was a chance, perhaps a very slight one, but recovery would be slow.

Calder continued his journey to Cairo and then to Europe. An opportunity to aid Harry Feversham had slipped away. The Arab who could not speak his own name was Abou Fatma of the Kabbabish tribe, and his presence, wounded and helpless upon the Nile steamer between Korosko and Assouan, meant that Harry Feversham's carefully laid plan for the rescue of Colonel Trench had failed.

As Calder stepped into a carriage at Assouan, his heart heavy with the failure to find Harry Feversham, miles away in Hampshire, Lieutenant Sutch was traversing a high white road. The landscape stretched out beneath him, a vast common of heather and gorse, but Sutch's mind was far from the rolling fields. Like many who spend their lives in solitude, he had cultivated the habit of voicing his internal monologue. As he guided his pony slowly and reluctantly forward, the words escaped him again and again: "I foresaw there would be trouble. From the very beginning, I foresaw it."

The road dipped suddenly into a hollow, revealing a steep descent flanked by dense forests of pine leading down to a small railway station. The sight of the gleaming rails cutting straight into the afternoon sun, converging in the distance like a single line of destiny, unsettled Sutch further. He reined in his pony, staring with a furrowed brow at the red-tiled roof of the station.

"I promised Harry to say nothing," he murmured, finding a fragile comfort in the repetition. "I promised faithfully in the Criterion grill-room."

A distant whistle pierced the air, sharp and clear, breaking his gloom. He watched the white smoke of an approaching train stretch across the horizon like a ribbon.

"I wonder what brings him," he muttered, doubt coloring his voice. Then, steeling himself, he added, "Well, it's no use shirking." With a flick of his whip, he urged the pony down the hill, reaching the station just as the train pulled to a halt. Only two passengers disembarked: Colonel Durrance and his servant. They emerged immediately onto the road. Sutch hailed Durrance, who walked purposefully to the side of the trap.

"By all means," Sutch replied, though the stiffness in his tone betrayed the reluctance in his heart. His suspicion was well-founded. The telegram had merely announced a visit, omitting its purpose, yet its dispatch served as a warning that something grave had transpired regarding Ethne Eustace and Durrance. Sutch knew Durrance had come to renew his inquiries about Harry Feversham—questions Sutch was sworn to parry. After all, he reasoned, it would not be difficult to deceive a blind man. Ethne herself had harbored the same thought, feeling a similar relief. The lieutenant was so relieved that a pang of pity surfaced within him.

"I was very sorry, Durrance, to hear of your bad luck," he said as they drove up the hill. I don't speak of compensations and patience; that is the chatter of the comfortable who have never suffered. We know that for a young, active man in a career where action is vital, there are no compensations if his path is abruptly cut short through no fault of his own."

"Through no fault of his," Durrance repeated slowly and thoughtfully. "I agree. It is only the man whose career ends through his own fault who receives compensation."

Sutch glanced sharply at his companion. Was Durrance referring to Harry Feversham? Did he possess enough knowledge to make such a reference, or was it mere coincidence that his words were so strikingly apt?

"Compensations of what kind?" Sutch asked, unease tightening his chest.

"The chance of knowing oneself, for one thing—the chief thing. He is halted, stopped in his career, perhaps disgraced." Sutch flinched at the word. "Yes, perhaps... disgraced," Durrance echoed. "Well, the shock of disgrace is, after all, his opportunity. Do you see? It is his opportunity to know himself at last. Until that moment, his life has been a sham and an illusion; the man he believed himself to be never existed. Now, at the end, he knows the truth. Once he knows it, he can begin to retrieve his disgrace. Oh, there are compensations for such a man. You and I know a case in point."

Sutch no longer doubted that Durrance was deliberately speaking of Harry Feversham. He possessed some knowledge, though Sutch could not fathom how he had acquired it. The information was not entirely accurate, and the inaccuracy did Harry a disservice. This was the primary reason Sutch did not feign ignorance regarding Durrance's allusion. Years had not dimmed his profound regard for Harry; he loved him with a woman's concentrated intensity and could not bear for his memory to be slighted.

"The case you and I know of is not quite in point," Sutch argued. "You are speaking of Harry Feversham."

"Who believed himself a coward and was not one. He commits the fault that stops his career, discovers his mistake, and sets himself to the work of retrieving his disgrace. Surely it is a case quite in point."

"Yes, I see," Sutch agreed. "There is another view—a wrong view, as I know—but I thought for a moment it was yours—that Harry fancied himself brave and was suddenly brought up short by discovering he was a coward. But how did you find out? No one knew the whole truth except myself."

"I am engaged to Miss Eustace," Durrance said.

"She did not know everything. She knew of the disgrace, but not of the determination to retrieve it."

"She knows now," Durrance replied sharply. "You are glad of that—very glad."

Sutch was unaware that any movement or exclamation had betrayed his joy. His face undoubtedly showed it, but Durrance could not see. Puzzled, Sutch did not deny the imputation.

"It is true," he said stoutly. "I am very glad that she knows. I can quite see that from your point of view it would be better if she did not. But I cannot help it. I am very glad."

Durrance laughed, not unpleasantly. "I like you the better for being glad," he said.

"You are wrong. There is Captain Willoughby. He came to Devonshire six weeks ago. He brought with him a white feather, which he gave to Miss Eustace as proof that he withdrew his charge of cowardice against Harry Feversham."

Sutch stopped the pony in the middle of the road. He no longer concealed the joy this news inspired. In fact, he forgot Durrance's presence entirely. He sat silent and still, radiating a happiness he had never known in his life. He was an old man now, well into his sixties, an age when blood runs slow and pleasures are sober, gray things, and joy has lost its feverish edge. Yet, a buoyant gladness welled up in his heart, a sensation reserved only for youth. Five years ago, on the pier of Dover, he had watched a mail packet steam away into darkness and rain, praying that he might live to witness this great moment. He had lived, and it had come. His heart swelled with gratitude. It seemed to him that a great burst of sunlight had broken across the world, transforming it into a place of vibrant color and joy. Ever since the night he stood outside the War Office in Pall Mall, watching Harry Feversham touch his arm and speak his despair, Sutch had been oppressed by a sense of guilt. Harry was Muriel Feversham's son, and Sutch, for that reason alone, should have watched over him in his boyhood since his mother was gone, and fathered him in his youth since his father did not understand. He had heard her voice in his dreams, gentle yet insistent: "Since I was dead, since I was taken away to where I could only see and not help, surely you might have helped. Now, however, the guilt was lifted from his shoulders, removed by Harry Feversham's own act. "I knew," he exclaimed. "I knew he wouldn't fail. Oh, I am glad you came today, Colonel Durrance. It was partly my fault that Harry Feversham ever incurred that charge of cowardice. He feared to miss a word. Durrance recounted the story of the Gordon letters and their recovery by Feversham. It was all too brief for Lieutenant Sutch.

"No, indeed," Durrance replied. "She said not a word about it, she never showed me the feather, she even forbade Willoughby to hint of it, sending him away from Devonshire before I knew he had come. You are disappointed at that," he added quickly.

Sutch was startled. It was true he was disappointed; he was jealous of Durrance, wishing Harry Feversham to stand first in the girl's thoughts. It was for her sake that Harry had undertaken his difficult and perilous work. Sutch wished her to remember him as he remembered her. Durrance laughed.

Sutch jumped in his seat. It was mere chance, of course, that Durrance continually guessed with such singular accuracy; still, it was uncomfortable.

"I have said nothing which could in any way suggest that I was surprised," he said testily.

"That is quite true, but you are none the less surprised," Durrance continued. "I don't blame you. You could not know that it is only since I have been blind that I have begun to see. Sutch turned quickly toward Durrance.

Never in the thirty years of his residence had there been such noise and flurry. His servants scrambled frantically through the passages, searching for forgotten articles of his old traveling kit, but Sutch remained undeterred. He was in a boyish fever of excitement. After three decades of inactivity, living on the world's half-pay list, something miraculous had finally fallen to him: a mission requiring energy, tact, and decisive action. He was to be employed again, and he dreaded the short interval before he could begin, fearing some unforeseen hindrance might relegate him back to obscurity.

"I shall be ready this afternoon," he announced briskly to Durrance over breakfast. "I'll catch the night mail to the Continent. We might go up to London together, for London lies on your way to Wiesbaden."

"No," said Durrance. "I have just one more visit to pay in England. I didn't think of it until I was in bed last night. You put it into my head."

"Oh," observed Sutch, "and whom do you propose to visit?"

"General Feversham," replied Durrance.

Sutch laid down his knife and fork, staring at his companion with surprise. "Why in the world do you wish to see him?"

"I want to tell him how Harry has redeemed his honor, how he is still redeeming it. You said last night you were bound by a promise not to inform him of Harry's intentions or success until the son returned. But I am bound by no such promise. I believe such a promise bears hardly on the General. Nothing in the world would pain him more than the proof that his son was a coward. Harry might have robbed or murdered; the old man would have preferred either crime to this. I shall cross into Surrey this morning to tell him that Harry never was a coward."

Sutch shook his head.

"He won't understand. He'll be grateful, of course, and glad that Harry has atoned, but he'll never comprehend why he incurred the disgrace in the first place. In the end, he'll only be glad because the family honor is restored."

"I don't agree," said Durrance. "I believe the old man is rather fond of his son, though he'd never admit it. I rather like General Feversham."

Sutch had seen very little of the General in the last five years. He could not forgive him for his share in Harry's ruin. If the General had possessed the capacity for sympathy or comprehension of the boy's nature, the white feathers would never have been sent to Ramelton. Sutch pictured the old man sitting sternly on his terrace at Broad Place, unaware of his own culpability, posing instead as a martyr whose son had become a shame to all the dead Fevershams whose portraits hung darkly on the high walls. Sutch felt he could never endure talking patiently with such a stubborn man and was certain no argument would sway him. He had not troubled to consider whether Durrance's news should reach Broad Place.

"You are very thoughtful for others," he remarked to Durrance.

"It's not to my credit," said Durrance. "I practice thoughtfulness out of an instinct of self-preservation. Selfishness is the natural, encroaching fault of the blind; I know that, so I guard against it."

That morning, Durrance traveled by branch lines from Hampshire into Surrey, arriving at Broad Place in the glow of the afternoon. General Feversham was now within months of his eightieth year. Though his back was as stiff and his figure as erect as it had been when he first presented Harry to his Crimean friends, he had shrunk in stature, and his face seemed smaller. Durrance had walked with the General on the terrace only two years prior; even blind, he noticed the change. Old Feversham walked with a heavier step, and a note of puerility had entered his voice.

"You've joined the veterans before your time, Durrance," the General said. "I read of it in a newspaper. I would have written had I known where to write."

If he suspected the purpose of Durrance's visit, he gave no sign. He rang the bell, and tea was brought into the great hall where the portraits hung. He inquired after officers from the Sudan he knew, discussed the iniquities of the War Office, and feared the country was heading for the deuce.

"Everything through ill-luck or bad management is going to the devil, sir," he exclaimed irritably. "Even you, Durrance, you are not the same man who walked with me on my terrace two years ago."

The General had never been remarkable for tact, and his solitary life had brought no improvement. Durrance could have countered with a *tu quoque*, but he refrained.

"But I come upon the same business," he said.

Feversham sat up stiffly in his chair.

"And I give you the same answer. I have nothing to say about Harry Feversham. I will not discuss him."

He spoke in his usual hard, emotionless tone, as if addressing a stranger. Even the name was uttered without the slightest hint of sorrow. Durrance began to wonder if the fountains of affection had entirely dried up in the General's heart.

"It would not please you, then, to know where Harry Feversham has been, and how he has lived during the last five years?"

There was a pause—not long, but significant—before the General answered.

"Not in the least, Colonel Durrance."

The answer was uncompromising, yet Durrance relied on the pause preceding it.

"Nor on what business he has been engaged?" he continued.

"I am not interested in the smallest degree. I do not wish him to starve, and my solicitor tells me he draws his allowance. I am content with that knowledge, Colonel Durrance."

"I will risk your anger, General," said Durrance. "There are times when it is wise to disobey one's superior officer. This is one of them. Of course, you can turn me out of the house. Otherwise, I shall relate to you the history of your son and my friend since he disappeared from England."

General Feversham laughed.

"Of course, I can't turn you out of the house," he said, adding severely, "but I warn you that you are taking an improper advantage of your position as my guest."

"Yes, there is no doubt of that," Durrance answered calmly. He proceeded to tell his story: the recovery of the Gordon letters from Berber, his meeting with Harry Feversham at Wadi Halfa, and Harry's imprisonment at Omdurman. He brought the narrative down to that very day, ending with the news of Lieutenant Sutch's departure for Suakin. The General listened to the entire account without interruption, without even stirring in his chair. Durrance could not gauge the spirit in which he listened, but he drew comfort from the fact that the General did listen and offered no argument.

For some time after Durrance finished, the General sat in silence. He raised his hand to his forehead, shading his eyes as though the speaker could see him, and remained thus. Even when he spoke, he did not lower his hand. Pride forbade him to show those portraits on the walls that he was capable of such a natural weakness as joy at his son's reconquest of honor.

"What I don't understand," he said slowly, "is why Harry ever resigned his commission. I could not understand it before; I understand it even less now since you have told me of his great bravery. It is one of those queer, inexplicable things. They happen, and there's all that can be said. But I am very glad that you compelled me to listen to you, Durrance."

"I did it with a definite object. It is for you to say, of course, but for my part, I do not see why Harry should not come home and enter again into all that he lost."

"He cannot regain everything," said Feversham. "It is not right that he should. He committed the sin, and he must pay. He cannot regain his career for one thing."

"No, that is true; but he can find another. He is not yet so old that he cannot find another. And that is all that he will have lost."

General Feversham removed his hand from his forehead and shifted in his chair. He looked quickly at Durrance, opened his mouth to ask a question, but changed his mind.

"Well," he said briskly, as though the matter were of no particular importance, "if Sutch can manage Harry's escape from Omdurman, I see no reason why he should not come home."

Durrance rose. "Thank you, General. If you can have me driven to the station, I can catch a train to town. There's one at six."

"But you will stay the night, surely," cried General Feversham.

"It is impossible. I start for Wiesbaden early tomorrow."

Feversham rang the bell and ordered a carriage. "I should have been very glad if you could have stayed," he said, turning to Durrance. "I see very few people nowadays. To tell the truth, I have no great desire to see many. One grows old and a creature of customs."

"But you have your Crimean nights," said Durrance, cheerfully.

Feversham shook his head. "There have been none since Harry went away. I had no heart for them," he said slowly. For a second, the mask lifted, and his stern features softened. He had suffered greatly during these five lonely years of old age, though no acquaintance had ever detected a look or heard a sentence suggesting such pain. He had shown a stubborn front to the world, making it a matter of pride that no one could point a finger and say, "There's a man struck down." But on this one occasion, in these few words, he revealed to Durrance the depth of his grief. Durrance understood how unendurable the chatter of friends about the old days in snowy trenches would have been. An anecdote recalling a specific act of courage would hurt as keenly as a story of cowardice. The whole history of his lonely life at Broad Place was laid bare in that simple statement: there had been no Crimean nights because he had no heart for them.

The wheels of the carriage rattled on the gravel.

"Good-bye," said Durrance, holding out his hand.

"By the way," said Feversham, "to organize this escape from Omdurman will cost a great deal of money. Sutch is a poor man. Who is paying?"

"I am."

Feversham shook Durrance's hand in a firm clasp.

"It is my right, of course," he said.

"Certainly. I will let you know what it costs."

"Thank you."

General Feversham accompanied his visitor to the door. There was a question on his mind, but it was delicate. He stood uneasily on the steps of the house.

"Didn't I hear, Durrance," he said with an air of carelessness, "that you were engaged to Miss Eustace?"

"I think I said that Harry would regain all that he had lost except his career," Durrance replied.

He stepped into the carriage and drove off to the station. His work was ended. There was nothing left for him to do but wait at Wiesbaden and pray that Sutch might succeed. He had devised the plan; it remained for those with eyes to see to execute it.

General Feversham stood on the steps watching the carriage until it disappeared among the pines. Then he walked slowly back into the hall. "There is no reason why he should not come back," he said. He looked up at the pictures. The dead Fevershams in their uniforms would not be disgraced. "No reason in the world," he murmured. "And, please God, he will come back soon." The dangers of an escape from the Dervish city, remote among the sands, began to loom large on his mind. He admitted to himself that he felt very tired and old. Many times that night he repeated his prayer, "Please God, Harry will come back soon," as he sat erect upon the bench that had once been his wife's favorite seat, gazing out across the moonlit country to the Sussex Downs.

Before the great mud wall was erected to enclose the House of Stone in Omdurman, only a thorn zareeba circled that noisome prison and its immediate surroundings. It stood upon the eastern border of the town, a squalid capital of an empire, devoid of flowers, grass, or shade. A brown, stony plain, scorched by the relentless sun, supported a straggling city of hovels crawling with vermin and poisoned by disease.

Between the prison and the Nile, no houses stood. At this time, prisoners were permitted to stumble in their chains down the half-mile of broken, sloping earth to the riverbank during daylight, to draw water and perform ablutions. For the native or the negro, escape was not difficult; along that bank, dhows were moored, and the wide foreshore served as a bustling market-place. Captives rubbed elbows with friends, conspired plans of flight, or slipped into the thickest of the crowd to reach the first blacksmith, where the price of iron outweighed any risk. Yet, even on their way to the smithy, their fetters drew no special notice in Omdurman. Slaves wore them as a daily habit, and hardly a street in that long, brown, treeless squalor was free from the clink of a man walking in chains.

For the European, however, escape was a different matter entirely. There were not so many white prisoners that each remained unnoticed. Relays of camels stationed through the desert, vast sums of money, long preparations, and above all, devoted natives willing to risk their lives were the first necessities for evasion. Camels could be procured, but their drivers might vanish; preparations could be made, only to be undone by the gaoler's whip at the slightest suspicion of hidden funds; a devoted servant might shrink at the last moment. Colonel Trench began to lose all hope. He knew his friends were working for him. Sometimes the boy bringing his food would bid him be ready; sometimes, during parades of the Khalifa's troops where he was shown as an emblem of Turkish destiny, a man would jostle his camel and whisper encouragement. But nothing ever came of these whispers. He watched the sun rise daily beyond the river's bend, burning across the sky, while the months dragged on one after another.

Towards the end of August, in the year Durrance returned home blind from the Sudan, Trench sat in a corner of the enclosure, watching the sun drop westward with an agony of anticipation. The heat and burden of the day were nothing compared to the horrors each night renewed.

Twilight arrived, bringing Idris es Saier, the great negro of the Gawaamah tribe, and his fellow-gaolers.

"Into the House of Stone!" he cried.

Praying and cursing, with the sound of pitiless whips falling perpetually upon the backs of the lagging, the prisoners jostled and struggled at the narrow entrance. Already, some thirty captives occupied the interior, lying upon the swamped mud floor or propped against the walls in the last extremities of weakness and disease. Two hundred more were driven in at night, penned there until morning. The room was perhaps thirty feet square, with four feet occupied by a solid pillar supporting the roof. There was no window; a few small apertures near the ceiling made a pretense of ventilation. Into this foul, pestilent hovel, the prisoners were packed, screaming and fighting. The door slammed shut, utter darkness replaced the twilight, and a man could not distinguish the outlines of the heads of his neighbors who wedged him in.

Colonel Trench fought like the rest. He coveted a corner near the door with a fierceness he had never felt in his free days. Once there, he would find shelter from blows and the stamping feet, a support against which to lean his back during the ten interminable hours of suffocation.

"If I were to fall! If I were to fall!"

That fear haunted him, working like a drug producing madness. If a man went down amid that yelling, struggling throng, he never got up again—he was trampled out of shape. Trench had seen such victims dragged from the prison each morning; he was a small man. Therefore, he fought for his corner in a frenzy like a wild beast, kicking with his fetters, thrusting with his elbows, diving under arms, burrowing between others, tearing clothes, using nails and fists, and striking heads with the chain dangling from the iron ring about his neck. He reached the corner in the end, streaming with heat and gasping for breath, spending the rest of the night holding it against all comers.

"If I were to fall!" he gasped. "O God, if I were to fall!" He shouted aloud to his neighbor—for in that clamour nothing less than a shout was audible—"Is it you, Ibrahim?" A like shout answered him, "Yes, Effendi."

Trench felt some relief. Between Ibrahim, a great tall Arab of the Hadendoas, and Trench, a friendship born of common necessity had sprung up. There were no prison rations at Omdurman; each captive depended on his own money or the charity of friends outside. Money came to Trench secretly from Assouan or Suakin, but there were long periods of no help, and he lived on the charity of Greeks who had sworn conversion to the Mahdist faith, or starved with such patience as he could. There were times when Ibrahim had no friend to send him a meal. Thus, each man helped the other in need. They stood side by side against the wall at night.

"Yes, Effendi, I am here," Ibrahim said, groping in the black darkness to steady Trench against the wall.

A fight of violent intensity raged in an extreme corner of the prison. So closely packed were the prisoners that with each advance and retreat of a combatant, the whole jostling crowd swayed in a rhythm from end to end, side to side. They swayed, fighting to keep their feet, fighting even with their teeth. Above the din of hard breathing, the clank of chains, and imprecations, rose now and then a wild sobbing cry for mercy or an inhuman shriek, stifled as soon as uttered, showing a man had gone down beneath stamping feet. Missiles were flung across the prison—foul earth gathered from the floor—and since none knew from what quarter they came, heads were battered against heads in the effort to avoid them. All this happened in the blackest darkness.

For two hours Trench stood in that black prison, ringing with noise, rank with heat. Eight hours followed before the door would open, allowing him to stumble into the clean air and fall asleep in the zareeba. Standing on tiptoe to lift his head above his fellows, he could barely breathe; the air was moist and sour. His throat was parched, his tongue swollen and stringy like a dried fig. It seemed to him that the imagination of God could devise no worse hell than the House of Stone on an August night in Omdurman. It could add fire, he thought, but only fire.

"If I were to fall!" he cried. As he spoke, his hell was made perfect, for the door opened. Idris es Saier appeared in the opening.

"Make room," he cried, throwing fire among the prisoners to drive them from the door. Lighted tufts of dried grass blazed in the darkness, falling upon the bodies of the captives. So crowded were they that they could not avoid the missiles; in places, they could not lift their hands to dislodge them from shoulders or heads.

"Make room," cried Idris. The whips of his fellow-gaolers enforced his command, lashes falling upon all within reach, clearing a little space within the door. Into that space a man was flung, and the door closed again.

Trench stood close to the door. In the dim twilight coming through the doorway, he caught a glimpse of the new prisoner: a man heavily ironed, slight of figure, and bent with suffering.

"He will fall," Trench said, "he will fall to-night. God! if I were to!" Suddenly the crowd swayed against him, and curses rose louder and shriller than before.

The new prisoner was the cause. He clung to the door with his face against the panels, through the chinks of which actual air might come. Those behind plucked him from his vantage, jostled him, pressed him backwards to take his place. He was driven as a wedge is driven by a hammer, between this prisoner and that, until at last he was flung against Colonel Trench.

The ordinary instincts of kindness could not live in the nightmare of that prison house. In the daytime, outside, prisoners were often drawn together by a bond of common misery; the faithful often helped the infidel. But to fight for life during the hours of darkness, without pity or cessation, was the one creed and practice of the House of Stone. Colonel Trench was like the rest. The need to live, if only long enough to drink one drop of water in the morning and draw one clean mouthful of fresh air, was more than uppermost in his mind. It was the only thought he had.

"Back!" he cried violently, "back, or I strike!" As he wrestled to lift his arm above his head to strike the better, he heard the man who had been flung against him babbling English incoherently.

"Don't fall," cried Trench, catching his fellow-captive by the arm. "Ibrahim, help! God, if he were to fall!" While the crowd swayed again and shrill cries and curses rose, deafening the ears and piercing the brain, Trench supported his companion. Bending his head, he caught again, after so many months, the accent of his own tongue. And the sound of it civilized him like the friendship of a woman.

He could not hear what was said; the din was too loud. But he caught shadows of words once familiar to him, spoken to him, which he had spoken to others—as a matter of course. In the House of Stone, they sounded most wonderful. They had magic. Meadows of grass, cool skies, and limpid rivers rose in grey, quiet pictures before his mind. For a moment, he was insensible to his parched throat, the stench of the prison, and the oppressive blackness. But he felt the man he supported totter and slip, and again he cried to Ibrahim:

"If he were to fall!"

Ibrahim helped as only he could. Together they fought and wrestled until those about them yielded, crying:

"Shaitan! They are mad!"

They cleared a space in that corner, setting the Englishman down upon the ground, and stood in front of him lest he be trampled. Behind him upon the ground, Trench heard every now and then, in a lull of the noise, the babble of English.

"He will die before morning," Trench cried to Ibrahim. "He is in a fever!"

"Sit beside him," said the Hadendoa. "I can keep them back."

Trench stooped and squatted in the corner. Ibrahim set his legs well apart, guarding Trench and his new friend.

Bending his head, Trench could now hear the words. They were the words of a man in delirium, spoken in a voice of great pleading. He seemed to be telling a tale of the sea.

"I saw the riding lights of the yachts—and the reflections shortening and lengthening as the water rippled—there was a band, too, as we passed the pier-head. What was it playing? Not the overture—and I don't think that I remember any other tune...." He laughed with a crazy chuckle. "I was always pretty bad at appreciating music, wasn't I? except when you played," and he came back to the sea. "There was the line of hills upon the right as the boat steamed out of the bay—you remember there were woods on the hillside—perhaps you have forgotten. Then came Bray, a little fairyland of lights close down by the water at the point of the ridge ... you remember Bray, we lunched there once or twice, just you and I, before everything was settled ... it seemed strange to be steaming out of Dublin Bay and leaving you a long way off to the north among the hills ... strange and somehow not quite right ... for that was the word you used when the morning came behind the blinds—it is not right that one should suffer so much pain ... the engines didn't stop, though, they just kept throbbing and revolving and clanking as though nothing had happened whatever ... one felt a little angry about that ... the fairyland was already only a sort of golden blot behind ... and then nothing but sea and the salt wind ... and the things to be done."

The man in delirium suddenly lifted himself upon an elbow, and with the other hand fumbled in his breast as though searching for something. "Yes, the things to be done," he repeated in a mumbling voice, sinking to unintelligible whisperings with his head fallen upon his breast.

Trench put an arm about him and raised him up. But he could do nothing more, and even to him, crouched close to the ground, the noisome heat was almost beyond endurance. In front, the din of shrill voices, screams for pity, swaying and struggling, went on in that appalling darkness. In one corner, men sang in a mad frenzy; in another, a few danced in their fetters, or rather tried to dance. In front of Trench, Ibrahim maintained his guard; beside Trench lay in the House of Stone, in the town beyond the world, a man who one night had sailed out of Dublin Bay, past the riding lanterns of yachts, and had seen Bray, that fairyland of lights, dwindle to a golden blot. For three days, Feversham drifted in a feverish haze, his mind a chaotic storm. Trench was his anchor, fetching water from the Nile, sharing his meager rations, and standing guard alongside Ibrahim in the shadow of the House of Stone. Three nights passed in this vigil, a grim rhythm of survival. But on the fourth morning, the fog lifted. Feversham woke to the stark reality of the present, seeing Trench's face bent over him, clear and real. At first, the sight triggered a phantom terror, a nightmare memory from boyhood where shadows loom too large. He waved a weak arm to dismiss the illusion, but as his gaze swept the enclosure—the hard blue sky, the trampled earth, the chained figures of his fellow prisoners—the truth settled in with crushing weight. He grabbed Trench, clutching him as if fearing he would vanish, and then, slowly, a smile broke through.

"I am in the prison at Omdurman," he whispered, the words tasting like salvation. "Actually in the prison! This is Umm Hagar, the House of Stone. It seems too good to be true."

He slumped back against the wall, the tension draining from his frame. To Trench, the tone sounded almost ironic, a mask of indifference worn by a man who had survived too much. Yet Trench knew the difference. This was not the pose of a hardened cynic; it was the raw, genuine relief of a man who had finally reached his destination. The peril of Dongola was over. He had found Trench. He was in Omdurman. It was a place of death, yes, but it was also the longed-for goal, the place where his purpose could finally be fulfilled. He might have been hanging on a gibbet miles down the Nile, vultures circling his shoulders, his life's work undone. Instead, he was within the walls of the House of Stone.

"You have been here a long while," Feversham said.

"Three years."

Feversham scanned the zareeba. "Three years of it," he murmured. "I was afraid I might not find you alive."

Trench nodded. "The nights are the worst. It's a wonder any man survives a week, yet I have lived through a thousand." Even to him, the feat seemed impossible. "A thousand nights in the House of Stone!" he exclaimed.

"But we may go down to the Nile by daytime," Feversham urged, rising with sudden alarm at the sight of the thorn fence. "Surely we are allowed so much liberty. An Arab at Wadi Halfa told me so."

"And it is true," Trench confirmed, pointing to the earthen bowl of water at his side. "I filled that at the Nile this morning."

"I must go," Feversham insisted, lifting himself up. "I must go this morning." His voice rose with excitement, prompting Trench to whisper urgently, "Hush. There are many prisoners here, and among them many tale-bearers."

Feversham sank back, not just from weakness but in obedience. "But they cannot understand what we say," he argued, his voice losing its edge.

"They can see we talk earnestly. Idris would know within the hour; the Khalifa before sunset. Heavier fetters, the courbatch—we cannot speak. Lie still. You are weak, and I am tired. We will sleep, and later we will go to the Nile."

Trench lay down beside him and was instantly asleep. Feversham watched him, noting the deep lines etched into Trench's face by three years of suffering. It was not until noon that Feversham stirred.

"There is no one to bring you food?" he asked.

"Yes," Feversham replied, his voice steady. "A boy should come. He should bring news as well."

They waited until the gate of the zareeba swung open. The enclosure instantly transformed into a cage of wild beasts. Gaolers distributed the *aseeda*, a moist cake of dhurra that barely sustained life. The strong fought the weak for scraps; groups battled for dominance. Men gaunt as skeletons hopped and leaped within the limits of their chains, falling in stolid despair if they missed a bite. Thirty gaolers, armed with rhinoceros-hide courbatches, watched impassively, their weapons forgotten in the chaos of hunger.

Amidst the food-bearers, a young boy hid behind the crowd, looking around with irresolution. Before long, he was spotted. Knocked down, his food snatched, he screamed. The sound drew Idris-es-Saier himself to the scene.

"For whom do you come?" Idris demanded, shoving the prisoners aside.

"For Joseppi, the Greek," the boy answered. Idris pointed to the corner where Feversham lay. The boy approached, holding out empty hands to explain his lack of provisions. Squatting by Feversham's side, he began to speak, then loosened a gazelle skin from his waist and let it fall. Inside was a chicken. Feversham and Trench ate, breakfasted, and dined on that single bird.

An hour later, they were permitted to leave the zareeba and walk to the Nile. They moved slowly, halting frequently. At the water's edge, where prisoners unloaded dhows and paddled in the mud, the air was thick with shouting men. The gaolers were visible but out of earshot.

"Yes, we can talk here," Trench said. "Why have you come?"

"I was captured in the desert, on the Arbain road," Feversham admitted slowly.

"Yes, masquerading as a lunatic musician with a zither. I know. But you were captured by your own deliberate wish. You came to join me in Omdurman. I know."

"How do you know?"

"You told me. During the last three days you have told me much. Very much," Trench continued, his voice low. "You came to join me because five years ago I sent you a white feather."

"And was that all I told you?" Feversham asked anxiously.

"No," Trench replied, dragging out the word. He sat up, turning his face away so Feversham could not see his expression. "No, that was not all. You spoke of a girl, the same girl you mentioned when Willoughby, Durrance, and I dined with you in London. I know her name now—her Christian name. She was with you when the feathers came. I had not thought of that possibility. She gave you a fourth feather to add to our three. I am sorry."

A silence stretched between them, heavy with unspoken history. Then Feversham spoke slowly.

"For my part, I am not sorry. I am not sorry she was present. I think, on the whole, that I am rather glad. She gave me the fourth feather, it is true, but I am glad of that as well. Without her presence, without that fourth feather snapping from her fan, I might have given up there and then. Who knows? I doubt if I could have stood up to the three long years in Suakin. I used to see you and Durrance and Willoughby, men who had once been my friends, going about the work I was meant to do. You can't think how the mere routine of a regiment, which I cursed heartily, appealed as something desirable. I could so easily have run away. I could have slipped onto a boat and gone back to Suez. And the chance for which I waited never came—for three years."

"You saw us?" Trench asked. "And you gave no sign?"

"How would you have taken it if I had?" Trench remained silent. "No, I saw you, but I was careful that you should not see me. I doubt if I could have endured it without the recollection of that night at Ramelton, without the feel of the fourth feather to keep the memory actual and recent. I should never have gone down from Obak into Berber. I should certainly never have joined you in Omdurman."

Trench turned quickly to his companion. "She would be glad to hear you say that," he said. "I have no doubt she is sorry about her fourth feather, sorry as I am about the other three."

"There is no reason that she should be, or that you either should be. I don't blame you, or her," Feversham said, falling silent as he looked toward the river. The air was shrill with cries; the shore was thronged with Arabs and negroes in robes of blue, yellow, and dirty brown. The work of unloading went on busily. Across the river, the palm trees of Khartum stood against the cloudless sky, and the sun moved westward. In a few hours, the horrors of the House of Stone would begin. But for now, both men thought of the elms by the Lennon River, of a hall with an open door echoing to a waltz, where a girl and a man stood with three white feathers fallen upon the floor. "Glenalla," she replied, eager to share gossip about the village and the prospects of Miss Eustace's marriage. "It's a poor mountain spot, but perhaps you wish to visit her?"

"Yes," Feversham said, rising to leave. "Please have my horse brought around."

The landlady, undeterred, noted that a similarly dark-complexioned gentleman had visited a fortnight prior seeking the same address. Feversham dismissed the coincidence, paid for his meal, and rode out, telling the innkeeper he would not return to Ramelton.

Four miles on, he turned onto a track leading to a small bay of the lake, riding past white cottages into a purple hollow. The village of Glenalla appeared quiet and unplanned, scattered across the landscape until he reached a solitary house and, beyond it, a small grey church perched on a tiny plateau. A path of white shell-dust led to its wooden gate, where a grey collie barked.

Feversham stopped. The dog's markings were familiar. He dismounted, tethered his horse, and stepped into the churchyard. The collie sniffed him doubtfully before bursting into an exuberant welcome, jumping and whirling around him with sharp, excited cries. Feversham laughed, patting the animal, but his amusement vanished instantly as he looked toward the open church door.

Standing in the threshold was Ethne Eustace.

She did not move or speak as he approached. When he reached her, she simply said, "Harry."

The silence between them was heavy with six years of absence. To the casual observer, little had changed, but to them, the differences were stark. Feversham was thinner, his face wasted, bearing the signs of the hard life in the House of Stone. Yet, Ethne saw more than his physical toll. She noticed the disappearance of the timid diffidence that had once plagued him; he was now quietly self-possessed, neither arrogant nor insecure. He had faced a terrible trial and emerged knowing he had not failed.

"It is not all harm which has come of these years," Ethne said softly, her face lighting up. "They were not wasted."

Feversham thought of her lonely years in this remote village, a choice she would never have made otherwise. He looked into her face, noting the deepening character lines—the mark of suffering and wisdom.

"I am sorry," he said. "I did you a great wrong six years ago, and I need not."

She held out her hand. "Will you give it me, please?"

For a moment, he misunderstood. "That fourth feather," she clarified.

Feversham reached into his coat and shook two ostrich feathers into her palm. He offered her the larger one, but she insisted on both. There was no point in keeping Castleton's feather; it had served its purpose. He handed them over, and she clasped them tightly against her breast, smiling with a pride that surprised him.

"I have the four feathers now," she declared.

"Yes," Feversham replied. "All four. What will you do with them?"

"Do with them!" she laughed in scorn. "I shall do nothing with them. I shall keep them. I am very proud to have them to keep."

Just as she had once kept his portrait, she kept the feathers. While Feversham felt they were dead things, crushed by the weight of their history, Ethne held them as living tokens of redemption.

"Colonel Trench was here a fortnight ago," she mentioned casually. "He told me you were bringing it back to me."

"But he did not know of the fourth feather," Feversham corrected. "I never told any man that I had it."

"Yes," Ethne said seriously. "You told Colonel Trench on your first night in the House of Stone at Omdurman. He told me. I no longer hate him."

Feversham was glad to hear it, acknowledging Trench as a great friend. Ethne fell silent for a moment before recalling their drive from Ramelton years ago, when they had spoken of true friends—those recognized instantly and never lost, even in death.

"I said one could always be sure of such friends," she continued, her voice trembling slightly. "And you answered that one could make mistakes. Yes, I disagreed. I believed one might seem to make mistakes for a long time, but in the end, one would be proved right. I remembered those words clearly when Captain Willoughby brought me the first feather, filled with remorse. I remembered them again today, though I have no room left for remorse. I was right, you see. I should have clung to my faith. But I did not."

Her voice shook as she admitted her youthful haste and lack of understanding. She opened her hand to gaze at the white feathers one last time, then turned and walked inside the church. Feversham followed her, the weight of the past finally laid to rest.

The sanctuary of Glenalla was a vessel of profound stillness, the kind of quiet that settles over a landscape after a storm has passed. Ethne sat in the shadowed corner of a pew, her posture rigid, while Harry Feversham stood beside her, a sentinel guarding a peace that felt fragile as glass. Afternoon sunlight filtered through the high, narrow windows, casting a golden haze across the vaulted ceiling, but the warmth did not reach the chill between them. Outside, the summer air drifted through the open door, carrying the distant murmur of the world, yet inside, time seemed to have suspended itself.

"I am glad you remembered our drive," Ethne began, her voice steady despite the tremor in her hands. She did not look at him; her gaze was fixed on the empty space ahead of her, as if staring into the future she intended to build without him. "What we said there matters. It is vital that you remember. Because, Harry, though I have brought you back to me, I am sending you away again. You will become one of those absent friends whom I cherish precisely because they are gone."

She spoke slowly, the words having been rehearsed in the dark hours of the night for two weeks. When she first saw him, restored after six years of silence and suffering, the sheer weight of his return had paralyzed her tongue. She had feared she would never utter the necessary truths. But standing now, facing the man who had endured hell for her sake, she had forced herself to recall the necessity of the moment. She had retreated to this pew, gathered her scattered courage, and prepared to speak.

"It would be kinder for both of us," she continued, her tone even, devoid of the trembling she felt in her soul, "if I show no sign of the cost this separation demands. You will know, surely, and you must know: not a single moment of your six years has been wasted in my eyes. But that truth needs no display of emotion to be understood. So I speak plainly."

"I know you will grieve," she resumed, "just as I do. But there is no other path. At least, you are home now, with the right to be. That brings me comfort. But there are greater reasons for us both to find solace. Colonel Trench told me enough of your captivity to confirm what we both see clearly: this second parting, however hard, is a mere shadow compared to the first, at Ramelton House six years ago. I felt utterly alone after that day, separated by a barrier that seemed eternal. We would never have met again. I am certain of it. But you have shattered that barrier with your pain and your bravery. I am as sure of that as I am of my own name. Therefore, though we shall not see one another in this life, the 'afterwards' is guaranteed for us both. We can wait for it. You have waited with such strength, and I can too, drawing my strength from your victory."

She fell silent, and the church held its breath. To Feversham, her words were a balm to parched earth. They lifted him so high that the six years of hiding in corners, of solitary endurance, of heartbreaks and physical agony, shriveled into insignificance. They had borne fruit. He remembered the bitter words she had once spoken, the ones he had feared would haunt him to his grave. Those predictions were false. The words ringing in his ears now were these, gentle and forgiving.

He was not surprised by her proposal of separation. He had heard rumors of her engagement. He did not argue. He sensed she had more to say, and she did, though she hesitated. This was the final interview; once he crossed the threshold, she would never speak to him again until death. She delayed the moment of his departure with long pauses, hoping he might speak of his travels, of the dangers he faced. She was ready to discuss the politics of the Sudan with him. But he waited, silent and still.

"I am going to be married," she said finally, the admission hanging heavy in the air. "Immediately. I am to marry a friend of yours, Colonel Durrance."

There was no pause before Feversham replied. "He has cared for you a long while. I was unaware until I left, but reflecting on everything, I suspected it, and soon I was sure."

"He is blind."

"Blind!" Feversham exclaimed, the word bursting from him. "He, of all men, blind!"

"Exactly," Ethne said. "He—of all men. His blindness explains everything: why I marry him, why I send you away. It was after he lost his sight that I became engaged to him. It was before Captain Willoughby brought me the first feather. Between those two events, I resolved that two lives should not be ruined because of me."

"My life was not ruined," Feversham interrupted fiercely. "Please believe that."

"Partly it was," she returned softly. "I know you would not admit it for my sake, but it was. I was determined that a second should not follow. When Colonel Durrance went blind—you know the man he was, you can imagine what losing his sight meant to him, the loss of everything he loved—"

"Except you."

"Yes," Ethne answered quietly. "Except me. So I engaged him. But he has grown remarkably sharp. You cannot guess how keen his mind has become. A hint reveals the whole hidden truth to him. At present, he knows nothing of the four feathers."

"Are you sure?" Feversham demanded suddenly.

"Yes. Why?" Ethne turned to face him for the first time since sitting down.

"Lieutenant Sutch was at Suakin while I was at Omdurman. He knew I was a prisoner. He sent messages, he tried to organize my escape."

Ethne started. "Oh," she said. "Colonel Durrance certainly knew you were in Omdurman. He saw you in Wadi Halfa and heard you had gone south into the desert. He was distressed; he asked a friend for news, and the friend found you in Omdurman. He told me himself—and yes, he said he would try to arrange your escape. No doubt he did so through Lieutenant Sutch. He has been in Wiesbaden with an oculist; he only returned a week ago. Otherwise, he would have told me. Very likely he was the reason Sutch was at Suakin, but he knows nothing of the four feathers. He only knows our engagement was broken abruptly; he believes I have no thought left for you. But if you return, if we meet again, however calmly, however indifferently, he would guess. He is so quick he would be sure." She paused, whispering, "And he would guess right."

Feversham saw the blood rush to her forehead, deepening the color of her cheeks. He did not move, did not lean closer, giving no sign to make this farewell harder.

"Yes, I see," he said. "And he must not guess."

"No, he must not," Ethne agreed. "I am glad you see that too, Harry. The straight and simple thing is the only path for us. He must never guess, for, as you said, he has nothing left but me."

"Is Durrance here?" Feversham asked.

"He is staying at the vicarage."

"Very well," he said. "It is fair that I tell you I had no thought you would wait. I had no wish for you to; I had no right to such a wish. When you gave me the fourth feather in that room at Ramelton, with the music drifting through the door, I understood your meaning. There was to be a complete, irrevocable end. We were not to be mere acquaintances. So I said nothing of the plan that formed in my mind at that very moment. You see, I might never have succeeded. I might have died trying, or perhaps I might have shirked the attempt. It would be time enough to speak if I returned. So I never formed the wish that you should wait."

"That was what Colonel Trench told me," Ethne said.

"I told him that too?"

"On your first night in the House of Stone."

"Well, it is just the truth. The most I hoped for—and I did hope for it every hour—was that, if I came home, you would take back your feather, and that we might—not renew our friendship here, but see something of one another afterwards."

"Yes," Ethne said. "Then there will be no parting."

She spoke simply, without a sigh, but she looked at Harry Feversham and smiled. In that look and that smile lay the terrible cost of the separation to her. Understanding this now, Feversham comprehended, with a completeness he had never known in his lonely vigil, what it must have meant six years ago when she was left with her pride and heart alike stricken.

"How much trouble you must have gone through!" he cried. She turned to look at him.

"Not I alone," she said gently. "I passed no nights in the House of Stone."

"But it was my fault. Do you remember what you said when the morning came through the blinds? 'It's not right that one should suffer so much pain.' It was not right."

"I had forgotten those words—a long time ago—until Colonel Trench reminded me. I should never have spoken them. When I did, I did not think they would live so vividly in your thoughts. I am sorry I spoke them."

"Oh, they were just enough. I never blamed you for them," Feversham laughed. "I used to think they would be the last words I heard when I turned my face to the wall. But you have given me others today to replace them."

"Thank you," she said quietly.

There was nothing more to say, yet Feversham wondered why Ethne did not rise. It did not occur to him to speak of his travels; the occasion was too solemn, too vital. They were deciding the most critical issue of their lives. Once decided, as it now was, conversation seemed impossible. Ethne, however, kept him at her side. Though she sat calmly, her face grave, her heart ached with longing. Just a little longer, she pleaded to herself. The sunlight was retreating from the church walls. She measured the shrinking patch of golden glow. When the stone turned cold and grey, she would send Harry Feversham away.

"I am glad you escaped Omdurman without the help of Lieutenant Sutch or Colonel Durrance," she said. "I wanted everything done by you alone, without interference." Silence followed. She glanced toward the wall, watching the golden space narrow. "You suffered horribly at Dongola," she whispered. "Colonel Trench told me."

"What does it matter now?" Feversham answered. "That time seems far away."

"Had you anything of mine with you?"

"I had your white feather."

"But anything else? Any little thing you received from me on other days?"

"Nothing."

"I had your photograph," she said. "I kept it."

Feversham leaned down toward her. "You did!"

Ethne nodded. "Yes. The moment I went upstairs that night, I packed your presents and addressed them to your rooms."

"Yes, I got them in London."

"But I put your photograph aside first, to keep. I burnt all your letters after addressing the parcel and taking it down to the hall. I had just finished burning them when I heard your step on the gravel in the early morning beneath my windows. But I had already saved the photograph. I have it now. I shall keep it and the feathers together." After a moment, she added, "I rather wish you had had something of mine with you all the time."

"I had no right to anything," Feversham said.

A narrow strip of gold remained on the grey stone.

"What will you do now?" she asked.

"I shall go home first and see my father. It will depend on how we meet."

"You will let Colonel Durrance know. I would like to hear about it."

"Yes, I will write to Durrance."

The slip of gold vanished; the clear light of a summer evening filled the church, a light without radiance or color.

"I shall not see you for a long while," Ethne said, and for the first time her voice broke in a sob. "I shall not have a letter from you again."

She leaned forward, her head bowed as tears gathered in her eyes. Yet she rose bravely from her seat, and together they walked out of the church side by side. She leaned toward him as they walked so that their shoulders touched.

Feversham untied his horse and mounted. As his foot touched the stirrup, Ethne caught her dog close to her chest.

"Good-bye," she said. She did not try to smile anymore; she held out her hand. He took it and bent down from his saddle, close to her. She kept her eyes steadily upon him, though tears brimmed in them.

"Good-bye," he said. He held her hand for a moment, then released it and rode down the hill. He rode a hundred yards, stopped, and looked back. Ethne had stopped too. With this space between them and their faces turned toward one another, they remained. Ethne made no sign of recognition or farewell. She simply stood and looked. Then she turned away and walked up the village street toward her house alone, very slowly. Feversham watched her until she entered the gate, but she became dim and blurred to his vision before she even reached it. He saw, however, that she did not look back.

He rode down the hill. The wrong he had done so long ago was not even by his six years of labor to be destroyed. It was still to live, its consequence to be sorrow till the end of life for another than himself. That she took the sorrow bravely and without complaint, doing the straight and simple thing as her loyal nature bade her, did not diminish Harry Feversham's remorse. On the contrary, it taught him yet more clearly that she least of all deserved unhappiness. The harm was irreparable. Other women might have forgotten, but not she

The words arrived that evening, spoken with a quiet finality that shattered the silence of the room. Ethne retreated to her farmhouse, the summer heat having long since given way to a chill that seeped through the floorboards. She lit the fire, watching the coals glow with a stillness that betrayed the storm raging within her. The moment she had waited for, the half-hour of Harry Feversham's presence, had come and gone. Now, she sat alone in her remote hill village, more isolated than she had been since Willoughby sailed away that August morning. For months, she had lived for those fleeting minutes of his company; now that they were past, the emptiness of the world pressed in on her. Yet, a part of her nature had always anticipated this hollow ache, knowing that sending Harry away would trigger a desperate urge to recall him. She forced herself to remember that she had seen this coming, that she must look beyond the immediate pain. Tomorrow, she would realize they had not parted forever; tomorrow, she would weigh this separation against the tragic parting at Lennon House and see how small it truly was. As she shivered by the fire, wondering what Harry would do now that he had returned, she felt the warm weight of Dermod's old collie nuzzling her hand. The dog sensed her distress with an instinctive certainty. Ethne rose, cupping the animal's head in her hands and kissing its fur. He was ancient, she thought, destined to die soon and leave her alone, leaving her to wait years before her own great moment arrived.

A knock at the door broke her reverie. A servant announced that Colonel Durrance was waiting.

"Yes," Ethne replied, rising to meet him. She did not shy away from the role she had assigned herself; she stepped out from the secret chamber of her grief the moment she was summoned. She conversed with her visitor as though nothing extraordinary had occurred an hour prior, discussing their marriage and the reconstruction of Lennon House. It was difficult, but she had grown accustomed to hardship. Only tonight did Durrance make her path steeper. After the maid served tea, he asked her to play the Musoline Overture on her violin.

"Not tonight," Ethne said, feigning fatigue. "I am rather tired." But the words slipped out before she could retract them. Ethne resolved that in the small matters as well as the great, she must not evade responsibility. "Still, I believe I can manage the overture," she said with a forced smile, retrieving her instrument. She played from start to finish. Durrance stood at the window, his back to her, until the final note faded. Then he approached her side.

"I was rather a brute," he said softly, "to ask you to play that piece tonight."

"I wasn't eager to play," she answered, setting the violin down.

"I know. But I needed to find out something, and I knew no other way."

Ethne turned a startled face toward him.

"What do you mean?" she asked, her voice tight with suspense.

"You are rarely off your guard," Durrance continued calmly. "Only when you play. Once before, when you performed that overture, you were vulnerable. I thought if I could get you to play it again—the one once strummed in a dingy café at Wadi Halfa—I might catch you off balance."

His words stole her breath and drained the color from her cheeks. She rose slowly, staring at him with wide eyes. He could not possibly know. It was impossible. He did not know.

Yet Durrance pressed on.

"Well? Did you take back your feather? The fourth one?"

These were the incredible words. Durrance spoke them with a faint smile. It took Ethne a long moment to comprehend that he had actually uttered them. Her senses felt overstrained, as if reality itself were bending; but he repeated the question, and she could no longer deny or misinterpret what she heard.

"Who told you of any fourth feather?" she asked.

"Trench," Durrance answered. "I met him at Dover. He only mentioned the fourth feather," he clarified. "I already knew of the three. Trench would never have spoken of the fourth unless I had known of the three beforehand. Otherwise, I would not have met him as he disembarked at Dover. I would not have asked, 'Where is Harry Feversham?' Knowing of the three was sufficient."

"How do you know?" Ethne cried, a note of despair in her voice. As she moved closer, he gently took her arm.

"But since I know," he protested, "what difference does it make how I know? I have known for a long time, ever since Captain Willoughby brought the first feather to The Pool. I waited to tell you until Harry Feversham returned, and he came today."

Ethne sat back down, stunned by this unexpected revelation. She had guarded her secret so meticulously that realizing it had been open knowledge for a year struck her like a blow. Yet, amidst her confusion, she understood she needed time to regain her composure. She spoke of trivialities to buy moments.

"You were in the church, then? Or did you hear us on the steps? Or did you meet him as he rode away?"

"Not one of your guesses is correct," Durrance smiled. Ethne had chosen the right subject to delay the inevitable. Durrance possessed vanities like any other man, particularly one that had blossomed since his blindness: he prided himself on the acuteness of his perception. It delighted him to make discoveries that no one expected a blind man to achieve, and to announce them unexpectedly. It was an added pleasure to explain the steps of his deduction to a puzzled audience.

"Not one of your conjectures is right, Ethne," he said, practically inviting her to question him further.

"Then how did you find out?" she asked.

"I knew from Trench that Harry Feversham would return soon. This afternoon, I passed the church. Your collie dog barked at me, so I knew you were inside. But a saddled horse was tied beside the gate. Someone else was with you, and not someone from the village. Then I had you play, and that told me exactly who rode the horse."

"Yes," Ethne murmured vaguely, barely listening. "Yes, I see." Then, in a firm voice that signaled her regained self-control, she asked: "You went away to Wiesbaden for a year. You left just after Captain Willoughby arrived. Was that the reason?"

"I went because neither you nor I could sustain the game of pretences we were playing," Durrance explained. "You pretended you had no thought for Harry Feversham, that you hardly cared if he lived or died. I pretended not to have discovered that, above all else, you cared for him. Eventually, one of us would have failed, each in turn. I dared not fail, nor did you dare. I could not let you, who said 'Two lives must not be spoilt because of me,' live through a year believing two lives had been ruined. And you, on your side, dared not let me, who said 'Marriage between a blind man and a woman is only possible when there is more than friendship on both sides,' know that on one side there existed only friendship. We were so close to failing that I had to act."

"You did not fail," Ethne said quietly. "It was only I who failed."

She blamed herself bitterly. She had set herself the highest task—to shield this man from knowledge that would crown his calamities—and she had failed. He had tried to protect her from understanding that she had failed, and he had succeeded. It was not merely humiliation that troubled her, the realization that the man she thought she had deceived had deceived her. Rather, she felt she ought to have succeeded, for her failure had robbed him of his last chance at happiness. That was the sting.

"But it was not your fault," he said. "Once or twice, as I mentioned, you were off your guard, but the convincing facts were not revealed that way. When you played the Musoline Overture before, on the night Willoughby brought you such good news, I took that happiness of yours as inspiration for your playing. You must not blame yourself. On the contrary, you should be glad I have found out."

"Glad?" she exclaimed.

"Yes, for my sake, glad." Looking at him in wonder, he continued, "Two lives should not be spoilt because of you. Had you had your way, had I not found out, not two but three lives would have been ruined because of you—because of your loyalty."

"Three?"

"Yours. Yes—yours, Feversham's, and mine. It was hard enough to keep the pretence during our few weeks in Devonshire. Admit it, Ethne! When I went to London to see my oculist, it was a relief; it gave you a pause, a rest to drop the pretence and be yourself. It could not have lasted long even in Devonshire. But what when we lived under the same roof, seeing each other every hour of every day, with no visits to the oculist? Sooner or later the truth must have come to me. It might have crept up gradually, suspicion adding to suspicion until doubt vanished. Or it might have flashed out in one terrible moment. But it would have been clear. And then, Ethne? What then? You aimed at a compensation; you wanted to make up to me for the loss of what I love—my career, the army, the special service in strange lands. A fine compensation to sit before you knowing you married a cripple out of pity, thereby crippling yourself and forfeiting the happiness that is yours by right. Whereas now—"

"Whereas now?" she repeated.

"I remain your friend, which I would rather be than your unloved husband," he said very gently.

Ethne made no reply. The decision had been wrested from her hands.

"You sent Harry away this afternoon," Durrance noted. "You said goodbye to him twice."

At the word "twice," Ethne lifted her head, but before she could speak, Durrance explained: "Once in the church, again upon your violin." He picked up the instrument from the chair where she had laid it. "It has been a very good friend, your violin," he said. "A good friend to me, to us all. You will understand that, Ethne, very soon. I stood at the window while you played. I had never heard anything in my life half so sad as your farewell to Harry Feversham, and yet it was nobly sad. It was true music; it did not complain." He laid the violin back on the chair.

"I am going to send a messenger to Rathmullen. Harry cannot cross Lough Swilly tonight. The messenger will bring him back tomorrow."

It had been a day of profound emotion and surprise for Ethne. As Durrance bent toward her, he noticed she was weeping silently. For once, tears had their way with her. He took his cap and walked noiselessly to the door. As he opened it, Ethne rose.

"Don't go for a moment," she said, leaving the fireplace to stand in the center of the room.

"The oculist at Wiesbaden?" she asked. "Did he give you hope?"

Durrance paused, meditating on whether to lie or speak the truth.

"No," he said finally. "There is no hope. But I am not so helpless as I feared I might be. I can get about, can't I? Perhaps one of these days I shall go on a journey, one of the long journeys among the strange people in the East."

He left the house to carry out his errand. He had learned his lesson long ago, and the violin had taught it to him. It had spoken again that afternoon, though with a different voice, offering him the same message: true music cannot complain.

The sun hung heavy over the terrace of Broad Place, baking the English countryside into a shimmering haze. Two elderly men sat amidst the morning papers, the air thick with the scent of stale ink and pipe tobacco. The elder, General Feversham, flipped a page with a practiced flick.

"I see Osman Digna has returned to Suakin," Feversham noted, his voice raspy. "Trouble seems inevitable."

"Oh," replied the younger man, Lieutenant Sutch, setting his paper down as if dismissing a triviality. "He won't cause much harm." Yet, as he spoke, the quiet English fields dissolved from his vision. In their place rose the blinding white city of Suakin, shimmering under a relentless Red Sea sun, surrounded by brown plains tangled with halfa grass and distant hills looming toward Khor Gwob.

"A stifling place, isn't it, Sutch?" Feversham asked briskly.

"Appallingly so," Sutch admitted, his eyes drifting shut. "I heard of an officer who collapsed on parade at six in the morning, struck down by the sun even through his regulation helmet. It's a town of damp, suffocating heat. Still, I was glad to be there—very glad."

"Yes," Feversham agreed, his tone sharp. "The ibex, then?"

"No," Sutch corrected. "Every ibex within miles had been shot by the English garrison."

"No? Then what kept you busy?"

"Yes, that's it, Feversham. Something to do."

They returned to their newspapers, but soon a footman delivered a small stack of letters to each. Feversham scanned the envelopes with a keen eye, selected one, and let out a grunt of satisfaction. He retrieved a pair of spectacles, perched them on his nose, and reached for his cigar case.

"From Ramelton?" Sutch asked, lowering his paper.

"From Ramelton," Feversham confirmed. "I'll light a cigar first."

He placed the letter on the garden table between them, drew out a fresh cigar, and, despite Sutch's evident impatience, lit it with deliberate slowness. To the old general, a letter from Ramelton was a luxury demanding every accessory of comfort. He settled deeper into his chair, stretched his legs, and smoked until the draw was perfect. Only then did he open the correspondence.

"From him?" Sutch whispered, leaning forward.

"No," Feversham replied softly. "From her."

Sutch nodded, understanding the weight of the silence. Feversham read the letter slowly, while Sutch fought the urge to peek over the table. When the general finished, he reread the first page.

"Any news?" Sutch asked casually.

"They are delighted with the house now that it's rebuilt."

"Anything else?"

"Yes. Harry has completed the sixth chapter of his war history."

"Excellent!" Sutch exclaimed. "You'll see; he'll write it well. He has imagination, he knows the terrain, and he was there when the war happened. He saw the bazaars, the underside of it all."

"Yes," Feversham murmured. "But you and I won't read it, Sutch. No... I suppose you can, since you have years ahead of me."

He turned back to the letter. Again, Sutch pressed, "Anything more?"

"Yes. They are arriving in a fortnight."

"Good," Sutch said. "I shall stay."

He paced the terrace and returned to find Feversham holding the letter on his knees, a look of profound perplexity etched on his face.

"You know, Sutch, I never understood," Feversham said. "Did you?"

"Yes, I think I did."

Sutch offered no explanation. It was better that way; Feversham could not comprehend without a heavy burden of self-reproach.

"Do you ever see Durrance?" the general asked suddenly.

"Yes, quite often. He is abroad just now."

Feversham turned to his friend. "He came to Broad Place when you went to Suakin and talked to me for half an hour. He was Harry's best man. Well, that too I never understood. Did you?"

"Yes, I understood that perfectly."

"Oh!" Feversham sighed. He asked for no further clarification. As always, he took the questions he couldn't answer and set them aside, out of his thoughts entirely. He didn't move to his other letters. Instead, he continued smoking, gazing out over the summer landscape and listening to the distinct sounds rising from the fields.

Sutch had already read through all of Feversham's correspondence.

"I've been thinking," Feversham said finally. "Have you noticed the date of the month, Sutch?"

Sutch looked up sharply. "Yes," he replied. "Next week is the anniversary of our attack on the Redan, and Harry's birthday."

"Exactly," Feversham said. "Why shouldn't we restart the Crimean nights?"

Sutch leaped from his chair. "Splendid! Can we muster a tableful, do you think?"

"Let's see," Feversham said, ringing a handbell to summon the servant for the Army List. The two veterans bent over the list, planning their reunion.

But the story requires one final word of another figure. That night, after the invitations were sent from Broad Place and the house fell silent, a man leaned over the railing of a steamer anchored at Port Said. He listened to the rhythmic chanting of Arab coolies tramping up and down the planks, carrying coal baskets between barges and the ship's side. The clamor of the town drifted across the water to his ears. He imagined the flare of braziers on the quays, the glowing portholes, and the dark funnels of the anchored ships stretching ahead and behind.

Attended by a servant, he had returned to the East. Early the next morning, the steamer pushed through the canal, and by sunset, it emerged into the chilling waters of the Gulf of Suez. Kassassin, Tel-el-Kebir, Tamai, Tamanieb—the attack on McNeil's zareeba—Durrance relived these years of his prime, the years of plenty. Within that western land, preparations were advancing steadily, destined one day to roll up the Dervish Empire and crush it into dust. On the glacis of the ruined fort of Sinkat, Durrance had once promised himself a role in that great endeavor, but the desert he loved had rejected him, casting him out.

Yet, the boat steamed southward into the Red Sea. Three nights more, and though he would not witness it, the Southern Cross would rise slantwise into the sky.
