The Empress’s jealousy over a mere captive woman struck me as absurd—yet perilous. My mind, trained to act with soldierly precision, swiftly calculated the risks. My duty to Atlantis, as decreed by the High Council, demanded I play my part, even if it meant sacrificing Nais. Still, even as I bowed before Phorenice and pledged obedience, a plan to spare the prisoner flickered in my thoughts.

She smiled, her fingers brushing my arm, her eyes alight with that dangerous allure that had felled kings and priests alike. “In this, as in all things, your will is mine,” I said.

The merchants and townsfolk, drawn by the commotion, now crowded the royal quay, offering dry robes and shelter. Though I cared little for discomfort, the stench of the harbor’s filth made the change welcome. Phorenice, ever the performer, played the gracious ruler—until her gaze flicked back to Nais. “See she does not escape,” she ordered the port-captain, her voice sweet as poisoned honey.

An hour later, bathed and reclothed in plain garb, I waited as Phorenice emerged, adorned in jewels looted from a cringing merchant. “Pretty, are they not?” she mused, admiring their glitter. “A fine reward for our little spill.” She flashed me a conspiratorial grin. “Today, Deucalion, we play at courtship. No guards, no ceremony—just us.”

We took to her litter, her slaves bearing us along the harbor’s edge. The sight of the near-empty docks gnawed at me. Once, these waters teemed with ships; now, war and decay had withered trade to a trickle. The merchants who remained had either turned sycophantic courtiers or starved rebels. Yet I held my tongue. Phorenice cared little for such woes unless they served her whims.

She led me to a stone house perched on the tide-washed rocks, where her latest marvel lay: machines that drew gold from seawater. “A gift from the Gods,” she declared, though her smirk betrayed the lie. “Or perhaps just my own cleverness.” The contraptions were simple—water sluiced over metal plates, leaving behind flecks of gold—yet their yield was staggering. “My priests call it divine. But you, Deucalion, know better.”

Her pride was intoxicating, her mind as sharp as her blade. Yet beneath the brilliance lurked a capricious cruelty. She spoke of executing a smith for daring to wear gold, as if such tyranny were mere practicality. I listened, weighing her words, knowing Atlantis would never know peace while she ruled.

Then danger struck.

Fishermen, desperate and starving, swarmed from their stinking huts. They had seen Phorenice’s jewels, and hunger outweighed fear. I stepped from the litter, sword drawn, but she laughed and joined me, strapping on her own blade. “Shall we dance, Deucalion?”

The fight was brutal. They came like rabid beasts, unskilled but relentless. Phorenice fought like a demon, each stroke precise, each life taken with chilling grace. I matched her, my shield torn from a fallen foe, my sword carving a bloody arc. Yet even as we stood victorious amid the corpses, she could not resist gloating.

“Your backs will crawl when I return,” she promised the survivors, her voice silken with menace. “I’ll have you flayed, impaled—unless you prove yourselves worthy foes now.”

The fools hesitated. I knew then: this was no mere skirmish. It was a glimpse of the chaos that festered beneath Atlantis’s glittering decay—and of the woman who revelled in it.
