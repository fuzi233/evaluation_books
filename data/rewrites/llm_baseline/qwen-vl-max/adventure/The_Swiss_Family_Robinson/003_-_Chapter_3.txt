"I won’t bore you with a detailed account of our first day," my wife began, "though I confess I spent most of it watching your signals and worrying about your safety. This morning, I rose early and, seeing your reassuring sign that all was well, quickly responded. Then, while the boys still slept, I sat down to think—how could we improve our situation?

“It’s simply impossible,” I said to myself, “to stay here much longer. The sun beats down relentlessly on this bare rock, and our only shelter is this flimsy tent, where the heat is even worse than outside. Why should I and my little ones not do our part? Why shouldn’t we help build something useful?

“If only we could trade this bleak, unhealthy spot for a cool, shady home, we’d all feel better—healthier, happier. Among those lovely woods Fritz and you explored, there must be some perfect place to settle. I’m certain of it—and I’ll find it.”

By then, the boys were awake. I noticed Jack quietly working near where Fritz had left the jackal. Watching closely, I saw him cut two long strips from the animal’s hide, clean and scrape them carefully, then take a handful of large nails from his pocket and drive them through the skin, points outward. Next, he cut wide strips of canvas sailcloth, doubled them over, and laid them across the raw side of the hide so they covered the flat nail heads.

At this point, Master Jack came to me with a polite request: would I please stitch the canvas to the wet hide for him? I handed him needles and thread—but couldn’t bring myself to take the joy away from him.

Yet when I saw how determinedly he struggled with his clumsy fingers, I gave in. Overcoming my disgust, I finished lining the clever dog collars he’d made. Then I was asked to complete a fine leather belt he’d crafted for himself. I suggested he find a way to keep the hide from shrinking.

Ernest, though teasing Jack about his project, offered a sensible solution. Jack immediately put it into action—he stretched the hide flat on a board and placed it in the sun to dry.

My plan for an expedition met with eager approval from the boys. We quickly gathered supplies: weapons, food, water. The older boys took guns, while I carried the water flask and a small hatchet.

Leaving the camp neat and orderly, we set off toward the stream, followed by the dogs. Turk, who had gone with you on your first trip, seemed to understand our purpose at once and proudly led the way.

As I watched my sons—each with a gun—I felt deeply grateful to you, dear husband, for teaching them firearms as children. Their safety now depended on them.

We filled our water jar, crossed the stream, and climbed to the high point you described, where the view opened up like a promise. At the sight of the lush greenery and distant hills, a warm, hopeful feeling stirred in me—one I hadn’t known in a long time.

A small wood caught my eye, and we headed toward it. But soon, thick grass taller than the boys’ heads blocked our path. We turned to follow the open beach on our left, reaching a point closer to the wood before heading back into the reeds.

We hadn’t escaped the tall grass entirely. Just as we strained forward, a loud rushing sound sent us scrambling back. A huge bird burst into flight. Both boys raised their guns, but it was already too far away.

“Oh dear, what a pity!” Ernest exclaimed. “If only I’d had my light gun—and if it hadn’t flown so fast, I’d have brought it down!”

“Ah yes,” I replied gently, “no doubt you’d make a fine hunter—if every creature waited patiently for you to aim.”

“But I didn’t expect anything to rise right under our feet!” he protested.

“A good shot,” I said, “must always be ready for surprises. Birds and beasts don’t send warnings when they’re about to flee.”

“What kind of bird was it?” Jack asked.

“An eagle!” Franz declared. “It was so big!”

“Just because it’s big doesn’t mean it’s an eagle,” Ernest scoffed.

“Let’s see where it was sitting,” I said.

Jack darted forward—and instantly, another bird, even larger, exploded upward with a terrifying cry.

The boys stared, stunned. I laughed. “Well, you’re quite the hunters! You’ll certainly keep my larder well stocked!”

Ernest flushed, close to tears. Jack grinned, pulled off his cap, bowed low, and called after the fleeing bird: “Adieu for now, sir! I hope to meet again!”

After searching, we found a crude nest of dry grass—empty, though broken eggshells lay nearby. We realized the young birds had fled into the grass, which we could see waving gently as they ran.

“Now look here, Franz,” Ernest said, “how could this possibly be an eagle? Eagles never build on the ground. And their young can’t run as soon as they hatch—that’s only true of game birds. The white belly and dull red wing feathers I saw suggest bustards. And the moustache-like feathers above the beak? That’s the Great Bustard.”

“My dear boy,” I said, “your eyes were sharp, even if your hands weren’t ready. And perhaps it’s just as well we didn’t disturb the family.”

Chatting like this, we finally reached the wood. Birds sang and fluttered among the branches, but I discouraged the boys from shooting any. We stood in awe of the trees—so vast, so strange. What we thought was a forest was actually just a cluster of about a dozen trees, each supported by roots that formed arches and braces around the trunk, with the main stem rooted firmly in the center.

I gave Jack some twine. He climbed one of the open-air roots and measured the trunk—it was nearly eighteen yards around. Though I saw no fruit, the foliage was thick and cool, casting deep shade over soft, green grass—free of thorns or brambles. It was the most beautiful resting place imaginable. We enjoyed our midday meal there, refreshed after the heat of our journey.

The dogs joined us later. They’d stayed behind on the shore and now lay down, sleeping peacefully without begging for food—a surprise, since they usually demanded scraps.

The longer we stayed, the more enchanted I became. If only we could live in a shelter among those noble branches, I’d feel safe and content. It seemed foolish to search elsewhere. So I decided to return to the shore and see if the waves had brought anything useful from the wreck.

Before leaving, Jack persuaded me to finish his belt and the spiked collars for the dogs. He’d been carrying the board all along, so the hides could dry properly in the sun.

Now fully dried, I stitched them easily. Jack tied on his belt with pride, holstered his pistols, and marched around like a general. Ernest fitted the collars on the dogs.

Back at the shore, we found many items scattered by the tide—valuable, but too heavy to carry. We rolled casks beyond high tide and dragged chests higher onto the beach. As we worked, the dogs busied themselves among the rocks, diving into crevices and pools, then leaping back with something in their mouths.

“They’re eating crabs,” Jack said. “No wonder they haven’t been hungry lately.”

Sure enough, they were catching tiny green crabs everywhere.

Later, Juno began digging in the sand, uncovering round objects she devoured eagerly.

Ernest examined them and reported calmly: “She’s found turtle eggs.”

“Oh!” I exclaimed. “Then let’s share the treasure!” But Mrs. Juno wasn’t pleased. We had to drive her away to collect a couple of dozen eggs, which we tucked into our bags.

Suddenly, we spotted a sail approaching beyond the cliffs. Ernest insisted it was our raft. Little Franz grew pale—savages, he feared. For a moment, I hesitated.

But we hurried to the stream, crossed the stepping stones, and reached the landing place—where we joyfully met you.

“So, dear husband,” my wife said, “do you approve of our exploration? Tomorrow, will you please pack everything and take us to live among my wonderful trees?”

“Well, little wife,” you replied, “so that’s your idea of comfort—perching on a tree hundreds of feet high, like birds? If only we had wings or a balloon, it might be a fine plan!”

“Laugh all you like,” she said, “but my idea isn’t as absurd as you claim. We’d be safe from jackals at night. And I’ve seen in Switzerland a lovely arbour built high in a lime tree, with a strong floor and a staircase. Why can’t we build something similar—where we can sleep safely?”

“I’ll consider it seriously,” you said. “Perhaps something can come of it. For now, supper is done and night is falling. Let’s pray and rest.”

Under the tent, we all slept deeply—like marmots—until dawn. When I woke, I found my wife already awake, and we discussed our plans.

Regarding her suggestion, I said, “To move would be difficult. We must think carefully. First, I believe we were guided here providentially. This spot is secure—surrounded by cliffs, accessible only through the ford. From here, we can reach the ship and salvage its cargo. Suppose we stay until we bring everything ashore?”

“I agree partly,” she replied, “but the heat here is unbearable. While you and Fritz wander in cool woods, I suffer in the glare. As for the ship’s contents—so much has washed ashore already. I’d rather give up the rest than risk venturing out to sea again.”

“I see your point,” I said. “What if we move to your chosen spot, but keep this rocky area as a storehouse and refuge? I could make it safer by blowing up parts of the rock with gunpowder. But first, we need a bridge—to cross with our belongings.”

“Oh, I’ll fry before we leave if we wait for a bridge!” she exclaimed. “Why not just carry things on our backs and wade across, like we’ve done? The cow and donkey can carry much.”

“That they will,” I said, “but we’ll need baskets and bags. If you focus on making those, I’ll start the bridge. It won’t be used once—there may be floods, or accidents.”

“Well, well,” she sighed, “I submit. But please begin at once—I can’t wait to leave. And your idea of fortifying this place is excellent. Storing gunpowder here, away from our home, will be safer.”

“Gunpowder is both the most dangerous and most useful thing we have,” I said. “So we must guard it carefully. Soon, I’ll carve a chamber in the rock—safe from fire and damp.”

By morning, we’d decided: we would move. Work for the day was assigned. The boys, thrilled, called it our “journey to the Promised Land.” Only the bridge-building seemed a waste of time.

Everyone wanted breakfast so work could begin. We milked the cow and goats, then ate a hearty meal of biscuit boiled in milk. Then I set off for the wreck to gather planks for the bridge.

Ernest and Fritz joined me. We soon reached the current and drifted swiftly out to sea. Fritz steered, and as we passed the islet at the bay’s entrance, we were amazed to see thousands of seabirds—gulls and others—rising like a storm cloud, screaming wildly.

Fritz grabbed his gun, but I stopped him. I was curious—what drew such a swarm? Taking advantage of a fresh breeze, I hoisted the sail and sailed toward the island.

Ernest was delighted by the flying pennant; Fritz scanned the sandy shore where the birds settled.

Then he shouted, “Look! There’s a giant fish—those birds are feasting on it! Let’s get closer, Father!”

We couldn’t land directly, but we managed to moor the raft near the scene. Carefully, we approached.

It was a monstrous shark—its flesh torn apart by the birds. Watching them fight, scavenge, and squabble was a grim spectacle.

“There was nothing here yesterday, Father,” Fritz said. “How did it end up stranded?”

“Why, it’s the shark!” Ernest said. “Your shark! I can see the wound you hit!”

“You’re right,” I said. “Though it’s hard to tell through all that pecking. Look at those jaws—the snout, the rows of teeth! Thank God we escaped them. Let’s see if these birds will spare us a piece of skin. It’s rough—when dried, it could be used like a file.”

Ernest jabbed the ramrod into the crowd, killing several birds and scattering the rest. Fritz cut broad strips of skin, and we returned to the boat.

Seeing boards scattered along the shore—just what I needed—I collected them quickly. We made a raft to tow behind us, returning home without visiting the wreck.

As we sailed, I directed Fritz to nail part of the sharkskin flat on boards to dry in the sun, and the rest around the rounded mast.

“Will that work, Father?” he asked. “It’ll be crooked when dry.”

“That’s exactly what I want,” I said. “It might prove useful bent. It’ll make excellent shagreen—like sandpaper for smoothing wood.”

“Shagreen?” Ernest asked. “Isn’t it made from asses’ hides?”

“And rightly so,” I said. “The finest comes from Turkey, Persia, and Tartary—from horse and ass skins. The roughness is created by pressing corn grains into the hide while it’s still soft. Once dried, the grains are removed, leaving a permanent texture. In France, it’s made from the skin of a hideous fish called the angel-fish.”

“Angel-fish?” Fritz laughed. “What a name for something ugly!”

“There are bad angels too,” Ernest said quietly. “Best let people decide for themselves.”

We were nearing shore. Lowering the sail, we moored the raft and boat safely.

No one was in sight. We called out cheerily—and soon heard a reply. My wife and the boys rushed from behind the rocks, each carrying a bundle wrapped in a handkerchief. Franz held up a landing net.

They were surprised to see us back so soon. We explained our discovery. Then the bundles were opened—revealing dozens of large crawfish, scrambling wildly as they were dumped on the ground. The boys chased them in laughter, capturing one only to see another escape.

“Father!” Jack exclaimed. “Did you ever see such crawfish? Thousands of them—we’ve got at least two hundred! Look at their claws!”

“No doubt you found them, Jack?” I asked.

“No!” he said. “Franz did! He and I went toward the stream while Mother worked. Franz was picking up pebbles and alabaster—some pretty, some to strike sparks, some he swore were gold.”

“Jack! Jack!” Franz called. “Come see the crabs on Fritz’s jackal!” We threw it there, and sure enough, it was crawling with them. Are you glad we found them, Father? Will they be good to eat?”

“Excellent,” I said. “And we should be thankful—food comes to us daily.”

After sharing stories, my wife cooked the crawfish. We then went to haul the planks ashore. Even this simple task required planning. I improvised rope harnesses for the cow and donkey to drag each plank from the water to the streambank.

Jack showed me where he thought the bridge should go. I agreed—it was the best spot: banks close together, steep, and nearly equal in height.

“How can we measure if the planks are long enough?” I asked. “A surveyor’s table would help.”

“Try a ball of string, Father,” Ernest suggested. “Tie a stone to one end, throw it across, pull it back, and measure the line!”

We did just that. The distance was eighteen feet. Adding three feet on each side, I calculated twenty-four feet as the needed length.

How to lay the planks? That was harder. We decided to discuss it at dinner.

While we rested, my wife showed me her sewing. With great effort, she’d made two large canvas bags for the donkey. Without a proper needle, she’d used a nail to punch holes for each stitch. I praised her ingenuity and patience.

Dinner was eaten quickly—we were all eager to resume work. A new idea struck me: how to transport one end of a plank across the water. I began to plan it at once.
