The weight of our guilt from the morning’s transgression pressed heavily upon us as we witnessed the pain it inflicted upon the man we revered above all others. The blow dealt to his gentle heart was cruel, and those of us who had conspired in it could scarcely bear the sight. His beloved child had betrayed his trust—his entire family had united in deception against him. Dear, forgiving soul! I believe now, my love, that none of us could have repeated such an offense, knowing the anguish it caused. Kindness was as natural to him as breathing, and to wound him was an act of barbarity, like striking a defenseless child.

Once the deed was done, we who had wronged him groveled in remorse, seeking his pardon. The scenes of reconciliation, the shared prayers, the final parting—all were etched in sorrow. When his ship sailed away, Theo and I stood upon the shore, hand in hand, silent and ashamed. Until his departure, she had remained dutifully at his side, occupying her old place in the household, while I lingered at the edges, unwelcome. The atmosphere at home was strained: Aunt Lambert, usually so affectionate, turned sharp-tongued; little Hetty grew restless, murmuring, *“I wish we were gone.”*

At last, they departed, and Theo was mine alone. We retreated to Winchester and Hampton, though our wedding trip was shadowed by melancholy. It felt as though we had buried a father rather than celebrated a union. I instructed Sampson to announce our marriage in the papers (an act which later made my wife blush in the poor man’s presence), and we settled into modest lodgings in Bloomsbury, where our quiet married life began.

I wrote to my mother in Virginia, informing her of my marriage in the barest terms, emphasizing my intention to study law and seek employment either at home or abroad. The reply came not from her, but through Mrs. Mountain, who coolly relayed that Madam Esmond preferred all further correspondence to pass through her. My family’s outrage was almost comical—my uncle’s butler in Hill Street greeted me with a stare of theatrical horror, and when we encountered Lady Warrington in her chair, she nearly choked on her own indignation.

*“I—I wonder you dare look me in the face!”* she spluttered.
*“Nay, madam, why deny me so precious a privilege?”* I replied, sweeping her the grandest of bows before she snapped her chair shut and ordered her bearers onward.

Even little cousin Miles, exercising in Hyde Park, defied his parents’ orders long enough to shout, *“I’m not to speak to you, but sending my love to Harry isn’t speaking, is it? And you’re very pretty!”* before his scandalized groom hauled him away.

*“You see how my relations receive me,”* I remarked to Theo.
*“As if I married you for their sake!”* she answered, her eyes alight with such devotion that all else seemed trivial.

And so we were happy—blissfully so. The winter passed in cozy contentment: evenings by the fire, nights at the theater (where we eagerly anticipated the debut of my *Pocahontas*), and occasional visits from friends. Even the formidable Madame de Bernstein, after initial disdain, thawed toward Theo, though she made it clear we were no longer *du monde*. *“You may come amuse me, my dears,”* she declared, *“but you must use the back stairs.”*

The Dowager Countess of Castlewood extended a tepid welcome, inviting us to her Kensington assemblies, but the cost of cards and coaches soon outweighed the pleasure, and we withdrew without regret. Theo, radiant even in her mother’s modest jewels, cared nothing for the glittering dowagers who ignored her. Only one insolent fop—some friend of Will’s—dared send her a billet, an impertinence I swiftly discouraged with a threat.

Our most faithful visitor was Maria, now Mrs. Hagan, who often dined with us, sighing over her *“Polidore”* with theatrical rapture. Even the formidable Dr. Johnson occasionally lumbered in, though his manners—snorting over his tea, contradicting everyone—tried Theo’s patience. His opinion of my tragedy was blunt: *“I’d rather hear your wife’s prattle than your decasyllables.”*

Yet upon that tragedy rested all my hopes. My funds dwindling, I staked everything on *Pocahontas*—only to face disaster when Garrick, once obsequious, now sneered at my lack of noble patrons. Even Lord Bute, whom Sampson had rashly promised, refused to attend, scandalized that an actor (Hagan) married to a nobleman’s daughter should appear on stage.

The blow fell on the eve of rehearsal. A letter arrived, crushing my last hope of royal favor. *“His lordship cannot condescend to witness a play tainted by such associations.”*

That evening, Theo had prepared a tender supper, unaware of the ruin looming over us. I forced a smile, blaming fatigue for my pallor—how could I tell her that our future now hung by the thinnest of threads?
