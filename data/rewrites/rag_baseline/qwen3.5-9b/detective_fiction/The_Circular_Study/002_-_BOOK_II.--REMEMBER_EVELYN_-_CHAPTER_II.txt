A singular suspension of time, thick with awe, gripped Mr. Gryce before he could reclaim his composure. The atmosphere was a suffocating blend of dim light and hushed reverence, the room itself echoing a romantic age long dead. Lying outstretched, a grotesque parody of repose, was the house's master, his chest pierced by a sacred symbol that screamed a violent contradiction against the earthly fury required to drive the dagger home. Such a tableau was enough to unsettle even the hardened constitution of this veteran officer of the law, confusing a mind accustomed to solving grave problems but never quite prepared for this specific nightmare. Yet, the paralysis lasted only a heartbeat. Before the shadow behind him could voice his own bewildered shock, Mr. Gryce stepped forward, planting himself beside the prostrate form.

Death was absolute; there was no mistaking the cessation of breath. His first instinct was clinical verification, his hand falling to the wrist to check the pulse, his gaze locking onto the eyes. Those eyes, frozen in an expression of silent reproach, demanded a fortitude of spirit that Gryce summoned instantly. The body was still warm, yet undeniably cold in its finality. Convinced, he refrained from disturbing the wound, instead turning his scrutiny to the weapon itself. It was no common tool of murder, but a curiosity imported from some distant eastern bazaar. Its presence hinted at suicide, yet the angle of entry and the precise placement of the wound defied the logic of self-destruction. Clues were sparse. Despite the visceral evidence of a sudden, ferocious assault, the well-appointed apartment bore no marks of a struggle. Save for a scattering of rose petals on the floor, the room exuded a deceptive peace and quiet luxury. Even the massive central table, positioned where the master had stood moments before, offered no immediate sign of the tragedy unfolding at its edge. Superficially, everything remained undisturbed, as if the shock of the blow had failed to ripple outward to the objects surrounding the victim.

Yet, a closer inspection revealed a chaotic inventory of taste. Only a man of complex intellect and refined palate could have amassed such a collection within such confined space: pipes, pens, portraits, weights, measures, Roman lamps, Venetian glass, rare porcelains, medals, rough metalwork, manuscripts, a scroll of music, a pot of blooming flowers—and, most strangely, a row of electric buttons. As Mr. Gryce's fingers brushed one, the crimson glow burning within the cage of fretted ironwork overhead flared instantly into a sickly green glare, bathing the room in ghastly hues. Seizing another button, he pressed it, grateful to witness the return of a mild, white radiance that dispelled the spectral tint. "Childish tricks for a man of his station," Gryce muttered, though the thought faltered as he glanced again at the upturned face. Could the owner of such sharply cut, purposeful features possess an instinct so juvenile? No. The face itself was arresting, exerting a magnetic pull that disrupted the flow of his investigation whenever it crossed his path. To attribute folly to such a visage was to admit one's own ignorance of human nature. Thus, the lamp, with its scientific mechanisms and shifting lights, took on a new weight, suggesting a meaning rooted in the advanced studies evident in the books and apparatus littering the room.

Allowing the white light to steady, Gryce shifted his focus to the walls, draped in tapestries and curiosities. Nothing immediately aided his search for the truth, except for a single anomaly: a bent nail, ripped from its socket. It was the nail that had once held the cross resting now upon the dead man's heart. The cord suspending the cross still clung to it, tangling its red threads with the scarlet ribbon emerging from the victim's wound. Who had torn down that symbol of peace? Not the victim; with such a grievous injury, the movement would have been physically impossible. Furthermore, the nail and the resulting vacancy on the wall lay at the extreme periphery of the circular apartment, far removed from the fallen body. Another hand had performed this act, placing the symbol directly beneath the dying man's fading breath—a peculiar display of religious hope or frantic remorse that Gryce could afford only a fleeting consideration amidst the golden urgency of the moment.

Behind the table, halfway up the wall, hung a portrait, the sole large image in the chamber. It depicted a young girl of striking, almost painful beauty. Her attire and hairstyle dated the painting to the twilight of the Civil War. There resonated within her gaze the same haunting intellectual charm visible in the prone man, and despite the contrast between her fairness and his darkness, a familial resemblance argued a shared lineage. Beneath the portrait were affixed a sword, a pair of epaulettes, and a medal awarded for valor during the conflict. "Mementoes that may illuminate our task," Gryce mused.

Moving onward, he encountered a narrow curtain, its dark hue blending seamlessly with the drapery on the adjacent walls, rendering it invisible until now. It was not a window, for the apertures in this unique apartment were situated high near the ceiling. Instead, it concealed an entrance to another chamber. Pushing it aside, Gryce entered a narrow closet housing a bed, a dresser, and a small table. The bed was the narrow cot of a bachelor, while the dresser bespoke a man of luxurious habits and meticulous order. Both were pristine, save for a silver-backed comb missing from the dresser, now found lying on the floor at the far end of the room. Alongside it, a pearl-handled parasol stood on a small pedestal near the door, signaling the recent presence of a woman.

Identity followed swiftly. A small, unmistakable token linked this visitor to the individual who had alerted the police: a tiny black sequin, a milliner's delight, resting on the threshold separating this room from the study. Drawn by its sparkle, Gryce stooped to inspect it, only to catch his eye on a second sequin further along the floor, and a third a few paces beyond. The final one lay near the large central table. These bright, sparkling drops traced a dainty trail that affected him profoundly. As a minute observer, he knew that such garnitures were strung on a fragile thread; once severed, the sequins fell one by one, creating a map of a woman's movements. Whether it was the delicacy of the clue or the irony of fate trapping the vain in their own vanity that moved him, a smile played on his lips as he turned his gaze back to the table.

Before examining the furniture, he sought the origin of the loose thread. Had it snagged on a projection? He scanned the room; all chairs were cushioned and smooth. Then, his eyes landed on the cross. Its base featured intricate gold filigree. Could this delicate wire have caught in the woman's dress as she tore the cross from the wall, initiating the cascade of sequins? Rushing to the spot where the cross had hung, he searched the floor at his feet but found nothing until he reached the rug beneath the prostrate man. Amidst the long hairs of the bearskin lay another sequin, confirming that the woman in shimmering attire had stooped there before him. Satisfied, he returned to the table for a thorough, minute examination.

The results were promising. A smile curved Gryce's lips as he rubbed his finger across a specific spot near the inkstand, and with practiced care, he lifted the stand only to replace it in its exact original position. Had he anticipated a hidden compartment? One could never tell; a detective's face rarely betrays its secrets. Lost in contemplation before the table, he was startled by a quick step behind him. Styles, the officer, had completed his sweep of the house and now stood ready to report.

"What is it?" Gryce asked, turning sharply.

Styles gestured toward the staircase visible through the antechamber door. "Go up!" he indicated.

Gryce hesitated, glancing around the room, which currently held his entire attention. The officer's agitation broke the silence. "Come! I've found the culprit. He's in a room upstairs."

"He?" Gryce was visibly surprised by the pronoun.

"Yes; there is no doubt. When you see him—but what is that? Is he coming down? I'm certain no one else is in the house. Don't you hear footsteps, sir?"

Gryce nodded. Someone was definitely descending. "Let us retreat," Styles suggested. "Not because he is dangerous, but because it is vital you observe him before he observes you. He is a very strange-acting man, sir. If he enters here, he will surely incriminate himself. Where shall we hide?"

Gryce recalled the small room he had just vacated and guided the officer inside. Once secured, he drew the curtain shut, leaving only a narrow loophole. The approaching footsteps grew louder, accompanied by the rapid, labored breathing of the intruder.

"Does he know someone has entered? Did he see you upstairs?" Gryce whispered urgently.

Styles shook his head, pointing eagerly toward the opposite door. The awaited figure had just lifted the portière and stood fully in view. Both men stared. Was this the murderer? This pale, gaunt servant, carrying a tray with a single glass of water? So astonished was Gryce that he looked to Styles for clarification. But the officer, suppressing his own surprise at this peaceful apparition, urged patience. Together, they watched as the man advanced, stopped, cast a single look at the body on the floor, and then dropped the glass with a low cry that quickly escalated into a howl.
