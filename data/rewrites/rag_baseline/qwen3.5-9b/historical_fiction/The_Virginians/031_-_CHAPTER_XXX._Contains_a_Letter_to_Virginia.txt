The air inside the White Horse Ordinary tasted of stale tobacco and cheap claret, a thick suspension of gossip and humidity. Mr. Warrington entered, finding the room already saturated with the noise of Parson Sampson. The clergyman sat amidst a circle of country gentlemen, his face flushed with a rosy, artificial vitality that seemed to defy the damp London evening outside. He spoke in rapid bursts, weaving tales of Kensington and Pall Mall, of prisons and racecourses, constructing a narrative where names shifted like sand but the essence remained: a world of wicked fun and dangerous intrigue. To these listeners, accuracy was irrelevant; the texture of the lie mattered far more than the grain of the truth. If Sampson called Mrs. Polly by the name of Lucy, or attributed a duel to the wrong regiment, the audience swallowed the error with a smile, eager for the next bout of laughter.

When Warrington stepped into the light, the room's rhythm fractured. Sampson stopped mid-sentence, his eyes widening until they lost focus. With a whisper meant only for the squire beside him—a muttered invocation of debt—he scrambled from his chair. He did not walk; he stumbled forward, propelled by a frantic need to bridge the distance between the parson and the Virginian. "My dear sir! My conqueror of spades!" Sampson cried, his voice cracking with exaggerated delight. "You look fresh! Well-preserved!"

Warrington returned the greeting with a polite warmth, observing the chaplain's rosy gills with genuine appreciation. The squire, recognizing the newcomer, gestured for Sampson to resume his seat, attempting to steer the conversation back to Lord Ogle and a grocer's wife, but his interruption was drowned out by a shout and an oath regarding a gouty toe. Sampson apologized, turning his attention fully to Warrington. He delivered the news of Castlewood with the precision of a courier: messages from the Viscountess, updates on the waters at Harrogate, the fortunes of Mr. Will, and the distress of Molly over the missing valet, Gumbo. He wove these fragments into a tapestry of local significance, ensuring that even the highest titles sounded intimate.

As the other guests drifted away, leaving the room quieter, the dynamic shifted. Sampson, emboldened by the solitude, poured toast after toast, his energy seemingly infinite. Warrington matched him, his demeanor warming, becoming generous and open. Why should the chaplain endure the expense of an inn when Warrington offered a room? The order went forth: Gumbo was summoned to prepare the quarters, and Sampson was to inspect the stables. There, amidst the smell of hay and leather, the connection deepened. Sampson recognized his own horse, a creature that whinnied and nuzzled its old master with a familiarity that transcended language. Warrington, struck by the bond, declared the animal a gift. Sampson seized the offer, collapsing into tears of gratitude that mingled with the scent of Bordeaux wine, and the two friends walked arm-in-arm toward Madame Bernstein's lodgings, carrying the lingering odors of the stable into the drawing room.

Madame Bernstein received the chaplain with a mixture of politeness and amusement. She tolerated his broad jokes and rapid speech much as she tolerated a novel dish on its first few servings, but her patience held firm only for cards. "The cards do not cheat," she stated, her tone final. "They speak the truth." Yet, when her mood lifted, she invited Sampson to lead grace before meals. Initially hesitant, citing the high stakes of her games and his depleted funds, Sampson eventually secured a small cache of coins from Warrington's overflowing purse, allowing him to join the table.

Warrington embraced this arrangement with the ease of someone adapting to a new ecosystem. His attire remained dark and severe, yet cut with a sharp elegance that drew comparisons to Hamlet, complete with ribbons and stockings pulled down. He acquired the accoutrements of his station: fine shaving plates, brocade bedgowns, French watches with painted backs, and snuff boxes crafted by artisans. A retinue of grooms, jockeys, and tradesmen waited in his anteroom, filtered through Gumbo before reaching the Virginian. In parallel, Warrington pursued the arts of gentility, mastering the foil and the minuet under the tutelage of foreign instructors. He learned to dance with a grace that silenced Lady Maria's critics and to ride with a confidence that rivaled Colonel Washington, dismissing the need for further instruction in the native Virginian style.

Sampson flourished in this environment, his knowledge of the world expanding alongside his patron's wealth. He became a confidant, dispensing stories that were half-truth, half-fiction, seasoned with the wine of a hundred dinner tables. Warrington listened with admiration, captivated by the way the older man's wit aged like a vintage, gaining depth rather than losing it. This symbiotic relationship culminated in a formal exchange of letters, preserving the moment of transition.

To Mrs. Esmond Warrington of Castlewood, Richmond, Virginia:

"HONOURED MADAM—Your letter of 20 June, conveyed by Mr. Trail of Bristol, has reached me safely. I thank you for your counsel and for the remembrances of our ancestral home, which I cherish despite the distance.

"I wrote previously of the journey's minor accidents and the kind reception I received. Since then, I have profited from the fine weather and the excellent company, forging acquaintances among the nobility. Among them are the Earl of Chesterfield, the Earl of March and Ruglen, soon to be Duke of Queensberry, and the Duchess, a celebrated beauty of the Queen's time. These figures frequent my aunt's assemblies, the most crowded gatherings in this bustling place. On my journey, I also stayed at Westerham with Lieutenant-General Wolfe, a veteran of Marlborough's campaigns, whose son, Lieutenant-Colonel James Wolfe, is engaged to Miss Lowther and holds the esteem of the entire army.

"I acknowledge receipt of the quarterly allowance of 52l. 10s. via Mr. Trail. While I currently possess sufficient means, I intend to practice rigorous economy to maintain my credit for the upkeep of horses, Gumbo, and the necessaries of a gentleman of my standing. The linens and clothes I brought will suffice for some years, though I may require finer shirts for occasions of consequence.

"I am relieved to report that I have not needed your family pills. Gumbo, however, has benefited greatly from English beef, ale, and air, growing fat and saucy. He sends his duty to his mistress and requests that Mrs. Mountain remember him to his fellow servants, particularly Dinah and Lily, for whom he purchased posy rings at Tunbridge Fair.

"Regarding my education, I assure you I remain diligent. I have masters in fencing and dancing, and Reverend Mr. Sampson, having joined me to drink the waters, occupies a room at my lodging. We break together and read in the mornings; he assures me I am not quite the dunce I once appeared. Our studies include Mr. Rapin's History, Dr. Barrow's Sermons, Shakespeare, Pope's Homer, and a French translation of an Arabian tale collection. Several scholars reside here, including Mr. Richardson, author of the works you and my brother cherished. He expressed pleasure upon learning his writings occupy a shelf in Virginia and extended his respects to my mother. Mr. Richardson is a short, stout man, lacking the fiery brilliance often associated with genius.

"My aunt and cousin, Lady Maria, send their affectionate greetings. Enclosed is a note for Mountain. I remain, Honoured madam, your dutiful son, H. ESMOND WARRINGTON."
