Of course, the young men had spent considerable time discussing their respective homes, the people within them, and recent events. They exchanged detailed accounts of their histories since their last separation. "How are Harry's dogs?" George asked. "And little Dempster? And good old Nathan, along with the rest of the household?" Conversely, Harry inquired about Mountain's health and whether Fanny had blossomed into a lovely young lady.

Harry confessed that at one point he had been deeply infatuated with Parson Broadbent's daughter. He admitted to losing significant sums at cards and consuming large quantities of strong spirits with the parson himself, solely to create opportunities for proximity to the girl. Yet, heaven forbid! Madam Esmond would never have consented to him wasting his prospects on Polly Broadbent.

Turning the conversation, Harry noted that Colonel G. Washington's wife was a beautiful woman, possessing a gentle nature, a pleasant demeanor, and a substantial fortune. He had recently escorted her to Richmond, where he paid a formal call upon Madam Esmond. George recounted the entire encounter with great humor, describing the terrifying ceremonial protocols of the meeting and the biting politeness displayed by his mother toward Mr. Washington's young bride.

"Never mind, George, my dear!" Mrs. Mountain interjected cheerfully. "The Colonel has taken another wife, but I am certain that once, two young gentlemen I know ran a very close race to become the stepsons of a towering giant six feet two in his boots." Indeed, Mrs. Mountain was perpetually engaged in matchmaking in her mind; she could not watch two people play cards or share a cup of tea without imagining their union for life. It was she—the gossipy instigator—who had spread the rumor concerning the unfortunate Indian woman.

As for Madam Esmond, she had met the suggestion with scorn when Parson Stack delivered it, declaring, "I should as soon fancy Mr. Esmond stealing the silver spoons, or marrying a negro woman out of the kitchen." However, while she disdained finding fault with the poor Biche and even thanked her for nursing her son during his illness, she treated the Indian woman with such chilling hauteur that the girl retreated to the servants' quarters, attempting to drown her disappointment in alcohol.

George painted a far from cheerful picture of his two-month stay at home. "The birthright is mine, Harry," he lamented, "yet you are the favorite. God help me! I suspect my mother almost grudgingly accepts my existence. Why did I take precedence over your worship into the world? Had you been the elder, you would possess the finest cellar, ride the best horse, and be the most popular man in the countryside. Whereas I have nothing to commend myself except a glum countenance that frightens people. I should have been the second son, setting up as a lawyer, traveling to England to earn my degrees, becoming a parson, and saying grace at your honor's table. The times are out of joint, sir. O cursed spite, that ever I was born to set them right!"

"Why, Georgy, you're reciting poetry, I protest!" Harry exclaimed. "I think, my dear, someone else spoke those verses before me," George replied with a smile. "'Tis from one of your books. You know every book ever written, I believe!" cried Harry. He then recounted seeing the two authors at Tunbridge, bowing respectfully to them. "Not that I cared much for their works, lacking the wit to appreciate them fully," Harry admitted, his voice catching slightly. "But I remembered how my dear old George used to speak of them, and that is why I loved seeing them."

He added, "Say, dear, it feels like a dream to see you again. Think of that bloody Indian with a knife at my George's throat! I'd like to offer Monsieur de Florac something for saving you—but I have little left now, save my little gold knee-buckles, which aren't worth two guineas."

"You hold half of what I have, child," George assured him. "We shall divide it as soon as I have settled the debt with the Frenchman." Upon hearing this, Harry erupted not merely into blessings but into fervent oaths, expressing his intense love and relief, swearing that there was no brother in the world comparable to George. For several days following his brother's arrival, Harry's eyes trailed George constantly. He would pause mid-meal, dropping his knife and fork, or stop reading his newspaper to burst into laughter. Walking together on the Mall or in Hyde Park, Harry would survey the crowd as if presenting a trophy: "Behold, gentlemen! This is he. This is my brother, who was dead and is now alive again! Can any man in Christendom boast of such a sibling?"

Naturally, Harry believed George should fulfill his promise to pay Museau's heirs the ransom sum. This issue had caused George considerable distress at home. With Museau deceased, Madam Esmond argued with great eagerness—and not a little rancor—that the agreement was void; her son was now free. She deemed the man a rogue from the start and refused to pay the wages of iniquity. Though Mr. Esmond possessed a small independence from his father and could squander his patrimony as he chose, being of age and master of his funds, his mother would not participate in such extravagance. Giving twelve thousand livres to a group of peasant farmers in Normandy—whom Britain was currently at war with, and who would likely funnel the money to priests and the Pope—was sheer wickedness in her eyes. She would not subscribe to such sin. If George wished to squander his father's money (though she noted he had previously shown restraint, especially regarding Harry's welfare), why not give it to his own flesh and blood, to poor Harry, who had suddenly lost his inheritance, rather than to a band of priest-ridden peasants in France?

This dispute raged between mother and son throughout George's final days in Virginia. It remained unresolved. On the morning of his departure, after a sleepless night, Madam Esmond approached his bedside and asked if he still intended to throw away his father's property. He replied, in depths of grief and confusion, that his word was given and he must act according to his honor. She answered that she would continue praying for Heaven to soften his proud heart and enable her to endure her heavy trials. The last image George held of his mother's face was hers standing beside his bed, pale and with tearless eyes, before she turned away and slowly exited the chamber.

"Where did you learn the art of winning everyone to your side, Harry?" George continued. "How do you and the entire world begin as friends? Teach me a few lessons in popularity. Nay, perhaps I won't need them; when I discover certain people hate me, I find myself rather pleased than angered."

Initially, at Richmond, Mr. Esmond Warrington—the sole survivor of Braddock's defeat, a victim of immense illness and hardship—had been a favorite among the townsfolk, receiving both private and public kindness. The parson had glorified his escape in a sermon; neighbors visited the fugitive; the family carriage was summoned, and Madam Esmond and George returned the courtesy calls. George recalled thinking some charming little caps were set at him. But his mother routed these admirers off, frightening them with her prodigious haughtiness, causing his popularity to dwindle before the incident that sealed his fate occurred.

"I wasn't jolly enough for the officers," George explained. "I didn't care for their drinking bouts, dice games, and swearing. I was too sarcastic for the ladies; their tea parties and gossip stupefied me almost as much as the men's bluster and horse talk. I cannot tell you, Harry, how lonely I felt in that place, amidst the scandal and squabbles. I regretted my prison almost, finding myself wishing repeatedly for the freedom of thought and the silent ease of Duquesne. I suppose I am very shy; I can speak unreservedly to very few. Before most, I sit utterly silent. When we were at home, it was you who dominated the table conversations, occasionally earning a smile from our mother. When she and I were together, we shared no common subjects, speaking scarcely at all until we began disputing over law and divinity."

"So the gentlemen decided I was supercilious and a dull companion (and, indeed, I think their judgment was correct)," George mused. "And the ladies thought me cold and sarcastic—they could never discern if I was serious or joking, generally voting me a disagreeable fellow before my character was completely ruined. That went hand-in-hand with the appearance of the poor Biche. Oh, what a nice character they constructed for me, my dear!" George cried out in a transport of rage. "And what a pretty life they led me after Museau's unlucky messenger appeared among us! The boys hooted the poor woman if she ventured onto the street; the ladies dropped half-curtsies and crossed to the other side of the room. That precious clergyman moved from one tea table to another, preaching on the horrors of seduction and the lax principles young men learned in papist countries and brought back with them. The poor Fawn's appearance at home just weeks after my return was declared a scheme between her and me; the best-informed agreed she had waited on the other side of the river until I signaled her to join me in Richmond. The officers bantered me at the coffee house, cracking clumsy jokes about the woman I had chosen. Oh, the world is a nice, charitable world!
