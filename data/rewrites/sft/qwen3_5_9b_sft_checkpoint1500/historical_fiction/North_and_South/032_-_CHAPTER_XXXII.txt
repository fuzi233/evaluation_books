"What! To remain denounce-worthy—dragged, perhaps, in chains?"

For the entirety of the following day, the three sat together in silence. Mr. Hale spoke only when his children pressed him, forcing him, as it were, into the present moment. Frederick's grief, having spent its initial fury, now settled into a heavy, silent shame; he was acutely aware of how utterly he had been worn down by emotion, yet beneath this surface pride lay a sorrow for his lost mother that was profound and enduring, destined to haunt his days without utterance. Margaret, though less volatile in her first shock, now suffered a deeper, more lingering pain. Tears frequently clouded her eyes, and a mournful tenderness clung to her speech, intensified whenever her gaze met Frederick's and her thoughts turned to his imminent departure. Yet, despite her personal anguish, she found relief in his leaving, driven by a fierce, protective love for her father. The terror that seized Mr. Hale, fearing his son's capture, had grown since Mrs. Hale's death, feeding exclusively on his obsessive dread. He started at every unfamiliar noise and refused comfort unless Frederick sat hidden from the sight of anyone entering the room.

Towards evening, Mr. Hale broke the silence. "Will you accompany Frederick to the station, Margaret? I must know he has departed safely. Promise me you will confirm he has cleared Milton, at the least?"

"Certainly," Margaret replied softly. "I shall like it, Father, if you will not be lonely without me."

"No, no!" he protested, his voice tight with anxiety. "Unless you tell me you have seen him off, I shall constantly imagine someone knows of him and has stopped him. Go to the Outwood station. It is equally near, yet sparsely populated. Hire a cab there; there is less likelihood of him being spotted. What hour does your train depart, Fred?"

"Ten minutes past six. Nearly dark. Pray, what shall you do, Margaret?"

"Oh, I can manage," she assured him, her voice gaining a steeliness born of necessity. "I am growing braver, harder. The road home is well-lit, even if darkness falls. Last week, I walked out much later."

Margaret breathed a sigh of relief when the final farewell between the dead mother and the living father concluded. She hurried Frederick into a cab, eager to curtail a scene that she knew would tear her father apart further, who was already terribly shaken. Due to a common error in the Railway Guide regarding arrival times at minor stations, coupled with the cab's delay, they arrived at Outwood with nearly twenty minutes to spare. The booking office was closed, precluding the purchase of tickets. Instead, they descended the steps to the ground level, traversing a broad path of cinders that cut diagonally across a field bordering the carriage road. There, they paced back and forth, wasting away the fleeting moments allotted to them.

Margaret's hand rested within Frederick's arm, and he clasped it affectionately. "Margaret! I intend to consult Mr. Lennox concerning the possibility of clearing my name, thereby securing liberty to return to England whenever I please. This endeavor is undertaken chiefly for your sake, lest anything should befall my father. His state is dreadful; he looks profoundly altered. I beseech you to persuade him to entertain the Cadiz scheme. Consider: what could you endure if he were taken away? You possess no friends nearby; our circle of relations is curiously barren."

Margaret struggled to stifle tears at the tender, piercing anxiety with which Frederick laid before her a spectre she feared was inevitable, given the toll the last few months had exacted from Mr. Hale. Yet, she rallied her spirits. "Two years have witnessed such strange, unforeseen transformations in my life that I feel it futile to calculate precisely what I ought to do should some future catastrophe occur. I strive to attend solely to the present."

She paused. They stood motionless beside the stile leading into the road, bathed in the dying light of the setting sun. Frederick held her hand, his gaze fixed on hers with wistful, searching intensity, reading there a depth of care and trouble that her words failed to convey. Continuing, she said, "We shall correspond frequently, and I promise—for I perceive it will soothe your mind—that I will disclose every tribulation I encounter. Father is..." She hesitated, giving a barely perceptible start. Frederick felt the sudden twitch of her hand and instantly turned his face toward the road. Along the path, a horseman rode slowly, passing directly by the stile where they stood. Margaret bowed, and received a stiff, curt return.

"Who is that?" Frederick demanded, before the rider vanished from earshot.

"A gentleman named Mr. Thornton," Margaret replied, her cheeks slightly flushed, her spirit somewhat drooping. "You have encountered him before."

"Merely his back. An unprepossessing figure. What a scowl!"

"Some grievous matter has vexed him," Margaret offered, her tone apologetic. "You would scarcely deem him unprepossessing had you beheld him with Mother."

"I suppose it is high time I procure my ticket. Had I foreseen the darkness, we need not have returned the cab, Margaret."

"Oh, do not fret about that," she interjected gently. "I may hire a cab here, or return via the railway, where shops, people, and lamps line the entire route from Milton. Think not of me; safeguard yourself. I am ill with the thought that Leonards may share your carriage. Examine the interior thoroughly before boarding."

They retraced their steps to the station. Margaret insisted on entering the glare of the gas lamps within to secure her ticket. Several idle young men loitered near the station master. Margaret believed she had glimpsed the countenance of one of them previously and returned a proud, haughty glance of offended dignity against his impudent stare of undisguised admiration. Rushing to her brother, who stood outside, she seized his arm. "Have you secured your luggage? Let us wander upon the platform," she said, her voice fluttering slightly at the prospect of imminent solitude, her newfound courage leaking away faster than she wished to admit.

A footstep followed them across the flagstones, halting simultaneously with theirs. Both froze, gazing down the tracks and listening for the whistle of the approaching train. Neither spoke; their hearts swelled with a tumultuous fullness. In another moment, the train would arrive; in a minute, Frederick would be gone. Margaret almost repented the haste with which she had urged him toward London; it appeared to court additional risks of discovery. Had he sailed for Spain via Liverpool, he might have escaped within hours.

Frederick turned, facing the bright flare of the gas lamp, which burned with vivid anticipation of the train. A man dressed as a railway porter stepped forward—a disreputable figure who appeared drunk into a state of brutishness, though his faculties remained sharp.

"By your leave, miss!" he barked, shoving Margaret roughly aside and seizing Frederick by the collar. "Your name is Hale, I presume?"

Before Margaret could comprehend how it occurred, her vision swam as everything danced before her eyes, Frederick executed a swift, practiced maneuver, tripping the man. With a heavy thud, the intruder crashed onto the platform, falling from the three-foot elevation down to the soft earth beside the railroad. He lay there, stunned.

"Run, run!" Margaret gasped. "The train is here! Was it Leonards? Oh, run! I will carry your bag!"

Seizing his arm, she pushed him forward with all her feeble strength. A carriage door swung open; he leaped inside. As he leaned out, shouting, "God bless you, Margaret!" the train surged past her, leaving her standing alone and shattered. A profound sickness and faintness overwhelmed her, making her grateful to retreat into the ladies' waiting-room and collapse into a seat for a brief respite. Initially, she could only gasp for air. The urgency, the sickening alarm, the hairbreadth escape—it all crashed over her. If the train had not arrived at that precise second, the man would have risen and summoned aid to arrest him. She wondered if he had regained his footing, whether she had seen him stir, if he had sustained serious injury. Venturing out, the platform was brightly lit yet utterly deserted. She moved to the edge and peered over cautiously. No one was there. Relief washed over her, for otherwise, terrible nightmares would surely haunt her sleep. Even so, trembling and affrighted, she felt incapable of walking home along the lonely, dark road, illuminated only by the distant blaze of the station. She resolved to wait for the down train and ride it home. But what if Leonards recognized her as Frederick's companion? She scanned the area before risking entry into the booking-office to purchase her ticket. Only railway officials lingered, speaking loudly amongst themselves.
