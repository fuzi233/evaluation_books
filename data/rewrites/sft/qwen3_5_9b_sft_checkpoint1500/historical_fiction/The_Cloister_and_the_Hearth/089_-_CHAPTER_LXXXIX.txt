To describe the inner turmoil of a mind so sensitive, a conscience so tender, and a heart so devoted to God within such a brief span of time following such a sudden and terrible shock would require a vocabulary beyond human reach. Imagine yourself in his shoes, bearing the weight of that specific burden. If I were to chronicle every thought, the book would indeed need to end here. Instead, let us focus on his next two decisive actions, which reveal the state of his spirit once the initial storm of anguish had finally calmed.

After spending a night beside the dying hermit, exchanging mutual spiritual consolations, Clement did not head back to Rotterdam. His destination was Tergou. There, he intended to face his mortal foe, the burgomaster, armed with a parchment—a document whose history was, in its own right, a tale of romance—to force the return of what rightfully belonged to Margaret.

By the time he reached Tergou, Clement was heated and covered in dust. He paused at a public fountain, unwrapping black bread and drinking deeply from the cool water. Mid-meal, however, a female servant burst forward, breathless, begging him to accompany her immediately to shrive her dying master. With a swift motion, Clement returned his bread to his wallet and followed her without a single word spoken.

She led him directly to the Stadthouse. As they crossed the threshold, Clement drew back slightly, a faint shudder passing over him at the sight of where she was leading. Yet, he quickly composed himself and ascended the stairs. At the top, propped high by pillows, lay his deadly enemy. To all outward appearance, the old man looked already like a corpse.

From the doorway, Clement watched the scene for a moment, recalling the tower, the forest, and the letter. "Peace be with you," he whispered softly, his voice trembling just enough to betray the effort it took.

The sick man received him with eager gratitude, as best his failing strength allowed. "Thank Heaven, Father," he cried, "you have arrived in time to absolve me from my sins. Pray for my soul, both you and your brothers."

"My son," Clement replied, "absolution comes only after confession. In this act, there must be no concealment, for the salvation of your soul depends upon it. Consider carefully wherein you have most grievously offended God and the Church, while I pray for the wisdom necessary to guide you."

Clement knelt and offered a prayer. Rising, he addressed Ghysbrecht with a facade of calm, "Now, my son, confess your sins."

"Oh, Father!" the sick man exclaimed, "They are many and great."

"Then great must be your penitence, my son, so that you may find God's mercy equally vast."

Clasping his hands, Ghysbrecht began to speak, displaying every sign of contrition. He admitted to eating meat during Lent, frequently missing Mass on Sundays and feast days, and casually neglecting other religious observances, listing them with scrupulous detail.

Upon finishing, the friar spoke quietly, "'Tis well, my son. Those are faults. Now, to your crimes. You ought to have begun with those."

"Why, Father?" Ghysbrecht asked, confused. "If these are not crimes, what else lies to my account?"

"Am I confessing to you, or are you confessing to me?" Clement asked, his tone sharpening with severity.

"Forgive me, Father! I meant to confess to you. But I do not know what you mean by 'crimes.'"

"Do you claim to be free of the Seven Deadly Sins?"

"Heaven forbid I should be guilty of them. I know them not by name."

"Many who commit them cannot name them. Begin with the sin that leads to lying, theft, and murder."

"I am clean of that, any way. What do you call it?"

"Avarice, my son."

"Avarice?" Ghysbrecht stammered. "I have been a thrifty man all my life, yet I have kept a generous table and never forgot the poor. But alas, I am a great sinner! Perhaps the next one will catch me. What is the next?"

"We have not finished with this one. Remember, the Church cannot be trifled with."

"But Father, am I in a fit state to trifle with Her now? Avarice? Avarice?" The old man looked utterly puzzled and innocent.

"Hast thou ever robbed the fatherless?" the friar inquired.

"Me? Robbed the fatherless?" Ghysbrecht gasped, recoiling. "Not that I mind."

"Once more, my son," Clement warned, his voice hardening, "I am forced to tell you that you are trifling with the Church. Wretched man! Another evasion, and I shall depart. Let demons gather round your bed and drag you down to the bottomless pit."

"Oh, do not leave me! Do not leave me!" shrieked the terrified old man. "The Church knows everything. I must have robbed the fatherless. I will confess. Where do I start? My memory for names is shattered."

His defense was skilled, but in this instance, it failed.

"Hast thou forgotten Floris Brandt?" Clement asked, his voice like stone.

The sick man threw himself up in bed, seized by a pitiable terror. "How did you know that?" he cried.

"The Church knows many things," Clement said coldly, "by many paths dark to you. Wretched impenitent soul, you called her to your side, hoping to deceive her. You said, 'I will not confess to the cure, but to some friar who knows not my misdeeds. Thus I will cheat the Church on my deathbed and die as I have lived.' But God, kinder to you than you are to yourself, has sent you one whom you could not deceive. He has tested you; He was patient, warning you not to mock Holy Church. Yet all is in vain. You cannot confess because you remain as stubborn as stone. Die, then, as you have lived."

Turning his back, Clement prepared to leave. But Ghysbrecht, driven to the brink of despair, lunged forward and grabbed the friar's robe. "Oh, holy man, mercy! Stay! I will confess all, everything. I robbed my friend Floris. Alas, if it had ended there, for he lost little by me. But I withheld the land from Peter, his son, and from Margaret, Peter's daughter. Yes, I always intended to return it, but I could not, I simply could not."

"Avarice, my son, avarice," Clement repeated gently. "Happy for you, it is not too late."

"No!" Ghysbrecht protested. "I will leave it to her by will. She will not have long to wait; perhaps a month or two at most."

"For a month's possession, thou wouldst damn thy soul forever, thou fool!"

Ghysbrecht groaned, pleading with the friar to be reasonable. Clement, however, remained firm yet gentle, persisting with infinite patience until he loosened the dying man's grip on property that did not belong to him. There were moments when Clement's patience was severely tested, standing on the verge of drawing forth the deed he carried specifically for this purpose. But having weathered yesterday's emotional outbreak, he was now on guard against his own anger. Furthermore, he had conquered a deep-seated personal revulsion toward the man, strong enough to make his own skin crawl throughout this struggle for the miser's soul. Finally, without uttering a single word regarding the deed, he succeeded in winning the man to make immediate and full restitution.

How exactly this restitution was effected will be recounted briefly elsewhere, along with certain peculiar effects produced upon Ghysbrecht by the act, and the terms under which Ghysbrecht and Clement eventually parted ways.

I promised to relate two acts of the latter, indicative of his mind. This is the first. The second requires only two words.

As soon as Clement was absolutely certain that Margaret had regained her inheritance and stood as a wealthy woman—

He vanished.
