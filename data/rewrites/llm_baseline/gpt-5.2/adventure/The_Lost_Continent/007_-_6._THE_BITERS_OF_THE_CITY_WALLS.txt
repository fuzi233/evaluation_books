So this, then, was my welcome back in Atlantis’s capital—and my first taste of the new order that had risen while I was away.

I will not pretend to courage I did not feel. My dullness that day was chosen, not forced; a stiffness put on like a cloak. In the elder time, duty had been simple: to serve the king was to serve the Clan of the Priests, for the king had been their chosen vessel, and their head. But Phorenice had forged herself into power as a smith forges a blade—by heat, hammer, and her own will. If Zaemon spoke truth, he was the Priests’ mouth, and the Priests stood at open defiance of her. And where, in that split, was an honest man to plant his foot without slipping into treason against someone?

Cold reason, at least, offered one narrow plank: a government exists for the country’s good. I told myself I would choose by that measure. I would learn which hand—Phorenice’s, Zaemon’s, or any other—promised Atlantis the sounder harvest, the steadier hearth, the quieter nights. And at once a third thread showed itself in the tangle: talk of rebels, of another faction strong enough to ring the capital with iron and hunger. Before I bound myself to any banner, I would weigh theirs also.

So on that very night—the same day my foot had touched the old shore again—I went out to gather my own tidings.

Within the royal pyramid no one forbade me. The banquet had ended like a feast struck by lightning. With Tarca twisting on the floor and showing those frightful leprous scars as if they were proof of prophecy burned into flesh, even the most ravenous court-bellies had found their appetites fled. The smell of roasted meats and warmed wine had turned sour in the nostrils, as though the very spices had taken offense. Phorenice sat a while longer, glowering over the tables in silence, her anger thick enough to taste. At last her eyes came to me, and something in them softened, as a fire softens when a hand closes a damper.

“You may be an honest man, Deucalion,” she said at length, “but you are a monstrous cold one. I wonder when you will thaw?”

She smiled with it—an expression that promised either spring or a trap.

“I think it will be soon,” she added. “But for now I bid you farewell. In the morning we will take this country by the shoulders, and see it in some new order.”

Then she went, Ylga following. Because my rank gave precedence, I left next while the others stood and made their salutes. Yet I stopped beside Tarca first and laid my hand on his unclean flesh. The touch was like laying fingers on damp stone—alive, but wrong with suffering.

“You are an unfortunate man,” I said, “but I can admire a brave soldier. If relief can be gained for your plague, I will use interest to procure it for you.”

His thanks came mangled and low from his ruined mouth. Some of those nearest shuddered theatrically, as if the gesture of pity might soil them. I turned on them.

“Your charity, my lords, seems of as small account as your courage. You made a fine show of disbelieving Zaemon and sneering at his priesthood. But when it comes to laying a hand on him, you discover a discretion which, in the old days, we should have named with an ugly word. I had rather be Tarca, with all his uncleanness, than any of you as you stand.”

They gave me my due salutation at last, stiff and offended, and I walked from the hall without another glance.

At the grand gate of the pyramid I called for the officer of the guard and demanded exit. He bowed to the ground readily enough, but began with a careful reluctance.

“My lord’s attendants have not yet come up?”

“I have none.”

“My lord knows the state of the streets?”

“I did twenty years back. I shall be able to pick my way.”

“My lord must remember the city is beleaguered,” he persisted. “The people are hungry. They prowl in bands after nightfall, and—I make no question my lord would conquer in a fight against whatever odds, but—”

“Quite right,” I cut him off. “I covet no street scuffle to-night. Lend me, I pray you, a sufficiency of men. You will know best what are needed. For me, I am accustomed to a city with quiet streets.”

So they detailed a score of sturdy fellows for my escort. In double file they took station on either hand, and we marched out from the close perfumed air of the pyramid—heavy with incense, warmed wine, and the greasy sweetness of feast-smoke—into the cool moonlight of the city. My intent was to tour the walls and read, by sight and sound, the posture of these rebels beyond.

But the gods chose to teach me something else first.

The city I walked through that night was not the old capital I had known—the patient accretion of ages, where comfort and splendour had grown together like two vines twining the same trellis. Splendour remained, and was vastly increased. Whole wards had been torn away to make space for new palaces, new pyramids of the wealthy. Under the moon they stood like cliffs of dressed stone, their faces clean and proud, their angles sharp as a chisel’s first cut. I could not help admiring the brain and hand that made such works possible; indeed, seeing them silvered by the night, I almost understood why foreign barbarians worship great buildings as gods, believing no mere human could set them up without some hidden giant’s aid.

Yet even in that beauty the cost was advertised, as plainly as smoke tells of fire. From each plot where one of these stately piles now earned moonlight, a hundred families had been driven out to harbour as they might in open air. Every niche held sleepers. Every shadow held watchers—lean, fierce, half-made beasts of men, ready to pour out and rob or kill any wayfarer weaker than themselves. The city had its own night-language now: the hiss of whispered bargains, the pad of bare feet, the distant argument of hunger. Even our escort’s sandals sounded too loud, too rich.

I am no pamperer of the common people. I have always held that if a man is left to hunger and shiver, he will work to win bread and cloth; and if he will not, then let him die, and the State is rid of worthless flesh. But what I saw went past the stern justice of necessity into blind oppression. Starved bodies lay in gutters, bones standing out through skin as if the flesh had been scraped away. Here and there the wind carried the sour odor of sickness and unwashed humanity, and the sharper stench of things dead too long. It was plain that, save for the favored few, the people of this great capital lived under a weight that crushed thought flat as grain under a millstone.

And yet, though I abominated it, I could not at once call it treason. By the ancient law of the land, all the people, great and small, were the servants of the king to be used without question for his purposes. Phorenice stood in the king’s place. So I forced my eyes upward—above misery and squalor—to the stars hung like cold lamps in the purple night, and to the High Gods said to dwell among them. I asked for guidance, if any guidance could pass through the thick noise of a city that no longer slept.

In time the street-windings brought us to the walls. We coursed beside them, answering the sentries’ drumbeats as we passed—short, hard signals that chopped the quiet into pieces. At last we came to the great gate under the charge of the captain of the garrison.

Here there was commotion. Laughter rose into the still air—laughter with teeth in it—and now and again the snarl and roar of a great beast, and now and again the shriek of a hurt man. The sounds were thrown back by stone, multiplied in the hollow spaces of the gateworks, until the whole place seemed like a throat clearing itself of something foul.

This was no scene to be stumbled upon by chance. The entrance gates of our capital had been built in the old strength: four pairs of valves, each a monstrous block of stone two man-heights square and a man-height thick, with the wall doubled to receive them. Between the inner and outer walls lay an open circus, a killing-yard by design—an empty space intended to swallow an enemy. One set of valves opened only when the other was shut, the hidden machinery so linked that a careless moment could not betray the city. To force them without engines would be like pushing down the royal pyramid with bare hands.

My escort sounded the horn that hung from the wall. A warder came to an arrow-slit, and began the ancient formalities—long and tedious even in calm times. To-night the noise from within drowned speech again and again, so that each question had to be repeated, each answer shouted.

At last the rites were satisfied. The warder drew back stone bars and bolts and set ajar one tremendous valve. I and my men crowded into the passage within—the chamber formed by the thickness of the wall between the two doors. Then he pulled the opened valve to behind us and came bowing through the press.

“I have no vail to give you,” I said. “Get on with your duty. Open me the other door.”

“With respect, my lord, it would be better I should first announce my lord’s presence. There is a baiting going forward in the circus, and the tigers are as yet mere savages, and no respecters of persons.”

“The what?”

“The tigers, if my lord will permit them the name. They are baiting a batch of prisoners with two great beasts which the Empress—whose name be adored—has sent to aid us keep the gate. But if my lord will, there are ward rooms leading off this passage, and galleries which run out commanding the circus. From there my lord can see the sport undisturbed.”

The mere lust for killing excites only disgust in me. But I suspected Phorenice’s purpose in this—and I wanted to see her scheme with my own eyes. So I stepped into the warder’s lodge and on into the galleries that commanded the circus through narrow arrow-slits.

The old builders had meant these slits for slaughter: if the outer doors were forced, an enemy could be shot down in the circus without striking back—a death-trap that fed itself. As a place to watch, they served too well.

The circus below lay bright with moonlight, and the air that rose from it was acrid with blood, as if iron had been boiled and spilled. There was no sport in what was being done. It was mere killing, and it turned my stomach. I am no prude. Give a prisoner weapons, set him in a pit with beasts of reasonable strength, and let him fight for his last breath—there is something in that which a brave man may watch without shame. A war prisoner has earned death by the natural law, and often prefers to strike one last honest blow rather than be taken by the headsman’s axe. But this was not battle. This was insult.

To begin with, the beasts were no fair match for single men. Twenty armed might have fled from them. When the warder said tigers, I had supposed the great cats of the woods. But these were cave-tigers—huge striped monsters of such ponderous strength that, in hunger, they will drag down a mammoth if they find it away from its herd.

How they had been taken alive I could not guess. Hunter of beasts though I had been all my days, I approached the slaying of a cave-tiger with stratagem and infinite caution. To trap one living and bring it to a city on a chain lay beyond my boldest plans. Yet there they were, and in them I saw another proof of Phorenice’s genius: her mind did not stop at the customary tools.

Their purpose was plain. While those two cave-tigers ranged loose in the circus, no living thing could cross from one gate to the other. They were a living barrier, a new defense that needed no sleep and feared no arrow. Bronze collars ringed their throats, each collar fastened to a massive chain that ran to the wall, to be paid out or hauled in by windlass in hidden galleries. At ordinary times, they could be tethered one near each end, leaving a passage clear between the doors. The litter of bones and other filth showed how often that arrangement had been used.

But now the chains were slackened—though men stood by each windlass—and the two great brutes prowled, the links clanking and chinking behind them like iron laughter. Eight bodies lay stark on the pavement, dead and uneaten. The tigers nuzzled them, pawed them as one might paw at a toy, but made no beginning to feed. It was killing they wanted, not meat; or they had been taught to wait until the entertainment ended.

Presently a valve of the farther gate swung back an arm’s length, and a prisoner, resisting, was thrust out. He fell on his face. After one look about him, he lay still, eyes to the stone, as if by refusing to move he could refuse the game. The gate clapped shut. The tigers turned their heads. Behind the arrow-slits, men muttered wagers, their voices buzzing with anticipation.

There were niceties of cruelty even in this. The windlasses clanked; the chains were drawn in by a score of links. One tiger crouched, lashed its tail, and launched itself in a thunderous spring. The chain snapped taut; the links sang with strain; the beast’s roar shook the walls. It missed the prone man by a hand’s breadth. The watchers shrieked with delight. The other sprang and missed too, and again the hidden men cheered as if they had witnessed a clever trick at a festival.

The prisoner did not stir. One more cowardly, and he would have run; one more brave, and he might have faced death upright. This man chose the middle course: he let death come, and had doggedness enough to wait for it without flinching.

The tigers, used to the sport, ceased their wild springs. They lay down and began to purr, rolling on their sides, rubbing themselves luxuriously, like house-cats made monstrous. The man lay as he had fallen.

Slowly each brute drew to the end of its chain and began to reach toward the prisoner with an outstretched forepaw. The male could not touch him. The female could just reach with the tip of a claw. Each light stroke raised a red scratch on the man’s bare side. Still he would not move. I thought the keepers must slack out more links or else let the vile play sink into tedium.

But there was worse learning in store.

The male tiger rose and turned, backing against the chain. A yell of applause from behind the slits told that the watchers knew what came next. Stretched to the utmost of its length, the beast kicked sharply with one hind paw.

I heard the crunch of ribs as the pads struck. The prisoner’s body was spurned away as one tosses fruit. It did not fly far. The she-tiger knew the maneuver; she caught him as he bounded, nuzzled him a moment, then tossed him high and sprang after him, leaping to her full splendid height.

The onlookers found it magnificent. Their voices made a hideous music, ringing off the stone like a drumbeat at a slaughter. My gorge rose. The man had been killed without lingering once the tigers reached him—one light blow from such paws would slay the strongest alive—but to see the body toyed with was an insult to our race’s pride.

Yet I had not come to preach. I had come to learn how Atlantis now balanced itself, and what weight Phorenice had set on the scales. So I stood with a still face behind the arrow-slit and watched.

The cave-tigers tired of their play. One and then the other rose and prowled again over the littered pavement, chains scraping and chinking. They did not eat. Killing had swollen in them; they waited for more victims, as if they knew their keepers’ habits better than their own hunger.

Again the windlasses clanked, drawing the beasts away from the doorway. Again a valve of the farther door swung ajar, and another prisoner was thrust out—struggling.

Sickness seized me at once. It was a woman.

It was not that I had never seen women die. A general who has done his fighting has killed women as well as men, and seen women earn their death-blow with fierce battling. But this—this helpless sacrifice, stripped for a crowd’s amusement—was wanton cruelty, and it took all my restraint not to interfere. Still, policy has always demanded that the individual be sacrificed for the greater need. And it was becoming clearer with every breath of that blood-tainted air that Atlantis wanted a minister as much as it wanted mercy. So I watched on, outwardly cold.

But inwardly my heart quickened. Here was no sullen victim. Whether from fear or courage, it was plain she meant to fight, to hurt, to carve something back from fate before she was taken. The watchers sensed it too; their wagers rose into a hum of appreciation.

The prisoners were stripped before being thrust out—cloth has value, even when life is treated as if it has none. So the woman stood bare-limbed in the moonlight, skin pale against the dark stone.

She set her back to the gate she had come from and faced the circus with a glowing eye. Gods—there were times in my younger years when I would have leaped down sword in hand and fought beside her for the sheer delight of such a battle. But now I held myself, because the needs of a kingdom are heavier than the needs of one brave creature. And it should be noted: if I did not jump, no other man there that night had manhood enough to try.

She bent, chose a bone from the litter, and beat off its head against the wall. Then she put the broken end to her teeth and worked it to a sharper point. I saw her teeth gleam in the moonrays as she bit—white, resolute, like a tool used without pity.

The cave-tigers prowled nearer, high as her head as they walked, and yet they seemed to neglect her. That was part of their torment: the quiet padding, the ceaseless circling, the sense of enormous life choosing its moment. I could see her breast heave with the effort of not breaking. Many a victim would have been sick and helpless under such noiseless pressure.

But she was bolder than I had thought. She did not wait for the spring. She attacked first.

As the she-tiger strolled toward her and began to turn, the woman flung herself forward in a sudden leap and struck viciously at the beast’s eye with her sharpened bone. The onlookers roared their approval. The tiger’s eye was not darkened, but the weapon drew blood, a smart flesh wound, and the beast bellowed in surprise and pain, scampering away to make space for a rush.

The woman did not await it.

With a shrill scream she ran at full speed straight across the moonlight toward the shadowed stretch of wall beneath my gallery—toward the very thickness where the arrow-slits lay. Then, throwing every tendon into the spring, she made the most prodigious leap I believe any human ever made, driven by terror and desperate intelligence. In a later day I measured it; and though her run gave her force enough that her feet clung a moment to the roughness of the wall, it remains certain that in that splendid bound her feet must have dangled a man-height and a half above the pavement.

The end of the leap left her with five fingers hooked on the sill of the arrow-slit from which I watched. The sill sloped and the stone was smooth; alone, she would have slid back. I shot out my hand and gripped her wrist. Instantly she hauled herself up until both knees rested on the sill, and her fingers twined around to seize my wrist in return—like a drowning swimmer fastening on a rescuer.

Now you will suppose she poured out thanks and prayers, thinking only of safety. There was nothing of that in her.

Panting savagely, wordless at first, she tightened her grip on the sharpened bone with her free hand and lunged through the arrow-slit. With the hand that held my wrist she dragged me closer, to give her blow more certainty. I was so unprepared, and her fury was so fierce and sudden, that the first thrust near gave me my quietus. I grappled with her as gently as might be, the thickness of the wall between us, and stripped her of the weapon. Then I held her captive, firm but not brutal, until the wildness in her might lessen.

“That was an ungrateful blow,” I said. “But for my hand you’d have slipped and been the sport of a tiger’s paw this minute.”

“Oh, I must kill some one,” she panted, “before I am killed myself.”

“There will be time enough to think upon that some other day,” I answered. “For now you are far enough off meeting further harm.”

“You are lying to me. You will throw me to the beasts as soon as I loose my grip. I know your kind: you will not be robbed of your sport.”

“I will go so far as to prove myself to you,” I said.

I called for the warder. “Bid those tigers be tethered on a shorter chain,” I ordered, “and then go yourself outside into the circus and help this lady delicately to the ground.”

The word was passed. The windlasses answered with their clanking iron song. The great brutes were hauled nearer their posts until they could not reach the center. The warder went out, cautious as a man walking near a fire, and the woman was helped down. I came out into the circus myself and joined her under the moonlight.

But the men who had been watching were not suited by this change. Their laughter died into angry cries. From the farther gate a stone valve opened in haste, and a man strode out, armed and flushed with drink and cruelty.

“By all the Gods!” he shouted. “Who comes between me and my pastime?”
