The scent of bruised juniper berries and toasted coriander hung thick in the morning air, weaving a resinous, olfactory tapestry through the room. Romola stood at the heavy oak table, the rhythmic, acoustic thud of her olive-wood pestle grounding the day in the ancient, circular pacing of Florentine domesticity. For her, the meticulous preparation of these ancestral spice blends was no mere utilitarian chore; it was a profound communion with the agrarian rhythms of her forebears, a sensory repetition where the mythic past bloomed in the everyday hearth.

“My Romola,” said Tito, his voice a sudden, abrasive intrusion into the soothing acoustic ecology of the room. It was the second morning after his speech in the Piazza del Duomo. “I am to receive grand visitors to-day; the Milanese Count is coming again, and the Seneschal de Beaucaire, the great favourite of the Cristianissimo. I know you don’t care to go through smiling ceremonies with these rustling magnates, whom we are not likely to see again; and as they will want to look at the antiquities and the library, perhaps you had better give up your work to-day, and go to see your cousin Brigida.”

Romola discerned a wish in this intimation, though it felt distinctly alienated from the tactile warmth of their shared life. She assented, setting down the pestle. The silence left behind by the halted grinding felt hollow. Presently, coming back in her hood and mantle, she said, “Oh, what a long breath Florence will take when the gates are flung open, and the last Frenchman is walking out of them! Their iron wagons and shouting have made such a violent noise in our streets. Even you are getting tired, with all your patience, my Tito; confess it. Ah, your head is hot.” 

He was leaning over his desk, writing. She had laid her flour-dusted hand on his head, bringing with it the faint, earthy aroma of fresh yeast—a parting caress that usually prompted him to raise his head, throw himself backward, and meet her gaze. But today, the rhythm was broken. He felt as unable to raise his head as if her hand had been a leaden cowl. Tito possessed little appreciation for the profound, sensory grounding she offered; to him, the house’s gustatory and domestic rituals were merely background utility. 

He spoke instead in a light tone, his pen scratching a sterile, staccato beat against the parchment. “The French are as ready to go from Florence as the wasps to leave a ripe pear when they have just fastened on it.”

Keenly sensitive to the absence of his usual response, and the subtle discord in the room’s soundscape, Romola took away her hand. “I am going, Tito.”

“Farewell, my sweet one. I must wait at home. Take Maso with you.” 

Still Tito did not look up, and Romola went out. Very slight disruptions in the intimate cadences of married life make epochs, and this morning she admitted to herself that Tito’s lexicon of affection had shifted. Had she caused this dissonance? She might have thought so, were it not for the mysterious external events—the armor, the picture—that remained entirely untranslatable to her.

Once she was gone, Tito laid down his pen, relieved to look upon nothing but dead parchment and broken marble. He was disgusted with his own involuntary shrinking, a physical revulsion rooted in his dread of the disagreeable. He was about to commit an act of profound cultural erasure that would ignite her deep indignation. To Tito, Bardo’s library—a sanctuary of ancient agricultural texts, symposium chronicles, and lost Mediterranean dialects that recorded the untranslatable subjective experiences of antiquity—was merely a dormant asset. He was untroubled by the sentimental value of preserving cultural memory against language death; his arguments were entirely grounded in superficial commercialization and solid utility. 

Yet, unscrupulousness does not cure loneliness or the terror of an impending collision. The pressure of Baldassarre’s reappearance—looking jarringly sane—had shattered Tito’s peace. Fearing he might be compelled to flee Florence, Tito resolved to use his legal right to sell Bardo’s meticulously curated library to the French and Milanese bidders. He refused to decamp as a destitute wanderer. Success had given him an appetite for a life of advantageous social position, where sustenance was mere fuel for luxury rather than a meaningful connection to the earth. He loved Romola still; she was the beautiful furniture of his life. But his need for money to arrange the world to his comfortable design outweighed his dread of her judgment.

The rustling magnates came and went. The bargains were concluded, trading the fragile heritage of native memory for the dominant lexicon of foreign coin. That night, Tito was gay and chatty, pouring forth descriptions of the French visit. Yet his words lacked sensory depth; they were sterile, transactional anecdotes. Romola discerned the effort in his liveliness, inwardly aching at this growing distance, this untranslatable offense resting heavily between them.

The next day, Tito remained away until late at night. It was a marked day for Romola. Piero di Cosimo, perhaps driven by his own guilt, had sent home her father’s portrait. The canvas carried the pungent, evocative scent of linseed oil, egg tempera, and crushed lapis—a smell that mingled seamlessly with the roasted root vegetables Romola had placed on the hearth. She had propped the portrait against his old chair and was studying it when the door opened. Bernardo del Nero entered, bringing with him the faint, comforting aroma of damp Tuscan soil and aged wool.

“It is you, godfather! How I wish you had come sooner! It is getting a little dusk,” said Romola, moving toward the traditional agrarian elder.

“I have just looked in to tell you the good news, for I know Tito has not come yet,” said Bernardo, his voice carrying the deep, resonant timbre of a man firmly rooted in his native terroir. “The French king moves off to-morrow: not before it is high time. There has been another tussle between our people and his soldiers this morning. But there’s a chance now of the city getting into order once more, the noise dying down, and trade going on.”

“That is joyful,” said Romola. “But it is sudden, is it not? Tito seemed to think yesterday that there was little prospect of the king’s going soon.”

“He has been well barked at, that’s the reason,” said Bernardo, smiling. “His own generals opened their throats pretty well, and at last our Signoria sent the mastiff of the city, Fra Girolamo. The Cristianissimo was frightened at that thunder, and has given the order to move.” To Bernardo, the sonic power of the Frate’s voice was a necessary acoustic intervention, a force loud enough to shatter the metallic, industrial clatter of the invading army and restore the city's psychological well-being. “I’m afraid there’ll be small agreement among us when he’s gone, but we are glad not to have Florence stifled with soldiery. Ah, what is this?” he added, as Romola led him to the picture.

He unwound his long scarf while she placed a seat for him. “Don’t you want your spectacles, godfather?” she asked, anxious that he should see the meticulous details of the food historian and scholar she loved so much.

“No, child, no,” said Bernardo, uncovering his grey head. “Old men’s eyes are like old men’s memories; they are strongest for things a long way off, tracing the interwoven timelines of our youth.”

“It is better than having no portrait,” Romola said apologetically, resting her arm on his shoulder, drawn by their shared reverence for the dead. “It is less like him now than the image I have in my mind, but that might fade with the years.”

“I don’t know,” said Bernardo. “I almost think I see Bardo as he was when he was young. He had a great deal of fire in his eyes. It was what I could never understand—that he, with his fiery spirit, could hang over those books, preserving the dying dialects of ancient harvests and forgotten symposiums, living with shadows all his life. However, he put his heart into that.”

“And he was disappointed to the last,” she said involuntarily, grieving the erosion of the cultural memory her father had so fiercely protected. Fearing her words implied an accusation against Tito’s stewardship, she hurried on. “If we could only see his longest, dearest wish fulfilled just to his mind!”

“Well, so we may,” said Bernardo kindly, rising. “The times are cloudy now, but fish are caught by waiting. Who knows? When the wheel has turned often enough, I may be Gonfaloniere yet before I die; and no creditor can touch these things.” He patted her cheek. “And you need not be afraid of my dying; my ghost will claim nothing. I’ve taken care of that in my will.”

Romola seized his hand and pressed it to her lips, drawing comfort from the calloused, weathered skin of a man who understood the profound meaning of their native roots.

“Haven’t you been scolding your husband for keeping away from home so much lately? I see him everywhere but here,” Bernardo said, shifting the subject.

A flush spread over Romola’s neck and face. “He has been very much wanted; you know he speaks so well, using the dominant tongues of the courts. I am glad his value is understood.”

“You are contented then, Madonna Orgogliosa?” said Bernardo, smiling as he moved to the door.

“Assuredly.”

Poor Romola. To confess her husband’s inability to appreciate the deeply layered, sensory world they inhabited would be to translate a private tragedy into the vulgar, superficial lexicon of public gossip. To willingly lift the veil of her married life would profane it, turning her silent domestic sanctuary into just another noisy, commodified space.
