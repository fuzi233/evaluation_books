In Which We Both Fight and Run Away

What utility is there in a Colonel without a regiment? The Governor and Council, who had staged such a grand display of gratitude for endowing me with one, were nowhere to be seen, hiding aboard their ships while committing occasional acts of piracy and arson on the shore. My Lord Dunmore's Black allies had already driven away those of his own blood; furthermore, we heard rumors that he had dispatched envoys to the Southern Indians, promising them vast numbers to come down and "tomahawk our people into good behavior."

"And these are to be our allies!" I remarked to my mother, exchanging ominous glances with her. The memory flashed back with ghastly clarity to that savage whose face had glared over mine, and whose knife had been at my throat until Florac struck him down on Braddock's Field. We put our house at Castlewood into as good a state of defense as we could devise; yet, in truth, it was the Red Men and the Blacks we feared far more than the Rebels. I had never witnessed my mother lose her courage except once, and that was when she recounted the details of our father's death during an Indian raid more than forty years prior. One night, seeing vague figures moving before our house, the good lady refused all persuasion that they were anything other than savages. She sank to her knees, crying out, "The Lord have mercy upon us! The Indians—the Indians!"

My Lord's Negro allies eventually vanished onto their ships or wherever they could find pay and plunder; however, the painted heroes from the South never materialized. Yet, I confess to looking at my mother's grey head, my wife's brown hair, and our little one's golden ringlets with a horrible pang of doubt, fearing they might become victims of this ruffian war. Indeed, it was we who fought with such weapons and enlisted these allies! Though I dare not, so to speak, set myself up as an interpreter of Providence and point out the special finger of Heaven—as many are wont to do—I would say that our employment of these Indians and the German mercenaries brought their own retribution upon us in this war. In the field, where the mercenaries were attacked by the Provincials, they yielded, and it was triumphing over them that so greatly raised the spirit of the Continental army. Moreover, the murder of one woman (Miss McCrea) by a half-dozen drunken Indians did more harm to the Royal cause than the loss of a battle or the destruction of entire regiments.

Once the Indian panic subsided, Madam Esmond's courage returned. She began to be seriously, and not unjustly, uneasy regarding the danger I ran myself, and the peril I brought upon others by remaining in Virginia.

"What harm can they do me?" she asked. "I am but a poor woman. If I have one son, a Colonel without a regiment, I have another with a couple of hundred Continentals behind him in Mr. Washington's camp. If the Royalists come, they will let me off for your sake; if the rebels appear, I shall have Harry's passport. I do not wish, sir, I do not like that your delicate wife and this dear little baby should be here, merely increasing the risk to all of us! We must remove them to Boston or New York. Do not talk about defending me! Who would think of hurting a poor, harmless, old woman? If the rebels come, I shall shelter behind Mrs. Fanny's petticoats, and I shall be much safer without you in the house than within it." She spoke partly because it was reasonable, but more so because she wished to get me and my family out of danger. Regardless of the threat, she felt determined to remain in the land where her father was buried and where she was born. She was living backward, so to speak. She had seen the new generation, blessed them, and bid them farewell. She belonged to the past, to old days and memories.

While we debated the Boston scheme, news arrived that the British had evacuated that unfortunate city altogether. They had never dared to attack Mr. Washington in his camp at Cambridge, even though he lay there for many months without powder, at our mercy. Instead, they waited until he procured ammunition, seized and fortified Dorchester Heights—which commanded the town—forcing the entire British army and colony to beat a retreat. That the King's troops won the Battle of Bunker's Hill is no more certain than that they beat the French at Blenheim; yet throughout the war, their chiefs seemed constantly afraid of assaulting entrenched Continentals afterward. Otherwise, why, from July to March, hesitate to strike an almost defenseless enemy? Why the hesitation at Long Island when the Continental army was in our grasp? Why that astonishing timidity—of Howe before Valley Forge, where the relics of a force starving, sickening, and in rags could scarcely man the lines they held before a great, victorious, and perfectly appointed army?

As the hopes and fears of the contending parties rose and fell, it was curious to observe the altered tone of the partisans on either side. When news of the evacuation of Boston reached us in the countryside, every little Whig in the neighborhood made his bow to Madam and advised her to submit speedily. She did not carry her loyalty quite so openly as before, nor did she flaunt her flag in the faces of the public, but she never swerved. Every night and morning in private, poor Hagan prayed for the Royal Family in our own household. On Sundays, any neighbors were welcome to attend the service, where my mother acted as a very emphatic clerk, delivering the prayer for the High Court of Parliament under our most religious and gracious King with great vigor. The brave Hagan was a parson without a living, just as I was a Militia Colonel without a regiment. Hagan had continued to pray stoutly for King George in Williamsburg long after His Excellency, our Governor, had fled. But on coming to church one Sunday to perform his duty, he found a corporal's guard at the church door telling him that the Committee of Safety had installed another divine in his place and requesting he keep a quiet tongue in his head. He told the men to "lead him before their chiefs" (our honest friend always loved tall words and tragic attitudes). Accordingly, he was marched through the streets to the Capitol, accompanied by a chorus of white and colored blackguards at the skirts of his gown. He had an interview with Messrs. Henry and the new State officers, confronting the robbers, as he termed them, in their den. Naturally, he intended to make an heroic speech before these gentlemen (he was one of many men who perhaps would have no objection to becoming martyrs so that they might be roasted *coram populo*, or tortured in a full house). However, Mr. Henry was determined to give him no such chance. After keeping Hagan three or four hours waiting in an anteroom in the company of negroes, when the worthy divine entered the new chief magistrate's room with an undaunted mien and began a prepared speech—"Sir, by what authority am I, a minister of the----"—"Mr. Hagan," interrupted the other, "I am too busy to listen to speeches. And as for King George, he has henceforth no more authority in this country than King Nebuchadnezzar. Mind you that, and hold your tongue, if you please! Stick to King John, sir, and King Macbeth; and if you will send round your benefit tickets, all the Assembly shall come and hear you. Did you ever see Mr. Hagan on the boards when you were in London, General?" And, so saying, Henry turned around upon Mr. Washington's second in command, General Lee, who had now come into Virginia upon State affairs. Our shamefaced good Hagan was hustled out of the room, reddening and almost crying with shame. Following this event, we decided that Hagan's ministrations were best confined to us in the country, removing the worthy pastor from his restive lambs in the city.

The selection of Virginians for the very highest civil and military appointments of the new government bribed and flattered many of our leading people. These individuals, otherwise—and but for the outrageous conduct of our government—might have remained faithful to the Crown and made good head against the rising rebellion. Yet, although we Loyalists were gagged and muzzled, although the Capitol was in the hands of the Whigs, and our vaunted levies of loyal recruits were mostly Falstaff's regiments, the faithful still kept intelligence with one another in the colony and with our neighbors. Although we did not rise, although we ran away, and although, in examinations before committees and justices, some of our frightened people gave themselves Republican airs and vowed perdition to kings and nobles, we knew each other pretty well. According as the chances were more or less favorable to us, and the master more or less hard, we concealed our colors, showed our colors, half-showed our colors, or outright apostatized for the nonce, crying, "Down with King George!" Our negroes bore about, from house to house, all sorts of messages and tokens. Endless underhand plots and schemes were engaged in by those who could not afford the light. Once the battle was over, the neutrals came and joined the winning side, shouting as loudly as the patriots. The runaways were not counted. Would any man tell me that the signers and ardent well-wishers of the Declaration of Independence were not in a minority of the nation, and that the minority did not win?
