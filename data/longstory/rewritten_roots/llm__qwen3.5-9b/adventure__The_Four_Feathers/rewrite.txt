Lieutenant Sutch was the first guest to arrive at Broad Place, reaching the estate around five o'clock on a sun-drenched afternoon in mid-June. The old red-brick mansion, perched on a southern slope of the Surrey hills, glowed warmly against the dark, pine-clad depths of the surrounding forest, resembling a rare jewel warmed by the sun.

Sutch limped across the hall, where the portraits of the Feversham lineage ascended one above another toward the ceiling, and stepped out onto the stone-flagged terrace at the rear. There he found his host sitting upright as a boy, gazing southward toward the Sussex Downs.

"How's the leg?" General Feversham asked, rising briskly from his chair. He was a small, wiry man; despite his white hair, he possessed an alertness that was purely physical. His face was bony, framed by a high, narrow forehead and steel-blue, inexpressive eyes that suggested a barrenness of mind.

"It gave me trouble during the winter," Sutch replied. "But that was to be expected."

Feversham nodded, and for a moment, both men fell silent. From the terrace, the land sloped steeply down to a vast plain of brown earth and emerald fields, dotted with dark clusters of trees. Through the sunlight, voices drifted up—small but distinct. In the distance, near Horsham, a coil of white smoke from a passing train snaked rapidly among the trees, while on the horizon, the Downs rose, patched with white chalk.

"I thought I should find you here," Sutch said.

"It was my wife's favorite corner," Feversham answered in a voice devoid of emotion. "She would sit here by the hour. She had a peculiar fondness for wide, empty spaces."

"Yes," Sutch agreed. "She had imagination. Her thoughts could populate them."

For many years, Sutch had been puzzled by the qualities in the General that had attracted Muriel Graham—a woman renowned equally for the refinement of her intellect and the beauty of her person—and he could never decipher the mystery. He had to settle for knowing that for some inexplicable reason, she had married a man so much older than herself and so diametrically opposed to her in character. Sutch retreated into memory, sitting in his garden chair, recalling a season in London following his return from the China station, before he had participated as an officer of the Naval Brigade in that disastrous charge on the Redan. He was genuinely curious to observe Harry Feversham. He did not admit that this curiosity stemmed from more than the natural fascination of a man disabled in his youth who had made the study of human nature his hobby. He simply wanted to know if the boy resembled his mother or his father—that was all.

Thirteen years had passed, yet the month remained June. Harry Feversham's health had recovered, though in a quieter manner and surrounded by fewer people than before. His circle was confined to a single room situated high within a sprawling, irregular block of buildings that loomed over Westminster like a grim fortress. To a stranger strolling southward across the suspension bridge in St. James's Park under the cover of night, the sudden sight of tier upon tier of illuminated windows rising to such dizzying heights might evoke the fantasy of a mountain rising in the heart of London, teeming with industrious gnomes.

Harry occupied a flat on the tenth floor of this edifice, secured during his annual furlough from his regiment in India. It was within the dining room of this apartment that a simple ceremony unfolded. The space was furnished in a dark, restful style, and despite the chill air suggesting winter, a cheerful fire crackled warmly in the hearth. A large bay window, left uncovered by blinds, offered a commanding view of the city below. Around the dinner table sat four men, all smoking.

Harry Feversham appeared largely unchanged, save for a fair moustache that contrasted sharply with his dark hair—a natural result of maturity. Now a man of medium height, long-limbed, and well-built like an athlete, his features retained the same intensity they had possessed thirteen years prior, when Lieutenant Sutch had scrutinized them so closely.

Two of his companions were fellow officers on leave in England, whom Harry had met earlier that day at his club. First was Captain Trench, a diminutive figure nearing baldness, possessing a small, sharp, and resourceful face paired with black eyes of remarkable vitality. Beside him sat Lieutenant Willoughby, an officer of a distinctly different caliber. With a round forehead, a thick snub nose, and a pair of vacant, protruding eyes, Willoughby projected an aura of invincible stupidity. He spoke rarely, and when he did, his remarks often drifted toward points long forgotten, which he seemed to laboriously reconstruct in his mind. He constantly twisted a moustache whose ends curled upward toward his eyes with ridiculous ferocity. He was a man easily dismissed as insignificant until one paused to reconsider; for he was born as stubborn as he was foolish. If his stupidity caused harm, his obstinacy prevented him from acknowledging it. Unpersuadable and deaf to reason, he clung tenaciously to his few ideas, content to turn them over in his mind until they felt satisfactory.

The fourth guest at the table was Durrance, a lieutenant of the East Surrey Regiment and Feversham's close friend, who had arrived specifically in response to a telegram.

It was June 1882. While civilians watched Egypt with anxiety, soldiers viewed the situation with eager anticipation. Arabi Pasha, defying threats, was steadily reinforcing the fortifications of Alexandria, while further south, a greater danger swelled ominously like a gathering thunderstorm. Just a year prior, a slender, tall youth from Dongola named Mohammed Ahmed had marched through the villages of the White Nile, preaching with the fervor of a revivalist preacher the arrival of a Savior. The passionate victims of Turkish taxation had listened, hearing the promise whispered by the wind through withered grass and finding holy names imprinted even upon the eggs they collected. By 1882, Mohammed had proclaimed himself that Savior and secured his initial victories against the Turks.

"There will be trouble," remarked Trench, and this became the central theme for three of the four men. Yet, in a rare lull, the fourth—Harry Feversham—spoke of a different matter entirely.

"I am very glad you could all join me tonight," Feversham explained to Durrance. "I also sent a telegram to Castleton, one of our own officers, but he is dining with a senior official at the War Office and departs for Scotland shortly, so he couldn't make it. However, I have news of a sort."

The three men leaned forward, their minds still preoccupied with the looming conflict in the East. But Feversham's news bore no relation to the prospect of war.

"I only returned to London this morning from Dublin," he admitted, with a hint of embarrassment. "I have been staying there for several weeks."

Durrance lifted his gaze from the tablecloth to look steadily at his friend. "Yes?" he asked.

"I have come back engaged to be married."

Durrance raised his glass to his lips. "Well, here's luck to you, Harry," he said, and that was the extent of his congratulation. The sentiment was delivered curtly, yet to Feversham's ears, it contained everything necessary. The bond between these two men was not one defined by effusive affection; neither required such displays. They were acutely aware of the depth of their connection, valuing it as a sturdy tool forged for a lifetime of difficult use. It was a friendship never spoken of aloud, yet both were deeply grateful for it as a rare and undeserved gift. They knew, however, that such loyalty carried the potential for heavy obligations. Should sacrifice become necessary, it would be made silently, without mention. Perhaps it was precisely this understanding of their mutual strength that compelled them to such reticence.

"Thank you, Jack!" Feversham replied. "I appreciate your good wishes. It was you who introduced me to Ethne; I shall never forget it."

Durrance set his glass down without haste. A moment of silence ensued, during which he stared fixedly at the tablecloth, his hands resting on the edge of the table.

"Yes," he said in a level tone. "I did you a good turn then." He seemed poised to elaborate, uncertain of how to phrase it, but Captain Trench's sharp, pragmatic voice intervened before he could proceed.

"Will this make any difference?" Trench asked.

Feversham replaced his cigar between his lips. "You mean, shall I resign my commission?" he queried slowly.

"I don't know," Durrance interjected, seizing the opportunity to rise and walk to the window, turning his back to his companions.

Feversham interpreted this abrupt movement as a rebuke and addressed Durrance's back rather than Trench. "I don't know," he repeated. "It requires thought. There are many factors. On one side, naturally, stands my father and my career, however modest it may be. On the other, there is her father, Dermod Eustace."

"He wants you to quit your commission?" Willoughby asked.

"He certainly suspects the Irishman's aversion to constituted authority," Trench laughed. "But does he require you to share his views, Feversham?"

"It is not merely that." Still addressing Durrance's back, Feversham continued his explanation. "Dermod is elderly, his estates are crumbling, and there are other complications. You know, Jack?"

This was a direct appeal, yet Durrance responded absent-mindedly: "Yes, I know," adding, as if quoting a familiar maxim, "If you need any whiskey, tap twice on the floor with your foot. The servants understand."

"Precisely," Feversham acknowledged. He weighed his words carefully, maintaining his gaze over his companions' shoulders toward his friend. "Furthermore, there is Ethne herself. Dermod chose a fitting name for her. She belongs to her country, and more specifically, to her county. She carries the love of it in her bones. I do not believe she could find happiness in India, nor anywhere outside the reach of Donegal—the scent of its peat, its streams, and the warm friendliness of its hills. That must be considered."

He awaited a response, receiving none, and pressed on. Durrance, however, harbored no intention of reproach. He sensed that Feversham was speaking and wished earnestly for him to continue, yet he paid no attention to the words being uttered. Standing rigidly before the window, he faced the glare of Pall Mall shooting upward into the sky, watching the banks of lights ascend as the city stretched northward, accompanied by the phantom rumble of a million carriages. Far below, St. James's Park lay silent and black, a quiet pool of darkness amidst the glitter and noise. Durrance desperately wanted to retreat into the privacy of that park, but he dared not move without drawing attention. So, he kept his back turned, pressing his forehead against the cool glass, hoping his friend would keep talking. He was confronting a sacrifice that must remain unspoken, betrayed by no sign.

Feversham continued, and while Durrance ignored him, Captain Trench offered his closest attention. It became evident that Harry was presenting serious considerations, not mere excuses. Eventually, Trench was satisfied.

"Well, I drink to you, Feversham," Trench declared, "with all due sentiment."

"I too, old man," Willoughby said, dutifully following his superior's lead.

They toasted their comrade, and as their empty glasses clattered on the table, a knock echoed at the door. The two officers looked up. Durrance turned from the window. Feversham called, "Come in," and his servant entered bearing a telegram.

Feversham tore open the envelope casually, scanned the contents with equal nonchalance, and then sat perfectly still, his eyes fixed on the pink slip of paper, his face transforming instantly into extreme gravity. He remained thus for a considerable time, less stunned than profoundly thoughtful. Absolute silence filled the room. Feversham's three guests averted their eyes.

Durrance walked alone to his lodgings, his mind drifting back nearly two years to a peculiar whim of old Dermod Eustace. That day had brought him, unwillingly, to the house by the Lennon River in Donegal, where he unexpectedly met Dermod's daughter, Ethne. She defied every expectation those who first spoke with her father might hold. Durrance had spent a night there, and throughout that evening, she had played her violin. Seated with her back to her audience, as was her custom, she ensured that no glance or gesture could break the concentration of her thoughts.

The melodies she produced still rang in his ears. The girl possessed a rare gift; the strings of her violin seemed to answer the questions of her bow. Among these sounds was a particular overture—the *Melusine*—which carried the very sob of the waves. Durrance had listened in wonder, for the instrument spoke to him of countless things the player herself could not know: of long, perilous journeys and the faces of strange lands; of the silver path across moonlit seas; of beckoning voices rising from the desert's edge. Its tone grew deeper, more mysterious, telling tales of immense, unattainable joy and profound, eternal grief, elevated by a noble grandeur. It whispered of longings beyond words, yet contained not a single note of mere complaint.

That night, sitting in silence while Ethne's face remained turned away, Durrance had drawn a lesson from her playing that he struggled to keep clear in his mind: *True music does not complain.* Consequently, the following morning, as he rode into the Row, his blue eyes gazed upon the world from his bronzed face with no less of his customary friendliness.

He waited at half-past nine by the cluster of lilacs and laburnums at the end of the sand. Yet Harry Feversham did not appear that morning, nor for the next three weeks. Since graduating from Oxford, it had been their custom to meet here at this specific hour whenever both happened to be in town. Durrance felt a growing puzzle, as if he had somehow lost his friend entirely.

Meanwhile, rumors of war swelled into certainty. Finally, when Feversham kept the tryst, Durrance received news. "I told you luck might look my way. Well, she has. I'm going out to Egypt on General Graham's staff. There's talk we may run down the Red Sea to Suakin afterward."

The exhilaration in his voice brought an unmistakable envy to Feversham's eyes. It struck Durrance as strange—even at that peak of his own fortune—that Harry should envy him, though it was strangely pleasant. Interpreting the emotion through his own ambitions, Durrance said sympathetically, "It is rough on you that your regiment has to stay behind."

Feversham rode beside him in silence. As they approached the shaded benches beneath the trees, he spoke: "That was expected. The day you dined with me, I sent in my papers."

"That night?" Durrance asked, turning in his saddle. "After we had gone?"

"Yes," Feversham admitted, correcting himself. He wondered if it had been intentional, but Durrance rode on silently. Once again, Harry sensed a reproach in his friend's quietude, and once again, he was mistaken. Suddenly, Durrance broke the silence with hearty laughter.

"I remember. You gave us your reasons that night. But for the life of me, I can't help wishing we were going out together. When do you leave for Ireland?"

"To-night."

The morning air was still fresh; the lime and chestnut trees retained their early green, and since May was late that year, their blossoms still hung delicately white like snow against the dark rhododendrons. The park shimmered in a haze of sunlight, and the distant roar of the city sounded like tumbling river water.

"It is a long time since we bathed in Sandford Lasher," Durrance remarked.

"Or froze in the Easter vacations in the big snow-gully on Great End," Feversham replied.

Both men felt that on this morning, a volume in their book of life had closed. Having enjoyed reading it, they did not know if future chapters would match its promise, so they looked backward through the leaves before putting the book away forever.

"You must stay with us, Jack, when you come back," Feversham said.

Durrance had trained himself not to flinch, and he did not, even at the anticipatory use of "us." If his left hand tightened on the reins, the gesture escaped his friend's notice.

"If I come back," Durrance said. "You know my creed. I could never pity a man who dies on active service. I would very much like to come by that end myself."

It was a simple creed, fitting the simplicity of the man who spoke it. It boiled down to this: dying decently was worth far more than many years of living. He uttered it without melancholy or foreboding. Still, fearing his friend might misinterpret his words, he glanced quickly at Feversham's face. He saw only that same puzzling look of envy.

"You see, there are worse things that can happen," Durrance continued. "Disablement, for instance. Clever men might manage to live with it. But what in the world should I do if I had to sit in a chair all my days? It makes me shiver to think of it," he added, shaking his broad shoulders to shake off the fear.

"Well, this is the last ride. Let us gallop."

He released his horse, and Feversham followed suit. Side by side, they raced down the sand. At the bottom of the Row, they stopped, shook hands, and parted with the kindest of nods. Feversham rode out of the park; Durrance turned back, walking his horse toward the shaded seats.

Even as a boy at his home in Southpool, Devonshire, near a wooded creek of the Salcombe estuary, Durrance had felt a restless urge to sail down that creek and out over the open sea, dreaming of queer, outlandish countries and peoples beyond the dark, familiar woods. This restlessness had grown with him, so that "Guessens"—even after inheriting its farms and lands—remained in his thoughts merely as a place to return to, not an estate to inhabit his life.

Now, he deliberately exaggerated this restlessness, setting it against the truth of Feversham's words. *Ethne Eustace would hardly be happy outside her county of Donegal,* he reasoned. *Therefore, even had things fallen out differently, there might have been a clash. Harry Feversham arrived in Dublin late that night, fresh from his ride with Durrance in the city. By the following noon, he had crossed Lough Swilly aboard a small cargo steamer that ventured weekly up the Lennon River to Ramelton. Waiting for him on the quay was Ethne, seated in her dog-cart. She extended a hand and offered a smile—not merely the polite greeting of a hostess, but the warm, steady gaze of a comrade.

"You are surprised to see me," she observed, reading the astonishment in his eyes.

"I am always surprised," he replied honestly. "Because you always surpass my expectations of you." As he spoke, the quality of her smile shifted; it deepened, transforming from simple camaraderie into something tender and intimate.

"I shall drive slowly," she said once their luggage was secured. "I brought no groom today on purpose. Guests arrive tomorrow; we have only this day together."

She guided the cart along the broad causeway beside the river, then turned up the steep, narrow streets of Ramelton. Feversham sat in silence beside her. It was his first visit to the town, and he watched with a mixture of curiosity and a strange sense of possession—the pride of someone discovering a piece of his own soul. He noted the dense thicket of tall trees climbing the far bank, the weathered grey bridge spanning the water, the rhythmic singing of the current over shallow stones, and the sleepy, dreamlike quiet of the settlement. All of it belonged to Ethne; all of it was woven into her life.

At twenty-one, Ethne was tall, strong, and remarkably supple. Her shoulders possessed a sturdy squareness proportional to her height—lacking the exaggerated sloped elegance favored by older generations, yet possessing a natural grace that compensated fully. In her movement, she was light-footed as a deer. Her hair was a rich dark brown, coiled neatly at the nape of her neck, framing cheeks flushed with a vibrant color. Her eyes were a striking, clear grey, meeting others with an engaging, unguarded frankness. Her character mirrored her appearance: honest, simple, and possessing the straightforward strength that encompasses gentleness while excluding violence.

There was a tale still told in Ramelton regarding her courage—a story that always thrilled Feversham. Years ago, on that very steep hill leading down to the river, her horse had taken fright at the clatter of a passing pail and bolted. The reins had fallen loose just as disaster loomed. Seated helpless in the cart, with the animal tearing toward the sharp curve of the bridge below, Ethne acted with remarkable coolness. She leaped over the front of the cart as it pitched forward, balanced herself along the shafts, snatched the reins from the horse's neck, and brought the beast to a halt mere yards from the precipice.

However, she also carried the flaws inherent in such powerful qualities, though Feversham was not yet aware of them.

During the early part of the journey, Ethne remained nearly as silent as her companion, her mind seemingly elsewhere. Only when they left the town and hit the straight, rolling road toward Letterkenny did she turn to him abruptly.

"I saw this morning that your regiment has been ordered from India to Egypt," she said. "If I hadn't come across your path, you would have gone with them. There would have been opportunities for distinction. I have hindered you, and I am very sorry. Naturally, you couldn't know there was a chance of transfer, but I can imagine how hard it is to be left behind. I blame myself."

Feversham stared ahead for a moment, stunned. Then, his voice rough with emotion, he replied, "You need not."

"How can I avoid blaming myself?" she pressed, her tone steady and level, stating a fact as simply as breathing. "Suppose you had gone to Egypt and the worst had happened... I would have been lonely, of course, for all my days. But I would have known with absolute certainty that when those days were over, you and I would see much of one another."

Feversham caught his breath as if struck by pain. The girl's eyes remained fixed on his face, yet he sat motionless, unable to find words. He kept his lips sealed, and Ethne continued, unperturbed.

"You see, I can endure the absence of those I care for better than most. I don't feel I've truly lost them," she mused, searching for the right phrasing. "Life moves along in a dull fashion, and then suddenly a face emerges from the crowd of acquaintances. You recognize it instantly as a friend—or rather, you realize it belongs to someone you have long sought and are now glad to recover. Such friends are rare, but they are the ones that count. One does not lose them, whether they are absent or..."

"Unless," Feversham interjected slowly, "one has made a mistake. Suppose the face in the crowd is merely a mask? Mistakes can be made."

Ethne shook her head decisively. "Not of that kind. One may appear to err, perhaps for a long time. But in the end, one is proven not to have erred."

Her implicit faith seized the man, torturing him until silence became impossible.

"Ethne," he cried out, "you don't know—"

But at that precise moment, Ethne reigned in her horse, laughed, and pointed her whip.

They had reached the crest of a hill a couple of miles from Ramelton. To their left, stone walls enclosed open fields; to their right, a wood of oaks and beeches. Built into the left-hand wall was a scarlet letterbox, exactly where Ethne's whip was pointing.

"I wanted to show you that," she interrupted. "It was here I used to post my letters to you during those anxious times."

Feversham let his confession slip away. "The letterbox is very convenient," he admitted.

"Yes," Ethne agreed, driving on until they reached a spot where the park wall had crumbled. "That's where I used to climb over to post them. There's a tree on the other side of the wall just as convenient. I used to run down the half-mile of avenue at night."

"There might have been thieves," Feversham exclaimed.

"There were thorns," Ethne corrected, turning through the gates and driving up to the porch of the long, irregular grey house. "Well, we still have a day before the dance."

"I suppose the whole countryside is coming," Feversham remarked.

"It dare not do anything else," Ethne laughed. "My father would send the police to fetch them if they stayed away, just as he fetched your friend Mr. Durrance. By the way, Mr. Durrance has sent me a gift—a Guarnerius violin."

The door swung open, revealing a thin, lanky old man with a fierce, peaked face resembling a bird of prey. This was Dermod Eustace, the master of Lennon House. A biographer of Dermod Eustace would require a cautious mind. Though the old master of Lennon House had not lain in his grave for twenty years, he had already swollen into legend. Anecdotes had attached themselves to his memory like barnacles, and anyone with a flair for invention could easily fabricate tales that locals would accept as gospel. Yet, there were undeniable facts. He practiced an ancient, tyrannical hospitality, keeping his doors open on the road to Letterkenny and forcing bed and board upon strangers, as Durrance had once discovered. He was a man of another century, glaring angrily at a topsy-turvy world, finding reconciliation only after heavy drinking. He was somewhat of an intoxicated Coriolanus, believing the people should be shepherded with a stick, yet retaining surprising manners even toward the lowest women.

The townspeople of Ramelton boasted with pride that even at his worst—galloping down the steep cobblestones on a seventeen-hand white mare, accompanied by his inseparable collie, swaying drunkenly yet miraculously maintaining his saddle—he had never ridden down anyone except a man. Two further points defined him: he was rather afraid of his daughter, who wisely kept him guessing whether she was displeased, and he had developed a great fondness for Harry Feversham.

Harry saw little of Dermod that day. The old man retired to his "office," leaving Feversham and Ethne to spend the afternoon fishing for salmon in the Lennon River. It was an afternoon as restful as a Sabbath; the birds were still. Her carriage radiated a royal grace that marked her instantly. Feversham watched her with a despairing wonder, marveling at the chance he had once had to own her.

Only once did her endurance crack, and then only for a fleeting second. While dancing with Feversham, her gaze drifted to the windows where the dawn was breaking—a pale, cold light seeping through the blinds.

Feversham suddenly felt her entire weight press against his arms. Her complexion drained, turning grey and exhausted. Her eyes squeezed shut tight before forcing themselves open again. He feared she would faint.

He stepped aside and stood facing her until her strength returned. "Thank you," she said gratefully, and the spinning wheel of the dance caught them once more.

It was strange that he should be urging her to find courage while she thanked him for support, yet neither noticed the ironic reversal of their positions. Ethne was too drained by the strain of those final hours. Feversham, having witnessed the depth of her suffering in the pallor of her face and the agony in her eyes, realized fully how deeply he had wounded her. He no longer spoke of losing her; he ceased to think of his own loss entirely. Instead, he clung to her words: *"I wonder if it is right that one should suffer so much pain."*

Those words echoed in his mind, resonating with the unique timbre of her voice. He was certain he would hear them echoing above the noise of the world when he died, serving as his eternal condemnation. Because, he knew, it simply was not right.

The ball ended soon after. Carriages departed, and the guests retired to the smoking room or their bedrooms. Feversham, however, lingered in the hall beside Ethne. She understood his purpose.

"There is no need," she said, turning her back to him to light a candle. "I have told my father. I told him everything."

Feversham bowed his head in silent agreement. "Still, I must wait and see him," he stated firmly.

Ethne did not object, though she glanced at him sharply, her brow furrowed in perplexity. Waiting for her father under such circumstances required a courage she respected, though it also stirred a flicker of apprehension within her. Twice he opened his mouth to speak, but no sound emerged. Eventually, he turned to the table, lighting his own candle and Feversham's. He turned back, moving quickly enough that Ethne stepped forward instinctively to intervene, but he did nothing more than stare at Feversham again, holding the gaze for a long, uncomfortable time.

Finally, he picked up his candle. "Well—" he started, then stopped. He snuffed the wick with his scissors and tried again. "Well—" and halted once more. His candle offered no inspiration. With that, he turned and walked heavily up the stairs. The old man's forbearance was perhaps the least of Harry Feversham's punishments.

In that sweltering month of May, Durrance cast his gaze upward from Wadi Halfa, his heart turning eagerly toward home. Unbeknownst to him, five hundred miles to the south, separated by the vast expanse of the Nubian Desert and the treacherous Belly of Stones, events of profound consequence were unfolding.

On the abandoned track linking Berber and Suakin, the Wells of Obak lay buried deep among shifting dunes. To the east, a strip of trees marked the boundary between the sands and a hard, stony plain crowned with granite hills; to the west, the desert stretched fifty-eight waterless miles toward Mahobey and Berber on the Nile. It was a landscape of such utter flatness that a mere tuft of grass, knee-high, could appear from a mile away as a tree offering shade for a midday rest, while a pile of stones no larger than one might find beside a road under repair seemed to rise like a formidable hill. This specific May offered no spot more desolate than the Wells of Obak. Towards the end of August, in the year Durrance returned home blind from the Sudan, Trench sat in a corner of the enclosure, watching the sun sink westward toward the plain with an agony of anticipation. However intolerable the heat and burden of the day, it was nothing compared to the horrors each night renewed.

Twilight arrived, bringing with it Idris es Saier, the great Negro of the Gawaamah tribe, and his fellow jailers.

"Into the House of Stone!" he cried.

Praying and cursing, with the sound of pitiless whips falling perpetually upon the backs of the lagging, the prisoners jostled and struggled at the narrow entrance. Already, some thirty captives occupied the interior, lying on the swampy mud floor or propped against the walls in the final extremities of weakness and disease. Two hundred more were driven in at night, penned there until morning. The room was perhaps thirty feet square, with four feet occupied by a solid pillar supporting the roof. There were no windows; a few small apertures near the ceiling offered a mere pretense of ventilation. Into this foul, pestilential hovel the prisoners were packed, screaming and fighting. The door slammed shut, plunging them into utter darkness so thick one could not distinguish the outline of a neighbor's head.

Colonel Trench fought like the rest. There was a corner near the door he coveted with a ferocity he had never felt in his free days. Occupying that spot would grant him shelter from blows, stamping feet, and the bruising shackles of neighbors; it would also provide a support against which to lean his back during the ten interminable hours of suffocation.

That fear haunted him whenever he was driven inside. It acted upon him like a drug inducing madness. For if a man once went down in that yelling, struggling throng, he never rose again—he was trampled into shapelessness. Trench had seen such victims dragged from the prison each morning; and he was a small man. Thus, he fought for his corner in a frenzy like a wild beast, kicking with his fetters, thrusting with his elbows, diving under arms, burrowing between others, tearing at clothes, using his nails and fists, and even striking heads with the chain dangling from the iron ring about his neck. Eventually, he reached the corner, streaming with sweat and gasping for breath. For the rest of the night, he spent it holding that position against all comers.

*"If I were to fall!"* he gasped. *"O God, if I were to fall!"*

He shouted aloud to his neighbor—for in that clamor, nothing less than a shout was audible—*"Is it you, Ibrahim?"* A similar shout answered him, *"Yes, Effendi."*

Trench felt a measure of relief. Between Ibrahim, a great tall Arab of the Hadendowa, and Trench, a friendship born of shared necessity had blossomed. There were no prison rations in Omdurman; each captive depended on his own money or the charity of friends outside. From time to time, money came to Trench from his friends, smuggled secretly into the prison by a native arriving from Assouan or Suakin. But there were long periods when no aid arrived, and he survived on the charity of Greeks who had sworn conversion to the Mahdist faith, or starved with as much patience as he could muster. There were times, too, when Ibrahim had no friend to send him a meal. So, each man helped the other in need. They stood side by side against the wall at night.

*"Yes, Effendi, I am here,"* Ibrahim groaned, groping with his hand in the black darkness to steady Trench against the wall.

A fight of extraordinary violence raged in an extreme corner of the prison. The prisoners were so tightly packed that with every advance of one combatant and retreat of another, the entire jostling crowd swayed in a rhythmic motion from end to end, side to side. They swayed, fighting to keep their feet, fighting even with their teeth. Above the din of hard breathing, the clank of chains, and their imprecations, rose occasionally a wild sobbing cry for mercy or an inhuman shriek, stifled immediately, revealing that a man had fallen beneath the stamping feet. Missiles were thrown across the prison—even dirt gathered from the floor—and since no one knew whence they came, heads were battered against heads in the frantic effort to dodge them. All this occurred in the deepest darkness.

For two hours, Trench stood in that black, noise-filled, rank-smelling prison. Eight more hours awaited before the door would open, allowing him to stumble into the clean air of the zareeba and sleep. Standing on tiptoes to lift his head above his fellows, he could barely breathe; the air was moist and sour. His throat was parched, his tongue swollen and stringy like a dried fig. It seemed to him that the imagination of God could devise no worse hell than the House of Stone on an August night in Omdurman. He thought, *If I were to fall!* and as he spoke, his hell became complete, for the door opened.

Idris es Saier appeared in the threshold.

*"Make room! Make room!"* he cried, throwing fire among the prisoners to drive them from the exit. Tufts of dried grass ignited in the darkness, falling onto the bodies of the captives. So crowded were they that they could not avoid the flames; in places, they could not raise their hands to brush them from their shoulders or heads.

*"Make room!"* cried Idris. The whips of his fellow jailers enforced his command, lashes falling upon everyone within reach, clearing a small space at the door. Into that gap a man was flung, and the door closed again.

Trench stood close to the door. In the dim twilight filtering through the doorway, he caught a glimpse of the new prisoner: a man heavily ironed, slight of figure, and bent with suffering.

*"He will fall,"* Trench muttered. *"He will fall tonight. God! If I were to!"* Suddenly, the crowd swayed against him, and the curses rose louder and shriller than before.

The new prisoner was the cause. Clinging to the door with his face pressed against the panels, through the cracks of which actual air might seep, he was plucked from his vantage by those behind. He lay in the deep shadows of the prison compound, beneath a harsh blue sky, on dry, trampled earth. Around him, the ragged figures of fellow prisoners dragged their heavy chains or lay prostrate in the throes of sickness. The meaning settled over him with chilling certainty.

"I am in the prison at Omdurman," Feversham declared, his voice trembling with disbelief. "Actually *in* the prison! This is Umm Hagar, the House of Stone. It seems too good to be true."

Leaning back against the rough wall, he exhaled with profound relief. To Trench, the tone of satisfaction sounded almost ironic—like the words of a man posing as indifferent to pain and pleasure, someone who claimed such vast experience that joy and sorrow could no longer move him.
