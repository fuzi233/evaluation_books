A grave, white-haired seneschal approached their table, bowing with courteous deference to inquire if Gerard Eliassoen was among their party. Upon receiving confirmation, the official announced, "The Princess Marie wishes to confer with you, young sir; I am to conduct you to her presence."

Instantly, every face within hearing snapped toward the speaker, etched with a mixture of sharp curiosity and covetous envy directed at the man destined to enter a princess's chamber. Gerard rose immediately to obey.

"I wager we shall not see you again," Margaret said calmly, though a faint flush colored her cheeks.

"That you will," he replied, leaning close to whisper, "This is my good princess; but you are my queen." Turning back to the assembly, he added aloud, "Wait for me, I pray you; I shall return presently."

"Ay, ay!" Peter called out simultaneously, waking from his reverie.

With Gerard gone, the pair—dressed in simple, homely garb yet standing beside the man summoned by royalty—became the focal point of neighboring gazes. Observing this shift in attention, William Johnson stepped forward, feigning surprise while claiming kinship.

"And to think that I was at your backs, and you saw me not!"

"Nay, cousin Johnson, I saw you long since," Margaret answered coolly.

"You saw me, and spoke not?"

"Cousin, it was for you to welcome us to Rotterdam, as it is for us to welcome you at Sevenbergen. Your servant denied us a seat in your house."

"The idiot!" someone muttered.

"And I had a mind to test the proverb, 'Like maid, like master,' for there is often truth in bywords." William Johnson flushed a deep purple. Realizing Margaret's keen insight and suspicion, he wisely chose action over argument. He insisted they accompany him home immediately, vowing to demonstrate whether they were truly welcome in Rotterdam.

"Who doubts it, cousin? Who doubts it?" the scholar interjected.

Margaret thanked him graciously but hesitated to depart at once, citing a desire to hear the minstrels perform again. About a quarter-hour later, Johnson renewed his offer, noting that many guests had already departed. Only then did her true motive surface.

"It would be poor manners to our friend; moreover, he risks losing us. He knows not where we lodge in Rotterdam, and the city is vast; we have already parted company once."

"Oh!" exclaimed Johnson. "We shall remedy that. My young man—ahem! I mean my secretary—shall remain seated here to await him and escort him to my residence. He shall lodge with me and no other."

"Cousin, we shall be too burdensome."

"Nay, nay! You shall see whether you are welcome or not—you, your friends, and your friends' friends, if necessary—and I shall hear what the Princess requires of him."

Margaret felt a sudden thrill of joy at the prospect of Gerard lodging under the same roof as herself, followed swiftly by a slight misgiving. "But if your young man proves thoughtless and wanders off, and Gerard misses him?"

"He wander? Leave the spot where I placed him and ignore my command? Ho! Stand forth, Hans Cloterman!"

A figure clad in black serge and dark violet hose rose abruptly, stepping forward without a tremor of muscle. He was a solemn, precise youth, the very embodiment of gravity and starched propriety. At his appearance, Margaret, overwhelmed with happiness, struggled to maintain her composure. Whispering to Johnson, she declared, "I would stake my hand against the fire for him. We are entirely at your command, cousin, once you have issued your orders."

Hans was instructed to take a seat at the table, wait for Gerard, and guide him to Ooster-Waagen Straet. He complied silently, settling into the designated chair with calm efficiency. Margaret, Peter, and William Johnson then withdrew together.

"And indeed, it is time you retired to bed, father, after all your travels," Margaret remarked.

Hans Cloterman remained seated, waiting for Gerard with a demeanor of perfect business-like patience. Minutes ticked by, failing to stir any impatience in the flawless young man. Johnson dismissed the notion of his secretary abandoning his post for sport or youthful frivolity with a laugh of scorn.

As Gerard failed to appear, the patient Hans, no longer under his employer's watchful eye, amused himself by drinking goblets of strong Corsican wine. He consumed them solemnly, silently, and at precisely measured intervals. The wine proved potent, and Cloterman's resolve wavered. An hour after Gerard's departure, the model secretary finally succumbed to the notion that creation demanded he toast all good fellows, specifically naming the Duke of Burgundy present.

Rising gingerly but with exaggerated solemnity, he filled his ninth cup. Standing to his full height, he instantly toppled onto the grass, spilling the cold liquid over several ankles—whose owners scrambled away—but remaining utterly unmoved in his own long face. Even in the total eclipse of reason, his expression retained its gravity, primness, and infallibility.

Meanwhile, the seneschal led Gerard through winding passages to the door of the pavilion. There, young nobles, embroidered and feathered, sat as sentinels guarding the heir-apparent, shuffling cards by the flickering red light of torches held by their servants. A whisper from the seneschal prompted one of them to rise reluctantly, stare at Gerard with haughty surprise, and enter the pavilion. Moments later, he returned, beckoning the pair through further corridors until they landed in an antechamber. Here, three more youths, dressed like living tapestries of fur and embroidery, were deeply engrossed in the educational art of dice.

"You cannot see the Princess; it is too late," one declared.

Another agreed, "She passed this way but moments ago with her nurse. She is gone to bed, doll and all. Deuce—ace again!"

Gerard prepared to withdraw. However, the seneschal, wearing an incredulous smile, replied, "The young man is here by the Countess's orders; please conduct him to her ladies."

On this cue, a superb Adonis rose with an air of injury and led Gerard into a room where eleven ladies sat or lounged, chatting like magpies. Two, more industrious than the rest, played cat's-cradle with fingers as nimble as their tongues. At the sight of a stranger, all chatter ceased as if halted by a single complex mechanism, and every eye turned upon Gerard, as though the same invisible thread that silenced the tongues had guided the gaze.

Gerard, already uneasy, felt doubly disconcerted by this battery of stares and lowered his eyes to the floor. Then, spotting a vulnerability, the cowards giggled, enjoying their newfound prowess, much like hares fleeing ponds or frogs scuttling into water. A duenna intervened severely, "Mesdames!" and the women were instantly abashed, as if a string of modesty had been pulled.

The duenna took charge, marching Gerard before her in solemn silence. His heart sank, and he nearly turned to flee. "What must princes be," he thought, "when their courtiers are so freezing? Doubtless they derive their breeding from him they serve."

These reflections were interrupted when the duenna ushered him into a smaller room where three ladies worked and a pretty little girl tuned a lute. Dressed richly yet without ostentation, the duenza approached the lady hemming a kerchief and spoke softly. That lady turned toward Gerard with a smile, beckoning him closer. She did not rise, but set aside her work; her slight movement was suffused with grace, ease, and courtesy.

Conversation began immediately. "Margaret Van Eyck is an old friend of mine, sir, and I am right glad to receive a letter from her hand. Thank you, sir, for delivering it safely. Marie, my love, this is the gentleman who brought you that charming miniature."

"Sir, I thank you a thousand times," the young lady replied.

"I am glad you feel indebted to her, sweetheart, for our friend requests a small service in return."

"I will do anything on earth for him," the young lady responded with ardor.

"Anything on earth is nothing in the world," the Countess of Charolois stated quietly.

"Well, then, I will... What would you have me do, sir?" Gerard realized he had stumbled into high society.

"My sovereign demoiselle," he said gently, trembling slightly, "where there have been no pains, there needs no reward."

"But we must obey mamma. All the world must obey."

"That is true. Then, our demoiselle, reward me, if you will, by allowing me to hear the stave you were singing before I interrupted you."

"What! You love music, sir?"

"I adore it."

The little princess looked inquiringly at her mother, who smiled in assent. Taking her lute, she sang a *romaunt* of the day. Though only twelve, she was a well-taught and diligent musician. Her small fingers swept the chords with courage and precision, striking out arpeggios clear, distinct, and bright like twinkling stars. Yet, the true charm lay in her voice. It was not powerful, but round, clear, full, and ringing like a bell. She sang with a modest eloquence, lacking the tricks of feeling. Too young to be theatrical or sentimental, nothing was forced; everything flowed naturally.
