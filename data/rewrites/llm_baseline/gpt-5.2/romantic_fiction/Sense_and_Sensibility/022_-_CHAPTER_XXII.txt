Marianne had never been fashioned for indulgence. A rough joke, a coarse turn of phrase, a complacent ignorance parading as wit—any of it set her whole spirit in arms; and now, with her feelings still raw and feverish from disappointments she would not name aloud, she could scarcely endure the Miss Steeles’ very voices. Their laugh, pitched too sharp, seemed to rasp against the quiet grain of Barton like a blunt knife over a board; their footsteps on the path had a brisk, restless tap that broke the countryside’s more honest music—wind in hawthorn, a cart’s distant creak, the small, measured talk of labourers going home. Marianne met them with a chill that needed no speech. Every attempt at intimacy on their side fell, rebuffed, before it could bloom into familiarity.

It was chiefly, Elinor thought, that Marianne’s unvarying coldness drove the sisters toward herself; for soon both showed her a preference, Lucy in particular, who missed no opening to draw Elinor into conversation, to press their acquaintance forward with a confidence that wore the mask of frankness. Lucy Steele was naturally quick: she could seize a point, turn it neatly, and, for half an hour at a time, be truly diverting. Yet her cleverness had never been ripened by any steady cultivation. She spoke with an air of knowing more than she did, and the little slips—the wrong name, the confused fact, the bold opinion built on no foundation—betrayed, in spite of her constant effort to appear at advantage, a mind left hungry. Elinor could pity that neglect; she could not so readily soften toward what she discerned besides—an ease in flattery, an eagerness to insinuate, a habit of shaping herself to each listener, which betrayed a want, not of information, but of delicacy and rectitude. Attention from such a quarter, however neatly offered, had a hollowness to it that made it less a compliment than a trial.

One morning, as they walked back from the Park toward the cottage, the lane lay steeped in late-summer warmth. A cart went by in the distance; its wheels sang a thin, intermittent song over gravel, and the hedges held the low hum of bees. Lucy kept pace at Elinor’s side, her voice pitched to be confidential without being quite a whisper.

“You will think my question a very odd one, I dare say,” said she at last, after watching her companion’s face with a cautious sidelong look; “but pray—are you personally acquainted with your sister-in-law’s mother, Mrs. Ferrars?”

Elinor did think it odd; her countenance owned it as she answered, with measured civility, that she had never seen Mrs. Ferrars.

“Indeed!” Lucy returned, with an air of gentle wonder. “I declare I thought you must have met her at Norland sometimes. Then perhaps you cannot tell me what sort of a woman she is?”

“No,” said Elinor, guarding her real opinion, and not disposed to gratify what looked very like impertinent curiosity. “I know nothing of her.”

Lucy’s eyes lingered on her as if weighing the effect of every syllable. “I am sure you think me very strange for inquiring in such a way,” she said; “but perhaps there may be reasons. I wish I might venture—but however, I hope you will do me the justice of believing I do not mean to be impertinent.”

Elinor answered with calm courtesy; and they walked on, the sound of their steps rising and falling together on the hard path. For a few minutes Lucy was silent. Then, as if her own thoughts would not let her rest, she began again, a little unsteadily, yet with the manner of one resolved to be noticed.

“I cannot bear to have you think me impertinently curious. I would rather do any thing in the world than be thought so by a person whose good opinion is so well worth having as yours. And I am sure I should not have the smallest fear of trusting you. Indeed, I should be very glad of your advice how to manage in such an uncomfortable situation as I am. But there is no occasion to trouble you. I am sorry you do not happen to know Mrs. Ferrars.”

“I am sorry I do not,” Elinor replied, struck into real astonishment, “if it could be of any use to you to know my opinion of her. But really, I never understood that you were at all connected with that family, and therefore I confess I am surprised at so serious an inquiry into her character.”

“I dare say you are,” said Lucy quickly, and she gave a little dutiful laugh, as if to soften what she meant to sharpen. “And I am sure I do not wonder at it. But if I dared tell you all, you would not be so much surprised. Mrs. Ferrars is certainly nothing to me at present—but the time may come—how soon it will come must depend upon herself—when we may be very intimately connected.”

She looked down as she spoke, bashful in her pose, yet stealing a glance up from beneath her lashes to catch the impression she had made.

Elinor’s breath caught. For an instant her mind refused the path Lucy offered it; it turned aside as from something too strange to be true. “Good heavens!” she cried at last. “What do you mean? Are you acquainted with Mr. Robert Ferrars? Can you be?” The thought of such an alliance, of such a sister-in-law, brought no pleasure with it.

“No,” Lucy answered. “Not to Mr. Robert Ferrars—I never saw him in my life; but—” and here she fixed her eyes full upon Elinor’s face, as if she would force belief by steadiness, “to his eldest brother.”

What Elinor felt in that moment was a sudden, violent astonishment—so keen it might have been agony, had not disbelief sprung up at once, like a hand flung out to keep her from falling. She turned toward Lucy in silent amazement, struggling to comprehend not only the meaning, but the motive of such a declaration. Her colour changed; but she stood firm, too practiced in self-command to be betrayed into any outward excess.

“You may well be surprised,” Lucy continued, taking Elinor’s silence for the effect she wished. “For to be sure you could have had no idea of it before; for I dare say he never dropped the smallest hint of it to you or to any of your family. Because it was always meant to be a great secret, and it has been faithfully kept so by me to this hour. Not a soul of all my relations knows of it but Anne; and I never should have mentioned it to you if I had not felt the greatest dependence in the world upon your secrecy. And besides, I really thought my behaviour in asking so many questions about Mrs. Ferrars must seem so odd that it ought to be explained. And I do not think Mr. Ferrars can be displeased when he knows I have trusted you; because I know he has the highest opinion in the world of all your family, and looks upon yourself and the other Miss Dashwoods quite as his own sisters.”

She paused, as if to allow that last phrase to settle and do its work.

Elinor, for a few moments, could not speak. The lane seemed suddenly too narrow; the air, though warm, felt thin. Then she forced herself to steadiness—forced her voice into a calm that would neither implore nor accuse.

“May I ask,” she said, “if your engagement is of long standing?”

“We have been engaged these four years.”

“Four years!”

“Yes.”

The number struck Elinor with a dull, heavy certainty, as if time itself had been used against her; and yet, even while shock ran through her, something in her mind still struggled to question, to doubt, to find an escape from believing. “I did not know,” said she, “that you were even acquainted till the other day.”

“Our acquaintance, however, is of many years’ date. He was under my uncle’s care, you know, a considerable while.”

“Your uncle!”

“Yes; Mr. Pratt. Did you never hear him talk of Mr. Pratt?”

“I think I have,” Elinor answered, with an effort that felt like holding a trembling vessel steady while it brimmed.

“He was four years with my uncle, who lives at Longstaple, near Plymouth. It was there our acquaintance began; for my sister and me was often staying with my uncle, and it was there our engagement was formed—though not till a year after he had quitted as a pupil; but he was almost always with us afterwards. I was very unwilling to enter into it, as you may imagine, without the knowledge and approbation of his mother; but I was too young, and loved him too well, to be so prudent as I ought to have been. Though you do not know him so well as me, Miss Dashwood, you must have seen enough of him to be sensible he is very capable of making a woman sincerely attached to him.”

“Certainly,” Elinor replied—she scarcely knew what she said; and then, as if her very honesty must rally to defend him, she added, with a returning grasp at the idea of his honour and of Lucy’s possible deception, “Engaged to Mr. Edward Ferrars!—I confess myself so totally surprised, that really—I beg your pardon; but surely there must be some mistake of person or name. We cannot mean the same Mr. Ferrars.”

“We can mean no other,” Lucy cried, smiling as if at an absurdity. “Mr. Edward Ferrars, the eldest son of Mrs. Ferrars, of Park Street, and brother of your sister-in-law, Mrs. John Dashwood, is the person I mean. You must allow I am not likely to be deceived as to the name of the man on who all my happiness depends.”

“It is strange,” said Elinor, in a perplexity that was pain itself held on a tight rein, “that I should never have heard him even mention your name.”

“No—considering our situation, it was not strange. Our first care has been to keep the matter secret. You knew nothing of me, or my family, and therefore there could be no occasion for ever mentioning my name to you; and as he was always particularly afraid of his sister’s suspecting any thing, that was reason enough for his not mentioning it.”

Elinor’s heart sank lower; yet her countenance did not sink with it. She repeated, because she could not help it, because the words seemed to measure her misery with arithmetic coldness, “Four years you have been engaged.”

“Yes; and heaven knows how much longer we may have to wait. Poor Edward! It puts him quite out of heart.”

As she spoke she drew from her pocket a small miniature, wrapped and handled with the practiced tenderness of possession. “To prevent the possibility of mistake, be so good as to look at this face. It does not do him justice, to be sure, but yet I think you cannot be deceived as to the person it was drew for. I have had it above these three years.”

The little painting lay in Elinor’s hand. She had seen that face in life with a thousand quiet meanings—seen it in lamplight at Barton, in the softened morning in the parlour, seen it attentive, embarrassed, kind. Here it was reduced to colour and shadow, yet unmistakable: Edward’s eyes, Edward’s line of brow and mouth, the very expression she had learned to read. Whatever her fear of deciding too hastily, whatever wish she had of detecting falsehood, could not survive that evidence. She returned it almost instantly, acknowledging the likeness in a voice that sounded, to her own ears, like someone else’s.

“I have never been able,” Lucy went on, “to give him my picture in return, which vexes me sadly; for he has always been so anxious to get it! But I am determined to set for it the very first opportunity.”

“You are quite in the right,” Elinor answered, and marvelled at her own composure, as one marvels at keeping a delicate sauce from curdling while the fire beneath is too fierce.

They walked a few paces in silence. A bird startled from the hedge; its wings made a sudden clap of sound, then the lane was quiet again but for their steps and the distant murmur of the Park. Lucy spoke first.

“I am sure I have no doubt in the world of your faithfully keeping this secret,” said she, “because you must know of what importance it is to us not to have it reach his mother; for she would never approve of it, I dare say. I shall have no fortune, and I fancy she is an exceeding proud woman.”

“I certainly did not seek your confidence,” Elinor said, and though her words were civil, there was a steadiness in them that refused to be coaxed. “But you do me no more than justice in imagining that I may be depended on. Your secret is safe with me; but pardon me if I express some surprise at so unnecessary a communication. You must at least have felt that my being acquainted with it could not add to its safety.”

As she spoke, she looked earnestly at Lucy, searching—without quite knowing what she sought—for any involuntary change, any crack in that smooth surface: guilt, malice, triumph, fear. Lucy’s countenance did not alter.

“I was afraid you would think I was taking a great liberty,” Lucy said, as if injured by the idea and yet pleased to have produced it. “I have not known you long, to be sure—personally at least—but I have known you and all your family by description a great while; and as soon as I saw you I felt almost as if you was an old acquaintance. Besides, in the present case, I really thought some explanation was due to you, after my making such particular inquiries about Edward’s mother. And I am so unfortunate that I have not a creature whose advice I can ask. Anne is the only person that knows of it, and she has no judgment at all—indeed, she does me a great deal more harm than good; for I am in constant fear of her betraying me. She does not know how to hold her tongue, as you must perceive; and I was in the greatest fright in the world t’other day, when Edward’s name was mentioned by Sir John, lest she should out with it all. You can’t think how much I go through in my mind from it altogether. I only wonder that I am alive after what I have suffered for Edward’s sake these last four years—every thing in such suspense and uncertainty; and seeing him so seldom—we can hardly meet above twice a-year. I am sure I wonder my heart is not quite broke.”

She took out her handkerchief and wiped her eyes; but Elinor, though she heard every word, felt little softness rising in answer. The soundscape of Lucy’s complaint—its rehearsed cadences, its little pauses for effect—seemed to Elinor like a song performed too loudly in a room where someone is trying to listen for truth.

“Sometimes,” Lucy continued, as if following a script of suffering, “I think whether it would not be better for us both to break off the matter entirely.” As she said this, she looked directly at Elinor, waiting.

“Pardon me,” Elinor replied, startled into a more open firmness than she intended, “but I can give you no advice under such circumstances. Your own judgment must direct you.”

“To be sure,” Lucy resumed after a silence, “his mother must provide for him sometime or other; but poor Edward is so cast down by it! Did you not think him dreadful low-spirited when he was at Barton? He was so miserable when he left us at Longstaple to go to you, that I was afraid you would think him quite ill.”

“Did he come from your uncle’s, then, when he visited us?” Elinor asked—each fresh detail, against her will, knitting the story tighter.

“Oh yes; he had been staying a fortnight with us. Did you think he came directly from town?”

“No,” Elinor answered, and the memory rose with cruel clarity, “I remember he told us he had been staying a fortnight with some friends near Plymouth.” She remembered, too, how she had wondered at his saying no more, at his silence even on their names.

“Did not you think him sadly out of spirits?” Lucy repeated.

“We did indeed,” Elinor said, and every syllable felt like it cost her. “Particularly so when he first arrived.”

“I begged him to exert himself for fear you should suspect what was the matter; but it made him so melancholy—not being able to stay more than a fortnight with us, and seeing me so much affected. Poor fellow! I am afraid it is just the same with him now; for he writes in wretched spirits. I heard from him just before I left Exeter.” And with a carelessness that was not carelessness at all, she drew a letter from her pocket and showed Elinor the direction. “You know his hand, I dare say—a charming one it is; but this is not written so well as usual. He was tired, I dare say, for he had just filled the sheet to me as full as possible.”

Elinor looked. The strokes, the turns of the letters, the very manner of the flourish—she knew them. The miniature might have been stolen, bought, come by accident; but this could not. A correspondence so intimate and so sustained did not exist without a promise. In those few moments, her strength failed her so suddenly that the world seemed to tilt: the hedge, the sky, Lucy’s bonnet ribbon—each swam a little. Her heart sank within her; her limbs felt unsteady; she could hardly stand.

But necessity, that stern household deity, demanded her composure. She made an exertion—not dramatic, not visible to any but herself—and held her face and voice in order again, as one steadies a dish one must serve, though the kitchen behind is in disorder.

“Writing to each other,” Lucy said, tucking the letter away, “is the only comfort we have in such long separations. Yes, I have one other comfort in his picture; but poor Edward has not even that. If he had but my picture, he says he should be easy. I gave him a lock of my hair set in a ring when he was at Longstaple last, and that was some comfort to him, he said, but not equal to a picture. Perhaps you might notice the ring when you saw him?”

“I did,” Elinor answered, in a voice that, to any ear but her own, would have sounded perfectly composed. Under it lay distress beyond any she had ever known—mortification, shock, confusion, a sense of having been fed, for weeks, on hopes that now turned bitter in the mouth. The past—every look, every hesitation, every gentle attentiveness—seemed to rearrange itself with a new, cruel logic, as if some old family recipe had been recited in a dialect she had never truly understood, and now the meaning, once translated into plain fact, had changed the taste of every remembered moment.

They had reached the cottage. The familiar threshold was there, the hush of the little rooms, the safer, softer sounds of home. The Miss Steeles sat with them only a few minutes before returning to the Park; their voices and their footsteps retreated down the path until the lane’s natural quiet reclaimed itself.

Then Elinor was at liberty to think—and to be wretched.
