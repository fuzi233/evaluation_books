Several days slipped by, and it became clear the small girl had no real fondness for any soul in Mrs. Bretton’s household. She was not wilful, not disobedient, not even strictly naughty—yet no one could glance at her and feel fully at ease, fully tranquil. She moped as no grown grieving exile could mope: her tiny, soft face bore lines of homesickness plainer than the face of a man pining for half-remembered Europe from the farthest antipodes. She looked, at times, half old, half unearthly.

I, Lucy Snowe, have never claimed an overheated, wandering imagination. Yet whenever I turned a doorknob to find her huddled in a far corner, her head propped on her own miniature fist, the room felt not lived in, but haunted. On moonlit nights, waking to see her small white nightgown figure kneeling upright in her bed, praying with the ferver of some devout precocious saint, I felt thoughts rise in me that were hardly more steady than the child’s own frayed, yearning mind. I rarely caught the words of her whispered prayers, many of them unspoken entirely—but the few that reached my ear always tangled around the same two phrases: *Papa. My dear papa*. This was a one-ideaed nature, that rare monomaniac streak I have always thought the cruellest curse a human soul can bear.

No one can guess how that quiet, gnawing grief might have ended if left unchecked—until it turned, all at once, one sunny afternoon. Mrs. Bretton coaxed her out of her usual corner, lifted her to the window seat, and told her to pass the time counting how many ladies walked down the street. She sat listless, hardly glancing out, making no effort to count—until I, my gaze fixed on her face, saw a startling shift in her dark eyes. The heavy, dull glaze cleared; her small overcast brow lifted; her drooping, sad features lit up with a sudden, sharp, burning eagerness.

“It *is*,” she breathed.

She was gone from the room before I could move, swift as a sparrow taking flight. I had no idea how she wrenched the front door open—perhaps it was ajar, perhaps the servant Warren obeyed her sharp, impulsive order without thinking. I watched from the window as her small black dress and braided pinafore (she hated proper frilled pinafores with all her might) darted halfway down the street. I was half-ready to turn and tell Mrs. Bretton the child had run mad, that someone must chase her at once, when a man’s tall figure swept her up off the pavement, wrapped his heavy dark cloak around her, and turned back to the house he had seen her dart from.

I expected he would leave her in a servant’s arms and go on his way—but he stepped inside, paused a moment in the hall, then climbed the stairs. Mrs. Bretton’s flustered, surprised greeting told me at once they were old acquaintances. She looked half ready to scold him for appearing unannounced, and he smiled gently, his deep northern Scots lilt softening his words: “I could not leave the country without seeing for myself how she settled here.”

“But you will unsettle her entirely,” Mrs. Bretton said.

“I hope not. How is papa’s little Polly?” he added, turning to the child, and set her gently on her feet before his chair.

“How is Polly’s papa?” she replied at once, leaning her whole weight on his knee, gazing up into his sharp, stern, knotty-browed face.

It was no loud, sobbing reunion, for which I was grateful—but the quiet, brimful weight of feeling hung in the air heavier than any noisy outburst could. Mr. Home was hard-featured, proud and unpolished all at once, but there was no mistaking the soft emotion behind his sharp eyes. He laid his hand on the top of her small head.

“Kiss Polly,” she said. He did.

I almost wished she would sob hysterically, just to release the tight, heavy feeling in my own chest. But she made no noise at all. She had gotten everything she wanted, and she moved through the rest of the afternoon like a girl in a trance of perfect contentment. She looked nothing like her father in feature, but every thread of her stubborn, single-minded spirit was his, as surely as water poured from a flagon into a cup.

“Polly,” he said, after a time, “go to the hall. You will find my great-coat on a chair. Reach into the pocket, bring me the handkerchief you find there.”

She obeyed, darted off, and returned deftly a moment later. He was still talking to Mrs. Bretton when she came back, and she waited silent at his knee, the handkerchief clutched in her small hand. When he did not seem to notice her, she lifted his unresisting hand, opened his fingers one by one, tucked the handkerchief into his palm, and curled his fingers closed over it. He still did not speak or look at her, but a moment later he lifted her to his knee, and she curled against his chest. For the next hour, neither spoke, neither looked at the other—and both were perfectly satisfied.

At tea that evening, the tiny girl’s behaviour held all of us watching. First she directed Warren to move the chairs, slow and firm: “Put papa’s chair here, and mine right next to it, between him and Mrs. Bretton. *I* will hand his tea.” She took her seat, beckoned her father closer, and when the teacup was passed down the table, she intercepted it, insisting she must stir the sugar, pour the cream, no one else could do it right. The sugar tongs were far too wide for her small hands, so she had to use both to wield them. The silver cream jug was almost too heavy for her to lift, the bread plates too weighty, but she never broke a single thing. A lesser girl would have fumbled and spilled everything—but she was as steady as a little old housewife, fussing over her father like he was her whole world. Her father, blind and soft as all parents are, smiled down at her. “She is my whole comfort,” he told Mrs. Bretton, and Mrs. Bretton, who doted on her own son as her own whole comfort, nodded in full sympathy.

That son, Graham, returned home partway through the evening. I had known all day he was due back, and Mrs. Bretton had waited for him every hour. When he burst in, sixteen years old, tall and fair, with a shock of wavy auburn hair and that easy, charming half-smile that made him look far more mischievous than he really was, the whole circle by the fire broke into bustle. He had met Mr. Home years before, so they greeted each other as old friends—and for a long while, he paid the tiny girl no mind. When Mr. Home stepped out of the room for a moment, Graham leaned against the hearth, his eyes fixed on the small figure perched on a high chair by the table, bent over a scrap of handkerchief she was pretending to hem, pricking her finger over and over with a needle that looked almost too big for her hand, leaving tiny red dots along the cambric, silent and focused, as proper as any grown lady.

“Mother,” he said, half laughing, “there is a young lady in this room I have not been introduced to.”

“Mr. Home’s little girl, you mean,” his mother said, warning in her voice. “Don’t you dare tease her, Graham. I will not have you making a butt of that child.”

“Little girl?” Graham laughed, ignoring her protest, and turned straight to the small figure. “Miss Home, might I have the honour of introducing myself? No one else seems willing to do the service for us. Your obedient servant, John Graham Bretton.”

She looked up at him, slowly set down her needle, her thimble, her scissors, climbed very carefully down from her high perch, and curtsied with a gravity so profound it could have graced a royal drawing room. “How do you do?” she said.

“I am very well, thank you, though a little fatigued from my long journey,” he replied, matching her formal tone. “I hope I find you in good health, ma’am?”

“To-rer-ably well,” she pronounced, slow and deliberate, and then tried to climb back up to her high chair. Realising it required clambering and scrambling, a terrible breach of decorum in front of a strange young gentleman, she gave up entirely and settled on a low stool instead. Graham pulled his own chair closer, right across from her.

“I hope you find my mother’s house a convenient place to stay?” he asked.

“Not par-tic-er-er-ly,” she said. “I want to go home.”

“A very laudable desire, ma’am, but I intend to oppose it. Mamma and Mistress Snowe there are dreadfully dull, you see, and I am counting on you to give me some amusement.”

“I will be leaving soon with papa,” she told him. “I will not stay here long at all.”

“Nonsense,” he said, grinning. “You will stay. I have a pony you can ride, and dozens of books full of pictures you have never seen.”

“Are you going to live here now?” she asked, her head tilting.

“I am. Does that please you? Do you like me?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“You are queer. Your hair is long and red.”

“Auburn, if you please,” he said, fluffing his leonine tawny waves with great good humour, proud of the colour. “But even with my long red hair, I cannot possibly be queerer than you are, Miss Home.”

“I think I shall go to bed now,” she said, primly standing up.

“Little thing like you should have been in bed hours ago,” he teased. “I bet you stayed up on purpose just to see me arrive.”

“I stayed up for papa, not for you,” she said, sharp as a tack. “You are not nearly so important.”

“Very well,” he laughed. “I will be your favourite soon, you will see. You will like me better than papa before you know it.”

She said good night to Mrs. Bretton and then to me, hesitating a moment as if debating whether Graham deserved the same courtesy—before he reached down, caught her up with one hand, and lifted her straight up over his head, so she could see her own small startled face in the mirror above the mantel. The sudden, unceremonious disrespect of it sent her into a huff.

“For shame, Mr. Graham! Put me down at once!” she cried, indignant, and the moment her feet touched the floor she planted them firmly and said, “I wonder what you would do if I picked *you* up the way you picked me up—lifting you with one hand the same way Warren lifts the little stray cat.”

With that, she turned on her heel and marched off to bed, leaving Graham chuckling behind her at the hearth.
