I write these lines from India to my kin in England with a singular purpose: to lay bare the reason for my refusal to extend the right hand of friendship to my cousin, John Herncastle. The silence I have maintained regarding this rift has been misconstrued by family members whose regard I hold dear, leading them to believe my distance stems from mere pride or caprice. I implore you to withhold judgment until you have read the following narrative. I swear upon my honor that what follows is the absolute, unvarnished truth.

Our estrangement traces its roots to a momentous public event in which we were both bound: the storming of Seringapatam, under the command of General Baird, on the fourth of May, 1799. To understand the gravity of our divergence, I must briefly revisit the atmosphere preceding the assault, specifically the wild rumors circulating within our camp concerning the treasures hoarded within the Palace of Seringapatam.

Among these tales, none was more extravagant than the legend of the Yellow Diamond, a gem renowned in the ancient chronicles of India. Tradition holds that this stone was once set in the brow of the four-armed deity who embodies the Moon. Its unique hue, coupled with a superstitious belief that it resonated with the divine presence it adorned—growing and fading in brilliance with the lunar cycle—earned it the enduring name in India: **THE MOONSTONE**. A similar, though lesser, superstition once gripped ancient Greece and Rome, applying not to a diamond serving a god, but to a semi-transparent stone of lower rank, believed to be swayed by lunar tides; this too bestowed a "moon" name upon the gem, a designation collectors still recognize today.

The saga of the Yellow Diamond begins in the eleventh century of our era. Then, the conqueror Mahmoud of Ghizni swept across India, seizing the holy city of Somnauth and stripping the centuries-old temple of its riches. Yet, amidst the looting, the moon-god alone escaped the greed of the invading forces. Preserved by three Brahmins, the inviolate deity, bearing the Yellow Diamond upon its brow, was spirited away by night and transported to Benares, the second sacred city of India.

Here, in a new sanctuary—a hall inlaid with precious stones beneath a roof upheld by pillars of gold—the moon-god was reinstalled. On the very night the shrine was consecrated, Vishnu the Preserver appeared to the three Brahmins in a vision. The deity breathed his divine essence upon the Diamond resting on the god's forehead. The priests knelt, veiling their faces in their robes, as the deity commanded that the Moonstone be guarded perpetually by three priests in rotation, day and night, unto the end of human generations. They bowed to this decree. The god foretold doom for any presumptuous mortal who dared seize the sacred gem, and for every soul and lineage that would inherit it thereafter. This prophecy was etched in gold letters above the shrine's gates.

Centuries passed, generation following generation, as the descendants of the three guardians kept their vigil. By the early years of the eighteenth century, during the reign of Aurungzebe, Emperor of the Moguls, chaos returned to the temples of Brahmah. Under his command, the shrine of the four-handed god was desecrated; sacred beasts were slaughtered, idols shattered, and the Moonstone was snatched by a high-ranking officer in the imperial army.

Unable to reclaim their treasure by force, the guardian priests pursued it in disguise. Generations succeeded one another; the sacrilegious warrior perished in misery, and the Moonstone, carrying its curse, passed from one unlawful hand to another. Yet, through every shift of fortune, the heirs of the three priests maintained their watch, awaiting the day Vishnu would restore their sacred charge. Time marched from the first to the final years of the eighteenth century until the Diamond fell into the possession of Tippoo, Sultan of Seringapatam. He ordered it mounted as an ornament in the hilt of a dagger, commanding it be kept among the choicest treasures of his armory. Even then, within the Sultan's own palace, the three guardian priests, disguised as officers of Tippoo's household who had feigned conversion to the Muslim faith, kept their secret vigil.

Thus ran the fanciful tale of the Moonstone as recounted in our camp. It left no mark on any of us save my cousin, whose penchant for the marvelous led him to accept it as fact. On the eve of the assault, he was absurdly incensed that I and others dismissed the story as mere fiction. A foolish quarrel ensued, and Herncastle's volatile temper overwhelmed him. In his boastful manner, he declared that if the English army conquered Seringapatam, we would see the Diamond gleaming upon his finger. The remark drew a roar of laughter, and so, we all believed that night, the matter ended.

Let us now turn to the day of the assault itself.

My cousin and I were separated at the outset. I did not witness him as we forded the river, nor when we raised the English flag at the first breach, nor as we crossed the ditch and fought our way into the town. We met only at dusk, once the place was ours and General Baird had discovered the corpse of Tippoo beneath a heap of the slain. Herncastle and I reunited then.

We were each assigned to a party ordered by the General to curb the plunder and confusion that inevitably followed our victory. The camp-followers had already committed abominable excesses; worse, soldiers had found their way through an unguarded door into the palace treasury, loading themselves with gold and jewels. It was in the courtyard outside the treasury that my cousin and I met to enforce discipline upon our own troops. I observed that Herncastle's fiery temper had been driven to a frenzied state by the terrible slaughter we had endured. In my judgment, he was wholly unfit to perform the duty entrusted to him.

There was riot and confusion within the treasury, though no violence that I witnessed directly. The men disgraced themselves with a good-natured recklessness; rough jests and catchwords flew among them. Suddenly, the legend of the Diamond resurfaced as a mischievous jest. "Who's got the Moonstone?" became the rallying cry, causing the plundering to flare up anew wherever it was momentarily halted. While I vainly attempted to restore order, I heard a dreadful yell on the opposite side of the courtyard and ran toward the sound, fearing a fresh outbreak of pillage.

I reached an open door and found the bodies of two Indians—judging by their dress, officers of the palace—lying across the entrance, dead. A cry from within hurried me into a room that served as an armoury. There, a third Indian, mortally wounded, was sinking at the feet of a man whose back was turned to me. The man spun around the instant I entered. I saw John Herncastle, torch in one hand, a dagger dripping with blood in the other. As he turned upon me, a stone set like a pommel at the end of the dagger's handle flashed in the torchlight, gleaming like fire. The dying Indian sank to his knees, pointed to the weapon in Herncastle's grasp, and spoke in his native tongue: *"The Moonstone will have its vengeance yet on you and yours!"* With those words, he fell dead upon the floor.

Before I could intervene, the men who had followed me across the courtyard crowded in. My cousin rushed to meet them like a madman. "Clear the room!" he shouted at me, "and set a guard on the door!" The soldiers fell back as he threw himself upon them, brandishing his torch and dagger. I stationed two sentinels from my own company, men I could trust, to hold the door. For the remainder of that night, I saw no more of my cousin.

Early the next morning, while plundering continued, General Baird announced publicly by beat of drum that any thief caught in the act, whoever he might be, would be hanged. The provost-marshal was present to demonstrate the General's seriousness. In the throng that gathered, Herncastle and I met again.

He held out his hand, as was his custom, saying, "Good morning."

I paused before returning the gesture.

"Tell me first," I said, "how the Indian in the armoury met his death, and what those final words meant when he pointed to the dagger in your hand."

"The Indian met his death, as I suppose, by a mortal wound," Herncastle replied. "What his last words meant, I know no more than you do."

I studied him closely. The frenzy of the previous day had subsided. I resolved to grant him another chance.

"Is that all you have to tell me?" I asked.

"That is all," he answered.

I turned my back on him, and we have not spoken since.

It must be understood that what I write here concerning my cousin (unless some necessity arises to make it public) is intended solely for the information of the family. Herncastle has uttered nothing that would justify me in accusing him before our commanding officer. He has been taunted more than once regarding the Diamond by those who recall his angry outburst prior to the assault; yet, as one might imagine, his own memory of the circumstances under which I surprised him in the armoury has been sufficient to keep him silent. It is reported that he intends to transfer to another regiment, ostensibly to separate himself from *me*. Whether this is true or not, I cannot bring myself to become his accuser—and I believe with good reason. If I were to make this matter public, I possess only moral evidence. I have no proof that he killed the two men at the door; I cannot even assert with certainty that he killed the third man inside, for my own eyes did not witness the deed being committed. It is true that I heard the dying Indian's words; but if those words were merely the ravings of delirium, how could I contradict such a claim based on my own knowledge? Let our relatives, on either side, form their own opinions on what I have written and decide for themselves whether the aversion I now feel toward this man is well or ill-founded.

Though I attach no credence to the fantastic Indian legend of the gem, I must acknowledge, before concluding, that I am influenced by a personal superstition in this matter. It is my conviction—or perhaps my delusion—that crime carries its own fatality. I am not only persuaded of Herncastle's guilt; I am even fanciful enough to believe that he will live to regret it if he keeps the Diamond, and that others will live to regret taking it from him if he gives it away.
