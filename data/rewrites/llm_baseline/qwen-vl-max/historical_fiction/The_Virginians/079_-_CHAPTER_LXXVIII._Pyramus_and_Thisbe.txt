Years later, while going through old papers at home, I came upon a letter sealed and labeled in my mother’s familiar hand: “From London, April, 1760. My son’s dreadful letter.” When it became mine, I burned it—no family record should preserve the tale of that domestic sorrow and rupture for future Warringtons to read, or disobedient sons to cite as precedent. For the same reason, I destroyed the reply she sent me during that time of anger, defiance, and unrest.

Consumed by the agony of separation from my beloved, and justifiably blaming Mrs. Esmond for the deepest pain I had ever known, I wrote to Virginia a letter that might have been more measured—but in which I strove to maintain decorum and restraint. I did not know what motives had driven her, but I held her accountable for the ruin of my life, which she had chosen to shatter with her own hand. She had torn apart my union with a virtuous, innocent young woman whose hopes, health, and joy were now forever crushed by her interference. The deed was done, as I feared, and I would say no more about the doer—she stood before God alone. Yet I could not hide from my mother the depth of the injury she had inflicted: so great and fatal that neither her life nor mine could undo it. My allegiance to her was broken; I could never again be the dutiful, respectful son I once was.

Madam Esmond answered with a letter of remarkable dignity—her writing always elegant, refined, and precise. She uttered no reproach, no harsh word. Coldly, she informed me that she had laid our dispute before the tribunal of God, seeking His guidance. As a mother, she said, she was ready, in humble submission, to face divine judgment. Might I, as a son, be equally prepared to answer for myself when the Great Judge called? Would I be able to prove that I had honored my father and mother, as the law demanded? O popoi! My grandfather quoted a line of Homer in his memoir—how in every trial, we summon the gods to witness our suffering. When pride, greed, ambition, or desire to rule over others takes hold, how often do we cry out to Heaven, demanding justice on our side? In our great American conflict, both sides appealed to the skies, sang Te Deum at victory, and declared with confidence that right must prevail. Was America right because she won? Then was Poland wrong because she lost? How have I strayed into this digression about Poland, America, and all manner of things—while all the while thinking of a small woman now gone, who turned to Heaven and cited its own scriptures, as if to challenge the Almighty itself, because her son wished to marry whom she disapproved?

We appeal, we curse, we kneel, we beg for blessings, we demand judgment—yet the world moves forward. We struggle, rage, weep, reconcile, race, win, lose, pass away, and new strugglers take our place. Our days end; night falls; morning dawns, but shines no more upon us.

I showed my letter—declaring my rebellion and refusal to obey—to Mr. Lambert, in duplicate. I wanted him to understand my position, and my resolve: whatever threats or quarrels the future might bring, I would never regard my separation from Theo as anything but forced. Whenever I could see her again, I would. My promise to her was eternal—*in secula seculorum*, or at least as long as I lived. I implied that she, too, was bound to me, and her poor father knew it well. He might separate us—as he might give her poison, and she, gentle and obedient, would take it and die—but the death or division would be his doing. Let him bear the guilt. He was tender-hearted, almost weak in his affection for his children, especially her. He could not bring himself to inflict such torment. We had tried to part—we could not. He had attempted to keep us apart—it was beyond his power. The bars were raised, yet the young couple—the maid within, the knight without—still loved each other. The wall was built, but Pyramus and Thisbe whispered across it. Even amid his grief and confusion, Uncle Lambert had a sense of humor. He could not help seeing how absurd his role had become. Light began to pierce through the plaster and lime of that wall—the lovers’ hands reached through, then their heads—indeed, the wall's best course was to step aside.

I do not recall the events day by day, nor does it matter much for posterity. When my descendants face their own love troubles, they will find their own way out. I believe I did not return to Dean Street. Instead, driving in open air was considered good for Miss Lambert’s health. I acquired a fine horse and rode beside her carriage. The old woman at Tottenham Court grew quite familiar with us, nodding and winking as we passed. I suspect she had seen many such pairs before and welcomed them into her cottage with quiet kindness.

Doctor Heberden and the country air worked wonders on Miss Lambert. Hetty remained her constant companion, and sometimes, on holidays, Master Charley rode my horse while I took the carriage. How much love-making did Hetty hear—and with what admirable patience she listened! I don’t say she stopped attending Methodist sermons, but when we had a closed carriage, she kindly looked out the window. And then there were the letters—countless ones—running back and forth! Gumbo’s legs were always in motion between my lodgings and Dean Street; and Mrs. Molly, the girl’s maid, was constantly shuttling messages from Bloomsbury. By autumn, when the leaves turned pale, Miss Theo’s cheeks were rosy again, and Doctor Heberden pronounced her fully restored. What else occurred during this blessed time? Mr. Warrington completed his great tragedy *Pocahontas*, which this time was accepted by Mr. Garrick (Dr. Johnson having spoken favorably of it), and my cousin Hagan was cast as Captain Smith. Hagan’s engagement was made only when needed. I had helped him and his family financially—perhaps beyond my means, especially given my feud with Madam Esmond. Her reply to my angry letter of April reached me in autumn, written from Virginia—war for war, control for control. But these threats frightened me little. My mother’s thunder could not reach me. My conscience—or my casuistry—offered alternative interpretations of her scriptural texts, so her words carried no weight against my purpose. I told no one—neither maternal nor paternal kin—about my new love. Why argue with them? It was better to live in the moment, savoring sweet joys, and let the critics grumble or the elders advise at their leisure.

Besides Madam Esmond, in my fury, I wrote scathing notes to Uncle Warrington, Aunt Madame de Bernstein, and my Lord or Lady of Castlewood (I forget which), thanking them for spoiling the greatest happiness of my life, and promising them equal gratitude from their devoted relative. Business brought the jovial Baronet and his family to London earlier than usual. Madame de Bernstein was never sorry to return to Clarges Street and her cards. I saw them. They found me well. They assumed the match was off—I chose not to correct them. The Baroness, heartened by my cheerfulness, made many sly jokes about my philosophy and worldly wisdom. She remained determined to find me a rich match—and I fear I paid many compliments to a wealthy young daughter of a soap-boiler from Mile End, whom she wished to place in my arms.

“You court her with infinite wit and esprit, my dear,” she said, pleased. “But she understands half of what you say, and the other half frightens her. This tone of persiflage is fine among us, but spare it, my dear nephew, among these common folk.”

Miss Badge married a gentleman of royal blood, though reduced fortune, from a neighboring island. I trust Mrs. Mackshane has forgiven my jests. There was another at my aunt’s house who understood my banter no better—this was an elderly lady who had lived through James II’s reign and remained as worldly as ever under King George. I loved her company. But since my children may read this, I cannot tell a hundred tales of the great figures she knew—George I and his ladies, St. John and Marlborough, the reigning king and the late Prince of Wales, and their quarrel—but my modest muse sings only for boys and virgins. Son Miles cares not for court gossip, or if he does, he has fresh news from Carlton House, just as scandalous. No, my dear wife, you need not shake your powdered locks at me! Papa will not disgrace the nursery with ancient scandals, nor bring shame to our chaste bread-and-butter.

Yet one story I cannot omit. My aunt told it with great relish—for, to do her justice, she despised pretentious virtue and delighted in mocking the self-righteous. In her later years, she wore no mask. And in that, she was better than some whitewashed saints. To the tale. My Lady Warrington, one of the tallest and most pious women of her time—always speaking of goodness and “Heaven in her eye” like the heroine in Addison’s tedious play (which still survives on stage, unlike others)—had the world in her other eye, and a sharp desire to rise in it. When my marriage with you seemed broken off, what did she do but try the very arts she’d used on poor Harry—with similar failure—and fail again with me? It was not Beauty—Miss Flora was already claimed by my master—(and what a master! I bow in awe at such noble connections!)—it was Dora, the Muse, who was set to languish for me, pity me, read my “godless” tragedy, applaud me, console me. Meanwhile, what of Beauty? Can it be believed that my stern aunt gave a grand entertainment for my Lady Yarmouth, presented her boy to her, and placed little Miles under her protection? That much is certain. But could it be that she sent her daughter to stay at my lady’s house, which our gracious lord visited daily, with the aims old Aunt Bernstein attributed to her? “Had it not been for that apoplectic fit,” Bernstein said, “your aunt meant to make a Countess of her own blood in the Warrington family!” [Compare Walpole’s letters in Mr. Cunningham’s edition. See the supper at N. House, showing what noblemen would do for a king’s mistress, and the amusing account of waiting for the Prince of Wales before Holland House.—EDITOR.] My neighbor and kinswoman, my Lady Claypole, is dead and buried. Grow white, ye daisies, upon Flora’s tomb! I can see my little Miles, in a bright uniform of the Norfolk Militia, led by his parent to the lady the King delighted to honor, and the kind old Jezebel placing her hand upon his curly head. I am accused of being a lukewarm royalist; but surely I can compare those times with ours, and acknowledge the difference between the late sovereign and the present—one born a Briton, who has set an example of decency and virtue for every family in the empire. [The Warrington MS. is dated 1793.—ED.]

Thus my life flowed in the sweetest occupation. Happy myself, I could afford to forgive those who, after all, had done me no real harm—only added spice to my joy by briefly blocking my path. No firm plans were made, but Theo and I knew a day would come when we would never say farewell again. Whether it came in a year or ten, we were ready to wait. Day after day, we discussed our dreams, with Hetty as confidante. On our drives, we spied cottages we thought suitable for young people of modest means, devised economies, and dreamed of simple joys. We were Strephon and Chloe, indeed. A cottage and a brown loaf would satisfy us! Gumbo and Molly would serve us—as they have done ever since. At twenty, who fears poverty? Our trials would only strengthen our bond. The “sweet sorrow” of each parting made the next meeting sweeter. And when we parted, we rushed home to write those precious letters—written by young men and women in such circumstances. Though my wife keeps them all in a tin sugar-box in her bedroom, and I confess I once glanced at them, even finding some charming—I hereby charge my heirs and executors to burn them unread at our deaths. Especially I instruct my son the Captain (who, I know, finds reading manuscripts unpleasant) to carry out this duty. Those secrets whispered to the penny-post, or exchanged between Molly and Gumbo, were meant for us alone. No descendant shall overhear them.

We received brief letters telling us how our dear Harry continued in the army, serving as aide-de-camp to General Amherst after the death of his own illustrious commander. By mid-October, news arrived of the Capitulation of Montreal and the fall of Canada. Hal added a postscript: he would request leave and visit the old lady at home, who wrote as sourly as a bare-headed captain, as Warrington noted. I guessed why—though her claws could not reach me. I had written thoroughly to my brother about my affairs in England.

Then, on October 25th, came the news: His Majesty had died suddenly at Kensington, and George III now reigned. We grieved little. What do hearts tuned to love care for the Atridae? A modest, handsome, brave new prince—we gladly accepted the common belief that he possessed every virtue, and joined the loyal crowd in hailing his accession. It mattered little to us, as we thought, two young sweethearts whispering love stories in our corner.

But who knows how great events affect individuals? Did not our little Charley, at the Chartreux, wish impiously for a new king—because on the new monarch’s accession, Doctor Crusius granted his boys a holiday? He, I, Hetty, and Theo (now strong enough to walk many miles) heard the heralds proclaim the new king before Savile House in Leicester Fields. A pickpocket stole a gentleman’s watch and chain nearby, was caught, and taken to Bridewell—all due to the king’s accession. Had the king not died, the gentleman wouldn’t have been in the crowd; the chain wouldn’t have been stolen; the thief wouldn’t have been caught and whipped. In this way, many of us, more or less indirectly, were touched by the great change, and even we humble souls felt its ripple.

For instance, my Lord Wrotham, a close friend of the Savile House family, admired Martin Lambert deeply—calling him one of the finest gentlemen in the world. And my Lord Bute, newly powerful with the king, eager to reward merit, was strongly inclined toward Mr. Lambert thanks to Wrotham’s influence.

Parson Sampson, my old friend (and Harry’s), who had been in and out of jail countless times lately, harbored enduring hatred for the Esmonds of Castlewood but lasting affection for me and my brother, was staying in Hal’s room at my lodgings (hunted from his own by bailiffs). I liked having him near—a more entertaining Jack-friar never walked in cassock. He also shared my rhapsodies about Miss Theo, never tired (so he swore) of hearing me speak of her, admired *Pocahontas* and *Carpezan* with honest passion, and could recite passages with such force that Barry or cousin Hagan couldn’t surpass him. Sampson served as go-between for Lady Maria and her relatives who hadn’t disowned her. Always in debt, he was happiest drinking ale or sharing tears with friends in similar straits. His knowledge of pawnbrokers’ shops was vast—he claimed he could get more money on an item than any man in his profession. He never paid his debts, but readily forgave others’. Poor as he was, he always found ways to help his sister. A more generous, kind, amiable rogue never grinned behind bars. Some say I enjoy parasites around me. I admit I greatly liked Sampson and valued him more than many better men.

When he heard Lord Bute entered the cabinet, Sampson vowed he must appoint me to a post worthy of my birth and merit. He insisted I attend his levee. I refused? The Esmonds were proud as Lucifer; and my lineage was as noble as any in Europe. Demmy! Where was Bute when the Esmonds ruled counties, fought wars, and went on Crusades? Where were they? Beggarly Scots, barely clothed—by George! tearing raw fish in their islands. But now the times had changed. The Scots were in luck. Mum’s the word! “I don’t envy him,” Sampson said, “but he shall provide for you and my dearest, noblest, heroic captain! He SHALL, by George!” he’d roar. When the king ordered *Richard III* at Drury Lane, my chaplain cursed, swore, but insisted we go to Covent Garden to see *Carpezan* too.

One morning, he burst into my room, where I lay late, waving the newspaper and shouting “Huzza!” with all his might.

“What is it, Sampson?” I asked.

“Not your brother’s promotion—though he’ll get it soon enough. But someone else’s! Huzzay! His Majesty has appointed Major-General Martin Lambert Governor and Commander-in-Chief of Jamaica!”

I leapt up. News indeed! Mr. Lambert would go to his government. Who would go with him? I had supped with young gentlemen at the Cocoa-Tree. Gumbo had a note from my dear mistress the night before, delivering the same news, but delayed delivery. Theo begged me to meet her at the old place at noon the next day—without fail. [In the Warrington MS., nothing is said of what the “old place” was. Perhaps a reader of Notes and Queries can enlighten us, and who Mrs. Goodison was.—ED.]

There was no little alarm when we reached our meeting place. Papa had accepted the appointment and would depart soon. A frigate would be provided, and his family would accompany him. Merciful powers! Were we to be parted? Theo’s pallor returned. Aunt Lambert feared she would faint. One of Mrs. Goodison’s girls ran with a bottle of salts from the workroom. “Going away? Going in a frigate, Aunt Lambert? Taking her from me? Great God! Aunt Lambert, I shall die!” She improved when mamma came up with the salts. You see, the women met me—knowing Theo’s delicate state, how could they not feel for her? But the General was too busy with levees, ministers, and settling affairs to learn of our walks. Even when orders were issued for the ladies’ outfits, Mrs. Goodison—once a seamstress to Miss Molly Benson, who remembered Miss Esmond of Virginia perfectly—“even when the order was placed for three ladies,” she said demurely, “I thought I could do no harm in completing it.”

Need I say how agitated Mr. Warrington was returning to his lodgings that evening? No more walks, no rides to dear Hampstead or Islington, no more letters carried by Gumbo and Molly! Gumbo wept so profusely that I was moved by his loyalty and gave him a crown-piece to share supper with the girl—turns out she was his sweetheart. What, you too, unhappy Gumbo, torn from the maid you love? I was ready to shed tears with him.
