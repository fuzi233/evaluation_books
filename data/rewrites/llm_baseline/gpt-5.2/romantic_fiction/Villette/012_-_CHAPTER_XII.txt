Behind the house in the Rue Fossette there was a garden—no countryside’s sweep, no honest horizon of fields, but still a breadth of green startling in the city’s stone heart. Memory, which sweetens what it cannot restore, insists it was always pleasant; yet even then, with blank walls all round and hot pavement close by, a single shrub seemed a mercy, and an enclosed plot of earth—planted, watered, kept—felt like a living room for the soul.

There was a story fastened to it, as ivy fastens to grey masonry. People said Madame Beck’s house had once been a convent; and that in the centuries when this quarter was not yet overbuilt—when it was more avenue than alley, more tilled ground than flagstone—something had happened here that left fear as an inheritance. A black-and-white nun, so ran the vague tradition, showed herself at certain seasons, in certain nights, in some part of the neighbourhood. The city had long since walled the ghost in with houses, yet convent-relics remained: enormous fruit-trees, old as a superstition, their roots lifting the soil as if remembering a different world.

At the foot of one—a Methuselah pear-tree, dead in most of its limbs yet faithful in a few that still each spring burst into perfumed snow, and each autumn hung honeyed pendants—there was, if you scraped away the mossy earth between half-bared roots, a glimpse of slab: smooth, hard, black as sealed ink. People whispered it was a vault-door; that below, where grass now grew and flowers bloomed, a girl had been buried alive by a monkish conclave for some sin against her vow. Her bones were dust, but her shadow, they said, had outlasted generations; and moonlight, and wind in leaves, mocked timid eyes with a black robe and a white veil fluctuating through the thicket.

Romantic rubbish, perhaps; yet the garden had honest charms besides fear. On summer mornings I rose early to enjoy them alone; on summer evenings I lingered solitary, keeping tryst with the rising moon, taking one kiss of the cooling breeze, tasting—more in fancy than in fact—the freshness of dew descending. The turf lay vivid; the gravel walks were white as sifted flour. Nasturtiums, sun-bright and pepper-scented, clustered about the roots of doddered orchard giants; and when you brushed their leaves, the air gave back a green, sharp note, as if the plant itself had a tongue.

There was a large berceau shaded by an acacia; when its blossoms came, they shed sweetness like warmed honey, and bees made a soft industry beneath. There was a smaller bower, more hidden, nestled in the vines that ran along a high grey wall. Their tendrils knotted together, their clusters hung in loving profusion where jasmine and ivy met and married them—jasmine breathing out its slow, thick perfume, ivy smelling of shade and stone and persistence.

At high noon, when Madame Beck’s school turned out rampant—externes and pensionnaires abroad together, vying with the neighbouring boys’ college in brazen exercise of lungs and limbs—the garden was trodden and trite enough. The very air shook with it: shrill laughter, stamping feet, the scrape of shoes on gravel, the harsh clap of hands, noise that rose and fell like a market. But at sunset, or at the hour of salut, when the externes were gone home and the boarders were quiet at their studies, then the garden drew its breath again. Then you could hear, not the girls, but the bells of St. Jean Baptiste—sweet, soft, exalted—sending their sound through the streets like a benediction that asked nothing back.

One evening I walked there thus, detained farther within twilight than usual by the still-deepening calm. The day’s heat had retreated; now the flowers, which no sunshine could coax into such confession, answered the dew’s persuasion. Their breathing came up at me from the beds: earth-damp, bruised leaf, a last warm spice from the nasturtiums, the faint milky odour of vine sap. In the oratory window a light shone; I knew the Catholic household were gathered for evening prayer—a rite from which, as a Protestant, I now and then exempted myself. Solitude and the summer moon whispered, “One moment longer: stay with us. All is truly quiet now; for another quarter of an hour your presence will not be missed.”

One side of the garden was skirted by the rear of a long line of premises belonging to the neighbouring college—blank stone, high wall, with only a few attic loopholes and one lower casement said to mark a master’s chamber or study. Along that wall ran a narrow alley forbidden to the pupils: “l’allée défendue.” Any girl setting foot there would have earned as severe a penalty as Madame Beck’s mild rules permitted. Teachers might go there; but the shrubs grew thick and close, weaving overhead a roof of branch and leaf that let the sun through only in rare chequers. In daylight it was seldom entered; after dusk it was shunned as if it had teeth.

From the first, the very gloom tempted me. For a long time fear of seeming singular kept me away; but by degrees, as people grew accustomed to my habits and to such quiet shades of peculiarity as were engrained in my nature—born in and with me, no more to be parted with than my identity—I became a frequenter of that strait path.

I made myself gardener there. Between the closely ranked shrubs some tintless flowers struggled—pale things, as if they had forgotten their own names. I cleared away the relics of past autumns that choked a rustic seat at the far end. Borrowing from Goton, the cuisinière, a pail of water and a scrubbing-brush, I set to work. The water smelled of iron from the pump; the brush, wet and rough, rasped the wood till the mould yielded, and the damp rot’s sourness rose into the leaf-shadowed air. Madame saw me and smiled approbation—whether sincere or not I cannot tell; but she seemed sincere.

“Voyez-vous,” cried she, “comme elle est propre, cette demoiselle Lucie? Vous aimez donc cette allée, meess?”

“Yes,” I said; “it is quiet and shady.”

“C’est juste,” returned she with an air of bonté; and she recommended me to confine myself to it as much as I chose, adding that as I was not charged with surveillance I need not trouble myself to walk with the pupils—only I might permit her children to come there to talk English with me.

On the night in question I sat on the hidden seat I had reclaimed from fungi and mould, listening to what seemed the far-off sounds of the city. Far off they were not; the school stood in the centre. Five minutes’ walk would have brought you to the park; scarce ten to palatial buildings. Quite near, wide streets blazed with gaslight and life; carriages rolled there to balls or the opera. The same hour that tolled curfew for our convent—extinguishing each lamp, dropping the curtain round each couch—rang for the gay city the summons to festal enjoyment.

I did not think much of that contrast. Gay instincts my nature had few. Ball or opera I had never seen; when I wished to see them, it was not as one who hopes to partake, but as one who would look on a new thing from outside, tasting it with the eyes, not the blood.

A crescent moon hung in the sky—not full, but young. Through a space in the boughs overhead I saw her, and a few stars beside. They were no strangers where all else was strange: my childhood knew them. I had seen that golden sign, the dark globe in its curve, leaning back on azure beside an old thorn at the top of an English field in long past days, just as it now leaned back beside a stately spire in this continental capital.

When I thought of those days, passive as I lived, little as I spoke, cold as I looked, I could feel. About the present it was better to be stoical; about the future—such a future as mine—to be dead. In catalepsy and a dead trance I held the quick of my nature down, as one holds down the lid of a pot that would boil over.

Whatever could excite—certain accidents of the weather, for instance—I almost dreaded, because they woke the being I was always lulling, and stirred up a craving cry I could not satisfy. Once a thunder-storm broke; a hurricane shook us in our beds. The Catholics rose in panic and prayed loud to their saints. For me, the tempest took hold with tyranny. I dressed and crept out upon the casement ledge near my bed, feet set on the roof of a lower adjoining building. It was wet; the slates were slick under my shoes; the night was pitch-dark. Within the dormitory they huddled round the night-lamp, prayers running together in fright. I could not go in. Too resistless was the delight of staying with the wild hour, black and full of thunder, pealing out an ode language never delivered to man—too terribly glorious the clouds split and pierced by blinding bolts, and then the sudden thick hush in which you heard rain begin, like a thousand soft fingers on leaf and glass and stone.

After that storm I longed, achingly, for something to fetch me out of my present existence and lead me upwards and onwards. Such longings, and all of a similar kind, it was necessary to knock on the head; which I did figuratively, after the manner of Jael to Sisera, driving a nail through their temples. Unlike Sisera they did not die; they were but stunned, and at intervals would turn on the nail with a rebellious wrench; then did the temples bleed and the brain thrill to its core.

To-night I was not so mutinous. My Sisera lay quiet in the tent, slumbering; and if his pain ached through those slumbers, something like an angel—the ideal—knelt near, dropping balm on soothed temples, holding before sealed eyes a magic glass of sweet solemn visions repeated in dreams. Jael, the stern woman, sat apart, relenting somewhat over her captive, yet more prone to dwell on the faithful expectation of Heber coming home. By which I mean: the cool peace and dewy sweetness of the night filled me with a mood of hope—not hope on any definite point, but a general sense of encouragement and heart-ease.

Should not such a mood, so tranquil and unwonted, have been the harbinger of good? Alas, no good came of it.

Presently the rude Real burst in—coarse, repellent as she too often is. In the intense stillness, with the high wall and the thick shrubs hemming my narrow walk, I heard a sound: a casement creaked. Before I could look up and mark in which story, or by whose hand, a tree overhead shook, as if struck by a missile; something dropped prone at my feet.

Nine was striking by St. Jean Baptiste’s clock. Day was fading, but it was not dark. The crescent moon gave little light; yet the last gilding where the sun had beamed, and the crystalline clearness of the sky, sustained the summer twilight. Even in that dark walk I could, by stepping into an opening, have read small print.

I stooped. It was a box—a small casket of white and coloured ivory, cool under my fingers, polished smooth as a well-used spoon-handle. Its loose lid opened in my hand. Violets lay within—violets so fresh their scent sprang up at once, that deep, bruised sweetness which is half flower, half earth, half memory. They smothered a closely folded bit of pink paper, a note superscribed, “Pour la robe grise.”

I wore indeed a dress of French grey.

Good. Was this a billet-doux? A thing I had heard of, but never handled. Was it this sort of commodity I held between finger and thumb? Scarcely: I did not dream it for a moment. Suitor or admirer my very thoughts had not conceived. Other teachers had their romances; pupils over fourteen spoke with certainty of future bridegrooms as if of a harvest already sown. Two or three were affianced by their parents and had been so since childhood. Into that realm my speculations had never once had warrant to intrude.

If the other teachers went into town, or took a walk on the boulevards, or only attended mass, they were very sure to meet some “individual of the opposite sex” whose rapt gaze assured them of their power to strike and attract. I cannot say my experience tallied. I went to church; I took walks; I am convinced nobody minded me. The whole Rue Fossette could testify to having received an admiring beam from our young doctor’s blue eyes at one time or other; I am obliged, however humbling, to except myself. As far as I was concerned those blue eyes were guiltless, calm as the sky whose tint they shared.

So I heard the others, wondered at their gaiety and security, but did not trouble myself to look up and gaze along the path they were so certain of treading.

This then was no billet-doux; and in settled conviction to the contrary I quietly opened it. It ran thus—I translate:

“Angel of my dreams! A thousand, thousand thanks for the promise kept: scarcely did I venture to hope its fulfilment. I believed you indeed to be half in jest; and then you seemed to think the enterprise beset with such danger—the hour so untimely, the alley so strictly secluded—often, you said, haunted by that dragon, the English teacher—une véritable bégueule Britannique à ce que vous dites—espèce de monstre, brusque et rude comme un vieux caporal de grenadiers, et revêche comme une religieuse—”

(The reader will excuse my modesty in letting this flattering sketch of my amiable self retain the slight veil of the original tongue.)

“You are aware,” it went on, “that little Gustave, on account of his illness, has been removed to a master’s chamber—that favoured chamber whose lattice overlooks your prison-ground. There, I, the best uncle in the world, am admitted to visit him. How tremblingly I approached the window and glanced into your Eden—an Eden for me, though a desert for you!—how I feared to behold vacancy, or the dragon aforesaid! How my heart palpitated with delight when, through apertures in the envious boughs, I at once caught the gleam of your graceful straw-hat, and the waving of your grey dress—dress that I should recognise amongst a thousand. But why, my angel, will you not look up? Cruel, to deny me one ray of those adorable eyes!—how a single glance would have revived me! I write this in fiery haste; while the physician examines Gustave, I snatch an opportunity to enclose it in a small casket, together with a bouquet of flowers, the sweetest that blow—yet less sweet than thee, my Peri—my all-charming! ever thine—thou well knowest whom!”

“I wish I did know whom,” was my inward comment; and the wish bore even closer reference to the person addressed than to the writer. Perhaps it was meant for one of the engaged pupils; perhaps for some pretty pensionnaire with a vow of secrecy and a brother or cousin in the college next door. In that case there was no great harm intended—only an irregularity.

Yet “la robe grise, le chapeau de paille”—here was a clue, and a confusing one. The straw hat was a common garden screen worn by a score besides myself. Grey dresses were then in vogue; Madame Beck herself wore grey just now; another teacher and three of the pensionnaires had grey of the same shade and fabric as mine.

While I pondered, I knew I must go in. Lights moving in the dormitory announced that prayers were over and the pupils going to bed. Another half-hour and all doors would be locked, all lamps put out. The front door yet stood open, inviting the coolness of the summer night into the heated house. From the portress’s cabinet shone a lamp, showing the long vestibule and the street-door closing the vista.

All at once the bell rang—quick, not loud: a cautious tinkle, a warning metal whisper. Rosine darted from her cabinet to open. The person she admitted stood two minutes in parley; there was demur, delay. Rosine came to the garden door, lamp in hand; she stood on the steps lifting her light and looking round vaguely.

“Quel conte!” she cried, with a coquettish laugh. “Personne n’y a été.”

“Let me pass,” pleaded a voice I knew; “I ask but five minutes;” and a familiar shape, tall and grand—as we of the Rue Fossette all thought it—issued from the house and strode down among beds and walks.

It was sacrilege: a man in that place at that hour. But he knew himself privileged, and perhaps trusted to the friendly night. He wandered the alleys, looking right and left, trampling flowers, breaking branches in his haste. The sound of his steps—however careful—changed the whole acoustic of the garden: the quiet that had held the bell-notes tenderly now flinched; leaves shook; a snapped twig cried out; somewhere a bird stirred and made a sleepy protest. He penetrated at last the forbidden walk.

There I met him.

“Dr. John! it is found.”

He did not ask by whom; his quick eye saw the casket and the note in my hand. “Do not betray her,” he said, looking at me as if I were indeed the dragon described.

“Were I ever so disposed to treachery, I cannot betray what I do not know,” I answered. “Read the note, and you will see how little it reveals.”

Perhaps you have read it already, I thought; and yet I could not believe he wrote it. That style could hardly be his. Besides, I was fool enough to feel there would be hardship in his calling me such names. His look vindicated him: he grew hot, and coloured as he read.

“This is indeed too much: this is cruel, this is humiliating,” fell from him.

I thought it cruel too, seeing his countenance so moved. No matter whether he was to blame or not, somebody must be more to blame; and in my heart there stirred, not triumph, but a reluctant pity, like the way one pities a singer who has been made ridiculous by a cheap refrain.

“What shall you do about it?” he asked me. “Shall you tell Madame Beck what you have found, and cause a stir—an esclandre?”

I thought I ought to tell, and said so; adding that I did not believe there would be stir or esclandre. Madame was far too prudent to make a noise over an affair of that sort connected with her establishment.

He stood looking down, meditating. He was too proud and too honourable to entreat my silence where duty evidently commanded speech. I wished to do right, yet loathed to grieve or injure him.

Just then Rosine glanced out through the open door. She could not see us, but between the trees I could plainly see her; her dress was grey, like mine.

That circumstance, taken with certain previous transactions, suggested to me that perhaps the case—however deplorable—was one in which I was under no obligation to involve myself.

Accordingly I said, “If you can assure me that none of Madame Beck’s pupils are implicated in this business, I shall be very happy to stand aloof from all interference. Take the casket, the bouquet, and the billet; for my part, I gladly forget the whole affair.”

“Look there!” he whispered suddenly, as his hand closed on what I offered; and at the same time he pointed through the boughs.

I looked.

Behold Madame—shawl, wrapping-gown, slippers—softly descending the steps and stealing, cat-like, round the garden. In two minutes she would have been upon Dr. John.

If she moved like a cat, he, quite as much, resembled a leopard: nothing could be lighter than his tread when he chose. He watched; and as she turned a corner, he took the garden in two noiseless bounds. A leaf scarcely stirred; yet the air felt altered, as if the night itself had held its breath. She reappeared—and he was gone.

Rosine helped him, instantly interposing the door between him and his huntress.

I too might have got away; but I preferred to meet Madame openly. Though it was my frequent custom to spend twilight in the garden, never till now had I remained so late. I was sure she had missed me, had come in search of me, meant to pounce on the defaulter unawares. I expected a reprimand.

No.

Madame was all goodness. She offered not even a remonstrance; she testified no surprise. With that consummate tact—never surpassed, I think, by any living thing—she even professed merely to have issued forth to taste “la brise du soir.”

“Quelle belle nuit!” cried she, looking up at the stars—the moon was now gone down behind the broad tower of Jean Baptiste. “Qu’il fait bon! que l’air est frais!”

And instead of sending me in, she detained me to take a few turns with her down the principal alley. The gravel sounded softly under our steps; a distant carriage rolled somewhere beyond the walls with a dull, rich rumble; nearer, the leaves whispered and then were still again, as if listening too. At last we re-entered. She leaned affably on my shoulder by way of support in mounting the front-door steps; at parting her cheek was presented to my lips, and “Bon soir, my bonne amie; dormez bien!” was her kindly adieu.

I caught myself smiling as I lay awake and thoughtful on my couch—smiling at Madame. The unction, the suavity of her behaviour offered, for one who knew her, a sure token that suspicion of some kind was busy in her brain. From some aperture of observation—through parted bough, open window—she had doubtless caught a glimpse, remote or near, deceptive or instructive, of the night’s transactions.

Finely accomplished as she was in the art of surveillance, it was next to impossible that a casket could be thrown into her garden, or an interloper cross her walks to seek it, without her catching intimation—by shaken branch, passing shade, unwonted footfall, or stilly murmur. And though Dr. John had spoken low, the hum of his man’s voice seemed, to my sense, to pervade the whole conventual ground, altering the night’s soundscape as surely as a bell alters air.

What things were transpiring, she might not yet know; but a delicious little ravelled plot lay tempting her to disentanglement; and in the midst, folded round and round in cobwebs, had she not secured “Meess Lucie” clumsily involved—like the foolish fly she was?
