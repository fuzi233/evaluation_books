Some men turn hard and old at forty, while others carry the fire of youth right through their eightieth year. Margaret Van Eyck’s heart was an evergreen. She loved her young namesake, Margaret Brandt, with all the ardour of a girl half her age, and this fondness was no fleeting fancy: she had a sharp eye for character, and saw in the younger Margaret that rarest of qualities, that which wins the regard of an intelligent woman most of all in another of her sex: unshakable genuineness. But even beyond Margaret Brandt’s own sterling nature, there was a force in the old artist’s bosom pulling her close that neither she nor the girl ever spoke of out loud.

Human nature.

It is a truth that surprises most folk who never stop to observe their own hearts: we warm far faster to the people we have benefited than we ever do to the people who benefit us. Greek philosophers noted this thousands of years back, and the old British balladeer stamped it into immortal verse, when he wrote of the soldier who could not bring himself to kill his old comrade, not for any kindness the comrade had done him, but for all the kindness he had given the man:
“I heard, and thought how side by side
We two had stemmed the battle's tide
In many a well-debated field,
Where Bertram's breast was Philip's shield.
I thought on Darien's deserts pale,
Where Death bestrides the evening gale,
How o'er my friend my cloak I threw,
And fenceless faced the deadly dew.
I thought on Quariana's cliff,
Where, rescued from our foundering skiff,
Through the white breakers' wrath I bore
Exhausted Bertram to the shore:
And when his side an arrow found, I sucked the Indian's venom'd wound.
These thoughts like torrents rushed along
To sweep away my purpose strong.”

The assassin’s hand was stayed not by memories of favours received, but favours he had given.

Margaret Van Eyck had lavished care on Margaret Brandt: she had broken all her usual quiet routines to make the journey out to Sevenbergen to visit her, nursed her through the worst of her grief-stricken fever, soothed her, petted her, and pulled her through far better than any apothecary’s medicine could have done. No wonder her heart opened wide to the girl on the receiving end of all that goodness, till she loved her far more tenderly than she had ever loved Gerard, even though she had first gone to visit the girl purely out of fondness for him.

So when she saw the rose colour creep back into Margaret Brandt’s cheeks, and read the scrap of parchment that had proven Gerard was alive and on his way to Italy, she set aside her own long-laid plans without a single murmur.

“Sweetheart,” she said, “I had hoped he might stay in Italy five or six years, come back rich, and above all come back a master painter. But your happiness stands above all that, and I can see plain as day you cannot live a full life without him. So we will send for him home as fast as we possibly can.”

“Ah, madam! You see my very thoughts,” the young woman breathed, and hung her head, blushing deep. “But how do we get word to him? That is far beyond my wits. He has gone to Italy, but I do not know what part. Wait! He named the cities he planned to visit: Florence was one, Rome the next. But—” She paused, and the sensible girl in her quickly realised a letter addressed to “My Gerard, Italy” would almost certainly go astray, and she looked to her older friend with desperate, imploring eyes.

“You have come to exactly the right person, at exactly the right time,” the old lady smiled. “Hans Memling was with me only this morning. He is off to Italy no later than next week, he says, ‘to improve his hand’. And it could not come a moment too soon, I can tell you.”

“But how will he find my Gerard?” Margaret frowned.

“Why, he knows your Gerard, child! They have supped at my table together more than once, thick as thieves. Their work is the same, after all: he will visit every church, every artist’s workshop Gerard planned to see, and he is bound to cross paths with him sooner or later. So sit down and write him a long letter, copy out that pardon of ours that proves you are well and waiting for him, and I will answer for the messenger. In six months at the very farthest, Gerard will hold it in his hands. And when he does, you know he will kiss it, tuck it next to his heart, and fly home to you. What are you smiling at? And why are your cheeks so bright red, and why are you smothering me in hugs, silly thing? Happy days are coming to my little pearl at last.”

Down in the kitchen meanwhile, Martin Wittenhaagen sat by the fire with a black jack of ale before him, while Reicht Heynes spun at her wheel beside him, and pestered every last scrap of gossip out of him that night.

For all Margaret Van Eyck’s light gibe at his “need to improve his hand”, Hans Memling was a genuine painter, an old pupil of Jan Van Eyck and his sister, a good soul at heart who had only one real fault: he loved a draught of brown ale far more than it deserved. That soft spot kept him from ever amassing wealth, and he was forever popping back to Margaret Van Eyck’s door for a free meal, or a few small coins to tide him over. That meant she had a firm hold on his loyalty, and she knew full well he would never trifle with any commission she entrusted to him.

The letter was duly written, and left in Margaret Van Eyck’s care. The following week, Hans Memling turned up fresh from a trip through Flanders, and she pressed the letter into his hands, plus a piece of gold to cover his travel expenses. He was in such a tearing hurry to set off, she thought it a good sign: he would be in Italy all the faster, to deliver their news.

But horses that snort and rage to bolt before the start often slow to a crawl the second they are out of the gate. No sooner had Hans got as far as the main inn in Tergou, than he spotted two of his favourite drinking buddies sitting in the bay window. He stepped in for one parting glass, and before he knew what was happening, his friends insisted on treating him, then the landlord insisted on buying the next round, and by the end of the night his tongue was as loose as if the wine had been oil. He bragged to the whole convivial crowd that he was off to Italy to show the Italians how to paint, sang stories of his battles with a pike, recounted his romantic conquests (none of the women in question were there to contradict him), and spilled every little secret he had in no time at all. Before he knew it, he was blabbing that he was carrying a letter to a local lad, a good fellow named Gerard. He slapped Sybrandt, Gerard’s own brother, on the back so hard he nearly knocked the breath out of him.

Sybrandt slipped around the table to avoid the blow, and drank in every single word. It was the first time he had learned Gerard was in Italy at all. To make sure he had not misheard, he pretended to doubt it.

“My brother Gerard is never in Italy,” he lied.

“You lie, you cur!” Hans roared, instantly irascible from the ale, too far gone to realise the man he had just praised and whacked was the same one he was arguing with. “If he is ten times your brother, he is in Italy. Look here! Read that address!” He flung the letter down on the table.

Sybrandt picked it up, pretended to examine it gravely, then shrugged and said he could not read. But one man in the crowd, proud of his rare skill with letters, picked it up and read it out loud for all to hear: “To Gerard Eliassoen, of Tergou. These by the hand of the trusty Hans Memling, with all speed.”

“Beautifully written,” the reader marvelled, tracing each letter with his finger.

“Ay!” Hans declared, grandiloquent. “Small wonder! That writing comes from a famous hand: Margaret Van Eyck herself. Blessed be Jan Van Eyck’s memory! She is an old, old friend of mine.” He rambled off into forty different unrelated topics after that, none of them worth remembering.

Sybrandt slipped out of the tavern quietly, and ran to find his brother Cornelis. The two leant their heads together over the news. Italy was thousands of miles away. If they could just keep Gerard there forever?

“Nothing would keep him away from his Margaret for long,” Cornelis said, frowning.

“Curse her!” Sybrandt spat. “Why did she not die when she was so close to it?”

“She would outlive the plague just to spite us,” Cornelis grumbled, furious that the girl had the selfishness to refuse to die just to make their lives easier. The two black sheep of the Eliassoen family put their heads together, tainting each other’s malice more and more, until they hatched a plot: if Gerard stayed in Italy for good, they would never have to give him his fair share of their father’s inheritance. But for all their scheming, they had no idea how to pull it off, until it felt like Satan himself whispered an idea into both their ears at the same moment.

“THE BURGOMASTER!” they both shouted, at once.

They went straight to Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, who received them instantly. A man in the throes of torturous suspense will grab at any scrap of information, for certainty may be painful, but it is never half as unbearable as waiting.

“You have news of Gerard?” he asked, leaning forward, eager.

They told him all about the letter, and Hans Memling, off to Italy. His eyes darted restlessly as he listened. “Who wrote the letter?”

“Margaret Van Eyck,” they said, assuming the handwriting on the address must match the writing inside.

“Are you sure?” He pulled open a drawer, and drew out an old document Margaret Van Eyck had written when she was negotiating to buy property from the town. “Did it look like this?”

“Exactly the same writing,” Sybrandt lied boldly.

“Good. And what do you want of me?” the burgomaster said, his heart racing, but his manner so carefully careless it made the two brothers stagger. They fumbled with their bonnets, stammered, beat around the bush, and finally confessed they wanted him to write a false letter, something that would make Gerard stay in Italy, to swap it for the real one in Hans’s wallet.

While they hemmed and hawed, torn between respect for the burgomaster and the sneaking suspicion he was just as big a rogue as they were, thoughts raced through the old man’s sharp head: Vengeance said let Gerard come home, so he can feel the full weight of the law. Prudence said keep him a thousand miles away. Then prudence added: why do this dirty work for a gamble? Why let these two fools have the power to ruin your reputation? But the overpowering certainty that Gerard was holding a secret that could destroy him, mixed with his native caution, made him speak at last.

“It is my duty to help townsfolk who cannot write. But I will take no responsibility for what the letter says. Tell me what to write.”

“Something about that Margaret,” Sybrandt said.

“Ay! That she is false, that she has married another man, I’ll warrant you,” Ghysbrecht said, dry.

“Nay, burgomaster, nay! Not for all the world!” Sybrandt cried. “Gerard would never believe it, or only half believe it, and he would sail straight home to find out the truth. No. Tell him she is dead.”

“Dead? At her age? He will never credit it.”

“He will far sooner credit that than the other. She was at death’s door not long ago. So it is barely even a lie, after all.”

“Hmph. And you think that will keep him in Italy?”

“We are sure of it, are we not, Cornelis?”

“Ay,” Cornelis put in. “Gerard’s whole dream was to go to Italy. He would only ever come back for his Margaret, not for the likes of us. He despises his own family, always has.”

“It will be a bitter pill for him to swallow,” the old hypocrite said, his eyes glinting with hidden pleasure.

“It will be for his own good in the end,” Cornelis shrugged. “Famine does not care to wed Thirst, and Gerard will not bother coming back for us.”

“And the grief you two are planning for him, so coolly?” Ghysbrecht spoke sarcastically, but he was already tasting his own long-awaited vengeance.

“Oh, a lie is not like a blow from a battle axe,” Sybrandt said. “It cuts no flesh, breaks no bones.” He snuck a venomous little glance at the burgomaster’s famously broken nose.

Ghysbrecht’s face darkened with rage when the adder’s tongue struck that old, unhealed wound. It worked exactly as the brothers hoped: the old man bristled with hate.

“Very well,” he snapped. “Tell me what you want written, and I will write it. But mark this: you bear all the blame if anything goes wrong. It is not the hand that writes that commits the deed, it is the tongue that dictates it.” The brothers agreed eagerly, sneering behind their backs at how easy he was to manipulate. Ghysbrecht drew his inkhorn towards him, set the sample of Margaret Van Eyck’s handwriting in front of him, and asked a few questions about the size and shape of the real letter—when the door burst open.

Jorian Ketel stumbled into the room, looking furious that the burgomaster was not alone.

“Thou seest I have serious business on hand,” Ghysbrecht said, annoyed.

“Ay, but this is graver. I bring good news, but it is not for every pair of ears.”

The burgomaster stood, and pulled Jorian aside into the deep window embrasure, leaving the two brothers to hear the low, urgent hum of their conversation. Before long, Ghysbrecht sent Jorian out to saddle his mule. He turned back to the two brothers, his manner suddenly cold enough to make them shiver. “I prize the peace of households, but this is no business to rush into. We will see about it later. We will see.”

“But burgomaster! The man will leave any minute now! It will be too late!” Sybrandt protested.

“Where is he now?”

“At the inn, still drinking.”

“Then keep him drinking. We will see. We will see.” He sent the two confused, disheartened brothers on their way.

To understand why he changed his tune so abruptly, we must step back a few hours. That very same morning, young Margaret Brandt had run into Jorian Ketel outside her front door. He had scowled and tried to walk past her, but she had stopped him.

“Wait! You are the good man who saved Gerard’s life, are you not? Why have you never come to see me since? And why never came to collect the parchments? Was it not true about the hundred crowns I promised you?”

Jorian snorted, but when he saw her candid, open face, he realised there had been a terrible mistake. He told her how he had come to her house, and Martin had turned him away, and he had thought she was lying.

“Alas,” she said, “I knew nothing of that. I was lying at death’s door. Come, follow me.” She led him out into the garden, and showed him the spot where the parchments Gerard had hidden were buried. “Martin wanted to dig them up, but I would not let him. Gerard put them there, and I swore no one would move them but you, who earned them so well.”

“Give me a spade!” Jorian cried, overjoyed. “Wait—are you sure they are still there? He is a suspicious man, that burgomaster.”

“I will take full blame if any human hand has touched them,” Margaret swore.

“Then leave them just two more hours, I beg you, good Margaret!” Jorian yelped, and ran all the way to the Tergou Stadthouse a happy man.

Ghysbrecht rode along the road towards Sevenbergen, Jorian striding beside him, promising that in an hour’s time all the missing town parchments would be safely in his hands.

“Ah, master!” Jorian said. “Lucky for us it was not a thief who stole them.”

“Not a thief? What else would you call him?” Ghysbrecht grumbled.

“With all respect, I call him a jackdaw. This is jackdaw’s work, through and through: steal the thing you need least, and hide it away. I should know—I was raised side by side with a tame chough. We were born the same year, but he cut his teeth long before me, and for years my life was pure misery. If there was a hole in your hose no bigger than a groat, he would stick his beak in it like a gimlet. He would steal whatever the household needed most, even if he had no use for it at all. My mother lost her thimble every time she turned her back. My father, a cobbler, lost his awl, his wax, his twine every single morning. One day my mother told him to his face he would corrupt half a dozen children, and he cocked his eye at her, then stole the baby’s shoe right off its foot. That Gerard is exactly the same. Those parchments were no more use to him than a thimble is to a bird. He stole them for pure mischief, and hid them, and you would never have found them but for me.”

“I believe you are right,” Ghysbrecht said, relaxing. “I have fretted over this far more than I needed to.”

When they reached Peter Brandt’s gate, though, he grew uneasy. “I wish the parchments had been buried anywhere but here.”

Jorian reassured him. “The girl is honest and kind. She had nothing to do with stealing them, I swear.” He led the burgomaster into the garden. “There, master, if a face can be trusted, that is the spot.
