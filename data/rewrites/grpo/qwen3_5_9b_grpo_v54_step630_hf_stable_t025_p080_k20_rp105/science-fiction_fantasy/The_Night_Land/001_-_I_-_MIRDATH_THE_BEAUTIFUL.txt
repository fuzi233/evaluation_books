"And I cannot touch her face,
And I cannot touch her hair,
And I kneel to empty shadows—
Just memories of her grace;
And her voice sings in the winds
And in the sobs of dawn
And among the flowers at night
And from the brooks at sunrise
And from the sea at sunset,
And I answer with vain callings..."

It was the Joy of the Sunset that finally broke my silence. I had wandered far from my home, walking alone, pausing frequently to watch the Battlements of Evening pile themselves higher against the sky. I felt the strange, comforting weight of the Dusk settling over the world, a solemn joy that filled my chest until I thought I might laugh aloud in my throat, standing solitary amidst the twilight.

Then, from the trees bordering the road to my right, a voice answered me. "And thou also!" it said, with a glad understanding that made me laugh again, though only half-believing that any true human could share such a mood. It felt more like a sweet delusion, a spirit tuned perfectly to my own solitude.

But the voice spoke clearly, calling me by my name. I stepped to the side of the road to see who it was, expecting a phantom, but found a living woman. She was the Lady Mirdath, known throughout the County of Kent for her beauty, and a neighbor to me, for her guardian's estates bordered my own. Until this moment, we had never met; my life had been consumed by books and physical exercises, leaving me ignorant of the local gossip that painted her in such glowing terms. I was content with my studies, confident in my own strength and speed, believing no man could match me except in the pages of fiction.

Now, I stood instantly, hat in hand, answering her gentle banter as best I could while peering through the gloom at the stranger before me. Rumour had failed to capture her true essence. She stood jesting with a spirit so sweet, claiming a cousinhood that was, in fact, true, as the realization struck me. She made no fuss about it, addressing me frankly by my boyhood name and allowing me to call her Mirdath. She invited me through a secret gap in the hedge, a path she used when slipping away for country frolics disguised as a village maid—a ruse she admitted to, though I suspected she didn't fool many.

I climbed through the gap and stood beside her. She appeared tall, yet I was a head taller. She invited me to walk with her to the house so I might apologize to her guardian for my long neglect, her eyes shining with mischief and delight at my tardiness. Suddenly, however, she grew serious, raising a finger to hush me as she heard something in the nearby wood. A rustle of leaves, followed by the sharp crack of a dry twig in the stillness, confirmed her warning.

Three men burst from the trees. I shouted a warning, shielding Mirdath with my left hand and gripping my oak staff in my right. The attackers made no sound, rushing forward with the gleam of knives visible in the dim light. I moved with glad, brisk energy to meet them. Behind me, a shrill, sweet whistle pierced the air—Mirdath signaling her dogs, perhaps also summoning the household servants.

There was no time for reinforcements. The need was instant. I stepped forward, driving the end of my staff into the body of the man on the left, dropping him like a sack. I struck sharply at the head of the second, cracking his skull until he fell instantly. The third I met with a solid punch to the jaw; he staggered but did not require a second blow, collapsing to join his fallen companions. I laughed with a proper pride, noting the bewilderment on Mirdath's face as she regarded me through the hushed evening.

Before we could speak, three great boar-hounds bounded up, released by her whistle. She struggled to keep the brutes from attacking the men on the ground, while I beat them back to prevent them from mauling the wounded. Soon, shouts erupted, and the light of lanterns cut through the night as the footmen of the house arrived with cudgels. They hesitated, unsure whether to strike me or aid me, but seeing the men on the ground and recognizing my name, they kept their distance with evident respect. Mirdath, however, showed no desire to separate herself from me; instead, a deeper sense of kinship settled between us.

The servants asked how to handle the footpads. I left the matter to them, offering a purse of silver. They administered swift justice, and I heard their cries echoing long after we departed.

At the Hall, I was introduced to her guardian, Sir Alfred Jarles, an old and venerable man whom I knew slightly due to our neighboring estates. He thanked me with honorable courtesy, and Mirdath praised me, albeit quaintly. That evening, I dined and then returned to the grounds with Mirdath. She was more friendly than any woman had ever been, treating me as if we had known each other forever. I felt the same; it was as though we shared an innate understanding, finding common ground without surprise, only delight.

One thing captivated her that night: my ease with the three attackers. She asked plainly if I was truly so strong. When I laughed with youthful pride, she seized my arm to test it herself. She released it suddenly, gasping in astonishment at its hardness and strength. Afterward, she walked silently beside me, never straying far, seemingly thoughtful. If she found strange pleasure in my strength, I found constant wonder in her beauty, which seemed even lovelier in the candlelight.

Days brought further delights. We shared a unique bond, finding joy in the Mystery of the Evening, the Glamour of Night, and the Joy of Dawn. One evening, wandering the park, she half-unthinkingly declared it an "elves-night." She stopped abruptly, fearing I wouldn't understand, but I was on familiar ground. I replied calmly that the Towers of Sleep would grow that night, and I felt in my bones we might find the Giant's Tomb or the Tree with the Great Painted Head. I stopped suddenly; she gripped my arm, her hand shaking. Breathless, she urged me to continue. With half-understanding, I mentioned the Moon Garden, an old fancy of mine.

In truth, Mirdath cried out in a strange, low voice, halting me to face her. She questioned me earnestly, and I answered with equal intensity, suddenly excited to realize she knew these things too. She revealed she had knowledge of this strange dreamland, believing herself alone in the world with it, until now. The marvel of sharing such a secret world was overwhelming. She noted that this was likely why she had called to me on the road, though she had learned of our cousinship before, having seen me ride past often. Perhaps she had been daintily irked that I paid so little heed to Lady Mirdath the Beautiful. I had been distracted, but human enough to have noticed her had we met sooner.

I was utterly stirred by the wonder of our shared knowledge. Yet, upon closer questioning, I found much in my fancies foreign to her, and much in hers meaningless to me. Despite this, there was always a new thread one could pick up that the other could complete, bringing gladness and amazement to both. Hour by hour, we wandered and spoke, growing older in dear knowledge and sweet friendship.

Time passed in a blur until a hullabaloo of shouts, baying dogs, and lantern light interrupted us. Mirdath realized we had lost track of time, and her guardian, uneasy about the earlier footpads, had ordered a search. We turned homeward, but the dogs found us before we reached the lights. Having grown accustomed to me, they leaped about me friendly, and the men returned to report all was well to Sir Jarles.

From that day on, I walked the quiet country road every evening, entering by the hedge-gap. Often I found Mirdath walking in the woods, accompanied by her great boar-hounds. I had begged her to do this for her safety, and she seemed willing to please me, though she was often perverse, striving to plague me as if testing how far she could push my endurance.

I recall one night vividly. Approaching the gap, I saw two country maids emerging from Sir Jarles' woods. Ignoring them initially, I followed them when they curtseyed with exaggerated grace. I asked who they were; the taller one merely simpered and curtseyed again, leaving me in doubt but driven by wonder to follow. They moved swiftly and sedately, as if fleeing a rapist, leading me to the village green where a great dance was underway with torches, a fiddler, and plenty of ale.

They joined the dance, partnering only each other and carefully avoiding the torches. I was certain they were Mirdath and her maid. When they danced my way, I stepped over to ask for a dance. The tall one answered with a simper that she was promised, handing her hand to a great, hulking farmer-lout. She danced with him, using all her skill to avoid his clumsy steps, relieved when the dance ended.

I knew then, despite her disguise and the darkness, that it was Mirdath. I walked across, whispering her name, telling her to end this unwisdom and offering to take her home. She turned from me, stamped her foot, and returned to the lout. After another dance, she asked him to escort her part of the way, which he eagerly accepted. Another lad accompanied them. As soon as they moved out of the torchlight, the rough lads grabbed the women's waists, not caring who they held.

Mirdath could no longer endure. Crying out in sudden fear and disgust, she struck the roughman embracing her so hard he loosened his grip, swearing great oaths. He immediately returned, trying to kiss her. She loathed him to death, beating him madly in the face, but to no avail. I was close. She screamed my name. I caught the lout, hitting him once—not to harm him permanently, but to ensure he remembered me—and threw him into the side of the road. The second lout, hearing my name, fled.

I caught Mirdath by the shoulders and shook her soundly in my anger. I sent the maid on ahead; she went forward without instruction. Thus, we reached the hedge-gap, Mirdath very hushed but walking close to me, deriving some secret pleasure from my nearness. I led her through to the Hall, bidding her goodnight at a side door she held the key to. She replied in an utter quiet voice, lingering as if reluctant to leave.

The next morning, she was full of impudence. When I asked her why she persisted in her waywardness, denying my need for companionship, she became instantly gentle and winsome, sensing my weariness. She brought out her harp and played dear old melodies from our childhood, deepening my love for her. She walked me to the gap that night, accompanied by her three boar-hounds. I followed her silently until she was safely inside the Hall, sending the dogs away quietly. She sang a love-song all the way home, unaware of my presence. Whether she loved me, I could not tell, though she certainly had a nice affection for me.

On the following evening, I found a cleverly dressed man of the Court standing in the gap with Mirdath. He eyed me insolently when I approached. I lifted him aside. Mirdath turned bitter upon me, calling me uncouth and brutal to a smaller man. I felt a pang of astonishment and pain, realizing she had no true love for me, or she would not have shamed me so before a stranger. There was a semblance of justice in her words, yet the man could have shown more spirit, and she had no right to shame her true friend and cousin. I bowed low to Mirdath, then apologized to the man, acknowledging my lack of courtesy. I turned and walked away, leaving them to their happiness.

I walked twenty miles that night, unable to rest. Deadly in love with Mirdath the Beautiful, my spirit and body ached with the sudden loss. For a week, I walked in other directions. Eventually, I returned to the old path. As I approached the gap, I saw Mirdath walking just outside the great wood, with the Courtier's arm around her. They were lovers; she had no brothers or youthful male kin.

When Mirdath saw me, she shamed instantly, pulling the lover's arm away and bowing, her face changed in color. I bowed low, a young man myself, and passed on, my heart dead within me. As I walked, I saw the lover return to her, wrapping his arm around her again. They looked after me as I went stiff and desperate, but I did not look back.

For a month, I avoided the gap, my love raging and my pride hurt. Yet, in that time, my love acted as a leaven, creating a sweetness and tenderness unknown to me before. Love and Pain shape the character of man. By the end of that time, I saw a little way into life with an understanding heart and resumed my walks past the gap. Mirdath never appeared, though one evening one of her boar-hounds emerged from the wood to nose against me friendly-wise. I waited, but she did not appear, and I passed on, my heart heavy but free of bitterness.

Two weary weeks passed, and I grew sick with longing. Finally, I resolved to enter the gap and approach the Hall. One evening, I did so, walking to the gardens. Lanterns and torches illuminated a great company dancing in quaint dress. A horrid dread seized me: could this be her marriage dance? No, I reasoned; I had heard of no marriage. She was turning twenty-one, ending her wardship, and this was likely a festival in her honor.

It was a bright spectacle, though I was heavy-hearted. The company was gay, the lights plentiful, and a great table spread with silver and crystal. Mirdath stepped out of the dance, lovely but pale.
