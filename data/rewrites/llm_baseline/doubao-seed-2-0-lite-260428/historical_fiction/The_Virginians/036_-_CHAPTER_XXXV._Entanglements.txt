It was plain the good Colonel had taken counsel with his wife, and the two had resolved to spirit their little Hetty away from Tunbridge Wells as swiftly as possible, to draw her clear of the charming young stranger who had unwittingly stolen her heart. For men, the old remedy of absence and distance often cures such passing infatuations easily enough, but I have long observed that the “alibi treatment” rarely works half so well on women. Many of them will travel leagues and stay away for months, and still that quiet, obstinate fondness clings to them, unshaken by climate or miles apart. You may rebuke them, neglect them, even use them roughly, and still these soft, deluded creatures will hold fast to their fidelity.

Nay, after long and wide observation of human nature, I will go so far as to say that there are few surer ways to secure the adoration and loyalty of a woman than a judicious dash of occasional neglect, a brisk dose of indifference as an alterative, and only doling out small sips of fondness and attention at long, sparing intervals. Too much love, too constant, quickly loses its flavour: the wives most indifferent to their husbands, the ones who yawn in their faces and prefer the company of fools to their adoring partners, are almost always the women cloyed to sickness on the sugarplums of overindulged devotion. But do not mistake me, dear ladies – I speak only in jest! I would never advocate anything approaching harshness towards your sex, and will say plainly that you are a hundred times more faithful and tender than our own rough, fickle kind.

Which brings us back: what, then, is the use of the Lamberts hurrying Hetty home, if the little maid will love Harry Warrington just as far away as she did when he was at hand? She will not make a scene at their parting, of course. She is far too ashamed of her secret to betray it, and Harry is far too preoccupied with his own affairs to guess it exists at all. He never sees the quiet grief lingering under her smiles, the sorrow hidden in every sidelong glance. He imagines her happy to get back to her music, her poultry, and her little rose garden, and does not even trouble to ride a mile along the road with their departing carriage. By the time he returns from his own afternoon engagements, the Lamberts are gone from Tunbridge Wells entirely: their windows stand open, and a card stuck in one of the panes announces the apartments are once more to let. A brief, passing twinge of sorrow at the empty rooms crosses his mind, but by four o’clock he is seated at dinner at the White Horse, calling loudly for his bottle.

That very same hour, when the Lamberts stop to sleep at their friend’s house in Westerham, poor little Hetty chokes on her tea. When she wakes the next morning, her cheeks are pale as wilted roses, and dark circles ring her eyes. It was the thunder, she tells her family. The night was so hot, she could not sleep. She will feel better, she swears, when they reach home the next day. When they do, she passes the gate where Harry fell from his horse, the bed he lay in while recovering, the chair he sat in by the parlour fire – and it feels like a hundred years have passed in the space of a few short weeks. She looks at the little girl who used to run through the garden calling her chickens and watering her flowers, and feels like a stranger to that old Hester Lambert. In one single night of quiet heartache, she has lived long, long years that no medicine of poppy or mandragora will ever wash into the soft, easy sleep she knew before Harry Warrington came into her life.

Down at the Tunbridge Wells assembly rooms, Maria Esmond watches the Lambert carriage roll away, and lets out a grim little sigh of relief. When Harry comes into his aunt’s card room later, flushed with good wine from the White Horse, he laughs gaily at Madame Bernstein’s teasing about which of the Lambert girls is his sweetheart, and says he loves them both like sisters. He cannot imagine a finer gentleman than the Colonel, and says the Duke is so fond of him he ought long ago to have been made a general. The Baroness teases him that he ought to make interest with the Countess of Yarmouth, the King’s favourite, to push for the promotion – the Countess is half German, and Madame Bernstein mimics her thick accent for Harry’s amusement, and crows that he carries off the hearts of all the old women in high society. “You do, don’t you, Harry?” she smirks, glancing at her niece Maria, who flinches as if stabbed by the remark. “You play cards like a grand seigneur, you never cheat – not that I blame anyone for a little cheating when they can get away with it, all of us do it, I and Maria included. But you might do very well to lose a few hundred to Lady Yarmouth gently, to win her favour. Why should Your Excellency not be made Governor of Virginia one day, eh?”

Harry asks why his aunt cannot introduce him at court herself, and the old lady’s rouged cheek flushes uncharacteristically. “I am not in favour at Kensington,” she says. “I may have been once, but there are no faces kings hate to see more than the ones they wish to forget. We all have little accounts we wish we could wipe off the slate, don’t we, Harry?” Both Harry and Maria turn bright red at that line, and the Baroness lets out one of her sharp, unpleasantly knowing laughs. Both of them have secrets they would rather keep hidden, secrets that would shock polite society if they were dragged to light.

Harry’s secret, for one, is heavy on his mind. For three days now, he has missed a little leather pocketbook he knows he had in his possession the night he got very drunk at the White Horse, and his servant Gumbo carried him up to bed. No one has seen it: his servants, the inn staff, none of them can find a trace. He dare not advertise for it, dare not make any public fuss, for the pocketbook is stuffed full of Lady Maria Esmond’s love letters. Pages and pages of giddy, overwrought professions of eternal fidelity, family secrets the Esmonds never dreamed of sharing, sharp, snide remarks about half the people they meet in society, her complaints of his attentions to other women – the whole foolish, tender farrago that every woman has written once in her life, before she married the man she eventually settled down with, and would die before letting anyone else read. Imagine if all your old love letters, the ones you wrote to your Edward before you broke off the engagement and married Mr. Jones, were laid out on the drawing room table for your husband to read? You would not like it one bit, no more than Lady Maria did. I have resolved, for my own part, never to write an unkind word about anyone ever again, lest my own old letters fall into the wrong hands. All my characters shall be perfect angels, every woman chaste and beautiful, every man wise and generous, when this book is done.

But for now, we are dealing with men and women who have not yet grown their angel wings, all of them with little failings of their own. Madame Bernstein has fallen asleep in her armchair after dinner, a little overstuffed with food and wine, Mr. Harry Warrington has wandered off to play billiards with Count Caramboli, and the chaplain Mr. Sampson is whispering softly to Lady Maria, careful not to wake the old Baroness. He remarks that Harry is wasting his youth in idleness, that a man of his fortune and station ought to find a place at court, take up a post in the service, and choose a wife of suitable rank. He mentions Harry’s distant Warrington cousins in Suffolk, the old knight Sir Miles, who has a daughter just about Harry’s age and reputed to be very beautiful, and Maria flushes bright red, and snaps that she sees no point in Harry mixing with those dull Suffolk country boobies who spend all their time drinking beer and hunting foxes.

Sampson leans in closer, his voice very soft. “I fear Mr. Warrington is in trouble, your ladyship. Six days ago, the night he dined at the White Horse and drank too freely, he lost a pocketbook full of letters.”

“Letters?” Maria gasps.

“Very private letters,” Sampson nods gravely. “He has been in a terrible state about it, making quiet inquiries. Gracious – your ladyship, are you unwell?”

Maria lets out three shrill, piercing screams, and tumbles straight off her chair in a dead faint.

The noise wakes Madame Bernstein from her nap, and she staggers to her feet on her tortoiseshell cane, flushed and wild-eyed from her dreams of old court days. “What have you done, sir?” she snarls at Sampson. He puts on a perfect look of innocent alarm, swears he did nothing but mention the lost pocketbook of letters, and the lady fainted. The Baroness dashes cold water on her niece’s face, until Maria lets out a feeble moan and begins to come around.

Her aunt glares down at her, grim and unforgiving. “You know exactly what has happened,” she says, her voice sharp as a knife. “The Esmond family curse, is it not? Letters lost, secrets out, the same old foolish story we have lived through a dozen times before! I thought you were wiser than to throw yourself away on a boy young enough to be your son. You will not rob Henry Esmond’s boy of his fortune and his youth, not while I am alive. I will not leave you a single shilling of my money if you ruin him.”

Maria, who has always been so meek and obedient to her aunt, suddenly flares up in defiance. “I do not care one penny for your paltry money! Harry Warrington has given me his word, and he has written me letters of his own. He will die before he breaks his promise to me.”

The old Baroness pleads with her, offers her five thousand pounds from her own personal fortune, right now, if she will only release Harry from his promise. Maria laughs in her face. She will not take it, she says. She has Harry, and she has ten times five thousand pounds to look forward to. She does not mind waiting – she is no silly young chit in a hurry for a husband, she can wait patiently, through whatever years it takes, for Harry to be hers. “If my letters are published to the world tomorrow, what of it?” she cries. “They hold secrets about the whole Esmond family, not just mine. I do not fear them. I have Harry’s word, and I trust it.” And she flings out of the room, just as Sampson comes back with the doctor, leaving Madame Bernstein shaking so badly she needs a full glass of cordial to steady herself, shocked to her very core by just how deep this entanglement runs.
