About a week later, the two friends sat side by side at their easels, yet their spirits were worlds apart. Pietro worked with erratic bursts of energy; he would dash wildly at his canvas, accomplishing wonders in mere moments before collapsing into silence, only to resume his labor in a fresh rage and finally lay down his brush with a heavy groan. Throughout this tempestuous display, the shrewd Dutchman remained unflappable, working steadily and offering calm smiles. Plainly speaking, Gerard—who possessed the rare ability to master every man he befriended—had successfully harnessed Pietro's Onagra. Together, they were painting playing cards, a venture designed to "boil the pot."

Once finished, the indignant master took up his completed work to begin his daily rounds in search of customers. Gerard earnestly begged him to take the deck of cards as well, urging him to attempt a sale. Pietro initially struck a pose of pure venom, but eventually, embracing Gerard in the traditional Italian style, he relented. First, however, he dried the freshly finished cards in the fierce sunlight of that blessed climate. Left alone, Gerard sketched a few Greek letters and then repaired a small tear in his hose.

His landlady discovered him thus occupied and inquired with ironic amusement whether there were no women in the house. "When you have finished that," she said, "come and speak with Teresa, my friend—the woman I mentioned to you, whose husband is hardly worth much, yet boasts of his acquaintance with the great."

Gerard descended the stairs, and who should meet him but the Roman matron. "Ah, Madam," he exclaimed, "is it you? The good lady told me nothing of this. And the little fair-haired boy—is he well? Is he none the worse for his voyage in that strange boat?"

"He is well," replied the matron.

"Why, what are you two discussing?" asked the landlady, staring alternately at them with wide eyes. "And why do you tremble so, *Teresa mia*?"

"He saved my child's life," Teresa declared, struggling to compose herself.

"What!" cried the landlady. "My lodger? And he never told me a single word of such a thing! Are you not ashamed to look me in the face?"

"Alas, speak not harshly to him," the matron interceded gently. She then turned to her companion and poured forth a glowing account of Gerard's conduct. During this recital, Gerard stood blushing like a maiden, scarcely recognizing his own actions, as gratitude painted them in hues far more beautiful than reality.

"And to think you should ask me to serve your lodger, of whom I knew nothing save that he bore your good word, oh, Fiammina! That was sufficient for me. Dear youth, in serving you, I serve myself."

It was followed by an eager discourse between the two women regarding how to breach the thick wall of fees, commissions, and legal cunning that separated the patrons of art from an unknown artist in the Eternal City. Teresa smiled sadly at Gerard's naivety in having left specimens of his skill at the doors of the mighty without proper introduction.

"What!" she exclaimed. "Without promising the servants a share—and without even tipping them to allow the Signori to view your merchandise! It might as well have been cast into the Tiber."

"Well-a-day!" sighed Gerard. "Then how is an artist to find a patron? For artists are poor, not rich."

"By seeking a city nobler and less greedy than this," Teresa advised. "*La corte Romana non vuol' pecora senza lana*" (The Roman court does not want sheep without wool). She fell into thought and promised to return the next day. The landlady congratulated Gerard immediately upon her departure. "Teresa has something in her mind," she remarked.

Scarcely had Teresa gone when Pietro returned with his picture, looking black as thunder. Gerard exchanged a glance with the landlady and followed him upstairs to offer comfort.

"What! Have they allowed you to bring home your masterpiece?"

"As heretofore."

"More fools they, then."

"That is not necessarily the worse."

"Why, what is the matter?"

"They have bought the cards!" Pietro shouted, hammering the empty air furiously left and right.

"All the better," Gerard replied cheerfully.

"They flew at me for them! They were enraptured. They tried to conceal their longing, but could not. I saw, I feigned, I pillaged; curse those simpletons!" He threw a dozen small silver coins onto the floor, leaped upon them, dancing with basilisk-like eyes, kicking them assiduously until they spun and scattered across the room.

Down went Gerard on his knees, following the maltreated innocents directly, transferring them tenderly into his purse.

"You should rather smile at their ignorance and put it to profit," he said.

"And so I will," Pietro answered, his voice thick with concentrated indignation. "The brutes! We shall paint a pack a day; we shall set the whole city gambling and ruining itself, while we live like princes upon its vices and stupidity."

"There was one of the Queens, though," Pietro continued, his tone softening. "I had fain have kept back. 'Twas you who painted her, brother. She had lovely red-brown hair and sapphire eyes, and above all, a soul."

"Pietro," said Gerard softly, "I painted that one from my very heart."

The quick-witted Italian nodded, his eyes twinkling mischievously. "You love her so dearly, yet you leave her."

"Pietro," Gerard replied, "it is precisely because I love her so dear that I have wandered this weary road."

This poignant conversation was abruptly interrupted by the landlady calling from below, "Come down, you are wanted." He descended, and there stood Teresa once more.

"Come with me, Ser Gerard."
