The church wall bore the scorch marks of letters, a supernatural exclamation asking why no mass was held for the Duke during this time of turmoil. The spectacle had drawn a crowd, their faces fixed in gaping wonder until the phenomenon faded, leaving only the silence of the stone. Yet, the Corporation, unwilling to lag behind divine loyalty to a temporal sovereign, promptly commissioned masses. From this pious transaction, the Cure, an old friend of ours, extracted a handsome sum—a fact he certainly did not regret, given his discerning taste for hard cash. But I shall not detain you with such trivial miracles.

Denys pleaded for his arms. "Why disgrace him as well as break his heart?" he asked.

"Then swear on the cross of thy sword not to leave the bastard's service until the sedition is quelled." Reluctantly yielding to necessity, Denys delivered three volleys of oaths, reclaiming his weapons and his liberty.

The troops halted at an inn known as "The Three Fish." Marion, seeing him, cried out, "I'm out of luck; who would have thought to see you again?" Noticing his somber demeanor rather than amusement at her blunt jest, she inquired what troubled him. He confessed his plight. She, however, took a bright view of the case. Gerard was too handsome and well-behaved to come to harm; moreover, the women would always be on his side. She reasoned that things must either go well or ill. If well, he would join a company bound for Rome; if ill, he would return home, perhaps arriving before his friend, for he had fighting to do in Flanders by all accounts. She then produced gold pieces, which he steadfastly refused, urging her repeatedly to keep them. Denys was somewhat convinced by her logic, for she concurred with his own wishes, and he found comfort in her honesty. It made him think a little better of that world in which his poor little friend walked alone.

Small detachments of foot soldiers, reduced to pairs and trios, were already straggling toward Flanders, many penniless, passing from town to town under the bailiffs' orders for food and lodging. Anthony of Burgundy overtook these scattered groups, gathering them under his standard until he entered Flanders at the head of six hundred men. Upon crossing the frontier, he was met by his brother Baldwyn, bringing men, arms, and provisions. Anthony organized his force and marched in battle array through several towns, meeting not only no impediment but great acclamations. This display of loyalty drew comments that were not altogether gracious.

"This rebellion of ours is a bite," growled a soldier named Simon, who had elected himself Denys's comrade.

Denys said nothing, but made a silent vow to St. Mars to shoot Anthony of Burgundy dead should the rebellion, which had cost him Gerard, prove anything other than a rebellion.

That afternoon, they came in sight of a strongly fortified town. A whisper ran through the little army that this was a disaffected place. Yet, when they drew near, the great gate stood open, and the flanking towers were manned by a single sentinel apiece. The advancing force broke formation, marching carelessly forward.

Within a furlong, the drawbridge across the moat rose slowly and creaking until it stood vertical against the fort. The very moment it settled into this warlike attitude, the portcullis rattled down at the gate, and the towers and curtains bristled with lances and crossbows. A stern hum ran through the Bastard's front rank and spread to the rear.

"Halt!" cried he. The word traveled down the line, and they stopped. "Herald to the gate!" A pursuivant spurred out, halted twenty yards from the gate, raised his bugle with the herald's flag hanging round it, and blew a summons. A tall figure in brazen armor appeared over the gate. Fiery words passed between him and the herald, audible only in their import: the herald blew a single keen, threatening note at the walls and galloped back with war in his face. The Bastard moved out of the line to meet him. Their heads had not been together two seconds ere he turned in his saddle and shouted, "Pioneers, to the van!" In a moment, hedges were leveled, the force took the field, and encamped just out of shot from the walls. Mounted officers flew south, east, and west to friendly towns for catapults, palisades, mantelets, raw hides, tar-barrels, carpenters, provisions, and all materials for a siege.

The bright perspective mightily cheered one drooping soldier. At the first clang of the portcullis, his eyes brightened and his temples flushed. When the herald returned with battle in his eye, he saw it instantly, and for the first time in many days cried, "Courage, tout le monde, le diable est mort."

If that great warrior heard, how he must have grinned!

The besiegers encamped a furlong from the walls and made roads. They kept their pikemen in camp ready for assault and sent forward their sappers, pioneers, catapultiers, and crossbowmen. These opened the siege by filling the moat, mining, or breaching the wall. Since much of this work had to be done under close fire of arrows, quarrels, bolts, stones, and rocks, these artists "had need of a hundred eyes." They acted in concert with a vigilance, individual intelligence, daring, and skill that made a siege very interesting, even amusing to lookers-on.

First, they advanced their carpenters behind rolling mantelets to erect a stockade high and strong on the very edge of the moat. Some lives were lost, but not many; for a strong force of crossbowmen, including Denys, rolled their mantelets up and shot over the workmen's heads at every besieged who showed his nose, and at every loophole or aperture commanding the spot. Covered by this condensed fire, they soon raised a high palisade between themselves and the ordinary missiles from the pierced masonry.

But the besieged expected this. At night, they ran out their boards or wooden penthouses on top of the curtains. The curtains were built with square holes near the top to receive beams supporting these structures—the true defense of medieval forts. From here, the besieged delivered missiles with far more freedom and variety of range than they could shoot through the oblique but immovable loopholes of the curtain or the sloping crenelets of the higher towers. On this, the besiegers brought up mangonels, setting them hurling huge stones at these woodworks and battering them to pieces. Contemporaneously, they built a triangular wooden tower as high as the curtain, keeping it ready for use just out of shot.

This was a terrible sight to the besieged. These wooden towers had taken many a town. They began to mine underneath the part of the moat the tower stood frowning at, making other preparations to give it a warm reception. The besiegers also mined at another part, aiming to get under the square barbican and throw it down. All this time, Denys was behind his mantelet with another arbalestrier, protecting the workmen and making excellent shots. These ended by earning him the esteem of an unseen archer, who every now and then sent a winged compliment quivering into his mantelet. One struck within an inch of the narrow slit through which Denys was squinting.

"Peste," cried he. "You shoot well, my friend. Come forth and receive my congratulations! Shall merit such as thine hide its head? Comrade, it is one of those cursed Englishmen, with his half-ell shaft. I'll not die till I've had a shot at London wall."

On the side of the besieged was a figure that soon attracted great notice by promenading under fire. It was a tall knight, clad in complete brass, carrying a light but prodigiously long lance, with which he directed the movements of the besieged. When any disaster befell the besiegers, this tall knight and his long lance were pretty sure to be concerned in it.

My young reader will say, "Why did not Denys shoot him?" Denys did shoot him; every day of his life; other arbalestriers shot him; archers shot him. Everybody shot him. He was there to be shot, apparently. But the abomination was, he did not mind being shot. Worse, he became so demoralized as not to seem to know when he was hit. He walked his battlements under fire, as some stout skipper paces his deck in a suit of Flushing, calmly oblivious of the April drops falling on his woollen armor. At last, the besiegers grew spiteful and would not waste any more good steel on him, cursing him and his impervious coat of mail. He took those missiles like the rest.

Gunpowder has spoiled war. War was always detrimental to the solid interests of mankind. But in old times it was good for something: it painted well, sang divinely, furnished Iliads. But invisible butchery, under a pall of smoke a furlong thick, leaves no one the better for it. A poet with his notebook may repeat, "Suave etiam belli certamina magna tueri"; but the sentiment is hollow and savors of cuckoo. You cannot "tueri" anything but a horrid row. He didn't say, "Suave etiam ingentem caliginem tueri per campos instructam." They managed better in the Middle Ages.

This siege was a small affair, yet a writer or minstrel could see it and turn an honest penny by singing it; so far, the sport was reasonable and served an end.

It was a bright day, clear but not quite frosty. The efforts of the besieging force were concentrated against a space of about two hundred and fifty yards, containing two curtains and two towers. One was the square barbican; the other had a pointed roof built to overlap, resting on a stone machicolade, by which means a row of dangerous crenelets between the roof and the masonry grinned down at the nearer assailants, looking not unlike the grinders of a modern frigate with each port nearly closed. The curtains were overlapped with penthouses somewhat shattered by mangonels, trebuchets, and other slinging engines. On the besiegers' edge of the moat was what seemed at first sight a gigantic arsenal, longer than it was broad, peopled by human ants and full of busy, honest industry, displaying all the various mechanical science of the age in full operation. Here the lever at work, there the winch and pulley, here the balance, there the capstan. Everywhere heaps of stones, piles of fascines, mantelets, and rows of fire-barrels. Mantelets rolled, the hammer tapped all day, horses and carts in endless succession rattled up with materials. Only, on looking closer into the hive of industry, one might observe that arrows were constantly flying to and fro, that the cranes did not tenderly deposit their masses of stone but flung them with an indifference to property, though on scientific principles, and that among the tubs full of arrows, tar-barrels, beams, fagots, and other utensils, here and there a workman or soldier lay flatter than is usual in limited naps, with something more or less feathered sticking in him, and blood and other essentials oozing out.

At the edge of the moat opposite the wooden tower, a strong penthouse, which they called "a cat," might be seen stealing toward the curtain, gradually filling up the moat with fascines and rubbish which the workmen flung out at its mouth. It was advanced by two sets of ropes passing round pulleys, each worked by a windlass at some distance from the cat. The knight burnt the first cat by flinging blazing tar-barrels on it. So the besiegers made the roof of this one very steep and covered it with raw hides, so the tar-barrels could not harm it. Then the knight made signs with his spear, and a little trebuchet behind the walls began dropping stones just clear of the wall into the moat. At last they got the range, and a stone went clean through the roof of the cat, making an ugly hole.

Baldwyn of Burgundy saw this, losing his temper, ordered the great catapult battering the wood-work of the curtain opposite to be turned and leveled slantwise at this invulnerable knight. Denys and his Englishman went to dinner. These two worthies, eternally on the watch for one another, had made a sort of distant acquaintance and conversed by signs, especially on a topic that in peace or war maintains the same importance. Sometimes Denys would put a piece of bread on the top of his mantelet, and then the archer would hang something of the kind out by a string; or the order of invitation would be reversed. Anyway, they always managed to dine together.

Now the engineers proceeded to the unusual step of slinging fifty-pound stones at an individual. This catapult was a scientific, simple, and beautiful engine, very effective in vertical fire at the short ranges of the period.

Imagine a fir-tree cut down and set to turn round a horizontal axis on lofty uprights, but not in equilibrium; three-fourths of the tree being on the hither side. At the shorter and thicker end was fastened a weight of half a ton. This butt end, just before discharge, pointed toward the enemy. By means of a powerful winch, the long tapering portion of the tree was forced down to the very ground and fastened by a bolt; the stone placed in a sling attached to the tree's nose. This process, of course, raised the butt end with its huge weight high in the air, keeping it there struggling in vain to come down. The bolt was now drawn. Gravity, an institution which flourished even then, resumed its sway. The short end swung furiously down, the long end went as furiously round up, and at its highest elevation flung the huge stone out of the sling with a tremendous jerk. In this case, the huge mass missed the knight but came down near him on the penthouse, going through it like paper and making an awful gap in roof and floor.
