By six of the evening Florence had begun to breathe again, as a cook breathes when the oven-door is shut at last and the long roast is safely in the dark heat. The entry of the new Charlemagne—so the flatterers named him—was fairly over; and those who had been stationed high on turreted roofs, where the city’s stone ribs rose into air, had had their fill of magnificence. From that height the procession had uncoiled along the Pisan road like a bright, dangerous river laid upon the valley’s green; drums had rolled as steadily as pestles in a mortar, trumpets had blared, and the tramp of horses had beaten the earth in thick, impatient measures. The bells, caught in the same fervour, flung their excited peals across the roofs until every narrow street seemed to ring like metal struck too often.

There was no obliging sunlight to turn banners into flame or plumes into spun gold. Yet the very lack of glitter made the thing more distinct—no veil of dust rose to soften edges—and when the picked troops came close they showed their several nations as plainly as different grains in one sack. Tall, tough Scotch archers; Swiss halberdiers, heavy and grim as if carved from mountain stone; nimble Gascons ready to wheel, to climb, to spring; cavalry wherein each rider looked like an errant knight with spear set and charger champing. It was a comfort—comfort being the word the prudent always seek—to repeat to oneself that such strength would injure nobody but the enemies of God.

With that assurance the eye could take its pleasure in the rest: nobles and knights and pages of chosen lineage; sword-hilts bossed and jewelled; satin scarfs embroidered with devices that pretended at once to piety and love; gold chains lying on brocade as fat as poured honey; jewelled aigrettes trembling above brows; horse-trappings stiff with gold thread; mantles that seemed to have drunk whole vats of dye. And over the head of the Most Christian King a canopy, borne by select youths, rose and swayed—its embroidery and pearls catching the lamplight of the day as a glazed tart catches firelight. To sum it with an old diarist whose spelling lagged behind his wonder: it was great magnificence.

For the Signoria, standing on their platform against the gates, grandeur had come mixed with the taste of bitterness. Their orator, Messer Luca Corsini, had had his Latin welcome marshalled and rehearsed like a recipe memorised to the syllable; but when rain began—cold, sour rain that slicked plumes and set horses stamping—his well-studied periods broke off and ran together like ink in wet parchment. Men and beasts grew impatient; the press of bodies jostled dignities out of their places; and the learned city, shamed out of its own language, was forced into a makeshift welcome in hurried French.

In that sudden confusion Tito Melema found the sort of moment he could use. As one of the secretaries he stood behind the Signoria among officials, those lesser personages who are flung together like trimmings when a great dish is served and all hands crowd the table. “Somebody step forward and say a few words in French,” Soderini said, his temper clipped short by the rain.

No one of high importance wished to risk a second stumble.

“You, Francesco Gaddi—you can speak.” But Gaddi, mistrusting his own readiness, shrank back. He thrust Tito forward instead, as one pushes a nimble boy toward a task when the older man fears his own hands. “You, Melema.”

Tito stepped forward in an instant, with that air of profound deference which came as naturally to him as walking. He spoke the few needful words in the name of the Signoria—no more, no less, as neatly portioned as a careful cook’s pinch of salt—and then, with the grace of one who never seems to hurry though the world hurries him, he gave way and let the king pass on.

His presence of mind, which had failed him in the terrible crisis of the morning, served him this time like a well-trained livery servant—faithful enough when danger was not visible. When men complimented him afterwards on the service, he laughed it off as nothing; and to those who had not witnessed it he let Gaddi have the credit. Tito was popular for the same reason sweetmeats are popular: they flatter the palate. The touchstone by which men try us is most often their own vanity.

Other things, too, had turned out worse than the ingenious preconceptions. The Florentine procession of clergy and laity, that had been meant to meet the king in orderly splendour, found its way choked and was driven to a makeshift course through back streets, so that the encounter could happen only at the Cathedral. And if the young monarch under the canopy, seated on his charger with his lance along his thigh, had looked more like a Charlemagne and less like a hastily modelled grotesque, the imagination of his admirers would have been better served. One might have wished that the scourge of Italian wickedness and “Champion of the honour of women” had had a less miserable leg, and only the common sum of toes; that his mouth had been less reptilian in its width of slit; his nose and head less exorbitant in outline. But the thin leg rested on cloth of gold and pearls; the face was only a brief interruption—some inches of flesh—amid black velvet and gold and the blaze of rubies and the brilliant tints of the embroidered, bepearled canopy. Great magnificence still.

The people had cried Francia, Francia, with an enthusiasm measured by the canopy’s splendour; and, according to immemorial custom, they tore that same canopy to pieces for spoil as greedily as children tear sugared bread, each eager for his own sweet fragment. Royal lips had kissed the altar as due. At last, after mishaps, the royal person and retinue were lodged in the palace of the Via Larga; the rest of the nobles and gentry were dispersed among Florence’s great houses; and the terrible soldiery were encamped in the Prato and other open quarters. The business of the day was ended.

But the streets, under the November stars, had an aspect Florentines scarcely knew. Instead of the usual gloom—broken only by a feeble lamp here and there before a saint’s image at a corner, or a redder stream of light from an open doorway—lamps hung in the windows of all houses. Light lay along the stones so that men walked as securely as by day. Yet light did not quiet the city’s ear. Under that illumination every sound declared itself: footfalls were crisp; distant laughter rose and sank; the murmur of talk drifted out of doorways; and, threaded through it all, the persistent after-ringing of bells seemed to live in the bones of the houses. There are nights when a city’s noises nourish the spirit, like hearth-crackles and a spoon against a bowl; and there are nights when the same noises, multiplied by excitement, become a kind of coarse grinding that leaves the mind raw. Florence had the second sort of music now: splendour lay on the surface, but the soundscape was bruised by power.

Along those illuminated streets, at about eight, Tito Melema walked homeward. He had been exerting himself throughout the day under the pressure of anxieties kept hidden like a knife in one’s sleeve; and at last he had slipped away unnoticed from after-supper gaiety. Now, with leisure to face his circumstances, he hoped to adjust himself to them and to probabilities until he could rid himself of what he called childish fear.

If only he had not been wanting in the presence of mind necessary to recognise Baldassarre in that morning’s surprise—if only he had been able to meet the apparition with a steadier face—it would have been happier for him on all accounts. For he still winced under the thought that he was deliberately inflicting suffering on his father; in his easier, more pleasant imaginings he would have preferred Baldassarre prosperous and content, like an old vine that has done its bearing and is allowed to rest. But he had left himself no second path now. There could be no longer any conflict. The one thing to do was to take care of himself.

He was advancing from the Piazza di Santa Croce along the Via dei Benci, his mind half full of calculations that had no taste or scent—mere dry measures—when, nearing the angle that turned into the Borgo Santa Croce, his ear was struck by music that was not evening revelry but vigorous labour: the music of an anvil.

The hammer’s rhythm cut cleanly through the city’s other sounds, as if it insisted on an older law than bells and trumpets. Tito gave a slight start and quickened his pace. The sounds had suggested a welcome thought. He knew them. They came from the workshop of Niccolò Caparra, famous resort of Florentines who cared for curious and beautiful iron-work. What made the giant at work so late? But so much the better; Tito could do that small bit of business tonight instead of tomorrow morning.

Preoccupied though he was, he could not help pausing a moment as he came before the shop. The wide doorway stood at the truncated angle of a great block of houses, surmounted by a little loggia roofed with fluted tiles and supported by stone columns with roughly carved capitals. Within, the red light—thick, hot, like the glow seen through the slit of a bread-oven—framed the black relief of Niccolò himself. The smith’s grand figure rose and fell in rhythmic labour; his huge arms lifted, descended; and each time the hammer fell it seemed to drive not only the metal but the night’s very din into order. Two slighter ebony figures—one at the anvil, one at the bellows—set off Niccolò’s massiveness as thin herbs set off a joint of meat.

Tito darkened the doorway with a very different outline, standing silent, for it was useless to speak until Niccolò should deign to pause. That was not until the smith had beaten the head of an axe to its due sharpness and dismissed it from the anvil. In the meantime Tito, with a glance round, satisfied himself that the object he sought had not disappeared.

Niccolò turned, rested his hammer on his hip, and gave an unceremonious but good-humoured nod. “What is it, Messer Tito? Business?”

“Assuredly, Niccolò; else I should not have ventured to interrupt you when you are working out of hours, since I take that as a sign that your work is pressing.”

“I’ve been at the same work all day—making axes and spear-heads,” Niccolò said, and the words came out with the bluntness of struck iron. “And every fool that has passed my shop has put his pumpkin-head in to say, ‘Niccolò, wilt thou not come and see the King of France and his soldiers?’ and I’ve answered, ‘No: I don’t want to see their faces—I want to see their backs.’”

“Are you making arms for the citizens, then, Niccolò, that they may have something better than rusty scythes and spits in case of an uproar?”

“We shall see. Arms are good, and Florence is likely to want them. The Frate tells us we shall get Pisa again, and I hold with the Frate; but I should be glad to know how the promise is to be fulfilled if we don’t get plenty of good weapons forged? The Frate sees a long way before him; that I believe. But he doesn’t see birds caught with winking at them, as some of our people try to make out. He sees sense, and not nonsense.” Niccolò’s bellows-man worked the handle with a steady creak between phrases, like an accompaniment. “But you’re a bit of a Medicean, Messer Tito Melema. Ebbene! so I’ve been myself in my time, before the cask began to run sour. What’s your business?”

“Simply to know the price of that fine coat of mail I saw hanging up here the other day. I want to buy it for a certain personage who needs a protection of that sort under his doublet.”

“Let him come and buy it himself, then,” Niccolò said, blunt as ever. “I’m rather nice about what I sell, and whom I sell to. I like to know who’s my customer.”

“I know your scruples, Niccolò. But that is only defensive armour: it can hurt nobody.”

“True: but it may make the man who wears it feel himself all the safer if he should want to hurt somebody. No, no; it’s not my own work; but it’s fine work of Maso of Brescia. I should be loth for it to cover the heart of a scoundrel. I must know who is to wear it.”

Tito saw there was no kneading this man into a softer shape. “Well, then, to be plain with you, Niccolò mio, I want it myself,” he said. “The fact is, I am likely to have a journey to take—and you know what journeying is in these times. You don’t suspect me of treason against the Republic?”

“No, I know no harm of you,” Niccolò answered, with the same blunt fairness. “But have you the money to pay for the coat? For you’ve passed my shop often enough to know my sign: you’ve seen the burning account-books. I trust nobody. The price is twenty florins, and that’s because it’s second-hand. You’re not likely to have so much money with you. Let it be till to-morrow.”

“I happen to have the money,” Tito said. He had been winning at play the day before, and his purse was still heavy. “I’ll carry the armour home with me.”

Niccolò reached down the finely wrought coat. It fell together into little more than two handfuls, supple as a net drawn up from water, each ring catching a brief spark from the forge-light. Tito felt, even through his gloves, the faint chill of metal that had been hanging away from the fire: a coldness like night caught in iron.

“There, then,” Niccolò said, when the florins had been told down on his palm. “Take the coat. It’s made to cheat sword, or poniard, or arrow. But, for my part, I would never put such a thing on. It’s like carrying fear about with one.”

The words struck Tito with an unpleasant exactness. Fear—named aloud—seemed suddenly louder than the anvil’s beat, as if the city’s whole clamour had condensed into that one syllable. But he smiled, and his smile had the practiced ease of one who seasons bitterness with a little sweetness so others will swallow it.

“Ah, Niccolò, we scholars are all cowards. Handling the pen doesn’t thicken the arm as your hammer-wielding does. Addio!”

He folded the armour under his mantle and went quickly out again, crossing the Ponte Rubaconte while the lamps burned above the street and the night’s noises—bells remembered, feet, distant cries, the far-off clink of weapons being made—followed him like a second garment.
