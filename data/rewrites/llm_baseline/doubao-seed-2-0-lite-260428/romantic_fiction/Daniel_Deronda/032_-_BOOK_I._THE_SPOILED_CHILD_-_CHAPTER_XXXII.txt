Every generation has clung to the favorite old truth: a love fierce enough to rearrange your whole world is no rational choice, but a kind of fated isolation that makes all your long-held opinions and steady resolutions feel like strangers to you. Daphnis raving with passion would never have been cured by mastering Heraclitus’s philosophies; Tristan, drugged by that magic love philtre, would have had all his logic fray to threads no matter how deep his study of Duns Scotus; and Romeo, falling for Juliet the second he saw her, would not have paused to argue over Ptolemaic astronomy while he stood beneath her balcony. Yet not all powerful love burns that blind, unconnected flame. It can weave itself into every last corner of the soul, tie itself to hopes you never knew you held, and echo the grand, ancient orbits of all that has been and all that will be.

When Deronda returned to London, he could assure Sir Hugo that he had left Grandcourt in no doubt: he could walk away from his distant, far-from-certain inheritance for a guaranteed fifty thousand pounds. There was no clear sign yet that Grandcourt would take the offer, but it was obvious he wanted to keep their dealings friendly.
“And what did you make of the future bride, seeing her up close?” Sir Hugo asked, half teasing.
“I liked her far better than I did in Leubronn,” Deronda answered. “Roulette was never the right setting for her — it pulled out something sharp, almost demon-like, under that cool self-possession. At Diplow she seemed far softer, warmer, less guarded. There was a whole different light in her eyes, a different tenderness to the line of her mouth.”
“Don’t you go flirting with her too much, Dan,” Sir Hugo laughed, playful as ever. “If you rile Grandcourt up when they come to the Abbey for Christmas, it’ll muck up all my plans.”
“I can stay in town for the holidays, sir,” Deronda said evenly.
“No, no — Lady Mallinger and the children couldn’t bear Christmas without you. Just don’t stir up trouble — unless you can engineer a duel and shoot Grandcourt, in which case a little inconvenience would be well worth it.”
“I don’t think you’ve ever seen me flirt,” Deronda said, not amused.
“Oh, haven’t I though?” Sir Hugo prodded, delighting in his annoyance. “You look at every woman so soft-eyed, talk to them in that gentle, persuasive Jesuit way. You’re a dangerous young man — the sort of Lovelace who makes all the Clarissas chase you instead of you chasing them.”
It was useless to be furious at a tasteless joke, of course — but the anger always hits before you remember how little sense your irritation makes. Few friendly remarks sting worse than being told you always look like you’re doing something you never once intended to do. Sir Hugo’s definition of flirting had to be wildly peculiar, Deronda told himself; he knew for a fact he had never flirted in his life. He was only relieved the baronet knew nothing about him buying back Gwendolen’s lost necklace, which would have given him endless ammunition for this sort of teasing.
He resolved to be far more careful going forward — especially on his first visit to Mrs. Meyrick’s, now he was back from the continent. Mirah was the kind of creature it was nearly impossible to greet without letting tender interest show in every look and every word.
Mrs. Meyrick had written to keep Deronda updated on Mirah’s wellbeing at their home. “We fall more in love with her every single day,” her letter ran. “At breakfast we all turn to the door, waiting for her to walk in, and we watch and listen to her like she’s a visitor from some brand new, undiscovered country. I haven’t heard a single word from her that makes me doubt a thing about her. She’s perfectly content, and brimming over with gratitude. My girls are learning so much from her, and they’re trying to find her more pupils — she’s determined not to eat idle bread, to work for her living just like the rest of us. Mab says our whole life feels like a fairy tale now, and her only fear is that Mirah will turn back into a nightingale and fly away. Her voice is perfect: not loud or harsh, but searching, melting, like the memory of all the best old times. That’s the kind of voice us old folks melt for.”
She didn’t write to mention the quiet awkwardness Amy and Mab had felt when they’d accompanied Mirah to the synagogue. Their favorite Jewish heroine was Scott’s Rebecca, and seeing Mirah’s real faith didn’t sit quite so easy with them — but they held their tongues out of respect, for Mirah’s religion was such a tender, fragile thing to her no one would dare poke at it carelessly. After a few days, though, practical, reformer Amy couldn’t hold the question in any longer.
“Forgive me for asking, Mirah,” she said gently, “but does it feel right to you, that the women have to sit separated behind a railing in the gallery?”
Mirah blinked, soft and surprised. “I never thought anything else of it. It’s all I’ve ever known.”
“And — do you really like seeing all the men keep their hats on during service?” Mab asked, testing the smallest possible point of difference.
“Oh yes,” Mirah said. “I love everything I’ve always seen in that place. It brings back all my oldest, most precious feelings — feelings I wouldn’t trade for anything in the whole world.”
After that, not a single one of the generous Meyrick girls would dream of criticizing any part of her faith. It was stitched straight into the fibre of her heart, never a set of dry rules or propositions to her.
“She says she’s a very bad Jewess, and barely knows half of her people’s beliefs,” Amy whispered later, when Mirah had gone to bed. “Maybe it’ll just fade away slowly, and she’ll become Christian like the rest of us, if she grows to love us enough, and never finds her mother. It feels so strange, to follow the Jewish faith in this day and age.”
“Oh no no no!” Mab wailed. “I wish I wasn’t such a clumsy, terrible Christian! How could an ugly, fumbling Christian who’s always dropping her sewing ever convert a perfect, beautiful Jewess who doesn’t have a single fault?”
“I know it’s wicked,” sharp little Kate said, “but I can’t help hoping her mother is never found. There might be something awful waiting for her out there.”
“Nonsense, my dear,” Mrs. Meyrick said, shooting her girls a playful, mischievous glance. “I’d wager Mirah is exactly the spitting image of her mother. What a joy it would be for that woman to get her lost daughter back! I suppose none of you think a mother’s feelings are worth counting for anything — a dead mother is so much more convenient than a live one, isn’t it?”
“Fair point, little mother,” Kate laughed. “We’d far rather value you cheap and keep you alive forever.”
Neither the Meyrick girls — who’d scraped together all their scattered knowledge through odd books and eager, independent foraging, as clever young women so often had to do — nor Deronda himself, with all his fine, privileged education, had ever stopped to realize how little they knew of modern Judaism, of the living, beating inner history of the Jewish people. The Chosen People had always been treated like a group selected for everyone else’s story, their thoughts a thing that should have been entirely something else. Deronda, like every other gentleman he knew, had always written Judaism off as some eccentric, fossilized old form of faith that no cultivated man needed to bother studying, leaving it all to specialists. But Mirah — running terrified from one cruel parent, and aching with every fibre of her to find the other — had flashed on him the long-forgotten truth: Judaism was something alive, throbbing in real human lives, the very framework that made the whole world make sense for them. He had caught that new curiosity while wandering on the lazy holiday he’d taken with Sir Hugo before heading home, stopping every time he saw a synagogue to peer through its doors, hunting down books about Jewish history to bring back with him. That awakening, that shift from smugly assuming you already know everything about a subject you never cared about, to suddenly caring deeply, to realizing all your old opinions were just ignorance, was the best cure for boredom there could ever be — the kind no doctor could write you a prescription for. It made those weeks of aimless lounging feel full of purpose.
It was on that journey that he first walked into a synagogue, in Frankfurt, when their party stopped there on a Friday. He’d seen the Juden-gasse, the old Jewish quarter, years before, remembered its picturesque twisty old houses — but now he didn’t stare at the architecture. He stared at the people, his thoughts linking every face to all the centuries of their race’s long history, stirring that deep historical sympathy in him that had always shaped his quiet, gentle character. When a young man is handsome, well-mannered, well-educated, and comfortably off, no one ever bothers asking what he actually thinks, right? They’ll happily write him off as a pleasant, clever young fellow without ever digging into his deeper convictions. Only when he gets older, a little slovenly, a little stout, do people start noticing his peculiarities, and count themselves lucky if those peculiarities aren’t offensive. But anyone who wants to understand how future events shape Deronda has to know who he really was at twenty-five, far beneath the surface of polite small talk.
His vivid, sharp impressions of the world had always made him seem like an enigma to his friends, his feelings feeling vague and unmoored to them. His early-woken sensitivity had bloomed into a wide, all-encompassing sympathy that threatened to stop him from ever committing to a firm, clear path. The second he tried to take a firm stance against anything, his imagination filled with the other side’s humanity — he felt like the Sabine warriors of old, about to throw his spear at nothing but the flesh of his own flesh, people he could find something to love in. He was so used to seeing the world the way it must look to every other person that strong, blind partisanship felt like a lie to him. His soft, flexible sympathy blended with his overactive reflective mind to dilute that sharp indignation against wrongdoing that moral force depends on. He was one of the least vice-touched young men alive, but he hated vice gently, seeing it not as some abstract evil, but as a flaw in a messy, individual human life that deserved understanding and pity. He was fiercely democratic in his care for ordinary working people, but intensely conservative too, tied by affection and memory to old long-sanctioned traditions no logic could ever make him let go of. He devoured every book on government and religion he could find, but shrank from letting go of those old familiar forms full of happy memories. He couldn’t help loving the world’s losing causes, he realized, and shrank from the bitter triumph of people who chased success at any cost, turning their backs on everyone who lost. That over-wide, over-reflective sympathy was in danger of paralyzing him entirely, robbing him of the ability to stand firm against cruelty, to choose a clear circle of fellowship to stand beside. In the last few years, he’d become so sharply aware of this flaw that all he ached for was some external event, some sudden flash of insight, that would pull him out of his drift, compress all his wandering, scattered energy into a single clear path. He no longer cared about hoarding knowledge, or taking up a random respectable career, unless both could flow alongside his deepest emotions. He dreaded that hollow, dead sort of intellectualism, the kind that knows every single trivial fact about a violet’s chemistry except what a violet actually smells like. He refused to live his life without making some small, real difference for the better — but he couldn’t find the road. He blamed his upbringing, the strange, unmoored circumstances of his birth that had laid no special demands on him, given him no fixed certainties to build his life around. He was drifting into a meditative numbness, he knew, slipping further and further away from that life of passionate, practical good he’d always dreamed of as the only life worth living. He wanted to hold onto those soft, sacred feelings that make life taste worth living, even when his overactive mind threatened to grind everything down into meaningless dust. That quiet undercurrent hummed beneath everything he did, as he pretended to study law, or make polite dinner conversation.
As he wandered the warm Frankfurt evening, heading for the old orthodox synagogue, that imaginative rush of connection to all that long-forgotten history drowned out the small, ugly little annoyances he met on the way. He’d stopped at an old bookshop to ask the time of the service, and a precocious, friendly Jewish boy had enthusiastically directed him to the old traditional Rabbinical school synagogue, not the fancy new Reform temple — before charming him out of an extra thaler for a very common book, insisting it was “very rare and hard to find.” Across the shop counter, two grizzled, shabbily dressed peddlers in long coats and wide low hats yelled back and forth to each other in Yiddish. Deronda laughed at himself for his silly earlier alarm that Mirah’s unknown relatives would all be somehow crass or unpleasant; no group of people had a monopoly on guile or rough edges. He paid his overpriced bill, turned his back on the bookshop, and walked into the old synagogue just as the sun set, where a full congregation of men had gathered for the service.
He happened to sit a little distance away from an elderly man with a full white beard, whose fine sharp profile could have easily been Italian as it was Hebrew. The man glanced at him again and again until their eyes met; Deronda looked away, not wanting to intrude, only to find an open prayer book gently pushed across the bench toward him. He bowed his thanks. The congregation settled, the reader climbed the raised platform, and the service began. Deronda flipped through the German translation of the Hebrew text, saw they were chanting psalms and old scripture passages, and gave himself over to the raw, overwhelming emotion of the chanted liturgy — the same feeling you get listening to Allegri’s Miserere or Palestrina’s Magnificat, that sears through even if you don’t understand a single word. It was that wordless yearning to escape the limits of your own small weakness, to invite all goodness into you and let it stay — that selfless lifting of joy that cries Gloria in excelsis that such goodness exists. That feeling drew all its power from the knowledge that every note he heard had been sung by generations of struggling people before him. The deep rolling voice of the chazan, the high sweet notes of the little boys’ choir, all the men swaying back and forth in prayer, the plain shabbiness of the small building where a faith that had shaped half the world’s religion still echoed in a quiet forgotten corner — all of it blended for Deronda into one single expression of a shared history, tragic and glorious at once. He was shocked by the strength of his own feeling, it felt like a divine presence filling the dark room before he even had words to explain it.
When the last note of the chant died away, and he looked around at all the indifferent, ordinary faces, a cold thought darted through him: he was probably the only person in the whole congregation who had felt that deep surge of feeling, everyone else had just been going through dull, mindless routine. He had just bowed to his kind neighbor and started to move toward the door when he felt a firm hand on his arm. He turned, that sharp unpleasant jolt of being grabbed by a stranger running through him, and saw the white-bearded old man leaning in. “Excuse me, young gentleman,” he said in soft German. “Allow me — what is your parentage? What is your mother’s family name, her maiden name?”
Deronda flinched, pulling his arm sharply away from the man’s touch. “I am English,” he said coldly.
The stranger stared at him doubtfully for a long moment, then just lifted his hat and turned away. Deronda walked back to his hotel, telling himself he could not have acted any differently. How could he possibly admit to a total stranger that he did not even know his own mother’s maiden name? The man had been wildly out of line, asking such a personal question unprompted, no doubt just struck by some random passing resemblance that meant nothing at all. The incident was trivial, he told himself — but his sharp, instinctive dislike of the question meant he never mentioned it to the Mallingers, alongside his usual habit of keeping his private enthusiasms to himself. Sir Hugo was one of the kindest men alive, especially to women, doing deeds others would call wildly romantic — but he never saw those deeds as romantic, and always laughed at grand, far-off motives or convictions that flew far beyond polite everyday life. That was the biggest difference between him and Deronda, who could never get through a breakfast at the table without letting his mind wander off to grand, quiet thoughts that had nothing to do with the casual polite conversation around him.
Those Frankfurt memories were still bright and humming in his mind, stoked by his determination to do right by Mirah. That stranger’s question about his parentage, which he had so quickly shaken off as trivial, left him newly resolved to be extra careful when tracking down Mirah’s missing family. If he uncovered something cruel and ugly, was he obligated to tell her, and destroy the peaceful, happy new life she had built with the Meyricks?
He arrived at their house at four o’clock, the time he had written to announce, and found Mirah sitting at her work near the open piano, only Mrs. Meyrick and Mab in the room, surrounded by their walls of old beautiful engravings. Her hair was neat and soft, her dress spotless and simple, her face glowing with a quiet, radiant happiness that a painter could have copied straight to put in the middle of a nativity scene, right next to the angels singing of peace on earth. Mirah immediately thought of how different she looked from the desperate girl Deronda had found on the river bank, and spoke first the second they greeted each other.
“See how different I am from that miserable creature by the water? All because you found me, and brought me to the very best people alive.”
“It was only luck that I was the one who found you,” Deronda said gently. “Any other good man would have done exactly what I did.”
“That’s not the right way to think about it,” Mirah said, shaking her head with soft, unshakable gravity. “I don’t think about what any other man might have done. I think about what actually happened. It was you. No one else.”
