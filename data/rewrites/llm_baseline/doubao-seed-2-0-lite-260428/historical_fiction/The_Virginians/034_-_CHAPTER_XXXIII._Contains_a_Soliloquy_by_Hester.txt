The instant Martin Lambert, Colonel in his Majesty’s army, realized the small secret his youngest daughter’s trembling outburst had laid bare, his first surge of feeling was wrath towards the unknown lad who had stolen his Hetty’s heart right out from under his roof and out of the little family circle he had guarded so long. “A murrain on all scapegraces, be they English or heathen Virginian!” he roared to his wife that evening. “I wish this one had dashed his nose against any doorpost in the kingdom but ours.”

His gentle spouse Molly only smiled, laying a soft hand on his arm. “Perhaps it is our calling to cure him of being a scapegrace, my dear. That fall he took on our threshold feels almost providential, does it not? You laughed at me when I said so before, but if Heaven did not send that young gentleman to us, who did? It may well prove a blessing for every last one of us, that he came.”

“Ah, but it’s hard, Molly!” the Colonel groaned. “We cherish them, tend them through every fever, scrape and save every penny patching our own old coats so they might have what they want. We lie awake all night if they so much as complain of a headache, work from dawn till dark to grant every silly little fancy they take, and they call us their dearest pappy, their darling daddy, swear no father in the world is half so good. Then come Tuesday morning, I am king of my own house. By Tuesday evening some Prince Whippersnapper saunters through the door, and my reign is over. A whole lifetime of love and nurture is forgotten in a trice, all for a pair of blue eyes, a pair of lanky shanks, and a head of flaxen hair.”

“Is it not written that a woman shall leave all she knows to follow her husband?” Molly reminded him, her cheeks dimpling. “Our own courtship was scarcely three months long, you will recall, dear Martin.”

“Human nature, the jade, what can you do?” the Colonel sighed. “I do not deny I was over the moon when you left your father for me,” she added softly. “And you did your duty right well, my excellent wench! Perdition catch my soul, but I do love thee still, Molly!” the Colonel laughed, squeezing her hand. “But mark you: your father hated me, and rightly so, for stealing his treasure. And if I ever do get sons-in-law, I shall hate every last one of them, just as your father hated me.”

“Do not be absurd, Martin Lambert! Of course my girls will have husbands one day!” the matron cried, half indignant, half amused.

“Nay, my dear, think of the old rotten tooth in my left jaw,” he reasoned. “It is perfectly natural for that tooth to fall out eventually. But when the dentist yanks it, it is also perfectly natural for me to yell with pain. Do you suppose I love Hetty any less than that tooth, or that this does not hurt ten times worse?”

No woman, of course, ever truly resents the thought of her daughter marrying, no matter how fathers stamp their feet and rage at the son-in-law invading their home. Mothers and grandmothers live their own courtships all over again through their girls: they pull out the old laces and muslins packed in lavender at the back of their wardrobes, see the postillion’s white ribbons fluttering once more, and climb back into the postchaise for their own wedding day all over again.

“It will be a sad thing, parting with her,” Molly sighed.
“So you have already settled that, have you?” the Colonel teased. “Had I better head out to the grocer’s and order raisins and currants for the wedding cake right now?”
“And then I shall have to sail all the way to Virginia to visit her when she goes,” Molly went on. “How many miles is it to Virginia, Martin? Thousands, I should think.”
“A hundred and seventy-three thousand three hundred and ninety-one and three quarters by the nearest route, my love,” he replied gravely. “The one that cuts straight through Prester John’s kingdom. The other way takes you all around Persia—”
“Then give me the route with the least sea possible, I beg you! I cannot abide those horrid pitching ships!” Molly protested. “I only hope Rachel Esmond Warrington and I shall be better friends than we were at school. She had a very high spirit, that girl, back then.”
“Had we not best start stitching the baby-linen already, Mrs. Lambert?” her husband interjected, wide with mock wonder. Molly, for her part, saw no cause for wonder at all—she had already spied the prettiest lace caps and bibs in Mrs. Bobbinit’s toy shop down the high street.

That same Sunday afternoon, when Hetty lay on her pillow with flushed feverish cheeks and eyes puffed from crying, her mother looked down at her with perfect calm, even a quiet pleasure, at the girl’s woe. Hetty was not only heartsick, she was furious at herself for letting her secret slip. She had not even named the feeling to herself before that sudden outburst laid it bare, and now she burned with shame as if she had committed some terrible crime and been caught red-handed. She raged at her own weakness, swore she would never forgive herself for such a humiliation. Like a young panther struck by the hunter’s dart, she snapped and snarled at everyone around her, lashing out at her sister, her parents, anyone who dared come near.

“What business has any young woman indulging in this silly nonsense?” she cried, voice cracking. “Mamma, I ought to be whipped and sent straight to bed. I know perfectly well Mr. Warrington does not care a fig for me. I dare say he far prefers French actresses, or the little milliner girl at the toy shop, and he is right to. They are far better than me. What a fool I am, to burst out crying like a ninny over nothing, just because Mr. Wolfe mentioned that Harry played cards of a Sunday! I know he is not half so clever as papa—he is stupid, I am certain he is stupid—but he is not half so stupid as I am. Of course I can never marry him. How could I leave you and Theo and go all the way to America? Of course he has some sweetheart back in Virginia, or Tunbridge, or Jericho, somewhere. He is a great estate heir in his own country, he would never look twice at a poor half-pay officer’s daughter with not twopence to her name. You used to tell me I cried as a baby wanting the moon. I am still that silly baby. Do not talk to me, Mrs. Lambert, I am. And the best part is, he does not know a single thing about this, and I would rather cut my tongue clean out of my head than tell him.”

She threatened poor Theo with all sorts of dire punishments if her sister dared betray her secret. Little Charley, meanwhile, was entirely preoccupied with cheese-cakes and had not even noticed her outburst, and of course her parents and Theo all swore they would not breathe a word of it.

“I almost wish we had never come to Tunbridge Wells at all,” Molly sighed to her husband later that night.
“Nonsense, my dear,” the Colonel said. “Human nature will be human nature. Hetty’s own mother told me she had a little fancy for a young curate long before she fell head over heels in love with me. I myself had my heart pricked a dozen different ways before you took possession of it entirely. Our children must follow the same path we walked, I suppose. And to do the girl credit, she is disguising her feelings far better than you might expect. I defy Mr. Warrington to guess the state of her heart from the way she behaves to him.”
“A daughter of ours would never fling herself at any gentleman’s head!” Molly declared with great dignity.
“Nor fling a teacup at him either, my dear,” the Colonel laughed. “That girl treats Harry Warrington like a little vixen. Every time he calls, she boxes his ears one way or another. She is barely civil to him. And knowing what I know, I would never dream of scolding her for it.”
“She has no need to be rude at all,” Molly huffed. “Our Hetty is good enough for any gentleman in England or America. If their ages suit, why should they not marry after all?”
“Why should he not ask her himself, if he wants her?” the Colonel shrugged. “Half of me wants to hitch the horses to the carriage right now and drive us all home this very night.”
“Depend upon it, Martin, these things are wisely ordered for us,” Molly said softly. “Harry Warrington did not turn up at our gate half-drowned for no reason. If that marriage is decreed in Heaven, a marriage it shall be.”

The Colonel only shook his head, smiling at his wife’s eternal matchmaking. “Well,” he said, “you know full well if any of our children wanted our ears served up in a fricassee, we would cut them off and hand them over.” Molly laughed at the absurd image, and he leaned over to tweak her soft little ear, streaked now with just a few silver threads from decades of shared joy and sorrow.

That evening, Harry Warrington arrived not just for tea, but to invite the entire Lambert family to a special dance he was hosting at the Assembly Rooms, in their exclusive honor.
“A dance, for us?” Theo cried, clapping her hands. “Oh Harry, how delightful! I wish we could start this very minute!”
“For a wild Virginian, you are the most civilised young man I ever met,” the Colonel teased. “My dear, shall we dance a minuet together ourselves?”
“We have done exactly that before, Martin Lambert!” his wife laughed. The Colonel hummed a stately minuet tune, snatched a plate from the tea table to use as a pretend hat, bowed and flourished it while Molly dropped her deepest, most graceful curtsey.

Only Hetty sat glum and frowning in the corner.
“Have you no word of thanks for Mr. Warrington, child?” Theo asked her.
“I never cared much for dancing,” Hetty mumbled. “What is the point of standing opposite some stupid man and parading down a room with him?”
“Mercy, thank you for the compliment!” Harry said, confused.
“I did not say you were stupid—only, I meant country dances!” Hetty snapped, biting her lip when she caught Theo’s amused glance. She remembered she had just told herself Harry was stupid a few hours prior, and her sister’s gentle reminder made her bristle as if Theo had screamed a terrible rebuke.
“I hate dancing, there, I own it!” she tossed her head.
“But you loved it only last year!” her mother interposed.
“That was when I was a child! Clearly I have grown into an old crone now,” the Colonel joked. “Or perhaps Miss Hester has got the gout?”
“Fiddle!” Hetty snapped, stamping her small foot.
“What is a dance without a fiddle?” her unflappable papa grinned.

Harry’s face fell. He had come all the way over to make them happy, and the girl he liked best was telling him she hated dancing, and mocking every last guest and pastime at the Wells. He left that evening sore and confused, and when he found Molly alone for a moment, he asked what he could possibly have done to offend Hetty so. Molly’s heart softened for the honest lad even more, and she was sorely tempted to spill Hetty’s secret right then and there.

Theo cornered Hetty in their shared bedroom later, after their parents had said goodnight, to remonstrate with her sister for her rudeness. But Hetty would not hear a word of it, pacing the floor, cheeks flaming, as she poured out the soliloquy that churned in her heart:
“Suppose he hates me now? I fully expect he does. I hate myself. I scorn myself for being such an idiot. Why should he not hate me? I abused him, called him a goose, every cruel name I could think of, all the while knowing full well he is not stupid at all. I know I have sharper wits than he does. I only like him because he is tall, and has blue eyes, and a pretty nose. What an utter fool a girl must be to like a man just because he has a pretty face! I am a fool, and you cannot say a single word to the contrary, Theo. I said all those horrid things to him just to hurt myself, to punish myself the way I deserve. It made me glad for a moment to see him angry, because then he would think Hetty Lambert is a sour, ill-tempered little vixen, and that would prove to everyone that I am not chasing after Mr. Harry Warrington. Our papa is ten times better than he is. I will stay here by papa forever. Even if Harry asked me to run off to Virginia tomorrow, I would say no. You are worth more to me, my Theo, than every last Virginian that ever drew breath on this earth. If you liked him, I would never breathe a word of my own feelings. I would even get a rope ladder to help you elope with him, I would fetch the parson myself to marry you two, and then I would live alone forever. I would stay here and take care of papa and mamma, look after all the poor folk in the village, read all the tedious sermons I hate, and die without telling a single soul my secret. I know I will die young, anyway.”

The two sisters clung to each other and kissed, just as their mother knocked softly at the door, having overheard a snippet of their talk through the wainscot, to tell them it was long past time to sleep. Theo’s eyes closed almost at once, and she drifted off to rest. But poor Hetty tossed and turned for hours, wide awake, the new ache in her chest throbbing sharp every time she thought of how she had treated Harry. Only when the first pale streaks of dawn spilled over the window did she finally fall asleep, curled up tight against her sister’s side, a faint stain of dried tears glistening on her soft flushed cheek.

Most of us play with sharp, edged tools when we are young, and cut ourselves as a result. At first the cut stings so bad we drop the knife and wail like wounded babies. A very small, unlucky few stab themselves so deep the wound proves mortal, and they perish young. But heaven be thanked, most of us who get pricked by Love’s sharp arrows, scarred and tattooed all over with little heart wounds, heal in time, and go on to live full, lively lives. We too have tasted earthly joy, we too have lived, and gone on. By the time you are older, you will look back on a first sentimental disappointment and never once think you are at risk of an early grave. Let us trust there is no need for a sexton anywhere near Miss Hetty. But the very moment she wakes, that small sharp Care that pricked her last night will still be there, tearing gently at her little heart, softened just a little by her youth and her tears.
