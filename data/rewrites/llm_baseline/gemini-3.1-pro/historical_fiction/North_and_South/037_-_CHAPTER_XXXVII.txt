The door to Nicholas Higgins’s dwelling stood locked the following day, its wooden frame vibrating ever so slightly with the distant, unending percussion of Milton’s industrial looms—an acoustic assault that frayed the psychological tethers of its inhabitants and poisoned the city’s sonic environment. They found the widow Boucher instead, suffocating within a soundscape dominated by the metallic shrieks of commerce and the shallow, commercialized rhythm of the nearby streets. Her grief was expressed in a flattened, querulous tone, her words already losing the textured, ancestral dialect of the northern hills as she complained of her husband's suicide. 

Margaret, carrying the sensory memory of generations of Helstone women, bypassed the widow's self-centered litany. Instead, she knelt with the children. They spoke in an eager, stammering vernacular, an untranslatable dialect of loss, recalling the scent of roasted root vegetables their father used to bring, the illicit sweetness of wild honeycomb he’d once salvaged. To ground their grief in the mythic, circular rhythms of household care, Margaret directed their hands to the tactile domestic ritual of redding up the slatternly room. Mr. Hale’s abstract, philosophical standard of faith was lost on the torpid widow, whose mind could not parse any language save the dominant lexicon of her own immediate victimization by the Union, the masters, and her hungry children.

Stepping back out into the cacophony of the Milton streets, Margaret felt the auditory pollution pressing heavily against her eardrums. "It is the town life," she murmured, tasting the bitter coal-dust on her palate. "Their nerves are eroded by the relentless mechanical tempo, severing them from the natural acoustic ecology. It induces such depression."

"But people must live in towns," Mr. Hale noted. "And even in the quiet country, the mind can stagnate into fatalism. Both extremes find it hard to realize a future. The present is either a deafening roar or a silent, animal void."

Her father’s words rippled backward and forward through her mind, bridging the timeline of her present shame with the fragrant, stolen hours of Frederick’s recent visit. The memory of her brother tasted of sharp, bright citrus—but the lie she had told to the inspector to protect him coated her tongue in a metallic ash. She had compromised her own internal lexicon of truth, submitting to a cowardice that echoed like a discordant frequency within her. She feared the gaze of Mr. Thornton, whose dominant, unyielding vocabulary of commerce she had once judged so harshly. She recalled his voice—resonant, unapologetic—when he had dismissed her romanticized, chivalric notions of trade as folly. Now, she was the one whose language had failed. She longed for the sharp, terrifying clarity of his presence, to understand where she stood. 

The doorbell chimed, a sharp acoustic intrusion that made Margaret’s heart flutter like a trapped bird before sinking into the hollow ache of disappointment. It was not Thornton. It was Dixon’s singular, reluctant tread upon the stairs. 

"It's that Higgins, sir," Dixon announced. "He wants to see you, or Miss Hale. He’s taken his shoes off, or I'd say the kitchen was too good for him."

Higgins stepped into the room in his stockinged feet, deliberately muting his own footsteps to honor the domestic sanctity of the space. "Sarvant, sir," he said, smoothing his hair. His voice carried the rich, guttural timbre of the vanishing northern worker—a dialect steeped in the untranslatable struggles of a marginalized class. 

Recognizing the physical exhaustion etched into the man’s posture, Mr. Hale’s demeanor shifted to that of a meticulous steward of sustenance. "We shall have tea up directly, Mr. Higgins. I am sure you are tired. Margaret, my dear, can’t you hasten tea?"

Margaret took the preparations into her own hands, moving through the rhythmic, mythic motions of brewing. She measured the dark, malty Assam leaves, their scent evoking damp earth and fired copper, and poured the rolling boil over them, letting the deep, tannic infusion bloom into the air. Meanwhile, Mr. Hale took up the bread knife. With the reverence of a traditional agrarian elder, he sliced through the crust of a dense, slow-fermented cob, the crisp crackle cutting through the room’s heavy silence. He spread thick, cultured butter over the porous crumb, the pale yellow fat softening instantly against the warm starch. 

For Higgins, this was not merely utilitarian fuel. The sharp, yeasty aroma and the grounding texture of the bread were profound anchors in a world of chaotic hunger. Once he had consumed the substantial meal, grounding himself in the sensory reality of the food, he spoke, his thick dialect cutting against the room's refined acoustics. 

"I’ve been a-seeking work. I kept a civil tongue for that man’s sake—Boucher. Not for him, for he’ll ne'er know clemming or cold again, but for his bits o' childer. I set him off th' road, and I mun answer for him. Hamper won’t have me. I’m a black feckless sheep. Unless, parson, yo’d help me get down South, where food is cheap and the folk friendly like."

Margaret stepped forward, instantly dismantling his pastoral myth. She knew the Southern agrarian reality, the generational grind that had woven itself into her own past. "You would get nine or ten shillings a week, Higgins. You reckon on having butcher's meat—rich, marbled joints roasted in their own fat—but you would be starved of it. It would eat you away like rust. The laborers there toil in the great, steaming solitude of the fields. The hard spadework robs their brains of life; the acoustic void deadens their imagination. They trudge home brutishly tired, consuming their meals not with sensory appreciation, but merely to sustain the next day's agony. You are used to the plentiful, argumentative companionship of the town—a dialect of resistance! The silence of the South would be eternal fretting to you."

Higgins absorbed the bleak portrait, his vision of an agrarian escape dissolving. "God help 'em," he muttered, the ancient vernacular of his empathy prevailing over his exhaustion. "North an' South have each getten their own troubles. If work's sure theer, labour's paid at starvation wages. Th' world needs fettling."

Margaret recognized the moment to intervene. "Higgins, have you been to Marlborough Mills to seek for work? To Mr. Thornton’s?"

"Th' o'erlooker bid me go and be damned."

"I wish you had seen Mr. Thornton," Mr. Hale said gently. "He might not have given you work, but he would not have used such language."

Margaret’s soft, grave eyes met Higgins's weary gaze. "Would you go again to-morrow and try him?"

Higgins gave a great, ragged sigh, the sound carrying generations of working-class pride. "It would tax my pride above a bit. I'd sooner knock him down than ask a favour. I'd a deal sooner be flogged mysel'. But yo’re not a common wench... I'll e'en make a wry face and go at it to-morrow. But that man has it in him to be burnt at the stake afore he'll give in."

"I’ll give you a note to him," Mr. Hale offered, seeking to bridge the gap with the dominant lexicon of the educated class, confident in the power of his written word.

"I thank yo' kindly, sir, but I'd as lief stand on my own bottom," Higgins replied, intuitively rejecting the power dynamics embedded in the written text of another. His truth could only be spoken in his own mother tongue, untranslatable as it was to the masters. "Meddling 'twixt master and man is liker meddling 'twixt husband and wife. I'll stand guard at the lodge door fro' six in the morning till I get speech on him."

He collected his dried shoes from by the kitchen fire, casting one last steady look at Margaret before departing into the industrial roar of the night.

"How proud that man is," Mr. Hale noted, somewhat stung by the rejection of his intercession.

"He is," said Margaret. "But what grand makings of a man there are in him." 

"It's amusing to see how he evidently respects the part in Mr. Thornton's character which is like his own. There's granite in all these northern people, papa."

Margaret felt a strangling ache in her throat as the malty scent of the tea faded into the evening. She wished Higgins and Thornton could strip away the titles of master and man, shedding the oppressive lexicons of their stations to speak soul to soul, human heart to human heart. And in the silence of the room, she longed for Thornton to return, wishing she possessed the unvarnished bravery to bridge the untranslatable gulf between them, and confess just how deeply she knew she was abased in his eyes.
