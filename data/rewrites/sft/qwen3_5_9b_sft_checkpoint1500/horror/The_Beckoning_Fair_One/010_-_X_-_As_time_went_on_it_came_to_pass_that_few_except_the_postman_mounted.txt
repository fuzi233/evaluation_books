Time passed, and the ascent of Oleron's stairs dwindled to a solitary frequency: save for the postman, no footfall echoed there. Even the postman grew scarce, his tread a ghostly memory heard perhaps once or twice weekly. Letters piled up from his publishers, demanding the manuscript of his new book, yet Oleron stalled his replies, allowing the first missive to rot, then the second, until the silence of the third remained unbroken.

Outside, the world bloomed into a deceptive brightness. Privet bushes choked the chopper-like notice boards, and flower-women lined the kerbs of the streets where Oleron once wandered. He bought flowers daily, his room screaming for a constant renewal of petals, and he answered its hunger with a frantic, unthinking generosity. Yet the journey outward to procure them gnawed at him, a physical irritation that deepened into a profound relief when he returned inside. A subtle shift occurred within him, a metamorphosis of sensation that was not a return to his former state but an expansion, a widening aperture admitting a new, specific terror. *Lux orco, tenebrae Jovi.* He named this dread agoraphobia—a paralyzing horror of air and space, of the vast emptiness that might leap upon his unguarded back.

Soon, he engineered a solution: his food and flowers arrived at his threshold. He rubbed his hands together over this expedient, a cold smile touching his lips. *Better!* Now he could dictate the terms of his existence, choosing whether to step out or remain. And swiftly, this choice hardened into a pleasure. He delighted in being immured.

Yet joy eluded him, or rather, it twisted into a grotesque form. He fretted, his throat tight with a weakness that bordered on weeping, yet a dark consciousness refused to trade his sadness for the noisy mirth of the outside world. Noise itself became an acute torment, unbearable to his senses. On those rare occasions he ventured forth, he clung to walls, fingers tracing friendly railings for comfort. Inside, he drifted from room to room in silent slippers, pausing for long moments to close doors with a gentle, absolute quiet, savoring the stillness that surrounded him. Sundays became a torture, for the fine weather had drawn the followers of the long-nosed Barrett into the square beneath his windows. Men and women raised anguished cries, struggling with their gods, while Barrett himself, face upturned and eyes shut, prayed with a fervor that pierced the very air, flooding Oleron's ears with a torrent of outpouring. One day, mid-rhapsody, Oleron leaped to his blind and dragged it down, hearing his own name erupt from the crowd in a fresh deluge of sound.

Sometimes, standing in the silence, he would whisper softly, calling "Romilly!" and waiting for a response that never came. More often, his whispers dissolved into nameless appeals.

A single spot in his sanctuary claimed his increasing persistence: the threshold of his bedroom door. By standing here, having opened every interior door but the heavy outer one, he could survey the entirety of his domain—the whole of the sitting-room, most of the bedroom, and fleeting glimpses of the kitchen, bathroom, and study. He lingered there, breathless, finger pressed to his lips. One afternoon, gazing from this vantage point, a thought struck him with sudden clarity: *Had Madley, that dead man the vicar spoke of, ever grasped the strategic power of this position?*

Now, light itself became a source of dread, surpassing even the darkness. Direct sunlight, filtering through his windows as the earth rotated, burned like a flame in his brain; diffused light was a dull, numbing ache. He began, hour by hour, to lower his crimson blinds. He made brief, daring sorties to perform this ritual, always ensuring his retreat remained accessible. Soon, lowering the blinds became a methodical exercise, transforming his chambers into the blood-red gloom of a photographer's darkroom.

One day, as he lowered the blind of his study and retreated, a soft laughter bubbled from him. "*That* bilks Mr. Barrett!" he murmured, the baffling of the zealot bringing him a moment's mirth.

But the following day, a chill ran through him that left him trembling for an hour. Seizing the cord to darken the window above the seat where he had found the harp-bag, he stood safely in the embrasure, back shielded, when movement caught his eye. The tail of a black-and-white checkered skirt vanished around the corner of the house. Was it illusion? If he had rushed to the window on the opposite, blinded wall, the skirt would surely have passed—but he was *almost* certain it was Elsie. An agony of suspense gripped him as he listened for the scratch of her tread on the stairs.

Silence. For three or four minutes, nothing came, and then a long, shuddering breath escaped him.

"By Jove, but that would have compromised me horribly!" he muttered, the words echoing in the red-lit room. "Horribly compromising... *no* woman stands that... not *any* kind of woman... oh, compromising in the extreme!"

Yet happiness remained a stranger to him. Fits of quiet weeping seized him, rising and falling like the fitful clouds drifting over the square below. Perhaps, if he was not happy, he was not unhappy. Before unhappiness could exist, something must be taken away, and nothing had yet been removed. Nothing had been given. He waited, in that flower-laden, terrifyingly enticing apartment, for the granting, the fulfillment of his art.

He paid no mind to the dwindling stack of coins in his drawer, nor to the fact that he had stopped working. Stopped working? They who believed Oleron had ceased labor were fools. He was only *beginning*. He was preparing a work of such magnitude, birthing a Mistress in the gestation of his Art. Let him endure this period of probation, this poignant waiting, and men would see. How *should* men know this Fair One of Oleron's until Oleron himself knew her? Radiant creations are not cast off like casual greetings. Those committed to fathering them must weep wretched tears, must swell with vain, presumptuous hope, must pursue the capricious, fair, mocking, slippery spirit that forever eludes, ensuring the chase never slackens. Let Oleron hunt this Huntress a little longer... she would be sparkling and panting in his arms yet. Oh, no: they were vastly mistaken who thought Oleron had quit his toil!

If everything else slipped from his grasp, he willingly let it go. So do we all when our Fair Ones beckon. At first, we wink, promising to put Her Ladyship through her paces, to neglect her for a day, to flout her jealous wiles when she wheedles; perhaps a heartless sprite lurks within us, never fooled. But in the end, all falls away. She beckons, beckons, and all goes.

Thus Oleron maintained his strategic post within the frame of his bedroom door, watching, waiting, smiling with a finger on his lips. It was his duty, his worship, his troth-plighting, the sum total of his knowledge of Love. When he found himself, intermittently, hating the dead man Madley, wishing he had never lived, he felt that hatred, too, served as an acceptable offering.

But as he prepared himself for this marriage, moping and chafing because the Bride made no sign, a discovery surfaced—one he ought to have made weeks prior.

It was a thought of Madley that triggered it. Since that night, years ago, when he had naively believed a little studied neglect would bring the lovely Beckoner to her knees, using her own jealousy to drive her away, he had not laid eyes on those fifteen discarded chapters of *Romilly*. They lay back in the window-seat, forgotten, erased from his mind. But his own jealousy of Madley awakened a mirror image in his heart: *her* jealousy of her jilted rival of flesh and blood. And there, in the shadows, they resurfaced. *Fool,* he thought. *Had he truly expected his Desire to manifest while the evidence of his divided allegiance still stood?* What, when she possessed a passion so fierce, so centered, that it had not hesitated at the double destruction of her rival? *Fool,* he thought again.

But if *that* was the sole pledge and sacrifice she demanded, she should have it—yes, and quickly!

He reached for the manuscript from the window-seat and carried it to the fire. He kept the blaze alight always; the heat coaxed out the final lingering scent of the flowers that banked his room. Time meant nothing to him; long ago he had allowed his clock to run down, a foolish measurer of hours in the face of stupendous transformations. But he knew it was late. He knelt before the fire, holding *Romilly*, ready to feed it.

Before he could finish undoing the fastening holding the sheets together, a start jerked through him. He whipped his head over his shoulder, listening intently. The sound had not been loud—not more than a tap, repeated two or three times—but it filled Oleron with alarm. His face darkened as the rhythm resumed.

A voice floated up from the landing.

"Paul!... Paul!..."
