Winding through the sun-dappled, cut-throat channels of the strange dry riverbed, I stumbled on what I first mistook for a small low shrub – a forward picket, I hoped, of a far larger army of vegetation stretching deeper into the wild. I knelt to study it closer, and spotted a single small, unrecognizable fruit dangling from its thinnest branch. Too wary of the alien landscape to risk poisoning, I did not reach for it, never guessing that hundreds of eyes burned bright over the rim of the nearest boulders, all fixed on me, all holding their breath to see if I would take that first bite.

I moved on, encountering one slightly bigger plant after another, until I found myself wading through dense clustered growth of the same kind, and realized they were not shrubs at all, but stunted dwarf trees. By the time I neared the bank of this second fork of the old river, the channels were so choked with them I could barely pick my way across the stretches I could not leap over. Once, a great roar of motion burst from the foliage near me, like thousands of birds erupting from an ivy-choked wall, but when I spun to look, there was nothing to see.

Soon I came upon a stand of full-sized, heavy-fruited trees lining the edge of a wide hollow that had clearly once been a lake basin. A thick forest seemed to spill down from the left ridge to fill the old lake bed: while the trees on the upper slopes were of a dozen varied kinds, every tree in the hollow bore fruit. I wandered a few paces down the soft slope of moss-mingled grass, and dropped my tired body to rest. A little further down, a tiny dwarf tree hung heavy with the rosiest little apples, no bigger than a cherry, its top bough sitting level with my outstretched hand. I plucked one, bit into it, and found it impossibly delicious.

I was already lifting my hand to pluck a second, when a gale of children’s shouting and bright, brook-clear laughter burst out, startling me half out of my skin – not with fear, but joy.
“He likes our apples! He likes our apples! He’s a good giant! He’s a good giant!” hundreds of tiny voices chorused.
“But he’s a giant!” one small, skeptical voice objected.
“He *is* rather big,” another conceded, “but littleness isn’t everything! You won’t avoid growing big and stupid if you don’t take care of yourself, anyway.”

I propped myself up on one elbow and stared. Above me, below me, all across the slope, stood a throng of children of every age: some barely steady on their running legs, others closer to twelve or thirteen, with three or four older teens hanging back in a quiet little knot, far less excitable than the rest. They chattered in giddy groups, argued and teased and contradicted each other like a crowd of grown city-dwellers, but with far more merriment, kinder manners, and sharper sense. I guessed they had seen me reach for a second apple and taken that as proof the first had pleased me, but I could not fathom how that had led them to decide I was good – though I did not blame the one child who had urged caution. I did not speak, afraid I would startle them all away, certain I would learn more by listening than by asking questions. That I could understand every word they said did not strike me as strange, after all: to understand is no more a miracle than to love.

A soft murmur of movement rippled through the crowd, and a bright, roguish little boy with a face like sunshine stepped forward, to press a huge, coarse green apple into my palm. The whole throng fell dead silent, every pair of eyes fixed expectantly on me.
“Eat, good giant,” he said.

I sat up, smiled my thanks, and bit into the fruit – only to fling it as far from me as I could the second the bitter, sour flesh hit my tongue.
A roar of pure delight went up. They swarmed me all at once, clambering over my legs, my arms, my shoulders, kissing my face and my hands, tangling their small fingers in my hair, until I collapsed back into the soft grass, nearly smothered under the weight of the horde of lovely little goblins. “Good giant! We knew you would come! You dear, good strong giant!” they cheered, their babble rising and falling, peals of joy bursting over and over from hundreds of clear small throats.

Then silence fell again, sharp and sudden. The children on top of me scrambled off, the rest backed away, and they tugged at my sleeves to yank me to my feet. Every sweet face that had been crinkled with laughter a moment before was pinched with worry.
“Get up, good giant!” a little girl begged. “Hurry, much hurry! He saw you throw his apple away!”

I was already on my feet before she finished, following the line of her pointing hand up the slope. There on the brow stood a loutish, sullen, ill-favoured fellow a few inches taller than myself. He looked furious, but I saw no reason to fear him at first: he carried no weapon, and then I realized every last one of my small friends had vanished without a trace.

He began to lumber down the slope, and I climbed upward to claim higher, firmer footing. He growled like a feral beast as he turned to face me. When I reached a flat level patch I stopped and waited for him. As he drew near, he held out one hand as if in greeting, but then snapped it back as if to strike, before holding it out again, gesturing pointedly to the spot where I had thrown the green apple. I made a face of open disgust, shook my head to reject his claim to the fruit. He let out a howl of unthinking rage that clearly screamed, *Do you dare say my apple was not fit to eat?*

“Even the best tree can grow one bad apple,” I told him. I do not know if he understood a single word I said, but he lunged forward a step, and I braced for the fight. He waited a beat to attack, though, until a second brute just like him, who had crept up silent behind my back, was close enough to strike – then they both rushed me. I landed a solid blow right to the first one’s face, but the second clubbed me hard across the back of the head, and between the two of them I was overpowered in seconds.

They dragged me through the upper woods to the squalid village where their tribe lived: a huddle of miserable huts cobbled together from fallen branches and loose stones. They threw me inside one, shoved me to the dirt floor, and kicked me until I could barely breathe. A woman stood by watching, her face blank and indifferent. In all the rest of my captivity there, I could barely tell the men apart from the women, their features so blunt and unvaried. More than once I wondered if I had stumbled on some sort of half-sentient fungoid people, gifted with just enough awareness to move, to snarl in anger, and to grasp at what they wanted. Their food – a mess of bitter tubers, acrid bulbs, and coarse unripe fruit – was so repulsive I could barely stand to touch it, and nothing enraged them more than seeing me turn up my nose at their meals. The women cuffed me, the men kicked me, every time I refused to swallow a bite.

I spent that night on the dirt floor, half too sore to move, but I slept deeply, and woke the next morning a little refreshed. They dragged me back down to the valley, tied my ankles to a tree with a long rope, and pressed a flat, sharp-edged stone into my left hand. When I shifted it to my right, they kicked me until I switched it back, then gestured that I was to scrape all the bark off every non-fruiting branch they left within my reach. They kicked me once more for good measure, and walked away.

I set to the dreary work at once, hoping that if I did as they told me placidly, they would grow careless, leave me mostly to myself – so I could watch for my chance to escape. Luck was with me: one of the tiny dwarf apple trees grew right beside my post, and every other minute I plucked one of its small, sweet fruits and ate it, feeling my strength seep back into my sore limbs faster than I could have hoped.
