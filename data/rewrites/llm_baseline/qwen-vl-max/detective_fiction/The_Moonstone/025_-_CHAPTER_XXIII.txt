I had kept the pony-chaise ready, just in case Mr. Franklin decided to leave by the evening train. When I saw his luggage being carried down the stairs—followed closely by the young man himself—I knew at once he had made up his mind.

“So you’ve really decided to go, sir?” I said as we met in the hall. “Couldn’t you wait a few days longer? Give Miss Rachel one more chance?”

The foreign polish that had once clung to Mr. Franklin seemed to have worn off entirely now that the moment of departure had come. Instead of answering me, he simply handed me a letter from her ladyship. Most of it repeated what I’d already heard in earlier correspondence. But there was an added note at the end about Miss Rachel—one that explained why Mr. Franklin had become so resolute.

“You’ll wonder,” she wrote, “that I allow my own daughter to keep me completely in the dark. A diamond worth twenty thousand pounds has vanished—and yet I’m left to assume that Rachel knows exactly what happened. That she’s bound by some unspoken obligation, imposed by someone unknown to me, for reasons I cannot even guess. Could I possibly allow myself to be treated this way? In Rachel’s current state, yes. She is utterly shattered—her nerves frayed beyond endurance. I dare not speak of the Moonstone again until time has calmed her. To help, I’ve dismissed the police officer. He’s no closer to solving the mystery than we are. This isn’t a matter for strangers. His presence only adds to my suffering and drives Rachel into despair whenever she hears his name.

“My plans are settled as much as they can be. I intend to take Rachel to London—not just for a change of scene, but to seek the best medical advice available. Can I ask you to meet us there? My dear Franklin, you must imitate my patience. Wait, as I do, for a better time. Your help in the search for the jewel is still seen as a fault—by Rachel, at least. You acted in good faith, but your efforts, unknowingly, threatened to expose her secret. It’s impossible to forgive such consequences when neither of us could have foreseen them. She cannot be reasoned with—she can only be pitied. For now, you and Rachel are better apart. All I can advise is: give her time.”

I returned the letter, deeply sorry for Mr. Franklin. I knew how devoted he was to Miss Rachel, and I could see the pain in his face as he read her mother’s words. “You know the proverb, sir,” I said gently. “When things are at their worst, they’re sure to mend. Things can’t get much worse than they are now.”

Mr. Franklin folded the letter without a word, clearly untouched by my attempt at comfort.

“When I came here from London with that cursed Diamond,” he said, “I don’t think there was a happier household in England. Look at it now—shattered, divided, poisoned by suspicion and secrets! Do you remember that morning at the Shivering Sand, when we talked about my uncle Herncastle and his birthday gift? The Moonstone has done the Colonel’s vengeance, Betteredge—through means he never dreamed of!”

With that, he shook my hand and stepped out toward the chaise.

I followed him down the steps. It broke my heart to see him leave the place where he’d spent the happiest years of his life. Penelope—deeply affected by everything that had happened—came running over, tears streaming down her face, to say goodbye. Mr. Franklin kissed her. I waved my hand, silently saying, *You’re welcome, sir.* A few other maids peeked around the corner, drawn by the moment. He was one of those men women instinctively liked.

At the last moment, I stopped the chaise. “Sir,” I said, “would you please write to us? Just let us know you’re safe.”

He didn’t seem to hear. He was scanning the house, the grounds—taking in every detail as if bidding farewell to a lifetime. “Where are you going?” I asked, holding onto the chaise, hoping to learn something.

He pulled his hat low over his eyes. “Going?” he echoed. “I’m going to the devil.”

The pony startled at the word—as if struck by a sudden moral horror. “God bless you, sir, wherever you go!” I managed before he disappeared from sight. A kind soul, despite his flaws. A pleasant gentleman. He left behind a quiet emptiness in the house.

That Saturday night was bleak and heavy. I kept myself occupied with my pipe and my copy of *Robinson Crusoe*. The servants—except Penelope—passed the time talking about Rosanna’s suicide. They were convinced she had stolen the Moonstone and killed herself in fear of being caught. Penelope, of course, held fast to her original belief—that Rosanna was innocent. But her theory failed where Miss Rachel’s did: it couldn’t explain Rosanna’s trip to Frizinghall or her strange actions with the nightgown. Pointing this out to Penelope was like throwing rain on a waterproof coat—no effect. Truth be told, my daughter inherits my resistance to reason—and she’s far ahead of me in that department.

On Sunday, the close carriage that had been waiting at Mr. Ablewhite’s returned empty. The coachman delivered a message for me and written instructions for my lady’s maid and Penelope.

The message said my mistress had decided to take Miss Rachel to her London home on Monday. The instructions listed clothing and meeting times. Most of the staff would follow. My lady found Rachel so reluctant to return to the house after all that had occurred that she planned to travel directly from Frizinghall to London. I was to stay behind, managing the estate until further notice. Those who remained would receive full wages.

This reminded me of Mr. Franklin’s words about our broken household. The more I thought of him, the more uneasy I grew. By Sunday evening, I’d written to Mr. Jeffco—the valet of Mr. Franklin’s father—asking him to let me know what his master’s son intended to do upon arriving in London.

Sunday night was even duller than Saturday. We ended the day as countless others do—anticipating sleep and falling asleep in our chairs.

I don’t know how Monday affected the rest of the household. But it shook me. Sergeant Cuff’s first prediction came true: I received news from the Yollands.

I had seen Penelope and the maid off at the station with the luggage and was walking the grounds when I heard my name called. Turning, I saw Limping Lucy—the fisherman’s daughter—standing before me. Despite her limp and her thinness (a great drawback, in my view), she had qualities: sharp eyes, a clear voice, beautiful brown hair. Her crutch marked her misfortune; her temper, her greatest flaw.

“Well, my dear,” I said, “what brings you here?”

“Where’s the man you call Franklin Blake?” she demanded, gripping her crutch and glaring.

“That’s no way to address a gentleman,” I replied. “If you wish to inquire about my lady’s nephew, you should say Mr. Franklin Blake.”

She took a step closer, her eyes blazing. “_Mr._ Franklin Blake?” she spat. “Murderer Franklin Blake would be a better name for him.”

I recalled Mrs. Betteredge’s advice: when a woman tries to provoke you, turn the tables and provoke her instead. They expect your defense—but not your calm mockery. One word does it. I smiled pleasantly. “Pooh!”

Her rage exploded. She balanced on her good foot and slammed her crutch three times on the ground. “He’s a murderer! He’s a murderer! He killed Rosanna Spearman!”

A few workers nearby looked up, recognized her, and quickly looked away.

“He killed Rosanna?” I repeated. “Why do you say that, Lucy?”

“What difference does it make? What does any man care? If she’d thought of men the way I do, she might still be alive!”

“She always thought kindly of _me_, poor soul,” I said softly. “And I tried to treat her kindly too.”

I meant to soothe her. I hadn’t realized how deeply she was hurting. Wretchedness often breeds defiance in humble folk. My words softened her. She bowed her head and rested it on her crutch.

“I loved her,” she whispered. “She lived a terrible life—treated badly, misled—but she never lost her sweetness. She was an angel. We could have been happy together. I had a plan—we’d go to London, live like sisters, earn our living with our needles. She had a good education, wrote well, was quick with her needle. I’m educated too, write well—maybe not as fast—but we could have made it. And now? What happens this morning? Her letter arrives. She says she’s done with life. She bids me farewell forever. Where is he?” she cried, lifting her head. “Where’s this gentleman I’m supposed to speak of respectfully? Ha, Mr. Betteredge! The day will come when the poor rise against the rich. I pray God they start with _him_. I pray God they start with _him_.”

Here was another decent Christian—broken by injustice. Even the parson might hesitate to preach to her now. I did nothing but steer her back to the point.

“What do you want with Mr. Franklin Blake?” I asked.

“I want to see him.”

“For something specific?”

“I have a letter for him.”

“From Rosanna Spear:”

“Yes.”

“Sent to you in your own mail?”

“Yes.”

Was the truth finally coming? Were answers offering themselves? I had to wait. Sergeant Cuff had left his mark on me. I could feel the detective fever returning.

“You can’t see him,” I said.

“I must. I will.”

“He left for London last night.”

Limping Lucy stared at me—then turned without a word, heading back toward Cobb’s Hole.

“Wait!” I called. “I expect news of him tomorrow. Give me the letter. I’ll send it.”

She paused, bracing herself on her crutch. “It must pass from my hands to his,” she said. “No other way.”

“Shall I tell him what you’ve said?”

“Tell him I hate him. And tell him the truth.”

“Yes, yes. But the letter—”

“If he wants it, he must come back here and take it from me.”

And with that, she limped away. The detective fever burned through my dignity. I followed, trying to coax her into speaking. No luck. I tried her mother next. Good Mrs. Yolland could only cry and offer a drop from the Dutch bottle. I found the fisherman on the beach. “Bad job,” he muttered, mending his net. Neither parent knew anything more than I did. Only one option remained: write to Mr. Franklin.

I waited anxiously for the postman Tuesday morning. He brought two letters. One from Penelope—brief and reassuring—said my lady and Miss Rachel had arrived safely in London. The second, from Mr. Jeffco, delivered shocking news: Mr. Franklin had already left England.

Upon reaching London, he’d gone straight to his father’s residence. But timing was bad. Mr. Blake senior was buried in parliamentary business, busy with a private bill. Mr. Jeffco led Franklin into the study. “My dear Franklin! Why surprise me like this? Anything wrong?”

“Yes. Something’s wrong with Rachel. I’m terribly distressed.”

“Grieved to hear it. But I can’t talk now.”

“When can you?”

“My dear boy, I won’t lie. Not until the end of the session. Goodnight.”

“Thank you, sir. Goodnight.”

Outside, the conversation was shorter. “Jeffco, what time does the tidal train leave tomorrow?”

“Six-forty, sir.”

“Wake me at five.”

“Going abroad, sir?”

“Going, Jeffco—wherever the railway takes me.”

“Shall I tell your father?”

“Yes. Tell him at the end of the session.”

Next morning, Mr. Franklin had departed for foreign lands. No one—even he—knew where. Europe, Asia, Africa, America—each equally likely, according to Jeffco.

This news sealed any hope of connecting Limping Lucy and Mr. Franklin. Penelope’s belief—Rosanna died of unrequited love for Franklin—was confirmed. But the letter? Was it merely a farewell? Or did it contain the confession Franklin suspected Rosanna had tried to make? It was sealed—unknown to me, to her parents, to everyone. We all suspected Lucy had known Rosanna’s secret. We begged her to speak. She refused. Servants still searched the rocks near the Shivering Sand—where Rosanna had been traced—searching in vain. Tides rose and fell. Summer passed into autumn. The Quicksand hid both her body and her secret.

By Tuesday, I’d learned of Franklin’s departure and my lady’s arrival in London. Wednesday brought nothing. Thursday, a letter from Penelope.

She reported a doctor had advised amusement for Miss Rachel—flower shows, operas, balls. Miss Rachel, to her mother’s surprise, embraced it all. Mr. Godfrey had visited—still evidently fond of his cousin, despite his earlier rejection. He’d been warmly received and immediately added Rachel’s name to one of his charities. My lady was despondent and had met twice with her lawyer. Rumors swirled about a distant relative—Miss Clack—who had sat beside Mr. Godfrey at the birthday dinner and had a taste for champagne. Penelope was astonished she hadn’t yet called. “She’ll be on us soon enough,” she wrote, then added, “And if you’re sent to her after me, please don’t believe a word she says about me.”

Friday passed quietly—except for a dog showing signs of infection behind the ears. I gave him syrup of buckthorn and put him on vegetables and pot liquor. Excuse me for mentioning it—it slipped in. But he was a good dog, and deserved proper care.

Saturday, the final day of the week, was also the last day of my account.

The morning post brought a surprise: a London newspaper. The handwriting puzzled me—until I compared it with the money-lender’s address in my pocketbook. It matched Sergeant Cuff’s.

I opened it eagerly. On one page, a police report was circled in ink. Here it is:

“LAMBETH—Shortly before court closed, Mr. Septimus Luker, dealer in ancient gems, carvings, intagli, etc., approached the magistrate for advice. He complained of persistent harassment by three Indian street performers who had returned repeatedly despite police warnings. They attempted to enter his premises under pretense of charity. Warned from the front, they reappeared at the back. Mr. Luker expressed concern that robbery might be planned. His collection includes rare classical and Oriental gems of immense value. He had recently dismissed an Indian ivory carver on suspicion of theft and feared collusion between the worker and the street performers. Their goal might be to create a disturbance and gain access during chaos. The magistrate replied that while the annoyance warranted legal action, no evidence of robbery existed. Mr. Luker should secure his valuables and consult the police for additional precautions. Mr. Luker thanked the magistrate and withdrew.”

One of the ancients once said: “Look to the end.” Now, looking to the end of these pages, I find my story concluding naturally. We’ve moved from marvel to marvel—and now, the greatest marvel of all: Sergeant Cuff’s three predictions fulfilled in less than a week.

And so, the tale ends.
