Despite the past friction between Lord Castlewood and Harry, the two men remained cordial acquaintances. Their friendship was marked by a generous spirit; if George Warrington required financial aid, an introduction to the Court, or a seat in the House of Lords to witness a debate, his cousin was invariably ready to oblige. Lord Castlewood was a witty and agreeable companion, eager to advance his relative's interests so long as his own were not compromised. Indeed, he promised to use his influence to secure a position for Mr. Warrington, a gesture warmly embraced by the Virginian.

George's reluctance to return to America stemmed from more than mere sentimentality. While the idle existence of a planter might have suited him had it included independence, in Virginia he felt stifled—both the first and, in his view, the worst-treated subject of his mother's dominion. He dreaded the prospect of bringing his young bride back to a life defined by tedious subordination, domestic bickering, and the inevitable jealousies that threatened to sour their union. In contrast, London offered congenial society, the allure of potential distinction, and a freedom that poverty could not diminish. Thus, he clung to Lord Castlewood's offer of patronage, choosing to believe in the sincerity of a kinsman whose own circumstances were far from enviable.

Lord Castlewood proved remarkably gracious upon learning of George's engagement to Miss Lambert. He visited her parents, lavished praise upon the young couple, and conducted himself with such charm that the Lamberts, previously wary of his reputation, began to view him through a lens of charity. They convinced themselves that the world must be wicked indeed to malign such a man. Within their household, Lord Castlewood seemed transformed; he shed his worldliness at the door, becoming witty and lively until he departed. Perhaps he was genuinely better among them, or perhaps he simply found a temporary harbor from the storms of his usual life.

Mrs. Lambert, however, saw something deeper in his affection for her daughter. When she gently suggested that it was "not too late" for him to seek her hand, Lord Castlewood recoiled, interpreting her words as a plea for romance rather than repentance. With a melancholy grace, he confessed his inability to change his nature. "A man who learns languages late can never master them," he mused, comparing his situation to the acquisition of virtue. "Goodness cannot be learned at my age; I can only admire it in others." He painted himself as a man condemned by fate, a creature of selfish nature and harsh education, destined to remain on the wrong side of Lazarus. Yet, despite his self-deprecation, he held Mrs. Lambert's hand with pitying tenderness. Though he believed his redemption impossible, the lambert family, moved by his apparent sorrow and the kindness of his demeanor, continued to pray for him, unaware that his "good feelings" were fleeting and his capacity for genuine change limited.

With Harry away on service and George firmly engaged to Theo, Mrs. Lambert's matchmaking energies shifted toward her son, Jack. She envisioned a wealthy American bride for him, a plan that clashed with her husband's desire to keep his son in the army. Nevertheless, the social circle expanded. The Ladies of Castlewood became regular guests at the Lamberts', attending card parties and assemblies where high society mingled freely. Mrs. Lambert, finding acceptance and admiration for her daughters, welcomed these connections wholeheartedly.

Meanwhile, another suitor entered the scene: Mr. Van den Bosch, a wealthy merchant of Dutch extraction. Initially, he had been a figure of the lower commercial class, taking his daughter Lydia to modest outings in Islington and attending Baptist meetings. However, upon Lydia's return from school, her grandfather relocated them to an elegant estate in Bloomsbury. This move alienated their former neighbors—the merchants and tradesmen of Virginia Walk—and drew the attention of the elite. Soon, the old merchant abandoned his humble chapel for a pew in the prestigious Queen Square Church, living adjacent to Mr. George Warrington's residence.

This shift alarmed Mr. Draper, who warned George of the Van den Boschs' dubious origins. He revealed that the patriarch had amassed his fortune through risky land speculation, privateering, and the Guinea trade, and that his lineage was marred by scandal. Undeterred, the old gentleman confronted George directly. He dismissed Draper's accusations as slander, boasting of his immense wealth and expressing bewilderment that George preferred Miss Lambert to his own granddaughter, Lydia. He argued that while Lydia possessed beauty and fortune, George's choice of Theo was inexplicable.

Yet, beneath the transactional dialogue lay a touching dynamic between the grandfather and granddaughter. The old man, reflecting on his own youthful regrets and harsh treatment of his son, indulged Lydia completely. He confessed that he had moved to the fashionable quarter solely to please her, admitting that he cared little for fashion or theater unless it brought joy to his "little minx." Their interactions were filled with affectionate teasing and physical endearments, a stark contrast to the calculated maneuvering of the adults around them.

As the conversation wound down, a subtle tension arose. George recounted the encounter to Theo, omitting certain details of the grandfather's boasts regarding Lydia's inheritance. This omission suggests that while George maintained his engagement to Theo, the allure of the wealthy American heiress lingered in the background, leaving the outcome of these romantic intrigues uncertain. For now, however, the innocence of the young lovers stood firm against the encroaching complexities of the adult world.
