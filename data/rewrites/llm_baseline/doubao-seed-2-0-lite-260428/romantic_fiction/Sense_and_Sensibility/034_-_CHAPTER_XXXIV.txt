Mrs. John Dashwood was so fully convinced of her husband’s sound judgment that she called on both Mrs. Jennings and Lady Middleton the very next day, and her confidence was richly rewarded. Even Mrs. Jennings – the lively older woman whose home housed Elinor and Marianne – proved far from beneath her notice, and as for Lady Middleton? Fanny declared her one of the most charming women alive.

The fondness was entirely mutual. Both women shared a core of cold, self-serving selfishness that drew them to each other like magnets, and they found perfect kinship in their bland, unyielding dedication to polite performance, and their mutual lack of curiosity for anything that fell outside the narrow bounds of their own small social circles. The stiff, distant reserve that made Fanny such a hit with Lady Middleton did not endear her to Mrs. Jennings, however. To the older woman, Fanny was nothing but a proud, chilly creature with no warm words for her husband’s sisters, who sat silent for at least seven and a half of the 15 minutes she spent in their Berkeley Street lodgings.

Elinor burned to know if Edward Ferrars had arrived in town, but she would never bring herself to ask Fanny outright – and Fanny would never have volunteered the information, not until she could proudly announce Edward’s engagement to the wealthy Miss Morton was finalized, or until her husband’s schemes to secure favors and money from Colonel Brandon came to fruition. She was convinced Elinor and Edward still pined hopelessly for each other, so every chance to separate them, in word or deed, felt like a necessary safeguard.

But the news Fanny would not share soon came from another quarter. Lucy Steele turned up not long after, all melodramatic distress, to beg Elinor’s sympathy: Edward had arrived in London with the Dashwoods, but he dared not sneak to Bartlett’s Buildings to see her, for fear their secret engagement would be discovered. The pair were wild to meet, but for now, letters were the only comfort they could share. Edward confirmed he was in town himself, twice calling on their Berkeley Street lodgings while the rest of the party was out on their morning walks, leaving his calling card on the dining table each time. Elinor was glad he had thought to stop by, but far more relieved that she had missed him.

The Dashwoods were so enraptured by the Middletons that, despite their habitual reluctance to spend a single unnecessary penny, they decided to host a grand dinner for them. Their acquaintance barely begun, they issued the invitation to their newly leased, very fine house on Harley Street, which they had taken for three months. Elinor, Marianne, and Mrs. Jennings were all invited too, and John Dashwood made a point of extending a warm welcome to Colonel Brandon, who was always eager to be wherever the Miss Dashwoods were, and accepted the invitation with far more pleasure than surprise. Mrs. Ferrars, Edward’s formidable mother, would be in attendance – though no one could tell Elinor if Edward or his brother Robert would be joining the party. The chance to finally meet the matriarch was enough to spark Elinor’s full curiosity: she no longer felt the sharp, panicky anxiety that would have plagued her months earlier at the thought of making the woman’s acquaintance, but her interest in seeing what Mrs. Ferrars was really like had never faded.

That quiet interest quickly curdled when she learned the Miss Steeles had also been added to the guest list. The two sisters had flattered Lady Middleton so relentlessly that even though Lucy lacked elegance and her sister Anne was downright unrefined, the Middletons had invited them to spend two weeks at their Conduit Street home – and the pair had timed their visit to start just a few days before the Dashwoods’ dinner. Their connection as nieces to the man who had cared for Fanny’s brother for years would never have earned them a spot at her table on their own, but as Lady Middleton’s guests, they could not be turned away. Lucy, who had long dreamed of getting close to Edward’s family to gauge their opinions and find a way to win their favor, could barely contain her joy when she received her invitation.

For Elinor, the news could not have been more different. She immediately assumed Edward, who was staying under his mother’s roof, would of course be invited to his own sister’s dinner – and the thought of seeing him for the first time after all their tangled history, face to face with Lucy standing beside him, felt almost unbearable. Those fears were unfounded, however, and it was Lucy herself who dispelled them, smug and triumphant, when she told Elinor that Edward would definitely not be at the Harley Street dinner. She even tried to twist the knife further, claiming he was staying away because his affection for her was so strong he could not hide it if they were in the same room.

The fated Tuesday arrived, and Lucy clutched Elinor’s arm as they climbed the stairs to the dining rooms together, the whole party trailing the servant in one unbroken line. “Pity me, dear Miss Dashwood!” Lucy whispered, her voice trembling for dramatic effect. “You are the only person here who can understand. I can barely stand. Good heavens – in a moment I will meet the woman who holds every bit of my future happiness in her hands, the woman who will one day be my mother!” Elinor almost teased her that they might be about to meet Miss Morton’s future mother-in-law, not hers, but she chose instead to assure Lucy sincerely that she did pity her – a response that left Lucy utterly bewildered, as she had fully expected Elinor to be overcome with unquenchable envy.

Mrs. Ferrars was a small, thin woman, ramrod straight to the point of stiffness, her face sharp and sour to the point of being openly unkind. Her complexion was sallow, her small features plain and lifeless, saved from total insipidity only by a permanent furrow between her brows that screamed of unyielding pride and ill temper. She was not a woman of many words, matching her speech exactly to the small number of ideas that occupied her mind, and not a single syllable she spoke was directed at Elinor. She eyed the older Dashwood sister with the clear, premeditated intent to dislike her no matter what happened.

Elinor could not be made unhappy by that treatment now. A few months prior, it would have broken her heart – but Mrs. Ferrars no longer had the power to distress her. The obvious difference between the matriarch’s coldness to her, and the warm favor she and Fanny lavished on Lucy, only made Elinor smile, even as it amused her. She could not help but find the whole scene ridiculous: the two women fawning over Lucy, the very person they would most want to humiliate in the world if they knew half of what Elinor did, while Elinor – who held no power to hurt them at all – was deliberately snubbed at every turn. She watched the Miss Steeles simper and flatter to hold onto that misplaced favor, and found herself thoroughly despising all four of them for their petty, foolish spite. Lucy was glowing from the special attention she received, and Anne Steele would have been perfectly happy if everyone spent the rest of the night teasing her about her beloved Dr. Davies.

The dinner was grand, the servants numerous, every detail arranged to showcase Fanny’s love of display and John’s ability to pay for it. No trace lingered of the supposed poverty John liked to complain about, even as he poured thousands into “improving” the Norland estate and had once come within a thousand pounds of being forced to sell his shares at a devastating loss. The only poverty on display was in the conversation, which was remarkably thin. John Dashwood had nothing worth saying, Fanny even less, and most of their guests shared the same flaw: almost everyone in attendance lacked either natural wit, polished grace, good humor, or basic warmth. When the ladies retired to the drawing room after dinner, that lack of substance was impossible to ignore. The men had at least livened the discussion with politics, enclosure laws, and horse training, but once they left, the ladies had no topic to occupy them until coffee was served beyond comparing the heights of two nearly same-aged little boys: Harry Dashwood, Fanny and John’s son, and Lady Middleton’s second son William. Since only Harry was present, no measurement could settle the argument, and everyone repeated their own firm, unproven opinion over and over, as if sheer repetition would make it true. The two mothers politely insisted each other’s son was the taller, the two grandmothers fiercely defended their own grandson’s height, Lucy claimed both boys were remarkably tall for their age with no difference at all, and Anne Steele switched sides every other sentence to compliment each mother in turn. Elinor once stated she thought William was taller, a comment that offended Fanny and Mrs. Ferrars so deeply she refused to add another word on the subject. Marianne, when called on for her opinion, offended everyone by saying she had no opinion at all, having never once thought about the matter.

Shortly after the gentlemen joined the ladies in the drawing room, John Dashwood spotted the beautifully painted pair of screens Elinor had made for Fanny back at Norland, newly framed and hung to decorate the room. He grabbed them eagerly, holding them out for Colonel Brandon to admire. “These are all my eldest sister’s work!” he said. “As a man of taste, I know you will love them. I do not know if you have ever seen her art before, but everyone agrees she paints exceptionally well.” The Colonel, who cared nothing for pretense of being an art connoisseur, praised the screens as effusively as he would have praised any piece of work Elinor had touched, and soon the whole room was crowding around to look. Mrs. Ferrars, who had no idea who had painted them, asked to see them, and Fanny handed them over, casually mentioning they were Elinor’s work.

“hmph. Very pretty,” Mrs. Ferrars said, barely glancing at them as she shoved them back to her daughter. Fanny flushed a little, for a moment thinking her mother had been unnecessarily rude. “They are very pretty, ma’am, are they not?” she said – then immediately panicked that she had been too kind to Elinor, and added, “Don’t you think the style is exactly like Miss Morton’s painting? She paints so delightfully – that last landscape of hers was absolutely stunning!”

“Beautiful indeed,” Mrs. Ferrars agreed. “Miss Morton does everything perfectly.”

Marianne could not hold her tongue. She had already been furious at the way Mrs. Ferrars had treated Elinor all night, and this petty, deliberate dig at her sister to praise a stranger they did not even know made her snap. “That is the strangest sort of admiration I have ever heard! What do we have to do with Miss Morton? Who knows her, who cares for her? We are talking about Elinor!” She snatched the screens out of Fanny’s hands to admire them the way they deserved to be admired.

Mrs. Ferrars drew herself up, face thunderous. “Miss Morton is Lord Morton’s daughter,” she said, her voice sharp with rebuke. Fanny was seething, and John Dashwood was white with panic at his younger sister’s audacity. Elinor was far more upset by Marianne’s outburst than she had been by Mrs. Ferrars’ rudeness – but Colonel Brandon’s eyes were fixed on Marianne, and all he saw was her loyal, tender heart, the fierce sisterly love that would not stand to see Elinor snubbed even in the smallest way.

Marianne’s emotions would not be contained. The cold cruelty of Mrs. Ferrars’ behavior toward Elinor all evening filled her with a premonition of all the heartbreak and pain Elinor might face at this woman’s hands, the same kind of wounded grief she herself had endured at Willoughby’s hands. Driven by that rush of affectionate feeling, she crossed to Elinor’s chair, wrapped her arms around her sister’s neck, pressed her cheek to Elinor’s, and whispered, “Dearest, dear Elinor – do not mind them. Do not let them make you unhappy.” She could not say another word, her spirits overwhelmed, and she buried her face in Elinor’s shoulder and burst into tears.

Every eye in the room turned to them, and almost everyone was concerned. Colonel Brandon was on his feet and moving toward them before he even realized what he was doing. Mrs. Jennings clucked sympathetically and pressed her smelling salts into Marianne’s hand, and Sir John flew into such a rage at Mrs. Ferrars for upsetting the girl that he immediately crossed the room to sit next to Lucy Steele and whispered the whole shocking story to her, complaining loudly about the old harridan’s behavior.

Within a few minutes, Marianne had composed herself enough to end the fuss and return to her seat, though her mood remained subdued for the rest of the night. “Poor Marianne,” John Dashwood whispered quietly to Colonel Brandon, as soon as he could get his attention. “She has never had as strong a constitution as her sister, she is very high-strung and nervous. And you have to pity a young woman who was once a great beauty, losing her looks like this. You would not guess it now, I expect, but Marianne was remarkably handsome just a few months ago – every bit as pretty as Elinor. Now you see how overset she gets.”
