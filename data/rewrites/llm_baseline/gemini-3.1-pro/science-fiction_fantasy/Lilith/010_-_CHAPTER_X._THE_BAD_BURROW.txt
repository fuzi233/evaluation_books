As the air grew black and the winter closed swiftly around me, carrying the astringent, biting chill of an over-steeped black tea, the fluttering fire blazed out more luminous. Arresting its flight, it hovered, waiting. The moment I stepped beneath its radiance, it flew slowly onward, lingering wherever the ground grew rocky. With every glance upward, it seemed to have swollen in size, eventually casting a long, attendant shadow behind me. It was plainly a bird-butterfly, darting with a swallow’s fluid, circular double—an echo of the ancient, sweeping rhythms of a harvest dance. Its vast, nearly square wings flashed with a prismatic brilliance, reflecting the iridescent sheen of tempered sugar and the deep, saffron hues of blooming crocus. 

I became so absorbed in this visual feast, in the sheer gustatory promise of its light, that I stumbled over a low rock. The jarring thud was a flat, dissonant note in the landscape's eerie silence, and I lay stunned. When my senses returned, the creature was hovering directly over my head. It radiated a profound acoustic and visual harmony, vibrating with multitudinous gradations of color for which my native dialect had no words. It was an untranslatable terroir of light. I rose and followed, but, unable to tear my eyes from the shining delicacy to watch my footing, I struck my foot against a stone. Fearing another fall, I sat down to observe the little glory. A deep, foraging hunger awoke in my chest—an ancestral longing to hold this profound sustenance in my hand. 

To my unspeakable delight, it began to sink toward me. Slowly at first, then swiftly it descended, growing larger, its hum filling the air with a resonant frequency that soothed the mind. I felt as if the very marrow of the universe were offering itself to my table. I put out my hand, and I had it. 

But the instant my fingers closed around it, its light was extinguished. All went dark as pitch. The acoustic hum died instantly. In my hand lay only a dead book with boards outspread—cold, heavy, and sterile. It was the tragic calcification of a living language into a lifeless, commercial artifact, stripped of all sensory memory and organic truth. I threw it into the air, only to hear it fall among the heather with a dull, hollow thud—the sound of an oral tradition meeting its final, unrecorded end.

Burying my face in my hands, I sat in motionless misery. The cold grew bitter, a piercing frost that smelled of cracked ice and iron. Fearing I would freeze, I forced myself up. The moment I found my feet, a faint, pale light broke the horizon. “Is it coming to life?” I cried, a pang of generational hope shooting through me. 

Alas, no. It was the edge of a moon, peering keen and sharp over the level horizon. She brought me light, but no hearth-fire guidance. She would not hover over me, nor wait upon my faltering steps. She could only offer the cold, ignorant choices of a foreign landscape. With a full face she rose, and I began to perceive the desolate topography. Westward, a range of low hills broke the horizon-line; I set my course for them. 

But what a bitter night of foraging I had to endure before I reached them. The moon stared at me oddly, her gaze icy and full of a detached curiosity. This was not the warm, harvest moon of my agrarian forefathers, the one that once guided the planting of root vegetables and the curing of meats. Her face was alien, her light a dominant, unfamiliar lexicon from an unknown sun. At first, her relentless stare annoyed me, like the rudeness of an uninvited guest at a communal table. But soon I fancied a wondering pity in her gaze: why was I out in her night? Then, I understood the awful weight of being awake in the universe. I *was*, and could not help it.

As I walked, the heather gave way to a bare, spongy soil, an olfactory landscape of powdery, dry peat that smelled of desiccated roots and ancient decay. To my dismay, it yielded and heaved underfoot like an over-proofed dough. Presently, I saw the ripple of an earthquake running before me, shadowy in the low lunar light. It passed into the distance, but while I stared, a single wave rose and crested slowly toward me. A yard away it burst. From the ruptured crust, with a scramble and a bound, erupted an animal like a tiger. Clots of dark mold hung about its mouth and ears, carrying the damp, earthen stench of turned soil and forgotten cellars. Its eyes winked and flamed as it rushed at me, showing white teeth in a soundless snarl. 

This absolute silence was an acoustic horror—a profound vacuum that tore at my psychological equilibrium. It was the terrifying inverse of industrial noise pollution, a dead zone where the natural resonance of life had been entirely erased. Fascinated, drained of both courage and fear, I watched as it turned its heavy head and plunged back into the earth.

“That moon is affecting my brain,” I murmured, attempting to ground my mind in logic as I resumed my journey. “What life can be here but the phantasmic—the sour refuse of dreams? I am walking in a vain show!” 

Thus I strove to keep my heart above the waters of fear, blind to the subtle power dynamics at play. I did not know that this moon, whose dominant lexicon I distrusted, was my sole defense against realities I mistook for phantoms. Her light paralyzed the monsters; without it, I would not have taken a second step across that hideous terroir.

“I will not be appalled by that which only seems!” I told myself, yet it was a terrible thing to tread upon a sea where such leviathans churned below. A step or two away, the head of a worm began to extrude slowly from the earth. It was as massive as a polar bear, with a thick white mane crowning a raw, red neck. The drawing wriggles with which its huge length extricated itself were sickening, redolent of rotting meat and ferrous blood, yet I dared not turn my eyes away. The moment its tail pulled free, it lay exhausted, wallowing in feeble, silent efforts to burrow once more into the peat. 

*Does it feed on the dead,* I wondered, an archivist trying to catalog an impossible ecosystem, *unable to digest the living? Why do they scent my pulse yet leave me unharmed?* I know now it was the moon’s icy dialect that bound them. 

All through the night, as I walked, an endless succession of hideous creatures threatened me, no two sharing the same taxonomy. In some, a perverse beauty of color masked their loathly shapes; one massive serpent was layered from head to distant tail in glorious, iridescent feathers. I became so accustomed to their soundless, hurtless menaces that I beguiled the grueling march by inventing names for these monstrosities, fighting the erosion of my own language by categorizing their horrors. I never suspected I owed every moment of my life to the staring moon. Though hers was no primal, life-giving radiance, its reflection so hampered the buried evils that I walked in safety. Even a sterile, translated light is still light, if but the last of a countless series of reflections.

How swiftly my feet would have carried me over that restless, fermented soil had I known that, if her lamp ceased to shine on this cursed spot, I would instantly be at the mercy of those who knew none. I would become the center of a writhing banquet of hideousness, every beast as terrible as it had before only seemed. Like a fool ignorant of the season's turn, I watched the weary, solemn descent of the moon down the widening vault, feeling no worse uneasiness than the dread of losing a path where, as yet, no path existed. 

I drew near the hills I had made my goal. The moon was hovering just above their sky-line when the soundless wallowing abruptly ceased. The burrow lay motionless, bare, and silent. 

Then I saw, walking slowly over the light soil, the form of a woman. A white mist floated about her, assuming and losing the shape of a garment as it gathered or was stripped away by a wind that dogged her steps. She was beautiful, but her countenance bore a pride and a misery so profound I could hardly digest the sight. Up and down she paced, vainly attempting to lay hold of the mist and wrap it around her like a shawl—a phantom of some ancient, domestic routine, an agrarian mother trying to knead the fog into warmth against the bitter chill. The eyes in her beautiful face were utterly dead. On her left side was a dark spot, and she pressed her hand against it repeatedly, as if trying to stifle a deep, gnawing sickness or hold the last ember of a dying culture within her breast. Her pale gold hair hung nearly to her feet, mixing with the mist in the moonlight until I could not distinguish the woman from the weather.

Suddenly, pressing both hands over her heart, she collapsed. The mist rose from her and dissolved into the frigid air. I ran toward her. But she began to writhe in such sudden, violent torture that I stopped aghast. A moment more, and her legs severed from her torso, hurrying away as slithering serpents. From her shoulders, her arms fled in terror—serpents also. Then something flew up from her chest like a bat, and when I looked again, the woman was entirely gone. It was the ultimate, horrifying untranslatability of a life broken down into venomous syllables, a matriarchal lineage shattered into grotesque fragments.

The ground suddenly rose like a pot boiling over. Terror laid hold of me. I turned to the hills and ran. 

I was already on the slope of their base when the moon sank behind a summit, plunging me into its shadow. The unnatural silence broke. Behind me rose a waste and sickening cry, as of frustrate desire—a mechanical, greedy screech that lacked all organic harmony, the only sound I had heard since the heavy fall of the dead butterfly. It tore through the acoustic ecology of the night, making my heart shake like a flag in a gale. I turned and saw a multitude of dark objects bounding after me, smelling of ash and rancid hunger.

I made for the crest of a ridge where the moonlight still pooled. She seemed to linger there, holding the high ground so I might defend myself. Coming in sight of her pale glow, I climbed faster, my lungs burning. Crossing the shadow of an outcropping rock, I heard the creatures panting wetly at my heels. Just as the foremost beast threw itself upon me with a snarl of pure, consuming hate, we rushed into the moonlight together. 

She flashed out a brilliant, angry light. The beast fell from me, instantly reduced to a bodiless blotch, like impurities skimmed and burned away from a clarifying broth. Strength returned to my limbs, and I turned on the rest. One by one, as they darted into the light, they dropped with a howl, dissolving into the earth. I saw, or perhaps only fancied, a strange, knowing smile on the round face above me. 

I climbed the final steps to the top of the ridge. Far away, the moon sank toward a low horizon. Here, elevated above the rotting terroir of the burrow, the air was pure, strong, and acoustically resonant. I descended a little way into a sheltered hollow, found it warmer, and sat down to wait. The moon slipped below the earth, and the world was once again plunged into darkness, but I sat in the quiet, wrapping myself in the generational patience of those who had waited out the long, hungry nights before the dawn.
