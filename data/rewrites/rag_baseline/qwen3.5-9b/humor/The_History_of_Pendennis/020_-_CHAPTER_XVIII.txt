Among the passengers sat a corpulent gentleman from the City, clad in grey stockings, accompanied by his pallid son. Their composure shattered upon learning that the coach was being driven by Mr. Foker of Saint Boniface College, a figure renowned for his universal amiability and driving prowess, allegedly surpassing even the legendary Tom Hicks. Meanwhile, Arthur Pendennis ("Pen") occupied the roof of the conveyance, surveying the coach, the passengers, and the countryside with boundless curiosity. As the university came into view, his heart swelled with anticipation; the majestic panorama of ancient towers, soaring pinnacles, towering elms, and the gleaming river unfolded before him.

Prior to this journey, Pen had spent several days at his uncle's lodgings in Bury Street. Despite this isolation, the Major escorted Pen into select vacant mansions, registering his name on the candidate list of one. Pen's reaction to this bureaucratic inclusion was one of ecstatic gratification. Reading his own inscription—"Arthur Pendennis, Esquire, of Fairoaks Lodge... proposed by Major Pendennis, and seconded by Viscount Colchicum"—he experienced a thrill of intense validation. Eager to bypass this waiting period, Pen mentally appropriated the stucco halls, vast libraries, and drawing rooms as his own inheritance. As the afternoon school bell tolled, signaling the start of classes, the group moved toward the playground. They encountered the formidable Doctor carrying his grammar book; Foker retreated uneasily, whereas Pen advanced, blushing slightly before shaking the dignitary's hand, laughing at the memory of the Latin Grammar that had previously disciplined him. Pen emerged from this encounter characterized by generosity, good nature, and a profound, self-satisfied confidence.

The tour continued to the familial brewery, Foker's Entire, a sprawling complex of buildings dominating the vicinity of Grey Friars, its name emblazoned on countless local pub signs. The senior partner and manager honored the "young lord of the vats" and his friend, serving them potent brown stout in silver flagons. The strength of the beverage was so considerable that it appeared to affect not only the drinkers but also Mr. Harry Foker's horse, Stoopid. The animal galloped frantically toward the western end of the town, endangering street vendors and pedestrians, causing the cab steps to collide with posts and forcing Stoopid to sway precariously on his boards.

Despite the chaos, the Major remained delighted by Pen's association with Foker, listening intently to the latter's straightforward anecdotes. He treated the two youths to a sumptuous dinner at a Covent Garden coffee-house, followed by a theatrical outing. The pinnacle of the evening occurred when Mr. and Mrs. Agnes Foker, visiting London, invited the Major and Arthur Pendennis to dinner at Grosvenor Street. He instructed Pen to consult *Debrett* regarding the genealogies of the Earls of Rosherville, encouraging him to weave subtle, complimentary historical allusions into conversation—a task suited to Pen's poetic inclinations. Although Mr. Foker lacked aristocratic lineage or extensive formal education, he maintained a tradition of serving family porter post-prandially, a custom the Major pledged to uphold despite his personal aversion to beer. True to his word, the Major sacrificed his comfort on the night of the dinner, allowing Foker to deliver his customary toast. The Major's suppressed grin at the worthy gentleman's joke speaks volumes about the shared camaraderie of the occasion.

Mrs. Agnes Foker, deeply devoted to her son Harry and possessing a disposition marked by extreme benevolence rather than wisdom, welcomed her son's friend with warmth. She surprised Pen by detailing the rigorous curriculum her beloved son was undertaking, expressing concern for his health. Elder Foker erupted in hearty laughter at her remarks, while the younger Foker exchanged a knowing glance with his friend. Mrs. Agnes then recounted her son's history from infancy, cataloging his miraculous survivals against measles, whooping cough, and near-drowning incidents, as well as the harsh disciplinary measures inflicted by the schoolmaster—a doctor she refused to forgive. After an hour of incessant maternal narration, she declared the two Messieurs Pendennis to be delightful company. When pheasants were presented as the second course, which the Major lauded as the finest specimens he had ever witnessed, Mrs. Agnes revealed they originated from Logwood—a fact the Major recognized immediately. She expressed hope that they would visit her establishment at Christmas or during Harry's vacation.

Later that evening, in Bury Street, as they prepared for bed, the Major offered Pen a final piece of mentorship. He commended Pen's successful allusion to Agincourt and a member of the Rosherville family, noting that while Mrs. Agnes had missed the reference, it was exemplary for a novice. He emphasized that securing a proper introduction (*entree*) grants equal access to high and low society, requiring no more effort to maintain standing in elite circles than dining with a lawyer in Bedford Square. He urged Pen to exercise extreme caution in selecting acquaintances during his Oxford studies, reminding him that the first step in life is paramount. Before departing, the Major reminded him to correspond with his mother and request a frank from Mr. Foker, a courtesy the latter appreciated.

Upon returning home, Pen penned a humorous chronicle of his London exploits—the theater, the Friars, the brewery, and the Foker soirée—to his mother, who awaited him at Fairoaks in solitude. Her heart overflowed with ineffable love and tenderness for her son. She and her sister Laura read his letters repeatedly, reflecting on them with the characteristic introspection of women. These missives represented Pen's inaugural step into the world, a perilous journey where even the bravest may falter and the strongest fail. The narrative concludes with a plea to fellow travelers: may they find supportive arms along the path, merciful forgiveness at the end, and the guiding light of love. Without these virtues, the traveler remains blind, navigating a dark and cheerless existence.
