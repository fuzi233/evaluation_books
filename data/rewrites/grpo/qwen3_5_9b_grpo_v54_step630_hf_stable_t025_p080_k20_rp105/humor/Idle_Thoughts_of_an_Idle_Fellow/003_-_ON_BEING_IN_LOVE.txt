You've certainly been in love, haven't you? Or if not, it's coming. Love is like the measles; we all have to catch it eventually. Like the measles, too, we only get it once. Once you've had it, you're immune. You can wander through shady woods, picnic in leafy aisles, and stare at sunsets without fear. You can join a family trip down the Rhine or sneak into a wedding ceremony just to see a friend off. You can dance a waltz until your feet ache and then nap in a dark conservatory, catching nothing worse than a sniffle. You can walk under the moonlight or through the rushes without tripping. You can look into sunny eyes without getting dazzled. You can listen to the siren song of romance and sail on with your helm tight. You can hold a white hand, but no electric shock binds you to it.

No, we never fall in love twice. Cupid fires only one arrow per heart. Love's attendants—respect, admiration, affection—are welcome guests who stay forever, but the Great Master himself makes only one royal visit and then leaves. We like, we cherish, we are very fond of—but we never love again. A man's heart is a firework that flashes once toward heaven. Like a meteor, it blazes brightly for a moment, lighting up the world below, and then the night of our mundane life closes in. The burnt-out shell falls back to earth, useless and ignored, slowly turning to ash. Once, breaking free from our chains, we dared, like old Prometheus, to climb Olympus and steal fire from the gods. Happy those who, before the flame dies, can use it to warm their hearths.

Love is too pure a light to survive long among the dirty air we breathe, but before it's choked out, we can use it to start a cozy fire of affection.

After all, that warm glow suits our small, cold parlor of a world better than the burning spirit of love. Love belongs in a mighty temple, where the organ plays the music of the spheres. Affection burns cheerfully when love's white flame flickers out. Affection is a fire you can feed daily, piling higher as winter approaches. Old men and women can sit by it with thin hands clasped; children can nestle in front; friends and neighbors can find a corner by its side; even shaggy dogs and sleek cats can toast their noses at the bars.

Let us heap kindness upon that fire. Throw in your pleasant words, your gentle hand-holds, your thoughtful deeds. Fan it with humor, patience, and forbearance. Let the wind blow and rain fall; your hearth will remain warm, and the faces around it will shine brighter than the sun outside.

I'm afraid, dear Edwin and Angelina, you expect too much from love. You think your little hearts have enough fuel for this fierce passion to last your entire lives. Ah, young fools! Don't rely on that unsteady flicker. It will dwindle as the months pass, and there is no refilling the tank. You will watch it die in anger and disappointment. To each, it will seem the other is growing cold. Edwin sees bitterly that Angelina no longer runs to the gate smiling and blushing; when he coughs, she doesn't cry and hug him, saying she can't live without him. At best, she suggests a lozenge, implying she'd rather get rid of the noise.

Poor Angelina, too, sheds silent tears because Edwin has stopped carrying her old handkerchief in his waistcoat pocket.

Both are shocked by the other's change, but neither sees their own. If they did, they wouldn't suffer so much. They would blame the right place—the smallness of human nature—hold hands over their shared flaw, and rebuild their home on a more earthly, lasting foundation. But we are so blind to our own faults and so alert to others'. Everything that goes wrong is always someone else's fault. Angelina would love Edwin forever if only he hadn't changed. Edwin would adore Angelina forever if only she hadn't changed.

It is a bleak hour when the lamp of love goes out and the fire of affection isn't lit yet, forcing you to grope in the cold dawn of life to start it again. May it catch before the day is done. Many sit shivering by dead coals until night falls.

But why preach? Who feels the rush of young love in their veins can believe it will ever slow down? To a twenty-year-old, it seems impossible he won't love as wildly at sixty as he does now. He can't recall any middle-aged man acting frantically attached, so he believes his own love will never fade. Nobody loves as he does, so the rest of the world's experience doesn't count. Alas! By thirty, he joins the ranks of the sneerers. It's not his fault. Our passions, good and bad, end with our blushes. We don't hate, grieve, rejoice, or despair in our thirties like we did in our teens. Disappointment doesn't suggest suicide, and success doesn't intoxicate us.

As we age, we play everything in a minor key. There are few majestic passages in the later acts of life's opera. Ambition aims lower. Honor becomes reasonable and adapts to circumstances. And love—love dies. "Irreverence for the dreams of youth" creeps like a killing frost upon our hearts. Tender shoots and expanding flowers are nipped and withered. Of a vine that yearned to wrap around the world, there remains only a sapless stump.

My fair friends will call this rank heresy. Far from men not loving after boyhood, it's not until they have gray hair that they think their protestations worthy of attention. Young ladies form their ideas of men from novels written by their own sex. Compared to the monsters masquerading as men in that nightmare literature, Pythagoras' plucked bird and Frankenstein's demon were average specimens of humanity.

In these so-called books, the chief lover, or Greek god (they don't specify which one; it might be hump-backed Vulcan, double-faced Janus, or driveling Silenus), resembles the whole family in being a blackguard. This elderly party in the stories has no claim to even the little manliness his classical prototypes had. He is a listless, effeminate noodle past forty. Yet, oh! the depth and strength of his emotion for some bread-and-butter schoolgirl! Hide your heads, ye young Romeos and Leanders! This *blase* old beau loves with hysterical fervor requiring four adjectives for every noun.

It is well, dear ladies, that you study only books. Did you read mankind, you would know that a boy's shy stammer tells a truer tale than a man's bold eloquence. A boy's love comes from a full heart; a man's is often the result of a full stomach. Indeed, a man's sluggish current isn't love compared to the rushing fountain that wells up when a boy's heart is struck by the heavenly rod. If you want to taste love, drink the pure stream youth pours at your feet. Don't wait until it becomes a muddy river before stooping to catch its waves.

Or is it that you like its bitter flavor? That the clear, limpid water is insipid to your palate, and the pollution of its after-course gives it a relish? Must we believe those who tell us a hand foul with the filth of a shameful life is the only one a young girl wants to be caressed by?

That is the teaching bawled out day and night from between those yellow covers. Do they ever pause to think, those devil's lady-helps, what mischief they are doing crawling in God's garden, telling childish Eves and silly Adams that sin is sweet and decency is ridiculous? How many innocent girls do they not degrade into evil-minded women? To how many weak lads do they not point out the dirty by-path as the shortcut to a maiden's heart? They don't write of life as it really is. Speak truth, and right will take care of itself. But their pictures are coarse daubs painted from the sickly fancies of their own diseased imaginations.

We want to think of women not—as their own sex shows them—as Lorelei luring us to destruction, but as good angels beckoning us upward. They have more power for good or evil than they dream. It is just at the age when a man's character forms that he falls in love, and then the lass he loves makes or mars him. Unconsciously, he molds himself to what she wants, good or bad. I'm sorry to be ungallant enough to say I don't think they always use their influence for the best. Too often the female world is bounded hard within the limits of the commonplace. Their ideal hero is a prince of littleness, and to become that, many a powerful mind enchanted by love is "lost to life and use and name and fame."

And yet, women, you could make us so much better if you would. It rests with you, more than with all the preachers, to roll this world a little nearer heaven. Chivalry is not dead; it only sleeps for lack of work. You must wake it to noble deeds. You must be worthy of knightly worship.

You must be higher than ourselves. It was for Una that the Red Cross Knight did war. No painted, mincing court dame could have the dragon slain. Oh, ladies fair, be fair in mind and soul as well as face, so brave knights may win glory in your service! Oh, woman, throw off your disguising cloaks of selfishness, effrontery, and affectation! Stand forth once more a queen in your royal robe of simple purity. A thousand swords, now rusting in ignoble sloth, shall leap from their scabbards to fight for your honor against wrong. A thousand Sir Rolands shall lay lance in rest, and Fear, Avarice, Pleasure, and Ambition shall go down in the dust before your colors.

What noble deeds were we not ready for in the days when we loved? What noble lives could we not have lived for her sake? Our love was a religion we could have died for. It was no mere human creature like ourselves that we adored. It was a queen we paid homage to, a goddess we worshiped.

And how madly we did worship! And how sweet it was to worship! Ah, lad, cherish love's young dream while it lasts! You will know too soon how truly little Tom Moore sang when he said there was nothing half so sweet in life. Even when it brings misery, it is a wild, romantic misery, unlike the dull, worldly pain of after-sorrows. When you have lost her—when the light goes out from your life and the world stretches before you as a long, dark horror—even then a half-enchantment mingles with your despair.

And who wouldn't risk its terrors to gain its raptures? Ah, what raptures they were! The mere recollection thrills you. How delicious it was to tell her you loved her, that you lived for her, that you would die for her! How you raved, what floods of extravagant nonsense you poured forth, and oh, how cruel it was of her to pretend not to believe you! In what awe you stood of her! How miserable you were when you offended her! And yet, how pleasant to be bullied by her and to sue for pardon without knowing what your fault was! How dark the world was when she snubbed you, the little rogue, just to see you look wretched; how sunny when she smiled! How jealous you were of everyone around her! How you hated every man she shook hands with, every woman she kissed—the maid who did her hair, the boy who cleaned her shoes, the dog she nursed—though you had to be respectful to the last! How you looked forward to seeing her, how stupid you were when you did see her, staring without speaking! How impossible it was to go out without eventually ending up opposite her windows! You didn't have the pluck to go in, but you hung about the corner and gazed. Oh, if the house had caught fire—it was insured, so it wouldn't matter—and you could have rushed in to save her at the risk of your life, terribly burned and injured! Anything to serve her. Even in little things, it was so sweet. How you watched her, spaniel-like, to anticipate her slightest wish! How proud you were to do her bidding! How delightful it was to be ordered about by her! To devote your whole life to her and never think of yourself seemed so simple. You would skip a holiday to lay a humble offering at her shrine and feel more than repaid if she only deigned to accept it. How precious everything was that she had hallowed with her touch—her little glove, the ribbon she wore, the rose that nestled in her hair, whose withered leaves still mark the poems you never look at now.

And oh, how beautiful she was, how wondrous beautiful! It was as some angel entering the room, and all else became plain and earthly. She was too sacred to be touched. It seemed almost presumption to gaze at her. You would as soon have thought of kissing her as singing comic songs in a cathedral. It was desecration enough to kneel and timidly raise the gracious little hand to your lips.

Ah, those foolish days, those foolish days when we were unselfish and pure-minded; those foolish days when our simple hearts were full of truth, faith, and reverence! Ah, those foolish days of noble longings and noble strivings! And oh, these wise, clever days when we know money is the only prize worth striving for, when we believe in nothing else but meanness and lies, when we care for no living creature but ourselves!
