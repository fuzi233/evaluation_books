I couldn’t even begin to put into words the absolute chaos cats and dogs have put me through this morning. It all started with Gustavus Adolphus—Gusty, as the downstairs folks shorten it to, to save their breath. Out in a big open field or a sprawling common? He’s the best-behaved, sweetest dog you could ask for. Indoors, though? He’s a 70-pound walking natural disaster who has never once grasped that a standard terraced house was not built to accommodate his personal brand of nonsense. Stretch one paw lazily, and two chairs plus your favorite knickknack shelf full of tiny porcelain trinkets go toppling. Wag his tail once, and the whole room looks like a rampaging toddler with a wrecking ball just passed through. Breathe too hard at the parlor fire, and it blows out like a birthday candle.

At dinnertime he creeps under the table, lies quiet for ten whole minutes, then leaps up like he just remembered he left the gas on. The first clue we get is the table doing a very dramatic impression of someone trying to pull a somersault. We all cling to its edges for dear life like we’re bobbing in a storm-tossed life raft, trying desperately to keep the roast beef on the horizontal. But Gusty, fully convinced we’re hatching some evil plot to force-feed him Brussels sprouts, panics worse, and the whole scene ends with the table overturned, the entire dinner smashed, and three sprawling, infuriated grown-ups covered in gravy and mashed potatoes.

This morning he barreled in like he was an American cyclonewith a waggy tail, and his very first move was to swipes my full coffee cup straight off the table, dumping the entire contents dead-center of my brand new waistcoat. I shot up so fast my chair tipped over, muttered a few very choice words, and made a beeline for him. He bolted for the door, and skidded straight into Eliza coming in with a full tray of eggs. Eliza yelped “UGH” and plopped straight down on the carpet, every single egg abandoned its tray and splattered across the rug in a messy yellow splay, and Gusty vanished out of the room. I yelled after him very firmly that he better hot-foot it downstairs and not let me see his face for the next hour at least. He seemed to agree, dodged the coal scoop I accidentally hurled in his direction, and vanished. I dried myself off, finished my cold toast, and assumed he was moping in the back yard.

Ten minutes later, though, I peeked into the passage and he was perched right at the top of the stairs, staring daggers at something on the step just below him. It was Tittums, our kitten. She’s roughly the size of a buttered penny roll, her back arched so high she looks like a tiny fuzzy rainbow, and she’s swearing so foully I’d turn bright red if the local parson overheard it. She swears worse than any medical student on a pub crawl. I dabble in the occasional curse myself, but next to her I’m a rank amateur.

Between you and me—keep this strictly confidential, please, I’d hate for your wife to hear I said this, the ladies never quite get the point—swearing is good for a man. It’s the safety valve that lets all your pent-up bad temper escape as harmless huffing, instead of festering inside and wrecking your whole mental state. When you’ve stepped on a lego barefoot, and you’ve said, “Oh bless you, my sweet dear fellow, what in the name of all the planets made you decide to leave that scattered right where my bare foot would land? Are you physically incapable of looking down? You wonderful clever thing you!” you feel ten times better. It does the same soothing work as slamming doors or smashing furniture, but it’s way cheaper. Swearing clears your head like a penny’s worth of gunpowder blowing out a soot-clogged washhouse chimney. A little explosion every now and then is good for everyone. I don’t trust a man who never swears, never kicks a footstool across the room in frustration, never stabs the poker into the fire like he’s got a personal grudge against the coals. If you’ve got no outlet for all the tiny annoyances of daily life, they don’t disappear—they sit next to you, slowly turning from tiny petty nuisances into big bitter grudges that breed hatred and spite.

I tried explaining that to my aunt once, and she was furious. Said I had no business having bad temper to vent in the first place. Sure, Aunty.

That’s exactly what I told Tittums, too. I told her she ought to be ashamed of herself, raised in a perfectly respectable Christian household and all. I don’t mind hearing a fully grown cat curse up a storm, but a tiny kitten? It feels like seeing a toddler puff on a cigar, downright tragic. I tucked her into my pocket and headed back to my desk to write, promptly forgot she was there, and by the time I looked over she’d squirmed out onto the table, was trying to swallow my pen, stuck her whole leg in the open ink pot and tipped the whole thing over, licked the ink off her leg, and then swore at me like I was the idiot who left an open ink pot unattended.

I plopped her down on the floor, and Tim—our annoying two-year-old fox terrier who goes around acting like a wise 80-year-old Scotch collie—started yapping at her like he’s the neighborhood moral police. I wish Tim would mind his own business, honestly. It had nothing to do with him. Tittums’ mom heard the racket, trotted in, and scratched Tim right on the nose, which I was extremely pleased about. I herded all three of them out into the passage, where they’re still rolling around brawling as I write this. I’m covered in ink, my waistcoat is still stained with coffee, and if any other cat or dog dares come bounding over to bother me this morning, they better pre-book their own funeral contractor and pay for the daffodils themselves.

For all that chaos, though? I absolutely adore cats and dogs, general. They’re far better companions than almost any human being I’ve ever met. They don’t argue with you, they don’t corner you at dinner and loudly mention to Miss Brown that they always heard she was madly in love with Mr. Jones, who just married Miss Robinson last week. They never mix up your wife’s cousin for her husband and accidentally call you the father-in-law. They never look at the poor young writer with fourteen tragedies, sixteen comedies, seven farces, and two burlesques stuffed in his desk and ask him why he hasn’t written a hit play yet.

They never pull you aside and say “I’m only saying this for your own good, but you’ve really let yourself go lately, and remember that time you messed up the office holiday party two years ago?” They don’t say, sarcastic as anything, “Oh yeah, you’re real helpful whenever I actually need a hand, aren’t you?” They never pout and tell you you’re not half as nice as you were when you first started dating. To them, you’re always the same, no matter what mood you’re in. They’re merry when you’re giddy, quiet when you want to think, and sad when you’re moping.

“Wanna run wild and tear around the drawing-room? Don’t even worry about the furniture, I’m right here,” your dog will say, frisking around your ankles, “we’ll scamper across the fields, chase the geese out of the old farmer’s yard, and have the best day ever. C’mon!” Or if you want to sit quiet and think? The cat will curl up on the arm of your chair, purring so loud it rumbles, and the dog will stretch out on the rug, half-asleep but keeping one eye cracked open just in case you suddenly decide you want to chase rats.

And when you bury your face in your hands and wish you’d never been born? They don’t sit up very straight and go “well you brought all this on yourself, I hope it’s a lesson to you.” They just nudge at you softly. The cat will climb up on your shoulder, knead your hair with her tiny paws, and purr so hard it feels like she’s saying “Oh buddy, that sucks. I’m here.” The dog will look up at you with those big, true, dopey eyes, like he’s telling you “you’ve always got me, yeah? We’re in this mess together, no matter what.”

Dogs don’t ever stop to ask if you’re right or wrong, rich or poor, a sinner or a saint. You’re their pal. That’s all that matters. If luck turns to garbage, everyone else abandons you, your reputation’s in the gutter? That stupid, loyal, big soft dog will stick right by you, guard you, comfort you, and even die for you if he has to. People love to go on and on about how humans are the smartest, noblest, best creatures in the whole universe. But that gin-soaked lout leaning against the pub wall? He’s supposed to be intellectually superior to that loyal dog? That two-bit lying scammer who cheats everyone out of their money, never does a kind deed in his whole life? He’s nobler than that brave, selfless dog? Sure, mate, whatever you say. We all know who the better creature is. We’re all just stupid enough to love the dogs and cats more, anyway. We love stupid things, don’t we? Men hate super clever women, every woman’s ideal man is some guy she can call a “dear old stupid.” It’s so nice to hang out with something that’s dumber than you are, no hidden agenda, no snarky comments.

Everyone says cats are selfish, worldly wise, only looking out for their own comfort. It’s total slander. A cat who’s your friend will stick with you through thick and thin, no questions asked. I had one cat once who followed me absolutely everywhere, even all the way down the high street, until people started staring so hard I had to beg her as a personal favor to stop escorting me past the bakery. She’d sit up for hours waiting for me to come home late, and meet me at the door, which made me feel almost like a married man—except she never asked where I’d been and then refused to believe me when I told the truth. Another cat I owned snuck into the cellar every single day to lap up beer drippings from the barrel, and got so drunk she’d stagger around the living room. Whenever she caught a big rat, she’d drop it dead in the middle of our group, stand there waiting for everyone to clap, making all the girls shriek so loud the neighbors would knock on the door to check if we were being murdered.

I love all those spooky old rat tales, too. The story of Bishop Hatto, who burned all the starving villagers to death in his barn, then thousands of rats swam the Rhine to his tower and ate him alive? The Pied Piper of Hamelin, who piped all the rats away, then took all the children when the mayor cheated him out of his pay? I swear the Pied Piper still wanders around our streets today, piping so soft only the kids can hear it. That’s why little kids stop mid-game, go all quiet and solemn, staring off into space—they’re listening to that magic music we grown ups can’t quite hear over all the loud blaring noise of the world. One day soon the music will get loud enough for all of us to hear, and we’ll drop all our stupid little toys and follow, too.

I only wish more people could love their pets without being completely ridiculous and sappy about it. The gushing young ladies who kiss their long-haired stray dog’s nose right in front of every eligible young man, the old ladies who feed their poodle a mutton cutlet for breakfast every day and skip church on Sundays to keep the dog company? There are whole families where the entire conversation revolves around their stupid dog from morning till night. If you want to borrow their garden roller, or ask their daughter out, or get them to donate to the very necessary Society for Banning Solo Cornet Players in Theater Orchestras, you have to win the dog’s approval first. If the dog snaps at you? You’re banned forever. “If Fido doesn’t like you,” the dad will say thoughtfully, “then you can’t be a good person. Bless his little heart.” Ugh.

It’s funny, isn’t it? Even that stuffy dignified old pussy cat with her slow walk and sleepy eyes was once a feral little kitten bouncing off the walls, so full of life she didn’t know what to do with all of it. Doesn’t that remind you of when we were young? Those moonlit walks home where we were so bursting with life we couldn’t just walk, we had to skip and jump and wave our arms and yell, scaring the local farmers’ wives so bad they’d scuttle into the hedges, and we’d laugh so hard tears came out of our eyes. Our pulses are slow and steady now, our joints ache, we sit in our easy chairs with our pipes and sneer at “silly teenage enthusiasm.” But man, wouldn’t we all give anything to feel that bright, wild, full life in our veins again, even for just ten seconds?
