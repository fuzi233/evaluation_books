"Miss Clack sends her warmest regards to Mr. Franklin Blake. Regarding the fifth installment of her modest chronicle, she must confess that the gravity of Lady Verinder's passing renders her utterly incapable of expanding upon such a tragic event as she might otherwise desire. To compensate for this deficiency, she has appended to her own writings extensive excerpts drawn from authoritative texts in her library, all pertaining directly to this dreadful occurrence. May these selections serve, Miss Clack earnestly prays, as a clarion call to awaken the conscience of her esteemed relative, Mr. Franklin Blake."

In reply, "Mr. Franklin Blake acknowledges Miss Clack's kind sentiments and expresses his gratitude for the fifth chapter. While declining to voice any personal reservations regarding the nature of her supplementary material, he respectfully informs her that these additional documents are superfluous to the specific objective he intends to achieve with the manuscript."

"Miss Clack graciously accepts the return of her extracts. With maternal affection, she reminds Mr. Blake that, being a creature of faith, it is inconceivable that he could possibly intend to cause her offense. Yet, her devotion remains undiminished; she pledges that should illness ever strike him down, she shall once again place these very extracts at his disposal. Meanwhile, prior to commencing the concluding sections of her narrative, she humbly inquires if she is granted permission to render her contribution whole by incorporating insights gained from subsequent revelations concerning the mystery of the Moonstone."

"Mr. Franklin Blake regrets that he cannot accede to Miss Clack's request. He reiterates the directive originally given when she commenced her account: she is strictly bound to record only her own direct observations of people and events as they appeared in her private journal. Any later discoveries must be left to the pens of those who were present as actual eyewitnesses."

"Miss Clack is deeply pained to disturb Mr. Blake further after having her extracts rejected and her scholarly interpretations forbidden. Though acutely aware that she appears to have been dismissed, she declares that adversity has taught her the virtue of perseverance. Her sole remaining query concerns the publication of their exchange itself: does Mr. Blake prohibit the inclusion of this correspondence within Miss Clack's narrative? An explanation of the constraints imposed upon her role as an author seems a matter of basic fairness. Furthermore, Miss Clack is eager that her letters stand as evidence in their own defense."

"Mr. Franklin Blake consents to Miss Clack's proposal, provided she understands this agreement as the formal termination of their correspondence."

"Before sealing this exchange, Miss Clack feels compelled, out of a sense of Christian duty, to inform Mr. Blake that his final letter—which she perceives as intentionally offensive—has failed to achieve its aim. She gently urges him to retreat to the solitude of his chamber and reflect upon whether the discipline required to elevate a frail woman above the reach of insult deserves more profound admiration than he currently offers. Should he grant her this assurance, Miss Clack solemnly vows to return the complete series of her extracts to Mr. Franklin Blake."

*(Signed)* **DRUSILLA CLACK**
