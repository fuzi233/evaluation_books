If my readers have kept pace with me thus far, they will find it unsurprising to learn that existence in Flatland is, by most accounts, profoundly tedious. I am careful to specify that I do not refer to the absence of conflict; battles, conspiracies, factionalism, and the chaotic turbulence of political intrigue abound, providing a raw drama that mimics the histories of higher dimensions. Nor shall I deny that the peculiar friction between the exigencies of daily survival and the rigorous demands of mathematics creates a unique intellectual spark, a constant state of conjecture verified instantly by the laws of geometry, a zest for discovery that inhabitants of Spaceland can scarcely fathom. Yet, when viewed strictly through the lens of aesthetics and art, life here is a barren plain, devoid of color and texture. How else could it be? Our entire visual universe—our landscapes, our historical tableaux, our portraiture, our floral arrangements, our still lifes—is reduced to a singular dimension: the line. We possess no variety save for the subtle gradations of brightness and shadow, a grayscale existence where nuance is merely a matter of tone rather than hue.

It was not always so. According to the sagas preserved by tradition, during a fleeting epoch spanning perhaps half a dozen centuries, a transient splendor bathed the lives of our ancestors. Legend attributes this revolution to a solitary figure, a Polygon whose name varies wildly among storytellers, who stumbled upon the constituents of primary colors and a rudimentary technique for applying pigment. Initially, he decorated his own dwelling, then ventured to adorn his slaves, subsequently his father, his sons, and finally, in a moment of supreme vanity, himself. The dual utility of convenience and beauty quickly won universal acclaim. Wherever Chromatistes—the name by which authoritative historians uniformly designate him—displayed his variegated silhouette, he commanded instant attention and earned profound respect. In a world defined by angles and proximity, no one needed to "feel" his presence to distinguish friend from foe; no one mistook his front for his back. His neighbors could ascertain his position and orientation without straining their calculative faculties, avoiding the jostling and collisions that plague the colorless masses. Furthermore, his voice was liberated from the exhausting labor required by us pale Squares and Pentagons to loudly proclaim our individuality amidst crowds of indistinguishable Isosceles triangles.

This fashion ignited like wildfire. Within a week, every Square and Triangle in the district had emulated Chromatistes, while only a handful of conservative Pentagons resisted the tide. A month or two later, even the Dodecagons fell under the spell of the innovation. Less than a year passed before the practice permeated all ranks save the very highest nobility. Naturally, the custom radiated outward from Chromatistes' district to neighboring regions, and within two generations, the only beings in all Flatland remaining devoid of paint were the Women and the Priests. Here, Nature seemingly erected an insurmountable barrier, pleading against the extension of this cultural shift to these two classes. For the Innovators, multiplicity of sides served as the necessary pretext for differentiation. "Distinction of sides implies distinction of colours"—this was the sophistry that circulated from mouth to mouth, converting entire cities overnight to the new aesthetic. Yet, this axiom held no sway for our Priests and Women. The latter possessed only one side, and thus, speaking plurally and pedantically, NO SIDES AT ALL. The former, if they dared to assert their claim to being true Circles rather than merely high-class polygons with an infinite number of infinitesimally small sides, boasted—and Women confessed and deplored—that they too lacked sides, blessed instead with a continuous perimeter, a circumference. Consequently, these two classes perceived no logical force in the argument linking sides to color. While the rest of society succumbed to the fascinations of corporal decoration, the Priests and Women alone remained pure from the pollution of paint.

Immoral, licentious, anarchical, unscientific—label them as you will—those ancient days of the Color Revolt constituted the glorious childhood of Art in Flatland. Alas, this childhood never ripened into manhood, nor even reached the bloom of youth. Life itself became a delight, simply because to live implied to see. Even a modest gathering transformed into a spectacle; the richly varied hues of an assembly in a church or theater were said to have proven so visually overwhelming that they distracted our greatest teachers and actors from their duties. But nothing rivaled the unspeakable magnificence of a military review. Imagine the sight: a line of battle composed of twenty thousand Isosceles suddenly pivoting, exchanging the somber black of their bases for vibrant oranges and purples along their flanks and acute angles; the militia of Equilateral Triangles blazoned in tricolors of red, white, and blue; the artillerymen, Squares arrayed in mauve, ultramarine, gamboge, and burnt umber, rapidly rotating near their vermilion cannons; the dashing, flashing procession of five-colored and six-colored Pentagons and Hexagons careering across the field in their roles as surgeons, geometricians, and aides-de-camp. These spectacles were surely sufficient to lend credence to the famous tale of an illustrious Circle, overwhelmed by the artistic grandeur of the forces beneath his command, casting aside his marshal's baton and his royal crown to declare that he would henceforth wield only the artist's pencil.

The magnitude of this sensuous development is partially evidenced by the very lexicon of the era. The common utterances of ordinary citizens during the Time of the Color Revolt appear to have been suffused with a richer tinge of word and thought. To that vibrant epoch we remain indebted for our finest poetry and for whatever rhythm still echoes in the more scientific discourses of these modern, gray days.
