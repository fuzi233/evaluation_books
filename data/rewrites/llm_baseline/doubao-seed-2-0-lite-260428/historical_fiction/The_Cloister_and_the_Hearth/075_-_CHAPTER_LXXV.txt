Clement drew a slow, heavy sigh. He began to wonder if he had chosen the wisest path with this fiery, unruly soul laid bare at his feet. Young as he was, though, he had already drunk deep of the hard-won wisdom of the Church: one of the first lessons he had been taught was the grace of pause. When tangled difficulties arose, to do or say nothing at all, hold his tongue, and pray for clarity, until the knots of the matter softened and unspooled a little on their own.

So he kept his peace, knelt quietly a while, and prayed for wisdom to guide her. The only small movement he made was to gently try to withdraw his foot from where it rested near her. But the distraught penitent flung her arms tight around it with a piteous, half-sobbing cry, clinging to it convulsively, hot tears dripping down onto his worn leather sandal. The full agony of her tangled shame and true penance revealed itself then, as a bright, burning crimson crept all the way up her throat, where she lay quivering on the cold stone at his feet.

“My daughter,” Clement said, his voice soft and steady, “take courage. Torment yourself no more over this Gerard who lives only as a ghost of your own fancies now. As for me, I am Brother Clement, sent to you by Heaven this very day to comfort you, and help you save your soul. You have made me your confessor, and so I claim your obedience, as is my right.”

“Oh yes, anything,” she sobbed.

“Leave this pilgrimage at once, and turn straight back for Rome. Penance done wandering foreign roads, far from the people and places that know you, is worth very little. The temptations we are called to defeat, lest we perish, lie right where we have built our lives. We do not run from them to hunt down newer, showier trials that never cut close to the quick. It is easy to wash the feet of strangers when our own faces are hidden behind masks. It is far, far harder to practice quiet meekness and charity with the people we live beside every day.”

“I will never, never lift a hand to harm her again,” she gasped.

“Nay, I speak not only of your servants,” he corrected her, “but of all your household dependents, your kinsmen, your friends. This shall be your penance: the very last thought before you sleep each night, and the first after matins at dawn, call to mind your sin, and the boundless goodness of God. From that remembrance, learn to be humble and gentle when those around you stumble and err. The whole world courts the rich and powerful, but you shall seek out the poor—not the common beggars at the crossroads, who for the most part are neither honest nor truly destitute. Seek instead those worthy souls who blush to beg for aid, yet are in dire need of help you can give. To give to them is to lay up treasure in Heaven, to lend directly to God. And you shall marry a good, faithful son of the Church.”

“Me? I will never marry,” she cried, shocked.

“You will marry within the year,” he said firmly, gentle but unshakable. “I entreat you, and command you, to take for your husband a man who fears the Lord. For you are soft, unshaped clay. Mismatched to a cruel or godless man, you will crumble to nothing. But wedded to a worthy husband, by the grace of God, you may live as a pious, charitable princess—ay, and die a saint, one day.”

“Me?” she whispered, disbelieving.

“You,” he said.

He bade her rise then, and set off to begin the good work he had laid out for her. She pushed herself up to her knees, pulled off her mask, and cast one long, eloquent, tearful look up at him, before dropping her eyes meekly to the ground.

“I will obey you as if you were an angel sent straight from Heaven. I am so happy, and yet so unhappy—for my heart tells me I will never look upon your face again. I will not leave until I have washed and dried your feet.”

“That is unnecessary,” he told her. “I have already excused you this bootless, unnecessary penance.”

“It is no penance to me,” she wept. “Ah, you do not truly forgive me if you will not let me tend to your poor, tired feet.”

“Then so be it,” Clement said, resigned, and thought to himself with a faint, amused softness that truly, women will cling fast to the smallest, most trivial small acts of piety. These weak-seeming creatures, who gravitate toward tiny, tender gestures as the stars are drawn to the great central light of the heavens, hold flashes of angelic intelligence no man can match, though. When the princess had finished drying his feet, she looked up at him with tears glistening in her beautiful eyes, and murmured with a singular tenderness and goodness he had not expected from her:

“I will have masses said for her soul. May I?” she added, timid, as if afraid to overstep.

The words caught him entirely off guard, and a faint warm blush rose to the monk’s cheek, and softened the cold, clear blue of his eyes. It was such a gentle, gracious thought, coming from a soul he had just moments before judged so shallow and flighty.

“It is a gracious, blessed thought,” he told her. “Do as you will. Oftentimes such kind acts fall back on the one who gives them like blessed dew. But I am your confessor, not hers. It is your soul I must bend all my strength to save now, or woe will fall on my own. My daughter, my dear daughter, I see good angels and ill angels fighting for your soul this very day, this very moment—oh, fight on the side of good, for your own sake. Do you remember every word I bade you do?”

“Remember?” the princess echoed. “Sweet saint, every syllable that has fallen from your lips is carved deep into my heart.”

“Then one last word, before we part. Pray much to Christ our Lord, and little to his saints.”

“I will,” she promised.

“That is the best, truest word I have the light to give you. So we part on it. You will return to the place that becomes you best, your father’s house. I go back to the work of my holy order, the work of God.”

“Farewell,” the princess faltered. “Farewell, you whom I have loved too well, hated too bitterly, known and revered too late. Forgiving angel, farewell—for ever.”

The monk caught those faint, sighing words, even half-breathed.

“For ever?” he cried aloud, sudden ardor lighting his whole face. “Christians live for ever, and love for ever—they never part for ever! They part as the earth parts from the sun at dusk, only to meet far brighter a little while later. You and I part here for the span of a single human life. And what is that life? One short line in the great, endless story of the Church, whose son and daughter we are. One handful of sand in the endless dunes of time, one single drop in the ocean of eternity. Farewell—for this little, fleeting moment we call a life! We part now in trouble and sorrow, but we shall meet in perfect peace. We part now as creatures of clay, but we shall meet as immortal spirits. We part in a world full of sin and grief, but we shall meet where all is purity and divine love, where no ill passions can ever touch us, where Christ reigns, and all His saints stand around Him clad in white. There, in the turn of an hourglass, in the burst of a bubble on the water, in the passing of a single wisp of cloud, she, and you, and I, shall meet again. We shall sit at the feet of angels and archangels, apostles and saints, and glow with unutterable joy, bathed in the light that streams from the shadow of God upon His throne, for ever— and ever— and ever.”

And so they parted. The monk stood erect, his eyes turned heavenward, glowing with the sacred fire of holy zeal. The princess walked slowly away, turning again and again to cast one lingering glance of awe and soft, tender regret back on that inspired, glowing figure.

She returned home subdued, her heart purged and made soft. Clement, in the fullness of time, reached Basle, and took up his new duties: he taught at the university, and preached to the crowds of the town and all the surrounding villages. The life he led could be summed up in just two words: unceasing deep study, and rigorous, devout self-mortification. The reader has already been given a small glimpse of his soul. At Basle, he grew ever deeper in holy zeal and sacred knowledge. The other brethren of his order soon began to recognize in him a true heir to the saints and martyrs of the early Church.
