To fully comprehend the shifting dynamics of the relationship between Lucy and M. Paul, one must first contextualize the rigid ecclesiastical architecture governing their interactions. They existed under the vigilant scrutiny of Rome, a force that monitored its offspring through the mystical lattice of the confessional—a sliding panel of revelation to which M. Emanuel approached with increasing frequency. Before their covenant of friendship could be formally ratified, an external power intervened, introducing a variable of religious orthodoxy into their private equation.

Lucy reflects on the nature of their bond, noting that while M. Paul had previously demonstrated undeniable partiality toward her, the explicit declaration of their status required careful calibration. He had addressed her as "sister," a designation she accepted with gratitude, provided it remained strictly platonic. Given his vow of celibacy, the risk of this terminology evolving into a romantic implication appeared negligible. However, the subsequent nights revealed a profound disconnect between expectation and reality. Lucy anticipated a display of fraternal intimacy, perhaps a shared joke or a word of encouragement, yet M. Paul maintained a deliberate emotional distance. His lessons became quieter, more grave, devoid of the brotherly warmth she craved. When he finally departed, offering only a hurried, shy nod, Lucy interpreted this silence as an accident of circumstance, a temporary lapse in communication that patience would surely correct.

Yet, the distance persisted and widened. Despite his earlier invitation for her to test his resolve—to tease and probe—he proved inaccessible. Unlike others who might exploit such privileges, Lucy found herself paralyzed by passivity. Repelled, she retreated further into isolation, unable to vocalize her confusion or demand clarification. It seemed her internal calculations regarding their alliance contained a fatal error, leaving her desperate for time to rectify the discrepancy.

The turning point arrived on a customary evening when M. Paul allocated his generous hour to reviewing weekly studies. The classroom was empty, bathed in the cooling shadows of twilight, while the courtyard beyond glowed with the final blush of the setting sun. Through the open doors, the scene was vibrant: M. Emanuel tended to his beloved orange trees and cacti, his dark coat and jet-black hair illuminated by the crimson light. He moved with a gentle, fatherly demeanor, attended by Sylvie, a small, affectionate spaniel that seemed to belong to him more than the students. As he worked, extinguishing the thirst of the plants with water from the well, the atmosphere shifted from communal activity to solitary contemplation.

As the clock tolled another hour and the sunlight faded, Lucy realized her lesson would be brief. She waited, hoping for a final exchange, but the opportunity passed. M. Paul finished his ablutions, drying his hands in a stone basin, and paused to gaze at the pale moon rising against the opal sky. Sylvie, restless and eager, whined to break his reverie. He picked up the dog, sauntering across the courtyard with tender affection, before stopping abruptly at the front doorsteps. He looked back at the moon and the looming cathedral, tasting the crisp air of dusk, before scanning the building with a keen, searching glance. Whether he bowed remains uncertain, but the moment passed; he vanished behind the closed door, leaving Lucy alone with her unanswered questions.

Armed with a heavy heart, Lucy prepared for the evening's ennui, choosing solitude over the company of her fellow inmates. Within her desk, hidden beneath the lid, she sought refuge, arranging her books until she noticed a new pamphlet in lilac paper, inserted recently during dinner. Curiosity compelled her to open it. It was not a narrative or a poem, but a theological treatise preaching Romanism with a honeyed, persuasive voice. It promised comfort and indulgence to the Protestant convert, avoiding threats of damnation in favor of gentle guidance. The author appealed directly to the emotions, offering the solace of praying deceased loved ones out of purgatory—a concept Lucy found amusingly contradictory compared to the simpler peace of denying purgatory altogether.

Despite its shallow, sentimental tone, reminiscent of childhood Methodist tracts, the booklet possessed a strange cheerfulness. Then, she noticed the inscription on the flyleaf: "From P. C. D. E. to L—y." Laughter erupted within her, not of amusement, but of sudden clarity. The initials connected Père Silas, the Jesuit director, with Paul Emanuel. The pieces of the puzzle clicked into place. The penitent had confessed everything to his director, revealing the secret covenant of fraternity and the adoption of a sister. How could such a union be sanctioned by the Church? The implication was terrifying: Père Silas must have annulled the pact, warning the priest of its spiritual peril and commanding strict separation.

While the idea of a supernatural adversary haunting the background was unsettling, it was preferable to the alternative: the possibility that M. Paul himself had spontaneously changed his mind. Seeking confirmation, Lucy stepped out into the garden. The weather had turned foul, with a clammy fog rolling in from the marshes, making the alleys damp and dreary. Yet, she heard Sylvie's excited barking. Rushing to the window, she saw the dog sprinting through the gloom, leading her to the *allée défendue*. There, amidst the rain-soaked shrubs, M. Paul was digging furiously with a spade. His movements were frantic, his brow knit in concentration, as if trying to unearth something buried deep within the earth—or perhaps burying his own turmoil. Sylvie, sensing her mistress's approach, barked insistently at the windowpane, urging Lucy to join him in his labor. In this silent, muddy tableau, Lucy read a ruffled mood, a storm of internal conflict manifesting in the physical act of digging, leaving her to wonder if the friar's command had indeed severed their bond forever.
