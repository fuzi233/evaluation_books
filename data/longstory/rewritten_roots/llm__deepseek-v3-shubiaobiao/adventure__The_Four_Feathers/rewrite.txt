Lieutenant Sutch was the first to arrive at Broad Place that afternoon. The June sun hung low over the Surrey hills, painting the old Feversham estate in hues of amber and deep green. The house, nestled among pines, stood like a sentinel overlooking the rolling plains below.

Sutch limped across the hall, his gaze drifting over the generations of Fevershams staring sternly from their portraits. He found General Feversham on the terrace, his posture rigid, eyes fixed on the distant Sussex Downs.

“How’s the leg?” the general asked without turning.

“Troublesome in winter,” Sutch replied. “But no matter.”

Feversham gave a curt nod. Silence settled between them, broken only by the murmur of laborers in the fields below. A train snaked along the horizon, its smoke a fleeting scar against the sky.

“I thought I’d find you here,” Sutch remarked.

“My wife’s favorite spot,” Feversham said flatly. “She liked empty spaces.”

“She had the mind to fill them,” Sutch murmured.

The general shot him a blank look but let the comment pass.

“Glad you came early,” he said instead. “I wanted your thoughts. Today isn’t just the anniversary of the Redan. At the very hour we stood ready in the dark—”

“West of the quarries,” Sutch interrupted, his voice tight. “I remember.”

“—my son was born. I thought, if you’ve no objection, he might join us tonight. He’s home. He’ll enter the service soon enough—best he hears what’s ahead.”

“By all means,” Sutch agreed, curiosity flickering. He had never met Harry Feversham, though he’d long wondered which parent the boy took after—the iron-willed general or his late wife, whose keen intellect had always been a mystery in her choice of husband.

That evening, Harry sat quietly at the table, listening as the men traded stories of the Crimea—tales of frozen trenches, reckless charges, and the slow, grinding horror of war. The boy’s dark eyes never wavered from the speakers, his face mirroring every grim detail as if the battles unfolded before him. Once, when a major spoke of the agonizing wait before an advance, Harry’s shoulders tensed as though he, too, braced for the order.

Then his gaze darted backward—just once—and Sutch stiffened. He knew that look. He’d seen it on green recruits mid-battle, the moment before panic took them.

No one else seemed to notice. The stories rolled on, each more brutal than the last. A broken officer’s disgrace. A surgeon who’d slit his own throat at the whistle of a bullet.

Harry sat motionless, his fingers digging into his knees, his face bone-white.

“Strange, isn’t it?” the general mused. “Some men simply break. No explaining it.”

Sutch leaned toward the boy. “You understand any of this?”

Harry’s eyes met his, dark and unreadable.

Before he could answer, the general scoffed. “Harry? Of course he does. He’s a Feversham.”

But Sutch wasn’t so sure.

When the clock struck midnight, Harry rose obediently, pausing at the door as if the darkened hall beyond held unseen terrors. Then he stepped through, candle in hand.

Sutch followed moments later.

The boy stood in the hall, the flame trembling as he stared up at the portraits—generations of soldiers, stern and unyielding. Their eyes seemed to bore into him, judging, condemning.

For a long moment, Harry didn’t move. Then, slowly, he bowed his head.

As if he’d been found wanting.

Sutch cleared his throat. “Harry.”

The boy turned, calm as stone.

“If you ever need to talk—” Sutch began, offering a card.

“Thank you, sir,” Harry said, his voice flat. He took it without another word and climbed the stairs, the candlelight fading into the dark.

Back in the dining room, glasses clinked as the men toasted.

“To Harry Feversham!” they roared. “May he honor the name!”

Upstairs, the boy lay rigid in bed, the echoes of their cheer twisting like a knife in his gut.

In his mind, he saw a disgraced officer slinking through London’s shadows.

A surgeon’s lifeless hand, a blade glinting in lamplight.

And both faces were his own.

Thirteen years had passed since that June night when Harry Feversham’s birth was celebrated with such fanfare. Now, in the same month, another toast was raised in his honor—this time in a quieter setting, among a handful of close companions.

The gathering took place high above London, in a flat perched on the tenth floor of a formidable building near Westminster. From its bay window, the sprawling city stretched out below, its lights shimmering like scattered stars. The room itself was warmly lit, the fire crackling against the unseasonable chill, casting long shadows across the dark wood and leather furnishings.

Four men sat around the dining table, cigars in hand, their conversation drifting between casual banter and the weightier matters of the day. Harry Feversham, now a man of twenty-six, had grown into his features—his dark hair and fair mustache framing a face that still carried the quiet intensity Lieutenant Sutch had once scrutinized.

His guests were a mix of old comrades and new acquaintances. Captain Trench, sharp-eyed and quick-witted, dominated the conversation with his restless energy. Lieutenant Willoughby, by contrast, was a man of few words, his thick brows and stubborn jaw hinting at a mind slow to change but impossible to sway once settled. The fourth, Jack Durrance, Harry’s closest friend, had arrived in response to a telegram, his presence a steadying force in the room.

The talk, inevitably, turned to the unrest brewing abroad—Arabi Pasha’s defiance in Egypt, the whispers of rebellion along the Nile. But it was Harry who, after a lull, steered the conversation elsewhere.

“I’ve news of my own,” he said, a faint hesitation in his voice. “I’ve just returned from Dublin.”

Durrance’s gaze lifted, steady and unreadable.

“I’m engaged,” Harry continued, watching his friend’s reaction.

For a heartbeat, no one spoke. Then Durrance raised his glass.

“Luck to you, Harry,” he said simply.

The words were brief, almost curt, but they carried the weight of years of unspoken understanding. There was no need for more between them.

“Thank you,” Harry replied, then added, with deliberate lightness, “You’ve only yourself to blame. You introduced us, after all.”

Durrance set his glass down carefully, his expression unchanged. “A good turn, then.”

The silence that followed was broken by Trench’s practical interjection.

“Will this change things?” he asked bluntly.

Harry exhaled a slow stream of smoke. “You mean, will I leave the service?” He glanced toward Durrance, who had risen and moved to the window, his back to the room. “I don’t know yet. There’s much to consider.”

He spoke carefully, weighing each word—his father’s expectations, the demands of his career, the obligations to Ethne’s family, her own ties to Ireland. It was clear the decision was not a simple one.

Trench, ever the pragmatist, seemed satisfied. “Well, here’s to you, Feversham,” he said, lifting his glass once more. Willoughby echoed the sentiment, his voice gruff but sincere.

Then, as if summoned by the moment’s gravity, a knock sounded at the door. A servant entered with a telegram.

Harry took it, read it, and went very still.

The room held its breath. Durrance remained at the window, his face turned toward the city’s glow. Willoughby studied the ceiling. Trench stared into the fire, his sharp eyes missing nothing.

Outside, the distant strains of a military band floated up from Wellington Barracks—the tattoo, crisp and commanding at first, then fading into a faint, almost playful melody.

Harry crumpled the telegram abruptly and tossed it into the flames.

“I don’t know,” he said again, as though resuming a conversation no one else could hear.

No one pressed him. The fire consumed the paper, leaving only ash.

The evening wound down, the talk drifting back to safer topics—plans, promises of future meetings. At eleven, the chime of the Westminster clock signaled the end. The guests rose, exchanged farewells, and departed into the night.

But as Trench and Willoughby walked away through St. James’s Park, the former’s curiosity could not be contained.

“That telegram,” he mused. “Castleton was dining with a War Office man tonight. If Feversham won’t speak of it, perhaps Castleton will.”

Willoughby, ever agreeable, nodded. “Prudent to ask.”

And with that, they turned their steps toward Albemarle Street, where answers—or more questions—might await.

Jack Durrance walked back to his lodgings alone, his thoughts drifting to a day two years past when old Dermod Eustace, in one of his unpredictable whims, had summoned him to the house by the Lennon River in Donegal. There, much to Durrance’s surprise, he had been introduced to Dermod’s daughter, Ethne.

She had been unlike anything he expected—unlike anything anyone expected after meeting her father. That evening, she had played her violin, her back turned to her small audience, as was her habit. She played not for their admiration, but for the music itself, lost in its currents. The melodies had stayed with him ever since, woven into his memories. There was one in particular—the *Melusine* overture—that had carried the very sigh of the sea, a sound so vivid it seemed to pull him toward distant shores.

That night, the violin had spoken of things Ethne herself could not know—of journeys under strange skies, of moonlit paths across restless waters, of silent deserts whispering with unseen voices. It had murmured of joys just beyond reach, of sorrows vast and eternal, and of longings too deep for words. But never had it uttered a note of complaint.

Now, as he walked through the London streets, those same melodies returned to him, sharper for the knowledge that Ethne’s face would always be turned away from him. He held tight to the thought he had drawn from her music that night: *True music does not complain.* And so, when he rode into the Row the next morning, his blue eyes were as steady and friendly as ever beneath the brim of his hat.

He waited by the lilacs and laburnums at the end of the sand, as had been their custom for years—but Harry Feversham did not come. Nor did he appear for the next three weeks. Durrance was puzzled. It was as though he had lost his friend as well.

Then, at last, Feversham kept their appointment, just as the rumors of war hardened into certainty. Durrance greeted him with news.

“Luck’s turned my way at last. I’m to join General Graham’s staff in Egypt. There’s talk we might push further—Suakin, perhaps.”

The eagerness in his voice was unmistakable, and for a moment, something flickered in Feversham’s eyes—envy, sharp and unguarded. It struck Durrance as strange, even in the flush of his own excitement, that Harry Feversham should envy *him*. Strange, and oddly pleasing.

“Hard luck your regiment’s staying behind,” Durrance said sympathetically.

Feversham rode in silence for a while before answering. “That was expected. The night you dined with me, I sent in my papers.”

“That night?” Durrance turned sharply in his saddle. “After we left?”

“Yes,” Feversham said, accepting the correction—if correction it was.

Durrance said nothing for a long moment, then laughed, the sound easy and unforced. “I remember. You gave us your reasons. Still, I can’t help wishing we were going out together. When do you leave for Ireland?”

“Tonight.”

“So soon?”

They turned their horses and rode back beneath the trees. The morning was still fresh, the chestnuts and limes bright with new green, the last of the May blossoms clinging like snow to the branches. The park shimmered in the early sun, the distant murmur of the city like the rush of a far-off river.

“It’s been years since we swam at Sandford Lasher,” Durrance mused.

“Or froze in that gully on Great End during Easter vac,” Feversham replied.

Both men felt it then—the closing of a chapter. The years behind them had been good, and the years ahead were uncertain. So they lingered in the memories before turning the page.

“You must stay with us when you return,” Feversham said.

Durrance did not flinch, though the word *us* struck like a blade. His grip on the reins remained steady.

“If I return,” he said lightly. “You know my creed. I’d never pity a man who died on service. I’d choose no better end myself.”

It was a simple belief, as straightforward as the man who held it. Death in battle was preferable to a life half-lived. He spoke without melancholy, without foreboding—but when he glanced at Feversham, he saw again that look of envy, stark and unreadable.

“There are worse things,” Durrance went on. “Disablement, for one. Clever men might manage, but what would I do, chained to a chair? The thought alone chills me.” He shook his shoulders, as if to cast off the dread. “Enough of that. This is our last ride—let’s gallop.”

He spurred his horse forward, and Feversham matched him stride for stride. They raced down the sand, the wind in their faces, the past at their backs. At the end of the Row, they reined in, clasped hands briefly, and parted with little more than a nod. Feversham rode out of the park; Durrance turned back, walking his horse slowly toward the shade of the trees.

His thoughts drifted again to Ethne, to the life she would have with Feversham. It was better this way, he told himself. She would never have been happy far from Donegal. And if it could not be him, then at least it was Harry.

He was deep in these musings when a voice called his name.

“Mrs. Adair,” he said, reining in beside the rails.

She was a neighbor from Devonshire, a woman of quiet elegance with thick brown hair and a face of unusual pallor—though at this moment, there was a touch of color in her cheeks.

“I’ve news,” Durrance said. “Two things. First, Harry Feversham is to be married.”

“To whom?” she asked quickly.

“You should know. It was in your house he first met her. Ethne Eustace.”

Mrs. Adair’s face brightened. “They’ll marry soon?”

“Nothing stands in their way.”

“I’m glad,” she said softly, almost with relief. “And your second news?”

“As good as the first. I’m off to Egypt with Graham.”

Her smile faltered. The color drained from her face. “You’re pleased, I suppose.”

“Beyond words,” Durrance replied, his voice warm with anticipation. “The sooner the better. I’ll dine with you before I go, if I may.”

“My husband will be glad to see you,” she said, her tone cooling.

Durrance noticed nothing. He was too caught in his own thoughts, too eager for the horizon. He rode away, unaware that the threads of fate were already weaving a new bond between himself and Feversham—one that would draw them together again in ways neither could foresee.

A week later, his preparations complete, Durrance stood on the deck of the mail-boat bound for Calais. The night was wet, the pier nearly empty save for a handful of well-wishers. As the ship pulled away, he leaned against the rail, watching England fade into the rain.

Then, for an instant, beneath the flickering gaslight, he saw a face—a face so like Harry Feversham’s that his breath caught. But no, it couldn’t be. Feversham was in Donegal, listening to Ethne’s violin beneath an open sky.

Yet when the light steadied, the face leaped clear from the shadows—haggard, haunted, the face of a man utterly alone.

“Harry!” Durrance shouted over the wind.

But the figure did not move. The ship sailed on, and the pier vanished into the dark.

An illusion, Durrance told himself. A trick of the rain and the lamplight.

He had been too busy that week to dwell on the telegram that had arrived days before, too preoccupied to unfold his newspaper. He did not know that Harry Feversham had come to see him off.

Nor did he know why.

Harry Feversham arrived in Dublin by the night mail after his ride with Durrance in the Row. The next morning, he crossed Lough Swilly aboard a small cargo steamer that made its weekly journey up the Lennon River to Ramelton. As the boat docked, he spotted Ethne waiting for him on the quay, her hands resting lightly on the reins of her dog-cart. She greeted him with a smile—warm, but with the ease of a comrade rather than the tenderness of a lover.

"You didn’t expect me," she remarked, noting his expression.

"I never do," he replied. "You always surpass what I imagine."

At that, her smile softened, deepening into something more intimate. She flicked the reins, urging the horse forward. "I’ve brought no groom on purpose," she said. "We’ll take our time. Guests arrive tomorrow—today is just for us."

They moved along the riverside causeway, the town unfolding around them—the steep, narrow streets, the old stone bridge arching over the swift, dark water, the trees crowding the far bank. Feversham absorbed it all with quiet fascination. This was Ethne’s world, and every detail of it seemed to belong to her.

She was twenty-one then, tall and strong, with a grace that defied the rigid expectations of her time. Her hair, dark brown and thick, was coiled at the nape of her neck, and her clear grey eyes met his without hesitation. There was a steadiness to her, an unshakable honesty that made her seem older than her years. She was fearless, too—Feversham had heard the story of how she had once stopped a bolting horse with nothing but her own quick wits and nerve.

For a while, they rode in silence. Then, abruptly, she turned to him.

"I saw in the papers this morning," she said, "that your regiment has been ordered to Egypt. If not for me, you might have gone. There would have been chances for distinction. I’ve held you back, and I’m sorry for it."

Feversham stiffened. His voice, when he spoke, was rough. "You needn’t be."

"But I am," she insisted. "And I blame myself all the more because I don’t see things as others do. If you had gone, and if the worst had happened, I would have grieved—of course—but I would have known, absolutely known, that we’d meet again."

She said it simply, as if stating an obvious truth. Feversham felt the words like a blow. He wanted to speak, to confess, but the weight of what he carried choked him.

Ethne, unaware, continued. "I don’t feel loss as others do. When people I care about are gone, it’s never truly an ending. Somewhere, somehow, we’ll find each other again."

"Unless," Feversham said slowly, "one has been deceived. What if the face you trust is only a mask?"

She shook her head. "No. I don’t believe in mistakes like that. Not in the end."

Her certainty was a knife twisting in him. He opened his mouth—*Ethne, you don’t know*—but before he could speak, she reined in the horse, laughing, and pointed ahead.

They had reached the top of a hill. A scarlet letter-box stood embedded in the stone wall.

"That’s where I posted my letters to you," she said. "During the worst of it."

Feversham swallowed hard. "Convenient," he managed.

She grinned. "And that gap in the wall—I used to climb through it at night to reach the post. Half a mile down the avenue in the dark."

"You might have been hurt."

"Only by the thorns," she replied lightly, turning the horse through the gates of Lennon House.

---

The house was old and sprawling, its grey stone weathered by time. As they approached, the door swung open, and Ethne’s father, Dermod Eustace, emerged—a gaunt, fierce-eyed man whose demeanor softened slightly at the sight of Feversham.

"Welcome," he said brusquely. "Make yourself at home. If you want whiskey, stamp twice. The servants know." And with that, he retreated inside.

Dermod Eustace was a relic of another era, a man who clung to the old ways with a stubborn pride. He ruled his household with a mix of tyranny and hospitality, compelling guests to stay whether they wished to or not. Yet for all his bluster, he was oddly fond of Feversham—and, despite himself, a little afraid of his daughter.

That afternoon, Feversham and Ethne wandered down to the Lennon River, where the water rushed over rocks in a smooth, unbroken sheet. They sat by the bank, talking little, listening to the river’s voice. The hours slipped by, golden and slow, until the sunset painted the sky and the shadows stretched long across the grass.

It was a moment Feversham would remember forever—because the next night, everything changed.

---

The ball was in full swing when Ethne took his arm and led him from the crowded drawing room into the empty hall. The front door stood open, letting in the cool night air. From the ballroom behind them came the swell of music, the laughter of dancers.

"The post is in," Ethne said, crossing to a side table. "Letters for you—and this."

She held out a small white box. It was light, too light. Feversham took it, frowning at the unfamiliar handwriting. He broke the seal and lifted the lid.

Three white feathers drifted out, settling soundlessly on the floor.

His face went pale.

Ethne stared at them, then at him. "What does this mean?"

"That some things can’t be hidden," he said hoarsely.

She shook her head, bewildered. "It’s a joke—a cruel one, but still—"

"No." His voice was steady now, his eyes locked on hers. "They were sent in earnest."

"By whom?"

Feversham hadn’t considered that. The message itself was the blow; the messengers hardly mattered. But Ethne snatched up the box, pulling out three visiting cards.

"Captain Trench. Mr. Castleton. Mr. Willoughby." She looked up. "Do you know them?"

"All three were officers in my regiment."

She knelt, gathering the feathers in her hand as if touching them might make sense of this. "Were they right to send these?"

"Yes."

The word was final. He offered no defense.

Ethne’s voice was very quiet. "You would have married me without telling me any of this, wouldn’t you? If these feathers hadn’t come?"

He didn’t answer. He didn’t need to.

Slowly, deliberately, she twisted the ring from her finger and laid it on the table. "Take it back."

Feversham picked it up, the metal cold in his palm.

Then she pointed to the feathers. "These are yours too."

He hesitated. "There are four."

She said nothing, but her fan—white feathers set in ivory—was missing one of its plumes.

She had added her own to the three.

Habit carried them through the evening—the effortless banter, the practiced smiles, the illusion of joy woven so seamlessly that no one in Lennon House suspected the rift between them. Harry Feversham watched Ethne move through the crowd, her laughter light, her steps effortless, as though no shadow had fallen upon her heart. Yet beneath the masquerade, she bore a weight that threatened to crush her, just as he felt the four feathers searing against his chest, a brand of shame he was certain must be visible to all.

It astonished him that the room did not turn on him in unison, that no whisper of *coward* hissed through the music. But he no longer feared exposure. What did it matter now? He had lost Ethne. And as he watched her—her proud bearing, the quiet command in her grey eyes, the way she stood apart from every other woman in the room—he wondered, with a dull ache, how he had ever been fool enough to believe he deserved her.

Only once did her composure falter. They were dancing together when her gaze drifted toward the windows, where the first pale light of dawn crept behind the blinds.

"Look," she murmured, and suddenly her weight sagged against his arms. Her face drained of color, exhaustion etching lines where joy had been. Her eyelids fluttered, then opened again, weary and resigned.

"The morning," she whispered, her voice frayed at the edges. "Is it right that one should suffer so much pain?"

"Hush," Feversham urged, stepping closer, shielding her from curious eyes. "Just a little longer. Only a few minutes more."

She steadied herself, nodded, and let the dance carry her away.

The irony of it—him, of all people, offering her strength—was lost on them both. For Feversham, her moment of vulnerability had laid bare the depth of the wound he’d dealt. Her words clung to him, sharper than any blade: *Is it right that one should suffer so much pain?* He knew they would haunt him long after this night, long after everything.

When the last guests had departed and the house settled into silence, Feversham lingered in the hall with Ethne.

"You needn’t wait," she said, her back to him as she lit a candle. "I’ve already told my father everything."

Feversham nodded but did not move. "I must see him."

She turned then, studying him with a frown, as though she could not reconcile this quiet resolve with the man she thought she knew.

Dermod Eustace emerged from his study, his steps slow, his face aged by more than years. He stared at Feversham for a long, wordless moment, his mouth working soundlessly before he finally managed, "Well—" He stopped, adjusted the candlewick with unsteady fingers, and tried again. "Well—"

No other words came.

At last, he sighed, picked up his candle, and muttered, "If you want whiskey, stamp twice. The servants will hear you."

Then he trudged upstairs, leaving silence in his wake.

His restraint, his inability to voice the condemnation Feversham expected, was perhaps the harshest punishment of all.

---

Alone in her room, Ethne stood at the open window, breathing in the cool dawn air. The world outside was colorless, drained of life—a reflection, she thought, of the days ahead.

She had work to do.

Methodically, she gathered every token Harry Feversham had ever given her, determined to erase him from her life. But as she held each one—a book, a trinket, a letter—memories rose unbidden, and her resolve wavered.

In the end, she kept only one thing: his photograph, locked away where she would not have to see it. The rest she packed carefully into a box, sealed it, and left it in the hall to be sent away.

Then she burned his letters.

One by one, they blackened and curled in the flames, ink dissolving into ash, words vanishing like whispers on the wind. She watched until the last ember died, leaving only feathers of grey dust.

A sound outside her window startled her—a footstep on the gravel. Instinctively, she snuffed out her candle and sat motionless, listening. The birds sang; the river murmured. Then, faintly, the footsteps retreated.

Loneliness settled over her like a shroud.

Her gaze fell upon a violin case. Without thinking, she lifted the instrument—Durrance’s gift, though she did not remember it—and drew the bow across the strings.

Somewhere in the house, a guest stirred at the sound of music drifting through the halls. But Ethne played on, softly, as though the notes could fill the emptiness around her.

She did not yet know that the violin in her hands was the one Durrance had sent. She only knew that, for now, it was her sole companion in the silence.

The night of August 30th hung heavy over London, thick with rumors of battle from Egypt. Crowds gathered beneath the gas-lamps of Pall Mall, their faces upturned toward the War Office’s glowing windows, waiting for news of Kassassin. Lieutenant Sutch, leaning against the parapet of the Junior Carlton Club, felt a touch on his arm.

Harry Feversham stood beside him, his face pale, his eyes burning with feverish intensity. "I could have been there," Harry muttered, his voice unsteady. "Out in the desert, under the stars—or dead. What would it have mattered?"

Sutch said nothing. He saw the ruin in Harry’s face—the sleepless nights, the restless hands, the nerve-strung tension of a man walking a razor’s edge. Without a word, he led him away from the crowd.

They met the next night in the quiet corner of a dimly lit grill-room, where the hum of engines mimicked the throb of a ship’s machinery. Over dinner, Harry told his story—the feathers, the disgrace, the flight from Lennon House. He spoke calmly, deliberately, as if recounting another man’s tragedy.

Sutch listened in silence, his hand shielding his eyes when Harry spoke of the hidden telegram, the moment his fate had sealed itself. But when Harry finished, Sutch did not scorn him. Instead, his voice trembled with remorse.

"I should have spoken that night at Broad Place," he said. "I understood. Your father never would."

Harry shook his head. "He never will."

Sutch clenched his fist. "If only I had known—if only I’d seen what it would cost you."

Harry leaned forward, his voice low. "There’s a way back. Not for honor, perhaps, but for something." He drew out the four white feathers and laid them on the table. "These are my debts. Trench, Castleton, Willoughby—each must take his back. I leave for Egypt tomorrow."

Sutch stared, hope flaring in his eyes. "You mean to force them to rescind?"

"If chance allows. If they ever stand in peril, and I can step between."

"And the fourth?" Sutch asked quietly.

Harry’s gaze dropped. "That one is different. Ethne... she said something before the feathers came. Something about seeing each other again—afterwards." He swallowed. "If I can undo this, perhaps that might still be true."

Sutch exhaled slowly. "You’ll wait for opportunities that may never come."

"I’ll wait as long as I must."

Silence stretched between them. At last, Sutch reached out, gripping Harry’s arm. "Then go. And if you don’t return—I’ll tell your father what I can."

The next evening, under a storm-lashed sky, Harry boarded the packet to Calais. Sutch stood on the pier, watching until the ship vanished into the dark. The rain soaked through his coat, but he did not move.

He was thinking of Muriel Graham’s son—and praying he would live long enough to see him come home redeemed.

The desert stretched silent and endless beneath the harsh glare of the sun. Lieutenant-Colonel Durrance adjusted his field glasses, scanning the barren ridges one last time before snapping the case shut.

"Nothing," he said.

"Nothing, sir," echoed Captain Mather.

A nod, and the detachment moved—scouts fanning ahead, the main column falling into formation, the mountain guns creaking into position behind. They descended from the desolate heights of Khor Gwob, thirty-five miles southwest of Suakin, into the deceptive greenery of the Sinkat plateau.

This was the final push—the last reconnaissance before the withdrawal. The morning had been a brutal slog through scorched rock and crumbling shale, the camels laboring up the gulley with their usual ill-tempered groans. Now, at last, the valley sprawled before them, dotted with shrubs and stunted trees, a mirage of life in the waste.

The men straightened in their saddles, spirits lifting despite the heat. They advanced in a diagonal line across the plateau, the only sounds the clink of harness, the soft thud of padded camel feet, and the occasional rush of startled pigeons taking flight. The land was alive in its own quiet way—gazelle bolting at their approach, flickering brown and white against the sand.

It was hard to believe this place had once been a thoroughfare—caravans winding south to Erkoweet, Suaki nobles building their summer retreats. Now only ruins remained.

Three hours under the hammer of the sun, and the fort came into view—broken walls, gaping windows, the sky visible through skeletal beams. Three years since Osman Digna had razed it, but nature had waged its own siege since. Trees pressed close, their branches clawing at the stone as if to drag it back into the earth. Only the front remained clear, where three great Gemeiza trees stood sentinel over the wells.

The men dismounted, grateful for shade. Durrance and Mather circled the ruins, pausing at the southern corner.

"Someone's been here," Mather observed.

Durrance nudged the ashes with his boot. A charred twig glowed faintly beneath his heel. "Recently."

Inside the fort, the walls were split by young saplings forcing their way through cracks. The rear was a steep slope, a ditch below—the last defense of a doomed garrison. Durrance sat on the parapet, gazing out at the hills where Tewfik Bey had once strained his eyes for reinforcements that never came.

Mather sighed beside him. "The Guards are already on their way to Suez. A week, and it’s our turn."

Durrance smiled. "I’ll be back."

"You *like* this place?"

"I do. And the people."

Mather shook his head. It was that very understanding, that rare sympathy, which had propelled Durrance ahead of sharper, colder men. He saw past the fanaticism, the forced allegiances—he knew these tribes, their grudges, their pride.

They sat in silence, the heat pressing down, the men asleep in the shade. Then Durrance's eyes sharpened, fixing on a distant tree where pigeons still wheeled nervously.

"Odd," he murmured.

Mather followed his gaze. "They’ve settled everywhere else."

Durrance didn’t answer at once. Then, quietly: "Take a dozen men. Surround it."

The soldiers moved like shadows, fanning out. Before they could close the ring, branches thrashed—an Arab burst from cover, spear in hand, shield slung across his back. He ran, then stopped abruptly as rifles leveled.

He walked back without protest, meeting Durrance’s gaze without defiance or fear.

"Abou Fatma," he said. "Of the Kabbabish."

Durrance switched to English. "You speak it?"

"A little. Learned in Khartum."

For an hour they talked. Then the Arab was gone, vanishing toward the hills as the detachment prepared to move on.

Mather fell in beside Durrance as they rode northwest, away from Sinkat. "Well?"

"Gordon’s servant," Durrance said. "Had a letter—meant for Cairo. Hid it in a wall in Berber when the town fell. Claims it’s still there."

Mather frowned. "If it’s true—"

"It’s curious," Durrance agreed.

The sun dipped behind the hills, staining the sky violet, then rose-pink, then emerald. Durrance watched it, thoughts drifting. *If only they’d let us push west last year. Before Khartum fell. Before Berber.*

That night, beneath a sprawl of stars, he lay awake. A week, and he’d sail for England. Three years since he’d seen home. Three years of battles, sieges, the heat and the dust—good years, for all that.

Mather yawned beside him. "One week."

Durrance chuckled. "I’ll be back."

"You’ve got people waiting?"

"A few. Three months to see them."

He’d surprise Harry Feversham—maybe in Donegal, maybe London. Ride in the Row again. But in the end, he’d return. His work was here.

He slept.

Far to the east, Abou Fatma reached Suakin. He told his story first to Captain Willoughby in the Governor’s Palace, then again in the bazaar—louder this time, where a Greek lounging outside a café caught fragments of it.

The Greek—lean, sun-darkened—leaned in with a offer of merissa. "Could you find the house again?"

Abou Fatma grinned, sketching lines in the dust, unaware Berber had been rebuilt.

The Greek listened carefully. Then, jingling coins: "Best keep this between us."

The Greek was Harry Feversham.

And after three years of waiting, his chance had come.

The June sun bathed London in a golden haze as Lieutenant Durrance stepped into Hyde Park, his first taste of home after years of exile. Beneath the shade of the trees, he sat, a solitary figure weathered by distant sands, his eyes lingering on the effortless grace of the women who passed—their laughter, their lightness, a world away from the harsh frontiers he had known.

Then, amidst the crowd, a familiar face turned toward him. Mrs. Adair, clad in mourning black, approached with a quiet deliberation, her sharp gaze scrutinizing him as if measuring the toll of his absence.

"You didn’t know," she said, before he could speak. "My husband died eighteen months ago. A hunting accident."

Durrance fumbled for words. "I hadn’t heard. I’m sorry."

She accepted his condolences with a silence that was neither cold nor warm, merely impenetrable. When she finally spoke again, it was with deliberate control. "Harry Feversham and Ethne Eustace never married."

The statement struck him like a sudden gust of wind. "Why?"

Mrs. Adair’s lips thinned. "Ethne keeps her secrets. The engagement ended the night of a dance at Lennon House—just before you left England."

Durrance’s mind flashed to the troubled face he had glimpsed on Dover pier—Harry, vanishing into the night. "Where is he now?"

"Gone. No one knows where."

"And Ethne?"

"She lives in Glenalla, a village in Donegal. The house burned, her father ruined. She has little left but the mountains."

Durrance absorbed this quietly, his thoughts churning. Mrs. Adair watched him, her curiosity piqued by his stillness.

"You’re not rushing to her?"

He met her gaze evenly. "There are things to understand first."

In the weeks that followed, Durrance haunted his club, probing old acquaintances for answers. None could—or would—tell him more. Until, at last, Lieutenant Sutch appeared, wary as a fox scenting a trap.

"You know what happened to Harry," Durrance pressed.

Sutch bristled. "I gave my word not to speak of it."

"Then answer this—would it betray Harry if I sought Ethne’s hand?"

The old lieutenant hesitated, torn between loyalty and honesty. Finally, grudgingly, he conceded: "No. It would not."

That night, Durrance boarded the train for Holyhead, the wind of a new course filling his sails.

The farmhouse perched on the moorland’s edge, a mile above the village, where the heather crept into the garden and the bridle-path ended at its door. Around it, the hills rose like an amphitheater, their colors shifting with the seasons—emerald in spring, purple in summer, russet in autumn—as if they clothed themselves deliberately to please the eyes of the girl they sheltered. No trees softened their granite slopes, yet they stood as guardians, rugged but kind.

Durrance knew he had reached the right place when the sound of a violin greeted him. The music floated through the open windows like an invitation, and he hesitated, his breath catching. A hope he had barely dared to entertain flared within him. Tethering his horse, he stepped through the gate.

Inside, the house was warm and welcoming, a stark contrast to its rough exterior. Sunlight spilled across polished oak and gleaming brass, and through the windows, the land sloped away toward the distant glint of the lough. Durrance could picture it in winter—the fire glowing, the wind howling outside, the room a refuge against the cold.

Ethne met him without surprise. "I thought you would come," she said, her smile bright.

He laughed suddenly as they shook hands, his gaze falling on the violin resting nearby. It was unmistakable—pale in color, with a small repaired flaw near the bridge.

"You kept it," he said.

"I did," she replied, her voice steady. "There was a time I needed to be reminded of sure friends. It helped."

He took the violin carefully, as if it were something sacred. "You’ve played it?"

"Yes. Though not for a long while. It spoke too much of things I wished to forget."

She said this plainly, without evasion, and Durrance felt a quiet admiration.

He stayed a week, and in that time, they walked the hills together, whether in rain or sun. She showed him her country with a fierce pride, demanding his admiration. In the evenings, she played, the music speaking of heights and depths beyond words. Durrance watched her, counting his chances.

Yet neither spoke of Harry Feversham. The shadow lingered, unacknowledged but present.

On his last day, they sat by a rushing stream, the water leaping between black rocks. He spoke of Egypt—of mirages like glassy lakes, of heat that danced above the sand, of nights beneath infinite stars.

"It grows on you," he said. "You never feel alone there. You feel closer, somehow, to the things—and people—that matter."

Her eyes were bright, her lips parted in a smile. He moved nearer.

"And you go back?" she asked.

He hesitated. "To Wadi Halfa. For two years, I suppose."

She knelt beside him. "I’ll miss you."

The words hung between them. Then, quietly, he said, "You needn’t."

She drew back slightly. "There’s my father. I couldn’t leave him."

"That’s not what I meant."

She looked troubled. "You have your work. I heard the satisfaction in your voice when you spoke of it. That’s your true path. I won’t be the one to turn you from it."

He faced her then, abandoning caution. "I am for you, Ethne. I believe you are for me. I thought once I could live out my days in the East, content. But I had schooled myself to that because I believed you lost to me."

She shook her head, gentle but firm. "No."

They stood. "Goodbye, then," she said. "But not to friendship."

He smiled. "May I write to you?"

"If you like. But only of other things."

He left her there, on the hillside, and walked down to where Dermod Eustace waited by the trap. The old man fell into step beside him, faltering but determined.

"You have no word of Harry Feversham?" Dermod asked, his voice wistful.

"None," Durrance admitted.

Dermod sighed. "I should curse him. But I’d still like to know how he fares."

Durrance said nothing. As he rode away, his thoughts turned to the man who had once been his friend—and the mystery that had driven him into shadow.

In the sweltering month of May, while Durrance gazed northward from Wadi Halfa, his fate was being shaped five hundred miles to the south, beyond the Nubian desert and the treacherous Belly of Stones. There, in the barren stretch between Berber and Suakin, lay the wells of Obak—deep, silent, and half-buried beneath shifting dunes.

To the east, a ragged line of trees marked the edge of a stony plain, broken by jagged hills of granite. To the west, the desert sprawled, lifeless and unbroken, for fifty-eight miles until it touched the Nile at Mahobey. Here, the horizon was a cruel illusion—tufts of grass masqueraded as shade, scattered stones pretended to be shelter. And the sun, relentless, burned the earth to dust by day, while the wind howled cold by night, reshaping the land with every gust.

Vultures circled overhead; sand-grouse darted between the dunes. The bones of camels, long picked clean, lay half-buried in the sand, remnants of caravans that had once passed this way. A crumbling hut of bent branches whispered of a time when men had lived here. Now, there was only silence—a heavy, waiting silence, as though the desert itself held its breath.

Yet in this forsaken place, one man waited.

Each dawn, he led two swift Bisharin mares from the shelter of the trees, watered them at the well, and sat motionless for hours, his eyes fixed on the dunes to the west. Then, as the sun climbed higher, he would retreat again into the shadows, feeding the beasts carefully before vanishing for the day.

For five mornings, he kept this vigil alone.

On the sixth, as he turned to leave, a figure crested the distant ridge—a man leading a donkey. The Arab by the well tensed, his gaze locking onto the animal’s grey hide. But then he saw the driver—a negro, his legs dark against the pale sand, his face shrouded beneath a jellab. The Arab relaxed, feigning indifference as the stranger approached.

*"Salam aleikum,"* the negro greeted, laying down his spears and shield.

*"Aleikum es salam,"* the Arab replied, his voice flat.

"You are Abou Fatma?"

A nod.

"Two days past, a Bisharin named Moussa Fedil found me in Berber’s market. He fed me, then bid me bring this donkey to you at Obak."

Abou Fatma barely glanced at the beast. *"Tayeeb,"* he murmured, as if it meant nothing.

But the negro lingered. "Moussa Fedil expects me back in three days. Give me a token, so he knows I’ve done his bidding."

With deliberate slowness, Abou Fatma drew his knife, notched a stick thrice at each end, and handed it over. The negro tied it into his robe, filled his waterskin, and trudged back the way he had come.

Only when he had vanished did Abou Fatma move swiftly. He led the donkey into the trees, examining its shoulders. There—a small cut, stitched shut. He pried it open, extracting a scrap of bladder no larger than a fingernail. Inside, a message in Arabic:

The writer was Harry Feversham.

Disguised as a Dervish, his skin darkened, his hair twisted into matted locks, he moved through the new streets of Berber—a town of gaping pits and raw construction. The old city lay in ruins a mile distant, its alleys now skeletal rows of crumbling walls. Foxes denned in the rubble.

He had planned to slip in under cover of night, retrieve what he sought, and vanish before dawn. Instead, he walked openly among the crowds, ears straining for the name *Yusef*, tongue locked in silence lest his accent betray him. Fear gnawed at him, but beneath it burned a desperate resolve—a belief that the world could not be so cruel as to demand this of him and then let him fail.

Three days earlier, as Berber’s walls first rose before him, that fear had nearly broken him. The low brown ramparts, the swaying palms beyond—they had seemed not refuge, but death’s maw. His legs had trembled; he had turned to flee, stumbling in blind panic across the sand.

But then he stopped.

The years of preparation, the nights spent whispering Arabic in filthy bazaars—had they been for nothing? Would he always be the man who had resigned his commission in shame?

And Ethne—

He dropped to the ground, pulling his coat over his head, shutting out the world. In the darkness, her face came to him—clear, unwavering. The memory of that summer night in Donegal, the feathers fluttering to the floor, the quiet promise in her voice: *"I should have no doubt that you and I would see much of one another afterwards."*

When he rose, the sun was sinking. He walked toward Berber, passed through its gates, and did not look back.

A month after leaving Wadi Halfa, Durrance reached London. Among the letters waiting at his club was one from Ethne, edged in black. The envelope bore the same somber border, and within, her message was brief—she was staying with Mrs. Adair in Hill Street and hoped he might call when convenient.

He went the following afternoon and found her alone.

"You didn’t write to me in Halfa," she said. "I thought you’d be on your way home before any letter reached you." She paused, then added quietly, "My father died last month."

Durrance nodded. "I feared as much when I saw the border. I’m sorry, Ethne. You’ll feel his loss deeply."

"More than I can say," she replied, her voice steady but softened with sorrow. Then, after a moment, she continued, "Would you like to hear how it happened?"

She told him—how Dermod had slipped away in the early morning, not so much felled by illness as worn down by time. His passing had been strangely marked by a resurgence of his old commanding spirit.

"He knew," she said. "And that afternoon, just after he had spoken to me, he called his dog. The animal leaped onto the bed at once, though Father’s voice was barely a whisper. He nosed his way under Father’s hand and whined. Then Father told me to leave them—to shut the door and not return until the dog summoned me."

She paused, her fingers tightening slightly on the arm of her chair.

"I waited outside. From seven until one in the morning. No lamp was lit in his room. I imagine him lying there in the dark, the windows growing brighter as the daylight faded, just him and the dog, alone to the very end." Her voice did not waver, but Durrance saw the tension in her hands. "It was his wish. But it was hard."

Durrance said nothing. There was nothing to say that she did not already know. He understood those hours—the slow creep of twilight into night, the silence behind the door, the weight of waiting.

At last, Ethne spoke again.

"Before he sent me away, he spoke of someone. Can you guess who?"

She looked directly at him, her expression unreadable. Durrance leaned forward, a sudden, breathless hope flaring in his chest—only to be dashed as she answered her own question.

"Harry Feversham."

Durrance stiffened. His gaze dropped to the floor, concealing the sharp twist of disappointment—and something darker.

"Father was always fond of him," Ethne continued, her tone even. "I wondered if you had heard anything—where he might be, what he’s doing."

Durrance stood abruptly and moved to the window, his back to her. When he spoke, his voice was tight, controlled. "No. I haven’t." Then the control cracked. "And I don’t care to. He was my friend once, but now—" He turned, his words bitter. "If he had been half the man we believed, you wouldn’t have spent those six hours alone outside that door."

Ethne did not flinch. "You’re going back to Glenalla?" she asked after a silence.

"Yes. Alone."

Another pause. Then Durrance circled her chair, his voice low. "You once said you might tell me why your engagement ended."

"You already know," she replied.

"I don’t. Your father let slip a word or two. He asked after Feversham the last time I saw him. But I know nothing for certain. I want you to tell me."

She shook her head. "Not now."

The quiet stretched between them until Durrance broke it again.

"I’ve one more year in Halfa. After that—I won’t stay. Not even if you remain at Glenalla."

Ethne turned the words over carefully. "We aren’t children," she said at last. "You have your life to consider. We should be wise."

"Wisdom isn’t always caution," he countered, frustration edging his voice. "Sometimes it’s daring."

For a long moment, she was silent, her face averted. Then, very softly, she asked, "Do you want me so much?"

Before he could answer, she turned to him, her eyes earnest. "Try not to. For this year—try not to think of me at all. You have your work. Try to forget me."

There was no cruelty in the words, only regret—the regret of someone parting with a friend. And Durrance, hearing only what he wished, took it as proof that she feared holding him back.

Mrs. Adair’s entrance ended the conversation, and Durrance left with that fragile hope intact.

That evening, under the same tree where he had once waited for news of Feversham, an old acquaintance joined him—Captain Mather.

"You’ve seen the papers?" Mather asked.

"No. What is it?"

Mather handed him a folded sheet, pointing to a brief paragraph. It noted the recovery of letters exchanged between Gordon and the Mahdi, retrieved from a wall in Berber and delivered to Suakin.

"The Arab we met at Sinkat—Abou Fatma—must have gone back after all," Durrance mused.

"Hardly worth the risk," Mather remarked.

"Perhaps not," Durrance agreed absently, his mind already elsewhere—on Ethne, on her return to Donegal, on the year ahead.

He did not see then what the paragraph truly meant. That news had come to him in the very place he had once sought it, but the significance was lost in the dark.

Letters would find their way to Ethne that autumn, carefully worded but betraying, here and there, the hope he could not suppress. And she, reading them, would wrestle anew with the question she could not answer: how to keep from breaking a second life, when the first had already been shattered in her hands.

The violin, for once, offered no solace. The hills held no reply.

The Nile lay still beneath the full moon, its surface gleaming like polished bronze under the pearly sky. At Wadi Halfa, three officers lounged on the grass knoll above the river, the bullfrogs’ chorus drifting from the island midstream. The desert beyond stretched vast and ghostly, its stones glittering like scattered jewels under the frost-white expanse.

Colonel Dawson struck a match, the flare briefly illuminating his troubled face.

"I hope no harm has come to him," he muttered, tossing the match aside. "I wish I could believe otherwise."

Lieutenant Calder, Durrance’s closest friend in the garrison, exhaled a slow cloud of smoke. "He left eight weeks ago today."

Major Walters, ever the optimist, shrugged. "Durrance isn’t one to hurry back to a desk. Give him a desert campfire and a few sheiks to talk to, and he’ll spin tales till the stars fade. He’ll turn up."

"But he’s three weeks overdue," Dawson countered.

Walters gestured toward the eastern horizon. "He went toward Berenice, through quiet country. Not like the western routes where trouble brews."

Calder tapped his pipe thoughtfully. "I wonder if something else delayed him. That last night before he left—something was off. He was restless, distracted. He expected a visitor, someone who never came."

Dawson leaned in. "Who?"

Calder shook his head. "I don’t know. But when his servant woke him at dawn, Durrance was still waiting on his veranda, as if the man might still arrive."

The next morning, over breakfast, Calder finished his story. "The only person Durrance spoke to in Tewfikieh was a Greek musician. Hardly the visitor he’d wait up for."

Then, abruptly, Calder froze. His gaze fixed on the mess-room doorway.

Durrance stood there, dust-streaked and sunburnt, listening intently to the voices around him.

"Durrance!" Dawson sprang up. "When did you get back?"

Calder didn’t move. He watched as Durrance descended the steps, his movements just a fraction too careful, his eyes never quite meeting theirs.

The others fussed over his delayed return, but Calder said nothing—until Durrance reached for his brandy-and-soda and missed the glass by inches.

Later, in Durrance’s office, Calder dropped all pretense. He placed his pipe deliberately into Durrance’s outstretched hand.

The hot bowl seared his skin. Durrance recoiled.

Calder’s voice was soft. "How did it happen?"

Durrance’s shoulders sagged. "A gust took my helmet. I chased it—ran too far under the sun. I woke up blind."

Calder fetched the garrison’s doctor, but the prognosis was grim. "Cairo," the Syrian said. "A specialist. Quickly."

When they were alone again, Calder hesitated before the stack of unopened letters. Two bore Irish postmarks.

"From her?" he asked gently.

Durrance’s jaw tightened. "Don’t read them."

But Calder did. Ethne’s letters were warm, full of Donegal’s hills and village life, her words threaded with quiet friendship.

When he finished, Durrance laughed—a sharp, bitter sound. "Thank God she refused me. A blind man turns selfish, Calder. A burden. She’s better free of that."

Calder studied him. "You’re sure she meant the refusal?"

"Twice," Durrance said flatly. "Now, help me answer her. The rest can wait."

Outside, the desert wind whispered over the sand, indifferent to the ruin of a man’s hopes.

The drawing-room of the house in Hill Street was bathed in the golden light of a June afternoon. Ethne stood by the open window, her gaze fixed absently on the street below, where carriages rolled toward the park, their polished surfaces glinting in the sun. Behind her, Mrs. Adair sat at the tea-table, her fingers drumming lightly against the porcelain. Both women were waiting, listening—though neither would admit it—for the sound of wheels stopping at the door.

At half-past five, the distant jingle of a cab reached them. Mrs. Adair’s head lifted sharply.

"That’s a cab," she said.

Ethne leaned forward, her fingers tightening imperceptibly on the windowsill. "Yes," she murmured, then exhaled. "But it isn’t stopping."

The sound faded, and Mrs. Adair’s gaze slid toward the clock. "Colonel Durrance is late." Her voice was casual, but her eyes lingered on Ethne with undisguised curiosity. There had been something in that single word—*yes*—that carried more tension than relief.

"I wonder why you didn’t meet him at the station?" she ventured.

Ethne didn’t turn. "He might think I came because I pitied him. I won’t have that."

A silence stretched between them before Ethne spoke again, her voice low. "You remember the telegram I showed you? The one from Lieutenant Calder?"

"Announcing his blindness? Yes."

"I want your word you’ll never mention it. He must never know I received it."

Mrs. Adair’s lips pursed. "If you insist. But I confess I don’t understand why."

"Two lives must not be ruined because of me."

The words were firm, but beneath them lay a current of unease. A year had passed since Ethne had last stood in this room and told Durrance to forget her. And yet, letter after letter had proven he hadn’t—not even the last, scrawled in a child’s uncertain hand from Wadi Halfa, where he had released her from any obligation.

She had answered without hesitation. But now, waiting, she felt the weight of what she had promised. Not just a marriage, but a performance—one she must sustain without faltering.

*Two lives*, she reminded herself.

Mrs. Adair’s voice cut through her thoughts. "Are you certain marriage won’t ruin them both? You, bound to a man who cannot share your world. He, forever wondering if he’s a burden."

"No," Ethne said, too quickly. Then, softer: "He must never wonder that."

She turned back to the window, her mind racing ahead. Durrance was not a man of subtlety; he had always taken people as they were. But blindness might have sharpened other senses—not just hearing, but perception.

The cab arrived without her noticing. Only when the bell rang did she startle, her pulse quickening.

Mrs. Adair slipped away, leaving Ethne alone as the door opened. She braced for the shock of seeing him diminished, but Durrance entered with an ease that disarmed her. His steps were measured, his posture straight. Only the slight hesitation as his hand brushed the back of a chair betrayed him.

"You moved by the window," he said, turning unerringly toward her. "I heard the street grow louder when you stepped aside."

Ethne’s breath caught. "Then it isn’t true? You’ve recovered?"

His smile was wry. "It’s true. I haven’t."

She forced herself forward, pouring tea with steady hands. Small talk carried them—his journey, the absurdities of navigating a world gone dark. He spoke lightly, even laughing at his own stumbles, and some of her tension eased.

Then, without warning, his tone shifted.

"I wrote to you from Wadi Halfa. Could you read it?"

"Perfectly."

"Calder—of the Sappers—helped. You don’t know him."

The name was tossed out like a challenge. Ethne froze. Was this a test? Did he suspect she had known of his blindness before his letter? That her pledge had been pity, not desire?

Her voice stayed even. "It was clear enough. And my answer stands. I take back nothing."

Durrance leaned forward, his face unreadable. "Good. But there’s my side to weigh."

He spoke methodically, as if reciting a rehearsed argument. Blindness meant isolation, a narrowing world. For a soldier, it was the end of everything—travel, command, purpose. All he had left, he said, was her.

"Do you bring more than friendship?" he asked abruptly.

Her answer came without hesitation. "There is nothing I wish for more than our marriage."

It was true. Harry Feversham was gone, lost to the past. She *did* wish it.

Durrance’s hand found hers, his grip firm. "Thank you."

Relief flickered through her. Then Mrs. Adair returned, deftly steering conversation to future plans—Ethne staying with her in Devonshire, Durrance consulting specialists.

When he left, Ethne returned to the window, watching his cab disappear into the stream of carriages. The fear hadn’t left her. Durrance had always been straightforward, but blindness had honed him. He listened now—not just to words, but to what lay beneath them.

*Two lives shall not be ruined because of me*, she thought again.

But it was no longer a resolution.

It was a prayer.

The summer months slipped by, each day thickening the unease in Ethne’s heart. One morning, as sunlight spilled through the open windows of Mrs. Adair’s house overlooking the Salcombe estuary, the words she had long held back finally broke free.

"I’m afraid," she murmured, her fingers tightening on the window ledge.

Behind her, Mrs. Adair did not glance up from her book, though a faint smile touched her lips. "Afraid of what? That some mishap befell Colonel Durrance in London yesterday?"

"No," Ethne replied slowly. "He’s here now—crossing the lawn toward us."

Mrs. Adair’s smile deepened, though she kept her eyes on the page. "I thought as much," she said, so softly the words barely carried.

Ethne barely heard them. Her gaze remained fixed on the figure approaching—steady, unhurried, his stick brushing idly against the grass as though out of habit rather than necessity.

"Do you think," she asked abruptly, "that he still hopes to regain his sight?"

Mrs. Adair arched a brow. "Why else would he travel to London so often to consult his oculist?"

"I’m afraid," Ethne repeated, turning sharply toward her friend. "Haven’t you noticed how quick he’s grown? How he reads silences, fills in unspoken words, deciphers every hesitation? Sometimes I think he sees straight into the corners of my mind. He reads me like a child’s primer."

Mrs. Adair’s reply was cool. "You’re at a disadvantage now. You no longer have your face to shield your thoughts."

"And his eyes tell me nothing at all," Ethne added.

The truth of it was undeniable. Once, Durrance’s gaze had been as open as her own, his expressions easy to decipher. Now, blindness had reversed their roles—where she had once been transparent, she now felt laid bare before a man who had grown unnervingly perceptive.

Mrs. Adair, however, was more intrigued by Ethne’s rare moment of vulnerability. The girl who had once been frankness itself was becoming something else—restless, guarded, a creature of evasions.

"There’s something, then, you must hide from him?" she pressed.

"Yes."

"Something important?"

"Something I *must* keep from him," Ethne insisted, though even as she spoke, she wondered if it was already too late.

She stepped onto the terrace, the morning air doing little to steady her nerves. Below, the lawn sloped toward the creek, where sunlight dappled the water and a lone sailboat tacked lazily against the breeze. She drew a breath, marshaling her composure before descending to meet Durrance.

His steps were sure, his stick a mere prop as he moved toward her. She matched his pace, though each step felt weighted.

"You saw your oculist yesterday?" she asked quickly. "What did he say?"

Durrance shrugged. "That we must wait. Only time will tell if a cure is possible."

Ethne searched his face, as though doubting his words. "But must *we* wait? Surely—"

"It’s wiser," he cut in smoothly, then, with a sudden shift: "It was Mrs. Adair, wasn’t it, who suggested I return to Guessens while you stayed here?"

The question caught her off guard. "I was distraught when I heard of your accident. Laura proposed it. Of course, I agreed."

Her voice held a plea—for understanding, for reassurance. Durrance obliged with a smile. "I know you did, Ethne."

The tension in her shoulders eased slightly, but Durrance was not finished. "Kind of her," he mused, "though it keeps you from your own country. I remember something Harry Feversham once said—"

He spoke the name without hesitation, but Ethne felt the pause that followed like a held breath. Testing, listening—for what? A flinch? A tremor? She gave none.

"—that you could never be truly happy far from Donegal."

"I wasn’t thinking of that when I asked why we must wait," Ethne protested. "That would make me selfish. I only wondered—why delay?"

Durrance’s answer was slow in coming. "I was thinking of Feversham just then. There’s something I’ve long wanted to ask you. Years ago, at Glenalla, you said you might one day tell me what happened that night at Lennon House. What ended your engagement. What sent him away."

Ethne’s fingers curled into her palms. "I’d rather not. It’s done with. Please don’t ask me."

Durrance relented at once. "Very well. I’d never wish to pain you."

"It’s not pain," she corrected quietly. "It’s just... it was so long ago. I think of him now as someone who’s dead."

A movement caught her eye—a small boat nosing into the bank downstream, a stranger stepping ashore, peering uncertainly toward the house. Relief flooded her.

"There’s a visitor," she said quickly. "I’ll see to him."

She hurried forward, grateful for the interruption. The man was of middling height, with a blunt nose, thick mustache, and an air of vague determination.

"Can you tell me if this is The Pool?" he called as she approached.

"Yes. Mrs. Adair is on the terrace."

"I came to see Miss Eustace."

Ethne turned back, startled. "I am Miss Eustace."

The stranger studied her, then gave a satisfied nod. "I’ve had some trouble finding you. Went all the way to Glenalla for nothing. Hard luck, with my leave so short."

Ethne frowned. "I’m sorry, but why were you looking for me?"

His mustache twitched. "You’ve forgotten my name, no doubt. But you’ve heard it before—five years ago. I’m Captain Willoughby."

The name struck like a blow. Ethne’s breath froze. Her hands clenched at her sides.

Willoughby remained unperturbed. "I understand your displeasure, Miss Eustace. None of us foresaw you’d be present when the feathers reached Feversham."

"How do you know I was?"

"Feversham told me."

"You’ve *seen* him?" The cry tore from her before she could stop it, raw and startled.

Willoughby blinked. "A year ago, at Suakin. Else why would I be here?"

Ethne’s pulse hammered. "Why *are* you here?"

From his coat, he produced a small, stained white feather. "To give you this."

Ethne recoiled. "Why?"

"Three feathers were sent to Feversham—three accusations of cowardice. This is one of them. I withdraw mine. I take it back."

"And you bring it to *me*?"

"He asked me to."

The world tilted. The feather in her palm was weightless, yet it carried the burden of years. Willoughby’s voice seemed to come from a distance, but she clung to his words, desperate to hear them all.

"Come," she managed, her voice steadier than she felt. "Tell me everything."

She led him to a secluded spot by the creek, where the water murmured and the hedges enclosed them like a secret.

"Begin," she said. "Leave nothing out. Not even his words—if you remember them."

She held the feather tightly, her mind reeling. Somewhere, somehow, Harry Feversham had reclaimed his honor. And now, at last, she would learn how.

For now, that was enough. The past was alive again, and with it, a hope she had long buried.

Ethne sat with Captain Willoughby in the quiet garden, the evening air warm around them. The white feather lay in her palm, a fragile thing, yet heavy with meaning.

"I won’t interrupt," she promised as Willoughby settled beside her. Yet within moments, she broke that vow.

"I was Deputy-Governor of Suakin," he began. "One night, in that sweltering heat—brine thick in the air, sleep impossible—a man came to me. He refused to give his name, but it was Feversham."

Ethne leaned forward. "How did he look?"

Willoughby frowned, struggling to recall. "Like any man who’d spent years in the Sudan—worn, hardened. Nothing remarkable."

She sighed, disappointment flickering. Five years without word of Harry Feversham, and now this man could offer her nothing of his face, his voice, the way he moved. But she pressed on. "Tell me everything."

Willoughby obliged. Feversham had arrived with letters—old dispatches from Gordon in Khartoum, lost when Berber fell to the Mahdists. Hidden in a crumbling wall, recovered only by Feversham’s daring.

"He went into Berber alone," Willoughby said.

A soft breath escaped Ethne. "He went in alone."

The story unfolded—the weeks of waiting, the danger thick in the streets, the constant fear of discovery. Feversham, moving like a shadow, trusting no one, sleeping in ruins. And then, the moment: digging into the mud-brick wall, his fingers closing around the letters—only for a lantern’s light to flare behind him.

Ethne tensed, her grip tightening on the feather. "What happened?"

"He ran. Struck down one man, then another. No hesitation. No fear."

Willoughby shook his head, baffled. "That’s what I don’t understand. A man who resigned his commission rather than face battle—yet in that moment, not a trace of fear."

Ethne understood. She saw it now, clear as the sky above them. It had never been the fear of pain or death that haunted Harry—only the specter of dishonor, the dread of failing when it mattered most. And in Berber, he had not failed.

She laughed, sudden and bright. "He needed no one’s faith but his own."

Willoughby misread her joy. "You gave him the feathers back, didn’t you? So he could redeem himself."

Her smile faded. "No. I gave him four feathers that night. Three for the accusations—and a fourth of my own. I meant to sever everything."

She looked down at the feather in her hand, its edges frayed with time. "But he sent this back. Not for forgiveness. Not for me. Because he knew I’d want to know he’d succeeded."

Pride swelled in her chest, fierce and unyielding. This was his victory, his alone. And in that, she found a strange, aching gladness.

Then, a movement at the garden’s edge. She turned.

Durrance stood in the gap of the hedge, watching them.

Ethne stood frozen as Colonel Durrance called her name from the entrance of the garden enclosure. Her fingers tightened around the yellowed feather in her palm, her breath stilled. Beside her, Captain Willoughby shifted uneasily, but her grip on his arm silenced him.

Durrance stepped forward, his movements unnervingly sure for a blind man. "Ethne?" he called again, his voice sharpening with uncertainty.

Willoughby whispered, "He can't truly be blind—he's looking right at us."

Ethne barely breathed. "He sees nothing. Stay still."

Durrance paused, his head tilted as though listening. Then, with sudden decision, he turned and strode toward the creek, muttering, "Where in the world can she be?"

Only when his footsteps faded did Ethne release Willoughby. "That was close," he muttered.

She ignored the remark, her mind racing. "You’ve told me everything?"

"Everything I remember."

"And this happened last spring?"

"Yes."

Her next question was quiet, deliberate. "Why didn’t you bring this to me last summer?"

Willoughby shrugged. "Short leave. I was hunting ibex in the hills."

"I see," she said, her voice flat. "Good sport?"

"Fair enough."

A bitter irony twisted in her chest. Last summer, she had been free. If Willoughby had come then—but he hadn’t. The moment was lost, and with it, the future she might have had. Yet her face betrayed nothing.

"You might have written," she suggested.

"Feversham didn’t seem urgent. He left for Suez soon after."

"I must find him," she murmured. "He must come back."

Willoughby hesitated. "Even if he does, there are still two feathers unaccounted for. Castleton is dead—killed at Tamai."

"Dead?" A flicker of satisfaction crossed her face.

Willoughby frowned. "Trench is worse off—captured, a prisoner in Omdurman."

She shrugged. "I don’t care to hear of Trench."

"You would if you knew what Omdurman means," he countered.

But Ethne was already moving on. "Where did Feversham go?"

"Back to England, I assume. He had funds."

"Good." She glanced toward the house. "You must leave now. Durrance mustn’t know you were here."

Willoughby balked. "Durrance was Feversham’s friend. He should know—"

"No." Her voice was steel. "He doesn’t know why my engagement ended, and it must stay that way."

"Why?"

"Because I am engaged to him now."

Willoughby stiffened. "Then I’ve wasted my time bringing this feather."

She curled her fingers around it. "You haven’t. I’m grateful."

Willoughby studied her, then sighed. "It’s a muddle. Rough on Feversham. Rough on Durrance. And—" His gaze softened. "Rough on you, too, Miss Eustace."

She didn’t answer. Instead, she led him to his boat, her thoughts a storm beneath a calm surface.

As Willoughby pushed off, Mrs. Adair appeared on the bank, her eyes alight with curiosity. "Who was that?"

"A friend," Ethne replied smoothly. "Captain Willoughby."

Mrs. Adair’s smile was knowing. "Of Colonel Durrance’s regiment?"

"No. The North Surrey."

"Ah. Feversham’s old regiment."

Ethne met her gaze without flinching. "We were speaking of him, yes."

Willoughby, caught between them, hesitated.

"Goodbye, Captain," Ethne said firmly.

With a resigned nod, he rowed away.

Mrs. Adair watched him go, then turned to Ethne. "Durrance is dining with us tonight."

"How kind of you to invite him," Ethne replied, her tone light. But her fingers tightened around the feather in her hand.

As they walked back to the house, Ethne’s mind was already elsewhere—on a man lost to the desert, on a future she could no longer grasp, and on the quiet, relentless weight of the past.

Mrs. Adair was not a woman to ask for explanations outright. Behind her calm, unreadable expression lay a mind that preferred to uncover secrets through quiet observation rather than direct inquiry. She would rather peer through a keyhole than walk through an open door, and knowledge gained by subtlety was always more satisfying to her than anything freely given.

So when she noticed the small white feather Ethne treasured, or puzzled over Captain Willoughby’s surprising news about Harry Feversham, she said nothing. Instead, she filled the lunch table with light conversation, though she might as well have spoken to the walls.

"You haven’t heard a word I’ve said, have you?" she finally exclaimed.

Ethne laughed, unapologetic, and retreated to her room afterward, craving solitude. At her window, she revisited Willoughby’s story, each detail sending a thrill through her. The regret that he hadn’t brought this news a year earlier—when she was still free—was fleeting, swallowed by the sheer joy of it. Her pride, once shattered by Harry Feversham’s actions, was now restored, and by the very man who had broken it. The thought warmed her, sent her pulse racing as if youth itself had returned.

She unlocked a drawer in her dressing-case and withdrew the one keepsake she had saved from Harry—his portrait. She traced the lines of his face, not with longing, but with quiet gratitude. He had needed no faith from her to inspire him, no encouragement to keep him steadfast. She placed the portrait back, tucking the white feather beside it before locking them away together.

Outside, the afternoon light softened into gold, the shadows of the trees stretching long across the lawn. But her heart ached for Donegal—for the heather-clad hills and the voice of the wind over granite. There, joy like this would have been complete. Here, she had only Dermod’s old collie for company, and to him she whispered her thoughts, her voice sometimes breaking unnoticed.

She pieced together what Willoughby had left unsaid—Harry’s years hiding in Suakin, the fear of being recognized by old friends, the terror in Berber, the desperate search for water in the desert. Each moment painted itself vividly in her mind, and with each, she wondered: *Where was I then? What was I doing?*

The evening crept in, the creek turning silver under the fading light, the rooks settling noisily into the trees. By dinner, her spirits had lifted so visibly that even Mrs. Adair couldn’t ignore it. The meal passed with an ease that had been absent for months. Durrance, too, seemed lighter, his usual tension gone.

"You’ll play for us tonight?" he asked as they rose from the table.

"Yes," she answered. "With all my heart."

The violin had stayed locked away for too long—a barometer of her emotions. When the world was kind, it sang; when it was cruel, it stayed silent. Tonight, it would speak freely.

Mrs. Adair lingered as Ethne left. "You’ve seen the change in her?"

Durrance’s smile was bright. "How could I miss it? She’s always been kind, but tonight—there’s something more."

Mrs. Adair said nothing, her face unreadable, and slipped out to the terrace while Ethne prepared to play.

Durrance found Ethne alone in the drawing room, the moonlight spilling through the open windows. She tightened the strings of her violin, then extinguished the lamp, leaving only the silver glow of the night.

"What shall I play?"

"The *Musoline Overture*," Durrance said. "The first piece you played for me at Ramelton. I want to hear it again—to compare."

She lifted the violin to her chin, and as the music filled the room, she forgot Durrance entirely. The melody carried her away—across the creek, over the sea, to some distant shore where a man might stir in his sleep beneath the stars and hear, if only for a moment, a message of friendship woven into the notes.

The chords swelled, proud and bright, echoing the joy she had reclaimed today. For a fleeting instant, she imagined the music as a bridge between worlds, a place where she and Harry Feversham might meet, if only in passing. She lingered on the final notes, half-expecting an answer.

And then, from the shadows behind her, Durrance spoke.

"Ethne, do you know where I last heard that overture?"

She blinked, startled back to the present. "At Ramelton, you said."

"No. Not played by you. Not really played at all. Just a fragment of it, badly strummed on a zither by a Greek in a café at Wadi Halfa."

Her breath caught. "This overture?"

"Yes. The Greek was Harry Feversham."

Silence. The answer had come, not across oceans, but from the man beside her. She didn’t question why Durrance had kept this secret until now, or what had brought Harry to that café. It didn’t matter. The music had been the bridge, and he had spoken through it.

"When?" she asked softly.

"February this year." Durrance leaned forward. "Let me tell you."

And so, from the shadows, he began.

Ethne remained motionless, her violin resting across her knees, her gaze fixed on the silvered garden beyond the open window. She held herself deliberately still, as if by maintaining the posture, she could dissolve the presence of Durrance in the room and hear only the voice of Harry Feversham speaking to her through the night. The moonlight pooled at her feet, a luminous pathway stretching toward the trees, and she focused on it, willing herself into the scene Durrance described—to that distant café in the sands of Wadi Halfa, where Harry had been.

"It was the night before I rode east into the desert for the last time," Durrance began, his voice low.

"The middle of February," Ethne murmured. "The fifteenth?"

"Yes. The fifteenth."

She repeated the date softly, as though marking it in her memory. A fleeting thought crossed her mind—where had she been that day? What had she been doing when Harry stood beneath that foreign sky, playing a melody meant for her? The irony of it struck her—that she should only now, months later, under this same moon, receive his message.

Durrance continued, painting the scene: the squalid café, the jeering crowd, the mismatched troupe of performers—an old Greek couple, their daughter, and the bearded young man hunched over a zither. The discordant scrape of the violin, the clumsy waltz, the girl’s thin voice singing—all of it a pitiful spectacle beneath the vast indifference of the desert stars.

"And then," Durrance said, "the young man played a tune. At first, it was nothing but stumbling notes, but then—a phrase emerged. A ghost of a melody, but familiar."

Ethne’s breath caught. "From the *Musoline Overture*?"

"Yes. The same piece you played tonight."

A shiver passed through her. Harry had no ear for music—she remembered that well. Yet he had labored to learn this fragment, note by halting note, until he could summon some echo of it from the strings. The thought of him, alone in that place, painstakingly recreating the melody that bound them, was almost too much to bear.

"He played it badly, of course," Durrance admitted. "But it was unmistakable. When he stepped outside, I threw a sovereign into his hat. He turned—and despite the beard, I knew him. Before he could stop himself, he said my name."

Ethne’s fingers tightened around her violin. "Then there was no mistake."

"None. It was Harry Feversham."

*But why?* The question burned in her mind. Willoughby had said Harry had means—his father’s allowance. He had no need to scrape for coins in a backwater café. What had driven him there?

Durrance told of their brief exchange—Harry’s whispered plea to be let go, his evasion, the missed chance when Durrance rode east at dawn. And then, the final blow: Calder’s letter, confirming that Harry had vanished south, beyond the patrols, into the enemy’s desert.

Ethne stood abruptly, crossing to the window. The night air was cool against her skin, but it did nothing to ease the fire in her chest. "What will happen to him?" she asked, her voice steady only by sheer will.

"At best, capture. At worst—" Durrance didn’t finish.

She already knew. Harry had gone to find Trench. To fulfill what he saw as his duty. To atone.

The letter trembled in her hands as she read it again, the words stark in the moonlight. There was no mistaking the truth. She should say something, but the weight of it crushed her voice. Instead, she stepped silently over the windowsill, into the garden, where the shadows could swallow the agony Durrance must not see.

Behind her, his voice followed, earnest, oblivious to her flight. "I wanted you to know. I thought—perhaps you’d understand why I waited to tell you."

But Ethne was already gone, vanishing into the night as soundlessly as Harry had slipped into the desert.

Ethne had hoped to slip away unnoticed, but Mrs. Adair, seated on the terrace in the shadow of the house, saw her. The girl moved swiftly, almost desperately, as though fleeing from something unseen. Mrs. Adair frowned. Moments earlier, she had watched the drawing-room window darken as Ethne extinguished the lamp, leaving only the silver glow of moonlight. The abrupt shift from light to shadow had twisted something sharp in Mrs. Adair’s chest—a private, searing bitterness.

She had not moved. Instead, she listened, catching the faint strains of Ethne’s violin, the low murmur of voices afterward. The music had been different tonight—freer, fuller, as though something long restrained had finally broken loose. And then, just as suddenly, Ethne had fled.

Durrance’s voice, still speaking within the empty room, drew Mrs. Adair closer. She stepped silently to the window, unseen, and listened.

*"I began to realize,"* he was saying, his voice warm with rueful amusement, *"even that first afternoon in London, that what you felt for me was only friendship. Strange, isn’t it? Blindness made me see clearer. If I could still look into your face, I might have believed whatever you wished me to. But you had no defenses left. And I—well, I had learned to listen."*

Mrs. Adair did not stir. Durrance spoke as if to an audience, yet there was no one there.

*"I should have ended our engagement then. But I was selfish. I warned you, Ethne—selfishness is the blind man’s curse. I delayed. I lied about my sight, pretended there was still hope when there was none. Because I wanted time. Time for your friendship to become something more."* His voice softened. *"Tonight, I burned my bridges. I told you everything—about my eyes, about Harry Feversham, the danger he’s in. And do you know why? Because for the first time, I heard it in your voice—the change I’d waited for. Laughter without restraint, music with your heart in it. As if, at last, you were free."*

But Ethne was not there to hear. She was in the garden, the collie dog pressing against her leg, the moonlight painting the creek in quicksilver. She had fled because another moment, another word, and the careful pretense of these past months would have shattered. Durrance already knew the truth—had seen past the walls she’d built—but she could not bear to face it.

The night was still, the tide whispering against the bank. She sat on the bench, struggling to steady herself. This morning, she had believed Harry Feversham restored to her. Tonight, she had lost him again—perhaps forever. He would pay his debt to the last measure, and she hated him for it. *Wasn’t it enough?* she thought fiercely. *To redeem himself in the eyes of even one of the three?*

But no. He had gone south, into that merciless land. Willoughby’s hints of Omdurman’s horrors haunted her. She had refused to hear the details then; now, she tormented herself imagining them.

Inside, Durrance’s voice fell silent. He called Ethne’s name once, twice—then touched the violin left on her chair. The string’s vibration hummed through the quiet. He straightened, his face hardening into comprehension.

Mrs. Adair did not move as he stepped to the window.

*"Ethne?"* His hand brushed her sleeve. *"No. Not Ethne."*

*"No,"* Mrs. Adair agreed.

Durrance absorbed this, then asked quietly, *"Did you hear what I said?"*

*"Enough."* She tilted her head. *"You told her about Feversham. And she ran because she still cares for him."*

Durrance exhaled sharply. *"The change in her tonight—you noticed it too?"*

Mrs. Adair smiled thinly. *"Brought by a Captain Willoughby. They spoke privately in the garden. She was… radiant afterward."*

Durrance’s jaw tightened. *"A token?"*

He turned away, gripping the terrace rail. *"Then I was wrong. It wasn’t me who freed her tonight."*

Mrs. Adair watched him, the moonlight etching the lines of his face. *"What will you do?"*

*"If Feversham’s in Omdurman, there may still be a way. From Suakin, perhaps…"* He shook his head. *"But Ethne can’t know. Not yet."*

He took his leave, moving with deliberate steadiness across the garden. Mrs. Adair watched until the darkness took him, marveling at the control in his stride—no sign of the blow he had just been dealt.

Ethne, meanwhile, stood by the water’s edge, the dog leaning against her. The night was vast, indifferent. She had lost Harry twice in a single day. And tomorrow, she would have to begin the pretense all over again.

For Durrance must never know.

The night was still and deep when Durrance returned to "Guessens," his house nestled among the fields. His servant waited, lantern in hand, but Durrance dismissed him with a quiet word. "Put out the lights and rest," he said, moving past the hall into his study—though *study* was too gentle a name for a room lined with the trophies of a life lived boldly.

In the darkness, he wandered the room, his fingers tracing the contours of cups and goblets, each one a memory etched in silver or pewter. The thin-stemmed goblet from a regimental steeple-chase at Colchester, the dull gleam of a college trophy from Oxford days, the silver-mounted hoof of a favorite horse—they were more than objects. They were fragments of a past now veiled from his sight.

He paused at the rack of rifles, lifting one after another, their weight familiar in his hands. The Remington spoke of a lion stalked across the hills west of Berenice, felled at three hundred yards as the sun bled into dusk. Another whispered of ibex in the Khor Baraka, of antelope in the northern mountains of Suakin. A small Greener shotgun recalled winter nights on the Salcombe estuary, the kick of the stock against his shoulder as mallards burst from the reeds.

But the memories turned bitter. These were voices from a life he could no longer see, sights he would never witness again. He set the guns aside, his jaw tightening. A reckless longing seized him—to defy his blindness, to step beyond its cage and reclaim the world denied to him. His mind raced eastward, past the stars, to the scorching sun of Port Said, the chant of coalers under red braziers, the slow procession of ships through the Suez. He could almost smell the brine of the Red Sea, feel the grit of Yambo’s streets beneath his boots, hear the murmur of Suakin’s bazaars.

The desert called to him—the silence of the sands, the stark rise of rock-cones against the sky, the wind hissing through twilight. It was his inheritance, and he ached for it with a thirst fiercer than fever.

Then, abruptly, the trance broke. Birdsong filtered through the window—blackbirds and swallows greeting the dawn. The illusion shattered. There would be no more journeys. The chain of his affliction held him fast.

He climbed the stairs slowly, guided by the balustrade, and fell into bed as the sun rose. Sleep took him, heavy and dreamless.

The sun was already high over Dongola, baking the earth, when the crowd gathered before the house of Emir Wad El Nejoumi. They had come for sport—a white prisoner, captured near the wells of El Agia, now stood beneath the palms, guarded by four Ansar soldiers.

Harry Feversham was a ragged figure, stripped of dignity. His sun-blistered shoulders bore the marks of the desert, his wrists bound with palm rope, an iron collar locked around his neck. He grinned at the jeering crowd like a man unhinged, playing the madman for his life. If he could convince them he was harmless—a fool, not a spy—they might send him to Omdurman rather than the gallows looming black against the yellow sand.

But the act was harder than he’d imagined. Inside the Emir’s house, his fate was being weighed. Outside, the mob taunted him. An old hag danced before him, her song a graphic promise of mutilation and hell. Children blew war-trumpets in his ears until his head rang. A warrior tested his spear’s edge, lunging forward—only for Feversham to be yanked backward by the chain, choking as he fell.

Then came the summons. Dragged into the dim room, he faced Nejoumi.

"You are a spy," the Emir declared.

"A musician," Feversham insisted, laughing.

A broken zither was thrust into his hands. With stiff fingers, he plucked out the only melody he knew—the same tune Durrance had heard in Tewfikieh, the same Ethne had played the night before.

Nejoumi was unconvinced. He offered food—raw camel liver, salt-streaked and pungent. Feversham ate, though his stomach revolted, knowing hesitation meant fear, and fear meant death.

The questions came again: of Cairo’s defenses, of Assouan’s garrison, of whispers against the Sirdar.

"How would a Greek know such things?" Feversham countered.

Nejoumi’s patience snapped. Outside, they drenched his bonds, tightening them until the fibers bit into flesh. A noose coiled around his neck. He was marched to the gallows.

"Speak," Nejoumi commanded. "Or you die now."

Feversham grinned, shaking his head like a simpleton. His heart hammered, not with terror, but with a strange exhilaration. He had played his part.

Nejoumi sneered. Then, with a wave:

"Tomorrow, the Kaffir goes to Omdurman."

Only then did Feversham feel the agony in his wrists. Only then did he wonder—would Ethne ever know? Would the fourth feather ever return?

But for now, he had survived.

The days passed in a fragile semblance of peace at Lennon House, but beneath the surface, tension coiled like a hidden serpent. Mrs. Adair watched Ethne and Durrance with growing unease, waiting for the inevitable rupture that never came. Instead, they maintained an unspoken truce, speaking lightly, laughing often—a performance so convincing that even she, who knew the truth, sometimes doubted its artifice.

Durrance played his part with care. He spoke of his blindness as though it were no more than an inconvenience, a triviality easily borne. He praised Ethne’s companionship, her resilience, her unwavering brightness—words that cut deeper than silence, for they were kindnesses she did not deserve.

*"You see,"* he said one evening as they sat on the terrace, *"one adjusts to blindness. But one never adjusts to you, Ethne. Each time we meet, I find something new to marvel at."*

She smiled, though her heart ached. His words were both balm and blade, soothing the wound even as they twisted it.

Yet there were moments when the façade cracked. Durrance would catch her gaze lingering too long on the horizon, or notice the tremor in her hands when she thought herself unobserved. He said nothing, but he understood.

One afternoon, he handed her a telegram—four stark words:

She read it in silence, her fingers tightening on the paper. The image of Harry in that distant, sun-scorched hell rose unbidden—the stifling dark, the stench of unwashed bodies, the slow, grinding cruelty of captivity.

*"He is paying ten times over for a mistake that was never his alone,"* she whispered, more to herself than to Durrance.

Durrance, ever perceptive, pretended not to hear the grief beneath her words. *"Something can be done,"* he said briskly. *"Calder will arrange it. Money will not be spared. He is my friend, Ethne. You’ll see."*

She nodded, but her thoughts raced ahead. *If Harry could escape Trench, why not himself?* The hope was fragile, but it was all she had.

Later, when Durrance mentioned Castleton’s death, her response was swift and sharp. *"I am glad."*

Durrance stiffened. *"You never knew him."*

*"No,"* she admitted. *"But I am glad all the same."*

The slip was careless, and she knew it. Durrance filed it away, another piece in the puzzle he was assembling. Willoughby’s feather, Trench’s involvement, now Castleton—each thread led back to Harry’s disgrace. And Ethne, who had once turned from Harry in shame, now clung to the hope of his redemption.

But Durrance kept his silence. There would be time enough for truth later.

For now, they played their roles—Durrance the devoted suitor, Ethne the loyal companion—each hiding the cracks in their hearts.

And so the days passed, a brittle masquerade, until the moment when the fragile peace would shatter, and the secrets between them would demand their reckoning.

Captain Willoughby was a man who could clear a room without raising his voice. At his club, he was infamous for his relentless monologues—long, winding tales about people no one knew, delivered with the gravity of a man unveiling profound truths. His fellow members had long since learned to scatter at his approach, leaving him in solitary possession of the best-lit corner, a cigar clenched between his teeth, and the air thick with unspoken relief at his isolation.

It was in this state of splendid abandonment that the waiter found him one October afternoon, presenting a calling card with a deferential cough.

"Colonel Durrance?" Willoughby frowned, turning the card over in his fingers. Durrance had never sought him out before. Why now? A prickle of unease ran down his spine—had word of his visit to Kingsbridge somehow reached the man?

"Does Colonel Durrance know I'm here?" he asked sharply.

"Yes, sir."

Willoughby sighed. "Show him in, then."

He braced himself. If Durrance had come to pry into Ethne Eustace’s affairs, he’d find no willing informant here. Feversham and Durrance could settle their own quarrels.

Yet when Durrance entered, his demeanor gave nothing away. He moved with the deliberate ease of a man who had long adapted to blindness, accepting a cigar without hesitation.

"I’ll smoke," he said, striking a match. "They say a blind man can’t enjoy tobacco without seeing the smoke, but I’ve found that’s nonsense. If I let this go out, I’ll know." He exhaled slowly, the scent of good Havana filling the space between them. "Lucky to catch you, Willoughby. I’m only in town for the day."

Willoughby relaxed slightly. "Mather told me you’d been asking after me."

"Mather? Yes, from Suakin." Durrance leaned back. "We were wondering—did you ever meet an Arab called Abou Fatma? Kabbabish tribe?"

Willoughby’s pulse jumped. "Abou Fatma? No, can’t say I have."

Durrance’s voice remained casual. "Mather and I found him years ago near Sinkat. He’d hidden Gordon’s letters in Berber. You remember—they were brought to you in Suakin."

Willoughby’s fingers tightened around his pipe. "Ah, yes. But it wasn’t Abou Fatma who recovered them."

"No? Pity. I’d have liked to know he’d managed it." Durrance’s tone was light, but Willoughby wasn’t fooled. The man’s blindness had sharpened his other senses—he missed nothing.

"Gordon was dead," Willoughby said, too quickly. "No point in risking lives for old letters."

"For no *obvious* reason," Durrance corrected softly.

Willoughby stiffened. A trap? He fumbled for his matches, struck one, and sucked at his pipe to hide his unease.

Durrance let the silence stretch before shifting tactics. "Actually, I wanted to speak of Harry Feversham."

Willoughby nearly choked on his smoke.

"I ran into him at Wadi Halfa," Durrance continued, as if oblivious. "Fallen on hard times. I hoped you might keep an eye out for him in the Soudan."

Relief washed over Willoughby. So that was all. "Of course. Old friend of yours, wasn’t he?"

"One of the few." Durrance’s voice warmed. "Remember that dinner at his flat? Just before we left England. You, me, Feversham—and Trench."

Willoughby’s pipe gurgled. He swore as the match burned his fingers.

"Castleton was supposed to join us," Durrance mused.

"No, he wasn’t," Willoughby blurted. "Sent a telegram—dining elsewhere."

"Ah, yes. With someone from the War Office, wasn’t it?"

Willoughby’s blood ran cold. Durrance wasn’t just reminiscing—he was *hunting*.

A few pleasantries later, Durrance rose, leaving his cigar cold on the table. "Forgot about it," he said lightly. "Too busy thinking of Feversham."

As the cab carried him away, Durrance pieced together the truth. That telegram had been Feversham’s undoing—news of his regiment’s deployment, panic at the brink of war. And now, five years later, the man was redeeming himself in the harshest crucible imaginable.

Durrance smiled. Sutch would get the shock of his life.

He caught the night train to Devonshire, the puzzle finally complete.

The drawing room at The Pool was quiet, save for the ticking of the clock on the mantel. Durrance stood by the window, his hat in hand, ready to depart. He had timed it so there would be little room for lingering farewells—his carriage already waited at the steps of Guessens, luggage secured, his servant impatient by the door.

Ethne followed him onto the terrace, where Mrs. Adair stood at the top of the stone steps, her hands clasped tightly before her.

"I’d like a word with Colonel Durrance before he goes," Mrs. Adair said abruptly.

Ethne nodded. "Then we’ll say goodbye here." She turned to Durrance, her voice softening. "You’ll write from Wiesbaden?"

"The moment I arrive," he promised.

With that, Ethne remained on the terrace as Durrance descended the steps beside Mrs. Adair. The weight of pretense had lifted—the months of silent strain, of careful words and guarded looks, were over. Relief sharpened Durrance’s stride as they crossed the lawn, but Mrs. Adair lagged, her steps heavy.

"You’re leaving," she said, her voice low. "In two days, you’ll be in Wiesbaden, Ethne in Glenalla. The house will be empty. It’s already empty."

She glanced around. The garden, once vibrant, was dulled beneath a gray sky. The flowers had faded, the stream ran dark between the trees, and fallen leaves scattered like discarded memories.

"How long will you stay?" she pressed.

"As long as necessary."

"That tells me nothing."

He offered no elaboration, and she bristled at his silence. She knew nothing of his plans—whether he meant to release Ethne or hold her to her promise. The uncertainty gnawed at her.

"You don’t trust me," she accused, her voice edged with bitterness.

"Have I reason to?" His reply was quiet, but it struck like a lash. "You interfered. You told me of Willoughby’s visit when Ethne wished it kept secret."

"I thought you deserved the truth."

"Yet you were her friend."

"And yours," she countered, then laughed harshly. "But you never really saw me, did you? Not then, not now."

He didn’t answer, and the silence spurred her forward, words tumbling out in a reckless tide.

"I told you brutally because I wanted to hurt you. I *wanted* you to see—what you refused to see. There you were, always with Ethne, always in the dark together, while I stood alone on the terrace. Always outside." Her voice dropped to a whisper. "She never loved you, not truly. Only pity. Pity and fear. Fear of your blindness, of the burden it would be. But *I*—I was glad when I heard. Glad because it meant she would shrink from you, and I… I wouldn’t."

Durrance recoiled as if struck. The cruelty of it, the raw confession, was unlike anything he had known. Yet beneath the venom, he sensed a deeper desperation.

"You’re unjust to Ethne," he said quietly.

The words seemed to drain the fury from her. She faltered, then exhaled sharply. "Perhaps. But I’ve had little kindness from life to teach me fairness."

They reached the stile at the garden’s edge. He turned, and for the first time, truly looked at her—not past her, not through her, but *at* her.

"Tell me," he said.

And she did.

She spoke of a girl thrust into marriage by an imperious mother, of years stifled in gilded monotony. Of a husband kind but distant, of loneliness that gnawed like hunger. And then—of Durrance himself, a fleeting brightness in her gray world, a hope she had clung to until Ethne eclipsed it.

"I tried to keep you," she admitted, the confession bitter on her tongue. "I schemed to tie Ethne to another so you’d turn elsewhere. And I succeeded—only for you to leave for the Soudan. Three years gone. And when you returned…" She laughed mirthlessly. "She refused you, then claimed you out of guilt. And here we are."

Durrance absorbed it in silence. The smallness of it all struck him—how vast consequences sprang from quiet, desperate acts.

"I’m sorry," he said at last. It was all he could offer.

She studied him, then nodded once, sharply. "Goodbye, Colonel."

He climbed the stile and crossed the field without looking back.

Mrs. Adair remained, gripping the wooden rail. She had laid herself bare, and for what? No wound dealt, no victory won—only the certainty that when he returned (if he returned alone), there would be no easy friendship waiting.

The wind stirred the leaves at her feet. The garden was emptier than ever.

The land was merciless, and so were its people. That was Calder’s thought as the steamer carried him down the Nile from Wadi Halfa to Aswan, his furlough granting him a brief escape from the harshness of the Sudan. Leaning against the upper deck’s railing, he stared down at the barge lashed alongside, where native passengers crowded beneath the sun’s relentless glare. Among them, on a simple *angareb*, lay a motionless figure shrouded in black—whether man or woman, he couldn’t tell.

The two Arabs who had carried the stretcher aboard at Korosko now lounged in the stern, laughing as though the silent form beside them were nothing more than cargo. The sick man—if he still lived—had been left to the sun’s cruelty, untouched, unfed, while the gangrene spread. Calder clenched his jaw. This was the Sudan’s way: life and death written in dust, accepted without struggle.

As sunset painted the dunes gold, a wind rose, churning the river into whitecaps. Calder found himself relieved—at least the breeze might bring some small comfort to the dying man.

At dinner, he struck up a conversation with a German passenger. "You’re not a doctor, by chance?"

"A medical student," the man replied, adjusting his eyeglasses. "From Bonn. Here to see the cataracts, though they wouldn’t let me beyond Wadi Halfa."

"Then I’ll impose on your holiday," Calder said. "There’s a man below who needs help."

The German hesitated only a moment before nodding. "All experience is valuable."

They descended into the night, where the single swaying lamp cast erratic shadows over the barge’s deck. The wind had grown fiercer, spraying foam across the bow—and there, left in the wash, was the *angareb*, its occupant still unmoving beneath the black veil.

Calder grabbed a lantern. "They haven’t moved him."

Together, they crossed to the barge. The Arabs protested when Calder ordered the covering removed, but he ignored them, peeling back the cloth himself. The wound beneath was festering, alive with infection. The German recoiled.

"This wasn’t from a fall," he muttered. "A spear did this."

Calder bent low. The Arab’s eyes tracked his words—he understood English.

"Where are you from?" Calder pressed.

No answer came, but the man’s gaze sharpened at certain names: *Omdurman. Berber.* And then—*Effendi Feversham.*

A spark of recognition.

Calder’s pulse quickened. This was news he had waited for—news of Harry Feversham. But the Arab could say no more. His lips trembled, yet no sound escaped.

By morning, they delivered him to the hospital in Aswan. The doctors offered little hope. Recovery, if it came at all, would be slow.

Calder sailed on to Cairo, frustration gnawing at him. The chance to aid Feversham had slipped through his fingers. The wounded Arab—had he been able to speak—was Abou Fatma of the Kabbabish. His presence here, broken and silent, meant one thing: Feversham’s plan to rescue Colonel Trench had failed.

As Lieutenant Sutch guided his trap along the high chalk road of Hampshire, the afternoon sun stretched long shadows across the heath. The scent of gorse and pine filled the air, but his thoughts were far from the peaceful countryside—they lingered in the burning sands of the Sudan, where a man he loved as a son had vanished into the unknown.

*I knew there would be trouble,* he muttered aloud, a habit formed from years of solitude. *From the very beginning, I knew.*

The road dipped sharply, revealing a small railway station nestled in the valley below, its red-tiled roof bright against the green. The sight of the tracks, gleaming like silver threads, deepened Sutch’s unease. He reined in his pony, hesitating.

*I promised Harry I’d say nothing,* he reminded himself. *I swore it in the Criterion grill-room.*

The distant whistle of an approaching train broke his reverie. He flicked the reins and descended the hill, arriving just as the train shuddered to a halt. Only two passengers alighted: Colonel Durrance and his servant.

"You got my telegram," Durrance said as he approached the trap.

"Luckily, I was at home."

"May I impose on your hospitality for the night?"

"Of course," Sutch replied, though his voice lacked enthusiasm. He had expected this visit, dreaded it even, for he knew Durrance had come for answers—answers he was sworn to keep secret.

But as Durrance stepped into the trap, his movements careful in the blindness that had claimed him, Sutch felt a pang of remorse. He had been prepared to deceive, but how could he mislead a man who had already lost so much?

"I was sorry to hear of your misfortune," Sutch said as they drove off. "I know what it is to be cut down in your prime."

Durrance’s reply was measured. "There are compensations—for some men. A man who fails through his own fault gets the chance to know himself. To rebuild."

Sutch stiffened. Was Durrance speaking of Harry? Did he already know?

"You mean Harry Feversham," he said bluntly.

Durrance nodded. "A man who thought himself a coward and was not. He made a mistake, yes, but he’s spent years redeeming it."

"How do you know this?" Sutch demanded.

"I am engaged to Ethne Eustace."

Sutch’s breath caught. "She knew of his disgrace—but not his efforts to undo it."

"She knows now."

A fierce joy surged through Sutch. *She knows.* The words were a balm to years of guilt. He had failed Harry once, but now—now there was hope.

"Willoughby told her?" he guessed.

"No. She kept silent. She forbade Willoughby to speak of it to me."

"Then how—?"

"Someone else betrayed it," Durrance said. "But that doesn’t matter. What matters is that Harry must come home."

Sutch exhaled sharply. *Yes. He must.*

Durrance continued, his voice steady. "Ethne has not forgotten him. She stays with me out of pity, not love. Harry must return—for her sake, and his own."

Sutch hesitated, torn between loyalty and conscience. At last, he spoke.

"There’s something you should know. When Harry left, he believed he was parting from Ethne forever. He had no hope she would wait. He didn’t even want her to know his struggle. He thought only of proving himself—not reclaiming her."

Durrance smiled faintly. "That doesn’t change the truth. She loves him still. And I will not stand in the way."

The weight of years lifted from Sutch’s shoulders. "Then I will go to Egypt. I will find him."

"He’s in Omdurman," Durrance said quietly. "A prisoner."

The words struck like a blow. Sutch gripped the reins, his mind reeling.

"What must I do?" he whispered.

Durrance’s voice was firm. "Go to Suakin. I’ll give you letters—to Willoughby, and to a merchant who can fund your search. Bring him home, Sutch. For all our sakes."

Sutch nodded, determination hardening in his chest. *At last,* he thought. *After all these years, I can make amends.*

The heath stretched before them, vast and unchanging, but for the first time in years, Lieutenant Sutch felt the stirring of hope.

Lieutenant Sutch had not known such excitement in thirty years. Dawn found him already in motion, rousing his household with uncharacteristic bustle. Trunks were packed and repacked, orders barked down hallways, servants scrambling to meet his unrelenting demands. The quiet rhythms of his retired life had shattered overnight—now, at last, he had a purpose again.

"I leave this afternoon," he declared to Durrance over breakfast, his voice sharp with anticipation. "The night mail to the Continent. You’ll join me as far as London?"

Durrance shook his head. "One more visit before I go to Wiesbaden."

Sutch frowned. "Where?"

"General Feversham."

The name struck Sutch like a blow. He set down his fork, studying Durrance with disbelief. "Why? After all these years, why reopen that wound?"

"Because he deserves to know the truth," Durrance replied calmly. "You’re bound by a promise, but I am not. Harry was never a coward. His father should hear it from someone who saw his courage firsthand."

Sutch scoffed. "He won’t understand. All he cares about is the family name."

"Perhaps," Durrance conceded. "But I think there’s more beneath that iron mask."

The carriage rolled through Surrey’s wooded lanes, bringing Durrance to Broad Place by late afternoon. The house stood unchanged—a fortress of tradition, its high walls lined with the stern portraits of dead Fevershams. General Feversham, though frailer now, still carried himself like a soldier. Only the tremor in his step betrayed his years.

"You’ve joined the veterans too soon, Durrance," the general remarked as tea was served in the shadowed hall. His voice was brittle, stripped of warmth. "A pity."

Durrance ignored the jab. "I came on the same business as before."

Feversham’s spine stiffened. "And my answer hasn’t changed. I won’t discuss my son."

"Even if I told you where he’s been? What he’s done?"

A pause. Then, coldly: "Not interested."

Durrance leaned forward. "Then I’ll risk your displeasure." He spoke plainly—Berber, Wadi Halfa, Omdurman. The letters recovered, the risks taken, the imprisonment endured. Sutch’s mission to Suakin. The story unspooled like a campaign report, devoid of sentiment.

When he finished, the room was silent. Feversham sat motionless, one hand shielding his eyes. At last, he exhaled.

"What I still don’t understand," he muttered, "is why he resigned."

Durrance smiled faintly. "Some things defy explanation. But the rest—his honor, his courage—that’s clear enough."

Feversham’s hand fell. "He can’t regain his career."

"No. But he can reclaim everything else."

A flicker of something raw crossed the old man’s face. Pride? Regret? Durrance couldn’t tell. But when Feversham spoke again, his voice was quieter.

"Durrance," the general said abruptly, "who’s funding this escape?"

Feversham’s grip tightened on his cane. "It’s my right to bear the cost."

Feversham’s jaw clenched. "I assumed—"

Back in the hall, the general stood before the portraits. "No disgrace now," he murmured. Then, louder, as if willing the words into the stones: "Let him come home. Soon."

Outside, the moon silvered the Sussex Downs. For the first time in years, Feversham did not turn away from their beauty.

In those days, the House of Stone in Omdurman stood unguarded by the great mud wall that would later encircle it. Instead, a thorn zareeba marked the boundary of that wretched prison and the barren stretch around it. The building itself sat on the eastern edge of the city—a squalid, sun-scorched capital where no flower bloomed, no grass grew, and no tree cast shade. Only a sprawl of hovels, teeming with vermin and sickness, stretched across the hard, cracked earth.

Between the prison and the Nile lay an open stretch of land, and for now, the prisoners were permitted—so long as daylight held—to shuffle in their chains down the half-mile slope to the riverbank. There, they drew water and washed themselves. For the natives, escape was not impossible. The riverfront bustled with dhows and traders; captives mingled with friends, plotted their flight, or slipped away into the crowd to find a blacksmith willing to strike off their irons for a price. In Omdurman, the clink of chains was as common as the call of merchants.

But for a European, escape was another matter. A white prisoner was a marked man. Camels stationed in the desert, money, careful planning, and above all, loyal natives willing to risk their lives—these were necessities. Yet even then, plans crumbled. The camel drivers might vanish, preparations unravel under the lash of a suspicious gaoler, or a servant’s courage fail at the last moment.

Colonel Trench had begun to lose hope. He knew his friends were working for him—sometimes the boy who brought his food whispered for him to be ready; sometimes, during the Khalifa’s parades, a stranger jostled his camel with a word of encouragement. But nothing ever came of it. Day after day, he watched the sun rise beyond the palm trees of Khartoum, burning its path across the sky, while the months dragged on.

One evening in late August—the same year Durrance returned to England blind—Trench sat in the prison enclosure, watching the sun sink toward the western plains with a dull ache of dread. However unbearable the day’s heat, it was nothing compared to the horrors each night brought.

At twilight, Idris es Saier, the hulking gaoler of the Gawaamah tribe, and his men appeared.

"Into the House of Stone!" he bellowed.

Amid prayers and curses, the prisoners jostled toward the narrow doorway, lashed forward by whips. The chamber was already half-filled with thirty wretches sprawled on the filthy floor or slumped against the walls in exhaustion. Two hundred more were herded inside, packed shoulder to shoulder in the suffocating dark. The room was thirty feet square, its roof supported by a thick pillar. No window broke the walls; only a few narrow slits near the ceiling pretended to offer air. Into this reeking pit, the prisoners were driven, screaming and fighting. The door slammed shut, plunging them into blackness so complete that a man could not see his own hands.

Trench fought like the rest. There was a corner near the door he coveted with desperate hunger—shelter from blows, from trampling feet, from the bruising shackles of his neighbors.

The fear drove him to frenzy. He kicked, elbowed, clawed his way forward, striking with his chains, burrowing through the press of bodies until he reached the corner, gasping and drenched in sweat. The rest of the night would be spent defending it.

"Is it you, Ibrahim?" he shouted over the din.

A deep voice answered, "Yes, Effendi."

Relief washed over him. Ibrahim, a Hadendoa Arab, had become his ally in this hell. Prisoners at Omdurman starved unless they had money or friends outside. Trench had received aid in secret at times, but often he and Ibrahim had shared what little they had. Now, the tall Arab steadied him against the wall.

In the far corner, a brutal fight raged. The crowd swayed like a single beast, men cursing, gasping, some sobbing as they were trampled underfoot. Objects flew through the dark—clods of dirt, even stones—striking unseen targets. For two hours, Trench endured the stifling heat, the sour stench of sweat and filth, the ceaseless din. Eight more hours remained before dawn would bring release.

Then the door creaked open. Idris stood in the gap, a silhouette against the fading light.

"Make room!" he roared, hurling burning tufts of dried grass into the press of bodies. The prisoners shrieked as flames licked at them, unable to move, unable to escape the searing heat. The whips cracked, clearing a space by the door. Into it, a man was flung—a slight figure, bent under heavy irons. The door slammed shut again.

Trench glimpsed him in the dim light—pale, broken. *He won’t last the night.*

The new prisoner clung to the door, desperate for air, but the others tore him away, shoving him deeper into the crush until he stumbled against Trench.

"Back!" Trench snarled, raising his arm to strike—then froze.

The man was muttering in *English.*

Trench seized his arm. "Don’t fall! Ibrahim, help me!"

Together, they carved out a space, shielding the stranger as he slumped to the ground. His words came in fevered bursts, rambling, disjointed.

"...the riding lights of the yachts... the band playing... Bray, the fairyland of lights..."

Trench bent close, straining to hear. The voice spoke of Dublin Bay, of hills and woods, of a woman named Ethne.

"...it’s not right, the pain... the engines didn’t stop... just kept throbbing..."

Then, clearer:

"Will you take it back? The others did... all but the one who died at Tamai..."

Trench stiffened. *Tamai.* The name struck like a spark in the dark.

The voice babbled on, pleading, disjointed.

"...three little white feathers... the world’s at an end..."

Trench’s breath caught. *Three feathers.*

"Feversham!" he shouted, shaking the man. "Harry Feversham!"

But the figure in his arms only murmured, lost in delirium, speaking to ghosts of the past.

Trench stared into the blackness, his mind reeling. This broken man was the one who had vanished after the scandal of the feathers—the one who had been branded a coward.

And now, by some cruel twist of fate, he was here. In the House of Stone.

With Trench.

For three days, Harry Feversham drifted in and out of delirium, his words tangled in fever, his body broken by hardship. For three days, Lieutenant Trench—gaunt, sun-scorched, his own spirit worn thin by years in the House of Stone—brought him water from the Nile, shared his meager rations, and stood guard over him in the suffocating dark.

Then, on the fourth morning, Feversham woke to clarity. The hard blue sky stretched above him, the dust of the prison yard clung to his skin, and the face bending over him was no nightmare specter but Trench himself—alive, real.

Feversham exhaled, a slow, shuddering breath. "Omdurman," he murmured. "I’m actually here."

To Trench, the words sounded like madness—or worse, a cruel joke. But the relief in Feversham’s voice was unmistakable. He had *wanted* this. The prison was his destination.

"You’ve been here three years," Feversham said, scanning the thorn-walled zareeba, the skeletal figures shuffling in chains.

"Three years," Trench echoed. His voice was flat, but his fingers tightened around the bowl of water between them. "A thousand nights in that tomb."

Feversham sat up too quickly, his head swimming. "We must go to the Nile."

"Not so loud." Trench cast a wary glance at the other prisoners. "Idris has spies. Speak softly—or not at all."

They waited until the gates opened and the food-bearers entered, sparking a frenzy of elbows and fists. Amid the chaos, a boy slipped through, empty-handed but whispering in Greek. A gazelle skin hidden beneath his robe fell at Feversham’s side. Inside: a chicken. A feast.

An hour later, they walked toward the river, slow steps weighed down by weakness and chains.

"We can talk here," Trench said, eyes on the water. The gaolers were distant, the Nile’s babble drowning their words. "Why did you come?"

Feversham hesitated. "I was captured on the Arbain road. But you already know that."

"I know more," Trench said, still not looking at him. "You came because of a white feather. Because of *her*."

Feversham went very still. "What else did I say?"

"Enough." Trench exhaled. "I didn’t realize she was there that night. That she gave you a fourth feather. I’m sorry for that."

Feversham shook his head. "Don’t be. It kept me going. Without it, I might have given up in Suakin. Without it, I wouldn’t be here."

Trench turned then, his sunken eyes searching Feversham’s face. "You have a plan."

"More than a plan. Camels wait ten miles west. Rifles, water, provisions buried beside them. From there, we ride to Metemneh, then Berber, then Suakin."

Trench’s breath caught. "When?"

"When I can sit a horse for ten miles. When you can ride a camel for a week. Soon."

A pause. Then Trench laughed—a sound rusty from disuse. "Idris will come to you soon. He’ll preach about your soul and his starving children. Give him money. Ask to sleep outside the prison. Then, in a few nights, ask for me to join you."

It unfolded exactly as Trench predicted. Idris arrived, full of holy warnings and thinly veiled greed. Feversham paid. That night, he slept under open sky. Two nights later, Trench lay beside him.

Stars blazed overhead, indifferent to the muffled cries from the House of Stone.

"Three more days," Feversham murmured.

Trench exhaled. "I want to die at home. Not here. In my village, under trees I know."

Feversham didn’t laugh. "I understand."

They spoke then of England—of trout streams and theaters, of books left half-read years ago. The constellations wheeled above, unheeded.

"Three more days," Trench repeated.

"Only three," Feversham agreed.

And then, for a little while, they were neither in Omdurman nor England, but somewhere beyond, where the stars did not watch and the chains did not weigh. They slept.

Those words lingered on their lips as sleep took them. But dawn brought fever—a fire in Trench’s veins, a delirium that left him writhing by midday. The years in the House of Stone had exacted their toll, and now, at the brink of freedom, his body rebelled.

*"It’s nothing,"* Feversham insisted, grinding tamarinds between his fingers as Ibrahim shook his head.

*"Umm Sabbah,"* the old man muttered—the typhus that struck on the seventh day and left corpses in its wake.

Feversham refused to believe it. *"He won’t die here. Not like this."* But beneath his defiance, another fear festered: *Would the camels wait?*

Each morning, he glimpsed Abou Fatma by the Nile, robed in blue, signaling patiently. Each morning, Feversham gave no reply.

---

Trench woke swaddled in camel-cloth, his mind clear for the first time. The shadow of the prison wall stretched black across the sand.

*"Too long,"* Feversham said, crouching beside him, his voice raw from unloading cargo under the lash. *"Abou Fatma’s gone. The camel men wouldn’t wait."*

Trench barely flinched. The fever had drained him of despair. *"Then it’s over."*

*"Deferred,"* Feversham corrected. He pulled a scrap of paper from his robe—charcoal ink on stolen parchment. *"I wrote to a Greek in Wadi Halfa. Abou Fatma will return."*

Trench’s gaze drifted to a mound of fresh earth in the corner. *"What’s that?"*

Feversham knew. But he said nothing.

Mud bricks stacked twelve feet high, smooth as glass, sealing them from the world. Trench covered his face. *"We’ve built our own tomb."*

Feversham clenched his fists. *"Six months. What’s six months after years?"*

Feversham’s pulse roared. *Sutch. Here.*

A month later, the matchbox fell at their feet. *"Tomorrow. By the storehouse."*

The north wind cut sharp and cold across the desert as the camels surged forward, their pace quickened by the biting air.

"Faster," Trench urged through gritted teeth. "Idris may already know we're gone."

Feversham glanced back toward the distant glow of Omdurman. "Even if he does, it will take time to muster a pursuit. And it’s dark—they won’t move swiftly." His voice was steady, but his eyes kept returning to the horizon, half-expecting the glint of spears in the moonlight.

Trench did not look back. His gaze remained fixed ahead, though his mind raced with visions of the town behind them—the narrow alleys, the flickering torches, the shouts of men hunting for escaped prisoners. Fear coiled tight in his chest, but he dared not acknowledge it.

A sudden movement in the darkness ahead made them rein in sharply. Two figures emerged, and Abou Fatma’s low voice cut through the night: *"Instanna!"*

The camels knelt. Fresh mounts were brought forward, their saddles swiftly transferred.

"Are they strong?" Feversham asked as he worked.

"Anafi breed," Abou Fatma assured him. "But hurry." His eyes darted eastward, listening.

Trench’s hands trembled as he searched the ground. "The rifles—where are they?"

"Patience," Abou Fatma murmured, but Trench could not wait. The moment the weapons were unearthed, he clutched one like a drowning man seizing a rope.

"Ammunition?" he demanded.

"Yes. Rifles, revolvers, cartridges."

Trench exhaled, his fingers tracing the barrel. "Now," he whispered, a fierce grin splitting his face. "Now let them come."

They rode hard through the night, the land shifting beneath them—rocky slopes, valleys of soft sand, stretches of barren plain. Each mile stretched endlessly, the horizon taunting them with its unchanging distance. At times, distant figures forced them to detour, to crouch behind rocks with rifles ready.

By dawn, they reached a relay point where fresh camels waited. They pressed on, skirting the Nile’s western edge, the desert vast and empty around them.

Ten miles from Abu Klea, Abou Fatma gestured ahead. "The Berber road. Cross it tonight, and by tomorrow, we reach the river."

*If God wills,* Trench thought, his nerves fraying with every shadow.

They hid through the day, watching caravans pass like specks in the distance. At dusk, they slipped across the road and rode hard for the Nile. By dawn, they crouched behind a low stone wall, the river unseen but near.

Abou Fatma left to scout, returning at nightfall with news: "The boat is ready. But the roads are watched. We must move now."

They crossed under cover of darkness, sinking the boat behind them. Fresh camels waited, and they pressed inland, the terrain slowing them to a crawl.

Days blurred into nights. At Shof-el-Ain, Abou Fatma finally smiled. "We are safe. North to Assouan, west to Wadi Halfa—no more danger."

Trench turned to Feversham, his voice rough with emotion. "Thank you."

Feversham shook his head. "I served myself, no one else."

But Trench thrust out his hand. "Take it. After all this—take it."

Feversham untied a knot in his robe, revealing three white feathers—two small, one large. "Yours?"

"Yes."

"Then you know what to do."

Trench nodded. "No delay."

Feversham tucked the remaining feathers away. "Then we part here. You go to Assouan, then home."

"Home?" Trench echoed. "And you?"

"Wadi Halfa. Alone."

Trench hesitated, then gripped Feversham’s shoulder. "Ramelton. I won’t forget."

Feversham’s gaze drifted northward, as if he could see the distant hills. "Find her. Tell her… it’s done."

Trench rode off, glancing back once, twice—but Feversham stood motionless, already solitary, already gone.

The Southern Cross had vanished from the sky. The six years of waiting, of striving, were over. And yet, as Feversham mounted his camel and turned west, he felt not triumph, but hollow stillness.

The desert stretched before him, vast and indifferent. His work was finished.

But what came next?

The August sun burned high over Ramelton when Harry Feversham crossed the Lennon bridge, his silhouette sharp against the shimmering heat. The years in the desert had etched deep lines into his face, weathered his skin to bronze, and though he rode through the narrow streets of the town, no eyes turned in recognition. He was a stranger now, a ghost from another life.

At the crest of the hill, he turned onto the straight road to Letterkenny, his pace quickening as if chased by memories. The past six years had blurred into shadows, fading with each mile closer to this place. Here, happiness had once been his; here, too, he had known the depths of shame. He reined in briefly beside the church, its spire stark against the pale sky, and wondered—was Ethne still in Ramelton? Was old Dermod alive? What welcome awaited him?

But the road lay empty. Only the call of a lark overhead and the distant cry of a landrail answered him. He shook off the stillness and rode on, the past whispering in his ear with every stride of his horse.

The grove of trees appeared first, then the broken fence, the gate hanging askew. He turned in without hesitation, though the drive was choked with weeds, the house beyond a skeleton of blackened stone. Dismounting, he called out, his voice ringing through the ruins. No reply came. The gardens were wild, the lawns unkempt—abandoned.

Back in Ramelton, he stopped at the inn, handed his horse to the ostler, and ordered lunch. The landlady, eager with gossip, told him of Lennon House’s burning five years past, of Dermod Eustace’s death, of Ethne’s retreat to Glenalla.

“Another gentleman asked after her not long ago,” she added, eyes bright with curiosity. “Burnt as dark as you. A Colonel Trench.”

Feversham said nothing. He flipped through the guest book, saw the name, then mounted again. “I won’t return to Ramelton,” he told her, and rode out toward the hills.

The track to Glenalla wound upward, past scattered cottages and open fields. The village, when he reached it, was quiet—a handful of houses, a church, the lough gleaming in the distance. At the church gate, a collie barked, then, sniffing, erupted into joyous recognition. The dog remembered him.

And then—Ethne.

She stood in the doorway, motionless, her face unreadable until she spoke his name.

“Harry.”

No other words came. They measured the years in silence, the changes etched into each other’s faces. He saw the strength in her, the quiet endurance; she saw the trials carved into his, the resolve hardened by fire.

“It wasn’t all harm,” she said at last. “The years weren’t wasted.”

He thought of her solitude, the weight of time. “I’m sorry.”

She shook her head, held out her hand. “Give it to me.”

For a moment, he didn’t understand. Then he reached into his coat, shook two feathers into his palm—the ostrich plume, and Castleton’s.

“Both,” she said.

He handed them over. She pressed them to her breast. “I have all four now.”

“What will you do with them?”

“Keep them.” Her voice was fierce. “I am proud to keep them.”

They were not dead things to her, not relics of shame or regret, but proof—of courage, of redemption.

She spoke of Trench’s visit, of his confession about the fourth feather. “I don’t hate him anymore,” she said simply.

Feversham nodded. “He’s a good friend.”

A pause. Then, softly, she asked, “Do you remember our drive from the quay? What we spoke of?”

“Friends,” he said. “The ones you recognize at once, as if you’ve always known them.”

“And never lose,” she finished. “I said you couldn’t be wrong about them. Not truly.” She looked down at the feathers, then up at him. “I was young then. I didn’t understand. But I do now.”

Her fingers tightened around the white plumes. Without another word, she turned and walked into the church. Feversham followed.

The quiet of the little church wrapped around Ethne like a cloak. Sunlight streamed through the high windows, painting the rafters in gold, while the murmur of summer drifted through the open door. She settled into a pew near the aisle, her hands folded in her lap, and Feversham stood beside her, his presence both a comfort and a weight.

"I'm glad you remember our drive—what we said then," she began, her voice steady, her gaze fixed ahead. "It matters, you see, because even though I’ve got you back, I must send you away again. You'll be one of those distant friends—the kind absence doesn’t erase."

The words had been rehearsed in silence, night after night, yet now that they were spoken, they left her throat tight. When she had first seen him—alive, whole, returned to her after years of uncertainty—she had nearly faltered. But necessity had hardened her resolve. She would not let emotion betray her now.

"It will hurt," she admitted, "but there’s no choice. You’re home where you belong, and that’s a comfort. But there’s more than that. Colonel Trench told me enough of what you endured that I know we see clearly now. This parting? It’s nothing compared to the first one. That was a chasm. This is just distance."

She paused, letting the stillness settle between them. The truth was solid in her chest: they had been severed once, irrevocably, by shame and pride. Now, though they would not meet again in life, the barrier was gone. He had shattered it himself.

"You waited six years," she said softly. "You can wait a little longer. And so can I—because your victory gives me strength."

Silence. The church held its breath.

To Feversham, her words were a benediction. The years of exile, of gnawing solitude, of pain—they shriveled into insignificance. She had spoken the absolution he had scarcely dared hope for.

"You understand, don’t you?" she pressed when he didn’t answer.

"Yes." His voice was low. "But you must know—I never expected you to wait."

"I didn’t," she said frankly. "When I gave you that fourth feather, I meant it as an end. No half-measures. No lingering ties. You knew that."

He nodded. "I did. But I hoped—if I came back—you might take the feather back. That someday, somewhere, we might..."

"Then there’s no real parting," she interrupted gently. She turned her face to him then, smiling, and in that look, he saw the cost of her resolve.

His throat tightened. "What you must have suffered—"

"Not alone," she reminded him. "I didn’t sleep in the House of Stone."

"But it was my fault."

She shook her head. "No. What’s done is done. And now—there’s something else you should know. I’m to marry Colonel Durrance. Soon."

Feversham absorbed the blow without flinching. "He’s loved you a long time."

"Yes. And he’s blind."

"Blind?" The word struck like a lash. "Durrance? Of all men—"

"Exactly," she said. "Him, of all men. That’s why I must marry him. And why you must go." She drew a breath. "He doesn’t know about the feathers. He knows our engagement ended, but nothing more. If you stayed—if we so much as spoke again—he’d guess. He sees too clearly now, even in the dark."

Feversham understood. "He must never guess."

"No. He has nothing else left."

A long silence stretched. Ethne watched the sunlight retreat from the walls, measuring time.

"You had nothing of mine with you?" she asked suddenly. "In all those years?"

"Only the feather."

"I kept your photograph," she confessed. "All this time."

His breath caught. "You shouldn’t have."

"Perhaps not. But I did."

The last gold faded from the stone.

"What will you do now?" she asked.

"See my father. Then—whatever comes."

She rose abruptly. "Goodbye, Harry."

He mounted his horse, looking down at her. She held out her hand, and he took it, gripping hard before releasing her.

A hundred yards down the road, he turned. She stood where he had left her, watching. Then, without a wave, she walked away, slow and straight-backed, until the gate closed behind her.

He rode on, the weight of irreparable loss pressing down. The past could not be undone. Some wounds never healed. And for Ethne—there would be no reprieve. She would carry the quiet ache of this parting all her days, no matter what the world saw.

Loyalty had saved Durrance. But it would not save her.

The words that shattered Ethne’s careful silence were spoken that evening. She sat alone in the farmhouse parlor, the fire’s glow failing to warm the chill that had settled deep in her bones. Summer’s heat meant nothing now—not when the moment she had both longed for and dreaded had come and gone.

She had been alone in this remote village for so long, but never more so than now. From the day Willoughby had returned, she had counted the hours until Harry Feversham stood before her. And now that half-hour was over, leaving behind an emptiness sharper than she had feared. She had known this would come, had braced herself for the ache of sending him away. But knowing did little to soften the blow.

Dermod’s old collie pressed against her leg, nosing her hand with the quiet understanding of a creature who sensed grief. Ethne bent to kiss his graying muzzle, struck by the thought that he, too, would leave her soon. And then? Years stretching barrenly ahead, until the final release came.

A knock interrupted her thoughts. Durrance had arrived.

She rose, smoothing her skirts, forcing composure into her face. She had chosen her part long ago—she would play it now without flinching.

They spoke of ordinary things: the rebuilding of Lennon House, their future. But then Durrance asked for the *Musoline Overture*.

"Not tonight," she said, then changed her mind. If she faltered in small things, how would she endure the greater trials?

She played, the notes trembling with unspoken sorrow. Durrance stood at the window, his back to her, until the last chord faded. Then he turned.

"I was cruel to ask that of you tonight," he admitted.

"I didn’t wish to play," she answered, setting the violin aside.

"I know. But I needed to be sure."

Her breath caught. "Sure of what?"

"That you took back the fourth feather."

The words struck like a blow. Ethne stared at him, color draining from her face. "How—how could you know?"

"Trench told me of the fourth. The other three I knew long before."

She sank into her chair, stunned. All her secrecy, her careful silence—had it meant nothing?

"You went to Wiesbaden," she whispered. "After Willoughby came. Was that why?"

"Yes. We were both pretending—you, that you didn’t care for Harry; I, that I didn’t see it. Sooner or later, one of us would have failed. I couldn’t let you live thinking two lives were ruined because of you. So I left."

She closed her eyes. "I was the one who failed."

"No," he said gently. "You would have spent years shackled to a man you pitied, while he sat knowing he had stolen your happiness. That’s no compensation for blindness."

Ethne said nothing. The choice was no longer hers.

"You sent Harry away today," Durrance continued. "Twice. Once in the church. Again on your violin." He touched the instrument. "It has been a faithful friend. I heard your farewell—sad, but not bitter. True music never complains."

He turned to leave, but Ethne stopped him. "The oculist in Wiesbaden—did he give you hope?"

Durrance hesitated. "No. But I am not helpless. I can travel. Perhaps one day, I’ll go east—where the world is strange and new."

He left then, carrying the lesson the violin had taught him twice over: true sorrow does not lament. It endures.

The early summer sun bathed the terrace of Broad Place in golden light as two old soldiers sat with their newspapers after breakfast. The rustle of turning pages mingled with the distant hum of the countryside.

The elder of the two, General Feversham, glanced up from his paper. "Osman Digna’s back at Suakin," he remarked. "Looks like there’ll be fighting again."

Lieutenant Sutch barely looked up. "He won’t do much harm," he murmured, setting his paper aside. His gaze grew distant, the rolling English hills replaced in his mind by the scorching plains of Sudan—the white glare of Suakin, the tangled halfa grass, the jagged ridges of Khor Gwob shimmering in the heat.

"A stifling place, Suakin," Feversham mused, stretching his legs. "I heard of an officer who dropped from sunstroke at six in the morning, right through his regulation helmet."

"Dreadful heat," Sutch agreed. "But I was glad to be there—very glad." There was weight in his words, a quiet gratitude.

Feversham chuckled. "Ah, ibex hunting, was it?"

"No," Sutch replied. "The English garrison had shot them all for miles around."

"Ah. Something to do, then?"

"Yes," Sutch said softly. "Something to do."

They lapsed into silence, the rustle of newspapers resuming until a footman brought their letters. Feversham’s eyes gleamed as he sifted through the envelopes. He selected one, hesitated, then reached for his cigar case.

"From Ramelton?" Sutch asked, abandoning all pretense of indifference.

Feversham nodded, taking his time to light the cigar, savoring the anticipation. Only when the smoke curled satisfactorily did he unfold the letter.

"From *her*?" Sutch pressed.

Feversham exhaled slowly. "Yes." He read deliberately, while Sutch drummed his fingers on the table.

"Any news?" Sutch finally burst out.

"They’re pleased with the house now that it’s rebuilt," Feversham replied, turning a page.

"And Harry?"

"Finished the sixth chapter of his war history."

Sutch grinned. "Good. He’ll do it well. He knows the ground, saw the war from the bazaars—the underbelly of it all."

Feversham’s expression softened. "You and I won’t live to read it, Sutch." He paused. "Though you might. You’ve got years on me yet."

Sutch ignored the jab. "Anything else?"

"They’re coming here in a fortnight."

"Good," Sutch declared. "Then I’ll stay."

He paced the terrace, restless, while Feversham frowned at the letter. "You know, Sutch," he said at last, "I never understood it. Did you?"

"Yes," Sutch said simply. Some truths were better left unspoken.

Feversham let it drop. "Ever see Durrance?" he asked abruptly.

"Often. He’s abroad just now."

Feversham’s gaze grew distant. "He came here once, after you left for Suakin. Talked for half an hour. Harry’s best man, after all." He shook his head. "Never understood that either."

Sutch smiled. "I did."

Feversham sighed and turned back to his cigar. The past was a puzzle he no longer cared to solve.

Later, as the sun dipped lower, Feversham stirred. "Have you noticed the date, Sutch?"

Sutch looked up sharply. "Next week. The anniversary of the Redan. And Harry’s birthday."

Feversham’s eyes gleamed. "Why not revive the Crimean nights?"

Sutch leapt to his feet. "Splendid! Can we muster a table?"

Feversham rang for the Army List, and the two bent over it like generals plotting a campaign.

Far from the peace of Broad Place, a man stood at the rail of a steamer in Port Said, listening to the chant of coal-bearers under the flickering lights of the harbor. The clamor of the East rose around him—the braziers flaring on the docks, the shadowed hulls of ships at anchor.

Lieutenant Durrance had returned.

By dawn, the ship was moving through the canal, the desert slipping past in a haze of heat. Kassassin, Tel-el-Kebir, Tamai—the names echoed in his mind like old battle cries. The years of purpose, of action, lived again in memory.

Somewhere to the west, an empire was preparing to fall. He had once vowed to see it done. But the desert had claimed him in its own way.

Still, the ship steamed south. In three nights, though he would not see it, the Southern Cross would rise—a silent sentinel over the lands he had loved.

And so the story closed, not with an ending, but with the quiet turning of a page.
