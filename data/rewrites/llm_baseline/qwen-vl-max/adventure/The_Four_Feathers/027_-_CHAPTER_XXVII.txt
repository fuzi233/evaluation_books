These were the days before the great mud wall rose around the House of Stone in Omdurman. Then, only a thorn fence—thin and brittle—surrounded the prison and its grim courtyard. It stood on the eastern edge of the town, a place so foul and desolate it seemed to mock the very idea of civilization. Not a single flower bloomed within its walls. No grass grew, no tree offered shade. Only a barren, sun-baked plain stretched beyond, scorched by the relentless heat. Upon it sprawled a ragged city of hovels, crawling with disease and vermin, breathing out decay.

Between the prison and the Nile, no buildings stood. During daylight hours, prisoners were allowed to shuffle in chains down the half-mile slope to the riverbank—to fetch water, to wash, to endure their daily rites. For natives or blacks, escape was not impossible. Along the shore, dhows bobbed at anchor; river traffic had its harbor there, and the broad foreshore served as a bustling market. The space between the river and the prison teemed with life—crowds swarmed, friends met, plans were whispered, and sometimes a captive slipped into the throng and vanished toward the blacksmith’s shop, where iron could be bought for a price, and risk meant little.

Chains were common in Omdurman. Slaves wore them like clothing. The clink of iron echoed through every street of that long, brown, treeless city. But for a European prisoner, escape was another matter entirely. There were few white men in captivity, and each was marked, watched, hunted. To flee required camels hidden across the desert, money, months of secret planning—and above all, a native willing to risk his life. Camels might be arranged, but drivers often deserted. Preparations could be ruined by a sudden flogging—just suspicion of hidden wealth enough to bring the lash down. And even the most loyal servant might falter when the moment came.

Colonel Trench began to lose hope. He knew his friends were trying. Sometimes the boy who brought his food would nod silently, or whisper: *Be ready.* At times, during parades of the Khalifa’s troops, when he was paraded as a trophy of conquest, a man would brush against his camel and murmur encouragement. But nothing ever came of it. Each day, he watched the sun rise over the bend of the Nile behind the tall palms of Khartum, burning across the sky, and the months passed like dust in the wind.

One evening in late August—just after Durrance returned from the Sudan, blind and broken—Trench sat in the corner of the enclosure, staring westward as the sun sank toward the plain. His heart pounded with anticipation, though not of freedom. It was the dread of nightfall that gnawed at him. For however unbearable the heat and weight of the day, it was nothing compared to what came after dark.

As twilight fell, Idris es Saier—the towering negro of the Gawaamah tribe—appeared with his guards.

“Into the House of Stone!” he bellowed.

With curses and prayers, the prisoners surged forward, driven by whips that cracked without mercy over the backs of those lagging behind. They jostled and stumbled toward the narrow entrance of the prison. Inside, thirty captives already lay on the wet mud floor—some dying, others barely conscious. Two hundred more would be forced in, packed like cattle until morning. The room was thirty feet square, with a solid pillar at its center supporting the roof. No windows existed—only a few tiny holes near the top, pretending to let air in. Into this stinking, suffocating chamber they were herded, screaming, fighting, clawing at one another. The door slammed shut. Darkness swallowed everything. A man could not see the shape of the head beside him.

Trench fought like a beast. One corner near the door was his target—a spot that might shield him from blows, from trampling feet, from the grinding weight of shackles. If he could hold it, he might survive the ten endless hours of darkness and suffocation. But there was a constant fear in his mind, a poison that twisted his thoughts:

He had seen it happen. Once a man went down in the crush, he never rose again. He was crushed beneath hundreds of feet, flattened like clay. Trench was small. He fought with his elbows, his knees, his teeth. He kicked with his fetters, tore at clothes, dug under arms, burrowed between bodies. He used his nails, his fists, even swung the chain from the ring around his neck. At last, he reached the corner, drenched in sweat, gasping. He would defend it now, hour after hour.

“If I were to fall!” he choked. “O God, if I were to fall!” He shouted into the din—no less than a shout could be heard—“Is it you, Ibrahim?”

“Yes, Effendi,” came the reply.

Relief washed over him. Ibrahim, a tall Arab from the Hadendoas, was his companion in suffering. Between them, a bond had grown—not of friendship, perhaps, but of necessity. There were no rations in Omdurman. Each prisoner lived on charity or the money sent by friends outside. Trench received occasional sums, smuggled in by a native from Assouan or Suakin. But often, nothing came. Then he ate scraps from Greek converts who claimed faith in the Mahdi—or starved in silence. Ibrahim, too, sometimes had no one to send him food. So they helped each other. At night, they stood side by side against the wall.

“Yes, Effendi,” Ibrahim said, groping in the dark, steadying Trench with a hand.

A violent brawl erupted in a far corner. The crowd swayed with each lunge and retreat, like waves in a storm. Men fought with their teeth, their hands, their chains. Above the shrieks, the clank of iron, and the curses, came sudden sobs—then stifled screams—signs that someone had fallen. Dirt was thrown across the room, flung blindly. Heads collided in the effort to dodge. All of it happened in absolute blackness.

For two hours, Trench stood in that infernal place—ringed by noise, choked by heat. Eight more hours remained before dawn. He stood on tiptoe, lifting his head above the press, but still the air was thick, sour, moist. His throat burned. His tongue felt swollen, dry as a fig. He thought: *No hell could be worse than this—unless fire were added. But fire is already here.*

“If I were to fall!” he cried—just as the door opened.

Idris es Saier stood in the frame.

“Make room!” he roared. “Make room!”

And he hurled flaming tufts of dried grass into the darkness. The flames landed on backs, shoulders, heads. The prisoners couldn’t move. They couldn’t raise their hands. The fire burned them.

“Make room!” Idris repeated. Whips cracked. A path was cleared. A man was thrown inside. The door slammed shut.

Trench stood near the entrance. In the dim light from the doorway, he saw the new prisoner—a slight figure, bent under pain, bound heavily in iron.

“He will fall,” Trench muttered. “He’ll fall tonight. God! If I were to—”

Then the crowd surged again. Curses rose higher.

The newcomer clung to the door, pressing his face against the wood—any breath of air through the cracks was precious. Those behind pulled him away, shoved him back. He became a wedge, driven between men like a hammer drives steel. Finally, he was thrown directly into Trench.

In that prison, kindness died. By day, outside, prisoners shared misery, sometimes aid. But in the dark, survival was law. Pity was weakness. Trench was no different. His only thought was to live—long enough to taste water at dawn, to breathe clean air.

“Back!” he shouted. “Back, or I strike!”

As he raised his arm, he heard the man who had been thrown into him—muttering English.

“Don’t fall,” Trench said, grabbing his arm. “Ibrahim! Help! God, if he were to fall!”

And in the chaos, he caught the sound—his own language. After months of silence, the voice of home. It stirred something deep within him, like the touch of a woman’s hand. It calmed him. It reminded him of things lost.

He couldn’t hear the words clearly—the noise was too loud. But fragments drifted through the din: familiar phrases, once spoken in comfort, now twisted by fever. Images flooded his mind: green meadows, cool skies, rivers sparkling under sunlight. For a moment, he forgot the stench, the heat, the darkness.

Then he felt the man waver. “If he were to fall!” Trench cried.

Ibrahim stepped in. Together, they fought the crowd, clearing space. “Shaitan! They are mad!” the others yelled.

They set the Englishman down. Ibrahim guarded him. Trench crouched beside him.

Now, in a quiet moment, Trench could hear the words.

“They’re telling me about the sea,” the man murmured. “I saw the riding lights of yachts—how they shimmered, shortened, lengthened… there was music, too, as we passed the pier. What was it playing? Not the overture… I can’t remember any tune…” He laughed, crazily. “I’ve always been bad at music, haven’t I? Except when you played…”

He spoke of hills, woods, Bray—“a fairyland of lights”—and Dublin Bay. He remembered lunching there, just the two of them, before everything changed. “It seemed strange to leave you behind… among the hills… strange and not quite right…”

His voice trailed off. He lifted an elbow, fumbled in his breast pocket.

“Yes, the things to be done,” he mumbled.

Trench held him up. The heat was unbearable. The noise continued—shouts, moans, songs sung in madness, men dancing in chains. But beside Trench, a man who had sailed from Dublin Bay now lay dying in a foreign hell.

The memory of the sea, the salt wind, the ship’s bow cutting through water—it overwhelmed Trench. Practical, unimaginative as he was, he nearly wept.

Then the stranger spoke again.

“It’s funny—those three faces. Always the same. The man in the tent with the lancet… the man in Piccadilly… and mine. Funny. Not quite right. They grow big when I’m falling asleep… come close… won’t go away… frighten me…”

He gripped Trench’s arm, trembling.

“It’s all right, old man,” Trench said, gently. “It’s all right.”

The man relaxed. Then he began speaking again—this time of the future.

“Will you take it back?” he asked, hesitantly. “Really? The others have—all except the man who died at Tamai. And you will too!”

He sounded amazed, as if some miracle had occurred.

“Oh, it hasn’t been the best of times, of course. But I didn’t expect it to be. And at worst… I always had the afterwards to look forward to… supposing I didn’t run…”

Trench listened, intrigued. This was no ordinary delirium. It was a story unfolding—a life, perhaps a tragedy. And the word “afterwards” kept returning—like a theme in music. Was it the key?

The heat intensified. Darkness pressed heavier. The cries softened. Exhaustion took hold.

Trench leaned closer.

“I saw your light that morning,” the man whispered. “You put it out suddenly… did you hear my step on the gravel? I thought you did. It hurt…”

Then he protested fiercely: “No, no—I had no idea you’d wait. I didn’t want you to. Afterwards, maybe… but nothing more. Sutch was wrong…”

Trench frowned. “Feather.” He heard the word. But what did it mean?

Then came the name.

“Durrance.”

Trench froze.

This man knew Durrance!

Not just a fellow countryman—but someone who had known him, fought beside him, perhaps at Tokar or Tamai. A link formed between them.

Trench strained to hear more.

“Durrance stood at the window,” the man said, “after I told them about you, Ethne.”

Trench repeated the name. A woman—Ethne. He didn’t know her. But the voice went on.

“All the time I was thinking of sending in my papers… after the telegram… he stood at the window, back to me, looking across the park. I thought he blamed me. But now I think… he was making up his mind to lose you…”

Trench gasped. His blood ran cold.

He remembered—clearly—Durrance standing at a window, back turned. A telegram arrived—read slowly, painfully. Suspense filled the room. A man spoke of betrothal, of resigning. But surely—surely this wasn’t the same man?

Yet the name Ethne… Donegal… Dublin Bay… the feather…

“Good God,” Trench whispered. “Was it Ethne? Was it?”

Silence. Then the man spoke of a city of mud, a sun that burned, a man lying under a linen robe, drawing a face across three thousand miles—until sunset, when he gathered courage and walked to the gate.

Then—four words cut through the dark.

“Three little white feathers.”

Trench recoiled.

He had written those words. He had devised that message.

“Three little white feathers,” the voice repeated. “This afternoon we were under the elms by the Lennon River—do you remember, Harry?—just you and I. And then came three little white feathers. And the world’s at an end.”

Trench knew.

He knew the girl—Ethne. He knew the place. He knew the name.

“Feversham!” he cried. “Feversham!”

He shook the man. “Under the elms by the Lennon River—”

Images flashed—green shade, golden sunlight flickering through leaves—a mirage in the desert.

Feversham had been there. Under the elms. On the day the feathers came.

And now he was here—in the House of Stone.

But why?

Trench didn’t need to ask.

He knew.
