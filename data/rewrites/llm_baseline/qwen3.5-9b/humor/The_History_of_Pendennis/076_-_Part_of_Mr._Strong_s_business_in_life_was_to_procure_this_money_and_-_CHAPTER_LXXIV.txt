The train hurtled Arthur toward Tunbridge with a speed that felt almost cruel, leaving him little time to catch his breath but ample opportunity to dissect the wreckage of his own life. As the landscape blurred past, he reviewed every circumstance with a grim clarity, acknowledging the tragic destination to which his selfishness and capriciousness had steered him.

"Here lies the end," he murmured to himself, "of all my hopes, my aspirations, my romances, and my ambitions!" He weighed his choices with heavy regret. Whether yielding or stubbornly resisting, he found himself equally unfortunate. When he yielded to his mother's pleas, he refused an angel; when he obstinately rejected her, Laura—who had been thrust upon him anyway—would never have become an angel in his eyes. He realized he could never have given his heart at someone else's prompting, nor could he have truly known Laura's essence if he hadn't been allowed to discover her virtues directly. Conversely, imagine if he had accepted his uncle's advice, taken Blanche on that guarantee, secured a seat in Parliament, wealth, and a grand career. In that scenario, fortune would have arrived, only to leave him with a wife devoid of a dowry—a dowry he had foolishly traded for a heart.

"Why wasn't I more honest?" he asked, stung by the irony. "Or am I simply less so?" His uncle, a poor old man, would have suffered no pang accepting Blanche's fortune from wherever it came. Yet, the uncle was bitter, indignant, and nearly heartbroken by Arthur's scruples. Arthur was the one causing the dissatisfaction. He felt like a maimed, weak, imperfect wretch, utterly incapable of sustaining any fortune. He brought misery to everyone around him. What future awaited that poor, frivolous girl who would inherit his obscurity and share his diminished prospects? He lacked the ambition to stir himself or the self-esteem to console her—or himself—for his failures. Even if he wrote a bestseller running through twenty editions, he'd be the first to sneer at his own reputation. If he succeeded at the bar, achieving riches by bullying witnesses and twisting evidence, would that satisfy his longings? Was that a life worth living?

He cast his gaze at the passengers. He envied the priest opposite, who rarely lifted his eyes from his breviary until the train plunged into the Reigate Tunnel, forcing him to look up. Or perhaps the old gentleman next to him, who glowered with hatred over his newspaper. The priest shut his eyes to the world to focus on the book guiding him to eternity; his neighbor hated him as a monster, a tyrant, a persecutor, fantasizing about burning martyrs and pale, flame-lit faces. Both moved forward with absolute certainty, burdened by their own rigid logics.

"Would you care to see the paper, sir?" interrupted a stout gentleman holding a journal with a fiery article attacking the black-coated priest.

Pen thanked him, took the paper, and resumed his reverie without reading a single line.

"And yet," he pondered, staring blankly ahead, "would you adopt either of those men's creeds, with all their consequences?" He sighed deeply. "Ah, me! Every mortal must bear their own burden, forge their own faith, think their own thoughts, and pray their own prayers. To whom could I possibly explain it all, if I had the mind? Who could comprehend it? Who can judge another's shortcomings, missed opportunities, or the passions that overwhelm reason? Who can measure the extent of truth and justice a neighbor's mind is capable of perceiving? What invisible accidents, terrors of youth, or strokes of bad luck might have diverted the entire course of a life? A single grain of sand can alter a river's path; a thrown pebble can stop it entirely. Who weighs the circumstances, passions, and temptations that determine our good and evil accounts save One, before whose awesome wisdom we kneel, and at whose mercy we seek absolution?"

"This is where it ends," Pen decided. "Today or tomorrow will settle the account of my youth. A weary retrospect, alas! A sad history filled with pages I would dearly love not to revisit. But who hasn't grown tired? Who hasn't fallen? And who escapes that struggle without scars?"

His head dropped onto his chest. The young man's heart bowed humbly and sorrowfully before that Throne where Wisdom, Love, and Pity reside, offering a silent confession.

"What matters fame or poverty!" he thought. "If I marry the woman I've chosen, may I possess the strength and will to remain true to her and make her happy? If I have children, pray God teach me to speak and act truthfully among them, leaving them an honest name. There are no splendors awaiting my marriage. Does my life deserve any? I begin a new phase; may it be better than the last. I pray Heaven!"

As the train pulled into Tunbridge, Pen finished his reflections. He handed the newspaper to his neighbor, exchanged a polite farewell, and noted that the foreign clergyman in the opposite corner remained steadfastly glued to his book. Pen jumped from the carriage, clutching his carpet-bag, resolutely prepared to face his fate.

A fly whisked him rapidly from the station to Lady Clavering's house. During the ride, Arthur composed a speech in his head, intending to address Blanche. It was, in his own estimation, a virtuous, honest, and well-meaning oration—the best a man of his disposition could muster under the circumstances. Its gist was simple: *"Blanche, I fail to grasp the meaning of your last letter, nor whether my fair and frank proposal remains acceptable. You likely know why I must forego the worldly advantages a union with you offers—advantages I could not accept without feeling dishonored. If you doubt my affection, I am ready to prove it. Summon Smirke, and let us marry immediately. With all my heart, I promise to keep my vow, cherish you through life, and be a true, loving husband."*

Upon arriving at the hall door, Arthur was greeted by a domestic he didn't recognize. The man appeared surprised by the arrival of a gentleman carrying a carpet-bag but made no move to take it.

"Her Ladyship is not at home, sir," the man announced.

"I am Mr. Pendennis," Arthur replied. "Where is Lightfoot?"

"Lightfoot has gone," the servant answered. "My Lady is out, and my orders were..."

"I hear Miss Amory's voice in the drawing-room," Arthur interrupted, spotting the source of the melody. "Take the bag to a dressing room, please."

Ignoring the porter, he marched straight toward the apartment. As the door swung open, a warble of melodious notes floated out. Their Little Siren was at the piano, pouring her soul into the instrument with all her might and fascination. Master Clavering slept soundly on the sofa, indifferent to the music. Seated beside Blanche was a gentleman completely entranced by her performance—a piece of passionate, melancholy beauty.

At the sound of the opening door, the stranger startled upright with a "Hullo!" The music cut off abruptly with a small shriek from the singer. Frank Clavering woke from the sofa, and Arthur stepped forward, greeting the intruder warmly.

"Well, Foker! How do you do, Foker?"

Arthur glanced at the piano. Beside Miss Amory lay exactly the same purple-leather box he had seen in Harry's hand three days prior, outside a jeweler's shop in Waterloo Place. It was open, revealing a magnificent serpentine bracelet coiled around a white satin cushion, topped with a blazing ruby head and a diamond tail.

"How de-do, Pendennis?" Foker asked.

Blanche made several shrugging motions, signaling unrest and agitation. She covered the bracelet with her handkerchief, then rose, her hand trembling violently, to greet Pen.

"How is dearest Laura?" she asked.

The sight of Foker's face—looking up from his profound mourning with an expression both pitiful and bewildered—was something the reader must imagine for themselves. So too was the face of Master Frank Clavering, who surveyed the three figures with an air of supreme knowingness, managed to exclaim, "Here's a jolly go!" before vanishing with a snicker.

Pen had held his composure until that moment. But seeing Foker, whose ears and cheeks burned with shame, Arthur suddenly erupted into a fit of wild, loud laughter that terrified Blanche far more than any solemn display could have.

"And this was the secret, was it? Don't blush and turn away, Foker, my boy. Why, man, you are a pattern of fidelity. Could I stand between Blanche and such constancy? Could I stand between Miss Amory and fifteen thousand a year?"

"It is not that, Mr. Pendennis," Blanche declared with great dignity. "It is not money, it is not rank, it is not gold that moves me. It is constancy, it is fidelity, it is a whole trusting, loving heart offered to me—that I treasure. Yes, I treasure it!"

She reached for her handkerchief, then paused, realizing what lay beneath it. "I do not disown, I do not disguise. My life is above disguise.
