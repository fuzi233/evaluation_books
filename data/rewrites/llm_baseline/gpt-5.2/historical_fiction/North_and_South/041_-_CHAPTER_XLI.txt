So winter drew on, and the days, though they lengthened by a few grudging minutes, brought no increase of warmth to the heart. February’s pale sun shone as it always had, but in the Hales’ little drawing-room it seemed to fall upon the same quiet furniture, the same neat hearth, without coaxing any spring of hope to rise.

Mrs. Thornton, as might have been looked for, came no more. Mr. Thornton came at times; yet even his footfall belonged now to the passage that led to the study, not to the room where Margaret sat. He spoke with Mr. Hale; his business, tangled and overgrown during the strike, demanded him more closely than it had last year. Mr. Hale, who prized rare things as some men prize costly ones, valued the visits the more for their scantness, and praised the steadiness of Mr. Thornton’s mind. From such fragments as reached her—words carried out by a half-open door, a sentence repeated by her father with a gentle pleasure—Margaret could not even accuse Mr. Thornton of resentment. He neither sought her name nor avoided it; and when it came, it was spoken as calmly as if no sharp thing had ever passed between them.

Margaret could not lift her father’s spirits by the mere buoyancy of her own, as she had done in other days. Long anxiety, broken by storms, had beaten down the natural spring of her temper; now there was a dreary peace, like the hush after a mill-wheel has been stopped, when the ear expects thunder and hears only the thin drip of water. She made herself tasks, as if work could be a fire kindled without fuel: she taught the two younger Boucher children, bringing them to her parlour with their washed faces and clumsy shoes; she bent herself to goodness, and found it hard—hard, because her heart seemed numb to the end of all effort. If she did anything well, it was what she did without measuring it—the quiet tending of her father’s comfort. His smallest wish, scarcely shaped into speech, she forestalled; his moods, like changes of weather in a delicate spring, she felt before they fully came, and met with the ready tenderness that asked no thanks.

March brought tidings from Spain: Frederick’s marriage. He and Dolores both wrote. Dolores’s English went with a pretty, timid stumbling—Spanish words and turns of phrase slipping in as naturally as the scent of orange-blossom might cling to a garment carried across seas. Frederick, too, wrote with little inversions, little alien idioms, as if his very tongue were learning her country’s shape. Their letters had, to Margaret and her father, the charm of something young and careful; Dolores was evidently anxious, in every erased line and altered spelling, to please the unseen English relations of her husband. With the announcement came a mantilla—black lace, splendid and soft as shadow, chosen by Dolores herself for the sister-in-law she had never seen. Frederick had painted Margaret in such colours—beauty, wisdom, virtue—that the gift seemed like an offering laid down before an image.

Frederick’s worldly position rose too. Barbour and Co., one of the largest Spanish houses, took him in as a junior partner. Margaret smiled once, then sighed, remembering the vehement speeches of her younger self against trade: her preux chevalier turned merchant, trader! Yet even as the thought rose, she rebuked it. A Spanish merchant was not to her mind the same thing as a Milton mill-owner; her old confusions were unjust. Trade or no trade, Frederick was happy—very happy. Dolores must be charming; the mantilla was exquisite. And then Margaret returned to the present, which waited like an unlit room.

Mr. Hale had had, this spring, at times a difficulty in breathing—an oppression that distressed him while it lasted, and then passed away so completely that Margaret would not allow herself to be frightened. Still she longed to see the liability shaken off altogether; and so she urged him—urgently, almost with that quiet obstinacy she could put on when love was anxious—to accept Mr. Bell’s invitation to Oxford in April. Mr. Bell included Margaret too; nay, he wrote a special letter commanding her presence. But Margaret shrank from it as from a duty that asked more than she could give. To remain at home, alone, without the perpetual sense of responsibility, seemed to her like lying down at last after two years of watching.

When her father drove away to the station, the closing of the gate behind the wheels made a sound that startled her—so much had depended upon that daily coming and going of his step. The house seemed suddenly hollowed out. She stood and felt, almost stunned, the vastness of her liberty: no one now leaned upon her for cheerful care, if not for happiness; no invalid required planning; she might be idle, silent, forgetful—and, what seemed worth more than all, she might be unhappy if she liked, without having to hide it as one hides a forbidden thing.

For months she had stuffed her own griefs into a dark cupboard of the mind, and locked them there. Now she had time to take them out and look at them steadily. She sat for hours in the drawing-room, hardly moving, turning each remembrance as one turns over bitter herbs to know their true scent. She made herself face them with an unwincing resolution. Only once did she cry out aloud, stung by the thought of the faithlessness that had bred that abasing falsehood.

Now she would not so much as grant the temptation the dignity of strength. Her plans for Frederick had failed; the temptation lay there like a dead mockery. Seen by the light of what had followed, the lie was not even tragic—it was despicably foolish; and faith in truth appeared, in comparison, infinite wisdom.

In the nervous agitation of the moment she took up a book of her father’s that lay upon the table, opened it at random, and the words that met her eye seemed written for her self-abasement:

“Je ne voudrois pas reprendre mon cœur en ceste sorte: meurs de honte, aveugle, impudent, traistre et desloyal à ton Dieu, et sembables choses; mais je voudrois le corriger par voye de compassion. Or sus, mon pauvre cœur, nous voilà tombe dans la fosse, laquelle nous avions tant resolu d’eschapper. Ah! revelons-nous, et quittons-la pour jamais, reclamons la misericorde de Dieu, et esperons en elle qu’elle nous assistera pour desormais estra plus fermes; et remettons-nous au chemin de l’humilitè. Courage, soyons meshuy sur nos gardes, Dieu nous aydera.”

“The way of humility,” thought Margaret. “Ah! that is what I have missed. But courage, little heart. We will turn back; and by God’s help we may find the lost path.”

She rose at once, with the sudden need to do some work that should take her out of herself. To begin with, she called Martha as the girl passed the drawing-room door on her way upstairs. Martha’s manner was always grave, respectful—obedience crusted over individuality, as if life had taught her to be wooden for safety. Margaret tried gently to find what lay beneath, and found it difficult. Martha answered with short propriety, as if each personal word were a breach of rule. But Margaret touched at last upon the right chord when she named Mrs. Thornton.

Martha’s whole face brightened, as if a shutter had been flung open. With a little encouragement the story came out: her father, in earlier life, had been connected with Mrs. Thornton’s husband—had even once been able to do him a kindness, though Martha scarcely knew what, for she had been a small child then. Later, circumstances had severed the families. Martha’s father sank lower and lower from his first occupation as a clerk in a warehouse. Her mother died. She and her sister, Martha said plainly, would have been “lost” but for Mrs. Thornton, who sought them out, planned for them, cared for them with a steady hand.

“I’d had th’ fever, and were but delicate,” said Martha, the words gaining warmth as she went on. “And Mrs. Thornton and Mr. Thornton too—they never rested till they’d nursed me up in their own house, and sent me to th’ sea and all. Doctor said fever were catching, but they cared none for that—only Miss Fanny, and she went a-visiting them folk she’s going to marry into. So though she were frightened at th’ time, it’s ended well.”

“Miss Fanny going to be married!” Margaret exclaimed.

“Yes; and to a rich gentleman too, only he’s a deal older nor her. His name is Watson; and his mills is somewhere out beyond Hayleigh. It’s a very good marriage, for all he’s got such grey hair.”

At this, Margaret was silent long enough for Martha’s brightness to fade back into the familiar servant’s mask. Martha recovered propriety as if it were an apron she must not crease: she swept up the hearth, asked what hour tea was to be prepared, and left the room with the same composed face with which she had entered.

Margaret checked herself from a bad habit that had lately grown upon her—of imagining, whenever she heard anything that touched Mr. Thornton’s circle, how it would affect him; whether he would like or dislike it; whether he would feel it as she felt it. She scolded herself for it, as one checks a finger going to a sore place.

The next day she had the little Boucher children to teach, and afterwards she took a long walk. The evening ended with a visit to Mary Higgins. To her surprise Nicholas was already home; the lengthening light had deceived her into thinking it earlier. He too seemed to have come, in his way, a little nearer to humility; he was quieter, less sharp-edged in assertion.

“So th’ oud gentleman’s away on his travels, is he?” said he. “Little ’uns telled me so. Eh! but the’re sharp ’uns they are; I a’most think they beat my own wenches for sharpness—though m’appen it’s wrong to say so, and one on ’em in her grave. There’s summut i’ th’ weather, I reckon, as sets folk a-wandering. My measter—him at th’ shop yonder—is spinning about th’ world somewhere.”

“Is that the reason you’re so soon at home to-night?” asked Margaret, innocently.

“Thou know’st nought about it, that’s all,” said he, with a contempt that seemed rather against her ignorance than against herself. “I’m not one wi’ two faces—one for my measter, and t’other for his back. I counted a’ th’ clocks in th’ town striking afore I’d leave my work. No! yon Thornton’s good enough for to fight wi’, but too good for to be cheated. It were you as getten me the place, and I thank yo’ for it. Thornton’s is not a bad mill, as times go. Stand down, lad, and say yo’r pretty hymn to Miss Marget. That’s right; steady on thy legs, and right arm out as straight as a skewer. One to stop, two to stay, three mak’ ready, and four away!”

The child—Nicholas’s little boy—stood forth and began a Methodist hymn. The words were far beyond his understanding, full of phrases that fell on his young mind like pebbles on a tin roof; but the swinging rhythm had taken his ear, and he repeated it with an orator’s cadence, as if he were in the House itself. Margaret applauded as gravely as if he had been a trained singer. Nicholas called for another, and yet another—an odd sight, and to Margaret an unexpected one, that the man who had once scoffed should now be led, half-unconsciously, by his child’s chant into a kind of interest in sacred things.

When she reached home it was past the usual tea-time; yet she had the comfort of knowing no one had been waiting for her. The house, for once, asked nothing. She rested and let her thoughts come and go without watching another face to learn whether she must be grave or gay.

After tea she resolved to examine a large packet of letters, and pick out those that were to be destroyed. Among them she found four or five from Mr. Henry Lennox, all relating to Frederick’s affairs. She read them again carefully, at first only to see on how fine a chance her brother’s justification hung. But when she had finished the last, and weighed the pros and cons, a small revelation forced itself upon her—personal, unwilling. The stiffness of the words showed that Mr. Lennox had never forgotten his relation to her in any interest he took in the subject. They were clever letters; she saw that in a twinkling. Yet she missed in them any hearty, genial atmosphere—anything that warmed as well as enlightened. Still, they were valuable; she laid them carefully aside to be preserved.

Then she fell into a reverie; and that night her absent father ran strangely through her mind. She almost blamed herself for having felt solitude—and consequently his absence—as a relief. Yet these two days of quiet had set her up afresh, as a plant is strengthened by a brief shelter from wind. Plans that had lately seemed like tasks now looked like pleasures. The morbid scales seemed fallen from her eyes; she saw her position and her work more truly.

If only Mr. Thornton would restore the lost friendship—nay, if he would but come sometimes to cheer her father as in former days—though she herself never saw him, she felt as if her future life, not brilliant, might yet lie clear and even before her. She sighed as she rose to go to bed. In spite of “One step’s enough for me,” in spite of the plain duty of devotion to her father, there lay at her heart an anxiety, and a pang.

That same April evening Mr. Hale thought of Margaret almost as persistently as she was thinking of him. He had been fatigued by moving among old friends and old places in Oxford. He had exaggerated, in his sensitive fear, the change his altered opinions might make in their reception of him. Yet though some were shocked, grieved, even indignant in the abstract, when they saw the face of the man they had once loved, they forgot the opinion in the person; or remembered it only enough to deepen their tenderness.

Mr. Hale had belonged to one of the smaller colleges; he had been shy, reserved. Those who had cared in youth to penetrate to the delicacy beneath his indecision took him back now with a kind of protecting kindness—almost the manner men use to a woman. After years and an interval of such change, the renewal of that kindliness overpowered him more than any roughness could have done.

“I’m afraid we’ve done too much,” said Mr. Bell. “You’re suffering now from having lived so long in that Milton air.”

“I am tired,” said Mr. Hale. “But it is not Milton air. I’m fifty-five years of age, and that little fact of itself accounts for any loss of strength.”

“Nonsense! I’m upwards of sixty, and feel no loss of strength, either bodily or mental. Don’t let me hear you talking so. Fifty-five! why, you’re quite a young man.”

Mr. Hale shook his head. “These last few years!” he said. Then, after a minute’s pause, he raised himself from his half-reclining position in one of Mr. Bell’s luxurious easy-chairs, and spoke with trembling earnestness.

“Bell! you’re not to think that if I could have foreseen all that would come of my change of opinion, and my resignation of my living—no! not even if I could have known how she would have suffered—that I would undo it—the act of open acknowledgment that I no longer held the same faith as the church in which I was a priest. As I think now, even if I could have foreseen that cruellest martyrdom of suffering, through the sufferings of one whom I loved, I would have done just the same, as far as that step of openly leaving the church went. I might have done differently, and acted more wisely, in all that I subsequently did for my family. But I don’t think God endued me with over-much wisdom or strength,” he added, falling back into his old position.

Mr. Bell blew his nose ostentatiously before answering; it was his way of hiding softness. Then he said—

“He gave you strength to do what your conscience told you was right; and I don’t see that we need any higher or holier strength than that; or wisdom either. I know I have not that much; and yet men set me down in their fool’s books as a wise man—an independent character—strong-minded, and all that cant. The veriest idiot who obeys his own simple law of right, if it be but in wiping his shoes on a door-mat, is wiser and stronger than I. But what gulls men are!”

There was a pause. Mr. Hale spoke first again, as if his thought had gone on walking while the words stopped.

“About Margaret.”

“Well! about Margaret. What then?”

“If I die—”

“Nonsense!”

“What will become of her—I often think? I suppose the Lennoxes will ask her to live with them. I try to think they will. Her Aunt Shaw loved her very well in her own quiet way; but she forgets to love the absent.”

“A very common fault. What sort of people are the Lennoxes?”

“He—handsome, fluent, and agreeable. Edith, a sweet spoiled beauty. Margaret loves her with all her heart, and Edith with as much of her heart as she can spare.”

“Now, Hale; you know that girl of yours has got pretty nearly all my heart. I told you that before. Of course, as your daughter, as my god-daughter, I took great interest in her before I saw her the last time. But this visit that I paid you at Milton made me her slave. I went a willing old victim, following the car of the conqueror. For, indeed, she looks as grand and serene as one who has struggled, and may be struggling, and yet has the victory secure in sight. Yes, in spite of all her present anxieties, that was the look on her face. And so, all I have is at her service, if she needs it; and will be hers, whether she will or no, when I die. Moreover, I myself will be her preux chevalier—sixty and gouty though I be. Seriously, old friend, your daughter shall be my principal charge in life; and all the help that either my wit or my wisdom or my willing heart can give shall be hers. I don’t choose her out as a subject for fretting. Something, I know of old, you must have to worry yourself about, or you wouldn’t be happy. But you’re going to outlive me by many a long year. You, spare, thin men are always tempting and always cheating Death! It’s the stout, florid fellows like me, that always go off first.”

If Mr. Bell had had a prophetic eye, he might have seen the torch already sinking, and the composed angel standing close, beckoning. That night Mr. Hale laid his head upon the pillow where it never more would stir with life. In the morning the servant who entered spoke, and received no answer; drew near and saw the calm, beautiful face lying white and cold under the ineffaceable seal of death. His attitude was exquisitely easy. There had been no struggle—no pain. The action of the heart had ceased as he lay down.

Mr. Bell was stunned; and only recovered his wits enough to be angry at every suggestion of his man.

“A coroner’s inquest? Pooh. You don’t think I poisoned him! Dr. Forbes says it is just the natural end of a heart complaint. Poor old Hale! You wore out that tender heart of yours before its time. Poor old friend! how he talked of his— Wallis, pack up a carpet-bag for me in five minutes. Here have I been talking. Pack it up, I say. I must go to Milton by the next train.”

The bag was packed, the cab ordered, the railway reached in twenty minutes from the moment of decision. The London train whizzed by; it drew back some yards; Mr. Bell was hurried in by an impatient guard. He threw himself into his seat and shut his eyes, trying to make his mind take in the brutal swiftness of it—one alive yesterday, dead to-day. Tears stole out between his grizzled lashes. Feeling them, he opened his keen eyes and arranged his face into severe cheerfulness. He would not blubber before strangers. Not he.

Yet there were scarcely any to see. Only one man sat far from him on the same side. By-and-by Mr. Bell peered, to discover what kind of fellow he might be; and behind the great spread of the Times he recognised Mr. Thornton.

“Why, Thornton! is that you?” cried Mr. Bell, shifting hastily nearer. He shook Mr. Thornton’s hand vehemently; the grip ended suddenly, for his hand was needed to wipe away the obstinate tears that would come at such times. He had last seen Mr. Thornton with Hale.

“I’m going to Milton, bound on a melancholy errand. Going to break to Hale’s daughter the news of his sudden death!”

“Death! Mr. Hale dead!”

“Ay. I keep saying it to myself, ‘Hale is dead!’ but it doesn’t make it any the more real. Hale is dead for all that. He went to bed well, to all appearance, last night, and was quite cold this morning when my servant went to call him.”

“Where? I don’t understand!”

“At Oxford. He came to stay with me; hadn’t been in Oxford this seventeen years—and this is the end of it.”

For more than a quarter of an hour not a word was spoken. The train’s motion made its own relentless speech: iron upon iron, the steady clatter that never grieved, never pitied, driving through the country with the same hard haste whether it carried joy or bereavement. Then Mr. Thornton said, brokenly—

“And she!”—and stopped.

“Margaret, you mean. Yes. I am going to tell her. Poor fellow! how full his thoughts were of her all last night! Good God—last night only. And how immeasurably distant he is now! But I take Margaret as my child for his sake. I said last night I would take her for her own sake. Well—I take her for both.”

Mr. Thornton made one or two fruitless attempts before he could command his voice.

“What will become of her?”

“I rather fancy there will be two people waiting for her—myself for one. I’d take a live dragon into my house to live, if by hiring such a chaperon, and setting up an establishment of my own, I could make my old age happy with having Margaret for a daughter. But there are those Lennoxes!”

“Who are they?” asked Mr. Thornton, with a trembling interest.

“Oh, smart London people, who very likely will think they’ve the best right to her. Captain Lennox married her cousin—the girl she was brought up with. Good enough people, I dare say. And there’s her Aunt—Mrs. Shaw. There might be a way open, perhaps, by my offering to marry that worthy lady!—but that would be quite a pis aller. And then there’s that brother!”

“What brother? A brother of her aunt’s?”

“No, no; a clever Lennox—the captain’s a fool, you must understand; a young barrister who will be setting his cap at Margaret. I know he has had her in his mind these five years or more; one of his chums told me as much; and he was only kept back by want of fortune. Now that will be done away with.”

“How?” asked Mr. Thornton, too eagerly curious to be aware of the impertinence.
