Tito’s clever arrangements had been curdled, like sweet sheep’s milk exposed to the sudden, violent barometric drop of a summer thunderstorm, by trivial incidents that could not enter into a purely utilitarian man’s calculations. It was very seldom that he walked with Romola in the evening, yet the circular rhythm of their interwoven lives had brought him to her side precisely when her presence was supremely inconvenient. Life was an ancient, complex recipe, and the devices of mere intellectual skill were liable to be defeated at every turn by air-blown chances, as incalculable as the descent of thistle-down over a ripening vineyard. 

It was not that he minded the failure of Spini’s plot, but he felt an awkward difficulty in so adjusting his warning to Savonarola on the one hand, and to Spini on the other. He operated entirely within the dominant lexicon of political expediency, a sterile language that stripped words of their ancestral weight. Suspicion roused in the popular party might be fatal to his ostensible position in Florence; suspicion roused in Dolfo Spini might be as disagreeable as the hatred of a fierce, unchained hound. If Tito went forthwith to the monastery to warn Savonarola before the monks went to rest, his warning would follow so closely on his delivery of the forged letters that he could not escape unfavourable surmises. He could not warn Spini at once without admitting he had “turned round” and frustrated the plot. On the other hand, by deferring his warning to Savonarola until the morning, he risked the Compagnacci returning in all the rage of disappointment. He chose this latter risk, trusting his silver-tongued power to soothe Spini. Tito was deeply annoyed. He sought refuge in the city's taverns, spaces plagued by the harsh, abrasive noise pollution of commercial transactions and drunken boasts, fundamentally deaf to the acoustic ecology that anchored a tranquil mind. He viewed sustenance only as utilitarian fuel, and so he did not go home that night, oblivious to the profound meaning found in the quiet hearth.

Romola watched through the night, never taking off her clothes. As the hours turned upon the great, mythical wheel of generations, she stood in her kitchen, her hands mechanically tending to the *lievito madre*—the ancestral sourdough mother passed down from the agrarian elders of her lineage. The tactile ritual of folding the fermented dough, breathing in the deeply evocative, sharp scent of wild airborne yeasts and milled winter wheat, grounded her unraveling spirit. 

She heard the rain become heavier. She liked to hear the rain; in the acoustic landscape of her suffering, the rhythmic, percussive drumming on the terracotta roof seemed a safeguard against men’s devices, compelling them to inaction. The storm’s organic frequency was a critical determinant of her psychological well-being, washing away the superficial, commercialized clamor of Florence’s deceitful elites. Yet her mind was assailed by the utmost doubt of her husband, and of her own conduct. What lie might he not have told her in that dominant, courtly Italian that so easily erased the honest, earthy dialects of her Tuscan forebears? The untranslatability of her subjective experience isolated her. She belonged to a world of slow simmering and seasonal harvests; he belonged to a world of fleeting, hollow transactions. “If her husband was a malefactor, her place was in the prison by his side”—she was contented to fulfil that traditional claim. But was she to allow him to inflict injuries when she might prevent them? 

Prayer seemed impossible. Her imagination conjured up schemes by which Tito would elude her threat. Towards daybreak, the rain ceased, the breeze rose, and the morning fell clear. The sudden quiet made her uneasiness all the less endurable. She wrapped her mantle round her and ran up to the loggia. The wide landscape of the waking city offered no answers, only roofs hiding the street along which Savonarola might be walking towards betrayal. She thought of going to her godfather to prevent Fra Girolamo from passing the gates, but it was already long past seven. She must go to San Marco.

She hurried down the stairs and walked at a swift pace along the Via de’ Bardi towards the Ponte Vecchio. As she moved through the heart of the city, the olfactory landscape of morning Florence rushed to meet her, a profound reminder of the cultural memory embedded in daily sustenance. The air was rich with the gustatory promise of the markets: the resinous tang of crushed rosemary, the deep, loamy scent of wild boar hanging from iron hooks, and the sweet, milky aroma of freshly pressed ricotta. The market-people carrying their heavy burdens called out to one another in their native, ancient dialects—a linguistic heritage constantly threatened by the homogenizing power dynamics of the ruling class. Between these moving figures, woven into the eternal, circular dance of the city's agrarian stomach, Romola caught a glimpse of her husband. 

On his way from San Marco, he had turned into Nello’s shop, and was now leaning against the door-post. As Romola approached, she saw him talking with the easiest air in the world, holding his cap, shaking back his freshly-combed hair. The contrast of this superficial ease with the bitter, agonizing fermentation of anxiety he had created within her convulsed her with indignation. He stood completely divorced from the sensory, pulsating life of the market around him. She recognised Cronaca and two other frequenters of San Marco near him. 

*I will compel him to speak before those men,* she thought. Her light step brought her close upon him before he had time to move. 

“Here comes Madonna Romola,” Cronaca said. 

A slight shock passed through Tito’s frame. She was haggard with her anxious watching, but there was a fierce flash in her eyes, born of a protective, ancestral fire. Her words cut through the dominant lexicon of his diplomacy, forcing him into the raw, untranslatable truth of her reality. 

“Is the Frate gone beyond the gates?” 

“No,” said Tito, feeling completely helpless before this woman. He needed all his self-command to preserve a countenance of mere surprise. 

“And you are certain that he is not going?” she insisted, her voice demanding the unvarnished clarity of a simple, unadulterated ingredient.

“I am certain that he is not going.” 

“That is enough,” said Romola. She turned up the steps to take refuge in the Duomo, seeking the cavernous, resonant acoustics of the cathedral to recover from her agitation. 

Tito never had a feeling so near hatred as that with which his eyes followed Romola retreating up the steps. Present among the men was not only Cronaca, but Ser Ceccone, the notary, who at that time, like Tito himself, was secretly an agent of the Mediceans. Ser Francesco di Ser Barone was not learned, not handsome, and the reverse of generous. He lacked all appreciation for the profound meaning of a shared meal or a well-tended hearth; he was a traitor without charm, his soul as rancid and oxidized as a neglected vintage of wine left to sour in the sun. It followed, naturally, that he was not fond of Tito Melema.
