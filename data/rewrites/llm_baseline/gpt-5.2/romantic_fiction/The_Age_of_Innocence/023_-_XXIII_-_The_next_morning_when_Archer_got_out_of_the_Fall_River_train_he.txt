The next morning, when Archer stepped down from the Fall River train, Boston rose up around him like a kettle left too long to simmer: the air thick, the light white and hard, the whole city sweating through its clothes. Near the station the smells struck first—beer yeasting sour in open-door saloons, coffee burned and re-brewed, fruit gone soft in barrels and carts, the sweet rot of bruised skins. Men in shirtsleeves moved with the unselfconscious sprawl of people who share hallways and wash-basins; the clatter of wheels on stones and the metallic cough of a distant streetcar seemed to scrape at his temples. Even the sound had heat in it—flat, relentless, without the softening hush of trees.

He took a cab to the Somerset Club and ordered breakfast as if order itself could keep his mind from running ahead. He began with melon—cool, faintly musky, the knife passing through it with a wet whisper—then waited for toast and scrambled eggs, letting the paper lie open before him while his attention kept darting to the door, to the clock, to the way the waiters’ shoes slapped the floor. In the fashionable streets too there was an untidy, domestic air: caretakers in calico stationed on rich doorsteps, the Common looking like the aftermath of a picnic where the music had stopped but the trampled grass still remembered it. It was the kind of city where sound didn’t echo so much as sprawl—voices, harness-rings, a tinny barrel-organ somewhere forcing a tune into the heat.

To imagine Ellen here, in this slackened Boston, felt like trying to set a bright, sharp spice into boiled water: the flavor wouldn’t take. Yet a new energy had been in him since the night before, since he had told May—almost lightly—that he had “business in Boston” and would go on to New York the following evening. He had let the office letter, conveniently placed where fate could wink at him, do the work of explanation. The ease of it made him briefly ashamed; it had the glibness of other men’s contrivances. But he had no patience for looking too closely at himself. He smoked a cigarette, scanned the Commercial Advertiser, exchanged the usual greetings with men he knew, and felt, with a strange lucidity, that he was still inside the same world while somehow having slipped through the net of its hours.

At half-past nine he went to the writing-room and wrote a few lines. The pen scratched, the paper rasped under his hand; the familiar instruments of communication looked suddenly crude, like tools meant for coarser work than what he was trying to do. He ordered a messenger to take a cab to the Parker House and wait for the answer, then sat with another newspaper spread before him and began to calculate—absurdly—how long a cab would take.

“The lady was out, sir,” a waiter said at his elbow.

“Out?” Archer repeated, and the word tasted foreign in his mouth, as though he had never learned its meaning in the dialect of his own life.

He rose, went into the hall, found hat and stick, and walked out as if pushed. It could not be true—out at that hour? Why had he not sent the note the moment he arrived? The city, a minute ago familiar, became vast and empty, a place with too much air and not enough direction. He started across the Common toward the Parker House, and the noises thinned under the trees into a softer weave: leaves ticking in a faint stir, a distant hoofbeat, a few voices muffled by shade.

On the first bench under a tree she was sitting.

A grey silk sunshade made a small, disciplined shadow above her head—grey, not pink; he could not imagine now how he had ever dressed her in any brighter color. She sat as if she had nothing else to do, her profile drooping with the heat, her hair knotted low at the nape under her dark hat. The glove on the hand that held the parasol was long and faintly wrinkled, as though the day itself had creased it. The whole attitude was listless, yet contained, like a candle that has learned to burn without flicker.

He came nearer. She turned and looked at him.

“Oh,” she said; and for a second there was a startled sharpness in her face, as if some long-expected sound had been heard at the wrong time. Then it softened into a slow smile of wonder and content, as though she had stepped into a remembered room.

“Oh,” she murmured again, and without rising made a place for him on the bench.

“I’m here on business—just got here,” Archer heard himself explain, and, not knowing why, he began to pretend astonishment at finding her. “But what on earth are you doing in this wilderness?”

The words sounded foolish the moment they were spoken, as if flung across distances too great for speech. He had the sensation that if he did not keep talking she would vanish again into the glare.

“I? Oh, I’m here on business too.” She turned so that they were face to face.

The words hardly reached him as meaning; what reached him was her voice. He had not remembered it at all—low-pitched, with a faint roughness on certain consonants, like the grain in a wooden spoon when it strikes a bowl. Nothing of it had remained with him, as if memory, for all its tyranny, could not translate that particular sound into anything it could keep.

“You do your hair differently,” he said; and his heart thudded as though he had crossed some line.

“Differently? No—it’s only that I do it as best I can when I’m without Nastasia.”

“Nastasia; but isn’t she with you?”

“No; I’m alone. For two days it was not worth while to bring her.”

“You’re alone—at the Parker House?”

A flash of her old malice lit her eyes. “Does it strike you as dangerous?”

“No; not dangerous—”

“But unconventional? I see; I suppose it is.” She considered a moment, the parasol’s shadow shifting on her cheek. “I hadn’t thought of it, because I’ve just done something so much more unconventional.”

The irony lingered faintly, like a spice tasted after the mouth has swallowed.

“I’ve just refused to take back a sum of money—that belonged to me.”

Archer sprang up and moved away a step or two, as if the bench had burned him. She had furled her parasol and sat absently drawing little patterns in the gravel with its tip. The small rasping sound made him think of a knife on a plate.

He came back and stood before her.

“Some one—has come here to meet you?”

“Yes.”

“With this offer?”

She nodded.

“And you refused—because of the conditions?”

“I refused,” she said after a moment.

He sat down again beside her, as if sitting were the only way to keep his mind from swaying.

“What were the conditions?”

“Oh, they were not onerous: just to sit at the head of his table now and then.”

Silence fell. It had weight; it pressed his ribs in. His heart shut itself in that queer, obstinate way it had, and he groped vainly for words, as if the language he had inherited did not have a vocabulary for the reality before him.

“He wants you back—at any price?”

“Well—a considerable price. At least the sum is considerable for me.”

He paused, circling the question he could not avoid.

“It was to meet him here that you came?”

She stared, then laughed—a quick release. “Meet him—my husband? Here? At this season he’s always at Cowes or Baden.”

“He sent some one?”

“Yes.”

“With a letter?”

She shook her head. “No; just a message. He never writes. I don’t think I’ve had more than one letter from him.”

The mention brought color to her cheek, and Archer felt his own face grow hot, as if the air had thickened again.

“Why does he never write?”

“Why should he? What does one have secretaries for?”

She said it as if it were an innocent word, emptied of its history. Archer felt the old story—known, unspoken—rise like bitterness at the back of his throat. For a moment the question was on his tongue, but the memory of that one letter Count Olenski had written was too present; he swallowed the impulse and asked instead, carefully:

“And the person—”

“The emissary? The emissary,” she repeated, still smiling, “might, for all I care, have left already; but he has insisted on waiting till this evening… in case… on the chance…”

“And you came out here to think the chance over?”

“I came out to get a breath of air. The hotel’s too stifling. I’m taking the afternoon train back to Portsmouth.”

They sat looking straight ahead at the passing figures. The Common’s sounds went on—the far-off creak of a cart, a child’s high call, the dry percussion of shoes—yet around them there was a pocket of hush, as if the leaves above had decided to shelter this particular conversation.

Finally she turned her eyes to his face. “You’re not changed.”

He wanted to answer: I was, till I saw you again. Instead he stood abruptly and looked about at the sweltering park, at the trampled grass and the hot shimmer beyond the trees.

“This is horrible,” he said. “Why shouldn’t we go out a little on the bay? There’s a breeze; it will be cooler. We might take the steamboat down to Point Arley.”

She glanced up at him, hesitating, and he hurried on, as if speed could keep the world from catching them. “On a Monday morning there won’t be anybody on the boat. My train doesn’t leave till evening—I’m going back to New York. Why shouldn’t we?” He leaned toward her, the words breaking out of him. “Haven’t we done all we could?”

“Oh,” she murmured again—on the same note of reluctant yielding.

She rose, opened her sunshade, and looked around as if to consult the scene and confirm the impossibility of staying in it. Then her gaze returned to him.

“You mustn’t say things like that to me.”

“I’ll say anything you like—or nothing. I won’t open my mouth unless you tell me to. What harm can it do to anybody? All I want is to listen to you,” he stammered, hating the clumsiness of his own tongue.

She drew out a little gold-faced watch on an enamelled chain.

“Oh, don’t calculate,” he burst out; “give me the day! I want to get you away from that man. At what time was he coming?”

Her color rose again. “At eleven.”

“Then you must come at once.”

“You needn’t be afraid—if I don’t come.”

“Nor you either—if you do. I swear I only want to hear about you, to know what you’ve been doing. It’s a hundred years since we’ve met—it may be another hundred before we meet again.”

She wavered, her anxious eyes searching his. “Why didn’t you come down to the beach to fetch me, the day I was at Granny’s?” she asked.

“Because you didn’t look round—because you didn’t know I was there. I swore I wouldn’t unless you looked round.” The confession sounded childish as soon as he said it, and he laughed.

“But I didn’t look round on purpose.”

“On purpose?”

“I knew you were there; when you drove in I recognised the ponies. So I went down to the beach.”

“To get away from me as far as you could?”

She repeated, low and exact, as if quoting from the only language she trusted: “To get away from you as far as I could.”

He laughed again, this time with boyish triumph. “Well, you see it’s no use. I may as well tell you,” he added, “that the business I came here for was just to find you. But, look here, we must start or we shall miss our boat.”

“Our boat?” She frowned, puzzled, then smiled. “Oh, but I must go back to the hotel first: I must leave a note—”

“As many notes as you please. You can write here.” He drew out a note-case and one of the new stylographic pens, proud, absurdly, of its readiness. “I’ve even got an envelope—you see how everything’s predestined! There—steady the thing on your knee, and I’ll get the pen going in a second. They have to be humoured; wait—”

He banged the hand holding the pen against the back of the bench, like a cook knocking a stubborn bottle to start the pour. “It’s like jerking down the mercury in a thermometer—just a trick. Now try—”

She laughed, bending over the paper laid on the note-case. Archer walked a few steps away, staring with radiant, unseeing eyes at passersby who slowed to stare in their turn at the sight of a fashionably dressed lady writing a note on her knee in the Common. The scene had the innocent absurdity of something out of place, like a fine porcelain dish set down on a park bench.

She slipped the sheet into the envelope, wrote a name on it, and put it into her pocket. Then she stood up.

They walked back toward Beacon Street. Near the club Archer caught sight of the plush-lined herdic that had carried his note to the Parker House. Its driver, having performed this exertion, was bathing his brow at a hydrant as if the city itself had wrung him out.

“I told you everything was predestined! Here’s a cab for us. You see!”

They laughed at the small miracle of finding a public conveyance at that hour in that spot, in a city where cab-stands still seemed an imported notion. Archer glanced at his watch and saw there was time to drive to the Parker House before going to the steamboat landing.

They rattled through the hot streets and drew up at the hotel’s door. The building exhaled its own stale breath—polish, damp stone, and the faint sweet of iced drinks meant to pretend at coolness.

Archer held out his hand. “Shall I take it in?” he asked, meaning the letter.

Madame Olenska shook her head, sprang out, and vanished through the glazed doors.

It was barely half-past ten. Yet Archer’s mind filled the hotel at once with the emissary, seated among travellers with sweating glasses at their elbows, waiting for her with the patience of someone used to being obeyed. Archer began to pace before the herdic, the cobbles jolting the soles of his shoes at each turn. A Sicilian boy with eyes like Nastasia’s offered to shine his boots; an Irish matron thrust peaches toward him, their skins warm and faintly furry, the fruit-scent rising when she shifted the basket. Every few moments the doors opened and hot men in straw hats came out, glancing at him as they passed. It astonished him how often the door swung, and how alike the faces were—lank and weary, round and startled, lantern-jawed and mild—repeating themselves as if stamped from one mould by the day’s oppressive heat and the country’s habit of moving in crowds.

Then, in the stream, a different face flashed—pale too, dulled by heat or worry, but quicker, more conscious, more various, as if several expressions lived in it at once. Archer’s memory caught for an instant on a thin thread and then lost it; the face was gone, doubly foreign in that setting, and the thread snapped and drifted away.

He resumed his patrol, unwilling to stand watch in hand within view of the doors. His sense of time, unaided, became a torment. If she stayed so long, it could only be because the emissary had found her and was holding her with words, with pressure, with whatever vocabulary power uses when it means to be obeyed.

“If she doesn’t come soon I’ll go in and find her,” he said aloud.

The doors swung, and she was suddenly at his side.

They got into the herdic. As it jolted forward Archer pulled out his watch and saw she had been gone three minutes.

The windows clattered loose in their frames, making talk impossible; the city’s hard noises battered at them until the wharf came into view and the water lay beyond like a promise. On the half-empty boat they sat side by side on a bench and found they had almost nothing to say—or rather, what they had to say went better unspoken, passing between them in the relief of being away from walls, away from eyes, away from the thick, listening air of rooms.

As the paddle-wheels began to turn and the wharves and shipping slid backward through the veil of heat, Archer felt the familiar world of habit receding too, as if all the old arrangements were being gently unmoored. He longed to ask her if she felt it: that they were setting out on a voyage from which they might never return. But he was afraid of jarring something delicate in her trust. In truth he had no wish to betray it. There had been nights when the memory of their kiss had burned on his lips; even the day before, driving to Portsmouth, the thought of her had run through him like fire. Now that she sat beside him and the boat bore them out into an unknown stretch of day, the nearness had changed: it was deeper, quieter, like a heat held under a lid. A touch might break it.

The boat turned seaward and a breeze began to move around them. The bay, at first an oily undulation, broke into ripples tipped with spray. Behind them Boston remained under its sultry fog, but ahead lay ruffled water, distant promontories, a lighthouse bright in the sun. The soundscape changed with the air: the steady slap and churn of the paddles, the hiss of water, the rigging’s faint clink, the wind’s thin singing in the rails. Archer felt his mind ease under it, as if the right kind of sound could rinse the day clean.

Madame Olenska leaned back against the rail and drank in the coolness between parted lips. A long veil was wound about her hat, but her face was uncovered, and he was struck by the tranquil gaiety in her expression. She seemed to accept their adventure as a matter of course—neither frightened of unexpected encounters nor, worse, feverishly exalted by the possibility of them. That composure—so unforced, so unadvertised—made the world’s rules seem, for the moment, like a dialect one could step out of without apology.

At the inn where Archer had hoped they would be alone, the dining-room was filled with a strident party of innocent-looking young men and women. Their laughter rang too loudly against the bare boards; forks clinked, chairs scraped, voices rose and fell like a bad band practicing the same refrain. The landlord, proud of his information, told Archer they were school-teachers on a holiday.

Archer’s heart sank. “This is hopeless—I’ll ask for a private room,” he said.

Madame Olenska waited without objection, as if she had long ago learned how to move through the world without wasting strength on what could not be changed.

The room he found opened on a long wooden verandah, with the sea coming in at the windows. It was bare and cool. A coarse checkered cloth covered the table; a bottle of pickles stood like a greenish sentinel; and under a little cage a blueberry pie waited, its dark fruit showing through the crust’s cracks, the faint, homely perfume of baked sugar and berries rising even in that spare place. No more guileless-looking cabinet particulier had ever offered shelter to a clandestine couple. Archer fancied he saw Madame Olenska register the reassurance of its simplicity in the faintly amused smile with which she sat opposite him.

A woman who had run away from her husband—and reputedly with another man—was likely to have mastered the art of taking things for granted. Yet the quiet, unsurprised simplicity of her manner blunted his irony. By being so calm, so unastonished, she brushed away the conventions; and he felt, with a sudden tenderness, that to seek to be alone might be the most natural thing in the world for two old friends who had so much to say—if only there existed a language in which it could safely be said.
