Throughout history, it has been a favored belief that a powerful love possesses the nature of an isolated fate, to which the mind's opinions and habitual resolutions are entirely strangers. Consider Daphnis's frenzy, where it served him little to be convinced of Heraclitus's doctrines; or Tristan's potion-born passion, who, though as profound as Duns Scotus, would have had his reasoning shattered by that cup; or Romeo, seized suddenly by Juliet, where any objections he might have held against Ptolemy made little difference to his discourse beneath the balcony. Yet, not all love is thus, even when potent. Nay, this passion holds as vast a scope as any for allying itself with every operation of the soul; acknowledging effects from the imagined light of unproven firmaments and setting its scale to the grander orbits of what has been and shall be.

On his return to town, Deronda could assure Sir Hugo that he had implanted in Grandcourt's mind a clear understanding: that Deronda could secure fifty thousand pounds by relinquishing a prospect that was likely distant and not absolutely certain. Beyond this, however, he received no further sign of Grandcourt's true disposition regarding the matter than that the younger man remained inclined to maintain friendly communications.

"And what did you think of the future bride upon closer inspection?" asked Sir Hugo.

"I thought better of her than I did in Leubronn. Roulette was a poor setting for her; it brought out something of the demon. At Diplow, she seemed much more womanly and attractive—less hard and self-possessed. I perceived a quite different expression in her mouth and eyes."

"Don't flirt with her too much, Dan," said Sir Hugo, intending to be playfully agreeable. "If you make Grandcourt savage when they arrive at the Abbey at Christmas, it will interfere with my affairs."

"I can remain in town, sir."

"No, no. Lady Mallinger and the children cannot do without you at Christmas. Only, do not make mischief—unless you can stage a duel and manage to shoot Grandcourt, which might be worth a little inconvenience."

"I don't believe you have ever seen me flirt," said Deronda, not amused.

"Oh, haven't I, though?" replied Sir Hugo, provocatively. "You are always gazing tenderly at women and speaking to them in a Jesuitical manner. You are a dangerous young fellow—a kind of Lovelace who will cause the Clarissas to chase you rather than you chasing them."

Why be exasperated by a tasteless jest? Exasperation often precedes reflection on utility. Few friendly remarks are more irritating than the information that we constantly appear to be doing what we never intend to do. Sir Hugo's definition of flirting was, it was hoped, peculiar; for his own part, Deronda was certain he had never flirted. Still, he was relieved that the baronet knew nothing of the repurchase of Gwendolen's necklace to fuel his appetite for such banter.

He resolved to be on his guard in the future, particularly regarding his upcoming visit to Mrs. Meyrick's, where he intended to pay his first call since returning from Leubronn. For Mirah was certainly a creature in whom it was difficult not to display a tender interest, both in look and speech.

Mrs. Meyrick had sent Deronda a report of Mirah's well-being within her household. "We are growing fonder of her every day," she had written. "At breakfast, we all look toward the door expecting to see her enter; we watch her and listen to her as if she were a native from a new land. I have heard not a single word from her lips that casts doubt upon her. She is perfectly contented and full of gratitude. My daughters are learning from her, and they hope to secure her other pupils, for she is eager not to eat the bread of idleness but to work, like my girls. Mab says our life has become like a fairy tale, and her only fear is that Mirah will transform into a nightingale again and fly away from us. Her voice is simply perfect: not loud and strong, but searching and melting, like the thoughts of what has been. That is how old people like myself perceive a beautiful voice."

Yet Mrs. Meyrick omitted details that would have required her to admit that Amy and Mab, who had accompanied Mirah to the synagogue, found the Jewish faith less reconcilable with their desires in Mirah's case than in that of Scott's Rebecca. They maintained silence out of delicacy toward Mirah, for whom religion was too tender a subject to be touched lightly. However, eventually, Amy, a practical reformer, could not restrain a question.

"Excuse me, Mirah, but *does* it truly seem right to you that women should sit behind rails in a separate gallery?"

"Yes, I never thought of anything else," Mirah replied with mild surprise.

"And you prefer seeing the men with their hats on?" asked Mab, cautiously proposing the smallest point of difference.

"Oh, yes. I like what I have always seen there, because it evokes in me the same feelings—the feelings I would not exchange for anything else in the world."

After this, any criticism, whether of doctrine or practice, would have seemed to these generous little souls an inhospitable cruelty. Mirah's religion was woven into the very fiber of her affections and had never presented itself to her as a set of propositions.

"She says herself she is a very bad Jewess and does not fully know her people's religion," Amy remarked when Mirah retired to bed. "Perhaps it will gradually fade from her, and she will pass into Christianity like the rest of the world, if she grows to love us deeply and never finds her mother. It is so strange to be of the Jewish religion now."

"Oh, oh, oh!" cried Mab. "I wish I were not such a hideous Christian. How can an ugly Christian, who is always dropping her work, convert a beautiful Jewess who has no faults?"

"It may be wicked of me," said shrewd Kate, "but I cannot help wishing that her mother may not be found. There might be something unpleasant."

"I don't think so, my dear," said Mrs. Meyrick. "I believe Mirah is fashioned after her mother's pattern. What a joy it would be for her to have such a daughter brought back! But a mother's feelings are not worth reckoning, I suppose"—she shot a mischievous glance at her own daughters—"and a dead mother is worth more than a living one?"

"Well, and so she may be, little mother," said Kate; "but we would rather hold you cheaper and have you alive."

Not only the Meyricks, whose varied knowledge had been acquired through the irregular foraging to which clever girls are often reduced, but Deronda himself, despite his extensive masculine education, had been awakened by Mirah's appearance to the realization that he knew almost nothing about modern Judaism or inner Jewish history. The Chosen People have commonly been treated as a people chosen for someone else's sake; their thinking regarded as something (whatever it may be) that ought to have been entirely different. Deronda, like his neighbors, had viewed Judaism as a sort of eccentric, fossilized form that an accomplished man might dispense with studying and leave to specialists. But Mirah, with her terrified flight from one parent and her yearning for the other, flashed upon him the hitherto neglected reality that Judaism was something still throbbing in human lives, still offering them the only conceivable garment for the world. During the idle excursion he immediately undertook with Sir Hugo, he began to seek out the exteriors of synagogues and titles of books concerning the Jews.

This awakening of a new interest—this transition from the assumption that we hold correct opinions on subjects we neglect, to a sudden care for them and a recognition that our previous views were ignorance—is an effective remedy for ennui, which unfortunately cannot be prescribed by a physician. Deronda carried this change with him, enduring his weeks of lounging all the better. It was during this journey that he first entered a Jewish synagogue at Frankfort, where his party rested on a Friday. While exploring the Juden-gasse, which he had seen long before, he recalled its picturesque old houses; what his eyes chiefly dwelt upon now were the human types there. His thoughts, busily connecting them with past phases of their race, stirred that fiber of historic sympathy which had helped determine certain traits in him worth noting for those interested in his future.

True, when a young man possesses a fine person, no eccentricity of manners, a gentleman's education, and a present income, it is not customary to feel a prying curiosity about his way of thinking or peculiar tastes. He may very well settle into life as an agreeable, clever young fellow without passing a special examination on such matters. Later, when he becomes somewhat slovenly and portly, his peculiarities are more distinctly discerned, and it is considered a mercy if they are not highly objectionable. But anyone wishing to understand the effect of later events on Deronda should know a little more of what he was at twenty-five than was evident in ordinary intercourse.

It happened that the vividness of his impressions often made him more enigmatic to his friends, contributing to an apparent indefiniteness in his sentiments. His early-wakened sensibility and reflectiveness had developed into a many-sided sympathy threatening to hinder any persistent course of action: as soon as he adopted any antagonism, even merely in thought, he seemed to himself like the Sabine warriors in the memorable story—with nothing to meet his spear but flesh of his own flesh and objects he loved.
