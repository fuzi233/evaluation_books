The problem of how to commence this narrative properly has plagued me, leading me down two distinct paths of inquiry. The first involved scratching my head, a futile exercise that yielded no results. The second entailed consulting my daughter, Penelope, a strategy that, while initially chaotic, eventually birthed a novel approach.

Penelope suggested that I chronicle the events day by day, commencing with the moment we received word of Mr. Franklin Blake's impending visit. She argued that anchoring one's memory to a specific date acts as a powerful trigger, allowing recollections to surface with remarkable fidelity. Her only caveat was the retrieval of precise dates, a task she offered to undertake by consulting her personal diary. Having maintained this journal since her schooling, she claimed it as a repository of facts. However, when I proposed an alternative wherein she narrate the tale directly from her records, Penelope reacted with a fierce glare and a flushed countenance. "My journal is for my eyes only," she declared sternly, asserting that no living soul was entitled to its contents. When I pressed her on the meaning of such secrecy, she dismissed my concern with a sharp "Fiddlesticks!" to which I responded with a gentle "Sweethearts."

Adhering to Penelope's original plan, I begin on the twenty-fourth of May, 1848. I was summoned specifically to my mistress's sitting room.

"Gabriel," she said, "there is news that will astonish you. Franklin Blake has returned from abroad. He has been residing with his father in London and arrives tomorrow to stay until next month, intending to celebrate Rachel's birthday."

Had I possessed a hat at that moment, nothing short of profound reverence would have deterred me from hurling it against the ceiling. I had not seen Mr. Franklin since his boyhood, when he lived within our household. As I recalled him, he was the most delightful child imaginable—one who spun tops with precision yet shattered windows with equal frequency. Yet, Miss Rachel, who sat beside me, contradicted my sentiment. "I remember him," she interjected, "as the most atrocious tyrant who ever tormented a doll, and the hardest driver of an exhausted little girl in string harness that England could produce." Summarizing her feelings, she stated, "I burn with indignation, and I ache with fatigue, whenever I think of Franklin Blake."

You may naturally wonder how Mr. Franklin managed to vanish from our sight for the entirety of his adolescence and adulthood. The explanation lies in the unfortunate circumstances of his father. Lady Verinder's eldest sister wed the celebrated Mr. Blake, a man renowned both for his immense wealth and his protracted legal battle. For countless years, Mr. Blake harried the courts, attempting to displace the legitimate Duke and usurp his title. He emptied the pockets of lawyers and sowed discord among the populace regarding his claims. Tragically, both his wife and two of his three children perished before the judicial system finally rejected his pretensions. Upon losing everything, Mr. Blake concluded that the only way to balance the scales with his country was to deny it the honor of educating his son. "How can I trust my native institutions?" he famously asked, "after the manner in which they have treated me?" Compounded by a natural aversion to boys, including his own, the decision was inevitable. Young Franklin was removed from England and placed under the care of German institutions trusted by his father. Meanwhile, Mr. Blake remained in England, diligently working Parliament and publishing an unfinished treatise on the rightful Duke, a document still incomplete to this day.

Thus ends the history of Mr. Blake, Sr., leaving us free to focus on the Diamond, which ultimately brought the tragedy into our home via Franklin.

Far from forgetting us, the boy maintained a correspondence throughout his travels. He sent letters to my mistress, to Miss Rachel, and occasionally to me. Prior to his departure, he had borrowed from me a ball of string, a four-bladed knife, and seven shillings and sixpence—a sum whose color I cannot recall and never hope to witness again. His letters often solicited further loans. Through my mistress, I learned of his progress abroad; after mastering the German educational system, he traveled to France and Italy, becoming a sort of universal genius in their eyes. He dabbled in writing, painting, singing, playing instruments, and composing music, though I suspected he borrowed funds to support these pursuits in the same spirit as his childhood debts. Upon reaching majority, he inherited his mother's fortune of seven hundred a year, squandering it with the speed of water through a sieve. The greater his wealth, the greater his desires; there existed a chasm in Mr. Franklin's pocket that no amount of money could fill. Despite his itinerant lifestyle—"Post Office, Europe—to be left till called for"—he twice resolved to return to England. Each time, some unnamed woman obstructed his path. His third attempt, however, proved successful, as my mistress had previously reported.

By our reckoning, Franklin was twenty-five, a man of noble blood and high courage. By the time of his arrival, you possess as much knowledge of him as I did prior to his descent upon our estate.

The following Thursday was a day of exquisite summer beauty. Anticipating Franklin's arrival at dinner, my mistress and Miss Rachel departed for a luncheon with friends in the neighborhood.

Once they had gone, I inspected the guest chamber, ensuring everything was in order. As Butler and Steward of the house, I also held the keys to the late Sir John's cellar—a responsibility I accepted at my own insistence, finding it irritating to see anyone else manage such treasures. I retrieved some of our esteemed Latour claret, setting it outdoors in the warmth to aerate it before service. Feeling the heat agreeable to both wine and old age, I seated myself in my beehive chair near the back courtyard, intending to bask in the sun.

However, a soft, rhythmic thumping disturbed my tranquility. It sounded like the gentle beating of a drum emanating from the terrace above. Circling around, I discovered three individuals dressed as Indians in white linen frocks and trousers, gazing upward at the house. Each carried a small hand-drum slung across their chest. Following them was a slender, fair-haired English boy bearing a leather bag. Judging them to be wandering conjurers, I approached the leader, who displayed the most refined manners and spoke perfect English. He confirmed my assessment and politely requested permission to demonstrate his arts for the lady of the house.

Though I am not a grumpy elder and usually open to amusement, I am human. My greatest weakness, when I know a silver plate-basket is missing from the pantry table, is an immediate, paranoid alert triggered by strangers of superior appearance. Consequently, I informed the Indian that the mistress was away and gently advised him and his troupe to depart. He bowed gracefully, and they vanished.

Returning to my chair, I succumbed to a half-sleep, drifting into a doze akin to the state of the aged. I was abruptly awakened by Penelope rushing out as if the house were ablaze.

"What do you want?" I asked, startled.

She demanded the immediate arrest of the three Indian jugglers. "Father," she cried, "they know who is coming from London! They intend to cause mischief to Mr. Franklin Blake!"

Her mention of Franklin's name jolted me awake. I implored her to explain herself.

It transpired that Penelope had recently spoken with the lodge-keeper's daughter. Together, they had witnessed the Indians departing, trailed by the small English boy. Suspecting the boy was being abused by the foreigners—though the evidence for abuse was thin, perhaps merely due to his delicate, pale appearance—the girls had crept along the inner edge of the garden hedge. From there, they had observed the foreigners' activities on the road.

Their performance began with scanning the horizon, ensuring they were alone. Then, facing the house, they stared intently. A burst of unintelligible chatter ensued, followed by looks of uncertainty exchanged among themselves. Finally, they turned to the English boy, expecting assistance. The chief Indian, speaking English, commanded, "Hold out your hand."

Upon hearing these words, Penelope confessed her heart nearly leaped from her chest. I privately mused it might have been her corsets constraining her, but outwardly I remarked, "You give me goosebumps."

When the command was repeated, the boy recoiled, shaking his head in refusal. The Indian, however, asked kindly if he wished to be returned to London, abandoned where he had been found—an empty basket in a market, hungry, ragged, and forsaken. This offer apparently resolved the dilemma. Reluctantly, the boy extended his hand. The Indian produced a vial from his bosom, pouring a black substance resembling ink onto the boy's palm. Touching the boy's head and making mystical gestures, the magician commanded, "Look." The boy froze, standing rigid as a statue, gazing into the ink pooled in his hollowed hand.

To me, at that stage, it appeared to be mere juggling accompanied by a wasteful display of ink. I was beginning to drift back into sleep when Penelope's next words revived me.

The trio scanned the road again. The chief Indian addressed the boy: "See the English gentleman from foreign parts."

"I see him," replied the boy.

"Is it on the road to this house, and on no other, that the English gentleman will travel today?" asked the magician.

"It is on the road to this house, and on no other," the boy affirmed.
