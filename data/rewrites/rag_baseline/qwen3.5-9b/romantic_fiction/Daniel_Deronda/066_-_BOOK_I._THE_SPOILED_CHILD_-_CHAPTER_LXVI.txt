Meanwhile, Ezra and Mirah, figures whom Gwendolen had effectively erased from her mental calculus regarding Deronda, found their orbit tightening around him, illuminated with a clarity that cut through the fog of her indifference. Outside, the father Lapidoth had abandoned his daughter at the threshold, seized by a sudden, desperate gamble born of the need to convert mere survival into a wager. Possessing a sum exceeding the cost of immediate starvation, he felt no weight of consequence, no calculation of alternatives. Until total ruin, he had never weighed whether to seek Mirah again or face his son; in that initial panic, he recoiled from Ezra as one might shrink from any cage of social discomfort. Yet, the possession of Mirah's purse was sufficient to exorcise the specter of future want.

The gambling instinct is a tyrant more absolute than bodily hunger, which can be sated by distraction or intellect; the craving to watch the dice roll, to hold the mind in a perpetual suspension between chance and outcome, renders all other excitations inert. In its final, demonic phase, it becomes a joyless dissipation, demons seeking diversion on the burning clay of damnation. Yet even this abstract selfishness demands sustenance. Though Lapidoth's appetite for food and drink was modest, he had slid into a shabby existence where hunger required liquid capital. When, amidst the "Pyramids" advertised on a window-blind, he had doubled, trebled, and then lost Mirah's thirty shillings, he emerged with her empty purse in his pocket. Already, his mind balanced the scales: pawn the purse for an immediate stake, or return it to her with a restored countenance, claiming the funds settled a debt owed to him.

Among the sensibilities that remained potent in Lapidoth was the belief in his own claims—a conviction that his rights to his children's property superseded the justice of his son's resentment. After all, lodging with his offspring was the optimal solution. The more he contemplated meeting Ezra, the less he flinched; his imagination was intoxicated by the prospect of effortless gain, overshadowed by the threat of private humiliation. Luck had recently conspired against him, yet he awaited its reversal, hoping it might manifest through his daughter's affairs or the benefactors she had mentioned. Lapidoth banked on the allure of his own cleverness, an old habit sanctified by early experience; it is not only women who fail to perceive their fading charm or pretend not to be exhausted.

His rapid calculations led him to the small square in Brompton, hoping that pacing and watching might reveal Mirah entering or exiting, thereby smoothing his entry into the house. But evening had fallen—the twilight following the day of his first encounter with her. Weariness soon prompted him to consider ringing the bell, asking for her expected arrival time. Yet, approaching the house, he sensed her presence; he heard her singing. Inside, Mirah sat at the piano, pouring forth *"Herz, mein Herz,"* while Ezra listened with eyes closed. Suddenly, Mrs. Adam opened the door, her voice tight with embarrassment: "A gentleman below says he is your father, miss."

"I will go down to him," Mirah started, turning immediately to glance at her brother.

"No, Mirah, not so," Ezra replied, his decision absolute. "Let him come up, Mrs. Adam."

Mirah stood frozen, fingers pinching her palms, nausea rising in her stomach as she stared at Ezra, who had also risen, visibly shaken. Yet, there was an expression on his face she had never witnessed before: brows knitted, lips hardened with a severity that mirrored the cold fire in his eyes. When Mrs. Adam admitted the father, she could not help casting a glance at the tableau. Glancing from the younger to the elder man, she whispered to herself as she closed the door, "Father, sure enough."

The resemblance was one of outline, striking in its immediacy, though the expression had been carved into stark contrast by hidden, inconspicuous differences that transformed the genius of a Cromwell within the mold of a respectable parishioner. Lapidoth had preened a melancholy demeanor, yet a genuine wince twisted his frame as he spoke, "Well, Ezra, my boy, you hardly know me after so many years."

"I know you—too well—father," Ezra returned, his tone a slow, biting solemnity that rendered the word *father* a reproach.

"Ah, you are not pleased with me. I don't wonder. Appearances have been against me. When a man falls into straits, he cannot act as he pleases... I've suffered enough, I know," Lapidoth said quickly. In speech, he always regained a slickness and hardness. Turning to Mirah, he extended her purse. "Here's your little purse, my dear. I thought you'd be anxious about that note. I've emptied it; I had a score to pay for food and lodging. I knew you'd prefer I cleared myself. Here I stand—without a single farthing in my pocket—at the mercy of my children. You can turn me out if you like, without calling a policeman. Say the word, Mirah; say, 'Father, I've had enough of you; you made a pet of me, spending your all when I needed you, but I can do better without you now.' Say that, and I'm gone, out like a spark. I shan't spoil your pleasure again." Tears welled in his voice before he finished.

"You know I could never say it, father," Mirah answered, her anguish undiminished by the falsity of his words, save for the underlying wish to keep him in the house.

"Mirah, my sister, leave us!" Ezra commanded, his tone authoritative.

She looked at her brother falteringly, beseechingly, awestruck by his resolve, yet unable to depart without pleading for this father who felt like something grown painfully within her own flesh. Moving close, she took his hand, whispering low, though not unheard by Lapidoth, "Remember, Ezra—you said my mother would not have shut him out."

"Trust me, and go," Ezra said.

She retreated, but after ascending a few steps, she collapsed onto a seat, her heart pounding. If her brother's words drove him away, Lapidoth possessed a dim sense of the preparation awaiting him, yet he began to adjust, seeking a viewpoint of cool superiority against any humiliation. This haggard son, speaking from a sepulcher, displayed the incongruity that selfish levity recognizes in suffering, until the unrelenting pincers of disease grasp its own flesh. Any sermon delivered must be accepted as routine, like a passerby ignoring religious wailing in an open cathedral. Lapidoth was not born callous; he had manufactured it.

"This home that we have here," Ezra began, "is maintained partly by the generosity of a beloved friend who supports me, and partly by the labors of my sister, who supports herself. While we have a home, we will not shut you out. We will not cast you to the mercy of your vices. For you are our father, and though you have broken your bond, we acknowledge ours. But I will never trust you. You absconded with money, leaving debts unpaid; you forsook my mother; you robbed her of her child and broke her heart; you became a gambler, and where shame and conscience once sat, now sits an insatiable desire. You were ready to sell my sister—you had sold her, but the price was denied you. A man who has done these things must never expect trust again. We will share our food with you; you shall have a bed and clothing. We perform this duty because you are our father. But you will never be trusted. You are an evil man; you made the misery of our mother. That such a man is our father is a brand on our flesh that will never cease to sting. But the Eternal has laid it upon us; and though human justice were to flog you for crimes, and your body fell helpless before public scorn, we would still say, 'This is our father; make way, that we may carry him out of your sight.'"

Lapidoth, adjusting to the storm, had not foreseen its exact intensity or trajectory—that it would strike not around him, but through him. He could not anticipate this voice from the soul of his son, touching that spring of hysterical excitability Mirah had often witnessed when he sat at home sobbing. As Ezra concluded, Lapidoth threw himself into a chair and wept like a woman, burying his face against the table. Strangely, while this hysterical crying was an inevitable reaction to his son's words, it was also a conscious resource, a tool he had honed since youth to blunt resentment and disapproval.

Ezra sat silent, exhausted by the shock of his own irrepressible utterance, the outpouring of feelings he had carried in solitude for years. His thin hands trembled on the chair arms; he would hardly have found the strength to answer a question, feeling as though he had taken a step toward beckoning Death.

Opening the door, her immediate alarm was for Ezra, rushing to his side to take his trembling hand, which he pressed, finding support in it, though he neither spoke nor looked at her.
