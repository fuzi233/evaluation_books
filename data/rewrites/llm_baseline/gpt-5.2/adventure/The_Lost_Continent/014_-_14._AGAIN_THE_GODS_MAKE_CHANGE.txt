It would weary any hearth-teller to set down, blow by blow, how a scant handful of drilled fighters beat apart a camp that swarmed fifteen to one; the marrow of it was not heroics, but timing, and the taste of fear spreading quicker than fire in dry thatch.

The rebels had trusted their mine as a cook trusts a hidden storehouse—dig under the walls, pour in their numbers, and let the city fall like an overripe gourd. But of all who crawled into that dark throat, none crawled out again. First came the clamor from within Atlantis—sharp, broken, ungoverned—then, as if some giant hand had turned the sound-stone of the world, the cries inside changed their pitch. Panic in the open leaguer heard triumph behind the walls. A rumor is a wind; it finds every crack.

And these rebels, for all their wrongs and their Atlantean courage, had no true leaders left to hold the line. Their belief in each man’s equal voice made them a chorus with no conductor: loud, fierce, and apt to fall into discord the moment the beat was lost. Their weapons were poor too—blades that bent, spearheads that sat loose on shafts, slings with fraying thongs—tools that would have shamed a kitchen boy setting out to dress a boar.

So when their courage soured, it soured at once. We did not stand and receive them; we hunted. Turn a flank, drive through the center, worry the other flank, and again cut a passage through their mass—never letting them settle, never letting them hear their own breathing long enough to remember bravery. A charge is like a hot pan: touch and withdraw and touch again, and the hand learns pain. Soon their outer ring began to peel away toward the forests and the trampled corn-lands, and those still pressed in close fought only to buy themselves a gap to flee through.

No practiced soldier would boast of such work as if it were a fine duel. Had they met us on chosen ground with their terror quieted, they would have fought like cornered beasts, and sheer weight of bodies would have dragged down any phalanx Atlantis could have sent. Oppression had salted them; hunger had sharpened them. But that hour belonged to disorder, and we took it.

By sunset the siege was lifted as cleanly as a pot is lifted off a fire before it boils over. It was plain the rebellion might be ended without the city pouring on needless cruelty—because harshness, like rancid oil, only makes the next meal harder to stomach and the next hunger more desperate.

We followed the fugitives far enough to keep their retreat from stiffening into another stand. That chase drew us well away from the walls, and the day’s heat had baked the men until even their curses came out dry. So I chose a halt among half-ruined houses—broken shells that could be made into a temporary fort, their fallen stones as good as breastwork. Night in those lands was never merely night: it carried teeth, and sometimes claws.

Luck, in the manner of luck, arrived on cloven hooves. A small drove of little horses—more goat than horse to the eye, with split hooves that clicked on stone—came blundering past, startled by the rout. We dropped five before the rest vanished into the dark. The men set fires at once, and the smell of blood and singed hair rose under the ruined eaves. They worked like men who had done this a hundred times: slit, skin, and joint; fat rendered in a shallow pot; strips laid across green sticks to roast; darker portions thrust into embers to char and soften. The meat was lean and iron-tasting, but hunger is its own spice, and the smoke made a roof against the night.

Sentries paced beyond the light, calling their short cries to one another—the old soldier-music that keeps the mind from imagining footsteps where none exist. The wounded sat near the coals, washing grit from cuts, binding torn flesh with linen, heating blade-tips to sear what would not stop bleeding. In the ring of firelight the officers talked over the day’s charges as men talk over a recipe they mean to perfect—this turn here, that feint there, what failed, what held. Such talk is a soldier’s privilege, and to outsiders it is as dull as listening to a butcher argue bone by bone.

Sleep began to gather. One by one the men’s heads dipped; snores rose like a rough chant. My officers’ eyes were red with weariness, and they kept the conversation going only because my rank demanded it. Yet I was afraid—feverishly afraid—of being left alone with what waited in my skull when the world fell quiet. I pressed them on, making them answer, making them speak, until at last a sentry came up and saluted, and the interruption saved them.

“My lord,” he said, “there is a woman come up from the city whom we have caught trying to come into the bivouac.”

“How is she named?”

“She will not say.”

“Has she business?”

“She will say none. She demands only to see my lord.”

“Bring her to the fire,” I began—then checked myself. A woman who came alone to such a place did not come for comfort. “Stay,” I said instead, and rose. “Take me to her.”

At the outer line the night smelled different—damp earth, crushed fern, the faint sour tang of those boiling springs that threaded the land like veins of scalding broth. The sentry led me to where two others held a muffled figure.

“My comrades are holding her,” the first sentry said. “She might be a wench belonging to these rebels, with designs to put a knife into my lord’s heart, and then we sentries would suffer. The Empress seems to set good store upon my lord at present,” he added in his plain way, “and we know the cleverness of her tormentors.”

“Your thoughtfulness is frank,” I told him, and looked at the woman. Hood and cloak hid her face, but grief and love teach their own recognition. Even wrapped in darkness and wool, that shape was Ylga—Nais’s twin, and so close a mirror that my heart always stumbled when I saw her.

I told the sentries to release her without demanding her name, and led her away beyond earshot. The camp’s sounds dulled behind us: the crack of resin in flames, the low murmur of men settling, the soft clink of a guard’s gear. Out here, the land itself spoke—distant surf, insects that sawed at the air, and now and again the deep, hollow belch of a boil-spring.

“It is something of the most pressing that has brought you out here, Ylga?”

“You know me, then?” Her voice had that same edge I remembered—pride sharpened into bitterness. “There must be something warmer than the ordinary between us two, Deucalion, if you can guess who walks beneath all these mufflings.”

I did not answer that hook. “What is your errand?”

“Aye,” she said, and the word came out like a slap. “There’s my reward. All your concern’s for the message, none for the carrier. Well, good my lord—husband to the dainty Phorenice no longer.”

“This is news.”

“And true enough. She will have no more of you—divorces you, spurns you, thrusts you from her. And after the first splutter of wrath is done, then come pains and penalties.”

“The Empress can do no wrong,” I said, from habit and necessity both. “Speak respectful words of the Empress.”

“Oh, be done with that old fable. It sickens me.” She leaned nearer, and though the night hid her face I could feel her eyes fixed on mine. “She was mad for love of you, and now she’s mad with jealousy. She knows you gave Nais some of your priest’s magic, and that she sleeps until you choose to come and claim her—even if the day be a century from this.

“And if you want the method of her enlightenment, it is simple. There is another airshaft next to the one down which you did your cooing and billing, and it leads to another cell with another prisoner. The wretch heard all, and thought to buy his freedom by telling it.”

Her words had their own stink—betrayal, stale and sweet, like meat kept too long in heat. She went on, sharp as a cook’s knife.

“His news came late. With the pressure of the morning’s ceremonies, they forgot his ration, and when his gaoler remembered him at last, it was rather too late—Phorenice had tied herself publicly to a husband, and Nais had doubtless eaten her green drug. Still, fools will always try to barter what they have, and as was natural they chopped his silly head off for the attempt. And after that your Phorenice behaved as you may guess.

“And now you may thank me, sir, for coming to warn you not to go back to Atlantis.”

“But I shall go back,” I said, and felt the old fatal calm settle over me like ash. “If the Empress chooses to cut my head from its proper column, that is as the High Gods will.”

“You are more sick of life than I thought. But I think our Phorenice judges your case very accurately.” Her laugh was brief and without mirth. “It was permitted me to hear her rage. ‘Shall I hew off his head?’ said she. ‘Pah! Shall I give him to my tormentors and stand by whilst they do their worst? He would not wrinkle his brow at their fiercest efforts. No. He must have a heavier punishment, and one that endures. I shall lop off his right hand and his left foot, so he may be a fighting man no longer, and then I shall drive him forth crippled into the dangerous lands, where he may learn Fear. Beasts shall hunt him; the fires of the ground shall spoil his rest. He shall know hunger; he shall breathe bad air. And all the while he shall remember that I have Nais near me, living and locked in her coffin of stone, to play with as I choose, and to give over to what insults may come to my fancy.’

“That is what she said, Deucalion. Now will you go back to meet her vengeance?”

“No,” I said, because the word was the only sane thing in that moment. “It is no part of my plan to be mutilated and left to live.”

“So.” Ylga’s breath eased, as if she had been holding it. “Being a woman of some sense, I judged as much. And having some small kindness left for you, I have made a plan for your further movement. Does the name of Tob come back to you?”

“One who was captain of Tatho’s navy.”

“That same Tob. Gruff, rude, smelling vile of tar and old hemp—but with a sturdy honesty. Tob sails away this night for parts unknown, to found a kingdom with Tob for king, no doubt. He can find little to earn in Atlantis now, and he has scruples at seeing his wife and young ones hungry.” She spoke that last word as if hunger were a living thing she had watched gnaw men down to bone. “He told me this at the harbour side when I put my neck under the axe by saying I wanted carriage for you. Having me under his thumb, he spoke more freely than usual. You made an impression on Tob. He said—you will forgive his hearty disrespect—you were just the recruit he wanted, and whether you joined him or not, he would go to the nether Gods to do you service.”

“With him,” I said, “I learned something of fighting the greater sea beasts.”

“Well, go and do it again. Believe me, sir, it is your only chance. It would grieve me to hear the searing-iron hiss on your stumps.” She stepped closer, fingers catching my sleeve. “I bargained with Tob to get clear of the harbour forts before the chain is up for the night, and he will come to a point of shore we agreed upon and await you. Come, Deucalion—let me lead you there.”

“My girl,” I said, and meant it, “I owe you thanks.”

“Oh, your thanks.” The words were scorn, but her grip tightened a fraction as if she feared to let go. “Keep them. I did not come out here in dark and danger for mere thanks, though I knew well enough there would be little else offered. Now show me your walking pace. They will begin to want your countenance in the camp directly, and we need not tempt questions.”

So we left the bivouac lights behind us and went into the black. I carried my weapons ready, because that land bred sudden teeth—beasts that moved without sound, men whose hunger made them quiet. Ylga led, swift and sure, choosing the firmer ground by instinct. The night had a thick, wet smell—bruised leaves, mineral steam, the salt pull of the estuary ahead. Insects stitched their thin music into the air, and from time to time the earth sighed with the deep, simmering breath of hidden heat.

We spoke little, only what was needed: where the ground dipped, where a thorn-brake waited, where a boil-spring’s white vapor drifted like a ghost across the path. Once only did we touch—at a rivulet of scalding water that ran from a boiling spring toward the sea, its surface dimly smoking, its sound a steady, wicked hiss like fat spitting in a pan.

A fallen tree-trunk bridged it, slick with moisture.

“Are you sure of footing?” I asked. In that darkness, one slip would strip flesh as if it were skin from a rabbit.

“No,” she said simply, and reached out to take my hand.

I steadied her across; her fingers were cold despite the steamed air. When she reached the far side I loosened my grip. She let her hand slip away slowly, and I heard—more than saw—her take in a long breath that was almost a sigh. Then she turned and went on, and I followed.

Hour after hour we walked, the night turning from black to a thinner black. At last we topped a rise, and through the trees the estuary showed itself—a long, faint gleam, like poured metal under starlight. The ground near it was soggy, sucking at our feet, and the vegetation grew armed: barbs and spines that caught at cloak and skin. Ylga’s breath came harder.

When I offered her my arm, she snapped away from me. “I am no poor weakling,” she said. “If that is your only reason for wanting to touch me.”

But even pride grows tired. Presently we came out onto open beach where the worst of the thorns ended, and there ahead rode a ship at anchor a mile away—low, old-fashioned, strange under the starlight, with no machine-hum, no easy mechanical chitter, only the creak of timber and the hush of tide. The shore’s soundscape widened: surf breathing, pebbles ticking as waves withdrew, and far off the dull bellows of the burning mountains that fenced Atlantis like watchful beasts.

We went along the level sands toward the ship. Tob kept sharp watch. We were challenged as soon as we came within stone-throw.

“Halt there! Speak your business!”

Ylga called back, and when Tob recognized the appointed voices he grew civil. He called a crew, slacked anchor-rope until the hull ground against shingle, and thrust out the two steering oars like rough ladders for us to climb.

I turned to Ylga. “I will not forget what you have done. If the High Gods ever bring me back to Atlantis and power, you shall taste my gratitude.”

“I do not want to return,” she said. “I am sick of this old life here.”

“You have your palace,” I reminded her, because it was the obvious argument—“your servants, your wealth. Phorenice will not disturb you.”

“Oh, I could go back and be fan-girl tomorrow if I wished. But I do not want to go back.”

“This is no time for a gently nurtured lady to go forward,” I said. “I have been viceroy of Yucatan. Founding a foothold in new lands is hardship—privation—suffering you cannot guess. Few survive who go first, and those only of the hardiest.”

“I do not care. And besides, I can share the work.” Her voice warmed with a fierce insistence that made her seem suddenly nearer to Nais than ever. “I can cook. I can shoot a good arrow. I can make garments—yes, though they are cut from beast-skins and sewn with backbone sinews. Because you despise fine clothes, and because you have seen me only decked as fan-girl, you think I am useless. Bah, Deucalion. You know less of a woman than a boy new from school.”

“I have learned all I care to know about one woman,” I said, and the name I did not speak filled my mouth like salt. “And because of the memory of her, I could not presume to ask her sister to come with me now.”

“Aye,” she said, and bitterness returned like smoke on a wind-change. “Kick my pride. I knew well enough it was only second place to Nais I could get, all the time I wanted to come. Yet no one but a boor would remind me of it. Gods—and to think half the men in Atlantis have courted me, and now I am arrived at this.”

“I must go alone,” I said. “I would have been happier to take your esteem with me. But as it is, I suppose I shall carry only your hate.”

“That is the most humiliating thing of all.” Her voice broke, not into tears but into honesty. “I cannot bring myself to hate you. I ought to, after the brutal way you have scorned me. But I do not. I grow the fonder of you. And if I thought there was a way of keeping you alive and unmutilated here in Atlantis, I do not think I should point out that Tob is tired of waiting and will probably be off without you.”

Then she flung her arms around my neck and kissed me—hotly, recklessly, with the taste of salt air and smoke on her lips, and the tremor of a woman who has decided she will not be remembered as careful. She pulled back at once, as if ashamed of needing anything, and ran into the edge of the trees.

“But this is foolishness,” I called after her. “I must take you back through the dangers to some gate of the city, and then return.”

“You need not fear for me,” her voice came back from the shadow. “The unhappy are always safe. And besides, I have a way. It is my solace to know you will remember me now. You will never forget that kiss.”

“Fare you well, Ylga,” I cried. “May the High Gods keep you entirely in their holy care.”

No answer came. The forest swallowed her footsteps, but the forest was never silent; it took her into its own rustle and whisper, into the old language of leaves that no palace ever translated.

I turned down to the water, splashed in, and climbed aboard by the steering oars. Tob gave the word to haul-to, and the crew heaved at the rope with grunts timed to the ship’s creaking complaint. Timber and tide argued, then agreed, and the hull began to slide.

“Greeting, my lord,” Tob said when I came on deck, “but I’d have been pleased to see you earlier. We’ve small force and slow heels in this vessel, and the sooner we’re away and beyond pursuit, the safer for my woman and brats in that hutch of an after-castle. It’s long since I sailed so small and old-fashioned a ship as this. She’s no machines, and not even a steering mannikin. Look at the meanness of her furniture—and in your ear, I’ve suspicions of rottenness in her bottom. But she’s the best I could buy. If she reaches the place at the farther end I’ve got my eye on, we must make home there or be content to die, for she’ll never have strength to carry us farther or back. She’s been in the Egypt trade, and you know what that means for worm and rot.”

“You had hands enough before I came?” I asked.

“Oh yes. Fifty stout lads and eight women packed in somehow, and trouble enough to get them away. That thief of a port-captain wellnigh skinned us before he could see it lawful that so many useful fighting men might go out. Times are not what they were, and the sea trade’s about done. Sailor men of any skill have taken a woman or two and gone out in companies to try their fortunes. Why, I had trouble enough to get half a score to work the ship. All the rest are landsmen—raw, simple. If I land half alive at the other end, we shall do well.”

“With luck and a few good winds,” I said, “it should not take long to cross to Europe.”

Tob slapped his leg, as if I had offered him thin broth when he craved meat. “No savage Europe for me, my lord. Now see the advantage of being a mariner. I found once some islands to the north of Europe, separated from the main by a strait. I called them the Tin Islands, seeing that tin ore litters many beaches. I was driven there by storm, and said no word of it when I got back. And now, you see, it comes in useful. There’s no one in all Atlantis but me knows of those Tin Islands today. We’ll go and fight honestly for our ground, and build a town and a kingdom on it.”

“With Tob for king?” I said.

He hesitated, and the night wind filled the pause with canvas-flutter and rope-squeal. “Well, I’ve figured it out as such many a day. But I know when I meet my better, and I’m content to serve under Deucalion.” Then, with the practical cruelty of men who have buried hopes before, he added, “My lord would have done wiser to have brought a wife, though. I thought it was understood by the good lady who spoke to me at the harbour, or I’d have mentioned it earlier. The savages in my Tin Islands go naked and stain themselves blue with woad—filthy and brutish to look upon. Sturdy enough, should make good slaves; but one would have to get blunted in the taste before one could wish to father their children.”

“I am still husband to Phorenice,” I said.

Tob grinned, the grin of a man who has watched tides erase footprints. “The Gods give you joy of her. But it is part of a mariner’s creed—and you’ll grow into it—that wedlock does not hold across the seas. However, that matter may rest. My Tin Islands will delight you. A kingdom will not be so hard to carve as Egypt, or as you found in Yucatan. Beasts there, of course, and no one who can hunt need ever go hungry. Greater beasts are few: cave-bears and cave-tigers in small numbers, some river-horses and great snakes. But the greater lizards seem to avoid the land. As for birds, rarely one that can hurt a grown man. Oh, it will be a most desirable kingdom.”

He spoke of it as a man speaks of bread he has kneaded in his mind for years: every fold remembered, every rise imagined. Then he sighed, and the sigh carried a small private mourning.

“In truth I did imagine myself king, and there’s no denying. And there is not another man living I would have broken this voyage for but Deucalion. But don’t think I regret it. This breeze and the ebb take the old ship finely. See those fire baskets on the harbour forts? We’re abreast of them now. We’ll have dropped them and the city out of sight by daylight, and the flood will not begin to run up till then. But unless the wind hardens with dawn we may have to anchor when the flood makes. Tides run hard in these narrow seas. Aye—and there are shrewd tide-rips round my Tin Islands, as you shall see.”

When day came and showed the waters and the burning mountains hemming them beyond the shores, fear looked back over every shoulder. The estuary lay behind like a wide mouth, and men expected at any moment to see Phorenice’s navy come surging out—oar-blades flashing, sails bellying—bringing them back to servitude and the thin, sour gruel of the city’s squalid wards. I confess I shared their dread. The very air seemed to listen for pursuit, and every creak of our own timbers sounded like another ship’s answer.

But their brave words found no test. For the full flow of the tide we hung nearly in place, creeping so slowly it seemed the land slid past us by its own will. Not an oar nor sail showed behind. When the tide turned, the ship leapt forward as if relieved, and men breathed again, mightily comforted.

Tob’s woman came up with drink and would have had all pour libations to her as a future queen—making ceremony where the sea demanded attention. Tob cuffed her back into the after-castle and slammed the hatch, bidding the crew put the liquor to honest use.

“We are done with that foolery,” he said. “My Lord Deucalion will be king of this new kingdom we shall build in the Tin Islands, and a right proper king he’ll make, as you untravelled ones would know if you’d sailed the outer seas with him as I have done.”

In his boasting I heard regret—the ghost of his own crown—yet I held my tongue. From the moment I stepped aboard, my mind had begun to lay its own course, quiet as a cook measuring salt.

So we went down the great estuary. The others took pleasure from the desolate seas—no sails, no rivals, no patrols. It saddened me. I remembered when those waters had been whitened with shipping, when the soundscape of the sea was crowded with creaking yards, shouted orders, clanking chain, and the deep, comforting moan of commerce. Now there was only wind and wave, and the occasional cry of a distant bird—thin and lonely.

They had begun with barely two days’ provision. So, once clear of the estuary, we were forced into a coastwise crawl, putting into every likely river and sheltered beach to gather victual. There were days when the air was rich with fish-smell as men dragged nets and clubbed what glittered in the shallows; days when smoke lay low because we had killed and butchered on the sand, and the fat sputtered as it rendered, and the meat—rubbed with whatever bitter herbs we could find—hung in strips to dry in the wind. Salt was scarce, and so we learned again the old arts Atlantis had begun to forget: smoking slow to drive out damp, drying high to keep off insects, boiling down bones for broth that thickened into jelly when cooled, saving every scrap because waste is a luxury of safe cities.

“And when winter comes,” Tob said one evening as we sat with our rough food—fish roasted skin-on over coals, its flesh flaking white and sweet beneath fingers blackened by ash—“as its gales will be heavier than this old ship can stomach, I had determined to haul up and make a permanent camp ashore, and get a crop of grain grown and threshed before setting sail again. It is the usual custom in these voyages. And I shall do it still, subject to my lord’s better opinion.”

By then, after two months of this slow edging along the coast—two months measured in tides, in smoke, in the changing calls of shore-birds and the different voices of rivers—I saw my opening to speak what I had carried like a stone in my belly.

“Tob,” I said, “I am a poor, weak, defenceless man, and quite at your mercy. But what if I do not voyage all the way to the Tin Islands and oust you of this kingship?”

He brightened, though he tried to hide it under roughness. “Aye,” he grunted, “you are very weak, my lord, and mighty defenceless. We know all about that. But what’s else? Tell your meaning plain. I’m a common mariner, and understand little of your fancy talk.”

“Why, this,” I said. “It is not my wish to leave the continent of Atlantis. If you will put me down on any part of this side that faces Europe, I will commend you strongly to the Gods. I would I could give you money—or better, articles useful for your colonising—but you see me destitute.”
