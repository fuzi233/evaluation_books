Harry Warrington remained steadfast in keeping the grim promise his cousin had wrung from him. We trust no benevolent reader will judge him harshly for believing the engagement was anything but a burden he would gladly shed. Perhaps the beating inflicted upon poor Will served this very purpose. Harry likely reasoned thus: *"A fistfight between us is bound to escalate into a family quarrel. In such a fray, Maria will undoubtedly side with her brother. I, of course, will offer no retraction or apology. Will, in that scenario, may challenge me to settle the score physically, and I shall ensure I emerge as the victor. Amidst the chaos of the feud, the agreement will dissolve, and I shall be a free man once more."*

So, honest Harry laid his trap and pulled the fuse. Yet, when the explosion faded, no lasting damage was wrought—save for William Esmond's swollen nose and bruised eye, which plagued him for a week. Will did not issue a formal challenge to his cousin, Harry Warrington; consequently, neither man killed the other. Will was knocked down, but he rose again. How many sensible men would act similarly if they could settle their grievances privately, unseen by the public eye?

Maria did not align herself with her family in the dispute; instead, she championed her cousin, a stance supported by Lord Castlewood upon learning of the disturbance. "Will struck the first blow," the Lord declared, citing the chaplain's provocation. "It was not the first nor the tenth time he has been found quarreling while drunk. Mr. Warrington merely displayed proper spirit in defending his honor; it was for Will, not Harry, to seek forgiveness." Harry countered that he would accept no apology until his horse was returned and his wager settled. While the chronicler cannot ascertain from existing records how the bet was ultimately resolved, it is certain that the cousins soon met in the homes of mutual friends, avoiding further physical altercation.

Initially, Maria's elder brother had been content with his sister marrying any available suitor, especially a gentleman from Virginia. He viewed her as a woman past her prime, her gown trailing through life's path gathering briars, mud, rents, and stains. He imagined her retiring to his wigwam in the forest to be quietly disposed of, expecting Harry to outlive her elderly bride and console himself—or not—as he saw fit. However, following an audience with Aunt Bernstein upon arriving in London, the Lord reversed his opinion. He attempted to dissuade Maria from the match, feigning pity for the young man destined for misery due to a foolish promise made at twenty-one.

"Misery, indeed!" Maria retorted, confused by his sentiment. "Pity, forsooth?" Lord Castlewood believed there was none to be found. Maria, however, understood the concept of pity. Her brother had visited Aunt Bernstein, who had offered funds to break the engagement. She grasped the Lord's implication: Mr. Warrington was a man of honor, and she trusted him implicitly.

Enraged, the Lord departed for White's Club or similar haunts. It is probable Maria had correctly deduced the nature of his conversations with Madame Bernstein. *"And so,"* he mused, *"my virtue ends with the Mohock falling prey to others. There is no use in sparing him. 'Quem deus vult'—what was that schoolmaster's adage? If I do not claim him, someone else will. My brother has taken a bite; my dear sister intends to devour him whole. Here I have been at home, preaching respect for his youth and innocence, refusing to gamble beyond a sixpence, acting as his guardian and mentor. Why am I a fool fattening a goose only for others to feast upon? Few good deeds have I performed in this life, and this one benefits whom? Others. Talk of remorse! By all the fires and furies, the remorse I feel is for things I did not do and might have done! Why did I spare Lucretia? She hated me thereafter, and her husband met his predestined fate. Why have I let this lad off? Let March and the rest, who desire nothing of him, pluck him bare! I carry a bad reputation; I am the wicked lord women warn their sons against! Pardi, I am not a penny worse, only vastly unluckier than my neighbors, and my cursed weakness has been my greatest enemy!"*

The chronicler admits that attributing such internal monologues to a gentleman stretches credulity, yet history is filled with speeches invented by Livy and Thucydides that their heroes never uttered. Knowing Lord Castlewood's character intimately, we may reasonably infer these thoughts. A pack of wolves hunts this lamb; one hungry hunter stands aside while others feast. Has anyone not admired Lord Clive's boast upon returning from India, claiming surprise at how little he exploited his vast wealth? *"Upon my life,"* the hero of Plassey remarked, *"when I reflect on my opportunities, I am astonished I took so little!"*

To recount disagreeable tales of a gentleman forces a sensitive mind to pause. Thus, despite knowing early on the true nature of Lord Castlewood and his standing among contemporaries, the narrator withheld much unpleasantness, revealing only enough to show he was a nobleman unworthy of affection. True, Lord March and others would have gambled Mr. Warrington's last shilling and taken the winnings without scruple, exploiting every advantage of skill and insider knowledge—a common practice among gentlemen. Yet, when they played, they played fair, and when they lost, they paid.

Madame Bernstein hesitated to reveal everything she knew about her nephew's family disgrace. Touched by the Lord's initial forbearance toward Harry upon his European arrival and pleased by his compliance with her wishes, she listened to his cynical rantings. He called himself a fool for sparing a boy doomed to ruin, highlighted Harry's undeniable extravagance and spendthrift circle, his nights at Faro and Hazard, and his rides to Newmarket, asking why he alone should restrain the young man. When Madame Bernstein pleaded Harry's poverty, the Lord scoffed. *"Bah! He is heir to a principality that ought to belong to him, Castlewood's, and could restore our ruined family."* (In truth, Madame Bernstein believed Mr. Warrington's Virginia holdings were far greater than they actually were). Money-lenders abounded in the city, eager to lend on post-obits. The Lord knew this to his cost; during his father's lifetime, such creditors had devoured two-thirds of his miserable income.

Speaking with such desperate candor and ill humor, Madame Bernstein grew alarmed for her favorite and decided to caution him immediately. That evening, she began drafting a letter to Mr. Warrington. Yet, throughout her life, she was slow with her pen and disliked its use. *"I never knew any good come of writing more than 'bonjour' or business,"* she often said. *"Why write poorly when clever people exist to do it well? Even then, silence is best."* Instead, she dispatched a servant to invite Harry to tea the next day, intending to warn him.

However, the next morning she fell ill and could not receive him. She confined herself to her chambers for two days, and on the third, a major engagement occupied her attention. By the fourth day, Mr. Harry was off on his own expedition. In the whirlwind of London life, who sees their neighbor, their sibling, or their old friend? Days passed before the confidential conversation occurred.

She began by gently chiding him for his extravagance and wild frolics (though, in truth, she was charmed by both). He replied that young men must be young men, noting that his dutiful attendance upon his aunt had led to the acquaintanceship he currently enjoyed. Then, with some preamble, she warned him regarding his cousin, Lord Castlewood. At this, Harry burst into a bitter laugh. *"The good-natured world has told me plenty about Lord Castlewood already,"* he said. *"To advise a man of his rank, or any gentleman, 'Do not play with him,' is more than I care to do."*

"Oh, go on, aunt!" Harry interjected, with something resembling an oath on his lips.

"And have you played with your cousin already?" the worldly old monitoress asked.

*"Lost and won, madam!"* Harry answered gallantly. *"It does not become me to specify. If we have a bout with a neighbor in Virginia—over a bottle, a deck of cards, or a quarrel—we do not return home to tell our mothers. I mean no offense, aunt!"* Blushing, the handsome young fellow approached and kissed the old lady. He appeared brave and brilliant, adorned in rich lace, with a fair face and hair, wearing a fine new suit of velvet and gold. Upon departing, he bestowed his customary lavish tip upon her servants, who swarmed around him.

It was a rainy winter day, and to protect his fine silk stockings, Harry insisted on traveling in a chair. *"To White's!"* he commanded the driver, heading to the establishment where he spent much of his time. His friends might have wished for less frequent visits to this house of entertainment, but much credit is due to Mr. Warrington: having engaged in play, he fought his battles like a hero. Unflustered by good fortune and perfectly calm when luck turned against him, he embodied the ideal gambler. If Fortune is proverbially fickle to players, how many players are fickle to Fortune, fleeing her advances in fear?
