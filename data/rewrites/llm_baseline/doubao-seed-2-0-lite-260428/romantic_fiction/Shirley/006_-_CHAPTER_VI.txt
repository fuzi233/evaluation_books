The golden mid-morning sun slanted through Hollow's Cottage parlour window, wrapping Caroline Helstone in its soft warmth as she sat at Hortense Moore's side, her mind drifting far from the French grammar lesson unfolding before her. Again and again she missed the point of Mademoiselle's explanations, earning gentle chidings that she bore with a smile unclouded by frustration—for that sunlight seemed to carry with it a quiet, thrumming joy that had settled in her chest since first light, making her feel soft, bright, and brimming with quiet goodwill. In that mood, she looked her very best: a vision of gentle, unforced charm that caught the eye even at first glance.

Beauty had never been denied to Caroline. Her slight, girlish frame was lithe and pliant, every curve neat, every limb softly proportioned; her face was gentle and alive with expression, her brown eyes bright with a winsome, warm glow that slipped unawares into a person's heart, speaking quiet affection before her mouth could frame a word. Her lips were full and sweet, her skin delicate as rose petal, and her thick, wavy brown hair fell in loose, artful curls that she arranged with quiet, unshowy taste. Her dress matched her hair's soft warm brown merino for the winter, a simple collar edged with a narrow pink ribbon fastened at her throat with a tiny pink knot—no other trinkets, no costly adornments, and yet every stitch suited her perfectly.

That was Caroline Helstone for the world to see. Her character, her quiet hungers and hidden hopes, would reveal themselves soon enough.

Her story was simple, and tinged with quiet loneliness. She was born to two parents who separated almost immediately after her birth, their tempers too ill-suited to ever make a home together. Her mother was the half-sister of Hortense, Robert and Louis Moore's father, making her their distant cousin, no shared blood between them but a soft, gentle affection that had grown between her and the Moore siblings since they had arrived at Hollow's Mill. Her father, the dissolute brother of her uncle the rector, had made his wife's life a misery, and died young when Caroline was still a small child. She had never known her mother, taken away from her in infancy and never permitted to see her again, and for years her only guardian had been the gruff, sharp rector, a man ill-suited to raising a quiet young girl. He had paid no mind to her education, and if Caroline had not pushed for it herself, hungry for every scrap of learning she could get, she would have grown up almost entirely untaught. Even so, she carried a quiet, constant fear that she was less educated than other girls of her station, which made her jump at Hortense's kind offer to teach her French and fine needlework the moment the older woman had settled into the cottage.

Hortense, for her part, adored the task: it gave her a sense of importance, and she delighted in lording gently over her docile, sharp little pupil. She dismissed Caroline's quick progress as proof only of her own superior teaching methods, never once crediting the girl's sharp mind and eagerness to learn, even when Caroline knew things about subjects Hortense had never studied—she was entirely convinced Caroline had gleaned every last scrap of knowledge from her own conversation, a silly, unshakable little conviction that no amount of evidence could shift. Proud of her "positive intellect" and fond of dry, rigid studies, she kept Caroline nose-deep in French grammar as much as possible, assigning endless interminable "logical analyses" that Caroline found tedious beyond measure: hours spent picking apart principal and subordinate clauses, distinguishing determinative and applicative incidents, sorting full, elliptical and implicit propositions that tangled her thoughts into knots.
When she got stuck, she would sneak her book down to the counting-house while Hortense rummaged endlessly upstairs, rearranging her drawers for the tenth time that day, and Robert Moore would untangle the puzzle for her in two minutes flat, his clear, calm brain cutting through the jargon like a knife through butter. Caroline would leave the counting house glowing with gratitude, smiling after him like he had handed her a precious treasure, and daydream constantly that nature had made her a boy instead of a girl, so she could be his clerk and sit by his side in the counting house all day, instead of stuck in the parlour doing endless grammar drills with Hortense.

Very rarely, she was invited to spend the whole evening at the cottage. Half the time Robert was away at market, or calling on Mr Yorke, or shut away with a business visitor, but sometimes he was home, free and unoccupied, and those evenings passed so fast they were gone before she could count the hours. There was no room in all of England as perfect as that tiny parlour when the three cousins sat together: Hortense would soften as the day wound down, no longer scolding or fussing over cooking, and if Caroline cajoled her into picking up her guitar to play, she would grow perfectly good-natured, her warm skilled voice filling the room. Robert, released from the weight of his business worries, would sit near her, a soft, willing audience for all her chattering, gentle and friendly, and those hours were the brightest points in her quiet, uneventful life. The only flaw was that by the next morning, he would always retreat back into his quiet, distant work-focused self, and never planned those pleasant evenings on purpose, letting weeks pass before inviting her over again. Caroline, inexperienced and soft-hearted, puzzled over that habit constantly: if she had a source of that much happiness, she would use it often, never let it sit gathering dust on a shelf.
She never invited herself over, no matter how much she longed to come—even when Hortense pressed her to visit, she would refuse if Robert did not second the invitation. Which was why that morning, when he had invited her of his own free will, speaking so kindly that her chest had felt fit to burst with joy, she had carried that warmth with her every minute of the day.

The morning unfolded as usual. Hortense bustled back and forth between kitchen and parlour, scolding their maid Sarah one minute, checking Caroline's exercises the next. No amount of perfect work ever earned a word of praise from her; she was convinced praise undermined a teacher's dignity, and that constant, mild scolding was the only way to maintain her authority. If Caroline got her lesson perfectly right, Hortense would find some fault with her posture, her dress, or the way she held her head to correct instead.
Dinner brought its usual small skirmish: Sarah slammed the dish down on the table with a look that said plainly she thought the meal was not fit for a dog, but it was surprisingly savoury all the same: dried pea puree that Hortense had fussed over for an hour, lamenting the lack of proper haricot beans in "this desolate English country", a spiced baked meat and breadcrumb mould, oddly bruised greens, and a fruit pâté made from her grandmother's old Belgian recipe, tasting faintly of molasses instead of sugar. Caroline liked the strange foreign food well enough, and she was wise to: any hint of distaste for Hortense's cooking would have been a far worse sin in the older woman's eyes than any outright crime.

After dinner, Caroline coaxed Hortense upstairs to dress, navigating the task carefully: if she had ever dared say the sight of her cousin in curl papers, camisole and petticoat was unbecoming, Hortense would have worn that outfit for the rest of the day just to be stubborn. Instead, she pretended she was bored of the parlour and wanted to stretch her legs, and once they were in the bedroom, she persuaded Hortense it was far more efficient to dress for the evening right away, and fussed over her, arranging her collar and her hair while Hortense delivered a long, solemn speech about how frivolous fashion was. Even then, Hortense insisted on tying a thick ficher handkerchief tight around her throat and putting on a huge, servant-like black apron, ruining the neat effect entirely. To her, the ficher was a point of decency, and the apron the mark of a good housewife, a garment that somehow saved Robert untold amounts of money. She had tried to force Caroline to wear matching ones, and their only serious quarrel had been over that very demand—until Robert had overheard the dispute, and ruled that Caroline's small neat aprons and high collars were perfectly sufficient, especially with her long curls falling almost to her shoulders. There was no arguing with Robert, so Hortense had yielded, though she still thought Caroline's pretty, neat dresses were far from "beaucoup plus convenable" as a solid, homely outfit would have been.

All afternoon, the two women sewed, Hortense working on fine embroidery and lacework that strained her eyes, devoting entire days to mending two tiny holes in a stocking, a skill she had been forced to learn when she was barely old enough to hold a needle, at the age of five. She had been horrified to discover Caroline had no idea how to do the perfect, invisible stitch darning that duplicated the stocking fabric exactly, and had dug out a pair of stockings with the heels completely worn away for Caroline to mend—two years later, Caroline was still chipping away at a few rows a day, the task hanging over her like a tedious penance. Robert had once offered to sneak the stockings down to the counting house and burn them, but Caroline had known that would only result in a new, even more ruined pair of hose for her to mend, so she had stuck it out.

By late afternoon, rain was pouring down in torrents, the sky darkening completely long before twilight. Caroline's heart sank: what if Robert stayed over with Mr Sykes or Mr Yorke in Whinbury, waiting out the rain? Five o'clock struck, then six, the wind sighing through the trees outside, the parlour fire glowing ruddy as if it was already deep night.
"It will not clear until the moon rises," Hortense declared. "My brother will not come back until then. We will have coffee. There is no point waiting for him."
Caroline agreed meekly, and trundled off to the kitchen to tell Sarah to bring the tea things in, leaning her head against the mantel, her spirits drooping.
Sarah noticed her at once. "You look fit to cry, miss! All that work Cousin Moore puts you through, it's a downright shame."
"Nothing of the sort," Caroline said, then leaned forward, voice soft. "Sarah, does your master ever come home early from market when it's raining?"
"Hardly ever—but he's home now, as it happens. I saw Murgatroyd lead his horse round to the back yard five minutes ago, and I heard him talking to Joe Scott in the counting house about new frames for the mill, and four soldiers from Stilbro barracks to guard the next supply wagon."
Caroline's face lit up. She offered to help Sarah finish cutting out her new gown, and gave her a length of matching narrow satin ribbon for trimming, barely noticing when she snipped a little mistake in the fabric.
A moment later, the kitchen door opened, and Robert walked in, cold and damp from the rain. Caroline turned away for a second, trying to smooth the giddy smile off her face, but when she looked up at him her whole expression was glowing, bright with joy.
"We all thought you would not come back," she said.
"But I promised I would return soon. You expected me, didn't you?"
"I dared not, with it pouring so hard. You are wet and chilled—go change all your wet clothes, or you will catch your death!"
"My riding coat is waterproof. I only need dry shoes," he said, stepping close to the hearth to warm himself. He stood staring at the polished brasses on the mantelshelf, until he glanced down and caught sight of her face, upturned, flushed, smiling, her curls soft around her cheeks. Sarah had already slipped off to the parlour, detained by a scolding from Hortense over her "cafe au lait" that Sarah insisted was a waste of perfectly good coffee. Robert rested his hand for a light moment on Caroline's shoulder, leaned down, and pressed a soft, warm kiss to her forehead.
"Oh!" she breathed. "I was so miserable when I thought you would not come. I am almost too happy now. Are you happy, Robert? Are you glad to be home?"
"For tonight, at least, yes."
"Are you not fretting about your frames, your business, the war?"
"Not just now."
"Are you sure you don't think this cottage is too small, too narrow and dull for you?"
"Not this moment, no."
"Are you not bitter that the rich and great people around here forget you?"
He laughed, a little rough. "No more questions. I don't care about currying favour with them. I just want a livelihood, a position, a career."
"Your talent and your goodness will win it for you. You are meant to be great, you will be great."
"Lina, you judge me with your heart, not your head," he said, his dark face flushing softly, his lips twitching into a smile even as he tried to look serious. "Men are not as good as you think. I am no better than the rest of them."
"But you are better," she protested. "I feel it, here in my chest. I am your sincere friend, Robert."
He opened his mouth to reply, but before he could speak, Hortense and Sarah came bustling back into the kitchen, still arguing loudly over the merits of coffee made with milk instead of water. Robert only had time to meet Caroline's eyes, and answer her unspoken question if he could ever be her enemy, with a soft, quiet, "Could I be?" before he led her into the parlour, and settled her at his side at the table, the soft glow of the fire wrapping around both of them, the heavy rain beating soft against the windows outside.
