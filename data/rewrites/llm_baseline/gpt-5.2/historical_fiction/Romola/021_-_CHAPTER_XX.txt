The last week of Carnival had come round again, like a feast-day whose very merriment was grown habitual—salted into the life of Florence as surely as the city’s bread was salted with no salt at all. By daylight the streets already rang as if night had never ended: maskers in file, singing what Lorenzo’s taste had once made indispensable; the rigoletto footed in the piazzas under a sky of hard blue cold; and, mixed with the jests, the old brutality that Florence called its privilege—poles thrust crosswise to bar a passage until a tribute was paid for suppers and bonfires, and then, when the appetites were fed, the sport of stones, as if the very hands of boys were taught to fling pain for pastime.

Yet there was, too, a common picturesqueness that belonged to quick blood and quick senses—the smell of crushed orange-peel underfoot, hot chestnuts smoked at corners, and the far-off fat hiss from frying-pans where poor households made a little holiday out of oil and flour. Over all lay the city’s loudness—pipes and chanting, laughter and quarrel, the clack of wooden pattens on stone, the incessant cry of sellers—so that the air itself seemed braided of noise.

It was in the high morning, when Carnival spirits were still willing to lounge and recount last night’s pranks, that Tito Melema pressed forward briskly toward the Via de’ Bardi, and with him walked young Bernardo Dovizi—keen-eyed already, as if his glance were learning to pierce the future as well as the present. They talked eagerly, their words and gestures belonging to some thread of purpose that had little to do with the masquerade around them; and yet, now and then, each cast a rapid look aside, as if the crowd itself might contain a face that could alter their plans.

At the turning toward the Lung’ Arno, Tito’s quick survey caught precisely what he dreaded to meet at such a moment. A small figure in a white hood moved along with a timid, forlorn air—unmistakably Tessa. The very sight of her was like feeling a buried seed stir in the earth just when one had laid a garden-bed smooth and thought it orderly. Tito’s thoughts were full, and full of one unique sweetness: this evening was to be his betrothal to Romola.

The ceremony had been fixed sooner than had first been intended. The fitting-up of rooms in Bardo’s dwelling—scantily furnished always, save for the great library that had been a realm in itself—had been going on for some time, Tito’s florins turning into chairs and hangings, into the visible promise of a shared life. But though the marriage and betrothal had meant to wait until after Easter, when Tito’s year of probation, pressed on him by Bernardo del Nero, should be complete, a sudden proposition had come: he must go with Cardinal Giovanni to Rome, to aid Bernardo Dovizi—who was to gather books as other men gathered offices—in arranging a library, using that superior Greek which made Tito valuable. There was no refusing what lay so plainly on the road of advancement. Tito, therefore, had pleaded that the betrothal should be before his departure. If he and Romola were outwardly pledged as well as inwardly, parting would hurt less; and, more, he would have a claim that Messer Bernardo, and any other cautious elder, could not lightly nullify. The betrothal—rings exchanged, contracts signed—was more than half a marriage already, to be completed later by the nuptial blessing. Romola’s feeling had met his in this desire; the elders had been won.

And now, with departure to Rome looming the next day, he was hurrying to snatch a morning visit to Romola—to speak and to hear those last words that lovers, like cooks tasting the final seasoning, cannot omit before the dish is sent out to the table. It was not a moment when any recognition that could detain him was pleasant; least of all the recognition of Tessa.

As he continued to talk with Dovizi, there ran under his words an uncomfortable certainty: Tessa had seen him; she would follow. The direct way by the Arno and over the Ponte Rubaconte offered no easy escape. She would not dare to speak while he was not alone; so he meant to keep Dovizi at his side until he reached Bardo’s door. He quickened his pace, taking up new strands of talk; yet the sense of those blue eyes behind him grew stronger, as if he heard her small footsteps in the vast city’s roar. It irritated him—more, perhaps, because pity made evasion feel faintly cowardly, and yet he persevered.

He managed the parting at the doorway with Dovizi cleverly, saying his addio without turning his face in the one direction that would let him see a white hood hovering too near. As he went up the stone steps, he tried to persuade himself that perhaps Tessa had not seen him at all; or if she had, perhaps she would not follow. But that hope was thin as Carnival sugar.

When the visit was over, he came out again with a quick step and an air that pretended to notice nothing on either side. Yet the eye takes in more than the will permits, and before any touch came, he knew—knew too well—that she stood near, close to the doorway, small and waiting.

A hand caught his arm—not the light timid touch he might have expected, but a grasp tightened by desperation, for she had started forward when she saw his haste. When Tito stopped and turned, her face was frightened, as if she already feared the consequence of her boldness.

“Tessa!” The sharpness in his voice was new to her.

“Why are you here? You must not follow me—you must not stand about door-places waiting for me.”

Her eyes widened, the tears springing as quickly as a well that has been unstoppered; she could find no words. Tito’s fear leaped at once—not mere ridicule, but the risk of being seen in the Via de’ Bardi with a girlish contadina looking up at him with that unmistakable claim of sorrow. The street was of high, silent dwellings, not of traffic; but Messer Bernardo del Nero, or some other dangerous acquaintance, might appear at any instant. Even on any other day it would have been awkward. On this day it was intolerable.

And yet it was impossible—brutal—to drive her away with harshness. That folly with the cerretano, that bargain with his own ease, had lain quiet for months, and now rose up like an unseasonable weed in the very path of his triumph. He spoke hurriedly, because haste was the nearest thing he could offer in place of cruelty.

“Tessa, I cannot—must not—talk to you here. I will go on to the bridge and wait for you there. Follow me slowly.”

He turned at once and walked fast toward the Ponte Rubaconte. On the bridge he leaned against the wall of one of the quaint little houses that stood there at even distances, and watched for her coming.

It would have softened a harder man than Tito to see her approach. The round face that had once been all sun and childish laughter under the “Nunziata” door was paler now; and the air of her was like a creature accustomed to the crack of a stick and the harshness of hunger—like a little kid that has learned to flinch from the hand that ought to feed it. The bridge at that hour was the least frequented; only a few passed by, their footsteps and voices swallowed by the river’s broader sound.

Tito began at once, losing no time.

“Now, Tessa, I have very little time. You must not cry. Why did you follow me this morning? You must not do so again.”

“I thought—” Her voice was a whisper, and the sob rose in her throat as if it had been waiting there all these months for the mere sound of him. “I thought you wouldn’t be so long before you came to take care of me again. And the patrigno beats me, and I can’t bear it any longer. And always when I come for a holiday I walk about to find you, and I can’t. Oh, please don’t send me away from you again! It has been so long, and I cry so now, because you never come to me. I can’t help it, for the days are so long, and I don’t mind about the goats and kids, or anything—and I can’t—”

The sobs came fast then, and the tears—great drops that clung on her lashes and fell as if each were heavy with all the unspoken time. Tito felt the necessity like a knot: he must send her away at once; but he could not—could not—leave her in hopeless grief. He saw, dimly, a farther trouble beyond this moment; but the present difficulty pressed too hard to weigh distant consequences.

“Tessa, my little one,” he said, letting his voice soften into the old caressing tone which was for her like warm broth to a chilled stomach, “you must not cry. Bear with the cross patrigno a little longer. I will come back to you. But I’m going now to Rome—a long, long way off. I shall come back in a few weeks, and then I promise you to come and see you. Promise me to be good and wait for me.”

The remembered voice soothed her as surely as the scent of baked bread calms a child in a strange house. She lifted her eyes to him—trusting, still glittering with tears—trying, and failing, not to sob.

Again he urged, gently, “Promise me, my Tessa.”

“Yes,” she whispered. “But you won’t be long?”

“No, not long. But I must go now. And remember what I told you, Tessa. Nobody must know that you ever see me, else you will lose me for ever. And now, when I have left you, go straight home, and never follow me again. Wait till I come to you. Good-bye, my little Tessa: I will come.”

There was no help for it. He turned away and did not look back. He had no time to spare for seeing how she bore it; and he would not have been strong enough to bear the sight without making some promise that would entangle him further. When he did look round, he was already in the Via de’ Benci, where the bridge lay out of view. Tessa, obedient in her misery, would do what he had told her.

So the immediate difficulty ended; but it left in Tito’s mind a vexation, like a sour note lingering under music—this return of Tessa at the very instant when it was impossible to end all difficulty by undeceiving her. Yet his spirit was penetrated now by the hope of a first love and by all the prospects that flattered ambition. The future necessity of grieving Tessa was to him scarcely more than a faint cry of some small suffering creature in a thicket, heard by a merry cavalcade riding in sun on the open plain.

When, for the second time that day, he hastened over the Ponte Rubaconte, the thought of her did not perceptibly dull his happiness. He was muffled in his mantle—less against the cold than against the notice his dainty apparel might invite. He went up the stone steps by twos, and said to Maso, who met him,

“Where is the damigella?”

“In the library; she is quite ready, and Monna Brigida and Messer Bernardo are already there with Ser Braccio, but none of the rest of the company.”

“Ask her to give me a few minutes alone; I will await her in the salotto.”

He entered a room newly fitted up in the utmost contrast with the half-pallid, half-sombre tints of the library. The walls were bright with caprices—nymphs and loves sporting under blue sky among flowers and birds. There were red leather seats, a central table, two tall white vases; and a young faun, flute at his lips, modelled by a promising youth named Michelangelo Buonarotti, stood as if he had drawn a breath of outdoor air into the chamber. Even within doors, one had the sensation of sunlight and open space—the promise of pleasure as immediate as the scent of crushed violets.

Tito kept his mantle about him and looked toward the door.

Romola entered—white and gold—more than ever like a tall lily. Her white silk was bound by a golden girdle that fell with large tassels; above it the rippling gold of her hair, and above that again the white mist of her long veil, fastened on her brow by a band of pearls—Bernardo del Nero’s gift—and now parted off her face so that it floated backward. In the torchless daylight of the room, she seemed to carry her own light, like something refined out of purity and pride.

“Regina mia!” Tito took her hand and kissed it, still keeping his mantle round him. He stepped back, as lovers will, to look again at what they can hardly believe is theirs; and she stood, calm in delight, with that exquisite self-consciousness which rises under an admiring gaze.

“Romola, will you show me the next room now?” he said, checking himself, remembering that time might be short. “You said I should see it when you had arranged everything.”

Without speaking she led the way into a long narrow room, brightly painted also, but here only with birds and flowers—no frolic of naked deities, only leaves and wings and blossoms, as if the walls meant to breathe softly around more intimate things. The furniture was old. In an open cabinet between the two narrow windows were faded objects for a woman’s use or ornament, placed with careful reverence; above the cabinet hung the portrait of Romola’s mother. And below it, on the cabinet’s top, stood the crucifix Romola had brought from San Marco—dark, severe, a memory of sorrow set where daily eyes must meet it.

“I have brought something under my mantle,” Tito said, smiling. He threw off the large loose garment and showed the little tabernacle painted by Piero di Cosimo. The painter, in his strange, fantastic way, had carried out Tito’s intention charmingly, and so atoned for long delay.

“Do you know what this is for, my Romola?” Tito took her hand and led her toward the cabinet. “It is a little shrine, which is to hide away from you for ever that remembrancer of sadness. You have done with sadness now; and we will bury all images of it—bury them in a tomb of joy. See!”

As he took hold of the crucifix, a slight quiver passed over Romola’s face. But she did not resist; indeed she herself wished to still certain importunate memories and questionings that flitted like unexplained shadows across her happier thought. Tito opened the triptych and placed the crucifix within the central space; then closed it, turned the key, and set the little tabernacle where the crucifix had stood.

“Now, Romola, look and see if you are satisfied with the portraits old Piero has made of us. Is it not a dainty device? and the credit of choosing it is mine.”

Romola looked, and her eyes grew moist with joy. “Ah! it is you—it is perfect!” she said, gazing at the miniature Bacchus with his purple clusters. “And I am Ariadne, and you are crowning me! Yes, it is true, Tito; you have crowned my poor life.”

They stood hand in hand, looking at their imaged selves; and yet the living presence was more beautiful—she, lily-white and golden; he, dark and glowing above his purple tunic bordered with red.

“And it was our good strange Piero who painted it?” Romola said. “Did you put it into his head to paint me as Antigone, that he might have my likeness for this?”

“No,” Tito answered; “it was he who made my getting leave for him to paint you and your father, a condition of his doing this for me.”

“Ah! I see now what it was you gave up your precious ring for. I perceived you had some cunning plan to give me pleasure.”

Tito did not blench. Romola’s illusions about him, and the tenderness that made them, had long ceased to trouble him; they pleased him like the taste of honey that costs no labour.

“I might have spared my ring,” he said lightly; “Piero will accept no money from me; he thinks himself paid by painting you. And now, while I am away, you will look every day at those pretty symbols of our life together—the ship on the calm sea, and the ivy that never withers, and those Loves that have left off wounding us and shower soft petals that are like our kisses; and the leopards and tigers—they are the troubles of your life that are all quelled now; and the strange sea-monsters, with their merry eyes—let us see—they are the dull passages in the heavy books, which have begun to be amusing since we have sat by each other.”

“Tito mio!” Romola’s voice half laughed with love; then, with a sudden practical longing as if she could not bear even a small barrier between her and what was now theirs, she held out her hand. “But you will give me the key?”

“Not at all!” Tito said, with playful decision; and opening his scarsella he dropped the little key into it. “I shall drown it in the Arno.”

“But if I ever wanted to look at the crucifix again?”

“Ah! for that very reason it is hidden—hidden by these images of youth and joy.”

He pressed a light kiss on her brow, and she said no more. Strong souls submit when they feel no valid reason to resist, and her desire at that moment was for harmony, not debate.

They went then to join the waiting company; and presently a dignified little procession moved over the Ponte Rubaconte toward Santa Croce. Bardo, unaccustomed for years to leave his house, walked with a more timid step than usual; and the slow pace suited well the gouty dignity of Messer Bartolommeo Scala, who honoured the occasion with his presence, bringing his daughter Alessandra. It was customary, at a sposalizio, to have a long troop of kindred and friends—so long that laws had once been needed to limit the number to four hundred, two hundred on each side, because the guests must be feasted after this first ceremony as well as after the nozze itself, and the very beginning of matrimony could ruin a man as surely as a bad harvest. But Bardo, poor and proud, had kept himself free from appearing to trade on his family name; he would have no invitations given on the mere strength of friendship. The modest train of twenty behind the sposi were, with a few exceptions, friends chosen on personal grounds.

Bernardo del Nero walked as a vanguard before Bardo, who was led on the right by Tito, while Romola held her father’s other hand. Bardo had himself been married at Santa Croce, and insisted that Romola should be betrothed and married there, rather than in the little church of Santa Lucia near their house; for in his mind the great church stood always complete, with that final hope within it—that burial might one day be granted him among the Florentines who had deserved well. The way was short and direct and lay somewhat aloof from the loudest riot, if only they could return before the dances or shows began in the great piazza.

The west was red as they passed the bridge, laying a mellow light on the little procession and giving it a touch of solemnity, deepened by the blind father in their midst. After the ceremony, when Tito and Romola came out upon the broad steps with the golden links of destiny on their fingers, evening had deepened into a struggling starlight, and the servants had lit their torches. The flames hissed faintly in the cold air; and in the pause after church-words the city’s soundscape changed—less laughter near at hand, more distant chanting borne in one long drift, as if the very stones were listening.

A strange, dreary chant met their ears—a wail like a Miserere. At the far end of the piazza a stream of people seemed driven backward by something approaching from the Borgo de’ Greci.

“It is one of their masqued processions, I suppose,” Tito said. He was now alone with Romola, while Bernardo took charge of Bardo.

As he spoke, there rose into view, high above heads, a huge ghastly image of Winged Time with scythe and hour-glass, ringed by his winged children, the Hours. He stood upon a tall car covered wholly in black; the bullocks that drew it were black as well, their horns alone showing white above the gloom. In the sombre shadow of the houses, to those who saw from a distance, it was as if Time and his brood floated through the air—apparitions rather than pageant. Behind came what looked like a troop of the sheeted dead, gliding above the blackness; and as they glided slowly they chanted in that wailing strain, a sound that seemed to drain warmth out of the skin.

Cold horror seized Romola. In the first instant it was as if her brother’s vision—never effaced from her mind—were half fulfilled. She clung to Tito. He divined what rose within her and spoke quickly, as if his voice could shut a door against dread.

“What dismal fooling sometimes pleases your Florentines! Doubtless this is an invention of Piero di Cosimo, who loves such grim merriment.”

“Tito, I wish it had not happened,” she said. “It will deepen the images of that vision which I would fain be rid of.”

“Nay, Romola, you will look only at the images of our happiness now. I have locked all sadness away from you.”

“But it is still there—it is only hidden,” Romola murmured, scarcely aware she spoke, as if some part of her listened past his words to that chant fading down the piazza.

“See, they are all gone now!” Tito said, eager, drawing her attention to the open space where the black car had passed. “You will forget this ghastly mummery when we are in the light, and can see each other’s eyes. My Ariadne must never look backward now—only forward to Easter, when she will triumph with her Care-dispeller.”
