"I pity the man who travels from Dan to Beersheba and declares it all barren,' and yet, for those unwilling to nurture its gifts, the entire world remains desolate.' — STERNE: *A Sentimental Journey*

To label Daniel Deronda as merely "romantic" would be a profound misunderstanding of his nature. Beneath a surface of quiet composure and self-restraint burned a fierce, internal fire that allowed him to discover the poetry of everyday existence where others saw only routine. Perhaps true romance and poetic wonder are as abundant today as they ever were, vanishing only from the hearts of those whose spirits are too heavy, too indifferent to perceive them. They exist effortlessly beside the cold precision of science and within the clatter of railway cars, waiting only for a soul capable of awe to awaken them. Consider the vast machinery of the heavens above and the tender, nurturing breast of a mother below; how could these create beauty for a mind devoid of reverence and compassion? For Deronda, however, the chance encounter with Mirah felt as stirring as any mythic reunion of Orestes or Rinaldo. He sat awake for nearly the entire night, reliving the moment he first saw her by the riverbank with a startling, fresh vividness. When he attempted to read a book to quiet his racing thoughts, the ink seemed to dissolve, leaving behind a transparent screen through which he could see and hear every detail of those two hours anew. He saw not only what had happened, but endless possibilities of what might follow, fueled by a warm blood of hope and terrifying anticipation.

Something deep within his own history caused Mirah's desperate search for her mother to grip his imagination with unusual force. His initial urge was pure altruism: if living relatives existed in London, there were methods, subtle as a scientific experiment, to locate them. Yet, as he thought, the shadows of his own past bled into his concern for her. The desire to know his own mother was always haunted by a paralyzing dread, and now he imagined that Mirah's discovery could bring exactly that kind of horror. In the boat, she had spoken of her mother and brother with innocence, calling them "good"; but Deronda feared that ten or twelve years of separation could have twisted that goodness into something unrecognizable. Worse still, his ignorance of the Jewish community weighed heavily on him. Though he generally sided with the oppressed, his practical knowledge of Jews was limited to the ugliest stereotypes—the thieves lurking in alleys, the hypocrites in fine clothes. He assumed the educated had abandoned their faith to blend into society, and he felt a natural aversion to the idea that Mirah's lineage might drag her down into the squalor he associated with her people. He pictured, with chilling clarity, the reality behind her sweet, purified face: a dingy street, a hawk-eyed, unkempt woman haggling over a starving girl's clothes, or a slick, familiar young Jew eager to please gentlemen with his "knowledge." These were not insults born of malice, but the products of a mind governed by dread. If Mirah's family had been Christian, his fears would likely have been grounded in broader knowledge, but the unknown magnified the terror.

And what, then, was to be done for Mirah? She needed shelter and protection, and his chivalric spirit demanded that he secure allies beyond himself immediately. Yet, a strange contradiction held him back. The sheer power of the impression she made upon him led him to believe she must remain entirely independent of him. Vague, fantastical visions of their future lingered, fueling an anxiety stronger than any logical motive: he felt compelled to ensure that anyone observing his actions understood, from the start, the full history of his relationship with her. Secrecy regarding life's great ties and obligations repelled him; he hated the way a spell of intertwined sensitivities prevented him from revealing the truth. He had vowed to himself that since the truths that disgrace humanity are rarely of our own making, he would never use them to shame another person. This promise terrified him now, lest he falter into the philosophy that excuses the world by claiming it contains nothing better than one's own conduct.

For a fleeting moment, he resolved to pour out the entire adventure to Sir Hugo and Lady Mallinger at breakfast the following day. But the possibility that a new revelation might emerge during his next visit to Mrs. Meyrick silenced the impulse. Instead, he let sleep claim him, deciding to wait until he had seen her again before speaking a word to anyone else.
