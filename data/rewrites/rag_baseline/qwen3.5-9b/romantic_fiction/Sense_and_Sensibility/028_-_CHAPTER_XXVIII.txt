Nothing occurred during the next three or four days to make Elinor regret her decision to consult her mother; Willoughby neither appeared nor sent word. By the conclusion of this period, the sisters were engaged to accompany Lady Middleton to a gathering, an event from which Mrs. Jennings was excused due to the indisposition of her youngest daughter. For this occasion, Marianne, utterly dispirited and indifferent to her own presentation, prepared with a demeanor suggesting equal apathy toward attendance or absence. Following tea, she sat by the drawing-room fire until Lady Middleton's arrival, remaining motionless and lost in internal turmoil, oblivious to her sister's proximity. Only when summoned to the threshold did she start as though forgetting the expectation entirely.

Upon reaching the destination, they alighted from the carriages as the line permitted, ascended the stairs, and heard their names announced audibly from landing to landing before entering a hall brilliantly illuminated and packed with guests. The atmosphere was stiflingly hot. Having paid their obligatory courtesy to the hostess, they mingled in the throng, absorbing the discomfort their presence inevitably exacerbated. After a brief interval of minimal interaction, Lady Middleton seated herself at a game of Cassino. Since Marianne lacked the spirit to move, Elinor and her sister secured seats nearby. Long had they not occupied these positions before Elinor spotted Willoughby, standing mere yards away in animated discussion with a strikingly fashionable young woman. He quickly caught her gaze, offering a bow without attempting speech or approaching Marianne, though he clearly saw her, and resumed his discourse. Elinor instinctively glanced at Marianne to gauge her reaction. It was then that Marianne first perceived him; her entire countenance flushed with sudden joy, and she would have surged toward him had her sister not seized her arm.

"Good heavens!" Marianne cried, "He is there—he is there! Why does he not look at me? Why cannot I speak to him?"

"Compose yourself," Elinor urged urgently, "and do not broadcast your emotions to every guest. Perhaps he has not yet registered your presence."

This reassurance was futile; Marianne could neither believe it nor achieve composure. She sat in an agony of impatience that distorted every feature. Finally, Willoughby turned and regarded them both. Marianne sprang up, invoking his name with affectionate emphasis, and extended her hand. He approached, directing his words primarily to Elinor as if deliberately avoiding Marianne's gaze, determined not to acknowledge her posture, and inquired hurriedly after Mrs. Dashwood and the duration of their stay in town. Elinor, stripped of all presence of mind by such a direct address, remained speechless. Yet her sister's feelings erupted instantly. Her face burned crimson, and in a voice trembling with profound emotion, she demanded, "Good God! Willoughby, what is the meaning of this? Have you not received my letters? Will you not shake hands with me?"

He could no longer evade contact, yet her touch seemed painful to him; he held her hand for only a fleeting moment. Throughout this exchange, he visibly struggled to regain control. Elinor observed his features and noted a gradual shift toward tranquility. After a brief pause, he spoke calmly. "I took the liberty of calling in Berkeley Street last Tuesday and deeply regretted failing to find you and Mrs. Jennings at home. I trust my card was not misplaced."

"But have you not received my notes?" Marianne shrieked in wild anxiety. "Some error must exist—some terrible mistake. What is the meaning? Tell me, Willoughby; for heaven's sake, explain."

He offered no reply. His complexion altered, and all prior embarrassment resurfaced. However, upon catching the eye of the young lady with whom he had previously conversed, he felt compelled to exert himself anew. Recovering his composure, he stated, "Yes, I received the notification of your arrival in town, which you were so kind as to send," delivered a slight bow, and hastened away to join his companion.

Marianne, now pallid and unable to stand, sank back into her chair. Anticipating imminent fainting, Elinor attempted to shield her from public view while administering lavender water. "Go to him, Elinor," Marianne commanded as soon as she could speak. "Force him to come to me. I must see him again—I must speak to him instantly. I cannot rest; I shall find no peace until this is clarified. Some dreadful misunderstanding must be resolved. Go to him this moment."

"How can that be achieved?" Elinor countered gently. "No, my dearest Marianne, you must wait. This is not the setting for explanations. Wait until tomorrow."

With difficulty, she restrained Marianne from pursuing him personally. Persuading her sister to curb her agitation and maintain an appearance of composure until they could speak privately proved impossible; Marianne continued to succumb incessantly to her misery, emitting exclamations of wretchedness. Shortly thereafter, Elinor observed Willoughby exiting the room via the staircase. Informing Marianne of his departure, she argued that further communication was impossible that evening, using this as leverage to urge calm. Instantly, Marianne begged Elinor to plead with Lady Middleton to take them home, claiming she was too miserable to endure another minute. Though Lady Middleton was mid-game, she displayed sufficient politeness to acquiesce immediately, transferring her cards to a friend. They departed as soon as a carriage could be located.

Scarcely a word was exchanged during the return journey to Berkeley Street. Marianne existed in a silent agony, too overwhelmed even for tears. Fortunately, Mrs. Jennings had not yet returned, allowing the sisters to proceed directly to their private chambers, where hartshorn briefly restored Marianne's faculties. Undressed and tucked into bed, she expressed a desire for solitude. Elinor withdrew, leaving her sister to her grief while awaiting Mrs. Jennings' return, granting herself the rare opportunity to reflect on the past.

She could not doubt that a genuine engagement had once subsisted between Willoughby and Marianne, nor that Willoughby was now weary of it. Regardless of Marianne's lingering hopes, Elinor attributed such behavior solely to a fundamental change in sentiment, rejecting notions of mistake or misapprehension. Her indignation would have been far greater had she not witnessed the evident embarrassment that suggested a consciousness of his own misconduct, preventing her from believing him capable of playing with her sister's affections without ulterior motives. While absence might have dulled his regard and convenience prompted him to suppress it, the existence of such regard in the past remained undeniable to her.

Regarding Marianne, Elinor reflected with deepest sorrow on the acute pain of their current reunion and the even severer suffering that awaited them in its inevitable aftermath. In contrast, her own situation appeared more tenable; though separated from Edward in the future, she could continue to esteem him, finding support in her mind. Conversely, every circumstance seemed to conspire to intensify Marianne's misery, sealing her fate in a final, irreconcilable separation from Willoughby.
