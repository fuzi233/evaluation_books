"Croyez-vous m'avoir humiliée pour m'avoir appris que la terre tourne autour du soleil? Je vous jure que je ne m'en estime pas moins."
—FONTENELLE: *Pluralité des Mondes*.

Those words of lofty criticism had cut a fresh, stinging wound in Gwendolen's pride. She refused to acknowledge the shame she felt regarding her lack of musical training compared to Miss Arrowpoint. It was beneath her dignity to confess that she could not confidently question Herr Klesmer's artistic judgment, nor to admit to herself that every meeting with Miss Arrowpoint stirred a sharp, unwanted jealousy. It was not merely because Miss Arrowpoint was an heiress; it was because a girl who appeared physically unremarkable—slight of figure, features unassuming, complexion sallow—possessed a mental superiority that defied explanation. There was an exasperating thoroughness in Miss Arrowpoint's musical command, a fastidious discrimination in her tastes that commanded awe and prevented genuine admiration. One might suspect this quiet young lady secretly regarded Miss Harleth's talents as mediocre, yet she masked this judgment behind a veil of perfect kindness.

Gwendolen avoided dwelling on facts that cast an unfavorable light upon her own standing. The "Musical Magus" who had recently broadened her horizons was frequently away, traveling between London and Quetcham, creating opportunities for his opinions to shift toward greater admiration. Meanwhile, the evident pleasure her singing garnered at Brackenshaw Castle, the Firs, and elsewhere restored her equanimity. She preferred trust in approval over the sting of objection, content not to possess the parching thirst for perfection that drove others. Perhaps it would have been rash to claim she was inwardly exceptional, save for her rare grace of movement and a certain daring that gave piquancy to a common egoistic ambition. Though the set of her head suggested a hunger for supremacy, it was merely a matter of how that supremacy was pursued. For Gwendolen, a girl whose passion for being remarkable adhered strictly to the bounds of highest breeding and freedom from sordid needs, her internal rebellion was real enough. Yet, she lacked the speculative theories of the reformist woman; she satirized them. Her horizon remained that of the genteel romance, where the heroine's soul pours out in journals filled with vague power and rebellion, while her life moves strictly within the sphere of fashion. If she wandered into a swamp, the pathos lay in her wearing satin shoes. Nature and society had provided wirework restraints sufficient to hold a soul burning with a sense of what the universe was not captive within the ordinary forms, ensuring she did nothing particular.

Even in the novelty of the first winter at Offendene, Gwendolen felt this commonplace result threatening her. She was clear that she did not wish to live like ordinary young ladies, but unclear on how to assert her freedom or what specific acts would define her alternative life. Offendene served as a good backdrop, but the neighborhood offered little excitement beyond her initial presentations. Between sallies of satire that had offended Mrs. Arrowpoint, she filled the days with girlish devices. Her strongest assertion of individuality involved omitting Alice's lessons—believing Alice would excel in ignorance—and employing her with Miss Merry and the housemaid to arrange dramatic costumes for charades and theatrical pieces she intended to produce by force of will. Having never truly acted beyond *tableaux vivans* at school, she felt assured of her ability, fueled by memories of the Théâtre Français and her mother's praise of Rachel. She often dreamed of becoming an actress like Rachel, convinced her superior beauty outweighed the thin Jewish actress's. During the wet days before Christmas, they prepared Greek, Oriental, and composite costumes. Gwendolen attitudinized and speechified before a domestic audience, even pressing the housekeeper to swell the applause, though the housekeeper wisely declined a second invitation, noting that Miss Harleth looked far more regal in her own dress than in a baggy outfit with bare arms.

"Do I look as well as Rachel, mamma?" Gwendolen asked one day, displaying herself in a Greek dress with tragic intent.

"You have better arms than Rachel," Mrs. Davilow replied. "Your arms would do for anything, Gwen. But your voice is not so tragic as hers; it is not so deep."

"I can make it deeper, if I like," Gwendolen said provisionally, then decided firmly, "I think a higher voice is more tragic: it is more feminine; and the more feminine a woman is, the more tragic it seems when she does desperate actions."

"There may be something in that," Mrs. Davilow said languidly. "But I don't know what good there is in making one's blood creep. And if there is anything horrible to be done, I should like it to be left to the men."

"Oh, mamma, you are so dreadfully prosaic!" Gwendolen exclaimed. "As if all the great poetic criminals were not women! I think the men are poor cautious creatures."

"Well, dear, and you—who are afraid to be alone in the night—I don't think you would be very bold in crime, thank God."

"I am not talking about reality, mamma," Gwendolen said impatiently.

Her mother leaving the room, Gwendolen turned quickly to her cousin Anna. "Ask my uncle to let us get up some charades at the rectory. Mr. Middleton and Warham could act with us—just for practice. Mamma says it will not do to have Mr. Middleton consulting and rehearsing here. He is a stick, but we could give him suitable parts. Do ask, or else I will."

"Oh, not till Rex comes," Anna interjected eagerly. "He is so clever, and such a dear old thing, and he will act Napoleon looking over the sea. He looks just like Napoleon. Rex can do anything."

"I don't in the least believe in your Rex, Anna," Gwendolen laughed. "He will turn out to be like those wretched blue and yellow water-colors of his which you hang up in your bedroom and worship."

"Very well, you will see," Anna said stubbornly. "It is not that I know what is clever, but he has got a scholarship already, and papa says he will get a fellowship, and nobody is better at games. He is cleverer than Mr. Middleton, and everybody but you calls Mr. Middleton clever."

"So he may be in a dark-lantern sort of way. But he *is* a stick. If he had to say, 'Perdition catch my soul, but I do love her,' he would say it in just the same tone as, 'Here endeth the second lesson.'"

"Oh, Gwendolen!" Anna cried, shocked. "And it is very unkind of you to speak so of him, for he admires you very much. I heard Warham say one day to mamma, 'Middleton is regularly spooney upon Gwendolen.' She was very angry with him; but I know what it means. It is what they say at college for being in love."

"How can I help it?" Gwendolen said, somewhat contemptuously. "Perdition catch my soul if I love *him*."

"No, of course; papa, I think, would not wish it. And he is to go away soon. But it makes me sorry when you ridicule him."

"What shall you do to me when I ridicule Rex?" Gwendolen asked wickedly.

"Now, Gwendolen, dear, you *will not*?" Anna said, her eyes filling with tears. "I could not bear it. But there really is nothing in him to ridicule. Only you may find out things. For no one ever thought of laughing at Mr. Middleton before you. Every one said he was nice-looking, and his manners perfect. I am sure I have always been frightened at him because of his learning and his square-cut coat, and his being a nephew of the bishop's, and all that. But you will not ridicule Rex—promise me." Anna ended with a beseeching look that touched Gwendolen.

"You are a dear little coz," she said, gently pinching the tip of Anna's chin. "I don't ever want to do anything that will vex you. Especially if Rex is to make everything come off—charades and everything."

When Rex finally arrived, the animation he brought to Offendene and the rectory, along with his ready partnership in Gwendolen's plans, dispelled any inclination for ridicule that wasn't open and flattering. He was a fine, open-hearted youth, with a handsome face resembling his father's and Anna's, yet softer in expression and larger in scale. He possessed a bright, healthy, loving nature that found joy in ordinary innocence, leaving vice no temptation. His knowledge of vice lay in the outer courts of his mind, inaccessible to serious repulsion. He viewed vicious habits as "stupid stuff" done by "some fellows," keeping them at a distance. He returned Anna's affection fully, as expected of a brother whose pleasures exceeded hers, and he had never known a stronger love.

The cousins spent their time together, chiefly at Offendene, where Gwendolen held sway. Whatever she wished became a ruling purpose for Rex. The charades proceeded according to plan, alongside impromptu scenes. It was at Offendene that rehearsals and presentations took place, with Mrs. Davilow seeing no objection to Mr. Middleton joining now that Rex was present, especially as his services were indispensable.
