One bright afternoon, Mr. Harry Warrington made his usual way to the White Horse ordinary, a well-known haunt of country gentlemen and men of fashion in Tunbridge Wells. As he entered, he was greeted with familiar faces, among them the jovial and ever-cheerful Parson Sampson, whose presence always brought laughter and liveliness to the table. The parson, rosy-cheeked and full of spirits, was regaling the company with tales of London life—gossip from Kensington Palace, whispers from Pall Mall, news of Mr. Byng’s imprisonment, the latest odds at Newmarket, and who had become the toast of Covent Garden. Though not always accurate, these stories were rich in colour and charm, and more than sufficient for the tastes of the rural gentry gathered there.

The names might be mixed—Lord Squanderfield courting Mrs. Lucy instead of Mrs. Polly, Lady Jane smitten with an actor rather than Lady Mary—but such details mattered little. What mattered was the wit, the sparkle, the sense of the world beyond the countryside. And so the parson chattered on, drinking glass after glass, cheered on by his audience, who insisted he keep pouring.

A century ago, such a clergyman—a man equally at home in church, tavern, theatre, or gaming house—was not uncommon in England. He could ride with the best in the hunting field, sing a song after the play at Covent Garden, or call a hand at the card table with confidence. When Harry arrived, the parson’s face flushed deeper still—perhaps from claret, perhaps from modesty—and he whispered to the squire beside him, “Maxima debetur,” before leaping up, stumbling slightly in his haste, to greet the Virginian.

“My dear sir! My very dear sir! Conqueror of spades, clubs, and hearts!” the chaplain exclaimed, embracing Harry with warmth. “I am overjoyed to see you looking so fresh and strong!”

Harry returned the greeting with genuine pleasure. He admired the parson’s good humour and healthy glow. The squire in the drab coat, who knew Harry well, offered him a seat beside himself and called out for the parson to resume his tale about Lord Ogle and the grocer’s wife—though the place remained unnamed, as the sentence was cut short by a shout of pain: the parson had trodden on someone’s gouty toe.

Apologizing hastily, Sampson turned back to Harry and began sharing news from Castlewood: my Lord Castlewood sent his affectionate regards and had instructed him to come to Tunbridge to watch over the young gentleman’s conduct; Lady Viscountess and Lady Fanny had gone to Harrogate for the waters; Mr. Will had won at Newmarket and was now visiting the Duke; and Molly, the housemaid, was heartbroken over Gumbo, Harry’s valet.

Indeed, all the gossip of the estate had followed Sampson to the ordinary. He spoke proudly of Harry’s reputation throughout Hampshire—of his successes at Tunbridge, in every sense of the word—and expressed the hopes of many noble families that the young Virginian would not be spoiled by city life. His aunt, Madame Bernstein, would be pleased, he said, if Harry remained true to his roots.

As the guests left one by one, Harry found himself seated alone with the chaplain, their conversation growing warmer and more animated. The parson, though already well into his wine, declared cheerfully that “plenty” did not mean he couldn’t have “plenty more.” Toast after toast was raised, and Harry, now in high spirits, became generous and kind-hearted.

“Why should you pay for an inn?” he asked. “There’s a room at my lodgings—come stay with me.”

Gumbo was summoned to prepare the chamber, and Harry insisted the parson visit his stables. There, amid the scent of hay and horseflesh, Sampson recognized his own old mount—the very horse Harry had won from him in a game of cards. The beast whinnied with joy, nuzzling against the chaplain’s coat. Harry laughed and declared, “By Jupiter, Sampson, that horse is yours again—I’ll give it to you!”

The gift was accepted with emotion. The parson seized Harry’s hand, blessed him, then threw his arms around the horse’s neck and wept—not from sorrow, but from pure delight, tears mingled with Bordeaux and gratitude.

Arm in arm, they walked to Madame Bernstein’s lodging, bringing with them the fragrance of stable and saddle leather. Their cheeks were flushed, their eyes bright—evidence of a day well spent.

Madame Bernstein received her nephew’s chaplain kindly. She enjoyed Sampson’s bold jokes and lively talk, much like she relished a spicy dish or a new creation from her cook. But one pastime never grew stale: cards. “The cards don’t cheat,” she often said. “A bad hand tells you the truth plainly. And nothing flatters like a fine set of trumps.”

When in good humour, she would laughingly ask Sampson to say grace before dinner. At first, the chaplain hesitated to join the card games—he claimed her ladyship’s stakes were too high, and his pockets already empty from losing to the young Virginian. But thanks to Harry’s generosity, he soon had enough coin to take his place at the table.

Harry welcomed Sampson not only as a friend but as a mentor. He adapted quickly to English life—dressing with elegance, wearing black suits of impeccable cut, lace and linen of the finest quality. The gay Duchess of Queensberry once remarked, “With a star and ribbon, his stocking down, and his hair over his shoulder, he’d make a charming Hamlet. And I doubt he’s been the death of a dozen Ophelias—here and among the Indians.”

His possessions grew: fine shaving plates from toy-shop women, brocade bedgowns for lounging, French watches with painted backs and diamond settings, enamelled snuffboxes, swords, walking canes. A daily levee of grooms, jockeys, and tradesmen waited in his anteroom, admitted one by one by Gumbo, the groom of the chambers.

No single servant could manage such a household. Yet Harry took care to learn the arts befitting a gentleman. A French fencing master and dancing master taught him during the season, and within weeks he handled the foil with skill and grace. Lady Maria, herself a fine dancer, confessed with a sigh that no gentleman at court could walk a minuet more elegantly than Mr. Warrington.

Riding, however, he preferred his Virginian style. Though he took a few lessons from a local master, he declared that no one in England rode better than Colonel George Washington of Mount Vernon.

Sampson, meanwhile, found himself in better quarters than he had known in years. He was a man of the world, and his stories—real or imagined—delighted Harry. Young men look up to older ones, admiring their seasoned wit and worn-in anecdotes, drenched in the wine of countless dinners. To Harry, Sampson’s tales were fresh and enchanting.

In those days, such men existed—older, worldly figures who delighted in guiding young noblemen through society, teaching them manners, introducing them to gaming tables, acting as mentors at theatres and balls. They drank lemonade while the young lord sipped Burgundy, and staggered home with clear heads while their charges reeled. Mr. Sampson was one such man—perhaps the last of his kind.

And so, under Harry’s roof, he became not just a guest, but a tutor, confidant, and secretary. This is evident in the following letter, preserved in the Warrington family papers:

---

**To Mrs. Esmond Warrington of Castlewood,
At Her House at Richmond, Virginia**

Mrs. Bligh’s Lodgings, Pantiles, Tunbridge Wells
August 25th, 1756

Your letter of 20 June, delivered by Mr. Trail of Bristol, has reached me safely, and I thank you most sincerely for your kindness and wise counsel. I shall cherish the remembrances of our dear home, and I love it no less for having visited the ancestral land of our family in England.

I wrote to you by the last monthly packet, informing you of the small accident I suffered on the journey here, and of the kind friends who took me in. Since then, I have enjoyed the pleasant weather and excellent company, making many acquaintances among the nobility—friends whose company I trust you will not disapprove.

Among them are the illustrious Earl of Chesterfield, former Ambassador to Holland and Viceroy of Ireland; the Earl of March and Ruglen, heir to the Duke of Queensberry; and her Grace the Duchess, famed beauty of Queen Anne’s reign, who remembers my grandfather at Court. These and many others attend my aunt’s assemblies, which are among the most popular in this bustling resort.

On my way here, I stayed at Westerham with Lieut.-Gen. Wolfe, who served with my grandfather and General Webb in Marlborough’s wars. He has a son, Lieut.-Col. James Wolfe, engaged to Miss Lowther of the North—beautiful and well-regarded. Though only thirty, he is held in high esteem throughout the army, having served with distinction under His Royal Highness the Duke wherever British arms were deployed.

I thank you for your message regarding the quarterly allowance of £52 10s., which Mr. Trail will provide. I am not currently in need of funds, and through careful management—necessary, I admit, for maintaining horses, Gumbo, and the proper attire and equipage for a gentleman of our standing—I hope to preserve my credit without burdening you.

The clothes I brought from Virginia will serve me well for years, especially with care. While not quite as fine as those worn here, they are excellent for daily use. I may purchase a few finer shirts for grand occasions, but that is all.

I am grateful that I have not yet needed your famous family pills. Gumbo, however, has benefited greatly from them—he grows fat and cheerful on English beef, ale, and air. He sends his humble respects to you and asks Mrs. Mountain to remember him to all the servants, especially Dinah and Lily, for whom he bought posy rings at the Tunbridge Fair.

Beyond enjoying the pleasures of the place, I assure you I have not neglected my education. I have taken lessons in fencing and dancing. And when the Reverend Mr. Sampson, my Lord Castlewood’s chaplain, came here to drink the waters, he kindly agreed to take up residence at my lodgings. He breakfasts with me each morning, and we read together—Mr. Rapin’s *History*, Dr. Barrow’s sermons, Shakespeare, Mr. Pope’s translation of Homer, and (in French) a delightful collection of Arabian tales.

Several learned men have also been here—among them Mr. Richardson, author of the books you and Mountain and my dear brother so loved. When I told him his works were cherished in your library, he asked me to convey his respectful greetings to you. He is a short, stout man, with little fire visible in his eyes or manner.

My aunt and cousin, Lady Maria, send their warmest regards to you, and I enclose a note for Mountain. I remain, honoured madam,

---

*From my son. Received October 15 at Richmond. Sent: 16 jars preserved peaches, 224 lbs. best tobacco, 24 finest hams, per Royal William of Liverpool; 8 jars peaches, 12 hams for my nephew, the Rt. Honourable the Earl of Castlewood; 4 jars, 6 hams for the Baroness Bernstein; same for Mrs. Lambert of Oakhurst, Surrey; ½ cwt. tobacco. Packet of Infallible Family Pills for Gumbo. My Papa’s large silver-gilt shoe-buckles for H., and red silver-laced saddle-cloth.*

*What do you mien, you silly old Mountain, by sending an order for your poor old divadends dew at Xmas? I'd have you to know I don't want your 7l. 10, and have toar your order up into 1000 bitts. I've plenty of money. But I'm obleaged to you all same. A kiss to Fanny from—Your loving HARRY.*

*This note, which I desired M. to show to me, proves she hath a good heart, and wished to show gratitude to the family by giving up her half-yearly dividend (on £500 at 3%) to my boy. Hence I reprimanded her gently for daring to send money to Mr. E. Warrington without my knowledge. The note to Mountain is not so well spelled as the letter to me.*
*Mem.: Write to Revd. Mr. Sampson to inquire what theological books he reads with H. Recommend Law, Baxter, Drelincourt. Request H. to recite his catechism to Mr. S., which he has never fully mastered. By next ship: 3 jars peaches, ½ cwt. tobacco, hams for Mr. S.*

---

Time has long since passed the Warringtons into memory. Yet one wonders: how is it that two letters sent together—one carefully composed, the other rough and playful—could differ so much in spelling and grammar?

Perhaps Harry, eager to impress his mother, revised his letter with the help of a tutor or friend, correcting minor errors like “who I found and whom took me in.” But the note to Mrs. Mountain—written freely, without oversight—remained unpolished. Perhaps a milliner caught his eye, or a dancing bear passed by, or a jockey showed off a horse beneath his window. Such distractions are common even today.

And why should Harry write with equal care to a servant woman as to his mother? That, too, is natural. We all speak differently to different people. The letter to his mother is polite, measured, respectful—even if it omits certain truths. The note to Mrs. Mountain is honest, affectionate, and slightly mischievous.

And so, let us not judge too harshly. Every generation writes its letters with some reserve, some pretence. Parents wish their children to appear virtuous, obedient, and dignified. Children, in turn, may hide their follies, their joys, their secrets.

There must be distance between parent and child. There must be a gentle hypocrisy—a decorous fiction—that preserves dignity and affection. In the home, we are kings and queens. We expect respect, admiration, silence when we sleep, laughter at our jokes. But outside, we are free—free to dance, to tumble, to live.

So when someone says, “It was immoral of Harry to write such a prim letter while living a merry life,” let us smile and reply: “Yes, dear grandmamma, it was wrong of him. But I suppose you never had your fun when you were young.”

Of course she didn’t. And the sun never shone, the blossoms never bloomed, the blood never danced, the fiddles never sang in her springtime.

Eh, Babet! Mon lait de poule et mon bonnet de nuit!
Ho, Betty! My gruel and my slippers!

Go, ye frisky, merry souls! Dance, laugh, eat cakes and ale!
