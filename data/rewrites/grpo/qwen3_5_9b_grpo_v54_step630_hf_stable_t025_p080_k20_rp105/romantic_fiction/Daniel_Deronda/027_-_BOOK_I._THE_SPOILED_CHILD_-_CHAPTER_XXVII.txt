Desire trims the sails, but it is circumstance that provides the wind.

Outside, Grandcourt rode his magnificent black stallion, Yarico, with his groom trailing behind on Criterion, enjoying the leisurely journey from Diplow to Offendene. Inside, however, the air was thick with anticipation. Gwendolen sat before the mirror while her mother, Mrs. Davilow, gathered the cascading mass of her daughter's light-brown hair into a neat coil.

"Only gather it up easily and make a coil, mamma," Gwendolen instructed softly.

"Shall I bring you some earrings, Gwen?" Mrs. Davilow asked, her voice brightening as she studied their reflection. The mother couldn't help but notice the eyes seemed clearer, a shadow lifted from the face, restoring the placid youthfulness to the features. "You really want your earrings?"

"No, mamma. I shall wear none. I'll put on my black silk. Black is the only attire for refusing an offer," Gwendolen replied, offering one of her practiced, enigmatic smiles before rising to shed her dressing gown.

"Suppose the offer isn't made after all?" Mrs. Davilow ventured, a sly note of hope in her tone.

"Then that will simply be because I refused it beforehand," Gwendolen countered, her head tossing with a proud little jerk. "It comes to the same thing."

As she descended the stairs in her long black robes, she moved with a firm poise and elastic grace that had been absent recently, like a parched plant suddenly watered. Her mother watched, thinking, *She is quite herself again. It must be the joy of his coming. Can her mind truly be made up against him?*

Gwendolen would have been furious if that thought had been spoken aloud. In truth, for the last twenty hours, she had been trapped in a whirlwind of alternating images and arguments regarding her marriage to Grandcourt. The resolve she had formed had dissolved; the counterbalancing thoughts born of counterbalancing desires had left her in a state where no conclusion felt fixed. She did not intend to accept him. From the moment she received his letter, she had intended to refuse. Yet, this very refusal forced her to confront the unwelcome reasons head-on, stripping away her awe of them. Her imagination, fueled by uncertainty, reshaped her knowledge in a dozen different ways. By staring at a dubious object with a constructive mind, one can conjure twenty different shapes. The vague hesitations she had felt at the Whispering Stones now counted for nothing, submerged beneath a final, overwhelming repulsion.

*If it hadn't been for that day at Cardell Chase,* she told herself, *there would have been no obstacle.* On that day, she had not reasoned; she had acted with an impulsive force against which all questioning was merely a voice against a torrent. That impulse stemmed not only from maidenly pride and jealousy, but from a vague, terrifying dread of wrongdoing. She had no scruples about anything consistent with being a lady, but she shrank from the dim region of disgrace, guilt, and wrong-doing with mingled pride and terror. Even apart from shame, the idea of deliberately injuring another placed her in the realm of guilt.

But did she know the exact state of affairs regarding Mrs. Glasher and her children? She had promised not to interfere. Would another woman marrying Grandcourt actually obstruct those wishes, or cause real injury? Or might it be better for Grandcourt to marry? After all, what could not a woman achieve when married, if she knew how to assert herself? Here, too, was constructive imagination. Gwendolen possessed as accurate a conception of marriage—with its mutual influences, demands, and duties—as she did of magnetic currents or storm laws.

"Mamma managed badly," she summarized her mother's experience. *I shall manage quite differently.*

The trials of matrimony were the last topic Mrs. Davilow wished to explore with her daughter. *I wonder what mamma and my uncle would say if they knew about Mrs. Glasher!* Gwendolen debated inwardly. Not that she could imagine telling them, even if she weren't bound by silence. *I wonder what anybody would say; or what they would say to Mr. Grandcourt marrying someone else and having other children!* Considering what "anybody" would say released her from the difficulty of judging where everything was obscure. Her memories proved that "anybody" regarded illegitimate children as more shameful and socially deprived than illegitimate fathers. The verdict of the public seemed to absolve her of concern for Mrs. Glasher.

Yet, there was another source of distress. Public opinion could not erase the indignation and loathing she felt at being expected to unite herself with an outworn life, full of backward secrets that must have been more keenly felt than any association with her own. True, the question of love on her part had played little role in her view of Grandcourt. The desirability of marriage had always seemed rooted in feelings other than love; being enamored was the man's part, upon whom the advances depended. Gwendolen had found no objection to Grandcourt's manner of being enamored until she glimpsed his past, which she resented as a deliberate offense. His advances to *her* had been deliberate, and she felt a retrospective disgust for them. Perhaps other men's lives were similarly fraught with secrets, rendering the ignorant suppositions of the women they courted a farce.

These feelings of disgust and indignation had sunk deep. Though recent troubles had dulled them from passion to memory, it was their reverberating activity that kept her firm in her understanding: she would not accept Grandcourt. She had never meant to form a new determination; she had only been considering what might be thought or said. If anything could have changed her mind, it would have been the prospect of making things easy for "poor mamma"—a temptation she admitted. But no! She was going to refuse him. Meanwhile, the thought that he was coming to be refused was invigorating. She held the white reins in her hands again; a new current flowed through her, reviving her from the beaten-down consciousness left by her interview with Klesmer. She was no longer going to crave an opinion on her capabilities; she was going to exercise her power.

Was this what caused her heart to palpitate annoyingly when she heard the horse's footsteps on the gravel? When Miss Merry opened the door to announce that Grandcourt was in the drawing-room, the hours of preparation and the triumph of the situation seemed useless. She might as well have seen him suddenly appear in the midst of her despondency.

Walking into the drawing-room required her to concentrate all her energy into self-control, appearing gravely gracious. She gave her hand to him and answered his hope that she was well in a voice as low and languid as his own. A moment later, seated on two wreath-painted chairs—Gwendolen upright with downcast eyelids, Grandcourt two yards away, leaning an arm over the back of his chair and gazing at her while holding his hat in his left hand—anyone viewing them as a picture would have concluded they were in a stage of love-making suspense. And certainly, the courtship had begun. She already felt herself being wooed by this silent man seated at an agreeable distance, surrounded by a subtle atmosphere of attar of roses and an attention bent wholly on her. He, too, considered himself to be wooing; he was not a man to suppose his presence carried no consequences, and he was exactly the type to feel the utmost piquancy in a girl he had not found entirely calculable.

"I was disappointed not to find you at Leubronn," he began, his usual broken drawl tinged with amorous languor. "The place was intolerable without you. A mere kennel of a place. Don't you think so?"

"I can't judge what it would be without myself," Gwendolen replied, turning her eyes on him with a recovered sense of mischief. "*With* myself, I like it well enough to have stayed longer, if I could. But I was obliged to come home on account of family troubles."

"It was very cruel of you to go to Leubronn," Grandcourt said, ignoring the troubles, though Gwendolen—she hardly knew why—wished for a clear understanding at once. "You must have known that it would spoil everything: you knew you were the heart and soul of everything that went on. Are you quite reckless about me?"

It would be impossible to say "yes" seriously, nor "no." What else could she say? In her difficulty, she lowered her eyelids again and blushed over face and neck. Grandcourt saw her in a new phase and believed she was showing inclination, yet he was determined she should show it more decidedly.

"Perhaps there is some deeper interest? Some attraction—some engagement—which it would have been only fair to make me aware of? Is there any man who stands between us?"

Inwardly, the answer framed itself: *No; but there is a woman.* Yet how could she utter this? Even if she hadn't promised that woman silence, it would be impossible to enter the subject with Grandcourt. How could she arrest his wooing by beginning a formal speech—"I perceive your intention—it is most flattering, etc."? A fish honestly invited to be eaten has a clear path to decline, but how does one swim against a net? Apart from the net, would she dare say anything decisive? Gwendolen had no time to clarify. Compelled to silence, she waited. After a pause, Grandcourt asked, "Am I to understand that some one else is preferred?"

Impatient with her own embarrassment, Gwendolen decided to rush at the difficulty and free herself. Raising her eyes, she said with something of her former clearness and defiance, "No"—wishing him to understand, *What then? I may not be ready to take you.* There was nothing Grandcourt could not understand that would affect his *amour propre*.

"The last thing I would do is to importune you. I should not hope to win you by making myself a bore. If there were no hope for me, I would ask you to tell me so at once, that I might just ride away to—no matter where."

Almost to her own astonishment, Gwendolen felt a sudden alarm at the image of Grandcourt finally riding away. What would remain? Nothing but the former dreariness. She liked him to be there. She snatched at the subject that would defer any decisive answer.

"I fear you are not aware of what has happened to us. I have lately had to think so much of my mamma's troubles that other subjects have been thrown into the background. She has lost all her fortune, and we are going to leave this place. I must ask you to excuse my seeming preoccupation."

In eluding a direct appeal, Gwendolen recovered some self-possession. She spoke with dignity and looked straight at Grandcourt, whose long, narrow, impenetrable eyes met hers, mysteriously arresting them. The subtle drama between man and woman is often such that it cannot be rendered in words strung together like dominoes. The single word *Love* expresses no more of the myriad modes of mutual attraction than *Thought* informs you of your neighbor's mind. It was hard to tell on which side—theirs or his—the influence was more mixed. At that moment, his strongest wish was to be completely master of this creature, this piquant combination of maidenliness and mischief; that she knew things that made her start away from him spurred him to triumph over that repugnance, and he believed he would triumph. And she—ah, piteous equality in the need to dominate!—was overcome like the thirsty one drawn toward the seeming water in the desert, overwhelmed by the suffused sense that here, in this man's homage to her, lay the rescue from helpless subjection to an oppressive lot.

All the while they looked at each other. Grandcourt said slowly and languidly, as if of no importance, "You will tell me now, I hope, that Mrs. Davilow's loss of fortune will not trouble you further. You will trust me to prevent it from weighing upon her. You will give me the claim to provide against that."

The little pauses and refined drawls gave Gwendolen time to dream a life. As the words penetrated her, they had the effect of a draught of wine, suddenly making all things easier, desirable things not so wrong, and people generally less disagreeable. She had a momentary phantasmal love for this man who chose his words so well, a mere incarnation of delicate homage. Repugnance, dread, scruples—these were dim as remembered pains, while she tasted relief under the immediate pain of hopelessness. She imagined herself springing to her mother, playful again. Yet when Grandcourt ceased speaking, there was an instant where she was conscious of standing at the turning of the ways.

"You are very generous," she said, not moving her eyes, with a gentle intonation.

"You accept what will make such things a matter of course?" Grandcourt asked, without new eagerness. "You consent to become my wife?"

This time, Gwendolen remained quite pale. Something made her rise from her seat in spite of herself and walk to a little distance. Then she turned, hands folded before her, standing in silence.

Grandcourt immediately rose too, resting his hat on the chair but still holding it. The evident hesitation of this destitute girl to take his splendid offer stung him into a keenness of interest he hadn't known for years. None the less, he attributed her hesitation entirely to her knowledge of Mrs. Glasher. In that posture of preparation, he asked, "Do you command me to go?" No familiar spirit could have suggested more effective words.

"No," said Gwendolen. She could not let him go; that negative was a clutch. She seemed to herself to be, after all, only drifted toward the tremendous decision—but drifting depends on something besides the currents when the sails have been set beforehand.

"You accept my devotion?" Grandcourt asked, holding his hat by his side and looking straight into her eyes, unmoving. Their eyes meeting allowed any length of pause: but wait as long as she would, how could she contradict herself? What had she detained him for? He had shut out any explanation.
