No tender-hearted soul need start at the thought that Henry Esmond Warrington—young, straight-limbed, scarcely out of boyhood—was flung by a broken-kneed brute into the grave. A man is not so easily discharged from his story as from his saddle; and if every gentleman were to perish at the first stumble of a horse, the roads about London would be paved with heirs and the churchyards would want for room. Harry was not dead. He lay, rather, in a good bed under a good roof, with clean linen to his skin, lavender in the air, and the quietest country sounds—wind through hedgerows, a distant cart creaking like a slow fiddle-string, the soft chime of a bell seldom used—washing over his half-waking fever.

And to prove it, here is the very voice of the house that took him in: not the doctor’s report, nor a constable’s affidavit, but a letter—warm as bread, and as full of remembered flavours as it is of news—written by the lady who became, for a day, his Good Samaritan.

“TO MRS. ESMOND WARRINGTON, OF CASTLEWOOD  
At her House at Richmond, in Virginia

“If Mrs. Esmond Warrington of Virginia can call to mind twenty-three years ago, when Miss Rachel Esmond was at Kensington Boarding School, she may perhaps remember Miss Molly Benson—her class-mate—who has forgotten all the little quarrels which they used to have together (in which Miss Molly was very often in the wrong), and only remembers the generous, high-spirited, sprightly Miss Esmond, the Princess Pocahontas, to whom so many of our school-fellows paid court.

“Dear madam! I cannot forget that you were dear Rachel once upon a time, as I was your dearest Molly. Though we parted not very good friends when you went home to Virginia, yet you know how fond we once were. I still, Rachel, have the gold etui your papa gave me when he came to our speech-day at Kensington, and we two performed the quarrel of Brutus and Cassius out of Shakspeare; and ’twas only yesterday morning I was dreaming that we were both called up to say our lesson before the awful Miss Hardwood, and that I did not know it, and that as usual Miss Rachel Esmond went above me.

“How well remembered those old days are! How young we grow as we think of them! I remember our walks and our exercises, our good King and Queen as they walked in Kensington Gardens, and their court following them, whilst we of Miss Hardwood’s school curtseyed in a row. I can tell still what we had for dinner on each day of the week—what days the broth was thin as if it had heard of beef but never met it; what days the boiled mutton came grey and resigned; what mornings the porridge smelt of the copper pan, and what evenings the bread was newly cut and the butter set too hard to spread without tearing the crumb. I remember, too, how your little garden-plot was always better kept than mine, the earth neat as if you had combed it, whilst mine looked as if a hen had danced a jig upon it. So was Miss Esmond’s chest of drawers a model of order, whilst mine were in a sad condition.

“Do you remember how we used to tell stories in the dormitory, and Madame Hibou, the French governess, would come out of bed and interrupt us with her hooting?—a sound I can hear now, as plainly as I hear the sparrows in my eaves. Have you forgot the poor dancing-master, who told us he had been waylaid by assassins, but who was beaten, it appears, by my lord your brother’s footmen? My dear, your cousin, the Lady Maria Esmond (her papa was, I think, but Viscount Castlewood in those times), has just been on a visit to this house, where you may be sure I did not recall those sad times to her remembrance, about which I am now chattering to Mrs. Esmond.

“Her ladyship has been staying here, and another relative of yours, the Baroness of Bernstein; and the two ladies are both gone on to Tunbridge Wells. But another and dearer relative still remains in my house, and is sound asleep, I trust, in the very next room; and the name of this gentleman is Mr. Henry Esmond Warrington.

“Now, do you understand how you come to hear from an old friend? Do not be alarmed, dear madam! I know you are thinking at this moment, ‘My boy is ill. That is why Miss Molly Benson writes to me.’ No, my dear; Mr. Warrington was ill yesterday, but to-day he is very comfortable; and our doctor, who is no less a person than my dear husband, Colonel Lambert, has blooded him, has set his shoulder which was dislocated, and pronounces that in two days more Mr. Warrington will be quite ready to take the road.

“I fear I and my girls are sorry that he is so soon to be well. Yesterday evening, as we were at tea, there came a great ringing at our gate which disturbed us all, as the bell very seldom sounds in this quiet place unless a passing beggar pulls it for charity. You know that kind of ring, dear Rachel: not the civil little summons of a neighbour, but the harsh peal that sets the very spoons a-tremble in their saucers. The servants, running out, returned with the news that a young gentleman, who had a fall from his horse, was lying lifeless on the road, surrounded by the friends in whose company he was travelling.

“At this, my Colonel (who is sure the most Samaritan of men!) hastens away to see how he can serve the fallen traveller; and presently, with the aid of the servants and followed by two ladies, brings into the house such a pale, lifeless, beautiful young man! Ah, my dear, how I rejoice to think that your child has found shelter and succour under my roof! that my husband has saved him from pain and fever, and has been the means of restoring him to you and health! We shall be friends again now, shall we not?

“I was very ill last year, and ’twas even thought I should die. Do you know, that I often thought of you then, and how you had parted from me in anger so many years ago? I began then a foolish note to you, which I was too sick to finish, to tell you that if I went the way appointed for us all, I should wish to leave the world in charity with every single being I had known in it.

“Your cousin, the Right Honourable Lady Maria Esmond, showed a great deal of maternal tenderness and concern for her young kinsman after his accident. I am sure she hath a kind heart. The Baroness de Bernstein, who is of an advanced age, could not be expected to feel so keenly as we young people; but was, nevertheless, very much moved and interested until Mr. Warrington was restored to consciousness, when she said she was anxious to get on towards Tunbridge, whither she was bound, and was afraid of all things to lie in a place where there was no doctor at hand.

“My Aesculapius laughingly said he would not offer to attend upon a lady of quality, though he would answer for his young patient. Indeed, the Colonel, during his campaigns, has had plenty of practice in accidents of this nature, and I am certain, were we to call in all the faculty for twenty miles round, Mr. Warrington could get no better treatment.

“So, leaving the young gentleman to the care of me and my daughters, the Baroness and her ladyship took their leave of us, the latter very loth to go. When he is well enough, my Colonel will ride with him as far as Westerham, but on his own horses, where an old army-comrade of Mr. Lambert’s resides.

“And, as this letter will not take the post for Falmouth until, by God’s blessing, your son is well and perfectly restored, you need be under no sort of alarm for him whilst under the roof of, madam, your affectionate, humble servant,  
MARY LAMBERT.

“P.S. Thursday.

“I am glad to hear (Mr. Warrington’s coloured gentleman hath informed our people of the gratifying circumstance) that Providence hath blessed Mrs. Esmond with such vast wealth, and with an heir so likely to do credit to it. Our present means are amply sufficient, but will be small when divided amongst our survivors.

“Ah, dear madam! I have heard of your calamity of last year. Though the Colonel and I have reared many children (five), we have lost two, and a mother’s heart can feel for yours! I own to you, mine yearned to your boy to-day, when (in a manner inexpressibly affecting to me and Mr. Lambert) he mentioned his dear brother. ’Tis impossible to see your son, and not to love and regard him. I am thankful that it has been our lot to succour him in his trouble, and that in receiving the stranger within our gates we should be giving hospitality to the son of an old friend.”

So much for Harry’s death, and so much for the sort of face that buys credit without coin. Nature stamps some men early, as surely as a vintner brands his cask: frank brow, open eye, the very colour of health in the cheek, a look that seems to say, I have nothing hidden, ask me what you please. With such a countenance, Harry could hardly have begged a crust without being offered a chair. Even rogues trusted him while they robbed him.

Yet the boy—rosy as he looked—was not the simpleton that rosy cheeks sometimes advertise. He had his wits about him; and if age, with all its wrinkles, likes to pretend that cunning belongs chiefly to the grown, let any man recall the sly little commerce of schoolrooms: the practiced fibs, the obedient face masking rebellion, the delicate art of flattering Dr. Birch for five years, and calling it duty. The tongue learns early what the heart cannot translate; a child can speak humility in the master’s language while thinking mutiny in his own. There are dialects of the spirit that never pass into honest English, and perhaps never should.

Mrs. Lambert, at all events, had no mind for that sort of philosophy. Like most good women—and indeed like most bad—she was rich in sentiment, and her sentiment had a homely scent to it: tea-steam and clean sheets, beeswax, lavender, and the faint medicinal tang of whatever draught her husband had put between the patient and pain.

When Colonel Lambert had bled the young man, and with the steady hand of a soldier set the dislocated shoulder, he and his wife stood a moment at the bedside, fingers linked as if the touch itself were a prayer. Harry lay sunk in the best bed, the curtains drawn so the light fell mild upon his face. The laudanum—or whatever merciful mixture the Colonel favoured—kept him in a slumber that was not quite silence: now and then a small murmur, a word without shape, like a spoon tapping once against a cup in a dream.

“He is a pretty lad indeed,” Mrs. Lambert whispered; and in the same breath, because mothers compare as naturally as they breathe, she confessed with a pang that neither of her boys—Jack at Oxford nor Charles newly sent back to school after Bartlemytide—was half so handsome as the Virginian. The fallen stranger’s arm, where the bandage had been, was white as a lady’s; and the clean smell of soap on him made him seem not merely young but newly made.

“Yes, as you say, Jack might have been as handsome but for the small-pox,” she added; “and as for Charley—”

“Always took after his papa, my dear Molly,” said the Colonel, catching sight of his own honest face in a little looking-glass—cut border, japanned frame—that had served his guests for patches and powder, and himself for shaving.

“Did I say so, my love?” Mrs. Lambert asked, startled, as though her thought had spoken aloud.

“No; but you thought so, Mrs. Lambert.”

“How can you tell one’s thoughts so, Martin?”

“Because I am a conjurer, and because you tell them yourself, my dear,” he answered, with the quiet merriment of a man who has outlived cannon and can therefore afford to laugh at domestic alarms. “Don’t be frightened: he won’t wake after that draught I gave him. Because you never see a young fellow but you are comparing him with your own. Because you never hear of one but you are thinking which of our girls he shall fall in love with and marry.”

“Don’t be foolish, sir,” she said, and put her hand up to his lips to hush him, as if words themselves might be overheard through the wainscot. They had stepped softly into the dressing-closet adjoining the chamber: a snug little room with India curtains, Japan cabinets, and drawers that smelled of folded linen and dried lavender—an odour that seemed to sweeten the very breath. From the window one could see the garden-beds laid out as carefully as a cook’s mise en place: each herb in its place, each path swept clean, the green world trimmed to civility.

“You can’t deny it, Mrs. Lambert,” he resumed in a lower tone. “As you were looking at the young gentleman just now, you were thinking to yourself which of my girls will he marry? Shall it be Theo, or shall it be Hester? And then you thought of Lucy who was at boarding-school.”

“There is no keeping anything from you, Martin Lambert,” she sighed.

“There is no keeping it out of your eyes, my dear. What is this burning desire all you women have for selling and marrying your daughters? We men don’t wish to part with ’em. I am sure, for my part, I should not like yonder young fellow half as well if I thought he intended to carry one of my darlings away with him.”

“Sure, Martin, I have been so happy myself,” she answered, looking up at him with those best eyes which have coaxed and governed more surely than any rod, “that I must wish my girls to do as I have done, and be happy, too!”

“Then you think good husbands are common, Mrs. Lambert, and that you may walk any day into the road before the house and find one shot out at the gate like a sack of coals?”

“Wasn’t it providential, sir, that this young gentleman should be thrown over his horse’s head at our very gate, and that he should turn out to be the son of my old schoolfellow and friend?” she persisted. “There is something more than accident in such cases, depend upon that, Mr. Lambert!”

“And this was the stranger you saw in the candle three nights running, I suppose?”

“And in the fire, too, sir; twice a coal jumped out close by Theo. You may sneer, sir, but these things are not to be despised. Did I not see you distinctly coming back from Minorca, and dream of you at the very day and hour when you were wounded in Scotland?”

“How many times have you seen me wounded, when I had not a scratch, my dear? How many times have you seen me ill when I had no sort of hurt? You are always prophesying, and ’twere very hard on you if you were not sometimes right. Come! Let us leave our guest asleep comfortably, and go down and give the girls their French lesson.”

So the Colonel offered his arm, and she took it, and together they descended the broad oak staircase of the comfortable old hall. Along the walls hung the faces of foregone Lamberts—magistrates, soldiers, country gentlemen—each painted into a kind of stillness; and yet the house itself was never still. Its silence was alive: the faint clink of china in a cupboard, a servant’s step muffled by rush-matting, the distant lowing of cattle, and, now and then, that gate-bell—rare as thunder in a clear season.

Colonel Lambert was a gentleman with a pleasant, waggish humour, the sort that seasons authority without spoiling it. The French lesson he and his daughters conned together was not a dry list of verbs, but a scene from Monsieur Molière’s Tartuffe; and papa, amused at his own part, would call Miss Theo “Madam,” and bow with mock ceremony, as if they were at court and not in a country parlour.

They read a scene or two; the girls’ voices—fresh, careful, obedient—moving in and out like birds among branches, while the Colonel, arch and merry, took up Orgon’s lines with relish, tasting the French as if it were a sauce he knew well:

“ORGON. Or sus, nous voila bien. J’ai, Mariane, en vous  
Reconnu de tout temps un esprit assez doux,  
Et de tout temps aussi vous m’avez ete chere.  
MARIANE. Je suis fort redevable a cet amour de pere.  
ORGON. Fort bien. Que dites-vous de Tartuffe notre hote?  
MARIANE. Qui? Moi?  
ORGON. Vous. Voyez bien comme vous repondrez.  
MARIANE. Helas! J’en dirai, moi, tout ce que vous voudrez!  
ORGON. C’est parler sagement. Dites-moi donc, ma fille,  
Qu’en toute sa personne un haut merite brille,  
Qu’il touche votre coeur, et qu’il vous seroit doux  
De le voir par mon choix devenir votre epoux!”

Miss Theo, reading Mariane, could not keep from laughing and blushing on the right line, as if the very rhythm compelled her. The Colonel chuckled, pleased with his own art, and turned to his wife.

“Have we not read the scene prettily, Elmire?” he cried; and Elmira—Molly Lambert in her own name, but “Elmire” for the joke—admired him prodigiously, as did the daughters, and indeed almost everything their father said or did.

It is a sweet thing—more nourishing than many a fine dish—to see an honest man in his own house, with a loving wife by his side and loving, obedient children about him; to hear the unforced harmony of their daily talk, and the house’s natural music—voices not strained, doors not slammed, servants not bullied, silence not broken by needless racket. There are homes where every sound is a bruise; and there are homes where even the quiet has a flavour of peace, like warm milk with nutmeg at bedtime.

That first night at Oakhurst, Harry Warrington, sleeping under a potion and waking now and then with a feverish flutter, thought he heard an Evening Hymn. The sound came to him as if across an ocean and across years, threaded through the soft country hush—voices below, gathered together in a certain Name. In his half-delirium, the hymn was not Lambert voices at all, but his dearest brother George singing at home; and with that mistaken comfort, the boy’s breath eased, his fingers unclenched, and he went down again into sleep, as if the song itself had carried him there.
