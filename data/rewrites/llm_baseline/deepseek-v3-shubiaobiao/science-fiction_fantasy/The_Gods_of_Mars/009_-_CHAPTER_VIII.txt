I understood then why the black pirate had spun his strange tale—to distract me. For miles, he had sensed the approach of reinforcements. Had I not caught that fleeting glance toward the sky, the battleship would have been upon us, its boarding party descending like a swarm of deadly insects, extinguishing my fragile hope of escape.

But I was no novice in the dance of aerial combat. In an instant, I reversed the engines and sent our vessel plummeting a hundred feet. Above, the boarding party dangled from the battleship’s hull as it roared past. Then I angled sharply upward, thrusting the speed lever to its limit. My ship became a projectile, its steel prow aimed at the whirring propellers of the giant above.

If I could cripple them, we might yet slip free.

Dawn broke, revealing a hundred black faces peering over the battleship’s stern. A chorus of rage erupted. Orders were barked—too late. We struck the propellers with a rending crash. I reversed engines again, but our prow was lodged in the torn metal. For a heartbeat, we hung there—long enough for the enemy to swarm our deck.

There was no battle. We were simply overwhelmed.

Blades flashed toward me, but a command from Xodar stayed them. "Bind them," he ordered, "but do no harm."

His men obeyed, though the bonds they wrapped around my wrists might as well have been thread. Had I been Martian, they would have held. But Earth-born muscles could snap them when the time came.

Phaidor was bound beside me, our fates lashed together.

Soon, we were hauled aboard the battleship—a monstrous engine of war, crewed by a thousand black warriors. Their eyes gleamed with cruel fascination as they studied us, their jeers thick with vulgarity. Phaidor’s beauty drew particularly crude remarks, a stark contrast to the chivalry of Barsoom’s red men.

Xodar’s officers crowded close, intrigued by my origin. My close-cropped hair and thern-like complexion puzzled them, but the harness of a slain thern on my back suggested I was no friend of their ancient enemies. That earned me a sliver of respect.

The ship itself was a spectacle—gleaming decks of polished skeel wood, cabins inlaid with jewels, and officers adorned in harnesses crusted with gems. The commander’s trappings blazed with diamonds, a dazzling display against his ebony skin.

Phaidor and I were taken below, thrown into a cramped cell with a single porthole. The door clanged shut, barred behind us. Through the glass, I watched the ship drift southward, its damaged propellers being repaired.

Silence stretched between us. My thoughts churned—where were Tars Tarkas and Thuvia now? Even if they evaded pursuit, the wastes of Barsoom were no sanctuary. The red men or the green would kill them on sight. I should have stayed. I could have made them *understand* the lie of the Valley Dor.

Phaidor’s voice pulled me from my brooding.

"Do you know where they’re taking us?" she asked.

"To solve the mystery of the afterlife, I imagine," I said dryly.

"Worse," she murmured, a shudder in her voice. "No thern woman stolen by the black pirates has ever returned. They take no male prisoners. That alone tells of horrors waiting for us."

I met her gaze. "A just fate, perhaps. Don’t the therns steal pilgrims who float down the Iss? Wasn’t Thuvia a slave for fifteen years?"

Her eyes flashed. "You don’t understand. We are holy. To serve us is an honor. Without us, the weak would be devoured by the plant men or the apes."

"And the lie you feed the outer world? The false promise of paradise?"

She stiffened. "The outer races exist to sustain us. Should therns toil like common beasts?"

A cold realization struck me. "You *eat* them."

She looked at me with pity. "As you eat animals. We are gods. The flesh of lesser beings sustains us."

Disgust twisted my gut. Before I could retort, the door opened. Xodar entered, smirking.

"No point keeping you confined," he said. "You’ll never escape. Come—see what no outsider has ever witnessed and lived to tell."

He cut our bonds and led us above deck.

The sight stole my breath.

We soared over an endless expanse of ice—the southern polar cap. But ahead, the ice ended in a sheer cliff, plunging into a vast valley below. Xodar pointed to a deep scar in the land.

"The River Iss," he said. "It runs beneath the ice, through the Valley Otz."

As we flew, a village came into view—crude, desperate.

"Lost souls," Xodar explained. "Those who flee the pilgrimage or escape the therns. They’re harmless. We leave them to rot."

Soon, a black mountain loomed—flat-topped, ominous. The ship slowed, hovering over a gaping chasm a thousand feet wide. The walls were smooth, volcanic. Without hesitation, the vessel descended into the abyss.

Darkness swallowed us. Then, lights flared, revealing an impossible sight—a subterranean world.

A phosphorescent sea stretched below, dotted with islands and ships. The battleship settled onto the water, its propellers shifting for aquatic travel.

Xodar smirked. "The navy of the First Born. This sea feeds the red men’s reservoirs. Without our pumps, their farms would drown."

So *that* was the secret of the red men’s water supply.

We approached an island, docking beside an oval structure. Inside, a sunken pool held a strange vessel—black, sleek. Xodar barked orders in a guttural tongue. A hatch opened, and a sailor emerged.

"Take us to the gardens of Issus," Xodar commanded.

The sailor saluted and vanished. Moments later, an officer ushered us aboard. The vessel sealed shut, humming to life.

"Where are we going?" Phaidor whispered, clutching my arm.

"Down," I said.

She trembled. Martians fear deep water—a primal terror. The ship shuddered as we plunged. Water rushed past the portholes, swirling in eerie light.

Phaidor’s grip tightened. "Save me," she begged. "Name your reward. Anything. *I* am yours."

Before I could react, she threw her arms around me, pressing her lips to mine in desperate hope.

The ship dove deeper, carrying us toward the heart of darkness—and the dread goddess Issus.
