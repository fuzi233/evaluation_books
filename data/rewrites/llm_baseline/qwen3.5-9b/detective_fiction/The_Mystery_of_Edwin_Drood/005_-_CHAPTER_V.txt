John Jasper, returning home through the gloom of the Close, came abruptly to a halt. Before him stood Stony Durdles, burdened with his dinner bundle, leaning heavily against the iron railing of the burial ground that enclosed the ancient cloister arches. Beside him, illuminated by the pale moonlight, was a grotesque urchin clad in rags, hurling stones at the stonemason with the precision of a marksman. Some stones struck their target; others missed entirely, yet Durdles appeared utterly indifferent to the outcome. The boy, however, reacted with theatrical flair: if he hit, he blew a triumphant whistle through a jagged gap in his missing front teeth; if he missed, he yelped "Mulled agin!" and immediately adopted a more vicious stance for his next attempt.

"What are you doing to the man?" Jasper demanded, stepping out from the shadows into the silver light.

"Playing cock-shy with him," the urchin replied.

"Give me those stones in your hand."

"Yes, I'll shove 'em down your throat if you try to catch me!" the boy shouted, shaking himself loose and retreating. "I'll smash your eye if you don't watch out!"

"You little devil," Jasper said, "what has this man done to you?"

"He won't go home."

"And how does that concern you?"

"He pays me a halfpenny to pelt him home if I catch him lingering out past ten o'clock."

With this explanation, the boy began to chant, moving like a wild creature half-dancing, half-stumbling among the tatters of his dilapidated boots:

He swept his arm broadly on the final word and fired another stone at Durdles. This seemed to be a pre-arranged poetic signal, a cautionary note telling Durdles to move along or face further harassment. Jasper nodded invitingly toward the boy, deeming it futile to drag or coax him, and crossed to the railing where the stonemason sat in profound meditation.

"Do you know this fellow, this child?" Jasper asked, struggling to find a suitable descriptor.

"Deputy," Durdles nodded.

"Is that his name?"

"Deputy," confirmed the stonemason.

"I'm a servant up at the Travellers' Twopenny in Gas Works Garding," the urchin explained. "All the servants at the Travellers' Lodgings are named Deputy. When the beds are full and the travelers are asleep, I come out for my health."

Stepping back into the road, he resumed his chant: *"Widdy widdy wen! I—ket—ches—Im—out—ar—ter—"*

"Hold your hand!" Jasper cried sharply. "Don't throw while I stand so close, or I shall kill you! Come, Durdles; let me walk you home tonight. Shall I carry your bundle?"

"Not on any account," Durdles replied, adjusting his load.

Durdles had been lost in thought when Jasper approached, surrounded by his creations like a poplar author amidst his trees. "Mrs. Sapsea," he introduced, gesturing to a white, cold sarcophagus within the railings. "Mrs. Sapsea's monument." Then, "Late Incumbent," pointing to the broken column of the departed reverend. "Departed Assessed Taxes," indicating a vase and towel resting atop what looked like a bar of soap. "Former pastrycook and muffin-maker, much respected," he added regarding a simple gravestone. "All safe and sound here, sir, and all Durdles's work. The common folk are merely bundled up in turf and brambles; the less said about them, the better. A sorry lot, quickly forgotten."

"This creature, Deputy, is trailing us," Jasper noted, glancing back. "Shall he follow?"

The relationship between Durdles and Deputy was erratic. When Durdles turned slowly with the heavy gravity of a drunkard, Deputy executed a wide circle into the road, assuming a defensive posture.

"You never cried 'Widdy Warning' before tonight," Durdles observed suddenly, recalling—or perhaps inventing—an insult.

"Lie!" Deputy snapped, offering his only form of polite contradiction.

"Own brother, sir," Durdles remarked, turning back just as quickly, having forgotten the slight as soon as he remembered it. "Own brother to Peter the Wild Boy! But I gave him a purpose in life."

"At which he aims?" Jasper suggested.

"That's exactly it, sir," Durdles said, thoroughly pleased. "I gave him an objective. What was he before? A destroyer. What did he do? Nothing but destroy. What did he earn for it? Short sentences in Cloisterham jail. Not a person, not a piece of property, not a window, not a horse, nor a dog, nor a cat, nor a bird, nor a fowl, nor a pig, but what he didn't stone, simply because he lacked an enlightened goal. I placed that enlightened goal before him, and now he earns his honest halfpenny at three penn'orth a week."

"I wonder he has no competitors."

"He has plenty, Mr. Jasper, but he stones them all away."

"Now, I don't know what this scheme of mine amounts to," Durdles pondered, his expression thick with beer-induced solemnity. "I don't know what you'd precisely call it. Is it a sort of... National Education scheme?"

"I should say not," Jasper replied.

"I should say not," Durdles agreed. "Then we won't bother naming it."

"He's still following us," Jasper repeated, checking his shoulder. "Should we allow him?"

"We can't avoid passing the Travellers' Twopenny if we take the short cut, which is the back way," Durdles answered. "We'll leave him there."

They proceeded; Deputy, acting as a rear guard in open formation, invaded the silence of the hour and place by pelting every wall, post, pillar, and inanimate object along the deserted path.

"Is there anything new down in the crypt, Durdles?" Jasper asked.

"Something old, I suppose you mean," Durdles grunted. "It isn't a place for novelties."

"A new discovery on your part, then?"

"There's an old one under the seventh pillar on the left, as you descend the broken steps of the little underground chapel that used to be there. I figure (as far as I've figured it so far) that it's one of the old ones with a crook. Judging by the size of the passages, the steps, and the doors through which they entered and exited, those crooks must have been quite a nuisance to the old ones! If two of them met promiscuously, I'd say they'd often get hitched by the mitre."

Without attempting to correct the literal nature of this assessment, Jasper surveyed his companion—clad from head to toe in old mortar, lime, and stone grit—as if he himself were becoming imbued with a romantic fascination for this strange life.

"Your existence is certainly curious."

Whether he took this as a compliment or an insult remained unclear, but Durdles gruffly responded, "Yours is another."

"Well! Since my lot is cast in the same old, earthy, chilly, unchanging place, yes. But there is far more mystery and intrigue in your connection with the Cathedral than in mine. In fact, I'm beginning to consider asking you to take me on as a sort of student, or free apprentice, under your tutelage. Let me accompany you occasionally and see some of these odd nooks where you spend your days."

The Stony One replied vaguely, "All right. Everyone knows where to find Durdles when he's needed."

Which, if not strictly true, was approximately accurate if interpreted to mean that Durdles could always be found wandering somewhere in a state of vagrancy.

"The thing I dwell upon most," Jasper continued, pursuing his thread of romantic interest, "is the remarkable accuracy with which you seem to locate where people are buried. What is the matter? That bundle is in your way; let me hold it."

Durdles had stopped and backed slightly, searching for a ledge to rest his bundle, when Jasper relieved him of it.

"Just you give me my hammer out of that," Durdles commanded, "and I'll show you."

*Clink, clink.* The hammer was returned.

"Now, listen here. You pitch your note, don't you, Mr. Jasper?"

"Yes."

"So I tune mine. I take my hammer and I tap." (He struck the pavement, and the attentive Deputy skirmished to a wider range, fearing his head might be required.)

"Astonishing!"
