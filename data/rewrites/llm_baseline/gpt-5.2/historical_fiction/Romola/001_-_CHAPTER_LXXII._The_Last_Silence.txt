More than three centuries and a half ago, in the mid spring of 1492, the angel of the dawn travelled, as he travels still, with broad slow wing from the Levant to the Pillars of Hercules, and from the Caucasus over the snowy Alpine ridges to the dark nakedness of the western isles. He saw nearly the same firm outlines and unstable waters; the same mountain shadows lying long in the same valleys; olive-mounts whose leaves turned pale as fish-bellies when the light struck them sideways; pine-forests breathing out resin and coldness; broad plains green with young corn that smelled, even from the height of the sky, of damp earth newly broken, and of the sweet sourness of grass after rain. He saw domes and spires lifting themselves by river-sides, and masts along the many-curved sea-coast like reeds at the edge of a marsh, all in the same places where they rise to-day.

And as that faint travelling light slipped into the dwellings of men, it fell, then as now, on nestling children warm as loaves drawn from an oven; on the haggard waking of sorrow and sickness, when the mouth tastes bitter as if one had chewed wormwood; on the hasty rising of the hard-handed labourer whose palms smelled of iron tools and wet rope; and on the late sleep of the night-student, who had been questioning stars, sages, and his own soul for a hidden knowledge that might break through the barrier of brief life. River-courses that have shaped men have hardly changed; and the other streams—the life-currents in human hearts—ebb and flow still with the same great needs, the same great loves and terrors. Hunger and labour, seed-time and harvest, love and death: these are headings that return like the tolling of an old bell over new roofs.

If, instead of following the dim daybreak, our imagination pauses on a certain spot and waits for fuller morning, we may see a world-famous city whose outline has scarcely altered since the days when Columbus was still waiting and arguing for three poor vessels. The very standing of it there—by water, under hills—seems like an unviolated symbol amidst the flux of human things, reminding us that we resemble the men of the past more than we differ. And if the spirit of a Florentine citizen—one whose eyes were closed for the last time while Columbus’s petition was still in the air—could return and pause where our thought is pausing, he might believe there would be fellowship and understanding for him among the inheritors of his birthplace.

Let us suppose such a Shade is permitted to revisit the glimpses of golden morning, and stands once more on the hill of San Miniato, looking down on Florence from the south. He is clothed as he lived: the folds of his well-lined black silk lucco hang in grave unbroken lines from neck to ankle; his plain cloth cap with its becchetto—long strip of drapery to serve as scarf in case of need—sits above a penetrating face. Not handsome, perhaps, but with a firm mouth; the lip and chin close-shaven, as if he had always wanted to keep himself distinctly human, and not let beard and indulgence make a mask of him. That face is charged with memories: of keen bargaining in cool loggie; of councils where men’s voices rose and fell like a tide; of dinners in which the meat was carved with ceremony and the jokes were sliced as sharply.

As he looks, familiarity is stronger than any perception of change. He thinks he might descend among the streets and take up the busy life where he left it. For it is not only the mountains and the westward-bending river he recognises; not only Mount Morello’s dark side opposite, and the long valley of the Arno stretching its grey low-tufted luxuriance toward Carrara; and the steep height of Fiesole with its crown of monastic walls and cypresses; and the green and grey slopes sprinkled with villas he can name as he looks at them. He recognises things closer to daily walks. Though he misses the ring of towers—seventy or more once surmounting the walls like a diadem—his eyes do not dwell on the blank; they are drawn to the unique tower springing like a tall flower-stem from the square turreted mass of the old palace in the heart of the city, none the worse for four centuries. The great dome too—greatest in the world—once only a daring thought in the mind of a small quick-eyed man: there it rises still, its curves eclipsing the hills.

And the bell-towers—Giotto’s with its distant hint of colour, the graceful-spired Badia, and the rest—he remembers looking at them from the shoulder of his nurse, before he could even pronounce the names that later would sit in his mouth like well-known foods. “Surely,” he thinks, “Florence can still ring her bells with the solemn hammer-sound that used to beat on the hearts of her citizens and strike out the fire there.” In his memory, the bells were not mere ornament; they were a whole city’s breathing made audible—metal lungs that gave measure to fasting and feasting, to work and worship, to the hush before trouble and the roaring after a victory. He listens for them now as one listens for the first crackle from a hearth on a cold morning.

On the right stands the long dark mass of Santa Croce, where they buried famous dead—laying laurel on cold brows and fanning them with the breath of praise and banners. But Santa Croce had no spire then. Florentines were too full of great building projects to carry them all out in stone and marble: there were frescoes and shrines to pay for; there were rapacious condottieri to bribe, royalty to flatter, territories to purchase; façades and spires must wait. Now his eye catches the spire. “What architect can the Frati Minori have employed to build that?” he wonders, and the wonder has an edge, as if he had expected to taste a familiar broth and found an unknown herb in it. “If it had been built in my day, Filippo Brunelleschi or Michelozzo would have devised something of another fashion—something worthy to crown Arnolfo’s church.”

With a sigh, he lets his eyes travel to the city walls, and there the change draws wonder in earnest. Why are five of the eleven convenient gates closed? And why—above all—are the towers levelled that were once glory and defence? Has the world become so peaceful, then? Do Florentines dwell in such harmony that there are no conspiracies to bring ambitious exiles home again with armed bands at their back? These are difficult questions; it is easier, and pleasanter, to recognise the old than to account for the new.

There flows Arno, with its bridges just where they used to be: the Ponte Vecchio, least like other bridges in the world, laden with the same quaint shops where our Spirit remembers lingering on his way—perhaps to look at the progress of Messer Luca Pitti’s great palace, built with huge stones quarried from the hill of Bogoli behind; perhaps to transact business with the cloth-dressers in Oltrarno. The Pitti roof is hidden from San Miniato; but the yearning of the old Florentine is not for Messer Luca’s ambitious house. It is for the narrow streets and the humming piazze where life was thick as steam above a pot and every man’s voice seemed to carry flavor—salt of sarcasm, pepper of envy, honey of flattery—according to the company.

Is not the anxious voting with black and white beans still going on down there? Who are the Priori now, eating their soberly regulated official dinners in the Palazzo Vecchio—removes of tripe and boiled partridges, dishes whose smell clung to official robes as surely as incense clung to church hangings—seasoned by practical jokes against the ill-fated butt among those potent signors? Are not the significant banners still hung from windows, still distributed with decent pomp under Orcagna’s loggia every two months? He remembers the weight of civic ritual as something like the weight of a well-made loaf: dense, sustaining, and shared. Life had zest when he trod those marble steps and shared the dignities. His politics had an area as wide as his trade, stretching from Syria to Britain; but they had also the passionate intensity that belongs only to a narrow scene of corporate action, where men meet every day, measure each other with the eye, and cannot escape the echo of their own words. There, public life had its own acoustic law: the murmur under a loggia, the chant of a procession, the sudden shout in a piazza—each sound a thread binding the citizen to the common good, or tightening into a noose when factions pulled.

He loved his honours and his gains, the business of counting-house and guild, of council-chamber and committee. He loved his enmities too, and fingered the white bean meant to keep a hated name out of the borsa with more complacency than if it had been a golden florin. He loved to strengthen his family by a good alliance, and went home with triumphant light in his eyes after concluding a satisfactory marriage for son or daughter under his favourite loggia in the evening cool. He loved his game at chess there, and his biting jest, and even his coarse joke, as not beneath the dignity of a man eligible for the highest magistracy. In the way of office he had learned much: he had been of the Ten who managed war, of the Eight who attended to home discipline, of the Priori—Signori—heads of the executive; he had risen even to Gonfaloniere; he had gone on embassies to Pope and Venetians; he had been commissary to the hired army, directing inglorious bloodless battles where no man died of brave breast wounds, virtuosi colpi, but only of falls and tramplings. So he had learned to distrust without bitterness, to look on life mainly as a game of skill—yet not dead to traditions of heroism and clean-handed honour.

And it was part of his pride that he was tinctured with the learning of his age, judging not altogether with the vulgar but in harmony with the ancients. In his prime he had been eager for the most correct manuscripts; he had paid many florins for antique vases and disinterred busts—some truncis naribus, their noses missing like crusts broken off by time, but not the less “authentic.” In his old age he had made haste to see the first sheets of that fine Homer which was among the early glories of the Florentine press. Yet for all that he had not neglected to hang up a waxen image of himself under the Madonna Annunziata, nor to do penance in large gifts to shrines of saints whose lives had not been modelled on the study of classics; he had not even neglected liberal bequests toward buildings for the Frati, against whom he had levelled many a jest. The unseen Powers were mighty. Who knew—who was sure—that there was any name given behind which there was no angry force to appease, no intercessory pity to win? Were not gems medicinal, though they only pressed the finger? Were not all things charged with occult virtues? Lucretius might be right—he was ancient and a great poet. Luigi Pulci, too, suspected of believing nothing from the roof upward, had often sounded right over supper when wine and jokes circulated fast, though he was only a poet in the vulgar tongue. There were learned personages who maintained that Aristotle—wisest of men unless Plato were wiser—was thoroughly irreligious; and a liberal scholar must entertain all speculations.

But negatives might, after all, prove false; nay, seemed manifestly false, as circling hours swept past and turned round with graver faces. For had not the world become Christian? Had he not been baptised in San Giovanni, where the dome is awful with symbols of coming judgment, where the altar bears a crucified Image that disturbs perfect complacency like a sudden silence at the end of a song? He was not a pagan philosopher, nor a philosophising pagan poet, but a man of the fifteenth century, inheriting its strange web: belief and unbelief; Epicurean levity and fetichistic dread; pedantic ethics mouthed by rote and crude passions acted out with childish impulsiveness; a leaning toward self-indulgent paganism and yet inevitable subjection to conscience, which in the unrest of new growth filled the air with prophecies and presentiments.

He had smiled, perhaps, and shaken his head dubiously when simple folk spoke of a Pope Angelico who was to come and bring in a new order—purify Church from simony, clergy from scandal—too different from what existed under Innocent the Eighth for a shrewd merchant to calculate upon. But he felt the evils of the time nevertheless; for he had public spirit, and public spirit can never be wholly immoral, since its essence is care for a common good.

That very Quaresima—Lent—of 1492 in which he died, still erect in his old age, he had listened in San Lorenzo, not without a mixture of satisfaction, to the preaching of a Dominican friar, Girolamo Savonarola. The Frate denounced with rare boldness the worldliness and vicious habits of the clergy; he insisted that Christian men must not live for their own ease while wrong triumphed in high places, must not spend wealth in outward pomp even in the churches while fellow-citizens suffered want and sickness. The words had moved his audience to such a pitch that women even took off their ornaments and delivered them up to be sold for the needy—metal and gem transformed into bread, broth, and linen. It was an alchemy more persuasive than any scholar’s disputation, and it had its own sound: the soft clink of chains and rings dropped into a wooden chest, the little gasp when a clasp was undone, the hush afterward as if the very air had been chastened.

“He was a noteworthy man, that Prior of San Marco,” thinks our Spirit. “Somewhat arrogant and extreme—especially in his denunciations of speedy vengeance. Ah, Iddio non paga il Sabato! God does not pay on a Saturday. The wages of men’s sins often linger in their payment, and I myself saw much established wickedness of long-standing prosperity. But a Frate Predicatore who wanted to move the people—how could he be moderate? He might have been less defiant and curt to Lorenzo de’ Medici, whose family were the makers of San Marco. Was that quarrel ever made up? And our Lorenzo himself, with dim outward eyes and subtle inward vision—did he get over that illness at Careggi? It was a sad uneasy face he would carry out of a world that had given him so much; and there were strong suspicions his handsome son would play Rehoboam. How has it all turned out? Which party is likely to be banished and have its houses sacked just now? Is there any successor of the incomparable Lorenzo, to whom the Great Turk sends presents—rare animals, rare relics, rare manuscripts, or fugitive enemies—suited to the tastes of a Christian Magnifico who is lettered and devout, and also slightly vindictive? And what scholar dictates the Latin letters of the Republic—what fiery philosopher lectures on Dante in the Duomo and goes home to write bitter invectives against the father and mother of the bad critic who faults his spelling? Are wiser heads leaning toward alliance with Pope and the Regno, or inclining ears to orators of France and Milan?”

“There is knowledge of these things to be had in the streets below,” he says to himself, “on the beloved marmi in front of the churches, and under the sheltering loggie, where surely citizens still have gossip and debates, bitter and merry jests as of old. The buildings are there. The changes cannot be so great in uncounted years. I will go down and hear—I will tread the familiar pavement, and hear once again the speech of Florentines.”

Go not down, good Spirit; for the changes are great, and the speech of Florentines would sound as a riddle in your ears. The very cadence you trusted—the old civic music of voices and bells—has shifted; meanings have slipped as fish slip in the hand, and words once warm with shared use have cooled, hardened, or vanished. If you must go, mingle with no politicians on the marmi, or elsewhere; ask no questions of trade in the Calimala; confuse yourself with no inquiries into scholarship, official or monastic. Only look at sunlight and shadow on the grand walls built solidly and enduring in their grandeur. Look at the faces of little children, making another sunlight amid the shadows of age. Look, if you will, into the churches, and hear the same chants, see the same images as of old—the images of willing anguish for a great end, of beneficent love and ascending glory. See upturned living faces, lips moving to the old prayers for help. These things have not changed.

Sunlight and shadow bring their old beauty and wake the old heart-strains at morning, noon, and eventide; the children are still the symbol of the eternal marriage between love and duty; and men still yearn for the reign of peace and righteousness—still own that life to be the highest which is conscious voluntary sacrifice. For the Pope Angelico is not come yet.
