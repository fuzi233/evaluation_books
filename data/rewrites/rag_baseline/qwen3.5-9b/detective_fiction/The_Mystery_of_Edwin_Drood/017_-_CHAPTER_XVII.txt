The air in the London office of the Haven of Philanthropy was thick with the scent of stale ink and unspoken grievances. Half a year had elapsed since the last significant movement, and Mr. Crisparkle sat in a waiting room that felt less like a sanctuary and more like a holding cell for the weary. He waited for an audience with Mr. Honeythunder.

In his youth, amidst the athletic exercises of his college days, Mr. Crisparkle had known the professors of the Noble Art of fisticuffs. He had witnessed their gloved gatherings, where the clash of leather gloves echoed the rhythm of a drum. Now, observing the men who passed through the Haven, he found a disturbing symmetry. The phrenological contours of the Philanthropists' backs of heads mirrored those of the pugilists perfectly. Both groups possessed an exaggerated development of the organs responsible for the impulse to strike. These professors moved with an aggressive readiness, poised to engage any novice who happened to loiter nearby, much like the fighters of the Fancy. Preparations were underway for a moral exhibition on the rural circuit, with various backers promoting specific speakers as if they were heavyweight champions destined for a knockout. The intended resolutions were crafted with the tactical precision of boxing rounds. Among the officials, dressed in somber black, Mr. Crisparkle recognized the silhouette of a former benefactor, once famous as Frosty-faced Fogo, who had formerly managed the magic circle of ropes and stakes.

Yet, the resemblance ended there. The Philanthropists were poorly trained, bloated with what experts termed "Suet Pudding," lacking the temperamental steel of the boxers. Worse, their vocabulary was far rougher, and their code of conduct permitted actions that would shock the ring: boring opponents to distraction, striking a fallen man, kicking, stamping, and gouging without mercy. In these brutal particulars, the professors of philanthropy were far more noble than the professors of the fist.

Lost in this comparison, watching the crowd shuffle past—each individual seemingly engaged in the futile act of snatching value from another while offering nothing in return—Crisparkle's name was called. A shabby, underpaid stipendiary ushered him to Mr. Honeythunder's room. The usher was so destitute that he could scarcely have performed worse had he served a declared enemy of humanity.

"Sit down," commanded Mr. Honeythunder. His voice was tremendous, resonating with the authority of a schoolmaster addressing a recalcitrant boy.

Crisparkle seated himself. Honeythunder signed the final entries of a stack of circulars, summoning families without means to contribute instantly or face damnation. Another shabby employee collected the forms into a basket and departed.

"Now, Mr. Crisparkle," Honeythunder said, turning his chair halfway around. He squared his arms, resting his hands on his knees, his brows knitted in a expression that suggested he intended to finish the business quickly. "We entertain different views regarding the sanctity of human life."

"Do we?" Crisparkle replied.

"We do, sir."

"Might I inquire what your views are on that subject?"

"That human life is a thing to be held sacred, sir."

"And what are your views?"

"They are best known to yourself," Honeythunder roared, squaring his arms further.

"Candidly, I admit that. But you stated we differed. Therefore, you must have attributed some views to me. Which ones?"

"Here is a man—a young man," Honeythunder continued, as if the mention of youth compounded the tragedy—"swept off the face of the earth by a deed of violence. What do you call that?"

"Murder," Crisparkle answered.

"What do you call the perpetrator?"

"A murderer."

"I am glad to hear you admit so much," Honeythunder retorted, lowering his voice with malicious satisfaction. "I did not expect it."

"Please explain why those expressions are unjustifiable."

"I do not sit here to be browbeaten," Honeythunder shouted.

"As the only other person present, no one knows that better than I do," Crisparkle said quietly. "But I must interrupt your explanation."

"Murder!" Honeythunder resumed, entering a boisterous reverie, folding his arms and nodding with abhorrent reflection after every syllable. "Bloodshed! Abel! Cain! I hold no terms with Cain. I repudiate the red hand with a shudder."

Instead of rising to cheer, Crisparkle merely crossed his legs and said mildly, "Do not let me interrupt your explanation when you begin it."

"The Commandments say, no murder. NO murder, sir!" Honeythunder paused platformally, as if correcting Crisparkle for implying a license to kill.

"And they also say, you shall bear no false witness," Crisparkle observed.

"E-enough!" Honeythunder bellowed, with a solemnity that would have silenced a theater. "My late wards are now of age, and I am released from a trust I cannot contemplate without horror. There are the accounts you accepted on their behalf, and the balance you must receive. Let me tell you, sir, I wish that as a man and a Minor Canon, you were better employed." He nodded thrice, emphasizing the phrase.

Crisparkle rose, his face flushed but his composure intact.

"Mr. Honeythunder," he said, picking up the papers, "whether I am better or worse employed is a matter of taste. You might think I would be better employed enrolling myself as a member of your Society."

"Ay, indeed," Honeythunder shook his head threateningly. "It would have been better for you if you had done that long ago!"

"I think otherwise."

"Or," Honeythunder countered, "I might think one of your profession better employed discovering and punishing guilt rather than leaving that duty to a layman."

"My profession teaches that its first duty is toward those in necessity and tribulation, the desolate and the oppressed," Crisparkle said. "However, since I have determined that it is not my role to make professions, I say no more of that. But I owe it to Mr. Neville, and to his sister, to tell you that I know I understood Mr. Neville's mind and heart fully at the time of this occurrence. Without concealing what was tragic or requiring correction, I am certain his tale is true. Feeling this certainty, I befriend him. As long as it lasts, I will. If any consideration could shake this resolve, I would be so ashamed of my meanness that no man's or woman's good opinion could compensate for the loss of my own."

He spoke with a modesty akin to a schoolboy keeping a wicket in a breezy field. There was no self-assertion, only a staunch adherence to duty.

"Then who did the deed?" Honeythunder asked abruptly.

"Heaven forbid," Crisparkle replied, "that in clearing one man I should lightly criminate another! I accuse no one."

"Tcha!" Honeythunder ejaculated with disgust. This was contrary to the Brotherhood's usual principles. "And remember, sir, you are not a disinterested witness."

"How am I an interested party?" Crisparkle smiled innocently.

"There was a stipend paid to you for your pupil, which may have warped your judgment," Honeythunder said coarsely.

"Perhaps I expect to retain it?" Crisparkle returned, enlightened. "Do you mean that too?"

"Well, sir," the professional Philanthropist stood up, thrusting his hands into his pockets, "I do not measure people for caps. If people find hats that fit them, they can wear them. That is their lookout, not mine."

Crisparkle eyed him with just indignation.

"Mr. Honeythunder, I hoped to avoid commenting on the introduction of platform manners into private life. But you have provided such a specimen that silence would make me complicit. They are detestable."

"They don't suit you, I dare say."

"They are," Crisparkle repeated, ignoring the interruption, "detestable. They violate the justice due to Christians and the restraints due to gentlemen. You assume a great crime was committed by one I devoutly believe innocent. Because I disagree, what is your resource? To turn upon me, charging that I lack sense of the crime's enormity and am its accomplice! Next time, taking me as your opponent elsewhere, you set up a credulity, a unanimous profession of faith in a ridiculous delusion. I decline to believe it, and you fall back on claiming I believe nothing—that because I refuse to bow to your false god, I deny the true one! Another time, you discover War is a calamity and propose abolishing it with twisted resolutions tossed into the air like a kite's tail. I admit no discovery to you, possess no faith in your remedy. Again, you represent me as reveling in battlefield horrors like a fiend. Another time, you propose punishing the sober for the drunken. I claim consideration for the sober's comfort, and you proclaim I have a depraved desire to turn Heaven's creatures into swine! In all such cases, your movers, seconders, and supporters run amuck like mad Malays, attributing base motives recklessly and quoting figures as wilfully one-sided as an account showing only debt or only credit.
