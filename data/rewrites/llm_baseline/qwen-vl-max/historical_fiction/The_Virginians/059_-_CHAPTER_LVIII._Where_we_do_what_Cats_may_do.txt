It had long been the fashion among the young Virginians to embrace Jacobite sentiments with a fervour that, while not uncommon for their age, was more than mere youthful fancy. They admired the exiled Stuart line, and in particular the banished prince who had once acknowledged Madam Esmond’s father as Marquis of Esmond. Though she might have taken umbrage at such royal favouritism, Madam Esmond bore no ill will toward a family so quick to recognize her own noble lineage. As for the whispered scandal involving her sister, Madame de Bernstein, and the Old Chevalier—well, she dismissed it with a wave of her hand, declaring with unshakable truth that the first two Hanoverian monarchs were scarcely better in private virtue than any of the Stuarts. Yet, she acknowledged one undeniable fact: the king who sat on the throne, though not by birthright, was the king in practice. He had been solemnly crowned and anointed in church; he had vanquished his rival in battle. Thus, in her mind, duty called upon her sons to appear before him—not merely out of loyalty, but because they owed themselves that respect.

And if His Majesty should see fit to bestow a post, or a ribbon of blue or red upon either of them, Madam Esmond would not be surprised. She believed the King knew the Virginian Esmonds as well as any other noble house in the land. So it was that her sons were commanded to present themselves at court—and had they known, she would have been furious to learn that George wore Harry’s laced coat on the day of his visit to Kensington.

A century past, the King’s drawing-room stood open nearly every day to nobility and gentry alike. Loyalty, especially since the war had begun, found ample satisfaction in seeing the sovereign in person—perhaps a dozen times a month. The threat of enemy warships made sea voyages perilous, and thus the King, though longing for his native Hanover, remained reluctantly within England. A Hanoverian lady, beloved for her grace and devotion, endeavoured to ease his sorrow. From the lips of the Countess of Walmoden—honoured with a British title by imperial grace—the monarch could hear the voice of home.

It was to this sovereign that Mr. Warrington, through his uncle—a diligent courtier—requested introduction. Since Mr. Lambert too was bound for court to thank the King for his recent promotion, the two set off together, hiring a hackney-coach for the journey, as my Lord Wrotham’s carriage was now occupied by its rightful owner, who had returned to town.

They arrived at Kensington Palace Gate, where the sentries recognized and saluted General Lambert, and then walked modestly on foot to the summer residence of the Crown. Passing beneath the portico, they entered the gallery leading to the grand black marble staircase—richly adorned by Mr. Kent—and moved through several chambers hung with tapestry and lined with portraits and busts until they reached the King’s great drawing-room. There, amid masterpieces of art, hung Titian’s famed “Venus,” Rubens’ “St. Francis adoring the infant Saviour,” and other treasures of the brush. Here, among the courtiers, the gentlemen waited, as the King concluded his private council with those whom the newspapers of the time called his M-j-ty’s M-n-st-rs.

George Warrington, never having stepped inside a palace before, found himself entranced. He gazed upon paintings he had only read about—Vandyck’s portrait of Charles I with his Queen and children, Tintoretto’s “Esther before Ahasuerus,” its figures draped in opulent Venetian finery. He lingered so long in admiration that he barely heard General Lambert whispering names into his ear.

“There,” said the General, “are two of His Majesty’s Lords of the Admiralty—Mr. Gilbert Elliot and Admiral Boscawen. That last is your Boscawen, whose fleet fired the first shot in your waters two years ago.”

“That stout gentleman, all gold-braided, is Mr. Fox—the former Minister, now content with being Paymaster, though he still carries the auri fames on his person. Why, his waistcoat is a perfect Potosi!” laughed George.

“Aliena appetens—how goes the text? He loves to get money and spend it,” added Lambert.

“Yonder is my Lord Chief Justice Willes, speaking with my Lord Salisbury, Doctor Headley. If he serves God as he serves the King, Heaven will grant him high advancement. He lived in your grandfather’s time, loved by Dick Steele, hated by the Dean. With them stands my Lord of London, the learned Doctor Sherlock. The lords of lawn sleeves have lost half their honours now. I remember when I was a boy, my mother made me kneel before the Bishop of Rochester—the one who crossed the water and became minister to someone unnamed—Perkin’s Bishop.”

“The fair man beside him is Admiral Smith. President of poor Byng’s court-martial—he tried in vain to save him. Tom of Ten Thousand, they call him in the fleet. The French ambassador broke him when he was a lieutenant, for making a French ship lower its topsails. The King made him captain the next day.”

“And that tall, proud fellow?” asked George.

“My Lord George Sackville. Now that I am Major-General myself, he’ll treat me less like a footman. I wish Blakeney were here—soldier’s darling, brave and kind, unlike yonder poker of a nobleman.”

At this moment, the nobleman approached.

“I am your lordship’s very humble servant,” said Lambert, turning fully to his companion. “This is a young gentleman from America, recently returned from Braddock’s unfortunate expedition.”

“Oh, indeed!” replied the nobleman. “I have the honour of speaking to Mr.——?”

“To Major-General Lambert, at your lordship’s service. And this, sir, is Mr. Warrington. I must present you to His Majesty if Sir Miles tarries much longer.”

As he spoke, Lambert addressed only the young man, ignoring his colleague entirely. The nobleman retreated, startled, as if offended by such boldness. In those days, nobility was nobility, and expected reverence.

Soon, Sir Miles Warrington appeared in his bright red waistcoat, greeted warmly by both Lambert and his nephew. Their affection was evident. Sir Miles embraced the General, calling him “dear friend,” and lamented his departure from the house.

“We miss you terribly, General,” he declared. “My daughters fell in love with your ladies—upon my word, they did! My Lady Warrington and I debated endlessly how to make acquaintance with your charming family. We feel as if we’ve known you for years. And we love you—for your kindness to our dear nephews, though we confess we were jealous when they visited you so often.”

He passed a hand over his eyes, choking with emotion. “Carry my deepest respects to Mrs. Lambert,” he continued. “Thank her for her care of these boys—my own flesh and blood, my brother’s sons!”

While they conversed—George suppressing laughter with admirable restraint—the door to the King’s apartments opened. Pages preceded His Majesty, followed by his corpulent son, the Duke of Cumberland, resplendent in blazing scarlet. Behind them came various officers and ministers, among them George recognized instantly: Mr. Secretary Pitt—tall, stern-faced, with an eagle eye and a presence that commanded silence.

Even from a hundred years’ distance, I feel a shiver of awe as I see him pass. Not from fear of George II, nor dazzlement at the Duke’s imposing figure—but from the sheer weight of Pitt’s presence. The Great Commoner, the terrible Cornet of Horse! He strides across history like a colossus, wrapped in flannel, gouty shoes clumping, thunderbolt hands clenched. Perhaps dark words had passed between him and the King—long speeches, eloquent yet tedious, repulsive to the sharp old monarch who detested fine phrases and pompous brose.

Pitt moved solemnly through the crowd. The company drew back respectfully. The King walked slowly around the room, his son trailing behind, speaking briefly to select individuals.

The monarch was small, keen-eyed, fresh-complexioned, dressed plainly in snuff-coloured clothes and brown stockings, adorned only by the blue ribbon of the Garter. He spoke in a German accent, yet with ease and wit, addressing those he chose, bowing to others.

He knew General Lambert well—from Dettingen, from Scotland—and congratulated him heartily on his promotion.

“It is not always,” the King observed, “that we can do as we please. But I was glad, for once, to give myself that pleasure in your case, General. Your army holds no better officer.”

Lambert blushed deeply, humbled and proud.

Then the King turned to Sir Miles and the young man beside him.

“Who is this?” he asked, pointing to George Warrington, who stood in respectful silence, clad in Harry’s finest embroidered suit.

Sir Miles bowed low. “Your Majesty, this is my nephew, Mr. George Warrington of Virginia, who begs leave to pay his humble duty.”

“This, then, is the other brother?” the King remarked kindly. “Had he come late, the other would have spent all the money.”

He turned to the Bishop of Salisbury. “Why do you come out in this bitter weather? You’d do better staying at home.”

And so he passed on.

Sir Miles was visibly moved. He clasped George’s hands. “God bless you, my boy,” he exclaimed. “I told you you’d see the greatest monarch and finest gentleman in the world. Is he not so, my Lord Bishop?”

“That, that he is!” the bishop cried, raising his ruffled hands to heaven. “The best of princes and men!”

“Ah,” said Lambert, “that is Master Louis, my Lady Yarmouth’s favourite nephew.”

Just then, the Duke of Cumberland approached.

“Congratulate you on your promotion, Lambert,” he said cheerfully.

Sir Miles’s eyes nearly popped out of his head with joy.

“I owe it, sir, to your Royal Highness’s good offices,” the General replied.

“Not at all,” the Duke said. “You deserved it long ago. Always been a good officer. Perhaps there’ll be employment soon. This is the gentleman James Wolfe introduced to me?”

“His brother, sir.”

“Oh! The real Fortunate Youth! You were with poor Ned Braddock in America—a prisoner, lucky to escape. Come and see me in Pall Mall. Bring him to my levee, Lambert.”

And with that, the Duke turned away.

“It’s raining!” Sir Miles cried. “You came on foot, General? You and George must come home in my coach. You shall come with me—I say so! By George, you must!”

So he drove them back to Hill Street, presenting Lambert to Lady Warrington and the girls—Flora and Dora—and insisting they partake of a collation.

“What? Only cold mutton?” Sir Miles joked. “Well, an old soldier can eat cold mutton. And a good glass of my Lady Warrington’s own cordial—prepared with her own hands—will keep the cold wind out. Delicious cordial! Capital mutton! Our own, my dear General—six years old if a day. We keep a plain table, but the Warringtons have always loved mutton since the Conqueror. Our meal may look scanty—but we are plain people. I keep my servants on board-wages. Can’t afford seven-year-old mutton, you know.”

In front of George, Sir Miles recounted the court visit in glowing terms—so flattering that George felt almost embarrassed, unsure how to respond, how to contradict such praise before the family.

Lambert sat quietly, eyes wide. He had been to Kensington too—but had seen none of the wonders described.

“We are proud of you, dear George,” Sir Miles declared. “We love you—my dear nephew—we all love you, we’re all proud of you—not because you’re wealthy, nor blessed with lands and ancient name—but because you’ve used your talents wisely. Because you’ve fought and bled for your country, your king. You are truly worthy of the King’s favour.”

To Lambert, he added, “You’ve kindly dropped in on a country family. We hope to see you again, when our hospitality is a little less homely. Yes, by George, you must name a day when you and Mrs. Lambert, and your dear girls, dine with us. No refusal now—I won’t take it!”

Lady Warrington rose. “Will you accompany us to my drawing-room?”

Lambert declined, but the ladies refused to let George go.

“No, positively,” said Flora. “You shall not go. We must meet our cousin. We must hear about that dreadful battle, and your escape from the Indians.”

Tom Claypool entered, catching some of the tale. Flora listened, handkerchief pressed to her eyes.

“Why do you take your handkerchief, Flora?” asked little Miles. “You’re not crying a bit.”

When Lambert returned home, he could not resist telling his wife about the new family he had met.

A certain word—“humbug”—had lately entered fashion. And the General used it freely to describe the Warringtons.

He recounted the father’s eager hospitality, the mother’s exaggerated flattery, the daughters’ admiring glances, the tough mutton, and the abominable cordial.

Mrs. Lambert could not help comparing Lady Warrington’s treatment of poor Harry with her current enthusiasm for George.

“Is Miss Warrington really handsome?” she asked.

“Yes,” said the General, “very handsome—and the most astonishing flirt I’ve ever seen.”

“The hypocrite!” she exclaimed. “I have no patience with such people!”

To which the General replied, simply:

“Bo!”

“Why ‘Bo’, Martin?” she asked.

“I say ‘Bo!’ to a goose, my dear,” he answered.

And his wife swore she didn’t know what he meant—or what he was thinking.

And the General said:

“Of course not.”
