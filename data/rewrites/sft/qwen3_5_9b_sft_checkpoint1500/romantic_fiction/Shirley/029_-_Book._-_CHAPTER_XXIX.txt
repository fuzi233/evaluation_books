Louis Moore was accustomed to a life of profound quietude, a state far better suited to his temperament than the bustling clamor of the wider world. Possessing a vast inner universe within his own mind and heart, he bore the confinement of a small, still corner of reality with a patience that few others could emulate.

But tonight, Fieldhead itself seemed to hold its breath. An eerie hush fell over the estate, broken only by the absence of everyone else. Miss Keeldar, the entire Sympson family, and even the young Henry, had departed for Nunney to meet Lady Nunnely and her daughters. Even Sir Philip, the kind-hearted baronet, had requested the tutor's presence to facilitate introductions. Yet, Louis Moore would sooner have bargained for an audience with the ghost of the Earl of Huntingdon beneath the canopy of ancient oaks in Nunney Forest than accept the company of the Sympsons. He desired solitude, yes, but not the sterile silence of the boy-baronet or the stiff propriety of the matrons. Tonight, he craved something specific, something real and immediate.

Outside, the air crackled with the remnants of the equinox. The wild rains of the afternoon had spent themselves, leaving behind a sky of bruised blues and silvers. A great, solitary cloud drifted away, revealing not a serene ocean of azure, but a tempestuous sea illuminated by a glorious, moonlit gale. The moon herself seemed intoxicated by the wind, glowing with a fierce, glad radiance as if surrendering to the storm's embrace. No shepherd would watch for his goddess tonight; the mountains were bare, welcoming the spirit of Æolus.

Inside, the storm roared against the other gables and the hall front, but this end of the house remained a sanctuary. Moore stepped out of the schoolroom, dismissing the idea of shelter. "All the parlours are empty," he murmured, feeling a strange sickness at the heart of this cell-like isolation. "I am weary of this cage."

He moved into the hall, drawn by windows that offered an unobstructed view of the dark, swirling autumn sky. Carrying no lantern, trusting in the silver flood of moonlight that bathed the floors and walls, he wandered from room to room. It was as if he were trailing a phantom, searching for a trace of her presence. Finally, he stopped in the Oak Room. Unlike the cold, polished silence of the salon, this chamber held warmth. The hearth glowed red-hot, sending sparks tinkling in the intense heat, casting dancing shadows against the rug. Near the worktable, a chair sat waiting.

A question formed in his mind: Did the vision he had been tracking finally occupy that seat? He stood before it, his eyes alight with an intensity that betrayed a deep longing, as if a living companion awaited his words. And indeed, evidence of her recent visit lay scattered around the stand. A small satin bag hung from the chairback; the desk drawer stood open, keys resting inside. A silver pen, a delicate glove, a crimson berry clinging to a green leaf—these trifles decorated the scene, disrupting the order she usually maintained. While she might put things away neatly, it was these chaotic details that held a peculiar charm.

"You left your mark," he whispered, his voice low. "Careless, attractive creature!" He chuckled softly, shaking his head. "Hurried away in haste, doubtless, forgetting to tidy up. Why does she leave trails of fascination in her footsteps? Where did she learn to be so heedless without causing offense? There is always something to reprove in her, yet the scolding never stays as bitterness on the tongue. It melts away into a kiss. It takes less time to spend half an hour lecturing her than to admire any other woman for a lifetime. Am I rambling? Soliloquizing? Stop it."

He silenced himself, standing for a moment in thought before arranging his evening. He drew the heavy curtains over the broad window, shutting out the sovereign moon and her starry retinue. Adding more fuel to the dying fire, he lit a candle and set a second chair opposite the first, near the workstand. Then he sat, pulling a thick book of blank pages from his pocket and a pencil from his vest.

With a steady, cramped hand, he began to write, inviting the reader to lean close and witness his thoughts.

*"It is nine o'clock. The carriage will not return until past eleven. Until then, the house is mine. I may occupy her room, sit opposite her chair, rest my elbow upon her table, surrounded by her little mementoes.*

*I used to revere Solitude—a quiet, serious nymph, an Oread descending from mountain passes, clad in the blue mist of hills, breathing a chill, solemn beauty. I could court her serenity, imagining my heart content in her silent majesty.*

*But since that evening in the schoolroom, when she sat so near, opening her troubled mind to me, appealing to my strength, I have come to hate Solitude. She is cold abstraction, a fleshless skeleton, the daughter and mate of Death!*

*It is delightful to write of what holds the core of my heart. No one can take this book, and through this pencil, I can speak freely—to say what I dare not utter aloud, what I dare not think in the presence of the living.*

*We have scarcely met since then. Once, in the drawing-room, seeking Henry's books, she entered, dressed for a concert at Stilebridge. Her shyness—not mine—drove a silver veil between us. I have heard much cant about 'maiden modesty,' but truly applied, the words are apt. As she passed to the window, acknowledging me tacitly, I could think of her nothing but 'stainless virgin.' A delicate splendor seemed to robe her, modesty her halo. I may be the most foolish of men, and one of the plainest, but her shyness touched me exquisitely, flattering my deepest sensibilities. I felt like a stupid block, yet alive with a paradise of emotion as she turned her glance away, hiding the flush on her cheeks.*

*I know this is the chatter of a dreamer, a romantic lunatic. I do dream, and if she has infused romance into my prosaic nature, what choice do I have?*

*Sometimes she is a child! An unsophisticated, untaught thing! I see her looking up at me, entreating me to protect her from being smothered, to ensure she receives a strong narcotic. I see her confess that she is not as self-sufficient as people believe, admitting the need for sympathy. I saw a secret tear fall from her eyelash. She claimed I thought her childish, and I did. She imagined I despised her. Despair! It was sweet to feel both near to her and above her, conscious of a natural right and power to sustain her, just as a husband sustains his wife.*

*I worship her perfections, but it is her faults, her foibles, that draw me near, that nestle her to my heart. These flaws are the steps by which I ascend to dominion over her. If she were a trimmed, artificial mound, smooth and level, what foothold would I have? It is the natural hill, with its mossy breaks and hollows, whose slope invites ascent, whose summit brings pleasure.*

*To leave metaphors aside: She suits me. If I were a king and she the maid who swept my stairs, my eye would still recognize her qualities; a pulse would beat for her in my heart, though a gulf might make acquaintance impossible. If I were a gentleman and she served me, I could not help liking Shirley. Strip her of education, ornaments, sumptuous dress—all extrinsic advantages—and present her at a cottage door in a rough gown, offering me a drink of water with that same warm goodwill... I would still like her. I would linger to talk with that rustic. I would not feel as I do now, finding nothing divine, yet meeting her with pleasure, parting with regret.*

*How culpably careless she is to leave her desk open, where I know she keeps her money! The keys to all her repositories hang in the lock. There is a purse in that little satin bag; I see the tassel of silver beads. Such a sight would provoke my brother Robert. All her little failings would irritate him. If they vex me, it is a pleasurable vexation. I delight in finding her at fault. Were I always resident with her, she would be no niggard in providing themes for my lectures. I never lecture Henry, rarely feeling disposed to do so. If he errs—which is seldom—a word suffices, often a mere shake of the head. But the moment her mischievous countenance meets my eye, words crowd to my lips. From a taciturn man, she transforms me into a talker. Whence comes the delight I take in this discourse? It puzzles me. The more *crâne*, *malin*, *taquin* her mood, the clearer occasion she gives for rebuke, the more I seek her, the better I like her.*
