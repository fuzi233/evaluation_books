Deronda came out of the narrow house at Chelsea carrying a restlessness in his bones, a need for the rhythmic, grounding exertion of the body to burn off the heavy fumes of his temper. The city streets offered only the jarring, industrial clatter of iron-rimmed wheels on cobblestone—an acoustic violence that grated against his nerves. Seeking refuge from that mechanical dissonance, he walked toward the Chelsea Stairs, where the brine-scented Thames licked at the mossy wood. The air smelled of damp earth and the sharp, acidic bite of coal smoke. Refusing the enclosed, rattling confinement of a cab, he hired a wherry and took the oars himself, craving the physical friction of wood against water.

His errand lay downriver at Ram’s book-shop. He had missed Mordecai’s midday watch the previous day, and the desire to pull the man’s enigmatic thread had grown into a palpable hunger. The Jewish scholar was like a guardian of some ancient, endangered seed-bank, yet their last conversation had ended with a sudden withdrawal, an acoustic shutting of a heavy door that left Deronda listening to silence. As Deronda warmed to his labor, the repetitive sweep and pull of the oars became a meditative churning, kneading the water like a heavy dough. Through this rhythmic, cyclical exertion, his mindset shifted. He recognized his own utilitarian approach to Mordecai—treating the man merely as an ingredient in his search for Mirah’s family—and tasted the sourness of his own selfishness. He was foraging in another man’s life without respecting the soil from which the roots were pulled. 

*If I glean the facts I need,* Deronda thought, the wooden oars thudding softly in their locks, *I suppose I would be content to leave the rest of his harvest to rot. But perhaps we are two exiles passing on the same current, each carrying a native dialect the other might recognize, if only the noise of the world would quiet long enough for us to hear it.*

By the time the wherry approached Blackfriars Bridge, it was half-past four. The gray afternoon was boiling down into a glorious, sprawling saffron reduction, rendering the bruised purple clouds into a caramelized glaze across the western sky. The river absorbed this atmospheric nourishment, reflecting the monumental calm above with a luminous, trembling energy below. Brown sails glowed like steeped tea leaves, and heavy barges drifted out of the shadows, their passage a slow, mythic response to the turning of the day.

Deronda, flushed and steeped in the heat of his own exertion, drew his Inverness cape around his shoulders. As he fastened the collar, he looked up. There, leaning over the parapet of the bridge, bathed in the distinct, amber syrup of the dying light, was a face he could not mistake. It was Mordecai. The man was an illuminated study in bodily starvation and spiritual voracity—a fragile vessel worn thin by the abrasive, unceasing friction of a world that refused to chew upon his ancient truths. 

Mordecai had been watching the western horizon, holding the advancing boat in his gaze. As Deronda’s face lifted toward him, a visible tremor seized the frail scholar. It was as if the circular pacing of generations, the long-simmering prophecies of his ancestors, had finally boiled over into the present moment. With a white, emaciated hand, Mordecai beckoned. 

Deronda immediately signaled back. Mordecai waved his cap, his soul flooding with the deep, satiating completion of a master craftsman watching the first signs of fermentation in a long-tended dough. The prefigured friend had stepped from the golden broth of the sky; the mythic cycle had intersected with the mundane water of the Thames. 

In three minutes, Deronda had paid his boatman, trading the rhythmic splash of the river for the solid stone where Mordecai waited in absolute, reverent stillness. 

“I was very glad to see you standing here,” Deronda said, mindful of the fragile acoustic space between them, keeping his voice resonant and low. “I was intending to go on to the book-shop to look for you. Perhaps they mentioned my visit yesterday?”

“Yes,” Mordecai answered, his voice possessing the gravelly gravity of a millstone. “That was the reason I came to the bridge.”

The simple answer struck Deronda with its mysterious certitude. He searched the man’s face for madness, but found only the clarity of a deeply rooted elder. “You knew nothing of my being at Chelsea?”

“No; but I expected you to come down the river. I have been waiting for you these five years.” Mordecai’s deep-sunk eyes anchored themselves to Deronda’s with a pathetic, solemn dependency. 

“It will be a satisfaction to me if I can be of real use to you,” Deronda answered earnestly. “Shall we take a cab? You have likely walked enough, and your breath seems taxed.”

“Let us go to the book-shop. It will soon be time for me to be there. But now, look up the river,” Mordecai said, turning toward the current. He spoke in a breathless undertone, a hushed reverence that treated the bridge not as a thoroughfare of iron and commerce, but as an ancient, domestic hearth. “See the sky, how it is slowly fading. I have always loved this bridge. I stood on it when I was a little boy. It is a meeting-place for the spiritual messengers, the place where the deep waters of the past feed the present. The Masters taught that every order of things has its angel—its resonant tone carrying the full nourishment of what is afar. Here, away from the roaring engines of the streets, I have listened to the acoustic messages of earth and sky. This hour of fading light has sunk into me like water into dry earth. It was my own decline, pausing, waiting, until it brought me my new life—the vessel who will carry my harvest when this breath is all breathed out.”

Deronda stood in silence, deeply moved. His initial urge to label the man’s fervor as the delusions of a diseased organism melted away. He possessed too expansive a palate for human experience to reject a flavor simply because it was unfamiliar. The sheer conviction emanating from Mordecai nullified his outward poverty, draping him in the authority of a mythic patriarch, an elder who had stepped out of ancient agrarian lore to pass on the seeds of his lineage. 

They took a cab, suffering the jarring, industrial rattle of the city streets, a harsh sonic environment that forced them both into protective silence. Deronda felt the oppressive weight of a destiny he did not yet understand being placed upon him, but he resolved, *If I am to be a vessel for this man’s nourishment, I will not let it spill.*

Within ten minutes, they escaped the commercial din into the small, gas-lit refuge of Ram’s book-shop. The acoustic dampening of thousands of pages immediately soothed the air. The small room smelled of decaying calfskin, dust, and the vanilla-sweet decay of lignin—a dark pantry of forgotten harvests. They stood face to face, instinctively baring their heads. 

Mordecai leaned against the counter. His face bore the tragic, consumptive stamp of a forsaken temple—the yellowed pallor of his skin contrasting with his black hair and beard, his difficult breathing marking the rhythmic struggle of his nostrils. He looked upon Deronda with the yearning of a dying mother transferring the very marrow of her identity into her son. Opposite him, Deronda radiated the rich, youthful health of a deeply nourished life, his olive skin and masculine gravity offering a profound, receptive stillness. Deronda did not prejudge; he offered the rare, massive power of pure, empathetic listening, providing an acoustic sanctuary for Mordecai’s confession.

“You cannot know what has guided me to you and brought us together at this moment,” Mordecai began, his voice quiet, preserving his dwindling energy like a precious oil. 

“I am not impatient,” Deronda said. “I am ready to listen.”

“You see that I am dying,” Mordecai said. “You see that I am as one shut up behind bars by the wayside, who if he spoke to the crowds passing by, would be met only with the deafening roar of their commerce and their pity. But you have come in time.”

“I rejoice that I am come in time,” Deronda said, refusing to utter the cruel word *mistaken*. 

“The hidden reasons why I need you began afar off,” Mordecai continued, pressing his wasted yellow fingers to his breast. “They began when I was a youth studying in another land. Ideas—beloved, sustaining ideas—came to me, because I was a Jew. They were my life; I was not fully born till I tasted them. I counted my sleep, my waking, the bread I fed my body, and the sights that fed my eyes—I counted them all merely as fuel to keep the divine flame burning beneath the pot of my soul. But I engraved my thoughts in rocky solitudes. Disease came, and bound me with the iron that eats into the soul. I cried out, ‘How shall I save the life within me from being stifled with this stifled breath?’”

He paused, struggling for air in the narrow, spice-scented space. 

“Do not misunderstand me. I speak not as an ignorant dreamer from the inland valleys, who thinks ancient thoughts anew without knowing their vintage. English is my mother-tongue; England is the native soil of this body, which is but a breaking earthen pot around a fruit-bearing tree whose seed might make the desert rejoice. But my true palate was trained in Holland at the feet of my mother’s brother, a Rabbi skilled in deep, ancestral learning. When he died, I went to Hamburg, and then to Göttingen, to drink knowledge at all sources. I possessed myself of a handicraft. I learned to make needles to earn my daily bread, just as Joshua ben Chananja did after the destruction of the Temple, so that my soul could remain a temple of remembrance where the inner sanctuary is hope. I see the world as it is, and I see the world my vision will create anew.”

“I do not call your words raving,” Deronda assured him gently. “I have a keen interest in the story of a spiritual destiny embraced in youth.”

“A spiritual destiny,” Mordecai repeated, his tone correcting the English abstraction with his own visceral reality. “It was the soul fully born within me. It brought its own world—a medieval world where men made our ancient language live again in new psalms of exile. They absorbed the philosophy of the Gentile into the faith of the Jew, pressing the wine of foreign vineyards into the skins of our own traditions. One of their souls awakened within me. It tasted the dust of Spain and Provence; it heard the roar of the Crusaders and the shrieks of tortured Israel. And when its dumb tongue was loosed, it spoke the speech they had made alive with the new blood of their sorrow and their martyred trust. It sang with the cadence of their native strain.”

Mordecai leaned closer, his voice dropping to a hoarse, desperate whisper. “While it is imprisoned in me, it will never learn another language. It cannot.”

“Have you written entirely in Hebrew, then?” Deronda asked, recognizing the profound tragedy of an untranslatable life. 

“Yes,” Mordecai said, a deep sadness settling over him. “In my youth, I had the ranks of the great dead around me. But soon I found that the living were deaf to my frequency. I was left solitary. Family troubles called me to labor. The angel of death beckoned, his skirts brushing my path. I went to the men of our people—to the rich in influence, the rich in other wealth. I sought an ear for my harvest. But I found none to listen with understanding. I carried a bundle of Hebrew manuscript; I said our chief teachers are feeding the people ash instead of bread. But the scholar and the merchant were too busy listening to the clatter of the streets. Scorn stood as interpreter between me and them. One mocked me, saying that a new revelation would never answer in Hebrew in this modern world. He touched a bitter truth. The dominant lexicon has no room for the native dialect of my soul.”

“But though you are accustomed to Hebrew, few could use English better,” Deronda suggested, hoping to offer a bridge, a way to translate the man’s culinary memory into a language the present age could digest. 

Mordecai shook his head slowly. “Too late. My writing now would be like this gasping breath. If I used English, I would be beating a wooden board to summon those who only respond to the harsh clang of a factory bell. My soul has an ear to hear the compromises of its own speech. To write my truths in the language of the market would be to strip the meat from the bone. Men would smile and say, ‘A poor Jew!’ and the chief smilers would be of my own people, their cultural memory eroded entirely away.”

Despondency eclipsed him; his hands fell heavy to his sides. 

“I feel strongly with you,” Deronda said, his deep voice wrapping around Mordecai like a thick, warming cloak. “But what you have written need not be buried. Publication is within reach. If you rely on me, I will ensure it is preserved.”

“That is not enough!” Mordecai looked up, a pale flame reigniting in his sunken eyes. He stepped closer, grasping Deronda’s arm, his grip possessing the desperate strength of a drowning man clinging to a root. “You must be not only a hand, but a soul—tasting my belief, nourished by my reasons, seeing the harvest I point to! You will be my life. It will be planted afresh; it will grow. The generations are crowding on my narrow life like a bridge, and the bridge is breaking. You will take the inheritance the base sons refuse because they are too busy plowing over the tombs for gold. You will take the sacred inheritance of the Jew.”

Deronda went as pallid as the sick man before him. A compassionate dread washed over him—the terror of starving this desperate elder of his final meal, warring against the dread of swallowing an illusion and committing to a falsity. The sheer force of Mordecai’s acoustic dominance triggered a natural repulsion, yet Deronda’s exquisite empathy won out. Before speaking, he gently placed his warm palm over Mordecai’s straining, skeletal hand—a physical grounding, a breaking of bread without words. 

Then, measuring his tone so as not to shatter the quiet space between them, he said, “Do you forget what I told you when we first met? Do you remember that I said I was not of your race?”

“It can’t be true,” Mordecai whispered instantly, unshocked, feeling the sympathetic warmth of the hand upon his own. The bodily connection fortified the mythic cycle he knew to be turning beneath them. He followed his denial with a second assertion, welling up from the deep, untranslatable marrow of his conviction. “You are not sure of your own origin.”

“How do you know that?” Daniel asked, shrinking back, the acoustic harmony of the room suddenly ringing with a jarring, unexpected note. He pulled his hands away.

Mordecai relaxed his hold and fell back against the counter, his breath ragged but his eyes blazing with the fierce certainty of a man who had finally found the fertile soil for his seed. “I know it—I know it; what is my life else? Tell me everything: tell me why you deny.”
