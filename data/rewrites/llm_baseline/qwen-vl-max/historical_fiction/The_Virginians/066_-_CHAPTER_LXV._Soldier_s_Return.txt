Great Powers! Will the vanity of men—especially Frenchmen—ever cease? Can it be believed that, after the skirmish at St. Cas—a mere matter of cutting off a rearguard, as we all know—they had the gall to fire cannons at the Invalides in Paris and shout their triumph over our misfortune? Is there any nobility in huzzahing because five or six hundred brave soldiers were surrounded by ten thousand on a beach, and met the fate said to befall the last in line? I once thought of painting a vivid picture of London’s rejoicing over our victory at St. Malo—the polished guns paraded by our gallant sailors, the horse-grenadiers prancing proudly, the crowd waving hats, roaring cheers, stealing pockets, while friends watched from a balcony in Fleet Street, blessing this scene of British glory. But now that the French have so vulgarly copied our celebration and raised their own St. Cas against our St. Malo, I refuse to dwell on such stale pride. I say *Nolo*, not *Malo*—content enough, for my part, if Harry has returned from one campaign to another with his life intact. And have I ever hinted he was hurt? Have I not, out of kindness to my fair young reader, declared him as well as ever he’d been in his life? The sea wind had bronzed his cheeks, but the bullet that whistled past his curls left them unscathed. The ocean soaked his boots and clothes, yet spared his body. True, he had exposed the lapels of his coat to the enemy—but only briefly, withdrawing from sight as quickly as possible. And what are lapels, pray, but reverses? Coats have them, just as men do—and our duty is to wear them with courage and good humor.

“I can tell you,” said Harry, “we all had to run. When our line broke, the luckiest were those who reached the boats first. The French cavalry and infantry chased us down to the shore, mingling among us, slashing at our men, bayoneting them where they fell. Poor Armytage was shot ahead of me and dropped; I lifted him and staggered through the surf toward a boat. It was lucky the sailors in our boat weren’t frightened—though bullets flew around their ears, shattered their oars, and riddled their flag. But the officer in charge stayed calm as if sipping punch at Portsmouth—which we did, I assure you, the moment we landed. Poor Sir John wasn’t so fortunate. He never made it to the ship. The service has lost a fine soldier, and Miss Howe a true gentleman for her husband. These things happen, you see—and his brother inherits both the baronetcy and the commission.”

“It’s the poor lady I think of,” said Miss Hetty (to whom Harry was recounting his tale), “and the King. Why did His Majesty send Sir John Armytage into battle? A gentleman cannot refuse a command from such a source. And now he’s dead! Oh, how grieved must the King be!”

“I doubt not His Majesty will be deeply moved,” replied Papa, nodding sagely.

“Now you’re laughing!” Hetty exclaimed. “Do you mean to say that when a gentleman dies in service—almost at the King’s feet—the King of England won't feel for him?”

“If I thought that,” retorted Papa, “I’d be for the Pretender!”

“The sauce-box would make a fine head for Temple Bar,” remarked the General, who understood Hetty’s meaning beneath her words, sensing the storm of remorse, shock, and gratitude swirling in her heart now that the danger was past.

“No,” he continued, “my dear. If kings wept for every fallen soldier, what a burden they’d bear! I hold His Majesty in higher regard than to believe him so weak. And if Miss Hester Lambert got her Pretender, I suspect she wouldn’t be much happier. That family has never been famed for excessive emotion.”

“But if the King sent Sir John—Harry, I mean—to war, where he died, oughtn’t His Majesty to repent?” asked the young lady.

“If Harry had fallen, no doubt the court would have gone into mourning,” observed the General. “As it is, gentlemen and ladies wore colored clothes yesterday.”

“Why shouldn’t we light bonfires for a defeat, and don sackcloth and ashes after a victory?” asked George.

“I protest I don’t want to thank Heaven for helping us burn the ships at Cherbourg,” he added.

“Yes, you do, George,” said Harry. “Not that I have the right to speak, and you’re far cleverer than I. But when your country wins, you’re glad—I know I am. When I flee before Frenchmen, I’m ashamed—I can’t help it, though I’ve done it.”

“It doesn’t seem right,” he added gravely, “that Englishmen should have to do it.”

George smiled, but chose not to ask what the Frenchman himself might think.

“It’s a bad business,” Harry went on, still solemn. “But it’s lucky it wasn’t worse. The story goes that the French governor, the Duke of Aiguillon, was rather a wet hen. Our entire retreat could have been cut off—though, truth be told, we were in such a hurry to move ourselves. The local militia fought bravely, I’m glad to say, and many gentlemen volunteers stood firm at the front. They say the Chevalier de Tour d’Auvergne joined the fight despite the Duke’s orders. Officers who came back with lists of prisoners and wounded told General Bligh and Lord Howe about it. He’s a lord now—since news of his brother’s death reached home, George. He’s a brave man, whether noble or common.”

“And his sister—she was to marry poor Sir John Armytage—think what she must be going through!” sighed Miss Hetty, growing more sentimental by the day.

“And his mother!” cried Mrs. Lambert. “Have you seen her address in the papers to the electors of Nottingham? ‘Lord Howe being absent on public service, and Lieutenant-Colonel Howe with his regiment at Louisbourg, it rests upon me to beg your votes and support so that Lieutenant-Colonel Howe may fill his late brother’s place in Parliament.’ Isn’t she a gallant woman?”

“A Laconic woman,” quipped George.

“How can sons fail to be brave when raised by such a mother?” mused the General.

Our two young men exchanged glances.

“If one of us were to fall defending our country,” said George, “we’d have a mother in Sparta who’d write and think just so.”

“If Sparta is anywhere near Virginia,” remarked Mr. Harry, “then we do.”

“And to think we both faced the enemy—and both were beaten by him, brother!” he added pensively.

Hetty looked at him, seeing not the man of today, but the figure she remembered: staggering through the waves, his comrade bleeding on his shoulder, enemies close behind, bullets flying. And it was she who had driven him into that danger! Her words had provoked him. Yet now, returned, he gave her no rebuke. Except when questioned, he spoke little of his ordeal. With Hetty, he was grave and courteous; with the rest of the family, open-hearted and tender. But her taunts had wounded him deeply.

“Little hand,” his eyes seemed to say, “you should not have raised yourself against me. It is wrong to scorn anyone—much less one who has given so much for you and yours. I may not be quick-witted, but in spirit, in heart, I stand equal to the best—and the best of my heart has always belonged to your family.”

His silence, his quiet strength, and his dignified endurance won back in Hetty’s esteem the place he had lost during months of idle disgrace. She now honored him not for rank or wealth, but for courage. No longer sharp-tongued or mocking, she became humble, changed, better. And as she changed, so did the world around Harry.

He was no longer sulky or idle. He no longer doubted himself or defied others. His colonel reported his conduct as model and recommended him for one of the commissions freed by casualties. Though the expedition ended poorly, it brought him fortune. His fellow volunteers, returning to St. James’s Street, praised him highly. Their deeds became the talk of the town. Had Harry been a general slain in victory, Sir John Armytage could scarcely have drawn more sympathy than these volunteers received.

Newspapers brimmed with letters in their honor. Men of wit and feeling competed to write epitaphs for the fallen. The fate of Sir John’s fiancée was mourned. She was, as we’ve said, the sister of the courageous Commodore who had just returned from the ill-fated campaign and inherited his elder brother’s title—an officer as noble as himself, who had recently fallen in America.

My Lord Howe himself spoke warmly of Mr. Warrington, and thus Harry shared in the public adoration showered upon the volunteers. Doubtless thousands served as well or better—but the English love their gentlemen, and admire them when they distinguish themselves. These volunteers were hailed as paladins, heroes, by unanimous acclaim.

And so, as young noblemen do, they accepted their fame with grace. White’s and Almack’s lit up in their honor. St. James’s welcomed its new knights. Harry regained full favor among them. Hands reached out eagerly to greet him again. Even his relatives congratulated him. A letter arrived from Castlewood—where Aunt Bernstein had taken refuge—praising his valor and enclosing a small banknote as a token of her affectionate approval. It bore the frank of Lord Castlewood, who sent his regards to both cousins and invited them to visit his country seat.

And then, through the post, came a private letter—not perfectly spelled, but in a handwriting that made Harry smile. His cousin Maria Esmond wrote that she always loved to hear his praises (now echoing everywhere), sympathized with his fortunes, and begged him to keep a small place in his heart for her. Parson Sampson, she added, had preached a moving sermon on the terrors of war and the heroism of men who volunteered for danger in defense of their country.

Soon after, the chaplain himself wrote a passionate letter, saluting Mr. Harry as his friend, benefactor, and glorious hero. Even Sir Miles Warrington sent a basket of game from Norfolk—one bird, shot while perched, tied with a string and note reading “With love to my cousin”—the first victim of young Miles’s fowling piece.

Then, radiant with joy, Mr. Lambert visited the young men at their lodgings in Southampton Row and announced that Henry Warrington was to be officially appointed Ensign in the Second Battalion of Kingsley’s, the 20th Regiment—now reorganized as the 67th. Its colonel had not accompanied the regiment to Brittany; he was at Cape Breton, engaged in capturing the guns from Louisbourg—those very guns whose arrival in England had sparked such national jubilation.
