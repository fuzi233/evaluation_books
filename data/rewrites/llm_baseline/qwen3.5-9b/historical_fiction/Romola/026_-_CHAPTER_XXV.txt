While Baldassarre was seized by the voice of Savonarola, he remained unaware that another man had slipped through the rear doorway and stood nearby, watching him intently. It was Piero di Cosimo. The painter paid no attention to the sermon; he had come solely to observe the escaped prisoner. As the preacher paused, allowing the congregation to surrender to a wordless emotional surge, the newcomer stepped forward and touched Baldassarre's arm.

The painter looked around, tears still tracing slow paths down his cheeks, though a vigorous sigh suggested he felt the outburst was over. Speaking in a low tone, Piero asked, "Shall I cut your cords for you? I've heard how you were captured."

Baldassarre did not answer immediately. He cast a suspicious glance at the intruder. Finally, he replied, "If you will."

"Better come outside," Piero suggested.

Baldassarre looked at him with renewed suspicion. Seeing the doubt in the prisoner's eyes, Piero smiled, drew a small knife, and severed the bonds. He began to suspect that the notion of the prisoner's madness might not be entirely unfounded; there was something peculiar about the set of his face.

"Well," Piero mused internally, "if he causes trouble, he'll be tied up again quickly enough. The poor devil deserves a chance, at least."

"You seem afraid of me," Piero whispered, pressing further. "You don't wish to tell me anything about yourself?"

Baldassarre crossed his arms, savoring the long-forgotten sensation of muscle tension. He met Piero's gaze with less suspicion now, his tone carrying a quiet resolve. "No, I have nothing to tell."

"As you please," Piero conceded with a shrug. "But perhaps you seek shelter? We Florentines are notoriously hospitable to visitors with torn doublets and empty stomachs. There is a hospital for poor travelers outside every gate. If you wish, I can guide you there. Besides, there is no danger from your French soldier; he has been dispatched."

Baldassarre nodded silently, accepting the offer, and the two men left the church together.

"You wouldn't mind sitting for a portrait, would you?" Piero asked as they walked along the Via dell' Oriuolo toward the Gate of Santa Croce. "I am a painter, and I would pay you well for the likeness."

Suspicion flickered back into Baldassarre's glance. "No."

"Ah!" Piero said curtly. "Very well. Go straight ahead; you will find the Porta Santa Croce. Outside lies a hospital for travelers. So, you refuse any further service from me?"

"I thank you for what you have already done. I require nothing more."

"It is well," Piero muttered, turning away. *A mysterious old tiger,* the artist thought. *Truly worthy of a canvas. Ugly, carved with deep lines, looking as if the plow and the harrow had tilled over his heart. A fine contrast to my bland, smiling Messer Greco—my Bacco trionfante, who has married the fair Antigone in defiance of all history and propriety. Ah! His scholar's blood curdles uncomfortably at the sight of that old fellow's claw!*

When Piero re-entered the Piazza del Duomo, the vast multitude that had listened to Fra Girolamo was streaming out through every door. Their hurried departure testified to the profound importance they placed on the sermon, which had detained them from their daily labors. Leaning against an angle of the Baptistery, the artist watched the departing throng, delighting in the variety of their garments and the sharp, characteristic expressions on their faces—faces reminiscent of those Masaccio had painted fifty years prior, and ones that Domenico Ghirlandaio had not yet fully mastered.

That morning marked a peculiar occasion. The friar's audience, usually diverse, represented the full spectrum of Florence's classes and political factions more completely than ever. There were men of high birth, accustomed to public office, who had recently become prominent not only as enemies of the Medici and champions of popular government but as devoted *Piagnoni*, embracing the Frate's doctrines with fervor. Among them were figures of authoritative presence like Francesco Valori, possessing a hot, arrogant temperament greatly gratified by the immediate divine authority seemingly granted to bring about freedom in their own image. Others, like Soderini, displayed less of the ardent *Piagnone* and more of the seasoned politician.

There were also men of family, such as Piero Capponi, brave lovers of sober republican liberty who preferred action to argument, holding no specific objection to any idea that expelled the Medici and made room for public spirit. At their sides stood doctors of law, whose studies of Accursius and his peers had not extinguished their ardor; they too had become enthusiastic *Piagnoni*. One such was Messer Luca Corsini, who, on a memorable occasion yet to come, would raise his learned arms in street-level stone-throwing for the cause of religion, freedom, and the Frate.

Among the dignitaries carrying their black *lucco* or fur-lined mantles with habitual authority, there was a sprinkling of men with contemplative, sensitive faces: scholars bearing illustrious names like Strozzi and Acciajoli, already inclined to take the cowl and join the community of San Marco. There were artists, stirred to new heights by Savonarola's teaching, such as the young painter who had recently surpassed himself in his fresco of the divine child within the Frate's bare cell—an unconscious precursor who would one day himself wear the tonsure and be known as Fra Bartolommeo.

There was the mystic poet Girolamo Benevieni, perhaps hastening to deliver news of the beloved Frate's imminent arrival to his friend Pico della Mirandola, a man destined never to see another dawn. Well-born women appeared, attired with such scrupulous plainness that their refined grace served as the sole distinction separating them from their less aristocratic counterparts. Predominant among the crowd were the genuine *popolani* or middle class, representing both the Major and Minor Arts, acutely aware of purses threatened by war taxes.

Perhaps most striking and varied were the streams of poorer tradesmen and artisans. Their faith and hope in the Frate's divine message ranged from a rough, undiscriminating trust in him as the friend of the poor and enemy of the oppressive rich, to an eager consumption of biblical subtleties that captivated the sedentary artisan. These men found illumination in the long, dim spaces beyond their workbenches, where stitching seemed bathed in a pale flame resembling the light of Divine Science.

Yet, scattered among these devoted disciples were many who were not his followers at all. Some were Mediceans who, driven by fear and policy, had begun to display a feigned deference to the prevailing popular spirit. Others were sincere advocates of free government who viewed Savonarola merely as an ambitious monk—part sagacious, part fanatical—who had become a powerful instrument in the hands of the people and thus an unavoidable social fact. Even bitter enemies were present: members of the old aristocratic anti-Medicean party determined to seize control once more for certain chief families, or licentious young men who detested him as Florence's ultimate killjoy.

For the sermons in the Duomo had evolved into political events, attracting ears tuned to curiosity and malice as readily as to faith. Men of ideas, like the young Niccolò Machiavelli, attended to observe and report to friends in country villas. Men of appetite, such as Dolfo Spini, sought to hunt down the Frate as a public nuisance depleting game supplies, lying in wait for grounds of accusation.

Perhaps no preacher ever wielded such massive influence as Savonarola, nor faced such heterogeneous material to shape. A secret to this immense power lay in the highly mixed character of his preaching. Baldassarre, wrought into an ecstasy of self-martyring revenge, was merely an extreme case among the partial and narrow sympathies of that audience. In Savonarola's words, there were strains appealing to the finest susceptibilities of human nature, alongside elements that gratified low egoism, tickled gossiping curiosity, and fascinated timorous superstition.

His need for personal predominance, his labyrinthine allegorical interpretations of Scripture, his enigmatic visions, and his false certainty regarding Divine intentions never ceased, within his own large soul, to be ennobled by fervid piety, a passionate sense of the infinite, active sympathy, and a clear-sighted demand for the subjection of selfish interests to the general good—a trait shared by the greatest of mankind. But for the mass of his audience, the entire weight of his preaching rested on his strong assertions of supernatural claims, his denunciatory visions, and the false certainty that gave his sermons the urgency of a political bulletin. Once he had mastered that audience, it became necessary to his nature—and to their supposed welfare—to maintain that mastery.

The result was inevitable. No man struggles to retain power over a mixed multitude without suffering corruption; his standard must inevitably descend to meet their lower needs rather than rising to his own best insights. The mysteries of human character were rarely presented in a manner better suited to checking the judgments of facile knowingness than in the figure of Girolamo Savonarola. Yet, we can afford him a reverence that requires no closing of the eyes to fact if we view his life as a drama accompanied by great inward modifications during its outward changes.
