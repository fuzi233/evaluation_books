The fund was thriving. Through Miss Keeldar's example, the rectors' tireless efforts, and the quiet, efficient support of their spinster lieutenants, Mary Ann Ainley and Margaret Hall, a substantial sum had been raised. Managed with prudence, it was already alleviating the suffering of the unemployed poor. The neighborhood seemed to exhale; for two weeks, no cloth had been burned, no mill or mansion desecrated. Shirley felt the storm passing overhead, convinced that with the arrival of summer, trade would inevitably revive. Peace must return, and with it, a surge for commerce.

She often voiced this optimism to her tenant, Gérard Moore, whenever they crossed paths. Moore listened with a silence that frustrated her, prompting impatient glances for an explanation. Yet, he would smile with that peculiar sweetness that softened his mouth while his brow remained grave. "I, too," he would reply, "trust in the war's finite nature. It is my anchor. I work Hollow's Mill entirely on speculation; I sell nothing, for there is no market. I manufacture for a future day. Three months ago, this was impossible—I had exhausted my credit and capital. You know who rescued me. On the strength of that loan, I continue this bold game. Total ruin awaits loss, and gain is doubtful, yet I remain cheerful. As long as my hands are free to strive, I cannot be depressed. One year—nay, six months—of the 'reign of the olive,' and I am safe. Peace will indeed impulse commerce, as you say. But regarding the tranquility your fund has brought, I harbor doubts. Alms never truly tranquilize the working classes; they do not make them grateful. It is not human nature. They feel the humiliation of needing relief, just as we would. To whom should they be grateful? To you, perhaps to the clergy, but certainly not to us mill-owners. They hate us more than ever. Furthermore, the disaffected here correspond with leaders in Nottingham, Manchester, and Birmingham. They are disciplined; no blow is struck without deliberation. Like the sky threatening thunder that clears only to gather again, the danger is merely delayed. The storm is sure to break eventually. There is an analogy between the moral and physical atmosphere."

"Well, Mr. Moore," she would conclude, "take care of yourself. If I have done you any good, reward me by promising to do the same."

"I will," he replied, his eyes distant. "I wish to live, not to die. The future opens like Eden before me, yet within that paradise, I see a vision I prefer to any seraph."

"Do you? Pray, what vision?"

"I see—"

A maid bustled in with the tea service, breaking the spell.

Early May had been fine, the middle wet, but the last week, with the change of the moon, had cleared. A fresh wind swept away the silver-white rain-clouds, carrying them eastward until the vault above was pure blue, ready for the summer sun. That sun rose broad and golden on Whitsuntide. The gathering of the schools was marked by splendid weather.

Whit-Tuesday was the great day. The two large schoolrooms of Briarfield, built by the present rector at great personal expense, had been scrubbed, whitewashed, and adorned with flowers and evergreens—some from the rectory garden, two cartloads from Fieldhead, and a wheelbarrow's worth from the stingy domain of Mr. Wynne. Twenty tables, each seating twenty guests, were laid out with white cloths, surrounded by benches. Suspended above were at least twenty cages containing canaries, a fancy cherished by Mr. Helstone's clerk who delighted in their piercing song, knowing they carolled loudest amidst confusion of tongues. These tables were not for the twelve hundred scholars, whose feast would be in the open air, but for the patrons and teachers. At one o'clock the troops would arrive; at two, they would be marshalled; by four, they would parade the parish, followed by the feast, music, and speeches in the church.

Briarfield was chosen not for size or antiquity—Whinbury was larger, Nunnely older—but simply because Mr. Helstone willed it so, and his will was stronger than the objections of Boultby or Hall. They let him lead.

This anniversary had always been a trial for Caroline Helstone, dragging her into public view where she faced the wealthy and influential without her mother or aunt. She trembled at the prospect, a nervous soul fearing publicity. But this year, Shirley was with her, and the trial vanished, transformed into an enjoyment. Miss Keeldar was better than a host of ordinary friends; self-possessed, spirited, and easy, she commanded courage merely by her presence. The only fear was Shirley's punctuality. The heiress often lingered, and Caroline knew her uncle would not wait. At the moment the church clock tolled two, the bells would clash and the march begin. Caroline resolved to keep watch on her companion.

Whit-Tuesday saw Caroline rise almost with the sun. She and her maids spent the morning arranging the rectory parlours and setting out a collation of wine, fruit, and cakes. Then came the dressing. She wore her freshest white muslin, a costume the solemnity of the occasion exacted. Her new sash—a birthday gift from Margaret Hall, likely purchased by Cyril himself in exchange for a set of cambric bands—was tied by Fanny's dexterous fingers. Her simple bonnet was trimmed to match, and her white crape scarf suited her perfectly. She formed a picture not bright enough to dazzle, but fair enough to interest; sweetness of tint and grace of mien atoned for the lack of rich color. Her brown eyes and clear forehead revealed a modest, gentle, and harmonious mind. Neither lamb nor dove would fear her.

Yet, as Cyril Hall noted, she was an imperfect human being, fair in form but not so good or great as the withered Miss Ainley, who was currently putting on her best black gown and Quaker drab shawl in her narrow cottage chamber.

Caroline departed across sequestered fields and hidden lanes to Fieldhead. She glided under green hedges and across leas, her white garment unstained by dust or moisture. After the rains, everything was clean and dry under the glowing sun. She walked fearlessly through daisies and thick plantations until she reached Miss Keeldar's dressing room.

It was fortunate she had come, for Shirley would have been late. Instead of hurrying, the heiress lay on a couch, absorbed in a book. Mrs. Pryor urged her to rise in vain. Caroline wasted no words; she took the book and began the business of disrobing and rerobing Shirley herself. Indolent with the heat and gay with her youth, Shirley wanted to talk and linger, but Caroline persevered, fastening strings and inserting pins with rapid efficiency. Finally, as she united the last row of hooks, she chided Shirley for her unpunctuality. Shirley did look the picture of incorrigible carelessness, yet a very lovely one.

There was a striking contrast between them. Shirley possessed style in every fold of her dress; the rich silk suited her better than simplicity, and the deep embroidered scarf became her. Worn negligently but gracefully, it complemented the wreath on her bonnet. Her attention to fashion matched the frank light in her eyes and the rallying smile about her lips. Caroline took her hand, hurried her downstairs, and they sped through the fields, laughing, resembling a snow-white dove and a gem-tinted bird of paradise in social flight.

Thanks to Miss Helstone's promptitude, they arrived just in time. Before the trees hid the church, they heard the urgent summons of the bell. The trampling of feet and murmuring of voices grew louder. From a rising ground, they saw the Whinbury school approaching on the road, numbering five hundred souls. The rector and curate, Boultby and Donne, headed it. The doctor loomed large in full canonicals, walking with the dignity of a beneficed priest under a shovel-hat, flourishing his cane and inclining it dogmatically toward his aide-de-camp, Mr. Donne. Though narrow compared to his principal, Donne contrived to look every inch a curate, from his turned-up nose to his square-toed shoes.

*Walk on, Mr. Donne!* Caroline thought. *You have undergone scrutiny. Whether the white and purple figures watching you from the hill approve is another question.*

These figures ran down as the regiment passed. The churchyard was full of children and teachers in their finest holiday attire. Despite the district's distress, it was wonderful to see how respectably they had dressed; the British love of decency worked miracles. Poverty could reduce an Irish girl to rags but could not rob the English girl of the neat wardrobe necessary for self-respect. Besides, the lady of the manor, Shirley, gazed with pleasure on the well-dressed crowd. Her bounty had consoled many families and supplied children with new frocks. She knew it and was elated, glad that her money and influence had substantially benefited those around her. She could not be charitable like Miss Ainley; it was not in her nature. It relieved her to know there was another way, practicable for her character.

Caroline, too, was pleased. She had sacrificed dresses and ribbons to fit out her scholars and, unable to give money, had followed Miss Ainley's example by sewing for them.

Not only was the churchyard full, but the rectory garden was thronged with ladies and gentlemen walking among lilacs and laburnums. The house was occupied; gay groups stood at the open windows. In the parson's croft, the musicians of the three parish bands awaited. Fanny and Eliza, in smart caps and aprons, moved among them serving quarts of ale, brewed sound and strong by the rector's special orders. Whatever he touched must be managed handsomely; "shabby doings" were not endured under his sanction. Miss Keeldar shared this sentiment, and they mutually approved each other's arrangements.

Soon Caroline and Shirley were in the midst of the company. Caroline, usually retiring to a corner, moved through the parlours, conversing and smiling, seemingly a new creature. It was Shirley's presence that transformed her; the heiress's air did her a world of good. Shirley had no fear of people; unless positively offensive, she welcomed everyone, treating them kindly until proven otherwise. This disposition made her a general favorite, robbing her raillery of sting and giving her conversation a happy charm. Her intimate friendship with Caroline was distinct from this social benevolence; Caroline was the choice of her affection and intellect, while others were merely profiteers by her good nature.

Mr. Donne entered the drawing room just as Shirley sat on the sofa, center of a circle. She had forgotten her exasperation against him and bowed good-humoredly. Yet his disposition was evident: he knew neither how to decline with dignified pride nor how to meet with frank forgiveness. His punishment had impressed him with no shame. He passed by sheepishly with a scowling look. Nothing could reconcile him to his enemy, yet no passion of resentment could his lymphatic nature know.

"He was not worth a scene!" Shirley whispered to Caroline. "What a fool I was! To revenge on poor Donne his silly spite at Yorkshire is like crushing a gnat for attacking the hide of a rhinoceros. Had I been a gentleman, I should have helped him off by force. I am glad I only employed the moral weapon. But he must come near me no more. He irritates me. There is no amusement in him. Malone is better sport."

Malone seemed to wish to justify the preference. As Shirley spoke, Peter Augustus approached, all in *grande tenue*, gloved, scented, and with hair oiled to perfection. He bore a huge bunch of cabbage-roses, five or six in full bloom. He presented them to the heiress with a grace that defied the pencil. Who could dare say Peter was not a lady's man? He had gathered and given flowers, offering a poetic tribute at the shrine of Love. Hercules holding the distaff was but a faint type of Peter bearing the roses. Amazed at his own gallantry, he backed away with a husky chuckle of self-satisfaction, then stopped to verify by ocular testimony that he had indeed presented the bouquet.

Yes, there were the six red cabbages on the purple satin lap, held by a white hand with gold rings, while streaming ringlets half hid a laughing face drooping over them. Only *half* hiding! Peter saw the laugh; it was unmistakable. He was made a joke of; his chivalry was the subject of jest for two petticoats—Miss Helstone too was smiling. He felt seen through, and Peter grew black as a thunder-cloud. When Shirley looked up, a fell eye was fastened on her. Malone, at least, had energy enough in hate. She saw it in his glance.

"Peter *is* worth a scene, and shall have it, if he likes, one day," she whispered.

Then, solemn and sombre in color though bland in face, the three rectors appeared at the dining-room door. Having finished their duties in the church, they came for refreshment before the march. The large morocco-covered chair was left vacant for Dr. Boultby. Caroline, obeying Shirley's instigation to play hostess, hastened to serve her uncle's vast, revered friend with wine and macaroons. His churchwardens, patrons of the Sunday school, were beside him; Mrs. Sykes and other ladies flanked him, expressing hopes he was not fatigued and fears the heat was too great. Mrs. Boultby, believing her lord's face became angelic after a good dinner, bent over him, tenderly wiping perspiration from his brow. Boultby was in his glory, rumbling out thanks in a round, sound voice. He took no notice of Caroline except to accept her offerings, seeing only the macaroons, which he possessed of in a small handful. The wine Mrs. Boultby insisted on mixing with hot water, sugar, and nutmeg.
