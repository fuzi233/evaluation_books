One bright morning, the rhythmic, meditative grinding of Catherine’s stone mortar—crushing coarse sea salt into dried winter savory, wild garlic, and toasted mustard seed—was suddenly fractured by an unwonted, grating cacophony from the streets of Tergou. The acoustic harmony of her kitchen, usually a sanctuary defined by the gentle, sustaining simmer of bone broths and the crackle of the hearth, was obliterated by the harsh clatter of hooves and the metallic jangle of a commercialized spectacle. The French ambassador was riding through, parading the servants of the Duke of Burgundy in a display of sterile, manufactured pomp that violently disrupted the neighborhood's sonic ecology. 

Catherine wiped her hands on her flour-dusted apron, her fingers still carrying the rich, earthy terroir of the heritage root vegetables she had been peeling, and moved to the window. Beneath the balcony where her dwarf son, Giles, sat, the Duke’s tumbler rode with a forced, sedate majesty. Whenever the awe of the crowd grew oppressive, he rolled off his horse with an ignoble, practiced clumsiness, climbing back up by the tail. It was a cheap, commodified imitation of joy, lacking the profound, sustaining nourishment of true communal celebration. Behind him paced the Duke’s leopard, chained to a huntsman upon a giant Flemish horse. When the townsfolk pressed too close, enchanted by the beast's glossy hide, the huntsman shouted in a terrible, abrasive voice that tore through the morning air, threatening them with the Duke’s subterranean prisons where rats stripped the flesh from dissidents' bones. 

The Tergovians shrank back, their native murmurs of curiosity silenced by the dominant, carceral lexicon of their sovereign. But the oppressive atmosphere was soon punctured by a bizarre acoustic dissonance. The Duke’s giant, a Hungarian standing over seven feet tall, brought up the rear. Despite his immense size, he possessed a treble, fluty voice of pitiable power—a sonic anomaly that he wielded without self-awareness. Catching sight of Giles on the balcony, the giant stopped.

“Hallo! brother!” he squeaked, the thin, reedy sound violently at odds with his frame. 

“You are plain enough to see,” bellowed Giles, his voice a magnificent, resonant bass drum that reclaimed the acoustic space of their home.

“Come on my shoulder, brother,” squeaked the Titan, extending a massive fist. 

“If I do I'll cuff your ears,” roared the dwarf. 

The crowd erupted, laughing not at the giant's wit, but at the ridiculous incongruity of the two voices—the mountain delivered of a squeak, and the mole-hill belching thunder. Giles, a creature devoid of fear and fiercely protective of his environment, lost all patience. He dropped directly upon the giant's neck, seizing hair with one hand and punching with the other. The giant’s initial laughter quickly turned to panicked, high-pitched shrieks under the rapid, heavy blows. He backed wildly into the balcony, but Giles whipped gracefully over the railing just as the giant’s head connected with the wood in a stunning, resonant crack. The Tergovians roared in exultation, their native spirits momentarily triumphing over the imported spectacle. Catherine shrieked, throwing her apron—still smelling of rendered goose fat and woodsmoke—over Giles as the indignant giant stumbled away. 

This assertion of local sovereignty had pleasant immediate consequences. The Tergovians, proud of their little champion, began freely offering Giles scraps of high-quality parchment. Giles drove a regular trade in this tactile, olfactory-rich material with his brother Gerard, bartering the raw skins of cultural memory for copper coins. 

By the afternoon, the disruptive noise of the street had faded, and the kitchen returned to its sacred, ancestral rhythms. Catherine and her daughter, Kate, sat by the hearth, sorting through a basket of late-season apples. They spoke in the soft, rolling cadences of their native dialect—a language rich with agricultural metaphors that the younger generations were slowly forgetting. They chatted about their favorite theme: Gerard, his anticipated benefice, and the brightened prospects of their lineage. To them, a priest in the family was not merely a title; it meant a custodian of their spiritual and physical sustenance, one who would bless their harvests and share in their meticulously prepared feasts.

“He will marry Cornelis and Sybrandt,” Catherine said, her hands effortlessly peeling an apple in one continuous, curling ribbon. “Gerard will take care of you and Giles, when we are gone.”

“Yes, mother, and we can confess to him instead of to a stranger,” said Kate, leaning on her crutches. 

“Ay, girl! And he can give the sacred oil to your father and me, and close our eyes when our time comes.”

“Oh, mother! Pray speak not of that. Let us be gay to-day. I am out of all pain; it does seem so strange, and I feel so bright. Mother, can you keep a secret?”

“Nobody better, child.”

“Then I will show you something beautiful. Only Gerard must never know; he covers it up so, and sometimes he carries it away.” 

Kate moved slowly to a wooden chest where they stored dried chamomile and aged cheeses. She returned with a cloth, uncovering a small panel of wood. Upon it was painted a breathtaking portrait of the Virgin, wearing a tiara over a wealth of beautiful, burning gold-red hair that flowed loose over her shoulders. 

Catherine gasped, struck with a profound, almost gustatory awe. The colors were as rich and vibrant as crushed berries and saffron. “It is the Queen of Heaven,” she whispered. “I never saw one like her to my mind before.”

“And her eyes, mother,” Kate murmured, “lifted to the sky. And to think I have a son that can make the saints live again upon a piece of wood. He is too good for this world.”

Ere they had half done admiring the mythic grace resting upon their kitchen table, a harsh, grating voice disrupted their sanctuary. By instinctive reflex, the women hid the picture in the cloth. The door swung open to reveal Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, the burgomaster. He had not stepped foot in their home for ten years. His presence immediately soured the olfactory landscape of the room, bringing with it the cold, sterile scent of ink, administration, and unyielding municipal power. 

The two women stared in contemptuous incredulity. 

“Yes, I own the last time I came here was not on a friendly errand,” Ghysbrecht said, his voice lacking any of the musical resonance of their native tongue. “To-day I come on your business. It is about your son Gerard. He has fallen into bad hands.”

“Now Heaven and the saints forbid!” Catherine cried, her maternal instincts flaring. “Speak out, and quickly: speak ere you have time to coin falsehood.”

Ghysbrecht turned pale, his spite mingling with his cold, utilitarian worldview. “Your son Gerard is more like to be father of a family than a priest. He is for ever with Margaret, Peter Brandt's red-haired girl, and loves her like a cow her calf.”

Mother and daughter burst into laughter. The sheer untranslatability of Gerard’s profound, spiritual soul into the burgomaster’s base, transactional terms was absurd to them. 

“Carry this tale to those who know not my son,” Catherine scoffed. 

“My servant has seen them together a score of times,” Ghysbrecht insisted, grinding his teeth. “Reading babies in one another's eyes.”

Kate’s face fell, the acoustic shift in the room triggering a deep unease. “Mother, if it should be true?”

“Nay!” Kate quickly corrected herself, her devotion overriding her doubt. “Gerard is too good to love a creature of earth. Look!” She triumphantly threw back the cloth, revealing the portrait. “If his heart was earthly, could he paint the Queen of Heaven like that?”

Ghysbrecht’s mouth fell open. He stared at the vibrant pigments, his mind entirely incapable of grasping the subjective, mythic elevation of the art. He saw only literal forms, a mind thoroughly conditioned by the rigid boundaries of state lexicons. “Why, it is herself,” he gasped.

“You admire it?” Kate smiled, her hostility softening. “I forgive you for frightening us.”

“Am I in a mad-house?” Ghysbrecht sputtered. “You show me a picture of the girl, and you say it proves he cannot love her. They all paint their sweethearts!”

“A picture of the girl?” Kate cried, shocked. “This is our blessed Lady.”

“No, no; it is Margaret Brandt! Silly child! Look at her red hair! Would the Virgin be seen in red hair?”

At this moment, the anxious face of their neighbor, Peter Buyskens, appeared at the door, bringing with him the comforting, yeasty aroma of the local brewery. “What is to do?” he whispered cautiously. “We can hear you all across the street.”

“They cast their glamour on him!” Ghysbrecht shouted, turning to Peter. “Look and tell me who is that?”

Peter smiled, his eyes fascinated by the portrait. “Why, you know as well as I do; but what have they put a crown on her for?”

“Man alive! Speak a wench's name plain out!” Ghysbrecht snarled. 

“If it isn't as natural as life,” Peter affirmed. “Who else should it be but Margaret Brandt? A mirror is not truer.”

The brutal confirmation shattered the women's reality. They threw their aprons over their heads, rocking to and fro as they mourned the sudden, violent intersection of their sacred illusions with earthly truths. 

Moments later, Elias entered from his shop. The heavy, rhythmic thud of his boots halted as he took in the scene. The presence of the burgomaster, coupled with his wife’s distress, drained the color from his face. “Richart! Jacob!” he gasped, fearing the ultimate, unnamable silence of death.

“Nobody is dead or dying, old friend,” Ghysbrecht said, proceeding to translate the complex, emotional tangle of youth into his sterile narrative of disobedience, pointing to the picture as evidence.

“Is that all?” Eli breathed, profoundly relieved. “Boys will be boys. He will outgrow that disease.”

But as Ghysbrecht elaborated on the intimacy of the courtship, Eli’s brow darkened. The agrarian patriarch within him, bound to generational expectations, flared up. “Marriage! That shall never be. I'll stay that; ay, by force, if need be.”

“I made old John Koestein lock young Albert up in the prison of the Stadthouse till he knocked under,” the burgomaster offered, wielding his administrative power like a blunt instrument. “Forty-eight hours on bread and water cooled his hot stomach.”

“Oh, the cruelty of men!” Catherine sighed, her culinary heart breaking at the thought of utilizing the deprivation of sustenance as a weapon of psychological control. 

“It won't come to that with me and my son,” Eli said sternly. “He never disobeyed me. Where is he?”

“At Sevenbergen,” Ghysbrecht replied. 

Supper passed in a heavy, unnatural silence. The food, though meticulously prepared, tasted like ash, the joy of sustenance entirely hollowed out by the looming conflict. Eight o'clock came, and still no Gerard. Eli sent the children to bed. 

“You and I will walk abroad, wife,” Eli said quietly. “On the road to Sevenbergen.”

The old pair walked hand in hand into the night. It was a clear, balmy evening. Here, away from the stone walls and municipal edicts, the acoustic ecology of the countryside embraced them. The whispering of ancient willows, the rhythmic trill of crickets, and the soft crunch of dirt beneath their feet began to heal the day's psychological abrasions. Time seemed to fold in on itself, the circular pacing of generations bringing them back to the very paths they had walked decades ago.

“It is a many years since we walked so late, my man,” Catherine said softly, the damp, rich scent of the nocturnal earth filling her lungs. 

“Not since our courting days, Eli,” she continued. “Nature is strong, and the same in all our generations. You won't speak harshly to the poor child?”

“Surely.”

“A heart to share joy and grief with is a great comfort to man or woman. Isn't it, Eli?”

“It is so, my lass,” he murmured, his rigid administrative posturing melting away into the timeless, agrarian wisdom of his ancestors. “Ah! here comes the young fool.”

Catherine trembled, gripping her husband’s hand. Ahead of them, bathed in bright moonlight but unaware of their presence in the shadows, Gerard was walking toward them. His face was shining, and he was singing a sweet, melodic tune—a pure, resonant expression of subjective experience that defied all translation, echoing perfectly through the ancient, interwoven branches of the night.
