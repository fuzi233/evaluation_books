“And after all I’ve done for her,” moaned Mr. Reginald Cracknell, his voice cracking like a wet matchstick, his eyes glistening with the twin forces of heartbreak and excessive consumption of his legendary private stock, “after all I’ve done for her—she *throws me down*.”

Sally didn’t answer. The orchestra at the Flower Garden was playing with such relentless volume that even a shout would have been swallowed whole by the din. Plus, she was currently engaged in a life-or-death struggle to keep her feet from being stepped on by Mr. Cracknell’s erratic dance moves, which seemed to be choreographed by a drunkard who’d never heard of rhythm.

They shuffled past Miss Mabel Hobson, the Flower Garden’s newest “hostess,” who sat at her table like a queen exiled from her throne but still determined to look haughty. She wore old gold and black like a Victorian widow who’d just won the lottery, and as Cracknell passed beneath her gaze, he let out a mournful gulp, as if his soul had just been slapped by a feather duster.

“If I told you,” he whispered into Sally’s ear, “what… wait—was that your ankle? Sorry! I don’t know what I’m doing tonight. If I told you what I spent on that woman—you wouldn’t believe it. And then she throws me down. Just because I said I didn’t like her hat. It was a *rotten* hat. Didn’t suit her one bit. But that,” he grumbled, “is a woman all over!”

Sally yelped as his foot landed squarely on her instep. Cracknell mistook this for emotional protest and gallantly tried to smooth things over.

“I don’t mean *you’re* like that,” he said, puffing himself up like a wounded peacock. “You’re different. I could tell the moment I saw you. You’ve got a sympathetic nature. That’s why I’m telling you all this. You’re sensible. Broad-minded. You understand. I did *everything* for that woman. I got her this job here—can you believe how much they pay her? I starred her in a show once. Did you see those pearls she was wearing? I gave her those. And now she won’t speak to me. Just because I didn’t like her hat. I wish you could’ve seen that hat. You’d agree with me, I know, because you’re sensible and broad-minded and you *understand hats*. I don’t know what to do. I come here every night.”

Sally knew this. She’d seen him often, but this was the first time Lee Schoenstein, the gentlemanly master of ceremonies, had assigned him to her. “I come here every night and dance past her table, but she won’t look at me. What,” asked Cracknell, tears welling in his pale, watery eyes, “would *you* do about it?”

“I don’t know,” Sally admitted honestly.

“Nor do I,” said Cracknell. “I thought you wouldn’t, because you’re sensible and broad-minded... I mean, nor do I. I’m having one last try tonight, if you can keep a secret. You won’t tell anyone, will you?” he pleaded, leaning in like a man confessing to murder. “But I know you won’t, because you’re sensible... I’m giving her a little present. Having it brought here tonight. A little present. That ought to soften her, don’t you think?”

“A big one would do it better.”

Cracknell kicked her shin with a kind of horrified surprise.

“I never thought of that. Perhaps you’re right. But it’s too late now. Still, it might. Or wouldn’t it? Which do you think?”

“Yes,” said Sally.

“I thought as much,” said Cracknell, deflating like a punctured balloon.

The orchestra stopped with a thunderous *thump-bang*, leaving Cracknell clapping weakly in the middle of the floor like a lost child. Sally slipped back to her table. Her former partner, after a brief, confused glance around as if he’d misplaced his own dignity, zigzagged off toward his seat. The noise of conversation surged back in waves. The air was thick, hot, and full of voices. Sally pressed her hands over her closed eyes and remembered—again—that she had a headache.

Nearly a month had passed since her return to Mr. Abrahams’ employ. It had been a dull, leaden stretch of days, each one dragging like a bad dream. She felt cut off from the world, as if she’d been unplugged from humanity. Weeks had gone by without seeing a familiar face. None of her old boarding-house friends had crossed her path. Fillmore, no doubt guilt-ridden, had avoided her entirely. Ginger was busy chasing destiny on the south shore of Long Island.

She lowered her hands and opened her eyes. The room was packed, as usual. The Flower Garden was one of many establishments that had risen on the wave of New York’s dancing craze. Unlike its short-lived rivals, it thrived—probably because, as its owner claimed, it was “a nice place run nice.” Its ads boasted: “A supper-club for after-theatre dining and dancing,” “large and spacious, sumptuously appointed,” “one of the town’s wonder-places,” with “incomparable dance-floor, enchanting music, cuisine, and service de luxe.” Clearly, Isadore Abrahams thought highly of his own joint.

Once, Sally had liked it. In the beginning, it had been fun, stimulating, full of life. But now? Now it felt like a trap. She hadn’t had headaches before. And this crushing listlessness—this awful, soul-sucking depression—had turned her nightly work into a chore.

“Miss Nicholas.”

The orchestra had restarted, and Lee Schoenstein was introducing a new partner. Sally stood up automatically.

“This is my first time here,” said the man, as they bumped through the crowd. He was big, clumsy, and clearly not built for ballroom elegance. To Sally, it seemed the entire universe had become large and awkward. “It’s a swell place. I’m from upstate. We got nothing like this where I come from.” He cleared a space using Sally as a human battering ram. Despite her recent ordeal with Cracknell, she found herself wistfully missing that experience. This guy was definitely the worst dancer in America.

“Give me li'l old New York,” he declared, unpatriotically. “It’s good enough for me. I’ve been to some swell shows since I got to town. You seen this year’s *Follies*?”

“No.”

“You go,” he insisted. “You go! Take it from me—it’s a swell show. You seen ‘Myrtle Takes a Turkish Bath’?”

“I don’t go to many theatres.”

“You go! It’s a scream. I’ve been to a show every night since I got here. Every night regular. Swell shows—all of ‘em, except this last one. I cert’nly picked a lemon tonight. I was taking a chance, y’see, because it was an opening. Thought it would be something to say when I got home—‘I was at a New York opening.’ Set me back two-seventy-five, including tax, and I wish I’d gotten it in my kick right now. *The Wild Rose,* they called it,” he sneered, as if exposing a scandalous scam. “*The Wild Rose!* It sure made me wild all right. Two dollars seventy-five tossed away, just like that.”

Something stirred in Sally’s memory. Why did that title sound so familiar? Then it hit her—like a brick. It was Gerald’s new play. For weeks after returning to New York, she’d feared running into him—coming out of her apartment, him out of his. Then she’d read a paragraph in the paper: Gerald was touring with a new play. And “The Wild Rose”… yes, that was it.

“Is that Gerald Foster’s play?” she asked quickly.

“I don’t know who wrote it,” said her partner, “but let me tell you—he’s one lucky guy to get away alive. There’s fellows breaking stones on the Ossining Road who’ve done a lot less to deserve a sentence. Wild Rose! I’ll tell the world it made me go good and wild,” he added, spreading his humor like cheap butter. “Why, before the second act was over, the people were beating it for the exits, and if it hadn’t been for someone shouting ‘Women and children first,’ there’d have been a panic.”

Sally found herself back at her table, not quite sure how she’d gotten there.

“Miss Nicholas.”

She started to rise—and realized it wasn’t Schoenstein’s gold-teeth voice calling her. A man had seated himself beside her, speaking in clipped, precise tones that somehow felt oddly familiar. The fog lifted. Bruce Carmyle.

“I called at your place,” Mr. Carmyle was saying, “and the hall porter told me you were here, so I ventured to follow you. I hope you don’t mind? May I smoke?”

He lit a cigarette with a flourish. His fingers trembled slightly as he raised the match, but he pretended it was just a minor tremor of excitement. Bruce Carmyle believed in the strong man who rose above emotion. He knew this was awkward—but he refused to show it. He glanced sideways at Sally and thought: never had she looked more beautiful. Her cheeks were flushed, her eyes bright. The ghost of Uncle Donald, who had accompanied him on this expedition, faded into insignificance.

There was a pause. Mr. Carmyle puffed vigorously.

“When did you land?” Sally asked, needing to say something. Her mind was a jumble. Was she glad he was here? Sad? Probably glad, she decided. There was something calming about his cool, dark, stiff English demeanor. He was unlike Cracknell, unlike the man from upstate, unlike the feverish chaos of her nights. Just looking at him felt like stepping into a quiet room.

“I landed tonight,” said Bruce Carmyle, turning to face her directly.

“To-night!”

“We docked at ten.”

He turned away again. Mission accomplished. Let her ponder it.

Sally stayed silent. She understood the significance. His presence was a challenge. And yet—she barely felt anything. She’d been fighting too long. She felt like a swimmer who’d given up. The heat pressed down like a blanket. Her nerves screamed under the music and noise.

“Shall we dance this?” he asked.

The orchestra had begun a sultry, creamy melody—Broadway’s latest hit, overplayed to death. But now, somehow, it sounded fresh.

“If you like.”

Bruce Carmyle danced with the precision of a man who believed efficiency was divine. He’d mastered dance early in life, considering it part of a gentleman’s education. Sally, bracing herself for another collision course, found herself swept into the arms of a master. A man who danced better than she did. And suddenly—a miracle—a deep peace settled over her. The tension eased. The music, once stale and cheap, now felt almost sacred. Her entire attitude toward Bruce Carmyle shifted.

She’d never really analyzed her feelings for him. But one thing had been clear since their first meeting: she found him physically repulsive. Handsome, yes—but in a sinister way. She’d always recoiled from him. Now, carried away by the dance, that repulsion vanished. As if a wall had crumbled between them.

“Sally!”

She felt his arm tighten. His muscles quivered. She caught sight of his face—dark eyes blazing into hers—and stumbled, feeling oddly helpless. A shock jolted her awake. This dance hadn’t delayed the moment—it had only brought it closer.

In a whisper, carried away by the sudden blare of music, he repeated what he’d said under the trees at Monk’s Crofton, on that spring morning in England. Dizzily, she realized she was resenting the timing—but her mind was numb.

The music stopped. Clapping erupted, but Sally moved to her table. He followed like a shadow. Neither spoke. Bruce had said his piece. Sally sat, staring ahead, trying to think. She was tired—so tired. Her eyes burned. Could she fight anymore? Was anything worth fighting for?

The music stopped again. No response. The floor emptied. Feet shuffled to silence. The Flower Garden was quieter than it ever was. Even the chatter dimmed. Sally closed her eyes.

And from somewhere near the roof, a bird sang.

Isadore Abrahams kept his promises. He advertised a flower garden, and he’d tried to deliver. Paper roses hung from the walls. Real tulips bloomed in tubs by every pillar. And cages with birds dangled from the ceiling. One, startled by the sudden hush, began to sing.

Sally had often pitied these birds. She’d begged Abrahams to free them, but he’d ignored her. Yet now, as she sat wrestling with fate, the song didn’t sound like a plea. It sounded cheerful. Soothing. Healing. And suddenly, before her eyes, Monk’s Crofton appeared—cool, green, peaceful under the English sun—like an oasis in a desert.

She became aware that the master of Monk’s Crofton had placed his hand on hers. She looked down and shivered. She’d always disliked Bruce’s hands—strong, bony, covered in black hair. She’d hated the idea of them touching her. But she didn’t pull away. The vision of the garden flickered again… a haven…

He leaned in, whispering. The room was hotter, noisier, fuller than ever. The bird sang again—and now she understood its message: *Take me out of this!* What mattered, really? How, where, or by whom? Only that someone took her out.

Monk’s Crofton was cool and green and peaceful…

“Very well,” said Sally.

Bruce Carmyle, newly engaged, felt… off. Not because of Sally’s acceptance—though he’d hoped for more warmth. He was prepared to wait for that. But he couldn’t ignore the fact that he’d chosen the worst possible moment and place for his grand declaration. Moonlight, solitude, a single candle—those were the proper settings. The Flower Garden, despite its “nice-ness,” felt like a circus.

Music started again—but not the soft, romantic kind. It was a brassy, raucous rendition of a vulgar one-step, loud enough to drown any heartfelt confession. Couples swayed and collided across the floor. Behind him, two waiters paused mid-argument, embroiled in a passionate debate about spaghetti versus ravioli.

Romance was impossible. Bruce dropped into small talk.

“Deuce of a lot of noise,” he muttered.

“Yes,” said Sally.

“Is it always like this?”

“Oh, yes.”

“Infernal racket!”

“Yes.”

The romantic side of Bruce Carmyle wanted to cry. In his dreams, after the proposal, he’d envisioned himself bending tenderly over her, murmuring poetic truths. Here? He couldn’t murmur. He could barely breathe.

“Do you often come here?”

“Yes.”

“What for?”

“To dance.”

Bruce chafed. This was like his first ball at twenty—sweating behind a potted palm, talking to a girl in pink who looked like a storm cloud. He remembered the clammy collar, the unbearable silence. He didn’t want to remember it. Not now. Not at this moment.

“Darling,” he murmured, shifting his chair and leaning in, “you have made me so…”

“Batti, batti! I presto ravioli hollandaise!” cried one waiter.

“La Donna e mobile spaghetti napoli Tettrazina!” replied the other.

“…you have made me so…”

“Infanta Isabella lope de Vegas mulligatawny Toronto!” shouted the first.

“…so happy…”

“Funiculi funicula Vincente y Blasco Ibanez vermicelli sul campo della gloria risotto!” yelled the second, scoring a technical knockout.

Bruce gave up. He lit a cigarette, moodily. He felt it: everything was wrong.

The music stopped. The waiters disappeared, probably to start a feud. Silence returned—almost.

But Bruce’s emotions were jangled, out of tune. He couldn’t recapture the joy. All he had left was small talk.

“What has become of your party?” he asked.

“My party?”

“The people you’re with,” he said, still flustered. In his orderly world, girls didn’t dine alone.

“I’m not with anybody.”

“You came here by yourself?” he exclaimed, horrified. And as he spoke, the ghost of Uncle Donald reappeared, puffing disapproval through a walrus mustache.

“I am employed here,” said Sally.

Mr. Carmyle jumped.

“Employed here?”

“As a dancer, you know. I…”

Sally broke off. Something caught her eye across the room. A red-headed young man stood beside Mr. Cracknell’s chair, holding a basket. From within, a sharp, persistent yapping erupted.

Cracknell stirred. Took the basket. Lifted the lid. The yapping intensified.

He rose, clutching the basket like a soldier carrying a flag. With unsteady steps and a look of grim determination, he crossed the floor to Miss Mabel Hobson’s table. The next moment, the proud hostess was hugging a protesting Pekingese puppy to her chest, while Cracknell—seizing his moment—planted himself in the chair beside her. True love, it seemed, was back on track.

The red-headed man stared straight at Sally.

“As a dancer!” Bruce cried, finally noticing the scene. Of all the people in the room, only he had missed it. His thoughts were elsewhere—occupied by the looming specter of Uncle Donald.

A dancer! A professional dancer at a Broadway restaurant! Horror crept into Bruce’s mind. What did he really know about this girl? Was she worthy of him? Had he made a terrible mistake?

All his life, he’d recoiled from the “light-life” women of cities. Friends in London had married chorus girls, and Bruce had always viewed them as social pariahs. The passion that had brought him from South Audley Street to win Sally was fading fast.

Sally turned. And in her gaze, there was a honesty so pure it banished his doubts. He hadn’t made a fool of himself. Who could look at Sally and not see perfection?

A warm tide of feeling surged through Bruce Carmyle. Return of the hero.

And just then, the red-headed man smiled.
