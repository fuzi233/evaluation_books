The bitter, unmasked glint of hatred the burgomaster Ghysbrecht had fixed on him, fresh on the heels of his weeks of unjust imprisonment, had settled deep in Gerard’s bones: he knew the man would never rest until he was destroyed. So when he slipped around the shadowed corner of the old tower, he did not half-expect ghosts or wraiths. He expected cruel, hidden traps, laid by a treacherous foe to finish him off while his escape had barely been discovered by the town watch.

As he stole forward, a hand slipped soft but steady, brave as any soldier’s, into his own. Margaret was at his side, silent, ready to share whatever new peril waited for them.

The moment the full bulk of the haunted tower came into view, they froze, every limb turning leaden. Halfway up the stone face, a creature with a blazing fiery head, no smaller than an enormous overfed glowworm, crawled steadily up the wall. Its body was dark, but the outline blazed clear against the bright glow spilling from its head, and the whole thing stretched nearly four feet long. At the foot of the tower, a tall figure draped in pure white stood perfectly still, its silhouette exactly matching the shape of a robed woman. They both gasped, their hearts hammering hard enough to burst.
“The rope!” Gerard hissed, hoarse with awe. “It’s climbing the rope.”

Before their eyes, the fiery “worm” vanished through the gap of his old prison cell window. Its light flooded the small room within, painting the dusty window glass a deep, bloody red. The white figure at the base of the tower never stirred an inch.

Folks who do not collapse completely into hysterics when faced with what they believe is supernatural horror often fall prey to a very specific, strange madness: the wild, overwhelming urge to fling themselves directly at the terrifying object that transfixes them, the way a mesmerized bird hurls itself at the coiled snake. The great tragedian Macready used to play this beat masterfully in *Macbeth*, when Banquo’s ghost appears the second time: he would lunge, eyes averted, right at the shadow on the stage. That same wild impulse seized Margaret now. She gently set Gerard’s hand aside, stood dazed for half a breath, then let out a sharp, wild cry and darted straight for the spectre.

Gerard, ignorant of the common human quirk that drove her, was convinced the devil himself was luring her to eternal damnation. He dropped to his knees so hard his knuckles scraped the mossy turf, screaming his exorcism at the top of his terror-stricken lungs: “Exorcizo vos. In nomine beatae Mariae, exorcizo vos.”

Even as he shrieked the Latin prayers, he heard a thin, feeble cry of fear rise from the supposed spectre below. To learn that even hell’s minions could whimper with fright was the most reassuring shock of his life. He redoubled his chants, and soon spotted the ghastly white figure sinking to its knees at Margaret’s feet, blubbering and begging piteously for mercy.

A few minutes later, Kate and Giles trundled up to the tower, and stopped dead at the sight of the brand new rope dangling all the way from Gerard’s former cell window down to the grass at their feet.
“Ah, I see how it is,” Giles said, his plain, practical wits cutting straight through every superstition. “Gerard climbed down this rope. He’s free. I’m going up to check.”
“No, Giles, no!” Kate cried, her mind clouded by old, deep-seated superstition. “Can’t you see this is glamour? The evil one cast that rope out to trick you to your death! He knows how you love clambering up every high thing you see. Where would Gerard get a rope long enough to reach all this way, tied fast to a prison bar? That is not natural. Holy saints protect us tonight, for hell walks abroad in the dark.”
“Stuff and nonsense,” the dwarf hooted. “The road to hell goes downward. This rope leads up. I may live a hundred years and never get a chance at a climb this perfect again. I’d as soon break my neck and get it over with as spend my whole life wondering what was up there.” He launched himself at the rope with a cry of pure delight, light as a cat leaping for a table piled high with fresh fish. Every muscle in his gymnast’s body burned with excitement. The only concession he let Kate talk him into was tying her small lantern around the nape of his neck. “A light scares ill spirits away,” she insisted. With his broad, powerful arms hauling him upward and his short legs kicking light as feathers, Giles climbed the rope faster than Gerard had ever climbed down it. The lantern glowing on his neck turned him into that very same fiery glowworm Gerard and Margaret had spotted from the shadows. Kate watched him go, trembling all over with fear.

Then a figure burst out from behind the solid stonework of the tower, sprinting at her with a speed no mortal woman should ever be able to reach. Kate let out a thin, breathless cry that was all she could manage, her tongue sticking fast to the roof of her mouth with terror. She dropped her crutches, sank to her knees, hid her face in her hands and sobbed: “Take my body, but spare my soul!”
Margaret skidded to a halt, panting hard: “Wait, I’m just a woman!”
Kate, shaking so hard her teeth chattered, lifted her head: “Why, I am just a woman too! You scared half the wits clean out of me.”
They stared at each other, bewildered, Margaret laughing breathless: “And you scared me half to death, creeping up like that! But what of that fiery-headed thing? It was you, wasn’t it? You’re no harm. What are you doing out here at this hour of the night?”
“Nay, what are you doing out here?” Kate shot back. The realization dawned on them both at once: they had both come looking for Gerard. “You’re Margaret Brandt,” Kate breathed, and Margaret nodded. “That is all the better. You love him. Then Giles was right – he’s escaped.”

Gerard stepped forward then, and all doubt was gone. Before any more explanations could spill out, a horrible, unearthly roar boomed down from the tower, echoing like a tomb speaking:
The volume rose with every repetition. They looked up, and there was the dwarf, his arms stuffed full of old legal parchments, his face lit with feral glee, his skin glowing lurid red in the lantern light tied to his neck, the spitting image of some grinning hell imp. He hurled the parchments down at their astonished heads. Documents fluttered down like wounded wild ducks: some crumpled the second they hit the grass, some flapped hard mid-fall, others spread wide and wheeled slow, lazy circles through the night air. They had barely settled when the roar boomed out again, and another flood of parchments pattered down, until the whole lawn at the foot of the tower was whitened like fresh driven snow. Finally, the fire-headed little imp slid down the rope fast as a falling star, landed light as a feather, and held out his claw-like hand to his brother, ready to collect payment for the stolen stash of documents he had just delivered.

“Hush, speak quieter,” Gerard said, “Gather them all up, and follow us far away from this tower, somewhere safer.”
“Will you come home with me, Gerard?” Kate asked softly.
“I have no home,” he said grimly.
“You will not say that,” she protested. “After this cruel wrong, there is no one more welcome at your father’s house than you.”
“Father?” Gerard’s voice went hard as flint. “The man who was my father has turned my gaoler. I escaped from his hands, and I will never come within reach of them again.”
“It was not our father who did this,” Kate told him, and relayed every word she had overheard their brothers Cornelis and Sybrandt plotting, to frame him and hand him over to the burgomaster. But the wound was still too fresh to soothe. Gerard’s anger, raw and sharp, was fiercer than anyone had ever seen it. “Cornelis and Sybrandt are two mangy curs that have bared their fangs at me for years, but they never had the power to hurt me this bad. It was our father that gave the burgomaster leave to touch me – a free burgher of this town – or he would never have dared lay a finger on me. So be it. I was his son, he made me his prisoner. He has played his part. Now I will play mine. Farewell to Tergou, the burgh where I was born, where I lived honest, and where they threw me in a dungeon. As long as there is a single other town left in the world, I will never set foot in you again.”
“Gerard, Gerard!” Margaret whispered to Kate, “Do not argue with him now. Give his rage time to cool.” Kate turned to her, asked to look at her face, and the inspection pleased her. “That is a comely face,” she whispered back, “and no mischief-maker’s.”
“Fear me not,” Margaret smiled back. “I could never be happy without your love, just as I could never be happy without Gerard’s.”
“Those are sweet words,” Kate sobbed. “I never thought I would come to love you so fast. My heart wants to embrace you, but my lame legs will not let me.” Margaret leaned in, wrapped her arms gentle around Gerard’s sister, and kissed her warm. Gerard kissed Kate goodbye too, and she hobbled off on her crutches, the soft, patient sound of her last sigh drifting back to them across the grass. Margaret’s eyes filled thick with tears, but Gerard, caught up in the hard, bright edge of his resolve, did not notice.

As they turned toward the road to Sevenbergen, Giles nudged Gerard in the side with his armful of stolen parchments, and held out his open palm. Margaret tried to talk Gerard out of paying for the stolen goods. “Why take property that does not belong to us?”
“Why not? Spoil your enemy any way you can,” Gerard laughed.
“But might they not use this as an excuse to hunt you down and hurt you all over again?”
“What can they do? Do you think I am staying in Tergou after tonight? The burgomaster stole my liberty. If I could, I would take his life in return. What – is a man’s life worth more than his freedom? I cannot take his life, so I will take the first thing of his I can lay my hands on.” He pressed a handful of small silver coins into the dwarf’s palm, and the little man, overjoyed, shuffled off after his sister. Before long, their old friend Martin joined them, and the three of them set off fast down the dark road to Sevenbergen, leaving Tergou and all its treacheries far behind them.
