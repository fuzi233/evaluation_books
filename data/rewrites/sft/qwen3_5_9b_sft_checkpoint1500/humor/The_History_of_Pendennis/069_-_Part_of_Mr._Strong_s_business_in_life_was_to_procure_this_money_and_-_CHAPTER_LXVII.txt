While Pen was busy weaving his selfish parliamentary schemes within his own county, news arrived that Lady Rockminster had returned to Baymouth, bringing with her the object of his deepest dread and fascination: Laura.

At the announcement that his sister was near, Pen felt a sudden, sharp pang of guilt. His ambition soared higher than any other desire—he wished to stand supreme in her esteem, to be the worthy patron and protector she had inherited from their mother. Yet, how could he possibly brave the news he carried? How would he explain his meditated plans to that spotless judge? He imagined neither he nor Blanche could withstand Laura's dazzling, calm scrutiny. To reveal his worldly hopes and ambitions to her seemed like confessing a crime to an angel.

Driven by a mix of panic and hypocrisy, Pen wrote a letter filled with flowery phrases, grand protests of affection, and easy, biting satire. Yet, beneath the ink, he felt like a rogue caught in the act, terrified of his own duplicacy.

"How could a simple country girl inspire such trembling in an accomplished gentleman?" he wondered, shaking his head as he rode from Clavering to Baymouth. His worldly tactics, his diplomatic wit, and his cynical knowledge of human nature crumbled before her purity. He realized with a sinking heart that his affairs were in such a precarious position that he could not tell the truth to that honest soul. Riding along, he felt as guilty as a schoolboy forgetting his lesson before facing the dreaded master. Was truth not the ultimate authority? Did she not hold the book of judgment?

Meanwhile, Laura had blossomed under the charge of her "wayward" and absolute patroness, Lady Rockminster. Though she had seen enough of the world to gather some accomplishments, her spirit remained unbroken. While many girls, coddled from infancy, would have withered in the great careless world, Laura adapted with a trustful smile. She possessed a peculiar virtue—a "sparkling Kohinoor" in her bosom. It was not merely wealth, but a talisman of innocence and cheerful confidence. Like Cogia Hassan's famous stone, it fetched a price and lit up the darkness of life. Before a year had passed, she had won the hearts of everyone in Lady Rockminster's household, from the ancient, irritable dowager to the grumbling Swiss major-domo. She had softened the harsh mistress, charmed the kitchen staff, and even converted the stern footmen, Calverley and Coldstream, into enthusiastic supporters of her convalescence. She achieved this not through art or flattery, but simply by the force of her good nature and her capacity to please.

When Pen finally arrived, he expected the worst. He braced himself for Laura to treat him as a clumsy, ambitious outsider. Instead, she rushed down the stairs, embracing him so warmly that Pen nearly forgot the presence of the footmen. She kissed him, her cheeks flushed with health and beauty, her eyes beaming with a frank kindness that smote him deeply.

"My goodness, how kind you are to me!" Pen stammered, holding her hand. "I don't deserve it!"

"Mamma left you to me," Laura replied, brushing his forehead. "We made a pact, Arthur, last year. You were to come to me when troubled or happy. Are you happy now, or in trouble?"

Her arch, kind glance pierced him. "Do you know about the Parliament plan?" Pen asked.

"Everyone knows!" she exclaimed, her spirits soaring. "The doctor discussed it today. The *Chatteris* paper will print it tomorrow. Sir Francis Clavering retires, and Mr. Arthur Pendennis steps in! And, oh, Arthur, you mentioned Miss Blanche Amory? Is it true? 'Tout se sait,' as they say!"

"What! That too?" Pen gasped.

"Yes, dear Arthur. I've heard the whole tale. I received a letter from Blanche; she speaks so warmly of you! I hope... I know she feels what she writes. When is it to be, Arthur? Why didn't you tell me? May I not come to live with you?"

"My home is yours, dear Laura," Pen said, trying to sound firm. "Everything I have is yours. I didn't tell you because... nothing is decided. No words have passed between us. But you think Blanche could be happy with me?"

"Not a romantic fondness, I know," Pen hurried on, seeking to dismiss the thought. "Only a sober attachment. I need my wife on one side of the fire and my sister on the other. Parliament in the session, Fairoaks in the holidays, and my Laura never to leave me until someone with a right comes to take her away."

"Somebody with a right!" Pen muttered the phrase, feeling a strange, hot jealousy rise in his chest. Why did he feel angry at the invisible entity with the legal right to remove Laura? He was hurt that she received his news so lightly, as if his happiness were already guaranteed.

"I'll stay home and be Aunt Laura," Laura laughed, performing a perfect Parisian curtsey. "I'm an excellent housekeeper. I've learned to sing and dance with the money you sent me. When you become a Minister of State, Blanche shall present me!"

Just then, Lady Rockminster entered, extending a finger for Pen to shake. He bowed clumsily.

"So you are going to marry, sir?" the old lady declared. "And go into Parliament to replace that good-for-nothing Sir Francis. I wanted him to give my grandson his seat! I hope you're getting a great deal of money with Miss Amory. I wouldn't take her without a fortune."

"Sir Francis is tired of Parliament," Pen winced. "And I wish to try that career. The rest is premature."

"I wonder," Lady Rockminster sniffed, "when you had Laura at home, you could take up with such an affected little creature as that."

"I am very sorry Miss Amory does not please your ladyship," Pen smiled weakly.

"You mean it's no affair of yours! Well, I'm not marrying her, and I'm very glad. A little odious thing! When I think a man could prefer her to my Laura..." The old lady rambled on about the selfishness of modern youth, comparing the current generation of suitors—who wanted money—to the gallantry of her own time. She berated the young men for not falling in love with Laura, citing the three attaches at the Embassy who had dismissed her solely due to financial reasons.

"Where is your friend Bluebeard?" she demanded. "Warrington! Why doesn't he marry Laura? They all marry for money now. You are all selfish cowards!"

Pen defended himself with some heat, noting that she hadn't consulted Laura before making accusations.

"But you haven't," Laura interjected gently, taking Pen's hand. "He is going to marry somebody else. I intend to be very fond of her and live with them, provided he doesn't ask every bachelor why he doesn't marry me."

With her conscience cleared and Pen's examination passed without reproach, Pen found himself drawn constantly to Baymouth. Lady Rockminster, delighted to have a captive audience, ordered him to dine there daily to improve his manners. "Don't blush and stammer!" she chided, serving him champagne. "Almost all young men are selfish. My sons and grandsons were too, until I cured them. Come here, and let us teach you to behave properly."

Despite the Dowager's tyrannical ordering of the household, she was beloved, and her small circle included the local vicar and his family. After dinner, when the old lady napped, Pen and Laura would sit together, listening to her soft humming.

"If my music gives her a nap," Laura said kindly, "am I not glad?"

"Why didn't you write to me when you were ill?" Pen asked, blushing.

"What good could you do? I had Martha and the doctor. You are too busy with your books and politics," she teased gently. "I wrote when I was well."

Pen looked at her, ashamed to remember that during her entire illness, he had never written to her.

Through their frequent rides, Pen grew to appreciate Laura's frankness and the simplicity of her heart. Their mother's lingering shadow still influenced them; Laura had held back from Pen previously, fearing her mother's disapproval of their closeness. Now, with Arthur engaged to another, that barrier vanished. They spoke often of their mother, recalling the constant, silent devotion she had shown.

One day, Pen confessed his guilt. "Other people's children play on the grass where we used to play," he said, his voice hard. "Look at the magnolia we planted; it has grown huge. I've visited the cottages where Mother used to help. It's barely a year since she died, and the people care no more for her than for Queen Anne. We are all selfish. There are but few exceptions, like you, to shine like good deeds in this naughty world."

Laura looked down, touching the honeysuckle on her breast. "When you gave me that flower, you weren't selfish, Arthur."

"But it was a sacrifice!" Pen sneered, half-jesting.

"But your heart was kind," Laura insisted. "If you think humbly of yourself, the love diminishes, doesn't it? Perhaps your mother spoiled you by worshipping you. And as for the world, men must fight for themselves. If the world is wicked, why pursue it?"
