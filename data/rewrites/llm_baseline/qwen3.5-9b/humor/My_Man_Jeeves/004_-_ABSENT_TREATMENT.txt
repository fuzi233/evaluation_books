Let me tell you all about dear old Bobbie Cardew. It's a fascinating tale. I can't bother with high-flown literary flourishes, nor do I need to, because the story carries its own moral weight. If you're a gentleman, you'd better listen closely, for it serves as a stern warning; if you're a lady, you'll likely find it equally gripping, as it chronicles the precise moment a certain girl reduced a man to absolute despair.

To anyone who has recently crossed paths with Bobbie, it might come as a shock to learn that there was a time when his defining characteristic was a catastrophic lack of memory. Countless acquaintances, meeting him after his transformation, have expressed genuine surprise upon hearing this. Yet, it is undeniably true. Trust me on that.

In my early days of knowing him, Bobbie Cardew was arguably the most notorious "rotter" within a four-mile radius. People have called me a simpleton, but I was never in the same league as Bobbie. When it came to being a fool, he was a "plus-four" man, whereas my handicap was roughly six. Why, if I wished to secure his attendance at dinner, I would dispatch a letter at the start of the week, follow it with a telegram and a phone call the day prior, and then, mere half-hours before the appointed time, send a messenger in a taxi specifically tasked with ensuring he boarded the vehicle and that the chauffeur had the address correct. Even with this elaborate machinery, I usually succeeded in getting him, unless he had already fled town before my courier arrived.

The amusing irony was that Bobbie wasn't entirely devoid of intellect. Deep within him lay a stratum of common sense. I had witnessed him, on rare occasions, display a spark of almost human intelligence. But accessing that layer required dynamite. Or so I believed.

However, there existed another method of detonation that had escaped my notice: marriage. Specifically, marriage—the dynamite of the soul—that was what finally blew Bobbie's fuse. He wedded. Have you ever observed a puppy chasing a butterfly? The pup spots the fluttering insect, finds it appealing, yet remains oblivious to the inevitable sting until impact occurs. Thus it was with Bobbie. He fell in love, he married—with a jubilant whoop, treating it as the pinnacle of existence—and only afterward did reality set in.

His bride was not the type of girl one might expect Bobbie to idolize. And yet, I confess, I'm unsure why. Perhaps it was the allure of a woman earning her own living; to a man unused to labor, there is an undeniable romance in a girl who works for her bread.

Her name was Mary Anthony. She stood five-foot-six, possessed a magnificent cascade of red-gold hair, piercing grey eyes, and a chin of steely determination. She was a hospital nurse. When Bobbie shattered his leg playing polo, she was assigned by the medical staff to tend his wounds and soothe his brow with cooling unguents. Before a week had elapsed, the pair had dashed to the registrar's office and sealed their fate. Pure romance.

Bobbie broke the news to me at the club one evening, introducing me to Mary the following day. I admired her immediately. My name is Reggie Pepper (forgive the omission), nephew of the late Edward Pepper of Pepper, Wells, and Co., the colliery magnates. Though I inherited a substantial fortune and thus never needed to toil, I harbor a profound respect for those who earn a living under duress, particularly a woman who, as an orphan, had thrown herself into survival from a young age.

Mary and I became fast friends. We are not so today, but we shall arrive at that tragedy later. For now, consider the past. Mary viewed Bobbie as the greatest creation on earth, evidenced by the adoring glances she cast his way whenever she deemed me unaware. Bobbie reciprocated this sentiment. Consequently, I concluded that, provided dear old Bobbie remembered to attend the wedding, they stood a fair chance of happiness.

Fast forward a year. The narrative truly begins here.

They rented a flat and settled down. I visited frequently, keeping a close eye on proceedings. Everything appeared to be sailing smoothly. If this was what marriage entailed, I struggled to comprehend the widespread fear men held regarding matrimony. Surely, worse fates awaited a man elsewhere.

Then came the Incident of the Quiet Dinner, where love's youthful dream collided with a jagged rock, and chaos ensued.

I encountered Bobbie in Piccadilly, and he invited me to dinner at their flat. Like a fool, instead of fleeing to seek police protection, I accepted.

Upon arrival, Mrs. Bobbie presented a sight that left me staggered. Her golden hair was piled into intricate waves adorned with a dazzling diamond accessory. She wore a dress of such exquisite perfection that words fail to capture it; it was simply the limit. I reasoned that if this was her attire for a quiet, domestic dinner, no wonder Bobbie cherished domesticity.

"Here's old Reggie, dear," Bobbie announced cheerfully. "I've brought him home for dinner. I'll phone the kitchen to send it up now—what?"

Mrs. Anthony stared at him as if encountering a stranger. Her complexion shifted rapidly from scarlet to the pallor of a ghost, followed by a nervous, brittle laugh. It was a spectacle so captivating I wished I were perched in a tree eight hundred miles away. Recovering her composure, she smiled at me.

"I am so glad you could come, Mr. Pepper," she said.

From that moment onward, she performed admirably. She chattered incessantly, teased Bobbie, and played ragtime on the piano with feigned nonchalance. On the surface, it was a jovial gathering. However, lacking the keen insight of a detective, I noted her initial expression and realized she was exerting immense effort to maintain control. She would have traded every possession, including that diamond hairpiece, for a single, honest release of emotion. I endured some tedious evenings in my life, but none compared to this. Seizing my opportunity, I departed immediately.

Predictably, the next day I found Bobbie at the club looking as despondent as a lonely drop of gum at an Eskimo tea party.

Eager to discuss matters, he launched into conversation.

"Do you know how long I've been married?" he asked.

"Not exactly," I replied.

"Around a year, surely?"

"Not *around* a year," he lamented. "Exactly a year—yesterday!"

Lightbulbs flashed above my head.

"Yesterday was...?"

"The anniversary of our wedding. I had planned to take Mary to the Savoy and then Covent Garden. She insisted on hearing Caruso. I had the tickets for the box in my pocket. Do you know, throughout dinner, I harbored a vague suspicion that I had forgotten something, but I couldn't identify it?"

"Till your wife mentioned it?"

He nodded.

"She... mentioned it," he mused.

I declined further details. Women possessing hair and chins like Mary's may be angels most of the time, but when they shed their wings, they strike with full force.

"To be perfectly frank, old top," Bobbie confessed in a broken tone, "my stock is perilously low at home."

With little recourse available, I lit a cigarette and remained silent. Eventually, he excused himself. Standing at the window overlooking Piccadilly, I watched him stroll aimlessly, stop, resume walking, and finally enter a jewelry store. This confirmed my earlier observation: deep down, Bobbie retained a sliver of sense.

From this point, I became genuinely invested in Bobbie's marital predicament. While one naturally hopes for success in a friend's marriage, this situation differed. The average man is not Bobbie, nor is the average woman Mary. It was a collision of an immovable object and an unstoppable force. Here was Bobbie, a gentle chap in many respects but a chump of the highest order. Opposing him was Mary, determined to prevent his foolishness. Furthermore, Nature, on Bobbie's side, offered a protective armor: short-term memory loss. Memory deficits preserve a man's status as a chump; remove them, and he might evolve into something less amusing. Consider my own case. I am a chump. Had I retained half the lessons taught to me throughout my life, my hat size would currently be a nine. Instead, I forgot them. Bobbie operated on identical principles.

For approximately a week, the memory of that disastrous dinner served as a tonic. Even elephants, renowned for their recall, were outmatched by Bobbie during this period. However, the shock merely dented his mental armor; it did not pierce it. Soon, he returned to his habitual state of oblivion.

It was pitiful. The poor girl loved him and lived in terror. She recognized the thin edge of the wedge. A man who forgets his wedding anniversary after a year of marriage will eventually forget he is married at all. Realizing this, she had to act decisively before he drifted irretrievably away.

I attempted to reason with Bobbie when he confided in me one afternoon regarding a trivial item she requested he fetch—a book, perhaps.

"It's such a minor thing to cause such a fuss," Bobbie sighed. "She knows it's solely because I possess an infernal inability to remember anything. I can't recall a thing. Never could."

As he prepared to leave, he produced two sovereigns.

"Oh, by the way," he added.
