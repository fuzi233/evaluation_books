“Good Fortune is a flighty lass who never tarries long in one place: she brushes the hair from your forehead, steals a quick kiss, and flutters off before you can grasp her. But Dame Ill Luck? She presses you tight to her heart, swears she’s in no hurry, pulls a chair up to your bedside, and settles in to knit beside you for hours.”

The quiet, heavy sorrow Mirah had been bracing herself for, the lingering threat she’d half expected to creep up on her for weeks, had finally arrived, and it came dragging all its old, heavy baggage behind it.

She’d just left a charity morning concert in a stately Knightsbridge townhouse, where Klesmer had recommended her to sing for a crowd of wealthy patrons. Her performance dress was plain, unadorned black, wrapped over with a light dust cloak to shield it from the grime of the London streets, nothing about her marked to draw unwelcome notice. But the steady, even footsteps trailing her as the last of the departing guests climbed into their carriages sent a cold prickle up her spine, and her feet went heavy as lead. She knew that pace. It was the ghost she’d fled, the father she’d never thought she’d see again once she’d found her way to Mrs Meyrick’s hearth, once she’d been reunited with Ezra. She could not bring herself to turn and face him, every muscle tensed against the rush of old, tangled grief and shame that would crash over her the second their eyes met. If it was him, he would make her stop soon enough. She walked on, not speeding her steps—there was no outrunning the past—all the while wishing she had not given Mrs Meyrick her word not to hide anything about him. That regret hardened into a sharp, urgent purpose: she would not let him surprise her at her own door, not when Ezra might be caught off guard, not when she could soften the shock for the brother she cherished. She would face him of her own will, before they turned into the little quiet square that held her new home.

She was just rounding the square’s stone entrance when the footsteps drew level, a calloused hand curled firm around her wrist, and a voice with that old, honeyed, familiar lilt purred, “Mirah!”

She did not start. She had been waiting for that voice. Her face was grave as if she stood before an executioner, while his was arranged carefully to seem soft, penitent, eager to win her over. Once he had been a strikingly handsome man, bright of cheek and quick of smile, but now his skin was sallow, lined deep by years of wheedling favors and shrugging off consequences, and he carried that easy, impudent suavity of a man who has long since learned to court affection while expecting no real respect. Slim, agile, he looked far younger than his fifty-seven years, and his threadbare, ill-fitting coat was just the same as the one she remembered from the last awful day she’d run from him. The old, tangled anguish of shame and grief, revulsion and pity, swelled in her chest sharper than it ever had before: now she belonged to a world of people who loved her, who valued her, and every line of his worn, shabby frame made him a target for the very contempt she would have given her own life to spare him.

“It is you, father,” she said, her voice low and trembling.

“Why did you run away from me, little one?” he began, words tumbling fast, as if he had rehearsed them a hundred times before, his hands darting in little quick gestures like a shorthand language. “What did you ever have to fear from me? I never made you do a single thing against your will. I broke that terrible engagement for you in the Vorstadt because I saw it didn’t suit you, and you repaid me by vanishing, leaving me to face the consequences. I’d landed you a far better spot at the Dresden Vorstadt Theater, you see— I never told you, I wanted to surprise you. And you left me stranded there, forced to slip out of town in the dead of night to avoid being locked up for breaking contract. That was hard, after I’d given up every last thing to give you the singing education that was supposed to make your fortune. No other father on earth ever poured more of himself into his daughter than I poured into you. You saw how I bore that blow when your voice didn’t turn out to be the great fortune we’d hoped for, and I made the best of it. And when I had no one else in the world but you, when I was getting worn down fighting my way through life with nothing but my wits? You chose that exact moment to abandon me. Who else did you owe every single thing to, if not me? For all you cared, I could have died in a ditch.”

He stopped short, not for lack of excuses, but to hit that carefully calibrated pathetic climax, and he let out a sharp, feminine-sounding sob, fumbling for an old yellow silk handkerchief from his pocket. He truly, in that moment, believed Mirah had treated him cruelly— the kind of selfish sensitivity that grows strong in unscrupulous men, who keep a running tally of what the world owes them and never once bother to subtract the harm they have done.

But Mirah had held firm to her newfound courage, and for the first time in her life, she spoke to him with quiet accusation. “You know exactly why I left you, father. I learned I could not trust you, that you had lied to my mother. If I could have believed a single word you said, I would have stayed by your side and worked for you until my hands ached.”

“I never meant to deceive your mother, Mirah,” he said, sniffling and tucking his handkerchief away, voice thick as if he was fighting back more sobs. “I was going to bring you both back to her, but delays and bad luck got in the way, and then word came she was already dead. It was better for you to stay with me, anyway— Ezra was old enough to look after himself. No one had any claim on me but you. A close friend of mine wrote to tell me the news, and I sent him money to cover all the funeral expenses. Though, to be sure, there’s always the chance—” he added quickly, already guarding against the unlikely possibility Mirah might have proof otherwise “—that he lied to me just to get his hands on that cash.”

Mirah said nothing. She could not bear to say out loud the truth burning in her throat, that she did not believe a single word that came out of his mouth, and she only motioned for them to keep walking, afraid that their odd mismatched pair—her, neat and polished in the fine dress her new friends had gifted her, and him, shabby and foreign-looking, gesticulating wildly at her side—would draw stares from passersby.

“You seem to have done very well for yourself, little Mirah. You’re not wanting for anything, are you?” he said, eyes raking over her new coat, her neat boots, sharp as a crow picking over scraps.

“Good friends found me when I was destitute, and helped me find work,” Mirah said, her mind half already on the words she would have to say when they reached her door. “I give singing lessons, I perform at private houses. I just finished singing at a charity concert, today.” She paused, then added pointedly, “I have very good friends, who know every single thing about my past.”

“And you’d be ashamed for them to see your own father turned out like a beggar? I don’t blame you. I came all the way to England with nothing but the faint hope of finding you. A mad, foolish quest, but a father’s heart is superstitious— it feels a magnet drawing it somewhere, no matter how far. I could have done perfectly well staying abroad, when I didn’t have you to care for. I could have rolled through life as easy as a marble. But it’s a cruel thing to be all alone in the world, when your spirit is starting to wear thin. I thought my little Mirah would come to her senses, and repent leaving her old father behind. I’ve scraped and scrimped to get by, and I don’t know what I’ll be reduced to next. A man with my talents is useless in this country. Once your coat’s frayed at the cuffs, no one will give you a second look, no matter what you can do. I’ve already had to sink pretty low just to earn a single shilling.”

Mirah’s heart ached, her mind racing with ways to keep him from sinking further into degradation, before he cut in sharply: “Where do you live, Mirah?”

“Right here, in this square. We’re almost at the door.”

“Lodgings?”

“Yes.”

“Anyone there to look after you?”

“Yes,” Mirah said, meeting his sharp, darting gaze full on. “My brother.”

Lapidoth’s eyelids flinched as if lightning had struck right in front of his face, his shoulders twitching almost imperceptibly. After a long, visible pause, he said, “Ezra? How on earth did you find him?”

“It’s a long story. This is our door.” Mirah stepped up onto the stone doorstep, turning back to face him where he stood on the pavement below. Her heart was thudding hard at the thought of the meeting that was coming, of the son he had abandoned so many years before. Standing there, looking down at him, seeing the faint, shrinking unease in his frame, she felt that sharp, tender filial shame, that stab at the heart of the reverence she had always held for him no matter what he did. “He will never ask me to shut the door on you.”

“Wait a minute, my dearest,” Lapidoth said, voice dropping low and cajoling, the old familiar tone he had used to get his way when she was small. “What sort of man has Ezra grown into?”

“He is a good man. A remarkable man,” Mirah said, slow and firm, trying to steady her trembling voice as she tried to warn him, to prepare him for the man he would be standing before. “He was very poor when our friends found him, a humble workman. Twelve years ago, he was strong and happy, making his way to the East that he loved so dearly, but our mother called him back— because she had lost me. He went to her, and took care of her through every terrible hardship, worked himself to the bone for her until she died of grief, heartbroken. The cold bit through him on his journey back to her, and his strength never recovered. For years he’s grown weaker, always poor, always working— but he’s full of wisdom, great and noble in his soul. Everyone who meets him honors him. To stand before him is like standing before a prophet of God.” Mirah’s voice broke, her heart hammering in her chest. “Lies will do you no good there.”

She cast her eyes down, unable to bear the sight of the frustrated, ugly look that settled over his face. But Lapidoth never missed a beat, already spinning a new plan.

“Mirah, my love,” he said, soft and wheedling, “wouldn’t you rather I make myself a little more presentable before my son sees me? If you could lend me a small sum, say ten pounds, I could get myself a new set of clothes, and come home to you both as a father ought to, and then I could find myself a decent post. With a decent shirt and coat on my back, people would jump at the chance to hire me as a courier, instead of turning up their noses at a ragged-looking old mountebank. I want to come home to my children, forget all the hard times, and forgive all that’s past. You’ve never seen your father look so ragged. If you can give me the ten pounds right now, or meet me somewhere to give it to me, I could be properly fitted out by the day after tomorrow.”

Mirah could feel the temptation pulling at her, but she fought it. She looked back up at him, steady. “I hate to say no to you, father. But I promised I would never do things for you in secret. It hurts so bad to see you looking so needy, but we can bear that for a little while. Then you can have all the new clothes you need, and we will pay for them together.” She saw now exactly how wise Mrs Meyrick had been, to make her swear that promise.

Lapidoth’s easy smile dropped away, and he sneered. “You’re a hard little thing, aren’t you? Learned all sorts of fine little virtues, keeping promises that stop you from giving your own father a couple of pounds when you’re running around in fine silk dresses, the father who worshipped you as his little idol and gave up the best years of his life to raise you.”

“I know it feels cruel,” Mirah said, her lips going suddenly pale. This moment felt worse than the day she had walked to the river to drown herself. “But it is far crueler to break the promises that the people who love you trust in. That is what broke my mother’s heart. That is what broke Ezra’s health. We have to bear the bitterness of all our past mistakes now, together. Come in with me, just as you are. We will take care of you.”

“Tomorrow, then,” Lapidoth said, half turning on his heel away from her, from the trembling pale girl who now had the whole world of good, honorable people behind her. But he spun right back, hands patting restlessly at his pockets, voice soft and appealing again. “This has all knocked me for a loop, Mirah. I’ll gather my spirits by tomorrow. But if you’ve got a little loose change in your pocket, it wouldn’t break your promise to give me a trifle, just enough to buy a cigar to calm my nerves on the walk back.”

Mirah could not think straight. Her cold, trembling hands went into her pocket for her purse, and she held it out to him. He grabbed it immediately, squeezing her fingers in his, and said, “Goodbye, little girl. Tomorrow, then!” and vanished down the street.

He had not gone more than a dozen steps before he fumbled open the purse, counting out the two half-sovereigns and loose silver, and found pasted inside the fold a scrap of paper: Ezra’s beautiful, neat Hebrew script, their mother’s name, her birth, marriage, and death dates, and the prayer scrawled at the bottom: May Mirah be delivered from all harm. Mirah loved to tuck little pieces of Ezra’s writing in every one of her possessions. Lapidoth read it, and for a split second he saw his own wedding day, the bright, young, blameless man he had once been, full of plans to make his fortune writing, head over heels in love with his beautiful bride Sara, soft and sensitive to every one of her moods. He had traveled so very far from that young man. The memory was hollow, no feeling attached to it, like looking at a meal you could no longer taste. In the long lazy, selfish years of his life, he had gambled away his capacity for remorse, for unselfish regret, the same way a dying man loses the feeling in his extremities, and watches the numbness creep higher.

He snapped back to the present quickly enough, already calculating how much he could pawn the pretty, fine gift purse for, on top of the cash inside, and how he could squeeze more money out of Mirah without ever having to move into that house and live under the watchful, unblinking eye of that formidable, honorable son.

Back inside the little Brompton house, Mirah could not hold her pain in any longer. She found Ezra sitting by the fire, sorting through his old handwritten manuscripts, planning to give them to Deronda. All her months of careful self-control shattered, and she fell to her knees at his feet, clinging to his sobs tearing out of her, crying “Ezra, Ezra!”

He did not speak at once, his fear spurring him to imagine every worst possible harm that could have come to her, but Mirah fought to catch her breath, and blurted it all out through tears. “My father. Our father. He followed me home. I wanted to bring him inside, I told him you would never turn him away. But he said no, not now. He said he would come tomorrow. He begged me for money, and I gave him my purse, and he left.”

Ezra’s face softened, his fears fading. “Wait until you are calm, little one, and tell me every single thing that happened.” He took off her hat, and laid his warm, gentle hands on her head, and the quiet calm of him seeped into her until she could tell him the whole story, word for word.

“He will not come tomorrow,” Mordecai said, quiet and sure. Neither of them said what they both knew: that he would likely lurk around the square, waiting to catch Mirah alone again, to beg more money from her.

“Dost thou see?” he said after a long, quiet moment, his voice soft and ancient, like a Rabbi speaking wisdom handed down for a thousand years. “This is the lot of Israel. Grief and glory are tangled together as smoke and flame. It is because we have inherited the good of our line that we feel the weight of the evil. These things are wedded to us, just as our father was wedded to our mother.”

The street outside was ordinary Brompton, the clatter of omnibuses rumbling past the window, but his words might have come straight from the halls of the Babylonian Talmud. The old Rabbi said once that the Holy One spends His time making marriages— and the saying is only light to the ear of the man who hears it wrong. By marriages, he meant all the strange, wondrous, tangled combinations of the universe that weave together the stuff of all our joy, and all our sorrow, and all the messy, precious love that holds a family together no matter what.
