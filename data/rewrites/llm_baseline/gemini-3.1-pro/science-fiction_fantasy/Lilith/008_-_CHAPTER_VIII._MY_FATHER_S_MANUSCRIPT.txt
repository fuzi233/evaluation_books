I am filled with a profound, almost paralyzing awe of the manuscript I must now transcribe. The sun is shining a rich, clarified golden above me; the sea lies a deep, brine-scented blue beneath his gaze; the same agrarian world sends its foraging roots and climbing shoots up to the sun, and its flying things into the air which I have breathed from my infancy. Yet I know the outspread splendour is but a passing show, a fragile canopy that at any moment may, like the drop-scene of an old theatre, be lifted to reveal older, more mythic spaces interwoven into the very fabric of our domestic lives.

Shortly after my father’s death, I was seated one morning in the library, surrounded by his meticulous food archives. I had been rhythmically grinding toasted coriander and dried bitter-orange peel in a heavy stone mortar, seeking solace in the repetitive, grounding acoustics of the pestle, while somewhat listlessly regarding the portrait that hangs among the cookbooks. I knew the painted figure only as that of a distant ancestor, old Sir Upward, and found myself wishing I could unearth the lost regional dialects and forgotten culinary techniques of his era. I had just taken a brittle, hand-bound volume from the shelves and begun to read, inhaling the scent of decaying parchment and ancient tallow. 

Glancing up, I saw coming toward me—not between me and the heavy oak door, but between me and the portrait—a thin, pale man dressed in a rusty black coat the deep, oxidized shade of aged balsamic. He looked sharp and eager, with a notable nose that immediately reminded me of a ceramic jug my sisters used to call Mr. Crow. 

“Finding myself in your vicinity, Mr. Vane, I have given myself the pleasure of calling,” he said. His voice possessed a peculiar but not disagreeable timbre; it carried the resonant, unpolluted acoustic ecology of a pre-industrial age, a stark and soothing contrast to the mechanical, commercial drone of the modern city that usually bled through the library windows. “Your honoured grandfather treated me—I may say it without presumption—as a friend, having known me from childhood as his father’s librarian.”

It did not strike me at the time how old the man must be to have sustained such a lineage of service. “May I ask where you live now, Mr. Crow?” I said.

He smiled an amused smile, the wrinkles around his eyes suggesting the weathered face of a traditional agrarian elder. “You nearly hit my name,” he rejoined, “which shows the family insight. You have seen me before, but only once, and could not then have heard it!”

“Where was that?”

“In this very room. You were quite a child, however!”

I could not be sure that I remembered him, but the sensory imprint of him—a sudden scent of wild peat and crushed sage—felt familiar. I begged him to set me right as to his name.

“There is such a thing as remembering without recognising the memory in it,” he remarked, touching upon the very erosion of cultural memory that so often haunted my historical research. “For my name—which you have near enough—it used to be Raven.”

I had heard the name; marvellous, mythic tales passed down through the domestic generations had brought it to me. 

“It is very kind of you to come and see me,” I said, gesturing to a leather wingback chair. “Will you not sit down?”

He seated himself at once. “You knew my father, then, I presume?”

“I knew him,” he answered with a curious smile, “but he did not care about my acquaintance, and we never met. That gentleman, however,” he added, pointing to the portrait of Sir Upward, “was in his day a friend of mine yet more intimate than ever your grandfather became.”

At length, I began to think the interview a strange one. But in truth, it was hardly stranger that my visitor should remember Sir Upward, than that he should have been my great-grandfather’s librarian, tending to the linguistic and intellectual sustenance of our bloodline. 

“I owe him much,” he continued. “For, although I had read many more books than he, yet, through the special direction of his studies—which involved a deep, tactile communion with the earth and its seasons—he was able to inform me of a certain relation of modes which I should never have discovered of myself, and could hardly have learned from any one else.”

“Would you mind telling me all about that?” I asked, leaning forward, eager to digest this living history.

“By no means—as much at least as I am able: there are not such things as wilful secrets,” he answered, his tone suggesting only subjective experiences rendered untranslatable by the shifting power dynamics of modern lexicons. “That closet held his library—a hundred manuscripts or so, detailing raw ingredients and philosophies long before printing mass-commercialized such art. One morning I sat there, working at a catalogue of them, when he looked in at the door, and said, ‘Come.’ I laid down my pen and followed him—across the great hall, down a steep rough descent smelling of damp root cellars and fermenting earth, and along an underground passage to a tower he had lately built, consisting of a stair and a room at the top of it. The door of this room had a tremendous lock, which he undid with the smallest key I ever saw. I had scarcely crossed the threshold after him, when, to my eyes, he began to dwindle, and grew less and less. All at once my vision seemed to come right, and I saw that he was moving swiftly away from me. In a minute more he was the merest speck in the distance, with the tops of blue mountains beyond him, clear against a sky of paler blue. I recognised the country, for I had gone there and come again many a time, although I had never known this way to it.”

The pacing of his tale was circular, looping back into the deep time of my family. “Many years after, when the tower had long disappeared, I taught one of his descendants what Sir Upward had taught me; and now and then to this day I use your house when I want to go the nearest way home. I must indeed—without your leave, for which I ask your pardon—have by this time well established a right of way through it—not from front to back, but from bottom to top!”

“You would have me then understand, Mr. Raven,” I said, analyzing his words like a foreign recipe, “that you go through my house into another world, heedless of disparting space?”

“That I go through it is an incontrovertible acknowledgement of space,” returned the old librarian.

“Please do not quibble, Mr. Raven,” I rejoined. “Please to take my question as you know I mean it.”

“There is in your house a door, one step through which carries me into a world very much another than this.”

“A better?”

“Not throughout; but so much another that most of its physical, and many of its mental laws are different from those of this world. As for moral laws, they must everywhere be fundamentally the same.”

“You try my power of belief!” I said. “You take me for a madman, probably?”

“You do not look like one.”

“A liar then?”

“You give me no ground to think you such.”

“Only you do not believe me?”

“I will go out of that door with you if you like: I believe in you enough to risk the attempt.”

“The blunder all my children make!” he murmured, a sorrowful note vibrating in his chest. “The only door out is the door in!” 

I began to think he must be crazy. He sat silent for a moment, his head resting on his hand, his elbow on the table, and his eyes on the culinary archives before him. 

“A book,” he said louder, “is a door in, and therefore a door out. I see old Sir Upward,” he went on, closing his eyes, “and my heart swells with love to him:—what world is he in?”

“The world of your heart!” I replied; “—that is, the idea of him is there.”

“There is one world then at least on which your hall-door does not open?”

“I grant you so much; but the things in that world are not things to have and to hold. They cannot be tasted or touched.”

“Think a little farther,” he rejoined, probing the intersection of personal identity and native experience. “Did anything ever become yours, except by getting into that world? The thought is beyond you, however, at present! I tell you there are more worlds, and more doors to them, than you will think of in many years!”

He rose, left the library, crossed the hall, and went straight up to the garret, familiar evidently with every turn of the domestic geography. I followed, studying his back. His hair hung down long and dark, straight and glossy as wild kelp. His wide coat reached to his heels, and his shoes seemed too large for him, the footwear of a man who worked the heavy soil. 

In the garret, the olfactory landscape shifted to dry rot, desiccated herbs, and ancient timber. Light came through at the edges of the great roofing slabs, illuminating parts where there was no flooring. We had to step carefully from joist to joist. In the middle of one of these spaces rose a partition with a door; through it I followed Mr. Raven into a small, obscure chamber, whose top contracted as it rose and went slanting through the roof. 

“That is the door I spoke of,” he said, pointing to an oblong mirror that stood on the floor and leaned against the wall. 

I went in front of it and saw our figures dimly reflected in its dusty face. There was something about it that made me uneasy. It looked old-fashioned and neglected, but, notwithstanding its ordinary seeming, the eagle, perched with outstretched wings on the top, appeared threatful. 

“As a mirror,” said the librarian, “it has grown dingy with age; but that is no matter: its clearness depends on the light.”

“Light!” I rejoined, my voice deadened by the heavy acoustics of the cramped space; “there is no light here!”

He did not answer me, but began to pull at a little chain on the opposite wall. I heard a creaking—the raw, physical friction of iron on iron, sounding like the rhythmic turn of an ancient stone mill. The top of the chamber was turning slowly round. He ceased pulling, looked at his watch, and began to pull again. 

“We arrive almost to the moment!” he said; “it is on the very stroke of noon!”

The top went creaking and revolving for a minute or so. Then he pulled two other chains, now this, now that, and returned to the first. A moment more and the chamber grew much clearer. A patch of sunlight, thick and warm as clarified butter, had fallen upon a mirror on the wall opposite that against which the other leaned, and on the dust I saw the path of the reflected rays stretching toward the mirror on the ground. But from the latter, none were returned; the light seemed to go clean through the glass, swallowed completely. There was nowhere in the chamber a second patch of light!

“Where are the sunrays gone?” I cried, starved of visual logic.

“That I cannot tell,” returned Mr. Raven; “—back, perhaps, to where they came from first. They now belong, I fancy, to a sense not yet developed in us.” 

He then talked of the relations of mind to matter, and of senses to qualities, speaking of experiences that evade translation, crushed beneath the dominant lexicons of modern science. He went on to yet stranger things which I could not at all comprehend, speaking much about dimensions, telling me that there were many more than three, some of them concerned with powers which were indeed in us, but for which our modern tongues possessed no native dialect, and of which as yet we knew absolutely nothing. His words, however, I confess, took little more hold of me than the light did of the mirror, for I thought he hardly knew what he was saying. 

Suddenly, I was aware that our forms had gone from the glass, which now seemed full of a white mist that smelled sharply of cold ozone and crushed heather. As I gazed, I saw, growing gradually visible beyond the mist, the tops of a range of mountains, which became clearer and clearer. Soon the mist vanished entirely, uncovering the face of a wide, uncultivated heath, upon which, at some distance, was the figure of a man moving swiftly away. 

I turned to address my companion; he was no longer by my side. I looked again at the form in the mirror, and recognised the wide coat flying, the black hair lifting in a wind that did not touch me. The absolute acoustic void emanating from that glass—a silence so vast and untranslatable it threatened to swallow the very beating of my heart—broke my nerve entirely. I rushed in terror from the place.
