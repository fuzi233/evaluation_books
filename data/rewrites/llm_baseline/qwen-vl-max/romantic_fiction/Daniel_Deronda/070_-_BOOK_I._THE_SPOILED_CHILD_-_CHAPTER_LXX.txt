CHAPTER LXX. In the rich tapestry of human life, time flows in a way that blurs the boundaries between seasons—blossom and harvest coexist, sorrow and joy intertwine, and endings often carry the seeds of new beginnings. It is as if each soul lives through multiple eras at once: sowing and reaping, tending and celebrating, all within the span of a single breath. And so it is with love—among its deepest gifts is the quiet joy of shielding another’s heart from pain, transforming hardship into light, and turning memories of loss into wellsprings of sweetness. Daniel Deronda’s love for Mirah was shaped by just such tenderness. From her earliest steps, she had walked through thorns; when he first saw her, her face seemed to him a fragile portrait of sorrow. But now, bathed in warmth and peace, she shone like a rare flower—dark-edged yet soft, glowing under the sun of contentment. To her, even grief was no longer feared, for it belonged to a life she called only good—the life she shared with Deronda. He watched her, enchanted by the calm happiness that softened her every gesture, by the stillness of her repose, and found in her presence more fulfillment than any personal joy could offer. To spare her pain was enough.

Mirah knew nothing of Hans’s silent struggle or Gwendolen’s hidden ache. After learning that Deronda’s secret devotion had been meant for her, she easily understood Gwendolen’s concern as a natural gratitude toward one who had done so much good—a feeling Mirah herself had known well. Everything Deronda said about Mrs. Grandcourt confirmed this gentle interpretation, though he never spoke of their bond directly. Mirah believed, without question, that Deronda had been a guardian angel not only to her but to many others. The wonder was simply that she, among them all, should be granted the privilege of walking beside him always.

So when the bridal veil fell upon Mirah, it concealed no hesitation—only a deep reverence for the gift she received, a gift that demanded great things in return. Beneath the velvet canopy stood two souls whose union seemed blessed beyond words: a bride radiant with grace, a groom full of integrity. Their lips touched the wine of marriage with truth, their vows carried a weight of promise that few unions could match. They were married according to Jewish tradition, a ceremony simple yet profound. And since no faith insists that feasts must exclude those we hold dear, it is no surprise that among the guests were the entire Cohen family—save the baby, who wisely stayed home to practice her teething. How could Mordecai have borne to keep his friends of hardship from sharing in this joy? Mrs. Meyrick, understanding this deeply, welcomed the occasion with open arms—even the Jewish pawnbroker, whom she once distrusted. She came with her three daughters, all of them moved by the thought that Mirah’s marriage crowned a story they would treasure forever. Each had given freely—heart, care, kindness—to the girl who needed them most.

If Hans had been there, it might have been perfect. But Mab observed wryly that men often suffered for being inconvenient. Suppose, she mused, that Kate, Amy, and herself had all fallen in love with Mr. Deronda? But then, women were not so ridiculous. The Meyricks’ openness was rewarded when Mr. Cohen rose to speak—a speech unlike any other, honest and heartfelt, free of hollow flourishes. Jacob, barely old enough to understand, laughed softly during it, not disrespectfully, but with pride in his family’s moment. Adelaide Rebekah, in her new Sabbath dress, sat with solemn dignity throughout. Mordecai, eyes bright despite their depth, looked on with a serene affection, as if already standing beyond the world’s petty demands, yet still capable of deep feeling. Again and again, his gaze returned to Deronda, filled with trust and quiet love.

The feast was modest, but Mirah did not lack for treasures. As soon as the betrothal was known, friends had begun to send tokens of their joy. Sir Hugo and Lady Mallinger offered a complete set for Eastern travel, along with a locket inscribed: “To the bride of our dear Daniel Deronda, all blessings. H. and L. M.” The Klesmers sent a fine watch, engraved with a gentle message. But the most precious gift arrived on the morning of the wedding—from Diplow. A letter, written in trembling hands:

*Do not think of me sorrowfully on your wedding-day. I have remembered your words—that I may live to be one of the best of women, who make others glad that they were born. I do not yet see how that can be, but you know better than I. If it ever comes true, it will be because you helped me. I only thought of myself, and I made you grieve. It hurts me now to think of your grief. You must not grieve any more for me. It is better—it shall be better with me because I have known you.*

Preparations for their journey east began immediately. Deronda could not refuse Ezra’s wish to accompany them, though he knew the old man’s strength was fading. There were signs—subtle, unmistakable—that the final stage of his illness had come. Yet Ezra had said, “Never mind where I die, so long as I am with you.” He did not go. One morning, early, he told Deronda, “Do not leave me today. I shall die before it ends.” He dressed as usual, seated himself in his easy chair, Deronda and Mirah at his sides. For hours, he remained quiet, hardly speaking, but watching them with eyes that held a quiet certainty—as if to say, *I am at peace, though my breath grows thin.* Not until late afternoon, as the light dimmed, did he take both their hands in his. Looking at Deronda, he whispered: “Death is coming to me as the divine kiss—both parting and reunion. It takes me from your sight, but gives me full presence in your soul. Where thou goest, Daniel, I shall go. Is it not begun? Have I not breathed my soul into you? We shall live together.”

He paused. Deronda waited, hoping for more. But slowly, with effort, Ezra raised himself and spoke in Hebrew—the ancient confession of God’s unity, spoken by dying Jews for generations. Then he sank back gently, silent. Hours passed before his breath ceased, with Mirah and Deronda holding him close.

Nothing here for tears, nothing to mourn—no bitterness, no shame, no weakness. Only peace, beauty, and the quiet certainty of a death noble beyond words.
