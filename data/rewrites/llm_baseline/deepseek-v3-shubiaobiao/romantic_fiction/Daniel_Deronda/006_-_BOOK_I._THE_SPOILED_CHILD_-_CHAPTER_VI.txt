*"Do you think you have humiliated me by teaching me that the earth revolves around the sun? I swear I think no less of myself for it."*
—Fontenelle, *Plurality of Worlds*

Gwendolen had never before felt so keenly the sting of another’s superiority. She would never admit, even to herself, how bitterly she regretted not possessing Miss Arrowpoint’s musical education—something that might have armed her with the confidence to challenge Herr Klesmer’s judgment. Worse still was the quiet jealousy that prickled beneath her skin whenever she encountered Miss Arrowpoint, a jealousy not born of the other girl’s fortune, but of something far more irritating: the fact that this plain, unremarkable creature—slight of figure, with small features, tolerable eyes, and a sallow complexion—carried herself with an air of quiet intellectual dominance. Her musical precision, her exacting taste, her infuriating discernment—all of it made admiration unavoidable, even as it rankled.

Gwendolen, of course, preferred not to dwell on comparisons that cast her in an unflattering light. Since Klesmer was not always present to remind her of her shortcomings, she soon found ways to soothe her wounded pride. The admiration she received at Brackenshaw Castle and elsewhere was far more agreeable than criticism, and she was not one of those rare souls who thirsted for a perfection no one else demanded of her. In truth, she was not so very different from other girls, save for her uncommon grace and a certain daring that lent charm to an otherwise ordinary ambition.

She longed to be exceptional, but her dreams were bound by the genteel romances she devoured—stories where heroines poured out grand passions in their journals while their lives remained neatly confined to the sphere of fashion. She imagined herself as one of those tragic, satin-shod creatures, wandering into swamps of passion, yet never straying too far from propriety.

At Offendene, however, the days offered little in the way of adventure. Gwendolen was determined not to live like other young ladies, but she had no clear idea of how to distinguish herself. The neighborhood was dull, and even her earliest social triumphs had been fleeting. Between invitations, she amused herself with girlish pastimes—neglecting poor Alice’s lessons (reasoning that ignorance suited her cousin better) and instead enlisting her, along with Miss Merry and the housemaid, in the grand project of assembling costumes for future theatricals.

She had never acted, save for a few *tableaux vivants* at school, but she was convinced of her talent. Having seen Rachel at the Théâtre Français, she sometimes wondered if she might not surpass the famed actress—after all, was she not far lovelier than that thin Jewess? Until such grand ambitions could be realized, she contented herself with rehearsing Greek and Oriental heroics before an obliging audience of relatives and servants, though the housekeeper’s unfortunate remark—that Miss Harleth looked more regal in her own gown than in the "baggy thing with her arms all bare"—earned her a permanent dismissal from the proceedings.

"Do I look as well as Rachel, Mamma?" Gwendolen demanded one day, after striking a particularly tragic pose.

"Your arms are better," Mrs. Davilow allowed. "But your voice lacks her depth."

"I can make it deeper if I choose," Gwendolen declared. Then, with conviction: "A higher voice is more tragic—more feminine. And the more feminine a woman is, the more tragic her desperation becomes."

Mrs. Davilow sighed. "I don’t see the virtue in making one’s blood creep. If there’s villainy to be done, let the men manage it."

"Oh, Mamma, you are hopelessly prosaic! All the great poetic criminals are women. Men are timid, cautious creatures."

"And you, who tremble at shadows—do you fancy yourself a criminal, my dear?"

"I speak of art, not reality," Gwendolen snapped.

Later, when her mother had left the room, she turned to Anna with sudden purpose. "Anna, you must persuade Uncle to let us stage charades at the rectory. Mr. Middleton and Warham could join us—for practice, of course. Mamma says it wouldn’t do to have him rehearsing here. He’s a stick, but we could give him a suitable part."

"Oh, not until Rex comes!" Anna protested. "He’s so clever—he can play Napoleon perfectly. Rex can do anything."

Gwendolen laughed. "I don’t believe in your Rex. He’ll turn out to be as dull as those dreadful watercolors you hang in your room."

Anna’s eyes filled with tears. "You’ll see. He’s won a scholarship, and Papa says he’ll take a fellowship. He’s cleverer than Mr. Middleton—everyone says so."

"Clever in a dim, lantern-lit sort of way, perhaps. But he’s still a stick. If he had to declare, *‘Perdition catch my soul, but I do love her,’* he’d say it in the same tone as *‘Here endeth the second lesson.’*"

Anna gasped. "Gwendolen! It’s unkind to mock him—he admires you terribly. Warham told Mamma that Middleton is *spooney* over you."

Gwendolen arched a brow. "How unfortunate for him. *Perdition catch my soul* if I return the sentiment."

"You mustn’t ridicule Rex," Anna pleaded, eyes glistening.

Gwendolen relented, touching Anna’s chin lightly. "Very well, little coz. I’ll spare your Rex—especially if he helps make our charades a success."

And when Rex arrived, he proved himself more than willing. Good-natured, handsome, and utterly smitten, he threw himself into Gwendolen’s plans with boundless enthusiasm. Under his influence, the rectory and Offendene came alive with rehearsals, laughter, and the thrill of performance. Even the solemn Mr. Middleton was persuaded to take part, though Gwendolen’s mercilessly polite distance left him in a state of perpetual, hopeful agitation.

The grand performance was set, and Gwendolen’s anticipation burned brightest at the thought of Herr Klesmer’s presence among the guests. She had ensured his invitation, and she meant to dazzle him.

The evening unfolded smoothly—until the moment Hermione was to descend from her pedestal. The thunderous chord from Klesmer’s piano was meant to signal her grand entrance—but instead, a hidden panel flew open, revealing a painted scene of a dead face and a fleeing figure, starkly illuminated.

The audience gasped. But it was Gwendolen’s reaction that struck them dumb.

A scream tore from her lips. She stood frozen, her face a mask of pure terror—lips parted, eyes wide and unseeing. For a heartbeat, she was no longer Gwendolen Harleth, the poised and fearless beauty, but a creature consumed by dread.

Then, at her mother’s touch, she collapsed to her knees, trembling violently before allowing herself to be led away.

The guests murmured, exchanging theories—a draft, a trick of the candlelight, perhaps even spirits. Mr. Gascoigne, ever practical, locked the offending panel with a decisive turn of the key.

When Gwendolen returned, her composure was flawless, as though the incident had never occurred. Only Klesmer, with his artist’s eye, saw through the act.

*"A magnificent bit of plastik,"* he murmured to Miss Arrowpoint. *"She could not have chosen a finer climax."*

And Gwendolen, catching the praise, allowed herself a small, satisfied flush. If he believed it was all part of the performance, so much the better.

But the others knew better. And so, by unspoken agreement, the matter was dropped.
