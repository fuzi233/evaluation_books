Between what truly matters and what merely seems to matter, how can our world hope to judge wisely? When the scheming, indomitable mind of Sigsbee Manderson was shattered by a bullet from an unseen hand, the world lost nothing worthy of a single tear. Instead, it gained a memorable lesson: a harsh reminder of the vanity of the immense wealth this dead man had accumulated, a fortune built without earning a single loyal friend to mourn him or perform a single act to honor his memory. Yet, when the news of his death spread, it seemed to those living in the great whirlpools of commerce that the very earth shuddered under the blow.

In the lurid commercial history of his nation, no figure had ever so dominated the collective consciousness of the trading world. He occupied a niche entirely his own within its temples. While financial giants—strong leaders who directed capital and demanded millions for their labor—had existed before, Manderson possessed a unique distinction. About his head hung a pale halo of piratical romance, a sentiment particularly dear to the hearts of his compatriots, despite his role as the unquestioned guardian of stability, the extinguisher of manufactured crises, and the enemy of the raiding chiefs who plagued the borders of Wall Street.

His fortune, inherited from a grandfather who had once been a minor buccaneer of finance, had grown through his father, a man of quiet diligence who lent money but never speculated. Manderson, always possessing vast sums at his disposal, should have belonged to the newer American plutocracy, steadied by the traditions of great wealth. It was not so. His upbringing and education instilled in him European notions of a rich man's proper exterior, rooting in him an instinct for "quiet magnificence"—costliness that does not scream for attention. Nevertheless, much of the forty-niner spirit and the financial piracy of his forbears remained.

During his early career, dubbed his "bad manner" period, Manderson was little more than a genius gambler, a child prodigy whose hand was raised against every man's. He approached the complex dogfight of the New York Stock Exchange with a brain superior to any opposition. At St. Helena, it was declared that war is *une belle occupation*; thus, the young Manderson found his calling in the chaotic arena of speculation. Then came his transformation. Upon his father's death, when Manderson was thirty, a new revelation of the power and glory of the god he served seemingly struck him. With the sudden, elastic adaptability characteristic of his nation, he turned to steady labor in his father's banking business, shutting his ears to the roar of street battles.

Within a few years, he controlled the entire activity of the great firm whose unimpeachable conservatism and financial weight lifted it like a cliff above the angry sea of the markets. All mistrust regarding his youthful performances vanished. He was a different man. No one could authoritatively explain how the change occurred, though stories circulated of final words spoken by his father—the only man Manderson had truly respected or perhaps loved. Suddenly, Manderson towered above the financial situation. His name became currency in global exchanges; to speak Manderson was to invoke a vision of broad-based, firm American wealth. He orchestrated massive capital combinations, centralized continental industries, and financed grand designs of state and private enterprise with unerring judgment.

Many times, when he intervened to crush strikes or consolidate labor ownership, he sent ruin upon countless humble homes. If miners, steelworkers, or cattlemen defied him and incited disorder, he proved more lawless and ruthless than they. Yet, this was done in the pursuit of legitimate business ends. Tens of thousands of the poor might curse his name, but the financier and the speculator execrated him no longer. Stretching a hand to protect or manipulate the power of wealth across the country, forcible, cold, and unerring, he ministered to the national hunger for magnitude. A grateful country surnamed him the Colossus.

However, there was an aspect of Manderson in these later years that remained unknown and unsuspected except to a select few: his secretaries, lieutenants, and former associates from his hurling days. This small circle knew that Manderson, the pillar of sound business and market stability, harbored moments of nostalgia for the lively times when the Street trembled at his name. As one confidant described it, it was as if Blackbeard had settled down as a decent merchant in Bristol on the spoils of the main. Now and then, the pirate would glare suddenly, the knife in his teeth and sulfur matches sputtering in his hatband. During these spasms of regression, scores of tempestuous market raids were plotted in the inner rooms of Manderson, Colefax and Company. But they were never executed. Blackbeard would quell the mutiny of his old self and descend soberly to his counting house—perhaps humming a verse or two of "Spanish Ladies" under his breath.

Manderson allowed himself the harmless satisfaction, after the time for action had passed, of pointing out to some market tycoon that a coup worth a million to a depredator might have been made. "Seems to me," he would say with almost wistful regret, "the Street is getting to be a mighty dull place since I quit."

By slow degrees, this amiable weakness of the Colossus became known to the business world, which exulted greatly in the knowledge. At the news of his death, panic swept through the markets like a hurricane, for it arrived at a fatal juncture. Prices tottered and crashed like towers in an earthquake. For two days, Wall Street was a clamorous inferno of pale despair. Across the United States, wherever speculation had its devotees, drifted a waft of ruin and a plague of suicide. Even in Europe, not a few ended their lives, having tied their destinies to a financier most of them had never seen. In Paris, a well-known banker walked quietly from the Bourse and fell dead among a raving crowd, a phial crushed in his hand. In Frankfurt, one leaped from a cathedral tower, leaving a redder stain on the stone below. Men stabbed, shot, and strangled themselves, drank poison, or suffocated in their grief, driven mad by the loss of a cold heart in a lonely corner of England vowed to the service of greed.

The blow could not have fallen at a more disastrous moment. Philip Trent, a painter and the son of a painter, had secured a notable reputation within the English art world before reaching his twenties. His works sold readily, driven by an original, forceful talent and a working style that blended leisurely consistency with bursts of intense creative fervor. While his father's name and a substantial inheritance spared him the stigma of the struggling artist, Trent's greatest asset was an unconscious charm that endeared him to everyone. Possessed of good spirits and a lively, humorous wit, he combined these traits with a genuine, penetrating interest in other people. Though his judgment of character was sharp, it remained private; outwardly, he projected an image of perpetual enjoyment. Even when deeply engrossed in labor or indulging in whimsical nonsense, his face retained a spark of contained vitality. At thirty-two, he had not yet succumbed to the solemnities of age.

His meteoric rise to fame, vastly disproportionate to his artistic output, stemmed from a fleeting impulse. One day, reading a newspaper, he encountered a report of a murder committed aboard a railway train—a crime of a rarity unknown in England. The circumstances were baffling, involving two suspects. Intrigued by a novelty, Trent, who rarely engaged with such matters, began to pore over reports in various journals. His imagination, dormant in daily life, ignited; facts began to weave themselves into patterns familiar only from his artistic inspirations or personal adventures. By day's end, he had penned and mailed a lengthy letter to the editor of *The Record*, selected specifically for its comprehensive and intelligent coverage of the incident.

In this correspondence, Trent mirrored Edgar Allan Poe's approach to the Mary Rogers case. Relying solely on newspaper accounts, he highlighted the significance of seemingly trivial details and organized the evidence to cast severe doubt upon a key witness. Sir James Molloy, the editor, printed Trent's letter in bold typeface. Later that evening, *The Sun* announced the arrest and full confession of the accused man.

Sir James, a man acquainted with every stratum of London society, wasted no time in establishing contact with Trent. The two hit it off immediately; Trent possessed a native tact that effectively erased generational gaps. The great rotary presses in the basement of the *Record* building inspired a new enthusiasm in him. He had even painted there—a "machinery-scape" in the manner of Heinrich Kley—which Sir James purchased instantly.

Months later, the Ilkley Mystery arrived. Following an inviting dinner hosted by Sir James, the editor offered Trent a fantastically large sum to serve temporarily as his special correspondent in Ilkley.

"You can handle this," the editor urged. "You write well, you know how to converse with people, and I can teach you the technicalities of reporting in half an hour. You possess a head for mysteries, combining imagination with cool judgment. Imagine the satisfaction of pulling this off!"

Trent admitted it sounded like a grand adventure. After smoking and frowning, he convinced himself that only the fear of the unfamiliar held him back. Reacting against fear had become a fixed moral habit; thus, he accepted the offer.

He succeeded brilliantly. For the second time, he outpaced the authorities, earning a name spoken on every tongue. He returned to painting, feeling no pull toward journalism. True to his word, Sir James, knowing Trent's value in the art world, refrained from offering a lucrative salary—a courtesy extended by no other editor. Nevertheless, over the following years, Trent received approximately thirty requests for assistance in unraveling domestic and international puzzles. Sometimes, occupied by his own work, he declined; other times, he was beaten to the truth. Regardless, his sporadic collaboration with *The Record* cemented his status as one of England's most renowned figures. Characteristically, however, the public knew almost nothing of him personally. Trent had imposed strict silence regarding his identity upon the Molloy papers, and his colleagues wisely avoided advertising their own staff.

I commence this, my third and likely final dispatch to the *Record* regarding the Manderson murder, with a mixture of emotions. I feel a profound sense of relief; in my previous reports, I was compelled, for the sake of justice, to withhold crucial facts. To reveal them earlier would have alerted a suspect of exceptional boldness and resourcefulness, potentially allowing him to escape. Now, I set those facts forth. Yet, I confess, the tale of treachery and twisted intellect leaves a bitter taste. It reveals a revolting depth of motive beneath the surface of the crime, which I believe I have finally unraveled.

As recounted in my first dispatch, I arrived at Marlstone on Tuesday morning to find the body discovered near the house, surrounded by an enigma. I detailed the scene, the local theories, Manderson's domestic life, and his movements the preceding evening. I also noted a peculiar discrepancy: a quantity of whisky, far exceeding Manderson's nightly habit, had vanished from his private decanter. By the time of the inquest the following day, I had wired a brief summary of the coroner's proceedings. As I write, that day is not yet concluded, but my investigation has culminated in identifying the man who must now clear himself of the charge of killing Manderson.

Beyond the central mystery of Manderson's premature departure, two anomalies stood out immediately, seemingly obvious to any observer:

First, although the body was found mere yards from the house, no one in the residence claimed to hear a cry, scream, or gunshot during the night. Manderson was not gagged; the bruising on his wrists indicated a violent struggle; and there had undoubtedly been at least one pistol shot (criminals often miss their mark initially). This silence was particularly striking given that Martin, the butler, was a poor sleeper with keen hearing, and his bedroom window opened directly facing the shed where the body lay.

Second, Manderson had left his dental plate beside his bed. He had risen, dressed completely down to his necktie and watch chain, and stepped outdoors without inserting the plate he had worn daily for years. This was not a case of frantic haste; even in panic, one rarely forgets such a fundamental item essential for speech and appearance. Removing and reinserting a denture is second nature to anyone who relies on it.

Initially, these quirks offered no solution, merely fueling a suspicion that something lurked in the shadows, deepening the mystery of "how, why, and through whom" Manderson died.

However, a pivotal discovery in the first few hours of my investigation illuminated the path obscured by ingenuity.

I had previously described Manderson's sparsely furnished bedroom, contrasting sharply with the clutter of clothes and shoes, and its direct access to Mrs. Manderson's room. On the upper shelf, where Manderson's shoes were kept, I located the specific pair he wore the night before his death. While glancing at the row of impeccably crafted footwear, my attention snagged on a subtle flaw in this particular pair. They were lightweight lace-ups with thin soles and no toe-caps. Though old and polished, they showed signs of distress: a tiny split in the leather at the vamp, where the laces emerge, and a slight dragging of the stitches uniting the upper to the sole at the toe and outer edge.

These markings indicated only one thing: **these shoes had been worn by someone for whom they were too small.**

It was evident that Manderson, meticulous about his narrow feet, always wore perfectly fitting shoes. None of the other footwear in the collection bore such stress marks. Furthermore, the tears were fresh. The possibility that someone wore them after Manderson's death was negligible; the body had been found only twenty-six hours prior, and no one would wear Manderson's shoes post-mortem. Nor would Manderson have allowed another to borrow and ruin his favorite shoes while alive. The only other men in the house were the butler and two secretaries, yet I allowed my thoughts to wander freely, letting the clues guide me.

Psychologically, once the key fact—"Someone other than Manderson wore these shoes"—took hold, my mind rapidly reorganized all available data around this new premise. Suddenly, disparate details coalesced into a coherent narrative:
*   It was highly unusual for Manderson to consume large amounts of whisky.
*   His attire upon discovery was disheveled: cuffs pulled inside sleeves, shoes unevenly laced.
*   He appeared unwashed, wearing yesterday's evening shirt and collar.
*   His watch was missing from its dedicated leather-lined pocket.
*   Strangely, he had been communicative with his wife at bedtime, a rare occurrence.
*   Most notably, he had forgotten his false teeth.

These observations, which had previously seemed insignificant, now formed a cluster of evidence supporting a single, startling hypothesis: **"It was not Manderson who was in the house that night."**

At first, this seemed absurd. People had seen Manderson dine and depart with Marlowe. But if he did not return at ten? The logic held water. A faint light began to dawn over the entire case. I systematically reviewed the inconsistencies to understand why a masquerader would commit these specific errors.

Why force oneself into Manderson's narrow shoes? Because the intruder sought to create the illusion that Manderson was present. He intended to leave the shoes exactly where Manderson always left them, ensuring the maid would polish and replace them naturally. Similarly, the abandoned dental plate served the same purpose: to prove Manderson had retired to bed. This implied Manderson was already dead before the impostor arrived.

This theory explained the clothing as well. The trousers, waistcoat, and shooting jacket found in the bedroom—and seen by Martin on the telephone caller—were essential props. The impersonator needed Martin to recognize the uniform instantly. Crucially, neither Martin nor Mrs. Manderson ever saw the man's face. Martin viewed only his back as he crouched at the phone; Mrs. Manderson, half-asleep, heard a voice but saw nothing but a shadow near the door. The impostor wore Manderson's broad-brimmed hat, rendering facial features unnecessary. With the correct build, height, and powerful shoulders, combined with skilled mimicry, the deception was foolproof.

The method of entry further supported this. Entering via the front door would have risked detection by the alert Martin. Instead, the intruder used the garden window.

The disappearance of the whisky also finds an explanation. A man fresh from a bloody crime, needing courage before executing his elaborate ruse, would naturally seek comfort in alcohol. He drank before summoning Martin, perhaps more after succeeding in his initial trick, but stopped precisely when necessary to maintain his composure.

The climax of the operation involved the intruder entering Manderson's room, placing the stolen body there, stripping it of outerwear, and staging the scene. He concealed the murder weapon, rang for Martin, and posed at the telephone with his back to the door, mimicking Manderson's habitual posture. He witnessed burnings, lynchings, volleys of gunfire, and hangings; every day his soul recoiled anew at the absurdities spawned by misrule. On many nights, he slept amidst danger; on many days, he went hungry. Yet, there was not a single evening or morning when the visage of the woman he loved with hopeless devotion did not appear before him. He harbored a bitter pride in the enduring power of this obsession. It fascinated him as a phenomenon, astonishing and enlightening him. Nothing of the sort had ever visited him before. It validated much of what he had previously regarded with skepticism in the recorded experiences of mankind. He was not ignorant of the emotional world at thirty-two; however, the knowledge he possessed had been acquired without pursuit or purchase, devoid of intolerable memories. Broken to the harsh realities of sex, he remained troubled by its enigmatic history. He navigated life with a strange reverence for certain feminine weaknesses and a profound, simple terror of certain feminine strengths. He had maintained a lukewarm faith that something latent within him awaited awakening, summoned by a voice that would arrive in its own time, if ever, and certainly not through active seeking. He had not anticipated that, if this prophecy held true, the revelation might take a sinister form.

Two aspects of his feelings toward Mabel Manderson had utterly surprised him: the insane suddenness with which they erupted in full force, and their extravagant hopelessness. Prior to their arrival, he had been inclined to mock the permanence of unrequited passion as a generous, boyish delusion. Now, he realized he had been mistaken, living acutely in the knowledge of his error. In his imagination, the woman always appeared exactly as he first saw her, performing that great gesture of passionate joy as he passed unseen on the cliff's edge. That movement, signaling her liberation from torment, had confirmed with terrifying clarity his prior suspicion that her widowhood was merely a passport to happiness with a man she loved. He could not pinpoint the exact moment he first suspected this possibility. He believed the seed of the thought had been sown during his initial meeting with Marlowe; his mind would have automatically registered that such evident strength and grace, coupled with the tall young man's demeanor, could captivate any woman of unfixed affection. The connection between this observation and what Mr. Cupples had revealed about the Mandersons' marital life must have formed unconsciously in the depths of his mind. By the time he began searching for the motive of the crime, having verified the identity of the murderer, the notion presented itself as an established fact.

*Motive, motive!* How desperately he had sought an alternative explanation, turning his back on the grim reality that Marlowe—driven by a passion akin to his own, and perhaps privy to maddening truths about the wife's misery—had copied the foulest page from the book of Duns Scotus Bothwell. Yet, throughout his investigations and subsequent brooding, he found nothing that could compel Marlowe to such a deed—nothing but that temptation, whose full magnitude he could not comprehend but which, if it existed, must have pressed urgently upon a bold spirit where scruples had been paralyzed. If he could trust his senses, the young man was neither insane nor inherently evil. However, that could not exonerate him. Murder committed for a woman's sake was not a rarity, Heaven knew! While the modern feebleness of impulse in comfortable classes and their reliance on the machinery of detection had rendered it uncommon among them, it was far from impossible. All that was required was a man of equal daring and intelligence, his soul intoxicated by the fumes of a seductive intrigue, capable of planning and executing such a crime. A thousand times, with a heart heavy with anguish, he had tried to reason away the dread that Mabel Manderson knew too much about the intentions against her husband's life. That she knew the entire truth after the act was consummated, he could not doubt; her unforgettable collapse when the question of Marlowe was suddenly and bluntly posed had swept away his last hope that there was no love between them, and seemed to indicate a fear of exposure. In any event, she knew the truth after reading his manuscript. It was certain that no public suspicion had fallen upon Marlowe since. She had destroyed his notes and accepted his promise to keep the secret threatening her lover's life.

But it was the monstrous thought—that she might have known murder was brewing and had guiltily remained silent—that haunted Trent's mind. Could she have suspected, guessed something? Was it conceivable that she was aware of the entire plot, that she had conspired? He could never forget that his first suspicion of Marlowe's motive arose from the fact that the killer's escape route led through the lady's room. At that time, before seeing her, he had been willing to entertain the idea of her equal guilt and cooperation. He had imagined a passionate *hystérique*, merciless in both hate and love, a zealous accomplice, perhaps even the driving spirit of the crime. Then he had seen her, spoken with her, aided her in her weakness; and since their first meeting, such suspicions had seemed the vilest of infamies. He had seen her eyes and her mouth; he had breathed the woman's atmosphere. Trent was one of those who believes they can smell true wickedness in the air. In her presence, he felt an inward certainty of her ultimate goodness of heart. It meant nothing against this that she had surrendered momentarily, that day on the cliff, to the sentiment of relief at the end of her bondage, her years of starved sympathy and unfulfilled motherhood. He believed she had turned to Marlowe in her destitution; he did not believe she possessed knowledge of his deadly purpose. And yet, morning and evening, the sickening doubts returned. He recalled that it was almost in her presence that Marlowe had made his preparations in the murdered man's bedroom, and that it was through the window of her own chamber that he had fled the house. Had he forgotten his cunning and taken the risk of revealing himself then? Or, as Trent deemed more likely, had he continued playing his part with her while she slept, slipping away unnoticed? He did not think she had known of the masquerade when she testified at the inquest; her statement read like honest evidence. Or—though he scorned the question, it would never be silenced—had she lain awake, expecting the footsteps in the room and the whisper confirming the deed? Among the foul possibilities of human nature, was it possible that black ruthlessness and black deceit hid behind that good, straight, and gentle appearance? These thoughts rarely left him when alone.

Trent served Sir James faithfully, earning his pay for six months, before returning to Paris, where he resumed work with a lighter heart. His faculties had returned, and he began to live happier than he had expected among a motley tribe of friends: French, English, and Americans; artists, poets, journalists, police officers, hoteliers, soldiers, lawyers, businessmen, and others. His old faculty of sympathetic interest in his fellow humans won him, as in his student days, privileges rarely afforded to the British. He relished the rare experience of being welcomed into the bosom of a French family. He was granted access to the momentous confidences of *les jeunes*, finding them as convinced they had uncovered the secrets of art and life as the departed generation of ten years prior had been. The warmth of the French family home remained unchanged, identical in wallpaper and furnishings to those of the past. But *les jeunes*, he observed with regret, were fundamentally different from their predecessors. They were far shallower and more childish, possessing significantly less genuine intellect. The secrets they wrestled from the universe were not of the importance or interest of those extracted by the old guard. He believed and deplored this until one day he found himself dining in a restaurant beside a too-well-fed man whom, despite the ravages of comfortable living, he recognized as one of the *jeunes* of his own era. The two months that followed were a period in Trent's life that he has never since remembered without shuddering. He encountered Mrs. Manderson roughly six times, and each encounter left him baffled and maddened. Her demeanor was a cool, calculated friendliness—a precise mean between mere acquaintance and the first tentative stage of intimacy—that defied his attempts to categorize it.

At the opera, to his further astonishment, he found her seated with a certain Mrs. Wallace, a frisky matron he had known since childhood. It appeared that upon her return from Italy, Mrs. Manderson had somehow drifted into social circles to which Trent belonged solely by nurture and disposition. "It came of pitching my tent in your hunting grounds," she explained; several of his friends were neighbors. Trent retained a dim but horrid recollection of that specific occasion. He had been unlike himself, ill-at-ease and burning with shame, engaging in idiotic loquacity about his adventures in the Baltic provinces, only to find himself addressing exclusively Mrs. Wallace. By the time the other lady joined them, she had completely shed the slight agitation with which she had stopped him in the vestibule earlier. She spoke pleasantly of her travels, her new settlement in London, and mutual acquaintances, treating him with the casual grace afforded to anyone who had done her no injury.

During the latter half of the opera, sitting behind them in the box, Trent became conscious of nothing but the physical details of the women ahead: the angle of Mrs. Manderson's cheek, the mass of her hair, the line of her shoulder and arm, her hand resting on the cushion. That black hair had finally seemed to him a forest—immeasurable, pathless, and enchanted, luring him toward a fatal adventure. Pale and subdued by the end, he parted from them with a formality that masked his turmoil.

On the next occasion, at a country house where both were guests, and on subsequent meetings, Trent had forced himself under control. He matched her manner and acquitted himself, he thought, decently enough. Yet, beneath this surface propriety, he lived in an agony of bewilderment, remorse, and longing. He could make absolutely nothing of her attitude. It was beyond doubt that she had read his manuscript and understood the suspicion lurking in his final question at White Gables. So, how could she treat him thus? How could she interact with him frankly, as she did with every other man?

His intuition, however, insisted that an injury had been done and that she felt it acutely. Despite the total absence of differentiation in her outward behavior, he sensed warnings whenever they spoke apart briefly; each time, driven by fear, he ingeniously steered the conversation elsewhere. He formed two resolutions. First, that upon completing a commissioned work tying him to London, he would leave town and stay away forever. The strain was too great. He no longer burned to know the truth; he desired nothing to confirm his fixed internal conviction that he had blundered, that he had misread the situation, misinterpreted her tears, and written himself down as a slanderous fool. He speculated no more on Marlowe's motive for killing Manderson. Mr. Cupples returned to London, and Trent asked him nothing. He knew now that he had been right in those emphatic words: *"So long as she considered herself bound to him... no power on earth could have persuaded her."* He met Mrs. Manderson at dinner at her uncle's large, tomb-like house in Bloomsbury, spending most of the evening conversing with a professor of archaeology from Berlin.

His second resolution was simpler: he would not be with her alone.

Yet, a few days later, when she wrote inviting him to visit the following afternoon, he made no attempt to excuse himself. It was a formal challenge.

While she presided over the rites of tea, and for some time afterward, she joined his slightly fevered conversation on current events with such natural ease that he began to hope she had altered her resolve to corner him and speak gravely. She appeared entirely careless now, smiling in a way that reminded him, not for the first time since that night at the opera, of an old description of a Princess of Brunswick: *"Her mouth has ten thousand charms that touch the soul."* She led him on a tour of the beautiful room where she had received him, singling out treasures from the spoils of a hundred bric-à-brac shops, laughing over her quests and bargains. When he asked if she would delight him again with a favorite piece she had played at another house, she consented immediately.

She played with a perfection of execution and feeling that moved him now as it had moved him before. "You are a musician born," he said quietly when she finished, the last tremor of the music fading into silence. "I knew that before I first heard you."

"I have played a great deal ever since I can remember," she replied simply, turning to him with a smile. "It has been a great comfort to me. When did you first detect music in me?"

"Oh, of course," she continued lightly. "I was at the opera. But that wouldn't prove much, would it?"

"No," Trent said abstractedly, his mind still lingering on the music. "I think I knew it the first time I saw you."

Understanding dawned on him, turning him rigid. For the first time, the past had been explicitly invoked.

A short silence fell. Mrs. Manderson looked at Trent, then hastily looked away. Color began to rise in her cheeks, and she pursed her lips as if preparing to whistle. A short laugh broke from Trent despite himself, but he said nothing.

Like all anomalies, they tend to be either comically ridiculous or dreadfully solemn. Consider the phrase 'intelligent anticipation.' Were such a construct uttered in any other European country, it would pass unnoticed. Marlowe pondered for a moment. "None that I ever heard of," he replied. "Worship and prayer were utterly foreign to him, as far as I could discern, and I never heard him mention religion. I doubt if he possessed any genuine sense of God, or if he was capable of perceiving Him through emotion. He was almost ascetic in his habits, aside from his smoking. I lived with him for four years without ever witnessing him utter a direct verbal falsehood, despite his constant practice of deceit in other forms. Can you conceive the soul of a man who never hesitated to undertake actions designed to hoodwink others, who utilized every market trick to mislead, yet remained scrupulous in never telling a direct lie on the most trivial matter? At the word, Trent clapped his hands together with a muttered exclamation. The others regarded him in surprise.

"Chess!" Trent repeated, rising to approach Marlowe. "Tell me," he said, "what was the first thing I noticed about you at our initial meeting? It was your eye, Mr. Marlowe. I could not place it then, but I now recall where I had seen those eyes before. They belonged to no lesser man than the great Nikolay Korchagin, with whom I once shared a railway carriage for two days. "What was that you said about our having an appointment at half-past seven?" asked Mr. Cupples as the two emerged from the grand gateway of the apartment block. "Do we actually have such an engagement?"

"Certainly we do," replied Trent. "You are dining with me. Nothing properly celebrates this occasion but a dinner paid for by me. No, no! I invited you first. I have finally reached the bottom of a case that must be unique—a puzzle that has troubled my mind for over a year—and if that isn't sufficient cause to treat you to a meal, I don't know what is. Cupples, we shall not go to my club. Tonight is to be a festival, and appearing in a London club in a state of such pleasurable emotion would be enough to shatter any man's career. Furthermore, the dinner there is invariably the same; or, at least, they manage to make it taste identical regardless of the chef. The eternal dinner at my club has bored millions of members like myself, and it shall continue to bore them; but tonight, let the feast be spread in vain for everyone else. We will not go where the satraps congregate. We will go to Sheppard's."

"Who is Sheppard?" asked Mr. Cupples mildly, as they ascended Victoria Street. His companion moved with an unnatural lightness, and a policeman, observing his face, smiled indulgently at a look of happiness he could only attribute to alcohol.

"Who is Sheppard?" echoed Trent with bitter emphasis. "That question, if you will pardon me for saying so, Cupples, is thoroughly characteristic of the spirit of aimless inquiry prevailing in this restless age. I suggest we dine at Sheppard's, and instantly you fold your arms and demand, in a frenzy of intellectual pride, to know who Sheppard is before you will cross the threshold. I am not going to pander to the vices of the modern mind. Sheppard's is merely a place where one can dine. I do not know Sheppard. It never occurred to me that Sheppard existed. Probably he is a myth of totemistic origin. All I know is that you can get a slice of saddle of mutton at Sheppard's that has caused many an American visitor to curse the day Christopher Columbus was born.... Taxi!"

A cab rolled smoothly to the curb, and the driver received his instructions with a majestic nod.

"There is another reason I have for suggesting Sheppard's," continued Trent, feverishly lighting a cigarette, "which is that I am about to marry the most wonderful woman in the world. I trust the connection of ideas is clear."

"You are going to marry Mabel!" cried Mr. Cupples. "My dear friend, what excellent news this is! Shake hands, Trent; this is glorious! I congratulate you both from the bottom of my heart. And may I say—I don't wish to interrupt your flow of high spirits, which is very natural indeed, and I recall being just the same in similar circumstances long ago—but may I say how earnestly I have hoped for this? Mabel has seen so much unhappiness, yet she is surely a woman formed in the great purpose of humanity to be the best influence in the life of a good man. But I did not know her mind as it regarded yourself. *Your* mind I have known for some time," Mr. Cupples went on, with a twinkle in his eye that would have done credit to the worldliest of creatures. "I saw it at once when you were both dining at my house, and you sat listening to Professor Peppmuller and gazing at her. Some of us older fellows have our wits about us still, my dear boy."

"Mabel says she knew it before that," replied Trent, with a slightly crestfallen air. "And I thought I was acting the part of a person who was not mad about her to the life. Well, I never was any good at dissembling. I shouldn't wonder if even old Peppmuller noticed something through his double convex lenses. But however crazy I may have been as an undeclared suitor," he continued, returning to vivacity, "I am going to be much worse now. As for your congratulations, thank you a thousand times, because I know you mean them. You are the sort of uncomfortable brute who would pull a face three feet long if you thought we were making a mistake. By the way, I can't help being an ass tonight; I'm obliged to go on blithering. You must try to bear it. Perhaps it would be easier if I sang you a song—one of your old favorites. What was that song you used always to sing? Something like this, wasn't it?" He accompanied the following stave with a dexterous clog-step on the floor of the cab:

*"There was an old nigger, and he had a wooden leg. He had no tobacco, no tobacco could he beg. Another old nigger was as cunning as a fox, And he always had tobacco in his old tobacco-box."*

"Now for the chorus! Yes, he always had tobacco in his old tobacco-box."

"But you're not singing. I thought you would be making the welkin ring."

"I never sang that song in my life," protested Mr. Cupples. "I never heard it before."

"Are you sure?" enquired Trent doubtfully. "Well, I suppose I must take your word for it. It is a beautiful song, anyhow: not the whole warbling grove in concert heard can beat it. Somehow it seems to express my feelings at the present moment as nothing else could; it rises unbidden to the lips. Out of the fullness of the heart the mouth speaketh, as the Bishop of Bath and Wells said when listening to a speech of Mr. Balfour's."

"When was that?" asked Mr. Cupples.

"On the occasion," replied Trent, "of the introduction of the Compulsory Notification of Diseases of Poultry Bill, which ill-fated measure you of course remember. Hullo!" he broke off, as the cab rushed down a side street and swung round a corner into a broad and populous thoroughfare, "we're there already". The cab drew up.

"Here we are," said Trent, as he paid the driver and led Mr. Cupples into a long, panelled room set with many tables and filled with a hum of talk. "This is the house of fulfillment of craving, this is the bower with the roses around it. I see there are three bookmakers eating pork at my favorite table. We will have that one in the opposite corner."

He conferred earnestly with a waiter, while Mr. Cupples, in a pleasant meditation, warmed himself before the great fire. "The wine here," Trent resumed, as they seated themselves, "is almost certainly made out of grapes. What shall we drink?"

Mr. Cupples came out of his reverie. "I think," he said, "I will have milk and soda water."

"Speak lower!" urged Trent. "The head-waiter has a weak heart, and might hear you. Milk and soda water! Cupples, you may think you have a strong constitution, and I don't say you have not, but I warn you that this habit of mixing drinks has been the death of many a robuster man than you. Be wise in time. Fill high the bowl with Samian wine, leave soda to the Turkish hordes. Here comes our food." He gave another order to the waiter, who ranged the dishes before them and darted away. Trent was, it seemed, a respected customer. "I have sent," he said, "for wine that I know, and I hope you will try it. If you have taken a vow, then in the name of all the teetotal saints drink water, which stands at your elbow, but don't seek a cheap notoriety by demanding milk and soda."

"I have never taken any pledge," said Mr. Cupples, examining his mutton with a favorable eye. "I simply don't care about wine. I bought a bottle once and drank it to see what it was like, and it made me ill. But very likely it was bad wine. I will taste some of yours, as it is your dinner, and I do assure you, my dear Trent, I should like to do something unusual to show how strongly I feel on the present occasion. I have not been so delighted for many years. To think," he reflected aloud as the waiter filled his glass, "of the Manderson mystery disposed of, the innocent exculpated, and your own and Mabel's happiness crowned—all coming upon me together! I drink to you, my dear friend." And Mr. Cupples took a very small sip of the wine.

"You have a great nature," said Trent, much moved. "Your outward semblance doth belie your soul's immensity. I should have expected as soon to see an elephant conducting at the opera as you drinking my health. Dear Cupples! May his beak retain ever that delicate rose-stain!—No, curse it all!" he broke out, surprising a shade of discomfort that flitted over his companion's face as he tasted the wine again. "I have no business to meddle with your tastes. I apologize. You shall have what you want, even if it causes the head-waiter to perish in his pride."
