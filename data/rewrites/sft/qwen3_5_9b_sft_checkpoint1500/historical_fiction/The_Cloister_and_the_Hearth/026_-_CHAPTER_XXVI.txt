The afternoon following Gerard's injury found him no longer dizzy, but plagued by a restless irritability and a flood of peculiar fancies. In one such whimsy, he entreated Denys to procure a lemon for sucking. Denys, transformed from a rough soldier into a figure resembling a grandfather by the tenderness of his friendship, rose hastily. "Lemons?" he exclaimed, assuring Gerard he would have them "in the twinkling of a quart pot," and vanished to scour the local markets. They were rare luxuries in the north, and Denys was gone a considerable time. Gerard grew increasingly impatient until the shop door finally creaked open.

However, it was not Denys who entered. Instead, a towering, imposing figure glided softly inside. He was an elderly gentleman clad in a long, sober gown trimmed with rich fur, wearing cherry-colored hose and pointed shoes. A sword rested at his side within a fine Morocco scabbard, and around his neck hung a severe, starched ruff, treacherously stiffened into deep furrows by hidden wooden frameworks known as rebatoes. Upon his head sat a four-cornered cap bordered with fur, framing a majestic white beard that cascaded over his bosom. Gerard instantly recognized the vocation of this visitor; barring the weapon, this attire was the full regalia of a physician. Following closely at the elder's heels was a small boy carrying a basket brimming with phials, lint, and surgical instruments, all seemingly inviting scrutiny.

The old gentleman approached the bedside with soft steps and asked quietly, "How fares it with thee, my son?"

Gerard answered with gratitude that his wound caused him little pain, though his throat felt parched and his head heavy.

"A wound!" the doctor interrupted, his tone shifting to professional curiosity. "They told me not of that. Let me see it. Ay, ay, a good, clean bite. The mastiff had sound teeth that took this out, I warrant me;" and the physician's sympathy seemed to drain entirely toward the canine predator he had conjured, his imagined jackal. "This must be cauterized forthwith, or we shall find you starting back from water, turning somersaults in bed under our hands. 'Tis the season for raving curs, and one hath done your business; but we will baffle him yet. Urchin, go heat thine iron."

"But, sir," Gerard protested gently, "'twas no dog, but a bear."

"A bear!" the senior physician remonstrated severely. "Think what you say; 'tis ill jesting with the man of art who brings his grey hairs and long study to heal you. A bear, quotha! Had you dissected as many bears as I, or even a tenth thereof, and drawn their teeth to keep your hand in, you would know that no bear's jaw ever made this foolish trifling wound. I tell you 'twas a dog, and since you put me to it, I even deny that it was a dog of magnitude, but neither more nor less than one of these little furious curs that are so rife, running devious paths, biting every manly leg, and laying its wearer low—but for me and my learned brethren, who still stay the mischief with knife and cautery."

"Alas, sir!" Gerard hastened to correct him. "When did I say 'twas a bear's jaw?' I said, 'A bear;' it was his paw, now."

"And why didst not tell me that at once?"

"Because you kept telling me instead."

"Never conceal aught from your leech, young man," the senior continued. He was a good talker, albeit one of the worst listeners in Europe. "Well, it is an ill business. All the horny excrescences of animals—to wit, claws of tigers, panthers, badgers, cats, bears, and the like—and the horn of deer, and the nails of humans, especially children, are imbued with direst poison. You'd better have been bitten by a cur, whatever you may say, than gored by a bull or stag, or scratched by a bear. However, you shall have a good biting cataplasm for thy leg; meantime, let us keep the body cool: put out thy tongue!—Good!—Fever. Let me feel thy pulse: Good!—Fever. I ordain flebotomy, and on the instant."

"Flebotomy! That is bloodletting," Gerard murmured. "Humph! Well, no matter, if 'tis sure to cure me, for I will not lie idle here." The doctor assured him that flebotomy was infallible, particularly in this case.

"Hans, go fetch the things needful," commanded the man of art, "and I will entertain the patient meantime with reasons."

The physician then began to explain to Gerard that in disease the blood becomes hot, distempered, and increasingly poisonous. By removing a portion of this unhealthy liquid, nature would be compelled to create a purer fluid to fill the void. Therefore, bleeding served as both a cooler and a purifier, acting as a specific cure for all diseases, for all ailments were essentially febrile, regardless of what empirics might claim.

"But think not," he said warmly, "that it suffices merely to bleed. Any paltry barber can open a vein (though not all possess the skill to close it again). The art lies in knowing precisely which vein to empty for a specific disease. The other day, they brought me a man tormented with earache. I let him blood in the right thigh, and away flew his earache. By-the-by, he has died since then. Another came with a toothache. I bled him behind the ear, and relieved him in a jiffy. He, too, is now dead, as it happens. I bled our bailiff between the thumb and forefinger for rheumatism. Presently, he returned with a headache and drumming in his ears, holding out his hand over the basin; but I smiled at his folly, bled him in the left ankle sore against his will, and made his head as light as a nut."

Diverging from the immediate medical theme in the manner of an enthusiast, the reverend teacher proceeded to lecture further: "Know, young man, that two schools of art contend at this moment throughout Europe. There is the Arabian school, whose ancient oracles are Avicenna, Rhazes, and Albucasis; its revivers include Chauliac and Lanfranc. Then there is the Greek school, whose modern champions are Bessarion, Platinus, and Marsilius Ficinus, but whose pristine doctors were medicine's very oracles: Phoebus, Chiron, Aesculapius, and his sons Podalinus and Machaon, along with Pythagoras, Democritus, and Praxagoras, who invented the arteries, and Dioctes, 'qui primus urinae animum dedit.' All these taught orally. Then came Hippocrates, the eighteenth descendant of Aesculapius, of whom we possess manuscripts. To him we owe 'the vital principle.' He also invented the bandage and tapped for water on the chest; and above all, he dissected—yet only quadrupeds, for the brutal prejudices of the pagan vulgar withheld the human body from the knife of science. Him followed Aristotle, who gave us the aorta, the largest blood-vessel in the human body."

"Surely, sir," objected Gerard humbly, "the Almighty gave us all that is in our bodies, and not Aristotle, nor any Grecian man."

"Child! Of course He gave us the thing," the doctor retorted, his color rising. "But Aristotle did more; he gave us the *name* of the thing. But young men will still be talking. The next great light was Galen, who studied at Alexandria, then the home of science. He, justly malcontent with quadrupeds, dissected apes, as coming nearer to man, and bled like a Trojan. Then came Theophilus, who gave us the nerves, the lacteal vessels, and the pia mater."

This worried Gerard. "I cannot lie still and hear it said that mortal man bestowed the parts which Adam our father took from Him, who made him of the clay, and us His sons."

"Was ever such perversity?" said the doctor, indignant. "Who is the real donor of a thing to man? Is it he who plants it secretly in the dark recesses of man's body, or the learned wight who reveals it to his intelligence, thereby enriching his mind with knowledge? Comprehension is your only true possession. Are you answered?"

"I am put to silence, sir."

"And that is better still; for garrulous patients are ill to cure, especially in fever. I say, then, that Eristratus gave us the cerebral nerves and the milk vessels; nay, more, he was the inventor of lithotomy, whatever you may say. Then came another whom I forget; you do somewhat perturb me with your petty exceptions. Then came Ammonius, the author of lithotrity, and here comes Hans with the basin—to stay your volubility. Blow thy chafer, boy, and hand me the basin; 'tis well. Arabs, quotha! What are they but a sect of yesterday who, about the year 1000, fell in with the writings of those very Greeks and read them awry, having no concurrent light of their own?
