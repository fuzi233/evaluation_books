Just prior to Mr. Foker's departure from Oxford, a certain gentleman arrived at Boniface College. By turns of fate, this visitor had previously attended Cambridge, leaving abruptly due to a falling-out with the tutors and authorities there. His name was Horace Bloundell, a scion of the ancient Suffolk lineage known as the Bloundell-Bloundells of Bloundell-Bloundell Hall (a title the local undergraduates affectionately shortened). It was likely his pedigree, combined with the fact that Dr. Donne, the Master of Boniface, hailed from Suffolk and was possibly related to the family, that secured Bloundell's admission. After St. George's and a couple of other institutions rejected him, Boniface opened its doors.

A church living awaited the family, a position crucial for Mr. Bloundell to secure. While serving in a dragoon regiment, his third brother—who was meant to inherit the benefice—fell ill and died. Seizing the moment, Mr. Bloundell resolved to trade his crimson breeches and black shako for the sober black coat and white cravat of the English clergyman. Though setbacks at Cambridge briefly disrupted his plans, the determined former soldier remained undaunted, pivoting to win his victory elsewhere.

During Pen's second year, Major Pendennis visited his nephew, introducing him to a motley crew of university friends: the gentle and polished Lord Plinlimmon; the gallant, open-hearted Magnus Charters; the sly and witty Harland; the intrepid Ringwood, known as "Rupert" in the Union Debating Club for the courage displayed in his blunders; Broadbent, dubbed "Barebones" for his republican leanings and dissenter roots; and finally, Mr. Bloundell-Bloundell, who quickly claimed his spot among the elite.

Though the Major struggled to parse Harland's Greek citations or fully grasp Broadbent's heavy footwear and grimy hands, he was charmed by the gathering. He delighted in recounting stories to the lads. While London audiences grew weary of anecdotes, the Oxford students listened with honest sympathy, eager delight, raucous laughter, or deep respect—a reaction far superior to the metropolitan indifference. Occasionally, however, Mr. Bloundell's face twisted with scorn, revealing he already knew the tales. On one occasion, he boldly challenged the accuracy of a detail, offering his own version based on hearsay from the Club. The students watched in awe as their peer dared to interrupt the Major. Few understood the melancholy grace with which Major Pendennis simply accepted the correction, thanking Bloundell for fixing his error. Later, Bloundell openly disparaged "Old Pen," labeling him a glutton at Gaunt House, a notorious bore, and a complete fogey.

The Major reciprocated this disdain instantly. Sympathy between people is often mutual; if someone abuses me, I instinctively dislike them. Our preferences for others are as arbitrary as our likes and dislikes for flowers, wines, or books. We cannot explain why, but generally, if you hate Dr. Fell, he hates you.

"My boy," the Major declared over breakfast the morning after the party, "your dinner was *à merveille*. You carved splendidly—an art still vital in good households, aiding one in middle age. Young Lord Plinlimmon is amiable, a mirror of his mother, Lady Aquila Brownbill. Lord Magnus's republicanism suits a young patrician, though it grows tiresome later. Broadbent shows promise with his eloquence. Your friend Foker is delightful. But your acquaintance, Mr. Bloundell... strikes me as wholly ineligible."

"Bless my soul, sir!" Pen laughed. "Why, he's the most popular man in the university! We elected him President of the Barmecides the very first week. He's from an excellent family—the Suffolk Bloundells, descended from Richard the Lionheart's Blondel, bearing a harp in chief and the motto 'O Mong Roy'."

"A man may have a magnificent coat of arms and still be a tiger," the Major retorted, cracking his egg. "That man is a tiger. I'll wager he left his regiment—which was respectable, mind you—in bad odor. Look at him; there's the unmistakable scent of slang and bad habits. He frequents low gambling dens and billiard halls, haunts third-rate clubs. Notice the quantity of rings and jewelry? 'Scamp' is written across his face. Avoid him. Let us change the subject. Your dinner was slightly too lavish, but I don't mind you treating friends occasionally, provided you stay within your income. The cutlets were excellent, the soufflé light, and while the third bottle of champagne was unnecessary, you have a good allowance."

Poor Pen! The Major had no idea how often these extravagant dinners actually occurred. The reckless young host reveled in displaying his hospitality and culinary flair. Boys are obsessed with appearing knowledgeable about food and wine, viewing such expertise as the hallmark of a sophisticated rogue. They wink knowingly at glasses of claret and debate sauces, unaware that true wisdom comes only with age, realizing that a simple leg of mutton is often preferable to the feasts of popes. Little Pen, aspiring to be the "Admirable Crichton," felt compelled to master dining arts. Soon, however, his staircase would no longer be clogged with servants and ice-chilled champagne; instead, it would be besieged by creditors with sulky faces demanding payment.

Yet, the Major's advice fell on deaf ears. What young men crave is a perceived knowledge of life. A fellow who claims worldly experience, a "roué" with tales of adventure, commands admiration. It is human nature to respect such prowess, even if it is fabricated. How many schoolboys admit to loving *Don Juan*? It is a dangerous propagation of falsehood. One such corrupt individual can poison an entire college. The tutors at Boniface soon noticed a decline in moral tone and gentility following Bloundell's arrival.

While the magnates of neighboring St. George's College, including Pen, resisted Bloundell's flashy charms, others were wary. Broadbent labeled him "Captain Macheath," predicting a hanging. Foker, exercising extreme caution, avoided direct criticism but subtly warned Pen: "Better play whist with him than against him; better back him at Ecarte than bet against him. He plays remarkably well, and doesn't seem flush of cash either." Beyond these veiled hints, the prudent Foker said nothing.

But Pen, driven by an insatiable hunger for pleasure, ignored warnings. He equated enjoyment with "seeing life," quoting Terence, Horace, and Shakespeare to justify his actions. At this pace, he risked becoming a ruined rake in mere years.

One evening, after a supper party featuring a quiet game of *vingt-et-un*, the men prepared to depart. Bloundell, seizing a green wine glass meant for iced water, produced a pair of dice from his waistcoat pocket. With practiced ease, he tossed them onto the table, swept them up, and repeated the motion. Pen, usually content with humble backgammon, watched in fascination. Bloundell then sang the chorus from *Robert the Devil*, drawing Pen and others into the song. Before long, the group abandoned their caps and homes for the table, passing the green glass around until Pen, after throwing six consecutive wins, shattered it.

That night marked Pen's plunge into the deadly game of chance. Dice became a morning ritual as well as an afternoon pastime. Bloundell would arrive at Pen's rooms post-breakfast, and time would vanish as the dice rattled. They held secret sessions behind closed doors; Bloundell even crafted a felt-lined box to silence the telltale clatter. Once, they nearly caught Mr. Buck, a tutor, who heard "Two to one on the caster" through Pen's window. When Buck entered, he found them studying Homer, with Pen gravely discussing the navigability of the River Scamander.

Pen gained little wealth from these dealings, save for a smattering of odds and ends he could have read in a book. However, Captain Macheath possessed other talents. His stories of garrison conquests and exploits in the countryside held a magnetic, albeit destructive, allure. He spun tall tales of Parisian nights at the Palais Royal and the Café de Paris, boasting of waking to find twelve thousand francs under his pillow and a boot stuffed with Napoleons. Pen's eyes gleamed with envy, respecting the man who could command such riches. Macheath dismissed Pen's infatuation with Miss Fotheringay, mocking the idea of marrying her legitimately. Instead, he suggested Pen might as well wed a notorious actress, listing names of men who had squandered fortunes on her.

"It wasn't a shred of a character," Macheath sneered. Pen, naturally credulous and honest, swallowed these lies whole. He accepted Macheath's fabrications as historical fact, leading to profound humiliation. If he had been ashamed of his passion before, how could he feel now that the object of his adoration was revealed as a fraud? He never questioned the reliability of a man who spoke ill of all women.

During an Easter vacation, Pen decided to remain at Oxford, yet Bloundell dragged him to London anyway. They checked into a Covent Garden hotel where Bloundell held a credit line ("tick") and indulged in the city's pleasures. Bloundell, still a member of a military club, took Pen to dine there, driving them in a cab with trembling hearts lest they encounter Major Pendennis on patrol.
