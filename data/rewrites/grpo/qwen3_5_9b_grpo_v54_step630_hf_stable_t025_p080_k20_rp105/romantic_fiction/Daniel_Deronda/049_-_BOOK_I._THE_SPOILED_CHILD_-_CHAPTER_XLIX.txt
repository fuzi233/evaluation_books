Ever in his soul, that larger justice which makes gratitude triumph over resentment stood as the mark of regal natures, endowed with a wider life and a fuller capacity for joy. It was not the wit that exults under the strongest lens, showing goodness vanished into pulp, never worth a "thank you"—those are the devil's friars, vowed to be poor in love and trust yet forced to beg from a world that hoards some human property.

As Deronda parted from Gwendolen, he had carefully withheld the words, "I shall not see you again for a long while; I am going away," fearing that Grandcourt would interpret such a statement as implying the departure mattered to her. In truth, he was departing under circumstances so momentous to himself that even as he set out to keep his promise of calling upon her, he walked under the shadow of a solemn emotion that revived the deepest experience of his life.

Sir Hugo had summoned him to his chambers with a terse note: "Come immediately. Something has happened." This summons brought Deronda a strange relief; upon entering the baronet's study, he found himself received with grave affection rather than the distress he had dreaded.

"It is nothing to grieve you, sir?" Deronda asked, his tone carrying more restored confidence than genuine inquiry as he took the hand extended to him. There was an unusual depth in Sir Hugo's gaze and a subdued tremor in his voice. "No, Dan, no. Sit down. I have something to say."

Deronda obeyed, though not without a heavy presentiment. It was exceedingly rare for Sir Hugo to display such profound seriousness.

"Not to grieve me, my boy, no. At least, if there is nothing in it that will grieve you too much. But I hardly expected that this—just this—would ever happen. There have been reasons why I have never prepared you for it. There have been reasons why I have never told you anything about your parentage. But I have striven in every way not to make that an injury to you."

Sir Hugo paused, but Deronda remained silent. He could not say, "I have never felt it an injury." Even if those words had been true, he could not trust his voice to speak them. Far more than anyone else could know hung suspended in this moment as the secrecy was finally broken. Sir Hugo had never seen the grand face he adored so pale, nor the lips pressed together with such a look of pain. He continued with a more anxious tenderness, as if harboring a fresh fear of wounding.

"I have acted in obedience to your mother's wishes. The secrecy was her wish. But now she desires to remove it. She desires to see you. I will put this letter into your hands, which you can look at by-and-by. It will merely tell you what she wishes you to do, and where you will find her."

Sir Hugo held out a letter written on foreign paper. Deronda thrust it into his breast-pocket, relieved not to be called upon to read its contents immediately. The emotion on Daniel's face had overtaken the baronet's composure, visibly shaking him. Sir Hugo found it difficult to speak further. Deronda's entire soul was consumed by a question that was the hardest in the world to utter. Yet he could not bear to delay it. This was a sacramental moment; if he let it pass, he could never again recover the influences necessary to speak the words and meet the answer. For some moments, their eyes were cast down, and it seemed to both as if thoughts drifted in the air between them. Finally, Deronda looked at Sir Hugo and spoke with a tremulous reverence, dreading to convey indirectly the reproach that affection had stifled for years: "Is my father also living?"

The answer came immediately in a low, emphatic tone: "No."

In the mingled emotions that followed, it was impossible to distinguish joy from pain.

A new light had fallen upon the past for Sir Hugo as well during this interview. After a silence in which Deronda felt like one whose old creed had crumbled before he had religiously embraced another, the baronet spoke in a tone of confession. "Perhaps I was wrong, Dan, to undertake what I did. And perhaps I liked it a little too well—having you all to myself. But if you have had any pain which I might have helped, I ask you to forgive me."

"The forgiveness has long been there," said Deronda. "The chief pain has always been on account of some one else—whom I never knew—whom I am now to know. It has not hindered me from feeling an affection for you which has made a large part of all the life I remember."

It seemed one single impulse that made the two men clasp each other's hands for a moment.
