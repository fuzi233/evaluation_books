CHAPTER LXII. “Das Glück ist eine leichte Dirne, Und weilt nicht gern am selben Ort; Sie streicht das Haar dir von der Stirne Und küsst dich rasch und flattert fort Frau Unglück hat im Gegentheile Dich liebefest an’s Herz gedrückt; Sie sagt, sie habe keine Eile, Setzt sich zu dir an’s Bett und strickt.” —HEINE.

Mirah had been waiting—almost dreading—for the moment that now arrived: not with the suddenness of a storm, but with the quiet persistence of sorrow returning home. After singing at a morning charity concert in a grand house near Knightsbridge—invited by Klesmer, whose name still carried weight among those who valued music—she stepped out into the London street, wrapped in a simple black dress and a dust cloak, her thoughts heavy with the day’s performance and the memory of her father’s absence. Outside, a crowd lingered to watch the performers depart, but Mirah walked on, alone, her steps measured, her eyes fixed ahead.

Then she felt it—the presence behind her. Not a threat, not yet, but a rhythm matching her own. A shadow kept pace. Her breath caught. It wasn’t the kind of attention that drew from strangers’ stares, nor was it the sort that made her fear for her safety. No—this was deeper. This was recognition. And with it came a flood of dread, as if she were being hunted not by men, but by memory itself.

She did not turn. To look would be to surrender to the emotions she had long buried. If it was him—if it was her father—then he would force her to face him. She could not flee, not truly. But neither could she meet him unprepared. There was Ezra, her brother, whom she had promised Mrs. Meyrick she would protect from sudden shocks. That vow now pressed upon her like a weight. She must not let him see this man—not yet.

So she resolved to turn before reaching her door, to confront him on her own terms. She had nearly reached the entrance of the quiet square where they lived when the footsteps quickened. Then—there was a hand on her wrist, warm and insistent. A voice, familiar and strained, whispered, “Mirah!”

She stopped. Not with shock, but with a kind of resignation. The voice was his. The face—when she turned—was worn, lined, once handsome but now dulled by time and want. His eyes held a mixture of charm and desperation, as though he meant to beg while pretending to command. He wore shabby clothes, his hair grizzled and curly, his frame slight but active. He looked older than fifty-seven, though he was not. The man who had once been her father stood before her now, stripped of dignity, clinging to the last vestiges of suavity.

“Why did you run away from me, child?” he asked, his voice rising in false tenderness, hands gesturing as if to soothe. “What were you afraid of? I never forced you. I broke your engagement in Dresden because I saw it wouldn’t suit you. I had arranged something better for you at the theater—I didn’t tell you, so I could surprise you. But you left me stranded. I had to vanish, contract broken, reputation ruined. And after all I gave up—your education, your future—after everything I sacrificed—what did you do? You abandoned me.”

He paused, then suddenly sobbed—a woman’s sob—and pulled out a yellowed silk handkerchief, wiping his eyes with exaggerated emotion. “I loved you more than any father ever loved a daughter,” he said, voice cracking. “And you repaid me by leaving me alone. I might have died in a ditch, and you wouldn’t have cared.”

Mirah listened, her heart pounding. She knew the truth beneath his words—the lies woven into grief. Yet she did not flinch. For the first time, she spoke plainly.

“You know why I left you, father,” she said, voice trembling but firm. “I trusted no one. I believed you deceived my mother. If I could have trusted you, I would have stayed. But I couldn’t.”

Lapidoth blinked. The handkerchief slipped back into his pocket. “I never meant to deceive her,” he insisted, voice thick. “I wanted to bring you back. But circumstances changed. Then came news—she was dead. I stayed. It was best. Your brother could fend for himself. Only you mattered to me. I sent money to cover the costs. There’s one chance—maybe someone lied to me just to get the money.”

Mirah said nothing. She could not say what she truly thought—*I don not believe you*. Instead, she turned slightly, urging him forward. They walked together, side by side, a strange pair: her poised and elegant in English modesty, he restless and foreign, his gestures too eager, his presence too loud.

“You’ve done well for yourself,” he said, studying her. “You’re not poor.”

“I’ve had good friends,” she replied, keeping her voice low. “They helped me find work. I give lessons. I sing privately. I just performed at a concert.”

She hesitated, then added, “And I have people who know everything about me.”

A flicker of unease crossed his face. “And you’d be ashamed to show them your father like this?” he asked, almost pleading. “No wonder. I came here hoping to find you. A mad hope, perhaps—but a father’s heart is drawn by instinct. I could have done well abroad. Without you, I could have settled anywhere. But loneliness breaks a man. I thought you’d come to regret leaving me.”

He sighed. “I’ve struggled. I don’t know what comes next. My talents mean nothing here. People won’t hire a man who looks like he’s fallen apart. I’ve sunk low for a shilling already.”

Mirah’s mind raced. She could not let him sink further. But before she could speak, he asked, “Where do you live?”

“In this square,” she said. “We’re close.”

“In lodgings?”

“Yes.”

“Anyone to care for you?”

“Yes,” she said, meeting his gaze directly. “My brother.”

His eyelids fluttered. A brief tremor passed through his shoulders. Then, after a pause, he asked, “Ezra? How did you find him?”

“It would take time to explain,” she said. “Here we are.”

They stood at the doorway. Mirah stepped onto the threshold, turning toward him. Her heart beat fast. The thought of Ezra seeing this man—her father—filled her with shame and pity. And yet, in this moment, she felt a deep, painful reverence. She had always obeyed him. Now, she was choosing to defy him—not with anger, but with love.

“Stay a minute, *Liebchen*,” Lapidoth said softly. “What sort of man is Ezra?”

“A good man,” Mirah said slowly. “A wonderful man. He was poor when my friends found him—just a workman. Twelve years ago, he was strong and full of hope, going eastward, which he loved. But my mother called him back—because she had lost me. He came, cared for her through great suffering, worked until she died—died in sorrow. And Ezra… he lost his strength too. The cold took him when he returned. For years, he’s grown weaker—always poor, always working—but wise, thoughtful, noble. Everyone who knows him honors him. To stand before him is like standing before a prophet. Falsehoods are useless.”

She lowered her eyes, unable to bear the expression forming on his face—the frustration, the emptiness. But Lapidoth moved quickly. “Mirah,” he said, softening again, “wouldn’t it be better if I looked more respectable before your brother sees me? If I had a little money, I could buy new clothes. I could present myself properly. With a decent shirt and coat, I’d be welcome somewhere. I could even offer myself as a courier. I’d like to be with my children. To forget. To forgive.”

He paused. “But you’ve never seen me like this before. If you had ten pounds—just ten pounds—I could fix myself up by tomorrow.”

Mirah hesitated. She knew the temptation. She also remembered her promise—to Mrs. Meyrick, to Ezra, to herself. She looked at him, her voice quiet but firm.

“I don’t like to refuse you, father,” she said. “But I promised not to help you secretly. It’s hard to see you like this. But we can endure it. Soon, you’ll have new clothes. We’ll pay for them.”

Lapidoth’s smile faded. “You’re a strict young lady now,” he sneered. “You’ve learned useful virtues—keeping promises when your father needs a pound or two. Your father who gave up his life for you, who made you an idol.”

“It seems cruel,” Mirah whispered, her lips pale. “I know it does. But breaking promises breaks hearts. It broke Mother’s. It has broken Ezra’s. We must bear this. Bear it now. Come in. Let us care for you as you are.”

“Tomorrow,” Lapidoth said, turning away sharply—then stopping, hands digging into his pockets. “I’m a little shaken, Mirah. I’ll feel better by tomorrow. If you’ve got a bit of money in your pocket—just a trifle—it wouldn’t break your promise to lend me enough for a cigar.”

Mirah didn’t question. She reached into her pocket, her fingers numb, and handed him her purse.

He took it swiftly, pressing her hand gently. “Good-bye, my little girl,” he said. “Tomorrow.”

He walked away, only a few steps, then stopped. He opened the purse carefully, counted the coins—two half-sovereigns, some silver—and then noticed a small piece of paper tucked inside. On it, in beautiful Hebrew script, was written the name of her mother, the dates of her birth, marriage, and death, and a prayer: *May Mirah be delivered from evil.*

It was a gift Ezra had given her. A sacred thing.

Lapidoth read it. In that instant, he saw his youth—the bright-eyed groom, the beloved husband, the man who once dreamed of writing, of teaching, of love. But the memory was flat, tasteless—like food without flavor. He had traveled far from that man. And now, the past was just a shape without feeling.

He closed the purse, considering its value—not just the coins, but the object itself. Could he sell it? Could he return for more? He knew the risk: the son watching, the daughter guarded, the world now against him.

Meanwhile, Mirah entered the house, her composure shattered. She found Ezra seated at a table, quietly sorting through ancient manuscripts—texts he intended to pass on to Deronda. At the sight of her, he rose, alarmed.

She fell to her knees before him, clutching his legs, sobbing. “Ezra! Ezra!”

He said nothing. His concern was immediate, profound. He understood her pain without needing words.

After a moment, she raised her hand, still weeping. “Ezra… my father. Our father. He followed me. I wanted him to come in. I told him you would let him. But he said no—not now. Tomorrow. And he asked for money. I gave him my purse. And he went.”

Her voice broke. She had said it all.

Ezra waited until she calmed, then gently removed her hat, laid his hands on her head. “Wait,” he said softly. “When you are calm, tell me everything.”

She did. And when she finished, he said quietly, “He will not come tomorrow.”

Neither spoke the truth they both knew: that he might follow her again, beg again, try again.

Then, after a silence, Ezra said, “Seest thou, Mirah? Our lot is the lot of Israel. Grief and glory are mingled—as smoke and flame. Because we inherit the good, we feel the evil. These things are wedded to us, as our father was wedded to our mother.”

The words hung in the air. Though they were in Brompton, the tone could have come from a rabbi reciting ancient wisdom in Babylon. For the Rabbi had said, “The Omnipresent is occupied in making marriages.” And by marriages, he meant all the wondrous, tangled unions of fate—those that bring joy and those that bring sorrow—each shaping our lives, each weaving the threads of our destiny.
