The silence in the room was not empty; it was heavy, a physical weight pressing against the spine of the shelf-lined chamber where Wilbur Whateley's manuscript lay. To the experts of Miskatonic University, the document was a puzzle of impossible proportions. Its alphabet, vaguely reminiscent of the shaded scripts of Mesopotamia yet utterly alien to any known tongue, defied classification. Linguists offered only a consensus of bafflement: an artificial cipher, resistant to every method of decryption. The ancillary texts seized from Whateley's quarters added to the dread—a heavy tome bound in iron, inscribed in a script resembling Sanskrit, offering no illumination.

Dr. Armitage accepted the burden. His age, seventy-three, carried a specific gravity, a focus honed by decades of studying the mystical formulæ of antiquity. He viewed the symbols not necessarily as a sacred language of forbidden cults, but pragmatically as a cipher masking a modern vernacular. If the writer possessed the patience to compose such a vast text, he reasoned, he would not laboriously adopt a foreign syntax unless compelled by specific incantations. Thus, Armitage attacked the manuscript with the assumption that beneath the garb lay English.

He fortified himself with the accumulated lore of cryptography, immersing himself in the dense, dusty volumes of Trithemius, Porta, Vigenère, and the later treatises of Davys and Thicknesse. Night after night, amidst the arcana of *Poligraphia*, he worked. The process was slow, a grinding of stone against stone. He realized he faced one of the most intricate cryptograms ever devised, a grid of shifting keys known only to initiates. Progress was elusive; moments of clarity were shattered by unforeseen obstacles.

Then, as September approached, the fog lifted. Letters emerged from the chaos, distinct and undeniable. The text was English. On the evening of September second, the final barrier crumbled. Armitage read the first continuous passage of Wilbur Whateley's annals. It was a diary, written in a style that betrayed a dangerous mixture of occult erudition and profound illiteracy.

The entry, dated November 26, 1916, struck with the force of a physical blow. Written by a child of three and a half who resembled a boy of twelve, the voice was chillingly detached. *"Today learned the Aklo for the Sabaoth,"* it read. *"Which did not like, it being answerable from the hill and not from the air. That upstairs more ahead of me than I had thought it would be, and is not like to have much earth brain."*

The narrative descended further into the uncanny. A dog shot for biting the narrator. A grandfather reciting formulas. Visions of the inner city at the magnetic poles. Plans to clear the earth, to wait for the "outside" helpers who could not take bodies without human blood. The diarist wondered how he would appear when the earth was stripped clean, when there were no earth-beings left. *"They from the air told me at Sabbat that it will be years before I can clear off the earth... I wonder how I shall look when the earth is cleared."*

Morning found Dr. Armitage in a cold sweat, his mind trapped in a frenzy of wakeful concentration. He had not left the manuscript all night. The electric light hummed above him as he turned page after page, his hands shaking, deciphering the cryptic text with a nervous intensity. He had told his wife he would not be home; when she brought breakfast, he could barely swallow a mouthful. Lunch and dinner followed, eaten only in fractions.

By the middle of the next night, he drowsed in his chair, only to wake instantly from nightmares as hideous as the truths he had uncovered. On the morning of September fourth, Professor Rice and Dr. Morgan visited him. They departed trembling, their faces ashen. That evening, Armitage slept fitfully. By Wednesday, he was back at the manuscript, taking copious notes. Before dawn, he slept briefly in an easy-chair; by noon, his physician, Dr. Hartwell, demanded he cease work. Armitage refused, insisting the completion of the reading was vital, promising an explanation later.

That evening, as twilight fell, he finished his terrible perusal and sank back, exhausted. His wife found him in a half-comatose state. When her eyes wandered toward his notes, he cried out sharply, driving her away. Rising weakly, he gathered the scribbled papers, sealing them in a great envelope tucked into his inside coat pocket. He managed to reach home, but Dr. Hartwell was summoned immediately. As the doctor laid him to bed, Armitage muttered endlessly, *"But what, in God's name, can we do?"*

The following day, Armitage slept but remained partly delirious. He spoke of the imperative need to confer with Rice and Morgan, his wilder wanderings becoming increasingly alarming. He shouted for the destruction of a boarded-up farmhouse, referencing plans for the extirpation of all life by an elder race from another dimension. He claimed the world was in danger, that the Elder Things wished to strip the earth and drag it into some other plane of entity. At other times, he screamed for the *Necronomicon* and the *Dæmonolatreia* of Remigius, hoping to find a formula to check the peril. *"Stop them, stop them!"* he would roar. *"Those Whateleys meant to let them in, and the worst of all is left! Tell Rice and Morgan we must do something..."*

Despite his frailty, Armitage's physique held firm enough to sleep off the disorder without fever. He woke late Friday, clear-headed but sobered by a gnawing fear and a tremendous sense of responsibility. Saturday afternoon, he summoned Rice and Morgan to the library. For the rest of the day and evening, the three men tortured their brains in wild speculation and desperate debate. Books were pulled from stacks and secure storerooms; diagrams and formulas were copied with feverish haste. Skepticism vanished. All three had seen the body of Wilbur Whateley lying on the floor of that very building; none dared treat the diary as the ravings of a madman.

Opinions divided on whether to notify the Massachusetts State Police, but the negative prevailed. There were forces involved that could not be believed by those who had not witnessed the evidence firsthand. Late at night, the conference disbanded without a definite plan. Yet, all day Sunday, Armitage compared formulas and mixed chemicals from the college laboratory. The more he reflected on the hellish diary, the more he doubted the efficacy of any material agent against the entity Whateley had left behind—the earth-threatening thing destined to burst forth in a few hours.

Monday mirrored Sunday, a repetition of research and experiment. Tuesday brought a definite line of action, a plan to travel to Dunwich within a week. Then, on Wednesday, the great shock came. Tucked obscurely in a corner of the *Arkham Advertiser* was a facetious item from the Associated Press reporting a record-breaking monster spawned by Dunwich's bootleg whisky.

Armitage, half-stunned, telephoned for Rice and Morgan. Far into the night they discussed; the next day became a whirlwind of preparation. Armitage knew he was meddling with terrible powers, yet saw no other way to annul the deeper, malign meddling of others.
