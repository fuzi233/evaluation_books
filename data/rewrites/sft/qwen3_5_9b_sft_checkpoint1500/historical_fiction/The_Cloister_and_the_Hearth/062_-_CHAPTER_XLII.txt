Gerard woke not to hope, but to a hollow, suffocating despair. By the second day, his fever burned through his skull, leaving him ravaged and delirious. Beside his bed lay the tangle of his own rich auburn hair, once long and flowing like a woman's, now discarded. His condition deteriorated with terrifying speed; the leeches retreated on the fifth day, declaring their work futile. As the sun dipped below the horizon, Gerard succumbed to a profound slumber. Whispers circulated that he would awaken only to meet his end, yet an elderly woman, a professional nurse whose counsel held sway, offered a glimmer of faith. "His youth may still save him," she murmured, "if he can endure twelve hours of sleep."

His old landlady, moved by devotion, swept away the clutter and stood vigil beside his cot. She vowed a wax candle to the Virgin Mary for every passing hour of rest. Twelve hours passed, and the good soul knelt in gratitude, thanking Heaven. Twenty-four followed, though the nurse's joy began to curdle into doubt. By the thirtieth hour, she summoned the physician. "Shall we disturb him?" she asked anxiously. The doctor, a man of science, examined Gerard closely. "His breathing is steady, his hands warm," he observed, dismissing the learned physicians who would have pricked the patient to check his tongue. "Women possess a wisdom men lack," he reasoned gently. "Sleep has never harmed a fractured mind or a shattered heart."

Forty-eight hours later, curiosity became a nuisance, and the landlady struggled to bar the nosing crowds. Eventually, only Fra Colonna and his massive companion, Fra Jerome, were permitted entry. They took over from the women, sitting in silence. Fra Colonna gazed at his youthful friend with tears welling in his eyes, while the giant friar held his beads, staring at Gerard with the same calm, unflinching gaze he had fixed upon the churning sea during their previous confrontation.

Around the sixtieth hour, the landlady nudged Fra Colonna. He glanced over. Gerard's eyelids fluttered open slowly, as if emerging from a gentle doze. He stared blankly, drawing himself upright in the straw-filled bed. A hand rose to his temples, and he gasped—the hair was gone. Yet, seeing Fra Colonna, a smile of pure relief broke across his face. Then, abruptly, that smile froze. His features twisted into a mask of unspeakable agony, a sharp cry tore from his throat, and he buried his face against the pillow.

The landlady, knowing the crushing weight of bereavement, wept silently. Fra Jerome began chanting psalms from his breviary, filling the air with holy sounds. Gerard curled into the corner of the bed, shivering. Fra Colonna approached, whispering with trembling lips that nothing was lost. "The divine Muses are immortal!" he urged, trying to comfort the broken boy. "Transfer your love to them; they will never betray you, unlike mortal flesh." Overcome by emotion, the simple priest hurried away.

Fra Jerome remained. "Young man," he intoned gravely, "the Muses dwell only in the fantasies of pagans and dreamers. The Church alone offers earthly peace and eternal bliss. If the world has deceived you, if passion has rent your heart, the Church opens her arms wide. Consecrate your talents to her!" With those solemn words, he departed before Gerard could respond.

"The Church!" Gerard screamed, springing up and shaking a fist at the retreating friar. "Curses on the Church! Had it not been for her, I would not lie broken here, while she lies cold, frozen, in Holland! Oh, Margaret! My darling! My love! And I ran from you, fleeing the few months you had left! Cruel, cruel monsters! They allowed her to die! Death always leaves a sign, but the blind, selfish fools saw nothing—or cared less. I would have seen it, I who loved her! If I had stayed, I would have saved her, I would have saved her! An idiot! A fool to leave her for a single moment!"

He wept bitterly, a sound of raw, animal grief. Suddenly, fury erupted within him again. "The Church!" he roared, his voice echoing off the walls. "For whose sake was I cast out? May its curses fall upon me and the hypocrites who wear its name to wound my broken spirit! Accursed be the world! Ghysbrecht lives, yet Margaret dies! Thieves, murderers, harlots thrive forever, while only angels perish! Cursed be life! Cursed be death! And cursed be whoever forged them into such monstrous shapes!"

The friar heard no more than the wild yell of rage that chased him from the room, and wisely so. To have heard Gerard's blasphemy would have led to immediate condemnation, perhaps even burning at the stake.

As Fra Colonna descended the stairs, the landlady lingered outside, hearing the raised voice. Returning in alarm, she found Gerard dressing, his countenance distorted by tears. "What use is this lying here?" he muttered, his voice heavy with gloom. "Will I find what I seek within these four walls?"

"Saints protect us! Is he mad again?" she stammered. "What do you seek?"

"Oblivion."

"Oh, child, how young you speak!" she cooed, trying to soothe him. "You are far too young to talk of giving up."

"Young or old, what purpose remains for me to live?" he asked, his eyes empty.

He donned his finest garments, the fabric seeming to strain under the weight of his sorrow. "My pretty Gerard," she warned, growing pale, "know that today is Tuesday, not Sunday."

"Oh, Tuesday?" he mused, his gaze distant. "I thought it were Saturday."

"Nay, you have slept far too long. Never wear your finery on a working day."

"What I did while she lived, I did. Now I shall do whatever I did not do before. The past is dust. Here lies my hair, and with it, my former life. I served one Master faithfully, and behold, this is His reward. Now I shall serve another, and grant Him a fair trial."

"Alas!" she sighed, her voice trembling, "What darkness clouds your words? And who is this new Master whose service you wish to try?"

With that terrible confession hanging on his lips, the wretched boy walked out. His cap and feather sat jauntily askew, framing a face pale as ash and drawn tight by invisible lines of age. His limbs were weak, yet he moved forward, a sinewy shadow fleeing the light of God.
