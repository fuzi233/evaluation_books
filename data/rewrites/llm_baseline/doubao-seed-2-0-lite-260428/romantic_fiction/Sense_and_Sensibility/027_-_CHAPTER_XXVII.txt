“The way this unseasonably soft weather holds on,” Mrs. Jennings chattered over their breakfast table the first full morning the Dashwood sisters spent in her Berkeley Street home, “Sir John will kick up a terrible fuss next week when it’s time to leave Barton. There’s no sorrow like a sportsman’s, wasting a fine clear day indoors! Poor dears, I always pity them, they take it so near to heart.”

Marianne jumped to her feet, voice bright with sudden, unlooked-for joy, and darted to the window to press her palm to the fog-frosted pane. “I never even thought of that! This weather will keep so many of them lingering in the country just a little longer.”

That stray, lucky thought restored every spark of her good humor. She skipped back to the table, cheeks pink with hope. “It is perfect weather for their sport, of course. How they must enjoy it—though,” she added, her tone softening into a quiet, eager edge of anxiety, “it can’t possibly last. This late in the year, after all that rain we had back home? We’re bound to get a sharp frost any day now. Maybe even tonight, this absurd mildness can’t hold on much longer.”

Elinor cut in quickly, hoping to steer Mrs. Jennings’s sharp gaze away from reading Marianne’s secret, unspoken hope that the frost would drive Willoughby straight to London, and straight to her door. “I’ve no doubt Sir John and Lady Middleton will be in town by the end of next week, at any rate.”

“Aye, dear, you may be sure of it,” Mrs. Jennings laughed. “My daughter Mary always gets exactly what she wants.”

Elinor’s own silent guess was that Marianne would dash off a letter to Combe Magne that very afternoon, posted before anyone could catch a glimpse of the address. If she did, the note was written and slipped to the post boy so discreetly that even Elinor’s watchful eye couldn’t confirm it. Still, even with that small uncertainty hanging over her, Elinor could not be miserable when Marianne glowed, half giddy over the soft sun, half trembling with anticipation of the coming frost that would bring the man she loved straight to her.

Most of that first week was spent trundling through London’s streets dropping calling cards at Mrs. Jennings’s friends’ homes to announce her arrival. Marianne never once stopped checking the direction of the wind, craning to read the shifts in the clouds, pressing a cold hand to her cheek to convince herself the air had turned sharper.
“Elinor, don’t you feel how much colder it is than it was this morning?” she would say, hovering over her sister’s shoulder. “My hands are stiff even inside my muff. It wasn’t this sharp yesterday, was it? The clouds are pulling apart too—there’ll be bright, clear frost by afternoon, I just know it.”

Elinor alternated between smiling at her sister’s stubborn, tender optimism and aching for her, in case none of that hope amounted to anything. But Marianne was unshakable: every night’s bright fire glow, every morning’s crisp gray sky, was a sure sign the hard frost was coming, and Willoughby with it.

The sisters could not have asked for a kinder hostess. Mrs. Jennings never made them call on any of her stuffy old city acquaintances that would leave them flustered or ill at ease, her household ran on generous, warm terms, and Elinor soon found herself far more comfortable than she had expected. Their evening parties, whether held at home or out at a friend’s house, were mostly gatherings built around card games, with little to amuse anyone who didn’t care for the tables—but Elinor found far more joy in her daily chats with Colonel Brandon, who had a standing open invitation to the house, and stopped by nearly every day. He came ostensibly to check on Marianne, but he always lingered to talk to Elinor, and she could not miss how his quiet, gentle affection for her sister seemed to grow deeper and more tender by the day. It grieved her to watch him stare at Marianne with that soft, hungry earnestness, and to notice his spirits sinking lower and lower, far heavier than they had ever been back at Barton.

Roughly a week after their arrival, the thing they had both been half waiting for happened. When they trundled back from their morning drive, there was a visiting card laid on the entry table.
“Good God!” Marianne gasped. “He was here while we were out.”

Elinor’s own chest lightened at last, relieved to know Willoughby was actually in London. “Don’t worry,” she said, “he will call again tomorrow, I’m certain of it.” Marianne did not even seem to hear her, and the second Mrs. Jennings stepped into the hall, she snatched the precious card up and ran off to her room to stare at it.

That one small discovery sent Marianne’s already frayed anticipation spiking higher than ever. She could not sit still, could not read, could not sew, every hour of the day spent listening for the knock at the door that would announce him. The next morning, she insisted the others leave her behind when they went out to pay calls, certain Willoughby would come while the house was quiet.

When Elinor and the others returned, one glance at Marianne’s pale, tight face told her everything: Willoughby had not come. A servant walked in a moment later, setting a new note on the table.
“It’s for me!” Marianne cried, lunging forward.
“No, miss, it’s for Mrs. Jennings.”
Marianne snatched it anyway, her face falling when she saw the address. “It really is for her. How infuriating.”
“You were expecting a letter, though, weren’t you?” Elinor asked, unable to hold her tongue any longer.
“Yes, a little—hardly anything at all.” She paused, then lifted her chin to meet Elinor’s gaze. “You don’t trust me, do you?”
“Marianne, how can you say that to me?” Elinor said, flushing. “You are the one who never shares anything.”
“Me?” Marianne fired back. “You never tell anyone a single thing about yourself! We are exactly the same, you see. We both have nothing to hide, but you refuse to speak of what matters to you, and I—well, I never hide a single thing.”
Elinor could not argue. She was bound to keep Lucy Steele’s secret, and could not explain why she was so guarded, so she did not push Marianne for further openness.

Mrs. Jennings read the note aloud when she came in: it was from Lady Middleton, announcing she and Sir John had arrived in Conduit Street the night before, and inviting the three of them and the Dashwood sisters over for dinner the following evening. Sir John had been tied up with hunting business, and Lady Middleton had caught a bad cold, so they had not been able to call first at Berkeley Street.
The invitation was accepted, but Marianne fought going right up until the last minute. She still had not seen Willoughby, and she refused to risk missing his second call by being out of the house. It took Elinor nearly an hour of gentle persuasion to make her agree to come, for the sake of basic politeness to their hostess.

The party at the Middletons’ was as chaotic as Elinor expected. Sir John, barely settled in London, had already rounded up nearly twenty young people and thrown together an impromptu dance. His wife was mortified: back in the country, a last-minute dance was perfectly acceptable, but in London, where everyone was so fussy about their reputation for elegance, it would look terribly unrefined if word got out she had hosted a tiny, informal ball with only two fiddles and a sideboard of food for refreshments.
Mr. and Mrs. Palmer were there too. Mr. Palmer, who had deliberately avoided visiting his mother-in-law at Berkeley Street since their arrival, barely even nodded at the sisters when they walked in, glancing at them for half a second like he had no idea who they were, before tipping a lazy, indifferent salute at Mrs. Jennings from across the room.
Marianne scanned the entire ballroom the second she stepped over the threshold, and one quick look told her Willoughby was not there. She sank onto the nearest unoccupied chair, determined to neither enjoy herself nor speak to a single person.
After an hour or so, Mr. Palmer sauntered over to chat with the sisters, acting surprised to see them in town, even though Colonel Brandon had told him of their arrival ages ago, and he had once made a whole string of crude little jokes about their coming to London.
“I thought you two were still stuck in Devonshire,” he drawled.
“Did you?” Elinor said.
“When are you going back?”
“I have no idea,” she replied, and that was the end of their conversation.

Marianne had never hated dancing more in her life, and never left a party so sore and exhausted. She complained nonstop all the way back to Berkeley Street.
“Aye, I know exactly why you’re so worn out,” Mrs. Jennings said, winking. “If a certain nameless gentleman had turned up, you’d have danced till dawn no problem. And honestly, it was terribly rude of him not to show. He was invited, you know.”
“Invited?” Marianne gasped, going pale.
“Sir John ran right into him on the street this morning, my daughter Middleton told me.”
Marianne said nothing more, but her face was crumpled with hurt so sharp Elinor’s ached for her. She resolved that very minute to write to their mother at Barton first thing the next morning, tell her all her fears that Willoughby was not as sincere as he seemed, beg her to come to London and pull the truth out of Marianne before her heart broke irreparably. Her resolve hardened even more when she saw Marianne hunched over her desk after breakfast the next day, scribbling furiously at a letter that could not possibly be addressed to anyone but Willoughby.

Mrs. Jennings stepped out to run errands halfway through the morning, and Elinor sat down to begin her letter to her mother immediately. Marianne paced restlessly from window to window, too anxious to sit still, too raw to make conversation, before sinking down to sit huddled by the fire, lost in dark, quiet thought.
Elinor had barely gotten her letter half written when the front door knocker rapped, and the servant announced Colonel Brandon. Marianne, who had seen him coming up the steps through the window, hated the thought of making polite small talk with a guest just then, and slipped out of the room before he even crossed the threshold.
He looked far graver than usual, and even though he said he was glad to find Elinor alone, as if he had something very private to tell her, he sat twisting his gloves for several long minutes without speaking. Elinor was certain he had come to talk about Marianne, and waited patiently for him to begin.
At last he lifted his head, his voice thick with quiet agitation.
“When shall I congratulate you, Miss Dashwood, on gaining Mr. Willoughby as a brother?”
Elinor was completely taken aback. She had no answer ready, so all she could do was ask him what he meant.
He tried to force a smile, but it was wobbly. “Your sister’s engagement to Mr. Willoughby is common knowledge around town. Everyone talks of their upcoming marriage.”
“It can’t possibly be common knowledge,” Elinor said, surprised. “Her own family does not know it is settled.”
He looked shocked. “I beg your pardon, I did not mean to be impertinent. But I had no idea there was any secret kept! They correspond openly, and every soul in our circle—Mrs. Jennings, the Middletons, Mrs. Palmer—all speak of it as a done deal. I might still have doubted it, if I had not walked in just now and seen your sister’s handwriting on the letter the servant was carrying to post for Mr. Willoughby. I came to ask you, to hear it from someone I trust, that it is all settled. That there is no chance for… that there is nothing left for me to hope for.”
His quiet, unguarded admission of his love for Marianne hit Elinor like a soft, heavy weight. She could not speak for a long moment, and when she did, she knew she must be kind, even if she did not know the full truth of the situation. She told him that while neither Marianne nor Willoughby had ever formally announced an engagement, she had no doubt of their deep mutual affection, and that their correspondence came as no surprise at all.
He listened to her in silence, and the second she finished speaking, he stood up quickly, his face pale with grief.
“Then I wish your sister every happiness in the world,” he said, his voice shaking. “And I wish Willoughby the strength to deserve her.”
He tipped his hat, and left without another word.
Elinor sat staring at the half-written letter on her desk, no comfort at all in what had just passed. She was left with the heavy, sad weight of Colonel Brandon’s broken heart hanging over her, and her fear for Marianne’s future sharpened to a point. She could not even bring herself to wish for the pain of Brandon’s unrequited love to be lifted, not when every turn of events seemed to be leading straight towards the heartbreak that would confirm it forever.
