The rain lashed against the leaded glass of the library windows, a rhythmic drumming that felt, to Margaret Hale, like the frantic, indifferent heartbeat of a city that refused to acknowledge the quiet suffocation of its women. She stood before the wash-tub, the heavy scent of wet wool and soap filling the narrow kitchen, her gaze fixed upon the pile of muslin waiting to be starched. It was an absurdity, this domestic theater staged in the shadow of a grand social maneuver.

Her father had announced the invitation with a nervous tremor, a physical manifestation of the very anxiety he tried to mask by inspecting the dust motes dancing in the sunlight. *"My dear! I've asked Mr. Thornton to come to tea tonight."* The words hung in the air, heavy with implication. Mrs. Hale, usually a figure of serene domesticity, recoiled as if struck. *"Mr. Thornton! And tonight! What in the world does the man want here?"* she cried, her voice rising in querulous protest. *"And Dixon is washing my muslins, and there is no soft water with these horrid east winds!"*

Margaret watched her mother's distress with a mixture of pity and exasperation. She understood the mechanics of the complaint better than anyone; she knew how the wind could twist a woman's mood until it snapped. Yet, beneath the surface of her mother's panic, there lay a deeper, more insidious fear: the fear of the unknown guest, the merchant whose presence threatened to disrupt the fragile equilibrium of their genteel existence.

*"The wind is veering round, my dear,"* Mr. Hale ventured, peering out at the grey sky, arranging the cardinal points of the compass with the casual confidence of a man who believed the weather obeyed his whims.

*"Don't tell me!"* Mrs. Hale shuddered, wrapping her shawl tighter around her shoulders as if to ward off an invading chill. *"But east or west, I suppose this man comes."*

Margaret stepped forward, her movements efficient despite the fatigue gnawing at her bones. *"Oh, mamma, that shows you never saw Mr. Thornton. He looks like a person who would enjoy battling with every adverse thing he could meet with—enemies, winds, or circumstances. The more it rains and blows, the more certain we are to have him."* She spoke with the conviction of someone who had already mapped the terrain of his soul, seeing in his stoicism a reflection of her own fierce independence. *"But I'll go and help Dixon. I'm getting to be a famous clear-starcher. And he won't want any amusement beyond talking to Papa. Papa, I am really longing to see the Pythias to your Damon. You know I never saw him but once, and then we were so puzzled to know what to say to each other that we did not get on particularly well."*

Her father smiled, a genuine warmth breaking through his apprehension. *"I don't know that you would ever like him, or think him agreeable, Margaret. He is not a lady's man."*

Margaret wreathed her throat in a scornful curve, a gesture that betrayed both her youth and her growing defiance. *"I don't particularly admire ladies' men, papa. But Mr. Thornton comes here as your friend—as one who has appreciated you."*

*"The only person in Milton,"* Mrs. Hale muttered, her skepticism a shield against the intrusion of the new world. *"So we will give him a welcome, and some cocoa-nut cakes. Dixon will be flattered if we ask her to make some; and I will undertake to iron your caps, mamma."*

Many times that morning, Margaret had wished Mr. Thornton far away. She had envisioned other employments: a letter to Edith, a passage of Dante, a visit to the Higginses. Instead, she ironed away, listening to Dixon's complaints, suppressing the urge to let her sorrow spill over onto her mother's nerves. Every now and then, she had to remind herself of her father's regard for the stranger, to subdue the irritation of weariness that was stealing over her, threatening to bring on one of the bad headaches to which she had lately become liable.

When she finally sat down to tell her mother that she was no longer Peggy the laundry-maid but Margaret Hale the lady, she intended it as a jest, a playful assertion of her dual identity. But her mother took it seriously, her face clouding with the weight of lost status. *"Yes! If any one had told me, when I was Miss Beresford, and one of the belles of the country, that a child of mine would have to stand half a day in a little poky kitchen, working away like any servant, that we might prepare properly for the reception of a tradesman..."*

*"Oh, mamma!"* Margaret interrupted, lifting herself up with sudden energy. *"Don't punish me for so careless a speech. I don't mind ironing, or any kind of work for you and papa. I am myself a born and bred lady through it all, even though it comes to scouring a floor, or washing dishes. I am tired now, just for a little while; but in half an hour I shall be ready to do the same over again. And as to Mr. Thornton's being in trade, why he can't help that now, poor fellow. I don't suppose his education would fit him for much else."*

With that declaration, she retreated to her own room, unable to bear the friction between her mother's nostalgia and the harsh realities of the present.

In Mr. Thornton's house, at the very same moment, a parallel drama unfolded in a dining room that screamed of wealth and rigidity. A large-boned lady, long past middle age, sat at work amidst the grim grandeur of the space. Her features, like her frame, were strong and massive, carved from stone rather than flesh. Her face moved slowly from one decided expression to another equally determined, lacking the fluidity of emotion that characterized the softer sex. Those who looked at her once generally looked twice; even passersby in the street halted to gaze an instant longer at the firm, severe, dignified woman who never yielded in courtesy nor paused in her straight-onward course toward the clearly defined end she proposed.

She was handsomely dressed in stout black silk, not a thread worn or discolored, a testament to a discipline that bordered on the ascetic. She mended a large, long tablecloth of the finest texture, holding it up against the light to discover thin places requiring her delicate care. There was not a book in the room save for Matthew Henry's Bible commentaries, six volumes lying in the center of the massive sideboard, flanked by a tea-urn and a lamp. In a remote apartment, the clatter of a piano filled the air. Someone was practicing a *morceau de salon*, playing it with reckless speed, every third note on an average either indistinct or wholly missed, the loud chords at the end half of them false, yet perfectly satisfactory to the performer.

Mrs. Thornton heard a step, decisive and familiar, pass the dining-room door. *"John! Is that you?"*

Her son opened the door, revealing himself. *"What has brought you home so early? I thought you were going to tea with that friend of Mr. Bell's; that Mr. Hale?"*

*"Dress! Humph! When I was a girl, young men were satisfied with dressing once in a day. Why should you dress to go and take a cup of tea with an old parson?"*

*"I am not easily caught, mother, as I think you know. But I must not have Miss Hale spoken of in that way, which, you know, is offensive to me. I never was aware of any young lady trying to catch me yet, nor do I believe that any one has ever given themselves that useless trouble."*

Mrs. Thornton did not choose to yield the point to her son; pride, that ancient armor of her sex, held her fast. *"Well! I only say, take care. Perhaps our Milton girls have too much spirit and good feeling to go angling after husbands; but this Miss Hale comes out of the aristocratic counties, where, if all tales be true, rich husbands are reckoned prizes."*

Mr. Thornton's brow contracted, and he came a step forward into the room. *"Mother"* (with a short, scornful laugh), *"you will make me confess. The only time I saw Miss Hale, she treated me with a haughty civility which had a strong flavour of contempt in it. She held herself aloof from me as if she had been a queen, and I her humble, unwashed vassal. Be easy, mother."*

*"No! I am not easy nor content either. What business had she, a renegade clergyman's daughter, to turn up her nose at you! I would dress for none of them—a saucy set! if I were you."*
