The twilight air clung to the Cathedral Close like a damp shroud. Mr. Sapsea strolled through the misty veil, his footsteps echoing off the stone buildings. His hands were clasped behind his back, and his eyes scanned the shadows for the telltale sign of a blushing stranger. He had grown accustomed to playing the benevolent landlord, bestowing his patronage upon the residents of the Close, and Mrs. Sapsea's prize-winning garden was a testament to his generosity. As he walked, the faint scent of wet stone and moss filled his nostrils, a familiar comfort that reminded him of his place within the community.

Mr. Sapsea's thoughts turned to his new role as Mayor of Cloisterham. He took pride in being a pillar of society, and his elevation to office had only reinforced his conviction that mayors were the backbone of civilization. The image of himself rising to the occasion, knighted for his service, was a recurring fantasy that brought a smile to his face. His mind wandered to his friend, Mr. Jasper, who had recently become a regular visitor to the Close. Their conversations over port and backgammon had deepened their bond, and Mr. Sapsea admired Jasper's willingness to learn from his elders and his innate sense of propriety.

As he turned a corner, Mr. Sapsea caught sight of the Dean, engaged in conversation with the Verger and Mr. Jasper. He approached with a measured pace, his hands still clasped behind his back. The Dean's words hung in the air, and Mr. Sapsea felt a flutter of excitement – was it possible that Mr. Jasper was planning to write a book about the Close? He hastened his pace, eager to offer his assistance and bask in the reflected glory of his friend's supposed literary endeavors.

"Ah, Mr. Jasper," he said, his voice dripping with courtesy, "I couldn't help but overhear your conversation with the Dean. A book about us, you say? How thrilling!" He beamed at the prospect, his imagination running wild with visions of himself as a celebrated subject.

Mr. Jasper smiled politely, his eyes crinkling at the corners. "Oh, merely a whim, Mr. Sapsea. I'm afraid I have no intention of putting quill to paper."
