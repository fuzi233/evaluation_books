Of course, she knew he would marry Shirley.

The thought rose with Caroline as naturally as breath when she opened her eyes to the pale morning; it lay upon her heart as steadily as the counterpane upon her knees. He would marry her; he ought. Shirley had means, land, consequence—hands that could reach where his could not; a name that could stand between him and ruin as a stout wall stands between a hearth and the wind.

“She can help him,” Caroline said aloud, to give the sentence the ring of reason, to make it sound like duty and not like pain.

And then, with a sharpness that made her sit up, came the next truth, cruel as iron laid on a tongue: when they were married, she herself would be—nothing.

Forgotten; not maliciously, perhaps, not with deliberate unkindness, but softly, inevitably; as yesterday’s bread is pushed aside when the new loaf comes warm and fragrant to table. Where should she turn then? She had no art of making herself necessary; she had only the dumb, clinging fidelity that asks to be cherished and is ashamed of asking. To call him hers even in thought seemed presumption. She was poverty and incapacity; Shirley was wealth and power—and beauty too, and the right to be ardently loved.

Caroline could not summon a single honest objection. It was not a bargain, not a sordid traffic; Shirley’s feeling, whatever name it might yet bear, had nobleness in it. She would love—nay, she already loved—in a way that would make any man proud to be loved. Let it be so, then.

But afterwards—afterwards she could not stay.

That hollow comfort people offer—“you will still be his cousin, his sister in all but name”—she despised. She would be all or nothing to a man like Robert; she could not hover about the edges of his happiness, smiling with a hypocrite’s mouth while her heart was wrung. She could not take the place of friend without wounding herself, nor of foe without degrading him. She must either cleave to him as if she were part of him, or be sundered utterly—wide as the poles.

“Sunder me then, Providence,” she pleaded in her mind, with a desperation that was still prayer. “Part us speedily.”

Late that afternoon, while these thoughts worked like a hidden fever, the very form that haunted them passed the parlour window—Shirley Keeldar, strolling by with that air of careless wistfulness which, when she was quiet, seemed native to her as the scent is native to a crushed leaf. When she was animated, the careless part vanished; the wistfulness took a brighter tincture, and her mirth, when it came, never sounded thin or crackling, but full and flavoured, like something warmed through at the hearth.

She came in without waiting to be invited.

“What do you mean by not coming to see me this afternoon, as you promised?” were her first words.

“I was not in the humour,” Caroline answered; and because it was true, it was plain.

Shirley’s eye, quick as a blade, fixed on her.

“No,” she said. “I see you are not in the humour for loving me. You are in one of your sunless, inclement moods—when one feels a fellow-creature’s presence is not welcome. You have such moods. Are you aware of it?”

“Do you mean to stay long, Shirley?”

“Yes. I am come to have my tea, and must have it before I go. I shall take the liberty, then, of removing my bonnet, without being asked.”

She did so, and stood on the rug with her hands clasped behind her, regarding Caroline as if she would read her to the last line.

“A pretty expression you have,” she went on, not unkindly, rather with a sort of pity that pricked. “Wonderfully self-supported you look—you solitude-seeking wounded deer. Are you afraid Shirley will worry you if she discovers that you are hurt, and that you bleed?”

“I never do fear Shirley.”

“But sometimes you dislike her; often you avoid her. Shirley can feel when she is slighted and shunned. If you had not walked home in the company you did last night, you would have been a different girl to-day. What time did you reach the rectory?”

“By ten.”

“Humph! You took three-quarters of an hour to walk a mile. Was it you, or Moore, who lingered so?”

“Shirley, you talk nonsense.”

“He talked nonsense—that I doubt not; or he looked it, which is a thousand times worse. I see the reflection of his eyes on your forehead at this moment. I feel disposed to call him out, if I could only get a trustworthy second. I feel desperately irritated. I felt so last night, and have felt it all day.”

Caroline’s colour stirred, partly from shame, partly from the pain of hearing Robert’s name flung about as if it were a plaything.

“You don’t ask me why,” Shirley pursued after a pause. “You little silent, over-modest thing; and you don’t deserve that I should pour out my secrets into your lap without an invitation. Upon my word, I could have found it in my heart to have dogged Moore yesterday evening with dire intent. I have pistols, and can use them.”

“Stuff, Shirley! Which would you have shot—me or Robert?”

“Neither, perhaps. Perhaps myself—more likely a bat or a tree-bough. He is a puppy, your cousin—a quiet, serious, sensible, judicious, ambitious puppy. I see him standing before me, talking his half-stern, half-gentle talk, bearing me down—as I am very conscious he does—with his fixity of purpose; and then—I have no patience with him!”

She began to pace the room rapidly, repeating that she had no patience with men in general and with her tenant in particular; her dress brushed the furniture as a gust brushes leaves. Caroline, watching her, felt that the house itself was changed by her presence: it was not only sight, but sound—Shirley’s step, Shirley’s breath, Shirley’s quick, impatient turnings—made an atmosphere; and Caroline’s own troubled silence seemed to listen to it as much as to words.

“You are mistaken,” she urged, anxious in spite of herself. “Robert is no puppy, no male flirt; I can vouch for that.”

“You vouch for it!” Shirley wheeled round. “Do you think I’ll take your word on the subject? There is no one’s testimony I would not credit sooner than yours. To advance Moore’s fortune you would cut off your right hand.”

“But not tell lies. And if I speak the truth, I must assure you that he was just civil to me last night—that was all.”

“I never asked what he was. I can guess. I saw him from the window take your hand in his long fingers, just as he went out at my gate.”

“That is nothing. I am not a stranger. I am an old acquaintance—and his cousin.”

“I feel indignant, and that is the long and short of the matter,” Shirley said. “All my comfort is broken up by his manoeuvres. He keeps intruding between you and me. Without him we should be good friends; but that six feet of puppyhood makes a perpetually recurring eclipse of our friendship. Again and again he crosses and obscures the disc I want always to see clear; ever and anon he renders me to you a mere bore and nuisance.”

“No, Shirley, no.”

“He does. You did not want my society this afternoon, and I feel it hard. You are naturally somewhat reserved; but I am a social personage who cannot live alone. If we were but left unmolested, I have that regard for you that I could bear you in my presence for ever, and not for the fraction of a second do I ever wish to be rid of you. You cannot say as much respecting me.”

“Shirley, I can say anything you wish. Shirley, I like you.”

“You will wish me at Jericho to-morrow, Lina.”

“I shall not. I am every day growing more accustomed to—fonder of you. You know I am too English to get up a vehement friendship all at once; but you are so much better than common—you are so different to every-day young ladies—I esteem you, I value you; you are never a burden to me—never. Do you believe what I say?”

“Partly,” Shirley replied, smiling, still a little incredulous. “But you are a peculiar personage. Quiet as you look, there is both a force and a depth somewhere within, not easily reached or appreciated. Then you certainly are not happy.”

“And unhappy people are rarely good. Is that what you mean?”

“Not at all. I mean rather that unhappy people are often preoccupied, and not in the mood for discoursing with companions of my nature. Moreover, there is a sort of unhappiness which not only depresses, but corrodes; and that, I fear, is your portion. Will pity do you any good, Lina? If it will, take some from Shirley; she offers largely, and warrants the article genuine.”

Something in Caroline, long pinched and silent, softened like butter set near warmth. The house was still; through the window there came the country’s accustomed sounds—the faint call of a bird, a cart far off on the lane—clean, living noises that belonged to the old fields and hedgerows. Industrial rumour, when it came, came harshly and made the heart shrink; but these were the gentler voices that let a mind breathe.

“Shirley,” she said, and the word shook, “I never had a sister—you never had a sister; but it flashes on me at this moment how sisters feel towards each other—affection twined with their life, which no shocks of feeling can uproot; which little quarrels only trample an instant, that it may spring more freshly when the pressure is removed; affection that no passion can ultimately outrival, with which even love itself cannot do more than compete in force and truth. Love hurts us so, Shirley. It is so tormenting, so racking; it burns away our strength with its flame. In affection is no pain and no fire—only sustenance and balm. I am supported and soothed when you—that is, you only—are near, Shirley. Do you believe me now?”

Shirley’s eyes altered; the irritability in them gave way to a pleased softness.

“I am always easy of belief when the creed pleases me. We really are friends, then, Lina, in spite of the black eclipse?”

“We really are,” Caroline answered, drawing her down to sit beside her, and holding her as if she feared to lose the comfort of that nearness. “Chance what may.”

“Come, then; we will talk of something else than the Troubler.”

But at that moment the rector entered; his presence, like a draught of colder air, changed the tone of the room. Whatever Shirley had meant to speak of next was checked. Conversation took a safer course till, when evening brought Shirley’s departure, she detained Caroline a few minutes in the passage, away from other ears.

“Caroline,” she said in a lower voice, “I wish to tell you that I have a great weight on my mind; my conscience is quite uneasy—as if I had committed, or was going to commit, a crime. It is not my private conscience, you must understand, but my landed-proprietor and lord-of-the-manor conscience. I have got into the clutch of an eagle with iron talons. I have fallen under a stern influence which I scarcely approve, but cannot resist. Something will be done ere long, I fear, which it by no means pleases me to think of. To ease my mind, and to prevent harm as far as I can, I mean to enter on a series of good works. Don’t be surprised, therefore, if you see me all at once turn outrageously charitable. I have no idea how to begin, but you must give me some advice. We will talk more on the subject to-morrow; and just ask that excellent person, Miss Ainley, to step up to Fieldhead. I have some notion of putting myself under her tuition. Won’t she have a precious pupil? Drop a hint to her, Lina, that, though a well-meaning, I am rather a neglected character; and then she will feel less scandalized at my ignorance about clothing societies and such things.”

On the morrow Caroline found Shirley at a desk, grave as a little clerk of fate, with an account-book spread open, a bundle of bank-notes laid straight as ribbon, and a purse so well-filled it seemed to hump its own leather with importance. She looked serious—yet not at ease; her brow was knit, not from stinginess, but from perplexity.

“I have been casting an eye over the weekly expenditure in housekeeping,” she announced, tapping the account-book as if it were a witness that must answer. “Trying to find out where I could retrench. And I have just given audience to Mrs. Gill, the cook, and sent her away with a notion that my brain is certainly crazed. I have lectured her on the duty of being careful—in a way quite new to her. So eloquent was I on the text of economy that I surprised myself; for you see, it is altogether a fresh idea. I never thought, much less spoke, on the subject till lately.”

Shirley’s nostrils quivered, as if the very smell of kitchen plenty rose between her and her resolutions: butter, dripping, meat, candles, soap—commodities that had their own domestic tyranny, their own soft insistence.

“But it is all theory,” she continued, “for when I came to the practical part I could retrench nothing. I had not firmness to take off a single pound of butter, or to prosecute to any clear result an inquest into the destiny of either dripping, lard, bread, cold meat, or other kitchen perquisite whatever. I know we never get up illuminations at Fieldhead, but I could not ask the meaning of sundry quite unaccountable pounds of candles. We do not wash for the parish; yet I viewed in silence items of soap and bleaching-powder calculated to satisfy the solicitude of the most anxious inquirer after our position in reference to those articles. Carnivorous I am not, nor is Mrs. Pryor, nor is Mrs. Gill herself, yet I only hemmed and opened my eyes a little wide when I saw butchers’ bills whose figures seemed to prove that fact—falsehood, I mean.”

She half laughed, then checked herself, ashamed of laughing when she meant to be stern.

“Caroline, you may laugh at me, but you can’t change me. I am a poltroon on certain points; I feel it. There is a base alloy of moral cowardice in my composition. I blushed and hung my head before Mrs. Gill, when she ought to have been faltering confessions to me. I found it impossible to get up the spirit even to hint, much less to prove, to her that she was a cheat. I have no calm dignity, no true courage about me.”

“Shirley, what fit of self-injustice is this?” Caroline protested. “My uncle—who is not given to speak well of women—says there are not ten thousand men in England as genuinely fearless as you.”

“I am fearless, physically. I am never nervous about danger. I was not startled from self-possession when Mr. Wynne’s great red bull rose with a bellow before my face, as I was crossing the cowslip lea alone—stooped his begrimed, sullen head, and made a run at me; but I was afraid of seeing Mrs. Gill brought to shame and confusion of face. You have twice—ten times—my strength of mind on certain subjects, Caroline. You, whom no persuasion can induce to pass a bull, however quiet he looks, would have firmly shown my housekeeper she had done wrong; then you would have gently and wisely admonished her; and at last, I dare say—provided she had seemed penitent—you would have very sweetly forgiven her. Of this conduct I am incapable.”

She pushed the account-book away with a gesture that dismissed her own weakness.

“However, in spite of exaggerated imposition, I still find we live within our means. I have money in hand; and I really must do some good with it. The Briarfield poor are badly off; they must be helped. What ought I to do, think you, Lina? Had I not better distribute the cash at once?”

“No, indeed, Shirley. You will not manage properly. I have often noticed that your only notion of charity is to give shillings and half-crowns in a careless, free-handed sort of way, which is liable to continual abuse. You must have a prime minister, or you will get yourself into a series of scrapes. You suggested Miss Ainley yourself; to Miss Ainley I will apply. And, meantime, promise to keep quiet, and not begin throwing away your money.”

Shirley glanced at the notes and the purse as if they were both a burden and a temptation.

“What a great deal you have, Shirley! You must feel very rich with all that?”

“Yes; I feel of consequence. It is not an immense sum, but I feel responsible for its disposal; and really this responsibility weighs on my mind more heavily than I could have expected. They say that there are some families almost starving to death in Briarfield. Some of my own cottagers are in wretched circumstances. I must and will help them.”

“Some people say we shouldn’t give alms to the poor, Shirley.”

“They are great fools for their pains. For those who are not hungry, it is easy to palaver about the degradation of charity, and so on; but they forget the brevity of life, as well as its bitterness. We have none of us long to live. Let us help each other through seasons of want and woe as well as we can, without heeding in the least the scruples of vain philosophy.”

“But you do help others, Shirley. You give a great deal as it is.”

“Not enough. I must give more, or, I tell you, my brother’s blood will some day be crying to Heaven against me. For, after all, if political incendiaries come here to kindle conflagration in the neighbourhood, and my property is attacked, I shall defend it like a tigress—I know I shall. Let me listen to Mercy as long as she is near me. Her voice once drowned by the shout of ruffian defiance, and I shall be full of impulses to resist and quell.”

Caroline caught in that word—voice—because she too knew what it was to be altered by sound: how harsh cries could unmake mercy, how a clamour could stampede thought. Shirley’s imagination was already hearing the mob as if it had gathered under her windows.

“If once the poor gather and rise in the form of the mob,” Shirley went on, “I shall turn against them as an aristocrat; if they bully me, I must defy; if they attack, I must resist—and I will.”

“You talk like Robert.”

“I feel like Robert, only more fierily. Let them meddle with Robert, or Robert’s mill, or Robert’s interests, and I shall hate them. At present I am no patrician, nor do I regard the poor around me as plebeians; but if once they violently wrong me or mine, and then presume to dictate to us, I shall quite forget pity for their wretchedness and respect for their poverty, in scorn of their ignorance and wrath at their insolence.”

“Shirley, how your eyes flash!”

“Because my soul burns. Would you, any more than me, let Robert be borne down by numbers?”

Caroline’s answer was low, but it had in it a steadiness that surprised herself—like a quiet hearth that keeps its heat though no flame shows.

“If I had your power to aid Robert, I would use it as you mean to use it. If I could be such a friend to him as you can be, I would stand by him, as you mean to stand by him, till death.”

“And now, Lina,” Shirley said, softened and yet keenly observant, “though your eyes don’t flash, they glow. You drop your lids; but I saw a kindled spark. However, it is not yet come to fighting. What I want to do is to prevent mischief. I cannot forget, either day or night, that these embittered feelings of the poor against the rich have been generated in suffering: they would neither hate nor envy us if they did not deem us so much happier than themselves. To allay this suffering, and thereby lessen this hate, let me, out of my abundance, give abundantly; and that the donation may go farther, let it be made wisely. To that intent, we must introduce some clear, calm, practical sense into our councils. So go and fetch Miss Ainley.”

Without another word Caroline put on her bonnet and departed.

It might seem strange that neither she nor Shirley thought of consulting Mrs. Pryor; but they abstained wisely. To involve Mrs. Pryor would be to draw her into an embarrassment that would pain her without profiting the plan. She was better informed, more read, a deeper thinker than Miss Ainley; but of administrative energy—of executive activity—she had none. Secret almsgiving suited her; she would subscribe her modest mite willingly; but public plans, large schemes, councils and conferences—these would have only reminded her of a deficiency she already knew too well. Shirley understood this by instinct, and spared her.

Miss Ainley, when summoned to Fieldhead to deliberate on projects so entirely her own element, had a day bright as harvest to her spirit. She sat with honour and deference at a table furnished with paper, pen, ink—and, best of all, money—requested to draw up a regular plan for administering relief to the destitute poor of Briarfield. She knew them all: knew whose cupboard held only dry meal, whose children went to bed with hollow bellies, whose fire had gone out for want of coal, whose sickness had eaten the little strength their bodies owned. She had long studied, with a quiet perseverance, what would succour them best if ever succour could be had.

Now, as she answered the eager questions of the two young ladies, her knowledge came out clear and serviceable; and a meek exultation, the purest kind of pride, warmed her as she found herself at last able not only to pity but to act. When Shirley placed at her disposal three hundred pounds, Miss Ainley’s eyes filled with joyful tears; for she saw, as if already, the hungry fed, the naked clothed, the sick visited and comforted. Her fingers trembled over the notes, not with greed, but with reverence, as if they were a new language in which mercy could speak.

She drew up, quickly and sensibly, a simple plan for expenditure; and she assured them brighter times would now come round, for she doubted not the lady of Fieldhead’s example would be followed. She would try to get additional subscriptions, to form a fund. But first—the clergy must be consulted. On that point she was peremptory. Mr. Helstone, Dr. Boultby, Mr. Hall must be consulted; not only Briarfield must be relieved, but Whinbury and Nunnely also. It would be presumption in her, she said, to take a single step unauthorized by them.

In Miss Ainley’s eyes the clergy were sacred beings. The individual might be small; the office made him holy. Even the curates—trivial, arrogant, absurd as they could be—she viewed, in her sincere enthusiasm, as sucking saints. Point out their little vices and huge follies, and still she could not see them; the white surplice covered a multitude of sins. Her faith in their station was a kind of dialect she could not unlearn: words like “reverence” and “authority” sounded to her with meanings that would not translate into any colder tongue.

Shirley, knowing this harmless infatuation in her newly chosen minister, stipulated expressly that the curates were to have no voice in disposing of the money—that their meddling fingers were not to be inserted into the pie. The rectors, of course, were paramount and might be trusted: they had experience, some sagacity; and Mr. Hall at least had sympathy and loving-kindness for his fellow-men. But the youths under them must be set aside, kept down, and taught that subordination and silence best became their years and capacity.

Miss Ainley heard such language with horror; her very hands lifted a little, as if to ward off disrespect. Caroline, with a mild word or two in praise of Mr. Sweeting, calmed her. Sweeting was indeed her own favourite. The slices of sponge-cake and glasses of cowslip or primrose wine she had at different times administered to him, when he came to see her in her little cottage, had been offered with sentiments of truly motherly regard—simple fare, but made precious by the kindness steeped into it, and by the remembered scent of spring hedges in the drink.

The same innocuous collation she had once presented to Malone; but that personage had shown such open scorn of the offering she had never ventured to renew it. To Donne she always served the treat, and was happy to see his approbation proved beyond doubt by the fact of his usually eating two pieces of cake and putting a third in his pocket.

Indefatigable where good was to be done, Miss Ainley would immediately have set out on a walk of ten miles round to the three rectors, to show her plan and humbly solicit approval. Miss Keeldar interdicted that at once. No; she proposed, instead, to collect the clergy in a small select reunion that evening at Fieldhead. Miss Ainley was to meet them there; the plan should be discussed in full privy council.

Shirley managed accordingly to get the senior priesthood together; and before the old maid arrived, she had further talked all the gentlemen into the most charming mood imaginable. She herself took in hand Dr. Boultby and Mr. Helstone. The first was a stubborn old Welshman—hot, opinionated, obstinate—yet one who did a great deal of good, though not without making some noise about it. The second was Helstone, as he always was. Shirley had a friendly feeling for both, especially for old Helstone, and it cost her no effort to be delightful: she took them round the garden, gathered them flowers, spoke to them like a kind daughter. The air among the beds held the clean, bruised scents of leaf and bloom; their steps on the gravel made a homely music under the open sky.

Mr. Hall she left to Caroline—or rather, it was to Caroline’s care that Mr. Hall consigned himself.
