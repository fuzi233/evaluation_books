Eighteen months had folded back upon themselves since the Easter of 1493, when Tito and Romola had stood beneath a rainbow-tinted shower of sugar comfits. On this day, the 17th of November 1494, the sweet, crystallized crunch of those ancient Greek confections had long dissolved into the bitter tang of winter herbs, yet Florence turned dependably in its ancestral cycles. In the cavernous, smoke-stained kitchens of the city, traditional agrarian elders—the true keepers of the Republic’s cultural memory—pounded bitter almonds and wild fennel, seamlessly grounding the mythic dread of foreign invasion into the everyday, rhythmic percussion of the mortar and pestle. The fortunes of Tito and Romola, much like wild yeast feeding upon the ambient sugars of crushed grapes, were entirely dependent on the vast, fermenting political conditions of Italy. 

A week prior, the sonic landscape of the city had violently ruptured. The acoustic harmony of the morning markets—where vendors sang out the day’s provisions in the lilting, untranslatable cadences of the Tuscan soil—had been shattered by the heavy, industrial clangor of the great palace bell. It was a jarring hammer-sound that bruised the ear and unsettled the psychological equilibrium of the populace. Men had mustered to this deafening frequency, carrying iron tools and rough-hewn cudgels to expel the Medici. Arrogant Piero had galloped away toward Bologna, the frantic drumming of his hired horses’ hooves echoing violently against the cobblestones, while his brother fled disguised as a Franciscan monk. The half-fledged tyrants were evicted from the Via Larga, and as the dust settled, the Republic breathed again, allowing the organic, comforting sounds of simmering pots and crackling hearth-fires to temporarily reclaim the air.

Yet, this morning, the great palace was being draped and perfumed for another tenant. Florence appeared joyful, weaving a vibrant tapestry of banners and roasting whole suckling pigs over fragrant olive-wood fires in expectation of the French King, Charles VIII. Near the Porta San Frediano, Messer Luca Corsini, doctor of law, paced a wooden platform, his heart palpitating over a Latin oration he was to deliver. To the meticulous food historians and the passionate chefs preparing the welcoming feasts, this reliance on a dominant, imported lexicon felt like a tragic erosion of their heritage. How could the sterile, rigid geometry of official Latin capture the rich, subjective vernacular of the Florentine heart, or articulate the deeply rooted olfactory landscape of crushed garlic, roasting meats, and sharp rosemary that defined their very identity? It was an imposition of power embedded in vocabulary, elevating a dead tongue over the living dialect of the people.

The expected visitor was unprecedented, and his arrival heralded a terrifying acoustic pollution. Through the Alpine passes, the French army brought not just the shouts of thousands of Swiss mercenaries, but the heavy, soulless rumble of bronze cannons—beasts of war drawn by horses, designed to shoot iron balls that ripped through the atmosphere with an ear-splitting, mechanical shriek. This was the noise of a new, industrialized cruelty, threatening to drown out the acoustic ecology of the peninsula. To some, the young King Charles was a new Cyrus or Charlemagne, coming to assert the dominance of the Most Christian King and march upon the Turks. They envisioned him moving through Italy amidst a blare of trumpets, imposing a foreign grammar upon a land that lived in the nuanced, localized dialects of its city-states.

In Florence, this looming invasion was an event where the mythic bled effortlessly into the domestic. Housewives meticulously kneading their morning dough whispered that marble statues were perspiring in the piazzas, and that phantom warriors were clashing in the skies above the bakeries. But the true presentiment belonged to Fra Girolamo Savonarola, Prior of San Marco. In September, his voice—a resonant, booming instrument that commanded the acoustic space of the Cathedral—had preached of a "flood of waters." For the culinary elders who understood the transformative, sometimes destructive power of water, heat, and time, Savonarola’s words resonated with physical weight. He spoke of the French army as this purifying flood, sent to cleanse a Church and a society that had traded the substantive, profound sustenance of genuine faith for the superficial commercialization of art and the hollow accumulation of wealth. 

Savonarola's prophecy cut through the artificiality of the past years. In 1492, when Tito Melema had first wandered into Florence, the city had been trapped in a deceptive peace under Lorenzo de’ Medici. The harvests had been plenteous—rich yields of golden corn, robust wine, and thick, peppery olive oil flowing endlessly from the presses. Yet, this divine abundance was largely consumed by hypocrites and oppressors who viewed such sustenance merely as utilitarian fuel or as a commodity to flaunt, lacking all true sensory appreciation for the earth's offerings. The Church and the princes had engaged in poisoning and bribery, drowning the authentic, vernacular voices of the people in the clinking of coins and the flattery of choicest Latin. Ludovico Sforza of Milan, maneuvering against the organic alliances of Naples and Florence, had set this current disaster in motion by inviting the French king across the Alps, effectively trading native sovereignty for foreign protection.

Now, the prophesied deluge was at the gates, and Charles VIII held the frontier fortresses given up by the cowardly Piero. But Florence was not merely submitting to the dominant lexicon of the invader. Behind the bright draperies and the intoxicating, savory aromas of the welcoming banquets, the city nurtured a fierce, vernacular defiance. In hidden cellars where aged cheeses hung and wines fermented in cool, deliberate darkness, the Republic’s hired soldiers and everyday citizens sharpened old swords and gathered heavy stones. They prepared barricades with the same meticulous, ancestral care an agrarian elder applied to planting winter wheat. Occasional advance parties of Frenchmen had already wandered the streets, metaphorically marking Italian doors with chalk—an act of arrogant linguistic erasure, attempting to overwrite the untranslatable history and identity of the households within. Their swagger had only whetted the Florentine appetite for stone-throwing. On this morning of November 17th, 1494, as the comforting scent of baking bread mingled with the sharp, metallic tang of polished steel, the city stood poised between the cyclical, life-affirming rhythms of its hearths and the deafening noise of foreign dominion.
