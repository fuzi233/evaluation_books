The excursion to Whitwell, which Elinor had anticipated with a mixture of dread and resignation, ended not with rain and fatigue, but with a complete cancellation. Her preparations for discomfort were rendered unnecessary by a far more inconvenient reality: they never left the park at all.

By ten o'clock, the entire party gathered at the house, ready for breakfast. Despite the night's rain, the morning air was clearing, clouds dispersing to reveal patches of sun. Spirits were high, fueled by a collective determination to endure any hardship rather than miss the outing. They were eager, buoyant, and convinced that happiness was within reach.

However, the mood shifted abruptly when the letters arrived. Among them was one addressed to Colonel Brandon. As he took it, his face drained of color, and he excused himself, leaving the room instantly.

"What is the matter with Brandon?" Sir John asked, his tone laced with concern.

No one could answer.

"I hope he has received no bad news," Lady Middleton remarked. "Nothing short of extraordinary could make Colonel Brandon abandon my breakfast table so precipitously."

Five minutes later, he returned, looking pale but composed.

"No bad news, Colonel, I trust," Mrs. Jennings offered warmly as he entered.

"None at all, ma'am, thank you."

"Was it from Avignon? I pray it does not concern your sister's health."

"No, ma'am. It came from town; it is merely a letter of business."

"But how could a mere business letter discompose you so greatly?" Mrs. Jennings pressed, undeterred. "Come, Colonel, we deserve the truth."

"My dear madam," Lady Middleton interjected sharply, "consider your words."

"Perhaps," Mrs. Jennings speculated loudly, "it is to announce that your cousin Fanny is married?"

"No, indeed."

"Well, then, I know the sender. And I hope she is well."

"Who do you mean, ma'am?" Brandon asked, his cheeks flushing slightly.

"Oh! You know exactly who I mean."

"I am particularly sorry, ma'am," Brandon addressed Lady Middleton, "that I must receive this letter today. It concerns business requiring my immediate attendance in town."

"In town!" Mrs. Jennings exclaimed. "What possible business could keep you in town at this season?"

"My own loss is significant," Brandon continued, his voice steady despite the disappointment in his eyes, "in being forced to leave such a delightful party. Yet I am even more concerned because I fear my presence is essential to secure your admission to Whitwell."

The announcement struck the group like a physical blow.

"But if you simply write a note to the housekeeper, Mr. Brandon," Marianne suggested eagerly, "would that not suffice?"

He shook his head firmly.

"We must go," Sir John declared. "It shall not be postponed now that we are so close. You cannot travel to town until tomorrow, Brandon; that is settled."

"I wish it were so simple," Brandon replied. "But I am powerless to delay my journey by even a single day."

"If you would only tell us the nature of your business," Mrs. Jennings urged, "we might determine if it could wait."

"You would not be delayed six hours," Willoughby interjected smoothly, turning to Marianne, "if you postponed your journey until our return."

"I cannot afford to lose a single hour," Brandon insisted.

Elinor heard Willoughby whisper to Marianne, "Some souls simply cannot bear a party of pleasure. Brandon is one of them. He fears catching a cold, I daresay, and has invented this excuse to escape. I would wager fifty guineas that the letter was written by his own hand."

"I have no doubt of it," Marianne replied, her voice sharp.

"There is no persuading you to change your mind, Brandon, as I know you well," Sir John said. "Yet, I hope you will reconsider. Think of the two Miss Careys who traveled from Newton, the three Miss Dashwoods who walked up from the cottage, and Mr. Willoughby who rose two hours before his usual time solely to join us at Whitwell."

Brandon reiterated his sorrow at disappointing them but maintained that his departure was unavoidable.

"Well, then, when will you return?"

"We shall see you at Barton," Lady Addison added, "as soon as you can conveniently leave town. We shall postpone the Whitwell trip until your return."

"You are very kind. But the uncertainty of my return makes it impossible for me to promise anything."

"Oh! He must and shall come back," Sir John cried. "If he is not here by the end of the week, I shall go after him myself."

"Ay, do that, Sir John," Mrs. Jennings chimed in. "Perhaps you will discover the nature of his business then."

"I have no desire to pry into another man's affairs," Sir John muttered. "I suppose it is something he is ashamed of."

Just then, Brandon's horses were announced.

"You do not intend to ride to town, do you?" Sir John asked.

"No. Only to Honiton. From there, I shall take the post."

"Well, since you are resolved," Sir John said, "I wish you a safe journey. Though I truly hope you change your mind."

"I assure you, it is beyond my power."

He took his leave of the assembly.

"Shall I see you and your sisters in town this winter, Miss Dashwood?" he asked Elinor.

"I fear not at all."

"Then I must bid you farewell for a length of time I would rather avoid."

To Marianne, he offered only a polite bow and silence.

"Come, Colonel," Mrs. Jennings called out as he approached the door, "before you depart, do tell us what errand you are running."

He wished them a good morning and, accompanied by Sir John, exited the room.

Once he was gone, the polite restraint held by the group dissolved. Complaints and lamentations erupted universally, with everyone agreeing repeatedly on how provoking the disappointment was.

"I can guess what his business is," Mrs. Jennings declared triumphantly.

"Can you, ma'am?" nearly everyone asked in unison.

"Yes; it is about Miss Williams, I am certain."

"And who is Miss Williams?" Marianne queried.

"What! Do you not know Miss Williams? I am sure you must have heard of her. She is a relation of the Colonel's, my dear; a very close one. We shall not specify how close, for fear of shocking the young ladies." Lowering her voice, she leaned toward Elinor. "She is his natural daughter."

"Indeed!"

"Oh, yes; and she resembles him in her staring gaze. I dare say the Colonel will leave her his entire fortune."

When Sir John returned, he joined the chorus of regret regarding the ruined plans, though he concluded by suggesting that since they were all assembled, they must find some way to be happy. After brief deliberation, they agreed that while true happiness awaited them at Whitwell, they could achieve a tolerable composure of mind by driving about the countryside. Carriages were ordered; Willoughby's was first, and Marianne appeared happier than she had ever been as she stepped inside. He drove swiftly through the park, and they were soon out of sight. Nothing further was seen of them until their return, which occurred only after the rest of the party had come back. Both seemed delighted with their drive, speaking only in general terms that they had stayed in the lanes while the others ventured onto the downs.

It was decided that a dance would take place that evening, and everyone resolved to be exceedingly merry throughout the day. More of the Careys arrived for dinner, and the table seated nearly twenty guests, a number Sir John observed with great satisfaction. Willoughby took his customary place between the two elder Miss Dashwoods. Mrs. Jennings sat to Elinor's right, and they had not been seated long before she leaned forward, addressing Marianne in a voice loud enough for Willoughby to hear as well.

"I have found you out, despite all your tricks. I know where you spent the morning."

Marianne flushed and replied hastily, "Where, pray?"

"Did you not know," Willoughby added, "that we had been out in my curricle?"

"Yes, yes, Mr. Impudence, I know that perfectly well," Marianne retorted. "I was determined to find out *where* you had been. I hope you like your house, Miss Marianne. It is a very large one, I know; and when I come to visit, I hope you have furnished it anew, for it lacked furnishings greatly when I was there six years ago."

Marianne turned away in profound confusion. Mrs. Jennings laughed heartily. Meanwhile, Elinor realized that in her resolve to discover their whereabouts, she had actually instructed her maid to inquire from Mr. Willoughby's groom. Through this indirect method, she had learned that they had gone to Allenham, spending considerable time walking the gardens and touring the entire house.

Elinor struggled to believe this true, finding it highly improbable that Willoughby would propose entering the house, or that Marianne would consent, while Mrs. Smith resided there—a woman with whom Marianne shared no acquaintance whatsoever.

As soon as they left the dining room, Elinor questioned Marianne directly. Her surprise was immense when she discovered that every detail Mrs. Jennings had mentioned was accurate. Marianne was furious at her sister's doubt.

"Why should you imagine, Elinor, that we did not go there, or that we did not see the house? Is it not something you have often wished to do yourself?"

"Yes, Marianne, but I would not go while Mrs. Smith was there, and with no other companion than Mr. Willoughby."

"Mr. Willoughby, however, is the only person entitled to show that house," Marianne argued. "And since he arrived in an open carriage, it was impossible for anyone else to accompany us. I never spent a pleasanter morning in my life."

"I am afraid," Elinor replied gently, "that the pleasantness of an activity does not always prove its propriety."

"On the contrary," Marianne insisted, "nothing could be stronger proof. If there had been any real impropriety in what I did, I would have felt it at the time. We always know when we are acting wrongly, and with such conviction, I could have felt no pleasure."

"But, my dear Marianne, since this has already exposed you to such impertinent remarks, do you not now begin to question the discretion of your own conduct?"

"If the impertinent remarks of Mrs. Jennings are to serve as proof of impropriety, then we are all offending every moment of our lives," Marianne declared. "I value her censure no more than I would her commendation. I am not conscious of having done anything wrong in walking over Mrs. Smith's grounds or viewing her house. They will one day belong to Mr. Willoughby, and—"

"If they were one day to be your own, Marianne, you would not be justified in what you have done."

She blushed at the suggestion, yet it seemed visibly gratifying to her. After ten minutes of earnest reflection, she returned to her sister with great good humor.

"Perhaps, Elinor, it was rather ill-judged of me to go to Allenham," she admitted. "But Mr. Willoughby particularly wanted to show me the place, and it is a charming house, I assure you. There is one remarkably pretty sitting room upstairs; a nice, comfortable size for constant use. With modern furniture, it would be delightful. It is a corner room with windows on two sides. On one side, you look across the bowling green, behind the house, to a beautiful hanging wood; on the other, you have a view of the church and village, and beyond them, those fine bold hills we have admired so often. I did not see it to its full advantage, for the furniture was utterly forlorn—but if it were newly fitted up, a couple of hundred pounds, as Willoughby says, would make it one of the pleasantest summer rooms in England."

Had Elinor been able to listen without interruption from the others, she would have described every room in the house with equal delight.
