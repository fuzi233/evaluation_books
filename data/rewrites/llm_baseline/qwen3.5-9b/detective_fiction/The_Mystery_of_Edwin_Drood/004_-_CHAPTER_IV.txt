Accepting the jackass as the archetype of self-sufficient stupidity and conceit—a convention, perhaps, like several others, more traditional than justifiable—one finds the purest specimen of this breed in Cloisterham to be Mr. Thomas Sapsea, Auctioneer. Mr. Sapsea "dresses at" the Dean's residence; he has been formally introduced to the Dean in error; and he has even been addressed in the street as "My Lord," under the mistaken belief that he was the Bishop descending unexpectedly, sans chaplain. Mr. Sapsea boasts immensely of these honors, alongside his voice and his manner. Indeed, in the sale of landed estates, he has attempted the experiment of slightly chanting in his pulpit to approximate what he considers the authentic ecclesiastical tone. Consequently, when concluding a public auction, Mr. Sapsea finishes with the air of bestowing a benediction upon the gathered brokers, leaving the actual Dean—a modest and upright gentleman—looking decidedly inferior by comparison.

Mr. Sapsea enjoys numerous admirers. Indeed, the motion is passed by a significant local majority, even among those skeptical of his wisdom, that he constitutes a credit to Cloisterham. He possesses the magnificent qualities of being portentous and dull, possessing a rolling cadence in his speech and a corresponding roll in his walk; not to mention a certain grave, flowing gesture with his hands, suggesting he is perpetually on the verge of confirming the individual with whom he is conversing. Standing nearly sixty rather than fifty, with a flowing silhouette of belly and horizontal creases across his waistcoat, and reputed to be wealthy, he votes at elections in the strictly respectable interest. Morally satisfied that nothing has grown since his infancy except himself, how can the dim-witted Mr. Sapsea fail to be a credit to Cloisterham and society?

Mr. Sapsea's offices are located on the High Street, directly opposite the Nuns' House. They date from roughly the same period as the convent, irregularly modernized here and there as successive generations, increasingly preferring fresh air and light to fever and plague, allowed the building to steadily deteriorate. Above the doorway hangs a wooden effigy, approximately half-life-size, depicting Mr. Sapsea's father in a curly wig and toga, in the act of conducting a sale. The purity of the concept, and the natural appearance of the little finger, hammer, and pulpit, have been widely admired.

Mr. Sapsea occupies his dull ground-floor sitting room, which looks first onto his paved backyard and then onto his fenced-in garden. Before the fire sits a bottle of port wine—an early luxury, yet delightful on this cool, chilly autumn evening. The hearth is characteristically accompanied by his portrait, his eight-day clock, and his barometer. Characteristic, because he intends to stand firm against mankind, his barometer against the weather, and his clock against time itself. Beside Mr. Sapsea on the table lie a writing desk and stationery. Glancing at a scrap of manuscript, Mr. Sapsea reads it aloud with a lofty demeanor, then paces the room slowly with his thumbs hooked into the armholes of his waistcoat, reciting it from memory: so inwardly, yet with such immense dignity, that the only word audible is "Ethelinda."

Three clean wineglasses sit in a tray on the table. As his maid enters to announce, "Mr. Jasper is come, sir," Mr. Sapsea waves, "Admit him," and draws two wineglasses from the tray, claiming them as his due.

"Glad to see you, sir. I congratulate myself on the honor of receiving you here for the first time." Mr. Sapsea performs the hospitality of his house thus.

"You are very kind. The honor is mine, and my congratulations are mine."

"You are pleased to say so, sir. But I assure you, it is a satisfaction to me to welcome you to my humble home. And that is a sentiment I reserve for select individuals." An ineffable loftiness accompanies these words from Mr. Sapsea, implying: *You will hardly believe that your company can satisfy a man of my stature; nevertheless, it does.*

"I have long wished to know you, Mr. Sapsea."

"And I, sir, have long known you by reputation as a man of taste. Permit me to fill your glass. I shall also give you," says Mr. Sapsea, pouring his own, "'When the French come over, may we meet them at Dover!' This was a patriotic toast in my youth, and I remain fully convinced of its appropriateness for any subsequent era."

"You can scarcely be unaware, Mr. Sapsea," observes Jasper, smiling as the auctioneer stretches his legs before the fire, "that you know the world."

"Well, sir," comes the chuckling reply, "I think I know something of it; something of it."

"Your reputation for such knowledge has always intrigued and surprised me, prompting my desire to know you. For Cloisterham is a small place. Cooped up here myself, I know nothing beyond its borders and feel it to be truly diminutive."

"If I have not traveled to foreign lands, young man," Mr. Sapsea begins, then pauses—"You will forgive me addressing you as 'young man,' Mr. Jasper? You are much my junior."

"By all means."

"If I have not gone abroad, young man, foreign lands have come to me. They arrived via business, and I have capitalized on every opportunity. Consider it as taking an inventory or compiling a catalog. I spot a French clock. I have never seen one before in my life, but I instantly point my finger at it and declare, 'Paris!' I encounter cups and saucers of Chinese manufacture, equally unfamiliar to me personally: I point my finger at them and immediately say, 'Peking, Nanking, and Canton.' It is the same with Japan, Egypt, and bamboo and sandalwood from the East Indies; I point my finger at them all. I have even pointed my finger at the North Pole and declared, 'Made by Esquimaux spears, for half a pint of pale sherry!'"

"Remarkable, indeed, Mr. Sapsea, is your method of acquiring knowledge of men and things."

"I mention this, sir," Mr. Sapsea rejoins with unspeakable complacency, "because, as I stated, it is improper to boast of what one *is*; one must demonstrate *how* one became so, thereby proving it."

"Most fascinating. We were discussing the late Mrs. Sapsea."

"We were, sir." Mr. Sapsea fills both glasses and retreats the decanter to safety. "Before consulting your opinion as a connoisseur on this little trifle"—he holds it up—"which is *merely* a trifle, yet required considerable thought, sir, some slight fever of the brow, I should perhaps describe the character of the late Mrs. Sapsea, deceased three-quarters of a year."

Mr. Jasper, yawning behind his wineglass, lowers it to reveal a look of feigned interest. Its expressiveness is slightly diminished by the lingering yawn, with watery eyes still adjusting.

"About six years ago, or so," Mr. Sapsea continues, "when I had expanded my mind—to I will not claim the extent it currently holds, lest that seem presumptuous, but to the point of desiring another mind to be absorbed within it—I cast about for a nuptial partner. For, as I say, it is not good for man to be alone."

Mr. Jasper appears to commit this original notion to memory.

"At that time, Miss Brobity maintained, I will not call it the rival establishment to the one opposite the Nuns' House, but rather the parallel establishment downtown. The world believed she displayed a passion for attending my sales during half-holidays or vacations. Rumors circulated that she admired my style. Observations noted that as time progressed, my style became evident in the dictation exercises of Miss Brobity's pupils. A whisper, born of obscure malice, arose that one ignorant and besotted parent objected to it by name. But I doubt this. For is it likely that any rational human being would so expose themselves to what I term the finger of scorn?"

Mr. Jasper shakes his head. Not in the least likely.

In a grandiose state of absent-mindedness, Mr. Sapsea refills Mr. Jasper's glass, which is already full, and genuinely refills his own, which is empty.

"The essence of Miss Brobity, young man, was deeply imbued with reverence for Mind. She worshipped Mind, when launched—or, as I say, precipitated—upon an extensive knowledge of the world. When I proposed, she honored me by being so overwhelmed with a species of awe that she could articulate only two words: 'O Thou!' referring to myself. Her clear blue eyes were fixed upon me, her semi-transparent hands clasped together, pallor covered her aquiline features, and though encouraged to continue, she never uttered another syllable. I acquired the parallel establishment by private contract, and we became as united as circumstances permitted. Yet, she never could, nor ever did, find a phrase satisfactory to her perhaps-too-high estimation of my intellect. To the very end (a feeble action of the liver), she addressed me in those same unfinished terms."

Mr. Jasper closes his eyes as the auctioneer deepens his voice. Abruptly opening them, he says in unison with the deepened tone, "Ah!"—
