Tito had good reason to believe he was safe. Over the past three months, as he anticipated the exposure of the Medici conspiracy, he had used every moment to fortify his position. He had deepened his influence in Rome and Milan by acting as a conduit for secret intelligence—information aimed at undermining both the Friar and the popular faction. At the same time, he had cultivated the favor of the very party he was secretly betraying, carefully presenting himself as a loyal ally of their cause. And throughout this intricate balancing act, he had not withdrawn from the Medici circle; instead, he had grown more trusted, more indispensable to them.

It was easy for Tito to maintain such a triple role. The Medici themselves operated under a code that accepted deception as legitimate when directed against enemies—yet they saw no honor in betraying their own. Because Tito lacked their inherited loyalties, family ties, and long-standing prejudices, they never questioned whether he could be trusted. To them, his detachment made him ideal: a man without roots, unburdened by tradition, free to serve their ends with total flexibility. They failed to see that the very quality that made him useful—the absence of fixed allegiance—was also the absence of moral constraint.

Meanwhile, the Roman and Milanese supporters of the aristocratic faction, known as the Arrabbiati, were locked in constant covert warfare against Savonarola. In their world, duplicity was not only expected but rewarded. The deeper the lie, the higher the price. So, if Tito played a part with conviction, it would have been absurd to suspect him of insincerity—just as it would have been foolish to think any agent in such circles was anything but a master of disguise.

On the other side, the Piagnoni—the popular party—were men of strong convictions and direct speech. They admired Tito’s political alignment with them, especially since he showed no religious fervor. His lack of spiritual zeal actually worked in his favor; it made his support seem more genuine, less performative.

Under these conditions, Tito’s last three months had been marked by remarkable success. What mattered most to him was securing a future beyond Florence—a place where life was smoother, rewards greater, and talent properly honored. Rome and Milan offered such prospects, though currently Milan held the edge. If he could deliver crucial services to Duke Ludovico Sforza within the year, he stood to gain a prestigious post at the Milanese court—one that surpassed even what Rome might offer.

The revelation of the Medici plot had been something Tito had prepared for, though not its exact form. The arrest of Lamberto dell’Antella, found with a damning letter and a heart full of hatred for the Medici, was an unforeseen blow. Tito knew he could not escape suspicion—he had always known that. But his mind moved swiftly. He devised a path to safety that would spare him the worst consequences.

There is comfort in preserving one’s skin—but even a preserved skin feels the air around it. His calculations proved correct. Before returning home that night, he secured three vital outcomes: he would be cleared of all charges related to the Medici conspiracy; he would keep his secretaryship for another year unless he resigned; and, most importantly, the price he paid for these assurances would remain a state secret.

That price would have seemed steep to most men—and Tito himself would have preferred not to pay it. But necessity dictated otherwise. First, he turned to Francesco Valori, a member of the Ten who oversaw his position and also sat on the special council investigating the conspiracy. Valori, as we’ve seen, was a leader of the Piagnoni—a man of sharp intellect, fierce partisanship, and bitter personal grudges, especially toward Bernardo del Nero.

In a private meeting, after swearing Valori to secrecy, Tito confessed his role as a Medici agent—not out of loyalty, but because he believed their efforts doomed. He declared he had always favored the popular government and now sought justice. He even confided his dislike for Bernardo del Nero, framing it as a personal matter. Then came the key revelation: there was another Medici plot—this one aiming to restore Piero de’ Medici to power through a coup scheduled for mid-August. With certain guarantees, Tito claimed he could travel to Siena and Romagna, where Piero was gathering forces, and return with documentary proof.

To do so, however, he needed protection. His status as a Medici informant must remain hidden—even from those who suspected him. Otherwise, he would be compromised, unable to operate among them. He demanded immunity from prosecution and the right to retain his office for one year. Without these, he could not proceed.

Valori listened. But his thoughts were not on Tito’s morality. His focus was on ensuring the five accused men received the harshest punishment possible. There would be frantic efforts to save them. The evidence needed to be overwhelming—strong enough to deter others from sympathizing with mercy. In that era, justice often required dirty hands. Torture had already extracted confessions from Lamberto, but more was needed—especially against Bernardo del Nero, who had merely refused to betray the plot after failing to stop it. For Florence’s sake, Valori believed Bernardo must be shown in the worst possible light. Perhaps he didn’t realize how much his judgment was shaped by personal animosity.

Still, he approved Tito’s plan. He presented it to the council anonymously, persuading them of its value. By late afternoon, Tito was summoned before the Special Council. Standing calmly before twenty solemn Florentines, he revealed the new conspiracy—its timing, its aims, its intended execution. He spoke with quiet precision, using measured language that masked the gravity of his words. Yet there was something unsettling about his demeanor: the ease with which he admitted his motives, the way he seemed to acknowledge his own moral shortfalls without shame.

He delivered a commission to journey to Siena at dawn. And he left the chamber with a written guarantee of immunity and continued office.

Among those watching him, several formed a new opinion. This clever Greek was no longer fit for friendship—only for service. Unprincipled men were useful tools. They allowed those with scruples to remain clean while still getting things done. As the poet Pulci once said, “Many love the fruits of treachery, but no one loves the traitor.” Society had built gallows for murderers and martyrs alike—hissed at cowardice and at truth-tellers alike. Such contradictions were not signs of chaos, but of struggle—an attempt to order a world where virtue and vice walked hand in hand.

Tito was aware of the shift in how he was viewed. He possessed a clear sense of self, born of constant reflection and calculated action. He had never wanted disapproval. He had aimed to act so skillfully that others would never know he was acting against their standards. Only Romola had seen through him—because she refused to accept lies. That barrier between them had been erected by the impossibility of concealment.

Now, he felt the sting of judgment. Not because he had done wrong—though he knew he had—but because his actions could no longer be fully hidden. This was a revelation, not just to others, but to himself. He winced at the thought of being despised.

Yet he pressed on. He did not hate Bernardo del Nero. He liked Lorenzo Tornabuoni and Giannozzo Pucci. He had served them well—so well that if they had won, he would have been richly rewarded. But now, with their fall, could he abandon the pleasures of life? His offer of further evidence would likely change nothing for them—he was convinced they would survive. But without it, he would lose everything.

Was there any motive but self-interest? Perhaps some men tied their fate to family pride or ancient feuds. Others, like fanatics, believed in divine reward or apocalyptic renewal. But clear-minded men—like Pontanus, the Latin poet who welcomed the French king to overthrow his own patron—understood that survival meant compromise. Power shielded men from blame. Weakness invited scorn.

Tito sensed the first whispers of that scorn during his audience. It was faint, but real. And it marked a turning point. He was not stepping deeper into sin—he had no bond to the Medici stronger than his duty to his father. But his father’s betrayal had been hidden by lies. This act could not be concealed. It was public. It was a confession.

And Tito resented the cost.

Still, he looked ahead. In a year—or perhaps half—his life could begin anew. He had money safely stashed outside Florence. He was twenty-eight, at the peak of his abilities. Could he not shed the past like old clothes, cast aside the weight of mistakes, and step forward into a brighter scene?

As he rode toward Siena, reins loose, the wind in his face, he imagined a future free from regret. A future where he was no longer haunted by love gone wrong or unexpected returns.

But Tito did not know that, as he left the council chamber, he had brushed past a man in the dim lamplight—a man whose face he didn’t recognize. It was Ser Ceccone, also ready to sell information, also willing to betray those who had failed.
