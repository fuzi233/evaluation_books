Not a single event occurred during the night, nor did any attempt at communication between Miss Rachel and Rosanna succeed in deceiving the vigilance of Sergeant Cuff. I had anticipated that the Sergeant would depart for Frizinghall immediately upon sunrise. However, he remained in attendance, seemingly occupied with some prior task. Leaving him to his own devices, I ventured into the grounds shortly thereafter and encountered Mr. Franklin on his customary stroll along the shrubbery path. Hardly had we exchanged greetings when the Sergeant unexpectedly joined us. He approached Mr. Franklin with a polite demeanor, only to receive a response marked by distinct hauteur.

"Have you anything to say to me?" was the sharp retort Mr. Franklin offered in reply to the Sergeant's courteous morning wish.

"I have something to say to you, sir," the Sergeant replied gravely, "regarding the inquiry I am currently conducting here. You discerned the true direction of this investigation yesterday. Naturally, given your position, you are shocked and distressed. Equally naturally, you project your own anger regarding your family's scandal onto me."

"What do you want?" Mr. Franklin interjected, his tone cutting.

"I wish to remind you, sir, that I have thus far not been *proven* incorrect. Keeping that in mind, please recall simultaneously that I am an officer of the law acting under the authority of the mistress of this house. Under these circumstances, is it, or is it not, your duty as a responsible citizen to assist me with any special information you may possess?"

"I possess no special information," Mr. Franklin stated flatly.

Sergeant Cuff dismissed this answer as if it had never been uttered. "You may spare my time from being wasted on inquiries at a distance," he continued smoothly, "if you choose to comprehend me and speak freely."

"I do not understand you," Mr. Franklin replied, "and I have nothing further to say."

"One of the female servants—I shall not mention names—spoke to you privately last night."

Once again, Mr. Franklin cut him short; once again, he declared, "I have nothing to say."

Standing silently nearby, I recalled the movement within the swing-door the previous evening and the coat-tails I had observed vanishing down the passage. Sergeant Cuff had undoubtedly gathered sufficient evidence by this point to suspect that Rosanna had confessed something to Mr. Franklin Blake. This realization had just struck me when who should emerge from the end of the shrubbery walk but Rosanna Spearman herself! She was followed closely by Penelope, who was clearly attempting to guide her back toward the house. Observing that Mr. Franklin was not alone, Rosanna halted abruptly, displaying evident perplexity regarding her next move. Penelope lingered behind her. Mr. Franklin spotted the girls the moment I did. Meanwhile, the Sergeant, employing his diabolical cunning, feigned total ignorance of their presence.

All of this transpired in an instant. Before either Mr. Franklin or I could utter a word, Sergeant Cuff intervened seamlessly, maintaining the appearance of continuing our previous conversation.

"You need not fear harming the girl, sir," he addressed Mr. Franklin, speaking in a volume loud enough for Rosanna to hear every syllable. "On the contrary, I strongly advise you to honor me with your confidence should you harbor any genuine interest in Rosanna Spearman."

Mr. Franklin instantly adopted the guise of having failed to notice the girls. Responding in equally loud tones, he declared: "I take no interest whatsoever in Rosanna Spearman."

I cast my gaze toward the distant end of the walk. All I perceived was that the moment Mr. Franklin spoke, Rosanna turned around. Unlike her earlier hesitation, she did not resist Penelope; instead, she allowed my daughter to take her arm and lead her back inside. The breakfast bell rang precisely as the two girls vanished from sight—and even Sergeant Cuff was compelled to concede defeat.

"I shall proceed to Frizinghall, Mr. Betteredge," he said quietly to me, "and I shall return before two o'clock."

With that, he departed without further comment, and for several hours, we were fortunate to be free of his presence.

"You must make amends with Rosanna," Mr. Franklin said to me once we were alone. "I seem destined to say or do something awkward whenever that unfortunate girl appears. You witnessed it yourself: Sergeant Cuff laid a trap for both of us. Had he succeeded in confusing me or irritating her into breaking down, either she or I might have revealed something serving his purposes. On impulse, I saw no better escape than the course I took. It prevented the girl from speaking and demonstrated to the Sergeant that I had seen through his ruse. He was evidently listening, Betteredge, when I was conversing with you last night."

"He had done worse than merely listen," I thought privately. He had retained my admission that the girl loved Mr. Franklin and had calculated upon that fact when appealing to Mr. Franklin's supposed interest in Rosanna, specifically within her hearing range.

"As to listening, sir," I remarked, withholding the deeper truth for the moment, "we shall all be rowing in the same boat if this continues much longer. Prying, peeping, and eavesdropping are the natural occupations of those in our situation. Within a day or two, Mr. Franklin, we shall all be rendered speechless—for we shall all be listening to surprise one another's secrets, fully aware that we are doing so. Forgive my outburst, sir. The terrible mystery enveloping this house has intoxicated my mind like strong liquor, driving me wild. I shall not forget what you have told me. I will seize the first opportunity to rectify matters with Rosanna Spearman."

"You have not spoken to her yet about last night, have you?" Mr. Franklin asked.

"No, sir."

"Then say nothing now. I believe it unwise to invite the girl's confidence while the Sergeant watches for us to fall into his trap together. My conduct is inconsistent, Betteredge—is it not? I perceive no way out of this dreadful predicament unless the Diamond is traced to Rosanna. Yet, I cannot, and will not, aid the Sergeant in discovering the girl."

Unreasonable, no doubt. But it mirrored my own state of mind perfectly. I understood him entirely. If you will, for once in your life, remember your mortality, you may understand him as well.

The situation within and without the house, while the Sergeant was en route to Frizinghall, unfolded as follows: Miss Rachel awaited the arrival of the carriage intended to take her to her aunt's, remaining stubbornly confined to her chamber. My Lady and Mr. Franklin shared breakfast. Following the meal, Mr. Franklin formed one of his impulsive resolutions and departed hastily, seeking to calm his agitated mind via a lengthy walk. I was the sole witness to his departure; he assured me he would return before the Sergeant's arrival.

The weather, ominously forecast the previous night, had indeed changed. Heavy rain, falling shortly after dawn, gave way to a fierce gale. By midday, the air was biting and fresh, though the clouds occasionally threatened, the rain held off. It was a tolerable day for a walk, provided one possessed youth and strength capable of braving the powerful gusts sweeping in from the sea.

After attending to my mistress following breakfast, I assisted her in settling our household accounts. She mentioned the Moonstone only once, strictly prohibiting any discussion of it at that moment.

"Wait until that man returns," she instructed, referring to the Sergeant. "We *must* discuss it then; we are not obligated to speak of it now."

Leaving my mistress, I discovered Penelope awaiting me in my room.

"I beg you, father, to come and speak to Rosanna," she pleaded. "I am exceedingly uneasy concerning her."

I suspected the cause readily enough. However, it is a maxim of mine that men, being superior creatures, are bound to improve women if possible. Whenever a woman requests action—whether my daughter or anyone else—it is imperative that I insist upon knowing the reason. The more frequently you compel them to rummage their own minds for justification, the more manageable you will find them in all aspects of life. It is not their fault (poor souls!) that they often act before thinking; it is the fault of the foolish ones who indulge them.

Penelope's reason, in this instance, was expressed in her own words: "I am afraid, father, that Mr. Franklin has wounded Rosanna cruelly, albeit unintentionally."

"What drew Rosanna to the shrubbery walk?" I inquired.

"Her own madness," Penelope replied. "I can conceive of no other explanation. She was determined to speak to Mr. Franklin this morning, regardless of the consequences. I did my utmost to prevent her; you saw how hard I tried. If only I could have removed her before she heard those dreadful words..."

"There! There!" I soothed her. "Do not lose your composure. I cannot recall anything happening to alarm Rosanna."

"Nothing to alarm her, father. But Mr. Franklin stated he took no interest in her at all—and, oh, he said it in such a cruel voice!"

"He said it to silence the Sergeant," I explained.

"I told her that," Penelope admitted. "However, you see, father (though Mr. Franklin bears no blame), he has been mortifying and disappointing her for weeks and weeks. Now this arrives atop it all!
