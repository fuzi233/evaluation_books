The heavy knock came again, more insistent. They were surrounded.

Margaret’s breath hitched, but she forced herself to think. “Tell them he was here but has gone,” she whispered, then seized Gerard’s arm and pulled him toward the narrow steps leading to her father’s chamber. Her own room lay just beyond.

The pounding resumed.

“Who knocks at such an hour?” Martin’s voice was rough with feigned irritation.

“Open, or you’ll regret it!”

“I don’t open my door to thieves—honest folk are abed by now.”

“Open to the law, Martin Wittenhaagen, unless you want trouble.”

Martin grunted, recognizing the voice. “Dirk Brower? What brings you so far from Tergou?”

“Open, and you’ll find out.”

Slowly, Martin drew the bolt. The door flew open, and Dierich Brower stormed in with four others, while a sixth man slipped in from the rear.

“Now then, Martin—where’s Gerard Eliassoen?”

“Gerard?” Martin feigned indifference, though his pulse quickened. “He was here, but he’s gone.”

“Gone?” Dierich’s face darkened. “When?”

Martin shrugged, settling by the fire to mend his bowstring with deliberate calm. “His brother, that little imp Giles, came charging in on a mule, screeching like a banshee. Next thing I knew, Gerard was off. They say he’s bound for Italy—not that I’d know where that is.”

Dierich’s jaw tightened. The story held no cracks. A cleverer liar might have overplayed his hand, but Martin stuck to the tale he’d been given, spinning it just enough to seem plausible.

“Mates,” Dierich muttered to his men, “I told the burgomaster this was folly. That dwarf we saw riding through the storm—he must have warned Gerard. We’re too late.” He spat in disgust. “A wet cloak for our trouble, that’s all.”

Martin smirked, but the moment Dierich’s eyes narrowed, his grin faded.

“Still,” Dierich said, “we’ll search the place to satisfy the burgomaster.”

Martin’s stomach clenched. He forced himself to stay still as Dierich snatched the candle and led his men upstairs. Left alone, Martin’s mind raced. If Gerard was still inside, he had one chance—fight or flee.

He cracked the back door open, watching. The glow of the candle flickered in Peter’s room. *Fool! Jump now!* But no movement came. Then the light shifted to Margaret’s chamber. Still no escape.

A cold dread settled over him. If they found Gerard, it would mean death for the boy and heartbreak for Margaret.

Martin’s fingers twitched toward his knife.

Then—muffled cries from Margaret’s room.

His blood ran hot.

With a snarl, he moved fast. At each door, he mimicked Dierich’s voice. “Watch the window!” Then he barred both exits.

Bow in hand, arrow nocked, he stood at the foot of the stairs—a soldier ready for slaughter.

Two shots. That was all he needed. Two men down before they could close in. Then he and Gerard could take the rest, blade to blade.

The footsteps grew louder. The light spilled down the steps.

Martin’s breath steadied. His fingers flexed around the bowstring.

Let them come.

---

Dierich’s men tore through Peter’s chamber—ripping open cupboards, stabbing into curios, overturning the bed. Nothing.

Then Margaret’s room. Small. Bare. No place to hide, save the chimney.

Margaret sat up, blinking in the candlelight, her face pale with shock. Then she screamed.

Dierich held up a hand. “Peace, girl. We mean no harm. Lie still.”

“Thieves!” she gasped. “You come armed into my home—into my *bedroom*—”

“We’re looking for Gerard.”

“Gerard?” Her voice cracked. “You drove him away! You know this! Or are you not men of Sevenbergen? Would you shame an honest girl so?”

Dierich flinched. The others shifted uncomfortably.

“Enough,” he muttered. “He’s not here.”

Margaret’s eyes burned. “Then go. And pray your own daughters never suffer such an insult.”

Dierich retreated, his men stumbling after him.

“I told the burgomaster,” he grumbled. “A waste of time.”

The door slammed shut.

Silence.

Martin exhaled, lowering his bow.

For now, they were safe.

But the night was far from over.
