Our friend Pen, spying Mr. Bows, cried out “How d’ye do, Mr. Bows?” with such a brave, tavern-bell cheeriness that you would have thought he meant to pay for everybody’s dinner and whistle the band besides. Yet, for all that trumpet of a greeting, there was a blush on Arthur’s face—one of those sudden kitchen-fires that flare up in a man who has been playing with matches. Fanny, in the porter’s lodge, answered it at once: her cheek flew the same little red signal, as quick as if her blood had been taught to curtsy.

Bows and Arthur shook hands. Bows, with that mild, funereal irony of his, accepted Pen’s airy assertion that he was “just going up to Captain Costigan’s,” as though the captain’s chambers were a chapel and Pen was a punctual parishioner. Then fell a silence—gloomy, guilty, and thick as yesterday’s gravy. Pen tried to whisk it away by making a great rattling noise, as if sound could be stirred like soup and made wholesome by sheer clatter. The silence, of course, broke; but the gloom stayed—indeed it deepened, the way a cellar looks darker when you bring down one candle and discover, by its very light, how much is still black.

Pendennis began, in a jocular style, to tell over last night’s transactions, and attempted an imitation of Costigan wrangling with the check-taker at Vauxhall. It was not good. Who can counterfeit that perfection? Nobody laughed. Mrs. Bolton stared as if he were describing a recipe in Greek—couldn’t tell whether he was the check-taker or the captain being carved up. Fanny tried a timid giggle, like a spoon tapping the rim of a cup—little sound, no comfort in it. Old Bows looked as glum as when he used to saw out mournful music in an orchestra, or hammer a difficult piece on the old piano in the Back Kitchen, with all the mirth of a man chopping onions for a funeral pie.

Pen felt his story fail in his mouth; his voice sank, dwindled, flickered, and went out. And it was dark again. You could hear the ticket-porter under the archway, lolling along the flags; the clink of his boot-heels rang through the court like a metronome keeping time to everybody’s embarrassment. In such places, sound is a tyrant: a heel on stone, a latch clicked, a cough—each one sets the mind to listening for what it dreads.

“You were coming to see me, sir,” Mr. Bows said at last. “Won’t you have the kindness to walk up to my chambers with me? You do them a great honour, I am sure. They are rather high up; but—”

“Oh! I live in a garret myself, and Shepherd’s Inn is twice as cheerful as Lamb Court,” Pen broke in quickly, as though he could salt the conversation into palatableness.

“I knew that you had third-floor apartments,” Mr. Bows said, “and was going to say—you will please not take my remark as discourteous—that the air up three pair of stairs is wholesomer for gentlemen, than the air of a porter’s lodge.”

“Sir!” cried Pen, and his temper—like a candle in a draught—flared up the instant it was touched. Men are quarrelsome when they’re wrong, as cooks are touchy when the sauce has broken. “Will you permit me to choose my society without—?”

“You were so polite as to say that you were about to honour my umble domicile with a visit,” Bows went on, with the same patient sorrow in his voice, and the same old stage-door dignity in his words. “Shall I show you the way? Mr. Pendennis and I are old friends, Mrs. Bolton—very old acquaintances; and at the earliest dawn of his life we crossed each other.”

He pointed to the door with a trembling finger, hat in the other hand, a little theatrical in attitude—his vocabulary forever smelling faintly of footlights and paint. But he was not acting; Pen knew it, though he was minded to sneer at the old fellow’s melodramatic manner, as young gentlemen will sneer at an old knife that still cuts.

“Come along, sir,” Arthur said, with a poor sort of swagger, “as you are so very pressing. Mrs. Bolton, I wish you a good day. Good-bye, Miss Fanny; I shall always think of our night at Vauxhall with pleasure; and be sure I will remember the theatre tickets.”

He took her hand; he pressed it; it pressed him back—warm, quick, like a small loaf just pulled from the oven—and then he was gone.

“What a nice young man, to be sure!” cried Mrs. Bolton, delighted, as women will be delighted by a fine coat and civil words, as if politeness were a new kind of tea.

“D’you think so, ma?” said Fanny; but the question sounded less like doubt than like a child asking if she may have another slice.

“I was a-thinkin who he was like. When I was at the Wells with Mrs. Serle,” Mrs. Bolton continued, peeping through the curtain as if looking after a roast in a street-stall, “there was a young gentleman from the city, that used to come in a tilbry, in a white at, the very image of him, only his whiskers was black, and Mr. P.’s is red.”

“Law, ma! they are a most beautiful hawburn,” Fanny said; and you could have heard, under the words, the sighing little hiss of vanity, like fat meeting a hot pan.

“He used to come for Emly Budd, who danced Columbine in ‘Arleykin Ornpipe, or the Battle of Navarino,’ when Miss De la Bosky was took ill—a pretty dancer, and a fine stage figure of a woman—and he was a great sugar-baker in the city, with a country ouse at Omerton; and he used to drive her in the tilbry down Goswell Street Road; and one day they drove and was married at St. Bartholomew’s Church, Smithfield, where they had their bands read quite private; and she now keeps her carriage, and I sor her name in the paper as patroness of the Manshing-House Ball for the Washywomen’s Asylum. And look at Lady Mirabel—capting Costigan’s daughter—she was profeshnl, as all very well know.”

Thus, and more to the same pastry of romance, Mrs. Bolton talked—now peeping through the curtain, now cleaning mugs and plates, stacking them in the corner cupboard with the tidy pride of a woman who believes order is almost a virtue. She finished as she and Fanny shook out the dinner-cloth between them—snapping it so that the air smelt faintly of stale beer, damp linen, and yesterday’s cabbage—and folded it with solemn care into its drawer.

Costigan had once been made to understand Pen’s means and expectations pretty exactly; but the Captain had either forgotten what he learned years ago at Chatteris, or his natural enthusiasm had risen like yeast in warm weather and swelled the young man’s fortune to a splendid, imaginary loaf. Last night, strolling with Mrs. Bolton while Pen was on his little escapade with Fanny, the Captain had described Fairoaks Park as if it were the Garden of Eden with a title-deed; had dilated on the enormous wealth of Pen’s famous uncle the Major; and talked of funded and landed property with the intimacy of a man who has never had to pay for it.

Very likely, in the night, Mrs. Bolton had seasoned and stewed these statements in her own wise head, and gone to sleep tasting, in fancy, the rich gravy of them—visions of Fanny driving in her carriage, like that old comrade Columbine who had danced out of poverty and into patronage.

At the last fold of the cloth, mother and daughter, of necessity, came close together. As Fanny gave it the final crease, Mrs. Bolton put a finger under the girl’s chin and kissed her. Again that red signal flew out and fluttered on Fanny’s cheek. It was not alarm now. It was pleasure—warm, shamefaced, and delicious as the first spoonful of sweetened cream when you’ve been living on dry bread.

Poor little Fanny! Is love a sin, that it begins so pleasant and ends so bitter?

After the kiss, Mrs. Bolton declared she was going out upon business, and that Fanny must keep the lodge; which Fanny, after the faintest of objections—no more than the protest a hungry child makes before taking the second bun—consented to do.

So Mrs. Bolton took her bonnet and market-basket and departed; and the instant the door had closed and her footfall was swallowed by the court’s echoing stone, Fanny ran to the window that commanded Bows’s door and sat there, never once taking her eyes away. Betsy-Jane and Amelia-Ann buzzed in a corner, pretending to read a picture-book held topsy-turvy—a dreadful tract out of Mr. Bolton’s collection, full of moral skeletons and admonitions. They chattered like sparrows in a pantry; Fanny heard nothing. Her ears were full of the court’s little noises: a distant cart in the Strand grumbling like thunder; the court’s own small echoes; the whisper of her needlework bag; the occasional metallic click of a latch. All of it was only the accompaniment to one expected sound—the opening of Bows’s door.

At last she gave a little shake, and her eyes lighted up. He had come out. He would pass the door again. But her poor little countenance fell in an instant, as quickly as a soufflé collapses when the oven’s opened too soon. Pendennis, indeed, came out—but Bows followed him.

They passed under the archway together. Pen only took off his hat and bowed as he looked in. He did not stop to speak. And then, in three or four minutes—Fanny did not know how long; her sense of time had been boiled down to mere minutes and heartbeats—Bows returned alone and entered the porter’s room.

“Where’s your Ma, dear?” he asked Fanny.

“I don’t know,” Fanny said, with an angry toss, as if she were flinging salt at him. “I don’t follow Ma’s steps wherever she goes, I suppose, Mr. Bows.”

“Am I my mother’s keeper?” Bows said, with his usual melancholy bitterness; and then, changing to a gentler voice, called, “Come here, Betsy-Jane and Amelia-Ann; I’ve brought a cake for the one who can read her letters best, and a cake for the other who can read them the next best.”

He examined the young ladies with a patience he did not spend on grown people; and rewarded them with gingerbread medals, which they carried off into the court, crunching their triumph and making the very stones sound sweeter. Meanwhile Fanny drew out some work and pretended to be busy, plying her needle with great industry, as if she could stitch down her vexation. But her mind was in a ferment—anger, mortification, desire; all whipping together like eggs that refuse to be quiet.

Bows sat so that he could command the entrance from the lodge to the street; his pale eyes kept watch, as if he listened not only with his ears but with his very bones, for the footfall he feared. Yet the person he perhaps expected never returned. Mrs. Bolton came in from market at last, rustling parcels, the basket smelling of greens and onions and a bit of cheap fish—and found Mr. Bows in the lodge instead of the person she had expected. The reader may guess his name.

The interview between Bows and his guest, when they climbed to the apartment occupied by Bows in common with the descendant of the Milesian kings, was not particularly satisfactory to either. Pen was sulky. If Bows had anything to say, he did not care to say it before Captain Costigan—who stayed in the room the whole time of Pen’s visit, having quitted his bedchamber only a few minutes before Pendennis arrived.

We have already been admitted to the deshabille of Major Pendennis: will any man wish to be valet-de-chambre to our other hero? It appeared that the Captain, before issuing, had scented himself with otto-of-whisky. A rich odour of that delicious perfume breathed from him—sweet, sharp, and sorrowful; a spirituous cologne fit to preserve a man in pickle. The hand he held out in cordiality shook woefully; it was a wonder how it ever held the razor with which he daily operated on his chin.

Bows’s room, on the other hand, was as neat as Costigan’s was disorderly. His humble wardrobe hung behind a curtain. Books and manuscript music were trimly arranged on shelves. Over the old gentleman’s bed hung a lithographed portrait of Miss Fotheringay as Mrs. Haller, the actress’s sprawling signature in the corner like a flourish made by a woman in a hurry to be admired. Lady Mirabel, Bows reflected, wrote much better: her ladyship had laboured at penmanship since her marriage, and in common notes acquitted herself very genteelly. Bows loved the old handwriting best, though—the earlier manner, the first language of her hand before it had been disciplined into respectable shapes. He had but one specimen of the newer style, a note in reply to a song he had composed and dedicated to Lady Mirabel, by her most humble servant Robert Bows; and that precious scrap lay in his desk among his state papers, like a dried herb pressed between pages to keep a scent alive.

He was teaching Fanny Bolton now to sing and to write, as he had taught Emily in former days. It was in the nature of the man to attach himself to something. When Emily was torn away, he took a substitute—as a man looks out for a crutch when he loses a leg; or lashes himself to a raft after shipwreck. Some men give their hearts to a woman in youth, and end, in age, by being made miserable over a poodle’s cough. It was hard upon Bows—hard upon his pride as a man and his sentiment as a lover of old airs—that he should find Pendennis again upon his track, and in pursuit of this little Fanny, who was learning to shape letters as if each one might spell rescue.

Costigan, meanwhile, had not the least idea that his company could be anything but welcome to Messrs. Pendennis and Bows; and imagined Pen’s visit intended for himself. He expressed himself vastly pleased by the mark of poloightness, and promised in his own mind to repay the obligation—at least that obligation, which was far from the only debt he owed in this world—by several visits to his young friend.

He entertained Pen affably with news of the day, or rather of ten days previous; for Pen, being a journalist, remembered having seen some of the Captain’s opinions already printed in the Sporting and Theatrical Newspaper, Costigan’s oracle, from which he took his politics, his morals, and his weather. The Captain announced that Sir Charles and Lady Mirabel were gone to Baden-Baden, and were most pressing that he should join them.

Pen replied gravely that he had heard Baden was very pleasant, and the Grand Duke exceedingly hospitable to English.

Costigan answered that the laws of hospitalitee bekeam a Grand Juke; that he sariously would think of paying him a visit; and presently launched into remarks upon the splendid festivities at Dublin Castle, when his Excellency the Earl of Portansherry held the viceraygal coort there—festivities which, if they had ever occurred outside Costigan’s head, must have happened in some mythic Ireland where every glass is full and every story true. He spoke with relish, like a man tasting an old tale as if it were a stew re-warmed; and Pen, listening to these oft-told legends, recollected the time when he had half believed them, and had felt a certain respect for the Captain.

Emily and first love, and the little room at Chatteris, and the kind talk with Bows on the bridge, came back to him. He grew kindly disposed towards the two old friends; and when he rose to go, he shook the hands of both of them cordially. While the Captain talked, Pen had quite forgotten little Fanny Bolton; he had been absorbed in other selfish meditations, as men are apt to be when they stand near their own histories.

He only remembered her again as Bows came hobbling down the stairs after him—plainly bent upon following him out of Shepherd’s Inn, as though guarding a pantry from a cat.

That precaution was not lucky. The wrath of Mr. Arthur Pendennis rose at the poor old fellow’s feeble persecution. Confound him—what does he mean by dogging me? thought Pen; and once he was in the Strand and alone, he burst out laughing at the elder’s stratagem.

It was not an honest laugh, Arthur Pendennis. Perhaps the thought struck him as it struck you; for he blushed, ashamed of his own sense of humour, as if he had found himself chuckling over a burnt dinner he himself had spoiled.

He went off to banish the thoughts that occupied him, whatever those thoughts might be, and tried various places of amusement with indifferent success. He struggled up the highest stairs of the Panorama; but when he arrived, panting at the eminence, Care had climbed up too and stood at his elbow—quiet, faithful, and intolerable as a dripping tap in the night. He went to the Club and wrote a long letter home, exceedingly witty and sarcastic; and if he said not a single word about Vauxhall and Fanny Bolton, it was because he judged that subject—however pungent to him—would not be interesting to his mother and Laura, who preferred their correspondence without the flavour of such sauce.

Nor could the novels or the library-table fix his attention; nor the grave and respectable Jawkins (the only man in town), who wished to engage him in conversation; nor any of the amusements he tried after flying from Jawkins. He passed a Comic Theatre on his way home and saw “Stunning Farce,” “Roars of Laughter,” “Good Old English Fun and Frolic” blazing in vermilion letters on the gate—loud promises shouted in paint, as modern places shout everything, as if noise could be sold for merriment.

He went into the pit and saw the lovely Mrs. Leary, as usual, in a man’s attire; and that eminent buffo actor Tom Horseman dressed as a woman. Horseman’s travesty seemed to him a horrid, hideous degradation; Mrs. Leary’s glances and ankles had not the least effect. He laughed again—bitterly, to himself—as he remembered the effect she had produced on him the first night of his arrival in London, a short time—what a long, long time ago.
