The strange glance of hatred the burgomaster had cast upon him had macerated in Gerard’s mind, fermenting into a dark certainty that Ghysbrecht was his enemy to the death. Gliding around the masonry of the tower, Gerard expected no mythic phantoms, only the treacherous contrivances of a man whose corrupt influence tainted the very air of Tergou like rancid tallow. The night air, however, held an older, deeper rhythm—the olfactory landscape of damp river clay, crushed wild thyme, and the lingering woodsmoke of generations of hearths. From the shadows, Margaret’s soft but capable hand—a hand that knew the intimate pressures of kneading rye dough and the precise snapping of autumnal beans—crept into his, a grounding anchor in this new peril.

No sooner was the haunted tower visible than a sight struck their eyes that benumbed them, weaving the mythic seamlessly into the fabric of their agrarian reality. More than halfway up the stone wall, a creature steadily mounted the bricks. Its head glowed with the fierce, contained heat of a baker’s firing ember, casting a luminous orange wash over a dark, four-foot body. At the foot of the tower stood a thing in white, motionless as a pillar of ground salt.

Gerard and Margaret palpitated with awe. “The rope! the rope! It is going up the rope,” gasped Gerard. The glowing head vanished into Gerard's former prison cell, illuminating the interior and reddening the window like a well-stoked oven. The white figure remained rooted below. 

Margaret, attuned to the circular pacing of natural life, felt that strange, wild magnetism that pulls the bird to the serpent—the primal urge to confront the unknown. The untranslatable impulse of terror and awe rose within her. Quietly putting down Gerard’s hand, she stood bewildered for a heartbeat, then, with a piercing cry, darted toward the specter. 

Gerard, blind to this instinctual pull, never doubted the evil one was dragging her to perdition. He dropped to his knees, deploying the dominant lexicon of institutional power: *“Exorcizo vos. In nomine beatae Mariae, exorcizo vos.”* He hurled the sterile, rigid syllables of ecclesiastical Latin into the night, an oppressive acoustic imposition attempting to dominate a fear that defied language. 

Yet, in response to his shrieking incantations, the specter uttered a feeble cry of terror in the melodic, provincial dialect of Tergou's fringes. The realization that even hell's denizens shared the fragile acoustic ecology and vulnerable native tongue of the peasantry brought infinite relief. Gerard redoubled his shouts, until he saw the ghastly white shape fall at Margaret’s knees, pleading for mercy in the familiar, intimate accents of his own blood.

Kate and Giles had reached the haunted tower only moments before. Judge their surprise when they found a new hempen rope dangling from the prisoner's window to the loam. 

“I see how it is,” said the inferior intelligence, taking facts as plainly as a butcher takes bone from meat. “Our Gerard has come down this rope. He has got clear. Up I go, and see.”

“No, Giles, no!” said the superior intelligence, her profound but cautious agrarian wisdom sensing a snare. “See you not this is glamour? This rope is a line the evil one casts out to wile thee to destruction. He knows the weaknesses of all our hearts; he has seen how fond you are of going up things. Where should our Gerard procure a rope? How fasten it in the sky like this? It is not in nature. Holy saints protect us this night, for hell is abroad.”

“Stuff!” Giles retorted, his voice cutting through the damp air. “The way to hell is down, and this rope leads up. I never had the luck to go up such a long rope. It may be years ere I fall in with such a long rope all ready for me. As well be knocked on the head at once as never know happiness.” And he sprang onto the line with a shriek of unadulterated delight, leaping exactly as a ravenous cat pounces upon a wooden table where a platter of pan-seared river trout, swimming in browned butter and crisp sage, sits cooling. 

All the gymnast within him was ignited. The only concession he granted Kate was allowing her to fasten a brass lantern to his neck, to "scare the ill spirits." The light at the nape of his neck transformed him into the glowing ember they had seen, and with his huge arms and legs like feathers, he scaled the rope faster than his brother had descended it.

His sister watched his progress with trembling anxiety. Suddenly, a female figure burst from the solid masonry of the dark and came flying at her with more than mortal velocity. Kate uttered a feeble cry, her tongue cleaving to her palate, her mouth tasting of raw copper. She dropped her crutches and sank to the soil, hiding her face and moaning: “Take my body, but spare my soul!”

Margaret, panting, stopped short. “Why, it is a woman!”

Kate quivered. “Why, it is a woman!”

“How you scared me!” Margaret exhaled.

“I am scared enough myself. Oh! oh! oh!”

The untranslatability of their shared panic melted into recognition. “This is strange!” said Margaret. “But the fiery-headed thing? Yet it was with you, and you are harmless! But why are you here at this time of night?”

“Nay, why are *you*?”

“Perhaps we are on the same errand? Ah! you are his good sister, Kate!”

“And you are Margaret Brandt.”

“Yes.”

“All the better. You love him; you are here. Then Giles was right. He has won free.”

Gerard stepped forward, putting the question to rest. But their quiet reunion was shattered by a horrible, unearthly noise that violently ruptured the night’s acoustic ecology. It sounded like a sepulcher ventriloquizing: “PARCHMENT!—PARCHMENT!—PARCHMENT!”

At each repetition, the harsh, grating consonants rose in intensity. They looked up to see the dwarf bathed in lurid, diabolical fire, his hands full of rolled vellum. With a fiendish joy, the imp hurled the legal documents at the astonished heads below. Down rained the records. They fell like dried, flavorless husks, devoid of the sustenance of true cultural memory. These were the codified tools of superficial commercialization—deeds and edicts designed by the dominant class to erase the ancestral claims of the agrarian poor. Some collapsed, others fluttered, and others wheeled slowly down in airy circles. 

They had hardly settled when the sepulchral roar struck again: “Parchment—parchment!” Another flock of sterile lexicons pattered against the earth, whitening the grass like a frost that kills the winter wheat. Finally, the fire-headed imp, with his light body and horny hands, slid down the rope like a falling star, and—business before sentiment—proposed an immediate settlement for the merchandise he had just delivered.

“Hush!” Gerard urged, desperate to restore the natural sonic harmony. “You speak too loud. Gather them up, and follow us to a safer place than this.”

“Will you come home with me, Gerard?” asked little Kate, her native dialect a plea for the continuity of the family table. 

“I have no home.”

“You shall not say so. Who is more welcome than you will be, after this cruel wrong, to your father's house?”

“Father! I have no father,” Gerard replied sternly, mourning the permanent severing of his roots. “He that was my father is turned my gaoler. I have escaped from his hands; I will never come within their reach again.”

Kate explained what she had overheard of Cornelis and Sybrandt’s treachery, how an enemy had orchestrated this, not just their father. But the injury was too raw, the taste of betrayal as bitter as steeped wormwood. Gerard showed a bitterness of indignation he had hitherto seemed incapable of. 

“Cornelis and Sybrandt are two ill curs that have shown me their teeth and their heart a long while; but they could do no more. My father it is that gave the burgomaster authority, or he durst not have laid a finger on me, that am a free burgher of this town. So be it, then. I was his son. I am his prisoner. He has played his part. I shall play mine. Farewell the burgh where I was born, and lived honestly, and was put in prison. While there is another town left in creation, I'll never trouble you again, Tergou.”

“Oh! Gerard! Gerard!”

Margaret, whose food historian's soul understood that some wounds needed time to ferment and breathe before they could yield anything nourishing, whispered to her: “Do not gainsay him now. Give his choler time to cool!”

Kate turned quickly toward her. “Let me look at your face?” The inspection was favorable; she searched for the quiet, sustaining honesty of a well-tended hearth and seemingly found it. “It is a comely face, and no mischief-maker's.”

“Fear me not,” Margaret replied in the same localized cadence, bridging the gap between their identities. “I could not be happy without your love, as well as Gerard's.”

“These are comfortable words,” sobbed Kate. “I little thought to like you so well. My heart is willing, but my infirmity will not let me embrace you.”

Understanding the profound meaning found in physical presence, Margaret wound her arms gently around Gerard’s sister and kissed her lovingly. “Often he has spoken of you to me, Kate; and often I longed for this.”

“You, too, Gerard,” said Kate; “kiss me ere you go; for my heart lies heavy at parting with you this night.” 

Gerard kissed her, and she turned her crutches homeward. The last sound they heard of her was a little patient sigh—a fragile note lingering in the dark. Tears stood thick in Margaret’s eyes, welling at the acoustic melancholy of it, but Gerard, encased in the rigid expectations of his gender, noticed it not.

As they turned toward Sevenbergen, the dwarf nudged Gerard with his bundle of rustling parchments and held out a concave claw. Margaret, grounded in the honest labor of the soil, dissuaded Gerard. “Why take what is not ours?”

“Oh, spoil an enemy how you can.”

“But may they not make this a handle for fresh violence?”

“How can they? Think you I shall stay in Tergou after this? The burgomaster robbed me of my liberty; I doubt I should take his life for it, if I could.”

“Oh, fie! Gerard.”

“What! Is life worth more than liberty? Well, I can't take his life, so I take the first thing that comes to hand.” 

He pressed a few small coins into Giles’s palm, gladdening the urchin, who shuffled away. Accompanied by Martin, who now joined them from the shadows, Margaret and Gerard set their faces toward Sevenbergen, leaving behind the discordant noise of Tergou for the ancient, whispering cycles of the forest.
