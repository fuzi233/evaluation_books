"Great Powers! Will the vainglory of men, particularly the French, ever find its end?" cried Harry, his voice tinged with a mixture of indignation and weary amusement. "Can it truly be believed that following the skirmish at Saint-Cast—a mere tactical maneuver to sever a rear guard, as you know—they were so devoid of compassion as to waste countless pounds of gunpowder firing blindly at the Invalides in Paris? And then to boast and bluster over our misfortune! Is there any true magnanimity in cheering and huzzahing simply because five or six hundred brave souls were overwhelmed by ten thousand on a beach, meeting a fate often reserved for the stragglers?

"I once felt compelled to sketch a vivid portrait of the rejoicings in London celebrating our supposed glory at Saint-Malo. I imagined the polished cannons paraded by our gallant tars, the sturdy horse-grenadiers prancing proudly, while the mob waved hats, roared with cheers, and picked pockets. Meanwhile, our friends in a balcony overlooking Fleet Street watched, blessing this scene of British triumph. But now that the French Invalides have been so undignified as to mimic the Tower of London, setting up their Saint-Cast against our Saint-Malo, I scorn to revisit such a stale topic. I say *Nolo*, not *Malo*; let me be content if Harry returns from one expedition and another with his skin intact. And have I ever suggested he was even bruised? For fear of alarming my fair young readers, I declared he was as well as he had ever been in his life. The sea air merely bronzed his cheeks, and the whistling bullets spared his side-curls. The ocean dampened his gaiters and clothes without ever attempting to swallow him whole. True, he briefly exposed the lapels of his coat to the enemy, but only for the shortest possible duration, retreating from their gaze with all speed. What are lapels but reverses? Coats possess them, as do men; and our duty is to wear them with courage and good humor."

"I can tell you," Harry continued, recounting the tale to Miss Hetty, "that we were all forced to run for our lives. When our lines finally broke, survival belonged to whoever could reach the boats fastest. The French cavalry and infantry pursued us down to the shore, intermingling with us, hacking down our men and driving bayonets into the earth. Poor Armytage was shot ahead of me and fell. I scooped him up and stumbled through the churning surf until we reached a boat. It was fortunate the sailors aboard weren't terrified; shots whistled past their ears, shattered their oar-blades, and riddled their flag. Yet, the officer in command remained as cool as if he were sipping punch in Portsmouth—a drink we enjoyed upon landing, I assure you. Poor Sir John was less fortunate than I. He never made it to the ship. The service has lost a fine soldier, and Miss Howe a true gentleman to her husband. Such casualties happen; consequently, his brother receives the promotion—the baronetcy."

"It is the poor lady I am thinking of," Miss Hetty interrupted, her voice trembling slightly. "And the King. Why did His Majesty encourage Sir John Armytage to go? A gentleman cannot refuse a command from such a quarter. Now the poor gentleman lies dead! Oh, what a state of distress His Majesty must be in!"

"I have no doubt His Majesty will be in a profound state of grief," replied her father, shaking his head solemnly.

"Now you are laughing!" Hetty exclaimed, turning to him. "Do you mean, sir, that when a gentleman dies in his service, literally at his feet, the King of England feels nothing?"

"If I thought that, I vow I would support the Pretender!" the General interjected sharply. He saw the turmoil in Hetty's eyes—a mix of remorse, consternation, and overwhelming gratitude that the danger had passed—and knew her small heart was racing.

"No, my dear," the General said gently. "Were kings to weep for every soldier, what a burden you would place upon them! I hold a higher opinion of His Majesty than to suppose him so weak. Furthermore, if Miss Hester Lambert were to embrace the Pretender, I doubt she would find any greater happiness. That family was never renowned for excessive sentimentality."

"But if the King sent Harry—I mean, Sir John Armytage—directly into the war where he lost his life, ought not His Majesty to repent deeply?" the young lady pressed.

"If Harry had fallen, undoubtedly the court would have entered mourning," remarked the General. "As it stands, gentlemen and ladies wore colored clothing yesterday."

"Why shouldn't we light bonfires for a defeat and don sackcloth and ashes after a victory?" asked George.

"I protest, I do not wish to thank Heaven for aiding us in burning the ships at Cherbourg," George countered.

"Yes, you do, George! Though I have no right to speak, and you are certainly not cleverer than usual. Still, when your country wins, you are glad—I know I am. When I flee before the French, I am ashamed; I cannot help it, though I did it," admitted Harry. "'Tis not right, somehow, that Englishmen should have to do such things," he added gravely. George smiled but chose not to press his brother on what the Frenchman's perspective might be.

"'Tis a wretched business," Harry continued, his tone heavy. "But luckily, it was no worse. The story regarding the French is that their Governor, the Duke of Aiguillon, was something of a 'moistened chicken.' Our entire retreat might have been annihilated had we not moved with such haste. The French local militia behaved famously, I am happy to report, along with numerous gentleman volunteers who stood fast in the front ranks as they should. They say the Chevalier of Tour d'Auvergne engaged despite the Duke's explicit orders. Officers brought lists of prisoners and wounded to General Bligh and Lord Howe. He is now a lord, having received the title upon news of his brother's death reaching home, George. He is a brave fellow, whether titled or not."

"And his sister, who was betrothed to poor Sir John Armytage... consider her plight!" sighed Miss Hetty, recently prone to sentimentality.

"And his mother!" cried Mrs. Lambert. "Have you read her address in the papers to the electors of Nottingham? 'Lord Howe being now absent upon public service, and Lieutenant-Colonel Howe with his regiment at Louisbourg, it rests upon me to beg the favor of your votes...' Isn't this a gallant woman?"

"A laconic woman," observed George.

"How can sons fail to be brave when nurtured by a mother like that?" asked the General.

Our two young men exchanged glances. "If one of us falls defending our country, we have a mother in Sparta who would think and write thus," George noted.

"If Sparta is anywhere near Virginia, I reckon we have it," Harry remarked dryly. Then, pensively, he added, "To think that we should both meet the enemy and both be whipped by him, brother!"

Hetty looked at him, recalling the image of him the day prior: tottering through the water toward the boats, his comrade bleeding on his shoulder, enemies pursuing, bullets flying thick and fast. It was she who had driven him into that peril. Her words had stung him. Yet, now that he had returned, he offered no rebuke. Unless questioned, he spoke sparingly of his adventures. He was grave and courteous with Hetty, but especially frank and tender with the rest of the family. Those taunts, however, had wounded him.

"Little hand!" his demeanor seemed to whisper, "you should not have raised yourself against me! To scorn anyone is ill; to scorn one so devoted to you and yours is worse. I may lack quick wit, but in matters of the heart, I stand equal to the best, and the finest heart your family has known."

Harry's injustice, coupled with his magnanimous endurance of it, allowed him to reclaim Miss Hetty's esteem, erasing the loss suffered during his previous months of inglorious idleness. The respect she paid to the brave transformed her. She was no longer sharp-tongued or sarcastic regarding his conduct. In short, Miss Hetty was humbled, altered, and improved. Consequently, the entire world seemed to shift in Harry's favor, just as he shifted in theirs. No longer sulky or indolent, he ceased to despise himself or defy his neighbors. The colonel of his regiment reported his behavior as exemplary and recommended him for one of the commissions vacated by the expedition's casualties. Although the mission ended unluckily, it proved fortuitous for him.

His brother-volunteers, returning to St. James's Street, spoke highly of his conduct. These volunteers and their deeds became the talk of everyone. Had he been a general commanding, slain in the heat of victory, Sir John Armytage could hardly have garnered more sympathy than the nation showed him. The newspapers overflowed with letters in his honor, while men of wit and sensibility competed to compose epitaphs. The fate of his fiancée was widely mourned. As previously stated, she was the sister of the brave Commodore who had just returned from this unfortunate campaign, succeeding to the title of his elder brother—an officer as gallant as himself—who had recently fallen in America.
