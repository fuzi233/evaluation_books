“There are briars besetting every path,
Which call for patient care;
There is a cross in every lot,
And an earnest need for prayer.”

Margaret stepped out into the street with reluctance, her spirits heavy and her steps slow. But as she walked, the mere act of moving through Milton’s narrow lanes began to stir something within her—the fresh air, the distant hum of life, the rhythm of the city waking up around her. Before long, her pace lightened, her cheeks flushed with color, and her thoughts lifted from their inward burden. She began to notice the world again: men with hands deep in pockets, strolling idly; groups of girls laughing loudly, their voices sharp and defiant; others standing near the doors of gin-shops, smoking and muttering about passersby as if they owned the street.

She had planned to walk through the fields beyond the town, but the thought of that long journey now seemed daunting. Instead, she turned toward Bessy Higgins’s home. It wouldn’t be the quiet escape she’d hoped for, but perhaps it would be kinder—more meaningful.

Nicholas Higgins sat by the hearth, pipe in hand, when she entered. Bessy was rocking gently in her chair on the other side of the room. Nicholas paused mid-puff, stood, and pushed his chair aside for Margaret to sit. He leaned against the mantel, arms crossed, watching her as she asked after Bessy’s health.

“Hoo’s down i’ th’ spirits,” he said, “but better in body. Hoo doesn’t like this strike. Hoo likes peace more than anything.”

“This is th’ third strike I’ve seen,” Bessy sighed, as if that explained everything.

“Well,” Nicholas replied, “third time pays for all. See if we don’t bring the masters to their knees this time. See if they don’t come crawling back, begging us to return at our own price. We’ve missed our chance before, I’ll grant you—but not this time. This time we’ve laid our plans deep.”

Margaret looked at them both, curious. “But why do you strike?” she asked. “Is it simply refusing to work until you get your wages? That’s what I understand it to mean. And I admit—I know nothing about strikes. Where I come from, such things never happen.”

“I wish I were there,” Bessy murmured, eyes distant.

“But it’s not for me to grow tired of strikes,” Nicholas said. “This will be the last one I see. Before it ends, I shall be gone—to the Great City, the Holy Jerusalem.”

“He sees only the next world,” Nicholas added, “so he can’t bear this one. Me? I’m bound to make the best of this life. A bird in the hand is worth two in the bush. So that’s how we differ on the strike.”

Margaret hesitated. “But if people struck where I come from—if they stopped working—what would happen? The fields wouldn’t be sown, the hay wouldn’t be gathered, the harvest lost.”

“Well?” Nicholas challenged, puffing on his pipe.

“If the farmers couldn’t hire laborers,” she continued, “they’d have no crops to sell. Then where would the money come from to pay wages next year?”

He smoked on, silent. At last, he said, “I know little of your ways down South. I’ve heard they’re a people crushed, spiritless, too broken by hardship to even know when they’re being wronged. But not here. Here, we know when we’re being cheated. We’ve got blood in our veins. We won’t stand for it. We take our hands from the looms and say, ‘You may break us, but you won’t dominate us, my masters!’ And this time—by God—they won’t!”

“I wish I lived down South,” Bessy whispered.

“There’s much to endure there,” Margaret said softly.

“There’s sorrow everywhere,” she added. “Hard labor, little food, no rest. But at least it’s outdoors.”

“And away from the endless noise, the stifling heat,” Bessy agreed.

“It’s cold or wet sometimes,” Margaret said. “A young person can bear it. But an old man—rheumatism sets in, his body breaks down before its time. Yet he must keep working—or go to the workhouse.”

Bessy nodded slowly. “I thought you admired the South so much.”

“I do,” Margaret admitted, smiling faintly. “But I meant only that every place has good and bad. Since you’ve suffered here, I thought it fair you should know the burdens of the South too.”

“And you say they never strike there?” Nicholas asked sharply.

“No,” Margaret answered. “I think they have too much sense.”

“And I think,” he replied, tossing the ashes from his pipe with such force it shattered, “it’s not because they’re wise—but because they lack courage.”

“Oh, father!” Bessy protested. “What have you gained by striking? Remember the first strike—when mother died? We all had to suffer. You worse than any. And yet many kept going back week after week, taking the same wage, until all were back at work. Some became beggars for life.”

“Aye,” Nicholas said quietly. “That strike was poorly led. Men who were fools or not true men took charge. But this time—it’ll be different.”

Margaret waited, then said, “But still—you haven’t told me what you’re striking *for*.”

“Why,” he said, “there are five or six masters who’ve been paying us the same wages for two years—wages that kept us alive and let them grow rich. Now they come to us and say we must accept less. We won’t. We’ll starve them first—and see who works for them then. They’ll have killed the goose that laid their golden eggs.”

“So you plan to die,” Margaret said, “just to punish them?”

“No,” he replied. “I don’t plan to die. But I’m ready to die at my post rather than give in. That’s called honor in a soldier. Why not in a poor weaver?”

“But a soldier dies for his country—for others.”

He laughed, a dry, bitter sound. “My lass, you’re young. But don’t you think I can feed three people—Bessy, Mary, and myself—on sixteen shillings a week? Do you think I strike for myself? No. I fight for others—as much as any soldier. Only while he dies for strangers, I fight for John Boucher next door, whose wife is sick and has eight children too young for the factory. I fight for justice. Why should we earn less now than two years ago?”

“Perhaps trade is hard,” Margaret suggested. “Maybe they can’t afford to pay the same.”

“Trade?” Nicholas scoffed. “That’s just their excuse. It’s about wages. They control trade—manipulate it like a puppet show to scare us into submission. Their job is to squeeze us, to swell their profits. Ours is to stand firm—not just for ourselves, but for everyone around us. We help build their wealth. Shouldn’t we have a say in how it’s spent? We’ve saved money now. We’re resolved to stand together. Not one of us will return unless the Union says it’s fair. So hooray for the strike! Let Thornton, Slickson, Hamper, and their kind look to it!”

“Thornton?” Margaret echoed. “Mr. Thornton of Marlborough Street?”

“Aye,” Nicholas said. “Thornton of Marlborough Mill. He’s one of them.”

“What sort of master is he?”

“Have you ever seen a bulldog?” Nicholas asked. “Put one on hind legs, dress him in coat and breeches—and you’ve got John Thornton.”

Margaret smiled. “No,” she said, “I deny it. Mr. Thornton is plain enough, but he’s not like a bulldog—with its broad nose and snarling lip.”

“Not in looks,” he conceded. “But let him get hold of an idea—and he’ll cling to it like a bulldog. You could pull him off with a pitchfork before he’d let go. He’s worth fighting with. As for Slickson—he’ll lure his men back with sweet words, then cheat them once they’re under his thumb. He’s slippery as an eel. Like a cat—sleek, cunning, fierce. No honest fight with him. But Thornton—he’s stubborn as a door-nail. Obstinate, every inch. The old bulldog!”

Margaret turned to Bessy. “Poor Bessy,” she said. “You sigh over all this. You don’t like the struggle, do you?”

“No,” Bessy said heavily. “I’m weary of it. I wished I’d heard different talk in my final days—no more clashing, clanging, and arguing about work, wages, masters, and hands. Just peace.”

“Poor girl,” Nicholas said. “Your days are far from ending. You look better already for a change. And I’ll be here to keep things lively.”

“Tobacco smoke chokes me,” Bessy muttered.

“Then I’ll never smoke in this house again,” Nicholas replied tenderly. “But why didn’t you tell me sooner, you foolish wench?”

She said nothing for a moment. Then, so quietly that only Margaret heard: “I reckon he’ll want all the comfort he can get—from his pipe, or drink—before it’s done.”

Nicholas went outside, clearly to finish his pipe. Bessy spoke passionately. “Am I not a fool? Am I not, Miss? I knew I should have kept him home—away from those who tempt a man to drink during a strike. And there—my tongue quarreled with his pipe—and now he’ll go off, I know he will, whenever he wants to smoke. And no one knows where it might end. I wish I’d let myself be choked first.”

“Does your father drink?” Margaret asked gently.

“No—not really,” Bessy replied, voice still wild. “But what would you do? There are days—like everyone else—when you just ache for a change, a spark. I’ve bought bread from another baker just because I couldn’t face the same sight, the same sound, the same taste, day after day. I’ve longed to be a man, to go out and roam—even if only to find new work. And men—father, all men—feel that stronger than I do. What can they do? It’s no fault if they go to the gin-shop to make their blood flow faster, to see colors, reflections, things they never see otherwise. Father’s never been a drunkard—but maybe he’s gotten worse now and then. Still,” her voice softened, “at times like this, when hope starts high but the weight grows heavy—where’s the comfort? He gets angry, mad—all of them do. Then he’s exhausted from anger, and might do things he’d regret. Bless your sweet face, but you don’t know what a strike truly is.”

“Come, Bessy,” Margaret said softly. “I won’t say you exaggerate—I don’t know enough. But perhaps, since you’re unwell, you’re seeing only the dark side. There may be brighter things too.”

“You speak easily,” Bessy said, “who’ve lived in green places all your life, never known want or worry—or evil, for that matter.”

“Take care,” Margaret said, her cheeks flushing, her eyes brightening. “How you judge, Bessy. I go home to my mother—who is so ill, so terribly ill, that death is the only escape from her suffering. Yet I must speak cheerfully to my father, who knows nothing of her real condition. The only one who could comfort her—whose presence would mean more than anything—is falsely accused. If he came to see her, he’d risk his life. I tell you this—only you, Bessy. Don’t speak of it. No one else in Milton—perhaps no one in England—knows. Do I not have care? Anxiety? Though I wear fine clothes and eat well, do I not suffer?”

Bessy bowed her head. “I ask your pardon. Sometimes, when I think of my life—and the little joy I’ve known—I’ve believed I was one of those doomed to die by a falling star from heaven—‘And the name of the star is called Wormwood; and the third part of the waters became wormwood; and men died of the waters, because they were made bitter.’ One bears pain better if it feels destined. Otherwise, it seems pointless.”

“Nay, Bessy,” Margaret said. “God does not delight in pain. Don’t dwell on prophecy. Read the clearer parts of Scripture.”

“I dare say it’s wiser,” Bessy replied, “but where would I hear such grand promises—words so far removed from this dreary world, this town above all—except in Revelation? Many times I’ve repeated the seventh chapter just for the sound. It’s like music—like an organ. No, I cannot abandon Revelation. It gives me more comfort than any other book.”

“Let me come and read some of my favorite chapters,” Margaret offered.

“Aye,” Bessy said eagerly. “Come. Father won’t hear you. He says my talk is useless—has nothing to do with today’s matters. That’s his business.”

“Where is your sister?”

“Gone fustian cutting. I didn’t want her to go—but we must live. The Union can’t support us much.”

“I must go now,” Margaret said. “But you’ve done me good, Bessy.”

“I done you good?” Bessy asked, surprised.

“Yes,” Margaret said. “I came here sad, thinking my grief was the only one in the world. But hearing of your years of struggle—your endurance—makes me stronger.”

“Bless you,” Bessy whispered. “I thought all good came from gentlefolk. I’ll grow proud if I believe I’ve helped you.”

“You won’t,” Margaret said. “If you think about it, you’ll only puzzle yourself. That’s one comfort.”

“You’re unlike anyone I’ve ever seen,” Bessy murmured. “I don’t know what to make of you.”

“Nor I of myself,” Margaret said. “Good-bye.”

Bessy stopped rocking, staring after her.

“I wonder if there are many like her down South,” she whispered. “She’s like a breath of country air—freshens me up. Who’d have thought that face—bright and strong as the angel I dream of—could carry such sorrow? I wonder how she’ll sin. All of us must sin. I think a great deal of her. But father does the same. Even Mary. It’s rare that she notices much.”
