The librarian’s knuckles bleached white where he curled them around the edge of his pitted oak desk, fine dust motes spinning in the unearthly silver glow leaking through the high, unglassed library windows. “We must be on our guard,” he said, his voice low enough that it barely disturbed the hush of the surrounding shelves. “Or she will outwit us again. She would befool the very elect.”
“How are we even supposed to guard against her?” I asked, the cold prickle of unease creeping up the back of my neck.
“Every way there is,” he answered. “She fears her own child, and that fear curdled long ago to unending hate. She is in this house right now, only passing through to destroy her. To her, the birth of any child is the death of the parent that sired them, every new generation a natural enemy of the one that came before. Her daughter looks to her like an open, gushing channel, draining away the immortality she has convinced herself is her birthright, self-contained and unassailable. To plug that channel, she has hunted the girl since almost the hour she was born, with unrelenting, unforgiving malice.
“But every one of her schemes to kill her backfired. Instead of wiping the child out, the hunt left a whole thriving colony of the Little Ones settled in the realm she claims as her personal fiefdom, and that girl is their heart, their head, their sheltering wings. My Eve ached for that child for centuries, longed to care for her as a mother tends her firstborn, but we were not yet fit to raise her. So we carried her deep into the uncharted liminal wilderness, and for ages we knew nothing of her fate. But she was fostered by forces older than our worlds, young winged beings for her playmates, and she never knew a single moment of care until she found a wailing abandoned half-human infant tangled in the brambles, and the dormant mother-heart inside her woke wide open.
“One by one she has gathered dozens more of the lost children since, and that heart of hers still has room to grow. Her family is her whole world, no children ever were loved or protected better. Her authority over them is absolute, but she does not even know she holds it: it never shows itself except in quiet watchfulness and gentle service. She has forgotten entirely the life she lived before the children, and truly believes she walked out of the woods the first of their little family.”
He leaned forward, his gaze sharp. “You saved the life of the one that wants them all dead. That means your life now belongs to her and every last one of the Little Ones. Lilith the princess was already halfway to their colony when the barrier stream struck her down with its own vengeance, and she would have dissolved back into unmade shadow forever if you had not dragged her from the water. You did, and if not for the lingering curse of the stream, she would have been raging through their homes long before now.
“But she found a loophole. She barred herself from our shared three-dimensional world eons ago, the day she destroyed her old mortal body—she could never re-enter it, not unless she made direct physical contact with someone who still belonged to that physical plane. You gave her that exact chance. She never had it once, in all her thousands of years of life. The lightest possible touch of her hand brushed one of your muffled boots every single step you climbed that path out of the stream, anchoring herself to you. Right now, she is locked in that small back chamber, waiting for the exact second no one is watching to slip free.”
“She can’t know anything about that transdimensional door! She can’t possibly know how to open it!” I blurted, but my chest felt tight, and my words were far more confident than my thudding heart.
“Hush, hush!” the librarian whispered, one hand lifted to silence me, his eyes sharp. “She can hear through anything. There is no wall, no gap between realities that blocks her senses. You must leave right now, make your way straight to my wife’s cottage. I will stay here to keep guard over her.”
“Let me go straight to the Little Ones instead!” I cried.
“Beware that urge, Mr. Vane. Go to my wife, and do exactly what she tells you.”
His advice did not make sense to me. Why waste time running to his wife when I could rush to defend the children? Even then I only half-believed him, enough to ask questions but not enough to obey without pushing back. “First tell me, Mr. Raven—why on earth did you lock her up in that exact closet? The night I ran from your house, I stumbled straight into that very same nook!”
“That closet is no closer to our cottage, and no farther from it, than any other point in this entire library,” he said simply.
I shook my head, too stubborn to accept what I could not wrap my head around. “Then how is it that just earlier, you pulled a whole book from that door, when all I saw and felt was a single half-page of it sticking through? The other half, you said, existed somewhere on your library shelf. When you put the book back, won’t the other half stick through the door again? Does that mean two parts of the same book can exist at the same time in two separate places? Can one half be in space, somewhere, and the other half out of space, nowhere at all?”
“I wish I could explain it to you,” he answered, a note of gentle regret in his voice. “But your mind was not built to parse this kind of reality. This phenomenon is not just inexplicable to you—the very nature of it is something you cannot yet even perceive. I do not fully understand it myself, either. But you live every single day with things you do not, and can not, understand. You think you grasp gravity, or sunlight, or the way water flows, but your ‘understanding’ of those things is nothing more than being used to them, so you do not feel surprise when they happen. You accept them not because you get how they work, but because you have no choice—they are there, and they touch your life whether you like it or not.
“The truth is, no human being fully understands anything at all. The moment you realize you do not understand, that is your first tottering, unsteady step—not toward instant comprehension, but toward the day you will finally be capable of understanding. You are not used to these liminal rules, so you do not pretend to know them. No one can force this knowledge on you. But I can, perhaps, help you learn to believe.”
He walked to the closet door, and let out a low, soft whistle, then stood silent, listening. A heartbeat later, I heard or thought I heard the faint whir of wings, and looked up to see a pure white dove perch for a single instant on the top of the shelves above the old oil portrait, then glide down to land on Mr. Raven’s shoulder, tucking its head against his cheek. I watched the two of them move their heads gently in quiet conversation, but I heard not a single word. When I blinked, the dove was gone, and he was walking back to his seat.
“Why did you whistle?” I asked. “Sound from our world can’t possibly carry through that door, can it?”
“You are exactly right,” he said. “I whistled so that you would see I called her. It was not the sound itself that reached her, but the meaning behind the whistle. There is not a single minute left to waste. You must go.”
“I will go right now,” I said, turning for the library door.
“You will sleep tonight at our roadside hostelry,” he said, not as a question, but in a tone of quiet, unshakable authority.
“My heart is with the children,” I protested. “But if you absolutely insist—”
“I do insist. You can do nothing to help them otherwise. I will walk you as far as the mirror chamber, and see you off properly.”
He rose to his feet. A thunderous crash shook the closet door. The leopardess that was Lilith had flung her full weight against it. I looked back at him, my throat tight.
“Come on,” he urged.
Before we made it to the library exit, a howling, demon-like yell echoed after us, mixed with the rasp of sharp claws scoring deep gouges into the solid oak. I hesitated, half-turning back.
“To leave her there all alone, bleeding from that terrible wound—” I murmured.
“Nothing will ever close that wound,” he said, with a soft, heavy sigh. “It is not a cut in her flesh, but a gash through her soul. Annihilation itself is no death to evil. Only good, growing in the exact space evil once occupied, can kill evil for good. An evil thing will carry its own malice forever, until the day it chooses to stop being evil. That is the only thing that can slay it.”
I held my peace until a new, splintering crack of splitting wood reached us. “What if she breaks free?” I cried.
“Hurry,” he said. “I will rush back the second you are through the mirror, and re-arrange the mirrors to block her path after you.”
We ran, and burst breathless into the wooden mirror chamber. Mr. Raven grabbed the hanging chains, adjusted the dimming hood, then lined the three mirrors up in their correct, reality-warping alignment, stepping next to me in front of the largest standing mirror. Through the glass, the familiar mountain ranges were already clearing from the mist.
Between us, tearing the space open with a shriek no human throat could make, the huge spotted bulk of the leopardess Lilith burst. She leaped through the mirror as if it were no more than an open window, and settled instantly into a low, swift, ground-eating gallop straight toward the Little Ones’ hidden colony.
I stared in horrified dismay, and sprang through the mirror after her. Adam followed me at a casual, unhurried pace.
“You do not need to run,” he called. “You will never overtake her. She has had thousands of years to learn every shortcut through this world.” He turned in the opposite direction from her fleeing shape. “She knows more hidden tricks of reality than I care to count. We will do what we can, no more.”
“I am not going to let her hurt those children,” I said, breaking into a run, but I slowed a moment later, sick in my stomach as I watched her spotted shape dwindle to a dot on the horizon. I turned back to him.
“Doubtless we will do what we can,” he agreed. “But my wife already warned Mara, the white guardian, and she will do her part. You must sleep first. You gave me your word.”
“I will not break it,” I said. “But surely action comes before rest right now! We can not waste time sleeping while the children are in danger.”
“A man can do nothing that he is not fit to do,” he said, nodding toward the skyline. “Look. Did I not tell you Mara would do her part?”
I followed his gaze, and saw a bright white spot moving fast at a sharp diagonal, cutting straight across the path Lilith was taking.
“There she is!” he cried. “The spotted leopardess is fierce, but the white one is far stronger.”
“I saw them fight once before,” I said. “It did not look like a decisive win for the white one.”
“How could eyes that have never once woken fully to this world tell who won? The princess never once admits she was beaten—she never will. But she fled that fight. One day soon she will finally admit that kicking against the goad of her own unacknowledged goodness is useless, and that is the day her new dawn will finally begin. Come on, come. He who is not yet strong enough to act for the good of the children must hurry to sleep, so he will be ready when the time for action comes.”
