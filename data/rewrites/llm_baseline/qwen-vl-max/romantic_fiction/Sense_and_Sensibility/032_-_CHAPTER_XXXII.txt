CHAPTER XXXII. When Elinor shared the details of her conversation with Marianne, she expected a deeper reaction—perhaps even a spark of renewed hope or anger. But Marianne’s response was quiet, almost unnervingly so. She listened without protest, without defense for Willoughby, and without a single word to justify him. Her eyes filled with tears, not of rage, but of sorrow too deep for words. It was clear she accepted the truth—not just that Willoughby had deceived her, but that he had betrayed others before her.

Elinor saw the change in her sister: the way she now greeted Colonel Brandon with a softness she had once refused, how she spoke to him with a gentle respect tinged with pity. Her agitation had lessened, but it was replaced by something worse—a quiet despair. The loss of Willoughby’s affection had been painful enough, but now the full weight of his character came crashing down upon her. His betrayal of Miss Williams, the suffering of that young woman, and the chilling thought that he might have once meant to deceive her too—all of it settled into her heart like a stone. She could not speak of it, even to Elinor. Instead, she withdrew into silence, letting her grief fester in private, which only made Elinor ache more for her.

Mrs. Dashwood’s reaction, when she read Elinor’s letter, mirrored the daughters’ pain—though perhaps even more deeply. Her disappointment was profound, her indignation fierce. She wrote back at once, pouring out her sorrow and urging Marianne to endure with strength. To hear her mother speak of "fortitude" was heartbreaking—proof that this wound was far beyond anything mere time could heal. Mrs. Dashwood knew the source of the pain was not just lost love, but shame and humiliation, and she did not want her daughter to dwell on either.

She insisted the girls stay in London longer than planned, not because she wished them to linger in sorrow, but because she believed Barton would only make things worse. Every corner of the cottage reminded Marianne of Willoughby—the garden where they walked, the room where they talked, the very air that carried memories. By staying in town, surrounded by new faces and distractions, Marianne might find moments of respite, even if only brief ones. And besides, her son-in-law and his wife were coming to London soon. It was important, Mrs. Dashwood felt, that they see their brother.

Marianne agreed to follow her mother’s advice, though silently she resisted. She longed to be near her mother, to feel her comfort, to cry in her arms. But instead, she was left in a city where every person seemed to know the story, where gossip hummed through drawing rooms like a bitter wind. Still, she found solace in knowing that her sacrifice would bring some good—her sister would not have to face Edward so soon.

Elinor, too, had her own burden. Though she hoped to avoid Edward entirely, she feared it was impossible. Yet she took comfort in thinking that staying longer might help Marianne more than returning home would. And she guarded her sister carefully—ensuring no one mentioned Willoughby’s name. Her efforts were not wasted. Neither Mrs. Jennings nor Sir John dared speak of him in front of Marianne. Even Mrs. Palmer, though furious, kept her tongue in check.

But Elinorum could not escape the storm of outrage around her. Sir John raged about the man he once admired. “A good-natured fellow! A bold rider! And now—such a scoundrel!” he thundered. “I’d rather see him dead than speak to him again. Not even if we were both trapped in the same covert for two hours.” He vowed never to acknowledge him again, calling him a “deceitful dog” who once offered him a puppy from Folly’s litter—only to betray everyone.

Mrs. Palmer was equally incensed. “I’ll drop him immediately,” she declared. “I’m thankful I never really knew him. If Combe Magna weren’t so close to Cleveland, I’d never have minded—but now I hate him so much I won’t even say his name again. I’ll tell everyone what a useless creature he is.”

Yet her anger was matched by curiosity. She eagerly gathered news of the wedding—where the carriage was being built, who painted Willoughby’s portrait, where Miss Grey’s clothes could be seen. She passed it all along to Elinor, as if it were gossip worth sharing.

Lady Middleton, however, offered a different kind of relief. Calm, polite, and utterly unconcerned, she barely acknowledged the affair. Once or twice a day, she would murmur, “It is very shocking, indeed,” and then move on. No questions, no prying, no emotional demands. For Elinor, whose spirits were often crushed under the weight of others’ sympathy, this quiet dignity was a balm. Sometimes, she found herself valuing good manners above kindness, simply because they allowed her peace.

Colonel Brandon, meanwhile, remained a quiet presence. His inquiries were gentle, never intrusive. He had earned the right to speak openly with Elinor, having shown such care and compassion during the crisis. Their conversations were always respectful, sometimes tender. And though he never said much, the look Marianne gave him at times—pitying, softened—was enough to reassure him that his efforts had not been in vain. Elinor saw it too and dared to hope that one day, perhaps, there might be something more between them.

But Mrs. Jennings, ignorant of these subtleties, began to lose faith. After two days, she sighed, “Well, if it’s not Midsummer, it’ll be Michaelmas.” By the end of the week, she decided it wouldn’t happen at all. The Colonel’s silence, his continued seriousness, made her think the match was over. And with that, she began to imagine other possibilities—perhaps Miss Dashwood would claim the mulberry-tree and yew arbour after all. Mrs. Ferrars faded from her mind.

Early in February, less than two weeks after receiving Willoughby’s letter, Elinor delivered the final blow: he was married. She had taken care to learn the news first, so Marianne would not discover it in the papers, which she checked each morning with trembling hands.

Marianne heard it with quiet dignity. She said nothing at first, shed no tears—then, suddenly, they came, flooding out uncontrollably. She sat in silence for the rest of the day, wrapped in sorrow so thick it seemed to suffocate her.

The Willoughbys left London quickly, and Elinor hoped this might finally allow her sister to begin healing. Perhaps, slowly, she could coax Marianne out of the house again.

At the same time, the two Miss Steeles returned to London, newly arrived at their cousin’s home in Holburn. They appeared at Conduit Street and Berkeley Street, welcomed with open arms by their relatives. All except Elinor, who felt a cold knot tighten in her chest at their sight.

Lucy Steele, especially, seemed delighted. “I should have been *so* disappointed if I hadn’t found you here still,” she said, emphasizing the word with a smile. “But I always knew you’d stay. You told me you’d leave after a month, but I knew better. It would have been such a pity to go before your brother and sister came. And now—well, you’ll be in no hurry to leave, will you? I’m so glad you didn’t keep to your word.”

Elinor forced a smile. She understood Lucy’s meaning perfectly—she wanted to believe Elinor would stay long enough to marry Edward. But she held her tongue.

Mrs. Jennings, ever eager for drama, asked, “And how did you travel?”

“Not by stage,” Miss Steele replied proudly. “We came post all the way, and had a very smart beau with us. Dr. Davies was going to town, so we joined him in a chaise. He behaved most genteelly and paid ten or twelve shillings extra.”

“Oh, oh!” Mrs. Jennings clapped her hands. “Very pretty indeed! And the doctor is unmarried, I’ll wager.”

“Now, everybody laughs at me about the Doctor,” Miss Steele simpered. “My cousins say I’ve made a conquest. But I assure you, I don’t think of him at all. ‘Lord! Here comes your beau, Nancy,’ my cousin said the other day. My beau? Why, I don’t know who you mean. The Doctor isn’t my beau.”

“Aye, aye,” Mrs. Jennings chuckled. “That’s fine talk—but I see the man.”

“No, indeed!” Miss Steele protested, with mock seriousness. “And I beg you, if you ever hear it said, please contradict it.”

Mrs. Jennings promised she would—and Miss Steele beamed with satisfaction.

Then Lucy turned to Elinor. “You’ll go and stay with your brother and sister when they come to town, I suppose?”

“No,” Elinor replied gently. “I don’t think we shall.”

“Oh, yes, I dare say you will.”

Elinor let it pass. “How delightful it must be,” she added, “that Mrs. Dashwood can spare you both for so long.”

“Long a time, indeed!” Mrs. Jennings interjected. “Their visit has only just begun!”

Lucy fell silent.

“I’m sorry we can’t see your sister,” Miss Steele said, turning to Elinor. “I’m sorry she’s not well—” for Marianne had already left the room.

“You’re very kind,” Elinor replied. “She would be sorry to miss you, but she’s been plagued lately with nervous headaches. She’s not fit for company.”

“Oh, dear—that’s a great pity! But such old friends as Lucy and me—we ought to be able to see her. I’m sure we wouldn’t say a word.”

Elinor declined politely. “She may be resting—or dressed in her gown. She’s not ready to receive visitors.”

“Oh, if that’s all,” Miss Steele exclaimed, “we can just go and see her.”

Elinor braced herself—but before she could respond, Lucy sharply rebuked her sister. “Nancy, you mustn’t be so forward.” And just as before, Lucy’s sharp tone silenced her cousin, preserving the fragile peace.
