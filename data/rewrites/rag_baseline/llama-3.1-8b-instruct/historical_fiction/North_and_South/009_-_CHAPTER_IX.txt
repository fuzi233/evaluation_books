As the east wind howled through the streets of Milton, its icy fingers clawing at the shutters of the Hale residence, Margaret Hale huddled in the little drawing-room, her needle poised over the delicate folds of a lace handkerchief. Her mother, seated in her favorite armchair, seemed lost in thought, her eyes closed as if attempting to escape the bitter draughts that seeped through the cracks of their modest home. The air inside was heavy with the scent of damp wool and woodsmoke, and Margaret's hands moved deftly as she worked, her thoughts drifting to the tasks she had set aside for herself: a letter to Edith, a translation of Dante's verses, and a visit to the Higginses. But those pleasures would have to wait; her father had announced that their neighbor, Mr. Thornton, would be joining them for tea that evening.

Margaret's fingers faltered as she listened to her mother's querulous protests. "What does the man want to come here for?" she demanded, her voice like a rusty gate. "And Dixon is washing my muslins and laces, and there is no soft water with these horrid east winds, which I suppose we shall have all the year round in Milton." Mr. Hale, hovering near the window, gazed out at the smoke drifting lazily from the chimneys, his expression lost in thought. "The wind is veering round, my dear," he ventured, "but I'm afraid I've lost track of the points of the compass."

Margaret rolled her eyes good-naturedly, setting aside her needlework. "Don't tell me, Papa," she said, smiling wryly. "But, east or west wind, I suppose this man comes." Her words dripped with a mixture of resignation and annoyance, and her mother shot her a warning glance. Mr. Hale, however, seemed undeterred, launching into a description of Mr. Thornton's sturdy constitution and his ability to brave any adversity that came his way. Margaret listened with a skeptical ear, her mind wandering to the prospect of Mr. Thornton's visit and the stifling atmosphere it promised to bring.
