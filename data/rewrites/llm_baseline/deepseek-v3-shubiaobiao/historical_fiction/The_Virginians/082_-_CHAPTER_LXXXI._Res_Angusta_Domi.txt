As an older man now, looking back upon the impulsive leap into matrimony that I persuaded my dear Theo to take with me when we were scarcely more than children, I ought—by all respectable logic—to sit here in my comfortable chair, shaking my head in grave disapproval. As a father, mindful of the necessity of mutton chops and the urgency of settling the baker’s bill, I should wag a solemn finger at reckless youth and offer myself as a cautionary tale.

Yet truth compels me to confess that our marriage, so loudly lamented by prudent relatives, defied every gloomy prophecy. It has known trials, certainly—days of hardship borne with a patience that astonished our comfortably horrified kin; moments of grief softened, in time, by Heaven’s mercy; and, above all, blessings so profound I scarcely dare whisper them to the page, save in gratitude to the One who bestowed them.

To marry without a fortune is, by the code of society, a sin—a folly punishable by debt, drudgery, and the slow erosion of hope. And yet, do not thousands make that leap yearly, trusting only in Providence and their own hands? If young hearts waited for fully furnished cottages, stocked larders, and gilded security, the world itself would cease its turning. The gentlefolk linger at the brink, afraid to wet their silken slippers; the poor plunge in barefoot and battle the current.

Who would willingly consign a daughter to poverty? Who would counsel a son to drag a beloved girl from comfort into the grinding shadow of *res angusta domi*—the narrow means of home? I look at my wife and ask forgiveness for leading her into such straits. Yet I think, too, of the love that never faltered, of the strength God granted her to endure, and of the unexpected graces hidden in hardship—the kindness of friends, the sweetness of small joys, the dignity of labor. The prosperous, peering into our modest world, often marveled at our contentment. What seemed privation to them was, to us, life simply lived.

My father-in-law, the good General Lambert, had wed his Molly when he was but a penniless subaltern. They had known lean years and fat, and perhaps (though my Theo will never admit it) had even dispensed with parental permission in their youth. At any rate, they bore no grudge against matrimony and viewed our own venture with a soldier’s calm. When I confessed my finances to the General, I may have painted them with undue optimism; believing me master of a modest sum, he sailed for Jamaica with an easy mind. Theo’s dowry consisted of a few trinkets, some lace from her aunt, and twenty guineas in a purse stitched by her mother and sisters.

As for my own resources—I had counted among them a debt my mother steadfastly refused to acknowledge. The sums I had spent on the Virginia estate, she argued, were but an advance on my inheritance. Her spare coin was for Harry, my younger brother, who had entered His Majesty’s service with empty pockets and a belief (carefully nurtured by Madam Esmond) that he was the rightful heir. When news of my marriage reached her, her letters turned glacial. Harry, visiting home after the war in Canada, described her lavish entertainments in his honor—and the bitter clashes over his friendship with Colonel Washington, whom she detested.

Meanwhile, hints in Harry’s letters suggested another reason for his prolonged stay in Virginia: a certain young lady who had eclipsed even our dear Hester in his affections. When he finally confessed it—veiled in rhapsodies about beauty and virtue—I handed the letter to Theo with a heavy heart.

“I have feared this,” I said. “Poor Hetty!”

“Ah, poor Hetty,” murmured Theo, studying the carpet.

“It could never have been,” I sighed.

“No,” she agreed softly. “They would not have suited.”

Puzzled by her composure, I pressed her—until, with a blush, she confessed an old schoolgirl fancy for a mercer’s apprentice, now a bandy-legged fellow with red hair. The discovery amused rather than wounded me. How little we truly know even those we love best!

Our own means dwindled steadily. My tragedy, though praised by critics and even Mr. Kemble, earned me nothing but a kind offer from Lord Wrotham to publish it by subscription—a charity I refused. We retreated from Bloomsbury to humbler lodgings in Lambeth, still attended by loyal Gumbo and Molly. Relatives clucked at our "extravagance," though none offered aid.

Lady Castlewood, now resplendent in wealth, visited us with condescending cheer. “Poverty’s no shame, cousin,” she declared, surveying our cramped quarters. “Why, in Albany, I scrubbed floors and milked the cow!” She invited us to dine—on days when no dukes or bishops would be present. “Fine company don’t suit you now,” she explained kindly. Her patronage extended to advising Hagan, the actor who had married her sister, to take orders and seek a colonial chaplaincy—a plan later realized, though not through her purse.

My uncle’s family, meanwhile, maintained a pious distance. They spoke of me as a Grub Street wretch, my tragedy as a moral failure, and my wife’s plight as divine justice. Their charity, like their prayers, was for public display.

In desperation, I turned to Dr. Johnson, who received me with gruff kindness. He urged perseverance, suggested literary work, and—when I confessed my dwindling funds—cheered me with the thought that youth and a few hundred pounds were fortune enough. “Why, sir,” he said, “Goldsmith would buy a coach-and-six on less!”

Yet the booksellers, once flattered by my visits in borrowed finery, grew cold when payment was mentioned. One, an ex-footman, dismissed me with a sneer—until I retorted in kind. Later, in better days, I made my peace with him.

And so the year wore on, with hope as thin as our purse. But Heaven, as ever, writes its own endings.
