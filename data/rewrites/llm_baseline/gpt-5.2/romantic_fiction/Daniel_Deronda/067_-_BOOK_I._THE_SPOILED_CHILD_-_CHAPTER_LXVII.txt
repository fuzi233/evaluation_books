It was a sour sort of home-coming for Deronda, to pass from the ordered hush of the Abbey—where even footsteps seemed to remember their manners—into Brompton, and to learn, before he had well set his hat down, that Mirah’s father was installed among the few rooms that had lately begun to feel like a refuge. The very word “father” had a homely sound, as if it must bring a loaf and a blessing; but this man’s nearness came like a stale breath in a pantry—something that tainted without touching, that made one keep turning to see whether the milk had turned, whether the bread would mold faster because he was there.

Mirah had done what she could do, and no more. She had spoken of Deronda because truth, to her, was like water: if you have any to give, you must not pretend the pitcher is empty. Yet she had ladled it out with trembling care, leaving the bottom unstirred. She told her father of Deronda’s friendship with Ezra—how it had begun in that grave companionship of studies, with old papers and older sorrows; how sympathy had sealed it. But she spoke lightly, as one might sift flour to keep stones from showing. She said nothing of the water that had almost closed over her, nothing of hands that had pulled her back from it. She named Mrs. Meyrick’s shelter as if that were the first warmth she had known, and let her father imagine that Deronda had come through those kind women’s doorway, not out of a night of terror.

She could not—she simply could not—let her father’s soul breathe across the most sacred part of her relation to Deronda. There are intimacies that live by their own language; and in Mirah’s mouth, any mention of them in his hearing would have been like putting a tender, untranslatable word into a stranger’s coarse dictionary.

Lapidoth, for his part, did not press. There were reasons why he was content to leave the tale in a mist: he had no appetite for what might condemn him afresh. But he had a keen appetite for another fact—that his children possessed a beneficent friend who seemed “high in the world,” a man whose name might be used like a credit at a shop-counter. That prospect seasoned everything. It was as if he had found, at the back of a cupboard, a jar labeled with a rich preserve, and meant to keep it from others while he considered the best hour to open it.

Ezra told Deronda first—Ezra, who had learned to speak of pain as if it were part of his duty, like reading by a failing lamp.

“I am become calm in beholding him now,” he said, with the quiet of one who has trained his nerves by long fasting from hope. “And I try to think it possible that my sister’s tenderness, and the daily tasting a life of peace, may win him to remain aloof from temptation. I have enjoined her, and she has promised, to trust him with no money. I have convinced her that he will buy with it his own destruction.”

Deronda’s first sensation was repulsion so bodily that it might have been a sudden smell of burning cloth. The man who had blighted wife and children was there, close among their poor furniture and their patience, as if he had a right to the little clean places their lives had made.

He did not meet Lapidoth until the third day after the father’s arrival. Lapidoth had delayed, like an actor waiting for costume. The new clothes for which he had been measured were not ready at first, and the old ones would not do to greet a “personage.” So he kept watch, listening at windows, catching glimpses of Deronda’s comings and goings. When at last he saw him clearly, he started at Deronda’s youthfulness. Mirah had not dwelt on that, perhaps from delicacy, perhaps because reverence makes us forget numbers. Lapidoth had imagined a grave, established patron—something nearer to a magistrate than a young man with a quick eye and a sensitive mouth. And this youthfulness immediately stirred a new conjecture in him: Deronda’s real motive must be love of Mirah.

Better still, then. A tie to Mirah promised softer indulgences than a tie to Ezra. Lapidoth, who could be docile where it served him, began at once to behave with winning amiability and to work himself into easy domestication—an art he had once practiced on audiences, now attempted on children. He listened to Mirah’s music, praising with the air of a man who understood the very grain of her voice. He submitted to the strict dislike of smoke in Mrs. Adam’s parlor, and carried his German pipe outside, pacing the square where the common London noise—wheels, cries, the thin clink of milk-cans—never let the air become truly still. Mirah supplied him with tobacco, and even that innocent act had in it a bitterness for her: to keep a man content by feeding a habit was like keeping a wasp quiet with jam.

He was too acute to remonstrate at once against the refusal of money. Mirah told him plainly that she must persist, having pledged herself to Ezra. Lapidoth smiled, shrugged, submitted. He could wait. A man who has lived by chances knows how to sit in ambush under politeness.

When Deronda came again, Lapidoth was prepared—new clothes on, his hair more carefully disposed, his face arranged into an expression meant to suggest injured merit and present gratitude. He was in the room with Ezra, who had set himself, as part of his severe discipline, to tolerate his father’s presence whenever it was imposed, as if endurance were a form of worship.

Deronda was cold—colder than he intended to be, and yet could not help. He felt the man as a stain that might spread by mere contact of air. But Lapidoth did not flinch. He asked leave to remain while they read from the old chest of papers, and—more than that—he made himself useful. There were German manuscripts that resisted Ezra’s laboring eyes; and Lapidoth, with his still-strong sight, bent over the crabbed lines and helped to decipher them. It was a strange thing, that the very fingers which had so often snatched and squandered could now trace meaning patiently, if meaning led to favor.

From this he took occasion to suggest that a transcription might be desirable. He offered his services to copy the manuscript, and to make copies in Roman characters of any papers needing them. Deronda, seeing at least the shape of grace in willingness to be employed, accepted the offer. He caught, too, a look of pleasure in Ezra’s face—pleasure not in the father, but in the possibility of easing the work, saving his eyes, shortening the long task.

Yet Ezra’s pleasure was quickly guarded.

“Let all the writing be done here,” he said, quietly but firmly. “For I cannot trust the papers out of my sight, lest there be an accident by burning or otherwise.”

It was the voice of a man who has learned that “otherwise” holds whole histories of betrayal. Poor Ezra felt as if he had a convict on leave under his care. Unless he saw his father working, it was impossible to believe in good faith.

And by this arrangement Ezra fastened on himself a burden—his father’s presence—made painful not only by the deepest, longest associations, but also by Lapidoth’s restless temperament. At first, restraint had sat on him like an ill-fitting coat. The more he became familiarized with his situation, the more he wriggled for relief: going out to smoke; drifting about the room with sudden talk; flinging himself back in a chair and falling silent, yet never still—his eyebrows rising and falling, his mouth twitching into little expressions, his hands making dumb gestures as if he were forever rehearsing speeches he did not quite deliver.

If Mirah were present he slid into old habits with her, gossiping of former doings and companions, repeating quirks and stories, and recounting the plots of the plays he used to adapt—as if he could at will command again that brisk vivacity which had once won applause and payments. The very cadence of his talk carried faint echoes of footlights and cheap music, as if a whole commercial art-world still rattled in his voice. Those echoes were a particular torment to Ezra: the sounds were not merely sounds, but reminders, like a tune that brings back a hunger you thought conquered.

Mirah, when she was at home, tried to relieve Ezra by drawing her father down into the parlor and keeping watch there. It was a vigilant domestic kindness—like standing between a well and a child who keeps edging toward the drop. What is duty made of, but one difficult resolve repeated until it becomes the day’s very bread? The resolve is simple; the consequences are not. The difficulty lies in the daily unflinching support of consequences that mar the blessed return of morning with the prospect of irritation to be swallowed, or shame to be borne in silence.

Such consequences were being borne by these two—Mirah and Ezra—as by many other heroic children of an unworthy father; and to Mirah at least there lay the dread that it might stretch onward through the solid part of life, not a sharp calamity but a constant, gnawing presence.

Meanwhile Lapidoth’s being there raised a new, impalpable partition between Deronda and Mirah. Each dreaded the soiling inferences of Lapidoth’s mind; each, watching the other’s increased reserve and diffidence, interpreted it mistakenly. Deronda held back lest his feeling should be made a lever for Lapidoth’s selfishness; Mirah held back lest any softness of hers should be read by her father as a marketable thing. Their very delicacy, which had made every small act between them a kind of offering, now compelled them into a caution that felt like a chill cloth laid over warmth.

It was not long, however, before light came to Deronda—light of a kind he had not dared to ask for, and yet could not help thirsting after, as one thirsts when the air is full of kitchen-smells and one has been long hungry.

As soon as he could after returning from the Abbey, he had gone to Hans Meyrick’s rooms. There were reasons of friendship—more than one—for telling Hans at once the cause of his journey and the change it had brought upon his intentions. Hans was not in; he was said to be in the country for a few days. Deronda left a note and waited a week, half expecting one in return. But no word came. Deronda, fearing some freak of feeling in that susceptible nature—fearing, too, that the deferred Abbey sojourn had pinched Hans’s pride or hope—made a second call.

He was admitted into the painting-room and found Hans in a light coat without a waistcoat, his long hair still wet from a bath, but his face looking worn and wizened—anything but country-bright. Palette and brushes were in hand; he stood before his easel as if caught in the act of diligent work. Yet the whole thing—the posture, the equipment—had the air of having been got up on short notice, like a hurriedly set table meant to prove that dinner was always in preparation.

“You don’t look much as if you had been in the country, old fellow,” Deronda said, as they shook hands. “Is it Cambridge you have been to?”

“No,” said Hans curtly; and he threw down his palette with the air of a man who has begun to feign and suddenly dislikes himself for it. He pushed a chair forward for Deronda, then flung himself into another and leaned back, hands behind his head.

“I’ve been to I-don’t-know-where—No man’s land—and a mortally unpleasant country it is.”

“You don’t mean to say you have been drinking, Hans,” Deronda said, alarmed by the look in him.

“Nothing so good. I’ve been smoking opium.” Hans spoke as if he were confessing to some foolish experiment with a kitchen-spice that had turned a whole dish bitter. “I always meant to do it some time or other, to try how much bliss could be got by it; and having found myself just now rather out of other bliss, I thought it judicious to seize the opportunity. But I pledge you my word I shall never tap a cask of that bliss again. It disagrees with my constitution.”

“What has been the matter? You were in good spirits enough when you wrote to me.”

“Oh, nothing in particular. The world began to look seedy—a sort of cabbage-garden with all the cabbages cut.” He forced a creasing smile. “A malady of genius, you may be sure; and, in fact, I was tired of being virtuous without reward, especially in this hot London weather.”

“Nothing else? No real vexation?”

Hans shook his head.

“I came to tell you of my own affairs,” Deronda said, “but I can’t do it with a good grace if you are to hide yours.”

“Haven’t an affair in the world,” Hans returned, flightily, “except a quarrel with a bric-à-brac man. Besides, as it is the first time in our lives that you ever spoke to me about your own affairs, you are only beginning to pay a pretty long debt.”

Deronda was sure Hans’s manner was artificial—like paint hastily laid on, not yet dry—but he trusted that frankness would return if he gave his own.

“You laughed at the mystery of my journey to Italy, Hans,” he began. “It was for an object that touched my happiness at the very roots. I had never known anything about my parents, and I really went to Genoa to meet my mother. My father has been long dead—died when I was an infant. My mother was the daughter of an eminent Jew; my father was her cousin. Many things had caused me to think of this origin as almost a probability before I set out. I was so far prepared for the result that I was glad of it—glad to find myself a Jew.”

“You must not expect me to look surprised, Deronda,” Hans said, changing his posture, crossing one leg and examining the heel of his slipper as if the truth were there to be studied. “You knew it?”

“My mother told me. She went to the house the morning after you had been there—brother and sister both told her. You may imagine we can’t rejoice as they do. But whatever you are glad of, I shall come to be glad of in the end—when exactly the end may be I can’t predict.” His tone sank low, as it always did when he was out of humor with his lot and determined to make no spectacle of it.

“I quite understand that you can’t share my feeling,” Deronda said. “But I could not let silence lie between us on what casts quite a new light over my future. I have taken up some of Mordecai’s ideas, and I mean to try and carry them out, so far as one man’s efforts can go. I dare say I shall by and by travel to the East and be away for some years.”

Hans said nothing. He rose, seized his palette, and began to work the brush over it, standing before the picture with his back to Deronda. The room filled with the small sounds of painting—the light scrape, the soft tap—sounds that ought to have soothed, but here only sharpened the sense of a barrier. Even silence has its acoustic, and theirs was like an irritating air, made worse by London’s distant grind outside, the ceaseless commercial roar that creeps through walls and leaves no place for clean feeling.

Presently Hans spoke low, still without turning.

“Excuse the question, but does Mrs. Grandcourt know of all this?”

“No; and I must beg of you, Hans,” Deronda answered, angered into firmness, “to cease joking on that subject. Any notions you have are wide of the truth—are the very reverse of the truth.”

“I am no more inclined to joke than I shall be at my own funeral,” Hans said. “But I am not at all sure that you are aware what are my notions on that subject.”

“Perhaps not,” said Deronda. “But let me say, once for all, that in relation to Mrs. Grandcourt, I never have had, and never shall have the position of a lover. If you have ever seriously put that interpretation on anything you have observed, you are supremely mistaken.”

Silence fell again, and each felt it as one feels a draught in a room: it searches you out and makes you shift.

“Perhaps I have been mistaken in another interpretation, also,” Hans said at last.

“What is that?”

“That you had no wish to hold the position of a lover toward another woman, who is neither wife nor widow.”

Deronda’s heart gave a quick, painful leap—the question naming what he had tried to keep unnamed even to himself, because names can be traded, and he was learning how easily language becomes a currency in the wrong hands.

“I can’t pretend not to understand you, Meyrick,” he said. “It is painful that our wishes should clash. I hope you will tell me if you have any ground for supposing that you would succeed.”

“That seems rather a superfluous inquiry on your part, Deronda,” Hans answered, irritation breaking through his guarded tone.

“Why superfluous?”

“Because you are perfectly convinced on the subject—and probably have had the very best evidence to convince you.”

“I will be more frank with you than you are with me,” Deronda said, heated and yet sorry for him. “I have never had the slightest evidence that I should succeed myself. In fact, I have very little hope.”

Hans looked round hastily, as if he had been struck by something he did not expect, then turned back to the picture.

“And in our present situation,” Deronda went on, stung by the suspicion of insincerity and giving his words an offended emphasis, “I don’t see how I can deliberately make known my feeling to her. If she could not return it, I should have embittered her best comfort; for neither she nor I can be parted from her brother, and we should have to meet continually. If I were to cause her that sort of pain by an unwilling betrayal of my feeling, I should be no better than a mischievous animal.”

“I don’t know that I have ever betrayed my feeling to her,” Hans said, with a quick defensive edge, as if to prove he too had tried to behave with honor.

“You mean that we are on a level, then; you have no reason to envy me.”

“Oh, not the slightest,” Hans returned, bitterly. “You have measured my conceit and know that it out-tops all your advantages.”

“I am a nuisance to you, Meyrick. I am sorry, but I can’t help it,” Deronda said, rising. “After what passed between us before, I wished to have this explanation; and I don’t see that any pretensions of mine have made a real difference to you. They are not likely to make any pleasant difference to myself under present circumstances. Now the father is there—did you know that the father is there?”

“Yes. If he were not a Jew I would permit myself to damn him—with faint praise, I mean,” Hans said, still without a smile.

“She and I meet under greater constraint than ever,” Deronda continued. “Things might go on in this way for two years without my getting any insight into her feeling toward me. That is the whole state of affairs, Hans. Neither you nor I have injured the other, that I can see. We must put up with this sort of rivalry in a hope that is likely enough to come to nothing. Our friendship can bear that strain, surely.”

“No, it can’t,” Hans burst out, impetuously. He flung down his tools, thrust his hands into his coat-pockets, and turned to face Deronda, who drew back a little, amazed at the sudden blaze. Hans went on, the words rushing as if they had been bottled too long.

“Our friendship—my friendship—can’t bear the strain of behaving to you like an ungrateful dastard and grudging you your happiness. For you are the happiest dog in the world. If Mirah loves anybody better than her brother, you are the man.”

He wheeled away and dropped into his chair, looking up at Deronda with an expression that seemed to dare him to deny what had been said.

A shock passed through Deronda—something like the sudden discovery of sweetness when one has expected only bitterness, and is afraid of being deceived by the palate.

“It is a good-natured fiction of yours, Hans,” he said, distrustful of delight because he was unused to it.

“I am not in a good-natured mood. I assure you I found the fact disagreeable when it was thrust on me—all the more, or perhaps all the less, because I believed then that your heart was pledged to the duchess. But now, confound you! you turn out to be in love in the right place—a Jew—and everything eligible.”

“Tell me what convinced you—there’s a good fellow,” Deronda said, almost pleading, like a man who has smelled bread and must know whether it is truly baking or only imagined.

“Don’t ask. Little mother was witness. The upshot is, that Mirah is jealous of the duchess, and the sooner you relieve your mind the better. There! I’ve cleared off a score or two, and may be allowed to swear at you for getting what you deserve—which is just the very best luck I know of.”

“God bless you, Hans!” Deronda said, and held out his hand.

Hans took it and wrung it in silence—hard, as if to press pain into loyalty and make it stay.
