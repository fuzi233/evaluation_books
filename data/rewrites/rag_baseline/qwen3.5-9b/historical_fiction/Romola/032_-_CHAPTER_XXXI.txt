"Romola," Tito said on the second morning following his address in the Piazza del Duomo, "grand visitors arrive today. The Milanese Count returns, accompanied by the Seneschal de Beaucaire, the great favorite of the Cristianissimo. I perceive you harbor no desire to endure the hollow rituals required by such rustling magnates, figures we shall likely never encounter again. Since they intend to inspect the antiquities and the library, you ought to suspend your labors and accompany your cousin Brigida."

Romola detected the subtext—a request for respite—and immediately acquiesced. Yet, returning moments later, hooded and mantled, she declared, "Ah, what a prolonged exhalation Florence will undertake once the gates swing wide and the final Frenchman strides out! Even you, Tito, wear the signs of fatigue beneath your patience; confess it. Your brow burns."

Tito leaned over his desk, quill moving rhythmically across parchment. She reached out, intending a customary caress upon his head, but found her palm met resistance. Her hand rested there, yet he remained rigid, incapable of lifting his gaze as if her touch were a leaden cowl pressing down upon him. Instead, he maintained a light, perfunctory tone while his pen continued its work. "The French depart from Florence with the same indifference wasps show toward a ripe pear once they have extracted their fill."

Romola, acutely attuned to the vacuum where his usual engagement should have been, withdrew her hand. "I am leaving, Tito."

"Farewell, my sweet one. I remain here. Take Maso with you."

Still, Tito did not lift his eyes. Romola departed without further comment. In the annals of matrimony, trivialities often mark epochal shifts; this morning, for the first time, she acknowledged not merely that Tito had altered, but that his trajectory relative to her had shifted irrevocably. Was the fault hers? She might have conceded this point had not the armor and the painting suggested an external catalyst, a mystery entirely opaque to her understanding.

The moment Romola crossed the threshold, Tito lowered his pen and gazed upward, seeking refuge in the safety of parchment and fractured marble. A surge of disgust washed over him; he chided himself for failing to greet her as he invariably had. Had he possessed the capacity, he would have doubled his kindness, yet he found himself paralyzed by an involuntary recoil. This shrinking stemmed directly from the very traits that drove his desire to prove his affection—to succeed, to provide. He faced a precipice: a step he knew would ignite her profound indignation, requiring him to endure significant discomfort before securing her forgiveness. Tito's nature, devoid of defiance or audacity, leaned heavily toward preserving her tenderness. He was not shackled by sentimental scruples—his own rapid internal logic had dismantled such notions as irrelevant to utility—but his lack of scruple offered no shield against the visceral dread of pain. No device could insulate him from the agony of the impending collision; no bland persuasion could soften the impact of the desperate leap he was being driven to make, spurred by the terror of the tooth and the claw closing in.

A latent realization had crystallized earlier: his stubborn adherence to Bardo's directives regarding the library had devolved into sentimental folly, stripping both himself and Romola of tangible advantage. Only the events of the past week—the crushing pressure of a new imperative and the fleeting window of opportunity—had forged this resolution. Specifically, it was not until he beheld Baldassarre regaining his composure, nor until he sensed the compulsion to flee Florence, that he resolved to exercise his legal right to liquidate the library before the chance vanished amidst the bidding of French and Milanese interests. If exile became inevitable, he would not depart as a destitute wanderer. Accustomed to an agreeable existence, he sought to transport the means to sustain it. Among his priorities was carrying Romola with him, ensuring he did not drag any infamy into their flight. Success had ignited an appetite for the privileges of elevated social standing; the notion of decamping under dishonor, even laden with diamonds, appeared not as a temptation but as a grotesque alternative.

He could not achieve independence from the Florentines who treated him with mere polite regard, nor from Romola. She was the wife of his first love; he still loved her. She constituted essential furniture of his life, from which he recoiled at the thought of separation. He winced under her judgment, uncertain of the extent of her revulsion. The power a husband wields to risk betrayal—power a lover rarely possesses—proved insufficient to counteract Tito's acute unease. This was the leaden weight that held his head bowed, preventing him from meeting her eyes. Their pure light illuminated the prospect of struggle too vividly. Yet, helplessness was not an option. If they were to abandon Florence, capital was non-negotiable. Indeed, Tito could not construct a viable future without a substantial sum, and this necessity had been the root of all his transgressions. He would have embraced any sacrifice provided it lacked unpleasantness.

The rustling magnates arrived and departed; bargains were struck; Romola returned home. That night, however, nothing grave was spoken. Tito proved gay and chatty, recounting stories and descriptions of the French visit with a fervor unseen before. Romola perceived an effort in his vivacity, interpreting it as penitence born of his awareness that she had been wounded. She accepted this display, though an ache lingered within her at the widening chasm between them—an offense neither dared name.

The following day, Tito remained absent until late evening. It marked a significant date for Romola: Piero di Cosimo, spurred by the fear that he might have caused her pain in the preceding week, had dispatched her father's portrait. She propped it against the back of his old chair, studying it when the door opened behind her. Bernardo del Nero entered.

"It is you, godfather! How I wish you had arrived sooner; dusk is already falling," Romola said, approaching him.

"I stopped briefly to share good tidings, knowing Tito had not yet returned," Bernardo replied. "The French King departs tomorrow; delay is no longer permissible. Another clash occurred this morning between our citizens and his soldiers. Yet, there is now hope for the restoration of order and the resumption of trade."

"That is joyous," Romola responded. "Yet sudden. Yesterday, Tito seemed convinced the King's departure was distant."

"He has been well barked at, hence the retreat," Bernardo smiled. "His own generals opened their throats quite effectively, and finally, our Signoria unleashed the city's mastiff, Fra Girolamo. The Cristianissimo, frightened by that thunder, issued the order to move. I fear little consensus will emerge once he leaves, but at least all parties rejoice at the cessation of Florence being stifled by soldiery. The Friar barked successfully this time."

"Ah, what is this?" Bernardo asked, as Romola, grasping his arm, guided him before the portrait. "Let us examine."

He began unwinding his long scarf while she arranged a seat for him. "Do you require your spectacles, godfather?" Romola asked anxiously, wishing him to see precisely what she saw.

"No, child, none needed," Bernardo replied, uncovering his grey head as he sat with firm erectness. "At this distance, my aged eyes may surpass yours. Old men's vision resembles old men's memories; they retain their strength best for things seen long ago."

"It is preferable to having no portrait at all," Romola apologized after a brief silence. "It bears less resemblance to him than the image I hold in my mind, though that too may fade with the years."

She rested her arm upon his shoulder, drawn strongly toward him by their shared reverence for the dead. "I cannot say," Bernardo murmured. "Almost, I perceive Bardo as he was in youth more clearly than this picture depicts him in age. Your father possessed immense fire in his eyes when young. It remains a mystery to me how one with such an impatient spirit could dedicate his life to books and shadows. Nevertheless, he poured his heart into it."

Bernardo shrugged slightly at the end, yet Romola detected in his voice a sentiment resonating with her own. "And he died disappointed," she said involuntarily. Immediately fearing her words might be construed as an indictment of Tito, she hastened to continue, "If only we could witness his longest, dearest wish realized exactly as he envisioned!"

"Well, so we may," Bernardo said kindly, rising to don his cap. "Times are clouded, but fish are caught by waiting. Who knows? Should the wheel turn sufficiently, I may yet serve as Gonfaloniere before my death; no creditor can touch these assets." He glanced around, then turned to her, patting her cheek. "And need not fear my demise; my ghost claims nothing. I have ensured that in my will."

Romola seized the hand resting against her cheek and pressed it to her lips in silence. "Have you not been chastising your husband for his recent absences? I see him everywhere except here," Bernardo remarked, eager to shift the topic.

A flush spread rapidly over her neck and face. "He has been greatly missed," she replied. "You know he speaks so eloquently. I am pleased to know his worth is recognized."

"You are content, then, Madonna Orgogliosa?" Bernardo asked, smiling as he moved toward the door.

"Assuredly."
