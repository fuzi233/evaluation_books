“Tell me of those children, child,” Lady Maria said, as they paced the common where the turf lay close-cropped and resilient underfoot. The evening air held that mingled country-sweetness Tunbridge keeps—damp earth, trodden grass, a far hint of horse, and somewhere a kitchen-chimney sending up woodsmoke like a remembered comfort. “Those little ones I saw running about the house where they took you in—poor dear boy—after your dreadful fall. Oh, that fall, Harry! I thought I should have died when I saw it!”

“You needn’t squeeze one’s arm so,” she added, with a faint catch in her voice that tried to pass for playfulness. “You know you don’t care for me.”

“The people are the very best, kindest, dearest people I have ever met in the world!” Harry Warrington cried, turning his whole face toward praise as a hungry man turns toward supper. “Mrs. Lambert was a friend of my mother when she was in Europe for her education. Colonel Lambert is a most accomplished gentleman—has seen service everywhere: Scotland with his Royal Highness, Flanders, Minorca. No natural parents could be kinder than they were to me. How can I show my gratitude? I want to make them a present—I must make them a present!” And with that he clapped his hand into his pocket, as if the crisp bank-notes he had won were warm rolls that might be shared.

“We can go to the toy-shop, my dear, and buy a couple of dolls for the children,” Maria said quickly, seizing on the safest sort of generosity. “You would offend the parents by offering anything like payment for their kindness.”

“Dolls for Hester and Theo!” Harry broke out, laughing. “Why, do you think a woman is not woman till she is forty, Maria?”

The arm under his gave a small, involuntary wince—so slight it might have been from the evening chill; yet it had the same sharpness as when a too-bright knife hits bone.

“I can tell you Miss Hester by no means considers herself a child,” he went on, cheerfully blind, “and Miss Theo is older than her sister. They know ever so many languages. They have read books—oh! piles and piles of books! They play on the harpsichord and sing together admirable; and Theo composes, and sings songs of her own.”

“Indeed? I scarcely saw them,” Maria said, with care. “I thought they were children. They looked quite childish. I had no idea they had all these perfections, and were such wonders of the world.”

“That’s just the way with you women!” Harry cried, all young triumph and teasing, as if the world were a table set for him and he must taste everything. “At home, if me or George praised a woman, Mrs. Esmond—and Mountain too—would be sure to find fault with her!”

“I am sure I would find fault with no one who is kind to you, Mr. Warrington,” Maria sighed; and the sigh, though soft, seemed to drop into the grass between them like a spoon into an empty basin. “Though you are not angry with me for envying them because they had to take care of you when you were wounded and ill—whilst I—I had to leave you?”

“You dear good Maria!”

“No, Harry! I am not dear and good.” She withdrew her arm a hair’s breadth, then yielded it again—habit stronger than pride. “There, sir, you needn’t be so pressing in your attentions. Look! There is your black man walking with a score of other wretches in livery. The horrid creatures are going to fuddle at the tea-garden, and get tipsy like their masters. That dreadful Mr. Morris was perfectly tipsy when I came to you, and frightened you so.”

“I had just won great bets from both of them,” Harry said, pleased as a boy who has taken a hedge clean and lands with both feet. “What shall I buy for you, my dear cousin?”

And he told her, with the buoyancy that comes from sudden money and sudden applause, of his leap and his wagers—how, for the honour of Virginia, he would show them what jumping was; how, with a little practice, he could leap two foot farther. Maria listened and let herself be pleased; she had been trained to make others’ cheer her own, as a housekeeper sets out a dish she will not eat.

“But you must beware about play, child,” she said at last. “You know it hath been the ruin of our family. My brother Castlewood, Will, our poor father, our aunt—Lady Castlewood herself—they have all been victims to it: as for my Lord March, he is the most dreadful gambler and the most successful of all the nobility.”

“I don’t intend to be afraid of him, nor of his friend Mr. Jack Morris neither,” Harry answered, fingering the notes again, loving their dry crackle as if it were the crust of something baked to perfection. “What do you play at Aunt Bernstein’s? Cribbage, all-fours, brag, whist, commerce, piquet, quadrille? I’m ready at any of ’em. What o’clock is that striking—sure ’tis seven!”

“And you want to begin now,” Maria said, plaintive.

“Hey! Youth is youth, cousin!” Harry tossed his head. “And a young fellow must have his fling!”

So they turned toward the lodgings where pleasure waited, and already the Parade’s open orchestra was waking—fiddles scraping, testing, squeaking, that thin, nervous tuning like insects in a hedge. The sound lifted and worried the air; the very birds seemed to hold off until the humans had done with their noise. Maria heard it as one hears a door bolted on a room one longs for: her aunt was awake again, and she must go back to her slavery.

Harry never asked after that slavery. He must have known it, had he taken the trouble to think. He did not think. His mind was on cards and company, on the glittering cup held out to his age—he seized it as other lads do, not calculating the morning’s bitterness.

At Madame de Bernstein’s, they had, like worldly parents anxious to rid themselves of a troublesome child and go to their party, put the sun to bed while it was yet light. Curtains were drawn over day; candles flowered in sconces; and the rooms took on that warmed, crowded smell of wax and powder and hot spirits, of tea steeping too long, of lemon peel and grated nutmeg bruised into negus. There were little side-tables where plates appeared and vanished—biscuits, sugared comfits, slices of bread-and-butter curling at their edges—food not to nourish but to keep the hands busy while the eyes hunted.

One chair after another landed ladies at the Baroness’s door, more or less painted, patched, brocaded. Gentlemen arrived in gala raiment. Mr. Poellnitz’s star was the largest; his coat, the most embroidered. My Lord of March and Ruglen, when he came, was quite changed from the figure Harry had met at the White Horse: the tight brown scratch was exchanged for a neatly curled feather-top, with bag and grey powder; the jockey dress and leather breeches replaced by a rich French suit that gleamed like a well-glazed pastry. Mr. Jack Morris had just such another wig and suit of stuff, as close a copy as a kitchen-boy makes of his master’s dish.

Mr. Wolfe attended his beautiful mistress, Miss Lowther, and her aunt, who loved cards as the world loved them. And when Lady Maria appeared, it was plain her looks belied her aunt’s talk of her: her shape was very fine; her dress showed it; her complexion was exceeding fair by nature, and a dark frilled ribbon clasped by a jewel round her neck made her whiteness seem whiter. Her cheeks were no redder than other ladies’—and the roses, like the oranges in winter, were openly bought. An artful patch or two was supposed to add to her lustre. If the hoops were absurd, they were absurd in a way fashion forgives: the whole costume pleased, as sweetmeats please even when one knows they are more sugar than fruit.

As for the Baroness de Bernstein, when she took pains with her toilette she was magnificent still—handsome even—yet melancholy, and in a manner terrifying. Some faces show only the present; hers spoke in old accents of past power and passion. The rooms might glitter, the cards might slap and slide, yet there was something in that old lady’s eye like a winter pond—dark, still, reflecting flame without being warmed by it.

“My hostess,” said my Lord Chesterfield to his friend in a confidential whisper that he did not know was loud, “puts me in mind of Covent Garden in my youth. Then it was the court end of the town and inhabited by the highest fashion. Now, a nobleman’s house is a gaming-house, or you may go in with a friend and call for a bottle.”

“Hey! a bottle and a tavern are good things in their way,” my Lord March said, shrugging. “I was not born before the Georges came in, though I intend to live to a hundred. I never knew the Bernstein but as an old woman; and if she ever had beauty, hang me if I know how she spent it.”

“No, hang me—how did she spend it?” Jack Morris laughed out. “Here’s a table! Shall we sit down and have a game? Don’t let the Frenchman come in. He won’t pay. Mr. Warrington, will you take a card?”

So Harry found himself partnered with my Lord Chesterfield against Mr. Morris and the Earl of March.

“You have come too late, Baron,” Chesterfield said to the other nobleman advancing. “We have made our game. What, have you forgotten Mr. Warrington of Virginia—the young gentleman whom you met in London?”

“The young gentleman whom I met at Arthur’s Chocolate House had black hair, a little cocked nose, and was by no means so fortunate in his personal appearance as Mr. Warrington,” the Baron replied with easy presence, and with a smile that allowed truth to slip away without offence. “Warrington, Dorrington, Harrington—we of the continent cannot retain your insular names. I certify that this gentleman is not the individual of whom I spoke at dinner.”

He glanced kindly on Harry and sidled off to the embrasure where Mr. Wolfe and Miss Lowther talked in low tones. There the Baron chose to discourse on the Prussian manual exercise lately introduced into King George the Second’s army—matters Mr. Wolfe knew well, and which might have pleased him on any other night, but now fell like stale crumbs where a richer dish was desired. Still the Baron spoke, and believed himself charming.

At the start of the evening the Baroness received her guests personally, making her little speeches, laying out her courtesies as a cook lays out knives in order. But as rooms filled and tables were made up, Madame de Bernstein grew restless, then retreated at last with three friends to her own corner, where a table reserved for her was attended by her major-domo. There she sat down resolutely and never changed her place nor quit her game till cock-crow—fixed as a great landowner in her chair, drawing in and sending out coin and fate.

The charge of receiving the company devolved upon Lady Maria. She did not care for cards, but dutifully did the honours of the house, moving through the rooms with her silk rustling like dry leaves. Often she passed by Harry’s table and lingered—as if warmth might be had by standing near another’s fire.

“Come and cut the cards for us,” said Lord March as she paused on one of those wistful visits. “Cut the cards and bring us luck, Lady Maria! We have had none to-night, and Mr. Warrington is winning everything.”

“I hope you are not playing high, Harry?” Maria asked, timidly.

“Oh no, only sixpences,” my lord cried, dealing.

“Only sixpences,” echoed Mr. Morris, his round face fallen into such dismal lines that one might think each “sixpence” a slice cut from him. He must have been keenly alive to their value if the loss of a few could sour him so.

Lord Chesterfield sat opposite Harry, sorting his cards, calm as if he handled papers of state. No one could tell by that composed physiognomy whether fortune smiled or frowned on him. But a word—not altogether delicate—slipped from Mr. Morris, upon which his partner cried out with sudden heat, “Hang it, Morris, play your cards, and hold your tongue!”

Considering they were only playing for sixpences, his lordship too seemed strangely affected. Maria, still fondly behind Harry’s chair with her hand on its back, could see his cards, and saw a whole covey of trumps ranged in one corner. She had not taken away his luck. She was pleased, absurdly, to think she had cut the pack that dealt him those pretty trumps—pleased as a housewife is pleased when her yeast rises true, though another will eat the loaf.

As Lord March dealt, he said in a low voice to Harry, “The bet as before, Mr. Warrington, or shall we double it?”

“Anything you like, my lord,” Harry answered, very quietly, as if he were agreeing to a change of sauce.

“We will say, then—shillings.”

“Yes, shillings,” said Harry; and the game went on, the cards whispering and snapping like small wings, the counters clicking, the room’s talk and laughter swaying like a sea around that little island of play.

What came of that night—and of the succeeding nights—may be gathered from a letter written later, which was never delivered to the person addressed, but was found among Mr. Henry Warrington’s papers and made its way across the ocean like a bottle cast upon a tide:

“TUNBRIDGE WELLS, August 10, 1756.

“DEAR GEORGE—As White’s two bottles of Burgundy and a pack of cards constitute all the joys of your life, I take for granted that you are in London at this moment, preferring smoke and faro to fresh air and fresh haystacks. This will be delivered to you by a young gentleman with whom I have lately made acquaintance, and whom you will be charmed to know. He will play with you at any game for any stake, up to any hour of the night, and drink any reasonable number of bottles during the play.

“Mr. Warrington is no other than the Fortunate Youth about whom so many stories have been told in the Public Advertiser and other prints. He has an estate in Virginia as big as Yorkshire, with the incumbrance of a mother, the reigning Sovereign; but, as the country is unwholesome, and fevers plentiful, let us hope that Mrs. Esmond will die soon, and leave this virtuous lad in undisturbed possession. She is aunt of that polisson of a Castlewood, who never pays his play-debts, unless he is more honourable in his dealings with you than he has been with me. Mr. W. is de bonne race. We must have him of our society, if it be only that I may win my money back from him.

“He has had the devil’s luck here, and has been winning everything, whilst his old card-playing beldam of an aunt has been losing. A few nights ago, when I first had the ill-luck to make his acquaintance, he beat me in jumping (having practised the art amongst the savages, and running away from bears in his native woods); he won bets off me and Jack Morris about my weight; and at night, when we sat down to play, at old Bernstein’s, he won from us all round. If you can settle our last Epsom account please hand over to Mr. Warrington 350 pounds, which I still owe him, after pretty well emptying my pocket-book. Chesterfield has dropped six hundred to him, too; but his lordship does not wish to have it known, having sworn to give up play and live cleanly. Jack Morris, who has not been hit as hard as either of us, and can afford it quite as well, for the fat chuff has no houses nor train to keep up, and all his misbegotten father’s money in hand, roars like a bull of Bashan about his losses.

“We had a second night’s play, en petit comité, and Barbeau served us a fair dinner in a private room. Mr. Warrington holds his tongue like a gentleman, and none of us have talked about our losses; but the whole place does, for us. Yesterday the Cattarina looked as sulky as thunder, because I would not give her a diamond necklace, and says I refuse her because I have lost five thousand to the Virginian. My old Duchess of Q. has the very same story, besides knowing to a fraction what Chesterfield and Jack have lost.

“Warrington treated the company to breakfast and music at the rooms; and you should have seen how the women tore him to pieces. That fiend of a Cattarina ogled him out of my vis-à-vis, and under my very nose, yesterday, as we were driving to Penshurst, and I have no doubt has sent him a billet-doux ere this. He shot Jack Morris all to pieces at a mark: we shall try him with partridges when the season comes.

“He is a fortunate fellow, certainly. He has youth (which is not deboshed by evil courses in Virginia, as ours is in England); he has good health, good looks, and good luck.

“In a word, Mr. Warrington has won our money in a very gentlemanlike manner; and, as I like him, and wish to win some of it back again, I put him under your worship’s saintly guardianship. Adieu! I am going to the North, and shall be back for Doncaster.—Yours ever, dear George, M. et R.”

“To George Augustus Selwyn, Esq., at White’s Chocolate House, St. James’s Street.”
