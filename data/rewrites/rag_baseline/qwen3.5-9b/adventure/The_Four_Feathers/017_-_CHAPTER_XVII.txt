Mrs. Adair, on her side, demanded no explanations. Behind her pale, placid countenance lay a mind of tortuous and intriguing complexity. She preferred to peer through a keyhole even when the door stood wide open; knowledge gained through subtle maneuvering was infinitely more precious to her than any truth extracted by a blunt inquiry. Indeed, she avoided direct questions on a perverted principle, considering a day well-spent only if she had outwitted a companion into revealing some trivial tidbit spontaneously, rather than securing it at breakfast with a simple request. Thus, though she was mystified by the white feather upon which Ethne placed such store, and baffled by the good news of Harry Feversham that Captain Willoughby had delivered, she concealed her perplexity entirely throughout lunch. Instead, she plied her guest with conversation on indifferent topics. Mrs. Adair could be excellent company when she chose, and she chose now. Yet it was to no avail.

"I don't believe you hear a single word I am saying!" she exclaimed.

Ethne laughed and pleaded guilty. As soon as lunch concluded, she retired to her room, claiming an afternoon of solitude. Seated at her window, she slowly rehearsed the story Willoughby had told that morning, and her heart thrilled to it as to music divinely played. The regret that he had not returned a year prior, when she was free, was a mere trifle compared to the weight of the tale itself. It could not overshadow the profound gladness it ignited—a joy that had completely eclipsed her previous thoughts. The pride, shattered by Harry Feversham's blow in the hall at Lennon House, was now fully restored, and by the very hand that had dealt the wound. She burned with this restoration, most grateful to Harry Feversham for risking so much peril to reclaim it. A new exhilaration warmed the sunlight; her blood pulsed faster. Her youth was returned to her on that August afternoon.

Ethne unlocked a drawer in her dressing-case and withdrew the sole portrait of Harry Feversham's gifts she had retained. She rejoiced in keeping it. It depicted someone dead to her—she knew this well, for there was no treachery toward Durrance in her breast—but the subject was a friend. She gazed upon it with immense happiness and contentment, realizing that Harry Feversham had required no expression of faith from her to inspire him, nor encouragement to sustain him through the years on the plane of his high inspiration. Returning the portrait, she placed the white feather beside it and locked the two treasures together.

She returned to the window. Outside, a light breeze made the shadows of the high trees dance; the sunlight mellowed into a rich red hue. Yet Ethne belonged to her county, as Harry Feversham had long ago discerned, and her heart yearned for it in this moment. It was August; the first heather would soon bloom upon the Donegal hillsides, and she wished the good news had arrived there. The regret that it had not was her crumpled rose-leaf. Here she was in a strange land; there, amidst the brown mountains with their granite outcroppings and the voices of the streams, she believed the landscape would have shared in her new happiness. Great sorrows and great joys alike drew Ethne Eustace homeward, for there endurance was easier and gladness more complete.

However, she possessed one living tether to Donegal: Dermod's old collie dog, now her inseparable companion. To him she poured her confidence, and if her voice occasionally broke into tears, the dog would remain silent. Through him, she grasped truths Willoughby had omitted and Feversham had never voiced. Consider those three years of concealment in the cramped, crowded city of Suakin, while troops marched out to battle and returned dust-strewn, bleeding, and laurelled with victory. Harry Feversham had to slink away at their approach, lest an old acquaintance—Durrance, perhaps, or Willoughby, or Trench—notice him and pierce his disguise. The panic that seized him upon seeing the dark brown walls of Berber, the night spent in ruined acres, the stumbling search for a well among the shifting sandhills of Obak—Ethne formed vivid images of these incidents. As she contemplated each, she asked herself: *Where was I then? What was I doing?*

She sat in a golden mist until the lights shifted upon the still water of the creek, and the rooks wheeled noisily from the treetops to sort themselves for the night, warning her of evening. That night, she brought to the dinner table a buoyancy of spirit that surprised her companions. Mrs. Adair admitted that rarely had Ethne's eyes shone so starrily, or her cheeks glowed with such fresh color. More than ever, she was convinced Captain Willoughby had brought stirring news; more than ever, she was tormented by her futile attempts to guess its nature. Yet, despite her perplexities, Mrs. Adair participated in the talk, and that dinner passed with a freedom from embarrassment unknown since Durrance had returned to Guessens. For he, too, shed a burden of restraint; his spirits rose to match Ethne's. He answered laugh with laugh, and from his face, that habitual look of tension—the visage of a man straining to hear with all his might to compensate for lost sight—passed entirely away.

"You will play on your violin tonight, I think," he said with a smile as they rose from the table.

"Yes," she answered, "I will—with all my heart."

Durrance laughed and held open the door. The violin had remained locked in its case for the last two months. Durrance had come to view that instrument as a gauge and test. If the world was going well with Ethne, the case was unlocked, the instrument allowed to speak; if the world went ill, it was kept silent lest it reveal too much, opening old wounds to other eyes. Ethne herself knew it for an indiscreet friend. But it was to be brought out that night.

Mrs. Adair lingered until Ethne was beyond earshot. "You have noticed the change in her tonight?" she said.

"Yes. Have I not?" Durrance replied. "One has waited for it, hoped for it, despaired of it."

"Are you so glad of the change?"

Durrance threw back his head. "Do you wonder that I am glad? Kind, friendly, unselfish—these things she has always been. But there is more than friendliness evident tonight, and for the first time, it is evident."

A look of pity softened Mrs. Adair's face, and she departed without another word. Durrance claimed the entirety of Ethne's great transformation for himself.

Mrs. Adair drew up the blinds of the drawing-room, opened the window, and let the moonlight flood in. Then, seeing Ethne unlock the case of her violin, she stepped out onto the terrace. She felt she could not sit patiently in her company. Consequently, when Durrance entered the drawing-room, he found Ethne alone. She sat by the window, already tuning the strings of her violin. Durrance took a chair behind her in the shadows.

"What shall I play to you?" she asked.

"The Musoline Overture," he answered. "You played it on the first evening I came to Ramelton. I remember so clearly how you played it then. Play it again tonight. I wish to compare."

"I have played it since."

"Never to me."

They were alone in the room; the windows stood open; it was a night of moonlight. Suddenly, Ethne crossed to the lamp and extinguished it. She resumed her seat, while Durrance remained in the shadow, leaning forward with his hands upon his knees, listening—with an intensity of which he had shown no sign that evening. He was applying, as he thought, a final test upon which his life and hers should be decided. Ethne's violin would surely tell him whether he was right or wrong. Would friendship speak from it, or something more than friendship?

Ethne played the overture. As she played, she forgot Durrance was in the room behind her. In the garden, the air was still, summer-warm, and fragrant; on the creek, the moonlight lay like a solid floor of silver; the trees stood dreaming under the stars. As the music floated loudly across the silent lawn, Ethne had a sudden fancy that it might perhaps travel down the creek, over Salcombe Bar, and across the moonlit seas, striking small yet wonderfully clear, like fairy music, upon the ears of a man sleeping somewhere far away beneath the brightness of the southern stars, with the cool night wind of the desert blowing upon his face.

"If he could only hear!" she thought. "If he could only wake and know that what he heard was a message of friendship!"

With this fancy in her mind, she played with a skill she had never used before; she made of her violin a voice of sympathy. The fancy grew and changed as she played. The music became a bridge swung in mid-air across the world, upon which, for just these few minutes, she and Harry Feversham might meet and shake hands. They would separate, of course, forthwith, and each proceed upon their allotted path. But these few minutes would be a help to both along their separate ways. The chords rang upon silence. It seemed to Ethne that they declaimed the pride that had come to her that day. Her fancy grew into a belief.
