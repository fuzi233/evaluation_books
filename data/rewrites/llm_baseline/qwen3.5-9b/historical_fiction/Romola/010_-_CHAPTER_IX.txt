Tito plunged quickly into the throng. Though he offered Nello a casual shrug regarding his recent encounter, a fleeting longing stirred within him as he navigated toward the Corso degli Adimari. He wished, against reason, to catch sight of the pair of blue eyes that had gazed upward from beneath the white linen hood of the countrywoman at the festival. He knew well that face belonged to Tessa, yet he had carefully avoided naming her. What concern was Nello's business? Tito possessed an innate gift for silence—a talent that operated without conscious intent. Like many who find concealment effortless, he occasionally hid things that required no secrecy whatsoever, such as the mere fact that he had once spotted a flock of crows. However, this wistful thought for Tessa was swiftly overshadowed by the persistent memory of the friar whose gaze had held an unsettling significance for him. Why had that pale, fanatical figure stared specifically at *him*? Where else in his travels could he have seen such a face? "Folly!" he chided himself mentally. Vague memories cling to the mind like irritating cobwebs, demanding attention best dispelled by a sharp dismissal. With a mental shake-off, Tito turned his focus elsewhere.

By the time he emerged from the Corso into a side street, his sole concern was the height of the sun and the delay caused by the procession. He had missed his appointment with the room in the Via de' Bardi, where his arrival was anxiously anticipated. In his imagination, he rehearsed the scene: the blind man's face glowing with diffuse joy, like light filtering through translucent glass; the faint, rosy flush on Romola's cheeks and neck, adding a delicate femininity to her inherent nobility, enhanced further by a boyish openness in her expression and smile. Together, they were perfect companions around the invalid's chair. She appealed to him constantly, and he informed her, yet he felt strangely subject to Romola's simplicity. For the first time, he experienced a profound, undefined reverence in the presence of noble womanhood—a sentiment akin to the ancient worship of a goddess whose power was deeper and more primal than mere omniscience.

They had never been alone, leaving him unable to construct any plausible scenario of romantic intimacy. All he could conjure was a wild fantasy: that someday Romola would confess her love. He recalled a day in Greece, leaning against a sun-drenched wall, when a small, dark-eyed peasant girl had timidly approached, resting her water pot nearby. Shyly, she had asked for a kiss, pressing her round, olive-toned cheek against his in innocent trust. Tito was accustomed to love arriving in such unsolicited bursts. But Romola's love would never come this way. Would it ever come at all?—yet it remained the highest prize on which he had set his mind.

He was in the prime of his youth—not consumed by passion, but deeply impressionable. It was as natural for him to feel drawn to Romola as it was for white iris flowers to reflect in a sunlit stream. Lacking any pretense of superiority, he nonetheless sensed that Romola stood far above him. Many men have felt this distance before a wide-eyed, simple child. Still, Tito had achieved rapid success that might have emboldened others to presume too much. He had convinced fair judges that his talents matched his good fortune, wearing his status with such ease that no offense had been taken. He was destined for the finest Florentine society, a circle where fine plate abounded and the Signory permitted the wearing of rich brocades. Where else would a handsome young scholar be welcomed, capable of playing the lute and singing joyful songs? His bright countenance, easy smile, and liquid voice seemed to bring a holiday spirit to the weary and the sad. He was a professor likely to make the Greek classics beloved by the sons of great families.

But this was not Tito's entire fortune. He had sold all his jewels save for one ring he refused to part with, bringing him into possession of five hundred gold florins. The moment he first held this sum marked the climax of the first serious internal struggle his cheerful nature had faced. An importunate thought, previously ignored as a shadow trailing his steps, suddenly seized him. He was forced to pause and decide: would he submit to duty, or offer a refusal carrying irreversible consequences?

It was in the room above Nello's shop, which Tito now rented, that the elder Cennini delivered the final portion of the funds on behalf of Bernardo Rucellai, the buyer of the two precious gems.

"Ecco, giovane mio!" the respected printer and goldsmith declared. "You now possess a modest fortune. If you will take my advice, deposit these florins in a secure place where they may grow, rather than letting them slip through your fingers on banquets and youthful follies. It has become too fashionable among scholars, particularly those like our Pietro Crinito who believe their studies require scent and embroidery, to squander wealth with one hand until they must beg with the other. I have brought you the money; you are free to make a wise or unwise choice. I shall observe which way the scales tip. We Florentines consider a man a member of an Art only after he has demonstrated his skill and been matriculated; similarly, no one is truly initiated into the Art of Life until they have been thoroughly tempted. If you decide to invest your florins in usury, let me know tomorrow. Scholars may marry and should have provisions ready for the *morgen-cap*. Addio."

As Cennini closed the door, Tito turned, his smile fading, fixing his gaze on the table where the coins lay. He made no other move, standing with his thumbs hooked in his belt, staring downward in a trance-like state of intense concentration.

"A man's ransom!" Who had spoken those words? Wasn't five hundred florins precisely the price of a man's freedom? Suppose, under this midday sun on some distant, hot coast, a man of advanced years—one possessing lofty thoughts and a passionate heart, who decades prior had rescued a boy from beggary, filth, and cruelty, raising him tenderly as a father—was now toiling as a slave? Perhaps he was hacking wood or hauling water, beaten and bruised for lacking agility. Did he whisper to himself, "Tito will find me"? Could he not simply transport the manuscripts and gems to Venice, raise funds, and pursue his rescuer relentlessly? If this were certain, could Tito, seeing the gem-money before him, choose to remain in Florence, basking in the soft airs of promised love and prosperity, refusing to risk everything for his benefactor? No, certainly not, *if it were certain*.

Yet, nothing was certain. The galley had indeed been intercepted by a Turkish vessel en route to Delos, according to reports from the escaping companion ship. But there had been resistance, likely bloodshed, and a man had been seen plunging into the sea. Who survived amidst the chaos? What befell the multitude of possibilities? Had not Tito himself suffered shipwreck and narrowly escaped drowning? He had ample reason to fear the omnipresent danger of accidents that could render all plans futile. Rumors of pirate settlements on Delos were unreliable, possibly irrelevant. What would happen if he abandoned Florence for Venice, secured official letters, and sailed for the Archipelago? Most likely, he would be captured himself, spending all his florins on preliminary expenses before becoming another destitute wanderer, with no gems left to sell.

Tito saw this outcome with greater clarity than the moment he might locate his father and grant him deliverance. It would surely be unjust for him, in his full maturity, having endured the restrictions of schooling, to turn his back on promised love and distinction, only to lose both forever.

"And yet," he murmured to himself, "if I were certain Baldassarre Calvo was alive, and that I could free him through any effort or peril, I would leave now—I have the money. It was pointless to debate before. I would go to Bardo and Bartolommeo Scala and reveal the whole truth."

Tito did not explicitly acknowledge to himself that if those two men knew the full story, he would have no choice but to seek his benefactor, the rightful owner of the gems, whom he had ambiguously referred to as "lost." Nor did he admit what he already knew: that distinguished Greeks frequently made sacrifices, undertook repeated voyages, and petitioned crowned heads to liberate relatives from Turkish slavery. Public opinion did not view this as exceptional virtue.

This was his first genuine dialogue with his own conscience. Until now, he had followed the impulses of the moment, concealing half the truth without considering his motives long enough to confront them. What was the point of revealing everything? True, the thought had crossed his mind several times since leaving Nauplia that it was a relief to be rid of Baldassarre, and he would have preferred to know exactly who had fallen overboard. But such thoughts arise inevitably from relationships that have become burdensome. Baldassarre was demanding and grew stranger with age. Constantly scrutinizing Tito's mind to match his own inflated expectations, the elderly man—thick-set, heavy-browed, and bald, past sixty—had lost the attractiveness of his intellectual intensity, which had devolved into monotonous repetition. Stranded among strangers, unless he possessed the philosopher's stone, Baldassarre would find few admirers offering rank, youth, and beauty. Feelings fueled by novelty cannot build a home for dimmed spirits; if any love remains, it must be that rooted in memory, distilling the sweet balsam of fidelity and patient tenderness.
