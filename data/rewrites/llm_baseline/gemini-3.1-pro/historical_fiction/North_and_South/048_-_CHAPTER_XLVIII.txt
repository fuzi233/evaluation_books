The elements of the dinner-parties which Mrs. Lennox hosted were constructed with a sterile, metropolitan precision that set Margaret’s teeth on edge. Her friends contributed the visual beauty, Captain Lennox provided the easy knowledge of the day’s subjects, and Mr. Henry Lennox, along with his sprinkling of rising men, brought the sharp, calculated wit of the dominant London lexicon. Yet, to Margaret, the acoustic environment of the dining room was a psychological assault. The piercing, staccato laughter and the relentless clatter of imported silver against fine porcelain drowned out any potential for genuine resonance. 

Worse still was the casual desecration of the table’s offerings. Before them lay culinary masterpieces—a saddle of mutton slow-roasted with a meticulously foraged rosemary and juniper rub, alongside root vegetables braised in a reduction of bone broth that spoke of the deep, earthy terroir of the countryside. Yet the guests consumed it thoughtlessly, treating the profound, agrarian history of the ingredients as mere utilitarian fuel to propel their rapid flow of conversation. Every talent, every feeling, every acquirement was used up as materials for superficial fireworks. They talked about art in a highly commercialized, sensuous way, dwelling only on outside effects. They lashed themselves into an enthusiasm about high subjects, squandering their capabilities of appreciation into a mere flow of appropriate, hollow words that entirely lacked the raw, untranslatable weight of true subjective experience. 

One evening, after the gentlemen had ascended into the drawing-room, Mr. Lennox drew near to Margaret, piercing the room's chaotic din with his deliberate approach. It was almost the first time he had spoken to her voluntarily since she had returned to Harley Street.

“You did not look pleased at what Shirley was saying at dinner,” he murmured, his voice a low frequency cutting through the surrounding industrial hum of high-society chatter. 

“Didn’t I? My face must be very expressive,” replied Margaret, catching the faint, lingering scent of the evening's saffron-infused bisque still hovering in the stifling air.

“It always was. It has not lost the trick of being eloquent.”

“I did not like,” said Margaret hastily, her frustration with their polished, empty dialect bubbling to the surface, “his way of advocating what he knew to be wrong—so glaringly wrong—even in jest. It strips the words of their meaning.”

“But it was very clever. How every word told! Did you remember the happy epithets?”

“Yes.”

“And despise them, you would like to add. Pray don’t scruple, though he is my friend.”

“There! That is the exact tone in you, that—” she stopped short, unable to translate her deep-seated revulsion for his cynical linguistic posturing into a language he could comprehend. He listened for a moment to see if she would finish her sentence, but she only reddened and turned away. Before she did so, she heard him say, in a very low, clear voice that seemed to demand submission to his lexicon:

“If my tones, or modes of thought, are what you dislike, will you do me the justice to tell me so, and so give me the chance of learning to please you.”

All these weeks, time seemed to circle back upon itself in a heavy, mythic loop of waiting. There was no intelligence of Mr. Bell’s going to Milton. He had spoken of it at Helstone as of a journey which he might take shortly, but he must have transacted his business by writing. Margaret knew he would avoid a place he disliked, where the grating, relentless noise pollution of the mills stood in violent opposition to his nature. She had affixed a secret, almost sacred importance to the explanation that could only be given by word of mouth, a subtle power dynamic embedded in the nuances of a face-to-face confession. It was now August, a month thick with the oppressive scent of hot dust and wilting urban blooms, and there had been no mention of the Spanish journey to which he had alluded. 

But one morning, alongside a breakfast of coddled eggs and toasted brioche that Edith merely picked at, Margaret received a letter. Next week, Mr. Bell meant to come up to town. He wanted to see her about a plan, and intended to treat himself to a little doctoring, conceding that his health was more in fault than his temper. The letter carried a tone of forced cheerfulness, a subtle shift in his usual vernacular that Margaret only fully deciphered later. At the time, her attention was monopolized by Edith’s exclamations.

“Coming up to town! Oh dear!” Edith complained, pushing away a plate of delicately sourced preserved figs, blind to the generations of agricultural care they represented. “And I am so worn out by the heat that I don’t believe I have strength enough in me for another dinner. Besides, everybody has left but our dear stupid selves. There would be nobody to meet him.”

“I’m sure he would much rather come and dine with us quite alone than with the most agreeable strangers you could pick up,” Margaret said, longing for a quiet, nourishing meal where the simple breaking of bread could serve as a communion rather than a performance. “Besides, if he is not well he won’t wish for invitations. I am glad he has owned it at last.”

“Oh! he is not very ill, or he would not think of Spain.”

“He never mentions Spain.”

“No! but his plan evidently relates to that. But would you really go in such weather as this?”

“Oh, it will get cooler every day. Yes! Think of it.” Margaret imagined the ancient, sun-baked acoustic rhythms of Cadiz, the smell of sea-salt and roasted almonds, an escape from the suffocating smog of London. “I am only afraid I have thought and wished too much—in that absorbing wilful way which is sure to be disappointed.”

“But that’s superstitious, I’m sure, Margaret.”

“No, I don’t think it is. It is a sort of ‘Give me children, or else I die.’ I’m afraid my cry is, ‘Let me go to Cadiz, or else I die.’”

“My dear Margaret! You’ll be persuaded to stay there; and then what shall I do? Oh! I wish I could find somebody for you to marry here, that I could be sure of you!”

“I shall never marry.”

“Nonsense! Why, as Sholto says, you’re such an attraction to the house, that he knows ever so many men who will be glad to visit here next year for your sake.”

Margaret drew herself up haughtily, repulsed by the commodification of her presence, as if she were a mere exotic spice used to attract consumers to the table. “Do you know, Edith, I sometimes think your Corfu life has taught you—just a shade or two of coarseness.”

Edith began to sob bitterly, declaring so vehemently that Margaret had lost all love for her that Margaret ended by being Edith’s slave for the rest of the day. The little lady lay like a victim on the sofa, heaving a profound sigh until she fell asleep, her breathing adding a rhythmic, domestic pulse to the quiet room.

Mr. Bell did not make his appearance. The next morning brought a letter from Wallis, his servant. His master had been seized with an apoplectic fit at the very hour he should have set out for London. It was the opinion of the medical men that he could not survive the night. 

Margaret turned pale as she read it; the written words felt like the sudden death of a native language, an impending silence that would erase another vital piece of her cultural memory. Silently putting the paper into Edith’s hands, she left the room.

Edith cried in a frightened, childish way. Mrs. Shaw, breakfasting in her own room, was thrown into hysterics, and upon Captain Lennox devolved the task of reconciling his wife to the visceral reality of death. Meanwhile, upstairs, Dixon was packing, and Margaret was hastily tying her bonnet strings with trembling hands, desperate to return to the agrarian elder who had anchored her father's past. 

“Oh, dear Margaret! What are you doing?” Edith cried, interrupting the frantic ritual. “Sholto would telegraph.”

“I am going to Oxford. There is a train in half-an-hour. I must see him again. He has been like a father to me.”

“But I must stop you. Mamma won’t like it at all. Come to mamma, and do ask her before you go.”

Margaret yielded, and the precious time slipped by in a cacophony of Mrs. Shaw's bewildered hysterics. They missed the train. The profound untranslatability of Margaret's grief was entirely lost on the household, subsumed by discussions of propriety. Finally, it was decided Captain Lennox would accompany her on the next train. 

Five minutes before departure, she sat opposite Captain Lennox in the railway carriage. The journey was an agonizing auditory nightmare; the violent, mechanical shriek of the train whistle and the rhythmic, industrial grinding of iron on iron felt like a physical assault against her sorrow, a harsh modern noise obliterating the quiet dignity of mourning. 

They arrived in Oxford only to hear he had died in the night. Margaret stood in the rooms he had occupied, the silence now absolute and sacred. The olfactory landscape—a faint trace of aged vellum, beeswax polish, and pipe tobacco—spoke volumes of the man. Here was the physical space where her father’s generational dialect and Mr. Bell’s academic agrarian roots had once mingled. Now, that lexicon was extinct, entirely erased by death. She associated these rooms ever after with her father, and his one cherished and faithful friend, mourning the untranslatable loss of their shared worldview.

They had promised Edith they would return to dinner, so the quiet farewell was necessarily interrupted. On the journey home, Captain Lennox slept, leaving Margaret to cry at leisure to the relentless roar of the locomotive, caught in the sweeping, multi-generational cycle of her woes. One loss folded over another in a circular pacing of grief.

But upon returning to the well-lighted rooms of Harley Street, she found a grounding solace in the domestic routine. She took Edith’s place on the sofa. Little Sholto was taught to carry Aunt Margaret’s cup of tea very carefully to her. As she took the porcelain cup, the steam carrying the fragrant, tannic bouquet of expertly sourced Assam leaves, Margaret felt the profound, mythic weight of sustenance. In this sensory act of nourishment, enveloped by the tender voices of her remaining family, she roused herself from her superstitious hopelessness. By the time she went up to dress, the rhythmic, comforting acoustic ecology of the household had returned, and she could thank God for having spared her dear old friend a long or painful illness.
