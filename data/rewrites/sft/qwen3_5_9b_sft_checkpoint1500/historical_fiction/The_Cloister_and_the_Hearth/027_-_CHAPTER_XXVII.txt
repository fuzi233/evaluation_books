The physician returned home, his brow furrowed with a pain he termed "a bad burn." In the economy of human affairs, one often cauterizes the neighbor's finger while singering one's own. His housekeeper, a woman of practical wisdom, applied a mild salve of ancient recipe. He submitted to her ministrations with the patience of a lamb, his sole ambition being swift recovery, while simultaneously drafting a bill for shattered vials and arranging for the immediate imprisonment of his traveling guests. Before a magistrate, he swore an oath that these strangers, owing him money, plotted to flee the town instantly.

Alas, it proved his unluckiest day. His earnest, albeit dishonest, endeavor to commit perjury was thwarted by a circumstance he had never anticipated. He had, in fact, spoken the truth. And yet, in an affidavit! The officers, arriving at "The Silver Lion," discovered that the travelers had already taken wing.

Turning to the riverbank for intelligence, they learned the friends were heading upstream in hot pursuit. This fleeting escape owed everything to Denys's acute sense and observation. After a burst of laughter—so hearty it served as a tonic—he sobered abruptly. "Gerard Dusseldorf," he declared, "is no place for us. That old fellow departed with unnatural silence for such a chatterbox. We are strangers here; the bailiff is his friend. Within five minutes, we shall lie in a dungeon for assaulting a Dusseldorf dignitary. Are you strong enough to hobble to the water's edge?"

"The odds, Denys?" Gerard asked, his voice trembling with fever. "Untold, and entirely in favor of the boat. I pine for Rome; Rome is my path to Sevenbergen. There, we shall lie in a boat, but *upon* the Rhine—the famous, cool, refreshing Rhine. I feel its breezes already. The mere sight will cure a hop-and-a-thimbler's fever like mine. Away!"

Seeing his companion in this agitated state, Denys hastily settled accounts with the landlord, and they hurried to the river. Inquiry revealed that the public boat had departed half an hour prior, and no others would launch until evening. However, by questioning numerous locals and gathering a crowd, they secured an offer from an elderly man and his two sons to provide a private craft.

A bystander chuckled at this arrangement. "The current is far too strong for three oars."

"Then my comrade and I shall assist with the rowing," offered the invalid.

"No need," the old man replied kindly. "Bless your silly heart, he owns the other boat."

A powerful breeze filled a broad sail, and the men, rowing alongside, propelled the vessel at a spanking pace.

"Are ye better, lad, for the river breeze?"

"Much better. But truly, the doctor did me good."

"The doctor? You refused his remedies!"

"No, but I mean... you'll say I'm nothing—but knocking the old fool down somehow soothed me."

"Amiable dove! How thy little character unfolds daily, like a rosebud. I misread thee at first."

"Nay, Denys, take me not wrongly. I trust I bore with his idle threats, though his voice pierced my poor ears. Yet he was an infidel, or next to one, and such I am taught to abhor. Did he not imply we owe our inward parts to men with long Greek names, and not to Him, whose name is but a syllable, yet whose hand governs all the earth? Pagan!"

"So you struck him down forthright—as a true Christian."

"Now, Denys, cease your jesting. Do not take the part of an ill man. Had it been a thunderbolt from Heaven, he would have met his due; yet he took but a sorry bolster from this weak arm."

"What weak arm?" Denys inquired, his eyes twinkling. "I have lived among arms, and by Samson's hairy paw, I have never seen one more like a catapult. The bolster wrapped around his nose, and the two ends kissed behind his head; his forehead resonated. Had he been Goliath, or Julius Caesar, instead of an old quack, down he would have gone. Saint Denys guard me from such feeble opposites as thou! And above all, from their weak arms—you diabolical young hypocrite."

The river wound through many turns, shifting the wind from behind them to the side. They adjusted course to keep the boat from heeling, save for a five-year-old boy, the boatman's grandson and darling. This urchin had slipped aboard at the start; being too light to disturb the trim, he existed above, or rather below, the laws of navigation.

They sailed merrily, unaware that a whole posse of constables, armed with the bailiff's writ, pursued them. Though the wind gusted, the current ran deep and fast.

Suddenly, Gerard recalled that this route led to Rome, not Burgundy. "Oh, Denys," he said, his face pale, "this is not your road."

"I know it," Denys replied quietly. "But what can I do? I cannot leave thee till the fever breaks. It lingers still; thou art red and white by turns. I have watched thee. I must e'en proceed to Cologne, I fear, and then strike across."

"Thank Heaven," Gerard breathed, a touch of self-deception coloring his eagerness. "'Twere a sin to be so near Cologne and not behold it. Oh, man, it is a vast, ancient city of my dreams, which fortune denied me. Alas, by what cruel chance do I arrive now? Well then, Denys," he continued, less warmly, "it is old enough to have been founded by a Roman lady in the first century, sacked by Attila, and later marred by Norman Lothaire. It possesses a church for every week of the year, besides countless chapels, convents, and nunneries. Above all, there stands the stupendous Minster, unfinished, wherein rest the Three Kings who brought gifts to our Lord: Melchior with gold, Gaspar with frankincense, and Balthazar, the Black King, who carried myrrh. Over their bones rises the Shrine, the wonder of the world—a masterpiece of ever-shining brass brighter than gold, studded with exquisite imagery and brave colors. Two broad stripes run to and fro, adorned with jewels so great and rare that each could ransom a crown-wearer. Upon it stand the three kings curiously counterfeited; two in solid silver, richly gilt, bareheaded; but the Ethiopian king, ebony-skinned, bears a golden crown. In the midst sits our Blessed Lady, in virgin silver, holding Christ, flanked at the corners by four goodly waxen tapers that burn night and day. Holy eyes have watched and renewed that light unceasingly for ages, and holy eyes shall watch them in eternity. Legend says they burned when first found, and they shall light the earth unto its grave. There is also St. Ursula's Church, where lies the saint herself and her eleven thousand companions, virgins put to the sword by barbarous Moors on the twenty-third day of October, two hundred and thirty-eight. Their bones are piled in the vaults, many of their skulls displayed in the church. St. Ursula rests in a thin golden case upon the high altar, shown to humble Christians only on solemn days."

"Eleven thousand virgins!" cried Denys. "What babes German men must have been in days of yore. Would that all their bones might turn to flesh again, their skulls to sweet faces, as we pass through the gates. Odds are some are weary of their estate by this time."

"Tush, Denys!" Gerard chided gently. "Why wilt thou, being good, make thyself seem evil? If thy wishing-cap be on, pray that we meet the meanest of those wise virgins in the next world, and to that end, let us reverence their holy dust in this one. And there is the Church of the Maccabees, with the cauldron in which they and their mother Solomon were boiled by a wicked king for refusing swine's flesh."

"Oh, peremptory king! And pig-headed Maccabees! I would have eaten bacon with my pork-liver rather than change places at the fire with my meat."

"What scurvy words? It was their faith."

"Nay, bridle thy choler. Tell me, are there naught but churches in this thy vaunted city? For I affect Sir Knight rather than Sir Priest."

"Ay, marry. There is a university nearly a hundred years old. There is a marketplace, fairest in the world, surrounded by houses great as palaces. There stands a stupendous Senate-House covered with images, and at its head stands one of stout Herman Grin, a soldier like thyself, lad."

"Aye. Tell me of him! What feat of arms earned him his niche?"

"A rare one. He slew a lion in fair combat, with nothing but his cloak and a short sword. He thrust the cloak into the brute's mouth and cut its spine in twain. There stands the man's effigy, and beside it the lion's, to prove it. The like was never done but by three others, I ween: Samson, Lysimachus of Macedon, and Benaiah, captain of David's host."

"Marry! Three tall fellows. I would like well to sup with them all tonight."
