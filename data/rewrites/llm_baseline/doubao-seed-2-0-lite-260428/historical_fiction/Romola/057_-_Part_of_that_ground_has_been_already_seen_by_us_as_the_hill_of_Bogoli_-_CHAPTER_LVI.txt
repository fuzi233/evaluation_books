The summer morning’s warmth had already grown thick and oppressive on Romola’s skin by the time she turned off the shaded path along Florence’s walls, fresh from visiting San Marco, and made her way back into the tangled web of streets through the gate of Santa Croce.

Borgo La Croce was so still that the tap of her leather sandals on sunbaked paving stones echoed loud and clear in the hush, until she rounded a gentle curve and spotted a tiny figure not three paces ahead: a boy no older than three, clad in nothing but a thin white linen shift, who paused mid-waddle and blinked around the empty street, lost. At first she only saw his back—sturdy, square-shouldered, crowned with a cloud of coppery auburn curls—but the next instant he twisted toward her, his dark eyes swimming with unshed tears, his lower lip trembling and pushed out in a wail. A long shadow falling over him as Romola drew closer pushed his bewildered fear over the edge, and he burst into ragged, sobbing cries, big tears rolling down his plump brown cheeks.

Romola’s long-buried maternal instinct, the soft, untested core of all her deep, tender affection, rose unbidden. She pulled her veil back from her face, knelt on the warm stone, and wrapped her arms around him, pressing her cheek to his damp one while she murmured soft, lulling endearments in the Florentine lilt he knew. His sobs rose louder at first, but he made no move to squirm away. Then, as abruptly as children’s griefs often vanish, the wailing cut off entirely. His face smoothed out of its crumpled distress, and he stared up at her, mouth hanging open in awe.

“Poor little one, you’ve gone and lost yourself,” she said, kissing his curls. “Never mind. We’ll find your home again, I promise. I expect your mama is out looking for you right now.”

She guessed he had wriggled free the moment his mother’s gaze had strayed, and that his family would be close on his heels. “Oh, but you are a heavy, heavy lad,” she laughed, trying to heft him to her feet and finding him far too solid to carry. “Come then, you shall toddle along right beside me.”

His lips stayed parted in that still, shocked silence, one fist still clutching the hem of his shift as tight as ever, but the other slipped willingly into Romola’s pale, strong, soft hand.
“You do have a mama, don’t you?” Romola asked, as they set off down the lane, looking down at him with a sharp, unnameable yearning. He said nothing. A little girl might have chattered through a dozen stories by now, but this square, stubborn little man with the cloud of curls held his tongue.

But he knew his own neighborhood the moment they neared it. At the corner before the Church of San Ambrogio, he tugged hard on her hand, craning his neck to point down the side street.
“Ah, that’s the way home, is it?” Romola smiled. He only leaned forward and pulled harder, wordlessly urging her to walk faster. He made two more decisive turns of his own before they came to a short lane opening onto sunlit garden ground, and the little fellow dragged her toward the stone steps leading up to a small house at the lane’s end. He showed no sign of wanting to let go of her hand, and Romola had no wish to leave him until she was certain she had delivered him safe to his family.

They climbed the steps, blinking in the dimness of the shadowed landing after the bright street, until a flood of warm light poured out of an open doorway. They passed through a tiny lobby, and Romola paused at the second open door. No one inside had heard their approach.
On a low chair at the far end of the room, back half turned to the light, sat Tessa, one hand resting on the edge of a wicker cradle, her head lolled gently to one side, fast asleep by the swaying cradle’s side. Near the window, her back to the door, sat Monna Lisa, deaf as a post, chopping salad with slow, unhurried motions. Romola only had a heartbeat to take in the quiet, still scene before Lillo wrenched his hand free and scrambled across the floor to press his head against his mother’s arm, not waking her outright, but staring back at Romola from that safe vantage point, his face perfectly serious.

Tessa jolted awake as Lillo pushed against her, and looked up in dazed confusion. The second her eyes landed on Romola in the doorway, she shot to her feet, blushed bright crimson, and began to tremble, too startled to speak or step forward.
“Ah, we have met before, you and I,” Romola said, stepping forward with a soft smile. “I am so glad this is your little boy. He was crying all alone in the street, you see—he must have slipped away when you weren’t looking. We walked together for a little way, and then he knew the road home and brought me right to your door. You didn’t even miss him? That is a mercy, else you would have been half out of your wits with fear.”

The shock that Lillo had wandered so far wiped every other feeling from Tessa’s mind for a moment. The color drained from her face, she snatched Lillo by the arm and dragged him over to Monna Lisa, calling loudly, half-sobbing, right in the old deaf woman’s ear, “Oh Lisa, you are wicked! Why do you stand there with your back turned to the door? Lillo ran all the way out into the street!”
“Holy Mother of God!” Monna Lisa yelped, letting her salad spoon clatter onto the board. “Where were you, then? I thought you were right there, keeping an eye on him!”
“But you know I always nod off when I’m rocking the cradle,” Tessa protested, pouting.
“Well, well, we shall have to bar the outer door, or tie the little rascal to the table leg soon enough,” Monna Lisa huffed. “He’ll be as tricky as Satan himself before long, that’s the honest truth. But how did he find his way back?”

The question pulled Tessa back to the full consciousness of Romola standing in her parlor. She turned back to Romola, blushing and shy again, and Monna Lisa’s gaze followed hers. The old woman bowed low, then shuffled a step closer.
“Doubtless this most noble lady is the one who brought him safe home,” she said. “It shames me that he was found running the streets with nothing on but his shift—he kicked and screamed the whole morning when we tried to dress him, and his poor soft mother will never suffer a hand to be laid on him. What’s a half-deaf old woman to do, with no stick to threaten him, when his little legs get so strong? Look at those plump little legs, your nobleness.”

Lillo, hearing his legs discussed, hitched his shift up higher and stared down at his own round olive calves with calm, curious detachment. Romola laughed, stooped to give him a playful little shake and a kiss on the ankle, and the small, easy gesture chased the last of Tessa’s fear away. Back during that wild Carnival years ago, when Romola had stepped out of the crowd to save her, Tessa had asked Naldo what kind of woman could be so kind and vanish so fast, if she was the Holy Madonna herself. He had laughed and said no, not exactly a Madonna, just one of the good saints, and said no more. So Tessa had carried that half-childish, half-reverent image of Romola in her head ever since, and seeing her laugh and fuss over Lillo felt as warm and ordinary as a blessing from a friendly saint who would never smite anyone the way Nofri and the devil did.
“Ninna is asleep in the cradle,” Tessa said, pointing. “She’s pretty too.”

Romola went over to lean over the wicker cradle, and saw the baby stir, then blink open her bright blue eyes. “Ah, she’s waking up now,” Romola said. “You pick her up, and I’ll sit in this chair—may I?—and hold Lillo. Come here, little one.”
She sank into Tito’s favourite cushioned chair, and held out her arms. Lillo hesitated, pointed a tiny finger at her, half confused, half cross, and said, “That’s Babbo’s chair.” He couldn’t imagine what would happen if Babbo came home and found a stranger sitting in his spot.
“But Babbo isn’t here right now, and I’ll be gone soon,” Romola said, laughing, wondering for the first time what sort of man this Naldo was, who had a wife dressed like a contadina but every bit of her small space so neat and soft, no sign of hard work or want. Lillo climbed up into her lap, found it far softer than any he’d known, and immediately began prodding at the embroidery on her dress, her fingers, hunting for any trinkets besides the rosary hanging at her belt.

Tessa had been occupied soothing the fussy newly-woken Ninna, but now she sat down in her low chair near Romola’s knee, preening the baby’s little dress, a little jealous that the strange lovely lady seemed to notice the boy first, the same way Naldo always did.
“Lillo was going to be cross with me for sitting in Babbo’s chair,” Romola said, leaning down to kiss the baby’s tiny bare foot. “Will Babbo be home soon to want his seat back?”
“Oh no!” Tessa said. “You can sit there as long as you like. I shall be so sad when you go away. The first time you came to take care of me at Carnival, I thought it was magic—you came and vanished so fast. Naldo said maybe you were a saint, and that made me tremble a little, but I know saints are very good, and you have always been so kind. Now you’ve saved Lillo too. Perhaps you will always come and take care of me. That’s how Naldo was, once, long ago: he came and took care of me when I was all alone and scared at the San Giovanni feast, and I never knew where he came from—he was so beautiful and good. And you are, too,” she finished, looking up at Romola with soft, devout admiration.

“Naldo is your husband,” Romola said, watching the little boy’s dark, sharply defined eyebrows, far too distinct for a toddler. She did not phrase it as a question, and Tessa’s eyes went wide with wonder.
“Ah! You know him!” she said. “Perhaps you know Nofri, and Peretola, and our little house on the hill, and everything? Yes, his eyes are just like Lillo’s, but his hair is long and dark, not the curls. Oh—look!”
She reached up to the thin red silk cord slung around her neck, pulled out a tiny tattered parchment breve, a little piece of red coral charm, and a long dark curl of hair, carefully tied at the end, hanging alongside the sacred trinkets. She held it out to Romola, keeping it well out of Ninna’s grabbing little hands.
“I cut that just last week,” she said, laying the soft dark lock across Romola’s pale fingers. “See how bright it still is! The old ones get dull after a while, so when his hair grows long again, he lets me cut a new one, and I keep it with the breve. Sometimes he goes away for such a long, long time, and I think it helps keep me safe while he’s gone.”

A faint, cold shiver ran through Romola the moment the curl touched her skin. The second Tessa had spoken of her husband appearing mysteriously out of nowhere years before, a wild, leaping hope had risen in Romola’s chest, beating fast: that this woman was Tito’s true wife, that her own forced, miserable marriage was null and void before God and man. The curl in her fingers was exactly the same as the one she herself had cut from Tito’s head, five years before, and twisted around one of her own locks for safekeeping. She kept her face calm, gentle, determined not to startle this soft, ignorant, trusting thing before her.
“It is a very beautiful curl,” she said, resisting the urge to pull her hand away. “Lillo’s curls will darken to match it, I think. But when he leaves you to go away, you never know where he goes?”
“No,” Tessa said, tucking her charms back into the neck of her dress, out of the children’s reach. “But I know Messer San Michele watches over him, because he gave him a beautiful coat made all of little linked chains, and if he puts that on, no one can hurt him. And if you really are a saint,” she added, hesitating, that old dreamy awe of Romola rising again, “you would take care of him too, wouldn’t you, because you already take care of me and Lillo?”

A hot, agitated flush rose over Romola’s face, the moment the last faint doubt vanished: this was Tito’s secret household, hidden from her, hidden from all of Florence. For a wild, dizzy instant she felt almost exultant—she might not have to carry the weight of this false, suffocating marriage any longer. But the hope died fast, as she leaned her cheek against Lillo’s soft curls. “I will always take care of you if I ever see you in need,” she said, clear and steady. “But that chain coat—he did not have that when you two were first married, did he? Those days he would be gone far longer, right?”
“Oh yes!” Tessa nodded. “Much, much longer. I thought he would never come back. I used to cry so hard. They beat me back then, long ago, at Peretola where we kept the goats and mules.”
“How long were you married before he got that chain coat?” Romola asked, her heart thudding so hard she could hear it in her ears.
Tessa frowned, counting slowly on her fingers, and Romola watched the little moving fingers as if they would unlock the whole secret of her fate.
“The chestnuts were ripe when we married,” Tessa said, “and then they ripened again at Peretola before he came back, and then they ripened a third time on the hill. And then the soldiers came, and we heard the trumpets, and that was when Naldo got the coat.”
“So you had been married for more than two years before that,” Romola said. “And in which church were you two wed?”
Tessa flinched at her sharp, direct tone, and looked up at her, hesitating. She had babbled every little trivial memory without thinking until then, but that part Naldo had strictly forbidden her to tell anyone. “Naldo said I must never speak of that,” she said doubtfully. “Do you think he would not be angry if I told you?”
“It is right that you should tell me,” Romola said, her voice soft but carrying a quiet, unshakable authority no one had ever taught Tessa to disobey.
Her voice dropping to a whisper, Tessa spilled it all: there was no church, no proper parish priest, no banns. They had been married at the fair for the Feast of the Nativity, out in the piazza, by a wandering friar who had set up a makeshift altar for the crowds, then Tito had led her back into the church and vanished immediately. She had gone home, her mother had died, Nofri had beaten her worse than ever, and she had not seen Tito for months until Carnival, when he had found her following him through the crowds, and promised he would come back for her soon, and eventually he had taken her away to the little house on the hill of Bogoli.

Romola sat silent, her gaze far away. There was no proof, no legal marriage here. Her own bond to Tito was not void. All that hope had been for nothing. The cold weight of disappointment settled over her shoulders, and she loosened her hold around Lillo, leaning her cheek on her hand, not seeing the room around her. The little boy immediately squirmed, reached up both his brown arms to tug at her face, cross that her attention had drifted away. “Play with me again!” he demanded.

Romola roused, pulled him close again, and looked from his small earnest face to Tessa, who was staring up at her with that soft, uncomplicated reverence. A gentle, new resolve settled over her, like warm sunlight breaking through storm clouds. She pulled a small pair of scissors from the leather pouch at her belt, and cut off one long, rippling lock of her pale golden hair, while all three pairs of eyes—Tessa’s, Lillo’s, Ninna’s wide blue one—watched her move like curious little kittens.
“I have to leave you now,” she said, pressing the lock of hair into Tessa’s palm. “Leave this with you, to remember me. If ever you are in trouble, if ever you need help, you can think of me, and I promise I will come. I cannot tell you where I live, but I will find you if you ever need me. Addio.”
She set Lillo down gently, held out her hand to Tessa, who kissed it with a mix of awe and quiet sadness at her leaving. Romola’s mind was full of heavy, whirling thoughts, and she longed to be alone at once, but before she left, she leaned down to lay a gentle hand on Monna Lisa’s shoulder, bidding her farewell. Before the old woman could finish her low, deep bow, Romola was gone.

Tessa and Monna Lisa turned to each other at the same time, the two children clinging to Tessa’s skirts as if they too could feel the soft, strange, sacred hush in the room.
“Do you truly think she was a saint?” Tessa whispered right into Monna Lisa’s ear, holding up the golden lock.
Monna Lisa shook her head sharply, stroked the silky waves of the hair with a gnarled finger. “She is a great noble lady,” she said. “I saw women just like her, when I was young.”

Romola walked home through the sweltering afternoon, and sat alone in her rooms, turning the heavy truth over and over: her lot was unchanged. She was caught between two truths, warring in her chest.
