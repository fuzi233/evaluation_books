“The old fellow vanished—left for Arezzo the next morning, I reckon, not liking the scent of the French after his time in their hands. I went to the hospital to see if those monkish cooks had figured out whether he was still right in the head. They said he showed no signs of madness—just stared blankly at questions, as if planting a vine twenty miles away. A curious old cat, that one. I’d have liked to know more about him.”

It was in Nello’s shop, on the twenty-fourth of November—just one week after the French entered Florence—that Piero di Cosimo spoke these words. A small gathering of six or seven men had gathered there, unusually late in the afternoon, drawn by the electric tension that gripped the city. The air buzzed with anticipation: Florence stood poised on the edge of a political reckoning. Every public square teemed with people; every shopkeeper without a wife or assistant stood at his door, thumbs tucked into his belt, eyes scanning the streets. Artisans drifted through like idle splinters, ready to surge forward at any spark of news.

Nello had been plucking at his lute, half-sitting on the wooden board against the shop window, keeping watch over the piazza. Suddenly, he set the instrument aside.

“Ah!” he declared, with a note of satisfaction. “I wouldn’t have missed seeing those French soldiers waddling after their escaped prisoners! That’s what happens when I leave my shop to shave noble chins. Always—always something erupts in my piazza just when I’m gone.”

“Yes,” replied Piero, sharp as ever, “you should’ve been there—just to see your beloved Greek look as terrified as if Satanasso himself had seized him. I love watching your polished Messeri caught off guard, forced to show their true colours. What colour do you suppose a man’s liver is when he turns pale as bleached deer the moment some stranger grabs him?”

“Piero,” Nello retorted, “keep your sour sauce for your own eggs. What’s wrong with Tito Melema looking startled when a madman lunges at him? Your scholar isn’t like those brutish Swiss and Germans—whose heads are fit only for battering-rams, whose appetites are so vast they’d swallow a cannonball before breakfast. We Florentines value more than mere brute courage—something you can buy by the dozen from fools. When men realized they were smarter than oxen, they made oxen pull their carts. And when we Florentines saw we were smarter than others, we made them fight our wars.”

“Treason, Nello!” came a voice from the inner room. “That’s not the doctrine of the State. Florence is forging her weapons. And the Frate’s last vision showed Mars standing atop the Palazzo Vecchio, arm around San Giovanni Battista, who offered him a honeycomb.”

“Good enough, Francesco,” Nello replied calmly. “Florence has skulls thick enough to bombard Pisa with. The rest—the finer minds—can stay home to think and shave. As for our Piero here, if bravery means so much to him, let him take his biggest brush as a sword and his palette as a shield, then challenge the loudest-mouthed Swiss in the Prato to single combat.”

“_Va_, Nello,” growled Piero, “your tongue runs like a mill when the Arno floods—whether there’s grain or not.”

“Then let it be excellent grain,” Nello shot back. “For it would be as absurd to expect a grizzled painter like you to relish being pierced by a javelin as it would be to expect a man sharpened on the classics to enjoy having his face clawed by a wild beast.”

“There you go again,” Piero snapped, “trying to trap people into wearing sacks because you call them hose.”

“And who said anything about beasts or unarmed men charging into battle?” Piero countered. “Fighting is a trade—and not mine. I wouldn’t chase danger, but I could meet it if it came.”

“How come you’re so scared of thunder, then?” Nello pressed, determined to corner him. “You ought to understand why one man trembles at what another ignores—you who hide with the rats at the first sign of a storm.”

“It’s not courage or conscience,” Piero insisted. “It’s a sensitivity to loud noises. Nothing more.”

“Well,” Nello replied, “Tito Melema may have a peculiar sensitivity to being grabbed by an escaped prisoner. People are born with aversions—I can’t stand the smell of mint. Tito was born with a distaste for old men stumbling and clutching. Ecco!”

A ripple of laughter ran through the group. It was clear Piero’s dislike of Tito wasn’t shared by the others. The painter scowled, pulled tow from his pouch, and stuffed his ears in silent indignation. Nello continued, triumphant:

“No, my Piero, I won’t allow my _bel erudito_ to be slandered. Nor should Florence—especially now, when scholars are dropping like leaves at forty. Our Phoenix Pico has just flown straight to Paradise, as the Frate told us. And Poliziano, not two months ago, vanished—let’s hope he didn’t end up among the learned souls in Malebolge.”

“By the way,” said Francesco Cei, “have you heard Camilla Rucellari has bested the Frate in prophecy? Two years ago, she said Pico would die during the season of lilies. He died in November. ‘Not lily-time,’ scoffers said. But Camilla replied: ‘I meant the lilies of France—and look—they’re already under your nose.’ I say, _Euge, Camilla_! If the Frate can prove any of his visions matched this well, I’ll become a Piagnone tomorrow.”

“You’re too flippant about the Frate, Francesco,” said Pietro Cennini, the scholarly one. “We owe him much these past weeks—for preaching peace, quiet, and unity. Men of shallow sight would welcome the people breaking free from the Frate’s guidance now. And if the Most Christian King refuses to sign a fair treaty today, Fra Girolamo is the one we must trust to bring him to reason.”

“You speak truth, Messer Pietro,” Nello agreed. “The Frate is one of Florence’s firmest nails—so say the most respectable chins I’ve the honour to shave. But young Messer Niccolò mentioned earlier—no doubt Francesco feels the same—that visions stretch like Dido’s bull’s hide. To me, a dream means whatever comes after. As Franco Sacchetti says: a woman dreams of a serpent biting her, breaks a cup the next day, and cries, ‘See! I knew something would happen—now I know what the serpent meant.’”

“But the Frate’s visions aren’t like that,” interjected Cronaca. “He doesn’t just predict change—he says it will come swiftly. The Church will be scourged and renewed, the heathens converted. He’s not a slippery charlatan with loopholes. He’s—”

“What is this? What is this?” Nello cried, leaping from the board and peering out the door. “People are flooding into the piazza, shouting! Something’s happened in the Via Larga. Aha!” He burst out laughing, waving his cap. The others rushed to the door.

News from the Via Larga—exactly what they’d been waiting for. But the manner of its arrival surprised them all.

Carried aloft on a bench snatched from the street, sat Tito Melema, smiling with amused resignation. His cap had slipped off, dangling by the becchetto tied loosely around his neck. Spotting the group at Nello’s door, he raised a hand in recognition.

The next moment, he sprang from the bench onto a cart piled high with bales, positioned between the Baptistery and the Duomo steps. The crowd swarmed around him, clamorous as hungry fowl. But silence fell when he began to speak—his voice clear, warm, commanding:

“Citizens of Florence! I have no authority to speak except your will. But the news is good—and harmless to tell. The Most Christian King has signed a treaty honourable to Florence. Yet you owe this victory to one of your own—a citizen who spoke as the ancient Romans might have spoken. You owe it to Piero Capponi!”

A roar erupted. “Capponi! Capponi! What did he say?”

“Ah! He wouldn’t be sent from Herod to Pilate!”

“We knew Piero!”

“_Orsù_! Tell us—what did he say?”

As the clamour subsided slightly, Tito resumed:

“The King demanded too much—refused to yield—then declared, ‘I shall order my trumpets to sound.’ Then, Florentine citizens, your Piero Capponi—speaking with the voice of a free city—answered, ‘If you sound your trumpets, we will ring our bells!’ He tore the dishonourable terms from the secretary’s hands, ripped them apart, and turned to leave the royal presence.”

Another wave of shouts surged forth—demands for more.

“Then,” Tito continued, “the great majesty of France felt, perhaps for the first time, the true majesty of a free city. The King himself hurried from his seat to recall Piero Capponi. The spirit of Florence worked—not through force, but through a single, mighty word. And the King consented. The treaty is signed—preserving both Florence’s safety and her honour. The banner of France will fly over every Florentine galley as a sign of friendship and shared privilege—but above it, written boldly, will be the word: _Liberty_!”

“That is all I have to say—is it not enough? For it is glory to each of you, citizens of Florence, that you have among you a man who knows how to speak your will.”

Shouts rose once more. Tito surveyed the crowd with quiet amusement—each man believing Capponi had spoken for him, that he was the mind behind the mouthpiece. He enjoyed the irony: suddenly, an alien, a friend of the Medici, had become a herald of liberty, speaking to a people eager for meaning in a word they couldn’t define. He was glad he’d been seized and carried along—unplanned, yet perfect. Speaking to such crowds was easy, pleasant even. A man who could persuade needed fear nothing—could convince every faction he was deceiving the others.

From above, the faces of weavers and dyers were comical. Tito was growing comfortable in his role—so much so that he failed to notice the shift within himself. He stood with one hand holding his recovered cap, the other at his belt, a gentle smile lighting his long, bright eyes. He bowed respectfully before stepping down—when suddenly, his gaze met another.

This man was not part of the jubilant throng. Clean-shaven, hair closely cropped, wearing a plain felt hat—ordinary, yet unmistakable. One glance would not have confirmed it to anyone else—but to Tito, it was instant recognition. Not merely the escaped prisoner from the palace steps—but a face he had known long ago. In that split second, memory flashed: Baldassarre staring at him, the shock like a firebrand through his veins, the leap from the cart.

He would have jumped down anyway—he was in haste to reach the Palazzo Vecchio. This time, no hesitation, no betrayal. He vowed never to be caught off guard again. He would expect this face—like a recurring shadow in diseased vision.

But now, seeing Baldassarre shaven, clothed like a decent though poor citizen, the dread deepened. Madness seemed less likely. If he were sane—if he retained all his intellect and learning—why linger? Why not reveal himself?

Only one answer: vengeance. He was delaying to make it complete.

And yet, he delayed. That gave Tito a chance.

As Tito turned his back on the piazza, leaving behind the role he’d played, the crowd shifted focus. With his departure, their attention turned toward the entrances of the piazza. Then—mazzieri, mace-bearers, appeared from the Via de’ Martelli. Officials were coming. The syndics—those entrusted with the treaty—must be arriving. The treaty was signed. They were returning from the royal presence.

And Piero Capponi was among them.

The crowd parted silently. Voices softened. A profound hush settled—so deep that the tread of the syndics on the stone and the rustle of their black silk robes echoed like rain at night.

Four men approached. But the crowd waited not for the learned doctors Vespucci and Bonsi, nor for the popular Valori. The moment belonged to one man—strong, grave, unyielding, neither flattering the people nor bowing to power. A man shaped by fortune into a merchant, by nature into a soldier.

Only when he stepped into the piazza did silence break. A single, thunderous cry rang out:
“Capponi! Capponi! Well done, Capponi!”

The man turned, his face calm, his eyes filled with quiet pride. Two years later, Florence would give him a grand funeral—torches carried by all magistrates, banners trailing behind. But no one knows if he took pleasure in the praise delivered over his bier. Let us be content that he received thanks while he lived.
