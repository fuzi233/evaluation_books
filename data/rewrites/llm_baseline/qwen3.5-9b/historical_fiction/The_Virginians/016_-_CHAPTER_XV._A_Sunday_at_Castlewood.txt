The second day following Harry's arrival at Castlewood fell upon a Sunday. The chapel attached to the castle served double duty as the parish church for the village. A private door connecting the main house opened directly into a grand state pew reserved for the family. Upon the appointed hour, they all filed in with proper decorum, while a respectable congregation from the village filled the pews beneath them. High above, a few ancient, dust-laden banners hung from the rafters; Harry indulged in a pleasant fantasy that these had once been carried by retainers of his family during the Commonwealth Wars, in which his ancestors had fought with such loyalty and distinction.

Within the altar rails stood the effigy of the Esmond who lived in the time of King James the First—the common progenitor of everyone currently seated in the family pew. Madame de Bernstein, acting in her capacity as the Bishop's widow, never missed mass. She conducted her devotions with a gravity nearly as impressive as that of the ancestor depicted nearby, a figure with a square beard and red gown, forever kneeling on his stone hassock before his marble desk and open book, sheltered beneath his emblazoned coat of arms.

The clergyman was a tall, ruddy-faced, handsome young man who read the service in a lively, agreeable tone, imparting a dramatic flair to the scriptural passages. The music was excellent, performed by one of the young ladies of the family at the organ, though it might have been superior had it not been interrupted by a sudden burst of laughter emanating from the servants' pew. The cause was Mr. Warrington's lacquy, Gumbo. Knowing the tune assigned for the psalm, Gumbo launched into it with a voice so excessively loud and sweet that the entire congregation turned toward the "African warbler." Even the parson covered his mouth with his handkerchief, and the liveried gentlemen from London were thrown into a state of complete impropriety. Delighted by the attention he commanded, Mr. Gumbo continued his performance until it resembled a solo concert, silencing the clerk's voice entirely. The truth was, despite holding onto the hymnal alongside Pretty Molly, the porter's daughter—who had been the first to welcome the strangers to Castlewood—Gumbo sang and recited entirely by ear. He could not read a single syllable of the verses printed before him.

Once this impromptu chorus concluded, a brief sermon duly followed, though Harry found it far too short. In a discursive, familiar, and striking address, the clergyman recounted a scene witnessed the previous week: the execution of a horse-thief following the Assizes. He painted a portrait of the man, detailing his formerly good character, his loving family, and his agonizing separation from them. He depicted the execution itself with startling, terrible, and picturesque vividness. Unlike the stern, Calvinistic preachers Harry's mother preferred, this divine spoke not in archaic scripture phrases, but as one man of the world addressing other sinful souls who might profit from sound advice.

The unfortunate man, once a farmer with promising prospects, had succumbed to the vices of the age: drinking, card-playing, horse-racing, and cock-fighting. Against these sins, the young clergyman expressed generous indignation. The man had progressed to poaching and horse-stealing, crimes for which he now suffered. The minister rapidly sketched terrifying images of these rural offenses. He startled his listeners by describing how the "Eye of the Law" watched the poacher at midnight and set traps to ensnare criminals. He galloped the stolen horse over highways and commons, from county to county, yet showed Retribution always riding hard behind, seizing the malefactor at a country fair, dragging him before the justice, and refusing to unlock his manacles until they were dropped at the foot of the gallows. *Heaven be pitiful to the sinner!*

The clergyman acted out the scene. He whispered in the criminal's ear as he sat in the cart. He dramatically dropped his handkerchief onto the clerk's head. Harry recoiled as the cloth fell. The clergyman had been speaking for over twenty minutes; Harry felt he could have listened for an hour more and still believed he had been in the pulpit less than five minutes. The gentlemen in the great pew were thoroughly enlivened by the discourse. Occasionally, Harry noticed the servants' pew, observing their intense attention—especially Gumbo, his own man, sitting in an attitude of profound consternation. Yet, the smock-frocks seemed utterly unconcerned, striding out of the church as if nothing had occurred. Gaffer Brown and Gammer Jones accepted matters exactly as they came, while the rosy-cheeked, red-cloaked village lassos sat beneath their broad hats, entirely unmoved.

"My lord," the gentleman in the pew observed, nodding slightly as the divine's head and wig rose from the cushion, "Sampson has been strong today. He has assaulted the Philistines in great force."

"Beautiful, beautiful!" exclaimed Harry.

"Bet five to four it was his Assize sermon. He has been over to Winton to preach and to see those dogs," cried William.

The organist led the small congregation out into the sunshine. Only Sir Francis Esmond, dating from the time of James I, remained kneeling on his marble hassock before his stone prayer-book. Mr. Sampson emerged from his vestment in his cassock and nodded to the gentlemen still lingering in the great pew.

"Come up, and tell us about those dogs," said Mr. William, and the divine nodded with a laughing assent.

The gentlemen moved from the church into the gallery of their house, which connected them to the sacred building. Mr. Sampson made his way through the courtyard and soon joined them. Introduced by My Lord to the Virginian cousin of the family, Mr. Warrington, the chaplain bowed deeply and hoped Mr. Warrington would benefit from the virtuous example of his European kinsmen. Was he related to Sir Miles Warrington of Norfolk? Sir Miles was Mr. Warrington's father's elder brother. "What a pity he had a son!" the chaplain lamented. "'Twas a pretty estate, and Mr. Warrington looks as if he would become a baronetcy, and possess a fine estate in Norfolk."

"Tell me about my uncle," cried Virginian Harry eagerly.

"Tell us about those dogs!" added English Will, breathless.

"Two more jolly dogs, two more drunken dogs, saving your presence, Mr. Warrington, than Sir Miles and his son, I never saw. Sir Miles was a staunch friend and neighbor of Sir Robert's. He can drink down any man in the county, except his son and a few others. As for the other dogs Mr. William is anxious about—for Heaven hath made him a prey to dogs and all kinds of birds, like the Greeks in the *Iliad*—"

"I know that line in the *Iliad*," said Harry, blushing. "I only know five more, but I know that one." His head fell. He was thinking, *Ah, my dear brother George knew all the *Iliad* and all the *Odyssey*, and almost every book that was ever written besides!*

"What on earth" (mentioning only a place underground) "are you talking about now?" asked Will of his reverend companion.

The chaplain returned to the topic of the dogs and their exploits. He opined that Mr. William's dogs were more than a match for theirs. From dogs, the conversation drifted to horses. Mr. William was particularly eager regarding the Six Year Old Plate at Huntingdon.

"Have you brought any news of it, Parson?"

"The odds are five to four on Brilliant against the field," said the parson gravely, "but mind you, Jason is a good horse."

"Whose horse?" asked My Lord.

"Duke of Ancaster's. By Cartouche out of Miss Langley," replied the divine.

"Do you have horse races in Virginia, Mr. Warrington?"

"Haven't we!" cried Harry. "But oh! I long to see a good English race!"

"Do you... do you... bet a little?" continued his reverence cautiously.

"I have done such a thing," replied Harry with a smile.

"I'll take Brilliant even against the field, for ponies with you, cousin!" shouted Mr. William.

"I'll give or take three to one against Jason!" declared the clergyman.

"I don't bet on horses I don't know," said Harry, surprised to hear the chaplain speak thus, recalling his sermon from half an hour prior.

"Hadn't you better write home and ask your mother?" suggested Mr. William with a sneer.

"Will, Will!" called out My Lord. "Our cousin Warrington is free to bet, or not, as he pleases. Take care how you venture on either of them, Harry Warrington. Will is an old file, in spite of his smooth face. And as for Parson Sampson, I defy our ghostly enemy to get the better of him."

"Him and all his works, my lord!" said Mr. Sampson, bowing.

Harry was highly indignant at this allusion to his mother. "I'll tell you what, cousin Will," he said, "I am in the habit of managing my own affairs in my own way, without asking any lady to arrange them for me.
