Now, isn't that just the kind of tedious subject for a fellow who prefers to do absolutely nothing? But then again, the folks stuck inside looking out tend to miss ninety percent of the actual game. So, here I am, tucked away in my garden seat by the roadside, puffing away on my smoke of pure satisfaction and nibbling on the sweet, lazy leaves of idleness. From this perfect vantage point, I get a grand, sweeping view of the chaotic whirlpool churning past me on the Great High Road of Life.

It is an endless, wild parade. You can hear it day and night—the frantic clatter of millions of feet—some sprinting, some lumbering, some limping, but all rushing, all desperate in this feverish dash, all stretching their bones and hearts to reach a horizon that keeps moving further away with every step.

Take a look at the spectacle as it surges by—men and women, ancient and fresh, polite and rough, wealthy and penniless, cheerful and miserable—all hustling, bustling, scrambling. The burly ones shove the frail aside; the clever ones sneak past the simpletons; those in the rear elbow those ahead; and those in front kick, as they run, at everyone trailing behind. Peep closely at the fleeting show. There's an old gentleman gasping for air, and there a shy young lady being shoved along by a severe, sharp-chinned matron. There's a studious youth clutching a book titled *How to Get On in the World*, letting everyone trample over him while he stumbles along, eyes glued to his cover. There's a bored-looking chap having his elbow jabbed by a fashionably dressed woman; there's a boy gazing wistfully back at the sunny village he'll never see again; there's a broad-shouldered giant striding forward with an easy gait; and there, with a sneaky shuffle, a thin-faced, stooped fellow dodging obstacles on his path. There's a crafty rogue staring perpetually at the ground, weaving side-to-side, convinced he's making progress; and there, standing still with a noble countenance, a young man hesitating, torn between the distant goal and the muddy reality beneath his boots.

And now, a pretty girl enters the scene, her delicate features already etched with lines of worry at every step; now a weary man; now a hopeful lad.

A motley crew—a motley crew! Princes and paupers, sinners and saints, butchers, bakers, candlestick makers, tinkers, tailors, plowboys, and sailors—all jostling shoulder to shoulder. Here sits the counselor in his wig and gown; here crawls the old Jewish clothes-dealer under his dusty cap; here marches the soldier in his scarlet coat; here drags the undertaker's mute, his hatband dripping and gloves worn threadbare; here fumbles the musty scholar over his faded books; here dangles the scented actor with his flashy seals. Here shouts the slick politician about his legislative miracles; here holds aloft the wandering Cheap-Jack with his fake cures for human misery. Here stands the sleek capitalist and there the sinewy laborer; here the man of science and there the shoe-black; here the poet and there the water-rate collector; here the cabinet minister and there the ballet dancer. Here roars the red-nosed publican praising his vats; there lectures the temperance advocate at fifty pounds a night. Here sits a judge; there a swindler. Here a priest; there a gambler. Here a jewel-bedecked duchess, smiling and graceful; there a thin lodging-house keeper, irritable over cooking; and there, wobbling and strutting, a creature garbed in tawdry paint and finery.

Cheek by cheek they struggle onward. Screaming, cursing, and praying, laughing, singing, and moaning, they rush past side by side. Their speed never slows, the race never ends. There is no resting spot for them, no break by a cooling fountain, no pause beneath shady trees. On, on, on—through the heat, the crush, and the dust—or they will be trampled down and lost forever. On, with throbbing brains and shaking legs, on, until the heart turns sick and vision blurs, and a guttural groan signals to those behind that someone else has finally closed up shop.

And yet, despite the killer pace and the stone-hard track, who but a sluggard or a complete idiot could stay away from the course? Who, like the belated traveler who watches a fairy party until he snatches the goblin cup and leaps into the spinning circle, can watch this mad tumult and not be dragged right into the middle of it? Not I, certainly. I must confess that my garden seat, my pipe of contentment, and my lotus-leaves were rather unsuitable metaphors. They sounded very nice and philosophical in theory, but I fear I am not the type to sit in arbors smoking pipes when there is any real fun happening outside. I think I look more like the Irishman who, spotting a crowd gathering, sent his little girl out to ask if there was going to be a row—"Because, if there is, Father would like to be in it."

I love the fierce strife. I enjoy watching it. I love hearing stories of people getting on in it—battling their way bravely and fairly—that is, not slipping through by luck or dirty tricks. It stirs one's old Saxon fighting blood just like the tales of "knightly heroes fighting fearful odds" that thrilled us in our schoolboy days.

And fighting the battle of life means fighting against truly fearful odds. There are giants and dragons in this nineteenth century, and the golden casket they guard isn't nearly as easy to win as it appears in the storybooks. There, Algernon casts one long, final glance at the ancestral hall, wipes a tear from his eye, and sets off—to return three years later, rolling in riches. The authors refuse to tell us "how it's done," which is a shame, for it would surely prove thrilling.

But then, not one novelist in a thousand ever reveals the real story of their hero. They spend a dozen pages describing a tea-party, only to summarize a lifetime with phrases like "he became one of our merchant princes" or "he was now a great artist, with the world at his feet." Why, there is more real life in one of Gilbert's patter-songs than in half the biographical novels ever written. He explains to us every single step by which his office-boy rose to become the "ruler of the Queen's Navy," and details exactly how a lawyer with no briefs managed to become a great and good judge, "ready to try cases involving breaches of promise of marriage." It is in the petty details, not the grand results, where the true interest of existence lies.

What we really crave is a novel that shows us the hidden under-currents of an ambitious man's career—his struggles, his failures, his hopes, his disappointments, and his victories. It would be a massive hit. I am certain that the wooing of Fortune would prove quite as fascinating a tale as courting any flesh-and-blood maiden, though, by the way, it would read extremely similarly; for Fortune is, indeed, as the ancients painted her, very much like a woman—not quite so unreasonable and inconsistent, but nearly so—and the pursuit is much the same in either case. Ben Jonson's couplet puts it all in a nutshell:

"Court a mistress, she denies you;
Let her alone, she will court you."

Both scenarios boil down to the same truth: a woman never truly cares for her lover until he stops caring for her; and it is not until you snap your fingers in Fortune's face and turn on your heel that she begins to smile upon you.

But by that time, you hardly care whether she smiles or frowns. Why couldn't she have smiled when her smiles would have filled you with ecstasy? Everything comes too late in this world.

The good people say that it is perfectly right and proper for things to be this way, and that it proves ambition is wicked.

Nonsense! The good people are entirely wrong. (They always are, in my opinion. We never agree on a single point.) What would the world do without ambitious people, I should like to know? Why, it would be as flabby as a Norfolk dumpling. Ambitious people are the yeast that raises it into wholesome bread. Without them, the world would never get up. They are the busybodies who arrive early in the morning, hammering, shouting, and rattling the fire-irons, making it generally impossible for the rest of the household to remain in bed.

Wrong to be ambitious, forsooth! The men who are wrong are those with bent backs and sweating brows, cutting the smooth road over which humanity marches forward from generation to generation! Men are wrong for using the talents entrusted to them by their Master—for toiling while others play!

Of course, they are seeking their reward. Man is not given that godlike unselfishness that thinks only of others' good. But in working for themselves, they are working for us all. We are so tightly bound together that no man can labor for himself alone. Every blow he strikes in his own behalf helps to mold the universe. The stream, in its struggle onward, turns the mill-wheel; the coral insect, fashioning its tiny cell, joins continents to one another; and the ambitious man, building a pedestal for himself, leaves a monument to posterity.
