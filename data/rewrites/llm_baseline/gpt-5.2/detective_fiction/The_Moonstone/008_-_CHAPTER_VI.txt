I kept my own feelings locked up, and only said, as civil as might be, that I was ready to hear him out. Mr. Franklin stopped me with one of his quick looks.

“Don’t fidget, Betteredge,” he said; and began at the beginning, where the clue had first come into his hands—at another man’s table, after another man’s dinner, with the clink of plates still in the air and the smell of wine and roasted meat still hanging about the lawyer’s room at Hampstead.

He told me his discoveries about the Colonel and the Diamond had started from a visit, paid before ever he came down to us, to Mr. Bruff the family lawyer. A chance word, dropped when they were alone after dinner, let out that Mr. Franklin had been charged by his father to carry Miss Rachel a birthday gift. One question drew another, as gravy draws bread; and at last Mr. Bruff, between the walnuts and the decanter, named what the gift really was, and how the late Colonel Herncastle and Mr. Blake’s father had first struck up their ill-assorted friendship.

The thing was so out of all common experience that I mistrusted my own plain way of speaking it. Mr. Franklin’s telling had its own sharp relish; I set it down as nearly as possible in his words.

“You remember the time, Betteredge,” he said, “when my father was trying to prove his title to that unlucky dukedom. Well—at that same time my uncle Herncastle came back from India. My father discovered that he held certain papers likely to help him in the suit. He called on the Colonel, pretending good-will.

“The Colonel saw through him at once. ‘You want something,’ he said, ‘or you would never have compromised your reputation by calling on me.’ My father—finding there was no use in seasoning the truth—owned he wanted the papers.

“The Colonel asked a day to consider. And his answer came in a letter—an extraordinary letter—which Bruff showed me. He began by saying he wanted something of my father, and begged to propose an exchange of friendly services. The fortune of war—that was his phrase—had put him in possession of one of the largest diamonds in the world; and he had reason to believe that neither he nor his jewel was safe in any house, in any quarter of the globe, which they occupied together.

“Under those circumstances, he had determined to place the Diamond in some other person’s keeping. The person chosen was not expected to run any risk. He might lock it away anywhere especially guarded—like a banker’s or a jeweller’s strongroom. His chief responsibility was passive: he was to undertake, either himself or by a trustworthy representative, to receive at a prearranged address, on certain prearranged days in every year, a note from the Colonel, simply stating that he was living at that date. If the date passed and no note came, the silence was to be taken as certain proof that the Colonel had been murdered. Then, and then only, certain sealed instructions, deposited with the Diamond, were to be opened and followed to the letter. If my father accepted this strange charge, the Colonel’s papers were to be his in return. That was the letter.”

“What did your father do, sir?” I asked.

“Do?” says Mr. Franklin. “He did what a man does when he has that useful faculty called common sense. He declared the whole thing absurd. Somewhere in India the Colonel had picked up a wretched crystal and mistaken it for a diamond. As for danger of murder and all that—this was the nineteenth century, and any man in his senses had only to apply to the police.

“The Colonel was a notorious opium-eater; and, if the only way to get at the valuable papers was to treat opium as if it were proof, my father was willing to take the ridiculous responsibility—especially as it involved no trouble to himself. The Diamond and sealed instructions went into his banker’s strongroom; and the Colonel’s yearly letters, reporting him alive, were received and opened by Mr. Bruff as my father’s representative. No sensible man could have seen it differently. Nothing is probable unless it appeals to our own trumpery experience; and we only believe a romance when we see it in a newspaper.”

That told me plain enough that Mr. Franklin had his doubts of his father’s judgment. I tried him.

“And what is your own opinion, sir?”

“Let’s finish the story first,” he said. “There’s a curious want of system in the English mind, Betteredge; your question proves it. When we are not making machinery, we are, mentally speaking, the most slovenly people alive.”

“So much,” thought I, “for foreign schooling. He has picked up that way of girding at us abroad.”

He took up the thread again.

“My father got his papers,” he went on, “and never saw his brother-in-law again. Year after year, on the prearranged days, the prearranged letter came, and Mr. Bruff opened it. I have seen them in a heap—every one in the same curt business form:

“‘Sir,—This is to certify that I am still a living man. Let the Diamond be. John Herncastle.’

“That, and nothing more; regular to the day. Until six or eight months since, when the form altered for the first time. It ran:

“‘Sir,—They tell me I am dying. Come to me, and help me to make my will.’

“Mr. Bruff went, and found him in a little suburban villa, with its own grounds, where he had lived alone since leaving India. Dogs, cats, birds—creatures to be fed and soothed by familiar sounds and footsteps—but no human company, except the daily woman and the doctor at his bed.

“The will was simple. He had wasted most of his fortune on chemical experiments. He dictated three clauses, perfectly clear-headed. First, provision for the support and safe keeping of his animals. Second, a professorship of experimental chemistry at a northern university. Third, the Moonstone as a birthday present to his niece—on condition that my father acted as executor.

“My father refused at first; then gave way—partly because he was assured it would be no trouble; partly because Bruff suggested, in Rachel’s interest, the Diamond might after all be worth something.”

“Did the Colonel give any reason, sir,” I asked, “for leaving it to Miss Rachel?”

“He not only gave it—he wrote it into the will,” said Mr. Franklin. “You shall see it presently. One thing at a time. You have heard the will; now hear what followed his death.

“Before the will could be proved, the Diamond had to be valued. Every jeweller consulted confirmed the Colonel’s assertion: it was among the largest in the world. But valuing it exactly was difficult. Its size made it a phenomenon; its colour put it in a class by itself; and, worst of all, there was a flaw in the very heart of the stone. Even so, the lowest estimate was twenty thousand pounds. Conceive my father’s astonishment! He had been within a hair’s-breadth of refusing the executorship—and of letting such a jewel slip out of the family.

“That interest induced him to open the sealed instructions deposited with the Diamond. Bruff showed me the document with the other papers; and to my mind it suggests a clue to the conspiracy which threatened the Colonel’s life.”

“Then you do believe, sir,” I said, “that there was a conspiracy?”

“Not possessing my father’s excellent common sense,” Mr. Franklin answered, “I believe the Colonel’s life was threatened exactly as he said. The sealed instructions, I think, explain why he died quietly in his bed. If he died by violence—that is, if the yearly letter failed—my father was directed to send the Moonstone secretly to Amsterdam. There it was to be deposited with a famous diamond-cutter, and cut into four to six separate stones. Those stones were to be sold, and the money applied to the professorship of chemistry which the Colonel afterwards endowed by his will.

“Now, Betteredge—exert those sharp wits of yours, and observe what this points to.”

I exerted them. They were of the plain English pattern; and they muddled the matter till he took them in hand and set them straight.

“Remark,” he said, “how the integrity of the Diamond as one stone is made dependent on the Colonel’s life being preserved. He does not say to the enemies he fears: ‘Kill me—and you gain nothing; the jewel lies where you cannot reach it, in a bank.’ He says instead: ‘Kill me—and the Diamond will be the Diamond no longer; its identity will be destroyed.’ What does that mean?”

I had, as I fancied, a flash of foreign brightness.

“It means lowering the value, sir—cheating the rogues!”

“Nothing of the sort,” he said. “I have inquired. The flawed Diamond, cut up, would fetch more than the large imperfect stone; because four to six perfect brilliants could be cut from it, and those, collectively, would be worth more. If robbery for gain was the object, the instructions make it better worth stealing, and easier to dispose of—once it passed through Amsterdam.”

“Lord bless us!” I cried. “What was the plot then?”

“A plot among the Indians who originally owned it,” said Mr. Franklin, “with some old Hindoo superstition at the bottom. That is my opinion—and I have a family paper in my pocket that supports it.”

So there was the connection, now, between his thoughts and the three Indian jugglers that had shown themselves at our house.

“I don’t force my opinion on you,” he went on. “But the notion of chosen servants of an old superstition devoting themselves, through years and dangers, to watching for the chance of recovering a sacred gem, is perfectly consistent with what we know of Oriental patience and religious influence. I am an imaginative man; and the butcher, the baker, and the tax-gatherer are not the only realities to my mind.

“Let that guess go for what it’s worth. The practical question is: does the conspiracy survive the Colonel’s death? And did he know it when he left the birthday gift to his niece?”

I began to see my lady and Miss Rachel at the end of this chain, as one sees a candle at the end of a long dark passage. I listened hard.

“I was not very willing,” said Mr. Franklin, “when I discovered the story of the Moonstone, to be the means of bringing it here. But Bruff reminded me somebody must put my cousin’s legacy into her hands—and I might as well do it as another.

“After taking the Diamond out of the bank, I fancied I was followed by a shabby dark-complexioned man. I went to my father’s house for my luggage, and found a letter which unexpectedly detained me in London. I went back to the bank with the Diamond, and thought I saw the shabby man again. Taking it out once more this morning, I saw him for the third time—gave him the slip—and started by the morning train instead of the afternoon.

“Here I am, with the Diamond safe; and what is the first news I hear? Three strolling Indians have been at the house; and my arrival from London, and something I am expected to have about me, are two special objects of inquiry to them, when they think themselves alone. I don’t waste time on their pouring ink into the boy’s hand and telling him to look for a man at a distance and for something in that man’s pocket. I have seen that Eastern trick often enough. Hocus-pocus, as you and I think.

“The question is—am I attaching meaning to accident? or have we proof that the Indians are on the track of the Moonstone the moment it is removed from the bank?”

Neither of us liked the feel of that part of the business. We looked at each other; then away, at the tide creeping in, smoothing the Shivering Sand and covering it inch by inch, as if some soft-footed thing kept coming nearer without ever showing its face. The faint wash and draw of the water made its own talk, steady and secret, under ours.

“What are you thinking of?” Mr. Franklin asked suddenly.

“I was thinking, sir,” I said, “I should like to shy the Diamond into the quicksand, and settle the question that way.”

“If you’ve got twenty thousand pounds in your pocket,” he answered, “say so, Betteredge—and in it goes!”

It is odd, when you are anxious, what relief there is in the smallest joke. We found a sort of mirth at the idea of making away with Miss Rachel’s lawful property and getting Mr. Blake, as executor, into dreadful trouble—though, looking back, I can’t find where the merriment lay.

Mr. Franklin was the first to bring us back. He drew an envelope from his pocket, took out a paper, and gave it to me.

“We must face the Colonel’s motive,” he said, “for my aunt’s sake. Remember how Lady Verinder treated her brother from the time he returned to England to the time he told you he should remember his niece’s birthday. And read that.”

It was an extract from the Colonel’s will. I copy it here word for word:

“Thirdly, and lastly, I give and bequeath to my niece, Rachel Verinder, daughter and only child of my sister, Julia Verinder, widow—if her mother, the said Julia Verinder, shall be living on the said Rachel Verinder’s next Birthday after my death—the yellow Diamond belonging to me, and known in the East by the name of The Moonstone: subject to this condition, that her mother, the said Julia Verinder, shall be living at the time. And I hereby desire my executor to give my Diamond, either by his own hands or by the hands of some trustworthy representative whom he shall appoint, into the personal possession of my said niece Rachel, on her next birthday after my death, and in the presence, if possible, of my sister, the said Julia Verinder. And I desire that my said sister may be informed, by means of a true copy of this, the third and last clause of my Will, that I give the Diamond to her daughter Rachel, in token of my free forgiveness of the injury which her conduct towards me has been the means of inflicting on my reputation in my lifetime; and especially in proof that I pardon, as becomes a dying man, the insult offered to me as an officer and a gentleman, when her servant, by her orders, closed the door of her house against me, on the occasion of her daughter’s birthday.”

More followed: if my lady was dead, or if Miss Rachel was dead at the testator’s decease, the Diamond was to be sent to Holland according to the sealed instructions; and the proceeds were then to be added to the money left for the professorship.

I gave the paper back, troubled to the roots of me. Up to then I had believed the Colonel died as wicked as he lived. I can’t say the will turned me—only that it shook me.

“Well?” says Mr. Franklin. “Now you have the Colonel’s own statement, what do you say? In bringing the Moonstone here, am I serving his vengeance blindfold—or vindicating him as a penitent and Christian man?”

“It seems hard to say, sir,” I answered, “that he died with a horrid revenge in his heart and a horrid lie on his lips. God alone knows. Don’t ask me.”

He sat twisting the paper in his fingers, as though the truth might be squeezed out like juice from a lemon. His manner altered strangely. The brisk, bright young gentleman sank into a slow, solemn, pondering one.

“This question has two sides,” he said. “An Objective side and a Subjective side. Which are we to take?”

He had German learning as well as French; and now it seemed the German was stepping in. One of my rules is to take no notice of what I don’t understand. So I stared hard and held my tongue.

“Let’s extract the inner meaning,” he said. “Why did my uncle leave the Diamond to Rachel? Why not to my aunt?”

“That’s easy guessing, sir,” I said. “Colonel Herncastle knew my lady well enough to know she would have refused any legacy from him.”

“How did he know Rachel might not refuse too?”

“Is there a young lady alive, sir,” I answered, “who could resist such a birthday present as the Moonstone?”

“That’s the Subjective view,” says Mr. Franklin. “It does you credit, Betteredge, to take it. But there is another mystery. Why give it conditionally—only if her mother is alive?”

“I don’t want to slander a dead man,” I said. “But if he meant to leave a legacy of trouble and danger to his sister, by the means of her child, it must be conditional on his sister living to feel the vexation.”

“Oh! that’s your interpretation? Subjective again! Have you ever been in Germany, Betteredge?”

“No, sir. What’s yours?”

“I can see,” he said, “that his object may have been—not to benefit a niece he never saw—but to prove to his sister that he died forgiving her, and to prove it prettily by a gift to her child. A totally different explanation from yours, arising from a Subjective-Objective point of view. As far as I can see, one is as likely as the other.”

Having landed us in that comforting bog, he seemed to consider his duty done. He laid himself flat on his back on the sand and asked what was to be done next.

He had led so clearly up to this point, that I was unprepared for his sudden helplessness—his throwing his own burden on me. It was not until later that I learned, from Miss Rachel first of all, that these odd shifts in him came of foreign training; sent abroad at the age when character takes colour, he had been passed from nation to nation before any one tint could settle. The result was a man made up of sides that jarred together—busy and idle, cloudy and clear, determined and helpless by turns; French, German, Italian, with the English foundation showing through now and then like the bottom crust of a pie. Miss Rachel used to say the Italian side was uppermost when he unexpectedly gave in and asked you, sweet-tempered, to take his responsibility. That side was uppermost now.

“Isn’t it your business, sir,” I said, “to know what to do next? It can’t be mine.”

He stared up at the sky as if it held the answer.

“I don’t want to alarm my aunt without reason,” he said. “And I don’t want to leave her without warning if warning is needed. If you were in my place, Betteredge—one word—what would you do?”

In one word, I told him: “Wait.”

“With all my heart,” he said. “How long?”

Then I put it in plain order.

“As I understand it, sir, somebody is bound to put this plaguy Diamond into Miss Rachel’s hands on her birthday—and you may as well do it as another. This is the twenty-fifth of May; her birthday is the twenty-first of June. We have close on four weeks. Let’s wait and see what happens in that time; and warn my lady—or not—as circumstances direct.”

“Perfect, Betteredge—as far as it goes!” Mr. Franklin said. “But between this and the birthday, what is to be done with the Diamond?”
