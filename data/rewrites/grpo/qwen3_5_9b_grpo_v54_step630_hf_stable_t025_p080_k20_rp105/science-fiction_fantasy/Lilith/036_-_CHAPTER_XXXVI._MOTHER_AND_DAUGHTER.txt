A profound disgust burned within Lona, a fire directed not just at the people of this city, but specifically at the women among them. She yearned to flee, to abandon this place immediately. Yet, I stood firm against her impulse. To retreat now would be a willful failure where victory was still within reach; worse, it would shatter the spirits of the Little Ones, casting them into a peril far greater than the present. If we withdrew, the Princess would surely descend upon us without mercy. If we met her, the prophecy's thread remained intact. Mother and daughter were destined to collide. Perhaps Lona's radiant loveliness would strike the Princess's heart with such force that the conflict would end before it truly began. If violence threatened, I would stand between them, a shield of flesh and bone. And if that shield proved insufficient, if I found myself powerless to sway her, I was prepared, for the sake of my Lona, to strike the Princess's closed hand with pitiless force. I knew the doom awaited us; it was likely written that this very moment would seal her fate through our hands.

Without revealing the nature of my bond to the Princess, I laid out this grim scenario to Lona. Instantly, she agreed to accompany me to the palace.

High atop one of the great towers, the Princess had watched the dawn break over the sleeping city, spotting the approaching army of the Little Ones. The sight had seized her with a terror so overwhelming it stole her breath. She had failed in her quest to destroy them; they were upon her! The prophecy was about to fulfill its terrible promise.

When reason returned, she descended to the black hall and took her seat in the northern focus of the ellipse, beneath the opening in the roof. She needed to think. But her version of thinking required a specific delusion: she had to perceive herself not as she was, but as she chose to believe herself. To aid this realization, she had suspended a mirror slightly above and beyond her, invisible in the hall's darkness, designed to catch the full sunlight reflected from her own form. She waited for the meridian sun, hoping the vision of her own splendor would sustain her.

Shadows moved about her in the gloom. Whenever her inner eye caught sight of one, she refused to acknowledge it. Close beneath the mirror stood the Shadow that attended her walks, yet, lost in its own obsessions, she did not see him.

The city fell; its inhabitants cowered in terror. The Little Ones and their strange cavalry encamped in the square. The sun shone upon the Princess, and for a fleeting moment, she saw herself glorious. But the vision faded, and she remained seated. Night fell, cloaking the glass in darkness, yet she did not stir. A gloom, teeming with shadows, swelled within the palace. The servants shivered, paralyzed by the beasts of the Little Ones, daring not to leave the chamber. All night long, the Princess sat motionless, desperate to see her beauty again, desperate to try to think. But courage and will had grown weary of her and departed.

At dawn, we selected twelve of the tallest and bravest boys to join us. We mounted our great horses, while the Little Ones rode their small mounts and elephants.

The Princess sat waiting for the sun to grant her the joy of her own presence. The tide of light crept up the sky's shore, but until the sun stood directly overhead, not a single ray could penetrate the black hall.

Then he rose to our eyes and swiftly ascended. As we climbed the steep path to the palace, he climbed the dome of its great hall. He peered into its eye—and with sudden radiance, the Princess flashed upon her own sight. But she sprang to her feet with a cry of despair. Alas for her whiteness! A spot covered half her side, black as the marble surrounding it. She clutched her robe and collapsed back into her chair. The Shadow glided out, and she watched him depart.

We found the gate open as usual, passed through the paved grove to the palace door, and entered the vestibule. There, in her cage, lay the spotted leopardess, appearing asleep or lifeless. The Little Ones paused to gaze at her. Suddenly, she leaped up rampant against the bars. The horses reared and plunged; the elephants stepped back. In the next instant, she fell supine, writhed in quivering spasms, and lay motionless. We rode into the great hall.

The Princess still leaned back in her chair, bathed in the shaft of sunlight, when the noise of hooves struck her ears from the stones of the court. She started, listened, and trembled. Never had such a sound been heard in her palace. She pressed her hand to her side and gasped. The trampling drew nearer, entering the hall itself; moving figures that were not shadows approached her through the darkness!

To us, however, there was a splendour, a glorious woman centering the dark. Lona sprang from her horse and bounded forward. I dismounted and followed.

"Mother! Mother!" she cried, her clear, lovely voice echoing in the dome.

The Princess shivered. Her face grew almost black with hate, her eyebrows knitting together on her forehead. She rose to her feet and stood rigid.

"Mother! Mother!" Lona cried again, leaping onto the dais and flinging her arms around the Princess.

In an instant, I would have reached them—but in that split second, I saw Lona lifted high and dashed onto the marble floor. Oh, the horrible sound of her fall! At my feet she lay still. The Princess sat down, wearing the smile of a demoness.

I dropped to my knees beside Lona, raised her from the stones, and pressed her to my bosom. With indignant hate, I glanced at the Princess; she answered with her sweetest smile. I would have sprung upon her, seized her by the throat, and strangled her, but love for the child was stronger than hate for the mother. I clasped my precious burden tighter. Her arms hung helpless; her blood trickled over my hands, falling to the floor with soft, slow little plashes.

The horses sensed it—mine first, then the small ones. Mine reared, shivering and wild-eyed, thundering blindly down the dark hall, with the little horses following. Lona's horse stood gazing down at his mistress, trembling all over. The boys threw themselves from their mounts; failing to see the black wall ahead, they dashed themselves, along with mine, to pieces against it. The elephants advanced to the foot of the dais and stopped, trumpeting wildly. The Little Ones sprang upon them, standing horrified. The Princess lay back in her seat, her face that of a corpse, her eyes alone alive, wickedly flaming. She was again withered and wasted, as I had found her in the wood, her side marked as if by a great branding iron. But Lona saw nothing, and I saw only Lona.

"Mother! Mother!" she sighed, and her breathing ceased.

I carried her into the court. The sun shone upon a white face, bearing the pitiful shadow of a ghostly smile. Her head hung back. She was "dead as earth."

I forgot the Little Ones, forgot the murdering Princess, forgot the body in my arms, and wandered away, searching for my Lona. The doors and windows were crowded with brute-faces jeering at me, yet they dared not speak, for they saw the white leopardess behind me, hanging her head close at my heel. I spurned her with my foot. She held back a moment, then followed me again.

I reached the square: the little army was gone! Its emptiness roused me. Where were the Little Ones, *her* Little Ones? I had lost her children! I stared helplessly about me, staggered to a pillar, and sank upon its base.

But as I sat gazing at the still countenance, it seemed to offer a live, momentary smile. I never doubted it was an illusion, yet I believed what it said: I should yet see her alive! It was not she who was lost, but I; she would find me!

I rose to go after the Little Ones, instinctively seeking the gate by which we had entered. I looked around, but saw nothing of the leopardess.

The street was rapidly filling with a fierce crowd. They saw me encumbered with my dead, but for a time dared not assail me. Before I reached the gate, however, they had gathered courage. The women began to hustle me; I held on heedless. A man pushed against my sacred burden; with a kick I sent him away howling. But the crowd pressed upon me, and fearing for the dead that was beyond hurt, I clasped my treasure closer and freed my right arm. That instant, a commotion arose behind me; the crowd broke. Through the gap came the Little Ones I had left in the palace. Ten of them rode four elephants; on the two others lay the Princess, bound hand and foot, quite still save for her eyes rolling in their ghastly sockets. The two remaining Little Ones rode behind her on Lona's horse. Every now and then, the wise creatures that bore her threw their trunks back to feel her cords.

I walked on in front, leading the way out of the city. What an end to the hopes with which I entered this evil place! We had captured the bad Princess, and lost our all-beloved Queen! My life was bare; my heart was empty!
