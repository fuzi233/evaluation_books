Arthur Pendennis had just touched down in London, though his stay was intended to be fleeting. After dropping off his fellow travelers at a hotel near the West End, he hurried toward the Chambers in Lamb Court. Despite the building's reputation for being dreary, the specific rooms he sought were currently bathed in whatever patch of sunlight dared to penetrate the gloom. In legal chambers, freedom acts as a substitute for sunshine; the barristers may grumble about the damp, but they eventually settle in to enjoy their ease.

Pen's domestic announced that Warrington was also present. Naturally, Arthur bolted upstairs to his friend's room. As expected, the air was thick with the scent of tobacco, and George Warrington was already deep in his newspapers and reviews. The two friends exchanged greetings with the characteristic rough cordiality of young Englishmen—a style that hides immense warmth beneath a crust of rudeness.

"Well, young one!" Warrington grinned, pulling his pipe from his mouth.
"How are you, old boy?" Pen replied, extending a hand.

It was a standard exchange between two Britons who hadn't seen each other in months. Contrast this with the French, who would rush into each other's arms screaming *Ce bon cœur!* or the Germans, who would shower one another with Havana-scented kisses on their mustaches. The British method is distinct: "Well, young one" and "How are you, old boy" serve as substitutes for declarations of undying loyalty. Tomorrow, they will merely nod at breakfast; yet, each holds the other's fate in their hands, ready to share their purse or defend their honor with the loudest enthusiasm. Then comes the abrupt "Good-bye," followed by a polite "How-d'you-do" upon meeting again, with no letters sent in the interim. It is a curious, stoic form of friendship, as noted by Hardman, who claims to feel no affection whatsoever.

"Yes, we aren't demonstrative like those confounded foreigners," Hardman might say.

"Been in Switzerland?" Pen asked.
"Yes," Warrington confirmed. "Couldn't find tobacco fit to smoke until we reached Strasbourg, where I finally procured some caporal."

Warrington's mind was undoubtedly filled with grand sights and profound emotions stirred by nature's vast works, yet his enthusiasm remained coy, veiled behind a cloud of smoke. He would save his true feelings for confidential evenings and express them later in writing, where his travels enriched his prose with brilliant allusions and witty illustrations. Speaking rapidly, he outlined his tour: Switzerland, Northern Italy, the Tyrol, returning via Vienna, Dresden, and along the Rhine. Yet, he delivered this list in a sulky, reluctant tone, as if mentioning the places made him miserable.

Then, the young man began to speak of his own journey. He had been in the country, finding it unbearably boring, canvassing slowly, and was now heading to Tunbridge Wells to visit equally slow-moving acquaintances. "How hard it is to make an Englishman admit he is happy!"

"And the seat in Parliament, Pen? Have you sorted it out?" Warrington inquired.
"All set," Pen assured him. "As soon as Parliament reconvenes and a new writ is issued, Clavering retires, and I slide into his shoes."

"Do we stand as Liberal Conservatives, Government men, or independents?"
"Hem! Politics are quiet now; everyone's views are roughly aligned anyway. I haven't got enough acres to be a Protectionist, nor could I be one even if I owned the county. I'll mostly follow the Government, though I plan to push ahead on some social issues I've been researching during the vacation—don't smirk, old cynic. I've been compiling the Blue Books and intend to be forceful on Sanitation and Colonization."

"We reserve the right to vote against the Government, though we're usually friendly. We are friends of the people first and foremost. We lecture at the Clavering Institute and shake hands with intelligent mechanics. We believe the franchise needs significant expansion. Still, we're open to accepting office once the House hears our crack speeches and the Administration recognizes our merit."

"I am not Moses," Pen said, his voice tinged with melancholy. "I have no divine laws to descend from the mountain. I don't belong to the mountaintop, nor do I aspire to lead or reform humanity. My faith isn't strong enough for that, nor my vanity or hypocrisy. I won't lie to you, George; I promise. I'll only coincide with necessary falsehoods that keep the currency circulating. Give a man the benefit of his skepticism. If I find a good point in the House, I'll make it; if a good measure arises, I'll support it; if a decent position opens up, I'll take it and be grateful. But I wouldn't flatter a great man more than a mob. Now you know my politics as well as I do. Why bother being a Whig? Whiggism isn't a holy institution. Why not join the Liberal Conservatives? They've achieved far more for the nation than the Whigs ever could without them. Who converted both? The Radicals and the public. I think the *Morning Post* is often right and *Punch* often wrong. I don't claim a calling; I just seize opportunities. Let's talk about something else."

"The next thing after ambition is love, I suppose?" Warrington asked gently. "How are our young loves progressing? Are we trading chambers for a hearth? Are you divorcing me, Arthur, to marry a wife?"

"I suppose so," Pen admitted. "She's very good-natured and lively. She sings, and she doesn't mind smoking. She'll have a fair fortune—though my uncle predicts everything based on the Begum's generosity, expecting a handsome dowry. And I think Blanche is terribly fond of me," he sighed.

"That implies we accept her caresses and her money," Warrington quipped.

"Haven't we decided life is a transaction?" Pen countered. "I don't pretend to break my heart over her. I've told her plainly what I feel, and we're engaged. Since I last saw her, especially over the past two months in the country, I'm convinced she's grown deeper in love. Her letters, particularly to Laura, prove it. Mine have been straightforward—no rapturous vows, you understand—treating it as a settled affair, neither rushing nor delaying the conclusion."

"And Laura? How is she?" Warrington asked directly.

"Laura, George," Pen said, staring intently at his friend—"by heavens, Laura is the finest, noblest, and dearest girl the sun has ever illuminated."

His voice faltered; the words seemed difficult to extract. He reached out a hand to his comrade, who grasped it and nodded solemnly.

"Did you only discover that now, young un?" Warrington asked after a pause.

"Who hasn't learned things too late, George?" Arthur cried impulsively, gathering emotion as he spoke. "Whose life isn't a disappointment? Who carries an intact heart to the grave without some mutilation? I've never known anyone truly happy, or someone who didn't pay a terrible price to ransom themselves from Fate's grasp. Lucky if we're left alone afterward, having paid our fine, if the tyrant stops visiting. Suppose I've realized I've lost the greatest prize in the world, now that it's impossible to have? For years, I had an angel under my tent, and I let her go? Am I the only one—oh, dear old boy, am I the only one? Do you think my lot is easier to bear because I admit I deserved it? She's gone from us. God bless her! She might have stayed, and I lost her. Isn't it like Undine, George?"

"She was in this room once," George whispered. He recalled seeing her there, hearing her soft voice, witnessing her kind smile and shining eyes—the face remembered through sleepless nights, blessed and beloved forever, now departed. Only a glass that had held a nosegay and a Bible with Helen's handwriting remained as tokens of that brief flower of his life. "Say it's a dream," George murmured. "Better the memory of a dream than waking from a blank stupor."

The two sat in silence, lost in their respective thoughts, acutely aware of the other's presence. Pen broke the quiet, noting he needed to find his uncle and report on his progress. The Major had written in a foul mood; the old man was aging.

"I'd like to see you in Parliament, comfortably settled with a house and an heir, before I make my final bow," the Major had written. "Show me these documents, and then let old Arthur Pendennis make room for the younger generation; he's walked the Pall Mall pavement long enough."

"There's a kindness in that old heathen," Warrington observed. "He cares for someone besides himself—for you and your line. He wants the Pendennis progeny to multiply and inherit the land. The old patriarch blesses you from the club window at Bays's, only to be carried off and buried under the flags of St. James's Church, visible from Piccadilly and the carriage stands heading to the levee. An edifying end."
