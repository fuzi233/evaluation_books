I was still reeling in a fog of tangled thoughts, desperate for five quiet minutes to sort my wits back into order, when my daughter Penelope stepped square in my path—exactly the way her late mother always used to block my way on the stairs when she was set on prying a story out of me. She instantly demanded a full accounting of every word that had passed between Mr. Franklin Blake and me at our meeting by the Shivering Sand.

There was only one sensible move under the circumstances: snuff out Penelope’s curiosity before it could spark any further awkward questions. I told her Mr. Franklin and I had rambled on about foreign politics until we could not stand the sound of each other’s voices any longer, then both drifted fast asleep in the midday sun. Try that trick the next time your wife or daughter corners you for an answer you cannot give, and trust in the natural sweetness of women to kiss and make up for the slight no more than an hour later.

The afternoon dragged on, and Lady Verinder and Miss Rachel returned home. It goes without saying how stunned they were to learn Mr. Franklin had arrived on the early train and then immediately galloped off again on horseback. It goes equally without saying that they pounced with sharp, direct questions right away—and that my “foreign politics nap” lie did not hold water for a single second with them. Out of all ready excuses, I shrugged and told them Franklin’s sudden arrival was just one of his famous, unaccountable whims. When they pressed to know if his mad dash out on horseback was a second whim, I said yes it was, and slipped out of the bind in what I considered a very clever bit of improvisation.

I thought I had cleared all my hurdles with the family, but more waited for me back in my own quarters. Penelope turned up, true to that natural sweetness I’d counted on, to kiss and make up for my earlier fib—while armed with that equally unshakable natural female curiosity, to ask me what on earth was wrong with our second housemaid, Rosanna Spearman.

It seemed Rosanna had returned to the house in a most unaccountable state right after leaving Mr. Franklin and me by the Shivering Sand. Per Penelope, her face had flushed every shade of the rainbow: giddy and laughing for no reason one minute, sunk into unprovoked tears the next. She fired off a hundred rapid questions about Mr. Franklin Blake in one breath, then snapped angrily at Penelope for daring to imply a strange visiting gentleman could possibly be of any interest to her. Penelope caught her grinning as she scribbled Franklin’s name in the back of her workbox, then found her ten minutes later sobbing into the mirror, staring at her hunched, deformed shoulder. Could she have known Franklin before that day? Impossible. Could she have heard word of him from anyone else? Impossible again. I could personally vouch for how genuinely shocked Franklin had been when he saw the girl stare at him, and Penelope swore Rosanna’s endless curious questions were entirely unfeigned.

The whole tiresome back-and-forth dragged on until my daughter dropped a bombshell that struck me as the most absurd supposition I had ever heard in my life. “Father!” she said, dead serious. “There is only one explanation. Rosanna has fallen in love with Mr. Franklin Blake at first sight.”

You have read tales of beautiful young ladies falling head over heels at first sight, and you think that is natural enough. But a plain ex-reformatory housemaid with a crooked shoulder, falling instantly in love with a gentleman visiting her mistress’s estate? You will not find a more ridiculous premise in any storybook in all of Christendom. I laughed until the tears rolled down my cheeks.

Penelope took my merriment poorly, and in a very quiet, hurt voice said, “I never knew you could be cruel before, father,” and walked out.

Her words hit me like a dash of cold water straight to the face. I was furious at myself for the way I’d teased her—and for the sharp, unplaceable unease that settled in my bones the second she spoke. We will not speak of this any further, if you please. I regret drifting onto the topic, and you will understand why soon enough, as our story unfolds.

The dinner dressing bell rang before Mr. Franklin rode back from Frizinghall. I brought his hot water up to his room myself, fully braced to hear some disaster had struck during that long delay. To my great disappointment, and no doubt yours too, nothing untoward had happened at all. He had not spotted a single sign of the wandering Indian jugglers on his ride there or back. He had dropped the Moonstone off at the bank, described only as a high-value item of great price, and tucked the official receipt safe in his pocket. I trundled back downstairs feeling deflated, as if all the high tension around the diamond that morning had fizzled out to a very flat ending.

I never got to see how Franklin’s first meeting with his aunt and Miss Rachel went, of course. In my position as head butler, waiting at dinner (save for the highest of family festivals) would have undermined my authority in the eyes of the other staff—something Lady Verinder already teased me for doing far too easily, without me going out of my way to invite it. All the news I got from the dining room that night came from Penelope and our footman Samuel. Penelope said Miss Rachel had spent twice as long tending to her hair that evening as she ever had before, and she had never looked half so bright, half so pretty as when she glided down to the drawing room to meet Franklin. Samuel reported that keeping a respectful, blank composure before his betters while waiting on Mr. Franklin Blake was the hardest test of his entire training as a servant: the man was so obviously smitten he could barely string a coherent sentence together.

Later that evening, we could hear them singing duets through the open windows leading to the terrace: Franklin’s tenor trilling high, Miss Rachel’s lighter soprano sailing even higher over the top, and Lady Verinder at the piano shepherding them both over every tricky note, as if she was leading them safely across hedges and ditches all the way through the song. When I brought Franklin his brandy and soda in the smoking room much later, I found Miss Rachel had driven all thought of the Moonstone clean out of his head. “She is the most charming girl I have met since I came back to England!” was all I could drag out of him, no matter how gently I steered the conversation toward more serious business.

Towards midnight, I did my usual rounds to lock up the house, Samuel trailing after me as my second in command. When every last door was bolted save for the side door opening onto the terrace, I sent Samuel off to bed, and stepped outside to take a breath of fresh air before I turned in for the night. The night was still and close, the full moon hanging bright and round in the sky. It was so quiet out of doors that I could hear the faint, low murmur of the sea swell rolling in over the sandbar at the mouth of our little cove. The terrace side of the house lay cast in deep shadow, but the broad moonlight lit the full length of the gravel walk running along the house’s other adjacent side. When I lowered my gaze after staring up at the moon, I spotted the long shadow of a person stretched across the moonlit gravel, thrown forward from behind the far corner of the house.

Old and experienced as I was, I did not call out and give my presence away. But I was also old and heavy, and my boots crunched too loud on the gravel, betraying my approach. Before I could slip silently around the corner to catch the trespasser off guard, I heard light footsteps—more than one pair, I swore—scurrying away as fast as they could. By the time I rounded the corner, whoever the intruders were had already vanished into the thick shrubbery at the far end of the walk, hidden from sight behind the tangled trees and undergrowth. From there, they could easily climb our estate fence and slip out onto the public road. If I had been forty years younger, I might have stood a chance of catching them before they cleared the grounds. As it was, I doubled back to rouse Samuel, no alarms, no rousing the sleeping household, and the two of us took a pair of shotguns to comb every inch of the property, the shrubbery, all around the house, until we were certain no one was left lurking anywhere.

On our walk back, I spotted a tiny glint of light on the clean gravel, under the moon: a small glass vial, holding a thick, sweet-smelling liquor as black as ink. I said nothing of it to Samuel. But I remembered exactly what Penelope had told me earlier that day, about the Indian jugglers, and the little pool of black ink they had poured into the palm of the boy’s hand for their fortune trick. I knew instantly who I had disturbed skulking behind the house that night. The three Indians were there, creeping through the dark, bent on their heathen tricks to find out exactly where the Moonstone was hidden that very night.
