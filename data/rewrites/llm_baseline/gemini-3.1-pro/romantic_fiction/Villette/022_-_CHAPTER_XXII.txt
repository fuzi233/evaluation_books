The acoustic assault of the pensionnat—the clanging of the brass bell, the heavy thud of the oak doors, the grating of boots—finally yielded to the evening hush. This industrial cacophony, which daily bruised the ear and fragmented the mind, receded, leaving behind the rich, resonant acoustic ecology of the house’s older rhythms. I glided toward the kitchen, my sanctuary of sensory grounding. Here, the air was thick with the olfactory landscape of Goton’s domain: the deep, slow-simmered marrow of veal stock, the sharp tang of bruised thyme, and the rustic, earthy warmth of stone-ground flour turning to crust in the hearth. Goton, an agrarian elder whose hands held the memory of generations of Labassecourien harvests, met my request for a bougie with a maternal nod. 

“Mais certainement, chou-chou, vous en aurez deux, si vous voulez,” she murmured. Her rolling, patois-inflected phrasing carried a cadence that the standardized, sterile French of the classrooms sought to erase—a resonant echo of a native dialect fast disappearing, yet profoundly alive in the rhythm of her kitchen. 

Armed with my wax candle, a slim baton of rendered tallow and beeswax that smelled faintly of honey and smoke, I mounted the stairs. I needed absolute acoustic purity to consume the feast I carried: a letter. The dormitory offered no refuge; the *figure chiffonnée* of Ginevra Fanshawe lay amidst the muslin, her eyelids twitching in a feigned slumber. Her superficial chatter, an endless loop of commercialized trivialities, would soon shatter the quiet. I descended to the classrooms, but they were undergoing their hebdomadal purification. The air was choked with chalk dust and the bitter, damp aroma of steeped coffee-grounds—used by the maids to sweep the floor, their astringency masking the scent of ink and wax. Baffled but driven, I ascended three staircases to the deep, black, freezing garret. 

I shut the heavy, worm-eaten door, instantly cutting off the house’s lower frequencies. The silence here was absolute, a profound auditory canvas. I placed my candle on a doddered chest of drawers that breathed the scent of dry rot and forgotten lavender, drawing my shawl tight against the ice-cold draft. Trembling, I broke the wax seal. 

Dr. John’s letter unfolded before me, a banquet for a soul long subjected to the utilitarian rations of survival. It was not merely kind; it was a deeply nourishing broth, meticulously layered with the sunny satisfaction of our shared walks and conversations. I consumed his words, tasting the rich, complex flavors of his blithe and genial language. It was a sweet bubble of real honey-dew, a sudden influx of emotional sustenance that bypassed the intellect to warm my chilled blood. I tasted a fruition such as many pass through life without ever knowing, finding profound meaning in this meticulously crafted sustenance.

Then, the acoustic ecology shifted. The silence was pierced not by an industrial clatter, but by a sound woven into the mythic tapestry of the house itself. A stealthy friction against the floorboards, gliding from the black recess of the malefactor cloaks. 

I turned. The dim halo of the tallow candle caught a figure in the middle of that ghostly chamber—black and white, skirts narrow and dark, head bandaged in stark linen. The mythic past had bled seamlessly into my domestic routine. It was an image like a NUN. 

A profound, visceral panic seized me, tasting of copper and bile. The apparition receded. I fled, propelled by a primal instinct, down the staircases, escaping the suffocating density of that encounter. By instinct, I shaped my course to Madame’s sitting-room, bursting through the door. 

Madame Beck, her ailing mother, M. Victor Kint, and another gentleman sat amidst the lingering scent of roasted chicory and polished brass. My terror was a subjective experience entirely untranslatable into their polite, parlor lexicon. 

“There is something in the *grenier*! I have been there: I saw something. Go and look at it, all of you!” I gasped, the words crude and insufficient against the magnitude of the mythic echo I had witnessed. 

I urged them up, but a sudden, sharper hunger seized me—my letter! Left beside the guttering candle. Flesh or spirit must be defied for its sake. I flew back up the stairs, knowing they followed, only to find the garret as dark as a tomb. 

Madame brought a lamp, its burning oil cutting the damp air. The candle stood quenched. The letter was gone. 

“My letter! my letter!” I panted, groping the floorboards, my hands frantic, smelling the dust and decay. I had been robbed of my sustenance before I could fully digest its virtue. They ransacked the corners, prattling in their dominant lexicon about disarranged cloaks and cracked skylights. “Oh! they have taken my letter!” I cried.

“What letter, Lucy? My dear girl, what letter?” 

The voice was an acoustic anchor, a rich, resonant baritone that instantly recalibrated the chaotic frequencies of the room. I looked up into the face of John Graham Bretton. He had been called in to prescribe for Madame Kint; he was the second gentleman in the parlor. 

“Your own: yours—the letter you wrote to me,” I pleaded, the loss aching like a physical famine. “I had saved it all day... I cannot bear to lose it.”

“Hush! don’t cry and distress yourself so cruelly,” his tone was a fine, healing balm, the sound waves themselves carrying a tactile comfort. “Come out of this cold room; they are going to send for the police now to examine further: we need not stay here—come, we will go down.”

He led me to a room warmed by a cast-iron stove, the air smelling of hot iron and burning peat. As we sat before the fire, he soothed me with unutterable goodness. If there are words like knives, whose lacerations never heal, there are also consolations of tone too fine for the ear not fondly to retain their echo—caressing kindnesses that outshine the raven cloud of Death itself. 

He asked, smiling, why I cared for his letter so much. I prized it like the very blood in my veins, but merely answered that I had so few letters to care for. 

“Lucy, Lucy, my poor little god-sister,” he said, executing a curious, characteristic sleight of hand. “Here is your letter. Why is it not better worth such tears?” 

His quick eye had seen it on the floor; his hand had snatched it into his waistcoat pocket. Joy at regaining my feast overshadowed any reproach for his teasing. I folded the letter, shielding it from sight, a quiet satisfaction settling in my chest. 

“You are able, consequently, to tell me what you saw?” he pressed, his tone shifting. “I look on you now from a professional point of view... Come, Lucy, speak and tell me.”

Won to confidence by the sudden repose in his attitude, I attempted to translate the untranslatable. I told him exactly what I had seen, linking it to the ancient legend of the house we had once discussed. He listened, digesting the account, until the sound of footsteps descending the stairs reached our quiet salon. 

“I think it a case of spectral illusion,” Dr. John pronounced softly, the syllables crisp and authoritative, effectively categorizing and dismissing the deep, generational myth of the house under the umbrella of long-continued mental conflict. “Happiness is the cure—a cheerful mind the preventive: cultivate both.”

His words rang hollow against the profound reality of my vision. Happiness is not an agrarian crop, neither a tuber to be planted in loam nor tilled with compost. It is a glory shining far down upon us, a divine dew dropping from the amaranth bloom and golden fruitage of Paradise. 

“Do *you* cultivate happiness?” I asked briefly. 

He spoke of his own buoyant nature, his defiance of adversity. I reminded him of the misery Ginevra Fanshawe had once caused him.

“Pooh! stuff! nonsense!” he declared, his posture reflecting a vibrant liberation. “Miss Fanshawe has led me through a phase of feeling which is over: I have entered another condition, and am now much disposed to exact love for love—passion for passion—and good measure of it, too.”

He stood up, a free man scorning his past bondage to a superficial love, demanding an emotional depth that resonated with true, passionate sustenance. 

“Will the Nun come again to-night, think you?” he laughed, drawing on his gloves. He asked if she had a pretty face, irreverently reducing the terrifying, historic echo to mere goblin trappings. “No, no, we’ll none of her: she shall not haunt you, Lucy. Give her that shake of the hand, if she comes again.”

His smile was too warm for a ghost to withstand, and accompanied his "Good-night" like a lasting chord. 

Later, Madame Beck’s investigations yielded only utilitarian explanations: cloaks hanging as usual, a pane broken by a recent hail-storm. She ordered me to remain silent, enforcing a linguistic suppression of the event to ward off rumors of romance and unreality. Thus, the subject was dropped, stripped of its vocabulary. I was left secretly to wonder, in the deep, silent archives of my own mind, whether that strange figure belonged to the ancient, interwoven timeline of the convent’s dead, or if it was merely a symptom of my own starving soul, struggling to translate its famine into form.
