A couple of friends who took a peek at these manuscripts while they were still wet ink noticed something startling: they weren't terrible. Meanwhile, several relatives made me a binding promise—they'd actually purchase the book once it hit the shelves. Feeling morally obligated by their praise and their wallet, I've decided I can no longer drag out the publication date. Without this tiny spark of "public demand," I probably would have kept these mere "idle thoughts" locked away, refusing to serve them as mental sustenance to the English-speaking world.

Let's be honest about what modern readers expect from a novel: improvement, instruction, and elevation. If you're looking for a book that will lift your spirit higher than a cow could possibly climb, look elsewhere. I cannot, in good conscience, recommend this volume for any serious or useful purpose whatsoever.

My only suggestion? When you've grown weary of devouring "The Best Hundred Books" and need a palate cleanser, pick this up for thirty minutes. It won't teach you anything new, but it will certainly be a refreshing change of pace.

ON BEING IDLE. Now, this is a subject on which I flatter myself I am truly *au fait*. The gentleman who, when I was young, baptized me at the fountain of wisdom for nine guineas a term—no extras charged—used to claim he'd never known a boy capable of doing less work in more time. Meanwhile, my poor grandmother, while casually instructing me on the proper use of the Prayer-book, observed that it was highly improbable I would ever do much that I shouldn't, though she felt convinced beyond a shadow of a doubt that I would leave undone practically everything I should. I fear I've partially vindicated her prophecy. Heaven help me! I have done quite a few things I shouldn't have, despite my laziness. Yet, I have fully confirmed her judgment regarding the vast array of duties I neglected. Idling has always been my forte. I take no credit for it—it is a rare gift. While there are plenty of lazy folks and numerous slow coaches, a genuine idler is a rarity. He isn't a man slouching about with his hands in his pockets. On the contrary, his most striking trait is that he is perpetually, intensely busy. One cannot enjoy idling thoroughly unless one has ample work to do. Doing absolutely nothing when you have nothing to accomplish offers no fun; wasting time then becomes merely an occupation, and a terribly exhausting one. True idleness, like fine kisses, must be stolen to remain sweet.

Many years ago, when I was a young man, I fell violently ill—I couldn't quite discern what was wrong, save for a beastly cold. Presumably, it was something grave, for the doctor remarked I should have sought him a month prior, warning that if the condition persisted another week, he wouldn't answer for the outcome. It is extraordinary, but I've never encountered a physician summoned to a case who didn't discover that a single day's delay would have rendered recovery impossible. Our medical guide, philosopher, and friend resembles the hero in a melodrama: he invariably arrives on the scene just, and only just, in the nick of time. It is simply Providence.

Well, as I was saying, I was very ill and prescribed a month-long stay at Buxton, with strict orders to do absolutely nothing whatsoever. "Perfect rest," the doctor insisted. This seemed delightful. "This man clearly understands my plight," I thought, envisioning a glorious four weeks of *dolce far niente*, seasoned with just enough illness to lend it poetic flavor. I imagined rising late, sipping chocolate, and eating breakfast in slippers and a dressing gown. I pictured lounging in a garden hammock, reading sentimental novels with tragic endings until they slipped from my listless grasp, then reclining to gaze dreamily at the azure sky, watching clouds float like white-sailed ships, while birds sang and leaves rustled. Should weakness prevent outdoor activity, I envisioned sitting propped against pillows at an open window, appearing wasted and intriguing, causing passing beauties to sigh. Twice daily, I would descend in a Bath chair to the Colonnade to drink the mineral waters. Oh, those waters! Ignorant of their nature then, I was charmed by the fashionability of the phrase "drinking the waters," sounding distinctly Queen Anne-esque. But alas! After the first three or four mornings... Sam Weller's description of them tasting like "warm flat irons" barely scratches the surface of their hideous nausea. Nothing could hasten a sick man's recovery like knowing he must consume a glassful daily until cured. I drank them neat for six consecutive days, nearly killing myself; thereafter, I adopted the strategy of following each dose with a stiff glass of brandy-and-water, finding immense relief. Later, eminent physicians informed me the alcohol likely neutralized the chalybeate properties entirely. Fortunate I discovered the remedy.

Yet, drinking the waters was merely a minor torment compared to the misery of that unforgettable month—the most wretched period of my life. For most of it, I adhered strictly to the doctor's mandate, doing nothing but wandering the house and garden, and venturing out for two hours daily in a Bath chair. This broke the monotony slightly. There is more thrill to Bath-chairing—particularly for the unaccustomed—than meets the eye. A constant sense of danger pervades the passenger's mind, convincing him every moment that disaster looms, a feeling intensifying near ditches or freshly macadamized roads. Every approaching vehicle seems destined to strike him; every ascent or descent triggers calculations on whether the frail driver will lose control. However, even this diversion eventually failed to enliven the soul, and the ennui became unbearable. My mind began to crack under the strain. Lacking a robust constitution, I deemed it unwise to push it further. Thus, around the twentieth morning, I rose early, ate a hearty breakfast, and marched straight to Hayfield, at the foot of Kinder Scout—a charming, bustling town nestled in a lovely valley, boasting two delightfully pretty women. At least, they were then; one crossed the bridge and perhaps smiled, while the other stood at an open door, offering unpaid kisses to a red-faced infant. Years have passed; surely they have grown stout and sharp-tongued since.

Returning, I spotted an elderly man breaking stones, sparking such a fierce desire to use my arms that I offered him a drink to swap places. Being a kindly soul, he agreed. I tackled those stones with the pent-up energy of three weeks, accomplishing in thirty minutes more labor than he had managed all day. Yet, it elicited no jealousy. Having taken the plunge, I ventured deeper into dissipation, embarking on long morning walks and attending the band in the pavilion every evening. Still, the days dragged on, and I was heartily relieved when the final day arrived, whisking me away from gout-ridden, consumptive Buxton to London, with its stern industry and vibrant life. As the carriage thundered through Hendon that evening, the lurid glow of the great city warmed my heart. Later, as my cab rattled out of St. Pancras station, the familiar roar swelling around me sounded sweeter than any music I'd heard in months.

I certainly did not relish that month of enforced idleness. I enjoy idling when I *shouldn't* be idling; not when it is the sole option available. Such is my stubborn nature. The moment I prefer standing with my back to the fire, tallying debts, is when my desk is piled high with correspondence demanding immediate replies. The time I savor dawdling over dinner is when a heavy workload awaits. And if urgency demands an early rise, it is precisely then that I cherish lying awake an extra half-hour. Ah! How delicious to roll over and drift back to sleep—"just for five minutes." Do humans exist, aside from the protagonist of a Sunday-school tale for boys, who ever rises willingly? Some men find waking at the appointed hour utterly impossible. If eight o'clock is the required time, they linger until half-past. If circumstances shift and half-past becomes acceptable, they don't rise until nine. They resemble the statesman noted for being punctually half an hour late. They devise endless schemes: purchasing alarm clocks (clever devices that trigger at the wrong moment for the wrong person), instructing Sarah Jane to knock and call, to which they grunt "awry" before sinking back into slumber. I knew one fellow who would endure a cold bath; yet, useless, he would immediately return to bed to warm himself. I believe I could stay out of bed indefinitely once I managed to extricate myself. The true difficulty lies in wrenching the head from the pillow; no amount of pre-dawn resolve eases this task.

After wasting the entire evening, I declare to myself, "Well, no more work tonight; I'll rise early tomorrow." Fully resolved, I intend to do so—until morning. Then, enthusiasm fades, replaced by the reflection that stopping earlier would have been preferable. Add to this the dread of dressing; the more one contemplates it, the greater the urge to postpone. Strange indeed is this bed, this mimic grave, where we stretch our weary limbs and sink quietly into silence and repose. "O bed, O bed, delicious bed, that heaven on earth to the weary head," sang poor Hood. You are a kind old nurse to our fretful youths and maidens. Whether clever or foolish, naughty or good, you cradle us all in your maternal lap, hushing our wayward cries. The burdened man, the sufferer, the little maiden mourning a faithless lover—we lay our aching heads on your white bosom, and you gently soothe us to sleep. Our sorrow is profound when you refuse comfort. How agonizingly slow the dawn approaches when sleep eludes us! Oh, those dreadful nights tossing in fever and pain, lying like living corpses amidst darkness drifting toward light. And oh, those even more terrible nights sitting vigil beside another in agony, startled by falling embers from the low fire, hearing the clock's tick sound like a hammer striking out the life we watch fade.

Enough of beds and bedrooms. I have dwelt on them too long, even for an idle fellow. Let us step outside for a smoke. This wastes time equally well and looks less suspicious. Tobacco has been a boon to us idlers. Hard to imagine what the civil-service clerks before Sir Walter Scott's era found to occupy their minds. I attribute the quarrelsome spirit of medieval youth entirely to the absence of this soothing herb. With no work and no smoking, they fought endlessly. If peace held, they ignited deadly feuds with neighbors; if spare moments remained, they debated whose sweetheart was fairest, wielding battle-axes and clubs as arguments.

ON BEING IN LOVE. Of course, you've been in love! If not, it's coming. Love is like the measles; we all have to go through it. Like the measles, too, we catch it only once. Never fear catching it a second time. The man who has had it can wander into the most dangerous places and perform the most foolhardy tricks with absolute safety. He can picnic in shady woods, stroll through leafy aisles, and linger on mossy seats watching the sunset. He fears a quiet country house no more than his own club. He can join a family trip down the Rhine. He can, to bid a friend farewell, venture straight into the jaws of the marriage ceremony itself. He can keep his head during the whirl of a ravishing waltz and rest afterward in a dark conservatory, catching nothing worse than a cold. He can brave a moonlit walk down sweet-scented lanes or a twilight trek among somber rushes. He can climb a stile without danger, scramble through a tangled hedge without getting caught, and descend a slippery path without falling. He can look into sunny eyes without being dazzled. He hears the siren voices yet steers with an unwavering helm. He clasps white hands in his, but no electric, *Lulu*-like force binds him to their dainty pressure.

No, we never sicken with love twice. Cupid spends no second arrow on the same heart. Love's handmaids—respect, admiration, and affection—are our lifelong friends. Our doors may always remain open to them, but their great celestial master, in his royal progress, pays but one visit and then departs. We like, we cherish, we are very, very fond of—but we never love again. A man's heart is a firework that, once in its time, flashes heavenward. Meteor-like, it blazes for a moment and lights the whole world beneath with its glory. Then the night of our sordid, commonplace life closes in around it, and the burned-out casing falls back to earth, lying useless and uncared for, slowly smoldering into ashes.

Once, breaking free from our prison bonds, we dare, as mighty old Prometheus dared, to scale the Olympian mount and snatch from Phoebus' chariot the fire of the gods. Happy those who, hastening down before it dies out, can kindle their earthly altars at its flame. Love is too pure a light to burn long among the noxious gases we breathe, but before it is choked out, we may use it as a torch to ignite the cozy fire of affection. After all, that warming glow is more suited to our cold little back-parlor of a world than the burning spirit of love itself. Love should be the Vestal fire of some mighty temple—a vast, dim fane whose organ music is the rolling of the spheres. Affection will burn cheerfully when the white flame of love flickers out. Affection is a fire that can be fed day by day, piled higher as the wintry years approach. Old men and women can sit by it with their thin hands clasped; little children can nestle in front; friends and neighbors have their welcome corners by its side; and even shaggy Fido and sleek Titty can toast their noses at the bars. Let us heap the coals of kindness upon that fire. Throw on your pleasant words, your gentle hand-pressures, your thoughtful and selfless deeds. Fan it with good humor, patience, and forbearance. Then you can let the wind blow and the rain fall unheeded, for your hearth will be warm and bright, and the faces gathered round it will make sunshine despite the clouds without.

I am afraid, dear Edwin and Angelina, you expect too much from love. You think there is enough of your little hearts to feed this fierce, devouring passion for all your long lives. Ah, young folk! Don't rely too heavily on that unsteady flicker. It will dwindle and dwindle as the months roll on, and there is no replenishing the fuel. You will watch it die out in anger and disappointment. To each, it will seem that the other is growing colder. Edwin sees with bitterness that Angelina no longer runs to the gate to meet him, all smiles and blushes; and when he has a cough now, she doesn't begin to cry and put her arms round his neck, declaring she cannot live without him. The most she will likely do is suggest a lozenge, and even that in a tone implying she is merely annoyed by the noise. Poor little Angelina, too, sheds silent tears because Edwin has stopped carrying her old handkerchief in the inside pocket of his waistcoat. Both are astonished at the decline in the other, but neither sees their own change. If they did, they would not suffer as they do. They would look for the cause in the right quarter—in the littleness of poor human nature—join hands over their common failing, and start building their house anew on a more earthly and enduring foundation. But we are so blind to our own shortcomings, so wide awake to those of others. Everything that happens to us is always the other person's fault. Angelina would have loved Edwin forever and ever and ever if only Edwin hadn't grown so strange and different. Edwin would have adored Angelina through eternity if she had only remained the same as when he first adored her.

It is a cheerless hour for you both when the lamp of love has gone out and the fire of affection is not yet lit, and you must grope about in the cold, raw dawn of life to kindle it. God grant it catches light before the day is too far spent. Many sit shivering by the dead coals until night falls. But, there, of what use is it to preach? Who, feeling the rush of young love through his veins, can think it will ever flow feeble and slow? To the boy of twenty, it seems impossible that he won't love as wildly at sixty as he does now. He cannot recall any middle-aged or elderly gentleman known for exhibiting symptoms of frantic attachment, yet this doesn't stop him from believing in himself. His love will never fall, whoever else's may. Nobody ever loved as he loves, so naturally, the rest of the world's experience is irrelevant to his case. Alas! Alas! Ere thirty, he joins the ranks of the sneerers. It isn't his fault. Our passions, both good and bad, cease with our blushes. We do not hate, nor grieve, nor rejoice, nor despair in our thirties as we did in our teens. Disappointment doesn't suggest suicide, and we quaff success without intoxication. We take all things in a minor key as we grow older. There are few majestic passages in the later acts of life's opera. Ambition aims less ambitiously. Honor becomes more reasonable and conveniently adapts itself to circumstances. And love—love dies.

"Irreverence for the dreams of youth" soon creeps like a killing frost upon our hearts. The tender shoots and expanding flowers are nipped and withered, and of a vine that yearned to stretch its tendrils round the world, there remains but a sapless stump. My fair friends will deem all this rank heresy, I know. Far from a man not loving after passing boyhood, it is not until there is a good deal of gray in his hair that they think his protestations worthy of attention. Young ladies take their notions of our sex from novels written by their own, and compared with the monstrosities masquerading as men in that nightmare literature, Pythagoras' plucked bird and Frankenstein's demon were fair average specimens of humanity. In these so-called books, the chief lover—or Greek god, as he is admiringly referred to (by the way, they don't specify which "Greek god"; it might be hump-backed Vulcan, double-faced Janus, or even driveling Silenus, the god of abstruse mysteries)—resembles the whole family. However, he shares with them the trait of being a blackguard, and perhaps that is what is meant. To even the little manliness his classical prototypes possessed, he lays no claim whatsoever, being a listless, effeminate noodle on the shady side of forty. But oh! the depth and strength of this elderly party's emotion for some bread-and-butter schoolgirl! Hide your heads, ye young Romeos and Leanders! This *blase* old beau loves with an hysterical fervor requiring four adjectives for every noun to properly describe.

It is well, dear ladies, for us old sinners that you study only books. Did you read mankind, you would know that the lad's shy stammering tells a truer tale than our bold eloquence. A boy's love comes from a full heart; a man's is more often the result of a full stomach. Indeed, a man's sluggish current may not be called love compared with the rushing fountain that wells up when a boy's heart is struck by the heavenly rod. If you would taste love, drink of the pure stream that youth pours out at your feet. Do not wait until it has become a muddy river before stooping to catch its waves. Or is it that you like its bitter flavor—that the clear, limpid water is insipid to your palate and that the pollution of its after-course gives it a relish to your lips? Must we believe those who tell us that a hand foul with the filth of a shameful life is the only one a young girl cares to be caressed by? That is the teaching bawled out day by day from between those yellow covers. Do they ever pause to think, I wonder, those devil's lady-helps

I suppose I could enjoy feeling melancholy; there is a certain satisfaction in being thoroughly miserable. But nobody actually *likes* a fit of the blues. Still, everyone gets them, and yet, nobody seems to understand why. It's impossible to account for them. You're just as likely to hit a slump the day after winning a fortune as the day after losing your brand-new silk umbrella on a train.

The effect is roughly equivalent to suffering from a simultaneous attack of toothache, indigestion, and a severe cold. Suddenly, you become stupid, restless, and irritable—rude to strangers and potentially dangerous to friends. You're clumsy, maudlin, and quarrelsome, turning into a nuisance to everyone within a three-mile radius.

When the blues strike, you feel paralyzed by the need to act while simultaneously unable to think. You can't sit still, so you grab your hat and stride out for a walk. But before you've even reached the corner of the street, you regret coming outside and turn right back. You try reading a book, hoping for distraction, but suddenly Shakespeare sounds trite, Dickens is boringly prosaic, Thackeray is unbearable, and Carlyle is overly sentimental. You toss the book aside and start calling authors names.

Then you try to shoo the cat out of the room, only to kick the door shut on her tail. You decide to write a letter, starting with, *"Dearest Auntie: I find I have five minutes to spare..."* After fifteen minutes of staring at the blank page without generating another thought, you crumple the paper, slam the wet pen onto the tablecloth, and resolve to visit the Thompsons. As soon as you pull on your gloves, however, it hits you: the Thompsons are idiots, they don't have supper, and you'll be expected to hold the baby. You curse them, cancel the trip, and feel utterly crushed.

Now you bury your face in your hands, wishing you were dead and in heaven. You visualize your own sickbed, surrounded by weeping friends and relatives. You bless them all, especially the young and pretty ones, telling yourself they'll finally appreciate you then. You realize too late what they've lost, bitterly contrasting their future reverence with their current indifference. This makes you feel slightly better—for a second. Then you remember how foolish it is to think anyone would care if you blew up, got hanged, married, or drowned. Nobody cares. You've never been truly appreciated. You review your entire life and conclude you've been mistreated since birth.

Half an hour of this thinking ramps you up into a state of savage fury against everyone and everything, particularly yourself—the only person you can't physically kick thanks to anatomy.

Finally, bedtime arrives to stop you from doing something rash. You sprint upstairs, strip naked, leave your clothes scattered everywhere, blow out the candle, and jump into bed as if racing against the clock. For two hours, you toss and tumble, occasionally stripping off your sheets and putting them back on just to break the monotony. Eventually, you drift into an uneasy sleep, plagued by nightmares, waking up late the next morning. That's basically the routine for single men in the blues.

Married men handle it differently: they bully their wives, complain about dinner, and force the kids to bed. This creates quite a commotion in the house, which must be a huge relief to a man in the blues, since rowdy arguments are the only entertainment he can tolerate.

The symptoms are consistent, but people name the illness differently. Poets call it "a feeling of sadness"; 'Arry tells Jimee he's "got the blooming hump"; your sister claims she's just "out of sorts"; and the average guy says, "Old chap, I feel dreadfully miserable tonight." My usual line is, "I have a strange, unsettled feeling tonight... I think I'll go out." Though, strangely, it never happens during the day.

In broad daylight, when the world is buzzing with energy, we can't afford to sigh and sulk. The noise of work drowns out the tiny spirits singing their sad songs in our heads. During the day, we might be angry or indignant, but we aren't "in the blues." If something goes wrong at 10 AM, we swear and bang furniture. If it happens at 10 PM, we retreat to the shadows, read poetry, and ponder how hollow the world is.

Usually, trouble isn't the cause of the blues. Real misery is too harsh for sentimentality. We cry over pictures of tragedy, but we'd turn away immediately from the real thing. There's no luxury in genuine grief. We don't choose to play with sharp swords or cuddle a starving fox. Yet, many elderly ladies will call me a cynical brute for suggesting otherwise. They gaze at tiny shoes in lavender drawers, weeping for toddlers who have grown up, or clutch locks of hair from sons lost at sea. They'd say I'm wrong, but if they asked themselves honestly if dwelling on such sorrows feels pleasant, they'd admit it does. To some, tears taste as sweet as laughter.

As the old chronicler Froissart noted, the Englishman takes his pleasures sadly, and the Englishwoman takes her pleasures *in* sadness itself. I'm not mocking this. Sentiment keeps hearts tender in a cruel world. Men are cold and practical enough already; we wouldn't want women to be the same. Ladies, please remain soft and sentimental—you are the soothing butter to our coarse bread. Besides, sentiment is to women what fun is to us. They deserve their grief just as we deserve our humor.

Who can claim that a contorted face screaming in agony represents a higher form of happiness than a pensive face resting gently on a hand, gazing through time at a fading past? I am glad when I see Regret walking alongside me as a friend. I know the salt has washed from the tears and the sting has been removed from Sorrow's face before we kiss her pale lips. Time heals the wound until we can look back on our pain without bitterness. The burden becomes light, mixed with a sweet pity, much like when Colonel Newcome answers "adsum" to the roll call, or when Tom and Maggie Tulliver clasp hands beneath the swollen waters of the Floss.

Thinking of Tom and Maggie reminds me of George Eliot's phrase, "the sadness of a summer's evening." How perfectly true! Who hasn't felt the enchanting sorrow of a lingering sunset? The world belongs to Melancholy then—a thoughtful, deep-eyed maiden who avoids the glare of noon. She emerges only when "light thickens and the crow flies to the rocky wood." Her palace is twilight land.

There, she meets us. At her shadowy gate, she takes our hand and leads us through her mystical realm. We see no form, only hear the rustle of her wings. Even in the busy city, her spirit lingers: a somber presence in long, dull streets; a dark river creeping ghost-like under black arches, hiding secrets in its mud. In the quiet countryside, when trees blur against the night and bats flutter near our faces, the spell deepens. We stand by an unseen deathbed, hearing the sigh of the dying day in the swaying elms.

A solemn sadness reigns, bringing a great peace. Our daily worries shrink to insignificance. Bread, cheese, and even kisses cease to be the only goals. Unspoken thoughts flood in. Standing under Earth's darkening dome, we feel greater than our petty lives. Surrounded by dusky curtains, the world transforms from a dingy workshop into a stately temple, where, in the dimness, our groping hands might finally touch God.

It is a truly remarkable phenomenon. I sat down today with the grand ambition of composing something clever and entirely original. Alas, for the love of logic, I cannot conjure a single witty thought—unless, perhaps, the topic is simply being broke. I suspect my hands deep in my trouser pockets have triggered this specific train of thought. This is my default posture everywhere except when surrounded by my sisters, cousins, or aunts. In their presence, I must extract my limbs; they raise such a fuss, or rather, expound with such eloquent fervor on the subject, that I am forced to comply. Their collective refrain is always the same: "It is not gentlemanly."

I am hanged if I can comprehend the reasoning. I could easily accept that placing one's hands in *another* person's pocket (particularly if done by the owner) lacks gentility. But how, oh ye rigid guardians of appearance, can stuffing one's own hands into one's own pockets render a man less refined? Perhaps you are correct, though. Upon reflection, I recall hearing some individuals grumble most savagely about this habit. However, they were invariably elderly gentlemen. We young fellows, conversely, remain uneasy without our pockets occupied. We appear awkward and shifty, much like a Music Hall comedian stripped of his signature top hat—a creature that seems impossible to imagine yet somehow necessary.

Let us assume, however, that our pockets contain a few coins in the right hand and a bunch of keys in the left. Suddenly, we can face a female post-office clerk with relative dignity. It remains slightly perplexing, though, knowing exactly what to do with one's hands even while they reside comfortably within one's trousers, especially when empty.

Years ago, when my entire fortune occasionally dwindled to what the townsfolk call "a bob," I would recklessly spend a single penny solely to possess the change—all in copper—to enjoy the delightful jingle. One does not feel nearly so destitute holding eleven pence as one does clutching a solitary shilling. Had I been "La-di-da," that impecunious youth regarding whom we superiors are so fond of sarcasm, I would have exchanged that penny for two half-pennies immediately.

I speak with absolute authority on the subject of financial hardship. I have been a provincial actor. Should further proof be deemed necessary—which I doubt—I can add that I have also been a "gentleman connected with the press." I have subsisted on fifteen shillings a week; I have survived a week on ten, owing the remaining five; and I have endured a fortnight living literally on a greatcoat. It is astonishing what profound insight into domestic economics true deprivation affords. If you wish to ascertain the true value of currency, live on fifteen shillings weekly and observe how much you can save for attire and amusement. You will discover that waiting for a farthing's change is worthwhile, walking a mile to save a penny is sensible, treating oneself to a glass of beer is a luxury reserved for rare occasions, and wearing a single collar for four days is entirely feasible.

Try this regimen just before marriage; it serves as excellent preparation. Have your son and heir experience it prior to sending him to university; he will cease complaining about a hundred-pound annual allowance thereafter. There are certain individuals for whom this lesson would be transformative. Consider that delicate flower who cannot tolerate claret below ninety-four degrees and would sooner dine on cat meat than plain roast mutton. While such wretches exist, to the credit of humanity, they are largely confined to that terrifying and marvelous society known exclusively to lady novelists. Every time I hear someone debating a menu card, I feel an overwhelming urge to drag them to the bar of a common East End pub and force a sixpenny meal down their throat—beefsteak pudding for fourpence, potatoes for a penny, and half a pint of porter for another penny. The memory of that aroma—the mingled scents of beer, tobacco, and roasted pork—might eventually cause them to lower their noses slightly when presented with food in the future.

Then there is the generous soul, the cadger's delight, who parts freely with small change but never settles his debts. Such a person might learn a touch of common sense. "I always tip the waiter a shilling," explained a young government clerk with whom I was dining in Regent Street recently. "One cannot give him less, you know." I agreed with him regarding the impossibility of offering eleven shillings and sixpence, yet I resolved to one day lure him to an establishment near Covent Garden where the waiter, to better perform his duties, roams about in his shirt-sleeves—sleeves notably dirty when nearing the end of the month. I know this waiter intimately. If my friend offers him anything exceeding a penny, the man will insist on shaking his hand immediately as a token of supreme esteem; I am certain of it.

Many amusing anecdotes have been penned regarding poverty, yet the reality holds no humor. It is not funny to haggle over pennies. It is not funny to be perceived as mean or stingy. It is not funny to be shabby and ashamed of one's address. To the impoverished, there is absolutely nothing humorous about poverty. For a sensitive man, it is hell on earth. Countless brave gentlemen, capable of facing the labors of Hercules, have had their hearts shattered by its petty agonies. It is not the physical discomforts that are unbearable. Who would mind roughing it if that were the sole requirement? What did Robinson Crusoe care for a patch on his trousers? Did he even wear trousers? I forget; did he wander about as seen in pantomimes? What mattered if his toes protruded from his boots, or if his umbrella was made of cotton, provided it kept the rain away? His shabbiness troubled him not; there were no friends nearby to sneer.

Being poor is a mere trifling matter. It is being *known* to be poor that delivers the sting. It is not the cold that drives a man without a greatcoat to rush forward. Nor is it solely the shame of lying—which he knows will not be believed—that turns his face crimson when he claims greatcoats are unhealthy and that he refuses umbrellas on principle. It is easy to declare that poverty is no crime. Indeed, if it were a crime, men would not be ashamed of it. It is, however, a blunder, and is punished accordingly. A poor man is despised universally—by Christians and lords alike, by demagogues and footmen equally. No number of schoolboy maxims will earn him respect. Appearance dictates all in matters of human opinion. The man willing to stroll down Piccadilly arm-in-arm with London's most notorious rogue, provided the rogue is impeccably dressed, will sneak up a back alley to whisper a greeting to a seedy-looking gentleman. That seedy gentleman knows this better than anyone and will detour a mile to avoid an acquaintance. Those who knew him in his prosperity need never bother looking the other way. You can instantly distinguish the veteran from the novice; the hardened survivor who has struggled for years against the odds, versus the fresh victim desperately attempting to conceal his misery in a state of constant terror lest he be discovered. It is a universal law that all things are vanity, and everyone is vain. Women are notoriously vain; men are equally so, perhaps even more so if such a comparison is possible. Children, too, are deeply vain—especially children. Right now, one of them is pounding on my legs, demanding my verdict on her brand-new shoes. To be perfectly candid, I hold them in low regard. They lack symmetry, curve, and grace, possessing an indefinable lumpy quality (though I suspect they may have been put on the wrong feet entirely). However, honesty is not what she seeks; she demands flattery. Consequently, I pour over her footwear with a level of effusive praise that feels, in my own conscience, utterly degrading. Nothing less would satisfy this self-important cherub.

I once attempted the "conscientious friend" approach with her, but it failed miserably. She asked for my judgment on her overall conduct, specifically phrasing it as, "What do you think of me? Are you pleased with me?" Seizing the moment for some moral instruction, I replied bluntly, "No, I am not pleased with you." I reminded her of that very morning's events: waking the entire house at five A.M., knocking over a water jug and tumbling down the stairs at seven, attempting to bathe the cat at eight, and sitting on her father's hat at nine-thirty-five. My question was simple: How could a wise and good uncle remain satisfied with such behavior? Would she be grateful for my candor? Would she reflect on my words and resolve to lead a better life? Not a bit of it. Instead, she erupted into a howl of rage, declared herself "naughty" and labeled me a "bad man," and promptly ran to tell her mother.

Since that incident, whenever my opinion is solicited, I keep my true thoughts hidden, opting instead to express boundless admiration for her actions regardless of their merit. She nods approvingly and scampers off to broadcast my praise to the household. She treats my approval as a currency for mercenary gain; shortly after, I hear distant shouts of, "Uncle says I'm a good girl—I get two biscuits!" Off she goes, staring rapturously at her toes and muttering "pretty," a towering monument of two-foot-ten inches of pure conceit.

Years ago, during a festive holiday at a farmhouse, they placed a flower garland around the head of a cow. When they removed the wreath, she became instantly sulky and refused to stand still until it was replaced. This is not a tall tale; it is plain, sober truth. Cats, meanwhile, rival humans in their vanity. I have known a cat to storm out of a room if a visitor uttered a derogatory comment about felines, yet sit and purr for hours if offered a neat compliment.

I adore cats precisely because they are so unconsciously amusing. There is a comic dignity to them, a distinct "How dare you!" or "Get away, don't touch me" aura. Dogs, conversely, are the epitome of "Hail, fellow, well met." When I encounter a familiar dog, I slap his head, hurl opprobrious epithets, and roll him onto his back. He lies there gaping, completely unfazed. Try doing that to a cat! She would never speak to you again. To win a cat's favor, one must proceed with extreme caution. Start with "Poor kitty," followed by "Did I hurt you?" spoken in a tone of soothing sympathy. You likely mean neither, but the sentiment implies proper concern, often touching the cat's feelings so deeply that, if you are decently dressed and mannered, she will arch her back and rub her nose against you. Once this stage is reached, you may gently tickle her chin and the side of her head. The intelligent creature will then dig her claws into your leg, and suddenly, friendship reigns supreme, beautifully summarized by the nursery rhyme: *"I love little pussy, her coat is so warm... And pussy will love me because I am good."* The final lines reveal the cat's narrow definition of human goodness: it consists merely of stroking, patting, and feeding.

Fear not, however, that this limited view of virtue is exclusive to cats. We all tend to adopt a similar standard when judging others. A good man is one who is good *to us*; a bad man is one who fails to do what we want. Deep down, we harbor the conviction that the entire universe exists as a necessary appendage to ourselves. Our fellow creatures were created to admire us and serve our needs. In our own minds, we are each the center of the cosmos. You, dear reader, were brought into existence by a considerate Providence solely to read and pay for my writings; while I, in your estimation, am merely an instrument sent forth to provide entertainment for you. The stars exist to make the night sky interesting for our viewing, and the moon, with its mysterious face, is arranged specifically for us to flirt beneath.

We are much like Mrs. Poyser's bantam cock, who fancied the sun rose every morning just to hear him crow. "It is vanity that makes the world go round." I doubt any man has ever existed without it; if he did, he would be an incredibly uncomfortable companion. Such a person would undoubtedly be a good man—one worthy of being placed under glass, mounted on a pedestal, and studied like a school exercise. We would reverence him, but we would not love him. He would not be a human brother whose hand we wished to grip. Even angels, while excellent in their own right, might prove tedious company for us poor mortals. It is in our faults and failings, not our virtues, that we connect and find sympathy. We differ greatly in our noble qualities, but we unite in our follies. Whether pious, generous, honest, or truthful, these traits vary wildly among us. Yet, in vanity and kindred weaknesses, we can all join hands. Vanity is one of those natural touches that make the whole world kin.

From the Indian hunter boasting of his belt of scalps to the European general swollen with pride beneath rows of stars and medals; from the Chinese rejoicing in the length of his queue to the "professional beauty" enduring torturous diets to achieve a waistline resembling a peg-top; from the ragged little Polly Stiggins strutting through Seven Dials with a tattered parasol to the princess sweeping a drawing-room with a four-yard train; from 'Arry, winning laughter through vulgar chaff, to the statesman delighted by the cheers greeting his lofty speeches; from the African trader exchanging rare oils for glass beads to the Christian maiden selling her body for jewels and titles—all march, fight, bleed, and die beneath the banner of vanity. Yes, vanity is truly the motive power of humanity, and flattery is the grease that keeps the wheels turning.

If you wish to win affection and respect in this world, you must flatter. Flatter the high and the low, the rich and the poor, the silly and the wise. You will succeed brilliantly. Praise a man's virtues and another's vices. Compliment everyone on everything, especially on what they lack. Admire handsome men for their looks, fools for their wit, and boors for their breeding. Your own discernment and intelligence will soon be praised to the heavens. Everyone can be swayed by flattery. The "belted earl"—whatever that peculiar title entails—is easily overcome by it, just as every other human being is, from a duchess to a cat-food salesman, from a plow boy to a poet. Interestingly, poets are far easier to flatter than plow boys, for butter sinks better into wheaten bread than into oatcakes.

And yet, despite the brutal pace and the rocky path, who but a lazybones or a fool stays away? Who, like the latecomer watching a fairy party until he drinks the magic potion and jumps into the spinning dance, can watch this madness and not be pulled in? Not I, certainly. I admit it now: the arbor, the pipe, and the lotus leaves were just fancy words. They sounded deep and philosophical, but I fear I am not the type to sit quietly when there is fun happening outside. I remind myself of the Irishman who saw a crowd gathering and sent his daughter to ask, "Is there going to be a fight?" His reasoning? "'Cos, if so, father'd like to be in it."

I love the fierce struggle. I enjoy watching it. I love hearing stories of people climbing the ladder—fighting their way bravely and honestly, meaning not slipping through by luck or deceit. It stirs the old Saxon warrior blood within me, just like the tales of knights battling terrifying odds that thrilled us as schoolboys. Fighting the battle of life is indeed fighting against fearful odds. This nineteenth century is full of giants and dragons, and the golden treasure chest they guard isn't as easy to steal as the storybooks suggest.

In those books, Algernon takes one last look at the ancestral hall, wipes a tear, and departs—to return three years later, rolling in wealth. The authors never explain *how* it happens, which is a shame, for it would surely be thrilling. But then, not one novelist in a thousand tells the real story of their hero. They spend twelve pages describing a tea party, then summarize a lifetime with phrases like "he became a merchant prince" or "he was now a great artist with the world at his feet." Honestly, there is more real life in one of Gilbert's patter songs than in half the biographies ever written. He shows us every step of how his office boy rose to rule the Queen's navy, and explains exactly how a lawyer with no cases became a great judge, "ready to try a breach-of-promise suit."

It is in the small details, not the grand results, where the true interest of existence lies. What we truly crave is a novel revealing the hidden undercurrent of an ambitious man's career—his struggles, failures, hopes, disappointments, and victories. That would be a massive hit. I am certain that the courtship of Fortune would be just as fascinating as the courtship of any mortal maiden, though, coincidentally, it reads almost exactly the same. For Fortune, as the ancients depicted her, is very much like a woman—not quite as irrational, but nearly so—and the chase is identical in both cases. As Ben Jonson wrote: *"Court a mistress, she denies you; / Let her alone, she will court you."*

A woman rarely truly loves her suitor until he stops caring for her; similarly, Fortune doesn't begin to smile until you've snapped your fingers in her face and turned your back. But by that time, you hardly care if she smiles or frowns. Why couldn't she have smiled when her smiles would have filled you with ecstasy? Everything arrives too late in this world. Well-meaning folks claim this is proper and proves ambition is evil. Nonsense! Good people are always wrong (we never agree on anything). What would the world do without ambitious people? It would be as limp as a Norfolk dumpling. Ambition is the yeast that turns the dough into wholesome bread. Without them, the world wouldn't rise. These are the busybodies who wake early, hammering, shouting, and rattling the fire-irons, making it impossible for anyone else to stay in bed.

Wrong to be ambitious? Forsooth! The men are wrong who, backs bent and brows sweating, carve the smooth road over which humanity marches from generation to generation. They are wrong for using the talents entrusted to them by their Master—for toiling while others play. Of course, they seek rewards. Man is not born with that divine selflessness that thinks only of others. But in working for themselves, they work for us all. We are so tightly bound that no one can labor for themselves alone. Every blow struck in their own defense helps shape the universe. Just as a rushing stream turns the millwheel, and a tiny coral insect builds cells that join continents together, so the ambitious man, building a pedestal for himself, leaves a monument for posterity. Alexander and Caesar fought for personal glory, yet in doing so, they wrapped a belt of civilization around half the globe. Stephenson invented the steam engine to get rich; Shakespeare wrote his plays to afford a cozy home for Mrs. Shakespeare and the little ones.

Contented, unambitious people are fine in their own way. They make excellent backgrounds for great paintings and provide a decent, if not particularly bright, audience for the active spirits of the age. I have no complaint against contented people as long as they keep quiet. But please, for heaven's sake, don't let them strut around claiming they are the perfect model for humanity. They are the deadheads, the drones in the great hive, the street crowds lounging and gaping at those who work. Nor should they imagine, as they often do, that they are wise philosophers who have mastered the art of contentment. It may be true that "a contented mind is happy anywhere," but so is a Jerusalem pony, and the result is that both end up dumped anywhere and treated however.

"Oh, you needn't bother with him," people say. "He's perfectly contented as he is; it would be a pity to disturb him." Thus, the contented individual is ignored, and the dissatisfied man gets the job. If you are foolish enough to be content, don't show it.

And lo! Since that idiotic recommendation, I have been unable to expel the weather from my thoughts—or indeed, anything else. It is, without exaggeration, wretched weather. At the very least, it is so at the moment of my writing, and unless conditions improve miraculously by the time you read this, they surely will deteriorate further. According to us, the weather is perpetually abysmal. It resembles the government: invariably in the wrong. In summer, we complain it is stifling; in winter, that it is lethal; in spring and autumn, we fault it for lacking definition, wishing it would commit to a single state. If it is sunny, we lament the countryside's lack of moisture; if it rains, we beg for sunshine. If December passes without snow, we indignantly inquire where our traditional winters have vanished, acting as though we have been defrauded of a purchased commodity; yet, when snow arrives, our reaction is a disgrace to Christianity. We shall remain discontent until every individual manufactures their own private climate and keeps it strictly to themselves. Failing that arrangement, we would prefer total abolition.

However, I suspect this aversion is peculiar to urban dwellers. In the countryside, Nature is enchanting in every mood. Consider the snow: descending in mysterious, silent softness, blanketing fields and trees in white as if preparing for a fairy nuptial! How delightful a walk when the frozen earth resonates beneath our steps, when our blood thrums in the crisp air, and the distant bark of sheepdogs and children's laughter echo faintly and clearly like Alpine bells across the open hills! And then, skating! Gliding on wings of steel across swaying ice, creating whirring symphonies as we fly. Oh, how exquisite is spring—Nature at her sweetest eighteen! Ah, how tragically beautiful, with its golden radiance and the dying majesty of its colored forests—its crimson sunsets and ghostly evening mists, accompanied by the busy murmur of harvesters, laden orchards, the calls of gleaners, and festivals of praise! Even the rain, sleet, and hail seem merely Nature's dutiful servants when performing their simple tasks in the country; even the East Wind is no worse than a boisterous friend when encountered between hedgerows.

But in the city, where painted stucco blisters under the smoky sun, where sooty rain creates slush and mire, where snow piles in filthy heaps, and where chilling blasts whistle down dingy streets and shriek around gas-lit corners, no aspect of Nature charms us. Urban weather is like a skylark trapped in a counting-house—utterly out of place and obstructive. Cities ought to be enclosed, heated by hot-water pipes, and illuminated by electricity. The weather is a rustic maiden who does not shine in the metropolis. We enjoyed flirting with her in the hayfields, but she loses her allure when we meet her in Pall Mall. When a man vents, saying things like, "By the sun, moon, and stars, what carelessness allowed your delicate foot to crush my corn?" or similar invectives, he feels lighter. Swearing soothes the soul just as smashing furniture does, only it costs nothing. It clears a man out like a load of gunpowder clears a chimney. Occasional explosions are essential. I distrust anyone who never swears, never kicks a stool, or pokes the fire violently. Without an outlet, life's annoyances fester, turning petty grievances into deep-seated sorrows and breeding hatred. Swearing relieves the burden.

There is a universal rule in literature: all great literary men are shy. I am no exception, though acquaintances often claim my reserve is barely perceptible. I am relieved by this assessment; had it been otherwise, my life would likely be a disaster. In my youth, however, my shyness was a glaring defect, a source of profound misery for myself and genuine discomfort for everyone around me—particularly my female companions, who complained of it with bitter regularity.

The lot of the shy man is undeniably unhappy. Men dislike him, women despise him, and he despises himself. Time is the only cure, for use offers no relief. Yet, I once stumbled upon a delightful, albeit absurd, remedy published in the "Answers to Correspondents" section of a modest weekly journal. It went something like this: *"Adopt an easy and pleasing manner, especially toward ladies."*

Poor fool! I can easily imagine the grimace he wore reading that advice. "Adopt an easy and pleasing manner"? Forsooth! Do not try to manufacture a new personality, my dear young friend. Any attempt to suppress your true nature will inevitably result in you becoming ridiculously effusive and offensively familiar. Instead, be your natural self. If you do, you will merely be perceived as surly and dull-witted.

Despite the torment, the shy man possesses a form of revenge against society. He communicates his misery effectively by frightening others just as they fear him. He acts as a dampener on the entire room; even the most jovial spirits become depressed and nervous in his presence. Much of this stems from misunderstanding. Society mistakes his timidity for arrogant aloofness, his awkwardness for insolent carelessness, and his silence for suppressed rage. When terror strikes and he loses the power of speech, he is viewed as a victim of uncontrollable passion. To be misunderstood is the shy man's eternal fate; whatever impression he intends to project, the exact opposite is received. Jokes are taken as lies, sarcasm as stupidity, and flattery as malice. While these trials provide endless amusement for observers and fuel for comedians, they possess a deeply tragic core.

To be shy is to be lonely—a man severed from all companionship. He wanders the world yet remains unmixed within it. An invisible, impregnable wall separates him from his fellow humans. He watches the smiling faces and cheerful voices on the other side but cannot extend a hand to join them. He stands amidst merry groups, longing to connect, yet they glide past him, chatting happily, leaving him stranded. Whether in bustling streets, crowded rooms, or places of work and leisure, wherever humanity gathers, the shy man stands apart, shunned and solitary like a leper. His soul brims with love and yearning, yet the world remains unaware. The iron mask of shyness is welded to his face; the man beneath is unseen. Words of greeting rise to his lips only to vanish into silent whispers behind steel bars. Sympathy dies in his throat, and righteous indignation turns inward, poisoning him. All the hate, scorn, and love of a sensitive nature festers internally, curdling him into a misanthrope and cynic. Indeed, shy men, much like ugly women, endure a hard existence; surviving it requires the thick hide of a rhinoceros.

Fortunately, this condition is curable, a truth I vouch for from personal experience. Though I usually avoid discussing myself, for the sake of humanity, I must confess: I was once "the shyest of the shy." My knees would knock together whenever introduced to a pretty maiden. Recently, I finally conquered this demon. Alone, I entered a railway refreshment room and confronted a young lady in her element. I rebuked her for her lack of courtesy, insisting firmly on the deference due a traveler, and finally met her gaze directly. True, I departed moments later, perhaps appearing precipitous, but that was due to a change of heart, not cowardice.

One solace for the shy is that shyness proves nothing regarding intelligence. Bull-headed fools may sneer at nerves, but high-minded souls are not defined by moral brass. The horse is not inferior to the sparrow, nor the deer to the pig. Shyness signifies extreme sensibility, not self-consciousness or vanity. Conversely, conceit is the quickest antidote. Once you realize you are intellectually superior to everyone else, bashfulness vanishes. You cease fearing crowds, viewing them as a collection of lesser beings. Conceit is the finest armor a man can wear; on its smooth surface, the daggers of spite and envy slide harmlessly away. Without it, the sword of talent struggles to survive life's battles. Real conceit does not make one objectionable; rather, it fosters geniality and kindness. Such a person, satisfied with his own worth, cares little for external validation, allowing him to be truthful and comfortable with anyone from a duke to a street vendor.

In contrast, the shy man is overly humble, constantly anxious about others' opinions. However, this is understandable in youth, when character is still forming. Rarely does a man remain truly shy into adulthood; either inner strength sheds the burden, or the friction of daily life grinds it down. We seldom encounter genuinely shy adults unless in novels or on stage, where they are adored. In fiction, the shy hero is a blond, saintly figure whose dark hair and clumsiness are nonexistent. He loves devotedly, speaks softly, and suffers nobly until the heroine reveals her affection, leading to a happy ending. Reality, however, is far less romantic. The real shy man is darker, clumsier, and less devoted. His sole similarity to the fictional ideal is constancy. He remains faithful not out of virtue, but because looking one woman in the eye exhausts his entire supply of courage; he dreads the female sex so profoundly that he cannot entertain multiple suitors.

The non-shy man, conversely, faces constant temptation. Surrounded by roguish eyes and laughing lips, confusion leads him astray. The shy man, staring only at his boots, sees none of this and feels no urge. Happy, shy man! Though he secretly wishes to participate in the fray, cursing his inability to do so. Occasionally, he musters the courage to attempt romance, but it invariably ends in a humiliating fiasco, leaving him crawling out, limp and pitiable.

Tragically, shyness is a misfortune that garners no sympathy. Unlike losing an umbrella or getting a black eye, the shy man is treated as a living joke. His blushing, trembling legs, and precarious posture become the entertainment of the drawing room. Friends and relatives compound the cruelty by mocking him openly, mimicking his nervous walk, shaking hands as if holding hot plates, and demanding explanations for his stammer. They strut about, advising him to "be bold," while mothers preach illogical lessons on shame. Boys declare him worse than a girl, and girls indignantly deny that any woman could match his behavior.

And here lies the final irony: there is no such thing as a shy woman. Despite popular belief depicting women as timid fawns, the reality is starkly different. A twelve-year-old girl can be as cool as a cucumber while her twenty-year-old brother stammers beside her. Women enter concerts late, disrupting audiences with zero hesitation, while their husbands trail behind as crushed heaps of apology. From the first glance to the end of the honeymoon, women possess superior nerve in matters of love. Love is woman's business, and in business, we all discard our natural weaknesses. The shyest man I ever knew worked as a photographic salesman, proving that even the most reserved individuals can adapt when necessity demands it.

ON BABIES. Oh, yes, I do—I know a lot about 'em. I was one myself once, though not long—not so long as my clothes. They were very long, I recollect, and always in my way when I wanted to kick. Why do babies have such yards of unnecessary clothing? It is not a riddle. I really want to know. I never could understand it. Is it that the parents are ashamed of the size of the child and wish to make believe that it is longer than it actually is? I asked a nurse once why it was. She said: "Lor', sir, they always have long clothes, bless their little hearts." And when I explained that her answer, although doing credit to her feelings, hardly disposed of my difficulty, she replied: "Lor', sir, you wouldn't have 'em in short clothes, poor little dears?" And she said it in a tone that seemed to imply I had suggested some unmanly outrage. Since then I have felt shy at making inquiries on the subject, and the reason—if reason there be—is still a mystery to me. But indeed, putting them in any clothes at all seems absurd to my mind. Goodness knows there is enough of dressing and undressing to be gone through in life without beginning it before we need; and one would think that people who live in bed might at all events be spared the torture. Why wake the poor little wretches up in the morning to take one lot of clothes off, fix another lot on, and put them to bed again, and then at night haul them out once more, merely to change everything back? And when all is done, what difference is there, I should like to know, between a baby's night-shirt and the thing it wears in the day-time? Very likely, however, I am only making myself ridiculous—I often do, so I am informed—and I will therefore say no more upon this matter of clothes, except only that it would be of great convenience if some fashion were adopted enabling you to tell a boy from a girl. At present it is most awkward. Neither hair, dress, nor conversation affords the slightest clew, and you are left to guess. By some mysterious law of nature you invariably guess wrong, and are thereupon regarded by all the relatives and friends as a mixture of fool and knave, the enormity of alluding to a male babe as "she" being only equaled by the atrocity of referring to a female infant as "he". Whichever sex the particular child in question happens not to belong to is considered as beneath contempt, and any mention of it is taken as a personal insult to the family. And as you value your fair name do not attempt to get out of the difficulty by talking of "it." There are various methods by which you may achieve ignominy and shame. By murdering a large and respected family in cold blood and afterward depositing their bodies in the water companies' reservoir, you will gain much unpopularity in the neighborhood of your crime, and even robbing a church will get you cordially disliked, especially by the vicar. This "fetches" the parents (if I may be allowed a vulgarism) more than anything. They will pretend to laugh at the idea at first and will say, "Oh, nonsense!" You must then get excited and insist that it is a fact. You need have no conscientious scruples on the subject, because the thing's nose really does resemble its father's—at all events quite as much as it does anything else in nature—being, as it is, a mere smudge. Do not despise these hints, my friends. There may come a time when, with mamma on one side and grand mamma on the other, a group of admiring young ladies (not admiring you, though) behind, and a bald-headed dab of humanity in front, you will be extremely thankful for some idea of what to say. A man—an unmarried man, that is—is never seen to such disadvantage as when undergoing the ordeal of "seeing baby." A cold shudder runs down his back at the bare proposal, and the sickly smile with which he says how delighted he shall be ought surely to move even a mother's heart, unless, as I am inclined to believe, the whole proceeding is a mere device adopted by wives to discourage the visits of bachelor friends. I have always possessed a profound affection for eating and drinking, though in my youth, the passion leaned heavily toward the former. Back then, I boasted both a voracious appetite and a robust digestion. I recall one particular evening when a gentleman of dull eyes and a complexion the color of a bruised plum arrived to dine at our home. For approximately five minutes, he stared at me devouring my food, appearing utterly transfixed. Finally, turning to my father, he asked, "Does your boy ever suffer from dyspepsia?"

My father replied, "Never; I've never heard him complain of such a thing."

"Do you yourself suffer from dyspepsia, Collywobbles?" the guest inquired. (They called me Collywobbles, though it wasn't my actual name.)

"No, Pa," I answered promptly. Immediately following this declaration, I added, somewhat innocently, "But what *is* dyspepsia, Pa?"

The livid-complexioned stranger regarded me with a mixture of astonishment and envy. Then, in a tone dripping with infinite pity, he slowly intoned, "You will know... some day."

My late, beloved mother often remarked that she delighted in watching me eat. It remains a source of enduring satisfaction to me that I must have provided her with considerable gratification in that regard. Generally speaking, a growing, healthy lad who exercises regularly and avoids excessive studying can easily satisfy even the most demanding expectations regarding his consumption.

It is genuinely amusing to observe boys eating when you aren't paying for the privilege. Their concept of a "square meal" involves a staggering array of provisions: a pound and a half of roast beef accompanied by five or six substantial potatoes (preferably soapy ones, as they offer greater substance), generous portions of greens, four thick slices of Yorkshire pudding, followed by a pair of currant dumplings, several green apples, a penworth of nuts, half a dozen jumbles, and a bottle of gingerbeer. Once finished, they proceed to play at horses. One cannot help but wonder how they must disdain us men, who are required to sit quietly for hours after consuming merely a spoonful of clear soup and the wing of a chicken!

However, boys do not possess all the advantages. A boy never truly enjoys the luxury of being satisfied. He never feels full. He can never stretch out his legs, place his hands behind his head, close his eyes, and sink into the ethereal bliss that envelops the well-dined man. A dinner makes absolutely no difference to a boy. To a man, however, it acts like a good fairy's potion; afterwards, the world seems brighter and better. A man who has dined satisfactorily develops a yearning love for all his fellow creatures. He strokes the cat gently, cooing "Poor pussy" with tender emotion. He sympathizes with the musicians of the German band outside, wondering if they are cold, and momentarily ceases to hate his wife's relatives. A good dinner reveals the softer side of a man. Under its genial influence, the gloomy and morose become jovial and chatty. Individuals who spend the rest of the day looking as though they subsist solely on vinegar and Epsom salts break into wreathed smiles after dinner, exhibiting a sudden urge to pat small children on the head and speak vaguely to them about sixpences. Serious men thaw into mild cheerfulness, and snobbish young men with heavy mustaches forget to be objectionable.

I always feel sentimental after dinner. It is the only time I can properly appreciate love stories. When the hero clasps "her" in a final, wild embrace and stifles a sob, I feel as devastated as if I had played a disastrous hand at whist and turned up only a deuce. When the heroine meets her tragic end, I weep. If I were to read the same tale early in the morning, I would simply sneer at it. Digestion—or rather, indigestion—has a marvelous effect upon the heart.

If I wish to compose something truly pathetic, I consume a large plate of hot buttered muffins about an hour prior to sitting down to work. By the time I begin, an unutterable melancholy descends upon me. I envision heartbroken lovers parting forever at lonely wayside stiles while the sad twilight deepens around them, broken only by the tinkling of a distant sheep-bell. I imagine old men gazing at withered flowers until their sight dims with tears; dainty maidens waiting at open casements as "he cometh not," watching the golden tresses of their loves turn white and thin. The babies they once dandled have become grown adults burdened by their own pains, and the playmates they laughed with lie silent beneath the waving grass. They wait and watch until the dark shadows of the unknown night gather round them, and the world's childish troubles fade from their aching eyes. I see pale corpses tossed on white-foamed waves, deathbeds stained with bitter tears, and graves in trackless deserts. I hear the wild wailing of women, the low moaning of children, and the dry sobbing of strong men. It is all the muffins. I could not conjure a single melancholy fancy upon a mutton chop and a glass of champagne. A full stomach is a great aid to poetry; indeed, no sentiment of any kind can stand upon an empty one.

We lack the time or inclination to indulge in fanciful troubles until we have dispelled our real misfortunes. We do not sigh over dead dicky-birds while the bailiff is in the house, nor do we worry about whether our mistress's smiles are cold, hot, or lukewarm when we don't know where to find our next shilling.

Foolish people—that is to say, those who entertain opinions differing from mine—will tell you that mental distress is far more agonizing than bodily pain. This is romantic and touching theory, so comforting to the love-sick youth who looks down patronizingly on a starving man and thinks, "Ah, how happy you are compared with me!" So soothing to fat gentlemen who cackle about the superiority of poverty over riches. But it is all nonsense—all cant. An aching head soon makes one forget an aching heart. A broken finger will banish all memories of an empty chair. And when a man feels truly hungry, he feels nothing else.

We sleek, well-fed folk can hardly conceive what hunger is like. We know the sensation of having no appetite and ignoring dainties placed before us, but we do not understand the agony of sickening for food—to die for bread while others waste it, to gaze with famished eyes at coarse fare steaming behind dingy windows, longing for a penworth of pea pudding but lacking the penny to buy it. To feel that a crust would be delicious and a bone a banquet. Hunger is a luxury to us, a piquant, flavor-enhancing sauce. It is worthwhile to get hungry and thirsty merely to discover the depth of gratification obtainable from eating and drinking.

If you wish to thoroughly enjoy your dinner, take a thirty-mile country walk after breakfast and refrain from eating until you return. Your eyes will glisten at the sight of the white tablecloth and steaming dishes! You will sigh with contentment as you set down the empty beer tankard and pick up your knife and fork. Later, as you push back your chair, light a cigar, and beam upon everyone, you will feel incredibly comfortable. Just ensure, when adopting this plan, that the promised dinner actually exists, or the disappointment will be trying.

Many summers and winters have passed since then, my friend, and you have danced with the flickering firelight until you grew sad and gray. Your brilliant colors are fading, and the envious moths have gnawed your silken threads. You are withering like the dead hands that wove you. Do you ever think of those dead hands? You seem so grave and thoughtful sometimes that I almost believe you do.

Come, you and I and the deep-glowing embers; let us converse. Tell me in your silent language what you recall of those young days, when you lay upon my little mother's lap and her girlish fingers played with your rainbow tresses. Was there never a lad nearby? Never a boy who would seize one of those little hands to smother it with kisses, persistently holding it and thereby disrupting your creation? Was your fragile existence not often endangered by this same clumsy, headstrong lad, who would toss you disrespectfully aside so that—he, unsatisfied with one—might hold both hands and gaze up into the loved eyes?

I can see that lad now through the haze of the flickering twilight. He is an eager, bright-eyed boy with pinching, dandy shoes, tight-fitting smalls, a snowy shirt frill, a stock, and—oh! such curly hair. A wild, light-hearted boy! Could he be the great, grave gentleman upon whose stick I used to ride cross-legged? The care-worn man into whose thoughtful face I used to gaze with childish reverence, whom I called "Father"?

You say "yes," old screen; but are you quite certain? It is a serious accusation you bring. Is it possible? Did he have to kneel in those wonderful smalls to pick you up and rearrange you before being forgiven and having his curly head smoothed by my mother's little hand? Ah, old screen, did lads and lassies make love fifty years ago exactly as they do now? Are men and women so unchanged? Did little maidens' hearts beat the same under pearl-embroidered bodices as they do under Mother Hubbard cloaks? Have steel casques and chimney-pot hats altered the brains working beneath them?

Oh, Time! Great Chronos! Is this your power? Have you dried up seas and leveled mountains, leaving only the tiny human heart-strings to defy you? Ah, yes! They were spun by a Mightier than you, stretching beyond your narrow vision, for their ends are fixed in eternity. You may mow down leaves and blossoms, but the roots of life lie too deep for your sickle to sever. You refashion Nature's garments, but you cannot alter by a jot the throbbings of her pulse. The world rolls obedient to your laws, but the heart of man is not of your kingdom, for in its birthplace "a thousand years are but as yesterday."

I fear I am drifting away from my "furnished apartments," and I struggle to return. Yet, I have an excuse for this detour. It is a piece of old furniture that has led me astray, and fancies somehow gather around old furniture like moss around ancient stones. Our chairs and tables eventually become part of our lives, seeming like quiet friends. What strange tales the wooden-headed old fellows could tell if they chose to speak! What unsuspected comedies and tragedies have they not witnessed! What bitter tears have been sobbed into that old sofa cushion! What passionate whispers must the settee have overheard!

New furniture holds no charm for me compared to the old. It is the old things we love—the old faces, the old books, the old jokes. New furniture can build a palace, but it takes old furniture to create a home. Not merely old in itself—lodging-house furniture is generally that—but it must be old to *us*, old in associations and memories. The furniture of furnished apartments, however ancient in reality, is new to our eyes, and we feel as though we could never connect with it.

Furthermore, as with all fresh acquaintances, whether wooden or human (and there is surprisingly little difference between the two species), everything strikes you with its worst aspect. The knobby woodwork and shiny horsehair covering of the easy-chair suggest anything but ease. The mirror is smoky. The curtains need washing. The carpet is frayed. The table looks ready to collapse the moment anything rests upon it. The grate is cheerless, the wallpaper hideous. The ceiling appears to have had coffee spilled all over it, and the ornaments—well, they are worse than the wallpaper.

There must surely be a special, secret manufactory dedicated to producing lodging-house ornaments. Precisely the same articles appear in every lodging-house across the kingdom and nowhere else. There are the two—what do you call them?—standing one at each end of the mantelpiece, where they are never safe, draped in long triangular strips of glass that clank against one another and make you nervous. In more modest rooms, these works of art are supplemented by a couple of pieces of china that might each represent a cow sitting on its hind legs, a model of the Temple of Diana at Ephesus, a dog, or whatever else you fancy.

Somewhere in the room lies a bilious-looking object; at first, you mistake it for a lump of dough left by a child, but upon closer inspection, it resembles an undercooked Cupid. The landlady calls this a statue. Then there is a "sampler" worked by some idiot relative of the family, a picture of the "Huguenots," two or three Scripture texts, and a highly framed, glazed certificate stating that the father has been vaccinated, is an Odd Fellow, or possesses some similar distinction.

You examine these various attractions and then dismally inquire about the rent.
"That's rather a good deal," you say upon hearing the figure.
"Well, to tell you the truth," answers the landlady with a sudden burst of candor, "I've always had" (mentioning a sum considerably higher than the first), "and before that I used to have" (an even higher figure).

One shudders to consider what the rent of apartments must have been twenty years ago. Every landlady makes you feel thoroughly ashamed of yourself by informing you, whenever the topic arises, that she used to receive twice as much for her rooms as you are currently paying. Young male lodgers of the previous generation must have been of a wealthier class, or they must have ruined themselves. I would have been forced to live in an attic.

Curiously, in lodgings, the rule of life is reversed. The higher you rise in the world, the lower you descend in your accommodations. On the lodging-house ladder, the poor man stands at the top, while the rich man lies underneath. You begin in the attic and work your way down to the first floor. Many great men have lived in attics, and some have died there.

They say—though I suspect the speakers are merely people who ought to be ashamed of themselves—that the consciousness of being well-dressed imparts a blissfulness to the human heart that religion is powerless to bestow. I am afraid these cynical persons are sometimes correct.

I know that when I was a very young man (many, many years ago, as the storybooks say) and needed cheering up, I used to go and dress myself in all my best clothes. If I had been annoyed in any manner—if my washerwoman had discharged me, for instance; or if my blank-verse poem had been returned for the tenth time with the editor's compliments and regrets regarding lack of space; or if I had been snubbed by the woman I loved as a man never loves before—you'll note, by the way, it is truly extraordinary what a variety of ways of loving there must be. We all do it as it was never done before. I don't know how our great-grandchildren will manage; they will have to do it on their heads by their time if they persist in not clashing with any previous method.

Well, as I was saying, when these unpleasant sorts of things happened and I felt crushed, I put on all my best clothes and went out. It brought back my vanishing self-esteem. In a glossy new hat and a pair of trousers with a fold down the front (carefully preserved by keeping them under the bed—I don't mean on the floor, you know, but between the bed and the mattress)—I felt I was somebody. I felt there were other washerwomen; yes, and even other girls to love, who would perhaps appreciate a clever, good-looking young fellow. I didn't care; that was my reckless way. I would make love to other maidens. I felt that in those clothes I could do it.

Clothes have a wonderful deal to do with courting; they are half the battle. At all events, the young man thinks so, and it generally takes him a couple of hours to get himself up for the occasion. His first half-hour is occupied in trying to decide whether to wear his light suit with a cane and drab billycock, or his black tails with a chimney-pot hat and his new umbrella. He is sure to be unfortunate in either decision. If he wears his light suit and takes the stick, it comes on to rain, and he reaches the house in a damp and muddy condition, spending the evening trying to hide his boots. If, on the other hand, he decides in favor of the top hat and umbrella—nobody would ever dream of going out in a top hat without an umbrella; it would be like letting baby (bless it!) toddle out without its nurse—how I do hate a top hat! One lasts me a very long while, I can tell you. I only wear it when... well, never mind when I wear it. It lasts me a very long while. I've had my present one five years. It was rather old-fashioned last summer, but the shape has come round again now, and I look quite stylish.

But to return to our young man and his courting. If he starts off with the top hat and umbrella, the afternoon turns out fearfully hot, and the perspiration takes all the soap out of his mustache and converts the beautifully arranged curl over his forehead into a limp wisp resembling a lump of seaweed. The Fates are never favorable to the poor wretch. If he does by any chance reach the door in proper condition, she has gone out with her cousin and won't be back till late.

How a young lover made ridiculous by the gawkiness of modern costume must envy the picturesque gallants of seventy years ago! Look at them (on the Christmas cards), with their curly hair and natty hats, their well-shaped legs encased in smalls, their dainty Hessian boots, their ruffling frills, their canes and dangling seals. No wonder the little maiden in the big poke-bonnet and the light-blue sash casts down her eyes and is completely won. Men could win hearts in clothes like that. But what can you expect from baggy trousers and a monkey-jacket?

Clothes have more effect upon us than we imagine. Our deportment depends upon our dress. Make a man get into seedy, worn-out rags, and he will skulk along with his head hanging down, like a man going out to fetch his own supper beer. But deck out the same article in gorgeous raiment and fine linen, and he will strut down the main thoroughfare, swinging his cane and looking at the girls as perky as a bantam cock. Clothes alter our very nature. A man could not help being fierce and daring with a plume in his bonnet, a dagger in his belt, and a lot of puffy white things all down his sleeves. But in an ulster, he wants to get behind a lamp-post and call the police.

I am quite ready to admit that you can find sterling merit, honest worth, deep affection, and all such like virtues of the roast-beef-and-plum-pudding school as much, and perhaps more, under broadcloth and tweed as ever existed beneath silk and velvet. But the spirit of that knightly chivalry that "rode a tilt for lady's love" and "fought for lady's smiles" needs the clatter of steel and the rustle of plumes to summon it from its grave between the dusty folds of tapestry and underneath the musty leaves of moldering chronicles.

The world must be getting old, I think; it dresses so very soberly now. We have been through the infant period of humanity, when we used to run about with nothing on but a long, loose robe, and liked to have our feet bare. And then came the rough, barbaric age, the boyhood of our race. We didn't care what we wore then, but thought it nice to tattoo ourselves all over, and we never did our hair. And after that, the world grew into a young man and became foppish. It decked itself in flowing curls and scarlet doublets, and went courting, bragging, and bouncing—making a brave show. But all those merry, foolish days of youth are gone, and we are very sober, very solemn—and very stupid, some say—now. The world is a grave, middle-aged gentleman in this nineteenth century, and would be shocked to see itself with a bit of finery on. So it dresses in black coats and trousers, black hats, and black boots, and, dear me, it is such a very respectable gentleman—to think it could ever have gone gadding about as a troubadour or a knight-errant, dressed in all those fancy colors!

Ah, well! We are more sensible in this age. Or at least we think ourselves so. It is a general theory nowadays that sense and dullness go together. Goodness is another quality that always goes with blackness. Very good people indeed, you will notice, dress altogether in black, even to gloves and neckties, and they will probably take to black shirts before long. Medium goods indulge in light trousers on week-days, and some of them even go so far as to wear fancy waistcoats. On the other hand, people who care nothing for a future state go about in light suits; and there have been known wretches so abandoned as to wear a white hat. Such people, however, are never spoken of in genteel society, and perhaps I ought not to have referred to them here.

By the way, talking of light suits, have you ever noticed how people stare at you the first time you go out in a new light suit? They do not notice it so much afterward. The population of London have got accustomed to it by the third time you wear it. I say "you," because I am not speaking from my own experience. I do not wear such things at all myself. As I said, only sinful people do so. I wish, though, it were not so, and that one could be good, and respectable, and sensible without making oneself a guy.

I look in the glass sometimes at my two long, cylindrical bags (so picturesquely rugged about the knees), my stand-up collar and billycock hat, and wonder what right I have to go about making God's world hideous. Then wild and wicked thoughts come into my heart. I don't want to be good and respectable. (I never can be sensible, I'm told; so that doesn't matter.) I want to put on lavender-colored tights, with red velvet breeches and a green doublet slashed with yellow; to have a light-blue silk cloak on my shoulder, and a black eagle's plume waving from my hat, and a big sword, and a falcon, and a lance, and a prancing horse, so that I might go about and gladden the eyes of the people. Why should we all try to look like ants crawling over a dust-heap? Why shouldn't we dress a little gayly? I am sure if we did, we should be happier. True, it is a little thing, but we are a little race, and what is the use of our pretending otherwise and spoiling fun? Let philosophers get themselves up like old crows if they like. But let me be a butterfly.

"I remember, I remember, in the days of chill November, how the blackbird on the—" Well, I forget the rest. It is the opening line of the very first poem I ever mastered. As for "Hey Diddle Diddle, the cat and the fiddle," I simply dismiss it as frivolous nonsense, entirely lacking the gravitas required for true poetry. However, "I Remember, I Remember" was a different matter. Reciting it earned me fourpence. I knew the value was fourpence because the teacher explained that if I saved it until I had two pence more, I'd possess a sixpence. While that logic was irrefutable, it failed to move me; consequently, the money vanished by the following morning, spent on something I now recall with absolute clarity: nothing. That is the nature of Memory. She delivers nothing whole. She is a willful child whose toys are perpetually broken.

I vividly recall tumbling into a massive dust-hole as a toddler, yet I possess zero recollection of ever escaping. If I relied solely on my memory, I would be convinced I am still trapped inside. On another occasion—several years later—I was supposedly witnessing a thrilling romantic scene. The only distinct detail I retain is that at the climax, someone burst in with a sepulchral voice shouting, "Emily, you're wanted," sounding suspiciously like the police had arrived to arrest her. All the tender vows exchanged between us have evaporated completely. When we glance backward at life, it resembles a crumbling ruin: a shattered column where a grand portal once stood, a broken window frame marking a lady's bower, and a pile of blackened stones where a roaring fire once danced, all draped in tinted lichen and clinging ivy.

Yet, everything looks lovely through the softening haze of time. Even past sadness tastes sweet. Our boyhood days appear incredibly cheerful now, filled with nuts, hoops, and gingerbread. We conveniently forget the bullying, the toothaches, and the tedious Latin verbs—the latter particularly. We imagine ourselves as blissfully happy children and wish we could love again, ignoring the heartbreaks, sleepless nights, and the choking sensation in our throats when a beloved woman declared she could only ever be our sister. As if any man truly desires more sisters! Indeed, we see only the brightness, not the darkness. The sunshine casts no shadows on the past. The road behind us looks magnificent, stripped of its sharp stones. We dwell only on the roadside roses, mistaking the thorny briars that cut us for gentle vines dancing in the breeze. Thank goodness for this illusion; otherwise, the ever-lengthening chain of memory would consist only of bitter links, and today's sorrows would be relived tomorrow.

It seems the brightest aspects of existence are also its finest, so as our lives sink into the dark ocean of oblivion, the lightest, happiest moments float to the surface, remaining visible long after the angry thoughts and stinging pains are buried deep beneath the waves. This romanticized view explains why elders tell such tall tales about their youth. To them, the world was a superior place; boys were boys, girls were different, and winter actually felt like winter. Tales of heroic deeds and extraordinary events require three strong men to believe half of them. I enjoy hearing an old gentleman recount these exploits to a group of skeptical youths who dare not contradict him. Soon enough, he swears the moon shone every night during his childhood and that wrestling mad bulls in blankets was the school's favorite sport. It is a constant cycle: the old folks of our grandparents' era sang similar songs, and today's youth will drone identical nonsense to annoy the next generation.

"Oh, give me back the good old days of fifty years ago!" has been humanity's cry since Adam's fifty-first birthday. Pick up any literature from 1835, and you'll find poets and novelists begging for the same impossible return that German Minnesingers and Norse saga writers desired centuries prior. Even the ancient Greek philosophers sighed for it. By all accounts, the world has steadily deteriorated since its inception. One must conclude it was a delightfully pristine paradise when first unveiled to the public, pleasant enough even now if one stays in the sunshine and accepts the rain with good humor. Still, there is no denying it was sweeter in that dewy dawn of creation, when the feet of millions hadn't worn the grass to dust, and city noise hadn't chased silence away. Life must have been noble and solemn for those free-roaming ancestors, walking hand-in-hand with God under the vast sky, living in sun-drenched tents among grazing herds, taking simple needs from Nature herself. They toiled, spoke, and thought while the great earth rolled silently, untouched by trouble and wrong.

Those days are gone. Humanity's quiet childhood, spent in forest glades and by murmuring rivers, is forever ended. Life has deepened into adulthood amidst turmoil, doubt, and hope. The age of restful peace is over; we have work to finish and must hurry onward. What that work is, or our role in the grand design, remains unknown, though our unconscious hands help build it. Like tiny coral insects laboring deep underwater, we strive for petty personal goals, unaware of the vast structure we are constructing for God. Let us abandon vain regrets for days that will never return. Our task lies ahead, not behind. "Forward!" is our motto. Do not sit with folded hands, staring at the past as if it were the building itself; it is merely the foundation. Do not waste your heart and life mourning what *might* have been, neglecting the possibilities that await. Opportunities fly by while we lament lost chances, and the happiness present goes unnoticed because we are distracted by the happiness that fled.

Years ago, while wandering from the fireplace into the Land of Fairy-Tales, I encountered a valiant knight. He had conquered countless dangers and was known as fearless, save perhaps for those rare moments when even courage falters. One day, weary and disheartened by the toil of the road, he feared giant rocks hanging overhead would crush him, chasms lurked on either side, and dragons with bloody jaws guarded dark caves. A darkness thicker than night enveloped the path. Overwhelmed, the knight decided to turn back and seek an easier route for his gentle steed. But when he glanced behind, he marveled: the entire journey he had traveled had vanished. Instead, at his horse's heels lay a bottomless abyss, so deep no man could see its floor. Realizing retreat was impossible, Sir Ghelent prayed to Saint Cuthbert, spurred his horse, and rode forward bravely and joyfully. Nothing harmed him.

There is no turning back on the Road of Life. The fragile bridge of time sinks into eternity with every step taken. The past is gone, harvested, and no longer ours. No word can be unsaid, no step retraced. Thus, true knights press on boldly rather than weeping uselessly over what cannot be recalled. A new life begins every second. We must advance joyfully, regardless of our willingness, walking better with eyes fixed forward than with gaze cast backward.

A friend recently urged me enthusiastically to learn a miraculous system for perfect memory retention. I suspect his eagerness stemmed from my occasional habit of borrowing umbrellas and emerging mid-whist game declaring, "Lor! I've been thinking all along that clubs were trumps." Despite his attractive arguments, I declined. I have no desire to remember everything. Many things in a man's life are better forgotten. Specifically, there is that time, many years ago, when we acted dishonorably, deviating from the path of strict probity, and were unfortunately caught. Worse still, we paid the penalty: maddening hours of remorse, agonizing shame, and perhaps the scorn of those we loved. Let us forget. Oh, Father Time, lift those bitter memories from our overburdened hearts, for griefs arrive hourly and our strength is fleeting.

This does not mean the past should be buried. Life's music would cease if the chords of memory were severed. We should root out only the poisonous weeds, not the flowers, from the Garden of Mnemosyne. Recall Dickens' "Haunted Man"? He prayed for forgetfulness, only to beg for memory once granted. We do not wish for all ghosts laid; only the haggard, cruel-eyed specters must flee. Let the gentle, kind phantoms remain; we are not afraid of them. Alas, the world grows crowded with ghosts as we age. We need not visit grim churchyards or moated granges to encounter shadowy faces rustling in the night. Every house, every room, every creaking chair harbors its own ghost. They haunt the empty chambers of our lives, swirling around us like autumn leaves. Some are living, some dead—we know not. We once clasped their hands, shared our dreams, and felt our hearts join in a grip defying death. Now they are gone, lost forever. Their eyes will never meet ours again, their voices unheard. Only their ghosts remain, talking to us. We see them dimly through tears, stretching yearning hands toward air.

Ghosts are with us day and night. They walk beside us in the sunlit street, sit with us in the twilight, and peer from the windows of the old schoolhouse. We meet them in the woods where we shouted as boys. Can you not hear their low laughter behind the blackberry bushes and distant whoops across the glades? Here, in the quiet fields where we watched for her at sunset, she appears now in the dainty white frock we knew, with the big bonnet dangling from her hands and sunny brown hair tangled. Five thousand miles away!
