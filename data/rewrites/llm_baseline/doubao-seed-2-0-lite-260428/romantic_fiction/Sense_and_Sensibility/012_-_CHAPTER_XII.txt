The crisp golden light of an autumn Barton morning filtered through hawthorn hedgerows as Elinor and Marianne took their usual post-breakfast walk, when Marianne burst into a torrent of delighted speech that left Elinor blinking in disbelief at her sister’s uncharacteristic, giddy recklessness.

Willoughby had given her a horse, Marianne announced, cheeks flushed bright with joy—one he’d bred himself on his family estate in Somersetshire, perfectly sized and tempered for a lady to ride. She’d accepted it at once, no hesitation at all, chattering on about the wind in their hair as they galloped across the local downs, the way Elinor could ride it half the time, no strings attached. It had never crossed her mind that their small household had never planned to keep a horse: that accepting the gift would mean purchasing a second mount for a servant to tend it, hiring that very servant, even building a new stable or shed to house them both, all costs their mother’s already tight budget, stretched thin after leaving Norland Park, could barely bear.

Marianne brushed off every concern Elinor raised like it was a bothersome gnat. The extra servant would cost almost nothing, their mother would never object to making her happy, the park could easily lend a horse for the groom, even a rickety old shed at the edge of the cottage grounds would do for stabling. When Elinor gently pointed out that accepting such an extravagant, personal gift from a man she had known only a handful of weeks was far from proper, Marianne flared up at once.
“You are wrong, Elinor,” she said, her voice warm and fierce with conviction. “Time means nothing when two souls fit. I have not known Willoughby long in days, but I know him better than I know anyone in the whole world save you and mama. Seven years could not make some people understand each other, and seven days is more than enough for us. I would think it far more improper to take such a gift from our brother John, who we have lived with for decades but barely know at all, than from Willoughby, whose character I have studied and loved every minute of these past weeks.”

Elinor knew better than to push her on the point when her temper was so roused—opposition would only make Marianne dig her heels in deeper. Instead, she appealed to her sister’s soft, loving heart for their mother, painting a gentle picture of the quiet sacrifices Mrs. Dashwood would have to make to cover those extra, unplanned costs, and Marianne softened immediately. She promised she would not pressure their mother to agree to the gift, that she would tell Willoughby the next time she saw him she had to turn it down.

She kept her word. Later that very day, when Willoughby called at the cottage, Elinor overheard her murmuring her disappointment to him in a low voice, just out of earshot of the rest of the family, as she explained all the practical reasons the lovely horse could not be theirs. Willoughby’s sorrow was plain on his face, and after pressing her gently to change her mind and realizing he could not, he leaned in close, his voice soft only for her to hear.
“Marianne, the horse is still yours, even if you cannot take her now. I will keep her, name her Queen Mab, and care for her every day until you are ready to claim her. When you leave Barton to move into the home we will share, she will be waiting for you.”

Elinor’s breath caught. In the warmth of his tone, the easy use of her sister’s first name, the quiet, unspoken promise woven through every word, she saw it plain as day: they were already secretly engaged. The intimacy between them was no casual flirtation, no fleeting fancy. She could not even find it in herself to be surprised that two such open, unguarded people had hidden the news from their own family, letting them stumble across the truth by accident.

Little Margaret confirmed her suspicion the very next day, pulling her eldest sister aside with a face so full of important secrecy she could barely contain it. “Elinor! I know a huge secret. Marianne and Mr. Willoughby are going to be married very soon, I am sure of it.”
Elinor laughed softly, fond of her little sister’s overactive imagination. “You’ve said that almost every day since they met on Highchurch Down, Margaret. You swore a fortnight ago Marianne wore his miniature around her neck, and it turned out to be nothing but a portrait of our great-uncle.”
“But this is real!” Margaret insisted, bouncing on her toes. “He has a lock of her hair. I saw him cut it off last night, after you and mama stepped out of the parlour. They were whispering, and he begged her for something, then picked up her little embroidery scissors and snipped off a whole long curl of the dark hair that was tumbling down her back. He kissed it, folded it in a scrap of white paper, and tucked it right into his pocketbook.”
Elinor could not find it in herself to doubt her. Every part of the story lined up perfectly with all the small, tender moments she had watched unfold between the two of them.

Margaret’s sharp little eye for secrets nearly got Elinor in trouble a few evenings later, when the whole party was gathered at the Middleton’s park. Mrs. Jennings, forever nosy about romantic attachments, turned to the youngest Miss Dashwood and badgered her to name the quiet young man she was certain Elinor was secretly fond of. Margaret looked straight at Elinor, and grinned. “I can’t tell, can I Elinor?”
The whole room burst into laughter. Elinor forced a smile, but her chest felt tight. She knew exactly who Margaret was thinking of—Edward Ferrars, the quiet, kind man she had grown to love back at Norland, the very last person she wanted to become the butt of Mrs. Jennings’ endless, teasing jokes.
Marianne, trying to defend her, only made it worse, flushing bright red and snapping at Margaret. “Whatever silly ideas you have in your head, you have no right to go repeating them to everyone!”
“I didn’t make up any ideas!” Margaret protested. “You’re the one who told me about him in the first place!”
The company grew even more curious, Mrs. Jennings practically leaning out of her seat to get the name out of the girl. “Come on, Miss Margaret, tell us all! What is the gentleman’s name?”
“I won’t say his whole name,” Margaret said, proud of her discretion. “But it starts with an F. And he’s not a curate, and he’s not even a clergyman at all.”
Marianne was about to snap again, when Lady Middleton, who detested all unrefined joking of that sort, drawled out that it was raining very heavily outside. Colonel Brandon, ever quick to notice and ease other people’s discomfort, immediately picked up the subject, chatting at length about the forecast and the state of the garden paths, while Willoughby crossed the room to fling open the piano lid and drag Marianne over to play a duet with him. The awkward topic was dropped, and the moment passed—but Elinor’s heart was still racing long after everyone had moved on to other things.

That same evening, the whole group made plans for an outing the next day, to visit the grand, famous Allenham Court, sprawling twelve miles from Barton, that belonged to Colonel Brandon’s brother-in-law. The owner was travelling abroad, and would not allow visitors to tour the grounds without Brandon’s personal permission. The estate was famed for its rolling parkland and vast, glassy lake, and Sir John, who organised a trip there at least twice every summer, swore it was the most beautiful sight in all Devonshire. They would take open carriages, bring cold picnic feasts, and spend the whole day sailing on the lake and wandering the woods.
Only a few of the group thought it a poor idea, given that it had rained every single day for a fortnight. Elinor, who had already caught a lingering scratchy cold, gently persuaded her mother, who had started to come down with a chill herself, to stay home and rest rather than risk making herself ill out on the wet roads.
