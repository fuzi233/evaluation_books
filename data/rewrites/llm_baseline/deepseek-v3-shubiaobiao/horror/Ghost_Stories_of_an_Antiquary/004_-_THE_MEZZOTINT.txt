Some time ago, I recounted to you the curious and unsettling tale of my friend Dennistoun and his ill-fated pursuit of antiquities for the Cambridge museum. Though he spoke little of it upon his return, the story spread discreetly among his circle—particularly to a certain gentleman who presided over an art collection at another university.

This man, Mr. Williams, was of a practical disposition, not prone to fancies. His duties, after all, concerned the sober cataloging of English topographical engravings, not the acquisition of haunted manuscripts. Yet, as fate would have it, even the most mundane corners of scholarship may conceal darkness.

Among London’s dealers of such prints, none was more indispensable than Mr. J.W. Britnell, whose periodic catalogues were the lifeblood of collectors. Williams was no prodigal buyer—his museum’s holdings were already vast—but he perused Britnell’s lists with professional interest.

Then, in February of last year, a catalogue arrived upon his desk, accompanied by a brief note:

> *Dear Sir, We beg to call your attention to No. 978 in our accompanying catalogue, which we shall be glad to send on approval. Yours faithfully, J.W. Britnell.*

No. 978 was described thus:

> *978.—Unknown. Interesting mezzotint: View of a manor-house, early part of the century. 15 by 10 inches; black frame. £2 2s.*

Unremarkable, and the price absurd. Yet Britnell was no fool. Intrigued despite himself, Williams requested the item on approval, along with a few others, and thought little more of it.

The parcel arrived, as such things do, a day late—delivered to his college rooms on a Saturday evening. There, over tea with his golfing companion Professor Binks, Williams first examined the mezzotint.

It was mediocre work, the kind found moldering in provincial inns or neglected country houses. The engraving depicted a plain Georgian manor—three rows of sash windows, a modest portico, a lawn stretching before it. The margins bore only the initials *A.W.F. sculpsit*. On the reverse, a torn label hinted at the house’s name: *__ngley Hall*, in either Essex or Sussex.

Binks, idly turning the frame, remarked, *“Moonlight’s rather well done. And there’s a figure, isn’t there? Just at the edge.”*

Williams had not noticed it before—a dim shape, hooded, its back turned toward the house. He dismissed it as a flaw in the print.

The evening passed in harmless diversion—dinner, whist, tobacco. It was late when Williams, extinguishing his lamp, caught sight of the mezzotint again.

The figure was no longer at the edge of the lawn.

It had advanced.

Now it crawled on all fours toward the house, clad in a black shroud with a white cross upon its back.

Williams, heart hammering, locked the thing in a drawer and wrote a meticulous account of what he had seen. Sleep came fitfully.

The next morning, he summoned his colleague Nisbet as witness. When the drawer was opened, the figure was gone—but the window of the house, previously shut, now stood ajar.

*“He must have gotten in,”* Williams muttered.

They photographed the print, gathered statements, and resolved to watch it through the day. By evening, the scene had changed again: the window was closed, and the figure—now standing upright—marched across the lawn, clutching something small and limp in its arms.

Research at last revealed the truth. The house was Anningley Hall in Essex. The last heir of the Francis family had vanished as an infant in 1802. The father, an amateur engraver, died three years later, having just completed a mezzotint of the house.

And the figure?

*“Old Francis was ruthless with poachers,”* explained a fellow antiquary. *“Drove them all off the land—save one. The last of a very old family. Some say he took his revenge.”*

That night, Williams locked the mezzotint away. But he often wonders—if he were to look again, what fresh horror might he find?
