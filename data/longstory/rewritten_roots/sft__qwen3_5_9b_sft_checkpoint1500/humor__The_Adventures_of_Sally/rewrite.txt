If actors and actresses possess the same fragile constitution as children, prone to weeping at the first drop of rain, they also share the childlike ability to dance in puddles immediately after the storm passes. This fact did not escape Sally's notice, nor did it fail to astound her. Consider the sheer alchemy of lending her brother Fillmore twenty thousand dollars. In a single transaction, she had engineered a state of universal euphoria among the troupe of the Primrose Way Company. It was as if the Millennium had descended upon the theater district; the lethargy and despondency born of a week of paralysis evaporated instantly, falling away like a soaked cloak. The promotion of a certain "creature of the abyss"—the assistant stage manager—to the dizzying heights of Proprietor struck them as a plot point worthy of Shakespeare. Having once played the persecuted heroine whose fortunes turn around at eleven o'clock, they found the situation theatrically sound. Furthermore, now that Miss Hobson had departed, everyone finally admitted that her presence had been a blight, a literal cloud hovering over their prospects.

An atmosphere of unbridled optimism swept through the building. Cheerful rumors began to circulate with the speed of wildfire. Bowler-hatted Teddy received word directly from the lift-boy at his hotel that the theatrical ban would be lifted no later than Tuesday. Meanwhile, the cigar-stand girl at the Pontchartrain, a figure of considerable local authority, informed the man playing the butler that Toledo and Cleveland were set to open tomorrow night. The consensus was clear: the sun was breaking through the clouds, and Fate, weary of its hopeless task of keeping virtuous men down, would soon throw in the towel.

And so, Fillmore returned to himself. Every human being has a unique idiom for expressing elation. For Fillmore, it involved purchasing a brand-new waistcoat, acquiring a box of one hundred half-dollar cigars, and becoming excessively fussy about the composition of his luncheon. "I come here every night," Mr. Cracknell continued, tears welling in his pale eyes, "and dance past her table, but she won't look at me. What would you do about it?"

"I don't know," Sally admitted frankly.

"Nor do I," Mr. Cracknell sighed. "I thought you wouldn't, because you're sensible... I mean, nor do I. I'm making one last try tonight, if you can keep a secret. You won't tell anyone, will you?" He pleaded urgently. "I know you won't because you're sensible... I'm bringing her a little present tonight. A little gift. That ought to soften her, don't you think?"

"A bigger one would probably do the trick better."

Mr. Cracknell kicked her shin in a dismayed sort of way.

"I never thought of that. Perhaps you're right. But it's too late now. Still, it might work. Or wouldn't it? Which do you think?"

"Yes," Sally said.

"I thought as much," Mr. Cracknell replied.

The orchestra stopped with a thunderous crash, leaving Mr. Cracknell clapping feebly in the center of the floor. Sally slipped back to her table. Her former partner, after casting an uncertain, searching glance around as if he had misplaced a vital limb, zigzagged off in search of his own seat. The roar of conversation, previously drowned by the music, erupted with renewed vigor. The air was hot, thick, and heavy with voices. Pressing her hands over her closed eyes, Sally was reminded once again that she suffered from a pounding headache.

It had been nearly a month since her return to Mr. Abrahams' employment. It had been a dull, leaden existence—a monotonous succession of lifeless days where reality felt like a bad dream. In some strange, nightmare fashion, she seemed severed from her kind. Weeks had passed without seeing a familiar face. Fillmore, perhaps plagued by conscience, had not sought her out, and Ginger was busy charting his destiny on the south shore of Long Island.

Lowering her hands, Sally opened her eyes and surveyed the room. It was packed, as always. The Flower Garden was one of many establishments that had ridden the wave of New York's dancing craze to popularity. Doubtless, because its proprietor claimed it was a "nice place" run "nice," it had survived, unlike many rivals, to enjoy unvarying prosperity. Its advertisements boasted of being a "supper-club for after-theatre dining and dancing," describing it as "sumptuously appointed," "one of the town's wonder-places," featuring an "incomparable dance-floor," "enchanting music," and "service de luxe." From which one could gather, even without Isadore Abrahams' personal endorsements, that he thought highly of the enterprise.

There had been a time when Sally liked it. In her first months of employment, she found it diverting and stimulating. But those days were long gone. Back then, she never had headaches, nor this dreadful, listless depression that weighed her down, turning her nightly labor into a burden.

"Miss Nicholas."

The orchestra, rarely silent for long, started again. Lee Schoenstein, the master of ceremonies, was presenting a new partner. Sally rose mechanically.

"This is the first time I've been in this place," the man said as they bumped over the crowded floor. He was big and clumsy, of course. Tonight, it seemed to Sally, the whole world was big and clumsy. "It's a swell place. I come from up-state. We got nothing like this where I'm from." He cleared a space before him, using Sally as a battering ram, and Sally, despite enjoying her recent excursion with Mr. Cracknell, began to look back at it with wistfulness. This man was undoubtedly the worst dancer in America.

"Give me li'l old New York," the man from up-state declared unpatriotically. "It's good enough for me. I've been to some swell shows since getting to town. Seen this year's 'Follies'?"

"No."

"You go," he urged earnestly. "You go! Take it from me, it's a swell show. Seen 'Myrtle Takes a Turkish Bath'?"

"I don't go to many theatres."

"You go! It's a scream. I've been to a show every night since I got here. Regular every night. Swell shows, all of 'em, except this last one. I certainly picked a lemon tonight, alright. I was taking a chance, y'see, because it was an opening. Thought it would be something to say when I got home that I'd been to a New York opening. Set me back two-seventy-five, including tax, and I wish I'd got it in my kick right now. 'The Wild Rose,' they called it," he said satirically, as if exposing a low subterfuge. "'The Wild Rose!' It sure made me wild, alright. Two dollars seventy-five tossed away, just like that."

Something stirred in Sally's memory. Why did that title feel so familiar? Then, with a shock, she remembered. It was Gerald's new play. For some time after returning to New York, she had been haunted by the fear that stepping out of her apartment might lead to a collision with Gerald. Then she had seen a newspaper paragraph relieving her of that anxiety. Gerald was out on the road with a new play, and "The Wild Rose," she was almost certain, was indeed its name.

"Is that Gerald Foster's play?" she asked quickly.

"I don't know who wrote it," her partner replied, "but let me tell you, he's one lucky guy to get away alive. There's fellows breaking stones on the Ossining Road that's done a lot less to deserve a sentence. Wild Rose! I'll tell the world it made me go good and wild." The man from up-state, an economical soul who despised waste, spread his humorous efforts thinly but effectively. "Why, before the second act was over, the people were beating it for the exits, and if it hadn't been for someone shouting 'Women and children first,' there'd have been a panic."

Sally found herself back at her table without knowing clearly how she had gotten there.

"Miss Nicholas."

She started to rise, and realized suddenly that this was not the voice of duty calling her through the gold teeth of Mr. Schoenstein. The man who had spoken her name had seated himself beside her, talking in precise, clipped accents that were oddly familiar. The mist cleared from her eyes, and she recognized Bruce Carmyle.

"I called at your place," Mr. Carmyle said. "The hall porter told me you were here, so I ventured to follow you. I hope you do not mind? May I smoke?"

He lit a cigarette with something of an air. His fingers trembled as he raised the match, but he flattered himself that there was nothing else in his demeanor to indicate violent excitement. Bruce Carmyle's ideal was the strong man who rises superior

The crimson tide receded slowly from Sally's cheeks, and her heart, which had hammered a frantic rhythm at the mere echo of his voice, settled back into a steady, mundane beat. Once the initial shockwave had dissipated, she discovered to her own surprise that she was remarkably composed. Every time she had fantasized about this inevitable confrontation, knowing it was only a matter of time, a cold panic had seized her throat. Yet, standing now in the reality of it, the storm inside her had strangely evaporated. The chaotic events of the preceding night had drained her of the capacity for wild emotion, leaving her in a state of numb, iron-clad calm.

"Hullo, Sally!" Gerald announced.

His voice was thick, slurring over the vowels, and a goofy, vacant smile plastered itself onto his face as he swayed, propping himself against the doorframe with one hand. He wore his shirt sleeves rolled up, his collar discarded, radiating the damp, sodden aura of a man who had consumed a significant amount of spirits. His complexion was a blotchy, puffy white, surrounded by a halo of disrepute that seemed to drip from his pores.

Sally remained silent. Burdened previously by a crushing exhaustion, she now seemed to have entered a second phase, a sort of "Indian summer" of hyper-alertness where over-tired nerves sharpened into unnatural focus. She regarded him with quiet, cool, and entirely dispassionate scrutiny, as if he were a total stranger walking into her shop.

"Hullo?" Gerald repeated, the smile faltering slightly.

"What do you want?" Sally asked, her tone flat.

"Heard your voice. Saw the door open. Figured I'd drop in."

"What do you want?"

The weak grin that had been pinned to Gerald's face dissolved instantly. A single tear tracked a muddy path down his cheek. His intoxication had progressed to the maudlin stage.

"Sally... S-Sally..." He stammered, struggling with the difficult syllables. "I'm terribly miserable."

Something flickered at the back of Sally's mind, a ghost of a memory. *Has this happened before?* Then it clicked. It was simply Mr. Reginald Cracknell all over again.

"I think you had better go to bed, Gerald," she said steadily. Not a shred of him—neither his pathetic appearance nor his shameless woe—managed to pierce her armor.

"What's the use? Can't sleep. No good. Couldn't sleep. Sally, you don't know how worried I am. I see what a fool I've been."

Sally made a quick, sharp gesture, checking what she anticipated would be a belated apology for his treatment of her. She had no desire to stand there listening to Gerald weep over having ruined her life. However, it quickly became apparent that guilt was not the weight bearing down on his soul.

"I was a fool ever to try writing plays," he continued, his voice rising in a whiny crescendo. "Had a winner first time, but can't repeat. It's no good. Should have stuck to newspaper work. I'm good at that. Have to go back to it. Another frost hit tonight. No good trying anymore. Back to the old grind, damn it."

He wept softly, full of self-pity for his tragic plight.

"Very miserable," he murmured.

He stepped forward, lurched, and retreated to the safety of the doorpost. For an instant, Sally's artificial composure was pierced by a swift stab of contempt, but it vanished as quickly as it arrived, replaced once more by her suit of indifference.

"Go to bed, Gerald," she said. "You'll feel better in the morning."

Perhaps a glimmer of foresight regarding his morning prospects penetrated Gerald's muddled brain; he winced, and his demeanor darkened into a profound melancholy.

"Maybe I won't be alive in the morning," he said solemnly. "Might as well end it all. End it all!" he repeated, making a sweeping gesture that he abruptly halted as he clutched the friendly doorframe.

Sally was not in the mood for melodrama.

"Oh, go to bed," she said impatiently. The strange, frozen indifference that had gripped her began to thaw, revealing a growing reservoir of resentment—resentment against Gerald for degrading himself in public, and against herself for ever finding glamour in such a man. It humiliated her to recall how utterly she had once allowed his personality to master hers. Under the sting of this humiliation, she felt hard and pitiless. Vaguely, she sensed a curious transformation within her. Normally, the sight of suffering life stirred her immediate sympathy; but Gerald mourning the prospect of returning to a desk job made no appeal whatsoever—a fact the sufferer noted and commented upon.

"You're very unsympathetic," he complained.

"I'm sorry," Sally lied. She walked briskly to the door and shoved it open. Gerald, still clinging to his support like a man whose world foundations had just collapsed, moved out into the passage, attached to the handle. He released it and shuffled uncertainly across the hall. Finding his own door ajar, he staggered through the threshold. Watching him reach his journey's end, Sally retired to her bedroom, intending to terminate this disturbing night with a solid sleep.

Almost immediately, she changed her mind. Sleep was impossible. A feverish restlessness had taken hold. She donned a kimono and marched to the kitchen to check if her commissariat arrangements permitted a glass of hot milk.

As she recalled presenting the last of the milk to a sandy cat with a purposeful eye that had dropped through the window that morning for breakfast, her regrets for this thriftless hospitality were interrupted by a muffled *crash*.

She listened intently. The sound had originated from across the passage. Hurrying to the door, she opened it. Instantly, from behind the apartment opposite, erupted a perfect fusillade of crashes, each one seeming to her strained ears louder and more appalling than the last.

There is something about sudden, loud noises in the stillness of the night that shatters the most rigid detachment. A short while ago, Gerald, toy­ing with the idea of ending his sorrows violently, had left Sally unmoved; now, however, her mind leapt back to his words, and apprehension succeeded indifference. Undoubtedly, Gerald was in an irresponsible mood, under the influence of which he was capable of almost anything. Listening in the doorway, Sally felt a momentary spike of panic.

A brief silence followed the explosion, but as she stood there hesitating, the noise broke out again. This time it was so loud and compelling that Sally hesitated no longer. She ran across the passage and pounded on the door.

Whatever devastation was occurring within, it was plain moments later that Gerald had survived it. A dragging footstep echoed, and the door swung open. Gerald stood on the threshold, the weak smile back on his face.

"Hullo, Sally!"

At the sight of him, disreputable and clearly unscathed, Sally's brief alarm died away, replaced by the old feeling of impatient resentment. In addition to her other grievances, he had apparently frightened her unnecessarily.

"Whatever was all that noise?" she demanded.

"Noise?" Gerald considered the point, his mouth agape.

"Yes, noise," Sally snapped.

"I've been cleaning house," Gerald said with the owl-like gravity of a man just conscious that he is not wholly himself.

Sally pushed past him. The apartment she found herself in was almost an exact replica of her own, evidently decorated by Elsa Doland with a niggly, feminine care. Amateur interior decoration had always been Elsa's hobby. Even in the unpromising surroundings of Mrs. Meecher's boarding-house, Elsa had contrived a certain daintiness that Sally, lacking talent in that department, had always envied. Elsa's mind ran toward small, fragile ornaments, and she feared no over-furnishing. Pictures jostled one another on the walls; china of all descriptions cluttered little tables; there was a profusion of lamps with shades of parti-coloured glass; and plates lined the shelves.

One might say the plates were ranged and the pictures jostled, but it would be more accurate to say they had jostled and been ranged, for Sally could only reconstruct the scene by guesswork as Gerald began his "clean-up." She had walked in briskly but pulled up short, appalled by the majestic ruin awaiting her gaze. A shell bursting in the little sitting-room could hardly have created more havoc.

The psychology of a man of weak character under the influence of alcohol and disappointed ambition is not easy to plumb, for his moods shift with bewildering rapidity. Ten minutes prior, Gerald Foster had been in the grip of clammy self-pity; now, it seemed, this phase had returned. But in the interval, there had manifestly occurred a brief, adequate spasm of what appeared to be almost berserk fury. What caused it, and why it should have expended itself so abruptly, Sally was not a psychologist enough to explain; but ocular evidence convinced her of its existence. A heavy niblick, flung petulantly—or perhaps remorsefully—into a corner, revealed the weapon used.

Bleak chaos reigned everywhere. The floor was littered with every imaginable shape and size of broken glass and china. Fragments of pictures, looking as if chewed by some prehistoric beast, lay amid heaps of shattered statuettes and vases. As Sally moved slowly into the room,

Night approached with the timid hesitation of a shy guest, as though reluctant to interrupt the perfect, golden afternoon. Beyond the tree line to the west, a faint afterglow lingered like a fading whisper, while above the massive barn, a new moon hung sharp and silver, resembling a slender sickle. Stepping onto the gravel path, Sally bowed three times with solemn gravity—a superstitious charm against bad luck—and then stood still, breathing in the perfume of the evening. Life, she felt, was undeniably sweet.

The shadows, initially coy, soon settled into a determined resolve. They swept across the landscape with efficient speed, turning the sky a uniform, deep indigo speckled with quiet stars. The concrete ribbon leading to Patchogue and Babylon dissolved into obscurity, swallowed whole by the gloom. Toto, the small white woolly dog who had trotted out at Sally's heels, halted abruptly and let out a challenging squeak.

He had returned to procure provisions, the evening papers, and the yarn for the sweater Sally was currently knitting. Though invisible in the dim light, Sally heard him approach, and a warm, cozy thrill of happiness—one that had become her nightly ritual for the past year—welled up within her chest.

"Ginger!" she called.

He received a frosty greeting; Ginger, despite his professed love for canines, had never managed to extend affection to Toto. "It's wonderful what you've done for Toto, angel," Sally remarked as Ginger brushed past, evading the dog's enthusiastic leaps with practiced ease. "He's a different creature entirely."

"A bit of luck for him," Ginger replied, his tone cool.
