Durrance walked back alone to his lodging, and the streets of London—stone, soot, gaslight—fell away beneath the older, wetter country that memory carried in its mouth.

Two years ago, on a day when the air along the Lennon River tasted of peat smoke and rain-washed heather, old Dermod Eustace had sent for him by a whim that pretended to be hospitality and was in truth command. Durrance had gone unwillingly to the house in Donegal, expecting the blunt, agrarian tyranny of the father—salted talk, hard hands, a man who measured the world by fields and fences—yet it was the daughter who undid him.

Ethne had played the violin that night.

Not facing them—never facing them—because she would not let any stray gesture, any pitying blink, any approving smile, interrupt the exact thread of sound she was pulling from the strings. Her back was to the room, her shoulders still as a cook’s when the knife finds its rhythm. The fire in the grate crackled like fat spitting in a pan; outside, the river worried its stones; and between those domestic noises—those necessary, human noises—her bow drew a music that tasted of places she had never seen.

There was one piece, an overture—Melusine—whose opening had the salt-sob of waves against rock, the same note the Atlantic makes when it is hungry. It told him of long travel and strange faces; of moonlight laid like silver scales across open sea; of whispering voices from the underside of desert wind. It grew darker, deeper, as though the instrument had learned a dialect older than speech. It spoke of joys too high to be reached and griefs too immense to be complained of—griefs that carried a nobility simply because they did not bargain for mercy. It spoke of longings that no dominant language could pin down, longings that broke when translated and lost their flavour as surely as a crushed herb goes bitter.

And yet—never a note of mere complaint.

That thought, hard-won and held with care as one holds a hot dish steady, Durrance kept turning in his mind now, as he went through London: true music cannot complain. Perhaps that was why, when he rode into the Row next morning, his blue eyes looked out from his bronzed face with not a jot less of their usual friendliness. If the world meant to take from him, it would not be by teaching him to whine.

At half-past nine he waited where the lilacs and laburnums bunched at the end of the sand, their scent thick and sugary, like bruised blossoms in a bowl, and the bees working them made a small, contented industry of sound. But Harry Feversham did not come.

He did not come the next morning either, nor any morning for three weeks. Since Oxford it had been their habit—when both were in town—to meet there at that hour; and Durrance, puzzled, felt the uneasy absence of a familiar footfall in the day’s music. It was as though some key note had been removed from the park’s soundscape: the soft thud of hooves beside his, the shared cadence of talk. The city, meanwhile, grew louder with rumour—war spoken of everywhere, a rising industrial roar that flattened subtlety, until at last rumour hardened into certainty.

Then one morning Feversham kept the tryst.

Durrance had news ready on his tongue, bright as a new coin. “I told you luck might look my way. Well, she has. I go out to Egypt on General Graham’s staff. There’s talk we may run down the Red Sea to Suakin afterward.”

The lift in his voice brought a clear envy into Feversham’s eyes—an envy so plain Durrance could not miss it, and so strange it startled him into a brief, guilty pleasure. He understood it in the simplest way: a soldier wanting what another soldier had been offered.

“It is rough on you,” he said, with genuine sympathy, “that your regiment has to stay behind.”

Feversham rode beside him in silence, the leather of his saddle creaking with each shift of weight. The park was awake with early summer: birds calling in the chestnuts, distant wheels on the road like a river’s tumble, and the great city beyond making its constant, tiring thunder. At length, as they neared the chairs beneath the trees, Feversham spoke.

“That was expected. The day you dined with me I sent in my papers.”

“That night?” Durrance turned in the saddle.

“After we had gone?”

“Yes,” Feversham said, and wondered—as he often wondered now—whether corrections were meant to sting.

Durrance rode forward silently.

Feversham, feeling again that silence as a rebuke, misread it as he had misread so much lately. For Durrance’s thoughts were elsewhere, and when he spoke it was with a hearty simplicity and even a laugh.

“I remember. You gave us your reasons that night. But for the life of me I can’t help wishing that we had been going out together. When do you leave for Ireland?”

“To-night.”

“So soon?”

They turned their horses and rode westward down the long alley of trees. The morning was fresh enough to taste. The limes and chestnuts kept their early green; the May—late that year—still hung in delicate white clusters like sifted flour on branches, and the rhododendrons burned darkly beneath, red as coals under ash. Sunlight made a trembling haze over the grass. From far off, the city’s roar came and went like water over stones: not a single sound but a mass of them, a brutal mingling that swallowed the fine edges of hearing.

“It is a long time since we bathed in Sandford Lasher,” Durrance said, letting the remembered cold bite his skin.

“Or froze in the Easter vacations in the big snow-gully on Great End,” Feversham returned.

Both felt it—without naming it—that a volume in their book had come to its last page. The earlier pages were pleasant to reread; the next were unopened and might not keep their promise. So they looked backward, leaf by leaf, before putting the book away.

“You must stay with us, Jack, when you come back,” Feversham said.

Durrance had trained himself not to flinch at that “us.” He did not. If his left hand tightened on the reins, Feversham could not see it.

“If I come back,” Durrance said.

“You know my creed. I could never pity a man who died on active service. I would very much like to come by that end myself.”

It was a plain creed, spoken plainly. Not a prophecy, not a melodrama; merely a man stating that to die decently could be worth a good many years of merely existing. Even so, he feared for an instant that Feversham might hear something else in it, so he looked quickly at his friend.

He saw again that puzzling envy.

“You see there are worse things which can happen,” Durrance went on, as though to blunt the edge of any misunderstanding. “Disablement, for instance. Clever men could make a shift, perhaps, to put up with it. But what in the world should I do if I had to sit in a chair all my days? It makes me shiver to think of it.” He shook his broad shoulders as though to throw the very idea off. “Well—this is the last ride. Let us gallop.”

He let out his horse. Feversham followed. Side by side they raced down the sand, hooves striking a bright, quick percussion that rose cleanly above the distant urban din, a brief return to a sound that belonged to open air and muscle, not to engines and crowds.

At the bottom of the Row they stopped, shook hands, and—by the curtest nods—parted. Feversham rode out of the park. Durrance turned back and walked his horse up toward the seats beneath the trees.

As a boy at Southpool in Devonshire, on the wooded creek of the Salcombe estuary, he had always carried a restlessness in him like yeast in dough, working, lifting, never letting him lie flat. He would stand smelling tar and salt and wet rope, listening to the gulls’ ragged cries and the hush of tide on mud, and long to sail down the creek, out past the familiar dark woods, over the level sea toward queer outlandish countries and peoples. The restlessness had grown with him. “Guessens,” even when he inherited it with its farms and lands, stayed in his mind as a place to come home to, not a place to be contained by.

He exaggerated that restlessness now on purpose, as though by naming it he could make it do its work and carry him away from what hurt. And against it he set words of Feversham’s—words he knew were true: Ethne Eustace would hardly be happy outside Donegal. Even had things fallen out differently—so he phrased it, carefully—there might have been a clash. Perhaps it was as well that Harry Feversham was to marry Ethne, and not another than Feversham.

Thus he argued with himself until, for a moment, the world shifted.

The riders before him blurred and vanished. The ladies in coloured frocks beneath the trees dissolved into heat and light. The familiar English trees dwindled into ragged mimosas; the brown sand spread outward in a widening circle and took on the bright colour of honey; black stones began to heap themselves like coal and flash like mirrors in a ruthless sun. He was deep in anticipations of the Soudan—its glare, its thirst, its stripped-down truth—when his name was called softly in a woman’s voice.

He looked up. He was close by the rails.

“How do you do, Mrs. Adair?” he said, and stopped his horse.

Mrs. Adair gave him her hand across the rails. She was his neighbour at Southpool, a year or two older—a tall woman with thick brown hair that held many shades as if light had been kneaded into it, and a peculiar pallor to her face. At this moment the pallor had warmed; there was colour in her cheeks, as though news itself could season blood.

“I have news for you,” Durrance said. “Two special items. One—Harry Feversham is to be married.”

“To whom?” she asked quickly, too quickly.

“You should know. It was in your house in Hill Street that Harry first met her—and I introduced him. He has been improving the acquaintance in Dublin.”

Mrs. Adair already understood. The welcome in her eyes was unmistakable.

“Ethne Eustace!” she cried. “They will be married soon?”

“There is nothing to prevent it.”

“I am glad,” she said, and sighed as though a weight had been lifted from some private shelf of the heart.

“What is your second item?”

“As good as the first. I go out on General Graham’s staff.”

Mrs. Adair fell silent. Anxiety came into her eyes; the colour retreated from her cheeks as quickly as it had arrived, leaving her face pale again, a candlewick starved of air.

“You are very glad, I suppose,” she said slowly.

His answer left no room for doubt. “I should think I was. I go soon, too—and the sooner the better. I will come and dine some night, if I may, before I go.”

“My husband will be pleased to see you,” she said, and there was a chill in it, a small frost on the words.

Durrance did not notice. He had reasons of his own for making the most of this chance. He urged his enthusiasm aloud, laying it bare more for himself than for her, as a man might speak a vow into wind to hear it return to him. Mrs. Adair, to him, had always been a somewhat vague figure: handsome in a queer foreign way not uncommon along the Devon and Cornwall coasts, always well dressed, always friendly. Perhaps her chief merit in his eyes was that she had made friends with Ethne Eustace. Beyond that, his knowledge ended.

He was to learn more.

He rode away with the old regret that the fortunes of himself and his friend were, that morning, finally severed. Yet in truth—though he could not hear it yet—he had set the strands of a new rope twisting into place, a rope that would draw them together again in a strange and terrible relationship.

Mrs. Adair left the park too, and walked home very thoughtfully, as though listening for something she could not name amid the city’s many sounds.

Durrance had one week to provide his equipment and set his Devonshire affairs in order. It passed in a continuous hurry: lists, fittings, visits, signatures—life reduced to necessities packed tight. Each day his newspaper lay unfolded in his rooms, unread, like bread gone stale for want of appetite.

The general was to travel overland to Brindisi. Toward the end of July, on an evening of wind and rain, Durrance stepped from the Dover pier into the mail-boat for Calais. The air smelled of wet rope and seaweed and coal smoke; the wind worried at the boat’s rigging so that it sang and clattered, a harsh mechanical chorus. In spite of the weather and the gloom, a small crowd had gathered to give the general a send-off. When the ropes were cast off a feeble cheer rose, thin as watered soup, swallowed almost at once by the racket of wind and paddle and waves.

Durrance leaned on the bulwarks, looking back at England. He wondered idly whether this would be his last sight of it, and wished—sharply, unexpectedly—that some one of his friends had come down to see him go.

Then, for a moment, it seemed the wish was answered.

Beneath a gas-lamp on the pier he caught sight of a man, and the man had the stature and the likeness of Harry Feversham. Rain streaked the quay; the lamp’s tongue of light flickered in its glass; the wind jerked the flame’s steadiness into a nervous dance. Durrance rubbed his eyes and looked again. He could be certain only that a man stood there, and he could only vaguely distinguish the whiteness of a face.

An illusion, he told himself. A coincidence. Feversham would be in Ireland, most likely, under a clean sky in some high Donegal garden, listening to Ethne’s violin, the familiar domestic noises softened by grass and distance.

But as he turned away a lull came in the wind. The lights steadied, burning bright. For an instant the face leaped out from shadow—distinct in feature and expression—and Durrance’s breath caught as though he had swallowed cold water.

He leaned out over the side of the boat.

“Harry!” he shouted, the wonder in his voice breaking clean through the storm.

The figure under the lamp did not stir.

The wind gusted again. The lights wavered. The paddles churned; black water boiled; the mail-boat slid beyond the pier and the man under the lamp became once more a blur, a possibility, a smear of pale among wet boards.

It was an illusion, Durrance repeated. A stranger who happened to look like Harry Feversham.

It could not be Feversham—because the face he had seen, for that heartbeat of clarity, was haggard and wistful, stamped with an extraordinary misery: the face of a man cast out from among his fellows.

Durrance had been busy all week. He had clean forgotten the arrival of that telegram and the long, suspenseful perusal of it. His newspaper had lain open and neglected, its print mute as a dead language when no one speaks it.

But Harry Feversham had come to see him off.
