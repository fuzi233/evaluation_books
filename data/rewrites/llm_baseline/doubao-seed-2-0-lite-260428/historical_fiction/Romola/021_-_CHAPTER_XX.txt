It was the final week of Florence’s Carnival, and the city’s streets hummed at their fullest, loudest pitch. Masked processions wound past shopfronts, singing the raucous ballads Lorenzo the Magnificent had made a beloved, unshakable tradition decades prior; the crowd’s favourite rigoletto round dance spun across the main piazza, feet stamping on cobblestones beneath a sharp, frosty blue sky. Pranks of every stripe ran riot, from flinging sugared comfits at passersby to hurling stones – stones most of all. The boys and striplings who formed so large a part of Florentine crowds were as loud and unruly as crickets in summer tree boughs at Carnival’s height, and theirs was an ancient, unchallenged privilege: barring all thoroughfares with long poles until every traveller paid a tribute to fund the boys’ suppers and bonfires. The evening’s centrepiece entertainment was the traditional stone-throwing brawl, far from tedious in its chaos: maimings came in all degrees, and not uncommonly more than one soul was left dead in the street. Carnival’s pleasures were thus checkered, stained with grossness and barbarity so stark that a painter rendering it truly would have to hide his canvas face to the wall, save to pull it out for the grave historical work of justifying the reforming zeal later generations would dismiss as narrow-minded, unaware of the cruelty it sought to uproot.

Still, there was no shortage of innocent, picturesque merriment, the natural product of a people quick of wit, warm of spirit, sharp of sense. There was no heavy, sodden drunkenness of the northern bloods, no stealthy, southern penchant for a brawl to end in a hidden dagger thrust.

By high morning, Carnival’s revellers were still lingering over their wine and gossiping over last night’s jests, when Tito Melema strode briskly toward the Via de’ Bardi. At his side walked young Bernardo Dovizi, the sharp-eyed man who would later stare out at the world from Raphael’s portrait as Cardinal da Bibbiena, and the two talked so animatedly as they pushed through the throng along the Por’ Santa Maria that they barely glanced at the costumes and noise around them. Even so, both cast quick, wary glances over their shoulders at regular intervals. When they turned onto the Lung’ Arno leading toward Ponte Rubaconte, one of Tito’s rapid scans picked out a figure not far behind him: someone he ached not to be recognised by, not today.

His time and thoughts were fully claimed by a once-in-a-lifetime event: that evening, he and Romola were to be formally betrothed. The ceremony had been arranged on far shorter notice than originally planned: though they had been slowly readying the rooms in Bardo’s home, furnishing everything but the long neglected library, it had been agreed to wait both the betrothal and the wedding until after Easter, when the year of probation Bernardo del Nero had insisted on for Tito would be over. But then word had come from Cardinal Giovanni, summoning Tito to Rome to help Dovizi organise his new library, leveraging his unmatched fluency in ancient Greek – an opportunity far too good to turn down. Tito had begged the elders to move the betrothal forward, to bind him and Romola to each other before he left: the sooner the wedding could happen when he returned, and the less painful their parting would be, if they had exchanged rings and signed formal contracts that made their bond more than half legal, impossible for Bernardo del Nero or anyone else to nullify. Romola had shared his eagerness, and the elders had consented. Now Tito was snatching a brief morning visit before he turned to the chaos of packing for his departure the next day – and the last thing he could afford was to be detained by the wrong person.

That person was unmistakably Tessa. He caught sight of her timid, forlorn little figure drifting toward the same turn on the Arno bank, and knew in an instant she had seen him, that she would follow. There was no way to slip away from her on this straight road to the bridge, not unless he kept Dovizi at his side the whole way. She would not dare speak or approach him while he had company, he told himself as he quickened his pace, rambling on with new threads of conversation until he walked his companion all the way to Bardo’s front steps, pulled off a hasty “addio” without turning his face anywhere Tessa might be standing. As he climbed the stone steps, he tried to shake the unpleasant thought away, telling himself Tessa might not have seen him after all, or might have lost him in the crowd.

But when his morning visit with Romola was over, he stepped out the door so quickly he barely glanced left or right – and his eyes, without his choosing, picked out the small figure in a white hood lingering right beside the threshold. Before he could move, a hand landed on his arm, not a timid little touch, but a desperate, firm grasp: Tessa had seen him break into a run and lunged forward. When he turned to her, her face was white and frightened, as if she already feared she had angered him.

“Tessa,” Tito said, his voice sharper than she had ever heard it. “Why are you here? You must not follow me, you must not loiter outside doorways waiting for me.”

Her blue eyes flooded with tears, and she said nothing. Tito’s stomach twisted with fear of being seen: this quiet, residential street was no bustling marketplace, and Bernardo del Nero, or any other dangerous onlooker who knew of his betrothal plans, might turn the corner at any moment. It was far worse than awkward, not least on this of all days. But he could not bring himself to drive her off with cruel words, not to this soft, silly little contadina he had stumbled into a foolish, hidden tryst with months ago, never expecting it would sprout into this inconvenient, unseasonable tangle of trouble.

“Tessa, I cannot talk to you here,” he said hurriedly, softening his tone to avoid upsetting her further. “I will walk on to the bridge and wait for you there. Follow me slowly.”

He strode fast to Ponte Rubaconte, leaned against the quaint little stone house built into the bridge’s side, and watched for her coming. Even Tito’s hard heart would have softened at the sight of her, her round face paled and sadder than the day he had last parted with her at the door of the Nunziata. The bridge was nearly empty, the quietest in Florence, no one else nearby to see them.

“Tessa, I have very little time,” he said the moment she drew near. “Do not cry. Why did you follow me? You must never do this again.”

“I thought you would come back to take care of me long before this,” she whispered, fighting back a sob that rose unbidden at the new sharp edge to his voice. “My stepfather beats me. I can’t bear it any more. Every holiday I have, I walk the whole city trying to find you, and I never can. Please don’t send me away again. The days are so long, I don’t care about the goats and the kids any more, I just –”

Her sobs broke loose, great fat tears rolling down her cheeks. Tito pulled her close to soothe her, knowing he had to send her away but not cruel enough to break her heart entirely. He could not weigh distant consequences, not when the moment pressed so sharp.

“My little Tessa,” he said, falling back into the old soft, caressing tone she remembered. “Bear with your stepfather just a little longer. I am going away to Rome, a long journey, but I will be back in a few weeks, and I promise I will come see you. Promise me you will be good, and wait for me.”

The familiar sound of his voice was enough to half soothe her. She looked up at him, tears glistening on her lashes, trying and failing to stop sobbing. “Yes,” she whispered. “But you won’t be long?”

“Not long at all. But I must go now. And remember: no one can ever know you see me, or you will lose me forever. Go straight home when I leave you, and never follow me again. Wait for me to come to you. Goodbye, my little Tessa. I will come back.”

He turned and walked away without looking back, no time to spare to see how she took his departure. When he finally glanced over his shoulder he was already in the Via de’ Benci, far past the bridge, and he knew Tessa, so trusting and obedient, would do exactly as he had been told. For that day, at least, the trouble was over. The faint, distant necessity of hurting Tessa later lingered in his mind no heavier than a far-off cry from a small suffering animal buried in the thicket, unnoticeable to a merry cavalcade trotting across sunlit plains, too busy looking at the bright path ahead.

By the time he was hastening back across Ponte Rubaconte for the second time that day, the memory of Tessa did not dim his happiness at all. He pulled his mantle tight around him, not so much to ward off the cold as to hide his fine, dainty clothes from drawing extra notice, and leapt up Bardo’s stone steps two at a time.

“Where is the damigella?” he asked Maso, the servant who met him at the door.

“In the library,” Maso said. “She’s all ready. Monna Brigida and Messer Bernardo are already there with Ser Braccio, none of the other guests have arrived yet.”

“Ask her to meet me in the salotto for a few minutes alone.”

The room he stepped into was a vivid contrast to the pale, muted half-light of the library. Its walls were frescoed bright with playful scenes of nymphs and cherubs dancing under blue skies, wading through wild flowers and chasing birds. Red leather benches lined the walls, a single great oak table sat in the centre, and the only other furnishings were two tall white ceramic vases and a small marble faun playing the flute, carved by a promising young artist named Michelangelo Buonarotti. It felt like stepping out into open sun. Tito kept his mantle wrapped around him, his eyes fixed on the door, until Romola stepped through.

She looked like a tall lily come to life, all white silk and rippling gold: her dress cinched at the waist with a golden girdle strung with heavy tassels, her long hair spilling in waves down her back beneath a wispy white veil caught at her brow with a band of pearls, Bernardo del Nero’s betrothal gift. The veil fell back from her face, floating behind her.

*Regina mia* – my queen, Tito breathed, taking her hand to kiss it, still wrapped in his mantle. He stepped back to stare at her, and she stood there, calm and glowing, revelling in the warmth of his admiring gaze.

“Romola,” he said, remembering how little time they had, “you said you would show me the next room once you had finished arranging it. Can we go there now?”

She led him without a word into the narrow long room beside the salotto, frescoed as brightly, this time only with birds and wild flowers. The furniture was old, faded relics of feminine use and ornament arranged in an open oak cabinet between the two narrow windows, the portrait of Romola’s mother hanging above it, and the small plain crucifix she had brought home from San Marco sitting on the cabinet’s top edge.

“I have something hidden under my mantle,” Tito said, smiling as he threw the heavy garment off, revealing a small painted triptych tabernacle, the work of Piero di Cosimo. The eccentric painter had carried out Tito’s plan perfectly, making up for all his long delays. “Do you know what this is for, my Romola? It is a little shrine, to hide that sad old remembrancer away from you forever. You are done with sadness now. We will bury every memory of it here, in a tomb of joy.”

A faint, soft tremor crossed Romola’s face as Tito reached for the crucifix, but she did not stop him. She had long wanted to banish the lingering, unquiet shadows of old memories that still flitted across her new happiness, unanswerable questions she did not want to face. He opened the triptych, set the crucifix in its central niche, snapped the little lock shut, pulled the key out, and set the painted tabernacle in the exact spot the crucifix had stood.

“Look,” he said, turning her to face it. “See the portraits old Piero made of us? Am I not the purple-gowned Bacchus, crowning you Ariadne? It is perfect, is it not?”

“Ah, it is you,” Romola said, her eyes glistening with happy tears. “You have crowned my poor quiet life, Tito. That is exactly true.”

Their hands laced together as they stared at the painted scenes: the ship gliding across a calm blue sea, the never-wilting ivy winding around its frame, cherubs who had laid down their sharp arrows to shower soft rose petals over the pair, the once fierce tigers and leopards that represented all her old troubles, now tamed and sleeping at their feet. Even the strange sea monsters with their merry grins, Tito teased, were once the dull, heavy passages in old books that had become fun to read now that they could sit side by side.

“Tito mio,” Romola laughed, half teasing, and held out her hand. “Give me the key, won’t you?”

“Not a chance,” Tito said playfully, tucking the small key into his scarsella pouch at his belt. “I’m going to throw it in the Arno.”

“But what if I ever want to look at the crucifix again?”

“Exactly why we hide it,” he said, pressing a soft kiss to her brow. “Hidden away behind all these images of youth and joy.”

Romola did not argue. Strong souls submitted easily when they felt no real reason to resist. Together they joined the waiting company, forming a small dignified procession walking across Ponte Rubaconte toward Santa Croce. They moved slowly: Bardo, who had barely left his house in years, walked with unsteady, timid steps, and his pace suited the gouty, stately gait of Messer Bartolommeo Scala, who had come to grace the occasion alongside his daughter Alessandra. Once, the sumptuary laws of Florence had been forced to cap betrothal guest lists at four hundred, two hundred per family, because the feasts after both the formal *sposalizio* and the later wedding *nozze* had grown ruinously expensive, even the scholar Leonardo Bruno complaining his own betrothal had nearly beggared him. But Bardo, proud and poor, had refused to invite guests on the strength of name or status, and the small group of twenty following the couple were all close personal friends, no one else. Bernardo del Nero walked ahead as their vanguard, Bardo led by Tito on his right arm and Romola on his left. Bardo had been married at Santa Croce decades before, and had insisted Romola be betrothed and wed there rather than the tiny nearby church of Santa Lucia: he held a perfect mental image of the grand stone basilica, and hoped to one day be buried there, among the Florentines who had served their city well.

The short walk lay far from the noisiest Carnival chaos, and the red glow of the setting sun gilded their path, casting soft warm light over the small, solemn procession with the blind old father at its centre. By the time the betrothal ceremony was complete, the rings glinting gold on Tito and Romola’s fingers, dusk had fallen, dim struggling starlight overhead, and the servants lit their torches to light their way out.

As they stepped onto the broad church steps, a strange, dreary Miserere chant drifted to their ears. Down at the far end of the piazza, a crowd was surging toward them from the Borgo de’ Greci, pushed forward by something coming up the street.

“Only one of their masqued processions,” Tito said, staying close at Romola’s side while Bernardo del Nero shepherded Bardo ahead.

Slowly, the spectacle came into view, towering far above the heads of the onlookers: a huge, ghastly painted effigy of Winged Time, his scythe raised, his hourglass in his hand, surrounded by his winged children the Hours. He rode on a great high cart draped entirely in black, the bullocks pulling it swathed in black cloth too, only their white horns poking up above the gloom, so that to onlookers in the darkening shadow of the houses, it looked as if Time and his ghostly family were floating through the air. Behind the cart, a troop of sheeted, robed figures glided forward, chanting their wailing dirge.

A cold shudder of horror seized Romola. For a split second, it felt as if her dead brother’s old, unshakable apocalyptic vision from San Marco was coming true. She clung tight to Tito’s arm.

“What grim foolishness your Florentines love,” he said, laughing soft to calm her. “This is no doubt Piero di Cosimo’s work, that odd man who delights in this kind of grim merriment.”

“Tito, I wish it had never happened,” Romola whispered. “It deepens those old images from that vision I have tried so hard to forget.”

“Nay, my Ariadne,” Tito said, pressing her gloved hand tight. “You will not look at that ghastly mummery. I locked all sadness away from you, remember? Now you only look forward, to Easter, when you will triumph over every last trace of sorrow, with me.”
