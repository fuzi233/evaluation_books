Morning came as if it had been kneaded into me with firmer hands. The faintness of the night before loosened its grip; my thoughts rose, clear and ready, like yeast that has at last found warmth. I had just set my collar straight when a tap sounded; I called “Come in,” expecting a maid with water or linen. Instead, a rough fellow shouldered through the door as though it were a stable-gate and demanded, “Gif me your keys, Meess.”

“Why?” I asked, my fingers tightening on the bit of metal as if it were a talisman.

“Gif!” he repeated, with the impatience of a man who thinks obedience the only language worth learning; and he half-snatched them, adding, “All right! haf your tronc soon.”

He proved—by accident rather than courtesy—to be from the custom-house; the business, though ill-mannered, ended honestly enough. Yet the immediate question remained, sharp as hunger: where was breakfast to be had? I could not have told. Still, I went down; and, descending slowly—pausing on each step with a reluctance I did not confess—I noticed what fatigue had hidden from me: this was no mere roadside inn but a great hotel, built to swallow crowds and coin. Light poured through wide windows; painted walls tried to look festive; marble stairs lay underfoot, uncarpeted, cold, veined like fat through meat, and not overly clean.

All this splendour, set against the cramped closet they had given me for a chamber, brought on reflections I could not entirely stifle. Servants everywhere—waiters, chambermaids, ship-stewardesses—had a talent like experienced market-women: one glance and they weighed you, not in pounds but in consequence; they priced you by your gait, your glove, the meekness of your luggage. They had already set me down as a fractional thing, of no social importance and little cash. I saw it plainly; I did not deny it; I tried, as one tries to swallow coarse bread, to manage it without choking.

At last I reached a hall bright with skylight glare and found, by uncertain drifting, what proved to be the coffee-room. Entering it, I felt my courage shake as a thin cup trembles on a saucer. I was solitary, unsure, ashamed of my own ignorance, and convinced I was doing wrong—yet unable to stop, as if my feet belonged to some other will. I sat at a small table; a waiter brought breakfast; and I ate with little appetite for anything except safety.

The room held many other tables. All were occupied by men. If there had been a woman—any woman—I think I should have breathed more freely; but there was not one. Still, no one made a spectacle of me. A few glanced, no one stared. Perhaps what they excused with a shrug and a single label was this: “Anglaise!”

When breakfast was finished, I had to move again. But in which direction? Then an inward voice—thin as a remembered tune, yet persistent—spoke: Go to Villette. It was prompted by nothing grand: only a sentence Miss Fanshawe had tossed to me at parting, careless as a crumb offered to a bird.

“I wish you would come to Madame Beck’s; she has some marmots whom you might look after; she wants an English gouvernante, or was wanting one two months ago.”

Who Madame Beck was, where she lived, what kind of house she kept—these I did not know. I had tried to ask; the question fell unheard in that bustle of farewells, and she was gone. But I presumed Villette to be her city; and to Villette I resolved to go.

The distance was forty miles. I knew I was clinging to threads; yet when one is drowning, even a spider’s web looks like mercy. I asked about the diligence, secured a seat, and departed on the strength of a shadow.

If you are minded to call it rash, look first where I had stood: in a desert of prospects, with nothing dear to risk. Mine was the game where the player cannot lose and may win.

Though I deny myself an artistic temperament, I have at least that faculty of the artist—to make the most of present pleasure when it comes in a shape I can relish. I relished that day, even travelling slowly, even in cold rain. The country we crossed was bare, flat, and treeless; canals lay alongside the road like torpid green snakes, slick with slime; pollard willows stood in formal ranks; fields were trimmed and tilled as neatly as kitchen-garden beds. Above, the sky was one unbroken grey lid; the air was humid and still. Yet inside me fancy budded, and my heart, by some obstinate miracle, basked as if in sunshine.

All the while, however, anxiety crouched close, a beast waiting for dusk. I heard it in the continual undertone of my thoughts, as one hears, in a too-noisy town, the grinding of wheels even after the street is empty. It waited only for night to spring.

I had hoped to reach Villette before dark—to arrive with daylight’s decency about me. But our progress was slow; our stoppages long. Fog thickened; rain came fine and dense. By the time we reached the suburbs, darkness had settled on the city with a weight you could almost feel on your skin.

I remember passing through a gate where soldiers stood; lamplight showed their forms and bayonets. Then we left the miry chaussée and rattled over a pavement rough and flinty, the wheels biting and clattering as if the very stones protested our coming. In that harsh sound I felt the city before I saw it: a new acoustic world—echoing, metallic, crowded with foreign consonants.

At a bureau the diligence stopped; passengers alighted. My first concern was my trunk—a small matter in appearance, yet to me the difference between standing and falling. I knew enough of travel to understand that eagerness is punished: one must not pester but watch quietly, and when one’s own appears, claim it promptly.

So I stood apart, my eyes fixed on the portion of the roof where I had seen my little portmanteau stowed, and where other bags and boxes now lay piled. One by one they were removed—lowered, seized, carried off. Mine should have shown itself. It did not.

I had tied its direction-card with green ribbon so I might know it at a glance. No green fringe appeared. The last package went. The oilcloth cover was lifted. I saw clearly that nothing remained—not umbrella, cloak, cane, hat-box, band-box—nothing. And my portmanteau, with my few clothes and the little pocket-book clasping the remnant of my fifteen pounds—where was it?

I ask the question now; then I could not. I possessed no phrase of speaking French. Around me, the whole world seemed to gabble in French—French only—quick, heedless, absolute, a dominant lexicon with no doorway left for my tongue. The sound of it pressed on me like machinery; I felt my helplessness as keenly as hunger.

What was I to do?

I went to the conductor, laid a hand on his arm, pointed to a trunk, then to the diligence roof, and tried to form a question with my eyes. He misunderstood, seized the trunk I indicated, and was about to hoist it back up.

“Let that alone—will you?” said a voice in good English; then, correcting himself into French, “Qu’est-ce que vous faîtes donc? Cette malle est à moi.”

Those Fatherland accents—English struck through with home—were as restoring as warm broth to a chilled stomach. I turned, and without even noticing what sort of face I appealed to, said, “Sir—I cannot speak French. May I entreat you to ask this man what he has done with my trunk?”

There was a half-surprise, half-doubt in the stranger’s expression, as if weighing whether interference would be wise; but his tone, when he spoke, was neither hard nor terrifying. “What sort of trunk was yours?”

I described it, including the green ribbon; and at once he took the conductor “under hand,” and though the ensuing French storm meant nothing to my understanding, I knew—by his firm cadence, by the quick thrust and return of his speech—that he was raking the fellow fore and aft.

Presently he came back. “The man says he was overloaded, and confesses he removed your trunk after you saw it put on, and left it behind at Boue-Marine with other parcels. He promises to forward it to-morrow; the day after, therefore, you will find it safe at this bureau.”

“Thank you,” I said; but my heart sank as if a weight had been dropped into it. Meantime, what was I to do—tonight, in this city whose very street-sounds were foreign?

Perhaps he read the failure of courage in my face. He asked, kindly, “Have you any friends in this city?”

“No—and I don’t know where to go.”

In the pause that followed, he turned more fully beneath a lamp, and I saw him: young, distinguished, handsome—made by nature good enough for a prince, I thought, though I knew nothing of rank. He looked high but not arrogant; manly but not overbearing; pleasant, as if honour sat naturally in his features.

The consciousness of my own insignificance urged me to withdraw. But he stopped me with another question. “Was all your money in your trunk?”

How thankful I was to answer truthfully: “No. I have enough in my purse”—for I had near twenty francs—“to keep me at a quiet inn till the day after to-morrow; but I am quite a stranger in Villette, and don’t know the streets and the inns.”

“I can give you the address of such an inn as you want,” he said; “and it is not far off. With my direction you will easily find it.”

He tore a leaf from his pocket-book, wrote a few words, and put it into my hand. Distrust did not so much as occur to me; I might as soon have distrusted Scripture. There was goodness in his countenance and a clean, bright steadiness in his eyes.

“Your shortest way will be to follow the Boulevard and cross the park,” he added; “but it is too late and too dark for a woman to go through the park alone. I will step with you thus far.”

He moved on; I followed him into the darkness and the small soaking rain. The Boulevard lay deserted; its path was mire; trees dripped steadily like eaves. The park beyond was black as midnight. In the doubled gloom of fog and branches I could not see my guide; I could only follow his tread—sure, frank, unhesitating. Not the least fear had I. I believe I would have followed that tread through continual night to the world’s end.

When the park was behind us, he stopped. “Now you will go along this broad street till you come to steps; two lamps will show you where they are. Descend those steps. A narrower street lies below; follow it, and at the bottom you will find your inn. They speak English there, so your difficulties are now pretty well over. Good-night.”

“Good-night, sir. Accept my sincerest thanks.”

We parted. Long after, the remembrance of his face—lit, I am sure, with something not unbenignant to the friendless—and the sound of his voice, which spoke a chivalric nature to the needy and feeble as well as the youthful and fair, remained a cordial within me.

I hurried on through a magnificent street and square lined with grand houses; among them loomed huge outlines of overbearing piles—palace or church, I could not tell. The city’s noises rose and fell: the hard ring of hooves, the rasp of wheels, distant calls, doors clapping—an urban chorus that did not know how to soften itself for strangers. It struck me how sound can be a kind of authority: it orders your steps, shortens your breath, tells you whether you belong.

As I passed a portico, two mustachioed men slipped suddenly from behind the pillars, smoking cigars. Their dress had pretensions; their souls did not. They spoke with insolence; and though I walked fast, they kept pace, matching me stride for stride, their laughter and remarks snapping at my heels like dogs.

At last I met a sort of patrol; the hunters turned aside. But they had driven me beyond my reckoning. When I could collect my faculties, I no longer knew where I was. The staircase I had been told to find—the steps beneath two lamps—I must have passed long since. Puzzled, breathless, with every pulse throbbing in inevitable agitation, I stood amid wet stone and foreign sound and did not know where to turn.

To retrace my way was dreadful; I could not bear the thought of meeting those bearded, sneering simpletons again; yet I had no choice. I went back, searching for the steps, scanning each lamp as if it might be a beacon.

At last I came to an old, worn flight, and, taking it for granted that this must be the one indicated, I descended. The street it led to was indeed narrow—but it held no inn. On I wandered, the rain chilling my wrists and throat; the air smelling of damp stone and smoke. My knees trembled now; exhaustion came on like a heavy apron tied too tightly.

In a very quiet, comparatively clean and well-paved street, I saw a light burning over the door of a rather large house, loftier by a story than those around it. This, I thought—surely this at last must be the inn. I hastened forward.

It was no inn.

A brass-plate shone on the great porte-cochère. “Pensionnat de Demoiselles,” it read; and beneath, a name: “Madame Beck.”

I stopped short. A hundred thoughts volleyed through me at once—Miss Fanshawe’s careless phrase, my present helplessness, the lost trunk, the foreign city, the predatory men. Yet I planned nothing; I considered nothing. There was no time. Providence seemed to say, Stop here; this is your inn. Fate took me in a strong hand, mastered my will, directed my actions. I rang the bell.

While I waited, I would not reflect. I fixed my eyes on the street-stones where the door-lamp shone and counted them, noting the shapes, the wet glitter on their edges—clinging to these small, exact details as one clings to the taste of bread when the rest of life is uncertain.

I rang again. At last they opened.

A bonne in a smart cap stood before me. “May I see Madame Beck?” I asked.

Had I spoken French, she might not have admitted me; but hearing English, she presumed I was some foreign teacher come on business connected with the pensionnat, and even at that late hour let me in without reluctance.

The next moment I sat in a cold, glittering salon with a porcelain stove unlit, gilded ornaments, and a polished floor that threw back the lamplight without warmth. A pendule on the mantel struck nine. A quarter-hour passed. My heart beat so fast I felt it in my throat; my skin turned cold then hot by turns. I watched the great white folding-doors with gilt mouldings, waiting for one leaf to move.

All had been still—not a mouse stirred—when suddenly a voice spoke close at my elbow.

“You ayre Engliss?”

I almost bounded. Yet no ghost stood there—only a motherly, dumpy little woman in a large shawl, a wrapping-gown, and a clean, trim nightcap. She had entered by a small door behind me and approached in shoes of silence.

I said I was English. That was the extent of her insular speech; then she began at once to work away volubly in her own tongue. I answered in mine. She understood a little; I understood nothing; and between her torrent and my fragments we made an awful clamour and achieved scant progress.

At length she rang for aid. It arrived in the shape of a maîtresse partly educated in an Irish convent and considered a perfect adept in English. She was a bluff little person—Labassecourienne from top to toe—and she slaughtered the speech of Albion; but we could understand each other well enough.

I told her my plain tale for translation: that I had left my country to extend my knowledge and gain my bread; that I was ready to turn my hand to any useful thing not wrong or degrading; that I would be nurse to a child or maid to a lady, nor refuse even housework suited to my strength.

Madame Beck listened. I watched her face for pity or interest; I found neither. No gleam of sympathy crossed it; no shade of compassion softened it. She was not a woman to be moved an inch by feeling: grave and considerate, she seemed to consult only her judgment, weighing my narrative as a cook weighs flour—without malice, without mercy, intent on proportion and result.

“Il n’y a que les Anglaises pour ces sortes d’entreprises,” she said; “sont-elles donc intrépides ces femmes là!”

She asked my name, my age. She sat and looked at me as at an object to be placed in a household economy. A bell rang.

“Voilà pour la prière du soir!” she said, and rose.

Through the interpreter she desired me to depart now and come back on the morrow. But the idea of going out again—into that wet, lonesome, hostile street—was more than I could bear. I gathered what energy remained and spoke, addressing Madame herself, not the maîtresse—speaking firmly, yet with control, as one who bargains not for luxury but for shelter.

“Be assured, madame, that by instantly securing my services, your interests will be served and not injured: you will find me one who will wish to give, in her labour, a full equivalent for her wages; and if you hire me, it will be better that I should stay here this night. Having no acquaintance in Villette, and not possessing the language of the country, how can I secure a lodging?”

“It is true,” she said; “but at least you can give a reference?”

“None.”

She inquired after my luggage. I told her when it would arrive. She mused.

At that moment, a man’s step sounded in the vestibule, hasty, passing toward the outer door. Madame listened. “Who goes out now?” she demanded.

“M. Paul,” replied the teacher. “He came this evening to give a reading to the first class.”

“The very man I should at this moment most wish to see. Call him.”

The teacher ran; M. Paul was summoned and entered: a small, dark, spare man in spectacles.

“Mon cousin,” began Madame, “I want your opinion. We know your skill in physiognomy; use it now. Read that countenance.”

He fixed on me his spectacles as if they were instruments meant to pierce rather than observe. His mouth compressed; his brow gathered; everything in his look said he intended to see through me, and that no veil would serve.

“I read it,” he pronounced.

“Et qu’en dites vous?”

“Mais—bien des choses,” he answered, oracularly.

“Bad or good?”

“Of each kind, without doubt.”

“May one trust her word?”

“Are you negotiating a matter of importance?”

“She wishes me to engage her as bonne or gouvernante; tells a tale full of integrity, but gives no reference.”

“She is a stranger?”

“An Englishwoman, as one may see.”

“She speaks French?”

“Not a word.”

“She understands it?”

“No.”

“One may then speak plainly in her presence?”

“Doubtless.”

He gazed steadily, while my ignorance sat beside me like a gag. It struck me—bitterly, clearly—how language can strip a person of power more completely than poverty: you may be discussed, judged, accepted or refused, and have no right even to interrupt.

“Do you need her services?” he asked.

“I could do with them. You know I am disgusted with Madame Svini.”

Still he scrutinized. At last his verdict came, vague as all that had preceded it. “Engage her. If good predominates in that nature, the action will bring its own reward; if evil—eh bien! ma cousine, ce sera toujours une bonne œuvre.”

And with a bow and a “bon soir,” this uncertain arbiter of my destiny vanished.

Madame Beck engaged me that very night. By God’s blessing, I was spared the necessity of passing forth again into the wet and foreign dark, where every sound had seemed to say: you do not belong.
