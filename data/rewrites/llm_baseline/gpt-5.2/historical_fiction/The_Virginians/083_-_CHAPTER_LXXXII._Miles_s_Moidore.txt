Little Miles came into the world within a few days of that gracious Prince whose name is shouted by cannon and echoed by bells. For the royal infant there were illuminations that made the night a second noon; salutes that shook windows and ribbed the air with thunder; and a tide of people admitted, as if to a rare show, to gaze upon him lying behind his gilt railing, with noble nurses stationed like sentries at a shrine.

For our child there was no gilded gate, no courtly hush, no train of retainers. Yet there was a kind of ceremony in our narrow rooms in Church Street, Lambeth, if one has an ear for such things: the soft creak of the stair under Molly’s careful foot; Gumbo’s low humming as he made up a fire; the small domestic orchestra of spoon on earthenware, kettle-lid trembling, linen shaken out and laid smooth. The street had its own note too—the clop of carts, the call of hawkers, a baby’s cry answered by another from some neighbouring window—and, for all its poverty, it was a kinder sound than the grand noise of celebration made to flatter power. Here, in our “trou,” as my aunt named it, life went on in the honest key: the breath of coal-smoke, the damp of the river, and the warm, milky smell that filled Theo’s chamber like a promise.

Why was our boy not called George, like his father, like the young paragon at St. James’s? I had meant it so at first. But I had known, in those months, a scantness of affection from my own blood; and a little scapegrace of that same blood—one small kinsman with a brave heart—had given us what kindness could. Theo and I, agreeing without much debate, chose to set that kindness into the child’s name, as folk set a coin into a foundation-stone: not for show, but to be there, hidden and holding.

We wrote to our royal parents with due formality to announce the birth; and, with what courage we could muster, inserted it likewise in the Daily Advertiser, even naming our place—Church Street, Lambeth—as if it were a parish in a map and not a mark of our fallen fortunes. “My dear,” wrote Aunt Bernstein in reply, “how could you point out to all the world that you live in such a trou as that in which you have buried yourself? I kiss the little mamma, and send a remembrance for the child.” Her remembrance arrived very grandly: a fine silk coverlid with lace edging fit for a prince. It lay over our firstborn’s cradle like a bit of court embroidery dropped by mistake into a poor man’s house. Useful? Not much. The price of that lace, turned into honest loaves and broth-meat, would have served us better. Yet Theo’s eyes brightened, and Molly’s too; for women, who know what it is to keep a home together by the small economies of the day, have also a tender weakness for anything that makes their baby seem protected, honoured, splendid—even if only by borrowed finery.

Good Dr. Heberden came more than once to see my wife, and to make sure all went as it should. He recommended us a surgeon in the neighbourhood; but by God’s mercy Theo needed little beyond the care our landlady could supply, and Molly, and Gumbo’s patient attendance. Gumbo’s help had its own flavour and comfort. He could turn the scantest stores into something that smelled like plenty: water warmed with a twist of peel, if peel could be had; toast dried crisp at the edge of the grate; a bit of broth coaxed from bones and a few herbs, simmered till the room itself seemed to soften. The sound of it—quiet bubbling, a ladle tapping the pot—was as healing as the thing itself.

Our humble precinct was presently adorned by a blaze of wheels and gilding: Lady Castlewood’s chariot rolled to our little gate as if it had taken a wrong turning from a drawing-room. Her ladyship brought a pot of jelly, which she thought Theo might fancy; and, no doubt, it had already figured upon a banquet-table, quivering in a silver dish under chandeliers, before it was sent on to us as a charitable after-thought.

She stayed long enough to tell us of court ceremonies and the splendour attending the birth of the august heir. While she spoke, the air about her seemed to carry with it the scent of polished leather and perfume, and the faint metallic ring of her equipage outside—little sounds of wealth that make themselves heard even when one is trying to listen only to the needs of a newborn.

Mr. Johnson happened to visit me on one of those days when my lady countess’s carriage flamed up to our gate. He was struck by her magnificence, and made bows more respectful than graceful. She called me cousin with much affability, and—benign, condescending—helped to transfer her present from the silver dish into our crockery pan; for we could not afford to keep fine things merely because they were fine.

The Doctor tasted the sweetmeat and pronounced it excellent. “The great, sir,” said he, “are fortunate in every way. They can engage the most skilful practitioners of the culinary art, as they can assemble the most amiable wits round their table. If, as you think, sir, and, from the appearance of the dish, your suggestion at least is plausible, this sweetmeat may have appeared already at his lordship’s table; it has been there in good company. It has quivered under the eyes of celebrated beauties; it has been tasted by ruby lips; it has divided the attention of the distinguished company with fruits, tarts, and creams, which I make no doubt were like itself delicious.” And with that, the good Doctor absorbed a considerable portion of Lady Castlewood’s benefaction.

As for the epithet delicious, I must speak truth. Theo, after one small taste, put it away from her with a faint shiver; it clung to the tongue with that cloyed richness which, in sickness and weakness, is a burden rather than a pleasure. Molly, lifting her head with the sharp authority of a woman who has kept pantries and knows by smell what is safe, declared it mouldy. I do not know whether it was the jelly’s fault or the journey’s—or whether our poor room, with its coal air and damp, made all fine things seem a little stale. Yet the scene itself had its meaning: what the great throw away as garnish, the small must judge, accept, refuse, or make do with.

My boy had at least the privilege—so the world would say—of having an earl’s daughter for his godmother. That office was performed by his cousin, our poor Lady Maria, whose kindness to mother and infant went beyond praise. Having lost her own solitary chance for happiness of that sort, she yearned over our child with a tenderness that was painful to see, as if each soft touch were a remembrance and a renunciation together.

Captain Miles is now a mighty fine gentleman, his uniform in the Prince’s Hussars as splendid as any that ever bedizened a soldier of fashion. But he has too good a heart—let us trust—to forget, when he recalls his own infancy, that his limbs were once dressed in little garments prepared for a poor player’s child. The finery travels strange roads in this world: lace from a rich aunt; baby-linen from a humble home; a silver dish emptied into a crockery pan. So does kindness.

Sampson christened our boy in that very chapel in Southwark where our marriage had been performed. Never were the Prayer-book words read more beautifully, more impressively, than by the celebrant that day—until the end, when his voice failed him, and he and the little congregation were forced to wipe their eyes. Even the building seemed to listen: the hollow hush under the roof; the faint stir of clothes; the small cough that someone tried to swallow; then the child’s thin wail, cutting through the solemn cadence like a bird-call in a wood.

“Mr. Garrick himself, sir,” said Hagan, “could not have read those words so nobly. I am sure little innocent never entered the world accompanied by wishes and benedictions more tender and sincere.”

And now I have not told how it chanced the Captain came by his name of Miles. A couple of days before the christening—when I believe we had still meant to call our firstborn by his father’s name—there came a little patter of horse’s hoofs up to our gate, quick and eager as a child’s drumming on a table. Who should tug at the bell but young Miles, our cousin? I fear he had disobeyed his parents to make that undutiful journey; but he sat his little horse with the proud stiffness of a boy who has been given a kingdom the size of a saddle.

“You know,” says he, breathless with riding and excitement, “cousin Harry gave me my little horse; and I can’t help liking you, because you are so like Harry, and because they’re always saying things of you at home, and it’s a shame; and I have brought my whistle and coral that my godmamma Lady Suckling gave me, for your little boy; and if you’re so poor, cousin George, here’s my gold moidore, and it’s worth ever so much, and it’s no use to me, because I mayn’t spend it, you know.”

The child spoke in his own young fashion, with that plain, headlong honesty that needs no polish; and in it there was something that none of our grander relatives—so careful of names and precedence and the proper words—had given us: the unbought sound of loyalty. Even his boots contributed to the message. He was proud of them, and made them heard on our stair as he came up—a small soldier’s march in a poor house.

We took him to Theo in her room. She kissed him and thanked him; and the moidore—warm from his palm, bright as a dropped piece of sunshine—went into her purse, where it has remained from that day. The whistle and coral were laid by for the baby; the coin, with its foreign stamp and king’s head, seemed to carry more than value. It carried the boy’s defiance of the talk at home, his belief that what was said of us was “a shame,” and his desire, in the only language a child with rich relations can command, to make good the lack.

My mother, writing through her ambassador as usual, informed me of her surprise and displeasure on learning that my son had been christened Miles—a name not known, at least in the Esmond family. I did not care to tell the reason then. Names, in great houses, are treated as if they belonged to the family plate: to be kept bright, to be handed down in the approved pattern, to be counted and displayed. But there are other inheritances than the ones a herald can read. In after years, when I told Madam Esmond how my boy came by his name, I saw a tear roll down her wrinkled cheek. And afterwards I heard she had asked Gumbo many questions about the boy who had given his name to our Miles—our Miles Gloriosus of Pall Mall, Valenciennes, Almack’s, Brighton—who carried, under all his finery and public sound, that first quiet music of kindness: a child’s hoofbeats in Lambeth, a coin offered with shame-faced generosity, a name chosen not by pride but by gratitude.
