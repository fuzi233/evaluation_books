“Much adoe there was, God wot;
He wold love and she wold not.”

A year can be measured, if you like, by calendars and journeys—by how far the sun has paced along the hedgerows—but what grows within that span is not to be reckoned by miles any more than a field’s life is told by its acreage. One man goes away and comes back altered as if some secret ferment had worked through his blood; another stays at home where the same kettle sings, the same gate creaks, the same bees keep their old hours among peonies, and time merely lays a thinner glaze of habit over the days.

So it had been between the brilliant Gwendolen Harleth of the archery meeting and the Gwendolen who now moved somewhere far off, crushed into penitence; while at Pennicote, life kept to its familiar rounds, only with fewer guineas for trimming and fewer compliments to warm the drawing-room. The rectory was still a pleasant home. The red and pink peonies had flamed again on the lawn; the hollyhocks lifted their old spires along the hedge; and the rector’s days went forward with the wholesome regularity of a well-ordered kitchen, where the bread must be kneaded whether or not there is a grand guest expected.

He wrote his two ecclesiastical articles—peaceful authorship, done with windows open on fields and downs, not under the stale, thrice-breathed air of criticism. It was work that did not steal appetite, but rather seemed to make his dinner taste the better for a mind at ease; and it brought him no leanness of spirit—only that suffusive complacency which is like good broth: not showy, but nourishing.

Better still was the recovered delight in his favorite son. The loss of income was a real narrowing—fewer visits, fewer little indulgences—but this happiness outweighed it. Whatever hidden change disappointment had made in Rex, it showed itself less in gloom than in purpose. He came down to the rectory for the summer bringing Anna with him; and though he had nearly the old liveliness with his brothers and sisters, he kept the habits of the eager student—up early, and in the evenings shut away with his books as steadily as a baker turns back to his leaven.

“You don’t repent the choice of the law as a profession, Rex?” his father asked.

“There is no profession I would choose before it,” Rex said. “I should like to end my life as a first-rate judge, and help to draw up a code. I reverse the famous dictum. I should say, ‘Give me something to do with making the laws, and let who will make the songs.’”

“You will have to stow in an immense amount of rubbish, I suppose—that’s the worst of it,” the rector said, with the indulgent grumble that hides pride.

“I don’t see that law-rubbish is worse than any other sort. It is not so bad as the rubbishy literature that people choke their minds with. It doesn’t make one so dull. Our wittiest men have often been lawyers. Any orderly way of looking at things as cases and evidence seems to me better than a perpetual wash of odds and ends bearing on nothing in particular. And then, from a higher point of view, the foundations and the growth of law make the most interesting aspects of philosophy and history. Of course there will be a good deal that is troublesome, drudging, perhaps exasperating. But the great prizes in life can’t be won easily—I see that.”

“Well, my boy, the best augury of a man’s success in his profession is that he thinks it the finest in the world.” The rector’s eyes rested on him with that fatherly satisfaction which he carefully rationed, as one rations rich food that might turn to cloying. “But I fancy it so with most work when a man goes into it with a will. Brewitt, the blacksmith, said to me the other day that his ’prentice had no mind to his trade; ‘and yet, sir,’ said Brewitt, ‘what would a young fellow have if he doesn’t like the blacksmithing?’”

Rex was the romance of the rector’s later life: the son in whom a man comes to recognize something beyond his own measure, and therefore to picture a future eminence for him as easily as he pictures harvest when the wheat is still green. Only to his wife did he say with decision, “Rex will be a distinguished man, Nancy, I am sure of it—as sure as Paley’s father was about his son.”

“Was Paley an old bachelor?” Mrs. Gascoigne asked, with that domestic literalness which will put its finger on the wrong crumb.

“That is hardly to the point, my dear,” the rector said; and she felt she had spoken rather weakly.

This quiet trotting of time at the rectory was shared by the little household that had exchanged Offendene’s faded dignity for the low white house not a mile off, snugged in evergreens, known in the village simply as “Jodson’s.” Mrs. Davilow’s delicate face showed only a slight deepening of its mild melancholy; a few more silver lines ran through her hair; and the four girls had bloomed a little, as plants do when some taller growth is cut away and they find more light for themselves. The good Jocosa kept her useful neutrality toward splendor, as if splendour were a dish meant for those not “in a situation.”

On a July afternoon the low narrow drawing-room—widened in feeling by two quaint projecting windows—was full, for Rex and Anna were added to the usual group. The lattices stood wide open, letting in the scent of monthly roses and the soft murmur of the garden: bees worrying a flower, leaves whispering together, a far-off bird insisting on a phrase and then falling silent. Now and then, rare as a stone dropped in a well, came the sound of hoofs and wheels; the road’s brief disturbance made the following stillness clearer, as if quiet had a taste, and that taste could be rinsed and returned.

Anna, always a favorite with her younger cousins, had much to tell of her new London experience and acquaintances. When she had first visited alone, questions had pressed round her—questions that all edged towards Gwendolen’s house in Grosvenor Square, what Gwendolen had said, what other people had said, and whether Anna had seen her after she “knew about the yacht.” No—Anna had not. That answer left imagination to do what it pleased with the unknown vessel. All they had were two letters: one before the setting out, to say that Mr. and Mrs. Grandcourt were going yachting on the Mediterranean; another from Marseilles, briskly declaring that she was sure to like it, the cabins were very elegant, and she would probably not write again until she had filled a long diary with dittos. Besides, the movement had been mentioned in “the newspaper,” which to Isabel’s mind gave it a varnish of romance, and she threw in a corsair or two—an agreeable seasoning—so that the adventure might end well.

But when Rex was present, the girls, obeying instructions, never began that fascinating topic; and to-day the livelier talk had been of the Meyricks and their extraordinary Jewish friends. It startled these country minds, to whom “live Jews” seemed almost to belong to the pages of a book rather than to the pavement and the tea-table—some strange race out of Pliny that might sleep under the shade of its own ears. Bertha could not imagine what Jews believed now; she had a dim notion that they rejected the Old Testament because it proved the New; Miss Merry thought Mirah and her brother could “never have been properly argued with”; and gentle Alice, with the docility of inherited aversion, said she did not mind what they believed—she was sure she “couldn’t bear them.”

Mrs. Davilow corrected her, saying that great Jewish families in society were quite what they ought to be both in London and Paris, though she admitted that the commoner unconverted Jews were objectionable; and Isabel, book-devouring Isabel, asked whether Mirah talked just as they did, or whether one might be with her and not find out she was a Jewess.

Rex, who had no partisanship with Israelites—having made a troublesome acquaintance with the minutiae of their ancient history in the form of “cram”—amused himself by playfully exaggerating each speaker’s notion, while Anna begged them to see he was only joking.

The laughter was cut through by the entrance of a letter for Mrs. Davilow. A messenger had run with it in great haste from the rectory.

The air in the room seemed to tighten, as if the garden itself had hushed to listen. Mrs. Davilow read and re-read the enclosed telegram; her agitation held her silent, and all the young faces turned toward her, fear staining them as quickly as a spill of ink. No one dared speak. At last she looked up, remembering that their imaginations might run to something worse than the truth—something like her own first dread, which had made the words swim—and she said, with a sob that was half relief and half breaking, “My dears, Mr. Grandcourt—” She paused, as if the name itself were a weight in her mouth, and then began again: “Mr. Grandcourt is drowned.”

Rex started up as if something had been flung into the room. He could not help it; and Anna’s first look went to him, swift with a sister’s anxious tenderness. But Mrs. Davilow was reading what the rector had added on the paper enclosing the telegram, and Rex gathered himself.

“Can I do anything, aunt?” he said. “Can I carry any word to my father from you?”

“Yes, dear. Tell him I will be ready—he is very good. He says he will go with me to Genoa—he will be here at half-past six. Jocosa and Alice, help me to get ready. She is safe—Gwendolen is safe—but she must be ill. I am sure she must be very ill. Rex, dear—Rex and Anna—go and tell your father I will be quite ready. I would not for the world lose another night. And bless him for being ready so soon. I can travel night and day till we get there.”

Rex and Anna hurried away through sunshine that had suddenly become solemn, as if its warmth were now only the warmth that shines upon graves and weddings alike—impartial, relentless. They said nothing to one another. Anna’s mind clung to him with concern—would this news tear open what had been healing?—while Rex struggled with a crowd of thoughts that came upon him like a jangling of bells, rude, untimely, and against his will.

At the rectory gate he stopped.

“Nannie, I will leave you to say everything to my father. If he wants me immediately, let me know. I shall stay in the shrubbery for ten minutes—only ten minutes.”

He turned away into the green cover, where leaves whispered and a blackbird startled into a short, sharp flight—small natural sounds that ought to have soothed, but could not. There are moments when the mind, even in the presence of another’s death, makes egoistic escapes, picturing consequences for itself; and the very shame of it is like a bitter taste that will not be spat out. In Rex the shame rose instantly and spread an ugly light over the hurrying images that had thrust in with the thought, “Gwendolen is free.”

But no sooner did “Gwendolen free” rise than there rose also, like a wall built of stone and gold, “Gwendolen rich, exalted, courted.” In the earlier time, when both lives were fresh and untried, she had turned from his love with repugnance. What ground was there now to suppose her heart would be more open? The thoughts came like opposing chimes, each striking against the other, so that there was no clear music, only tumult.

During the last year he had worked himself into a calm resolve; and now three words—drowned, safe, Genoa—seemed enough to undo that hard-won order and fling him back into the wretched rise and fall of longing he believed hopeless. Worse, the longing’s very activity at such a moment felt like a kind of theft at a bedside.

Excuse him: it was not much more than eighteen months since he had been laid low by an archer who sometimes touches his arrow with a subtle, lingering poison. The disappointment of a youthful passion does not always leave its mark in one plain way: it may sharpen one nature and dull another, humble one and harden another, and leave a third apparently unchanged. Everything depends—not on the bare fact of disappointment, but on the nature that is struck and the force that stirs it.

In Rex’s well-endowed nature, brief as the hope had been, the passionate stirring had gone deep; the disappointment had been revolutionary, yet it had brought a beneficent new order which kept most of the old virtues. In certain respects he believed it had set the bias and color of his life. It was as if some strong flavor—once tasted—had made all the future meals remember the lost one, so that even the plain bread had a ghost of sweetness and pain.

Now, pacing between shrubs that smelt of crushed leaf and summer heat, he felt his inward peace no better than a city where the alarm-bell slackens work and makes tumult busy. His love had been sudden, clinging, penetrating: to have consciousness steeped in another’s personality, to have the strongest inclinations possessed by an image that keeps its dominion in spite of change and apart from worthiness—nay, to feel the passion cling faster for the tragic pangs inflicted by a cruel, reorganized unworthiness—this is a love that phlegmatic rationality stares at and calls unaccountable. Yet it exists, as undeniably as wind and wave, wrecking here, carrying to a triumphant voyage there. In a nature not brutish but capable of dignity, it may end not in mere appetite but in a devotedness that risks itself safely.

Rex had made up his mind to that companionship as if it had been some supremely dear creature stricken dumb and helpless, and he had turned all the future of tenderness into a shadow of the past. But he had also made up his mind that his life was not to be pauperized because he had renounced one kind of joy. He had begun again, counting what treasures remained. He had even felt a release of power that sometimes comes when you cease to be afraid of your own neck.

And now he was angry with himself that the sense of irrevocableness which ought, in reason, to have been as strong as ever, had been shaken by a change that could make no true change in his relation to Gwendolen.

He spoke the truth to himself roughly, as a man may swallow bitter medicine in one gulp.

“She would never love me; and that is not the question—I could never approach her as a lover in her present position. I am exactly of no consequence at all, and am not likely to be of much consequence till my head is turning gray. But what has that to do with it? She would not have me on any terms, and I would not ask her. It is a meanness to be thinking about it now—no better than lurking about the battle-field to strip the dead; but there never was more gratuitous sinning. I have nothing to gain there—absolutely nothing. Then why can’t I face the facts, and behave as they demand, instead of leaving my father to suppose that there are matters he can’t speak to me about, though I might be useful in them?”

That last thought—usefulness, plain duty—came like a firmer step on a slippery path. Rex turned and walked steadily into the house, and through the open door of the study.

There was his father, already packing a traveling-desk, the familiar implements of order laid out with the promptness of a man who has spent his life answering calls.

“Can I be of any use, sir?” Rex said, with rallied courage, as his father looked up.

“Yes, my boy; when I’m gone, just see to my letters, and answer where necessary, and send me word of everything. Dymock will manage the parish very well, and you will stay with your mother, or, at least, go up and down again, till I come back, whenever that may be.”

“You will hardly be very long, sir, I suppose,” Rex said, beginning to strap a railway rug. “You will perhaps bring my cousin back to England?”

He forced himself to speak of Gwendolen for the first time. The rector noticed the moment with satisfaction, as if a difficulty had been passed and a better tone secured between them.

“That depends,” he answered, as if this were a matter-of-course to be discussed like trains and luggage. “Perhaps her mother may stay there with her, and I may come back very soon. This telegram leaves us in ignorance which is rather anxious. But no doubt the arrangements of the will lately made are satisfactory, and there may possibly be an heir yet to be born. In any case, I feel confident that Gwendolen will be liberally—I should expect, splendidly—provided for.”

“It must have been a great shock for her,” Rex said, getting more resolute now the first twinge had been borne. “I suppose he was a devoted husband.”

“No doubt of it,” said the rector, in his most decided manner. “Few men of his position would have come forward as he did under the circumstances.”

Rex had never seen Grandcourt; no one in the family had talked to him about the man; he knew nothing of Gwendolen’s flight from her suitor to Leubronn. He only knew that Grandcourt, being very much in love with her, had made her an offer in the first weeks of her sudden poverty, and had behaved very handsomely in providing for her mother and sisters. That seemed natural—what Rex himself would have liked to do, had he possessed the power. Grandcourt had been a lucky fellow, and had had some happiness before he got drowned. Yet, even as he tightened the strap on the rug, Rex wondered—quietly, irresistibly—whether Gwendolen had been in love with the successful suitor, or had only forborne to tell Rex that she hated being made love to.
