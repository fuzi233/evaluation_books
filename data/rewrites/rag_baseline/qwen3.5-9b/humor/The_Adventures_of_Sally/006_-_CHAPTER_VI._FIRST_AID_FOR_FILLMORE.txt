The air in the Hotel Statler dining room was thick with the scent of stale coffee and the heavy, humid silence of a city holding its breath. Sally sat at the table, the porcelain cup warming her hands, but offering no warmth to the chill settling in her chest. The nursing of Mr. Faucitt had drained her physically, leaving her muscles leaden, but the fatigue was secondary to the hollow ache in her stomach. Gerald's voice on the telephone, flat and devoid of inflection, echoed in her mind. He had treated her return, after weeks of absence, as a trivial administrative detail rather than a reunion. It was a small cruelty, yet it felt monumental.

She poured a second cup of coffee, watching the dark liquid swirl. Men, she reasoned, were creatures of morning inertia; their spirits were suspended in amber until the shock of water or movement broke the seal. Soon, perhaps, Gerald would burst through the doors, transformed by the cold shower, vibrant and oblivious to the slight. For now, there was only the food and the waiting.

The door swung open, admitting a draft of lobby noise. A young man entered, scanning the room with the frantic, darting eyes of a man searching for a ghost. He was stout, his movements slightly awkward, and as he paused near the entrance, Sally felt a sharp intake of breath. The resemblance was uncanny—a mirror image of her brother Fillmore, complete with the same expansive jawline and the same tendency to occupy space inefficiently. It was Fillmore.

Puzzlement tightened her brow. Why was he here? New York was his domain, a place of vast interests and permanent residency. Yet, men of affairs were nomadic spirits, drifting wherever the wind of opportunity blew. He spotted her eventually, turning away from the wall to face her table. His expression was tight, a mask of nervous embarrassment that suggested a guilty conscience.

"Ah, Sally," he said, his voice cracking slightly. "Why?"

His demeanor was off-kilter, as if he were balancing on a tightrope stretched between two cliffs. She suspected he was bracing himself to deliver bad news—the announcement of an engagement made without her consent.

"What are you doing here?" he asked, his gaze flickering nervously. "I thought you were still in Europe."

"I returned a week ago," she replied, forcing a lightness into her tone. "But I've been tending to poor old Mr. Faucitt. He's been terribly ill. I've finally come to see Foster's play, *The Primrose Way*. Is it running?"

"It hasn't opened yet," Fillmore said quickly.

"Don't be absurd, Fill. It opened last Monday."

"No, it didn't. Have you seen the papers? They've shuttered all the theaters. Spanish influenza. Nothing is happening."

"I haven't had time to read the papers. Oh, Fill, what a tragedy!"

"Yes, it's dreadful. It's driving everyone mad. I've had the worst time imaginable."

"Why does it concern you?"

Fillmore cleared his throat, a dry, hacking sound. "Well, I—I didn't mention this. I'm involved with the production. Cracknell—he was at college with me—asked me to inspect it. Perhaps he intends to bring me in as an investor."

"I assumed he owned everything already."

"He has plenty, but these men love to share a fortune with a friend."

"Is it a good venture?"

"The play is excellent."

"That's what Mr. Faucitt said. But Mabel Hobson..."

Fillmore's face, usually a landscape of smug confidence, crumpled into genuine distress. "She is a nightmare, Sally! She cannot act, and she dominates every conversation. Yesterday, there was a scene over a missing paper knife—"

"A paper knife?" Sally asked, bewildered.

"One of the props. It vanished. I swear it wasn't my fault—"

"How could it be yours?" Sally demanded, genuinely confused. Love, she observed, seemed to have eroded Fillmore's mental fortitude, reducing him to a child blaming the nearest object for adult errors.

"Well, you know how angry women are. They blame the first available target. This paper knife..." His voice trailed off into a pained silence.

"Mr. Faucitt praised Elsa Doland."

"Oh, she's competent enough," Fillmore dismissed. "But the girl you need to witness is Miss Winch. Gladys Winch. She plays the maid. She appears briefly in the first act, saying only 'Did you ring, madam?' and similar trifles. But listen to her delivery! Sally, she is a genius! The finest character actress in a decade. Mark my words, soon her name will blaze on Broadway. Personality? Ask me! Charm? She composed the lyrics and melody for the show! Looks?..."

"All right, all right," Sally interrupted, sensing the end of the monologue. "And will you please explain how you managed to become engaged without consulting me?"

Fillmore flushed a deep crimson. "Oh, do you know?"

"Yes. Mr. Faucitt informed me."

"Well..."

"Well?"

"Well, I am only human," Fillmore argued weakly.

"Splendid admission. You've grown modest, Fill."

He had indeed changed since their last encounter. Someone, it seemed, had pricked his balloon and released the pompous air inside. If this transformation was due to the influence of Miss Winch, Sally found herself approving of the shift.

"I'll introduce you," Fillmore offered.

"I would like to meet her very much."

"I must leave now. I need to locate Bunbury. I thought he might be here."

"Who is Bunbury?"

"The producer. He's probably eating in his room. I should go up."

"You are busy, aren't you? Little marvel! It's fortunate they have you managing them."

Fillmore retreated, and Sally settled back, her earlier sting regarding Gerald's indifference fading. Poor Gerald; no wonder he sounded so distant.

Minutes later, the door opened again. Gerald entered, carrying the weight of the world in his shoulders.

"Oh, Jerry darling," Sally said, rising to greet him. "I'm so sorry. I've just learned about it."

Gerald sat heavily. His appearance matched his voice: a dull, nervous fog enveloped him like a wet wool coat.

"It's just my luck," he muttered, staring into his tea. "The kind of disaster reserved solely for me. Damned fools! Why shut the theaters when there is influenza? People crowd the streets all day; surely that spreads it more than a theater? Besides, it's all a hysterical panic. I don't believe in Spanish influenza. People catch colds and imagine they are dying. It's a manufactured scare."

"I don't think it's hysteria," Sally said gently. "Mr. Faucitt suffered greatly. That is why I delayed."

Gerald showed no interest in the illness or her tardiness. He stabbed a spoon aggressively into a grapefruit segment.

"We've been wandering these halls day after day, starving for entertainment... The company is falling apart. They are exhausted by endless rehearsals with no opening date in sight. They were eager a week ago, now they are dragging their feet. It will ruin the play. Of course it will. It's my first major opportunity, and I'm about to lose it."

Sally listened, a growing sense of desolation tightening her throat. She tried to be charitable, acknowledging his stress, yet she recoiled from the self-pity radiating from him. Vanity stung; her arrival, intended as a balm, had achieved nothing. She recalled Mr. Faucitt's assessment of Gerald, noting for the first time his remarkable egocentrism. He was projecting his needs onto her, demanding sympathy while erecting a wall against it. He wanted her to pity him, yet refused to allow her to cross the threshold. She felt shut out, irrelevant.

"By the way," Gerald said abruptly, "I have to keep her morale high constantly. So, for heaven's sake, do not reveal that we are engaged."

Sally's chin lifted sharply. "If being engaged to me is a hindrance..."

"Don't be ridiculous," Gerald pleaded, adopting a tone of pathetic grandeur. "Good heavens! It's unbearable! Here I am, consumed by worry, and you..."

Before he could finish, Sally's mood shifted with the speed of a lightning storm. A simple, grounding thought clarified the chaos. She was tired, gritty, and disheveled from the journey. The discordant notes of the world were merely reflections of her own unkempt state. If she washed and combed her hair, Gerald would appear normal again. Tranquility returned. She placed her hand over his with a gesture of contrition.

"I'm so sorry," she said. "I've been cruel, but I truly sympathize."

"I've had a terrible time," Gerald mumbled, accepting the peace offering.

"I know, I know. But you never mentioned you were glad to see me."

"Of course I am."

"Then why didn't you say so, you poor fish? And why didn't you ask if I enjoyed Europe?"

"Did you enjoy it?"

"Yes, except for missing you. There! Now my lecture on foreign travel is concluded, and you may continue detailing your woes."

Gerald obliged, speaking at length with little variation. It was clear in his mind that Providence had invented Spanish influenza specifically to dismantle his career. Yet, the brief storm had cleared the air. The isolation he had projected dissolved.
