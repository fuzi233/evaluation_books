The Awakening of Vengeance.

Tito Melema's journey to Rome, a move that smoothed the path for Romola's eventual departure, had been decided upon with startling abruptness at a supper held merely the previous evening. Tito had approached that gathering with high spirits, anticipating delicate meats, choice wines, and a circle of distinguished company. The venue was the renowned Orto de' Rucellai, the Rucellai Gardens, hosted by Bernardo Rucellai, a paragon of Florentine grandeur. His very name bore a symbolic weight, derived from *roccella*, a lichen of the Greek islands and Canaries that, when soaked in sunlight, yields a vivid purple dye known as *oricello*. Just as this humble plant, through the labor of merchants centuries prior, had transformed into a source of immense wealth for the Rucellai line, so too had Bernardo elevated his station. He had wedded a sister of Lorenzo de' Medici, served as an ambassador to France and Venice, and held the office of Gonfaloniere. A patron of the arts and learning, he had adorned the façade of Santa Maria Novella with black and white marble, cultivated gardens of rare trees, and hosted the Platonic Academy following Lorenzo's death. A writer of Latin and antiquarian lore, Bernardo stood as a monument to human potential, a figure so virtuous that nature herself might despair of creating another.

Lorenzo Tornabuoni had delivered Bernardo's invitation to Tito, though with a gravity that hinted at serious political machinations beneath the guise of social entertainment. To Tito, however, the gathering promised the furtherance of party aims through the medium of exquisite flavors and aged Greek wine. Aware that Bernardo was one of the Elect Twenty currently steering Florence, Tito felt assured of the event's importance. Yet, he harbored a lingering doubt regarding the intellectual caliber of the assembly. Having attended various somber dinners in the Rucellai grounds before, particularly those dominated by tedious philosophical discourses where guests were expected to swallow entire cosmologies, Tito feared the company might be both dull and pedantic.

It was a dark night when Tito traversed the streets, his form becoming visible only when he stepped from the shadow of one lantern into the flickering light of another. To any observer watching closely, the tall, graceful figure cloaked in a mantle was invariably accompanied by a stark contrast: a short, elderly man clad in a rough serge tunic and a felt hat. Their conjunction might easily be dismissed as coincidence, for the streets were crowded. However, when Tito halted at the side gate of the Rucellai gardens, his companion ceased moving as well. A servant, distracted by a prior errand, held the small portal open. Tito slipped inside quickly, offering his name, and vanished into the torch-lit thicket of evergreens. His follower entered immediately behind.

"Your name?" the servant asked, turning slightly.

"Baldassarre Calvo," the old man replied instantly.

"You are not a guest; all guests have passed."

"I attend Messer Tito, who has just entered. I shall wait in the gardens."

The servant hesitated, glancing around. "I received orders to admit guests only. Are you a servant of Messer Tito?"

"No, friend, I am not a servant. I am a scholar."

There is a specific cadence of voice, a tone of quiet conviction, that commands respect even from the most suspicious. The porter yielded immediately, allowing Baldassarre to step into the garden, where the heavy iron door clanged shut and locked behind him, sealing him among the shimmering bushes. Those firm, rapid answers marked a profound transformation in Baldassarre since their last encounter, a meeting that had ended with a shattered dagger.

That metamorphosis had begun in the aftermath of Tito's departure from Baldassarre's humble dwelling. At that moment, Baldassarre sat paralyzed by the aftershocks of a violent emotional outburst—a state where physical weakness is often paired with a crystalline clarity of thought. As if the dissipating storm of passion had swept away the mist, leaving a pristine sky, his mind was suddenly lucid. His limbs felt numb, his body cold, his hands trembling, yet he was not lost in darkness. Instead, the vague shadows of his usual existence had been replaced by the sharp, unyielding images of the past. He relived a life that had felt like a long, bitter preparation for the present moment.

For several minutes, he was so consumed by these visions that he forgot his surroundings. Then, the clarity extended outward, anchoring him firmly in the reality of the street outside. He recalled Monna Lisa and Tessa. Yes, *he* was the mysterious husband; he possessed another wife in the Via de' Bardi. Now, it was time to retrieve the broken dagger and vanish, leaving no trace. Hiding his frailty was the only semblance of power he retained. Leaning forward, he grasped the jagged shards of steel. Beside him lay a large, open manuscript—an unusual volume of Pausanias. Moonlight spilled across the page, illuminating the large Greek letters at the head: *ΜΕΣΣΕΝΙΚΑ. ΚΒ′.*

In times past, Pausanias had been a familiar friend to Baldassarre, yet recently, those pages had appeared as meaningless black scrawls on a wall, devoid of sense. But tonight, the moonbeam acting as a lens conjured the world anew. The letters bloomed into the vibrant history of Messenia, its desperate struggle against Spartan oppression. Baldassarre snatched up the book, though the light was too faint for further reading. It mattered not; he knew the chapter by heart. He read it silently, seeing the stoning of the traitor Aristocrates—the entire populace casting him out to rot unburied, erecting a pillar of verse to declare how time had restored justice to the unjust.

Words swarmed within him, vibrating with the resonance of a thousand memories. He forgot his age; he could have shouted aloud. The light had returned, the mother of knowledge and joy! In this surge of exultation, his limbs regained their strength. He leaped to his feet, clutching the broken blade and the book, and emerged into the broad moonlight.

A biting frost hung in the air, yet Baldassarre felt no chill. Only a radiant glow of conscious power warmed his blood. He paced the open spaces of the high ground, gazing down upon the sleeping city, crowned by mountains and guarded by silent towers. He beheld the pale gleam of the Arno and the valleys fading into snowy peaks, feeling himself the master of this vast domain. This mental empire, available to all in moments of singular clarity, was amplified for Baldassarre by the long nights when memory had been little more than a hollow echo. The city, once a weary labyrinth, now submitted to his will. His mind raced through its affairs with lightning-fast conjectures, reviving the sensation of a man who truly understood cities, whose vision was honed by vast experience, and who delighted in holding everything within the grasp of language.

Names! Images! His mind flooded through this treasury without pause, like an heir accepting a vast legacy. Yet, amidst this rushing tide, a dark deity presided in the inner sanctum of his consciousness—a god who seemed forgotten only during the preparations of sacrifice. Once the initial triumph of recovering power settled, his thoughts coalesced around Tito. That fair, slippery viper could no longer escape him. Thanks to the struggles of justice, the heart that usually refused tenderness now possessed sensitive fibers capable of being pricked by the sharp point of anguish. The soul that bowed to no moral right now bowed to the supreme lord of mortals: Pain.

He could now penetrate every secret of Tito's life. Some he already knew, but the failure of the broken dagger, which had seemed like a crushing defeat, was merely the prelude to true achievement. That sudden, blinding rage had shaken away the obstructions that had suffocated his soul. Twice before, his memory had returned in flashes triggered by sudden excitement—once defending himself from a rabid dog, once scrambling up a cliff to escape drowning waves.

Yet, a shadow lingered. If this light faded tomorrow, returning the dreary blankness, what would become of him? Though the light felt stronger and steadier now, the fear of regression gnawed at him. Could the dark fog return before dawn? This anxiety, though painful, felt like the genesis of renewed strength. He dreaded the idea of sinking further under the strain of this vigilant vigil on the hill, expending his final reserves. Seeking a sheltered corner, he nestled against a heap of warm garden straw and succumbed to sleep.

When he awoke, the sun had risen. The first moments were filled with a strange bewilderment: was he a man of dual identities? Had he woken to the life of a dim-sighted heir to a fallen greatness, or to the life of recovered power? Surely the latter, for the events of the night surged back with undeniable force: the recognition of the page in Pausanias, the flood of facts and names, the panoramic vision that had filled him with the glorious amazement of a Maenad waking atop a mountain.

He picked up the book again, reading and remembering simultaneously without effort. A name evoked deeds; a deed summoned a name. He encountered tales of unpardonable crimes, yet also stories of guilt that succeeded. Sanctuaries existed for swift-footed criminals; baseness wore armor, and the weapons of justice sometimes broke against it. So what then?
