XIII. The Woodman

Soon, however, troubling signs began to appear. Millarca grew increasingly weak, her body still recovering from the illness that had gripped her not long before. She remained confined to her room until well into the afternoon, rarely seen outside its walls. Yet, despite the fact that she always locked her door from within—never moving the key until the maid arrived to assist with her morning routine—it was discovered, by chance, that she was sometimes absent from her chamber in the early hours of dawn and at other times throughout the day, before she wished anyone to know she had stirred.

From the windows of the schloss, I saw her more than once during those pale, grey mornings, walking slowly through the trees toward the east, her movements stiff and unnatural, as though she were in a trance. This led me to believe she walked in her sleep. But that explanation offered no real comfort. How could she leave her room when the door was locked? How did she slip from the house without unlocking any door or window?

My confusion deepened when a far greater dread took hold—my dear child was fading. Her beauty diminished; her health waned in ways so strange, so horrifying, that I could no longer ignore the truth: something dark and unseen was draining her life.

At first, it was only terrible dreams—visions that left her trembling and pale upon waking. Then came the specter. She claimed it appeared to her at night, sometimes resembling Millarca, sometimes taking on the shape of a beast, indistinct and shadowed, circling the foot of her bed like a predator stalking its prey. Then came the sensations.

One evening, she described a feeling—not painful, but deeply unsettling—as if an icy stream ran across her chest. A few nights later, she felt sharp, piercing pains beneath her throat, as though two large needles had driven into her flesh. And then, suddenly, a slow, convulsive strangulation seized her—followed by unconsciousness.

I heard every word the old General spoke, for we were now driving over the short grass flanking the road that led to the roofless village—a place untouched by smoke for more than fifty years. As he recounted these events, my blood turned cold. For each symptom he described matched my own in chilling detail. The girl whose story he told—had she not been meant to be a guest at my father’s chateau, had fate not intervened? And worse—the habits, the secrets, the hidden behaviors he mentioned… they were unmistakably those of Carmilla.

The forest opened before us, and suddenly we stood beneath the crumbling chimneys and sagging gables of the ruined village. Above it loomed the dismantled castle, its towers and battlements half-swallowed by towering trees that rose like sentinels from the hillside. In a daze, I stepped down from the carriage. We said nothing, each lost in our own thoughts, yet united by a shared dread. Together, we climbed the slope and entered the vast, echoing chambers of the ancient fortress.

“And this,” said the General at last, standing at a great window and gazing out over the desolate village and the endless forest beyond, “was once the grand seat of the Karnsteins.”

“It was a cursed family,” he continued, his voice low and heavy. “Here, their blood-soaked history was written. It is cruel that even in death, they should continue to torment the living with their monstrous desires.” He pointed downward. “That is the chapel of the Karnsteins.”

Then, as if struck by a sudden thought, he added, “And I hear the axe of a woodman—working among the trees near the chapel. Perhaps he can tell us what we seek—perhaps he knows where Mircalla, Countess of Karnstein, lies buried. These simple folk remember the stories the nobility have long forgotten.”

“We have a portrait of Mircalla at home,” my father said. “Would you care to see it?”

“Not yet,” replied the General. “I believe I’ve seen the original. One reason I came to you sooner than planned was to investigate this very chapel.”

“What!” exclaimed my father. “You mean to say you think Mircalla is still alive? But she died over a century ago!”

“Not so dead as you suppose,” the General answered grimly.

“You puzzle me,” my father said, his eyes narrowing slightly—just as I had noticed before, a flicker of suspicion crossing his face. Yet the General carried no trace of madness. His manner was steady, even fierce.

“There remains but one thing that matters to me in these final years,” he said, stepping into the dim interior of the chapel, its arches looming high above us like the ribs of some long-dead beast. “And that is to bring justice to the monster—justice that may still be done by mortal hands.”

“What justice?” my father asked, bewildered.

“To decapitate her,” the General replied, his voice rising, his face flushing with rage. With a thunderous stamp, he raised a clenched fist, as if gripping an axe, and shook it violently in the air.

“Decapitate her?” my father echoed, stunned.

“Aye,” the General snarled. “With an axe, a spade, anything that will split her foul throat. You shall hear all—soon.” He moved forward, gesturing to a heavy wooden beam lying on the moss-covered floor. “That will serve as a seat. Your daughter is tired. Let her rest while I finish my tale.”

I sank onto the rough timber, grateful for the moment of stillness. Meanwhile, the General called out to the woodman, who had been clearing branches from the chapel’s crumbling walls. The man approached, axe in hand, weathered and strong, his face etched with the lines of a lifetime spent in the woods.

He knew nothing of the monuments here. But he told us of an old ranger—a man who lived with the priest about two miles away—who could point out every relic of the Karnstein line. For a small fee, he would return with him—if we lent him one of our horses.

“Have you worked in these woods long?” my father asked.

“All my life,” the woodman replied in his thick dialect. “Under the forester. My father before me. And before him, many generations. I could show you the very house in the village where my ancestors lived.”

“How did the village become abandoned?” the General asked.

“It was plagued by revenants,” the man said. “Several were found in their graves, tested, and destroyed—by decapitation, by stake, by fire. But still, the torment continued. Many villagers died.”

Then he paused, his voice dropping lower. “But then a Moravian nobleman passed through. He heard of the curse and, being skilled in such matters—many in his land are—he offered to end it.”

“He did,” the woodman continued. “There was a full moon that night. Shortly after sunset, he climbed the chapel tower, from which he could clearly see the churchyard below. From there, he watched until he saw the vampire emerge from his grave, lay down his linen shroud, and glide into the village to feed.”

“The nobleman descended, took the shroud, and returned to the top of the tower. When the vampire returned and found his clothes gone, he roared up at the man atop the steeple. The stranger waved him up. The vampire climbed—only to have his skull split open by a single blow of the nobleman’s sword. He fell into the churchyard. The stranger followed down the winding stairs, cut off his head, and delivered both body and head to the villagers the next day. They impaled and burned the corpse.”

“This Moravian nobleman had authority from the head of the Karnstein family to move Mircalla’s tomb—and he did. So thoroughly that no one remembers where it once stood.”

“Can you mark its location?” the General asked, his voice urgent.

The woodman shook his head, a sad smile on his lips. “No one living knows. And besides… they say her body was removed. But no one is certain.”

With that, he dropped his axe and turned away, leaving us alone in the silence of the ruin, as the General prepared to speak again—his voice thick with vengeance, and the shadows deepening around us.
