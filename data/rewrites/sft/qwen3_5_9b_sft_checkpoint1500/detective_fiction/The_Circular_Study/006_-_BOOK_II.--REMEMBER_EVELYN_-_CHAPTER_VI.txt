A peculiar quality marked the gaze with which this benevolent spinster regarded Detective Gryce—a look so enigmatic that a stranger might struggle to decipher it. For Gryce himself, it was a mirror reflecting a confusion he had rarely felt; he had seldom seen this lady burdened by an opinion of herself that fell short of perfect self-assurance.

"Miss Butterworth! What does this portend? Have you—?"

"There!" Her voice cut sharply, betraying a sudden, stinging shame. "You have cornered me at my old deceptions, and I am humbled accordingly, while you stand victorious. The gray parasol you kindly dispatched to my residence is not mine, though I was indeed in the chamber where you discovered it, exactly as your brilliant deduction suggested. Since evasion would serve no purpose against your acumen, I have come to confess."

"Gladly," Gryce murmured, his professional interest instantly piqued. He pulled a chair close to Miss Butterworth and sat. "You were there!" he repeated gently. "And when? I need not presume to inquire regarding your motives."

"But I must explain my purpose, or I shall find myself at a severe disadvantage," she replied, her demeanor shifting to one of grim resolution. "Though I dislike exposing my own frailties, I recognize the gravity of the situation. I fully comprehend your shock at discovering that I, a stranger to Mr. Adams, lacking the justification for my previous interference in police affairs, should have so utterly lost my bearings as to appear before you in this manner. This was not an affair that sought me; I sought it, sir. I wish I had journeyed to Jerico first—it would have cost me more coin and travel time—but it would have spared me this humiliation and the risk of public scandal. Mr. Gryce, I never intended to entangle myself in another murder investigation. I have demonstrated an aptitude for detection and earned, in the past, certain marks of your approval. Yet, my head was not turned by these accolades—at least, I believed it wasn't—and I remained sincere in my vow to adhere strictly to my profession, refusing to let myself be lured by any inducement you might offer into exercising gifts that had once brought me praise, if not happiness. However, the temptation arrived not from you, or I might have resisted it. Instead, a convergence of circumstances found me vulnerable and, to a degree, unprepared. In short, I was surprised into taking an interest in this affair. Oh, I am ashamed, so deeply ashamed that I strove with every fiber of my being to conceal my involvement. Thinking I had succeeded, I congratulated myself on my precautions, only to have this parasol thrust in my face, revealing that you, if no one else, knew that Amelia Butterworth had been in Mr. Adams's room prior to your arrival. Yet, I believed I had left no trace. Could you have seen..."

"Miss Butterworth, you dropped five small spangles from your gown. You wore a dress encrusted with black sequins, did you not? Furthermore, you displaced the inkstand. Well, I shall never again place blind trust in circumstantial evidence alone. I saw these tokens of a woman's presence, heard what the boy recounted regarding the well-dressed lady who sent him to the drugstore with a message for the police, and drew the inevitable conclusion—that it was this woman who brandished the assassin's dagger, not the deaf-and-dumb butler who, until now, has borne the weight of the blame. Thus, I was eager to find her, little anticipating the result of my efforts, or that I would soon owe you my most humble apologies."

"Do not apologize to me," she interrupted warmly. "I had no business to be there, or at least, I erred grievously by leaving those five spangles upon Mr. Adams's wretched floor. I was merely passing by the house. If I had been the woman I once was—a woman untouched by mysteries—I would have continued on my way rather than turning aside. Sir, it is a strange sensation to find oneself, however innocent, cast by an entire city as the motive or cause of a terrible murder, especially when one has spent time studying crime and pursuing criminals. I admit I do not relish the experience. But I courted it. If I had not been so curious... But it was not curiosity I felt. I will never claim I am subject to mere idle curiosity; it was the look upon the young man's face. But I forget myself. I am rambling when I ought to tell a coherent tale. Not my usual habit, sir—you know me well—but I am not entirely myself at this moment. I declare I am more disturbed by this revelation of my indiscretion than I was by Mr. Trohm's declaration of affection in Lost Man's Lane! Grant me time, Mr. Gryce; in a few moments, I shall be more lucid."

"I grant you time," he returned with a low, deferential bow. "The half-dozen questions I yearn to ask remain unspoken, and I sit here, as you must acknowledge, a monument to patience."

"So you surmise this deed was committed by an outsider," she broke in suddenly. "Most of the journals—I read them with great care this morning—ascribe the crime to the man you mentioned. There appears to be sound reasoning for doing so. The case is not simple, Mr. Gryce; it is fraught with complications—I recognized that immediately, and that is why... but I shall not squander another moment on apologies. You have a right to any fragment of fact I may have gathered during my unfortunate visit, and there is one detail omitted from every account of the murder. Mr. Adams had other visitors in those fatal minutes preceding his death. A young man and a young woman were with him. I saw them emerge from the house at the exact moment I was passing—"

"Please, Miss Butterworth, relate your story more simply. What first drew your attention to the house?"

"There! That is the second time you have reminded me to be direct. You will not have to again, Mr. Gryce. To begin: I noticed the house because I always notice it. I never pass it without contemplating its ancient history and speculating on its current inhabitants. Consequently, when I found myself before it yesterday afternoon, en route to the art exhibition, I naturally looked up. Whether by providence or chance, I cannot say, the inner door of the vestibule burst open at that precise instant, and a young man appeared in the hall, carrying a young woman in his arms. He seemed in a state of intense agitation, while she appeared to be in a dead faint. Before the crowd could take note, he placed her upright, took her arm, and hurried her down the steps and into the throng of passersby, among whom they vanished. I, as you may believe, stood rooted to the spot in astonishment, striving to discern their direction, and lingering long enough to peer into the venerable interior of this old house, which the young man had inadvertently left ajar. As I gazed in, I heard a cry from within. It was muffled and distant, yet unmistakably a scream of terror and anguish. Driven by an impulse I may live to regret, as it seems destined to plunge me into further unpleasantness, I rushed up the steps and entered, closing the door behind me lest others follow.

"So far, I had acted purely on instinct. Once inside that dim hall, however, I paused to question my business there and what excuse I should offer if I encountered the occupants. But a repetition of the cry, emanating clearly from the farthest room on the parlor floor, coupled with a sharp memory of the young man's wandering eye and drawn countenance as he staggered forth a moment before, with a nearly fainted woman in his arms, compelled me onward despite my feminine instincts. Before I knew it, I stood in the circular study before the prostrate form of a seemingly dying man. He lay as you likely found him shortly thereafter, the cross upon his breast and a dagger in his heart. Yet his right hand trembled violently. When I stooped to lift his head, he gave a shudder and then sank into eternal stillness. I, a stranger from the street, had witnessed his final breath, while the young man who had departed..."

"Can you describe him? Did you encounter him close enough for recognition?"

"Yes, I think I would know him again. I can at least describe his appearance. He wore a checked suit, exceedingly dapper, and was taller and finer-looking than usual. But his chief peculiarity lay in his expression. I have never seen on any face, not even on the stage at the climax of the most heart-wrenching tragedy, a greater accumulation of mortal passion warring with the imperative necessity for restraint. The young girl whose blond head rested on his shoulder looked like a saint trapped in the grip of a demon. She had seen death, but he... But I prefer not to interpret that expressive countenance. It escaped my view almost immediately, and likely calmed itself before the throng he entered, or we would be discussing him today. The girl seemed devoid of almost all feeling. I should not remember her."
