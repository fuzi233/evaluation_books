If any reader lingering over these pages finds themselves curious to trace the quiet, out-of-the-way places I reference, let the record stand clear: St Bertrand de Comminges and Viborg are no inventions, you could find their cobblestones and half-ruined chapels marked on any good map, if you dared to seek them out. When I wrote *Oh, Whistle, and I’ll Come to You*, the salt-crusted, wind-bitten shore of Felixstowe was the quiet, real foundation under every line of that tale.

As for all the scattered fragments of dusty, niche erudition that peek out between the lines of these stories? Hardly a one of them is rooted in real history. There never was, tucked away in the locked back stacks of any old library, no strange, obscure volume quite like the one I cite within this very story, *The Treasure of Abbot Thomas*.

*Canon Alberic’s Scrap-book* was first written in 1894, and printed not long after in the *National Review*. *Lost Hearts* slipped out to readers first in the pages of the *Pall Mall Magazine*. The five stories that followed, most of them read aloud to a huddle of close friends on frosty Christmas nights in the oak-panelled rooms of King’s College, Cambridge? I barely remember the exact order I drafted them in, save that I wrote *Number 13* in 1899, while *The Treasure of Abbot Thomas* itself was composed in the drowsy, sun-drenched height of summer 1904. You might not think a tale of buried monastic secrets and a thing that crawls after seekers of long-hidden gold could be born on a bright July afternoon, but the oldest, hungriest ghosts do not only walk in dark.

M. R. James

St Bertrand de Comminges clings to a wind-scoured Pyrenean spur, half-collapsed cobblestones sliding down its slope towards Toulouse, the air sharp with pine resin and the faint, perpetual damp of mountain mist. By 1883 it was little more than a ghost of its old bishopric self, fewer than a thousand huddled inhabitants, the vast cathedral on its hill looming over them like a sleeping stone giant, its crevices stuffed with centuries of forgotten candle wax and rot. It was spring of that year that Dennistoun, a Cambridge archaeologist who’d spent the last six months chasing lost medieval manuscript fragments across southern France, arrived from Toulouse at dawn, leaving his two far less enthusiastic travel companions to loiter over café crème in their hotel, promising to meet him the next morning for the short half-hour tour of the cathedral they’d grudgingly agreed to. Dennistoun, meanwhile, had a whole notebook to fill and dozens of photographic plates to burn, every nook and cranny of the great old church mapped, measured, captured on film before the sun dipped below the peaks.

The brusque landlady of the Chapeau Rouge fetched the sacristan for him that morning, and when the little wizened old man shuffled in, Dennistoun’s first thought was that he looked less like a church caretaker than a hunted animal. His shoulders were perpetually hunched as if bracing for a blow from behind, his eyes darting over his shoulder every few steps, the muscles in his jaw tight as fiddle string. Dennistoun first guessed he was henpecked, then suffering from a persistent delusion, but the longer he watched him, the more certain he grew the persecutor at his heels was far more formidable than any shrewish wife.

For hours Dennistoun was too absorbed in his notes and camera to pay the old man much mind, but every time he glanced up, the sacristan was hovering not three feet away, huddling against the cold stone wall or pressed into the shadow of a 14th century choir stall, flinching at every faint draft that whistled through the rafters. After a while Dennistoun began to feel guilty, certain he was keeping the old man from his lunch, maybe even making him nervous that he’d make off with the cathedral’s ivory crozier or the dusty stuffed crocodile hanging above the font.

“Go on home, won’t you?” he said at last. “I can finish my notes alone, lock me in if you like. I’ve at least two more hours here, and it’s freezing in this stone draft.”

The old man went white to the lips, so terrified his hands shook. “Monsieur cannot be left alone here! Never, never. Two hours, three hours, it makes no difference. I have breakfasted, I am not cold, monsieur.”

Dennistoun shrugged to himself, deciding the old fool could stay if he insisted. Before the two hours were up, he’d photographed every carving in the stalls, the half-ruined giant organ, Bishop John de Mauléon’s carved choir screen, the tattered fragments of tapestry and glass in the treasure chamber, the sacristan still at his heels, whipping around as if stung every time one of the great empty church’s strange, muffled noises drifted to their ears. Once, Dennistoun swore he heard a thin, metallic laugh, high up in the tower, as if someone was dragging a rusted key along the bars of a locked cage. When he glanced at the sacristan, the old man’s face was ashen. “It is no one,” he whispered. “The tower door has been locked for a hundred years.”

A moment later, Dennistoun was pausing before a dark, cracked altarpiece depicting the miracles of St Bertrand, the Latin text beneath the faded paint reading *Qualiter S. Bertrandus liberavit hominem quem diabolus diu volebat strangulare* — How St Bertrand delivered a man whom the Devil long sought to strangle. He turned to jest with the sacristan, only to find the old man on his knees, hands clasped so tight his knuckles shone white, tears streaming down his wrinkled cheeks, praying so hard his whole body shook. Dennistoun pretended to notice nothing, but a cold prickle ran up his spine. There was no rational reason for a local sacristan to be so broken by a 300 year old daub.

The short Pyrenean day was drawing to a close, the cathedral filling with long, inky shadows, the faint muffled footfalls and half-heard whispers that had drifted through the rafters all day growing louder, more insistent. The sacristan’s whole body tensed when Dennistoun finally packed away his notebook and camera, hurrying him towards the west door under the tower to ring the Angelus. The great bell Bertrande boomed out high above them, its voice rolling over the pine trees and down the mountain streams, and for a moment the whole town fell dead quiet.

On the steps outside, the sacristan hesitated, then blurted out that if monsieur was a collector of old books, he had something at home that might interest him, not a hundred yards from the church. Dennistoun’s mind immediately ran to the usual sad 16th century missal every small French town had tucked away, but he agreed to go, half amused, half vaguely wary that he might be getting led into some petty robbery, and made a point of mentioning his two large, travel-weary friends would be arriving from Toulouse at dawn the next day. The moment he said it, the sacristan’s whole face relaxed, as if a great weight had lifted from his chest. “That is well,” he said, bright for the first time all day. “Very well. It is safest to travel with company, sometimes.”

The little stone house they reached bore the carved shield of Alberic de Mauléon, collateral descendant of the old bishop, a canon of Comminges from 1680 to 1701, its upper windows boarded up, its walls streaked with decades of mountain rain. The door opened before they could knock, and a young woman stepped out, the sacristan’s daughter, her face pale and tight with a familiar, hunted anxiety. She brightened when she saw Dennistoun, and the two whispered for a moment out of Dennistoun’s earshot, the old man saying only “He was laughing in the church,” and the girl’s eyes going wide with terror.

Inside, the small high room was lit only by a flickering wood fire, a tall black crucifix with a painted wooden figure of Christ reaching almost to the ceiling. Under it stood a heavy oak chest, from which the sacristan drew a great folio volume wrapped in white cloth stitched with a crude red cross. When the cloth fell away, Dennistoun’s breath caught. It was Canon Alberic’s scrap-book, the sides stamped with his gold coat of arms, 150 pages almost every one bearing a fragment of illuminated manuscript: 7th century Genesis leaves, unrivalled 13th century English Psalter illuminations, and 20 pages of uncial Latin he recognised at once as fragments of the long lost *On the Words of Our Lord* by Papias, a text scholars had believed destroyed for 700 years. He would empty his entire bank account to buy this book, he decided, no matter the cost.

“Turn to the end,” the sacristan whispered, pale lips trembling.

Dennistoun turned the pages, past one treasure after another, to find two sheets of 17th century paper tucked into the back. The first was a carefully drawn plan of the cathedral’s south aisle and cloisters, marked with planetary symbols, a few Hebrew words, a gilded cross in the north-west corner, beneath it scrawled the notes of a 1694 treasure hunt: *Shall I find it? Thou shalt. Shall I become rich? Thou wilt. Shall I live an object of envy? Thou wilt. Shall I die in my bed? Thou wilt.*

He turned the leaf.

The sepia drawing that stared back at him hit him so hard he felt his knees go weak. At first glance it looked like a Biblical scene: King Solomon on his twelve-stepped throne, leaning forward with outstretched sceptre, his face twisted with a horror so deep it bordered on revulsion. Five guards stood below him, one dead on the pavement, his neck twisted so far his chin touched his spine, eyeballs bulging from their sockets. The other four were frozen half ready to flee, held fast only by their faith in their king. Their eyes were all fixed on the thing crouching in their midst. It was no man. A mass of matted coarse black hair covered a body almost reduced to skeleton, wire-taut muscles standing out under dusky pale skin that was also furred, the hands tipped with long, curved, horny talons like a vulture’s. Its eyes, touched in with blazing sepia yellow, burned with a beast’s unblinking, seething hate. Dennistoun would later show a photograph of the drawing to a notoriously unimaginative morphology lecturer, who refused to be left alone for three days after, and slept with a lamp lit by his bed for a fortnight. Everyone who saw the image said the same thing: it was not drawn from imagination. It was drawn from life.

The sacristan had his face pressed into his hands, whimpering. His daughter was telling her beads so fast the clicks blurred together.

“How much for the book?” Dennistoun managed to say, his throat dry.

“Two hundred and fifty francs.”

Dennistoun stared. It was worth thousands. He tried to protest, to offer more, but the old man shook his head violently, shoving the extra notes back at him. “Two hundred and fifty, no more. Take it. We cannot keep it any longer.”

Dennistoun paid, they signed the receipt, shared a glass of thin sour wine, and the sacristan straightened up for the first time all day, no longer glancing over his shoulder, a faint, shaky laugh breaking out of him. He insisted on walking Dennistoun back to the inn three, four times, and Dennistoun refused each time, eager to examine his new prize alone under his own lamp. As he left, the daughter pressed a heavy silver crucifix on a chain around his neck, her hands ice cold. “It is blessed,” she whispered. “Do not take it off before you lock your door. Then never be without it.”

Dennistoun laughed a little to himself as he walked back, the book tucked tight under his arm, the moon bright on the cobblestones. He heard faint steps behind him, but when he turned no one was there. Back in his inn bedroom, he locked the door behind him, set the scrapbook on the table, and slipped the crucifix off his neck to lay it down beside it for a moment while he lit his pipe.

Then he saw the hand.

Resting on the red tablecloth, not three inches from his elbow, was a pale dusky thing, all bone and sinew, covered in coarse black hair, the curved talons glinting sharp as shards of broken glass. He looked up, and there it was, standing right behind his chair, its other claw raised over his scalp, the matted black hair falling over its skeletal shoulders, no nose, its thin beast’s jaw lined with sharp yellow teeth, those burning yellow eyes fixed on him, full of a ravenous, hungry hate.

Dennistoun did not think. He grabbed for the silver crucifix on the table, screaming so loud the walls shook. The two inn serving men burst through the door seconds later, saw no shape, but were thrown bodily aside by a blast of cold sulphurous wind that tore past them and vanished out the window, to find Dennistoun crumpled on the floor, fainted dead away, the crucifix clutched so tight in his hand it had left a deep purple indent in his palm.

By dawn the next day his two friends had arrived. The sacristan crept to the inn at sunrise, listened to the landlady’s account of the night, and did not even look surprised. “I have seen it twice,” he said, when they pressed him. “A thousand times I have felt it breathing down my neck. It followed my family from Canon Alberic’s house for three generations. Now it is gone from us. I will sleep sweet now.” He told them no more, refused to speak of the book’s long history, and died that summer. His daughter moved away and married, never understanding the terror that had hung over her father his whole life.

Scrawled on the back of that terrible sepia drawing, in Canon Alberic’s own scratchy ink hand, were the lines no one noticed until weeks later, faded and brown:
The gold is mine. The bargain is struck. I shall die in my bed as promised. But the third who takes the book shall be his.

As near as surviving parish records can confirm, it was September 1811 when a dust-caked post-chaise rattled to a halt before the oaken doors of Aswarby Hall, deep in Lincolnshire’s flat, wind-scoured fens. The small boy who was its only passenger—Stephen Elliott, newly orphaned after his parents’ fever took them six months prior in Warwickshire—leaped down before the footman could open his door, his bare cheeks stung sharp by the early autumn air, and stared up at the house that was to be his new home.

It was a tall, square red-brick pile built in Queen Anne’s reign, a stark classical portico of stone pillars tacked on in 1790, its dozens of tall narrow windows glowing amber in the low evening light like smouldering coals, not glass. A pediment pierced by a round bull’s-eye window crowned the front, and two wings strung to the main block by glassed colonnades held the stables and out offices, each capped by a gilded vane that glinted like a shard of flame. Beyond, the park stretched flat and endless, dotted with ancient oaks and fringed by dark stands of fir that clawed at the soft blue sky. Far off, hidden in the trees at the park edge, the parish church clock struck six, the sound drifting slow and soft on the wind, and its golden weathercock was the only other thing catching the light. For a breath, Stephen felt a fragile, homesick sort of hope: this place might be kind to him.

No one in the shire could quite fathom why the austere, reclusive Mr. Abney—his distant elderly cousin, whose offer of shelter had come out of nowhere—would take a small orphan boy into his quiet, book-locked household. Cambridge’s Greek professor had once said no man alive knew more of the dark lost rites of late paganism than the master of Aswarby. His library held every existing volume on the Orphic mysteries, Mithraic worship, and the wild doctrines of the Neo-Platonists. The marble entrance hall held a great imported marble statue of Mithras slaying the bull, written up at length in the *Gentleman’s Magazine*, and he’d published a whole series of obscure papers on the secret superstitions of the late Roman empire. He was a man wrapped tight in his manuscripts, and the whole neighbourhood gossiped nonstop about his bizarre decision to take on the orphaned Stephen.

When the front door swung open, Abney himself darted out of his study, thin bony hands rubbing together so fast the knuckles whitened, his long pale face stretched into what he clearly thought was a welcoming smile.
“How are you, my boy? How old are you?” he blurted, then paused without waiting for an answer. “You aren’t too tired from the journey to eat your supper, I hope?”
“Quite well, thank you, sir,” Stephen said, bewildered.
“Good lad. And how old are you, my boy?”

The second time he asked, Stephen’s skin prickled. “I turn twelve next birthday, sir.”
“Your birthday—eleventh of September, isn’t it? Splendid. Nearly a whole year away. I like to write these things down, you see, in my little book. Quite sure you’re twelve? No mistake?”
Stephen nodded, and Abney waved the staid butler Parkes off to take the boy down to the housekeeper’s quarters.

Mrs Bunch was the warmest thing in the whole of Aswarby, plump, flour-dusted, perpetually wiping her hands on her apron, and she and Stephen were thick as thieves within fifteen minutes. Born locally fifty-five years prior, she’d lived at the Hall for twenty, and knew every crevice, every forgotten old story the place held. She answered all his silly eager questions: who built the broken stone temple at the end of the laurel walk? Why did the portrait of the old man on the staircase have a skull tucked under his hand, half hidden from view? But there were two stories she hesitated to tell, until one cold November evening Stephen sat cross-legged by her fire and asked, all serious, “Is Mr Abney a good man? Will he go to heaven?”

Mrs Bunch huffed, but her smile faded. “Bless the child, of course he’s kind. Don’t you know he took two little homeless waifs in, years back? One little gipsy girl, Phoebe, the one he found wandering the lanes, and then a little foreign Italian boy, named Jevanny, who played the hurdy-gurdy on the drive.” She spoke slow, picking at a loose thread on her apron. The girl had stayed three weeks, Mrs Bunch said, and then one morning she was gone. No tracks, no trace, no note. Abney had all the Hall ponds dragged for her, turned the park upside down, swore he was frantic with worry. But there’d been gipsy singing drifting around the house all the night she vanished, Parkes swore he heard voices calling for her in the woods, and everyone assumed her people had snuck her away. The boy, Jevanny? He’d disappeared the exact same way, one winter morning, left his hurdy-gurdy sitting on the highest pantry shelf, still covered in rosin dust. No one ever saw him again.

That night, Stephen had the dream. His bedroom was at the far end of the top floor passage, beside an old disused bathroom kept locked, its upper door half glass, the old muslin curtain long gone. He found himself staring through that glass, the moon shining bright through the bathroom window, and there on the lead-lined bath laid a figure: thin, dust-coloured, wrapped in a yellowed shroud, lips pulled tight in a faint, terrible smile, both hands pressed so hard to its chest Stephen thought he could see the bones digging through the skin. A thin, gurgling moan seeped out of its mouth, and its arms began to twitch.

Stephen woke screaming, standing barefoot on the cold passage boards, the moon full on his face. Against every instinct of a twelve-year-old boy, he tiptoed to the bathroom door and peered in. The bath was empty. He ran back to his bed and pulled the covers over his head.

Mrs Bunch sewed a new muslin curtain over that glass the next morning, no questions asked. Stephen told Abney about the dream over breakfast, and his cousin’s eyes lit up, scribbling pages of notes in his little leather notebook, muttering that this was exactly as the old texts had said. Abney began reminding him, almost daily, that the spring equinox was coming—ancient peoples believed it was a critical, dangerous time for young boys, he said, he ought to lock his bedroom window tight every night, Censorinus wrote whole chapters on the dangers that lurked for the young then.

Two weeks later, Mrs Bunch was mending Stephen’s nightgown when she yelped, holding it up to the fire. The left side of the chest was scored full of long, parallel slits, six inches long, some half-cut through the linen, as if someone had dragged long sharp nails slow, deliberate, right over where his heart lay. “They’re the same scratches that are on my bedroom door,” Stephen said, bewildered. “Too high for a cat to reach. I never made them.”

Mrs Bunch’s face went white as flour. She snatched a candle and ran upstairs, came back ten minutes later shaking. “Not a word to Mr Abney, you hear? They’re marks like my old uncle used to talk about, the ones the Chinese sailors say their ghosts leave. Lock your door every night before you say your prayers, Master Stephen. Lock it tight.”

The next evening, Parkes the butler stumbled into Mrs Bunch’s parlour, so flustered he’d forgotten to wipe the wine stains off his apron. “I’m not going down to the far end of the wine cellar after dark, not for no man,” he said. “Heard voices down there, two of them, whispering in a foreign tongue, talking about stolen hearts. Like two children, down there in the dark bin. You can go listen if you don’t believe me. I’m not going back.” He clammed up the second he noticed Stephen in the corner, pretended he was joking, and wouldn’t say another word.

March 24, 1812, was a day of screaming, tearing wind, the kind that made it sound like hundreds of unseen people were rushing past the house, screaming for something they’d lost. After luncheon, Abney found Stephen by the library shelf and touched his shoulder, his fingers cold as ice. “Come to my study at eleven tonight, Stephen. I’ve got something to show you, something that will shape your whole future. Don’t tell Mrs Bunch, don’t tell Parkes. Go to your room at your usual bedtime, no one needs to know.”

Stephen peeked through the library door on his way upstairs that evening. The old brazier he’d always seen in the corner was dragged out before the fire, smoking thick with stinking incense. A gilded silver cup full of dark red port sat on the oak table, beside a stack of yellowed handwritten pages. Abney was sprinkling more frankincense into the brazier, and didn’t notice the boy watching.

The wind died after ten, leaving the night dead still, the moon hanging full and white as a polished bone. Stephen stood at his open bedroom window, looking out over the mere, and heard strange cries drifting across the water: not owls, not waterfowl, high thin despairing wails, drifting closer, until they seemed to circle the shrubberies at the edge of the lawn. Then they stopped.

Two figures were standing on the gravelled terrace below his window. A girl, thin, in a ragged gipsy dress, hands clasped tight over her chest, that same faint terrible smile from his dream. Beside her stood a thin ragged Italian boy, black hair matted, his skin so translucent Stephen could see the moon shine right through his outstretched, claw-nailed hands. And on the left side of his chest, there was nothing but a wide black gaping hole, empty where his heart had been. A wordless, hungry scream flooded straight into Stephen’s head, no sound needed, so sharp it made his teeth ache. The two of them glided across the gravel, silent as shadows, and vanished around the corner of the Hall.

Stephen grabbed his candle and ran for Abney’s study, his heart hammering so hard he thought it would burst out of his own chest. The door was not locked, but his knocks got no answer. He heard a choked, gurgling cry from inside, then silence. He shoved the door open so hard it slammed against the wall.

The scattered pages on Abney’s desk told the whole story, for when Stephen was old enough to read them:
The ancients wrote that you could gain total power over the spirits that rule the elements, defy death itself, steal immortality, by absorbing the hearts of three living people under the age of twenty-one. Simon Magus did it, killing a young boy to steal his heart so he could fly and turn invisible. Hermes Trismegistus laid out the ritual plainly, step by step. For twenty years, I have worked at this. My first subject: Phoebe Stanley, gipsy girl, taken March 24 1792. My second: Giovanni Paoli, the hurdy-gurdy boy, taken March 23 1805. My third, final, perfect sacrifice: my cousin Stephen Elliott. His heart will be cut from his living body tonight, burnt to ash, stirred into the red wine, and the power will be mine. The disused bathroom and the far wine cellar make perfect hiding places for their remains. Their feeble ghosts can do no harm to a man of philosophic mind.

Abney was slumped back in his leather chair, eyes bulging, his face twisted with unnameable terror. A great ragged gaping wound tore through his left side, right over his heart. The clean sharp ceremonial knife he’d laid out on the table was still spotless, no blood on it. The study window stood wide open to the moon. The coroner ruled he’d been mauled to death by a stray wild cat that had wandered in from the fens. But Stephen knew better. He caught one last flash of two small figures vanishing into the dark of the fir wood that night, their hands pressed gentle to their chests, their long-lost hearts beating warm and whole at last, free of Aswarby Hall, free of the man who had stolen them.

Some years back, I had the pleasure of recounting to you the strange misadventure that befell my friend Dennistoun, hunting rare art for Cambridge’s museum. He spoke of it to very few on his return, but word of it trickled through museum circles—including to Mr. Williams, the soft-spoken curator in charge of a rival university’s topographical print collection.

At first, the story struck him as a useful reassurance: his institution was never in the business of hunting down cursed ancient manuscripts, that was the job of the nearby Shelburnian Library. He only had to tend to his quiet, homely work, filling gaps in the museum’s unmatched archive of English engravings and sketches of country houses, churches, and long-gone market towns. But as it turned out, even the most mundane, familiar field of collecting can hold dark, unlooked-for corners. And Mr. Williams was about to stumble straight into one.

Every collector of topographical art knows that Mr. J.W. Britnell, the London print dealer, is indispensable. His frequent, carefully catalogued lists of ever-changing stock were Williams’ constant bread and butter. Last February, one of those catalogues landed on his desk, tucked in with a short typed note from Britnell himself: *DEAR SIR, We beg to call your attention to No. 978 in our accompanying catalogue, which we shall be glad to send on approval. Yours faithfully, J. W. BRITNELL.*

Williams flipped immediately to No. 978. The entry read: 978.—Unknown. Interesting mezzotint: View of a manor-house, early part of the century. 15 by 10 inches; black frame. £2 2s.

It didn’t sound special. Two guineas seemed far too steep for a random, unidentifiable print. But Britnell knew his stock and his customers better than almost anyone, so Williams scribbled a postcard asking for it to be sent on approval alongside a handful of other items he’d marked. He gave it no further thought through the rest of his workday.

As parcels always do, it arrived a day later than expected, turned up by Saturday afternoon post after Williams had already left for the day. The museum attendant brought it round to his college rooms so he wouldn’t have to wait the whole weekend to sort through it, and he found it sitting on his table when he trundled in from a round of golf, soaked through at the cuffs, with his friend Professor Binks in tow.

The mezzotint was larger than he’d expected, cased in a dented old black frame, the kind you’d find hanging ignored in the parlour of a half-forgotten country inn. It was a shoddy, amateur piece, the kind of indifferent engraving that makes you squint to make out the lines. The house was plain, late 18th century, three tiers of sashed windows, a parapet dotted with stone urns, a small portico dead centre, trees huddled at its flanks, a wide empty lawn stretching out in front. The only mark on the margin read *A. W. F. sculpsit*. There was no other inscription. On the back, a torn paper label bore only the last scraps of two lines: —ngley Hall, and —ssex. Half the name was ripped clean away.

Williams turned it over with half-amused contempt, certain Britnell had misjudged this one. Five shillings, maybe, would have been a fair price. He poured the tea, and the two of them bickered good-naturedly over the terrible shots they’d played that afternoon, until Binks picked the framed print up off the side table.

“What’s this place, then?” he said, squinting at it. “There’s half a figure right at the front edge, isn’t there? The moonlight’s done remarkably well, for something so scrappy.”

Williams leaned over. He hadn’t noticed that before. A dark, smudged head, all muffled in heavy cloth, back turned to the viewer, staring straight at the house. Hardly even a distinct mark, just a blot of ink. Still, Williams told himself, it wasn’t worth spending museum funds on an unidentified print for two guineas. Binks left soon after, and Williams spent an hour flipping through gazetteers, frustrated that half the place name was missing, before he trundled off to college dinner.

Later that night, a small group of his colleagues drifted back to his rooms for whisky and whist. During a lull, Williams passed the mezzotint over to a man who dabbled in print collecting, not even glancing at it himself as he poured soda for his guests. The man frowned, peered at it for a long minute. “That’s far better work than it looks at first glance,” he said. “That figure on the lawn—uncanny, isn’t it? Looks like it’s crawling.”

Williams didn’t bother to look over. It was past midnight when the last guest left. He tidied the cups, blew out his main lamp, lit his bedroom candle—and his eye fell on the print, face up on the table.

He very nearly dropped the candle.

It was impossible. But it was right there, plain as day, dead in the middle of the lawn where no man had been before: a figure, crawling on all fours straight for the house, swaddled in thick black cloth, a stark white cross stitched to the back of its garment. The hair on the back of his neck stood straight up. He didn’t stop to argue with what his eyes were seeing. He grabbed the print by its corner, carried it across the passage to his second spare set of rooms, locked it tight in a drawer, barred both doors, and scribbled down a full, signed account of exactly what he had seen before he climbed into bed.

He woke late Sunday, his mind racing. He had two witnesses already, he reminded himself: Binks had seen the smudged head, the last guest Garwood had seen the crawling figure. He called his colleague Nisbet over for breakfast, and when they’d finished their toast, he ran across the passage, hauled the print out face down, and handed it to Nisbet, asking him to describe every single detail without prompting.

Nisbet blinked. “It’s a moonlit view of an English country house. Waning moon, thin clouds drifting. No figures on the lawn at all—wait. The ground floor window, left of the front door, that’s standing wide open.”

Williams snatched it from him. It was true. The crawling figure was gone entirely. The window stood ajar. “He got in,” Williams breathed, cold all over. He handed Nisbet his signed note from the night before, and the two men sat in silence for a long minute, realizing they were watching an old, unburied tragedy play out one frame at a time.

They tracked down Garwood, who confirmed he’d seen the figure halfway across the lawn the night before, the white mark on its back bright against the black cloth. Nisbet set up his camera to photograph the print, and the three of them agreed to leave it out on the table, locked up, to go for a long afternoon walk, to avoid gossiping about it to anyone else before they knew what they were dealing with.

They trundled back up his staircase just after five o’clock, and found the door unbarred. His long-serving college servant Filcher was sat opposite the table, staring at the print, his face ashen, his usual impeccable composure entirely gone. He jumped when he saw them. “Beg pardon, sir, I didn’t mean to sit,” he stammered. “But that picture… I wouldn’t let my little girl so much as glance at it. There’s a thing there, carrying a baby. It’s no fit sight for anyone, sir, not when you least expect it to pop out at you.”

The three men crowded round the print at once. The open window was now shut. The figure was back on the lawn, upright, taking long, ragged strides straight for the house. Its face was entirely swaddled in black cloth, nothing visible but a domed white forehead and a few straggling, lank hairs. It clutched a small limp child tight to its chest, its stick-thin bare legs eating up the grass. They took turns watching it until seven, and it never moved another inch. They left it to go to dinner, and when they came back, the figure was gone entirely. The house stood empty under the moon.

They pored over guidebooks late into the night, until at half-past eleven Williams hit the entry in Murray’s *Guide to Essex*, his fingers shaking as he read it out loud:
16½ miles, _Anningley_. The church has been an interesting building of Norman date, but was extensively classicized in the last century. It contains the tomb of the family of Francis, whose mansion, Anningley Hall, a solid Queen Anne house, stands immediately beyond the churchyard in a park of about 80 acres. The family is now extinct, the last heir having disappeared mysteriously in infancy in the year 1802. The father, Mr Arthur Francis, was locally known as a talented amateur engraver in mezzotint. After his son’s disappearance he lived in complete retirement at the Hall, and was found dead in his studio on the third anniversary of the disaster, having just completed an engraving of the house, impressions of which are of considerable rarity.

The college Bursar, old Green, came back from Brighton the next day and confirmed the house was Anningley Hall, no question. When Williams asked him what the old local story was, Green paused, his face going quiet. “Old Francis hated the poaching families that had lived on that land for centuries, you know,” he said. “He drove every last one of them off his estate, all but one. The last of that old line, bitter as the grave. I recollect just the same thing in my own parish.”

“What, like the man in *Tess o’ the Durbervilles*?” Williams put in.

Any soul who has trundled the rutted lanes of Eastern England knows the small, damp country houses dotting the landscape like half-forgotten tombs: stucco-faced Italianate blocks, red-brick bones peeking through cracks, set in parks of eighty to a hundred acres where the mist never fully lifts even at midday. For years I have been drawn to them, the grey split-oak palings sagging under ivy, the meres fringed with reeds that whisper after dark, the distant woods that hold shapes you cannot quite make out at the edge of your vision. I have daydreamed of owning one, of wandering its galleries, of turning the pages of a 13th-century Psalter in its dim library, of imagining the lives lived there long ago—though none of those daydreams ever brushed against the dark truth of Castringham Hall in Suffolk.

Much has been altered to the building since the events I recount, but the hallmarks remain: pillared portico, white stucco over far older brick, park fringe of gnarled oak, the mere that glints like dull steel at dusk. Only the one feature that set it apart from every other Suffolk country house is gone. From the park lawn, if you stood on the right side of the drive, you could once see a great ancient ash growing no more than six yards from the Hall’s west wall, its thick boughs brushing the plaster so close it looked like the tree had pressed its whole weight against the building, leaning in to listen to every word spoken inside. It had stood there since the moat was filled in and the Elizabethan hall raised, and by 1690 it had grown to a monstrous, full-grown bulk, its bark ridged like the skin of a leper.

That year, the Suffolk countryside was roiling with witch trials. To this day we cannot untangle how much of that universal, rabid fear was rooted in nothing but mass cruelty, how much was born of something far older and far worse. This story makes me loath to dismiss the old testimonies as mere superstition. You may judge for yourself.

Castringham gave up one victim to the noose. Her name was Mrs. Mothersole, and she was no ragged beggar: she owned a good cottage, had respectable farmers who swore to her good name, pleaded with the jury to spare her. But it was the testimony of the then-owner of Castringham Hall, Sir Matthew Fell, that sealed her fate. He swore under oath that he had watched her, three full moon nights running, climb the ash tree outside his west window in nothing but her thin shift, a curved black knife in her hand, snipping off tiny twigs and muttering to something hidden in the branches, something he could never see. Each time he tried to rush down and catch her, she heard his step, and by the time he reached the lawn, all he saw was a hare that ran across the path toward the village, moving faster than any hare had any right to move. The third night he chased it all the way to her cottage, banged on her door for 15 minutes, and when she opened it she was yawning, half-asleep, her bed still warm behind her, no twigs, no knife anywhere to be seen.

On that evidence, stacked on top of dozens of petty tales of blighted crops and sick children, Mrs. Mothersole was found guilty. She was hanged a week later, along with five other wretches, on the gallows hill outside Bury St Edmunds.

Sir Matthew, Deputy-Sheriff that year, stood on the drizzly March grass to watch her die. The other prisoners wept, or hung their heads, but Mrs. Mothersole stared at the crowd with a snarl on her face that a local diarist wrote made even the hangman flinch: every man who laid a hand on her said later her eyes glowed like no human’s should. She did not struggle. The only words she spoke, under her breath, repeated three times, were “There will be guests at the Hall.”

Sir Matthew could not shake the look she had given him, the venom in that quiet line. He rode home with the parish Vicar, Mr. Crome, that evening, saying he had never believed in witch-finding fanaticism, that he had only told the truth of what he saw, that the whole business had turned his stomach. The Vicar told him he had done his duty, and the pair tried to put it out of their minds.

A month and a half later, the May moon hung full and fat over the park. Sir Matthew’s wife was away tending her dying mother, so he invited Mr. Crome to stay for late supper. The two talked of estate business, Sir Matthew scribbling down notes of his last wishes for his lands that would prove very useful before long. When the Vicar made to leave at half past nine, they took a turn on the gravel walk behind the house, in full sight of the great ash.

Sir Matthew stopped dead.
“What is that scuttling up and down the trunk?” he said. “No squirrel would be out at this hour, they’re all asleep in their nests.”

Mr. Crome squinted at the shifting shape in the moonlight. He could not make out its colour, but he swore until his dying day that the thing had more than four legs. Before he could say a word, it vanished into a crevice in the bark, and the two men parted, neither speaking of what they had seen. They would never share another conversation again.

The next morning, Sir Matthew did not come down for breakfast. His servants banged on his bedchamber door until their knuckles ached, then broke it down. They found him dead in his bed, his skin black and bloated, his limbs twisted into impossible angles as if he had fought something invisible to the last breath. The casement of his west window was standing wide open.

The Vicar was fetched at once, and his surviving notes of that day still turn the blood cold. The physician from Bury could find no poison in Sir Matthew’s nightly tankard of small ale, no mark of intrusion on the chamber lock. The two respectable lay women who had come to lay out his corpse screamed and ran from the room before they had even washed his chest, their palms burning as if they had plunged them into a fire, their forearms swelling up so badly they could not work for six weeks after, no mark or puncture on their skin to explain the agony. When the Vicar and the physician pressed a magnifying crystal to the dead man’s chest, they found two tiny, almost invisible pricks, nothing more.

Helpless, the Vicar picked up the small Bible Sir Matthew read every night before bed, and tried the old forbidden practice of Sortes Biblicae, three times opening the book at random: the first line his finger landed on was Luke 13:7, *Cut it down*. The second, Isaiah 13:20, *It shall never be inhabited*. The third, Job 39:30, *Her young ones also suck up blood*.

The whole parish decided the death was the work of Papist conspirators, leftover malice from the Popish Plot, and Sir Matthew was buried with full honors. His son, the second Sir Matthew, inherited the estate, and never once slept a night in that west chamber. For forty years, no one did. A strange sickness plagued his cattle: every morning, any beast left out in the park overnight was found dead, black, twisted, those same two tiny pricks on their necks. He finally locked every cow, sheep and horse in sheds after dark, and the sickness stopped preying on livestock, moving only to wild birds and deer, no hunter ever catching sight of a predator to explain the deaths.

He died in 1735, and his son Sir Richard inherited. Sir Richard was a man obsessed with all things Italian, and set about ripping out the old red brick facade of Castringham to cover it in stucco, planting cheap Roman marbles in the gardens, building a silly little imitation Sibyl’s temple on the far bank of the mere. When he expanded the family pew in the parish church, the workmen disturbed a number of old unmarked graves—including Mrs. Mothersole’s, whose position was marked on a map Mr. Crome had drawn decades before. They pried open her oak coffin, and found nothing inside. No bones, no dust, no shred of cloth. It was completely empty.

The village whispered, crossing themselves when they passed the empty plot. Sir Richard laughed, and ordered the empty coffin burned.

One blustery 1754 morning, he woke after a miserable sleepless night: his chimney smoked, something scuttled and scraped at his window nonstop, and all his game in the park was dying off fast, ruining his reputation as a sportsman. He stormed down to breakfast, announcing he would not sleep another night in his current chamber, and demanded his housekeeper find him a west-facing room far from the bustle of the household.

“There is only one such room, Sir Richard,” the housekeeper, Mrs. Chiddock, said reluctantly. “It’s Sir Matthew’s old west chamber. No one has slept there in forty years.”

“Then move my furniture in there this afternoon,” he snapped. He pushed past her, wrenched open the long-shut door, threw back the shutters, and flung the casement wide. The smell that rolled out was close, earthy, sharp with something like rot. The great ash tree stood not a dozen feet outside the pane, its branches swaying slow.

He was still ordering Mrs. Chiddock to air the room out when a young man in black stepped into the doorway: William Crome, grandson of the old Vicar, newly elected Fellow of Clare Hall, riding through to Bury to drop off a packet of his grandfather’s old papers, including the notes of the first Sir Matthew Fell’s death.

Sir Richard read the old Sortes predictions, and snickered. “Cut it down, eh?” he said. “That’s fine advice. That infernal ash tree is coming down tomorrow, no two ways about it.” He grabbed the old family Bible from the shelf, flipped it open at random to test his luck. The line stared up at him black and stark: *Thou shalt seek me in the morning, and I shall not be*. He laughed it off, called it a silly coincidence, saw young Crome on his way, and went back to planning for his guests that evening.

Dinner was at five, with the Bishop of Kilmore, Lady Mary Hervey, Sir William Kentfield, and the rest of the party drinking late over cards before they all trundled off to their separate chambers.

The next morning, Sir Richard met the Bishop on the terrace, bleary-eyed. The Bishop nodded toward the open window of the west chamber, where he had spent the night.

“None of my Irish parishioners would ever sleep in that room, Sir Richard,” he said, smiling. “They say it is the worst luck in the world to sleep with an ash tree so close to your window.”

Sir Richard huffed. “It was no luck, it was the branches scraping against my glass all night that kept me awake from midnight to four. But the tree is coming down tomorrow, so I shall not hear any more of it.”

The Bishop frowned, and gestured at the great ash, standing clear across the lawn.
“Forgive me, Sir Richard,” he said, “but look for yourself. None of the nearest branches reach within a foot of that casement. There was no gale last night. Not a twig could have touched that glass.”

Viborg, tucked in the rolling green heart of Jutland, carries its old honors lightly: bishop’s seat, a gleaming new cathedral, a lily-dusted lake, storks that nest on every gable, and the nearby stone of Finderup where Marsk Stig’s party hacked fifty-six square-headed mace blows into King Erik Glipping’s skull on St. Cecilia’s Day, 1286. This is no guidebook, though: the story that follows took place not amid the well-trodden ruins, but inside the Golden Lion, one of the tiny handful of buildings that survived the 1726 fire that ate nearly every last old, carved, timber-framed structure in the town, cathedral and town hall and all.

It is a squat, imposing red brick house, its gables stepped with corbie stones, a faded biblical text carved deep above the entry lintel, its inner courtyard cobbled and lined with black half-timber that smells of centuries of beer spill and wood smoke. The first time my cousin Anderson, an English antiquary digging through suppressed late-Roman Catholic church records of Denmark, visited Viborg, he booked a stay there, and he has never dared step over its threshold since.

He’d come for the half-burned, fire-surviving papers locked in the Viborg Rigsarkiv, and told the landlord he needed a single large room that could work as both bedroom and study, no cramped cubby on the top floor. The second floor held nothing the right size, but the first floor offered a handful of options. The landlord pushed for Number 17, but Anderson turned it down at once: its windows faced nothing but the blank, mossy wall of the neighboring house, and would be pitch dark by mid-afternoon. Number 12, looking out over the cobbled main street, was perfect: long, high-ceilinged, three windows facing the road, its only notable furnishing a heavy old cast-iron stove etched with the scene of Abraham raising his knife over Isaac, the caption *1 Bog Mose, Cap. 22* stamped just above it. He settled in, washed off the travel dust, and wandered down to the dining hall a few minutes before supper was called, pausing to glance over the blackboard that listed all lodgers by room number. It was a boring list: a local lawyer, a German travelling salesman, a handful of Copenhagen bagmen. The only oddity he noted, familiar as he was with Danish hotels, was that no Number 13 appeared anywhere on the board. He laughed it off as the usual harmless folk superstition, and resolved to tease the landlord about it later that evening.

After supper he unpacked his books and papers, and realized the novel he’d been reading on the train was still tucked in the pocket of his heavy overcoat, hanging on a peg by the dining room door. He trotted down the stairs to fetch it, and turned back toward his floor, the corridor lit dim by guttering oil lamps. When he reached the door he thought was his, the handle wouldn’t turn, and he heard a quick, shuffling scurry of movement from directly inside, as if someone had been pressed to the other side, listening. He jerked back, and saw the number nailed to the wood: 13. He stepped left at once, found Number 12, and slipped inside, but long after he’d blown out his light and curled up to sleep, the thought nagged him: the blackboard had no Number 13 written on it, but the hotel very clearly had a door for it. The room even seemed to have shifted while he’d been out: the long stretch of wall by the far trestle table he’d set his portmanteau on was several feet closer now, the ceiling seeming to have stretched higher, as if the space had breathed and contracted in his absence. He told himself it was just the dim streetlamp light playing tricks, and drifted off.

The next day he spent buried in the archives, and stumbled across a cache of letters he’d never seen referenced anywhere: a furious flurry of notes between Reformation-era Protestant leaders and the last Catholic bishop of Viborg, Jörgen Friis, all arguing about a man named Nicolas Francken – a sorcerer, *Troldmand*, they called him, who practiced forbidden rites in a house the bishop owned, hidden somewhere in the town. The reformers screamed that he had sold his soul to the devil, that his magic was poisoning every household within three streets, but the bishop refused to hand him over to a secular court, demanding he be tried before the ecclesiastical tribunal instead. Anderson pored over the letters late into the afternoon, but the archivist Herr Scavenius closed the archive before he could read the final Protestant reply, and confessed as they walked back toward the town that no one had ever been able to find where Francken’s hidden house stood: the 1560 bishopric property survey that should have listed every parcel of land the church owned in Viborg had its critical town pages torn clean out, lost to history for nearly four hundred years.

Anderson wandered back to the Golden Lion after that, and remembered to pause at the stretch of corridor that should have held Number 13. The door was there, plain as day, and through the thick wood he heard slow, heavy footsteps pacing back and forth, stopping abruptly right on the other side of the panel. A thin, hissing breath pricked out through the keyhole, and he caught a whiff of sulphur and charred old parchment that made his throat tickle. He hurried to his own room, and found it had shrunk even further: the portmanteau the porter had set on the trestle by the far wall an arm’s length away from his bed was gone. He searched the wardrobe, the space under the bed, the bathroom nook, and could not find it anywhere, and told himself the silly maid must have moved it. He went to the right-hand window to get some air, and watched the street below, and after a minute spotted the shadow of the occupant of the supposed Number 13 leaning on his own windowsill, right next to Anderson’s. The shadow was tall, thin, its head wrapped in some kind of hooded drapery, and behind it a dull, flickering red light played over the wall opposite, pulsing like embers in a fire. Anderson leaned out to see more, and the shadow snapped back from the window as if it had felt his gaze, the red light snuffing out instantly. Anderson laid the end of his cigarette on the right-hand windowsill, and climbed into bed.

He woke the next morning to the maid bringing in hot water, and demanded to know where his portmanteau had been moved. The maid laughed, confused, and gestured behind him. Anderson twisted around, and there was his portmanteau, sitting exactly where the porter had set it the day before, on the trestle at the far end of the room. He stared, bewildered, and walked to the middle window to check the weather – and there, on the sill, was the stub of his cigarette. He swore he had left it on the right-hand sill. Confused, he headed down to breakfast, and spotted a pair of leather boots set outside what he thought was the Number 13 door. He turned back to glance at the number nailed to the wood, and his blood ran cold. The door was marked 14. His own room, 12, sat directly to the left of it. There was no gap, no extra door, no space at all between the two rooms that could possibly fit a thirteenth room. The stretch of wall between 12 and 14 was blank, papered over, completely solid.

That day back in the archives, Anderson found one final surviving letter from Bishop Friis, dated only two days after the Protestant leaders had threatened to storm Francken’s house and drag him out to burn him at the stake. The note said that Nicolas Francken had been “suddenly removed from among us”, and the case was therefore closed. No record existed anywhere of how he had died. No one had ever written down where his body was buried.

That evening at supper, Anderson sat next to the landlord Herr Kristensen, and asked him point blank if the Golden Lion ever had a Number 13. Kristensen laughed, and told him he’d run hotels for thirty years, and he knew perfectly well commercial travellers would refuse to sleep in a Number 13 even if you paid them. He had never numbered a room 13 at the Golden Lion, ever. There was no space for it. Room 12 sat directly next to 14, a solid brick wall between them, no extra nook, no closet, no unused chamber.

Anderson’s blood turned to ice. To calm his nerves, he invited the landlord up to his room for a cigar after ten o’clock, under the pretense of showing him photographs of old English market towns. As he waited for Kristensen, he sat down to write letters, and listened to the sounds from the other side of his far wall – the wall that the landlord had sworn was solid brick, separating him from the lawyer in Number 14. The quiet, staid lawyer who never spoke a word at breakfast was not just moving around. He was dancing. Bare feet thudded hard on the floor on the other side of the wall, a hundred rapid stamping steps, back and forth, back and forth, getting closer and closer, until Anderson could hear the faint scrape of heavy leather boots (not bare feet) scuffing on the wood right up against the panel, and the low, hissing breath he had heard through the Number 13 keyhole the night before. The wall between his room and that empty, impossible space was warm now, almost hot, as if there was a fire blazing on the other side, and he could see the dull, pulsing red glow seeping through the cracks where the wall met the floorboards. He fumbled for the door, but his own room had shrunk, the walls closing in around him, the cast iron Abraham and Isaac stove right at his elbow, and he realized with a sickening lurch that he was no longer in Number 12.

The door he’d thought was Number 12 was gone. He was inside Number 13, and it was only getting bigger.

I shall not tell you by what strange winding of chance the papers that make up this account came into my possession until the very last page of this telling. But first, I must explain exactly what form these documents take.

They are half the raw draft of the kind of garrulous, detail-heavy travel book that flooded print in the 1840s and 50s—of which Horace Marryat’s *Journal of a Residence in Jutland and the Danish Isles* is a perfect example. These volumes chased little-known corners of the European continent, filled with woodcuts and steel engravings, tips for inns and stagecoaches, pages and pages of transcribed chats with sharp-tongued innkeepers and garrulous peasants. They were cozy, unassuming, the sort of book you could flip through on a rainy evening with no sense you were about to stumble on something that would curdle the blood. But as the writing progressed, the travel notes fell away entirely, leaving nothing but a tight, breathless record of a single terrible personal experience, written almost up to the very eve of the events that ended it.

Its author was a man named Wraxall. All I know of him comes from these pages. He was past middle age, of independent means, entirely alone in the world, no permanent home in England, drifting from hotel to boarding house to hotel. He had published one prior book, a memoir of a holiday in Brittany, that I have never been able to trace—no bibliography lists it, so it must have come out anonymously or under a pseudonym. I judge he missed being elected a Fellow of Brasenose College, Oxford, by a hair. His one fatal flaw, the one for which he paid far too high a price, was unbridled, insatiable curiosity.

In the summer of 1863, he set out to write his new book on Scandinavia, a place almost entirely unknown to ordinary English tourists of the time. He chased down old Swedish histories and family memoirs, secured letters of introduction to minor nobility, and sailed for Stockholm. After a few weeks there, a local antiquary passed him a tip: a hoard of untouched family papers was stored in the herrgård, or manor house, of Råbäck, tucked deep in the forests of Vestergothland, and the owners had granted him full permission to study them.

Råbäck stands almost exactly as it does in Dahlenberg’s 1694 *Suecia antiqua et moderna* engraving: a red brick manor with stone trim, built soon after 1600 by a scion of the great De la Gardie family, ringed by ancient gnarled timber that blocks the wind and dims the summer sun. Past its walled kitchen garden, a dark close wood crowds the shore of one of the hundreds of small, still kettle lakes that pit the region, and beyond the park wall a steep knoll of bare rock rises, its thin soil dotted with firs. At the top of that knoll sits the parish church, hemmed in by tall dark spruce trees that swallow light even at midday. Its low aisles are crammed with painted wooden pews and galleries, the western gallery holds a gaily painted old organ with silver pipes, and the flat ceiling is covered by a 17th century Last Judgement so lurid it makes the skin crawl: leaping flames, falling cities, burning ships, screaming souls, and broad grinning brown demons reaching for them. Brass coronae hang from the rafters, the pulpit is carved to look like a dollhouse crammed with tiny painted cherubs and saints, and a stand holding three hourglasses is hinged to the preacher’s desk. No English church ever smelled like that one: of cold stone, old candle wax, and something sharp, like rot.

The strangest addition juts off the north aisle: an eight-sided mausoleum built for the De la Gardie family, lit by oval windows, topped by a black copper domed roof and the lumpy pumpkin-shaped spire that 17th century Swedish architects were so fond of. There is no door connecting it to the church. It has its own set of stone steps and a heavy oak portal on the north side, half-hidden by overgrown ivy. The path down from the churchyard to the village inn takes barely four minutes.

On his first day at Råbäck, Wraxall found the church door propped open, and scribbled down all those notes. He could not get into the mausoleum, though—he could only peer through the keyhole to make out marble effigies, engraved copper sarcophagi, and a riot of armorial carvings that made him desperate to get inside. The De la Gardie family received him with great kindness, but he chose to stay at the village inn rather than the manor, to avoid straining his faltering Swedish and give himself leave to wander unobserved at all hours.

The family papers he had come to study were perfect for his book: stacks of personal letters, diaries, neatly kept 17th century account books full of vivid, anecdotal detail about the district. The founding owner of the house, Magnus De la Gardie, loomed over all of them: a brutal, iron-willed man who had crushed a local peasant uprising with unsparing force, executing ringleaders and meting out punishments so harsh the stories of them had lingered for two hundred and fifty years. His portrait hung in the manor hall: Wraxall wrote that it was not a face of beauty or kindness, but of terrible, almost preternatural power—Count Magnus was, by all accounts, one of the ugliest men who ever walked the earth. That night, he walked back to the inn at dusk, the northern summer light still pale and golden, and jotted in his notebook: I must ask the sexton to let me into that mausoleum. I saw him tonight, standing on the north steps, fumbling with the lock.

The next morning, he cornered the innkeeper to dig for local traditions about the old count. The man would not meet his eye. He spoke of the wooden horse in the manor yard, where tenants who were late to work on the count’s demesne days were tied and flogged and branded. He spoke of the families who lived on land the count wanted, whose farmhouses burned to the ground on the coldest midwinter nights, with every soul inside, no trace of an arsonist ever found. But what he kept circling back to, the thing that made his voice drop to a hoarse whisper, was that Count Magnus had gone on the Black Pilgrimage, and brought something back with him.

The moment Wraxall asked what the Black Pilgrimage was, the innkeeper panicked. He mumbled that he had been called away to Skara, ducked out the door, and did not come back until long after dark.

Wraxall turned back to the manor papers, and soon forgot the exchange poring over 1705-1710 letters between Sophia Albertina in Stockholm and her cousin at Råbäck. When he finished that stack, he reached for the nearest unopened volume on the shelf, and found it was not an account book, but a 16th century manuscript full of alchemical treatises: The Book of the Phoenix, The Book of the Thirty Words, The Book of the Toad, the *Turba Philosophorum*. Tucked into its middle pages, on a blank leaf, was a few lines scrawled in Count Magnus’s own hand, titled *Liber nigræ peregrinationis*—the Book of the Black Pilgrimage. The text read: If any man desires to obtain a long life, if he would obtain a faithful messenger and see the blood of his enemies, it is necessary that he should first go into the city of Chorazin, and there salute the prince… One word had been scrubbed out, not well enough for Wraxall to miss it: *aëris*, of the air. Below it, a single line in Latin: *Quære reliqua hujus materiei inter secretiora*. The rest of this matter is hidden among the more private things.

Wraxall stared at the portrait of Magnus in the hall for a long time that evening before heading home, his head full of the grim old noble, half-mystic half-monster, who had dabbled in magic long buried. He walked the path from the manor in a daze, not noticing the scent of pine, not seeing the pale light glint off the lake, until he almost tripped over a weathered gravestone and realized he was already at the churchyard gate. He found himself muttering out loud, half to himself: Count Magnus, there you are. I should dearly like to see you. A clatter from inside the church—some woman cleaning dropped a metal candlestick on the stone floor—snapped him back to his senses. He laughed at himself for talking to a tomb, and walked down to the inn for supper.

That night, the innkeeper brought the parish deacon to meet him, and they arranged a tour of the mausoleum for the next day. Wraxall asked the deacon if he knew anything of the village of Chorazin. The deacon went very still, then said sharply that it was the cursed town from the gospels, where old tales said the Antichrist would one day be born. When Wraxall pressed him for more, he said he had forgotten all the old stories, and made his excuses to leave.

Left alone with the innkeeper, Wraxall badgered him until the man caved. He stood so close his breath fanned Wraxall’s face, and told the story his grandfather had witnessed, ninety two years prior:

Two local poachers laughed at the warnings, and said they would go hunt in the count’s forbidden wood after dark. The rest of the village begged them not to go, said they would meet folk walking who should not be walking, who should be six feet under the ground. The two men laughed them off. It was a light midsummer night, no foresters dared patrol that wood.

My grandfather sat right here in this inn parlour, the window open, the innkeeper said. He sat with three other men, and they listened. First there was nothing. Then they heard a scream, far off, as if a man’s very soul was being twisted right out of his body. All four of them clutched at each other, and sat shivering for three quarters of an hour. Then they heard someone laugh, barely three hundred ells away. It was not a man’s laugh. No man on God’s green earth ever laughed like that. Then they heard a great heavy door slam shut.

At dawn, they all went up to the priest, and told him to put on his robes and come bury Anders Bjornsen and Hans Thorbjorn. They were sure the two men were dead. The priest said he had heard the scream and the laugh too, from his own cottage, and he did not think he would ever sleep again. They went into the wood, and found the two men at the edge of the treeline. Hans Thorbjorn was alive, his back pressed to an oak tree, his hands pushing wildly at empty air, over and over, as if he was fending something no one else could see. They took him to the asylum at Nykjoping, and he died before the winter, still pushing. Anders Bjornsen was dead. His face was gone. All the flesh was sucked clean off the bones. There was nothing to hold his eyes shut. They laid him on a bier, covered his head with a cloth, and walked back to the village, singing the psalm for the dead. Halfway through the first verse, the man carrying the head of the bier stumbled. The cloth fell off. They all saw his eyes, staring up at them, no lids, no skin. They could not bear it. So the priest laid the cloth back over him, and they fetched a spade, and buried Anders Bjornsen right there in the wood. No one has ever dug him up.

The next morning, the deacon called for Wraxall after breakfast, and took him into the church, then out to the mausoleum. Wraxall noticed the iron key to the tomb was hanging on a nail right behind the pulpit, and the church door was never locked. A private return visit, to copy down all the epitaphs he did not have time for that morning, would be trivially easy. The mausoleum inside was imposing: carved marble monuments to generations of De la Gardies, and three large copper sarcophagi in the center of the domed room. Two of them had large crucifixes nailed to their lids. The third, Count Magnus’s, had no cross. It was engraved with a full-length portrait of the count, and bands of carvings around the sides: one showed a battle, cannons smoking, pikemen marching, one showed a public execution. The third carving showed a man running for his life, hair flying, hands outstretched, pursued by a short, hooded figure swathed in a cloak that swept the stone floor. The only part of the creature that stuck out from the hooded fabric was no hand, no arm: a writhing, suckered tentacle, like that of a devil fish. At the far edge of the panel, a cloaked man leaning on a stick stood watching the chase, his posture calm, intent. Three heavy steel padlocks were fixed to the lid of Magnus’s sarcophagus. One of them was unfastened, lying on the pavement.

Wraxall hurried back to the manor to finish his work, but that evening he drifted into a daze again, walking the path from the house without noticing where he was, until he snapped out of it to find himself at the churchyard gate, chanting out loud under his breath: Are you awake, Count Magnus? Are you asleep, Count Magnus? He could not remember how long he had been walking like that.

He fetched the mausoleum key from behind the pulpit, and went inside to copy the epitaphs. The light was failing fast, and he leaned close to Magnus’s sarcophagus, and realized two of the three padlocks were now lying loose on the stone floor, not one. He picked them both up, tried and failed to snap them shut, and left them on the oval window ledge. Only one padlock remained fastened. He wrote later, that the urge to pry that last lock open, to lift the lid and look inside at what lay there, was so strong his hands were shaking. He almost did it. Some strange luck held him back, for that one night.

The next morning, he received a batch of urgent letters from England about his failed investments, requiring his immediate return. His work at Råbäck was nearly done, travel across the North Sea was slow, and he packed his bags that afternoon, bade his hosts farewell, and set off for the coast. He never went back to Råbäck. He never locked the mausoleum door behind him.

He did not know, then, that the last remaining padlock on Count Magnus’s sarcophagus was a spring lock, that would pop open of its own accord the moment the last living person who had stood in that tomb and looked upon the carvings on the casket had crossed the churchyard threshold. He did not know that two of the things Count Magnus had brought home from the Black Pilgrimage were still waiting, free to follow him over the sea, all the way back to his hotel in London.

Full Term had just wrapped at St James’s College, the high table feast thick with port fumes and post-exam chatter, when a man beside the young Ontography Professor leaned over, napkin still tucked at his starched collar.
“I suppose you’ll be escaping off pretty soon now, eh, Professor?”
Parkins was neat as a newly bound lecture manuscript, his speech sharp and unhurried, no wasted syllables. “Yes,” he said. “My friends badgered me into taking up golf this term, you see. I’m heading for the East Coast—Burnstow, to be precise—for a week or ten days, to fix my swing. Plan on leaving tomorrow.”
The man on his other side, a bearded antiquary whose name no one at the table would remember long past the dessert course, perked right up. “Burnstow? You’ll do me a favour, then, won’t you? Go take a look at the Templars’ preceptory site up the coast there, and tell me if it’s worth running a dig there come summer.”
“Of course,” Parkins said at once. “Map out the location for me, and I’ll give you a full lay of the land when I’m back, or drop you a letter if you’d rather.”
“Not necessary, thanks. Only thing is, I’m bringing the family down that way in Long Vacation, and hardly a single English Templar preceptory’s ever been properly surveyed. Figured I could do some useful work on my afternoons off.”
Parkins almost sniffed. Useful? The man had never so much as catalogued a pottery shard properly, far as he knew, but he kept his polite little smile fixed in place.
“Hardly anything shows above ground these days,” the antiquary went on. “The sea’s eaten miles of that stretch of coast, you know. From the maps, it’s three-quarters of a mile north of the Globe Inn, far as I can tell. Where are you staying?”
“As a matter of fact, the Globe itself,” Parkins said. “Couldn’t get a spot anywhere else; all the winter lodging houses are shut up. Trouble is, the only large room they’ve got free is a double-bedded one, and they haven’t a spare corner to stow the extra bed. I need space to spread out my books and do a bit of work while I’m down, so I suppose I’ll just rough it for the week, even if it does feel odd having a whole second empty bed in what’ll double as my study.”
“Roughing it?” boomed bluff Mr Rogers across the table, nudging his neighbour so hard the man’s wine sloshed over the rim. “I’ll come down and occupy that bed for you! Be good company, keep you from getting bored all alone.”
Parkins froze, then forced a polite little laugh. “By all means, Rogers, nothing I’d like better. But I’m afraid you’d find it terribly dull. You don’t play golf, do you?”
“Thank the good Lord, no,” Rogers said.
“See, then? When I’m not writing I’ll be out on the links. You’d be stuck with nothing to do.”
“Nah, there’s bound to be someone I know hanging around. But hey—if you don’t want me there, just say the word. You’re always telling us truth never offends, right?”
Poor Parkins was scrupulously polite, pathologically honest, and Rogers knew it all too well. For a long moment he sat there, chest tight, trying to dig out an answer that wouldn’t come off as rude. “Well, if you insist on exact truth,” he said at last, “I was wondering if the room’s big enough for both of us to be comfortable, and… well, you might prove a bit of a distraction from my work.”
Rogers laughed loud enough to make the candelabra rattle. “Fair play, Parkins! I won’t come bothering you, don’t worry. Only thing is, I thought I’d be doing you a favour, keeping the ghosts off.” He winked so hard his eye almost stayed shut. “Sorry—forgot you hate all that silly ghost talk. No levity on the topic, got it.”
“Since you’ve brought it up,” Parkins said, voice rising a notch, “I will say I do not care for careless chatter about so-called ghosts. A man in my position cannot be seen to sanction that kind of popular superstition, you know full well. I have never hidden my views: even the faintest hint that such things might exist is a betrayal of every principle I hold dear.”
“Right you are, old man,” Rogers muttered under his breath, already half listening to someone else.

Next day, Parkins was on the first train out of Cambridge, and by dusk he was settled into that large double room at the Globe Inn. The space sat at the very edge of the building, three windows facing straight out to the sea—central pane dead on the water, left and right looking north and south down the shingle shore. Sixty yards, no more, separated the inn’s back wall from the tide line. Between them was a strip of rough grass dotted with rusted old anchors and weathered capstans, then a gravel path, then the wide stretch of grey beach. The rest of the inn’s guests were all golfers, save for Colonel Wilson, the retired military man whose voice was loud enough to rattle the windows on a stormy night, and who made no secret of his staunch Protestant disdain for the local vicar’s rather high church rituals.
Parkins spent the whole next day out on the links with the Colonel, and by late afternoon the man’s face was so purple and bristling with unspoken rage over a lost golf ball that Parkins decided the only sensible thing to do was skip walking back with him, let tea and tobacco mellow him out before dinner.
“I’ll take the shore path back,” he thought. “There’s still enough light to hunt down that Templar preceptory site. I can’t miss it, surely.”
He very nearly literally stumbled over it, his foot catching on a gorse root and a half-buried lump of stone, sending him tumbling straight into a patch of uneven ground pocked with low mounds and shallow hollows. When he brushed the turf off his trousers and looked around, every mound was crammed with flint and mortar, grown over with grass. This was the preceptory, no doubt about it. He paced out the clear circular arrangement of the humps, all that was left of the Templars’ round church, jotted dimensions down in his notebook, then wandered over to an oblong rise of turf he figured was the old altar base. At its northern edge, a patch of turf had been ripped away, leaving bare earth. He pulled out his pocket knife to scrape at the soil, to check for masonry underneath—and a whole chunk of earth caved in under the blade, revealing a small, rectangular artificial cavity carved into the old stonework.
He struck match after match, but the north wind snuffed every one out. Tapping around with the knife, he could tell the sides, top, and bottom of the little niche were smoothed almost perfectly flat. He thought it was empty, until he pulled the knife out and heard a sharp metallic clink against the stone. Reaching his hand into the dark cavity, his fingers closed around a small, heavy bronze cylinder. By the time he’d fished it out, the light was almost gone. It was four inches long, pitted with age, clearly very old.
The view as he turned to walk back to the inn was bleak and vast: the pale yellow sunset streaked the west over the links, the squat martello tower, the distant lights of Aldsey village, the pale ribbon of sand cut through at regular intervals by dark wooden groynes, the sea murmuring low as it dragged shingle back and forth. The bitter north wind was at his back, pushing him along. Halfway home, he glanced over his shoulder—and saw a figure, half-obscured by the dusk, running hard behind him, limbs flying, but never seeming to close the gap, not even by a single yard.
Parkins told himself he didn’t know the man, there was no reason to wait for him, but the hair on the back of his neck prickled anyway. He found his mind drifting to that old childhood story he’d read once, of Christian meeting the foul fiend on the desolate path. What would he do, he wondered, if he turned right now and saw a black shape silhouetted against the sunset, horns and wings and all? The figure behind never drew any closer, though, and by the time he’d clomped up the inn steps he’d almost forgotten about it.
Dinner went smooth, the Colonel had mellowed, Parkins won most of their bridge hands, and by midnight he was heading back up to his room, well pleased with the day. The inn’s boots stopped him in the passage.
“Beg pardon, sir,” the man said. “I was brushing your coat earlier, and a little bronze thing fell out your pocket. Sat it on your chest of drawers, sir—bit of an old pipe, I thought. Good night, sir.”
Parkins lit his candles and picked the object up off the chest. It was no pipe. It was a whistle, almost exactly the shape of a modern dog whistle, caked full of fine old sand. He carefully scraped the dirt out onto a scrap of paper, and walked over to the sea-facing window to tip the dust out into the dark night. The sky was bright with stars, the water glinting a faint silver, and on the beach right outside the inn, a lone figure was standing, stock still, staring up at the windows. He shut the casement, frowning at how late some people stayed out walking, then turned back to the whistle.
Scrubbing a little more grime off its surface, he found two lines of deeply carved Latin text. One read FLA FUR BIS FLE. The other was QUI EST ISTE QUI VENIT—Who is this who is coming?
“Bit rusty on my mediaeval Latin,” Parkins muttered to himself. “Well—best way to find out who’s coming, isn’t it? Just whistle him up.”
He blew, soft at first. The note that came out was thin, high, strange, and carried a weight of infinite distance, like it was echoing over miles of empty moor and shore. For a split second he could see it, clear as day: a wide dark expanse of night, a cold fresh wind blowing, and a single lonely figure standing right in the middle of it, doing something he couldn’t quite make out. Then a huge gust of wind slammed hard against the casement, so hard it shook the glass, and the vision shattered. He caught a flash of white seabird wing vanishing into the dark.
He couldn’t help himself, he blew the whistle again, harder this time. No vision came. Instead, a gale roared up out of nowhere, so strong the window fastenings creaked and snapped, and both his candles blew out, plunging the room into total darkness.
Parkins fought the casement shut for almost a full minute, pushing against it like he was straining to hold back a grown man, until the wind slackened all at once, and the window banged hard shut and latched itself. He fumbled for his matches, relit the candles, and saw no damage at all—no broken glass, nothing out of place. Upstairs, he could hear the Colonel thumping around in his socks, growling at the wind.
The gale howled on, rushing past the inn walls, crying so loud and desolate Parkins thought anyone of a fanciful turn would have been absolutely terrified. He couldn’t fall asleep for hours. He lay there, listening to someone in the next room tossing and turning restlessly in their bed, and when he finally shut his eyes to try to drift off, a terrible persistent vision played behind his eyelids, over and over again.
It was that same stretch of shore, dark, spitting thin cold rain. First there was nothing, then a man came running over the shingle, scrambling over groynes, glancing back over his shoulder every two seconds, face white with terror, so exhausted he could barely put one foot in front of the other. He half-fell, half-climbed over the last groyne, and huddled down behind it, shaking, staring back up the beach. Then, far in the distance, a pale fluttering shape came darting over the sand, arms outstretched, weaving back and forth, moving faster than any human could run. It stopped, bowed low to the sand, then darted forward again, straight for the huddled man behind the groyne.
Parkins couldn’t stand it. He sat bolt upright, fumbled for a match to light his candle, determined to stay up reading all night rather than watch that scene play out again. The match scraped, flared, and went out—but the second one caught. He pulled a book from his bag and read until he fell asleep, candle still burning on the table beside him.
When the maid came in at half past eight the next morning, Parkins was already up, lacing his golf shoes, getting ready to head out on the links with the Colonel for the day. She knocked on the door, and stepped inside.

The gas in the antiquary Somerton’s Berkshire study guttered low, casting wavering amber streaks across the tattered vellum of the *Sertum Steinfeldense Norbertinum* — that rare, rambling 1712 account of Germany’s Steinfeld Premonstratensian Abbey, its pages stiff with three hundred years of Eiffel mountain damp. He’d just finished copying a curious passage of medieval Latin, his pen hovering over the final line noting Abbot Thomas’s sudden, unforeseen death in 1529, at seventy-two years old.
“I suppose I’ll have to translate this,” he muttered to himself, and set the nib to a fresh sheet of foolscap, rendering the old monastic gossip plain:
To this day, the canons of Steinfeld whisper of a vast hidden treasure belonging to Abbot Thomas, that no searcher has ever unearthed. The tale goes that Thomas, in the full flush of his strength, buried a mountain of gold somewhere on the abbey grounds. When pestered for its location, he only ever laughed and said, “Job, John, and Zechariah will tell you — or your children’s children.” He’d add, light as a cold shadow, that he’d not begrudge a soul who dug it up. Of all his works, none matched the great painted glass window he set in the east end of the south aisle, its panels glowing with masterfully rendered figures that bore his own effigy and family crest. He also restored nearly every room of the Abbot’s Lodging, sinking a new well in its central courtyard, rimmed all around with marble carvings so fine they looked like frozen lace. He died, struck down by a sharp, unforeseen illness, in 1529, at seventy-two.
Somerton had only been chasing the provenance of scattered 16th-century German stained glass, the kind that flooded out of dissolved Continental abbeys after the Revolution to end up in English parish churches, private chapels, and cathedral crypts. But that passage hooked him fast.
He’d seen three tall, unassigned panels in Lord D——’s private chapel months prior: three full-length figures, each occupying their own light, the work clearly of a 1520s German hand, no provenance he could pin down. They were Job the Patriarch, John the Evangelist, Zechariah the Prophet — and each held a scroll scribbled with lines warped deliberately from the standard Vulgate text. Job’s read: *“There is a place for gold where it is hidden”*. John’s: *“They have on their raiment a writing which no man knoweth”*. Zachariah’s, the only one unaltered: *“Upon one stone are seven eyes”*.
No biblical or symbolic link joined the three men. He’d puzzled over that pairing for months, until the old abbey book laid the answer right at his feet. This was Abbot Thomas’s very own window, the one he’d installed at Steinfeld. A quick second trip to the chapel confirmed it: a stray fragment of adjacent glass bore the arms of Thomas von Eschenhausen, no mistake about it.
Somerton’s research curdled into quiet obsession. Midnight oil burned low on his manor desk, heaped with tracings and rubbings of the glass. Weeks later, he called his valet William Brown in, told him to pack two portmanteaus for a quiet trip abroad, giving no further explanation.
It was a crisp, sharp autumn dawn when Parsbury Rector Mr Gregory strolled down his carriage drive, his three youngest children clinging to his hands, to meet the postman. One envelope in the bundle bore a smudged foreign postmark, scrawled in rough, uneducated English handwriting, and once the children were herded off to breakfast he slit it open. It was from Brown, his friend Somerton’s usually unflappable valet:
> I write in great anxiety at my master’s wish to beg you to come at once. Master has had a nasty shock and keeps his bed, I never saw him so bad, no rest for nights. He says the short way here is drive to Coblenz and take a trap. I’m half out of my wits what with worry. It will be such a relief to see an honest British face among all these foreigners.
> Your obedt servt William Brown.
> P.S. The place is called Steenfeld, not what the old maps say.
The Parsbury parsonage was thrown into utter chaos. By sundown that same day Gregory was on the boat to Antwerp, bound for the Eiffel mountains.
Steinfeld was a bleak, half-ruined cluster of buildings, the abbey church stripped bare of its altars and shrines, surrounded by hulking 17th century stone ranges that leaked rain and creaked in the high mountain wind. The only inn was drafty, smelling of sour rye bread and mildew, and Brown was waiting at the door — his usual crisp valet uniform swapped for a crumpled tweed suit, eyes red with lack of sleep, every line of his face sharp with unspoken fear.
“Thank the good Lord you’re here, sir,” he said, barely able to get the words out. “Master’s better than he was, but he’s had horrors I can’t even speak of.”
Gregory was just reaching for the handle of Somerton’s bedroom door when a bloodcurdling scream ripped through the silence within: “In God’s name, who is that? Brown? Is that you?”
They hurried in to find Somerton propped up in bed, his usually calm face gaunt, beaded with cold sweat, his hands shaking so hard the candle on his nightstand wobbled. The moment he saw Gregory’s familiar face, he deflated, a groan of raw relief tearing out of him.
He rallied enough to eat a full dinner that evening, talking of catching the Coblenz train in 24 hours — before that old, wild edge of panic crept back into his voice.
“There is one thing I beg you to do for me, Gregory,” he said, gripping his friend’s wrist hard enough to leave a bruise. “Don’t ask me what it is, don’t ask why. I can’t explain it yet — it would undo every bit of good your coming has done. You are at no risk at all, not in the daytime. Brown will show you tomorrow. It’s only to put something back, to leave it where we found it. Please. And when you go to bed tonight — lock your door. Bolt it, twice. I insist. The things that wander here… they don’t bother locked doors. Good night.”
Gregory woke three times in the small hours, each time to a slow, soft, scraping drag across the bottom of his locked bedroom door. He lay frozen, clutching the fire poker he’d dragged from the hearth before sleeping, until the sound faded away each time. He never looked through the keyhole.
By sunrise he was outside with Brown, and the small, grim task they completed on the broken stone step of the old Abbot’s Lodging took less than half an hour. By mid-morning, Somerton was well enough to leave the village, and that night, stopped at a quiet wayside inn halfway to Coblenz, he leaned across the table, his voice dropping low, and told the full story.
“All that research, all that chasing the glass — it led me right to the treasure,” Somerton said, pushing his tattered notebook across the table. “I went back to the chapel that second time, determined to note every single mark on those three panels. I noticed right away John’s text spoke of a writing no man knew on the raiment of the saints — but all three figures had thick, opaque black borders painted around their mantles, no markings at all. I was stumped. Then Lord D—— fetched a broom to wipe the dust off the glass, and the hard bristle dragged a long scratch across Job’s border. Underneath, a streak of bright yellow stain shone through.
I scraped at the thick, post-firing black pigment with a pocket knife. It came away like dry ash. Under it, clear as day, was a capital yellow letter. I stripped every inch of that black pigment off all three figures in two hours flat. Each one’s mantle border was crammed full of letters, a cipher, no order to them at first glance.
I hunted through every old cryptography book I could lay my hands on — Trithemius’s *Steganographia*, Bacon’s *De Augmentis*, every medieval text on secret writing. Nothing fit. I was about to give up, when I flipped back to my notes of the saints’ poses. Job: one finger extended up from his right hand. John: two fingers raised in blessing. Zechariah: three fingers pointing skyward.
One. Two. Three. That was the key.
Skip one letter after the first, skip two after the next, skip three after that. And the words blazed out, clear as day, as if Abbot Thomas had written them right in front of me: *Decem millia auri reposita sunt in puteo in atrio* — Ten thousand pieces of gold are laid up in a well in the courtyard of the Abbot’s Lodging.
I booked my passage to Coblenz that same night. I arrived at Steinfeld, found the old well, the marble carvings around its rim just as the old book described. I pried out the seven-eyed stone Zechariah spoke of, set into the well’s inner wall, and climbed down.
I found the gold. All of it. Piled high, glinting in my lantern light.
But I found something else down there, too. Something Thomas had bricked in with it, to guard his treasure. Something that didn’t want its gold taken.
Somerton paused, his face ashen, and pushed the scrap of paper with the remaining undeciphered cipher letters across the table:
“The rest of the cipher?” He swallowed hard. “I haven’t had the nerve to read it yet. Not yet. I don’t think I want to know what else old Abbot Thomas promised it, all those hundreds of years ago.”
