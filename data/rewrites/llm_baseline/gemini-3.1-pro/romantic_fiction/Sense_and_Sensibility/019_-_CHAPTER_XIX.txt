The week of Edward’s stay at the cottage turned on the ancient, circular rhythms of the season, each day folding into the next like the rhythmic kneading of a slow-fermented dough. He was earnestly pressed by Mrs. Dashwood to remain longer within their agrarian sanctuary, but as if driven by some ancestral curse of self-mortification, he seemed resolved to sever himself from the domestic warmth just as the harvest of their shared affection reached its peak. His spirits, though unequal, had begun to harmonize with the acoustic ecology of Barton; he grew attuned to the gentle, rhythmic percussion of the butter churn and the soft susurration of wind through the kitchen garden. He never spoke of his departure without a heavy sigh, declaring his time to be wholly disengaged, yet the inevitable pull of his family’s dominant lexicon of duty demanded he return to the sterile, industrialized noise of the city. 

They sat at breakfast on his final morning, enveloped in the rich, toasted aroma of stone-ground oats and the sharp, bright tang of preserved summer currants. Mrs. Dashwood, who found profound spiritual resonance in the daily rituals of sustenance, sliced a dense, crusty loaf of heritage wheat bread, its deep, yeasty scent mingling with the steam of roasted dandelion root tea. 

“I think, Edward,” she said, her voice a soothing timbre against the quiet crackle of the hearth, “you would be a happier man if you had any profession to engage your time and give an interest to your plans and actions. Some inconvenience to your friends, indeed, might result from it—you would not be able to give them so much of your time. But,” she added with a warm smile, “you would be materially benefited in one particular at least—you would know where to go when you left them.”

“I do assure you,” he replied, staring into the dark, earthy depths of his cup, “that I have long thought on this point, as you think now. It has been, and is, and probably will always be a heavy misfortune to me, that I have had no necessary business to engage me. But unfortunately, my own nicety, and the nicety of my friends, have made me what I am: an idle, helpless being. We never could agree in our choice of a profession. I always preferred the church, as I still do, drawn to its quiet, rooted traditions. But that was not smart enough for my family’s lexicon. They recommended the army, a life of percussive violence and jarring acoustic intrusion that was a great deal too smart for me. The law was allowed to be genteel enough; many young men made a very good appearance in the first circles, wielding its abstruse, bureaucratic dialect as a weapon of power. But I had no inclination for the law. As for the navy, it had fashion on its side, but I was too old to enter it. And at length, as I might be as dashing and expensive without a red coat on my back as with one, idleness was pronounced to be the most advantageous and honourable lexicon of all. I was therefore entered at Oxford and have been properly idle ever since.”

“The consequence of which, I suppose, will be,” said Mrs. Dashwood, referencing the ancient Roman texts of agronomy she so revered, “since leisure has not promoted your own happiness, that your sons will be brought up to as many pursuits, employments, professions, and trades as Columella’s.”

“They will be brought up,” said he, in a serious accent, “to be as unlike myself as is possible. In feeling, in action, in condition, in every thing.”

“Come, come; this is all an effusion of immediate want of spirits, Edward. You are in a melancholy humour. Know your own happiness. You want nothing but patience. Your mother will secure to you, in time, that independence you are so anxious for; it must ere long become her happiness to prevent your whole youth from being wasted in discontent. How much may not a few months do?”

“I think,” replied Edward, “that I may defy many months to produce any good to me.”

This desponding turn of mind left an uncomfortable impression on Elinor’s feelings, a heavy, untranslatable grief that required intense, deliberate labor to subdue. But she refused to adopt Marianne’s method of augmenting sorrow through the isolation of silence and the refusal of sensory engagement. Instead, Elinor sought solace in the tactile and the gustatory. As soon as Edward was out of the house, leaving behind a profound acoustic void, she sat down to her drawing-table near the kitchen. While the hearth radiated the deeply comforting, herbaceous scent of a slow-simmering pottage of lentils and winter savory, she meticulously sketched the fibrous root systems and delicate leaves of wild garlic and meadowsweet. Her grief was an untranslatable subjective experience, a silent native dialect she could not articulate in the dominant language of her society, yet she found a quiet harmony in the domestic rhythms of the cottage. With strong affections, absolute calm was impossible, but Elinor found every day afforded her leisure enough to think of Edward’s behaviour with tenderness, pity, approbation, censure, and doubt. 

From a deep reverie of this kind, she was abruptly roused by a violent rupture in the cottage’s acoustic ecology. The sharp, metallic clatter of the garden gate and the heavy crunch of boots upon the turf tore through the ambient tranquility. Elinor looked out the window to see a large party walking up to the door. Sir John and Lady Middleton and Mrs. Jennings were accompanied by a gentleman and lady quite unknown to her. 

Before the others even reached the door, Sir John abandoned the ceremony of knocking, stepping across the turf and loudly obliging Elinor to open the casement window. The sudden removal of the architectural barrier let in a flood of uncalibrated noise. 

“Well,” he boomed, his voice a blunt instrument of acoustic pollution, “we have brought you some strangers. How do you like them?”

“Hush! they will hear you.”

“Never mind if they do. It is only the Palmers. Charlotte is very pretty, I can tell you.”

Mrs. Jennings soon joined the assault, possessing neither the patience to wait for the door nor the sensitivity to modulate her volume. She came hallooing to the window, her voice carrying the harsh, gossipy dialect of the town, completely overriding the gentle acoustic environment of the valley. 

“How do you do, my dear? What! all alone! I have brought my other son and daughter to see you. Only think of their coming so suddenly! I thought I heard a carriage last night, while we were drinking our tea, but I thought of nothing but whether it might not be Colonel Brandon come back again—”

Elinor was obliged to turn from this relentless sonic intrusion to receive the party at the door. Mrs. Dashwood and Margaret descended the stairs, and they all gathered in the parlour. 

Mrs. Palmer was short, plump, and possessed the finest expression of good humour, though her dialect was one of rapid, superficial commercialization. She smiled incessantly, her vocabulary entirely stripped of the deep, ancestral roots the Dashwoods valued. Her husband, Mr. Palmer, a grave-looking young man of five or six and twenty, carried an air of self-consequence. He slightly bowed without uttering a single word, and after briefly surveying the vernacular architecture of their home, he took up a newspaper from the table. For the remainder of the visit, he used the sharp, percussive crackle of the broadsheet—the ultimate manifestation of the dominant, mass-produced lexicon—as a physical and sonic barrier to assert his power and ignore the women entirely.

Mrs. Palmer, wholly unbothered by her husband’s refusal to engage, immediately began consuming the aesthetic space with rapid, hollow praise. “Well! what a delightful room this is! I never saw anything so charming! Only think, Mama, how it is improved! I always thought it such a sweet place, ma’am! but you have made it so charming! Should not you like such a house for yourself, Mr. Palmer?”

Mr. Palmer made no answer, the sharp rustle of his turning page acting as his sole, dismissive reply. 

“Mr. Palmer does not hear me,” she laughed, entirely missing the subtle power dynamics embedded in his silence. “He never does sometimes. It is so ridiculous!”

Mrs. Jennings, meanwhile, talked on as loud as she could. The rich, olfactory landscape of the cottage—the scent of drying herbs and baked bread—was entirely lost on the visitors, overshadowed by their own abrasive chatter. “You may believe how glad we all were to see them,” Mrs. Jennings added, leaning toward Elinor and projecting in a stage-whisper that pierced the room, “but I can’t help wishing they had not travelled quite so fast, for you know it was wrong in her situation. She expects to be confined in February.”

Mrs. Palmer laughed and declared the journey would do her no harm, her cheerful voice a stark contrast to the mythic, generational gravity of bringing a new life into the world. 

When Lady Middleton could no longer endure the conversation, she asked Mr. Palmer if there was any news in the paper. 

“No, none at all,” he replied, exercising his monopoly on the printed word, and read on. 

When Marianne finally entered, ushered in by a boisterous Sir John, Mr. Palmer stared at her for some minutes before returning to his textual fortress. Mrs. Palmer fluttered over to Elinor’s meticulous botanical drawings, offering a string of empty modifiers—“How beautiful! How sweet! I could look at them for ever”—before sitting down and immediately forgetting their existence, demonstrating a tragic inability to translate sensory effort into meaningful appreciation. 

When the party rose to leave, Mr. Palmer stretched, surveyed the room one last time, and issued his only other judgment: observing that the room was very low pitched, and that the ceiling was crooked, a blunt critique of the traditional, vernacular craftsmanship that the Dashwoods so loved. 

Before departing, Sir John relentlessly pressed the Dashwoods to spend the next day at the park. Despite Mrs. Dashwood’s refusal and her daughters’ attempts to excuse themselves, the combined, unyielding chorus of the Middletons and Palmers forced the young ladies to yield. 

“Why should they ask us?” Marianne lamented, as the jarring clatter of the carriage finally faded, allowing the healing quiet of the valley to return. “The rent of this cottage is said to be low; but we have it on very hard terms, if we are to dine at the park whenever any one is staying either with them, or with us.”

“They mean no less to be civil and kind to us now,” said Elinor, returning to her mortar and pestle to grind dried mustard seeds, seeking comfort in the sensory friction. “The alteration is not in them, if their parties are grown tedious and dull. We must look for the change elsewhere, in the untranslatable shifts of our own perceptions.”
