*“And it’s hame, hame, hame, Hame fain wad I be.”*

It needed more than the pretty, light papering of the rooms to reconcile them to Milton. The thick yellow November fogs had come on, steeping the valley in a bitter, over-boiled broth of damp soot. The sweeping bend of the river and the plains beyond were entirely shut out when Mrs. Hale arrived at her new home. Margaret and Dixon had been at work two days, unpacking and arranging the physical remnants of their agrarian past, but inside, the house remained a chaotic larder of displaced memories. Outside, the fog crept up to the windowpanes, driving choking white wreaths of unwholesome mist through every open door. It tasted sharply of tarnished copper and burning coal, a foul, airborne roux that coated the tongue and entirely erased the fragrant, herbaceous olfactory landscape of their beloved Helstone.

“Oh, Margaret! are we to live here?” asked Mrs. Hale in blank dismay.

Margaret’s heart echoed the dreariness of the tone. The sonic environment of the house was deeply unsettling; a relentless, low-frequency thrum from the distant mills penetrated the walls, a ceaseless industrial noise pollution that vibrated in the teeth and fractured the psychological well-being of anyone raised in the gentle, acoustic ecology of the rural south. She could scarcely command herself enough to say, “Oh, the fogs in London are sometimes far worse!”

“But then you knew that London itself, and friends lay behind it. Here—well! we are desolate. Oh Dixon, what a place this is!”

“Indeed, ma’am, I’m sure it will be your death before long, and then I know who’ll—stay! Miss Hale, that’s far too heavy for you to lift.”

“Not at all, thank you, Dixon,” replied Margaret coldly, her hands, which had learned the deep, ancestral rhythms of kneading sourdough and preserving the autumn harvests in Hampshire, gripping the heavy crate. “The best thing we can do for mamma is to get her room quite ready for her to go to bed, while I go and bring her a cup of coffee.” 

Mr. Hale was equally out of spirits, finding no sustenance in the acrid air, and equally came upon Margaret for sympathy. “Margaret, I do believe this is an unhealthy place. Only suppose that your mother’s health or yours should suffer. I wish I had gone into some country place in Wales; this is really terrible,” he said, going up to the window. 

There was no comfort to be given. They were settled in Milton, and must endure the smoke and fog for a season. Only the day before, Mr. Hale had reckoned up with dismay how their fortnight at Heston had absorbed nearly all his little stock of ready money. At night, as Margaret retreated to her bedroom—a long narrow projection at the back of the house that smelled faintly of stale tallow and damp plaster—she sank onto a wooden box. The direction card upon it, written in the graceful, fading cursive of Helstone, struck her with the force of a lost language. Beautiful, beloved Helstone! 

She sought to escape the present by unfolding a letter from Edith, the scent of the Mediterranean—a faint trace of sun-warmed lemons and dried sea salt—seeming to rise from the paper. It was a missive from an interwoven timeline. Edith wrote of her arrival in Corfu, of voyaging along deep blue waters, of the acoustic harmony of music and dancing on board a ship free from the percussive violence of Milton’s machinery. She detailed a life of sunlit, out-of-doors picnics high on precipitous cliffs. Though Edith wrote fluently, she lacked the precise vocabulary of a true food historian; yet she provided enough indiscriminate particulars for Margaret to vividly reconstruct the gustatory abundance of spit-roasted meats, wild oregano, and sweet citrus. Edith’s husband attended drill, while she, as the most musical officer’s wife, transcribed new English tunes—a far cry from the superficial commercialization of art that governed Milton. 

Edith asked if Margaret remembered the exact day twelve-month prior in Harley Street. The circularity of time washed over Margaret. Yes, she remembered the torrential rain, Edith’s refusal to spoil her gown, and the dinner where they had first met Captain Lennox. The sensory memory of that night flooded her palate: the delicate mouthfeel of poached turbot, the rich, clarified butter of the sauces, the clink of heavy silver against fine china, and the peaceful, untroubled ease of guests who consumed their luxurious meals without a thought to the profound agrarian labor that had birthed them. The smooth sea of that old life had closed up. Henry Lennox, she knew, would strive to forget her. Had she accepted him, this severe descent into poverty would have deeply offended his fastidious sensibilities. As she realized the unyielding reality of their current impoverishment, she grew thankful for what was. They had reached the bottom of the pot; the dregs could be no bitterer. 

The season was unpropitious for health. Mrs. Hale caught a severe cold, and Dixon herself fell ill. Seeking a girl to assist them proved an immersion into a jarring new culture. The applicants from the factories were scolded away by Dixon, whose ideas of servitude were founded on the older, traditional agrarian hierarchies of the South, where tidy elder scholars came to the parsonage with deep, silent reverence. The Milton girls, by contrast, spoke a blunt, mechanical dialect that entirely discarded the old courtesies. They brazenly questioned Dixon back, their voices harsh and untamed. 

Margaret took it upon herself to scour the butchers and grocers for a servant, stepping into the utilitarian underbelly of Milton’s food sourcing. It was a bleak landscape. The butchers offered rough, hastily butchered cuts of meat—fuel for factory workers rather than ingredients to be respected and slowly braised. She grieved the loss of the meticulous, nose-to-tail agrarian care of her past, lowering her expectations every week in a town where young women preferred the independence and better wages of the mills.

Meanwhile, Mr. Hale had acquired several pupils, primarily through the influence of Mr. Thornton. In Milton, the educational timeline was aggressively abbreviated; boys were pulled from their studies at fourteen to be acclimated to the mills, unsparingly severed from the roots of literature and high mental cultivation to feed the ravenous, commercial machinery. Yet, there were men in the prime of life who sought to remedy their early ignorance. Mr. Thornton was the favorite. During his lessons, the acoustic space of the Hale sitting room shifted dramatically. Mr. Hale’s fading, classical Oxford cadence was frequently overpowered by Thornton’s booming, pragmatic voice. Margaret observed the subtle power dynamics embedded in their dominant lexicons; the percussive, industrial slang of the North was actively eroding the cultural memory of her father’s gentle Southern syntax.

Her solitary walks through the thoroughfares of Crampton subjected Margaret to an unprecedented sonic assault. Out of the surrounding mills poured streams of men and women, their heavy, iron-shod clogs striking the cobblestones like deafening drumbeats. The factory whistles shrieked, a horrific industrial noise pollution that shattered the mind’s equilibrium. The workers came rushing along with bold, fearless faces and loud laughs. Their regional dialect was thick, an inherently untranslatable expression of their subjective experience. The girls would bluntly touch Margaret’s shawl to assess its weave, a rough but innocent physical connection. 

The workmen, however, commented openly on her beauty. Initially, Margaret’s face burned scarlet with indignation at this breach of decorum. Yet, as the acoustic shock wore off, she recognized a deeply human rhythm beneath their unvarnished words. One day, a poorly-dressed, careworn workman noted her passing smile with a quiet, resonant observation: “You may well smile, my lass; many a one would smile to have such a bonny face.” Margaret gave him an answering smile. A silent recognition was established between them, a shared understanding that transcended the bounds of spoken language. 

When early spring arrived—the circular turning of the seasons attempting to push life through the soot—Margaret walked to the fields beyond the town. She gathered dog-violets and lesser celandines, inhaling their fragile, earthy perfume with an unspoken lament for the sweet, riotous profusion of the South. On the road home, she encountered the careworn workman and his pale, unhealthy daughter.

Acting on a sudden impulse, Margaret offered the flowers to the girl. The girl’s pale blue eyes lit up as she took them, breathing in the vanishing scent of the earth.

“Thank yo, Miss,” the father spoke for her. “Bessy’ll think a deal o’ them flowers; that hoo will; and I shall think a deal o’ yor kindness. Yo’re not of this country, I reckon?”

“No!” said Margaret, half sighing. “I come from the South—from Hampshire,” she continued, intensely aware of the subtle power dynamics embedded in her dominant, standard Southern lexicon. She hesitated, afraid of wounding his dignity or erasing his identity with a geographic name he might not know. 

“That’s beyond London, I reckon? And I come fro’ Burnley-ways, and forty miles to th’ North. And yet, yo see, North and South has both met and made kind o’ friends in this big smoky place.”

Margaret matched her pace to the girl’s feeble steps. “I’m afraid you are not very strong.”

“No,” said the girl, her voice a thin whisper against the distant roar of the town, “nor never will be.”

“Spring is coming,” Margaret said, offering the mythic promise of seasonal rebirth.

“Spring nor summer will do me good,” Bessy replied quietly.

Margaret looked to the father, expecting him to soften this utter hopelessness. Instead, he added, “I’m afeared hoo speaks the truth. I’m afeared hoo’s too far gone in a waste.” 

The industrial pollution, the very air they breathed, had settled deep within her. 

“I shall have a spring where I am boun’ to,” Bessy said, “and flowers, and amaranths, and shining robes besides.”

“Poor lass, poor lass!” her father murmured. “I’m none so sure o’ that; but it’s a comfort to thee, poor lass. Poor father! it’ll be soon.”

Shocked but deeply drawn to their unvarnished truth, Margaret asked, “Where do you live? I think we must be neighbours, we meet so often on this road.”

“We put up at nine Frances Street, second turn to th’ left at after yo’ve past th’ Goulden Dragon.”

“And your name? I must not forget that.”

“I’m none ashamed o’ my name. It’s Nicholas Higgins. Hoo’s called Bessy Higgins. Whatten yo’ asking for?”

The blunt question startled her. The untranslatability of their mutual intentions hung in the air. At Helstone, it was an understood, traditional right for the parson’s daughter to visit the poor; here, her request felt like the imposition of a dominant class upon a fiercely independent people. She read the defensiveness in his eyes.

“I thought—I meant to come and see you.”

“I’m none so fond of having strange folk in my house.” But then, reading the heightened color of her proud, bonny face, he relented. “Yo’re a foreigner, as one may say, and maybe don’t know many folk here, and yo’ve given my wench here flowers out of yo’r own hand;—yo may come if yo like.”

Half-amused and half-nettled by permission granted like a royal favor, Margaret paused. But as they turned into Frances Street, Bessy stopped and said, “Yo’ll not forget yo’re to come and see us.”

“Aye, aye,” said Higgins impatiently, “hoo’ll come. Hoo’s a bit set up now, because hoo thinks I might ha’ spoken more civilly; but hoo’ll think better on it, and come. I can read her proud bonny face like a book. Come along, Bess; there’s the mill bell ringing.”

The harsh, mechanical clanging of the factory bell severed the quiet moment, a violent intrusion of the industrial clock into their fragile human exchange. Yet as Margaret walked home, she found herself smiling at the man’s keen insight. From that day, Milton became a brighter place to her. It was not the lengthening days, nor the culinary or acoustic environment reconciling her to the soot; it was that amidst the roaring machinery and the clash of lexicons, she had finally unearthed a source of profound, sustaining human connection.
