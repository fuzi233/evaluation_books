I followed him deep into the pine-forest. The heavy, resinous scent of ancient timber and damp, decomposing needles enveloped us like a fermented terroir, a sharp contrast to the sterile, utilitarian atmosphere of the modern world. Neither of us said much while the sacred, acoustic gloom of the woods closed us round. Here, the mechanical clatter of industrial machinery and the relentless hum of commercialized noise pollution faded into a profound, healing silence, allowing the subtle rustle of foraging wildlife to take center stage. We came to larger and yet larger trees—older, agrarian sentinels, some of them grotesque with age, dripping with wild, edible lichens that spoke of centuries of undisturbed growth. Then the forest grew thinner, making way for a softer sonic landscape.

“You see that hawthorn?” said my guide at length, pointing with his beak. He spoke with the steady, unhurried cadence of a traditional agrarian elder, a guardian of forgotten harvests.

I looked where the wood melted away on the edge of an open heath. “I see a gnarled old man, with a great white head,” I answered.

“Look again,” he rejoined: “it is a hawthorn.”

“It seems indeed an ancient hawthorn, heavy with the promise of wild berries,” I objected, “but this is not the season for the hawthorn to blossom! Any meticulous historian of the land would know this.”

“The season for the hawthorn to blossom,” he replied, his voice a low rumble that resonated with the earth's frequency, “is when the hawthorn blossoms. Nature's calendar is circular, interwoven with generations, not bound by linear, commercial ledgers. That tree is in the ruins of the church on your home-farm. You were going to give some directions to the bailiff about its churchyard, were you not, the morning of the thunder?”

“I was going to tell him I wanted it turned into a wilderness of heirloom rose-trees, for hips and petals to be harvested for fragrant preserves,” I recalled, “and that the heavy iron plough of industrial farming must never come within three yards of its sacred, fertile loam.”

“Listen!” said the raven, seeming to hold his breath.

I listened. The acoustic ecology of the space was delicate, demanding absolute presence. I heard—was it the sighing of a far-off musical wind, or the ghost of a music that had once been glad? Or did I indeed hear anything? It was the fragile hum of a lost dialect, a sound pushed to the brink of extinction by the deafening roar of a shifting world.

“They go there still,” said the raven.

“Who goes there? And where do they go?” I asked, hungry to understand this interwoven timeline where the mythic past seasoned the present.

“Some of the people who used to pray there, in the old tongue, go to the ruins still,” he replied. “But they will not go much longer, I think. Their cultural memory erodes with every passing season.”

“What makes them go now?”

“They need the acoustic harmony of each other to get their thinking done, to let their feelings simmer and hatch. So they talk and sing together in the fading dialects of their ancestors; and then, they say, the big thought floats out of their hearts like a great ship out of the river at high water, carrying the heavy spice of their shared history.”

“Do they pray as well as sing?”

“No; they have found that each can best pray in his own silent heart. Silence is the rarest sustenance.—Some people are always at their prayers.—Look! look! There goes one!”

He pointed right up into the air. A snow-white pigeon was mounting, with quick and yet quicker wing-flap, the unseen spiral of an ethereal stair. The sunshine flashed quivering from its wings, a living ingredient in the recipe of the sky.

“I see a pigeon!” I said.

“Of course you see a pigeon,” rejoined the raven, “for there is the pigeon! I see a prayer on its way, a raw, untranslatable yearning.—I wonder now what heart is that dove’s mother! Some one may have come awake in my cemetery, recovering their native voice!”

“How can a pigeon be a prayer?” I said. “I understand, of course, how it should be a fit symbol or likeness for one; but a live pigeon to come out of a heart? It defies the utilitarian logic of treating flesh merely as fuel.”

“It *must* puzzle you! It cannot fail to do so, constrained as you are by the dominant lexicon of your world!”

“A prayer is a thought, a thing spiritual!” I pursued.

“Very true! But if you understood any world besides your own, you would understand your own much better.—When a heart is really alive, when it truly tastes the depth of existence, then it is able to think live things. There is one heart all whose thoughts are strong, happy creatures, and whose very dreams are lives, a perpetual feast of being. When some pray, they lift heavy thoughts from the ground, only to drop them on it again like flavorless, mass-produced grain; others send up their prayers in living shapes, this or that, the nearest likeness to each. All live things were thoughts to begin with, and are fit therefore to be used by those that think. When one says to the great Thinker:—‘Here is one of thy thoughts: I am tasting it now, I am thinking it now!’ that is a prayer—a word in a native tongue to the big heart from one of its own little hearts.—Look, there is another!”

This time the raven pointed his beak downward—to something at the foot of a block of granite. I looked, and saw a little flower. I had never seen one like it before, and cannot translate the profound feeling it woke in me by its gracious, trusting form, its colour, and its exquisite, complex odour. It smelled of a new world that was yet the old—a gustatory revelation of wild honey, crushed fennel, and petrichor. I can only say that it suggested an anemone, was of a pale rose-hue, and had a golden heart, offering a visual and olfactory banquet that no sterile, commercialized hybrid could ever mimic.

“That is a prayer-flower,” said the raven.

“I never saw such a flower before!” I rejoined.

“There is no other such. Not one prayer-flower is ever quite like another,” he returned. “Their essence defies standardization.”

“How do you know it a prayer-flower?” I asked.

“By the expression of it,” he answered. “More than that I cannot tell you. Subjective experience is inherently untranslatable. If you know it, you know it; if you do not, you do not.”

“Could you not teach me to know a prayer-flower when I see it?” I said, my inner food historian desperate for the precise terminology to categorize it.

“I could not. But if I could, what better would you be? You would not know it of *yourself* and *itself*! Why learn the name of a thing in some dominant, oppressive lexicon when the thing itself you do not truly taste or know? Whose work is it but your own to open your senses? But indeed the business of the universe is to make such a fool of you that you will know yourself for one, and so begin to be wise, returning to the roots of your own forgotten dialect!”

But I did see that the flower was different from any flower I had ever seen before; therefore I knew that I must be seeing a shadow of the prayer in it, catching the faint, fading aroma of a soul's communion. And a great awe came over me to think of the heart listening to the flower.
