Fairy folk a-listening, they said, can hear the seed split in spring as plainly as a kettle beginning to sing; and the hedgerows, before they show a bud, have their own small chorus—sap rising in measured pulses, a thin, bright music that makes the invisible life feel near enough to touch. There are days when even a household seems to have such under-sounds: the click of a latch, the drawl of a voice, the hush that follows it—each a signal, each a kind of weather.

And Gwendolen—Gwendolen listened most to what was not said.

Deronda’s name moved through her thoughts with the persistence of a remembered flavor, something once tasted and afterward longed for in hours of dryness. She wondered what his mind fed on, what gave it strength. What were his ideas “about things”? How did he fill his days? She imagined him reading, judging, laboring at some noble aim—as if life had for him a recipe written in a clear hand, while she stood helpless before the pantry, proud, hungry, and not knowing how to begin.

“He said I must get more interest in others, and more knowledge, and that I must care about the best things—but how am I to begin?” she asked herself again and again, as if the question could be answered by repetition. She thought of the books he might name to her—those famous authors whose very names had the chill of physic bottles: Descartes, Bacon, Locke, Butler, Burke, Guizot. She carried them up to her own room in a rash armful, like provisions snatched in haste; she dipped into each, expecting that by quick tasting she might learn the whole dish. But the pages were dry on her tongue. They asked for a mind that could sit still and chew slowly, while her own days would not let her sit: constantly she must be on the scene as Mrs. Grandcourt, served up to the world in a glossy sauce of ease and triumph.

At Diplow, through the weeks of new year, her riding, hunting, visiting, and receiving were performed with the exactness of a woman determined not to spill a drop. She moved as if she had mastered all her part; and the neighbors, seeing only the surface, took the surface for the whole.

“She disguises it under an air of taking everything as a matter of course,” said Mrs. Arrowpoint. “A stranger might suppose she had condescended rather than risen. I always noticed that doubleness in her.”

To her mother, most of all, Gwendolen kept the smooth face. Poor Mrs. Davilow, with her worn delicacy and timid brightness, was deceived so far as to imagine that the distance now imposed was only the natural widening that marriage brings. She was fetched to lunch and to dinner with the Gascoignes, driven back soon after breakfast, and allowed brief calls from Gwendolen—calls in which Grandcourt waited outside, either seated in the carriage or on horseback, as if time itself were his to leash.

The second time Gwendolen proposed bringing her mother again with the Gascoignes, Grandcourt had first been silent; then, with that languid cruelty which turned contempt into a sort of indolent music, he drawled, “We can’t be having those people always. Gascoigne talks too much. Country clergy are always bores—with their confounded fuss about everything.”

Those words were like a knife laid quietly on the table: no flourish, only the certainty that it might be used. To have her mother classed under “those people” made Gwendolen’s blood run cold with pride and foreboding. Yet she could not tell the truth—could not say, “He wants to recognize you as little as possible; and besides, it is better you should not see my married life, lest you find out I am miserable.” She waved away every hint with practiced lightness. When Mrs. Davilow timidly spoke again of a house near Ryelands, Gwendolen said, “It would not be so nice for you as being near the rectory here, mamma. We shall perhaps be very little at Ryelands. You would miss my aunt and uncle.”

And all the while, this contemptuous veto, making her shrink from giving her family the look of troublesome pensioners, woke in her a stronger, more aching tenderness toward them. She looked back on her uncle’s cheerful complacency and busy kind management—even when mistaken—as a warmth of hearth and common bread, compared with the cold, high polish that chilled her daily. Perhaps, without knowing it, she was finding the mental enlargement Deronda had urged—not from hard authors that must be approached like tasks, but from that slow recognition of what love and familiar voices had meant to her.

One day, when Mr. and Mrs. Gascoigne were at Offendene, it was a surprise like a sudden shaft of sun through cloud to see Gwendolen ride up without her husband, only the groom behind. All were at lunch—the rector and his wife, the four girls, Miss Merry—and from the dining-room they could see her coming. Even the elder ones felt something of Isabel’s romantic imagination: the beautiful sister on the splendid chestnut, the creature holding its head as if proud to bear her, like a heroine returned out of the printed “happiness ever after.”

Mr. Gascoigne went to the door and gave her his hand. She sprang down with an alacrity that might well confirm their pleasant fancies; for to-day she was resolved to set her mother’s heart at rest, and the unusual freedom of coming alone gave her a momentary lift, as if she had been allowed to breathe.

The seven kisses—mother, aunt, cousins—were not so tiresome as they used to be. They were like small household rituals one submits to, and afterward remembers with a pang.

“Mr. Grandcourt is gone out, so I determined to fill up the time by coming to you, mamma,” said Gwendolen, laying down her hat and seating herself close by her mother. Then, with a playful, warning look, “That is a punishment to you for not wearing better lace on your head. You didn’t think I should come and detect you—you dreadfully careless-about-yourself mamma!” She touched the dear head with a caress, light as a feather, heavy as a blessing.

“Scold me, dear,” said Mrs. Davilow, flushing with delight. “But I wish there was something you could eat after your ride—instead of these scraps. Let Jocosa make you a cup of chocolate in your old way. You used to like that.”

Miss Merry rose at once and went out, though Gwendolen protested, “Oh, no, a piece of bread, or one of those hard biscuits. I can’t think about eating. I am come to say good-bye.”

“What! going to Ryelands again?” said Mr. Gascoigne.

“No, we are going to town,” said Gwendolen. She broke a piece of bread, crumbling it between her fingers, but did not carry it to her mouth, as if appetite had been taken from her by some inward nausea.

“It is rather early to go to town,” said Mrs. Gascoigne, “and Mr. Grandcourt not in Parliament.”

“Oh, there is only one more day’s hunting to be had, and Henleigh has some business in town with lawyers, I think,” said Gwendolen. “I am very glad. I shall like to go to town.”

“You will see your house in Grosvenor Square,” said Mrs. Davilow; and she and the girls watched every movement, as if trying to store it like winter fruit against her absence.

“Yes,” said Gwendolen, giving assent to the interest expected of her. “And there is so much to be seen and done in town.”

“I wish, my dear Gwendolen,” said Mr. Gascoigne, in the tone of a man conscientiously paying his due of counsel, “that you would use your influence with Mr. Grandcourt to induce him to enter Parliament. A man of his position should make his weight felt in politics. The best judges are confident that the ministry will have to appeal to the country on this question of further Reform, and Mr. Grandcourt should be ready for the opportunity. I am not quite sure that his opinions and mine accord entirely; I have not heard him express himself very fully. But I don’t look at the matter from that point of view. I am thinking of your husband’s standing in the country. And he has now come to that stage of life when a man like him should enter into public affairs. A wife has great influence with her husband. Use yours in that direction, my dear.”

To Gwendolen, the speech had the taste of bitter comedy—like being offered a sugared almond when one’s mouth is full of ashes. Grandcourt, “not expressing himself very fully”! Her “influence”! Those general maxims about husbands and wives, once so useful in girlish planning, now seemed like fine printed recipes for a kitchen that did not exist.

“I should be very glad, uncle,” she said, searching for the proper answer, as if politeness were a garment she must keep without stain. “But I think Mr. Grandcourt would not like the trouble of an election—at least, unless it could be without his making speeches. I thought candidates always made speeches.”

“Not necessarily—to any great extent,” said Mr. Gascoigne. “A man of position and weight can get on without much of it. A county member need have very little trouble in that way, and both out of the House and in it is liked the better for not being a speechifier. Tell Mr. Grandcourt that I say so.”

“Here comes Jocosa with my chocolate after all,” said Gwendolen, gladly seizing the interruption as one snatches at a screen. The cup came in with its familiar steam, the thick scent of cocoa rising—sweet, dark, homely, the ghost of old afternoons. Yet to her it was suddenly oppressive, as if comfort itself had turned heavy.

Mr. Gascoigne, pushing his chair aside and crossing his leg, looked satisfied with his own good sense and his duty done. He had decided that Grandcourt was proud, but pride belonged to old families like armor to ancestral portraits; it need not imply real unkindness. Mrs. Gascoigne felt the haughtiness more sharply, as a slight toward her husband, and she could not entirely keep the resentment from touching Gwendolen.

“Your uncle and Anna will very likely be in town about Easter,” she said. “Dear Rex hopes to come out with honors and a fellowship, and he wants his father and Anna to meet him in London, that they may be jolly together, as he says. I shouldn’t wonder if Lord Brackenshaw invited them, he has been so very kind since he came back to the Castle.”

“I hope my uncle will bring Ann to stay in Grosvenor Square,” said Gwendolen, venturing thus far for the sake of the present moment—while inwardly wishing she might never be obliged to bring her family near Grandcourt again. “I am very glad of Rex’s good fortune.”

“We must not be premature, and rejoice too much beforehand,” said the rector, whose pleasure in this topic was pure and permitted. “Not but that I am in correspondence with impartial judges, who have the highest hopes about my son, as a singularly clear-headed young man. And of his excellent disposition and principle I have had the best evidence.”

“We shall have him a great lawyer some time,” said Mrs. Gascoigne.

“How very nice!” said Gwendolen; the word slipped out with a faint edge of secret scepticism as to niceness in general, which could make it apply even to lawyers.

“Talking of Lord Brackenshaw’s kindness,” said Mrs. Davilow, leaning forward with an anxious eagerness to share her bit of good, “you don’t know how delightful he has been, Gwendolen. He has begged me to consider myself his guest in this house till I can get another that I like—he did it in the most graceful way. But now a house has turned up. Old Mr. Jodson is dead, and we can have his house. It is just what I want; small, but with nothing hideous to make you miserable thinking about it. And it is only a mile from the Rectory. You remember the low white house nearly hidden by the trees, as we turn up the lane to the church?”

“Yes, but you have no furniture, poor mamma,” said Gwendolen, and the tenderness in her voice turned at once to a melancholy, because it brought with it the sting of how this comfort had been bought.

“Oh, I am saving money for that. You know who has made me rather rich, dear,” said Mrs. Davilow, laying her hand on Gwendolen’s. “And Jocosa really makes so little do for housekeeping—it is quite wonderful.”

At that, Gwendolen felt her heart swell as if it would break its bounds. The room, with its clatter of plates and easy talk, became suddenly dangerous. She could not trust herself to sit there another moment.

“Oh, please let me go up-stairs with you and arrange my hat, mamma,” she said quickly, lifting her hand to her hair and perhaps making the disarray she pretended to correct.

They went into the black and yellow bedroom, with its familiar air of modest privacy. There Gwendolen looked round as if taking leave of an entire life.

“I suppose I shall never see all this again,” she said; and then she dropped into a chair before the glass with a little groan, as if fatigue were bodily. Her face had grown very pale in the effort not to cry.

“You are not well, dear?” said Mrs. Davilow, alarm creeping into her tenderness.

“No; that chocolate has made me sick,” said Gwendolen, putting up her hand to be taken.

“I should be allowed to come to you if you were ill, darling,” said Mrs. Davilow timidly, pressing the hand to her bosom. Something in her told her to-day—more strongly than before—that her child loved her, and needed her.

“Oh, yes,” said Gwendolen, leaning her head against her mother’s, speaking as lightly as she could manage. “But you know I never am ill. I am as strong as possible; and you must not take to fretting about me, but make yourself as happy as you can with the girls. They are better children to you than I have been, you know.” She lifted her face with a smile that asked pardon and forbade questions.

“You have always been good, my darling. I remember nothing else.”

“Why, what did I ever do that was good to you, except marry Mr. Grandcourt?” said Gwendolen, starting up with a desperate resolve to be playful, as if play could nail down emotion. “And I should not have done that unless it had pleased myself.” She tossed her chin and reached for her hat.

“God forbid, child! I would not have had you marry for my sake. Your happiness by itself is half mine.”

“Very well,” said Gwendolen, arranging her hat with fastidious care, each pin pushed in as if it could fasten her feelings too. “Then you will please to consider that you are half happy, which is more than I am used to seeing you.” She turned again with her old playful smile.

Then, as if the words had opened a door and she must rush through before she lost courage, she said quickly, “Now I am ready; but oh, mamma, Mr. Grandcourt gives me a quantity of money, and expects me to spend it, and I can’t spend it; and you know I can’t bear charity children and all that; and here are thirty pounds. I wish the girls would spend it for me on little things for themselves when you go to the new house. Tell them so.”

She put the notes into her mother’s hands and looked away at once, moving toward the door, as if the sight of gratitude would undo her.

“God bless you, dear,” said Mrs. Davilow. “It will please them so that you should have thought of them in particular.”

“Oh, they are troublesome things; but they don’t trouble me now,” said Gwendolen, turning to nod playfully, as if she must not let the act look serious even to herself.

She went through the rest of her visit and all the good-byes with quiet propriety, holding herself together as one holds a full cup steady across a room. And when she rode away, the chestnut’s hoofs striking a clean rhythm on the road, the winter air sharp in her nostrils, she said to herself with sarcasm that was half a sob held back: “I think I am making a very good Mrs. Grandcourt.”

She believed her husband had gone to Gadsmere that day. She had inferred it, as she had long ago inferred who were the inmates of the place he had called “a dog-hutch of a place in a black country.” And the knowledge set her nerves on edge with a strange heat. Contradictions tugged at her: why should it sting her that he showed concern for those beings on whose account she herself carried remorse like a hidden bruise? Had she not, before marriage, determined inwardly to speak and act on their behalf? And since he had lately implied he wanted to be in town because he was making arrangements about his will, ought she not to be glad of any sign that a conscience stirred in him toward Gadsmere?

Yet now, as a wife, the thought of his being there was like red iron laid close to her skin.

She had brought on herself, in her own eyes, an indignity that she could not wash away: the humiliation of being doomed to terrified silence lest her husband should discover with what sort of consciousness she had married him. And after the moments of secret hatred toward this man who from the first had cowed her, there came back always that heavy spiritual pressure which made submission seem inevitable. Any effort at freedom threatened only fresh and worse humiliation. She dared nothing except sudden impulse; least of all did she dare to plan a vague future where the only certainty was indignity.

Even remorse could not quite blot out what still seemed the worst result of her marriage—that she should ever make a spectacle of herself. The humiliation was lightened by thinking that only Mrs. Glasher knew the fact that gave it its sharpest point. Gwendolen had never connected the interview at the Whispering Stones with Lush’s agency; her vague terror gave every threat a shadowy omnipresence, as if fatal power moved without channels and without hands. In her mind the secret lay with Mrs. Glasher alone; and in the horrible letter there were words implying that Mrs. Glasher would dread disclosure to the husband as much as the usurping Mrs. Grandcourt did.

Another thing, too, Gwendolen counted as more hidden from her husband than it truly was—her own suppressed struggle, the desperate rebellion that frightened her by its very strength. Grandcourt could not imagine how life felt to her except where it touched his own will; he had no vision of the shadowy powers that governed her. But here he had a sensibility that was like divination. Seeing only what concerned himself, he saw it with false proportions: he magnified her inward resistance, and the magnifying only increased his satisfaction in mastering it.
