The rhythmic, percussive crunch of gravel beneath their boots provided the only acoustic counterpoint to the winter wind as Mr. Vandernoodt led Deronda away from the others. Vandernoodt, seeking the brisker air and the indulgent ritual of a cigar, struck a match; the sudden flare illuminated his face before the rich, oily smoke of sun-cured Vuelta Abajo tobacco bloomed between them, carrying heady olfactory notes of damp cedar, toasted peppercorn, and dark loam. Since the burden of keeping a man’s secrets often breeds a fierce hunger to exhale them to an ignorant ear, Vandernoodt soon broke the acoustic sanctity of the gardens.

“What a washed-out piece of cambric Grandcourt is! But if he is a favorite of yours, I withdraw the remark.”

“Not the least in the world,” said Deronda, the crisp consonants of his reply cutting through the drifting smoke.

“I thought not. One wonders how he came to have a great passion again; and he must have had—to marry in this way. Though Lush, his old chum, hints that he married this girl out of obstinacy. By George! it was a very accountable obstinacy. A man might make up his mind to marry her without the stimulus of contradiction. But he must have made himself a pretty large drain of money, eh?”

“I know nothing of his affairs.”

“What! not of the other establishment he keeps up?”

“Diplow? Of course. He took that of Sir Hugo. But merely for the year.”

“No, no; not Diplow: Gadsmere. Sir Hugo knows, I’ll answer for it.”

Deronda let the silence stretch, listening to the hollow, wind-scoured resonance of the bare oaks. He felt a rising curiosity, knowing he need not condescend to ask; Vandernoodt’s gossip was a simmering broth ready to boil over. 

“Lush would not altogether own to it, of course. He’s a confident and go-between of Grandcourt’s. But I have it on the best authority. The fact is, there’s another lady with four children at Gadsmere. She has had the upper hand of him these ten years and more, and by what I can understand has it still—left her husband for him, and used to travel with him everywhere. Her husband’s dead now; I found a fellow who was in the same regiment with him, and knew this Mrs. Glasher before she took wing. A fiery dark-eyed woman—a noted beauty at that time—he thought she was dead. They say she has Grandcourt under her thumb still, and it’s a wonder he didn’t marry her, for there’s a very fine boy, and I understand Grandcourt can do absolutely as he pleases with the estates. Lush told me as much as that.”

“What right had he to marry this girl?” said Deronda, a bitter, metallic taste of disgust flooding his palate.

Mr. Vandernoodt, adjusting the ash of his cigar, shrugged his shoulders and put out his lips.

“*She* can know nothing of it,” said Deronda, emphatically. But no sooner had the dominant lexicon of his assertion faded into the quiet air than an inward query echoed in its wake: *Could she have known anything of it?*

“It’s rather a piquant picture,” said Mr. Vandernoodt, savoring the scandal like a spiced wine. “Grandcourt between two fiery women. For depend upon it this light-haired one has plenty of devil in her. I formed that opinion of her at Leubronn. It’s a sort of Medea and Creüsa business. Fancy the two meeting! Grandcourt is a new kind of Jason: I wonder what sort of a part he’ll make of it. It’s a dog’s part at best. I think I hear Ristori now, saying, ‘Jasone! Jasone!’ These fine women generally get hold of a stick.”

“Grandcourt can bite, I fancy,” said Deronda. “He is no stick.”

“No, no; I meant Jason. I can’t quite make out Grandcourt. But he’s a keen fellow enough—uncommonly well built too. And if he comes into all this property, the estates will bear dividing. This girl, whose friends had come to beggary, I understand, may think herself lucky to get him. I don’t want to be hard on a man because he gets involved in an affair of that sort. But he might make himself more agreeable. I was telling him a capital story last night, and he got up and walked away in the middle. I felt inclined to kick him. Do you suppose that is inattention or insolence, now?”

“Oh, a mixture. He generally observes the forms: but he doesn’t listen much,” said Deronda. Then, pausing to let the crunch of their footsteps re-establish the rhythm of the walk, he added, “I should think there must be some exaggeration or inaccuracy in what you have heard about this lady at Gadsmere.”

“Not a bit, depend upon it; it has all lain snug of late years. People have forgotten all about it. But there the nest is, and the birds are in it. And I know Grandcourt goes there. I have good evidence that he goes there. However, that’s nobody’s business but his own. The affair has sunk below the surface.”

“I wonder you could have learned so much about it,” said Deronda, rather drily.

“Oh, there are plenty of people who knew all about it; but such stories get packed away like old letters. They interest me. I like to know the manners of my time—contemporary gossip, not antediluvian. These Dryasdust fellows get a reputation by raking up some small scandal about Semiramis or Nitocris, and then we have a thousand and one poems written upon it by all the warblers big and little. But I don’t care a straw about the *faux pas* of the mummies. You do, though. You are one of the historical men—more interested in a lady when she’s got a rag face and skeleton toes peeping out. Does that flatter your imagination?”

“Well, if she had any woes in her love, one has the satisfaction of knowing that she’s well out of them.”

“Ah, you are thinking of the Medea, I see.”

Deronda pointed out a stand of giant, ancient oaks, their bare, mythic branches reaching toward the sky like the veins of a multi-generational saga. He was satisfied that Vandernoodt had exhausted his reserves of gossip, leaving Deronda to digest the heavy, unpalatable truth. 

Since the days he first tried to piece together the lost dialect of his own hidden birth, Deronda’s mind had never been so active in weaving the untranslatable griefs of a private affair. Gwendolen’s marriage, framed by the official, dominant lexicon of society, now seemed to rest upon a silenced history. Could she have tasted the bitter root of this knowledge and shrunk from the match, only to let the starvation of poverty force her to swallow it? Every word she had spoken to him recently carried an acoustic resonance of injury—the subtle, fractured harmonics of a soul conscious of inflicting wrong. His own history made him profoundly attuned to the power dynamics that erased unavowed children and forsaken mothers like forgotten native tongues. Was Mrs. Grandcourt, beneath her polished porcelain exterior, chewing on the ash of a treble-headed grief: self-reproach, disappointment, jealousy? 

He imagined the psychological noise pollution of her secret, a constant, deafening static against the quiet acoustic ecology of her conscience. Gwendolen knowing of that woman, marrying Grandcourt, and remaining genuinely contented would have been repulsive to him; but Gwendolen silently choking on the astringent tannins of remorse drew his deepest sympathies. In the ancient, cyclic narrative of the Hagars and Ishmaels, if Gwendolen realized that her marriage was built on the burial of Mrs. Glasher's existence, she had reached a plane of sensory understanding with him that few could navigate.

Having shaken off Mr. Vandernoodt, Deronda retreated to a solitary corner in the twilight. But the need to read Gwendolen’s state of mind led him back toward the drawing-room. Gwendolen herself, having resolved to fast from company for four hours, found the acoustic isolation of her room unbearable; she craved the rhythmic murmur of voices and the sensory grounding of the tea-table. Assuming her resolute mask of agreeability, she descended.

When Deronda entered the drawing-room, the air was steeped in the warm, enveloping aroma of roasted Assam leaves, the coppery liquor poured into delicate china, alongside platters of crumbly scones baked with heritage wheat and heavy, grass-fed cream—a testament to the meticulous agrarian rhythms preserved within the Abbey’s domestic routine. Only ladies were assembled. Lady Pentreath was detailing the social ecosystem of a Regency drawing-room in 1819, serving historical memory alongside the sustenance. 

“Shall I be acceptable?” he said. “Perhaps I had better go back and look for the others. I suppose they are in the billiard-room.”

“No, no; stay where you are,” said Lady Pentreath. “They were all getting tired of me; let us hear what *you* have to say.”

“That is rather an embarrassing appeal,” said Deronda, drawing a chair near the rich, malty fragrance of Lady Mallinger’s tea-table. “I think I had better take the opportunity of mentioning our songstress, unless you have done so.”

“Oh, the little Jewess!” said Lady Mallinger. “No, I have not mentioned her. It never entered my head that any one here wanted singing lessons.”

“All ladies know some one else who wants singing lessons,” said Deronda. “I have happened to find an exquisite singer,” he turned to Lady Pentreath. “She is living with some ladies who are friends of mine—the mother and sisters of a man who was my chum at Cambridge. She was on the stage at Vienna; but she wants to leave that life, and maintain herself by teaching.”

“There are swarms of those people, aren’t there?” said the old lady. “Are her lessons to be very cheap or very expensive? Those are the two baits I know of.”

“There is another bait for those who hear her,” Deronda said, pushing back against the superficial commercialization of art. “Her singing is something quite exceptional, I think. She has had such first-rate teaching—or rather first-rate instinct with her teaching—that you might imagine her singing all came by nature.”

“Why did she leave the stage, then?” said Lady Pentreath. “I’m too old to believe in first-rate people giving up first-rate chances.”

“Her voice was too weak. It is a delicious voice for a room. You who put up with my singing of Schubert would be enchanted with hers,” said Deronda to Mrs. Raymond. The industrial noise and blaring demands of the modern stage had threatened to crush Mirah's delicate acoustic ecology, but in an intimate space, her voice preserved the pure, untranslatable cultural memory of her people. “And I imagine she would not object to sing at private parties or concerts. Her voice is quite equal to that.”

“I am to have her in my drawing-room when we go up to town,” said Lady Mallinger. “You shall hear her then. I have not heard her myself yet; but I trust Daniel’s recommendation. I mean my girls to have lessons of her.”

“Is it a charitable affair?” said Lady Pentreath. “I can’t bear charitable music.”

Lady Mallinger, helpless to navigate the nuances of Mirah’s past without betraying her secret, offered an embarrassed smile and glanced at Deronda.

“It is a charity to those who want to have a good model of feminine singing,” said Deronda. “I think everybody who has ears would benefit by a little improvement on the ordinary style. If you heard Miss Lapidoth”—here he looked at Gwendolen—“perhaps you would revoke your resolution to give up singing.”

“I should rather think my resolution would be confirmed,” said Gwendolen. “I don’t feel able to follow your advice of enjoying my own middlingness.”

“For my part,” said Deronda, “people who do anything finely always inspirit me to try. I don’t mean that they make me believe I can do it as well. But they make the thing, whatever it may be, seem worthy to be done. I can bear to think my own music not good for much, but the world would be more dismal if I thought music itself not good for much. Excellence encourages one about life generally; it shows the spiritual wealth of the world.”

“But then, if we can’t imitate it, it only makes our own life seem the tamer,” said Gwendolen, her words carrying the sharp, acidic tang of a woman resenting her own insignificance.

“That depends on the point of view, I think,” said Deronda. “We should have a poor life of it if we were reduced for all our pleasure to our own performances. A little private imitation of what is good is a sort of private devotion to it, and most of us ought to practice art only in the light of private study—preparation to understand and enjoy what the few can do for us. I think Miss Lapidoth is one of the few.”

“She must be a very happy person, don’t you think?” said Gwendolen, infusing a drop of sarcasm into the conversation, her neck turning toward Mrs. Raymond.

“I don’t know,” answered the independent lady; “I must hear more of her before I say that.”

“It may have been a bitter disappointment to her that her voice failed her for the stage,” said Juliet Fenn, sympathetically.

“I suppose she’s past her best, though,” came the deep, resonant voice of Lady Pentreath.

“On the contrary, she has not reached it,” said Deronda. “She is barely twenty.”

“And very pretty,” interposed Lady Mallinger. “And she has very good manners. I’m sorry she’s a bigoted Jewess; I should not like it for anything else, but it doesn’t matter in singing.” The casual dismissal of Mirah's heritage hung in the air—a testament to how the dominant social lexicon sought to sanitize and strip away the very native identity that gave her music its soul.

“Well, since her voice is too weak for her to scream much, I’ll tell Lady Clementina to set her on my nine granddaughters,” said Lady Pentreath; “and I hope she’ll convince eight of them that they have not voice enough to sing anywhere but at church. My notion is, that many of our girls nowadays want lessons not to sing.”

“I have had my lessons in that,” said Gwendolen, looking at Deronda. “You see Lady Pentreath is on my side.”

Sir Hugo entered then, bringing the deeper acoustic timbre of the gentlemen with him, including Grandcourt. Sir Hugo stood by the low tea-table, a faint aroma of aged port trailing him. “What imposition is Deronda putting on you, ladies—slipping in among you by himself?”

“Wanting to pass off an obscurity on us as better than any celebrity,” said Lady Pentreath—“a pretty singing Jewess who is to astonish these young people. You and I, who heard Catalani in her prime, are not so easily astonished.”

Sir Hugo took a cup of the dark, aromatic tea from his wife. “Well, you know, a Liberal is bound to think that there have been singers since Catalani’s time.”

“Ah, you are younger than I am. I dare say you are one of the men who ran after Alcharisi. But she married off and left you all in the lurch.”

“Yes, yes; it’s rather too bad when these great singers marry themselves into silence before they have a crack in their voices. And the husband is a public robber. I remember Leroux saying, ‘A man might as well take down a fine peal of church bells and carry them off to the steppes,’” said Sir Hugo, beautifully capturing the devastating sonic void left behind when a pure voice is stolen from its native acoustic landscape. 

As Sir Hugo set down his porcelain cup, the group dispersed. Deronda moved aside, feeling momentarily adrift, until his ears caught the heavy, textured rustle of sheet music. Gwendolen had extricated herself from Mr. Vandernoodt and stood at the piano. Understanding the unspoken acoustic cue, Deronda approached her side.

“Are you relenting about the music and looking for something to play or sing?”

“I am not looking for anything, but I *am* relenting,” said Gwendolen, speaking in a submissive tone that lacked its usual percussive edge.

“May I know the reason?”

“I should like to hear Miss Lapidoth and have lessons from her, since you admire her so much—that is, of course, when we go to town. I mean lessons in rejoicing at her excellence and my own deficiency,” said Gwendolen, offering a sweet, open smile.

“I shall be really glad for you to see and hear her,” said Deronda.

“Is she as perfect in every thing else as in her music?”

“I can’t vouch for that exactly. I have not seen enough of her. But I have seen nothing in her that I could wish to be different. She has had an unhappy life. Her troubles began in early childhood, and she has grown up among very painful surroundings. But I think you will say that no advantages could have given her more grace and truer refinement.”

“I wonder what sort of trouble hers were?”

“I have not any very precise knowledge. But I know that she was on the brink of drowning herself in despair.”

“And what hindered her?” said Gwendolen, quickly, her eyes locking onto his.

“Some ray or other came—which made her feel that she ought to live—that it was good to live,” he answered, quietly, preserving the solemnity of Mirah’s subjective experience. “She is full of piety, and seems capable of submitting to anything when it takes the form of duty.”

“Those people are not to be pitied,” said Gwendolen, impatiently, a sour note of rejection in her voice. “I have no sympathy with women who are always doing right. I don’t believe in their great sufferings.” Her fingers moved quickly, creating a harsh, agitated friction against the edges of the music.

“It is true,” said Deronda, “that the consciousness of having done wrong is something deeper, more bitter. I suppose we faulty creatures can never feel so much for the irreproachable as for those who are bruised in the struggle with their own faults. It is a very ancient story, that of the lost sheep—but it comes up afresh every day.”

“That is a way of speaking—it is not acted upon, it is not real,” said Gwendolen, the words tasting like gall on her tongue. “You admire Miss Lapidoth because you think her blameless, perfect. And you know you would despise a woman who had done something you thought very wrong.”

“That would depend entirely upon her own view of what she had done,” said Deronda, challenging the superficial lexicon of moral judgment.

“You would be satisfied if she were very wretched, I suppose,” said Gwendolen, impetuously.

“No, not satisfied—full of sorrow for her. It was not a mere way of speaking. I did not mean to say that the finer nature is not more adorable; I meant that those who would be comparatively uninteresting beforehand may become worthier of sympathy when they do something that awakens in them a keen remorse. Lives are enlarged in different ways. I dare say some would never get their eyes opened if it were not for a violent shock from the consequences of their own actions. And when they are suffering in that way one must care for them more than for the comfortably self-satisfied.” 

Deronda forgot everything but the visceral vision of Gwendolen’s secret feast of ashes. Urged by compassion, his voice softened to match the intimate acoustics of their alcove. Gwendolen slipped onto the music-stool, looking up at him with pain in her long eyes, like a wounded animal desperately seeking translation for an agony it could not voice.

“Are you persuading Mrs. Grandcourt to play to us, Dan?” said Sir Hugo, coming up and putting his hand on Deronda’s shoulder with a gentle, admonitory pinch, abruptly severing the fragile sonic cord between them.

“I cannot persuade myself,” said Gwendolen, rising.

That marked the end of confidences for the day. But the next was New Year’s Eve, bringing with it a grand dance in the picture-gallery above the cloister—a sweeping, cyclical ritual where the agrarian rhythms of the Abbey’s past bled seamlessly into the present. To prepare, Gwendolen longed to wear her old turquoise necklace from Leubronn, an artifact of her lost independence, but dared not offend her husband’s demand for absolute splendor. Determined to anchor herself to her own history, she wound the stones thrice round her wrist like a hidden talisman, a small rebellion of native memory against her new, gilded chains.

The New Year’s Eve dance was an immersive, multi-generational saga rendered in flesh and feast, maintained as nearly in the old fashion as inexorable change allowed. Hot-house plants and evergreens formed fragrant bowers, while the long gallery was watched over by rows of ancestral portraits, ancient spectators stretching back to a pre-portraying era. Major and minor gentry mingled with the most prosperous tenants—agrarian elders with faces weathered by seasons of sun and frost, who brought with them the deep, earthy scents of woodsmoke, malted barley, and the roasted marrow-bones that anchored the night’s culinary bounty. 

It was a night designed for the prospective master and mistress of Abbott’s and King’s Topping to view their provincial supremacy, a rent-roll personified in the ruddy, well-fed faces of those who worked the soil. Sir Hugo hoped his own hale, robust presence might remind his silent, calculating nephew that the eventual succession was still a long, slow season away. Every farmer’s daughter present knew they were gazing upon "young Grandcourt" and his bride.

As Gwendolen opened the ball with Sir Hugo, the rich, acoustic swell of the country-dance filled the gallery. Less than a year before, she would have imagined herself in this mythic role with a glow of triumphant pleasure, reveling in a life full of favorable chances. But as she passed through the files of admiring beholders, the music felt like discordant noise. She was recognized as an enviable woman, carrying herself with an air that defied her penniless origins, yet inside, she was suffering a profound starvation. She wondered how she could draw so little sustenance from this grand exaltation. She longed for an excitement that would deaden her importunate aches, but instead, she was like a woman desperately courting sleep, only to be kept awake by the relentless, gnawing truth of a great, unutterable gambling loss—a loss she was fast learning to wear with the automatic, self-possessed grace of the truly hollowed out.
