Two hours passed—about four o’clock—and I awoke, not from rest, but from the relentless grip of thirst. The exhaustion that had finally claimed my body earlier had been replaced by a deeper agony: my mouth burned, my throat felt like cracked leather, and every breath was a struggle. Sleep was impossible now. I’d been dreaming of cool water rushing over smooth stones beneath a green canopy of trees, only to wake in this endless, sun-baked wasteland. And then it came back—the truth Umbopa had warned us about: if we didn’t find water today, we would die.

No human could survive long without water in this furnace. I sat up, rubbing my grimy face with hands so dry and rough they felt like sandpaper. My lips were stuck shut, my eyelids glued together. It took effort just to open them. Dawn was near, but there was no freshness in the air—only a thick, oppressive heat that clung like smoke. The others still slept, their breathing shallow and uneven.

As light began to creep across the desert, I pulled out my pocket copy of *The Ingoldsby Legends*—a small book I’d brought for distraction—and opened it to “The Jackdaw of Rheims.” When I read the line, “A nice little boy held a golden ewer, Embossed, and filled with water as pure As any that flows between Rheims and Namur,” I snapped my cracked lips together—trying, failing—then laughed aloud. Not a real laugh, more like a broken cackle. The thought of that pure water drove me mad. If the Cardinal had been standing there with his bell, book, and candle, I would have lunged forward and drained that sacred basin, even if it was full of soap suds fit for washing the Pope’s hands. I’d have taken the curse of the Church upon me—anything to quench this fire inside.

I must have been half delirious. I imagined myself, a scorched, grizzled elephant hunter, suddenly appearing between the Cardinal and his boy, diving headfirst into the ewer, gulping down every drop. The image made me laugh again—loud enough to wake the others. They stirred, rubbing their faces, prying apart their gummed lips and eyes, groggy and disoriented.

Once fully awake, we gathered around, silent at first, then speaking in low voices. The situation was desperate. Not a single drop remained. We turned our bottles upside down, licked the rims—nothing. Good, who carried the flask of brandy, stared at it longingly, but Sir Henry snatched it away. “Drinking raw spirit will only kill us faster,” he said.

“If we don’t find water, we’re done for,” I said.

“If the old Dom’s map is accurate, there should be some,” I added.

But no one believed it. We all knew better than to trust a sketch drawn on a shirttail.

Then Ventvögel stood. The Hottentot moved slowly, scanning the ground, his eyes sharp. He stopped abruptly, exclaiming something guttural, pointing at the sand.

“What is it?” we shouted, rising instantly.

“Springbok tracks,” I said, stepping forward. “So what?”

“Springbucks never go far from water,” he replied in Dutch.

“No,” I said, “I forgot. Thank God.”

That tiny clue lit a spark. In moments like these, when hope is dead, even a faint sign can revive the soul. A single star in a black sky is better than none.

Ventvögel raised his snub nose and sniffed the air, like a ram scenting danger. Then he spoke again.

“I _smell_ water,” he said.

We nearly cheered. These men, born of the wild, had instincts sharper than any animal’s.

Just then, the sun rose—glorious, golden, blazing. And what it revealed stunned us.

There, not fifty miles away, glittering like silver under the morning rays, stood Sheba’s Breasts—two colossal mountains, linked by a sheer cliff, towering into the sky. Beyond them stretched the Suliman Berg, a vast chain of cliffs and plateaus, broken only by flat-topped peaks like those near Cape Town. The sight was beyond words. Two enormous peaks, each at least fifteen thousand feet high, shaped like breasts, their snow-capped summits like nipples. At times, mist curled beneath them, forming the outline of a sleeping woman, veiled in mystery.

Their bases swelled gently from the plain, smooth and round. The connecting cliff soared thousands of feet, sheer and unclimbable. On either side, ridges of rock ran into the distance. The scale was terrifying—so grand, so solemn, that we fell silent, awestruck.

For a moment, the sunlight danced on the snow and stone. Then clouds rolled in, wrapping the mountains in soft, ghostly mist. They vanished—like a secret hidden from view.

But our thirst returned, burning hotter than ever.

Ventvögel still insisted he smelled water. But we saw nothing. Only endless sand and scrub. We walked around the nearby hillock, searching every direction. No pan. No spring. Nothing.

“You’re a fool,” I snapped. “There’s no water.”

He lifted his nose again. “I smell it, Baas. It’s in the air.”

“Yes,” I said bitterly, “probably in the clouds. It’ll fall in two months—too late to wash our bones.”

Sir Henry stroked his beard. “Maybe it’s on top of the hill?”

“Rubbish!” Good scoffed. “Water on a hilltop? Never heard of such a thing!”

“Let’s go look,” I said. And so, with no hope left, we climbed the sandy slope, Umbopa leading.

Then he froze—petrified.

“_Nanzia manzie!_” he yelled. “Here is water!”

We rushed to him. And there, in a deep hollow at the very top of the koppie, lay a pool—dark, muddy, foul-looking. But it was water. Real water.

We didn’t hesitate. We dropped to our stomachs and drank—gulping, sucking, devouring it like gods tasting nectar. Heaven help us, we drank until we couldn’t breathe.

Then we stripped off our clothes and plunged into the pool, letting the tepid, brackish water soak into our parched skin. For those of you who’ve only known water from taps—hot and cold at your command—you cannot imagine the joy of that muddy bath. It was ecstasy.

Afterward, we ate what biltong remained—barely able to chew it before. We smoked a pipe and fell asleep under the shadow of the bank, beside the blessed pool, until noon.

All day we rested, grateful for the water, however bad. We gave thanks—not only to luck, but to the memory of da Silvestra, whose crude map had led us here. How the pool lasted so long, we couldn’t say. But I suspect it was fed by a hidden spring deep in the sand.

We refilled our bottles, reloaded our packs, and set off again under the moon. That night, we covered nearly twenty-five miles. No water found. But the next day, we found shade behind ant hills—a small mercy.

At dawn, the mists cleared, and Sheba’s Breasts loomed ahead—now only twenty miles distant. They seemed to rise directly above us, more magnificent than ever.

By evening, we marched again. By daylight the next morning, we stood on the lower slopes of the left breast. Our water was gone again. Thirst gnawed at us. Ahead, only snow—far, far above.

We rested briefly, driven by desperation, then continued, climbing the lava slopes. The mountain’s base was ancient lava, cooled and cracked from some forgotten eruption. The clinker beneath our feet was rough, tearing our soles. By eleven, we were exhausted—worn down by heat, hunger, and pain.

A few hundred yards ahead, large lava rocks offered shade. We limped toward them, collapsing beneath their shelter. There, on a ridge nearby, I noticed something strange—green growth covering the clinker. Soil formed from decomposed lava had collected, seeds carried by birds. But grass wouldn’t feed us.

Then I saw Umbopa rise, hobbling toward the patch. Minutes later, he was dancing, shouting, waving something green.

We scrambled over—hope flaring.

“It’s food and water!” he cried in Zulu. “Macumazahn!”

And then I saw it—wild melons. Thousands of them, ripe and heavy. We grabbed them, tore them open, and devoured them. Six each—perhaps more. They were sour, watery, but divine. We cut the rest in half and set them upright in the sun to cool through evaporation.

But melons aren’t filling. Soon we were ravenous again. Biltong remained, but our stomachs rebelled. We had to ration it.

Then—luck.

Across the desert, a flock of ten large birds flew straight toward us.

“_Skit, Baas, skit!_” whispered Ventvögel, dropping to his belly. We followed suit.

They were _pauw_—bustards. They passed within fifty yards. I grabbed a Winchester, waited, then jumped up. The birds clustered together—exactly as I’d hoped. Two shots. One fell—twenty pounds of meat.

In half an hour, we had a fire built from melon stalks, and the bird was roasting. We feasted—every scrap eaten, bones and beak left. We felt stronger.

That night, we carried melons and climbed higher. The air grew cooler—relief. By dawn, we were close to the snow line.

More melons appeared. Water was no longer a worry. But the climb grew steeper—barely a mile per hour. That night, we ate our last piece of biltong.

Still, no game. No streams. Odd, given the snow above. But we later learned all meltwater flowed down the north face.

Now, food became our fear.

For three days, our condition worsened. I’ll quote from my journal:

**21st May** —Started at 11 a.m. Cold enough to travel by day. Carried melons. Struggled all day. No more melons. No game. Halted at sundown. No food for hours. Suffered greatly from cold.

**22nd** —Started at sunrise. Faint, weak. Made five miles. Found patches of snow—ate some. Camped under a plateau. Bitter cold. Drank a little brandy. Huddled together, wrapped in blankets. Starvation and weariness crushing us. Ventvögel nearly died.

**23rd** —Started at sunrise. Frozen limbs thawed slightly. In dreadful shape. Fearful we won’t survive. Little brandy left. Good, Sir Henry, and Umbopa hold on. Ventvögel worse—Hottentots can’t endure cold. Hunger dulls, but stomach feels numb. Now level with the lava wall linking the two peaks. View is glorious. Desert behind us. Snow ahead, swelling upward. Nipple of the mountain rises four thousand feet. No living thing. God help us—we may be at the end.

I’ll stop the journal here. What follows needs full telling.

All day on the 23rd, we trudged slowly up the snow, stopping often to rest. We looked like ghosts—gaunt, burdened, dragging ourselves across the blinding white plain. Eyes scanned the horizon—useless. Nothing to eat.

We managed seven miles. Just before sunset, we stood beneath the nipple of Sheba’s left breast—a massive dome of frozen snow, soaring thousands of feet.

Weak as we were, we couldn’t help but admire the scene. The setting sun painted the snow blood-red, crowning the peak with golden glory.

“Look,” gasped Good. “We should be near the cave the old man wrote about.”

“Possibly,” I said. “If it exists.”

“Come, Quatermain,” Sir Henry urged. “Don’t doubt the Dom. Remember the water. We’ll find it.”

“If we don’t before dark,” I said, “we’re dead.”

Ten minutes passed in silence. Then Umbopa, walking beside me, gripped my arm.

“Look!” he said, pointing.

Two hundred yards away, in the snow, was a hole.

“The cave,” he said.

We hurried toward it. Yes—it was a cavern. Likely the same one da Silvestra described.

We reached it just as the sun vanished—no twilight here. Darkness fell fast.

We crawled inside. The cave was small. We huddled together, sharing the last drops of brandy—barely a sip each. Tried to sleep.

But the cold was unbearable. I believe the temperature was fifteen degrees below freezing. For us—drained by heat, hunger, and exhaustion—it was torture. Frost crept over us, biting fingers, toes, faces. We pressed closer, but our bodies had no warmth left.

Sleep came in fits—brief, uneasy. Perhaps that saved us. Had we slept deeply, I doubt we’d have woken.

Near dawn, I heard Ventvögel sigh. His teeth had chattered all night like castanets. Then—silence.

I didn’t think much of it. Thought he’d fallen asleep. His back leaned against mine. But it grew colder—then icy.

Then the sky lightened. Golden arrows pierced the snow. The sun rose above the lava wall, casting cold light into the cave.

And there, sitting among us—was Ventvögel. Stone dead.

We dragged ourselves from the corpse—fear of death too strong to bear its presence. Left him sitting, arms crossed over his knees.

Sunlight flooded the cave. Suddenly, someone screamed.

I turned.

At the far end of the cavern—only twenty feet long—sat another figure. Head bowed, arms hanging. I stared.

It was a dead man.

And he was white.

The others saw it too.

Our shattered nerves broke. One by one, we scrambled out of the cave—half-frozen, half-mad—leaving the dead behind.
