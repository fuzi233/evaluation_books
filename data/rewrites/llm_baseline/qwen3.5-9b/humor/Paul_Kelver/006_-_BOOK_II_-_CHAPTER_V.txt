"Correct." That was the single word that defined Doctor Florret and everything attached to him. He was a large man, but not *too* large—just the perfect specimen for the headmaster of a respectable middle-class school. Stout, yes, but not fat; he suggested comfort rather than gluttony. His hands were white and elegant. On his left hand sat a fine diamond ring that shimmered with a dignified glow rather than a flashy sparkle. When he spoke about mundane topics like the weather, his voice automatically elevated them to matters of state. His face was clean-shaven and radiated a kindness carefully balanced with caution.

Everything around him matched this standard. His wife was his perfect mirror, a feminine duplicate crafted specifically to complement him. Looking at them side-by-side, one might be tempted to believe that nature had finally reverted to its original blueprint, molding her directly from one of his ribs. Their furniture was sturdy and functional, built for use, not show. Their paintings hung on the walls exactly as etiquette dictated: visible, but never demanding attention. Doctor Florret always said the right thing, at the right time, in the right tone. If you weren't sure what the "correct" action was, you simply waited for him to demonstrate it; once he moved, it became obvious that nothing else could possibly have been acceptable. He held the correct opinions on everything. To disagree with him was to declare yourself a revolutionary.

In practice, however, as I soon learned at the cost of several wasted years, he adhered strictly to the methods deemed "correct" by English educators since the reign of Edward VI.

I tried desperately to keep up. I wanted to learn. Inside me burned that universal ambition of a boy who feels his center is everywhere and refuses to settle anywhere specific. If I passed a street orator, hatless and sweating, gesturing wildly toward utopian ideals, I immediately saw myself in his place: a Demosthenes swaying crowds, a statesman mesmerizing Parliament, the Prime Minister of England, adored by the entire nation. I imagined the opposition papers forced to publish reluctant praise. If I heard a distant drumbeat, I pictured vast fields of battle, with myself standing prominently in the front line, isolated and glorious. Since promotion in the British Army supposedly depended entirely on merit, I envisioned myself returning as Commander-in-Chief. Crowds would fill every decorated street; white hands would wave from every window. I'd hear the deep roar of welcome as I rode slowly at the head of my victorious troops on either a snow-white or coal-black charger (the color choice caused me much anxiety—I refused to let a single flaw ruin the day). I'd walk right into a lamppost or step on the toes of an angry old gentleman, and wake up.

Sometimes a drunk sailor would storm out of swinging doors and cause a scene; the smoke from a factory chimney would transform into a swaying ship's mast. Costers nearby would shout, "Ay, ay, sir! Ready, ay, ready!" I was Christopher Columbus, Sir Francis Drake, and Admiral Nelson, all rolled into one. Ignoring modern mapmakers, I would discover new continents. I would defeat the French—those useful French!—and die in the moment of victory. A nation would mourn me, burying me in Westminster Abbey. Or perhaps I would live, created a Duke. Both options had their charms; personally, I was indifferent. While boys reading letters from their mothers about severe toothaches on November 9th listened to Doctor Florret snort with disdain, I heard them on November 10th boasting about the glories of the Lord Mayor's Show. Their voices grew fainter and fainter, carried away by the ringing bells of Bow.

I saw myself as a young merchant prince. Nobles would crowd my counting house; I would lend them millions and marry their daughters. Later, hiding in a corner, I would overhear discussions about a new book and instantly become a celebrated author. Everyone would praise me, for I knew nothing about critics or reviews at the time. I wrote poetry, fiction, and history, and everyone read and marveled. The only snag was a crumpled rose petal stuck in the pillow under my swollen head: handwriting was torture, and spelling was a puzzle. I wrote with sorrow, covered in blotches and scribbled corrections. Almost, I abandoned the idea of becoming an author.

No matter which path I chose to reach the Elysian Fields of fame, I realized that education was the essential weapon I needed. So, with an aching heart and an aching head, I devoured my books. I picture myself now in my tiny bedroom, elbows propped on a wobbly, one-legged table, startled whenever my frizzy hair touched the lone candle flame. On cold nights, I wore my overcoat turned up around my neck, a blanket wrapped tight around my legs. Often, I had to stuff my fingers in my ears to block out the noisy life rising impertinently from downstairs. *"A song, of a song, to a song, a song, O! Song!"* *"I love, thou lovest, he/she/it loves..."* The words repeated endlessly until my own voice sounded like a strange buzzing insect surrounding me, while my head seemed to shrink until I raised my hands in fright, wondering if it was still intact on my shoulders.

Was I stupider than average, or is a boy's brain physically incapable of handling the demands of our educational system?

*"Latin and Greek,"* I heard the smooth tones of Doctor Florret repeat, echoing the solemn croak of Correctness forever, *"are useful as mental gymnastics."* My dear Doctor Florret and company, couldn't you, from your vast storehouse of truly necessary knowledge, select tools better suited to strengthen—and not overstrain—the mental muscles of children aged ten to fourteen? You, gentle reader, with a fully grown brain trained by years of practice to its subtleties, take me from your shelf. Take Browning, or even Shakespeare. You know this language well; it's your mother tongue. Translate this short passage for me; parse and analyze these few verses; break them down into their component parts! Now, will you maintain that it is beneficial for Tommy—a tear-stained, ink-splattered little brat—to be subjected to Aesop's Fables or Ovid's Metamorphoses in the same rigorous manner? Wouldn't it be just as sensible to insist that he practice his skinny little arms with one-hundred-pound dumbbells?

We were the sons of city men, of moderately well-off professionals, minor officials, clerks, and shopkeepers. Our paths led through the grind of the everyday world. Yet, nearly half our time was consumed by studies utterly useless to us. How I hated them, these tormenting shades of youth! Homer! How I wished the fishermen had asked him that absurd riddle sooner. Horace! Why couldn't that shipwreck have succeeded? It would have worked for anyone but a classicist.

Then, on one blessed day, a wondrous talisman fell into my hands.

Listen to me, you heavily burdened little brothers. Do not waste your resources on tops and marbles, nor on tucks (do you still call them "tucks"?), but scrape and save. Near Paternoster Row lives a good magician who, for silver, will provide you with a "Key" that opens the gates of Hades.

With this aid, the frogs of Aristophanes became my merry companions. I wandered eagerly through Wonderland alongside Ulysses. Doctor Florret was delighted with my progress, which was genuine, for now, at last, I was studying according to the laws of common sense: understanding first, explaining afterward. Let Youth, lest the folly of Age imprison it in ignorance, provide itself with "Keys."

But let me not claim credit due to another. It was Dan. Dan of the strong arm and soft smile, Dan the wise hater of all useless labor, sharp-witted and easy-going Dan, who made this grand discovery.

Dan entered the Lower Fourth a term later, but before a week had passed, he was handling Latin verse with an ease and dexterity suggestive of unholy dealings with the Devil. In a lonely corner of Regent's Park, ensuring no one was within earshot, he revealed his magic to me.

"Don't tell the others," he commanded. "Or it will get out, and then nobody will be any better off."

"But is it right?" I asked.

"Look here, young'un," Dan said. "What are you here for? What is your father paying school fees for—to play a silly game, or to learn something?"

"If it's only a game—we boys against the masters," Dan continued, "then let's play by the rules. If we're here to learn... well, you've been in the class four months, and I've just arrived, and I bet I know more Ovid than you do already." Which was true.

So I thanked Dan and shared his key with him. All the Latin I remember, for whatever good it may have done me, I owe to him.

Even greater value did I owe to the good fortune that his sound mother wit was available to correct my dreamy impracticality. From start to finish, he was my friend. To be the chosen friend of Dan, a shrewd judge of both men and boys, I consider no insignificant feather in my cap. He "took to" me, he said, because I was so "jolly green"—"such a rummy little mug."
