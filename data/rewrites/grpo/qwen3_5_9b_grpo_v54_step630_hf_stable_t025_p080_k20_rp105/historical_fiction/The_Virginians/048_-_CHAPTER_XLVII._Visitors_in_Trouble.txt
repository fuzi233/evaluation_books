The generous and compassionate nature of Mr. Gumbo had succeeded in softening the storm within his master's heart. Harry's second night in the spunging-house was less bitter than the first, for at least one soul remained to offer aid and pity. Yet, this single thread of kindness could not mend the fabric of his spirit. Towards the rest of the world, Harry's heart remained iron-hard and proud. He viewed them all—his pious Aunt Warrington, his lordly friend March, his cynical cousin Castlewood—as selfish and ungenerous failures. He had tested them all and found them wanting. He would not stoop to beg another favor from any of them, nor would he endure twenty years of prison for their sake. He was a fool, he thought bitterly, to have believed their empty promises and trusted in their feigned friendship. There was no friendship in this cold, cursed country, only transaction and betrayal.

He resolved to leave. He would trust no Englishman, whether great or small. His mind raced with escape routes: he might go to Germany to fight alongside the King, or return to Virginia to bury himself in the wilderness, hunting all day and becoming his mother's mere factor and land-steward. He would marry Polly Broadbent or Fanny Mountain, turn to the soil as a tobacco grower, and do anything rather than remain among these English "fine gentlemen." Thus, he rose with a cheerful mask upon his face, though his spirit burned with anger.

Early in the morning, the faithful Gumbo appeared in his master's chamber, having traveled from Bond Street. "I sought to bring more clothes, sir," the honest black man said, "but Mr. Ruff, the landlord, refused to let me take any more."

Harry showed no interest in the letters Gumbo carried. He opened one, then two, then three; they were all demands for payment. A fourth revealed the landlord's ultimatum: no more of Mr. Warrington's belongings would leave the house until the bill was settled, or else the goods would be sold and the black man forced to sleep elsewhere. Harry hesitated to let Gumbo remove his own clothes and portmanteau. The servant explained he had found refuge with friends at Lord Wrotham's house.

"With Colonel Lambert's people," Gumbo observed, his eyes searching his master's face intently. "And Miss Hetty fainted when she heard you were taken up. Mr. Lambert is a good man; he told me this morning that if you need him, I should tell you to send for him, and he would come."

Harry felt a pang of gratitude hearing that Hetty had been distressed by his misfortune. He did not fully believe Gumbo's account of her fainting, accustomed as he was to filtering his servant's speech for exaggeration. But when Gumbo mentioned the Colonel, the young Virginian's spirit darkened anew. "I shall send for Lambert," he thought, grinding his teeth. "The man who insulted me and threw my gifts back in my face! Even if I were starving, I would not ask him for a crust!"

Dressed and ready for breakfast, Mr. Warrington summoned Gumbo to deliver a brief note to Mr. Draper in the Temple, demanding the attorney's immediate attendance.

"The note was written with the haughtiness of a lord addressing a serf, not a freeborn English gentleman," Draper remarked later, though he had always tolerated Harry's insufferable condescension. "It is all very well for a fine gentleman to give himself airs, but for a fellow in a spunging-house? Hang him! I have a mind not to go." Nevertheless, Mr. Draper complied. He found Mr. Warrington in his misfortune even more arrogant than in the days of his utmost prosperity. The young man sat upon his bed like a monarch, draped in a splendid gown with his hair meticulously dressed, motioning for a chair for his servant.

"Excuse me, madam, but such haughtiness and airs I am not accustomed to!" the outraged attorney declared.

"Take a chair and proceed with your story, my good Mr. Draper!" Madame de Bernstein smiled, amused by the lawyer's indignation. She delighted in her nephew's insolence in adversity.

The course of action Draper was to pursue had been arranged the previous day between the Baroness and her man of business. Draper was an able man, usually capable of serving a client well, but he failed here, piqued by anger or simply unable to comprehend the gentleman he faced. One might say that the reader, being gentle, has perhaps noticed how non-gentlemen offend those who are, without understanding why. They do or say something that provokes scorn; the offender perceives this scorn, rages, and interprets natural speech as sneering indifference.

"Gumbo, fetch a chair for Mr. Draper!" Mr. Warrington commanded, folding his brocaded dressing gown around his legs as he sat on the dingy bed. "Sit down, if you please, and let us discuss my business. Much obliged for coming so promptly. Have you heard of this ill-luck?"

Draper had heard. "Bad news travels quickly, Mr. Warrington," he replied. "I was eager to offer my humble services the moment you required them. Your friends and family will be deeply pained that a gentleman of your rank should be in such a position."

"I have been imprudent, Mr. Draper. I have lived beyond my means." (Draper bowed.) "I gambled with gentlemen much richer than myself, and a cursed run of bad luck has carried away all my ready money, leaving me with liabilities exceeding five hundred pounds."

"Five hundred pounds currently in the office," Draper noted gravely.

"Well, this is such a trifling sum that I thought by contacting one or two friends yesterday, I could have paid my debt and gone home without further ado. I was mistaken. I shall thank you to assist me in raising the funds as soon as possible."

Draper made a sound of disapproval and pulled a long, grave face.

"Why, sir, it can be done!" Mr. Warrington stared at the lawyer, expecting agreement.

Indeed, it could be done. Draper had proposed to Madame Bernstein the day before to pay the money instantly and release Mr. Warrington. The lady had declared her intention to make the young gentleman her heir. Like everyone else, Draper believed Harry's hereditary property in Virginia to be vast in both value and extent. He held notes in his pocket and the Baroness's order to pay them under specific conditions. Yet, when Harry declared, "It can be done!" Draper's face lengthened. "It can be done in time, sir; but it will require considerable time. To access the property in England belonging to Mr. George Warrington upon his death, we must prove the event and release the trustees. Who will do either? Lady Esmond Warrington in Virginia, of course, will not allow her son to remain in prison, but we must wait six months for her response. Does your Bristol agent have authority to honor your drafts?"

"He is authorized to pay me only two hundred pounds a year," said Mr. Warrington. "I suppose I have no resource but to apply to my aunt, Madame de Bernstein. She will be my security."

"Her ladyship will do anything for you, sir; she has said so often," the lawyer assured him. "If she gives the word at this moment, you can walk out of this place."

"Then go to her, Mr. Draper. I did not wish to trouble my relations, but rather than continue in this horrible, needless imprisonment, I must speak to her. Tell her where I am and what has befallen me. Disguise nothing! Tell her I confide in her affection and kindness to release me from this... this disgrace," Mr. Warrington's voice trembling slightly as he wiped his eyes.

"Sir," Draper said, eyeing the young man, "I was with her ladyship yesterday when we discussed this most unpleasant business."

"What do you mean, sir? Does Madame de Bernstein know of my misfortune?" Harry asked.

"Every circumstance, sir; including the pawning of the watches and all."

Harry turned a burning red. "It is an unfortunate business, the pawning of watches and things you had never paid for," the lawyer continued. The young man sprang up from the bed, looking so fierce that Draper felt a flicker of alarm.

"It may lead to litigation and unpleasant remarks in court, sir. Those barristers respect nothing; and when they get a fellow in the box—"

"Great Heaven, sir, you do not suppose a gentleman of my rank cannot take a watch on credit without intending to cheat the tradesman?" Harry cried, agitated.

"Of course you meant everything honorable; only, the law may not think so," Draper winked. ("Hang the supercilious beast; I touch him there!") "Your aunt says it is the most imprudent thing she has ever heard of—to call it by no worse name."

"You call it by no worse name yourself, Mr. Draper?" Harry asked, speaking slowly, trying to maintain command of himself.

Draper disliked his looks. "Heaven forbid I should say anything between gentlemen—but between me and my client, it is my duty to say, 'Sir, you are in a very unpleasant scrape,' just as a doctor would tell his patient, 'Sir, you are very ill.'"

"And you cannot help me pay this debt, coming only to tell me I may be accused of roguery?" Harry demanded.

"Of obtaining goods under false pretenses? Most undoubtedly, yes. I cannot help it, sir. Do not look as if you will knock me down. A young gentleman with only two hundred a year from his mother orders diamonds and watches and takes them to a pawnbroker. You ask me what people will think, and I tell you honestly. Do not be angry, Mr. Warrington."

"Go on, sir!" Harry groaned.

The lawyer thought the day was his own. "But you ask if I cannot help pay this debt? And I say Yes—and here is the money in my pocket to do it now, if you like—not mine, sir, but my honored client's, your aunt, Lady Bernstein. However, she has a right to impose conditions, and I have brought them."

"Tell them, sir," Harry said.

"They are not hard. They are for your own good. If you agree, we can call a hackney coach and go to Clarges Street together, which I have promised to do regardless. Mr. Warrington, I name no names, but there was a question of marriage between you and a certain party."

"Ah!" Harry's countenance brightened.

"To that marriage my noble client, the Baroness, is most averse—having other views for you and believing it will be your ruin to marry a party of noble birth and title, yet, excuse me, not of first-rate character and much older than yourself. You had given an imprudent promise to that party."

"Yes; and she has it still," Harry admitted.

"It has been recovered. She dropped it by accident at Tunbridge," Draper explained. "My client informed me; indeed, her ladyship showed it to me. It was written in blue ink—"

"Never mind, sir!" Harry cried, turning almost as red as the ink he had used to write his absurd promise, the madness and folly of which had smote him a thousand times with shame.

"At the same time, letters compromising a noble family were recovered," the lawyer continued. "You had lost them. It was no fault of yours; you were away when they were found again. You may say that that noble family, and yourself, have a friend such as few young men possess. Well, sir, there is no earthly promise binding you—only idle words spoken over a bottle, which any gentleman may forget. Say you will not proceed with this marriage—give me and my noble friend your word of honor. Cry off, Mr. W.! Do not be such a fool, saving your presence, as to marry an old woman who has jilted scores of men in her time. Say the word, and I will step downstairs, pay every shilling owed in the office, and put you in my coach, either at your aunt's or at White's Club, with two hundred pounds in your pocket. Say yes; give us your hand! There is no use sitting grinning behind these bars all day!"

Thus far, Mr. Draper had dominated the conversation. Harry merely longed to be rid of the engagement from which his aunt wished to free him. His foolish flame for Maria Esmond had died long ago. If she would release him, how thankful he would be! "Come! Give us your hand and say done!" the lawyer urged with a knowing wink. "Do not stand shilly-shallying, sir. Law bless you, Mr. W., if I had married everyone I promised, I should be like the Grand Turk or Captain Macheath in the play!"

The lawyer's familiarity disgusted Harry, who shrank from Draper, scarcely realizing he was doing so. He folded his dressing gown tighter and stepped back from the proffered hand. "Give me a little time to think of the matter, if you please, Mr. Draper," he said. "Have the goodness to come to me again in an hour."

"Very good, sir, very good, sir!" the lawyer bit his lips, turning red as he seized his hat. "Most parties would not want an hour to consider such an offer. But I suppose my time must be yours. I will come again to see whether you are to go or stay. Good morning, sir, good morning," and he descended the stairs, growling curses beneath his breath. "Won't take my hand, won't he? Will tell me in an hour! Hang his impudence! I'll show him what an hour is!"

Draper returned to his chambers in a dudgeon, bullied his clerks, sent a messenger to the Baroness reporting that the young gentleman had demanded time for consideration—a formality, he was sure—and then saw clients and dined leisurely.
