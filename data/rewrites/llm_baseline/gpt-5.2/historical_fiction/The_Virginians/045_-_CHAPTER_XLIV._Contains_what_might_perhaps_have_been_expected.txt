Harry Warrington’s peace-offerings lay where he had flung them down—two morocco cases, their lids gaping like mouths that had been made to praise and were now forced to gape in stupid silence. Within, on white satin, the gold toys shone the more impudently because nobody would accept them: a repeater with its little pearl circlet and diamond knob; a necklace and rings that had cost him not merely coin but heat and haste in foreign shops; trifles that had been chosen with the bright, foolish notion that gratitude might be weighed and paid for like sugar or spice.

Colonel Lambert had rejected all.

And so Harry, who had come in with offerings like a penitent at a communion rail, went out with the wrath of a young conqueror balked, and with shame in the same breath. They had humbled him—before the girls. That was the sting. Not the money. Not even the slight. It was the sense of being exhibited once more as a prodigal, a scapegrace, a lesson for the family to point at while they polished their own virtue.

“They are so fine they won’t touch my hand,” he told himself, pacing his rooms. “And when I try to show common gratitude, they fling it back in my face.”

Parson Sampson, who had no notion of dignity that was not borrowed, had been staring at the open cases with a look that was almost hunger.

“Why, sir,” he said, his eyes lingering as a hungry man’s will linger on a roast fowl trussed and ready for the spit, “the things must be worth a fortune.”

“They cost money,” Harry answered shortly. “Not that I’d grudge ten times as much to people who have been kind.”

“No, faith, sir, not if I know your honour!” Sampson cried, quick to season his flattery as a cook seasons broth—too much salt, and always at hand.

Harry touched the repeater’s case, and his anger sharpened at the thought of Hetty’s eager face when she had once sighed for such a watch, as if wishing for it were only another kind of prayer. “They told me at Paris it was a bargain. Miss Hetty said she longed for a repeating watch.”

“Oh, what a love!” Sampson gushed. “The pearls—look, sir, the pearls! And the diamond knob—why, ’twould win any woman’s heart.”

“There’s an apple-woman passing with a basket,” Harry broke out. “I’ve half a mind to fling the thing to her.”

Sampson made a little cry as if the notion were a dagger aimed at his own ribs. Harry laughed once, harshly, and went out.

He had business in the City; business that now, each time he crossed those streets, seemed to him like a man walking through a market where his own flesh was for sale. Sampson, for all his devotion, peeled away early in the Strand with a confession of terror for Chancery Lane and its grim precincts. Harry went on alone.

At his broker’s he did what he had come to do. There were papers, signatures—light scratches of pen on parchment that felt, absurdly, like knives on bone—and presently he walked out with crisp bank-notes folded in his pocket. The broker, as if to sweeten the transaction with a supper-sauce, took him to one of the great City dining-houses. The rooms were warm with the thick smell of meats and beer, the clatter of plates, the comfortable roar of men who believed the world was stable because dinner came at the hour. Beef cut in shining slices, greens steamed to a dull surrender, bread that had the faint sour of yesterday’s leaven—Harry ate without tasting, or tasted only bitterness. Under the talk and knives and tankards, the City’s own sound rose like surf: wheels grinding, hawkers calling, bells, and that perpetual human hum which, if a man listened too closely, seemed less like life than like the turning of a millstone.

Afterwards the broker showed him the Virginian walk upon ’Change, where the tobacco-men loitered with their grave faces and their provincial air. Harry passed through it shamefacedly, as if each step sounded his disgrace.

What would his mother say—Madam Esmond far away, with the river wind in her windows and the quiet of the plantations about her—if she knew her son was carrying off, in a gambler’s bottomless pocket, so much of his father’s patrimony? He imagined those Virginia merchants looking at him and hearing their own voices name him: Young Esmond of Castlewood on the Potomac; losing at play; selling out again and again. The thought made his ears burn. Even the street-noise seemed to mock him. In Virginia, the loudest thing at daybreak might be a cock and the rattle of a pail; here the city never ceased clanging and scolding, as if it could drown thought by sheer racket.

Not until he passed under the traitors’ heads at Temple Bar and felt the City behind him did his spirits rise a little, like a man who has escaped a close room into air. He walked home by the Strand, glanced in at St. James’s Street—too early yet for the chocolate-house crowd—and reached his lodgings.

There he drew out his bank-notes. He took three—three small squares of rescue—and slipped them into a sheet of paper. On the inside he wrote, “Much good may they do you. H. E. W.” He sealed it carefully and directed it to the Reverend Mr. Sampson. He left the packet on the chimney-glass and told the servants to hand it to the parson when he came.

His phaeton was brought round. He meant to drive in the Park; but the east wind had teeth, and rain began to sift down, cold and straight as needles. Some irritation—weather, mood, fate—made him snap the reins instead toward St. James’s Street. By three he was at White’s again.

The club was still drowsy at that hour, men at dinner elsewhere; only a few loungers and the servants moving with trained silence. Lord Castlewood sat yawning over the Public Advertiser, newly come from Court.

“What shall we do?” my lord drawled. “A little piquet? Just an hour. I dine in Arlington Street at four.”

Harry, with that fatal readiness which in him passed for good nature, had no objection. “Just for an hour,” he said; and cards were called for.

“Upstairs?” Castlewood suggested. “Out of the noise?”

“Certainly,” Harry answered. “Out of the noise.”

It was a curious phrase for him to cling to, as if sound itself could be a moral influence. Down below, the club murmured, chairs scraped, glasses clinked; above, in the little tea-room, the world narrowed to green baize, the soft shuffle of cards, the click of counters, and two men’s breath. It was quieter—and therefore, perhaps, more dangerous. In the hush, each loss had a weight, each gain a pulse. There are silences that heal, like a field at dusk; and there are silences that sharpen appetite, like a kitchen before service when knives are being honed.

At five the club filled. Gentlemen came in after dinner; the play-room began to wake. Some put on gaming-jackets kept there for long sittings. In the coffee-room two toothpick-bearing wits leaned out through the blinds into the wet street.

“Whose phaeton?” asked one.

“The Fortunate Youth’s,” said the other.

“Not so fortunate the last three nights. Thirteen hundred lost last night to the table. Is Warrington here?”

“In the little tea-room with Lord Castlewood since three,” the waiter said. “Piquet.”

“What fun for Castlewood,” one of the men sneered, showing all his polished teeth.

The other growled. “Curse him. No right to be here. He doesn’t pay when he loses. Sir Miles Warrington told me Castlewood has owed him money on a bet these three years.”

“Castlewood doesn’t lose if he plays alone,” the first replied, shrugging. “A company flurries him. That’s why he doesn’t sit at the table.”

“Let’s go up and stop ’em,” the second muttered.

“Why? Better sport here. Look—the lamplighter’s up his ladder. And that old putt in the chair—did you ever see such a quiz?”

Just then Harry came out of the house. In the wet afternoon he seemed to have forgotten his phaeton had been waiting. The two men watched him spring in and lash the horses as if whipcord could beat back misfortune. Coachmen and chairmen scattered; he drove with a reckless speed that was like an insult to the rain. Presently Lord Castlewood was seen to enter a chair and be carried away.

Harry rattled up to his own door. The horses had stood all that time on the pavement, steaming under the drizzle; now their flanks quivered as if a cook had set a spit too close to the fire. At the door Gumbo, his servant, was talking with a country-looking girl who curtsied and tripped off. Gumbo, wherever he went, contrived to season the servants’ hall with pretty friendships.

“Has Mr. Sampson been here?” Harry asked from the seat.

“No, sar. Mr. Sampson have not been here,” Gumbo answered.

Harry told him to fetch the letter addressed to Sampson—the sealed one on the mantel in the glass. Gumbo, who could not read, took his time; Harry’s fingers drummed impatiently. When the packet was brought, Harry snatched it, turned his horses’ heads at once, and in the same instant the idlers at White’s saw him galloping back again through the wet street.

He entered the club with his jaw set. In the little tea-room he had noticed the bank already set, gentlemen bending over it with that absorbed look which is neither pleasure nor pain but a kind of fever. He went to the clerk’s desk, paid his account of the previous night, and sat down calling for fresh counters.

The week had been unlucky; to-night was worse. He could not win. The counters slid away from him as if the green cloth were a slope. He called for more. He was pale and very quiet; when spoken to, he answered with ease and politeness, like a man entertaining guests while his house burns behind him.

Lord March, with a heap of green and white counters in front of him, laughed as Harry rose at last.

“Hang it! Stay and mend your luck,” March said. “Take a hundred of mine and go on.”

“I have had enough for to-night, my lord,” Harry answered; and he went away.

He ate a broiled bone in the coffee-room—hot fat hissing under the fork, salt and smoke, the simple honest smell of meat scorched at the edge. It was the sort of food a man eats standing, without ceremony, when he can’t bear the softness of a chair. Then, about midnight, he walked back to his lodgings.

After a catastrophe, men often sleep. It is the waking that punishes. The night comes like a cook laying a heavy lid on a pot; all boils and passions are muffled. But at dawn the lid is lifted, and the stench of what has been left to scorch rises sharply.

Harry woke before the light. The room was a grey cave. He tried to pull his nightcap down and the blankets up, as if cloth could smother Yesterday. The watchman bawled some hour in the street, his voice part of London’s perpetual din—a city that never quite hushes, even in the darkest watch of the night. Harry remembered the repeater under his pillow, the pretty toy he had meant for Hetty. He pressed the knob.

Ting, ting, ting—six times, and then the half-hour in its little added flourish, like a bird foolishly singing in a storm.

Poor bright Hetty. And Maria—good Heaven, Maria, who was beginning to feel, in his irritated mind, like a refrain repeated too often. And his mother at home, who had built her life on old names and old duties and the sound of her own people’s speech, the Potomac words that Londoners laughed at when they heard them. What would she say when she learned that he had lost every shilling of his ready money—his winnings, and five thousand pounds besides—in three nights?

Castlewood could not have played him false. No. His lordship knew piquet better, but he was his kinsman. Harry forced himself to believe it, because there are lies a man must tell himself to keep from going mad. And yet the arithmetic was plain: when Harry counted his losses at the gaming-table and saw that they would swallow the remainder of his patrimony, he had passed the box and left the table. He had meant, at least, to stop before the last plank broke. But he had gone back. He had always gone back. That was the shame that tasted like bile.

Dawn seeped in at last, a miserable London light, thin as watered milk. The housemaid came to light the fire and admit the dun morning. Gumbo followed with the warmed dressing-gown, shaving-plate, and linen. The hairdresser arrived to curl and powder him; the soft swish of brush and puff made a ridiculous domestic music against his despair, as if a man could be made respectable by starch and scent.

At breakfast Parson Sampson came, eager and smiling, smelling faintly of last night’s wine and tavern cheer. He had been meant to return yesterday afternoon, but companions had held him to dinner at the St. Alban’s and made a night of it. He entered with the air of a man arriving at the second course, not knowing the first has been burnt.

“Oh, Parson,” Harry groaned, “’twas the worst night you ever made in your life! Look here, sir!”

He thrust the paper at him.

Sampson, with quick eyes, saw the broken seal. “Here is a broken envelope with the words ‘Much good may it do you,’ written within,” he said.

“Look on the outside!” Harry cried. “It was directed to you.”

Sampson’s face changed at once. Alarm—then calculation—then dread. “Has some one broke it open, sir?”

“Some one, yes. I did. Had you come as you said, you’d have found it full of bank-notes. As it is, they were all dropped at the infernal macco-table last night.”

“What, all?” Sampson whispered.

“All. With what I brought from the City, and all the ready money I have left in the world. Yesterday afternoon I played piquet with my cous—with a gentleman at White’s—and he eased me of what I had about me. Remembering there was still some money here, unless you’d fetched it, I came home, carried it back, and left it at the macco-table—every shilling besides that belongs to me.” Harry’s breath came short. “And—great Heaven, Sampson—what’s the matter, man?”

Sampson’s mouth worked, and then he burst into tears—real tears, not the theatrical dew he could summon for a sermon.

“It’s my luck,” he cried. “It’s my usual luck!”

“What!” Harry flared, anger snapping as a pan flares when grease catches. “You’re whimpering like a baby at the loss of a loan of a couple hundred pounds? Leave the room, Gumbo! Confound you, why are you always poking your woolly head in at that door!”

Gumbo, offended and slow, explained somebody below wanted to see master with a little bill.

“Tell him to go to Jericho!” Harry roared. “Let me see nobody. I am not at home!”

There was a murmur and scuffle on the landing and then silence. The noise, slight as it was, rasped Harry’s nerves; he turned on Sampson with savage contempt.

“Hadn’t you better take a bumper of brandy to keep your spirits up, Mr. Sampson? Hang it, man! Don’t snivel like a woman!”

“Oh, it’s not me,” Sampson said, tossing his head, struggling for pride. “I am used to it, sir.”

“Not you! Who then? Are you crying because somebody else is hurt?”

“Yes, sir,” the chaplain said, with a sudden spark. “Because somebody else is hurt, and through my fault. I have lodged many years with a bootmaker—a very honest man. A few days since, relying upon—upon a friend who promised to accommodate me with a loan, I borrowed sixty pounds from my landlord, which he was about to pay to his own. I can’t get the money. My poor landlord’s goods will be seized for rent; his wife and dear young children turned into the street; and this honest family ruined through my fault. But as you say, Mr. Warrington, I ought not to snivel like a woman. I will remember you helped me once—and I will bid you farewell, sir.”

He took his broad-leafed hat and walked out.

Harry, in a fury with himself, with the whole world’s crookedness, let out an execration and then—shamefully—a savage laugh. It sounded to his own ears like something low and foreign in his throat. From the stairs he called after the chaplain, “Sampson! hang you! come back! It’s a mistake! I beg your pardon!”

But Sampson, cut to the soul, went on. Harry heard the street-door slam. The thump of it seemed to strike his breast.

He sank into his chair. In his mind the filthy herd came crowding—gambling, debauchery, extravagance, reckless companions, dangerous women—wallowing and trampling like swine over the prostrate prodigal. The city’s morning noise filtered in, carts and cries and the far-off beat of London’s unceasing labour: a harsh soundscape in which repentance itself seemed drowned, as if the very air were too loud for prayer.

Yet he was not utterly overcome. He sprang up as if shaking animals from his clothes. He seized a great glass of brandy—the same fire-water he had mocked Sampson with—and swallowed it. Then he flung off his damask robe, rang for Gumbo, and ordered his coat.

Gumbo brought a fine green coat with plated buttons and a gold cord.

“Not that!” Harry roared. “A plain suit—the plainer the better. The black clothes.”

So Gumbo fetched the mourning-coat Harry had discarded months before. In it, he looked more like his own conscience.

Then, as a man in a ruined pantry gathers what silver remains before the bailiff arrives, Harry took up his valuables: his fine new gold watch; the repeater meant for Hetty; the necklace meant for Theo; his rings—half a dozen, save his grandfather’s old seal-ring which he kissed and laid back as if it were a relic that must not be pawned; three gold snuff-boxes; and lastly his purse knitted by his mother, with three shillings and sixpence and a little pocket-piece from Virginia. That purse—coarse wool, honest work—smelled faintly of home in a way the gold never could. It was a scent memory, untranslatable into London’s jargon, and it stung him.

He put on his hat and went out.

On the landing Mr. Ruff, his landlord, met him with bowing and cringing and a strip of paper a yard long. Ruff was one of the chief waiters and aides-de-camp of Mr. Mackreth at White’s; Mrs. Ruff was a milliner and had “accounts to make up.”

“Much obliged if Mr. Warrington will settle,” Ruff whined, thrusting the bill forward.

The sight of him was like a fly on a dish. Harry looked down over Ruff’s head with glum disdain.

“Of course I will settle,” he said.

“Perhaps his honour will settle now?”

“No, sir, I will not settle now!” Harry snapped, pushing forward.

“I’m very—very much in want of money, sir,” Ruff pleaded. “Mrs. Ruff is—”

“Hang you, sir, get out of the way!” Harry cried, and drove him backward against the wall so that the man almost toppled down his own landing. Harry tramped past and out into Bond Street.

As he went, the Guards were at exercise at the King’s Mews near Charing Cross. Drums and fifes cut through the air—clean, martial sounds, ordered and measured. For a moment the rhythm steadied him, as if a better life could be marched into by keeping step. He looked in at the gate and watched the drill.

“I can shoulder a musket at any rate,” he thought gloomily, and strode on.

He crossed St. Martin’s Lane for a little business, then made his way into Long Acre, to the bootmaker’s house where Sampson lodged. The air there was different: leather and wax and damp wool, the small domestic smell of honest poverty. A child cried somewhere; a kettle sang thinly in a back room. Compared with St. James’s, where money and noise and fashion made a glittering storm, this place had the worn quiet of a kitchen after supper, when the fire is low but human beings still breathe close together.

The woman of the house said Mr. Sampson was not at home but had promised to be in at one. Since she knew Mr. Warrington, she showed him up to the parson’s little room. Harry sat down by the window-seat and, having nothing to do with his hands but wring them, took up an unfinished sermon lying there.

The title—or rather, the subject—made him start: the Prodigal Son.

He tried to read, but the words slid like grease on a plate. The parson’s phrases about husks and swine and repentance seemed not doctrine but mockery.

Presently he heard the landlady’s shrill voice on the stairs, pursuing someone who climbed. Sampson burst into the room, breathless, followed by the sobbing woman.

“There’s only thirteen pound in the house, and he will be here at one, I tell you!” she was bawling, her apron half pulled off her shoulder as she clutched at Sampson like a creditor clutching a coat.

Sampson saw Harry and started; the landlady stopped short, then, in her agitation, seemed not to understand that a gentleman’s presence altered anything.

“Hush, hush! my good creature!” Sampson gasped, pointing. “Don’t you see Mr. Warrington? I’ve business with him—most important business. It will be all right, I tell you!”

He coaxed and ushered her out, the anxious children clinging to her skirts like burrs.

When the door shut, Harry rose.

“Sampson,” he said, “I have come to ask your pardon again. What I said this morning was cruel and unjust, and unlike a gentleman.”

“Not a word more, sir,” Sampson answered, cold and sad. He bowed and scarcely pressed the hand Harry offered.

“I see you are still angry.”

“An apology is an apology,” Sampson said, with bitterness that tried to pass for humility. “A man of my station can ask for no more from one of yours. No doubt you did not mean to give me pain. And what if you did?” His eyes moved round the poor little room as if counting its humiliations—the cracked mirror, the few books, the sermon sheets. “And you are not the only one of the family who has. I wish I had never known the name of Esmond or Castlewood—or that place yonder.”

He glanced at a picture above the fireplace—some view of the great house he had haunted so long in hope.

“My lord, your cousin, took a fancy to me,” Sampson went on. “Said he would make my fortune. Kept me as his dependant till fortune passed by me, and now refuses me my due.”

“How do you mean—your due?” Harry asked.

“Three years’ salary,” Sampson answered. “Owed me as chaplain of Castlewood. Seeing you could give me no money, I went to his lordship this morning and asked him. I fell on my knees, sir. But his lordship had none. Civil words, at least—saving your presence, Mr. Warrington—but no money. Five guineas, which he declared was all he had, and which I took. But what are five guineas among so many? Oh, those poor little children!”

“Lord Castlewood said he had no money?” Harry cried, the blood rushing to his face. “He won eleven hundred pounds yesterday of me at piquet—which I paid him out of this pocket-book.”

Sampson gave a short, ugly laugh. “I dare say, sir. One can’t believe a word his lordship says.”

“But there’s execution in this house, and ruin upon these poor folks to-morrow,” he added, and his voice broke on the last word as if it were bread too hard to swallow.

“That need not happen,” Harry said.

He drew out what he had—eighty guineas—and laid them down.

“Here are eighty, Sampson. As far as they go, God help you. ’Tis all I have to give. I wish to my heart I could give more as I promised; but you did not come at the right time, and I am a poor devil now until my remittances come from Virginia.”

Sampson stared as if the coins were a miracle and an accusation together. Then he went white. He dropped on his knees and seized Harry’s hand.

“Great powers, sir!” he cried. “Are you a guardian angel Heaven hath sent? You quarrelled with my tears this morning; I cannot help them now. They burst from a grateful heart. A rock would pour them forth before such goodness! May Heaven eternally bless you and give you prosperity! May my unworthy prayers—”

“Nay, nay! Get up, friend—get up,” Harry said, annoyed despite himself at the flood of words. He had come to give money, not to be drenched in worship. “There—there! Don’t be on your knees to me.”

“To Heaven who sent you!” Sampson cried, and flung open the door. “Mrs. Weston! Mrs. Weston!”

The landlady appeared instantly—she had been listening at the door all along, her domestic terror too sharp for manners.

“What is it, sir?”

“We are saved!” Sampson shouted. “We are saved! Kneel, kneel, woman, and thank our benefactor! Raise your innocent voices, children, and bless him!”

A universal whimpering rose. The children, tugged into the room, stared and began to cry by contagion; the mother fell to her knees. One child, bewildered, remained standing until the mother slapped her ear and hissed, “Drat it, Jane, kneel down and bless the gentleman!”

Harry stood among them, simpering in spite of himself, warmed by the consciousness of doing one good action in the midst of so much folly. Their gratitude had a sound—raw, high, broken, like a choir of kettles and crying infants—but it was human, and for a moment it quieted the harsher noises inside him.

He slipped away at last, leaving them in their benedictions, and walked out into Long Acre feeling lighter than he had in days, as if generosity were a kind of food that could nourish even the giver, and as if—by some old, half-forgotten Virginia measure of honour—a man might still redeem himself with bread and coin, even when he had wasted so much.

And all this while, in another thread of the day’s weaving, the girl Gumbo had been talking with at Harry’s door—countrified, curtseying—was Mrs. Molly from Oakhurst, attendant to the ladies there. Wherever that fascinating Gumbo went, he left admirers behind him in kitchens and servants’ halls, and the air of his partings was always full of soft laughter and whispering, like the last low talk over a banked hearth. It had been, I think, on a Wednesday evening that he and Mrs. Molly had taken a walk together; and they were lingering in the amiable courtesies of farewell when Gumbo’s master drove up from White’s and put an end to their twilight murmurs.
