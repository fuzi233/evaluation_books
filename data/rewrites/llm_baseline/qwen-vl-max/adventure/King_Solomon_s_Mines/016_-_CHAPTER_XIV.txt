Minutes passed, and the regiments assigned to flank the enemy had vanished into the shadows, moving silently along the rise of ground to avoid detection by Twala’s sharp-eyed scouts. The air was still, save for the occasional whisper of wind through the grass.

For over half an hour, no movement came from the Greys or their supporting regiment—the Buffaloes—whose role was to form the core of the army and endure the fiercest blows of battle. Both units were nearly at full strength, untouched by the earlier fighting. The Greys had been held in reserve during the morning’s clash, suffering only minor losses when they repelled a breakthrough attempt—an attack I had joined, only to be knocked unconscious for my efforts. The Buffaloes, stationed as the third line on the left, had barely seen action, since the enemy there had failed to break through the second line.

Infadoos, a seasoned commander whose wisdom was matched only by his caution, used this pause to address his men—the Greys—in stirring words. He spoke not of fear, but of honor: the privilege of leading the charge, the glory of standing beside the great white warrior from the stars, and the promise of rich rewards—cattle, rank, and lasting fame—for those who lived through the coming fight.

I looked down upon the long lines of warriors, their black plumes swaying like waves under a stormy sky, their faces grim and set. A deep sorrow filled me. In less than an hour, most—if not all—of these proud veterans, every one of them well past forty, would lie dead or dying in the dust. They were being sacrificed, deliberately, with the cold calculation of a master strategist who knows that sometimes the only way to win is to lose. Yet they knew it. And they accepted it without hesitation. Their duty was clear, their purpose absolute. Even now, facing certain death, not one showed fear. I stood among them, my own heart pounding, and could not help envying their calm courage. Never before had I seen such total devotion to duty, such utter disregard for the cost.

“Behold your king!” Infadoos declared, pointing toward Ignosi. “Go forth and die for him, as brave men do. May the name of any man who turns from death for his king be cursed forever. Behold your king—chiefs, captains, soldiers! Pay homage to the sacred Snake, and then follow, so that Incubu and I may show you a path into the heart of Twala’s host!”

A hush fell. Then, slowly, a murmur rose—a soft, rhythmic tapping, like distant thunder, as six thousand spears struck against shields. It grew louder, swelling into a roar that shook the mountains, echoing across the valley. Then, as suddenly as it began, it faded—until the royal salute burst forth.

Ignosi must have felt the weight of that moment. No Roman emperor, I thought, had ever received such tribute from men who were about to die.

He raised his battle-axe in acknowledgment, and the Greys moved forward in tight triple lines—three thousand strong, each line a thousand men deep. As they advanced, I saw the last companies reach the edge of the plateau. Then Ignosi took command of the Buffaloes, forming them into a similar threefold array, and gave the order to march. I followed, praying desperately that I might survive the ordeal. I’d faced danger before—but never anything so bleak, so hopeless.

By the time we reached the plateau’s edge, the Greys were halfway down the slope, descending onto a narrow strip of grassland that stretched like a tongue into the mountain’s curve. On the plain beyond, Twala’s camp was in chaos. Regiment after regiment surged forward in long, galloping strides, eager to block our advance before we could reach the open ground of Loo.

That tongue of land was scarcely four hundred yards deep at its widest point, narrowing to just ninety paces at the tip. The Greys, having descended in column, reformed into their triple-line formation at the broader end—and halted.

Then we—the Buffaloes—moved forward, taking position about a hundred yards behind the final line of the Greys, slightly higher up. From there, we watched Twala’s army approach. Though they had suffered losses, they numbered at least forty thousand, now pressing forward with fierce urgency. But as they neared the root of the tongue, they hesitated. Only one regiment could enter the gorge at a time. And there, seventy yards inside, guarded by towering boulder-strewn walls on either side, stood the Greys—unassailable except from the front, immovable as a fortress.

They stopped. No one rushed forward. There was no eagerness to meet such a disciplined, unyielding force.

Then, a tall figure emerged—plumed headdress nodding, surrounded by chiefs and attendants. I suspected it was Twala himself. He gave a command. The first regiment charged, screaming into the silence.

The Greys remained motionless until the enemy was within forty yards. Then, with a sudden roar, they lunged forward—spears aloft, shields clashing. The impact echoed like thunder. The two forces collided in a furious tide of steel and flesh. Spears flashed, blood flew, and the air rang with shouts and groans.

But the fight lasted only moments. The attacking line thinned rapidly. Then, like a wave breaking over a reef, the Greys surged forward, sweeping through the enemy ranks. The regiment was destroyed. But the Greys had lost a third of their number. Two lines remained.

They closed ranks, silent once more, waiting. And I caught sight of Sir Henry—his yellow beard visible as he moved between the rows, adjusting formations. He was alive.

We advanced to the battlefield, now littered with thousands of bodies—dead, dying, wounded—soaked in crimson. Ignosi issued an order: no enemy wounded were to be killed. We saw it carried out. It was a horror too terrible to dwell upon.

Now, a second regiment—white-plumed, white-kilted—charged the remaining two thousand Greys. Again, silence. Again, the same distance. Again, the sudden eruption of fury.

Shields met with a thunderous crash. The battle raged. This time, however, the outcome was uncertain. The newcomers were young, fierce, and fought with reckless energy. For a time, they seemed to push the veterans back. Hundreds fell each minute. The cry of “_S’gee, s’gee_”—the victor’s triumph—rose above the din.

Yet discipline held. Experience prevailed. One veteran was worth two youths. Just as we feared the Greys would fall, I heard Sir Henry’s voice cut through the noise. His battle-axe swept high, and the tide turned. The Greys stopped retreating. They stood firm. Like rocks, they weathered the storm. Wave after wave crashed against them, only to recoil.

Then they advanced—slowly, inexorably. The enemy broke. White plumes streamed in the wind as survivors fled. The Greys had won. But at what cost?

Of the three thousand who had marched out, fewer than six hundred remained. Bloodied, exhausted, yet still triumphant, they cheered, waved their spears, and ran forward—pursuing the fleeing foe for a hundred yards. Then they seized a knoll, formed a triple ring around its base, and held fast.

And there, atop the mound, I saw Sir Henry—unharmed—alongside Infadoos. Then Twala’s regiments rolled forward again, and the battle resumed.

At that moment, something strange happened inside me. I, who had always avoided conflict, who had fled from danger whenever possible, felt my chest burn with a fierce, unexpected courage. Warlike verses from ancient tales, bloody passages from scripture, flooded my mind. My blood, once frozen with dread, now raced through my veins. I longed to kill—not out of hatred, but out of some primal urge to stand with these warriors.

I glanced at the ranks behind us—faces taut, hands twitching, eyes blazing with battle-lust. And I wondered: did I look like them? Did I belong here?

Only Ignosi seemed unmoved—calm beneath his leopard-skin cloak, though even he clenched his teeth.

“I cannot wait,” I said. “Are we to stand here while Twala destroys our brothers?”

“Nay, Macumazahn,” Ignosi replied. “Now is the moment. Let us seize it.”

As he spoke, another regiment rushed past the ring on the mound and attacked from the near side.

Ignosi raised his axe. The signal was given. With a wild cry, the Buffaloes charged—like a tidal wave.

What followed, I can barely remember. A blur of motion, a roar of voices, flashes of steel through a red haze. When I regained my senses, I was standing near the top of the mound, close to Sir Henry himself. How I got there, I didn’t know. Later, he told me I’d been carried forward by the charge, then left behind as the Buffaloes were pushed back. He pulled me into safety.

The fighting continued. Again and again, the enemy surged forward, only to be driven back. The Greys stood firm—each man stepping where his comrade had fallen. Bodies piled up. The living stepped over the dead.

Infadoos remained cool, shouting orders, taunts, even jokes—anything to keep morale high. He moved into the thickest fights, bearing his share of the burden.

Sir Henry, his ostrich plumes gone, his hair streaming in the wind, fought like a berserker. His axe, his hands, his armor—all drenched in blood. He roared “_O-hoy! O-hoy!_” with each blow, shattering shields and skulls alike. None dared face him.

Then—suddenly—a cry: “_Twala, y’ Twala!_”

Out of the crowd stepped the king himself—one eye, armored, armed with axe and shield. He stared at Sir Henry.

“Where art thou, Incubu, the white man who slew my son Scragga?” he shouted. “Come, see if thou canst slay me!”

He hurled a _tolla_. Sir Henry caught it on his shield. It pierced the iron plate and stuck fast.

Then Twala charged—blow landing hard on the shield. The shock knocked Sir Henry to his knees.

But the fight ended abruptly. From the surrounding ranks, a cry of alarm rose. I looked up—and saw it.

On both flanks, the plain erupted with charging warriors. The flanking squadrons had arrived.

It was perfect timing. Twala’s army had focused entirely on the battle at the mound. They hadn’t expected the horns to strike. Believing the flanking forces hidden on the hilltop, they were unprepared.

In minutes, the battle was decided. Caught on both sides, shattered by the slaughter, Twala’s troops broke and fled. The plain became a river of running men. The army that had surrounded us melted away like mist.

We stood alone—like a rock after the tide receded.

But what a scene! Around us lay heaps of the dead and dying. Of the once-proud Greys, only ninety-five remained on their feet. Over three thousand four hundred had fallen.

Infadoos surveyed the ruins, binding a wound on his arm. “Men,” he said quietly, “you have upheld the honor of your regiment. This day will be spoken of by your children’s children.” Then he turned to Sir Henry. “Thou art a great captain, Incubu. I have known many brave men in my life—but none like thee.”

The Buffaloes began marching toward Loo. A message came from Ignosi—requesting Infadoos, Sir Henry, and myself to join him.

We left the Greys to tend the wounded, and followed.

Soon, we spotted Good sitting on an ant-heap, a hundred paces ahead. Beside him lay a Kukuana warrior—apparently dead.

“He’s wounded,” Sir Henry said.

Then, the body stirred. The Kukuana sprang up, knocked Good off the heap, and began stabbing at him. We rushed forward, horrified. The warrior delivered one final, vicious thrust—and shouted, “Take that, wizard!” before fleeing.

Good lay still. We approached, fearing the worst. But he was pale, faint—but alive, with a calm smile on his face.

“Capital armour this,” he murmured. “How sold that beggar must have been.” Then he fainted.

We found he’d been hit in the leg by a _tolla_, but the chain mail had saved him. A miracle.

He was placed on a wicker stretcher and carried with us.

At the gates of Loo, we found a regiment guarding the entrance. The officer saluted Ignosi as king and reported that Twala’s army had retreated into the town—Twala himself among them—but that they were broken and likely to surrender.

Ignosi consulted with us, then sent heralds to each gate. They offered safe passage and mercy to any soldier who laid down arms. If not, by nightfall, the town would burn.

Half an hour later, amid cheers, the bridge dropped across the fosse. The gates swung open.

We entered cautiously. Thousands of defeated warriors lined the streets, heads bowed, weapons at their feet. Officers led them in silent submission as Ignosi passed.

We marched straight to Twala’s kraal. When we reached the open space where we’d once seen the review and witch hunt, it was empty—except for Twala himself, seated before his hut, with only Gagool beside him.

It was a tragic sight. The mighty king, stripped of power, alone with a crone. No army, no courtiers, no wives—only silence and shame. Compassion stirred in me, despite his crimes. He was learning the cruel truth: that when you fall, no one remembers your greatness. You are forgotten. And in this case, he deserved no mercy.

We filed through the gate, crossed the courtyard, and approached him. At fifty yards, the regiment halted. Ignosi, with a small guard, advanced.

Gagool spat curses as we drew near.

Twala lifted his head for the first time. His single eye, blazing with rage, fixed on Ignosi.

“Hail, O king,” he said, voice laced with scorn. “Thou who ate my bread, and now, with the aid of the white man’s magic, hast seduced my armies and defeated my host. Hail! What fate dost thou prepare for me?”

“The same fate thou gavest to my father,” Ignosi replied, cold and firm. “The throne thou hast sat upon for so long.”
