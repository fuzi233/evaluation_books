At the corner of a long, straight, brick-built artery in the far East End of London—a thoroughfare defined by its lifelessness, composed merely of two drab walls upon which the level lines of perfectly even window-sills and doorsteps stretch in a weary, hypnotic perspective from end to end, resembling petrified diagrams of dead problems—stands a house that exerts a gravitational pull upon me. Often, when least conscious of my own footsteps, I awaken to find myself hurrying through noisy, crowded boulevards where flaring naphtha lamps illuminate fierce, patient, leaden-coloured faces; through dim-lit, empty avenues where monstrous shadows dance upon close-drawn blinds; through narrow, noisome lanes where gutters swarm with children and every open doorway vomits chaos; past reeking corners and across wastelands, until I finally reach the dreary destination of my memory-driven desire. Coming to a halt beside the broken railings, I find rest.

The house, larger than its neighbours, constructed when the street was still a country lane edging the marshes, strikes a discordant note of individuality amidst the surrounding harmony of hideousness. It is enclosed on two sides by what was once a garden, though now but a barren patch of stones and dust where clothes—odd, anyone should consider washing them—hang in perpetuity; while about the door remain the remnants of a porch, exposed in all its naked insincerity by the falling stucco. Occasionally, I drift hitherward during the day, when slatternly women gossip round the area gates, and the silence is shattered by the hoarse, wailing cry of "Coals—any coals—three and sixpence a sack—co-o-o-als!" chanted in a tone that the absence of response has stamped with chronic melancholy. Yet, then the street knows me not, and my old friend of the corner, ashamed of its shabbiness under the unpitying sunlight, turns its face away, refusing to acknowledge me as I pass.

Not until the Night, merciful alone of all things to the ugly, draws her veil across its sordid features will it, like some fond old nurse sought out in after years, open wide its arms to welcome me. Then, the teeming life it currently shelters, hushed for a time within its walls, and the flickering flare from the "King of Prussia" opposite extinguished, allows it to converse with me of the past, asking many questions and reminding me of many things I had forgotten. Into the silent street come the well-remembered footsteps; in and out the creaking gate pass, unseen by me, the well-remembered faces. We talk concerning them, as two cronies turning the torn leaves of some old album where faded portraits in forgotten fashions speak together in low tones of those now dead or scattered, exchanging now a smile and now a sigh, punctuated by many an "Ah me!" or "Dear, dear!"

"Children!" I say in surprise, for my father must have been past thirty-five before the House could have known him, and my mother's face is very close to mine in the darkness, so that I see the many grey hairs mingling with the bonny brown.

"Children," repeats the old House, irritably, seemingly displeased that its opinions are questioned—a failing common to old folk—"the most helpless pair of children I ever set eyes upon. Who but a child, I should like to know, would have conceived the notion of repairing his fortune by becoming a solicitor at thirty-eight, or, having conceived such a notion, would have selected the outskirts of Poplar as a likely centre in which to put up his door-plate?"

Even your mother knew nothing of it till years afterwards—so that no man should be a penny the poorer for having trusted his good name. I have no answer; nor does the old House wait for one, but talks on. "And your mother! To truly grasp the architecture of my early exile, one must first confront the absolute poverty of my universe at the age of seven. I existed within a microscopic empire of two, subjugated by a woman whose name was Mrs. Fursey, though time in that cottage seemed measured only by the decay of her patience. She was a square-built, bony artifact of a world I did not fully know, roughly forty years of age, though the years seemed to stretch and contract based solely on the rhythm of her knitting needles. Her dark hair fell in matted, feral clusters, and a vast, knobby beard obscured her face, rendering her less a gentle guardian than a beast draped in the ruin of whatever domestic civility had cast her out. She possessed a terrifying latent power—the power to dictate the terms of my existence—but she spent her days in a state of stagnant lethargy, only stirring to exert her dominance over my labor or my logic.

Our lives were bound by the daily ritual of the nursery fire, barricaded from the wider world by the heavy oak doors of the cottage. Beyond this, my existence was an enforced void. I was permitted no education, no heritage, and almost no language that mattered. Mrs. Fursey utilized silence as a tool of oppression, rationing her words to commands and corrections, ensuring my mind remained as barren as the rocky crags outside. I became a taciturn creature, my intellect starved, left to gaze into the abyssal depths of the hearth or trace the cold geometries of the smoke rising from the chimney. I was kept as a beast of the field—valuable only for the calories my labor saved her, and perhaps to serve as a mirror for her own desperate need for mastery.

The first crack in this fortress of solitude appeared not with a shout, but with a question born of a child's egoism. Sitting on a low chair before the fire, one knee supported in my locked hands, I watched the needle grate with monotonous regularity against her thimble. Suddenly, without moving, I said: "Then why did she take me in?" referring to my mother. The rasping click of the needle ceased abruptly.

"Took you in! What's the child talking about? Who's took you in?"

"Why, mamma. If she didn't want me, why did she take me in?"

Even while I propounded this, as I proudly felt, logically unanswerable question, I was glad that she had. The vision of my being refused at the bedroom window presented itself to my imagination. I saw the stork, perplexed and annoyed, looking as I had sometimes seen Tom Pinfold look when the fish he had been holding out by the tail had been sniffed at by Anna, and the kitchen door shut in his face. Would the stork also have gone away thoughtfully scratching his head with one of those long, compass-like legs of his, and muttering to himself? And here, incidentally, I fell a-wondering how the stork had carried me. In the garden I had often watched a blackbird carrying a worm, and the worm, though no doubt really safe enough, had always appeared to me nervous and uncomfortable. Had I wriggled and squirmed in like fashion? And where would the stork have taken me to then? Possibly to Mrs. Fursey's: their cottage was the nearest. But I felt sure Mrs. Fursey would not have taken me in; and next to them, at the first house in the village, lived Mr. Chumdley, the cobbler, who was lame, and who sat all day hammering boots with very dirty hands, in a little cave half under the ground, his whole appearance suggesting a poor-spirited ogre. I should have hated being his little boy. Possibly nobody would have taken me in. I grew pensive, thinking of myself as the rejected of all the village. What would the stork have done with me, left on his hands, so to speak. The reflection prompted a fresh question.

"Nurse, where did I come from?"

"Why, I've told you often. The stork brought you."

"Yes, I know. But where did the stork get me from?" Mrs. Fursey paused for quite a long while before replying. Possibly she was reflecting whether such answer might not make me unduly conceited. Eventually she must have decided to run that risk; other opportunities could be relied upon for neutralizing the effect.

"Oh, from Heaven."

"But I thought Heaven was a place where you went to," I answered; "not where you comed from." I know I said "comed," for I remember that at this period my irregular verbs were a bewildering anxiety to my poor mother. "Comed" and "goned," which I had worked out for myself, were particular favorites of mine.

Mrs. Fursey passed over my grammar in dignified silence. She had been pointedly requested not to trouble herself with that part of my education, my mother holding that diverging opinions upon the same subject only confused a child.

"You came from Heaven," repeated Mrs. Fursey, "and you'll go to Heaven--if you're good."

"Do all little boys and girls come from Heaven?"

"So they say." Mrs. Fursey's tone implied that she was stating what might possibly be but a popular fallacy, for which she individually took no responsibility.

"And did you come from Heaven, Mrs. Fursey?" Mrs. Fursey's reply to this was decidedly more emphatic.

"Of course I did. Where do you think I came from?"

At once, I am ashamed to say, Heaven lost its exalted position in my eyes. Even before this, it had puzzled me that everybody I knew should be going there—for so I was always assured; now, connected as it appeared to be with the origin of Mrs. Fursey, much of its charm disappeared.

But this was not all. Mrs. Fursey's information had suggested to me a fresh grief. I stopped not to console myself with the reflection that my fate had been but the fate of all little boys and girls. With a child's egoism I seized only upon my own particular case.

"Didn't they want me in Heaven then, either?" I asked. "Weren't they fond of me up there?"

The misery in my voice must have penetrated even Mrs. Fursey's bosom, for she answered more sympathetically than usual.

"Oh, they liked you well enough, I daresay. I like you, but I like to get rid of you sometimes." There could be no doubt as to this last. Even at the time, I often doubted whether that six o'clock bedtime was not occasionally half-past five.

The answer comforted me not. It remained clear that I was not wanted either in Heaven nor upon the earth. God did not want me. He was glad to get rid of me. My mother did not want me. She could have done without me. Nobody wanted me. Why was I here?

And then, as the sudden opening and shutting of the door of a dark room, came into my childish brain the feeling that Something, somewhere, must have need of me, or I could not be, Something I felt I belonged to and that belonged to me, Something that was as much a part of me as I of It. The feeling came back to me more than once during my childhood, though I could never put it into words. Years later the son of the Portuguese Jew explained to me my thought. But all that I myself could have told was that in that moment I knew for the first time that I lived, that I was I.

To truly comprehend the architecture of my childhood, one must first dismantle the illusion of normalcy that shrouded my family unit. My parents, though technically adults—my father nearing forty, my mother just past thirty—loomed over me not as seasoned elders, but as ancient, prehistoric figures trapped in a perpetual adolescence. They were creatures of elemental instinct rather than social convention. To them, marriage was not a contract of mutual support, but a symbiotic dependency: a husband who could never find equilibrium unless his wife hovered beside him, issuing frantic summons from his office—"Maggie, are you doing anything important? I want to discuss a matter of business"—only to retreat immediately upon hearing her absence; a wife whose entire emotional landscape was dictated by his proximity, glowing with adoration when he was present and dimming into shadow when he was not.

Living within this volatile ecosystem required a specific set of survival tactics. One learned early to keep one's feet hidden beneath the tablecloth, lest they be mistaken for someone else's and crushed underfoot. One avoided entering rooms without first announcing one's presence with a cacophony of noise, for the shock of finding a couple sitting rigidly in opposite corners, engrossed in silent communion, was enough to induce a nervous collapse. Yet, despite these hazards, my household operated with a peculiar fragility. I, poor Spud, wielded little authority here. My intrusions were met not with discipline, but with a sigh of relief and the immediate offering of a sofa cushion. However, my aunt, Fan, commanded a different kind of reverence. She moved through the house not with footsteps, but with the rhythm of a sustained, penetrating cough—a dry, rattling bark that seemed to echo the dying struggles of a ward of consumptives. Her entrance was a theatrical event; she would reverse her direction, backing into a room while singing softly, slamming the door shut with a violence that suggested she was fleeing a monster rather than greeting kin. Paradoxically, the more sympathy she lavished upon my parents, the more venomous their resentment became.

"I believe, Fanny, you hate seeing Luke and me happy together," my mother once snapped, descending from the kitchen as my aunt prepared to drop teaspoons at precise five-second intervals outside the drawing-room door. "Don't make yourself so ridiculous."

"Hate it!" my aunt replied, her voice surprisingly mild. "Why should I? Why shouldn't a pair of turtle-doves bill and coo, when their combined age is barely seventy, the pretty dears?"

My father, however, descended into vindictiveness. I recall a specific afternoon by the canal bank where he halted abruptly, his tone shifting as if I were personally responsible for Fan's existence. "Your aunt is the most cantankerous, the most..." He broke off, shaking a fist toward the setting sun. "I wish to God your aunt had a comfortable little income of her own, with a freehold cottage in the country, by God I do!" Immediately, shame washed over him, and he softened his posture. "Not but what sometimes, of course, she can be very nice, you know," he added quickly, glancing at me. "Don't tell your mother what I said just now."

The domestic drama was further complicated by Susan, our maid-of-all-work, a stout, elderly woman prone to sudden, inexplicable bouts of sleepiness attributed to "trouble." Yet, she boasted that her heart was always in the right place. Whenever she caught sight of my parents seated near one another, she would collapse into tears, claiming the sight of conjugal bliss reminded her of the glories of her own past marriage. As an earnest inquirer, I struggled to parse the logic of her memories. On Mondays, she wept into her Brussels sprouts, lamenting that she had been denied the privilege of watering "his" grave at Manor Park. By Tuesdays, her demeanor shifted to chilling intensity as she recounted plans to seize "him" by the scruff of his neck.

"But, I thought, Susan, he was dead," I ventured innocently.

"So did I, Master Paul," she countered, her eyes gleaming. "That was his artfulness."

"Then he isn't buried in Manor Park Cemetery?"

"Not yet; but he'll wish he was, the half-baked monkey, when I get hold of him."

"Who?"

"Your husband."

"Who says he ain't a good man?" Susan's mind leaped between tenses with bewildering speed. "If anybody says he ain't, I'll gouge their eye out!"

She justified her fervor by noting that he brought her a bottle of gin every time he returned home. At such moments, I would retreat to the safer, clearer atmospheres of German grammar or mixed fractions, seeking refuge from the chaos. We all suffered under Susan's erratic nature, save for my aunt, who refused to pretend to sentimentality.

"She's a lazy hussy," my aunt declared one morning while rinsing dishes, describing Susan as a "gulping, snorting, lazy hussy."

Whether it was because the drawing-room served as a showroom for imaginary guests, filled with Cowper's poems lying face-down on a wobbly table and half-finished crochet work draped over chairs like discarded lace, or simply because my father treated his profession as a game of make-believe—he would vanish to play cricket in the garden on dry days or ninepins in the hallway on wet ones, only returning when the front door knocker sounded—I cannot say. What I knew, however, was certain: as a child, I viewed my father's career not as a solemn vocation, but as a grand role-play. He was merely acting, surrounded by towering books and bundles of documents labeled with imposing titles but containing mostly blank paper. I existed within a microscopic empire of two, subjugated by a man I was conditioned to call "Master." He was a square-built, brutal artifact of a world I did not know, roughly sixty years of age, though time on the island seemed measured only by the decay of his humanity. Our lives were bound by the daily ritual of bathing in a salt-water pool, barricaded from the shark-infested ocean by a ridge of jagged stone. Beyond this, my existence was an enforced void. To truly comprehend the architecture of Doctor Florret, one must first dismantle the illusion of his benevolence. He was a man constructed entirely of correctness—a large specimen, neither towering nor diminutive, precisely calibrated to the dimensions required for the headmaster of a respectable middle-class institution. He was stout, suggesting abundance rather than gluttony; his hands were pale and sculpted, adorned on the left with a diamond ring that possessed the dull, steady luster of authority rather than the chaotic sparkle of wealth. He spoke of mundane meteorological phenomena with a cadence that bestowed dignity upon the weather itself. His visage, perpetually clean-shaven, radiated a benignity carefully tempered by discretion.

Every aspect of his existence mirrored this rigid geometry. His wife, the feminine archetype of his own perfection, stood beside him as if she had been carved directly from one of his ribs, a testament to the theory that original methods had been retroactively applied to their union. Their furniture was utilitarian, built for endurance rather than aesthetic flourish; their paintings adhered strictly to the sartorial rules of decorum, adorning the walls without demanding attention. Doctor Florret possessed the uncanny ability to utter the correct sentiment at the precise moment, in the exact tone, rendering doubt impossible. To observe him was to realize that deviation was synonymous with revolution; to disagree with him was to declare oneself an insurgent.

In practice, however, as I soon learned at the cost of several wasted years, he adhered dogmatically to the pedagogical doctrines established by English schoolmen since the reign of Edward VI. During Barbara's four years' absence, my adoration had grown and strengthened. To truly grasp the architecture of Paul Kelver's early exile, one must first understand the absolute poverty of his universe at the age of seven. I existed within a microscopic empire of two, subjugated by a man I was conditioned to call "Master." He was a square-built, brutal artifact of a world I did not know, roughly sixty years of age, though time on the island seemed measured only by the decay of his humanity. His dark hair fell in matted, feral clusters, and a vast beard obscured his face, rendering him less a man than a beast draped in the ruin of whatever civilization had cast him out. He possessed a terrifying latent power, yet he spent his days in a state of stagnant lethargy, only stirring to exert his dominance over my labor—collecting sea-birds or hauling faggots of wood down the ravine.

Our lives were bound by the daily ritual of bathing in a salt-water pool, barricaded from the shark-infested ocean by a ridge of jagged stone. Beyond this, my existence was an enforced void. I was permitted no education, no heritage, and almost no language. The Master utilized silence as a tool of oppression, rationing his words to commands and curses, ensuring my mind remained as barren as the rocky crags that caged us. I became a taciturn creature, my intellect starved, left to gaze into the abyssal depths of the ocean or trace the cold geometries of the stars. I was kept as a beast of the field—valuable only for the calories my labor saved him, and perhaps to serve as a mirror for his own desperate need for mastery.

I left him in the dark, initiating a careful descent down the rain-slicked precipices that formed the carceral walls of our world. Before I had traversed fifty paces, a sudden rupture of lightning tore the sky, pursued instantly by a shriek so guttural it arrested my breath. It was a sound stripped of all former sovereignty. I could not trace its origin through the howling wind, but then I heard the voice—his voice, diminished and frantic—commanding my return.

I obeyed, stepping back over the jagged stone, and found him frozen exactly where I had left him.

"You called me, master?" I asked, testing the word on my tongue.

"Yes, I did. Take my hand. Lead me to the cabin."

I guided him, marveling at the sudden frailty in his grip. The hand that had so often struck me down now clung to my shoulder with the desperate dependency of a drowning man. He found his bedding and collapsed onto it, a ruined monument of flesh.

"Bring the kid full of water," he gasped. "Quick!"

I brought the vessel. He plunged his face into it, bathing his head as if trying to wash away the fire of the heavens. Presently, he fell back against the damp wood, a heavy groan escaping his chest. "O God! It is all over with me," he whispered into the gloom. "I shall live and die in this cursed hole."

"What is the matter, master?" I asked.

He offered no answer, sinking instead into a rhythmic liturgy of curses and moans. When he finally fell still, I slipped out again into the dying tempest.

To reconstruct the architecture of my fall, one must first understand the absolute arrogance of my universe at the height of my youth. I existed within a microscopic empire of one, intoxicated by the delusion of greatness, believing myself destined for a throne of honor amidst the mud of reality. I had left London, the drums of ambition beating in my heart, the flags of glory waving in my brain. Yet, somewhat more than a year later, on a foggy, wet December evening, I sneaked back to it—not as a conqueror, but as a defeated creature, disgraced. Ah, that is a small thing, capable of redress; I returned to the city as to a hiding-place, a labyrinth of faces where, lost in the crowd, I might waste my days unnoticed until such time as I could summon the resolution to end this dead life.

I had been ambitious, dwelling again amid the bitter months that followed my return, I write in the past tense. I had been eager to carve a name, a position for myself. But were I to claim no higher aim, I should be doing injustice to my blood—to the great-souled gentleman whose whole life had been an ode to honor, to her of simple faith who had known no other prayer to teach me than the childish cry, "God help me to be good!" I had wished to be a great man, but it was to have been a great *good* man. The world was to have admired me, but to have respected me also. I was to have been the knight without fear, but, rarer yet, without reproach—Galahad, not Launcelot. I had learned myself to be a feeble, backboneless fighter, conquered by the first serious assault of evil, a creature of mean fears, a slave to every crack of the devil's whip, a feeder with swine.

Urban Vane, the architect of my ruin, I had discovered to be a common swindler. His play, a masterpiece of theft, had been plucked from the desk of a well-known dramatist whose acquaintance he had made in Deleglise's kitchen. The man had fallen ill, and Vane had been constant in his visits, feigning concern. Partly recovering, the man had gone abroad to Italy. Had he died there, as at the time was expected, the robbery might never have come to light. News reached us in a small northern town that he had taken a fresh lease of life and was on his way back to England. Then it was that Vane, with calm indifference, smoking his cigar over a bottle of wine to which he had invited me, told me the bald truth, adorning it with some touches of wit. Had the recital come upon me sooner, I might have acted differently; but six months' companionship with Urban Vane, if it had not, by grace of the Lord, destroyed the roots of whatever flower of manhood might have been implanted in me, had most certainly withered its leaves.

The man was clever. That he was not clever enough to perceive from the beginning what he has learned since—that honesty is the best policy—at least, for men with brains—remains somewhat of a mystery to me. Where once he made his hundreds among shady ways, he now, I suppose, makes his thousands in the broad daylight of legitimate enterprise. Chicanery in the blood, one might imagine, has to be worked out. Urban Vanes are to be found in all callings. They commence as scamps; years later, to one's astonishment, one finds them ornaments to their profession. Wild oats are of various quality, according to the soil from which they are preserved. We sow them in our various ways.

At first I stormed. Vane sat with an amused smile upon his lips and listened.

"Your language, my dear Kelver," he replied, my vocabulary exhausted, "might wound me were I able to accept you as an authority upon this vexed question of morals. With the rest of the world you preach one thing and practise another. I have noticed it so often. It is perhaps sad, but the preaching has ceased to interest me. For months we have been travelling with this play: 'Drama, in five acts, by Mr. Horace Moncrieff.' Not more than two hundred lines of it are your own—excellent lines, I admit, but they do not constitute the play."

This aspect of the affair had not occurred to me. "But you asked me to put my name to it," I stammered. "You said you did not want your own to appear—for private reasons. You made a point of it."

He waved away the smoke from his cigar. "The man you are posing as would never have put his name to work not his own. You never hesitated; on the contrary, you jumped at the chance of so easy an opening to your career as a playwright. My need, as you imagined it, was your opportunity."

"But you said it was from the French," I argued; "you had merely translated it, I adapted it. I don't defend the custom, but it is the custom: the man who adapts a play calls himself the author. They all do it."

"I know," he answered. "It has always amused me. Our sick friend himself, whom I am sure we are both delighted to welcome back to life, has done it more than once, and made a very fair profit on the transaction. Indeed, from internal evidence, I am strongly of opinion that this present play is a case in point. Well, chickens come home to roost: I adapt from him. What is the difference?"

"Simply this," he continued, pouring himself out another glass of wine, "that whereas, owing to the anomalous state of the copyright laws, stealing from the foreign author is legal and commendable, against stealing from the living English author there is a certain prejudice."

"And the consequences, I am afraid, you will find somewhat unpleasant," I suggested.

He laughed: it was not a frivolity to which he was prone. "You mean, my dear Kelver, that you will."

"Don't look so dumbfounded," he went on. "You cannot be so stupid as you are pretending to be. The original manuscript at the Lord Chamberlain's office is in your handwriting. You knew our friend as well as I did, and visited him. Why, the whole tour has been under your management. You have arranged everything—most excellently; I have been quite surprised."

Hodgson visited me one evening. "I require you to write me a comic opera," he declared, holding an open letter in his hand which he was perusing. "The public appears weary of these eternal translations from the French. I desire something English, something new and original."

Such a venture can forge a man, but it can also break him. Above all other artistic forms—if I may be pardoned for invoking the sacred term in connection with such a trivial subject—a comic opera demands experience.

I laid bare my fears. I omitted to mention that in my desk rested a four-act drama throbbing with humanity, with life itself, which had been my hope—fading day by day—to seize the theatrical public by storm and establish myself as a serious dramatist.

"It is exceedingly simple," Hodgson urged. "Provide Atherton ample comic business; you ought to manage that without difficulty. Give Gleeson something pretty in waltz time, and assign Duncan a role requiring her to change her frock every quarter-hour, and the task is accomplished."

"I'll tell you what," Hodgson continued, "I will take the entire troupe down to Richmond on Sunday. We shall hire a coach and depart the theater at half-past ten. It will afford you an opportunity to observe them. You can converse with them and discern precisely what they are capable of. Atherton harbors ideas; he will elucidate them for you. Then, next week, we shall draft a contract and commence work."

It was an opportunity too splendid to relinquish, even though I knew that if successful, I would find myself pinned down more firmly than ever to my role of jester. Yet, the composition of comic opera is lucrative.

A small throng had gathered in the Strand to witness our departure.

"Nothing wrong, is there?" inquired the leading lady, her tone laced with anxiety as she alighted a quarter-hour late from her cab. "Is it not a fire, is it?"

"Merely assembled to behold you," Mr. Hodgson explained, without raising his eyes from his correspondence.

"Oh, good gracious!" cried the leading lady. "Do let us escape swiftly."

"Box seat, my dear," returned Mr. Hodgson.

Accepting the proffered assistance of myself and three other gentlemen, the leading lady mounted the ladder with charming hesitation. Some delay occurred during her dismounting due to our low comedian, who twice feigned missing his footing, sliding back down into the arms of the stolid door-keeper. The crowd, composed largely of small boys delighted by the endeavor to amuse them, erupted in laughter and applause. Encouraged by this reception, our low comedian made a third attempt upon his hands and knees, and, gaining the roof, sat down upon the tenor, who smiled somewhat mechanically.

As we passed the first dozen or so buses, our low comedian greeted them by rising to his feet and bowing profoundly, thereafter falling back upon either the tenor or myself. Except for the tenor and myself, his performance appeared thoroughly appreciated. Upon reaching Charing Cross, however, nobody seemed interested in our progress; to the relief of the tenor and myself, he settled down.

"People occasionally ask me," said the low comedian, brushing dust from his knees, "why I engage in such antics off the stage. It amuses me."

"I was traveling up to London recently from Birmingham," he continued. "At Willesden, when the ticket collector opened the door, I sprang out of the carriage and ran off down the platform. Naturally, he chased after me, shouting at everyone to stop me. I eluded them for about a minute. You cannot imagine the excitement generated. Approximately fifty people abandoned their seats to discover the commotion. I explained to them when they caught me that I had been traveling second class with a first-class ticket, which was the truth. People assume I do this to attract attention. I do it for my own pleasure."

"It must be a troublesome method of amusing oneself," I suggested.

"Exactly what my wife says," he replied; "she can never comprehend the impulse that overtakes us all, I suppose, at times, to play the fool. As a rule, when she is with me, I refrain."

"She is not present today?" I asked, glancing around.

"She suffers terribly from headaches," he answered, "she hardly ventures anywhere."

"I am sorry." My words were not spoken out of mere politeness; I genuinely felt regret.

Throughout the drive to Richmond, this irrepressible urge to entertain himself overwhelmed him repeatedly. Through Kensington, he drew considerable attention by balancing a horn upon his nose. At Kew, he halted the coach to request permission from a young ladies' boarding school to enter for sixpence. At the base of Richmond Hill, he provoked a gathering while attempting to persuade a deaf elderly gentleman in a Bath-chair to permit his attendant to race us up the hill for a shilling.
