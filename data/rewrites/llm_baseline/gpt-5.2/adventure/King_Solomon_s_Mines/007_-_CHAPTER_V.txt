We had killed nine elephants, and for two days the camp smelt of hot blood and sun-struck hide while we worked. It is no light business, cutting tusks out clean and whole—the knife grates upon gristle, the saw bites, the great head lolls like a fallen monument, and all the while the flies come in a black, humming veil, tasting everything, claiming everything. When at last we had the ivory safe, we carried it in, piece by piece, and buried it under the sand beneath a big tree that stood up like a finger on the plain, visible for miles. It was a noble lot—solid, sound, creamy in the heart—averaging forty to fifty pounds the tusk. The pair from the great bull that had killed poor Khiva would, as nearly as we could judge, have turned the scale at one hundred and seventy pounds.

As for Khiva, there was little to bury. We laid what remained of him in an ant-bear hole and put an assegai beside him, that he might not go empty-handed into whatever country lies beyond this one. Then, on the third day, we shouldered our loads and marched again, with that odd mixture of hope and superstition a man carries when he hides treasure in the wild and must leave it behind him like seed in the ground—trusting to time, to luck, and to his own life.

After a long, grinding tramp, and mishaps enough that would make a book by themselves, we came at last to Sitanda’s Kraal by the Lukanga River—the true threshold of our venture. I remember it as a man remembers the taste of the first mouthful before a hard fast begins. On our right lay the scattered settlement: a few stone cattle kraals, patches of cultivated land near the water where the people coaxed a thin harvest out of the soil, and beyond them the wide veld rolling in tall grass, with little herds of game wandering as if they had been born of the wind. On our left yawned the desert—bare, abrupt, and uncompromising, as though someone had drawn a line with a knife and said: here nourishment ends.

Below our camp a small stream ran with a sound like a quiet throat clearing itself; on the far side rose a stony slope, the very one down which, twenty years before, I had watched poor José Silvestre creep back, used up and half dead after his attempt upon Solomon’s Mines. Beyond that slope the country died into waterless waste, with only karoo scrub crouching close to the sand as if afraid to be seen.

We pitched at evening. The sun, a great red ball, sank into the desert and threw long, coloured rays over it—scarlet, copper, and purple—till the whole emptiness looked dressed for a ceremony. Good stayed to see to camp; I took Sir Henry up the slope opposite, and we stood there and stared out. The air was so clear that far away, faint as breath on glass, I could make out blue outlines—here and there capped with white—of the Suliman Berg.

“There,” I said, “is the wall round Solomon’s Mines. But God knows whether we shall ever climb it.”

“My brother should be there,” Sir Henry answered, quiet as a man saying grace over a familiar table, “and if he is, I shall reach him somehow.”

“I hope so,” I said; and turned—then saw we were not alone.

Behind us stood Umbopa, the big Kafir who had attached himself to Sir Henry, gazing over our shoulders towards those far mountains as though he could taste them on the wind.

When he caught my eye he spoke, not to me but to Sir Henry, and he used the native name they had given him.

“Is it to that land thou wouldst journey, Incubu?” he asked, pointing with his broad assegai.

I checked him sharply; it is one thing for them to nickname a man among themselves, another for them to fling their heathen titles at a white gentleman’s face. Umbopa laughed—a low, self-contained laugh that set my temper on edge.

“How dost thou know I am not the equal of the Inkosi whom I serve?” he said. “He is of a royal house, no doubt; one can see it in his size and in his mien. So, mayhap, am I. At the least, I am as great a man. Be my mouth, Macumazahn, and say my words to the Inkoos Incubu.”

I did not relish his swagger, but there was weight in him, and curiosity in me. So I translated—adding, for form’s sake, my opinion that he was an impudent fellow.

“Yes, Umbopa,” Sir Henry said, “I would journey there.”

“The desert is wide and there is no water,” Umbopa went on. “The mountains are high and covered with snow. No man can say what lies beyond them behind the place where the sun sets. How shalt thou come thither, Incubu, and wherefore dost thou go?”

Sir Henry answered, and I gave it to Umbopa: he went because a man of his blood—his brother—had gone that way before; he journeyed to seek him.

“That is so,” said Umbopa. “A Hottentot I met on the road told me a white man went out into the desert two years ago towards those mountains with one servant, a hunter. They never came back.”

Sir Henry’s head lifted a shade. “How do you know it was my brother?”

“Nay, I know not. But when I asked what the white man was like, the Hottentot said he had thine eyes and a black beard. He said too that the hunter’s name was Jim—a Bechuana who wore clothes.”

“There’s no doubt,” I put in. “I knew Jim well.”

Sir Henry nodded, as though the last knot of uncertainty had been pulled tight. “If George set his mind upon a thing, he did it,” he said. “If he meant to cross the Suliman Berg he has crossed it, unless an accident overtook him. We must look for him on the other side.”

Umbopa understood our tongue better than he pretended. “It is a far journey, Incubu,” he said.

Sir Henry’s answer came slowly, with the steadiness of a man sharpening a blade. He spoke of a road a man will take if love drives him; of deserts crossed, mountains climbed, life held out in the hand like a small coin that can be spent or saved as Heaven orders.

I translated.

Umbopa listened and then, in his turn, poured out one of those strange, swelling speeches that sometimes come to Zulus like a song coming to a man who did not know he could sing.

“Great words, my father,” he said. “Words fit to fill the mouth. Thou art right. What is life? It is a feather. It is the seed of grass blown hither and thither—sometimes multiplying and dying in the act, sometimes carried away into the heavens. Yet if the seed be heavy and good it may travel a little way on the road it wills. Man must die. At the worst he can but die a little sooner. I will go with thee across the desert and over the mountains, unless I fall to the ground on the way.”

Then he paused and began again, as if he were listening to something the rest of us could not hear—the deep hush of the desert itself, the long breath of it, the way it swallows words and gives back only a faint echo.

“What is life?” he asked us—“you white men who are wise, who know the secrets of the world and of the stars, who flash your words from afar without a voice—tell me the secret of our life, whither it goes and whence it comes!”

We could not answer.

He answered himself.

“Out of the dark we come; into the dark we go. Like a storm-driven bird at night we fly out of the Nowhere; for a moment our wings are seen in the light of the fire, and lo! we are gone again into the Nowhere. Life is nothing. Life is all. It is the Hand with which we hold off Death. It is the glow-worm that shines in night-time and is black in the morning; it is the white breath of oxen in winter; it is the little shadow that runs across the grass and loses itself at sunset.”

When he stopped, the silence fell back, thick and listening. In that quiet, even the small sounds—our boots shifting on stone, the far hiss of wind in scrub—seemed important, as though the land itself judged what noises were fit to exist there.

“You are a strange man,” Sir Henry said at last.

Umbopa laughed again. “It seems to me we are much alike, Incubu. Perhaps I seek a brother over the mountains.”

I watched him sharply. “What dost thou mean?” I asked. “What dost thou know of those mountains?”

“A little,” he said. “A very little. There is a strange land yonder—a land of witchcraft and beautiful things; of brave people; of trees and streams and snowy peaks; and of a great white road. I have heard of it. But what is the good of talking? It grows dark. Those who live to see will see.”

He knew too much—enough to make a man uneasy. He read my look.

“Fear me not, Macumazahn,” he said. “I dig no holes for you to fall in. I make no plots. If ever we cross those mountains behind the sun, then I will tell what I know. But Death sits upon them. Be wise and turn back. Go hunt elephants, my masters. I have spoken.”

He saluted with his spear and walked back to camp; presently we found him squatting like any other fellow, cleaning a gun with patient care.

“That is an odd man,” Sir Henry said.

“Yes,” I answered. “Too odd by half. He knows something and won’t speak out. But there’s no use quarrelling. We’re in for a curious trip, and one mysterious Kafir won’t turn it one way or the other.”

Next day we set about our preparations. We could not drag our heavy elephant rifles and all our kit across a desert that offered no water and no shade. So we dismissed the bearers and made an arrangement with an old native whose kraal stood close by to keep our things until our return. It went against me to leave good tools and precious stores to the custody of a fellow whose eyes shone like a hyena’s whenever they slid over metal; still, I took precautions.

First I loaded all the rifles, left them at full cock, and told him that if he touched them they would go off. He tried at once with my eight-bore; it did go off, and drove a hole through one of his oxen that happened to be passing, besides knocking the old thief clean over with the recoil. He picked himself up, white-eyed with fright and rage, and had the impudence to demand payment for the ox; but nothing on God’s earth would make him put so much as a finger on a gun again.

“Put the live devils away up there in the thatch,” he said, “or they will murder us all.”

Then I told him that if, on our return, one of those things was missing, I would kill him and his people by witchcraft; and if we died and he tried to steal the rifles, I would come and haunt him, turn his cattle mad, and sour his milk till his life became bitter; and I would make the devils in the guns come out and talk to him in a way he would not like. After that he promised to guard them as though they were his father’s spirit. He was a superstitious old Kafir and a great villain.

When our surplus gear was thus disposed of, we laid out what the five of us—Sir Henry, Good, myself, Umbopa, and the Hottentot Ventvögel—would carry. It looked pitifully little when set on the ground, and yet it dragged like sin when you lifted it. For all our paring we could not get below about forty pounds a man. We took:

The three express rifles and two hundred rounds of ammunition.

Two Winchester repeating rifles for Umbopa and Ventvögel, with two hundred rounds.

Five Cochrane water-bottles, four pints each.

Five blankets.

Twenty-five pounds of biltong—sun-dried flesh, dark, hard, salt-edged, and precious as coin.

Ten pounds of mixed beads for gifts.

Medicine: quinine, and a few small instruments.

Knives, compass, matches, pocket filter, tobacco, a trowel, a bottle of brandy, and the clothes on our backs.

That was all. Nothing but what we must have; nothing we could spare.

With difficulty—and by the promise of a good hunting-knife apiece—I induced three miserable natives from the village to come the first stage, twenty miles, carrying each a large gourd of water. We meant to start in the cool of evening; my plan was to have them with us through the first night’s march so we might refill our bottles after. To them I lied gently and said we were going to shoot ostriches, which the desert did indeed hold. They clucked their tongues, shrugged, and said we were mad and would die of thirst—which, if truth be told, seemed a sensible prophecy. But knives are treasures where iron is scarce; so they agreed, likely thinking our deaths would not trouble them when once they had the steel.

All next day we lay about, resting and sleeping. At sunset we ate fresh beef and drank tea—Good remarking mournfully that it would be the last tea for many a day—and then we waited for the moon.

She rose about nine, cool and splendid, washing the land in pale light and laying a silver film over the rolling desert ahead. It looked solemn, quiet, and as indifferent to man as the star-powdered sky above. We got up, tightened straps, shifted loads. Yet we hesitated, for a moment, as men do on the lip of an action that cannot be called back.

We three white men stood together. Umbopa, spear in hand and rifle across his shoulders, fixed his eyes on the desert a few paces ahead as though he were already walking it in his mind. Ventvögel and the hired men waited behind with their water gourds.

“Gentlemen,” Sir Henry said at last, his voice low and deep, “we are going on about as strange a journey as men can make. It is doubtful we can succeed. But we are three men who will stand together for good or evil to the last. Before we start, let us pray to the Power who shapes the destinies of men, that it may please Him to direct our steps.”

He took off his hat and covered his face with his hands. Good and I did the same.

I am not, as a rule, a great hand at praying—few hunters are. Sir Henry I had never heard speak like that before, and only once since; yet I believe faith sat deep in him. Good too has religion in him, though his tongue is apt to run ahead of it. For my own part, I do not remember ever putting up a better prayer in my life than in that minute. The unknown has a way of stripping a man down to what he truly is, and the desert is the unknown made visible.

“And now,” said Sir Henry, “trek!”

So we started.

We had nothing to steer by save the far-off mountains and old José Silvestre’s chart—scratched by a dying man three centuries ago on a strip of linen; a poor thing to hang your life on, and yet our only hope. If we could not find the pool of bad water he had marked in the middle of the desert, roughly sixty miles from our start and as far again from the mountains, then—speaking plainly—we should likely die of thirst. The chances of finding one small stain of water in that vast sea of sand and karoo seemed to me near to nothing. Even if Silvestre marked it true, who could say it had not dried long ago, or been trampled in, or filled up by drifting sand?

Still we went on, silent as shadows in the moonlight, the sand heavy underfoot. Karoo bushes snagged at our boots and made us stumble; sand worked into our veldtschoons and into Good’s shooting-boots, and every few miles we had to halt and shake it out. The night stayed fairly cool, but the air was thick and close—creamy, like warm milk held too long in the mouth. Above us the sky was clean and hard with stars; under us the ground gave no sound but the soft, weary scuff of feet.

The silence was the worst of it—silence with weight, silence that pressed on the ears until a man became too aware of his own breathing. Good could not bear it and began to whistle “The Girl I left behind me,” but the tune fell dead and lugubrious into that emptiness, and he stopped.

Soon after came an incident that shook us at the time and made us laugh later. Good led, because he held the compass and, being a sailor, knew his way about it. We followed in single file. Suddenly he cried out—and vanished as though the sand had swallowed him. Next instant all around us broke loose a most extraordinary tumult: snorts, groans, thudding hooves, and the rushing hiss of bodies moving in the loose sand. In the pale light we glimpsed dim galloping forms half blurred by drifting dust. The natives dropped their gourds and were for bolting, but remembering there was nowhere to bolt to, flung themselves down and howled that it was ghosts.

Sir Henry and I stood staring; and our astonishment turned to downright amazement when we saw Good careering off towards the mountains as though mounted on a horse, halloaing wildly. Then he flung up his arms and came down with a thud.

We had walked into a herd of sleeping quagga. Good had literally fallen across the back of one; the beast rose in a panic and bolted with him astride. I shouted to the others that it was all right and ran to Good, fearing broken bones. But there he sat in the sand, shaken, frightened out of his life, yet unhurt—and, absurdly enough, his eye-glass still fixed in his eye as though it had been screwed there.

After that we trudged on without further mishap till about one o’clock, when we halted for half an hour, drank a little water—no more than wetting the mouth—and went on again.

On and on, until at last the east began to colour, faint at first like blush on a girl’s cheek. Primrose light seeped up, then turned to bars of gold, and dawn slid out across the desert. The stars faded; the moon grew sickly and thin, her face washed out, with mountain ridges cutting it like bones under skin. Then the sun sent spear after spear of light across the wilderness, firing the low mists until the whole desert trembled in a golden glare.

Even then we did not halt, though our shoulders ached and our tongues felt thick in the mouth, because we knew that when the sun stood clear we should not be able to move. At length we saw a little pile of boulders rising from the plain and dragged ourselves to it. By good fortune an overhanging slab made a ledge of shade, and beneath it the sand lay smooth and cool. We crept in like lizards and, after a sip of water and a strip of biltong—salt, leathery, slow to chew—we lay down and were asleep almost before we had settled our loads.

We woke about three in the afternoon to find the three natives preparing to return. They had had enough of desert; no number of knives would have bought another step. We drank more deeply this time, emptied our water-bottles, and refilled them from the gourds, then watched the men go until they were swallowed by distance.

At half past four we started again.

Now the loneliness took on a new edge. We were five specks in an immense, soundless place. A few ostriches moved far off, their necks like question-marks against the light; otherwise there was no life to be seen. It was too dry for game. We saw a deadly-looking cobra or two; no other reptiles. One creature, however, attended us in hordes: the common house fly. They came not as single spies but in battalions, a continual buzzing veil, tasting our eyes, our lips, the corners of wounds, the salt where sweat dried. There is something insolent in the fly—the way it thrives where nothing else can live, the way it insists upon its small music even when the world is burning. And it made me think, not for the first time, how much of a man’s courage is spent not on grand dangers but on petty torments that will not leave him alone.

At sunset we halted and waited for the moon. When she rose—beautiful and indifferent as ever—we went on again, and with one halt at about two in the morning trudged through the night until the sun at last ended our labour.

We drank a little, then flung ourselves down on the sand, worn out, and slept. There was no need to set a watch; nothing in that empty plain threatened us with tooth or bullet. Our enemies were heat, thirst, and flies; and I would sooner, a thousand times, have faced man or beast than that dreadful trinity.

This time there was no kindly rock to shelter us. By seven o’clock the sun had us in its grip and we woke with the sensation—no better word for it—that we were beefsteaks on a gridiron. We were being baked. The heat seemed to draw the very wet out of our blood; the glare hammered the eyes; the air danced over the sand as it dances over a stove.

“Phew,” I gasped, snatching at the cloud of flies that buzzed cheerfully about my head as though this were a picnic.

“My word!” said Sir Henry.

“It is hot!” Good echoed, stating the obvious like a man whose wits had begun to cook.

There was not a thumb’s breadth of shade anywhere. The desert lay around us in an unending, dazzling sheet, the karoo scrub crouching low, too mean to offer help.

“What is to be done?” asked Sir Henry. “We can’t stand this long.”

We stared at each other, minds dulled by the brute force of the sun.

“I have it,” said Good at last. “We must dig a hole, get into it, and cover ourselves with the karoo bushes.”

It was a poor remedy, but better than none. So we set to work with the trowel and our hands, clawing at the sand until, in about an hour, we had scraped out a space ten feet long by twelve wide and two feet deep. Then we cut what scrub we could with our knives and dragged it over.

We crept into the hole and pulled the bushes over us like a lid—everyone except Ventvögel, on whom, being a Hottentot, the heat seemed to have no great effect. The shelter saved us from the direct rays, but the air in that makeshift grave was thick and suffocating, hot with our own breath, tasting of sand, sweat, and bruised karoo. We lay panting, flies crawling and buzzing in the dim, and every now and then we wet our lips with the smallest allowance of water, forcing ourselves to stop where instinct would have drained the lot in an hour.

If the water failed, we knew what would follow, and the knowledge sat with us all day like a weight on the chest. Yet we endured—because there was nothing else to do but endure—and because, somewhere ahead in that glare and emptiness, an old scrap of linen promised a pool of bad water, and beyond that, the pale line of mountains promised either death or the other side.
