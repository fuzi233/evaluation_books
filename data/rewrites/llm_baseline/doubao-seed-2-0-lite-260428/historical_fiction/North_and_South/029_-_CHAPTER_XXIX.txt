“Some wishes crossed my mind and dimly cheered it,
And one or two poor melancholy pleasures,
Each in the pale unwarming light of hope,
Silvering its flimsy wing, flew silent by—
Moths in the moonbeam!”

The next morning, a thin, smog-dimmed slant of sun slipped through the Hale’s drawing-room window, and Martha trundled in with the post, one plump, scrawled envelope sticking out above the rest, addressed to Margaret in Edith’s unruly, looping hand. It was as affectionate and disjointed as the cousin who had written it: the warmth of it wrapped around Margaret’s chest the moment she broke the seal, for she had grown up with Edith’s whims and meanderings, and never thought to find them foolish.

The letter ran thus:
“Oh Margaret, it is worth crossing the whole ocean from England to see my boy! He is the most superb little creature alive, especially in his caps, and most especially in that pretty stitched one you sent him, you good, deft-fingered, patient dear! I’ve made every other mother on this base envious of him, and now I’m dying to show him off to someone new, to hear a fresh set of exclamations about how perfect he is—maybe that’s all the reason I’m writing, but no, nay, there’s a little bit of cousinly love mixed in too, I swear, and I want you here so badly I can barely sit still to write it. It would do Aunt Hale’s health a world of good, I know it! Everyone here is young and bright, our skies are always blue, the sun shines from dawn till dusk, the military band plays the loveliest tunes all day long, and to circle back to my favourite subject, my baby never stops smiling.
I keep wishing you were here to draw him for me, Margaret. No matter what mischief he’s up to at any given moment, that very thing is the prettiest, gracefullest, best pose he could possibly be in. I think I love him more than I love Cosmo, truth be told—he’s gotten stout and grumpy lately, or what he calls ‘too busy for nonsense.’ No, no, that’s not fair! He just walked in with news of a perfect picnic the officers of the *Hazard* (the warship anchored in the bay below) are throwing for all of us, and I take back every cross word I just said. Didn’t some old story say a man burned his hand to atone for a careless word? I can’t burn mine, it would hurt and leave an ugly scar, so I’ll take it all back as fast as my pen can move. Cosmo is every bit as much a darling as the baby, not a bit stout, and the most ungrumpy husband alive—only sometimes he is very, very busy. That’s not unkind to say, is it? It’s wifely duty, almost.
Where was I? I know I had something very important to tell you. Oh! Dearest Margaret, you absolutely must come visit us! It will cure Aunt Hale, I promise. Tell her doctor to order it, say the thick smoke of Milton is what’s been making her poorly. I’m sure it is! Three months of this soft, sunny air, where grapes grow wild as blackberries, and she’ll be as strong as she was when we were little girls.
I won’t even ask Uncle Hale to come, the letter grew a little stiffer, more carefully worded here, as if Edith was tiptoeing around the fact that Mr. Hale had left his old parish for the sake of his conscience, because I expect he disapproves of war, and soldiers, and bands playing loud, merry tunes. I know lots of Dissenters are in the Peace Society, and I was afraid he wouldn’t care for it—but if he does want to come, tell him Cosmo and I will do everything we can to make him happy. I’ll hide Cosmo’s red uniform coat and his sword, and I’ll make the band play all the most solemn, slow hymns they know, no loud ‘pomps and vanities’ allowed anywhere he can hear them. Dear Margaret, he’d be so welcome, even if I am a little bit awed of anyone who gives up everything for their beliefs. You never did anything that drastic, did you?
Tell Aunt Hale not to bother bringing any warm clothes, even if it’s late in the year before you can get here. You can’t imagine how hot it is! I tried to wear my finest Indian shawl to a picnic last week, and I kept repeating proverbs to myself like ‘Pride must abide’ to justify it, but it was no use. I felt like Mamma’s tiny dog Tiny all tricked out in elephant harnesses—smothered, hidden, nearly killed by my own finery—so I spread it out on the grass and made it a carpet for all of us to sit on.
Now, Margaret, if you don’t pack your bags the second you finish this letter and come straight over to see my baby, I’m going to assume you’re descended from King Herod!”

Margaret ached for even one single day of that life Edith described—no weight of care, no smog hanging thick over the streets, no constant quiet anxiety about her mother’s fading strength. She was not yet twenty, but the harsh pressures of their life in Milton had made her feel old and weary, and the thought of stepping into that bright, sunlit world even for a few hours made her chest tight with longing. She laughed merrily at the silliest lines of Edith’s letter, so caught up in the joke that she did not hear her mother enter the room, leaning heavy on Dixon’s arm.
“Whatever are you laughing at, Margaret?” Mrs. Hale asked once she had been settled onto the sofa, the effort of walking leaving her breathless.
Margaret handed her the letter, then read it aloud, and for a little while Mrs. Hale was fully distracted, guessing at every possible name Edith might have given her new baby, listing a dozen reasons each name might have been chosen.

Right in the middle of that gentle chatter, Mr. Thornton stepped through the door, carrying a wicker basket of sun-ripened peaches he had grown himself. He could not bring himself to deny the small, sharp pleasure of seeing Margaret, even for a moment: it was the stubborn, unruly impulse of a man who was usually so steady, so self-controlled, no one else would have guessed he was capable of such wilful spontaneity. His eyes took in Margaret’s presence in a single quick glance, he gave her one cold, distant bow, and after that he seemed determined never to let his gaze rest on her again. He stayed just long enough to set the fruit on the table, speak a few soft, kind words about hoping the fresh peaches would do Mrs. Hale good, and then he met Margaret’s eyes for one grave, distant farewell before he turned to leave.
Margaret sat back, pale and silent for a long minute.
“Do you know, Margaret? I’m really starting to like Mr. Thornton,” Mrs. Hale said softly.
Margaret forced herself to reply, her voice icy at first, then steadying. “He is very kind and attentive. There is no doubt of that.”
“I wonder his mother never calls on me,” Mrs. Hale went on. “She must know I’m ill, everyone in town hears I’ve got a water-bed set up in my room. I should so like to meet her. You have so very few friends here, Margaret.”
Margaret knew what her mother was really thinking: a tender, quiet craving to leave her daughter in the care of another steady, kind woman, when she was no longer there to look after her. She could not bring herself to speak, the thought of her mother’s coming loss sticking in her throat.
“Do you think you could go and call on Mrs. Thornton, and ask her to come see me?” Mrs. Hale asked after a pause. “Only once. I don’t want to be any trouble at all.”
“I will do anything you wish, Mamma,” Margaret said. “Only—when Frederick comes—”
“Ah, of course. We must lock the doors, we must let no strangers in. I hardly know if I dare wish him to come at all, some days. I have such terrible dreams about him.”
“Oh Mamma, we will take such good care of him,” Margaret promised fiercely. “I will stand guard before any harm can touch him. I will watch over him like a lioness over her cubs.”
“It will be a week at least before we hear from him, won’t it?”
“Longer, very likely.”
“We must send Martha away well before he arrives, so we don’t have to rush her off at the last minute and rouse her suspicions.”
“Dixon will remind us of that, I am sure,” Margaret said. “I was thinking, if we need extra help around the house while he’s here, we can hire Mary Higgins. She’s slack of work right now, and she’s such a good, steady girl, she’d do her very best. She’d sleep at home, and never need to come upstairs, so she would never even guess we have a guest staying.”
“Whatever you think best, whatever Dixon thinks best,” Mrs. Hale said, frowning a little. “But Margaret, you must stop using these horrid Milton provincialisms. ‘Slack of work’! What will Aunt Shaw say when she hears you talking that way when she comes home?”
“Oh Mamma, don’t make a bugbear out of Aunt Shaw,” Margaret laughed. “Edith picked up all sorts of military slang from Captain Lennox, and Aunt Shaw never said a single word about it.”
“But this is factory slang.”
“I live in a factory town,” Margaret shrugged. “Why shouldn’t I speak the language of it when I need to? I could tell you a dozen words you have never heard in your life, that would astonish you. I’ll bet you don’t even know what a knobstick is.”
“No, and I don’t want to know,” Mrs. Hale said. “It sounds dreadfully vulgar. I don’t want you using it.”
“Very well, dearest. I won’t. I’ll just have to use a whole long explanatory sentence instead.”
“I hate this Milton,” Mrs. Hale sighed. “Edith is perfectly right. It’s the thick smoke that has made me so ill.”
Margaret jumped to her feet, worried: she had seen a faint little flicker of doubt in her father’s mind lately, that his choice to move to Milton might be making his wife sick, and she did not want that quiet guilt to sink in deeper. She did not know if Mr. Hale had just stepped in the door and heard those words, so she began talking quickly to redirect the conversation, unaware that Mr. Thornton was just a step behind her father.
“Mamma’s teasing me for picking up all sorts of vulgar Milton habits since we moved here,” Margaret said. She had only been referring to the local factory slang, but she saw Mr. Thornton’s brow darken immediately, as if he thought she was calling the whole town and its people vulgar. Eager to avoid hurting his feelings, she turned to him directly, forcing a small greeting she had not planned to give.
“Mr. Thornton, don’t you think ‘knobstick’ is quite expressive, even if it doesn’t sound very pretty? Could I ever find a longer phrase to carry that same exact meaning, when I’m talking about the thing it refers to? If using local words is vulgar, I was dreadfully vulgar back in the New Forest too, wasn’t I, Mamma?”
Margaret almost never drew attention to herself in conversation, and by the time she finished speaking she was bright red, flustered, especially when Thornton only gave her a cold, distant, ceremonious little nod, as if he barely understood what she was saying at all, and stepped past her to speak to Mrs. Hale.
That was when Mrs. Hale made her soft request, that Mrs. Thornton come to call on her as soon as possible, even tomorrow if she could, so she could make her quiet plea for the woman to look after Margaret when she was gone. Mr. Thornton promised his mother would come, chatted gently for a few more minutes, then took his leave. Only when the door clicked shut behind him did Margaret feel some invisible, tight weight lift from her shoulders, her voice and her movements no longer stiff with awkward tension.
He never once looked directly at her while he was in the room, but his careful avoidance of her gaze told her he knew exactly where she stood in the room, exactly every movement she made. If she spoke, he gave no outward sign he was listening, but the very next thing he said to someone else was always adjusted to account for her words, sometimes even a direct answer to a comment she had made, offered to her father or mother as if it had never come from her at all. It was not rudeness born of ignorance: it was wilful, deliberate rudeness, born of the deep, sharp offence she had dealt him the day of the riot, when she had called him a coward to his face. He regretted the cruelty of it later, the second he was out of her sight, but he thought the cold distance served him well.
Margaret found herself thinking of him more than she ever had before. Not with any spark of romance, no—only with sharp, quiet regret that she had wounded him so deeply. She missed the old, comfortable antagonistic friendship they had shared, before the riot, and she found herself behaving with a gentle, quiet humility around him, as if she was making a silent, unspoken apology for the harsh words she had spat out in the heat of that terrible afternoon.
But Thornton still smarted from those words, they echoed in his ears every time he saw her, and he was proud of the sense of justice that made him go out of his way to do small, thoughtful kindnesses for her parents. He revelled in the quiet power he wielded, forcing himself to face her, to be in the same room as her, whenever he could find an excuse to do something that would give her father or mother pleasure. He told himself he hated seeing the woman who had humiliated him so publicly—but he was wrong. There was a sharp, stinging, bright pleasure in being near her, feeling her presence in the same room. He was never one to analyse his own inner feelings too closely, and he never guessed how much he longed for that quiet, painful closeness, even when he thought he was punishing her for the hurt she had done him.
