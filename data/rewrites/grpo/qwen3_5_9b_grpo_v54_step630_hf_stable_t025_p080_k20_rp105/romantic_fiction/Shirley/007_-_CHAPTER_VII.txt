Eighteen is the threshold where the fairy tale ends and the real world begins. Until then, life is a story we listen to—a marvelous fiction filled with heroes, demons, and enchanted landscapes. The moon is larger, the sun burns with divine fire, and the dangers are merely shadows in a dream. But at eighteen, the shores of Reality rise before us, blue and soft yet terrifyingly distant. We long to reach them, imagining a land where hunger and sorrow do not exist. Yet, every joy must be earned through a bloody struggle; the heart must be gilded with the sweat of combat before the wreath of victory can be worn.

At eighteen, we are blind to this. Hope smiles, promising a tomorrow of endless happiness. Love wanders in like a lost angel, welcomed with open arms. We do not see the quiver in his bow, nor the barb hidden in his arrow; we mistake the wound for a thrill of new life. We believe passion is an unqualified good, unaware that we are about to enter the school of Experience. Alas, Experience! No other teacher wears a face so frozen, a robe so black, or wields a rod so heavy. She is the only guide through life's wilds, forcing the novice to stumble and stray until she finds a safe path.

Caroline Helstone, just turned eighteen, had no desire to spend the evening with her uncle. The rectory study was sacred ground to her, a place she rarely intruded upon. She stayed away until the bell rang for prayers. The evening service was a ritual in the Helstone household, conducted by Mr. Helstone in his usual nasal, monotonous voice. Once the rite was over, his niece approached him.

"Good-night, uncle."

"Hey! You've been gadding abroad all day—visiting, dining out, and what not!"

"Only at the cottage."

"And have you learned your lessons?"

"Yes."

"And made a shirt?"

"Only part of one."

"Well, that will do. Stick to the needle, learn shirt-making and gown-making and piecrust-making, and you'll be a clever woman some day. Go to bed now. I'm busy with a pamphlet here."

Soon, Caroline was locked in her small bedroom. She donned her white dressing-gown, loosened her long, wavy hair, and leaned against her hand, staring at the carpet. Around her rose the visions of youth. Her thoughts spoke pleasantly; she smiled as she listened. To her, the spirit of Hope was a brighter presence in the room. This flattering prophet promised no more disappointment, only the true spring of morning. She could not suspect she was the sport of delusion. Her expectations seemed solid, warranted by the solid ground of her own heart.

"When people love, the next step is they marry," she reasoned. "Now, I love Robert, and I feel sure that Robert loves me. When I looked up at him after repeating Chénier's poem, his eyes sent the truth through my heart. Sometimes I am afraid to speak to him, lest I seem forward, lest I say too much and he disapprove. But tonight, he was so indulgent. How kind he was walking up the lane! His love-making—friendship, I mean—is not like the books; it is original, quiet, manly, sincere. I *do* like him. I would be an excellent wife to him. I would tell him of his faults, study his comfort, and cherish him. Now, I am sure he will not be cold tomorrow. I feel almost certain he will come here, or ask me to go there."

She combed her hair, long as a mermaid's, and glanced at her reflection in the glass. While plain people see no enchantment in their own image, the fair believe they must charm. Caroline saw a shape and head that, captured in that moment, would be lovely. The mirror confirmed her hopes, and she sought her couch in undiminished gladness.

She rose the next day with the same gladness. Entering her uncle's breakfast room, she greeted him with soft cheerfulness. Even the little man of bronze thought, for an instant, that his niece was becoming "a fine girl." Usually quiet and timid with him, she found many things to say today. She spoke of flowers blooming in the garden, the gardener's schedule, and the starlings nesting in the church tower.

Mr. Helstone opined that the birds were "like other fools who had just paired—insensible to inconvenience just for the moment." Emboldened by her good spirits, Caroline ventured a remark she had never dared before.

"Uncle," she said, "whenever you speak of marriage you speak of it scornfully. Do you think people shouldn't marry?"

"It is decidedly the wisest plan to remain single, especially for women."

"Are all marriages unhappy?"

"Millions of marriages are unhappy. If everybody confessed the truth, perhaps all are more or less so."

"You are always vexed when you are asked to come and marry a couple. Why?"

"Because one does not like to act as accessory to the commission of a piece of pure folly."

Mr. Helstone seemed glad to vent his mind. Caroline pressed further.

"But why," she asked, "should it be pure folly? If two people like each other, why shouldn't they consent to live together?"

"They tire of each other—they tire of each other in a month. A yokefellow is not a companion; he or she is a fellow-sufferer."

Caroline's reply was not naive; it was born of antipathy to his cynicism.

"One would think you had never been married, uncle. One would think you were an old bachelor."

"Practically, I am so."

"But you have been married. Why were you so inconsistent as to marry?"

"Every man is mad once or twice in his life."

"So you tired of my aunt, and my aunt of you, and you were miserable together?"

Mr. Helstone pushed out his cynical lip, wrinkled his brown forehead, and grunted.

"Did she not suit you? Was she not good-tempered? Did you not get used to her? Were you not sorry when she died?"

"Caroline," Mr. Helstone said, slamming his hand on the mahogany table, "understand this: it is vulgar and puerile to confound generals with particulars. In every case there is the rule and there are the exceptions. Your questions are stupid and babyish. Ring the bell, if you have done breakfast."

The breakfast was cleared away. Instead of leaving, Caroline sat by the window. Mr. Helstone looked at her uneasily but continued reading his paper, which featured dispatches from General Lord Wellington. He did not know that tumultuous thoughts were swirling in his niece's mind, thoughts revived by their conversation but planted years ago.

She reviewed his character, his sentiments on marriage, and the wide chasm between her mind and his. Across that chasm stood another figure, dim and sinister—the memory of her father, James Helstone. Rumors had reached her of his bad character. She remembered dark weeks spent with him in a great town, shut up in a high garret without a carpet, while he went out early and forgot to return. He had been furious, terrible, or imbecile. She recalled one night when he had raved, saying he would kill her because she was a burden. Her screams had brought aid, and she had never seen him again except as a dead man in his coffin.

She also had a mother, though Mr. Helstone never spoke of her. Caroline knew she was alive, but she was the drunkard's wife. Turning from the lattice, she broke the silence in a low, bitter voice.

"You term marriage miserable, I suppose, from what you saw of my father and mother's. If my mother suffered what I suffered when I was with papa, she must have had a dreadful life."

Mr. Helstone wheeled about, startled. He could not conceive that she had any thoughts or memories of her parents.

"Your father and mother? Who has been talking to you about them?"

"Nobody; but I remember something of what papa was, and I pity mamma. Where is she?"

"I hardly know," Mr. Helstone replied, avoiding the question. "I was little acquainted with her. I have not heard from her for years... Come, it is school-time. You go to your cousin at ten, don't you? The clock has struck."

Fanny entered to announce that the churchwardens needed Mr. Helstone. He hastened away, and Caroline set out for the cottage.

The road to Hollow's Mill inclined downward, and she ran almost the entire way. The fresh air and the thought of Robert revived her spirits. Upon arriving near the white house, she saw Moore at his garden gate. He stood in his belted Holland blouse and light cap.

"He has not his peer," she thought. "He is as handsome as he is intelligent. What a keen eye he has! What clearly-cut, spirited features... better than any of those shuffling curates... better than anybody; bonny Robert!"

She sought him out. When she challenged his sight, he would have vanished like a phantom if he could, but he was a tall fact and had to stand the greeting. His manner was brief—cousin-like, brother-like, friend-like, but anything but lover-like. The nameless charm of the previous night had vanished. He was no longer the same man.

Rude disappointment struck her. A lover masculine might demand explanation, but a lover feminine could say nothing. To speak would bring shame and inward remorse. Nature brands such demonstration as rebellion, repaying it later with the thunderbolt of self-contempt. The wise course is to endure. You expected bread and got a stone; break your teeth on it and do not shriek. You held out for an egg and received a scorpion; close your fingers firmly upon the gift and let it sting. In time, the scorpion dies, and you learn to endure without a sob.

Half-bitterness is strength; it is a tonic. Sweet force following acute suffering is a delusion. Energy remaining after the rack is dangerous, deadly when confronted with injustice.

Caroline recalled the ballad of "Puir Mary Lee," the peasant girl driven from her home by a false lover. Mary sits in the snowstorm, cursing Robin-a-Ree. But what of Caroline? Robert had done her no wrong; he had told her no lie. She was to blame for loving without being asked. Robert had seemed fond, but he was withdrawing because he did not wish his affections entangled in a marriage he deemed imprudent.

What was she to do? Pursue him and lose his esteem, or turn upon herself? If she had sense, she would be her own governor, resolving to subdue the disturbed realm of her emotions. She would look on life steadily, learning its severe truths.

It appeared she had sense. She quitted Robert quietly, without complaint or tear, and betook herself to her studies under Hortense. At dinner, she went home without lingering.

Later, in the rectory drawing-room, alone with her uncle drinking port, she faced a new difficulty: "How am I to get through this day?" She had hoped for another evening with Robert, but her mistake was learned. She could not settle down, convinced no chance would recall him.

He had visited twice before, treating her with peculiar reserve, yet looking at her relentingly. False Hope whispered he might come tonight. She tried to read, but her thoughts wandered. She tried to sew, finding every stitch tedious. She attempted a French composition and wrote only mistakes.

Suddenly, the door-bell rang sharply. Her heart leaped. She peered through the aperture. Fanny was admitting a visitor—a tall gentleman, just the height of Robert. For one second she thought it was him, exulting; but the voice asking for Mr. Helstone undeceived her. It was an Irish voice—Curate Malone.

It was a fact that at whatever house a curate dropped in, another soon followed. They were all on the run, chasing each other from lodging to lodging. Thus, Caroline's ears were tortured three times by the bell and the advent of undesired guests: Malone, followed by Donne, followed by Sweeting. More wine was ordered, and through the closed doors, Caroline heard their boyish laughter and vacant cackle. Her fear was lest they stay for tea, for she had no pleasure in serving that particular trio.

Fortune brought four more guests: Lady Sykes and her three buxom daughters, arriving in a pony-phaeton from Whinbury. The bell clanged a fourth time. Fanny announced, "Mrs. Sykes and the three Misses Sykes."

When receiving company, Caroline's habit was to wring her hands nervously, flush, and hurry forward, wishing herself at Jericho. She was deficient in finished manner. Accordingly, her small white hands maltreated each other as she waited.

In stalked Mrs. Sykes, a tall, bilious gentlewoman professing ample piety. In sailed her three daughters, a showy trio. In English country ladies, there is a certain expression stamped on the features that says, "I know I am the standard of what is proper; let everyone therefore whom I approach keep a sharp lookout, for wherein they differ from me, therein they are wrong."

Mrs. and Misses Sykes were pointed illustrations of this truth. Miss Mary wore her complacency with state; Miss Harriet carried it overbearingly, looking high and cold; Miss Hannah, conceited and dashing, flourished hers openly. The mother evinced it with the gravity proper to her age.

The reception was got through somehow. Caroline lied, saying she was glad to see them, hoping Mrs. Sykes's cough was better (it had lasted twenty years), and hoping the Misses Sykes had left their sisters well. The Misses Sykes replied with a simultaneous, majestic bow that ensured silence for the next five minutes.

Mrs. Sykes inquired after Mr. Helstone's rheumatism and preaching fatigue. On being assured he was well, they chorused that he was a "wonderful man of his years."
