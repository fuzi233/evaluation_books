Harry Warrington felt sincere, hot remorse for his past follies and missteps the moment he found himself locked in that grim, cobwebbed Cursitor Street lock-up house, tangled up in equal parts rage and humiliation at the vulgar indignity of being arrested like some common debtor. Still, he assured himself the unpleasantness could not possibly last: he had at least twenty wealthy, loyal friends who would rush to spring him the second he sent word. The only question was, to whom should he appeal first?

There was Mr. Draper, the obsequious family man of business; his kind uncle Sir Miles, who had offered him a permanent home and loved him like a son; his cousin Castlewood, who had won such enormous sums from him at the card tables; his noble companions at the Chocolate-House; his sharp, warm-hearted old aunt Madame Bernstein. Any of them would surely come to his aid, he was certain, though the more prying relatives might dole out a well-deserved scolding for his imprudence. Most of all, Harry was desperate to keep the affair quiet: he could not bear the thought of the ton knowing a gentleman of his standing had been subjected to such a low, common process as arrest.

As if the world does not eventually learn every trivial thing it cares to pry out! As if the dinner I will eat tonight, or the hole in the stocking I am wearing as I write these words, could possibly stay a secret from some nosy enemy who has a mind to dig it up—even with my boots laced tight and my door fully locked when I dressed! I mention that hole in the stocking only as an example. There is no private detail of our lives the curious public cannot root out, if it cares to look. But there is a consolation, stubbornly rejected by every man caught in his own moment of shame, that the world simply does not care. Think of the centuries of scandal the public has been forced to sit through! It is jaded, blasé, utterly tired of that sort of gossip. Are you dragged off to prison and convinced you are irrevocably disgraced? Do you go bankrupt under suspicious circumstances? Do you get caught cheating a friend out of money and imagine all of society will turn its back on you? Psha! Your shame is nothing but vanity. Brush the mud off your coat, smile as if nothing happened, walk back into society as normal, and no one will bat an eye. Do you imagine the whole world will pull out its handkerchief and weep inconsolably when you die? Why on earth would it care whether you disgrace or distinguish yourself in life? The world will talk, dine, joke, yawn, and move on, just as it always has. Do not be so conceited as to imagine your private little affairs matter half as much as you think they do, my boy.

But Harry Warrington chafed and paced his cell as if every soul in London was already whispering about the heavy hand laid on his fine, noble shoulder.
“What a fine sensation my arrest must have made at the club!” he thought, glowering. “That man Selwyn will no doubt crack a dozen cruel jokes at my expense, plague take him! Everyone around the card table will have heard the news. March will be panicking over the bet I have with him, and faith, I shan’t be able to pay it if I lose, now. They’ll all hoot and cheer that the so-called Savage from Virginia has finally been clapped in irons. How am I ever to show my face in public again?”

After a moment’s thought, half sharp, half naive, he decided: he would not send word first to any of his relations, or any of his grand friends at White’s. He would call Sampson, the chaplain. The man had landed in exactly this kind of scrape a dozen times before, he would know exactly what to do. At the first faint streak of dawn—Harry was convinced the sun had deliberately forgotten to make the turn down Cursitor Street that morning, the hours dragging so slow they felt like days—he sent a messenger to Long Acre, begging the reverend gentleman to come at once to give him advice and comfort.

Harry had no idea that summoning Sampson to his side was the same as leaning over the edge of a lion pit and yelling down to your friend to come join you. He probably assumed Sampson’s own troubles were long behind him, or more likely, he was so wrapped up in his own distress he barely spared a thought for anyone else’s. Once the messenger was gone, he relaxed a little, and asked for breakfast. The attendant who brought him his tea and bread asked if he wanted to order dinner, or eat at Mrs. Bailiff’s common table with the other resident gentlemen.
“No,” Harry told him loftily. “I will be long gone from this place before dinner is served.” The turnkey smirked, having heard that exact line from a hundred panicked young gentlemen before. “Well, sir, if you do end up staying, there’s fine beef and carrots on the table at two o’clock,” he said, and shut the door on Harry and his brooding thoughts.

Ten o’clock came, then eleven, then noon, and still no sign of Sampson. Instead, around midday, Gumbo arrived, toting a portmanteau of his master’s fine clothes, and flung himself roaring and sobbing on the floor at Harry’s feet. He swore a thousand oaths of loyalty, declaring he would die for his master, sell himself back into slavery all over again, do anything at all to get Mr. Harry out of this terrible place. Harry was moved by the boy’s obvious affection, and told him to get up off his knees, where he was grovelling and clinging to Harry’s legs.
“All you need do now, lad, is hand me my clothes to dress, and hold your tongue about this whole business,” Harry said sternly. “Not a single word to anyone, you understand?”

“Oh no, sir! Never to nobody!” Gumbo vowed, his face deadly solemn, as he fussed over his master, dusting powder into his hair and dressing him in his finest silk coat and lace cravat, until Harry looked as polished and splendid as if he were just stepping into his sedan chair to attend a reception at St. James’s.

All the love and servility Gumbo felt for his beloved master could do nothing to undo what had already been done, though. He was utterly incapable of keeping such a juicy, dramatic secret. The language of the enslaved—black slaves, and white domestic slaves alike—is all lies. The oppressed creature slips away into half-truths and evasions like a hunted fox darting for its burrow the moment it spots its pursuer, the tyrant master. These strange, leftover relics of feudalism run through every part of our old social life. Our servants, who are men and brothers just as we are, are all forced to act as hypocrites in front of us. They never speak naturally, or tell the full unvarnished truth. We would fly into a rage, call them impudent, and kick them out of doors if they ever did.

The truth was, Gumbo’s tongue had been wagging nonstop since the moment he found out his master was arrested. He had already shared the woeful news with dozens of his fellow footmen, bragging, embellishing, and gasping out the story at the footmen’s club frequented by the staff of half the leading noble houses of London. He had stopped for a mug of ale in Sir Miles Warrington’s butler’s quarters and told the whole tale twice over. He had run to Madame Bernstein’s household to grieve with the servants there, and then trotted over to Lord Castlewood’s staff to spread the word further. When he laid his hand on his heart and swore he had told no one of his master’s misfortune, everyone who knew him could easily gauge exactly how much truth that statement held.

At last, shortly after noon, a letter arrived, not from Sampson in person, but scribbled in his messy hand, sealed with a still-damp wafer, the ink barely dry. The unlucky chaplain wrote:
“Oh sir, dear sir, I have done everything a man possibly can for my dearest patron! You did not know what peril you were drawing me into, or else if I was to go to prison, I would have happily shared yours, instead of being locked in a lock-up house three doors away from you this instant.
It is true! As I was hurrying to your side, ready to face any danger for the kindest benefactor I have ever known, two villains with a writ against me seized me, and dragged me to Naboth’s jail, so close to your rooms that we could almost shout to each other across our back garden walls.
I had so much important advice to give you that I dare not commit to paper. I hope your fortunes mend soon. I pray my own cursed luck turns around too,
Your honour’s afflicted Chaplain-in-Ordinary, J. S.”

Since Sampson refused to write down the full story, we may as well fill in the gaps he left. Gumbo’s gossip had not yet reached Long Acre the night before, so Sampson knew nothing of Harry’s arrest until his messenger arrived that morning. Burning with gratitude for the favour Harry had done him only days before, he ran straight to Lord Castlewood’s house to beg for his help: he knew full well how enormous a sum Castlewood had won from his cousin at cards, and was sure the man would not let his own kinsman rot in jail for lack of a few pounds. His passionate, sincere speech of appeal fell on entirely deaf ears.

Castlewood cut him off sharply. “Did I not tell you two days ago, when you came begging me for money, that I am as poor as any beggar on the street?” he snapped. “No one has left me a fortune since then! Every penny I won from my cousin was swallowed up by my own creditors. Not only can I not help Mr. Warrington, I swear to you I had no idea he was in trouble, and I wrote a letter to him this very morning begging him to lend me money.” That exact letter would arrive at Harry’s lodgings a few hours later, sent via penny post.
“I must get him money, my lord!” Sampson cried. “He barely had a shilling left in his pocket after he bailed me out. I will pawn my cassock and my clerical bands before I let him stay in there!”
“Amen to that,” Castlewood said, not looking up from his newspaper. “Go pawn your bands, your cassock, anything you like. Your enthusiasm does you great credit.”

Lady Maria had heard Sampson talking to her brother, and guessed exactly what the conversation was about. She caught the chaplain the moment he stormed out of the study, and dragged him into the dining room, her face streaked with real tears of sympathy. “Tell me what has happened to Mr. Warrington?” she asked.
“Your ladyship already knows?” Sampson exclaimed, shocked.
“Have I not been in mortal terror ever since his servant brought us the dreadful news last night?” Maria said. “We heard it coming home from the opera, in Lady Yarmouth’s box—my lord, my sister-in-law, and I.”
“So his lordship knew all along?” Sampson said, his temper snapping. He swore out loud, and spilled the whole story of how Castlewood had won thousands off Harry at cards, then refused to lift a finger to help him, and raved about Harry’s extraordinary generosity to him, the chaplain.

Maria then burst into a furious, unsparing tirade against her own family, the kind of unvarnished truths warring relatives will spill about one another with no filter. She shrieked curses, ranted about every single member of the Esmond clan, and then charged straight for her brother’s study door, screaming so loud the alarmed Sampson fled out into the street to avoid the coming scene.

Castlewood looked up from his paper, surprised at his sister’s red-faced fury, and threw out a sharp oath to try and stop her onslaught. But Maria, normally timid around her brother, was far too riled up to be frightened now.
“So, my lord!” she yelled. “You sit with that poor boy in private at cards, you fleece him dry! You take every last shilling he has, and you won’t even lend him a single guinea of his own winnings now he’s penniless and in jail!”
“That damned chaplain has been running his mouth, has he?” Castlewood said.
“Dismiss him! Pay his wages and send him off, he’ll be glad enough to go!” Maria shouted.
“I keep him around to marry one of my sisters off if we ever need it,” Castlewood said, glowering at her.
“What sort of women could we ever be, when our men are all as vile as you?” Maria cried.
“Very true,” Castlewood said, shrugging.
“We are bad enough, but you? You have no courage, no honour, no basic human decency! Your own equals refuse to play cards with you any more, so you go after this innocent boy from Virginia, your own cousin, and rob him blind! It’s a shame! A disgrace!”
“Everyone plays their own game in this world, don’t they?” Castlewood said. “Didn’t you play your own game and win, Maria? Where did this sudden squeamishness about the poor Virginia lad come from? Did Harry break off your arrangement, or did your ladyship land a better offer? If you won’t take him, I know the old Methodist aunt in Hill Street will give him a choice of her two granddaughters, mark my words. Are you a fool, Maria Esmond? Even more of a fool than usual?”
“I am a fool to expect any of my brothers could act like an honest man!” Maria snapped back. “A fool to think you would ever help a relative in distress, or pass up the chance to fleece any easy victim you find.”
“Fleece him? Psha! What nonsense are you talking? You saw what sort of life that lad was leading for months past. Somebody would have stripped him of every penny, sooner or later. If I hadn’t won his money, some other gambler at White’s would have. And I never stopped you from chasing your little plan for him, did I? Why are you throwing a fit now, just because I reached out and took the money he was throwing at every man he met? Don’t you see this whole mess is the best possible thing that could have happened for you? You were chasing a whole principality out in Virginia, weren’t you? A little misfortune like a stay in jail will make that boy far more likely to cling to the woman who bails him out, and far less likely to run off chasing some pretty 18 year old girl. That silly promise of marriage he all but gave you a Tunbridge Wells? He won’t dare back out of it now. You were raging like a lion a minute ago, and now you might just end up a princess.”

Maria, who had charged at her brother like a wild lioness, settled down meekly at his feet to listen to the rest of his scheme.

Over on Clarges Street, Madame de Bernstein had been furious when Gumbo brought her the news of Harry’s arrest the night before. She could not sleep a wink for thinking of him, and none of her household staff dared face her bad temper. She knew very well arrest was a common enough event in London society, but she fretted over how deep her favourite nephew had gotten himself in debt. He was the only person in decades that cold, tired old woman had ever felt genuine affection for, and she was ready to help him, as long as he had not landed himself in truly irreversible trouble. She sent a messenger at dawn to her lawyer Mr. Draper, ordering him to find out exactly what writs were holding Harry, and how much money was owed.

Draper’s men found Harry locked up only a few streets away. The only debts out against him that day were Mr. Sparks the jeweller’s bill for all the fancy presents he had bought, and his Bond Street lodging house bill. Draper pointed out that paying them off would be no trouble at all, and Harry could be out by midday, back home to dress and come dine with Madame Bernstein by two o’clock, ready to show his face at the clubs and laugh off the whole mess. But the Baroness shook her head.
“My good Mr. Draper,” she said, “that boy has sown far too many wild oats already. I want him to come out of prison when he is fully cured of his folly, with no lingering hidden debts hanging over his head. You know no gentleman ever tells anyone the full list of his debts, not even his own lawyer. Let him stay there a day or two longer. It will do him more good than any scolding I could ever give him.”
