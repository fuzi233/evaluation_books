Wrapped entirely in the thunderous echo of Savonarola’s voice, Baldassarre did not notice the stranger slip through the great cathedral doorway behind him, or pause a few paces off to watch him. It was Piero di Cosimo, the painter, who had not come for the sermon at all—he had ventured into the Duomo purely to hunt down word of the escaped prisoner the city’s gossips had been chattering about for days. When the preaching fell to a hush, and thousands of listeners surrendered to a wordless, tear-choked swell of shared emotion, Piero stepped forward and tapped Baldassarre lightly on the bound arm.

Baldassarre turned, tears still rolling slow down the deep furrows of his cheeks, and huffed out a sharp, vigorous sigh as if shaking off a heavy, half-dreamed trance. The painter spoke low, so no one else might overhear:
“Shall I cut those cords for you? I heard how the French troops made you a captive.”
Baldassarre stared back suspiciously at this uninvited, helpful stranger, held his silence for a long beat, then said at last, “If you will.”
“Better step outside,” Piero murmured. “The crowd is too thick here.”
Baldassarre’s suspicion lingered, plain in his narrowed gaze, so Piero grinned, pulled a small sharp knife from his belt, and sliced clean through the frayed rawhide bonds. He found himself thinking the rumours of the old man’s partial madness were not far-fetched—there was something wild, unmoored, in the lines of his face. “Well,” he reasoned to himself, “if he does any mischief, they’ll tie him up again soon enough. The poor devil deserves at least that small chance.”
“You are wary of me,” he said again, quiet, as Baldassarre stretched his newly freed arms, relishing the long-forbidden feeling of unbound muscle. “You won’t tell me anything of who you are.”
Baldassarre’s look softened, steadied, no longer edged with distrust. “No. I have nothing to tell.”
“Just as you please,” Piero shrugged. “But I’d guess you’re in want of shelter, and may not know how kindly we Florentines treat ragged travellers with empty bellies and torn doublets. There is a hospital for poor pilgrims outside every one of our city gates. I could lead you straight to one. You’ve no need to fear the French soldier who took you, either—he was marched out of Florence at dawn yesterday.”
Baldassarre nodded, silent in acceptance, and the two men left the cathedral together.
As they tramped down the Via dell’Oriuolo toward the Porta Santa Croce, Piero added, “I am a painter, you see. Would you sit for me to paint your portrait? I would pay you coin, enough to keep you fed and warm for a week.”
Suspicion flared sharp back in Baldassarre’s eyes. He met Piero’s gaze and said firmly, “No.”
“Ah,” the painter said, curt, no offence taken. “Go straight ahead, and you will come to the Porta Santa Croce. The pilgrim hospital is just beyond its walls. You will take no other service from me, then?”
“I give you thanks for what you have already done,” Baldassarre said. “I need no more.”
“Very well,” Piero shrugged, and the two parted ways.
“A mysterious old tiger!” the artist thought, turning back toward the Duomo square. “Well worth putting to canvas. Ugly, lines carved deep as if plough and harrow have passed straight over his heart. What a perfect contrast to my soft, smiling Messer Greco—my *Bacco trionfante*, who has married the fair Antigone against all history and good sense. Aha, that pretty scholar’s blood ran cold at the old fellow’s grip on the steps the other night, didn’t it?”

When Piero stepped back into the Piazza del Duomo, the entire multitude that had come to hear Fra Girolamo was pouring out of every cathedral door, hurrying off to their shops, their mills, their market stalls at a run—proof of how completely the sermon had detained them from all their daily business. The artist leaned against the smooth stone corner of the Baptistery and watched the departing crowd, giddy with delight at the riot of colours in their garb, the sharp, distinct faces: faces exactly like the ones Masaccio had painted more than fifty years prior, the very faces Domenico Ghirlandajo still set into his frescoes today.

This morning was no ordinary occasion. The Frate’s audience, always a perfect cross-section of Florentine life, represented the city’s classes and political factions even more completely than usual. There were men of old noble birth, long accustomed to holding public office at home and representing Florence abroad, who had newly risen to prominence not only as enemies of the Medici and champions of republican rule, but as fervent Piagnoni who embraced every word of the Frate’s teaching and treated San Marco monastery as the seat of a new prophet Samuel. Some were men of commanding, handsome presence, like Francesco Valori, whose hot, arrogant temper was secretly gratified to have immediate divine authority to hand for bringing about freedom in exactly the way he saw fit. Others, like Soderini, had far less of the fiery Piagnone about them, and far more of the shrewd, calculating politician. There was Piero Capponi, plain, brave, no taste for fine doctrinal debate, a man who loved sober republican liberty more than words, who would always rather draw a sword than argue, and who had no quarrel with any idea that kept the Medici out and made space for public spirit.
At their heels were doctors of law, men who had pored over the tomes of Accursius and his fellow jurists for decades, but whose ardour had not been entirely consumed by study: Messer Luca Corsini himself, for example, who would soon raise his learned fists to hurl stones in street fights for the cause of religion, freedom, and the Frate. Sprinkled among these dignitaries, who carried their black lucco robes and furred mantles with the easy air of habitual authority, were crowds of more contemplative, sensitive faces: scholars bearing the great old names of Strozzi and Acciajoli, who had already made up their minds to take the cowl and join the community at San Marco. There were artists, fired to new, higher ambitions by Savonarola’s teachings, like the young painter who had lately surpassed himself in a fresco of the divine Child on the wall of the Frate’s bare cell—unaware that one day he would take the tonsure and the cowl himself, and be known as Fra Bartolommeo. There was the mystic poet Girolamo Benevieni, hurrying no doubt to carry word of the beloved Frate’s latest sermon to his friend Pico della Mirandola, who would never see the light of another morning. There were noblewomen dressed in scrupulous, almost excessive plainness, their refined grace the only mark that set them apart from their working-class sisters.
The great bulk of the crowd was made up of the genuine popolani, the middle classes of both the Major and Minor Arts, all acutely aware their purses were being gnawed thin by war taxes. More striking and varied than all the rest of the Frate’s disciples was the long stream of poorer tradesmen and artisans, whose faith in his divine message ranged from the simple, unthinking trust of the unlettered labourer in the Frate as the friend of the poor and the enemy of greedy oppressive nobles, to the eager hunger for every subtlety of biblical interpretation that takes such a strong hold on the sedentary artisan, illuminating the long dim hours at his workbench with a pale glow that feels to him like the very light of divine science.

But scattered through these ranks of the faithful were many who were no disciples of the Frate at all. Some were Medicean loyalists who had already, out of fear and political calculation, begun to pay feigned deference to the presiding spirit of the popular party. Others were sincere advocates of free government, who regarded Savonarola simply as an ambitious monk—half sagacious, half fanatical—who had made himself a powerful instrument with the people, and must be accepted as an unavoidable fact of social life. There were even some of his bitterest enemies: members of the old aristocratic anti-Medicean party, determined to seize the reins of power once more for their own leading families; or licentious young men who detested him as the great killjoy of Florence, who had turned all their favourite feasts, hunts, and revels into sin. The sermons in the Duomo had long since become political incidents, drawing the ears of curiosity and malice as much as of faith. The men of ideas, like young Niccolò Machiavelli, came to observe and write detailed reports for friends staying in distant country villas. The men of appetites, like Dolfo Spini, who was bent on hunting the Frate down as a public nuisance that had spoiled all his sport, came to feed their hatred and lie in wait for any slip that could be turned into a charge against him.

No preacher ever held a more massive influence than Savonarola, and no preacher ever had more heterogeneous, conflicting human material to work upon. Baldassarre, wrought to an ecstasy of self-martyring revenge by his words, was only an extreme case of the partial, narrow, personal sympathies that ran through every corner of that audience. In Savonarola’s preaching there were strains that appealed to the very finest susceptibilities of human nature, and elements that gratified low egoism, tickled gossiping curiosity, and fascinated timorous superstition. His hunger for personal predominance, his labyrinthine allegorical readings of Scripture, his enigmatic visions, his absolute false certitude about the Divine intentions, never ceased, in his own large soul, to be ennobled by that fervid piety, that passionate sense of the infinite, that active sympathy, that clear-sighted demand for the subjection of selfish interests to the common good, which he shared with all the greatest of mankind. But for the mass of his audience, all the power of his preaching lay in his bold assertion of supernatural claims, his denunciatory visions, and that false certitude that made every sermon feel as urgent and dramatic as a breaking political bulletin. Once he had that audience in his mastery, it was necessary to his nature—and necessary, he believed, for their welfare—that he keep that mastery.
The effect was inevitable. No man ever struggled to retain power over a mixed multitude without suffering some measure of vitiation; his standard must bend to their lower needs, not stay fixed to his own highest insight. The mysteries of human character have seldom been presented in a way more fitted to check the judgments of facile, quick knowingness than in Girolamo Savonarola. But we can give him a reverence that needs no shutting of the eyes to fact, if we regard his life as a great drama in which deep inward modifications accompanied every outward change. Up to this point, when his more direct action on political affairs had only just begun, it is probable his imperious need for ascendancy burned still completely hidden, dissolved in the great bright flame of his zeal for God and man. It was the custom of old, when an ox was led out for sacrifice to Jupiter, to chalk over all its dark spots and give the offering a false show of unblemished whiteness. Let us fling the chalk away, and say boldly: the victim is spotted, but it is not therefore in vain that his mighty heart is laid on the altar of mankind’s highest hopes.
