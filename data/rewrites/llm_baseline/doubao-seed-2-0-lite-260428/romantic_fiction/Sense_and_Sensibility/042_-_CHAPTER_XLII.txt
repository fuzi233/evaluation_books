Their final brief stop in Harley Street passed uneventfully: Elinor accepted her brother John’s over-cheerful congratulations that the sisters had made such good progress toward Barton without spending a penny of their own money, as well as his news that Colonel Brandon would be following them to Cleveland in a day or two’s time. That last visit drew their town-based family interactions to a close entirely. Fanny offered a faint, half-hearted invitation for them to stop by Norland any time they happened to be passing by—an event more unlikely than any other fate that could befall them, as both parties well knew—while John took Elinor aside to make a warmer, quieter promise that he would ride out to visit her the very second she was settled at Delaford. Elinor could not help but smile at the odd collective joke their social circle had fallen into: everyone seemed determined to cast Delaford as her inevitable future home. It was the very last place on earth she wished to live or even visit, and the absurdity of it felt all the sharper when Lucy, too, pressed her for a visit to the Delaford parsonage the moment the two of them parted in London.

The first days of April had barely broken pale dawn when two carriages set out from Hanover Square and Berkeley Street, prearranged to meet at a designated stretch of the high road. They would take two full days to make the journey, to spare Charlotte Palmer and her small baby too much jostling, while Mr. Palmer and Colonel Brandon rode ahead at a faster pace, planning to join the rest of the party at Cleveland shortly after their arrival. Marianne, who had ached for weeks to escape London, found she could not bring herself to leave without sobbing, once the final moment came. This was the very house where she had last let herself believe Willoughby loved her, where every bright, giddy, private plan they had woven together had lived before it burned to ash forever. To drive away from a city where he still walked its streets, chasing new acquaintances and new schemes that she would never be part of, felt like a small second goodbye to the ghost of the life she had almost had.

Elinor’s relief, by contrast, was unshakable. There was no one left in London she would miss, no more awkward forced displays of Lucy’s false friendship to endure, no risk of Marianne crossing paths with Willoughby on some accidental street stroll while he paraded his new wife. She looked ahead with quiet, soft hope to the quiet of Barton, where the slow, gentle air would stitch Marianne’s frayed peace of mind back together, and let her own quiet, tender hopes for the future settle steady.

The journey passed without a single mishap. On the second day, they crossed the border into Somerset, the county Marianne’s imagination had flipped between reverent longing and sharp, painful avoidance for months, and by mid-morning of the third day, their carriage rolled up the drive to Cleveland. The large, modern home sat nestled on a sloping green lawn, its sprawling pleasure grounds dotted with winding gravel paths, old fir trees, blooming mountain ashes, and drooping acacias that formed a thick, leafy screen to hide the service buildings from view. No grand formal park surrounded it, but the grounds stretched for acres, shrubberies newly green with spring leaves, wooded walks shaded and quiet.

Marianne’s chest tightened the second she stepped over the threshold. They were only eighty miles from Barton, and barely thirty miles from Combe Magna, Willoughby’s estate. Before any of her companions noticed she had slipped away—while the rest crowded around Charlotte to show her baby off to the housekeeper—she stole out through the winding, budding shrubberies, climbing all the way to the Grecian temple perched on the highest rise of the property. She leaned against its marble rail, staring out over the rolling green hills stretching southeast until her eyes burned, convinced if she squinted hard enough she could just catch a glint of Combe Magna’s roofs far off on the farthest ridge of the horizon. That sharp, precious ache, the misery that felt almost like joy, wrapped around her as she wept. When she wandered back to the house by a different path, grass damp under her boots, she resolved to spend every spare hour of her stay wandering these grounds alone, no one to pity her, no one to pester her to distract herself from the soft, lingering grief she cherished.

She returned just in time to join the rest of the party as they set out to explore the immediate grounds. The rest of the morning drifted slow and soft: they wandered the walled kitchen garden, admiring the pink buds on the fruit trees while the gardener grumbled about late spring blights, dawdled through the greenhouse where Charlotte laughed at Marianne’s pout when she spotted her favorite potted flowers nipped black by lingering frost, and visited the poultry yard to listen to the dairy maid bemoan hens abandoning their nests, foxes snatching chicks, and the entire promising new brood of vanishing. Marianne had planned nothing but long, sunlit walks for her entire stay, so she was completely caught off guard when a heavy, steady rain began pouring the second they finished dinner. She had been counting on a twilight walk back to the Grecian temple, and even a light damp chill would not have deterred her, but the rain fell so thick she could barely see ten feet from the front door.

The small party settled in for a quiet evening. Mrs. Palmer doted on her baby, Mrs. Jennings worked at her carpet stitching, they chattered about the friends they had left behind in London, sorted through Lady Middleton’s upcoming social engagements, and wondered if Mr. Palmer and Colonel Brandon had even made it as far as Reading that night. Elinor joined their conversation gently, while Marianne, as was her habit, quickly tracked down the tucked-away library no one else in the Palmer family ever used, and disappeared into the pages of a book. Charlotte Palmer was the warmest of hosts, open and unreserved, if a little scatterbrained and short on formal polite manners—her sweet, pretty face and genuine kindness made every small flaw endearing, her folly never cruel or conceited, though Elinor could not help but find her constant, bubbling laughter a faint, faint irritant.

The two gentlemen arrived late the following day, just in time for a very delayed dinner, and their arrival breathed new life into the party after an entire rain-soaked morning that had left them nearly out of things to discuss. Elinor had never been sure what to make of Mr. Palmer before, but in his own home he proved perfectly courteous to all his guests, only occasionally dry and sarcastic to his wife and mother-in-law, and capable of being perfectly pleasant company. He was only ever unkind when he put on airs of being far more clever and refined than everyone around him, fussy about his meals, inconsistent with his hours, pretending to disdain his baby while doting on it in private, and wasting half every morning at billiards instead of tending to his estates. All in all, Elinor liked him far better than she had expected, but she found she did not mind liking him no more than that: his selfishness, vanity, and love of luxury only made her think all the more fondly of Edward Ferrars, with his generous heart, simple tastes, and soft, quiet shyness.

Colonel Brandon, who had just returned from a trip to Dorsetshire, soon pulled Elinor aside to speak of Edward. He treated her like an old, trusted confidante, describing the small Delaford parsonage in great detail, listing all its flaws, and laying out the repairs he planned to oversee himself before Edward took up the post. He lit up at the sight of Elinor, even after only ten days apart, hung on every word she said, and deferred to her every opinion so openly that Mrs. Jennings’s long-held conviction that he was hopelessly in love with Elinor almost felt plausible. But Elinor knew better: she watched his face when Marianne complained of a scratchy throat and aching head, the faint, unspoken flash of alarm that no one else noticed, the way he tensed like he would run for a doctor before anyone else had even registered she was unwell. It was Marianne he loved, quiet and loyal as always, and Elinor smiled to herself that she was the only one who could see it.

On the third and fourth evenings of their stay, the rain cleared, leaving the air soft and cool at twilight. Marianne went out for long, wandering walks, not just on the dry gravel paths, but all the way out to the wildest, oldest parts of the grounds, where the grass grew long and soaked from the earlier downpour, the ancient trees twisting their branches overhead like a shaded canopy. She stayed out long after the sun set, tramping around in wet shoes and stockings, and by the time she came back, she was burning up with a terrible cold. For a day or two she brushed it off, insisting a single good night’s rest would cure her completely, but soon her fever spiked, her cough grew harsh, and her limbs ached too badly for her to deny how unwell she was. Everyone offered their favorite home remedies, all of which she stubbornly turned down, until Elinor gently persuaded her to take a single, mild, simple remedy before she finally climbed into bed for the night.
