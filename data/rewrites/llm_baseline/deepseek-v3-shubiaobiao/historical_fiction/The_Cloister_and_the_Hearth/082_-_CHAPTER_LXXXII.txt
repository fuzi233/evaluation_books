At this, Margaret’s tears fell freely. Luke whimpered in sympathy—a man’s frame housing a girl’s tender heart.

*"Prithee, weep not so, sweet mistress,"* he pleaded. *"I’d bring him back to life if I could, rather than see thee grieve so sore."*

Margaret wiped her eyes, her sorrow laced with bitterness. *"I weep not for his death, but for their doubled tongues."* Then, steadying herself, she laid a hand upon his shoulder and spoke with solemn certainty. *"Luke, he is not dead. The dying see clearer than the living. Listen—my father, in his last hour, when I, fool that I was, thought him healing, sat in that very chair and cried, ‘I see him! I see him!’—not like a failing man, but aflame. ‘Sore disfigured—on a great river—coming this way.’"*

She clutched Luke’s arm. *"If you had a woman’s heart, you’d pity me and find him. The father of my child!"*

*"Alack, I would if I knew how,"* Luke sighed. *"But how can I?"*

Luke pondered long, his mind slow but earnest. *"A great river… The Maas is wide, but had he sailed it, he’d be here by now. The Rhine, then—greater still, and long."*

*"Aye,"* Margaret agreed, hope flickering. *"Denys bade him come down the Rhine. Yet what if he turns aside before reaching home? What if he pines not for me as I for him?"* She searched Luke’s face, desperate for denial.

Instead, he said bluntly, *"It looks very like it. What a fool he must be!"*

*"What do we know?"* she protested.

Luke frowned. *"Let me think. My mind does not gallop."* At last, he struck upon a plan: he would ride to a station sixty miles up the Rhine, where all boats docked, and there intercept Gerard. Yet knowing neither face nor form, he would need proof.

*"Give me a letter for him,"* he urged. *"Else he may think me a liar."*

Margaret flushed. *"Good, kind Luke! How I’ve wronged thee!"* By supper, she pressed into his hands a long letter, bound with silk and sealed.

Luke weighed it, then spoke gravely. *"Say your father’s vision was true. Say I find this man, and he proves worthier than I think him—and comes home to thee. What then becomes of me?"*

Margaret’s cheeks burned. *"Heaven will reward thee. I shall kneel and bless thee all my days, love thee as a mother loves her son. I am aged by sorrow—thou shouldst not go. ’Tis selfish."*

*"Humph!"* Luke grunted. *"And if he reads thy letter, hears how thou pinest—and yet, traitor or fool, turns not home—what then? Must I die unwed, and thou lonely to thy grave?"*

Margaret trembled. *"If—God forbid!—he refuses… then he will return my marriage lines. I shall be honest, yet wretched. My boy no bastard. And then…"* She faltered. *"I could enter no man’s house but thine."*

*"Ay, but will you?"* Luke pressed.

*"Ask me not! Perhaps… when my wounds cease bleeding. Or else—"* Her voice broke. *"The Maas may take me. Luke, do not go! ’Tis madness!"*

But Luke, slow to conceive a thought, was slower to abandon one. At dawn, he departed, Margaret’s last gold angel in his purse.

Twelve hours later, two friars entered Holland from the south. Jerome, unable to preach in the Low Dutch tongue, pressed toward the coast, eager for England. Fate, however, wove another thread.

In a riverside village, Clement preached—and noticed a grey nun weeping.

*"Not for myself,"* she confessed, *"but for my lost sister. Lured from her vows, she now lives in sin at an inn, a decoy for men’s lusts. We dare not seize her—the baron’s servant owns the place, and would burn our convent."*

*"Souls are not saved by force,"* Clement mused. He persuaded Jerome to lodge at the convent while he attempted redemption.

Disguised as a horseman, Clement entered the inn, heart quaking but resolve steeled. He lured the fallen nun, Mary, with gold, then revealed himself—a Dominican crucifix in hand.

*"Dare you rob the Virgin?"* she had cried, recoiling.

*"You are no robber,"* he countered, *"or you would not care. Mary, your saint sends me!"*

Terror and remorse seized her. He spoke of the convent’s grief, of Ursula’s tears, of angels weeping. *"Sin is not sweet, nor holiness bitter. Eternity knocks."*

Trembling, she yielded. *"I would return—but how face them?"*

He bade her trample her jewels—*"the wages of sin"*—then led her forth. The landlord barred their path, but Clement, in righteous fury, hurled him aside. *"Touch her, and your soul shall roast!"*

Mary faltered at the convent gate, but Ursula flung it open, weeping. The abbess, though stern, embraced her. Clement, his task done, strode toward the Rhine—and toward the boy who waited with Margaret’s letter.

The letter was tender, aching—a woman’s plea to one who had left her in silence. She spoke of their son, his tiny hands, the mole on his finger. *"Know you any with such a mark? The mothers envy me—but I envy wives, for thou comest not."*

Some claimed him dead. *"But if thou wert dead, how could I live?"* Others called him false. *"This I’ll believe from no lips but thine."* She recalled her father’s dying vision—Gerard on a river, disfigured. *"Is it so? Hast thou lost a limb? Then thou need’st me more, not less."*

No reproach marred her words—only love, raw and unyielding.

Luke, bearing this letter, mingled with boatmen, seeking Gerard Eliassoen. At last, a black friar eyed him sharply.

Luke’s heart cracked. *"Why did the Lord make women?"* he muttered, dashing the letter down—then snatching it up again. He begged labor from the innkeeper, not for coin, but to keep his hands busy and his heart from breaking.

And so he waited, coopering barrels in fury, as the boats drifted down the Rhine.
