The two young men, seated in the quiet intimacy of their reunion, had spent hours dissecting the landscapes of their separate homes. They traded the minutiae of their histories since their last parting—the condition of Harry's dogs, the growth of little Dempster, the health of old Nathan, and the blossoming beauty of Fanny. News flowed like water between them, carrying tidings of Savannah and Georgia, where Parson Broadbent's daughter was betrothed to Tom Barker. Harry confessed with a flush of shame how he had once been deeply infatuated with Miss Broadbent, how he squandered his pocket-money at cards and drowned his sorrows in strong waters alongside her father, solely to secure a pretext for her company. Yet, heaven forbid, he admitted, Madam Esmond would never have sanctioned such a union; her daughter was far beneath the station of the Colonel's wife.

Colonel George Washington's wife, however, was a different matter entirely—a woman of considerable fortune, good nature, and undeniable charm. George recounted the scene with humorous relish, describing the "ceremonial" of his mother's introduction to the young American lady. He painted the picture of his mother's terrifying politeness, a veneer of civility stretched thin over a chasm of disdain. "Never mind, George, my dear!" Mrs. Mountain had laughed, her matchmaking spirit undeterred by the failure of this particular alliance. "The Colonel has taken another wife, but I feel certain that at one time two young gentlemen I know of ran a very near chance of having a tall stepfather six feet two in his boots." Mrs. Mountain was, indeed, the architect of many rumors; it was she who had spread the tale of the "poor Indian woman," a slander Madam Esmond had greeted with icy scorn. When Parson Stack had delivered the gossip, the Colonel's wife had retorted that she would sooner suspect Mr. Esmond of stealing silverware or marrying a negro woman from the kitchen. Though she treated the unfortunate Biche with a chilling hauteur that drove the Indian girl to drown her disappointments in gin among the servants, Madam Esmond could not bring herself to condemn her openly, offering a perfunctory thanks for her attendance during her son's illness.

Yet, despite the scandal surrounding the mother's housekeeping and the father's debts, the core of the conversation turned inward, to the brothers themselves. George sighed, the weight of his exile evident in his tone. "The birthright is mine, Harry," he said, "but thou art the favourite, and God help me! I think my mother almost grudges it to me. Why should I have taken the pass, and preceded your worship into the world? Had you been the elder, you would have possessed the best cellar, ridden the finest nag, and been the most popular man in the country. Whereas I? I have not a word to say for myself, save to frighten folks with my glum visage." He lamented the inversion of natural order, cursing the fate that compelled him to correct what he perceived as a time "out of joint."

"Why, Georgy, you are talking verses, I protest you!" Harry exclaimed, laughing.

"I think, my dear, some one else talked those verses before me," George smiled, acknowledging the echo of poetry in his brother's jest.

"It's out of one of your books," Harry cried, his voice thick with emotion. "You know every book that ever was written, I do believe!" He recounted the thrill of seeing the two authors at Tunbridge, bowing low before them not out of admiration for their wit—which he modestly dismissed—but out of a deep, personal reverence for his brother's past words. "I say, dear, it's like a dream seeing you over again," Harry stammered, clutching at the memory of danger. "Think of that bloody Indian with his knife at your throat! I should like to give that Monsieur de Florac something for saving you... but I haven't got much now, only my little gold knee-buckles, and they ain't worth two guineas."

"You have got the half of what I have, child," George reassured him gently. "And we'll divide as soon as I have paid the Frenchman."

Upon hearing this, Harry burst forth into a torrent of blessings and oaths, swearing that there had never been such a brother in the world as George. For several days following his arrival, Harry's eyes seemed unable to leave his sibling's path. He would drop his knife or newspaper mid-meal to laugh aloud, and when walking the Mall or Hyde Park, he would gesture proudly to the crowds, declaring, "Look here, gentlemen! This is he. This is my brother, that was dead and is alive again! Can any man in Christendom produce such a brother as this?"

However, the warmth of reunion quickly cooled when the topic of Museau arose. Of course, Harry believed George must honor his promise to pay Museau's heirs the ransom sum. This issue had plagued George at home with terrible intensity. After Museau's death, Madam Esmond had argued with fierce rancour, insisting the bargain fell to the ground. To her, paying twelve thousand livres to a band of Norman peasants—whom Britain was at war with and who would likely funnel the money to the Pope—was nothing short of subscribing to wickedness. She viewed it as the wages of iniquity. While she allowed that George was of age and legally capable of squandering his patrimony, she could not be a party to such extravagance, especially given Harry's sudden deprivation of his inheritance. She urged him to give the money to his own blood, to Harry, rather than to a foreign peasantry. This dispute, raging between mother and son throughout George's final days in Virginia, remained unresolved. On the morning of his departure, Madam Esmond had visited his bedside after a sleepless night, asking if he still intended to fling away his father's property. He replied, grief-stricken and perplexed, that his word was passed and he must abide by his honour. Her answer was a prayer for Heaven to soften his proud heart, and the last image George retained of his mother was her pale, tearless face standing by his side before she slowly withdrew.

"Now," George resumed, shifting the mood back to self-reflection, "where didst thou learn the art of winning everybody to thy side, Harry? And how is it that you and all the world begin by being friends? Teach me a few lessons in popularity." He contrasted his own isolation with Harry's ubiquity. In Richmond, he noted, Mr. Esmond Warrington—the sole survivor of Braddock's Field—had initially been a favorite, receiving kindness from the townsfolk and a glowing sermon from the local parson. The neighbors flocked to visit the fugitive, and the Esmond carriage rolled out to exchange courtesies. But his mother, with her prodigious haughtiness, routed off the admirers and frightened them with her demeanor. Before the disaster at Fort Duquesne, George's popularity had already begun to wane. He was not jolly enough for the officers, nor did he care for their drinking bouts, dice-boxes, and swearing. To the ladies, he was too sarcastic; their tea and gossip stupefied him just as the men's bluster did. He felt profoundly lonely amidst the scandal and squabbles, often regretting his prison and longing for the silence of Duquesne.

"So the gentlemen determined I was supercilious and a dull companion," George mused, "and the ladies thought me cold and sarcastic—unable to tell if I was in earnest or not. Generally, they voted me a disagreeable fellow before my character was quite destroyed. And then came the appearance of the poor Biche! Oh, a nice character they made for me, my dear!" He cried out in transport of wrath. "A pretty life they led me after Museau's unlucky messenger appeared! The boys hooted the poor woman if she stepped into the street; the ladies dropped me half-curtsies and crossed to the other side. That precious clergyman went from tea-table to tea-table preaching on the horrors of seduction and the lax principles young men learned in popish countries. Then, the poor Fawn's appearance at home a few weeks after my return was declared a scheme between her and me. The best-informed agreed she had waited on the other side of the river until I signaled her to join me in Richmond. The officers bantered me at the coffee-house, cracking clumsy jokes about the woman I had 'selected.' Oh, the world is a nice charitable world!"

His anger boiled over as he recalled the news of Harry's troubles. "I was so enraged that I thought of going to Castlewood and living alone there—for our mother finds the place dull, and the greatest consolation in Mr. Stack's ministry—when the news arrived of your female perplexity. I think we were all glad that I should have a pretext for coming to Europe."

"I should like to see any of the infernal scoundrels who said a word against you," Harry roared, pacing the room with violent energy. "And break their rascally bones!"

"I had to do something like it for Bob Clubber," George noted calmly.

"What!" Harry cried, horrified. "That little sneaking, backbiting, toad-eating wretch, who hangs about my lord at Greenway Court and spunges on every gentleman in the country? If you whipped him, I hope you whipped him well, George?"
