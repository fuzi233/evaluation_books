The rejection of his peace offerings stung young Harry Warrington like a lash. Wounded pride flared into anger—not only against Colonel Lambert but the entire family. *They humiliated me before the girls,* he fumed. *First they preach morality, then scorn my gratitude as if I were some scoundrel unworthy of their company.*

Parson Sampson, ever the sycophant, eyed the morocco boxes with covetous admiration. "Why, sir, these trinkets must be worth a fortune!"

"They cost enough," Harry muttered, though the expense mattered little to him—or so he told himself.

"The repeater alone is a hundred pounds," he said, recalling Hetty’s wistful remark about desiring such a watch.

"Oh, what a prize!" Sampson simpered. "A lady’s heart could be won with such a trinket!"

Harry’s jaw tightened. "Perhaps I should toss it to the next apple-woman who passes."

Business in the City saw Harry withdraw a substantial sum from his dwindling funds. The broker, ever the opportunist, led him to a bustling dining-house before guiding him through the Virginia merchants’ walk on ’Change. Every glance felt like judgment. *They know,* Harry thought. *They whisper of Madam Esmond’s son, squandering his patrimony at the tables.*

By afternoon, he was back at White’s, where idleness bred temptation. Lord Castlewood lounged over the *Public Advertiser*, yawning.

"Piquet?" Castlewood suggested.

"An hour," Harry agreed.

The game stretched longer. The stakes climbed higher.

By evening, the club’s whispers followed him:

"Fortunatus has lost again."

"Thirteen hundred last night. How much tonight?"

Harry left in a storm of self-loathing. The losses mounted—his ready money, his patrimony, everything.

Dawn brought no comfort. The weight of his folly pressed upon him like a physical ache. Beneath his pillow, the repeater watch—meant for Hetty—chimed mockingly.

The thought of Virginia, of home, was a knife’s twist.

Sampson arrived, groveling as ever—until Harry revealed the night’s disaster.

"Every shilling gone," Harry admitted bitterly.

The parson’s face crumpled. "My landlord—his family—they’ll be ruined! I borrowed against your promise!"

Harry’s anger flared, then cooled. He reached for his mourning coat, not the fine damask of recent weeks.

"Here." He thrust a purse at Sampson. "Eighty guineas. All I have left."

The parson fell to his knees, weeping gratitude. Harry endured the display, torn between satisfaction and disgust.

Outside, the drums of the Guards echoed at Charing Cross. *At least I can shoulder a musket,* Harry thought grimly.

He strode to Sampson’s lodgings, where the chaplain’s despair was palpable.

"Castlewood owes me three years' salary," Sampson confessed. "He gave me five guineas—claimed it was all he had."

Harry’s lip curled. "He won eleven hundred from me last night."

The truth settled between them.

Harry pressed the money into Sampson’s hands. The parson’s effusions were excessive, but the relief in the household was real.

As Harry walked away, the weight of his losses lingered—but so did the quiet satisfaction of one decent act.

For now, it would have to be enough.
