The air in the barranca tasted of wet stone and the metallic tang of cooling lava. We had slept under the open sky, and now our muscles felt like lead bars welded to our bones. Even in the Grand Canary, the dew fell with a chill that ignored the latitude. I usually prefer a run at dawn to shake off the stiffness, but here, the ground offered no purchase. Not three yards of level earth existed between the jagged ridges. Coppinger, a man whose medical degrees arrived with the frequency of seasonal migrations, began a series of dumbbell exercises using two lumps of bristly rock. I mimicked him, moving with the sluggish grace of a creature waking from a deep coma.

Two days prior, rain had carved a temporary vein through the earth. A trickle of water wound along the base of the ravine. We descended to it, washing the grime of the ascent from our faces and scrubbing our teeth with brushes that felt like luxuries reserved for kings.

"Now," Coppinger said, his voice echoing off the damp walls as we emptied our pockets onto the stones. "The provisions are dwindling, and the quality has degraded significantly due to storage in a local Spanish newspaper."

"Yours consists largely of tobacco ash."

"It will deteriorate further. We have significant scrambling ahead of us."

This was undeniable. We sat beside the stream, consuming the remainder of our supplies. Our destination was the fonda at Santa Brigida, ten miles away, where our equipment rested. Coppinger intended to conduct additional photographic surveys and measurements before departing this cluster of caves. Consequently, hunger awaited us soon, alongside the prospect of the Patron's vintage wine—a liquid that, with sufficient diplomatic maneuvering, could be procured in the mountain villages, rendering the offerings of Las Palmas hotels obsolete within a week.

To be honest, the two mummies Coppinger had already secured satisfied my modest ambitions. Wrapped in brittle goatskins that crumbled like dry parchment, the remains emitted clouds of dust upon contact. Yet Coppinger was relentless. He sought evidence of an ancient Guanche university or sacred college, mirroring the institution rumored to exist on the opposite side of the island. He would not cease until every cave in the cliff face had been excavated. With ample film stock and a flashlight apparatus, he deemed a return trip unnecessary. He seized the crowbar; I hoisted the rope. We ascended toward the ridge, scorched by the previous day's sun.

Accessing these caves was difficult; otherwise, they would have been plundered generations ago. Coppinger theorized that the ancients utilized retractable ropes of goat-skin to exclude unwanted visitors—a hypothesis plausible enough in the absence of contrary data. The cave mouths aligned in a horizontal row, suspended thirty feet beneath the ridge and fifty feet above the ravine floor. Spanish curiosity rarely ventured where walking was impossible.

Descending from below would have been cumbersome. Instead, we planned to rappel from the ridge, reach the base of the cliff, and repeat the process for each opening. Coppinger possessed courage and a steady head, yet his portliness and proximity to fifty rendered him vulnerable. I volunteered to lead, entering the caverns first to facilitate his descent. However, navigating a vertical face via a thin bootlace of a rope offers little assistance to the observer below, save for verbal encouragement.

For the initial three caves, which appeared to be mere storage dens, I urged Coppinger to bypass them. He refused, insisting on personal inspection. He classified one as a prison, another a pottery workshop, and a third a seminary for young priests. His imagination, characteristic of the thoroughgoing archaeologist, demanded validation for every anomaly. As the sun climbed higher, Coppinger's enthusiasm waned, though he persisted beyond the point of safety. I grew uneasy. The drop frequently exceeded eighty feet. Reluctantly, he conceded defeat regarding the remaining openings, claiming he would attempt them alone if I declined. He framed this as a concession, offering to rely solely on my untrained observations. I rejected the premise. Exhaustion weighed heavily upon me; sweat soaked my clothes, the sun pounded my skull, and my hands burned from friction against the rope.

Despite his fatigue, Coppinger remained fervent. "Consider the potential," he urged. "If you discover anything, it belongs to you. I retain no claim."

"I require no further mummies encased in leather."

"This is not a burial site. Do you lack the discernment to distinguish the openings? Perhaps you will stumble upon a discovery of significance."

"Very well."

I navigated the treacherous terrain, utilizing a fissure to avoid a two-mile detour. I repositioned the crowbar and rappelled downward, my hands stinging anew with each grip. Swinging into the cave mouth proved difficult due to the overhanging rock, resulting in a clumsy impact against my back. I maintained my hold on the rope; losing it would have meant certain entrapment, as Coppinger could not retrieve it from his position below.

Upon entry, the cave revealed itself as distinct from the others. While the previous chambers were irregular dens, this one featured walls smoothed by cutting tools, devoid of sharp angles. The interior sloped inward toward the ceiling, evoking an architectural style familiar yet unnamed. Several interconnected rooms and passages extended deeper into the earth. No inscriptions marked the walls, save for the presence of bats. I extinguished a cigarette, mindful of Coppinger's impatience with haste, and signaled my findings.

Coppinger's face registered anxiety. "Have you examined it thoroughly?"

"Naturally. What else could occupy my time?"

"Do not descend yet. I am securing the camera and lighting apparatus to the rope. Retrieve them, and capture approximately six exposures."

"As you wish."

I hauled the equipment upward and positioned the camera on a narrow ledge adjacent to the entrance. The resulting images would undoubtedly be monotonous, yet Coppinger valued them precisely for their lack of vibrancy. Careful attention to halation was necessary within the dark interior. I captured four chambers before reaching a section where the ledge was elevated and broad. I placed the camera, stabilized it with scattered stones, and prepared to recharge the flash unit.

As my weight settled onto the ledge, a sharp crack resonated through the chamber. I dropped six inches. Recovering instantly, I snatched the camera before it slid to the floor. A surge of satisfaction washed over me. This was a Guanche repository, hermetically sealed with cement. Beneath the debris, I discovered layers of material that chipped away cleanly. Each stratum consisted of two components: a glossy substrate resembling talc, overlaid with a dark, toffee-colored substance akin to wax, etched with intricate patterns.

Lacking expertise in such matters, I accepted Coppinger's assertions regarding Guanche capabilities as authoritative. He had emphasized their inability to write. Thus, I interpreted the scribed patterns as decorative rather than linguistic. Nevertheless, the material warranted extraction. Using the heel of my boot and a pocketknife, I quarried the sheets. They adhered tightly to the cavity wall, but by breaching the front, I accessed the lower layers. I hacked through the base, extracting a solid block measuring roughly twenty by fifteen inches. It appeared heavier than it was. After documenting the find, I lowered it to Coppinger.

With the caves exhausted, I descended. The artifact lay on the ground; Coppinger crouched beside it, vibrating with excitement.

"What is it?"

"I am unaware. Yet, it represents the most significant discovery in the history of the Canary Islands. It is yours, you unappreciated fool. Regrettably, you have compromised the integrity of the beginning and the end of a potentially priceless chronicle. This is my error. I should not have entrusted critical exploration to an untrained individual."

"You bear responsibility if errors occurred. You asserted that no writing existed among the ancient Canarians. I relied on your authority. To me, this material might have served as sustenance."

"This is not Guanche craftsmanship," Coppinger replied, his tone sharpening. "Observe the talc. Have you no vision? Consider the geological composition of the island. Talc does not occur here."

"I am not a geologist. Is this imported literature?"

"Certainly. It is Egyptian. Obvious upon inspection. The provenance remains a mystery. The script varies from known variants. Furthermore, the waxy substance is unprecedented. "If it suits you, I prefer to contemplate food. Ten miles of arduous travel await, and I am famished. Note that it is four o'clock. Descent and ascent consume considerable time."

Coppinger spread his coat, wrapping the artifact with delicate care, refusing to tie it lest the edges fracture. He insisted on transporting it personally, enduring the majority of the trek to Santa Brigida until sheer exhaustion compelled him to relinquish the burden. He offered no gratitude. "Given that it ultimately belongs to you, you may assume the load," he snapped.

"Do you realize," he murmured, "that you have uncovered the most valuable historical manuscript the modern era has witnessed? Your clumsiness during extraction has inflicted irreparable damage. It was a hard task, but those who hold higher offices learn to wear a passionless face before the people. Twenty years prior, these same fine obeisances had been rendered to me. Now, the Gods had seen fit to alter fortune. Yet, as I walked bent and humbly behind the heels of Tatho, though etiquette forbade noisy salutations to myself, it could not suppress kindly glances. Custom dictates that on such great ceremonial days of changing rulers, those present may offer petitions, accuse their retiring head with immunity from vengeance, or propose theories for the future governance of the State. I think it pardonable to my vanity to record that not a voice was raised against me, nor against any item of my twenty years of rule. Nor did anyone speak of alterations for the future. Even as we circled the chamber three times, all present offered their approval in generous silence.

Then, one behind the other, the new Viceroy and the old marched with formal steps over golden tiles beneath the pyramid. The great officers of state left their stations to join our train. At the far wall, we reached the door of the private chambers—an hour ago, mine own.

An army besieged the Sacred Mountain, guarding its single approach with narrow vigilance. Time was running out. The fight still raged at the gate, a miracle that the few priests had held so long. But the ocean rolled inland, an ever-growing flood. The mountains smoked and belched volleys of rock as the weight increased. Soon, the besiegers would see the fate threatening them. Madness would drive them to sweep all obstacles aside.

Yet Zaemon and his companion showed no haste. They lacked a temple, sacrifices, or incense. They had only the sky for a roof and the Gods for witnesses. Still, they performed the initiation with all the grand, majestic form accumulated over ages, refusing to curtail a single sentence.

Outside, a burning mountain burst with a bellowing roar as incoming waters met its fires. Phorenice's troops broke the last resistance, pouring into the groves and temples in a frenzy. Yet the two old men quivered through the ritual, never faltering.

I admitted I was less rapt. Standing bareheaded in the heat, I struggled to concentrate, torn by a thousand foreign emotions. A continent was meeting its end, and while Zaemon could focus entirely on the parochial duties of the priesthood, I could not. My mind was filled with anxieties regarding Nais. Still, I maintained a decent facade, responding to the required prompts and trying to memorize the sentences that were keys to power, rather than mere phrases of devotion.

It became clear that if the ceremony did not conclude soon, it would be cut short abruptly. Phorenice's legions swarmed onto the mountain crest, now aware of the catastrophe unfolding below. They were out of control, running like distracted beasts. Knowing the priests possessed the power to halt this wreck via the Ark, their natural impulse was to exact final vengeance on the initiates before it was too late.

My attention flickered between the soldiers closing in and the sea deepening its grip on the land. The fires ringing the mountain doubled in height, roaring unceasingly. Despite the distractions, the two old priests continued their work as if in a quiet temple, undisturbed by a single buzzing fly.

Finally,

The Ark possessed no rudder, no oars, no engine. It drifted solely according to the whim of the High Gods. Darkness swallowed the interior, thick with the scent of antiquity and the low, continuous groaning of the ship itself. I could not locate the fire-box to reignite the fallen lamp, so we navigated the blind darkness, accepting whatever the void dispensed. The waves tossed us in a merciless game. I clung to the side, anchoring Nais beside me. Words were unnecessary; our shared grief created a complete silence between us.

Atlantis had sunk, leaving behind great whirlpools and the spouting embers of drowned fires—a fleeting tribute to the Sea Gods. Perhaps, unseen in the black belly of the Ark, a vast hurricane followed to ensure the desolation was total. For seven days and seven nights, the turmoil raged, measured by the hour-glass hanging within the hull. Then the wind ceased, replaced by the rolling swell. It was regular, oily, distinct from the earlier chaos. Only then did I dare ascend the stairs to the hatch in the roof. Fresh air gushed down, washing away the foulness. As the Ark rode high above the sea, I descended to retrieve Nais, bringing her forth to bask in the curing rays of the Sun. Together, we offered thanks for the mercy of another day, then lay down to seize the rest we desperately needed.

Yet, exhaustion held me awake. I stared out at the oily, sunlit water. No land blurred on the horizon; the ring of ocean was unbroken. Above, the vault of heaven remained unchanged. The deep was littered with the wreckage of Atlantis—trees, squared timber, a smashed hide-boat, and occasionally the rounded corpse of a man or beast riding the swells alongside us. These drifts served as proof, if any were needed, that this was fact, not a nightmare.

Sleep finally claimed me, my hand clasping Nais's. When I awoke, she watched me with open eyes, tender and alert. Rest had restored our strength, breeding a dangerous complacency. We were now ready to accept the station assigned by the High Gods without complaint. Descending once more into the Ark's belly, we ate and drank, fortifying ourselves for the new life ahead.

Now, viewed in leisure, the Ark revealed its wonder. Though it floated for the first time in centuries, it bore no leaks, no cracks. Even the floor was bone-dry. Wood grain was visible, yet no sutures or joints marred the surface. Living timbers had been placed and then fused by an art lost to us, perfected by the Ancients. Subsequent treatments rendered the wood harder than metal, impervious to weather.

Inside the gloomy cavern of its hold, supplies waited. Great tanks lined the central alley, brimming with grain; sweet water filled others at the opposite end. Drugs, essences of animal life, and samples for sustenance occupied further spaces—all intended for the journey under divine guidance. Carved along every wall and partition in the crude style of a bygone age were depictions of every beast that once inhabited Atlantis. I examined them with a hunter's fascination; some were strange, species extinct along with the people who had immortalized them in stone. Weapons and craft-tools were also stored here.

For weeks, our existence unfolded as the Gods drove the Ark across the waters. We lacked governance; we could not alter our course by even a hair's breadth. The High Gods, having selected us as the sole survivors of Atlantis, held sole control of our fate. Into Their hands, we cheerfully surrendered our future.

Of the land we eventually reached, where we established our home and raised children, I shall speak in its proper place. Since this chronicle follows the strict order of events, I must first explain how I acquired the sheets upon which it is written. In a large coffer centered on the Ark's floor lay the entirety of the Mysteries accumulated over ages, inscribed with accuracy. I read portions during the passing days, and the awful magnitude of the powers they controlled appalled me. I had witnessed some of these forces unleashed in Atlantis, contributing to her destruction. But here were powers far greater—the great Secret of Life and Death that Phorenice discovered, leading to her demise—and other truths too terrible to commit to paper.

The burden of being custodian to these writings became unbearable. The longer the matter lingered in my mind, the more intolerable it grew. Finally, I seized hot irons and seared the wax on the sheets until every letter vanished. If I acted wrongly, the High Gods, in Their infinite justice, might punish me; if it was right that these secrets remain buried, They, in Their infinite power, might dictate them anew to worthy scribes. Thus, as the Ark swayed over the waves, I destroyed them. Later, upon reaching land, I rewrote the sheets with the account of how Atlantis was dragged to her death throes.
