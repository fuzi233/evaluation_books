Where my soul went in that swoon I cannot name. If she wandered, she kept her travel locked away as closely as a recipe whispered once and lost—no crumb left for Memory, no steam for fancy to follow. Perhaps she rose—light as sifted flour—and, for one trembling moment, scented the air of some eternal kitchen where no hunger gnaws and no pot ever boils dry. Perhaps, at the very threshold, an unseen hand—stern as a matron’s, gentle as a nurse’s—turned her back, tied her again, shivering and unwilling, to the cold clay of my body.

I know only how she returned: not with gladness, but with resistance, like dough that will not take the last water. Spirit and substance met again in a struggle, not an embrace. Sight came first—red and swimming, as if my eyes opened through blood. Hearing followed—violent, a crash of sound, like thunder striking close over the roof. Consciousness woke in fright; I sat up, appalled, staring as one dragged from deep water into a strange room.

At first nothing was itself. A wall did not look like a wall, a lamp not like a lamp; all things wore that queer, half-transparent air which might make a ghost as intelligible as a chair. Yet, as a hearth draws a room back into order, my faculties, one by one, settled to their places; the mechanism of life resumed its old, stubborn rhythm.

Still I did not know where I was. I had been lifted from the church-step where I fell—night and tempest were shut out by ceiling, glass, and plaster. Some house had received me. What house?

Half-dreaming, I tried to fit the place to the pensionnat in the Rue Fossette: a dormitory, perhaps, or one of the little rooms. But the glimpses I caught did not agree—no ranks of white beds, no long bare windows. “It is not Madame Beck’s own chamber?” I thought, and even as I questioned, my eye found an easy-chair covered in blue damask.

More followed: seats cushioned to match; then a whole parlour came slowly into focus—the steady, domestic fact of it. A wood fire lived upon a clear hearth; the carpet showed arabesques of bright blue against shaded fawn; the walls were pale, and over them ran a delicate, endless garland of azure forget-me-nots, lost and found again among gold leaves and tendrils. Between two windows hung a gilded mirror; the windows were curtained amply with blue damask. In the mirror I saw myself—not in a bed, but laid upon a sofa. I looked like something unseasonably kept: hollow-eyed, hair darker by contrast with the thin ash of my cheeks. This room, by the place of its doors and windows, was unknown; this house, unknown.

And yet—my brain, still unsteady, began to play its tricks with recognition. That blue arm-chair grew familiar. So did a certain scroll-couch, and the round centre-table with its blue cover bordered in autumnal foliage; most of all, two little footstools with worked covers, and a small ebony-framed chair whose seat and back were embroidered with brilliant flowers on a dark ground.

My heart gave a hard little leap. I searched with my eyes, and—strange miracle of the commonplace—old acquaintance sat everywhere, as if Time had folded back on itself and laid yesterday on top of ten years ago. “Auld lang syne” looked out from every corner: two oval miniatures above the mantelpiece—the pearl-edged, powdered heads, the velvet collars circling white throats, the swell of muslin kerchiefs, the lace ruffles; upon the shelf, two china vases and remnants of a tiny tea-service thin as eggshell, smooth as enamel; under glass, a white centre ornament, a classic alabaster group. And there—most piercing of all—handscreens with elaborate pencil-drawings finished like engravings: I felt my eyes ache at the sight, remembering the long hours when these same fingers, now so spare, had held the school-girl pencil and crept line by line over a tedious grace.

Where was I? Not only what place, but what year? These things belonged to other days, another country. Ten years ago I had bade them farewell; since my fourteenth year they and I had not met. The question broke from me, audible, helpless: “Where am I?”

A shape I had not noticed stirred, rose, and came forward—inharmonious with the room as a coarse apron would be beside a damask gown. It was a native bonne, in common cap and print dress. She spoke neither French nor English. Her phrases were in dialect—close as knots to my ear—and yielded me no sense. But she cooled my forehead and temples with water scented faintly—something clean and herbal in it, like bruised leaves after rain. Then she propped my cushion, signed that I must not speak, and returned to her place at the foot of the sofa.

She knit busily, eyes down; thus I could watch her without interruption, and wonder how she came to be set like a plain domestic utensil among these relics of my childhood. More—what had those scenes and those years to do with me now?

Too weak to untangle the riddle, I tried to settle it by dismissing it: mistake, dream, fever. Yet I knew there was no mistake; I was not asleep; and—confused as I was—I believed myself sane. I wished the room had been dimmer, that the familiar details would not show so clearly: the worked chair, the screens, the ornaments, the blue damask—precisely the same, to the least flaw and shade, as I had known in the drawing-room at my godmother’s house at Bretton. Only the room itself, in proportion and measure, seemed altered.

The tale of Bedreddin Hassan rose to my mind—carried in sleep from Cairo to the gates of Damascus. Had some Genius stooped through the storm that overcame me, lifted me from the church-step, and borne me high over land and sea to lay me before an English hearth? But no; I knew that hearth’s old fire burned no more. Its Lares had long since been carried away.

The bonne looked up, saw my eyes wide, and perhaps read in them an agitation she did not understand. She set down her knitting, poured water, measured drops from a vial into a glass, and came towards me. What dark draught was this—what distillation of foreign mercy?

There was no time to question; I swallowed it passively. At once a quietude, warm and heavy as broth taken after long fasting, flowed through my brain. Thought softened; fear subsided. The pain of weakness slid from my limbs; muscles slept. I could not move—yet, with the desire gone, the loss felt like relief. The bonne placed a screen between me and the lamp; I saw her rise to do it, but I do not remember seeing her sit again. Between the motion and its end, I fell asleep.

When I woke, all was changed.

High day lay over me—not summer’s gold, but the leaden gloom of raw, blustering autumn. Rain beat the casement; wind worried the trees with that low, long “wuther” which tells of a garden outside. Chill, whiteness, solitude surrounded me. Whiteness—because dimity curtains dropped before a French bed bounded my view.

I lifted them and looked out. My eye, ready for the long, whitewashed dormitory of the pensionnat, blinked and failed before the smallness of a cabinet: sea-green walls, one high lattice shaded with muslin festoons; a toilette-table dressed—like a lady for a ball—in white over pink; a polished glass above it, a lace-frilled pincushion; a small, low chintz arm-chair green and white; a washstand with grey marble top and pale-green ware. Nothing more was needed to furnish the tiny space.

And yet fear seized me. Not because the room was ugly—it was pretty enough—but because it was impossible. These were not merely chairs and glass and china: they were the very ghosts—if ghosts could be solid—of things I knew too well.

I knew that chair—its chintz pattern, its snugness. I knew the black carved frame of the glass, shining and foliated. I knew the milky-green china on the stand. I knew the stand itself, and the marble splintered at one corner. I was forced to recognize them, as I had recognized the rosewood and porcelain of the parlour the night before.

Bretton. Bretton. Ten years ago looked back at me from that mirror. Why did that past haunt me with such exactness, and yet return incomplete? Why give me furniture as faithfully as an inventory, and deny me the room, the street, the very air of the place?

On the toilette lay a crimson satin pincushion, ornamented with gold beads and frilled with thread-lace. I had made it myself. I sprang up, seized it, and examined it closely. There was the cipher, “L. L. B.,” wrought in gold beads, within an oval wreath embroidered in white silk—the initials of my godmother’s name: Louisa Lucy Bretton.

“Am I in England? Am I at Bretton?” I muttered. I pulled up the blind and looked out, half-ready to meet the calm old buildings and clean grey pavement of St. Ann’s Street, and at the end the minster towers; or, failing that, at least some town view—a rue in Villette, some street in some known city.

Instead, I looked through a frame of leafage clustering about the lattice, and out upon a grassy, mead-like level—a lawn-terrace and trees rising from lower ground beyond: high forest trees such as I had not seen for many a day. They groaned under the October gale; between their trunks lay the line of an avenue where yellow leaves drifted in heaps, or were whipped, one by one, before the sweeping west wind. Whatever landscape lay further seemed flat; the tall beeches shut it out. The place was secluded—and strange to me.

Once more I lay down. My bed stood in a little alcove. Turning my face to the wall, I hoped to exclude the bewildering room. But no—between the looped curtains hung a broad gilded frame holding a portrait.

It was a water-colour sketch: a boy’s head, well drawn—fresh, life-like, animated. He seemed sixteen: fair, sanguine, long hair with a sunny sheen, penetrating eyes, an arch mouth, a gay smile. A face parents might be proud of, sisters tender over; a romantic school-girl might almost have loved it in its frame. Those eyes looked as though, when older, they would flash quick responses to love; whether they would keep the steady beam of faith, I could not tell. On his lips lay a beautiful menace—caprice and light esteem ready to rise at any sentiment offered him too easily.

I forced myself to accept each new revelation quietly, and whispered, as if speaking a remembered list of household treasures:

“That portrait used to hang in the breakfast-room, over the mantelpiece—too high, I always thought. I remember mounting a music-stool to unhook it, holding it close, searching those bonny wells of eyes—hazel under lashes like a pencilled laugh—studying the cheek’s colour, the mouth’s expression.” I did not think fancy could better the curve of that mouth or the line of the chin; even my ignorance knew them for beautiful, and still I had pondered, perplexed, how what charmed could also pain so keenly.

Once, to test something I could not name, I had taken little Missy Home, lifted her up, and bade her look. “Do you like it, Polly?” I had asked. She never answered; she gazed long, and at last a shadow trembled through her sensitive eye as she said, “Put me down.” And I had put her down, thinking, “The child feels it too.”

Now I thought on it again, adding, against my will, a tenderness: he had faults; yet scarce ever was a finer nature—liberal, suave, impressible. The reflection closed on a word spoken aloud, as if it had been waiting in my mouth all night:

“Graham!”

“Graham!” echoed a sudden voice at my bedside. “Do you want Graham?”

I turned. The wonder did not thin; it thickened. If the pictured face on the wall had startled me, the living woman before me startled me more: tall, well-attired, in widow’s silk; a cap suited to her matronly braids; a face marked now beyond beauty, but not beyond character—good, firm, and sensible. She was little changed: somewhat sterner, more robust. She was my godmother—the distinct presence of Mrs. Bretton.

I kept quiet; inwardly, I shook. My pulse fluttered; the blood left my cheek, and cold lay there.

“Madam, where am I?” I asked.

“In a very safe asylum; well protected for the present. Make your mind quite easy till you are a little better. You look ill this morning.”

“I am so bewildered I scarcely know whether to trust my senses, or whether they mislead me in everything. But you speak English, do you not, madam?”

“I should think you might hear that. It would puzzle me to hold a long discourse in French.”

“You do not come from England?”

“I am lately arrived thence. Have you been long in this country? You seem to know my son?”

“Do I, madam? Perhaps I do. Your son—the picture there?”

“That is his portrait as a youth. While looking at it, you pronounced his name.”

“Graham Bretton?”

She nodded.

“I speak to Mrs. Bretton, formerly of Bretton, ——shire?”

“Quite right; and you, I am told, are an English teacher in a foreign school here: my son recognised you as such.”

“How was I found, madam, and by whom?”

“My son shall tell you that by-and-by,” she replied. “At present you are too confused and weak for conversation. Try to eat some breakfast, and then sleep.”

In spite of all I had undergone—fatigue, agitation, wet and cold—the true fever that had pressed on my frame was abating. For nine days I had taken no solid food; thirst had been my constant companion. Yet now, when breakfast was offered, hunger rose—sharp and faint at once—as if something within me, long starved, had begun to remember how to live. I drank the tea she gave—English tea, strong enough to carry warmth down into the chest—and ate the small allowance of dry toast. It was only a morsel, but it steadied me. Two or three hours later the bonne brought a little cup of broth and a biscuit: the broth smelled of patient simmering, of bones giving up their strength slowly, of salt measured and not squandered. I drank it as if it were medicine and kindness together.

When evening began to fall, the wind still harried the trees, and the rain streamed on like a continual pouring from some inexhaustible pail. I grew weary of my bed. The room, though pretty, felt too small—its very neatness confining. The chill and the gathering gloom pressed on my spirits; I wanted firelight—wanted not only to see it but to hear it: that domestic crackle and hush which gives courage, that little acoustic shelter against the world’s roar. Outside, the storm made a brutal music—raw, insistent, wearing; inside, I longed for the older, kinder sounds of a home: a kettle’s murmur, a cup set down on a saucer, the soft rustle of a newspaper turned.

And always I thought of the son of that tall matron. When should I see him? Not, surely, while I remained shut away.

At last the bonne came to make the bed for the night. She would have wrapped me in a blanket and set me in the little chintz chair; but I declined and dressed myself. It cost effort; my limbs still held that weak, watery uncertainty. Yet I finished, and sat down to take breath.

Then Mrs. Bretton appeared again.

“Dressed!” she exclaimed, smiling the smile I remembered so well—pleasant, not soft. “You are quite better then? Quite strong—eh?”

She spoke to me as she used to—half-kindly, half-authoritative. There was the same patronage in her voice and manner that, as a girl, I had always known from her—a sheltering, not insulting; the protection of the strong, as a tree shelters an herb. I made my request without ceremony.

“Do let me go down-stairs, madam. I am so cold and dull here.”

“I desire nothing better, if you are strong enough to bear the change,” she answered. “Come then; here is an arm.”

I took it, and we went down one flight of carpeted steps to a landing. A tall door stood open, admitting us into the blue-damask room.

The sight was like warmth itself. Domestic comfort sat there in order and glow. Amber lamplight filled the corners; the fire threw vermilion flush on the walls. And to complete the old picture, tea stood ready on the table—an English tea, shining and familiar: the solid silver urn of antique pattern, the massive pot to match, the thin porcelain cups dark with purple and gilding. I knew the very seed-cake, baked in its peculiar mould, which at Bretton always had its place. Graham liked it. There it was again, set before Graham’s plate, with silver knife and fork beside it. So Graham was expected. Graham might be in the house. In a few minutes—perhaps—I should see him.

“Sit down—sit down,” said Mrs. Bretton, as my step faltered a little in crossing to the hearth. She placed me on the sofa. I soon moved behind it, pleading the fire was too hot; in the shadow I found a seat that suited me better. She did not argue. She was never one to fuss: she let others have their way when it cost nothing essential.

She made the tea and took up the newspaper. I watched her with a kind of hungry attention. All her movements were young. She must have been above fifty, yet neither sinew nor spirit had rusted. Though portly, she was alert; though serene, capable of sudden impulse. Good health and good temper kept her green.

As she read, I perceived she listened—listened for her son. She would not confess uneasiness; yet the storm did not lull, and if Graham were out in that hoarse wind—still roaring, still unsatisfied—I knew well her heart would be out with him.

“Ten minutes behind his time,” she said, looking at her watch.

A minute later she lifted her eyes from the page and inclined her head slightly towards the door. Her brow cleared; she had heard something. Then even my less-practised ear caught it: the iron clash of a gate swung to; steps on gravel; the door-bell.

He was come.

Mrs. Bretton filled the teapot from the urn. She drew nearer the hearth the stuffed blue chair—hers by right, yet prepared as if for an habitual usurper. And when that one came up the stairs—soon, after some necessary attention to the disorder of a wild wet night—and strode in—

“Is it you, Graham?” she asked, hiding a glad smile under curt speech.

“Who else should it be, mamma?” demanded the unpunctual, taking possession irreverently of the abdicated throne.

“Don’t you deserve cold tea, for being late?”

“I shall not get my deserts, for the urn sings cheerily.”

“Wheel yourself to the table, lazy boy. No seat will serve you but mine. If you had one spark of a sense of propriety, you would always leave that chair for the Old Lady.”

“So I should—only the dear Old Lady persists in leaving it for me.” He looked towards me. “How is your patient, mamma?”

“Will she come forward and speak for herself?” said Mrs. Bretton, turning to my corner.

At the invitation I came forward. Graham rose courteously. He stood tall by the hearth, the very figure his mother’s pride would naturally fasten upon and feed. He looked at me with concern not unkindly worn, as if his mind had been with me since the night before.

“So you are come down,” he said. “You must be better then—much better. I scarcely expected we should meet thus, or here. I was alarmed last night; and if I had not been forced to hurry away to a dying patient, I certainly would not have left you. But my mother herself is something of a doctress, and Martha an excellent nurse. I saw the case was a fainting-fit, not necessarily dangerous. What brought it on I have yet to learn, and all particulars. Meantime, I trust you really do feel better?”

“Much better,” I answered calmly. “Much better, I thank you, Dr. John.”
