In the verdant town of Clavering, nestled in the western reaches of England, a gentleman named Pendennis dwelled during the reign of the illustrious King George. His name, etched on a weathered signboard adorned with a gleaming pestle and mortar, still lingered in the memories of the townspeople, evoking the faint recollection of a humble apothecary's shop in the bustling city of Bath. There, Pendennis plied his trade, catering to the needs of the gentry and the common folk alike, from attending the bedside of ailing gentlemen to peddling simple remedies to a farmer's wife across the counter. The townsfolk of Clavering, with their keen memories, could attest to these facts, unencumbered by the distractions of a metropolitan existence.

Yet, behind the façade of this unassuming apothecary lay a gentleman of refined education and a lineage as venerable as any in the county of Somerset. His Cornish ancestry traced the Pendennises back to the era of the Druids, and possibly even further. Through strategic marriages with the Normans, they had forged ties with the nobility of Wales and Brittany. Pendennis's academic pursuits had been promising, but fate intervened when his father's insolvency necessitated a drastic change in his son's trajectory. Forced to abandon his university studies in his second year, Pendennis reluctantly donned the apron and pestle, a profession he abhorred, only to be sustained by the promise of support from his maternal uncle, a London apothecary of modest standing.

After completing his apprenticeship, Pendennis parted ways with his coarse-minded mentor and established himself in Bath, bearing the modest banner of his medical credentials. The early years were marked by hardship, as he struggled to maintain his shop and its finery, as well as provide for his bedridden mother.
