Let me tell you about dear old Bobbie Cardew. It's a tale of such high moral import that you'd think I'd need a thesaurus to describe it, but honestly, the plot carries its own weight. It's a cautionary tale for the blokes among you—a stark warning against negligence—and a heart-wrenching chronicle of how a certain girl managed to reduce a man to absolute despair.

If you've known Bobbie recently, you might be shocked to learn that his defining characteristic used to be a memory so porous it could drain a bathtub in seconds. Dozens of acquaintances have gasped at this revelation, but I swear on my life, it's true.

Back in the day, Bobbie was the undisputed champion of "young rotters" within a four-mile radius. People call me a fool, but compared to Bobbie, I was a genius. While I was a minus-six on the "silly ass" scale, Bobbie was a plus-four. To get him to dinner, I'd employ a tactical assault: a letter on Monday, a telegram on Friday, a phone call on Saturday, and a taxi driver with a megaphone thirty minutes before the appointed hour. Even then, I'd often arrive to find he'd already fled town.

Yet, beneath this surface-level idiocy lay a strange, dormant intelligence. It was there, waiting for a detonator. I assumed that detonator was dynamite. I was wrong. The detonator was marriage.

Bobbie married. Have you ever seen a puppy chase a butterfly? The pup sees the fluttering wings, thinks it's glorious, and runs full tilt without realizing the butterfly is just a mirage. Bobbie fell in love with a whoop of joy, only to discover the butterfly was actually a brick wall.

His bride wasn't the type you'd expect a playboy to swoon over. She worked. To a man who hasn't lifted a finger in his life, there is a peculiar, magnetic romance about a woman who earns her keep. Her name was Mary Anthony. She stood five-foot-six, possessed a ton-and-a-half of fiery red-gold hair, piercing grey eyes, and a chin carved from granite. She was a hospital nurse. When Bobbie crashed his polo career, she was the one assigned to patch him up, and less than a week later, they were married. Pure, unadulterated romance.

I met her when Bobbie announced the news at the club. I admire anyone who can earn a living, especially a girl who'd grown up an orphan doing everything off her own bat. We hit it off splendidly. She worshipped Bobbie; Bobbie seemed to think she was the center of the universe. I figured, if they just remembered to show up for the wedding, they'd be golden.

Fast forward a year. They moved into a flat, and life seemed idyllic. I dropped by frequently, expecting chaos, but found only smooth sailing. I thought, "Marriage? What's the fuss?"

Then came the Quiet Dinner.

I met Bobbie in Piccadilly, and he invited me to dinner at the flat. A sane man would have bolted, but I went. When we arrived, Mrs. Bobbie was a vision. Her hair was piled into a diamond-studded mountain, and she wore a dress so spectacular it defied description.

"Here's old Reggie, dear," Bobbie beamed. "I've brought him home for dinner. Kitchen, send it up!"

Mrs. Bobbie stared at him as if he were a ghost. Her face cycled through scarlet, then pale as a sheet, then a nervous laugh. She recovered, smiled at me, and proceeded to act as if nothing was wrong. She chatted, teased Bobbie, and even played ragtime on the piano. But I saw the truth. She was holding back a scream, gripping her pearls until her knuckles turned white, all to maintain the facade of a perfect evening. I grabbed my hat and fled before she exploded.

The next day, Bobbie was as cheerful as a gumdrop in a freezer.

"Do you know how long I've been married?" he asked eagerly.

"A year?"

"Not about a year," he sighed. "Exactly a year. Yesterday!"

The lightbulb went off. "Yesterday was...?"

"The anniversary. I planned to take Mary to the Savoy and Covent Garden. She wanted Caruso. I had the tickets in my pocket. All through dinner, I felt like I'd forgotten something, but I couldn't place it."

"Till your wife mentioned it?"

He nodded.

"My stock's pretty low at home," Bobbie admitted, his voice cracking.

I sat there, lighting a cigarette, while he paced. Eventually, he wandered into a jewelry store. There was that stratum of sense again.

This was the beginning of the real drama. Bobbie was a chump, protected by Nature's armor: short-term memory loss. Mary, however, was determined to strip that armor away.

For a week, the memory of the missed anniversary stung him. But it was a scratch, not a wound. Soon, he was back to his old ways. Mary, terrified that a man who forgets his wedding day will eventually forget he's married at all, knew she had to act now.

I tried to reason with Bobbie. "You've got to pull yourself together," I told him. "Wedding anniversaries need to be on your mental checklist. It's a brainstrain, but you can't opt out."

"But why do women care about these rotten dates?" Bobbie grumbled. "Does it matter if I forgot the cat had measles? She knows I love her!"

"That's not enough for a woman," I snapped. "They want to be shown."

He chewed on his stick, muttering about how rummy women were, and walked away. I watched him go, knowing that if he insisted on getting into trouble, I'd just have to wait for the explosion.

Weeks turned into months. Then, in early spring, the thunderbolt struck.

I was watching the traffic on Piccadilly when Bobbie burst in, eyes bulging, face the color of a boiled oyster, waving a letter.

"Reggie! She's gone!"

"Gone? Who?"

"Mary! Gone! Left me!"

"Where?"

"Read this!" He shoved the paper at me.

*"MY DEAR BOBBIE,—I am going away. When you care enough about me to remember to wish me many happy returns on my birthday, I will come back. My address will be Box 341, London Morning News."*

"Well," I said, "why don't you wish her many happy returns?"

"She says 'on my birthday'."

"When is her birthday?"

"I've forgotten."

"Forgotten!" I roared. "Between January and December, surely?"

"Just somewhere in between."

"Think!"

"What's the use of thinking? I've been sparking my brain since I opened that letter!"

I rang for drinks and tried to guide him. "Can't you remember the last time? The weather?"

"Ordinary. Warmish. Or cold. I can't recall."

"Give me a clue."

"I gave her a present last year. Let's go to the shop and check the receipt."

"And what was the present?"

"I can't remember."

Ideas are like golf shots; sometimes you're on, sometimes you're off. Bobbie, usually devoid of two coherent thoughts in a morning, suddenly fired a brain-wave.

"Why not buy all twelve monthly horoscopes?" he suggested. "Find the one that matches her character."

We bought the books. We analyzed them. December people keep secrets and travel extensively. October people have original ideas and love moving. February people have wonderful memories.

"It's May," Bobbie argued, pointing to a book claiming May women are "capricious, a barrier to happy married life."

"No, it's February," I countered, citing the entry for women who are "determined to have their own way."

He tore the books apart, stamped on them, and burned them.

The next few days were transformative. Bobbie underwent a "soul's awakening." He was wearing his brain to the bone, suffering through a genuine crisis of intellect. I felt it was all for the best; he needed a jolt to evolve from a rotter into a slightly less terrible version of himself.

One day, he returned with a glimmer of hope.

"I'm on the trail! I remember we went to the Coliseum on her last birthday. They change the program weekly."

"Professor Some One's Terpsichorean Cats," I supplied.

"They were there! I'll go to the Coliseum and dig the date out of the archives."

Six days later, he returned triumphant. "They were in the middle of May!"

"If you have any sense," I warned, "there won't be another time."

That night, he called again. "I've got it! It was a matinee, so it narrows it to Wednesday the seventh or Saturday the tenth."

"Yes," I said. "Unless they had daily matinees."

"I've remembered something else! On the day we went to the Coliseum, I lunched with you both at the Ritz. We forgot money, so I wrote a cheque for ten pounds to the hotel."

"Hunt up your cheque-book," I instructed. "Count the ten-pound cheques to the Ritz between May fifth and tenth."

He gulped. "The eighth. Reggie, you're a genius!"

"Good night," I said, hanging up.

I called the hotel near the Strand. "Put me through to Mrs. Cardew."

"It's late," the operator said.

"Get on with it," I urged.

"What is the matter?" Mary's voice trembled.

"My feet are cold," I lied. "But I called because Bobbie has remembered."

"Oh! Is that Mr. Pepper?"

"Yes. He's remembered it."

She screamed. I've often wondered what the Exchange girls must hear; the howls, the screams, the tales of frozen feet. It must be fascinating.

"He's remembered it!" she gasped. "Did you tell him?"

"No."

"Mr. Pepper."

"Yes?"

"Was he—was he very worried?"

"Worried! He was the most worried man between here and Edinburgh. He's been worrying as if he were paid by the nation."

"Oh! is that Mr. Pepper?" she interrupted, her voice rising. "What brutes men are! What horrid brutes! How you could stand by and see poor dear Bobbie worrying himself into a fever, when a word from you would have put everything right, I can't—"

"But—"

"And you call yourself his friend! His friend!" Her metallic laugh was unpleasant. "It shows how one can be deceived. I used to think you a kind-hearted man."

"But, I say, when I suggested the thing, you thought it perfectly—"

"I thought it hateful, abominable."

"But you said it was absolutely top—"

"I said nothing of the kind. And if I did, I didn't mean it. I don't wish to be unjust, Mr. Pepper, but to me there seems to be something positively fiendish in a man who goes out of his way to separate a husband from his wife, simply to amuse himself by gloating over his agony—"

"But you made me promise not to—" I squeaked.

"And if I did, do you suppose I didn't expect you to have the sense to break your promise?"

I hung up, crawled into bed, and felt a surge of triumph.
