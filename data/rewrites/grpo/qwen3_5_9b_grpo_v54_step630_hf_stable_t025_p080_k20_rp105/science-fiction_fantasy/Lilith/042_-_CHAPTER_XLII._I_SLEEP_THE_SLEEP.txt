When consciousness returned, the earth beneath me felt damp and yielding, my path to the grave hardening into quicksand. The ancient river swelled in its bed, pushing against its banks with a rising roar. Soon, it would cascade over the precipice, splitting into twin torrents—one to reclaim the orchard valley, the other to potentially drown the monster horde, leaving the Evil Wood isolated between them. With this knowledge settling in my mind, I turned immediately toward the home of those who had sent me.

Reaching the precipice, I threaded my way through the branches, driven by a lingering hope to visit Mara's cottage one last time before the final sleep. I knew the crossing points even if the river had already claimed them. Yet, when I arrived, the door stood ajar. Inside, bread and water remained untouched on the table, swallowed by a profound, heavy silence. I called out, my voice echoing into the void, but received no answer. I turned away, the silence pressing against my ears.

A short distance further, I found a gray-haired man sitting on the sand, weeping openly.

"What troubles you, sir?" I asked gently. "Have you been abandoned?"

"I weep," he replied, his voice thick with grief, "because they will not let me die. I have gone to the House of Death, and its mistress, despite my age, refuses me. If you know her, intercede for me."

"Nay, sir," I answered. "I cannot. She refuses none whom it is lawful for her to receive."

"How do you know this?" he demanded. "You have never sought death yourself! You are far too young to desire it!"

"I fear your words suggest that, if you were young again, neither would you desire it."

"Indeed, young sir, I would not! And I am certain you cannot."

"I may not be old enough to wish to die, but I am young enough to wish to live! Therefore, I go now to learn if she will eventually take me in. You wish to die because you do not care to live; she will not open her door to you, for no one can die who does not long to live."

"It ill becomes your youth to mock a friendless old man. Please, cease your riddles!"

"Did not the Mother tell you something similar?"

"In truth, I believe she did; but I paid little heed to her excuses."

"Ah, then, sir," I rejoined, "it is plain you have not yet learned to die, and I am heartbroken for you. Such was I, too, but for the Lady of Sorrow. I am indeed young, yet I have wept many tears. Pardon me if I presume to offer counsel: Go to the Lady of Sorrow, and 'take with both hands' what she offers. Her cottage lies yonder. She is not inside now, but her door stands open, and bread and water rest on her table. Enter, sit, eat, drink, and wait for her appearance. Then seek her counsel, for she is true, and her wisdom is vast."

He broke into fresh weeping, and I left him sobbing. Whether he understood my words, I feared not. But Mara would surely find him.

Sunset had fallen, and the moon had not yet risen, when I reached the abode of the monsters. The place lay as still as stone until I crossed the threshold, after which a noise of rushing waters erupted, accompanied by a great cry behind me that I ignored.

Before reaching the House of Death, the cold became biting and the darkness absolute; they merged into one, seeping into my bones. Yet, the candle of Eve, glowing from the window, guided me, keeping frost and murk at bay within my heart.

The door stood open, revealing an empty cottage. I sat down, disconsolate.

A profound loneliness seized me, unlike any I had felt in my wanderings. Thousands were near, yet none were with me. True, they were alive while I was dead, but whether by their life or my death, we were severed. They lived, yet I was not dead enough to perceive them as living; doubt gnawed at me. They were distant, and I had no helper to lay me beside them.

Never before had I known such desolation. I chided myself, telling myself the solitude was merely an illusion: I was awake, and they slept—that was all. They lay so still, silent, yet they were with me now, and soon, very soon, I would be with them.

I dropped Adam's old spade, and the dull thud of its fall on the clay floor seemed to reverberate from the chamber beyond. A childish terror gripped me. I sat staring at the coffin door. But Father Adam, Mother Eve, Sister Mara would come soon, and then—welcome the cold world and the white neighbors! I pushed aside my fears, lived a moment more, and loved my dead.

Something moved in the chamber of the dead. It emitted a sound like a dim, far-off vibration, yet it was not what I knew as sound. My soul leaped into my ears. Was it a mere thrill of the dead air, too faint to be heard but quivering in every spiritual sense? I KNEW without hearing, without feeling it!

The presence was coming! It drew nearer! Within my desertion, an infant hope awoke. The noiseless thrill reached the coffin door, became sound, and struck my ear.

The door began to move, with a low, soft creak of its hinges. It was opening! I ceased listening, staring expectantly.

It opened a fraction, and a face appeared in the gap. It was Lona's. Her eyes were closed, yet the face itself seemed to see me. It was white as Eve's, white as Mara's, but it did not shine like theirs. She spoke, and her voice was like a sleepy night-wind in the grass.

"Are you coming, king?" she said. "I cannot rest until you are with me, gliding down the river to the great sea and the beautiful dream-land. The sleepiness is full of lovely things: come and see them."

"Oh, my darling!" I cried. "Had I but known! I thought you were dead!"

She lay upon my bosom, cold as ice frozen to marble. She threw her arms, so white and feeble, around me and sighed.

"Carry me back to my bed, king. I want to sleep."

I bore her to the death-chamber, holding her tight lest she dissolve from my grasp. Unaware that I was being watched, I carried her straight to her couch.

"Lay me down," she whispered, "and cover me from the warm air; it hurts—a little. Your bed is there, next to mine. I shall see you when I wake."

She was already asleep. I threw myself onto my couch, blessed as no man has been on the eve of his wedding.

"Come, sweet cold," I murmured, "and still my heart speedily."

Instead, a glimmer of light filled the chamber, and I saw Father Adam approaching. He held no candle, yet I saw him clearly. Beside Lona's couch, he looked down upon her with a questioning smile, then greeted me across the space between us.

"We have climbed to the top of the hill to hear the waters on their way," he said. "They will reach the den of the monsters tonight. But why did you not await our return?"

"My child could not sleep," I answered.

"She is fast asleep!" he rejoined.

"Yes, now!" I said. "But she was awake when I laid her down."

"She was asleep all the time!" he insisted. "She was perhaps dreaming about you—and came to you?"

"She did."

"And did you not see that her eyes were closed?"

"Now I think of it, I did."

"If you had looked before you laid her down, you would have seen her asleep on the couch."

"That would have been terrible!"

"You would only have found that she was no longer in your arms."

"That would have been worse!"

"It is, perhaps, to think of; but to see it would not have troubled you."

"Dear father," I said, "how is it that I am not sleepy? I thought I should go to sleep like the Little Ones the moment I laid my head down!"

"Your hour is not quite come. You must have food ere you sleep."

"Oh, I ought not to have lain down without your leave, for I cannot sleep without your help! I will get up at once!"

But I found my own weight more than I could move.

"There is no need: we will serve you here," he answered. "Do you not feel cold?"

"Not too cold to lie still, but perhaps too cold to eat!"

He came to the side of my couch, bent over me, and breathed upon my heart. Instantly, I was warm.

As he left me, I heard a voice and knew it was the Mother's. She was singing, and her song was sweet, soft, and low. I thought she sat by my bed in the dark; but before it ceased, her song soared aloft, seeming to come from the throat of a woman-angel, high above all the region of larks, higher than man had ever lifted his heart. I heard every word she sang, yet kept only this:

I thought I had heard this song before.

Then the three came to my couch together, bringing me bread and wine. I sat up to partake. Adam stood on one side of me, Eve and Mara on the other.

"You are good indeed, father Adam, mother Eve, sister Mara," I said, "to receive me! In my soul, I am ashamed and sorry!"

"We knew you would come again!" answered Eve.

"How could you know it?" I returned.

"Because here was I, born to look after my brothers and sisters!" Mara answered with a smile.

"Every creature must one night yield himself and lie down," Adam replied. "He was made for liberty, and must not be left a slave!"

"It will be late, I fear, ere all have lain down!" I said.

"There is no early or late here," he rejoined. "For him the true time then first begins who lays himself down. Men are not coming home fast; women are coming faster. A desert, wide and dreary, parts him who lies down to die from him who lies down to live. The former may well make haste, but here is no haste."

"To our eyes," said Eve, "you were coming all the time: we knew Mara would find you, and you must come!"

"How long is it since my father lay down?" I asked.

"I have told you that years are of no consequence in this house," Adam answered. "We do not heed them. Your father will wake when his morning comes. Your mother, next to whom you are lying—"

"Oh, then, it IS my mother!" I exclaimed.

"Yes—she with the wounded hand," he assented. "She will be up and away long ere your morning is ripe."

"I am sorry."

"Rather be glad."

"It must be a sight for God Himself to see such a woman come awake!"

"It is indeed a sight for God, a sight that makes her Maker glad! He sees of the travail of His soul, and is satisfied! Look at her once more, and sleep."

He let the rays of his candle fall upon her beautiful face.

"She looks much younger!" I said.

"She IS much younger," he replied. "Even Lilith already begins to look younger!"

I lay down, blissfully drowsy.

"But when you see your mother again," he continued, "you will not at first know her. She will go on steadily growing younger until she reaches the perfection of her womanhood—a splendour beyond foresight. Then she will open her eyes, behold on one side her husband, on the other her son—and rise and leave them to go to a father and a brother more to her than they."

I heard as one in a dream. I was very cold, but the cold caused me no suffering. I felt them place upon me the white garment of the dead. Then I forgot everything. The night about me was pale with sleeping faces, but I was asleep also, nor did I know that I slept.
