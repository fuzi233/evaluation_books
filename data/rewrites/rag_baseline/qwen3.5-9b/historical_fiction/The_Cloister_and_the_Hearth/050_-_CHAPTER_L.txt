Among the strangers of this foreign land, Margaret Brandt discovered a comparative happiness, a fragile sanctuary amidst the chaos of displacement. Yet, fortune, capricious as ever, soon spun a thread of unexpected relief. A civic dignitary, plagued by a whimsical and erratic illness that led him from physician to physician until he teetered on the brink of death, summoned Peter. The old healer found the man reduced to a husk, drained by bloodletting and purging. With a flourish of desperation, Peter emptied a battalion of bottles from the window, leaving the air thick with their scent; he whipped egg yolks into a frothy paste using neat Schiedam, administering it in cautious doses, followed by meat stewed in red wine and water, shredded with mild febrifuge herbs that caused no further harm. Slowly, the patient regained his footing, emerging as a grotesque hybrid of man and pillowcase. Being a voluble man, the dignitary spread Peter's reputation across every cobblestone street, granting the artist—who had long deserved renown in vain—a rapid ascent built entirely on luck.

Things appeared bright. The old man's pride was finally cheered, and his purse began to swell. Yet, he squandered much of this newfound wealth on sovereign herbs and choice drugs, intending to invest it all in the arcane arts. It was Margaret who intercepted this flow, white-mailing a portion of the gain. But the victory came too late; the happy excitement itself proved fatal.

One evening, as he bid her goodnight, his voice stumbled, inarticulate and fractured. By the next morning, he lay speechless, barely sensible.

Margaret, who had served as her father's attentive pupil for years, saw immediately that he had suffered a paralytic stroke. Trusting neither her own judgment nor the silence of the room, she ran to summon a doctor. One of those who had previously been obstructed by Peter's methods—and thus spared the fate of killing the dignitary—arrived cheerfully to confirm her fears. He advocated for immediate bleeding. She refused. "He was always against blooding," she insisted, "especially the old."

Peter survived, but he was never the same man again. His memory became a treacherous landscape, unreliable and foggy; naturally, he could no longer be trusted to prescribe. Patients, drawn by rumors of a new doctor, waited in vain for his convalescence. Misery stared her in the face, sharp and undeniable. Resolved to seek counsel and comfort, she turned to her cousin, William Johnson, from whom she had hitherto kept aloof due to pride and poverty.

She found him and his servant seated in the same room, neither appearing improved by liquor. Mastering every sign of surprise, she offered her greetings and announced her intent to discuss a family matter, glancing quietly at the servant to signal her presence. The woman took the cue, but not as expected.

"Oh, you can speak before me, can she not, my old man?"

At this familiarity, Margaret flushed crimson. "I cry you mercy, mistress. I knew not my cousin had fallen into the custom of this town. Well, I must take a fitter opportunity," she said, rising to depart.

"I wot not what ye mean by custom o' the town," the woman snapped, bouncing up. "But this I know: 'tis the part of a faithful servant to keep her master from being preyed on by his beggarly kin."

Margaret retorted sharply. "Ye are too modest, mistress. Ye are no servant. Your speech betrays you. 'Tis not till the ape hath mounted the tree that she shows her tail so plain. Nay, there sits the servant; God help him! And while so it is, fear not thou his kin will ever be so poor in spirit as to come where the likes of you can flout their dole." Casting a look of mute reproach at her cousin for remaining passive and silent throughout the exchange, she turned and walked out haughtily, refusing to shed a single tear until she reached home and allowed herself to think. Now, she faced the grim reality: two men to lodge and feed, plus another mouth arriving into the world.

Yet, this last arrival, though the most helpless of all, would become their best friend.

Nature was strong in Margaret Brandt—the same fierce instinct that drives beasts, birds, and insects to cunningly provide food and shelter for progeny yet unborn. Stimulated by this primal force, she sat and brooded, thinking intensely, calculating how to outrun destitution. Even with five gold pieces remaining, she saw starvation approaching with inevitable footsteps.

Her sex, when deviating from custom to think with male intensity, reasoned just as effectively as any man. She rose, ordered Martin to move Peter to another room, and transformed her own space into a sanctuary of order. She polished the glass globe, suspending it from the ceiling, dusted the crocodile, and nailed him firmly to the outside wall. After duly instructing Martin, she set him to play the role of a lounging sentinel at the street door, tasked with telling the crocodile-bitten visitors that a great, aged, and learned alchemist resided within, who in his moments of recreation amused himself by curing mortal diseases.

Patients soon arrived, demanding to see the leech. "That might not be," Margaret replied smoothly. "He is deep in his studies, searching for the grand elixir, and not even princes could command his speech. They must tell me their symptoms, and return in two hours." And oh, the mysterious powers of performance! When they returned, the drug or draught was invariably ready. For a worshipful patient, she would carefully scan his face, feel his pulse and skin, and listen to his tale, then walk softly to Peter's room. There, she would ponder how her father, whose system she had long observed, would treat the case. She would scribble an illegible scrawl filled with cabalistic letters, bring it down reverently, and show it to the patient. "Could he read that?" she would ask. The response was usually, "I am no reader," or, with admiration, "Nay, mistress, nought can I make on't."

"Ay, but I can. 'Tis sovereign. Look on thyself as cured!" If she had the materials on hand—and she was too good an economist not to favor those medicines already in her stock—she would sometimes allow the patient to watch her compound them, anxiously consulting the sacred prescription lest Great Science suffer in her hands. Thus, she sent them away relieved of cash but with pockets full of medicine and minds full of faith, humbugged to their hearts' content. *Populus vult decipi.* When they departed, she would retrieve two little boxes Gerard had made her; on one she wrote *Today*, on the other *Tomorrow*. Into *Today* she placed the smaller coins, and into *Tomorrow* the larger ones, alongside the few gold pieces that had survived the journey from Sevenbergen and the expenses of housekeeping in a strange place. In this way, she met current expenses and laid by for the rainy day she foresaw, mixing drugs with simples and vice with virtue. On this last score, her conscience pricked her sorely. After each day's comedy, she knelt and prayed God to forgive her "for the sake of her child." But lo and behold, cures were reported; thus, her conscience began to harden.

Martin Wittenhaagen had recently become a dead weight on her hands. Like most men who had endured great hardships, he had stiffened suddenly. Though less supple, he was as strong as ever, capable at his own pace of carrying the doctor herself round Rotterdam. Instead, he carried her slops.

In this new business, he displayed the qualities of a soldier: unreasoning obedience, punctuality, accuracy, dispatch, and drunkenness.

He fell among "good fellows"; the blackguards plied him with Schiedam, and he babbled and bragged. Doctor Margaret had risen very high in his estimation. All this brandishing of a crocodile for a standard, setting a dotard in ambush, disposing of slops, and taking good money in exchange struck him not as science but as something far superior: Strategy. He boasted in his cups and before mixed companies how "me and my General we are a biting of the burghers."

When this revelation had time to leaven the city, his General, Doctor Margaret, received a summons from the constables. They took her, trembling and begging subordinate machines to forgive her, before the burgomaster. Standing by his side were real physicians, a terrible row in long robes and square caps, accusing her of practicing unlawfully on the bodies of the Duke's lieges. At first, she was too frightened to speak. A novice like her, the very name of "Law" paralyzed her. But questioned closely, yet not so harshly as if she had been ugly, she told the truth. She had long been her father's pupil and had merely followed his system, curing many. "And it is not for myself in very deed, sirs," she pleaded, "but I have two poor helpless honest men at home upon my hands, and how else can I keep them? Ah, good sirs, let a poor girl make her bread honestly; ye hinder them not to make it idly and shamefully; and oh, sirs, ye are husbands, ye are fathers; ye cannot but see I have reason to work and provide as best I may."
