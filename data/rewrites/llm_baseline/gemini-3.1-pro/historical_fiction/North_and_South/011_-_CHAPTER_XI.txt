The lingering aroma of roasted mutton and the astringent warmth of steeped black tea had barely begun to fade from the parlor when Mr. Hale returned from seeing his guest downstairs. 

“Margaret!” he said, his voice cutting through the evening’s quiet domestic rhythm. “I could not help watching your face with some anxiety when Mr. Thornton made his confession of having been a shop-boy. I knew it all along from Mr. Bell, so I was aware of what was coming; but I half expected to see you get up and leave the room.”

“Oh, papa! You don’t mean that you thought me so silly?” Margaret turned from the table, her hands still carrying the tactile memory of the fine porcelain cups. “I relished that account of himself better than any other morsel he offered. Everything else revolted me with its metallic hardness. But he spoke of his past so simply, shedding the dominant lexicon of commerce and adopting a tender, unadorned reverence for his mother. I was far less likely to leave the room then than when he was boasting about Milton, wielding the vocabulary of industry as if there were not such another place in the world. He quietly professed to despise people for careless, wasteful improvidence, reducing their complex struggles to a sterile commercial ledger, without ever seeming to think it his duty to offer them the very nourishment and training his mother gave him. No, his history as a shop-boy was the one thing I found palpable and true.”

“I am surprised at you, Margaret,” said her mother, adjusting her shawl as if to ward off a sudden chill. “You who were always accusing people of being shoppy at Helstone! I don’t think, Mr. Hale, you have done quite right in introducing such a person to us without telling us what he had been. I was very much afraid of showing him how shocked I was. His father ‘dying in miserable circumstances.’ Why, it might have been in the workhouse.”

“I am not sure it was not worse than the workhouse,” replied her husband, settling into his chair. The room’s acoustic tranquility seemed to deepen, holding the weight of generational tragedy. “His father speculated wildly, failed, and then killed himself, unable to bear the disgrace. All his former friends shrank from the disclosures of his dishonest gambling—wild, hopeless struggles with other people’s money. No one came forward to help the mother and this boy. There was another child, a girl, too young to earn; but she had to be kept. So they left Milton. I knew he had gone into a shop, and that his earnings, with some fragment of property secured to his mother, kept them alive. Mr. Bell said they absolutely lived upon water-porridge for years.” 

Margaret shuddered at the gustatory bleakness of the image—a gruel of sheer utilitarian desperation, devoid of any sensory comfort, reducing the profound, life-affirming ritual of sustenance to mere fuel for survival. How, Mr. Hale did not know, but long after the creditors had abandoned hope, this young man returned to Milton. He went to each creditor, paying the first installment of his father’s debts. 

“There was no noise,” Mr. Hale emphasized, an acoustic detail that struck Margaret profoundly. “No gathering of creditors, no loud assertions of pride. It was done in absolute, honorable silence. All was paid at last, helped materially by a crabbed old creditor taking Mr. Thornton in as a partner.”

“That really is fine,” said Margaret. “What a pity such a nature should be tainted by his position as a Milton manufacturer.”

“How tainted?” asked her father.

“Oh, papa, by testing everything through the sterile standard of wealth. When he spoke of mechanical powers, he viewed them only as tools to extend trade. And the poor men around him—they are cast out of the pale of his sympathies because they lack his iron nature. He translates their poverty into vice.”

“Not vicious; he never said that. Improvident and self-indulgent were his words.”

Margaret hesitated as she collected her mother’s working materials, preparing to retreat into the evening’s cyclical close. She wished to offer an acknowledgment that would please her father, though the untranslatability of her own subjective experience made the admission difficult. “Papa, I do think Mr. Thornton a very remarkable man; but personally, I don’t like him at all.”

“And I do!” her father laughed. “Personally, as you call it, and all. But good-night, child. Your mother looks sadly tired to-night.”

Margaret had noticed her mother’s jaded appearance with mounting anxiety. The rhythms of Milton were a harsh rupture from their agrarian past in Helstone, where Mrs. Hale had moved perpetually in the fresh, sweet-scented open air. Here, the olfactory landscape was choked with soot, the air deprived of all revivifying principle. Domestic worries pressed upon the women in new, sordid forms, eroding their old cultural memories. Margaret frequently caught the hushed, mysterious consultations between her mother and Dixon in the bedroom—murmured acoustics of distress from which Dixon would emerge cross and weeping. Once, Margaret had stolen in and caught fragments of a prayer for strength to endure severe bodily suffering. Despite Margaret’s attempts to bridge this silence with gentle caresses, a profound secret remained withheld.

She lay awake long into the night, mapping out a way to restore some semblance of harmony to their domestic routine. She determined to hunt down a servant to assist Dixon, scouring the city’s chaotic register offices for days, absorbing the exhausting clatter of unlikely candidates.

One afternoon, navigating the heavy, smoke-laden atmosphere of the streets, she encountered Bessy Higgins. 

“Well, Bessy, how are you? Better, I hope, now the wind has changed.”

“Better and not better, if yo’ know what that means,” Bessy replied.

Margaret smiled gently at the subtle untranslatability of the girl’s native phrasing. “Not exactly.”

“I’m better in not being torn to pieces by coughing o’ nights, but I’m weary and tired o’ Milton, and longing to get away to the land o’ Beulah. And when I think I’m farther and farther off, my heart sinks, and I’m no better; I’m worse.” The mythic landscape of Beulah was no abstract theology to Bessy, but a visceral, sustaining hunger woven seamlessly into her everyday despair. 

Margaret walked beside her, her own healthy clinging to life shrinking from the specter of death. “Bessy, do you wish to die?” 

“If yo’d led the life I have, and getten as weary of it as I have,” Bessy said, her voice a fragile reed against the distant, rhythmic thumping of the mills. “And thought at times, ‘maybe it’ll last for fifty or sixty years’—and got dizzy and dazed, and sick, as each of them sixty years seemed to spin about me, and mock me with its length of hours and minutes... oh, wench! I tell thee thou’d been glad enough when th’ doctor said he feared thou’d never see another winter.”

“Why, Bessy, what kind of a life has yours been?”

“Nought worse than many others’, I reckon. Only I fretted against it, and they didn’t.”

“But what was it? You know, I’m a stranger here. I’m not so quick at understanding what you mean as if I’d lived all my life at Milton.”

“If yo’d ha’ come to our house when yo’ said yo’ would, I could maybe ha’ told you. But father says yo’re just like th’ rest on ’em; it’s out o’ sight out o’ mind wi’ you.”

Margaret absorbed the sting of this truth. “I had forgotten what I said for the time. I should have thought of it again when I was less busy. May I go with you now?”

Bessy gave her a sharp, assessing glance that melted into wistful longing. “I ha’ none so many to care for me; if yo’ care, yo’ may come.”

They turned into a small court branching off a squalid street. “Yo’ll not be daunted if father’s at home, and speaks a bit gruffish,” Bessy warned. “He took a mind to ye, and he thought a deal o’ your coming; and just because he liked yo’ he was vexed.”

Nicholas was not at home, but a taller, stronger sister was at the wash-tub. The girl knocked the furniture about in a rough, capable way, generating an acoustic violence—the scraping of wood, the slosh of lye-scented water, the clatter of the fire-irons—that physically assaulted Margaret’s senses. She shrank back, her psychological well-being instantly attuned to the torment such industrial dissonance must inflict on poor Bessy, who collapsed into the nearest chair.

Margaret asked the sister for a cup of water. While the girl clattered away to fetch it, Margaret unloosed Bessy’s bonnet strings. 

“Do you think such life as this is worth caring for?” gasped Bessy.

Margaret did not answer with words. She took the cup and held the water to Bessy’s lips. It was a profound, elemental communion. Bessy took a long, feverish draught, the sensory coolness of the liquid offering brief salvation, before falling back with closed eyes. 

She murmured, “They shall hunger no more, neither thirst any more: neither shall the sun light on them, nor any heat.” 

Margaret bent close. “Bessy, don’t be impatient with your life. Remember who gave it you, and made it what it is!”

“Now, I’ll not have my wench preached to,” came Nicholas’s voice. He had entered unheard beneath the metallic din of the wash-tub. “She’s bad enough, as it is, with her dreams and her methodee fancies, and her visions of cities with goulden gates. If it amuses her I let it abe, but I’m none going to have more stuff poured into her.”

Margaret faced him, navigating the stark power dynamic embedded in their clashing lexicons. “But surely you believe in what I said, that God gave her life, and ordered what kind of life it was to be?”

“I believe what I see, and no more. That’s what I believe, young woman. I did hear a young lass make an ado about knowing where we lived. And my wench here flushed up many a time at the sound of a strange step. But hoo’s come at last,—and hoo’s welcome, as long as hoo’ll keep from preaching on what hoo knows nought about.”

Bessy reached for Margaret’s arm, her native dialect carrying the weight of her desperate affection. “Don’t be vexed wi’ him. He’s a rare good man, is father—but oh! what he says at times makes me long to die more than ever, for I want to know so many things, and am so tossed about wi’ wonder.”

“Poor wench—poor old wench,” Nicholas softened. “I’m loth to vex thee; but a man mun speak out for the truth, and when I see the world bothering itself wi’ things it knows nought about, and leaving undone all the things that lie in disorder close at its hand—why, I say, leave a’ this talk about religion alone, and set to work on what yo’ see and know. That’s my creed. It’s simple, and not far to fetch.”

“Don’t think hardly on him,” Bessy pleaded, a feverish flame sparking in her eye. “I sometimes think I shall be moped wi’ sorrow even in the City of God, if father is not there. But you will be there, father! You shall! Oh! my heart!” 

She became ghastly pale, clutching her chest. Margaret caught her, drawing the weary head to rest upon her own bosom. She lifted the thin, damp hair from Bessy’s temples and bathed them with the remaining water, an ancient, mythic gesture of care integrated seamlessly into the squalid room. Nicholas understood her silent, urgent signs with the quickness of love. Even the sister moved with laborious gentleness at Margaret’s hushed command, restoring acoustic peace to the cramped space. 

When the spasm passed, Bessy roused herself. “I’ll go to bed. But yo’ll come again,—I know yo’ will—but just say it!”

“I will come again to-morrow,” Margaret promised. 

As Margaret rose, Nicholas struggled against his own rigid vocabulary to offer something transcendent. “I could wish there were a God, if it were only to ask Him to bless thee.”

Margaret walked home, her mind turning in sad, thoughtful circles. She was late for tea. At Helstone, such unpunctuality at meal-times was a disruption of the sacred agrarian rhythm, a great fault in her mother’s eyes. But here, the domestic timeline had frayed, and this irregularity had lost its power to irritate. Margaret almost longed for the old complainings as she surveyed the table.

“Have you met with a servant, dear?” Mrs. Hale asked.

“No, mamma; that Anne Buckley would never have done.”

“Suppose I try,” said Mr. Hale. “I may be the Cinderella to put on the slipper after all.”

Margaret, still steeped in the sensory exhaustion of the Higginses' court, could hardly smile. “What would you do, papa?”

“Why, I should apply to some good house-mother to recommend me one known to herself.”

“But we must first catch our house-mother.”

“She is coming into the snare to-morrow. My paragon pupil has told me that his mother intends to call on Mrs. and Miss Hale to-morrow.”

“Mrs. Thornton!” exclaimed Mrs. Hale. “Perhaps she may have a relation who might suit us. She sounded to be such a careful economical person, that I should like any one out of the same family.”

“My dear,” Mr. Hale said, alarmed. “Pray don’t go off on that idea. I fancy Mrs. Thornton is as haughty and proud in her way as our little Margaret here is in hers, and that she completely ignores that old time of trial, and poverty, and water-porridge, of which he speaks so openly.”

“Take notice that is not my kind of haughtiness, papa,” Margaret countered.

They cared too little to ask in what manner the son had spoken of the mother. Margaret recognized only that the cyclical routine of her morning would be disrupted; she must stay in to receive this call, delaying her return to Bessy, for her mother could never be left to bear the whole weight of entertaining such a formidable presence alone.
