Madame de Bernstein was scarcely less gratified than her nephews by the conclusion of Harry's final audience with Lady Maria. That evening, Lord George dispatched a billet detailing the proceedings, and shortly thereafter, his rarely-visited nephew, Lord Castlewood, arrived to pay his respects. With the business between Maria and Harry concluded, the necessity for reticence vanished; my Lord Castlewood was never one to hesitate when frankness served a purpose. "The game is played and over," he declared, shedding any remaining pretense. "Now that the moves, stratagems, and finesse are revealed, what need remains for hypocrisy?"

"She is my own sister," Lord Castlewood continued, his tone affectionate yet resigned. "She possesses few further opportunities to establish herself. While I may not have approved of the match in every respect, and I pity your ladyship's favorite Virginian youth, such a stroke of fortune could not simply be discarded. I was bound to support my flesh and blood."

"Your candour honors your lordship," Madame de Bernstein replied, "and your devotion to your sister is truly edifying!"

"Nay, we have lost the game, and I speak without bitterness," Lord Castlewood bowed smoothly. "It is not for you, who have triumphed, to harbor malice."

Madame de Bernstein protested that she had never been in better spirits. "Confess now, Eugene," she pressed, "that Maria's visit to Harry at the spunging-house—that touching surrender of all his gifts—was a stroke of your invention?"

"Pity for the young man, and a sense of what Maria owed to her friend, her affianced lover in misfortune, were surely sufficient motives for her actions," Lord Castlewood answered demurely.

"But 'twas you who advised her, my good nephew?"

With a shrug, Castlewood admitted he had counseled his sister to see Mr. Henry Warrington. "Yet, we might have won despite your ladyship," he mused, "had not the elder brother made his appearance. I have been attempting to console my poor Maria by reminding her that losing this wager was, after all, a piece of good fortune."

"Suppose she had married Harry, and then Cousin George had emerged?" the Baroness suggested.

"Effectivement!" cried Eugene, taking a pinch of snuff. "Let us be grateful to the grave for disgorging its dead in time! I must admit, Mr. George Warrington appears a man of sense, and not more selfish than other elder sons. My poor Molly fancied him a greenhorn, like his younger brother. She hoped he might share equally with Twin Junior; so infatuated was she, I believe she would have taken him. 'Harry Warrington with half a loaf might suffice,' I reasoned, 'but Harry Warrington with no bread, my dear!'"

"How no bread?" asked the Baroness.

"Well, no bread save at his brother's side-table," Eugene explained. "The elder brother said as much."

"What a hard-hearted wretch!" exclaimed Madame de Bernstein.

"Ah, bah! I play with you, aunt, *cartes sur table*!" Lord Castlewood laughed. "Mr. George merely did what anyone else would do; we have no right to be angry. Molly herself acknowledged as much after her initial grief subsided, and I brought her to listen to reason. The silly old creature! So wild about a young lad at her time of life!"

"'Twas a real passion, I almost believe," Madame de Bernstein observed.

"You should have heard her take leave of him. *C'était touchant*, I swear! Before George, I could not help myself. The young fellow, with muddy stockings and hair falling over his eyes, threw himself among us at dinner, making his offer to Molly in a frank, noble manner, and in good language. Her reply? *Begad*, it recalled Mrs. Woffington in the new Scottish play, *Douglas*. She clung to the lad, bidding him adieu in heartrending accents, then stepped out of the room in stately despair—no more chocolate, thank you. If she had committed a *mauvais pas*, no one could retreat with more dignity. 'Twas a masterly withdrawal after a defeat. We were starved out of position, but we retired with all the honours of war."

"Molly will not die of disappointment!" said the Baroness, sipping her tea.

Indeed, how could her ladyship know? She did not marry Dr. Tusher until late in life, nor assume the title of Madame de Bernstein until later. As the poet Old Dido noted, ignorance of misfortune breeds lack of compassion for the wretched.

In the little world, people quarrel, abuse one another, and remain estranged for ages. But in the great world, differences are temporary. Parties separate, then reunite without inquiry, granting a stray prodigal or puppy an amnesty regardless of the ugly company kept during absence. For six months, the Castlewoods and Madame de Bernstein had battled for possession of poor Harry Warrington, leaving the two branches of the Esmond family apart. Now, with the matter settled, they were free to meet again as though no division had ever existed. Madame de Bernstein drove her great coach to Lady Castlewood's rout, and the Esmond ladies greeted her with smiles, loving each other as fervently as before.

"So, sir, I hear you acted like a hard-hearted monster regarding your poor brother Harry!" the Baroness exclaimed, delighted and brandishing her stick at George.

"I acted solely upon your ladyship's hint, wishing to discern whether his kin sought him for himself or his reputed wealth," George replied, flushing slightly.

"Nay, Maria could not marry a pauper utterly destitute, whose elder brother vowed to give him nothing!"

"I did it for the best, madam," George insisted, still blushing.

"And so thou didst, O thou hypocrite!" cried the old lady.

"Hypocrite, madam! And why?" asked Mr. Warrington, drawing himself up with considerable state.

"I know all, my infant!" the Baroness declared in French. "Thou art very like thy grandfather. Come, I embrace thee! Harry has told me everything, and thou hast divided thy little patrimony with him!"

"It was but natural, madam. We have shared common hearts and purses since birth. I merely feigned hard-heartedness to test those people yonder," George said, his eyes filling.

"And wilt thou divide Virginia with him too?" asked the Bernstein.

"I cannot say so. It would not be just," replied Mr. Warrington. "Land must pass to the eldest-born, and Harry would not have it otherwise. Perhaps I shall die, or my mother may outlive both of us. But half of what is mine belongs to him. Remember, he was extravagant only because he was mistaken regarding his position."

"But it is a knight of old, it is a Bayard, it is the grandfather come to life!" cried Madame de Bernstein to her attendant as she prepared to retire.

That evening, when the lads departed, it was to poor Harry she gave two fingers, and to George the rouged cheek—a mark of favor so profound it caused George to blush as deeply as the often-dyed rose beside it.

Though Harry Warrington was naturally devoid of envy and held his brother as his chief guide and superior, a profound sense of humiliation and disappointment oppressed the young man following his deposition from the status of Fortunate Youth and heir to boundless Virginian territories. Friends at Kensington promised and vowed to love him all the better after his fall; Harry bowed low and expressed gratitude, yet he could not help noticing that at the state entertainment hosted by Lord Castlewood, George was all-consuming to the cousins. All conversation, compliments, and attentions centered on George, while Harry received notice only from poor Maria. She followed him with wistful glances, her eyes conveying dismal reproaches, as if blaming him for accepting her generosity. *"Ah!"* her gaze seemed to whisper, *"It is mighty well of you, Harry, to accept the freedom I offered, but I never intended for you to be so pleased at being let off."* She had given him up, yet she did not fully forgive him for taking her at her word. She would not have him, and yet she would. Oh, my young friends, how delightful the beginning of a love affair, and how undignified, sometimes, is the end! What a romantic vista opens before young Damon and Phillis—or their middle-aged counterparts—when, after declaring their artless loves, they twine their arms around each other's waists, gazing upon the charming *pays du tendre* spread before them! Into that realm, so intimately linked, they wander until extreme old age. Rocks and roaring rivers may lie ahead, but surely Damon's strong love carries Sweetheart across them? Dragons and dangers may block the path, but shall not his courageous sword cut them down? Then, at eve, how they rest cuddled together like two babes in the woods, moss their couch, stars their canopy, arms their mutual pillows!
