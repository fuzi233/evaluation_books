On a clear evening near the end of July, Daniel Deronda took his skiff out from Putney and let the Thames have him as it would. The river was his truest holiday—more faithful than drawing-rooms, more honest than books read out of obedience. In town with the Mallingers, where talk thickened like over-salted soup and a man’s hours were portioned out by other people’s appetites, he could find nowhere the same clean emptiness as he found between two reaches of water.

He had returned to England more than a year ago with the convenient understanding that his education was complete, and that now he would step, without stumble or protest, into the place prepared for him in English society. Sir Hugo—excellent, worldly, and satisfied with the world because it had been satisfied with him—had pressed law upon Daniel as one presses a steadying draught into a reluctant hand: not because it delighted, but because it filled time. Daniel had taken it up, and the pages had done what dry bread does to a mouth already parched—made him more conscious of thirst. The more he tried to fix himself to a profession, the more his uncertainty rooted itself, not like a weed you can pull, but like an old tree that has chosen its soil.

So he rowed. It was labor enough to be wholesome, solitary enough to be sweet, and its music—water lipping the blade, the hollow knock of oar against thole-pin, the far-off clink of chain and creak of timber—made a kind of acoustic shelter. Even the city’s noises came to him softened: wheels were muffled into a distant sigh; the sharp cries of boys and vendors became mere bright flecks on the air, like birds crossing a wide sky. He had often thought, without putting it into any learned phrase, how much a man’s courage is governed by what he hears. There are sounds that press the mind down—hammering, grinding, unrelenting iron—and there are sounds that give it room. The river, at least, did not bully.

As he pulled steadily, his own voice—half unconsciously—fell into subdued snatches of a melody that had clung to him all day, as a scent clings to the cuff of a coat after passing a garden wall. It was the gondolier’s song in Rossini’s *Otello*, carrying Dante’s words—those words that taste bitter on the tongue like burnt sugar: *Nessun maggior dolore che ricordarsi del tempo felice nella miseria*. He sang under his breath, not from any fashionable sentimentality, but because the cadence matched the stroke of the oars, and because the line itself had that terrible exactness which makes sorrow feel as if it were being named for the first time.

Near Kew Bridge, between six and seven, the Thames was no hermitage. There were saunterers on the towing-path; a few boats moved with slow purpose; and a great barge came pushing its heavy way, making the water thicken and lift in broad ridges. Daniel guided his boat aside, rested on his oar within a couple of yards of the river-brink, and let the barge go past.

His voice, continuing without his will, fell to a pianissimo on the last words—*nella miseria*—and the wail died into the river’s breath.

Then, close by, on the bank, he saw a figure that seemed like the bodily shape of the very misery he had been uttering.

A girl—hardly more than eighteen—slight, low in stature, with a face so small and fine it might have been carved out of some pale stone. Dark curls were pushed behind her ears under a large black hat; a long woollen cloak hung on her shoulders. Her hands were clasped before her and hung down as if she had forgotten they could do anything else. She stared at the river with a despair so still it was like an image set there for warning.

The shock of her presence made him stop singing at once. At the sudden silence she changed her attitude, as if the broken thread of sound had snapped inside her, and she turned with a frightened glance—then met his face. For a moment they looked straight at each other, longer than seemed possible between strangers. Her look had the timid directness of some gentle animal caught and not yet decided whether to flee—no blush, no appeal, only that startled watchfulness which is more moving than any confession.

Something in him sprang forward—pity, yes, but also a quick reverence, as if he had come upon a sacred distress. Yet she turned away almost at once and walked to a bench under a tree.

Poorly dressed, melancholy women were not rare along London’s edges; and the very delicacy that made her striking made it impossible for him to intrude. He turned his boat and rowed on, as fast as if he could put distance between himself and the ache her face had planted in him.

He could not. Again and again, as the water carried him, her image rose as distinctly as if it were pressed into a dark gem: brown-black drapery, white face, small features, the long lashes shadowing eyes too heavy with unshed tears. He found himself weaving possible histories behind her—then checked himself with a half-smile at his own romantic prejudice that beauty must have romance attached to it like a ribbon. And yet he could not quite scold himself down. There are sorrows that the world will not notice unless a delicate face bears them—like a rare dish left untouched because it is served in coarse ware; the hunger is the same, but the eye is bribed into attention only by a finer vessel.

He slackened his rowing, let the tide begin to take him back. The evening light lengthened; thought and feeling slid together in him as smoothly as water over weed. The river, widening and darkening, drew him into that solemn passivity which can feel like wisdom because it is quiet. As he drifted, he heard the day’s sounds thinning: fewer voices from the shore, fewer oars, the distant rumble of London held off by the broad coolness of the stream. The world’s industrial roar seemed, from here, like an impertinence—an endless grinding that pretended to be progress while it simply drowned the subtler lives beneath it.

By the time he came with the tide past Richmond Bridge the sun was near setting—his favourite hour, when tree and building mass into darkness against the double glow of sky and river, and every sound seems chosen, not accidental. He searched for a solitary spot to lodge his boat against the bank, one where he could lie back on the cushions and watch the light go out and the first stars answer, one by one, that ancient call of evening.

He found it on the bend opposite Kew Gardens: broad water before him taking the sky’s gold and rose, while he lay in shadow. He stretched himself on his back, hands behind his head, his face turned to the open west. So placed, he could see all round without being readily seen—an odd comfort to a man who had always disliked the notion that his mere appearance had power over other people’s attention.

He was beginning to lose himself in that half-speculative fancy of shifting one’s centre until the self feels as external as the landscape—when he became aware of movement on the opposite bank, where willow bushes made a low wavering line.

He turned his eyes, and in the first instant felt a darting presentiment—like the recognition of a flavour before you can name the herb.

It was the girl again.

The dying sunlight struck her small face so that it seemed lit from within, while everything around her sank into dusk. Daniel held himself motionless, afraid that any sudden stir might startle her away. She looked round, but the apparent emptiness seemed to reassure her. She slipped her hat among the willows, took off her woollen cloak, and sat down with a deliberateness that made his throat tighten. Then she dipped the cloak in the water and held it there a little while; drew it up with effort; rose.

The meaning was clear—terribly clear. She meant to wrap that soaked weight around herself as a drowning shroud, to make the river’s work swift and certain.

There was no time to hesitate about frightening her.

He rose, seized his oars, and drove across. Her place lay a little below him; the current helped. At the first sign of movement from the opposite bank, she sank down again, clutching the cloak half out of the water. She crouched and covered her face, as if hoping she had not truly been seen—as if the boatman might be merely crossing by accident.

But soon he was close, steadying his boat against the bank. He spoke gently, with the softness one uses toward a creature in pain.

“Don’t be afraid. You are unhappy. Pray, trust me. Tell me what I can do to help you.”

She lifted her head. The light was behind him now, so his face was clearer to her than hers was to him, and he saw recognition in her gaze. They looked at each other again—those same long moments in which speech seems too blunt.

At last she said, in a low sweet voice, her accent distinct in a way that suggested foreignness and yet could not be called foreign, “I saw you before,” and then, as if her mind were moving by music rather than by sense, she added, “*nella miseria*.”

He could not at first follow. He had a fear—strong and shameful—that her distress had loosened the mind’s hold on reality.

“It was you, singing?” she went on hesitatingly. “*Nessun maggior dolore*.”

The words, uttered in that soft undertone, seemed to carry their melody back into his ear.

“Ah, yes,” he said, understanding. “I am often singing them. But I fear you will injure yourself staying here. Pray let me take you in my boat to some place of safety. And that wet cloak—let me take it.”

He did not touch it without her permission. Even as he spoke he thought she shrank, clasping it more tenaciously; then her eyes held his with a question.

“You look good,” she said. “Perhaps it is God’s command.”

“Do trust me. Let me help you. I will die before I will let any harm come to you.”

She rose from her crouching posture, dragged the saturated cloak, then let it drop—too heavy for her tired arms. She stood with her delicate chilled hands laid one over the other at her waist, stepping back as if to keep a space, leaning her head forward as though she dared not lose sight of his face.

“Great God!” escaped him—so low, so solemn, it was like a prayer that had found a voice without intending it.

The cry was not only for her. A deep old wondering in him—about the unseen person to whom his own face must be like a sign—stirred as if it had been touched with fire. *Perhaps my mother was like this one.* The thought, long held back, rose now with a force that made it tremble through him.

The low words seemed to reassure her; she came close to the boat’s side. Daniel put out his hand. She had already slipped her tiny hand into his—his fingers closed around it—when some new terror struck her. She drew back and whispered, “I have nowhere to go—nobody belonging to me in all this land.”

“I will take you to a lady who has daughters,” he said at once. The answer sprang up because it promised a home-like safety: women’s shelter, women’s hands, women’s voices.

She hesitated still, and then, more timidly than ever, asked, “Do you belong to the theatre?”

“No,” he said, decisively. “I have nothing to do with the theatre.”

Then, with a quiet urgency, “I will put you in perfect safety at once; with a lady—a good woman. I am sure she will be kind. Let us lose no time: you will make yourself ill. Life may still become sweet to you. There are good people—there are good women who will take care of you.”

She drew back no more. She stepped into the boat easily, as if she were used to such movements, and sat on the cushions.

“You had a covering for your head,” he said.

“My hat?” She lifted her hands to her hair. “It is quite hidden in the bush.”

“I will find it,” he said, holding out his hand in a small restraining gesture as she tried to rise. “The boat is fixed.”

He jumped out, found the hat among the willows, lifted the wet cloak, wrung it as well as he could, and threw it into the bottom of the boat. The cloak had that heavy, sodden smell wool takes on when it has drunk river-water—cold, animal, earthy, as if the fleece remembered the rain and the field.

“We must carry the cloak away,” he said, forcing a cheerfulness into his voice, “to prevent any one who may have noticed you from thinking you have been drowned.”

He got in again and handed her the hat.

“I wish I had any other garment than my coat to offer you,” he went on. “But shall you mind throwing it over your shoulders while we are on the water? It is quite an ordinary thing to do, when people return late and are not enough provided with wraps.”

He held out the coat with a smile. She answered with the faintest melancholy smile—so slight it was like a ghost of courtesy—and put it on with a neat quickness that spoke of former habits, of a life in which one had learned to arrange oneself properly even when the heart was breaking.

“I have some biscuits—should you like them?” he asked.

“No; I cannot eat,” she said. “I had still some money left to buy bread.”

He began to row. For many minutes they went swiftly in silence. She did not look at him; she watched the oar. The dip and lift, the dripping, the clean creak, the small splash and hush—she seemed to cling to it as to a lullaby. The twilight deepened; the red flush died; little stars came out and held their places. The moon began to rise but was still caught among trees and building like a pale lamp behind lattices.

Daniel could not see her expression distinctly, and yet her face was present to him with a strange definiteness, as if distress itself had sharpened the lines. One anxiety troubled him: the possibility that her mind was disordered. The attempt on her life was unmistakable, and it coloured every other sign with dread. He longed to speak, to draw her into some steadying common talk—bread, a bed, warmth—but he held back. He did not want to claim the harsh authority of a benefactor, nor to speak to her as if distress gave him rights.

At last she spoke herself, quietly, as if the oar had taught her to begin.

“I like to listen to the oar.”

“So do I,” he said.

“If you had not come, I should have been dead now.”

“I cannot bear you to speak of that. I hope you will never be sorry that I came.”

“I cannot see how I shall be glad to live,” she answered, and the words had a weary exactness. “The *maggior dolore* and the *miseria* have lasted longer than the *tempo felice*.” After a pause she added dreamily, “*Dolore—miseria*—I think those words are alive.”

He was silent. To question her felt like an intrusion, as if he would be putting his hands into a wound to learn its depth. He felt the untranslatability of her inward suffering—how the very words she used were only a thin crust over something private and burning. Even the borrowed Italian, beautiful as it was, seemed a cloak that could not fit the exact shape of her despair.

She went on, still in that musing tone, as if speaking not to him but to some inward judge.

“I thought it was not wicked. Death and life are one before the Eternal. I know our fathers slew their children and then slew themselves, to keep their souls pure. I meant it so. But now I am commanded to live. I cannot see how I shall live.”

“You will find friends,” he said. “I will find them for you.”

She shook her head. “Not my mother and brother. I cannot find them.”

“You are English?” he said at last—clinging to any simple certainty. “You must be—speaking English so perfectly.”

She did not answer at once. She looked at him again, straining to see him in the mixed light, as if she were trying to decide whether he belonged to dream or waking. Sorrowful isolation had benumbed her sense of reality; one could see that the border between inner and outer had worn thin.

“You want to know if I am English?” she said at last.

“I want to know nothing except what you like to tell me,” he answered quickly. Then, still uneasy, “Perhaps it is not good for you to talk.”

“Yes,” she said, as if choosing. “I will tell you. I am English-born. But I am a Jewess.”

He was silent, with an inward start—less surprise than a sudden sense that something he ought to have understood had been waiting there plainly. The cast of her features, the delicate darkness, the way her voice held its music, might have led another to guess Spain; but her words placed her within a history deeper than geography, a people marked and named by others for centuries.

After a little while she asked, very low, and with a sadness that was like the cry of a small dumb creature, “Do you despise me for it?”

“Why should I?” he said. “I am not so foolish.”

“I know many Jews are bad.”

“So are many Christians,” he answered. “But I should not think it fair for you to despise me because of that.”

“My mother and brother were good,” she said. “But I shall never find them. I am come a long way—from abroad. I ran away; but I cannot tell you—I cannot speak of it.” Her voice broke, then steadied again with a stubborn gentleness. “I thought I might find my mother again—God would guide me. But then I despaired. This morning when the light came, I felt as if one word kept sounding within me—Never! never! But now—I begin—to think—” The words dissolved into rising sobs. “I am commanded to live—perhaps we are going to her.”

With that, her restraint gave way. She bent forward, buried her head on her knees, and wept—deeply, helplessly, as if the tears had been waiting for permission.

Daniel rowed on, his arms moving with the steady necessity of the stroke, his mind working in anxious turns. He had first thought to take her straight to Park Lane, to Lady Mallinger; and Lady Mallinger was kindness itself. But he saw, with a shiver of practical dread, the likelihood of Lady Mallinger not being at home; of footmen’s eyes resting on this wet-cloaked, delicate stranger; of bright stairs and hard questions; of the chill authority of a housekeeper or maid who would measure distress by its respectability and find it wanting. A mind already trembling might be frightened into flight—or into a second attempt.

He could not risk that.

Another refuge rose before him—small, safe, warm with the quiet decencies of poverty: Mrs. Meyrick’s little house at Chelsea. He had been there often since his return from abroad. He could see Mrs. Meyrick’s Quakerish neatness, the order that smelled faintly of clean linen and tea-leaves; the three girls with their unspoiled eagerness; the household’s gentle reverence for Hans Meyrick, away in Italy, which would make them ready to do anything asked by Deronda, Hans’s friend and idol. Those girls, with their innocent romance, would see at once in a lovely Jewess the echo of some tale they had cherished—Rebecca, perhaps—and would not begin by suspecting sin.

The vision steadied him. Once decided, he felt no more wavering.

The rumble of the cab toward Chelsea, after the river’s hush, was like being thrust back into a harsher world. Wheels clattered; hoofs struck sparks of sound from the stones; voices were sharpened by streets. The city’s noise, indifferent and incessant, seemed to press on the very skin—an oppression that made him more fiercely determined to win this girl into a pocket of quiet where she could breathe.

She was silent now, spent after weeping. In the cab she set down her hat and tried to rest her head, but the jolting would not let it settle. Still she dozed, and her sweet head hung helplessly from side to side like a tired child’s. Daniel watched her with a protection that felt both urgent and unearned. Her very presence—her voice, her careful English, her fragile beauty—was an appeal that made disbelief seem cruel.

And yet what had brought her to that riverbank?

He thought of an old story Plutarch tells: Delphic women, Maenads, who after torch-lit wandering lay down asleep in a market-place; and how the matrons came and stood silently round them to guard their slumber, and when they woke ministered to them tenderly and saw them safely to their own borders. He clung to that image as to a promise. He could trust the women he was going to—trust them not to let suspicion speak louder than pity.

As the cab jolted on, he had the strange sensation of growing older within the hour, as if this one act of drawing a stranger back from death had shifted the whole weight of his life. He had been questioning whether to take part in the world’s battle; but now the world had thrust a life into his hands, and the question was no longer abstract. Rescue—yes: but how to make sure that snatching someone from death would not be only the beginning of a different misery?

The moment of finding a fellow-creature can be like the moment of finding an idea—full of doubt and exultation, and heavy with consequences that cannot yet be named.
