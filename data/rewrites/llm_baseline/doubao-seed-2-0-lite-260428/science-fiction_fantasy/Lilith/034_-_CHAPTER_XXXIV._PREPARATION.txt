The echoes of their recent victory still hummed in the clearing when the exile from Bulika stepped forward to speak. She talked long of her home, of how its walls were more for show than defense, of the cruel, child-killing princess who ruled it, and of how the town’s people had long grown too cowardly to stand against her. Within days, the Little Ones chattered of nothing but Bulika—though not a one of them had any frame of reference for stone walls, cobblestones, or a settlement that stretched farther than the edges of the forest. It was then I first saw the shape of the woman’s design, even if I could not yet guess her full motive. The idea of seizing the city seized hold of Lona at once, and of me no less.

The children’s gifts and faculties were blooming so fast by this point I could spot no serious barrier to the enterprise’s success. I knew Lilith, that shapeshifting woman or leopardess, had one single vulnerable point: the doom foretold to her by the old prophecy of the land, tied to her own daughter. Between that curse hanging over her, the fear that gnawed at Bulika’s citizens, whatever risk the mission carried was worth taking. We could not fail. Once we held the city, this crowd of unruly, loving children would grow fast into a young, righteous people, ruling the wicked of Bulika with a firm, just hand and redeeming the whole broken nation. I will not pretend I had no personal ambition in the plan: it felt only right that Lona should take the throne that had been stolen from her mother, natural that she would make me her consort and chief minister. I would spend my whole life in her service, and with the Little Ones as the heart of our new state, we could build something nobler than any realm that had ever existed. I even harbored a silly, foolish little daydream of opening a gem trade between my old world and this one—something I would later be grateful was impossible, for it would have corrupted both.

I thought back to Adam’s old appeal, and suggested to Lona that we might seek out the hidden, life-giving water he had spoken of to speed the Little Ones’ growth. She judged it wiser to hold off. We knew nothing of what unforeseen hungers or changes that water might bring, and would far rather not unleash a new necessity on the children we did not understand. “They are perfect as they are,” she said. “Once we hold the city, we will search for water then.”

So we threw ourselves fully into preparations, parading the merry little companies over and over to drill them. Lona oversaw the commissariat, storing piles of dried fruit, foraging wild nuts, and coordinating hay harvests, while I drilled our small soldiers: teaching them to hurl stones true with slings, draw bows to the ear, and wield sharpened aloe spikes hardened as steel, fixed to long light shafts, that made even our youngest fighters a formidable force. The oldest girls carried no weapons for themselves: their only duty was to tend the toddlers too small to fight, and keep them safe out of harm’s way. The hardest part of training was teaching them to rally to their flag the instant the call sounded, no matter what caught their attention.

Lona herself had grown nearly to full womanhood, though she did not seem to notice. She was still the tallest of us all, her hair falling long down her back, and no trace of her soft childish beauty had faded in the least. When we had first met again after our long separation, she had set down the infant in her arms, wrapped her arms around my neck and clung silent to me, her face glowing bright with unspoken joy. Only when the baby whimpered did she spring back to tuck him safe against her chest. To see her tend to a stubborn, fussy, or irritable little one was to see the softest tenderness of a grandmother at work. It felt like I had known her for ages—for always, from before time had even begun. I could barely remember what my own mother had looked like, but whenever I pictured her, her face was Lona’s. Any sister or child I imagined, bore Lona’s features. My every thought turned to her: she was my heart’s true wife. She never sought me out unnecessarily, but she was always within earshot, and every choice I made I ran past her in my mind, rejoicing to see that she did her own work with quiet, unshakable independence, and was happiest of all at my side. She never neglected the smallest child for my sake, and my love for her only sharpened my sense of duty. Loving her and doing what was right were not the same, but they were inseparable. Her love flowed to me not in soft caresses, but in that perfect, unshakable recognition that I can only compare to the devotion of some divine, loyal creature. I never told her Lilith was her mother.

The forest teemed with birds whose iridescent plumage more than made up for the lack of flowers that could not grow without open water. Gathering piles of their shed feathers, I wove them in overlapping rows into a long, flowing mantle that fastened at the throat and waist, with slits for the arms. Lona sat and watched me work the whole two weeks it took, never asking what I was making, just waiting patiently to see the end result. When I set it on her shoulders, she first took it off and laid it at my feet out of quiet respect, then stroked the shimmering feathers, and carried it away with her. A few days later she came to me wearing the feather mantle, and handed me a tunic she had woven from the tough leaves of an evergreen shrub, strong as leather and patterned like scale armor. It was perfect for riding.

A troop of giant wild horses had wandered to the forest’s edge some weeks prior, and since they showed no fear of our strange small shapes, I had tamed the two finest for Lona and me. When Lona first looked down from the back of the great steed, after a lifetime of riding tiny forest ponies, her delight was plain to see, and the horse seemed as proud to bear her as she was to ride him. We exercised them every day until they obeyed our every signal instantly and feared nothing, and after that we rode them for every parade and drill.

The Bulika woman’s constant unshakable confidence kept my own hesitations at bay, even when the whole foolhardy enterprise seemed to loom too big. She swore the princess’s magic would have no power over the Little Ones, that our animal allies alone would outmatch any ragtag militia the city could scrape together, and that she herself could take on two grown men of Bulika with nothing but a stout stick. She confessed she was afraid of the leopardess Lilith—but I told her I was ready to face her. I still shrank from bringing all the very smallest children with us, and suggested Lona stay in the forest with the babies and the toddlers.

“I must come,” she said. “The sight of our gentle Little Ones will storm the hearts of Bulika’s women, especially the mothers. If there is any courage left in that city, it lives in the women.”

“You must not carry any children yourself,” I told her. “You will be needed everywhere.” She still had a habit of tucking one of the babies into the crook of her arm even on horseback.

“I cannot remember ever being without a child to care for,” she smiled, “but once we reach the city, I will do exactly as you ask.”

Her quiet, unwavering trust in a man who had failed her once shamed me. I had not started this movement, and I had no right to oppose it. I gave myself over entirely to her cause, ready to live or die by her side.

The open grassy plain that stretched between us and Bulika was rich with short grass, enough to feed our horses and the two milk cows we brought for the infants, but the elephants could not pluck the short grass with their thin, delicate trunks. We set all the Little Ones to cutting and bundling hay, enough that the elephants themselves could carry a several days’ supply, with extra foraging every time we halted. We stored nuts for our ursine allies, dried fruit for ourselves, and loaded our extra tamed horses and elephants with all the supplies we needed. We were ready to march.

Lona and I held one final full review, and I spoke to the crowd of bright, eager children. I told them they had not simply sprung out of nothing, as they liked to claim. They had all been stolen from Bulika when they were too small to remember, I said, their mothers sneaking them out of the city to save them from the cruel princess who killed every baby born. We were going to Bulika to find their mothers, and free them.

“There is danger ahead,” I warned them. “We may have to fight very hard to take the city.”

“We can fight! We are ready!” the boys roared.

“Mothers are worth fighting for,” I told them. “Only keep together, and obey your officers instantly. Strike hard, draw your arrows to the head, sling your stones straight and strong. Some of you may be hurt.”

None of them flinched. “We don’t mind!” they shouted.

“Some of you may even be killed,” I said.

“I don’t mind being killed!” yelled a small, bright boy riding a tiny bull that galloped and jumped like a racehorse. The whole crowd of children took up the cry.

Then Lona spoke, seated high on her great steed at my side, queen, mother, and sister to every one of them. “I would give my life to meet my mother,” she said. “If she wanted to kill me, I would let her. I would just kiss her, and then die.”

“Come on!” a girl shouted from the ranks. “We’re going home to our mothers!”

A sharp, cold pang pierced my heart. But I could not turn back. To retreat now would shatter the Little Ones, and leave them morally broken forever. There was no going back.
