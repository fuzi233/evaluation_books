Denys had brought her more than tidings; he brought her a living voice from the road, full of turns and tones that made the room feel less like a cell and more like a hearth. Yet the day was sweet and bitter together: sweet, because in this strange place she had found a true comrade; bitter, because by Denys’s very news she learned, plain as salt on the tongue, that Gerard was alone in a foreign land. Denys spoke of men who always meant to come home and never did; and Margaret, who had once fed hope with both hands as if it were a child, could not force herself to swallow Denys’s belief that Gerard would appear at her door some morning. If he had been coming, she thought, he would have come ere this.

Still, Denys was balm—warm, sharp, and a little wild, like a draught spiced overmuch for health’s sake. He called her his she-comrade, and the name, half mock, half vow, rang in the small house like a tune that would not die. He was forever striving to make her laugh by repeating his soldier’s “formula” and turning it into burlesque; and she, who had so little left to pet, made much of him. She hectored him feebly, too—over Denys as over Martin—because scolding was the one kind of authority grief still allowed her.

And she ruled the table as a captain rules a gate. Denys was not suffered to eat a single meal out of her house. If he tried to slip away, she would catch him by the sleeve, as if hunger itself were a deserter.

“It spoils you, Denys,” she would say, setting before him what she had contrived from the market—dark bread still warm in the heart, a small pot of pottage kept at the edge of the coals so it thickened and did not scorch, a dab of salted fish rinsed and flaked to take the worst brine out, onions cut fine so their bite would soften in the steam. “Good lack, to hear such ugly words come forth so comely a head: forbear, or I shall be angry: so be civil.”

Then Denys would sit up, as if the bench were suddenly a throne, and his struggle began—between the native courtesy bred into him long ago and the ruffian tongue he had learned among camps where men speak like stones striking sparks. He would bite down on an oath, swallow it, and replace it with some courtly phrase that sounded to him as strange as a foreign spice. It was ludicrous and touching; and even Martin, red-armed from the wash, would laugh under his breath.

For the house was all labour and sound. The basin in the corner kept time with Martin’s hands, slap and swill, slap and swill; the wet linen made a soft, heavy music as it was wrung; Denys’s iron, clumsy in his soldier’s grip, hissed and rasped over cloth; and Margaret, plaiting ruffs, let the stiffening paste dry under her fingers until it held shape—each pleat a little fence against disorder. Outside, Rotterdam’s noises drifted in—carts, calls, the thud of hooves—but within, the domestic rhythm steadied the mind better than any sermon: the honest acoustics of work, not the jangling show-noise of men who sell delight.

And, as it never rains but it pours, other persons began to solicit Margaret’s friendship—friendship and its shadow, obligation.

She had written, some while ago, to Margaret Van Eyck: a humble letter, carefully worded, with all her pride pressed flat between the lines. She told the lady she knew she was no longer the favourite she had been; she would keep her distance; yet she could not forget past kindness. Then, briefly—because pain, once set down, need not be embroidered—she told how many ways she had battled for a living. And she ended with a plea that tasted like ash even as she wrote it: let not her residence be betrayed.

“Least of all to his people,” she had begged. “I do hate them; they drove him from me. And even when he was gone, their hearts turned not to me as they would an if they had repented their cruelty to him.”

The Van Eyck woman was perplexed, and what she could not digest alone she took to another mouth. At last she made a confidante of Reicht; and the secret ran through Reicht, as through a cylinder, to Catherine.

“Ay, and is she turned that bitter against us?” Catherine said—good woman, yet with a sting in her goodness, as nettles grow near clean water. “She stole our son from us, and now she hates us for not running into her arms. Natheless it is a blessing she is alive and no farther away than Rotterdam.”

Then came the great pageant, and with it the loudness of the world. The English princess—now Countess Charolois—made a stately progress through the northern states of the duchy, with her stepdaughter beside her, the young heiress, Marie de Bourgogne. The old duke, most magnificent prince in Europe, put out his splendour as a rich man lays out a feast: not to feed hunger, but to dazzle eyes.

Troops of knights went by in armour that flashed like fire struck from steel; bevies of ladies followed, their garments rustling like fields of dry grain in a wind. Minstrels and jongleurs, story-tellers and tumblers, bards and players swelled the train; in every town there was fencing and dancing and rejoicing—the kind of rejoicing that drowns out quieter songs. Margaret, hearing of it, could not help thinking how such uproar, clattering from street to street, can make a man forget the older music of a mother’s voice, a lover’s dialect whispered in the dark, the small syllables that carry a whole life and cannot be translated into courtly French without losing their savour.

Richart invited all his people to meet him at Rotterdam and view the glitter. They had been there some days when Denys met Catherine by chance in the street. A warm greeting passed between them, for Denys could not help liking honest folk, and Catherine, once she had decided you were not her enemy, could be hearty as bread.

“Rejoice!” Denys cried, crowing like a cock at sunrise. “I have found the she-comrade.”

Catherine’s face, that had lifted, cooled again. She showed him—plainly, as one pours cold water on a boil—how much earlier he would have found Margaret had he stayed quiet at Tergou instead of vagabondizing it all over Holland.

“And being found,” she added, “what the better are we? Her heart is set dead against us now.”

“Oh, let that flea stick,” said Denys. “Come you with me to her house.”

But Catherine shook her head. She would not go where she was sure of an ill welcome.

“Them that come unbidden sit unseated,” she said; and would not budge. Let Denys be mediator; let him carry words back and forth until the rough edges wore down.

Denys undertook the office at once, with great pomp and confidence, as if he were marching between two armies with a truce flag.

He trotted to Margaret and began, as if warming the dish before serving it. “She-comrade, I met this day a friend of thine.”

“Thou didst look into the Rotter then, and see thyself,” she answered, for she could still parry.

“Nay, ’twas a female, and one that seeks thy regard; ’twas Catherine, Gerard’s mother.”

Margaret’s fingers went on plaiting, but her hands trembled. “Oh, was it?” she said at last; and the words were ice laid on fire. “Then you may tell her she comes too late. There was a time I longed and longed for her; but she held aloof in my hour of most need, so now we will be as we ha’ been.”

Denys tried to shake this resolution. He coaxed her—he pleaded that old wrongs could be salted down and kept, but were better eaten up and done with. She would not. Her bitterness sat in her mouth like burnt crust; she was sullen, and not to be sweetened.

Then Denys, changing tack as soldiers do when the ground will not take a charge, scolded her well. At that, all her pent breath broke; her strength went out in hysterics, and the room filled with a sound worse than any street uproar—grief made loud and helpless. Denys, frightened at what his bold tongue had uncorked, was taken off guard; and before he rightly knew it she had trapped him into a solemn promise never to speak of Catherine again.

He went back to Catherine crestfallen and told her what he had met—how Margaret would not yield an inch, how her heart had turned hard.

Catherine fired up; she told the family, and they fired up in their turn. What had been a sore became a feud, and burned fiercer with each retelling—each word adding its own pepper, each hearer tasting offence where perhaps there had only been pain. Little Kate alone tried to make excuses for Margaret, as if she could still remember what it is to be young and frightened and too proud to beg.

The very next day, while the house rang with its familiar labour-music—Martin enslaved and degraded by soapsuds, Denys ironing with the grave clumsiness of a man used to sword-hilts not linen, Margaret plaiting ruffs and watching her raw levies with a mistress’s eye—another visitor entered.

An old man, venerable at first sight, came in: on nearer view keen and wizened, with something sharp about him that made Margaret’s skin draw back before her mind had named him.

“Ah,” she cried; and swiftly turned her back, hiding her face with an invincible repugnance. “Oh, that man! that man!”

“Nay, fear me not,” said Ghysbrecht. His voice was dry, yet steady; not kind, but not mocking. “I come on a friend’s errand. I bring ye a letter from foreign parts.”

“Mock me not, old man.” She turned slowly round, as if turning cost her. She had been mocked enough by fate.

“Nay, see.” And he held out an enormous letter.

At once all else in the room—iron, suds, ruffs, grievances, pageants—fell away like husks. Margaret darted upon it; the paper was thick, the seal rough under her thumb. Her hands shook; her eyes shone. It was Gerard’s handwriting—those strokes and turns she could have known among a thousand, as one knows a loved voice in a crowd.

“Oh, thank you, sir,” she cried, and the words came hot and sudden. “Bless you for this. I forgive you all the ill you ever wrought me.”

She pressed the letter to her bosom with one hand, as if to warm it with her own life, and glided from the room so swiftly the others could not stop her. The door shut; the house’s sounds went on without her, but they seemed muffled now, as if the letter had drawn all hearing into itself.

When she did not come back, Ghysbrecht went away. As he passed, he cast a scowl at Martha—old accounts remembered without being spoken. Then the street took him, with its pageant-noise and common cries.

Margaret was hours alone with her letter.
