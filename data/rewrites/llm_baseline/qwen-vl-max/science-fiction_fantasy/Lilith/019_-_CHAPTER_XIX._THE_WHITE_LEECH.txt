I awoke one morning from a deep, dreamless sleep, my right hand throbbing with pain. The back of it was swollen, the skin taut and warm, and at the center of the inflammation was a small, triangular wound—clean, precise, like the mark left by a leech. By midday, the swelling had receded; by nightfall, the injury was nearly gone, leaving only a faint scar. I searched the cave with feverish care—every stone turned, every crevice probed—but found no creature that could have caused such a bite. Nothing larger than a beetle dwelled in the shadows.

Days passed in quiet vigil. The body on the stone slab remained motionless, eyes closed, breath still. Yet I knew it was not dead. Decay did not touch it. The air around it held no scent of rot. And slowly, imperceptibly, changes began: the sharp angles of her bones softened, her limbs gained a subtle roundness, her skin lost its brittle, parchment-like quality. If life were returning, it was doing so in whispers. If the tide of existence had once receded to the edge of oblivion, perhaps it now stirred again. My heart swelled with hope each time I saw these signs. But doubt followed close behind. Twenty times a day I leaned over her, seeking movement, listening for breath. Twenty times I despaired. Then I would remember the first moment I found her—cold, hollow, almost beyond recognition—and hope would rise again.

One night, after hours of wakefulness, I rose to step outside. The moon hung full and bright above the trees, casting silver light into the cave. The air inside was cool and clear, but the heat of the day lingered near the walls. As I passed her, I glanced once more at her face.

“Bliss eternal!” I whispered aloud. “Are those… her eyes?”

They seemed to glow—dark orbs, like voids carved from midnight, luminous in their depth, shining through the pale pallor of her cheeks. I froze, breath caught in my chest. I crept closer, pulse hammering. Then I saw—the lids were shut. A trick of the moonlight? A hallucination born of longing? My joy collapsed into sorrow. I turned away, fell to the floor, and wept.

But then—I remembered. Her eyes *had* been open. Just for an instant. Now the narrow slit was gone. Perhaps she had blinked. Perhaps she was awake, holding them closed. Either way, they had moved. Life had claimed them. I lay down, tears drying on my face, and slept.

That night, I was bitten again. I woke gasping, throat parched, body weak. The wound—same shape, same location—was healing as before. I searched the cave anew, more thoroughly than ever, but found nothing. No trace. No sign.

I concluded that some large aquatic creature, perhaps a leech of unnatural size, came from the hot river to feed. “If it seeks blood,” I mused, “then while I am here, it will not touch her.”

The next morning, I fed her as usual—a peeled grape, seeds removed, placed gently between her lips. This time, her mouth shifted. A tiny movement. A reflex. But it was enough. I KNEW she lived.

Hope surged within me. I began to think of clothing—something she could wear when she rose. I went to the forest, searching for materials. Among the dry husks of prickly pear leaves, I found fibrous skeletons, tough and flexible. I gathered them, dried them under the sun, and separated the layers into threads. With the sharp tip of an aloe leaf and natural fibers, I stitched together three layers of the material, fashioning two garments—one for her waist, another for her shoulders. I made sandals from woven strips, binding them tightly.

For days, no further change. But then—she took the grapes more deliberately. Her form grew fuller. Her skin gained warmth, color. Still, her eyes remained closed. And fear returned—not of death, but of something worse: that this growth was not life, but corruption, some monstrous fungus feeding on her flesh.

Then came the third bite. And the fourth. Each time, exactly every three days. Always at night. Always while I slept. Always one bite—on the neck or arm. Never during daylight. I lay awake for hours, listening, watching, but never heard it approach. Never felt it strike. It came like a shadow, left without a sound.

Eventually, I gave up trying to catch it. My strength waned. Blood loss was taking its toll. But I cared little. For across the cave, life was stirring. A soul was awakening. We would leave together. I would recover. She would rise.

The garments were finished. I stood back, admiring my work—clumsy, yes, but functional. I began weaving sandals.

Then, one night, I woke suddenly—drenched in sweat, breath ragged. I struggled to rise, desperate for air. Halfway to the entrance, a soft rustle stopped me.

A voice—soft, low, in my own tongue—spoke.

“I caught the vile thing,” it said. “In the very act.”

She was alive. She spoke.

I choked back my joy. “What creature?” I breathed.

“The creature that was biting you.”

“What was it?”

“A great white leech.”

“How big?”

“Not far from six feet long, I should think.”

“You saved my life—perhaps!” I said, my voice breaking. “But how? How could you touch it?”

“I did,” she replied simply. I thought I saw her shudder.

“Where is it? What did you do?”

“I threw it into the river.”

“It will return,” I warned.

“I do not think I could have killed it, even if I had known how.” She paused. “I heard you moaning. I rose. Saw it at your neck. Pulled it away. Could not hold it. Only managed to hurl it into the water. Heard it splash.”

“We’ll kill it next time,” I said. But then I fainted.

When I regained consciousness, the sun was high. She stood a few paces away, clad in the rough garments I had made. Even in that crude attire, she was magnificent—tall, poised, regal.

I HAD seen her eyes. They had shone all night. Darker than the deepest night, yet brighter than the dawn. Now they burned with cold fire.

She regarded me silently. Her face was pale, unreadable. No emotion—only question.

I rose. “We must go,” I said. “The white leech—”

I stopped. A strange smile touched her lips.

“Did you find me there?” she asked, pointing to the cave.

“No,” I answered. “I brought you here.”

“You brought me?”

“Yes.”

“From where?”

“The forest.”

“What have you done with my clothes… and my jewels?”

“You had none when I found you.”

“Then why did you not leave me?”

“Because I hoped you were not dead.”

“Why should you have cared?”

“Because I was alone. And I wanted you to live.”

“You would have kept me enchanted for my beauty!” she said, scorn lacing every word.

Her tone ignited my anger. “There was no beauty left in you,” I said. “Only bone and skin.”

“Then why not let me die?”

“Because you were of my kind.”

“Of YOUR kind?” she laughed bitterly. “I thought so. But I was wrong.”

“Doubtless you pitied me.”

“Never has woman deserved pity more—or less of any other feeling.”

Her expression twisted—pain, humiliation, fury—too deep for words. She turned away, silent.

Darkness filled her eyes—starless, infinite. The hatred for the one who had restored her had extinguished their light.

“Had you failed to rouse me,” she asked suddenly, without moving, “what would you have done?”

“I would have buried you.”

“Buried me? Buried THIS?” she shrieked, wheeling toward me, arms outstretched, eyes flashing like ice. “You speak of burying me! But I was not dead! I was in a trance!”

“Nay,” I said firmly. “I saw no sign of life. I saw only decay. Had I seen even a hint of rot, I would have buried you at once.”

“Dog of a fool!” she cried. “I was only asleep! Samoil—what a fate! Go fetch the she-savage from whom you stole this hideous disguise!”

“I made it for you,” I said. “It’s ugly, but I did my best.”

She straightened, tall and proud. “How long have I lain unconscious?”

“A woman could not make that dress in a day.”

“Not in twenty. Not in thirty.”

“Ha! How long do you pretend I’ve been unconscious? Answer me.”

“I cannot say how long you’d been there when I found you. But what remained of you was skin and bone. More than three months ago. Your hair was beautiful—nothing else. I did what I could.”

“My poor hair,” she murmured, drawing a great coil of it forward. “It will take more than three months to bring YOU back to life. I suppose I must thank you—though I cannot say I’m grateful.”

“There’s no need,” I said. “I would have done the same for any woman. Or man.”

“How is it my hair is not tangled?” she asked, running her fingers through it.

“It always drifted in the current.”

“Drifted? What do you mean?”

“I could not have revived you without bathing you daily in the hot river.”

She shuddered, staring at the rushing water. Then she turned to me.

“We must understand each other,” she said. “You have done me the two worst things possible—you forced me to live, and you put me to shame. Neither can I forgive.”

She raised her left hand and flung it outward, as if to push me away. Something cold and hard struck my forehead.

When I came to, I was on the ground—wet, trembling, half-drowned in memory.
