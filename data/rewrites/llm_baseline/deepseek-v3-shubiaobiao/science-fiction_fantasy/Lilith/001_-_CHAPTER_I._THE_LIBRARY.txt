The ink on my Oxford diploma had barely dried when I retreated to the family estate, seeking respite before the weight of inheritance settled on my shoulders. My parents had left me alone in this world long ago, and the house stood as a silent monument to generations I scarcely knew—generations whose ghosts lingered not in portraits, but in the spines of ancient books.

The library was a living thing, sprawling across the ground floor like an organism that had outgrown its shell. Rooms bled into one another through arches and narrow passages, each shelf a fossil record of shifting intellectual tides. I preferred the heart of it, the great chamber where books climbed the walls like ivy, their leather and vellum whispering under the weight of centuries. Ptolemy, Paracelsus, and the elder Bacon kept company with Maxwell and Darwin, their contradictions a comfort. Truth, after all, was a shapeshifter.

That evening, the rain had painted the windows gray until sunset split the clouds. A bloody light spilled through the glass, igniting the fountain on the lawn—then, as I turned, it found the only painting in the room. The portrait hung like an afterthought in a shallow niche, a man whose name I barely recalled, though his eyes now seemed to lock onto mine with unsettling clarity.

Then—movement. A flicker at the edge of vision. Near the farthest shelf, a figure stretched toward a book, gaunt and deliberate. I blinked; the air settled. A trick of the light, I told myself. Yet when I reached later for a volume on electromagnetism, the gap in the shelf yawned empty. By morning, it was back.

Three nights later, the library played another trick. The closet door—a sly thing disguised as shelves, its false backs labeled with lost titles—had always charmed me. But now, the slim vellum corner of a manuscript, wedged decades ago between facades, was gone. The butler, a man who had served my father as a boy, paled when I asked. He knew nothing. Yet his silence was heavier than his words.

I saw the old man again at dusk—his coat dark and polished with age, slipping through the closet door. I chased the shadow, found nothing. But when I returned to my chair, the manuscript was back in its place, snug as a bone in a grave.

The butler folded like parchment under my questions. There were stories, of course. A librarian named Raven, centuries old, who had served an ancestor obsessed with "unwholesome" books. They vanished together. Only Raven returned—sometimes.

"Let him visit," I said, running a finger along the restored manuscript’s edge. It didn’t budge. "But keep the maids from gossiping."

The butler nodded. He’d never seen the ghost. Only the books it touched.
