The winter deepened, yet the days grew longer without the promised warmth of hope that usually greets a February sun. Mrs. Thornton had withdrawn entirely from the household, and Mr. Thornton's visits, though occasional, were restricted to the study where he attended to his father. To Mr. Hale, these rare encounters seemed to hold a value precisely because of their scarcity. Margaret observed that Mr. Thornton spoke of her with a calm, steady friendliness, neither shunning nor seeking mention of her name, suggesting his silence stemmed from the complexities of his business affairs rather than any personal grievance.

Margaret herself lacked the spirit to lift her father's mood. The long period of anxiety and storm that had preceded this dreary peace had drained her mind of its elasticity. She busied herself teaching the younger Boucher children and striving for goodness, yet her heart remained dead to the effort; she performed her duties with painful punctuality but remained distant from any cheerfulness. Her life felt bleak, save for her silent, unconscious piety in comforting her father. She anticipated his quiet wishes with a meek obedience that was both complete and beautiful. Then came the news of Frederick's marriage. Letters arrived from him and Dolores; Dolores wrote in a mix of Spanish and English, while Frederick's letters showed the influence of his bride's tongue, filled with playful inversions.

Upon receiving Henry Lennox's letter, which cast little hope for Frederick's exoneration at a court-martial due to missing witnesses, Frederick had written to Margaret a vehement renunciation of England. He expressed a desire to "unnative" himself, refusing a pardon or a return to the country. At first, these words made Margaret weep, seeming so unnatural, but upon reflection, she recognized them as the raw poignancy of crushed hope. In his subsequent letters, Frederick spoke joyfully of his future, and Margaret found within herself the patience she had craved. The charming, timid letters from Dolores began to delight both Margaret and her father. The young Spaniard's evident desire to impress her lover's English relations revealed a feminine care in every correction, and her wedding announcement included a magnificent black lace mantilla chosen for Margaret, whom Frederick described as a paragon of beauty and virtue. With this union, Frederick's social standing soared; he became a junior partner in Barbour and Co., one of Spain's most extensive trading houses. Margaret smiled and then sighed, recalling her old rants against trade, watching her "preux chevalier" brother transform into a merchant. Yet, she rebelled against the confusion of equating a Spanish trader with a Milton millowner. Regardless, Frederick was undeniably happy, and the mantilla was exquisite.

Spring brought a new trial: her father experienced difficulties breathing, distressing him greatly. Though Margaret was less alarmed as the symptoms subsided, she urged him to accept Mr. Bell's invitation to visit him in Oxford that April. Mr. Bell's letter specifically commanded her attendance, yet Margaret felt a profound relief in remaining quietly at home, free from responsibility, allowing her mind and heart to rest in a way she had not for over two years.

When her father departed for the railroad, Margaret acutely felt the immense pressure she had carried. It was almost stunning to realize she was finally at liberty, with no one depending on her for cheer or care, no invalid to tend. She could be idle, silent, forgetful, and, most liberating of all, unhappy if she chose. For months, her personal sorrows had been stuffed into a dark cupboard; now, she had the leisure to retrieve them, to mourn, to analyze, and to seek a method of subduing them into peace. Sitting motionless in the drawing-room for hours, she reviewed the bitterness of every memory with unwincing resolution. Once, at the stinging thought of the faithlessness that bred her abasing falsehood, she cried aloud. She refused to acknowledge the force of that temptation anymore; her plans for Frederick had failed, leaving the temptation a dead mockery. The lie had been despicably foolish in the light of events, proving faith in truth to be infinitely wiser.

In her nervous agitation, she opened a book belonging to her father. The words that caught her eye seemed tailored to her state of acute self-abasement: a French passage urging her not to despair in shame, but to correct her heart through compassion, to reveal themselves from the pit, and to reclaim the path of humility. "The way of humility," Margaret thought. "That is what I have missed! But courage, little heart. We will turn back, and by God's help, we may find the lost path."

Rising immediately, she decided to engage in work that would take her out of herself. Calling in Martha as she ascended the stairs, she attempted to pierce the grave, respectful, almost mechanical manner that crusted over the servant's individuality. Finally, naming Mrs. Thornton struck the right chord. Martha's face brightened, and she recounted how her father, in his early life as a warehouse clerk, had once shown kindness to Mrs. Thornton's husband. Years later, when Martha and her sister were nearly grown and destitute after her mother's death, it was Mrs. Thornton who had sought them out, saving them from being "lost." Martha recalled how Mrs. Thornton and Mr. Thornton had nursed her through a fever, ignoring the doctor's warnings about contagion, while Miss Fanny visited others destined for marriage.

"Miss Fanny going to be married!" Margaret exclaimed.

"Yes, to a wealthy gentleman named Watson, older than she, with mills beyond Hayleigh. It is a very good match," Martha replied, quickly sweeping the hearth and retreating with her usual wooden demeanor. Margaret suppressed a bad habit of imagining how news of Mr. Thornton would affect him.

The next day, after lessons with the Boucher children and a long walk, Margaret visited Mary Higgins. To her surprise, Nicholas was already home, the lengthening light having deceived her about the hour. He seemed to have entered a phase of humility, quieter and less self-asserting.

"So the old gentleman is away on his travels?" Nicholas said. "The little ones told me. They are sharp, though one of them is in her grave. There's something in the weather that sets folk wandering. My master is spinning about the world somewhere."

"Is that why you are home so early?" Margaret asked innocently.

"You know nought about it," Nicholas replied contemptuously. "I am not one with two faces. I counted every clock in the town before leaving. Thornton is good enough to fight, but too good to be cheated. You got me the place, and I thank you. Thornton's is not a bad mill. Stand down, lad, and say your pretty hymn to Miss Marget."

The little boy recited a Methodist hymn, far above his comprehension, yet he repeated it with the cadence of a parliamentarian. When Margaret applauded, Nicholas demanded another, revealing an odd, unconscious interest in sacred things he had once scorned.

Returning home late, Margaret found comfort in knowing no one had waited for her. She examined a packet of letters, intending to destroy them, but paused at four or five from Henry Lennox regarding Frederick's affairs. Initially reading them to assess the chances of her brother's justification, she was struck by the personal revelation within their stiff wording. Lennox had never forgotten his connection to her. The letters were clever but lacked warmth; still, she preserved them as valuable.

After this task, a reverie took hold, and the thought of her absent father haunted her. She almost blamed herself for feeling her solitude as a relief, but these two days had restored her strength. Plans that seemed like tasks now appeared as pleasures. The morbid scales had fallen from her eyes. If Mr. Thornton would restore their friendship, or even simply visit her father as he once did, she felt her future might lie clear and even, though not brilliant. She sighed as she prepared for bed; despite her duty to her father, an anxiety and pang of sorrow lingered at her heart.

That April evening, Mr. Hale thought of Margaret with the same persistence she felt for him. He had grown weary of visiting old friends, fearing his altered opinions would alienate them. Yet, those who had once loved him for his delicate thought and feeling welcomed him with protective kindness, forgetting his views or remembering them only to add a tender gravity to their manner.

"I'm afraid we've done too much," Mr. Bell said. "You're suffering from living too long in that Milton air."

"I am tired," Mr. Hale replied. "But it is not the air. I am fifty-five."

"Nonsense! I am over sixty and feel no loss. Why, you're quite a young man."

Mr. Hale shook his head. "These last few years!" he said, then rose with trembling earnestness. "Bell, do not think that if I could have foreseen the suffering of the one I loved, I would have undone my change of opinion or my resignation. Even if I knew the cruellest martyrdom awaited me, I would have taken that step of openly leaving the church. I might have acted more wisely for my family, but I do not think God endowed me with over-much wisdom or strength."

Mr. Bell blew his nose ostentatiously. "He gave you strength to do what your conscience told you was right. I know I am set down as a wise man, strong-minded, yet the veriest idiot who obeys his simple law of right is wiser than I. What gulls men are!"

After a pause, Mr. Hale spoke of Margaret. "If I die..."

"Nonsense!"

"What will become of her? I suppose the Lennoxes will ask her to live with them. Her Aunt Shaw loved her, but she forgets to love the absent."

"A common fault. Who are the Lennoxes?"

"The Captain is handsome and fluent. Edith is a sweet, spoiled beauty. Margaret loves her, and Edith loves her as much as she can spare."

"Now, Hale, you know that girl has nearly all my heart. I became her slave during my visit here. She looks grand and serene, as if she has secured victory despite her struggles. All I have is at her service, and when I die, she shall be my principal charge. I will be her *preux chevalier*, gouty and sixty though I be. Seriously, your daughter shall be my life's work."

Mr. Bell had a prophetic eye; he might have seen the torch inverted and the angel of death beckoning. That night, Mr. Hale laid his head down on the pillow where it would never stir again. The servant entering in the morning found no answer, only a calm, beautiful face lying white and cold under the seal of death. The heart had ceased as he lay down.

Mr. Bell was stunned, recovering only when he could dismiss the idea of foul play. "A coroner's inquest? Pooh. Natural end of a heart complaint. Poor old Hale! You wore out that tender heart before its time." He ordered a carpet-bag packed and a cab hired, rushing to the railway station within twenty minutes. On the train, he tried to comprehend how a man alive yesterday could be dead today, tears stealing out between his lashes before he forced a severe cheerfulness.

Sitting far away on the same side was Mr. Thornton. Mr. Bell peered over the *Times* and recognized him.

"Why, Thornton! Is that you?" Mr. Bell exclaimed, moving closer and shaking his hand until it relaxed to wipe away tears. "I am going to Milton on a melancholy errand. To break to Hale's daughter the news of his sudden death!"

"Death! Mr. Hale dead!"

"Ay. He went to bed well last night and was quite cold this morning."

"Where?"

"At Oxford. He hadn't been there in seventeen years. This is the end of it."

Silence stretched for a quarter of an hour. Then Mr. Thornton said, "And she!" and stopped abruptly.

"Margaret you mean. Yes, I am going to tell her. Poor fellow! How full his thoughts were of her all last night! Good God! Last night only. And how immeasurably distant he is now! But I take Margaret as my child for his sake. I said last night I would take her for her own sake. Well, I take her for both."

"What will become of her?"

"I fancy two people will wait for her: myself, and the Lennoxes. I would take a live dragon into my house if I could make my old age happy with Margaret as a daughter. But there are the Lennoxes!"

"Who are they?" Mr. Thornton asked with trembling interest.

"Smart London people. The Captain married her cousin. There is her Aunt, Mrs. Shaw. And there is that brother!"

"What brother?"

"No, a clever Lennox, a young barrister who has been setting his cap at Margaret for five years. He was kept back by want of fortune, but that will be done away with."

"How?"

"She'll have my money at my death. If she likes him, I might find another way of getting a home through marriage. I'm dreadfully afraid of being tempted at an unguarded moment by her aunt."

Neither man was in a laughing mood. Mr. Bell whistled a long, hissing breath and shifted seats, while Mr. Thornton sat immovably still, eyes fixed on a spot in the newspaper.

"Where have you been?" Mr. Bell asked eventually.

"To Havre. Trying to detect the secret of the great rise in cotton prices."

"Ugh! Cotton and smoke. Poor old Hale! If you could have known the change it was to him from Helstone. Do you know the New Forest?"

"Yes."

"Then you can fancy the difference between it and Milton. What part were you in? Were you ever at Helstone?"

"I have seen it. It was a great change to leave it and come to Milton."

Mr. Bell took up his newspaper, resolved to avoid further conversation, while Mr. Bell focused on how to break the news.

She stood at an upstairs window; she saw him alight; she guessed the truth with an instinctive flash. Standing in the middle of the drawing-room, as if arrested by a thought that turned her to stone, she was white and immovable.
