Amyas had returned home and settled quietly into domestic life. For the following twelve months, little occurred worthy of historical record. His companion, Yeo, had installed himself as *major domo*, a title with no clear duties other than trailing Amyas everywhere like a lank greyhound and spending his evenings by the fireside. True to his nature as an old sailor, Yeo kept a Bible on his knees while busily crafting countless knick-knacks—both useful and frivolous—for the entire household, particularly for Ayacanora. Every week, he would humbly offer her a toy suitable only for a child; she would pout, be scolded by Mrs. Leigh, accept the gift, and store it away forevermore. Her entire soul was consumed with the desire to become an English maiden. She spent her days chasing Mrs. Leigh, eager to learn the secrets of the kitchen, the still-room, and, above all, the art of sewing clothes for herself and eventually for everyone in Northam. First, she wished to be a good housewife like Mrs. Leigh; then, a new idea blossomed within her: the concept of giving to those who could give nothing in return.

Ayacanora had grown accustomed to the boundless hospitality of the savages, but the notion of charity toward the destitute was entirely new. Observing Mrs. Leigh spend her spare hours working for the poor and visiting their cottages, and seeing Amyas distribute money, food, and goods throughout Northam, Appledore, and Bideford after receiving public thanks in church for his safe return, she was puzzled. Her understanding of a "thank-offering" was rooted in the practices of the Indians and Spaniards—human sacrifices and the decoration of temples with skulls and bones. Yet, Amyas, a plain old-fashioned churchman, adhered strictly to the instinctive rule of giving to the Church itself. Soon, the vicar of Northam was resplendent in a new surplice, and the altar gleamed with a magnificent flagon and salver of plate taken from the great galleon (presumably lost during the Civil Wars). Ayacanora understood this sacrifice, but the alms-giving remained a mystery until Mrs. Leigh explained simply that whoever gave to the poor gave directly to the Great Spirit, for the Spirit dwelt within them—and within Ayacanora, too, if she would only be quiet and listen instead of pouting and stamping her feet. The poor child absorbed this truth readily. She worked her fingers to the bone serving the elderly women of Northam, accompanied Mrs. Leigh with a loveliness that endeared her to all, and occasionally peeked from beneath her long black lashes to see if she had won a smile from Amyas. On days she succeeded, she behaved perfectly; on days she failed, she was thoroughly naughty, testing the patience of anyone less saintly than Mrs. Leigh.

However, regarding the splendor of her attire, there were no bounds. Each Sunday, she swept into church bedizened in Spanish finery, creating such a blaze and rustle that the good vicar felt compelled to humbly remonstrate with Mrs. Leigh about the disturbance she caused to the congregation's eyes and minds. Ayacanora retorted that she paid no attention to them, nor did they need to pay attention to her. If the *Piache* (the conjurer, in plain English) desired a present, he could have all her Mexican feather-dresses; she would not wear them—they were wild Indian things, unsuitable for an English maid—but they would suit a *Piache* perfectly. Thus, she dashed upstairs, retrieved the garments, and insisted so stubbornly on dressing the vicar in them that the good man fled in haste. Nevertheless, he managed to carry off one of the finest mantles, which he cleverly repurposed into an altar-cloth. For several years thereafter, the communion at Northam was celebrated upon a blaze of emerald, azure, and crimson that had once adorned the sinful body of an Aztec prince.

While Ayacanora flaunted her colors, Amyas watched her with a mixture of amusement and simple pride in her beauty, casting glances around the church as if to declare, "Behold the fine bird I have brought home!"

Another significant trouble she caused Mrs. Leigh was her treatment of the neighborhood ladies. They flocked to congratulate Mrs. Leigh and catch a glimpse of the fair savage, but Ayacanora snubbed them all—from the vicar's wife to Lady Grenville—with such effectiveness that few dared a second visit. Mrs. Leigh remonstrated, only to be met with floods of tears. "They merely come to stare at a poor wild Indian girl," Ayacanora cried. "I would not be made a spectacle of. Once I was like a queen, and everyone obeyed me; but here, everyone looks down upon me." When Mrs. Leigh asked if she would prefer returning to the forests, the poor girl clung to her like a baby, begging not to be sent away, declaring she would sooner be a slave in the kitchen than return to the "bad people."

Month after month passed, marked by foolish storms and foolish sunshines. Yet, Ayacanora existed under the shadow of a figure characterized by neither storm nor sunshine, but a perpetual, genial calm of soft grey weather that tempered all who entered its sphere. Her outbursts grew rarer, and she became increasingly rational, though no happier, day by day.

Gradually, small hints emerged confirming her identity, at least in the eyes of Mrs. Leigh and Yeo. Once familiar with the sight of houses, she indicated she had seen similar structures before. The red cattle seemed known to her, while the sheep puzzled her until she admitted they were far too small.

"Ah, madam," remarked Yeo, seizing every clue, "it is because she has been accustomed to those great camel-sheep—the llamas—in Peru."

Ayacanora's greatest delight, however, was a horse. The mere existence of tamed animals was a daily wonder to her, but riding one was the crowning miracle. After pestering Amyas in vain for a mount (he refused to risk breaking her pretty neck), she confided in Yeo that they should steal one. Foiled in that attempt, she turned to the vicar, offering to barter all her finery for his broken-kneed pony. The vicar, too honest to strike such a bargain, declined. Consequently, Amyas purchased her a jennet, which she mastered in a fortnight, riding like a very Gaucho.

Soon, another curious slumbering memory awoke. One evening, invited by Lady Grenville, the whole family traveled to Stow. Mrs. Leigh rode soberly on a pillion behind the groom, while Ayacanora cantered wildly around the moors like a released hound, attempting to drag Amyas into a race. That night, sleeping in the same room as Mrs. Leigh, she awoke screaming, sobbing out a long tale of how the "Old Ape of Panama"—her special abhorrence—had dragged her from her bedside into the courtyard. She claimed she had mounted a horse and ridden with an Indian over vast moors and high mountains down into a dark wood, where both the Indian and the horses vanished, leaving her suddenly transformed once more into a little savage child. The impression was so vivid that she could not be convinced it had not happened, if not that very night, then certainly on some previous occasion. Mrs. Leigh, in her pious manner, accepted this as a revelation from the Lord, telling the company the next morning that the Lord had shown the child her true identity: a girl exposed in the forests by her jealous stepfather. Neither Sir Richard nor his wife could doubt this explanation. It was probable that Don Xararte, though his home was Panama, had frequented Quito, for Yeo had seen him aboard the *Lima* at Guayaquil, one of the nearest ports. This would explain why she was found by Indians beyond Cotopaxi, the nearest peak of the Eastern Andes, assuming—as was highly likely—that the old man, believing her to be Oxenham's child, had conceived the fearful vengeance of abandoning her to the wilds.

Other fragments of truth surfaced one by one, all naturally connected to animals or natural objects. Whatever moral or affectionate impressions she had retained were erased by the long spiritual death of her forest exile; Mrs. Leigh could extract no trace of feeling regarding her mother or any early religious instruction. This final link was supplied thus:

Sir Richard had brought an Indian home from Virginia. His original name is uncertain, but he was likely the "Wanchese" mentioned alongside "Manteo." This man was to be baptized in the church at Bideford under the name Raleigh, with sponsors presumably including Sir Walter Raleigh himself, who may have been in town on Virginian business, along with Sir Richard Grenville. Naturally, all the notables of Bideford attended the baptism of the first "Red Man" to tread British soil. The mayor and corporation men appeared in full robes with maces and tipstaffs to honor this first fruit of the Gospel in the West.
