Madame Beck was a woman of unwavering consistency—indulgent toward the world at large, yet tender toward none of it. Not even her own children could sway her from the cool, measured composure of her stoic nature. She cared for them, yes—watched over their health, their interests, their futures—but there was no warmth in her guardianship. Never did she gather them into her arms, press kisses to their rosy cheeks, or murmur affectionate words.

I often observed her in the garden, seated at a distance while her little ones played under Trinette’s watchful eye. Her expression was one of careful calculation, as though perpetually weighing *leur avenir*—their future. And if the youngest, a frail but sweet-natured child, happened to spot her and came running, stumbling eagerly to clasp her knee, Madame would merely extend a hand to steady the impact. *"Prends garde, mon enfant!"* she would say, unmoved, allowing the child to linger briefly before rising—without a smile, without a kiss—to return it to its nurse.

Her eldest daughter, Désirée, was another matter entirely. The girl was mischief incarnate. *"Quelle peste que cette Désirée! Quel poison que cet enfant là!"* echoed through both kitchen and schoolroom. She had a gift for provocation—slipping into the servants’ quarters to tear their caps, spill their perfumes, or lay waste to the pantry, smashing jars of preserves and then artfully shifting blame onto the cook. Madame Beck saw it all, yet her only remark was a serene, *"Désirée a besoin d’une surveillance toute particulière."* And so she kept the girl close, though never once did she correct her outright. Surveillance, she believed, would suffice. It did not.

Deprived of the servants, Désirée turned her talents upon her mother, pilfering trinkets from Madame’s dressing table—brooches, rings, scissors—then denying all knowledge of them when confronted. Madame played along, pretending belief until she could track the stolen goods to their hiding places—a crack in the garden wall, a crevice in the attic. Then, with calculated patience, she would send Désirée out on a walk and reclaim her property. The child, ever her mother’s daughter, never betrayed a flicker of shame.

The second child, Fifine, was different—said to resemble her late father. Though she had her mother’s sturdy frame and bright blue eyes, her spirit was all her own: warm, impulsive, and prone to mishaps. One day, she tumbled down a steep flight of stone steps. Madame, ever alert, emerged at the sound and lifted the child with chilling calm. *"Cet enfant a un os de cassé,"* she observed. And so it was—the little arm hung limp.

"Let Meess take her," Madame directed, and within moments, she had departed in a fiacre to fetch a surgeon. The family physician was absent, but this did not deter her; she returned with another—a young man whose entrance I scarcely noted at first, so occupied was I with soothing Fifine’s cries. The child, in her broken English, protested: *"I will not you! I will Dr. Pillule!"*

The stranger laughed. "And Dr. Pillule is my very good friend," he replied in flawless English, "but he is three leagues away, and I am here in his place." With a few spoonfuls of *eau sucrée*—Fifine’s weakness—he coaxed her into compliance, then set to work. When none of the servants would assist, Madame herself stepped forward, steady where I trembled. *"Ça vaudra mieux,"* he said, turning from me to her.

His praise of her composure was effusive. *"Voilà un sang-froid bien opportun!"* And Madame, ever receptive to admiration, brightened under his approval. But it was not merely her firmness that pleased her—it was *him*. His bearing, his voice, the vivid energy of his presence. Even in the dim evening light, one could see he was no ordinary man. Tall, finely featured, with a mouth quick to smile and eyes that danced a little too brightly, too often.

Fifine adored him despite the pain he caused her, offering her hand in farewell. He patted it kindly, then departed with Madame, who chattered with uncharacteristic vivacity as he listened, amused.

I recognized him then—the same Englishman who had guided me through the rain-soaked park, who had assisted me at the bureau. His voice, his step, the very way he carried himself—it was unmistakable.

Dr. Pillule, as fate would have it, was called away for weeks, leaving his substitute to attend the Rue Fossette. And so he returned—often. Fifine recovered swiftly, yet his visits did not cease. Soon, Désirée, ever eager for attention, took to her bed with a feigned illness, and Madame played along with grave sincerity. Dr. John—as Fifine had christened him, and as we all now called him—humored the charade, though not without occasional glances of amused skepticism between mother and child.

Why he lingered, I could not fathom. He was independent, almost careless in his manner, yet there was a pensiveness about him, as though some deeper interest held him there. And though he treated me with the polite indifference one might afford a piece of furniture, I observed him all the same—his restless energy, his fleeting expressions.

Once, as he sat bathed in sunlight, I studied the warm tones of his hair and complexion, struck by the vividness of his presence. Lost in thought, I scarcely noticed when his gaze caught mine in the small mirror fixed by the window—the same mirror Madame used to spy upon the garden.

"Mademoiselle does not spare me," he said, a thread of dryness in his voice. "I am not vain enough to think it is my merits that draw your attention. Must it be some defect, then?"

I might have explained myself, but I did not. Let him think what he wished. There is a stubborn satisfaction in being misunderstood by those who will never truly see you.

And so I bent over my work and said nothing at all.
