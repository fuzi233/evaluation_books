Bereaved

Ten months had passed since we last beheld him, yet the interval felt like a lifetime, carving years into the man before us. He had wasted away; the cordial serenity that once defined his features had curdled into a deep, suffocating gloom. His dark blue eyes, usually sharp with intelligence, now burned with a sterner, colder light beneath his shaggy grey brows. This was no ordinary decay of spirit; the marks of angrier passions, darker than grief alone could leave, etched themselves upon his face.

We had not driven far when the General spoke, breaking the silence with the blunt, soldierly directness he favored. He launched into a tirade against the "hellish arts" to which his beloved niece and ward had fallen prey. With a bitterness that bordered on fury, he inveighed against Heaven's tolerance, wondering how such a monstrous indulgence of lust and malignity could be permitted to fester.

My father, sensing immediately that something extraordinary had transpired, asked him to detail the circumstances that warranted such vehement language.

"I would tell you all with pleasure," the General replied, "but you would not believe me."

"Why not?" my father pressed gently.

"Because," the General snapped, his voice tight with irritation, "you believe only in what fits your own prejudices and illusions. I remember when I was like you, but I have learned better."

"Try me," my father said calmly. "I am no dogmatist. Besides, I know you demand proof for your beliefs, and thus, I am predisposed to respect your conclusions."

"You are right," the General admitted, his gaze drifting gloomily over the opening glades of the forest. "I have not been led lightly into a belief in the marvelous—for what I have experienced *is* marvelous. Extraordinary evidence has forced me to credit what runs diametrically counter to all my theories. I have been made the dupe of a preternatural conspiracy."

I watched my father exchange a glance with the General, a look heavy with suspicion regarding the soldier's sanity. Fortunately, the General did not notice. He stared ahead, consumed by a grim curiosity.

"You are heading for the Ruins of Karnstein?" he asked suddenly. "A lucky coincidence. I was about to ask you to guide me there. I have a special purpose in exploring. There is a ruined chapel, isn't there? With many tombs of that extinct family?"

"So there are," my father said cheerfully. "Highly interesting. Are you thinking of claiming the title and estates?"

My father spoke with a lightness that betrayed no malice, but the General missed the courtesy of a friend's jest entirely. He did not smile; instead, his face grew grave and fierce, ruminating on thoughts that stirred both his horror and his rage.

"Something very different," he growled. "I intend to unearth some of those fine people. By God's blessing, I aim to accomplish a pious sacrilege here, relieving our earth of certain monsters so that honest folk may sleep without fear of assassins. I have strange tales to tell you, my dear friend, things I myself would have scouted as impossible just months ago."

My father looked at him again, but this time not with suspicion, but with keen intelligence and alarm.

"The house of Karnstein," my father corrected softly, "has been extinct for over a century. My late wife was maternally descended from them, but the name and title have long ceased to exist. The castle is a ruin; the village is deserted. It has been fifty years since a chimney smoked there; not a roof remains standing."

"Quite true," the General acknowledged. "I have heard much of that since I last saw you—much that will astonish you. But I had better relate everything in the order of its occurrence." He paused, his voice dropping. "You saw my dear ward—my child, I may call her. No creature could have been more beautiful, and only three months ago, none more blooming."

"Yes, poor thing!" my father exclaimed, his voice thick with emotion. "When I saw her last, she was indeed lovely. I was grieved and shocked beyond words, my dear friend; I knew the blow you suffered."

He took the General's hand, and they exchanged a firm, sympathetic pressure. Tears gathered in the old soldier's eyes, unashamed and raw.

"We have been old friends," the General whispered, his voice trembling. "I knew you would feel for me, childless as I am. She became an object of near interest to me, repaying my care with an affection that cheered my home and made my life happy. That is all gone. The years remaining to me on earth may be short; but by God's mercy, I hope to accomplish a service to mankind before I die, and to subserve the vengeance of Heaven upon the fiends who murdered my poor child in the spring of her hopes and beauty!"

"You said you intended to relate everything as it occurred," my father reminded him. "Pray do; I assure you, it is not mere curiosity that prompts me."

By this time, we had reached the fork where the Drunstall road, by which the General had arrived, diverged from our path toward Karnstein.

"How far is it to the ruins?" the General inquired, his eyes scanning the road ahead with anxious intensity.

"About half a league," my father answered. "Now, pray let us hear the story you promised."
