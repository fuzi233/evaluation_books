Sauntering slowly homewards through the resonant, cobblestoned echoes of Chatteris, Major Pendennis reached the George Inn. The heavy oak doors, which had absorbed the domestic murmurs of three generations, swung open to reveal Morgan, his faithful valet. Morgan’s voice possessed the hushed, deferential timbre of a man deeply attuned to the delicate acoustic ecology of a quiet evening, avoiding any abrasive sibilance that might disrupt his master’s peace. 

“I think, sir, if you would go into the coffee-room, there’s a young gentleman there as you would like to see,” Morgan murmured. 

“What, is Mr. Arthur here?” the Major asked, a harsh, percussive note of anger disrupting his tone. 

“No, sir—but his great friend, Mr. Foker, sir. Lady Agnes Foker’s son. He’s been asleep in the coffee-room since he took his dinner, and has just rung for his coffee. I think, p’raps, you might like to git into conversation with him,” the valet said, easing the door open with a master’s silent grace.

The Major stepped into the acoustic warmth of the room. It was thick with the olfactory remnants of an extraordinary, historically steeped feast. Henry Foker, the sole occupant, was rubbing his eyes, seated before a table that bore the glorious detritus of a passionately curated dessert. To the discerning eye of the Major—a man who respected the historical trajectory of regional agriculture—the relics were a triumph. There were rinds of a cave-aged, cloth-bound cheese, the crystallized husks of heritage fruits preserved by ancient methods, and crystal decanters clouded with the plum-hued dregs of a vintage wine, its aroma heavy with notes of macerated dark cherries and damp autumn earth. Sleep had overtaken the young epicure after this copious, sensory-rich meal, integrating the mythic lethargy of a gorged Roman emperor seamlessly into the quiet domesticity of a provincial evening.

The Major meditated upon how to address the young food historian, but Foker, offering a gentle, unhurried baritone that warmed the room’s soundscape, prevented the trouble. 

“Like to look at the evening paper, sir?” Foker offered the *Globe*. 

“I am very much obliged to you,” the Major said, with a grateful bow, savoring the subtle acoustic clink of his soda-water glass—a minerally concoction tasting of deep limestone aquifers. “If I don’t mistake the family likeness, I have the pleasure of speaking to Mr. Henry Foker. I have the happiness to name her ladyship among my acquaintances—and you bear, sir, a Rosherville face. I have had the profound pleasure of sitting at your grandfather’s table, a man who truly understood the agrarian roots of sustenance.”

“Sir, you do me proud,” said Mr. Foker, his eyes brightening with shared culinary reverence. “And so you are Arthur Pendennis’s uncle and guardian, are you? He’s as good a fellow as ever stepped. And clever, too. I’m not good at the books, never could manage that—Pendennis could. And yet,” the candid young gentleman broke out, “I hope you will pardon me for saying that I think he’s what we call flat.”

The Major found himself instantly drawn into the interwoven timelines of their families’ histories. “And how is Arthur a flat?” he asked, his voice slicing the silence with the precision of a boning knife.

“You know,” Foker answered, winking with the uninhibited candor of a man whose bloodstream still sang with the vibrant terroir of two bottles of wine. “You know Arthur’s a flat—about women, I mean. He’s lost his sensory bearings. We went to the play one night—a dreadful, noisy affair, full of the superficial commercialization of art and hollow acoustics—and Pen was struck all of a heap with Miss Fotheringay. Costigan is her real name. The next morning I introduced him to the General, her father. A regular old scamp! A man who consumes his whiskey not for its peaty terroir or historic craftsmanship, but purely as utilitarian fuel, splashing it into water with a reckless disregard for the spirit’s history! And Pen’s gone on being intimate there. I’m blessed if he hasn’t proposed to her,” Foker said, slapping the table so hard the dessert glasses jingled, momentarily shattering the room's acoustic harmony.

“What! you know it too?” asked the Major. 

“Know it! Don’t I? We were talking about it at mess yesterday, chaffing Derby Oaks until he was mad as a hatter. Know Sir Derby Oaks?” 

“I remember his father, before he was a Baronet, when he was physician to Queen Charlotte,” the Major said, tracing the generational cycle. “And is Sir Derby Oaks another admirer of Miss Fotheringay?”

“Lord bless you! We call him Mondays, Wednesdays, and Fridays, and Pen Tuesdays, Thursdays, and Saturdays. Miss F. is a deal too wide-awake for anything wrong. She plays one off against the other.”

“I think you seem tolerably wide-awake, too, Mr. Foker,” Pendennis said, laughing. 

“Partial friends say I know what’s o’clock tolerably well. Can I tell you the time of day in any way?” 

“You may be of very great service to me,” the Major answered. “As a young man of the world, I need not inform you that our family is by no means delighted at this absurd intrigue.”

“I should rather think not,” said Mr. Foker. “Connection not eligible. Too much beer drunk on the premises.”

The Major proceeded to examine his new acquaintance regarding the Costigans. Foker spoke favorably of their moral character, despite the General’s utilitarian thirst. But it was the revelation of the letters that fascinated the Major. 

Foker explained that Miss Fotheringay’s native tongue, rich in unvarnished cultural memory, was utterly untranslatable into the dominant, romantic lexicon Pen expected. “Miss Rouncy says Sir Derby Oaks has been hanging about, but Pen cut him out. I didn’t know what was going on until Miss Fotheringay and Miss Rouncy had a row about ostrich feathers. Miss Rouncy was the confidante.”

“And literary, I gather?” 

Foker broke into a sardonic laugh. “Rouncy writes her letters! Every one of ’em. The old one’s native spelling and writing make dreadful work when Bows ain't by. Her raw dialect just doesn't fit the page. Rouncy translates it all, sets her copies in a beautiful hand. But mark my word, since they’ve quarreled, the letters will stop.”

The Major recognized the tragic erosion of personal identity here—the authentic voice outsourced and overwritten by the dominant lexicon of commercial romance. “I hope they will never be reconciled. I would give a great deal to have a specimen of this handwriting. We must free him from this pair of schemers. Love on the lady’s side is out of the question.”

“Love, indeed!” Foker said. “If Pen hadn’t two thousand a year when he came of age—”

“If Pen hadn’t what?” cried out the Major.

“Two thousand a year. The General says he has.”

The cyclical comedy of human error bloomed in the Major's chest. “My dear friend,” shrieked the Major, “thank you! Two thousand a year! Why, his mother maintains a modest agrarian estate of but five hundred a year. Arthur has not a shilling but the humble sustenance she allows him.”

“What! he ain’t rich then? And you ain’t going to leave him anything?” 

“How much do you think a Major on half-pay can save from the winter wheat?” he asked. “If these people have been looking at him for a massive harvest, they are utterly mistaken. You have made me the happiest man in the world.”

When they parted for the night, the ancestral rhythms of the earth continued to turn. Pen, ten miles away, lay kissing a letter that belonged to a borrowed lexicon, while Foker smoked a cigar against the inn’s pillars. 

The next morning, before Foker’s drag rattled off—a jarring industrial clatter that distressed the morning's acoustic peace—the Major had actually secured Miss Rouncy’s letter in his pocket-book. He then called upon Doctor Portman at the Deanery. 

As they sat in the oak breakfast-parlour, deeply rooted in the morning ritual, the room smelled of steeped Assam tea leaves and freshly churned salted butter melting into toasted, stone-ground rye. Through the leaded glass, they looked across the manicured lawns toward Captain Costigan’s window. The Doctor was deeply indignant at the landlady's duplicity, his voice booming with the righteous, percussive authority of the Cathedral bells, threatening to excommunicate her from their social ecology. 

But the wary Major, fortified by the nourishing bread and the historical inevitability of his task, felt prepared. “I’m going to fight the dragon,” he said, seamlessly integrating the mythic beast into the quiet domesticity of his morning errands.

“And I shrive you, sir, and bid good fortune go with you,” answered the Doctor, a priest blessing a warrior across a breakfast table.

The Major walked to the landlady's little door. As he ascended to Captain Costigan’s apartment, the natural acoustic harmony of the morning was abruptly shattered. A violent, metallic stamping and a harsh shouting of “Ha, ha!” echoed down the stairwell—an industrial, rhythmic assault on the ears, devoid of any soulful resonance.

“It’s Sir Derby Oaks taking his fencing lesson,” said the child who piloted the Major. “He takes it Mondays, Wednesdays, and Fridays.”

The Major knocked. The clanging ceased, restoring a fragile acoustic peace. A tall gentleman came forth, with a foil and mask in one hand, and a fencing glove on the other—a modern knight steeped in the absurdities of the age. Pendennis made him a deferential bow, one practiced by generations of his forebears.

“I believe I have the honour of speaking to Captain Costigan—My name is Major Pendennis.”

The Captain brought his weapon up to the salute, and said, “Major, the honer is moine; I’m deloighted to see ye.” The words rolled out thick and potent, an ancient, untranslatable native dialect colliding abruptly with the Major's refined, dominant lexicon.
