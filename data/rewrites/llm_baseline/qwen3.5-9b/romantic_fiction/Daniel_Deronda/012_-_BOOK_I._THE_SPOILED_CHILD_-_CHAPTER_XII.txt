"O gentlemen, the time of life is short; To spend that shortness basely were too long..."

On the second day following the archery contest, Mr. Henleigh Mallinger Grandcourt sat at his breakfast table alongside Mr. Lush. The atmosphere surrounding them was undeniably pleasant: the summer breeze drifted through the open casements, allowing the dogs to wander freely onto the ancient green turf of the lawn; beyond lay the park, painted in soft purples and stretching toward a dense woodland border. Inside, the room possessed a stillness born of its sober, antique elegance—a quiet dignity that felt conscious and well-bred, standing in stark contrast to the restless clutter of vulgar furnishings.

Whether the gentlemen themselves were congenial was far less clear. Grandcourt had pulled his chair back to face the garden, propping his left leg atop another seat and resting his right elbow on the table while he smoked a large cigar. His companion, Mr. Lush, continued to eat. A half-dozen dogs of various breeds moved lazily between them, shifting their allegiance vacillatingly from one master to the other. These were dogs of immense privilege, capable of playing at hunger, content merely to be offered delicacies they refused to consume. All, save for Fetch, a magnificent liver-colored water spaniel. She sat with her forepaws firmly planted, her expressive brown gaze fixed upward upon Grandcourt with unwavering constancy.

In his lap, Grandcourt cradled a tiny Maltese dog adorned with a miniature silver collar and bell. Whenever his hand was free from the cigar or the coffee cup, it rested possessively on this small bundle of animal warmth. One might assume Fetch was jealous, wounded by her master's lack of acknowledgment. Eventually, her patience snapped; she gently laid her large, silky paw upon his leg. Grandcourt regarded her with an impassive face for half a minute before laying down his cigar to lift the indifferent Fluff close to his chin, bestowing affectionate pats all the while maintaining a grave stare at Fetch. Poor creature, she whimpered intermittently, seemingly struggling to suppress her visible displeasure, until she finally rested her head beside the pleading paw, gazing up with pitiful entreaty. Thus, at least, a lover of dogs might interpret the scene, and Grandcourt, reputed to adore animals, certainly acted upon such an interpretation. Yet, when the amusing anguish erupted into a howling bark, Grandcourt shoved Fetch aside without a word. He deposited Fluff carelessly onto the table, where the puppy's black nose dominated a salt cellar, and turned his attention back to his cigar, annoyed to find it needed relighting due to Fetch's interference.

Fetch, having commenced her wailing, discovered, like many of her sex, that stopping was difficult. Indeed, the second howl was louder, and the third matched it perfectly. "Turn out that brute, will you?" Grandcourt commanded Lush, neither raising his voice nor looking at him, as if expecting the slightest gesture to command immediate obedience. Lush promptly rose, lifted the surprisingly heavy spaniel despite his dislike for stooping, and carried her outside, disappearing for a couple of minutes before returning. He then lit a fresh cigar, positioned himself at an angle where he could observe Grandcourt's face without turning fully, and soon asked, "Shall you ride or drive to Quetcham today?"

"I am not going to Quetcham."

"You did not go yesterday either."

Grandcourt smoked in silence for a moment before replying, "I suppose you sent my card and inquiries."

"I went myself at four o'clock and informed them you would arrive shortly. They would naturally assume some accident prevented you from keeping your appointment, especially if you intend to go today."

Silence followed for several minutes. Then Grandcourt asked, "What men are invited here with their wives?"

Lush produced a notebook. "The Captain and Mrs. Torrington arrive next week. There are also Mr. Hollis and Lady Flora, along with the Cushats and the Gogoffs."

"A rather ragged collection," Grandcourt observed after a pause. "Why did you invite the Gogoffs? When writing invitations in my name, be kind enough to provide a list beforehand, rather than summoning a giantess upon me without my knowledge. She ruins the aesthetic of the room."

"You invited the Gogoffs yourself when you met them in Paris."

"What does my meeting them in Paris have to do with this? I instructed you to give me a list."

Grandcourt possessed two remarkably distinct voices. Up to this point, we had heard him speak in a superficial, interrupted drawl suggesting primarily languor and ennui. However, this latest remark emerged in subdued, inward, yet distinct tones—a sound Mr. Lush had long recognized as the manifestation of a peremptory will.

"Are there any other couples you wish to invite?"

"Yes; select some respectable people with a daughter or two. And include one of your damned musicians. But no comic fellows."

"I wonder if Klesmer would consent to join us once he leaves Quetcham. Nothing but first-class music will satisfy Miss Arrowpoint."

Lush spoke casually, yet he seized the opening to fix an observant gaze upon Grandcourt, who, for the first time, turned his eyes toward his companion. He waited silently, exhaling two long, luxurious puffs, before saying, perhaps in a tone even lower than before, but edged with perceptible contempt, "What in the name of nonsense have I to do with Miss Arrowpoint and her music?"

"Well, something," Lush replied jocosely. "You need not trouble yourself greatly, perhaps. But certain formalities must be observed before a man can marry a million."

"Very likely. But I am not going to marry a million."

"That is a pity—to squander such an opportunity and dismantle your own plans."

"_Your_ plans, I presume you mean."

"You have debts, you know, and matters may prove inconvenient eventually. The heirship is not absolutely certain."

Grandcourt offered no reply, and Lush pressed on. "Truly, it is a splendid opportunity. The parents demand nothing less than perfection, and the daughter's appearance and demeanor require no deductions whatsoever, as if she possessed not a single penny. She lacks conventional beauty, yet possesses the bearing to carry any rank. Furthermore, she is unlikely to reject the prospects you can offer."

"Perhaps not."

"The parents would allow you to do whatever you wished with them."

"But I should not wish to do anything with them."

Here, Lush paused briefly before speaking again, adopting a deep, admonishing tone. "Good heavens, Grandcourt! After your experiences, will you permit a mere whim to interfere with your comfortable settlement in life?"

"Spare your oratory. I know precisely what I intend to do."

"What?" Lush extinguished his cigar and shoved his hands into his side pockets, appearing exasperated yet determined to maintain his composure. "I am going to marry the other girl."

"Have you fallen in love?" The question carried a sharp sneer.

"I am going to marry her."

"You have already made her an offer, then?"

"No."

"She is a young lady with a will of her own, I imagine. Extremely suited to making a scene. She knows exactly what she desires."

"She does not like you," Grandcourt stated, wearing the faintest ghost of a smile.

"Quite true," Lush added, his sneer becoming more marked. "Nevertheless, if you and she are devoted to one another, that shall suffice."

Grandcourt ignored this comment entirely, sipping his coffee, rising, and strolling out onto the lawn, followed closely by the pack of dogs. Lush watched him depart for a moment, then resumed his cigar, lighting it slowly. He consulted his beard with inspecting eyes and fingers, finally stroking it with the air of someone who had reached a conclusion, murmuring in a subdued voice, "Check, old boy!"

Mr. Lush, a man of considerable ability, had known Grandcourt for fifteen years without failing to learn which measures were futile, though what strategies might succeed often remained obscure. At the start of his career, Lush held a fellowship and nearly entered the clergy for a college living; however, lacking enthusiasm for that path, he accepted the role of traveling companion first to a marquis, and subsequently to young Grandcourt, who, having lost his father early, found Lush so convenient that he permitted him to serve as prime minister in all his personal affairs. Fifteen years of habit had rendered Grandcourt increasingly dependent on Lush's dexterity, while Lush grew equally reliant on the lazy luxury afforded by his transactions on Grandcourt's behalf, which demanded no significant interruption.

One cannot claim that this prolonged association intensified Grandcourt's inherent disrespect for his companion, for such disdain had been absolute from the outset. Rather, it confirmed Grandcourt's belief that he might strike Lush whenever he chose—though he never actually chose to kick any animal, for the act of kicking is a compromising posture, and a gentleman's dogs should be kicked on his behalf. Instead, Grandcourt merely uttered words that might expose him to retaliation if his confidant possessed an independent spirit. But what son of a vicar, who has starved his wife and daughters of calico to send his male offspring to Oxford, can retain an independent spirit when bent on dining with refined discrimination, riding superior horses, and living generally amidst the most luxuriant honey-blossomed clover—all without labor?
