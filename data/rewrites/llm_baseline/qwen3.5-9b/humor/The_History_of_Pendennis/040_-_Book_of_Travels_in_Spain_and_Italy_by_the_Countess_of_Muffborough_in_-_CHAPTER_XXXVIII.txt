Better folk than Morgan, the valet, were not so well instructed as that gentleman regarding the true extent of Lady Clavering's riches. Upon her arrival in London, the city buzzed with a legend of her fortune that bordered on the mythical. Whispers circulated of indigo factories, opium clippers, and banks swollen with rupees. Stories swirled of diamonds and jewels belonging to native princes, alongside vast sums of interest paid by those very princes for loans contracted by their ancestors to Lady Clavering's father. Her account at the London banker was said to contain so many zeros that they created a corresponding number of "O"s of admiration in the minds of the listening crowd.

It was a known fact that an envoy from an Indian Prince, Colonel Altamont—the Nawab of Lucknow's favorite, an extraordinary figure rumored to have embraced Islam and survived a thousand wild adventures—was currently in England attempting to negotiate the sale of the Nawab's famous nose-ring diamond, *'The Light of the Dewan.'* Under the title of the Begum, Lady Clavering's fame rippled through London before she even descended upon the capital. Just as legal scholars like Delolme and Blackstone boasted that the British Constitution admits merit of every kind into its aristocracy, allowing the lowliest-born to wear the robes of a peer if they deserve it, so too did our good society operate on a similar, albeit more mercenary, principle. Though proud and jealous of its privileges, high society removes all barriers instantly for the wealthy. If an individual possesses enough gold, they are welcomed with open arms, invited to dinners, dances, and marriages, treated with the same affability one would show a common citizen.

Having overseen the arrangements for the country mansion, our friend the Chevalier Strong applied his taste to the fashionable London upholsterers preparing the town house for the Claverings. In decorating this elegant abode, honest Strong's soul rejoiced as if he were the proprietor himself. He hung and re-hung pictures, studied sofa placements, and interviewed wine merchants and purveyors. Simultaneously, the Baronet's factotum utilized the opportunity to furnish his own chambers and stock his cozy cellar. Friends complimented the neatness of Strong's quarters, and select guests sharing his cutlets discovered bottles of excellent claret to accompany their meals. The Chevalier was, as he put it, "in clover," residing comfortably in Shepherd's Inn. He was attended by a former Spanish Legionary and comrade, whom he had rescued from a breach at a Spanish fort and elevated to the rank of body-servant for both himself and his roommate. This loyal retainer was none other than the favorite of the Nawab of Lucknow, the valiant Colonel Altamont.

Ned Strong was a man of few curiosities and immense discretion; he avoided inquiring into the mysterious connection established shortly after their meeting at Baymouth between Sir Francis Clavering and the Nawab's envoy. The Colonel clearly held some secret leverage over Clavering, forcing the Baronet to submit. Strong, aware of Clavering's irregular past and lackluster military career in India, suspected the Colonel possessed a hold on Sir Francis that compelled obedience. In truth, Strong had long viewed Sir Francis as a man utterly weak in purpose, principle, and intellect—a moral and physical trifler.

After their initial meeting at Baymouth, Strong witnessed several private interviews between His Excellency and Sir Francis, the nature of which the Baronet refused to disclose. However, correspondence flowed via Altamont, who served as Clavering's ambassador in all matters. On one occasion, the Nawab's envoy was in a foul mood, crushing Clavering's letter in his hand and declaring with characteristic emphasis: "A hundred, be hanged! I'll have no more letters nor no more shilly-shally. Tell Clavering I'll have a thousand, or by Jove I'll split and burst him all to atoms. Let him give me a thousand, and I'll go abroad. I give you my honor as a gentleman I won't ask him for more for a year. Deliver this message, Strong, my boy. Tell him if the money isn't here next Friday at twelve o'clock, as sure as my name is what it is, I'll publish a paragraph in the newspaper on Saturday, and next week I'll blow up the whole concern."

Strong returned these dire words to his principal, whose reaction was immediate. On the appointed day and hour, the Chevalier appeared once more at Altamont's hotel at Baymouth with the required sum. Altamont, behaving as a true gentleman, settled his bill and departed on a foreign tour, embarking at Dover. Strong concluded it must be forgery that placed Clavering in the Colonel's power, noting that the Colonel likely held the original bill.

However, within the year, the Colonel returned. A disastrous run on the red had ruined him at Baden-Baden; no gentleman could withstand a color rising fourteen times in succession. Obligated to draw upon Sir Francis Clavering for funds to return home, Clavering—despite pressing financial needs for elections, his country estate, and the furnishing of his London home—found means to accept the Colonel's bill, though reluctantly. Overheard by Strong, Sir Francis cursed the heavens, wishing the Colonel were locked in a German debtor's prison for life. These subsidies were raised secretly from Clavering's wife, who, despite her sumptuous spending habits, inherited a sharp business acumen from her father, Snell. She granted her husband only a modest allowance, demanding precise accounts for any extra expenditures. Regarding the Colonel's demands, Clavering frankly told Strong he could not discuss it with his wife.

Part of Strong's duties involved procuring these funds. Consequently, in the Chevalier's apartments at Shepherd's Inn, numerous negotiations occurred between moneyed gentlemen and Sir Francis Clavering, resulting in the exchange of valuable banknotes and stamped paper. For a man accustomed to trading promises for cash since youth, prosperity rarely brings permanent relief; the money-lender inevitably returns. Finding it more convenient to meet creditors at Strong's lodgings than his own, Clavering relied heavily on the Chevalier. Although Strong possessed no personal wealth, his name appeared as the drawer on nearly all the bills of exchange Clavering accepted. Strong would discount these bills in the City, then parley with holders when they matured, securing installments or fresh acceptances in exchange for time. Much like the troops at Comorn, who maintained cheerfulness amidst the threat of assault and execution, hundreds of gallant spirits in this town dined in gaiety and slept comfortably, burdened only by the proximity of a bailiff and the noose of debt. Ned Strong, the old soldier, bore these trifling inconveniences with remarkable ease.

But we must leave these inhabitants of Shepherd's Inn to focus on Lady Clavering and her friends, who had been waiting at the doorstep of Grosvenor Place. They entered a gorgeous dining room, furnished in a middle-aged style that puzzled Lady Clavering until she laughed, remarking, "Unless... because me and Clavering are middle-aged people!" They were offered the copious remnants of the luncheon she and Blanche had just enjoyed.

Our little Sylphide, who barely touched food at dinner compared to the six grains of rice eaten by Amina, the friend of the Ghouls in the Arabian Nights, proved surprisingly active with her knife and fork, consuming a substantial portion of mutton cutlets. In this display of hypocrisy, she was believed to resemble other young ladies of fashion. Pen and his uncle declined the refection but admired the room with fitting compliments, pronouncing it "very chaste"—the proper term for such decor. Indeed, there were high-backed Dutch chairs of the seventeenth century, a sculptured carved buffet of the sixteenth, a sideboard looted from a Low Country church, and a large brass cathedral lamp over a round oak table. Old family portraits from Wardour Street, French tapestries, bits of armor, double-handed swords and battle-axes made of carton-pierre, looking-glasses, saintly statuettes, and Dresden china filled the space. Nothing, in short, could be chaster.

Behind lay the library, fitted with busts and books of uniform size, wonderful easy-chairs, and solemn bronzes in the severe classic style. Here, guarded by double doors, Sir Francis smoked cigars, read *Bell's Life in London*, and fell asleep after dinner, unless occupied at the billiard table or gambling houses in St. James's. Yet, what equaled the chaste splendor of the drawing-rooms? The carpets were so magnificently fluffy that footsteps made no sound louder than shadows. Upon their white grounds bloomed roses and tulips as large as warming-pans. High and low chairs, including bandy-legged specimens so attenuated that only a sylph could sit upon them, surrounded marquetry tables covered in marvelous gimcracks. There were china ornaments of all eras, bronzes, gilt daggers, *Books of Beauty*, yataghans, Turkish papooshes, and boxes of Parisian bonbons. Everywhere one sat, Dresden shepherds and shepherdesses stood ready at the elbow. Porcelain poodles, ducks, cocks, and hens populated the shelves, alongside nymphs by Boucher and shepherdesses by Greuze—truly chaste subjects. Muslin and brocade curtains framed gilt cages housing parrots and lovebirds, two squealing cockatoos that out-chattered one another, a clock singing tunes on a console table, and another booming the hours like Great Tom on the mantelpiece.
