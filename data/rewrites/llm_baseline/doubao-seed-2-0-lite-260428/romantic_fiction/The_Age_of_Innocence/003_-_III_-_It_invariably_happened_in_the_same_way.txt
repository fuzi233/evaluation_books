The ritual unfurled exactly as it did every single year. Mrs. Julius Beaufort never missed a single minute of the opera on the night of her annual ball—indeed, she deliberately scheduled the party on an opera night just to flaunt that she was entirely above fretting over household details, and that her staff of highly competent servants could pull off every last element of the entertainment without her ever stepping foot at home ahead of time.

The Beaufort brownstone was one of the rare houses in old New York that boasted a purpose-built ballroom, older even than the ones at Mrs. Manson Mingott’s and the Headly Chiverses’ estates. Back when most people still considered it merely “provincial” to lay a cheap crash mat over the drawing room floors and haul all the furniture upstairs for a party, the Beauforts’ permanent, unused ballroom was a mark of unassailable status: it sat shuttered 364 days a year, gilt chairs stacked in one corner, its grand crystal chandelier wrapped in linen storage bag, and that quiet, unarguable luxury was enough to make society overlook every last unseemly detail of Beaufort’s murky past.

Mrs. Archer, who loved turning her social philosophies into pithy little axioms, had once declared “We all have our pet common people we tuck away” — and daring as the line was, every exclusive drawing room in the city secretly agreed it rang true. But the Beauforts weren’t just common; more than one person whispered they were far worse. Mrs. Beaufort had been born Regina Dallas, a penniless exquisite beauty from one of South Carolina’s most respected old families, introduced to New York society by her well-meaning but perpetually impulsive cousin Medora Manson. If you were related to the Mansons and the Rushworths, you earned what Sillerton Jackson (who’d once frequented the old Tuileries court) called *droit de cité* — automatic, unchallenged entry to New York high society. But everyone wondered if Regina had forfeited that right the second she married Julius Beaufort.

No one was quite sure who Beaufort actually was. He passed for English: handsome, quick to laugh, sharp-tongued, wildly hospitable, and brilliant. He’d arrived in America with letters of recommendation from old Mrs. Manson Mingott’s English banker son-in-law, and had shot straight to the top of the city’s business circles in no time. But his habits were dissipated, his wit too cutting for stuffy old sensibilities, his origins completely unaccounted for, and when Medora Manson announced the engagement, everyone just sighed and added it to her long list of impulsive mistakes.

Folly works out as often as wisdom, though. Two years after the wedding, everyone agreed the young Mrs. Beaufort ran the most distinguished, sought-after household in New York. No one could work out how she pulled it off. She was quiet, almost indolent, the sharpest gossips even called her dull — but dressed like a marble idol, draped in pearls, growing blonder, softer and more beautiful with every passing year, she held court in her husband’s heavy brownstone palace and drew every notable name in the city to her doorstep without ever lifting a jeweled little finger. The people in the know whispered it was all Beaufort behind the scenes: training the servants, teaching the chef brand new recipes, telling the gardeners exactly what rare hothouse blooms to grow for the tables, picking the guest list, stirring the after-dinner punch, even dictating the short little notes his wife sent her friends. But he kept all that domestic work completely private, and presented to the world the air of a careless, wealthy millionaire strolling into his own drawing room like a casual invited guest, saying, “My wife’s gloxinias are a marvel, aren’t they? I believe she gets them sent over from Kew.”

Everyone agreed Beaufort’s whole magic was the way he could carry off absolutely anything. Even the persistent rumor that the international bank he’d worked for back in England had “helped him leave the country” rolled right off his back — and New York was every bit as strict about business morals as it was about social ones. He charmed his way past every whisper, won over the whole of New York high society, and for over twenty years people had said they were “going over to the Beauforts’” with the same unshakable confidence they said they were visiting old Mrs. Manson Mingott’s, plus the added lovely certainty that they’d get plump hot canvasback ducks and rare vintage wine, not tepid unlabeled Veuve Clicquot and stale warmed-up croquettes shipped up from Philadelphia.

As usual, Mrs. Beaufort stepped into her opera box right before the Jewel Song, and when she stood up at the end of the third act, wrapped her opera cloak around her lovely bare shoulders, and slipped out, every New Yorker in the opera house knew the annual ball would begin half an hour later. The Beaufort house was the pride of the city, the one everyone dragged their foreign guests to tour, especially on ball night. The Beauforts had been the first in New York to own their own red velvet carpet, rolled down the front steps by their own liveried footmen under their own custom awning, no need to rent it along with the supper and ballroom chairs. They’d also started the revolutionary custom of letting ladies leave their cloaks in the front hall, instead of tramping all the way up to the hostess’s bedroom to re-curl their hair over a gas jet — Beaufort had joked once that he assumed all his wife’s friends had maids to make sure their coiffures were perfect before they left home. The whole house had been deliberately laid out with the ballroom at the end of a long vista of connected drawing rooms: sea-green, crimson, and gold bouton d’or, so guests could walk solemnly down their length, see the hundreds of candlelit chandeliers glinting off the polished parquet floors, and past that the conservatory, where camellias and tall tree ferns arched their pricey fronds over black and gold bamboo seats.

Newland Archer drifted in later than most, as befitted a young man of his social standing. He handed his overcoat to a footman in pristine silk stockings (the stockings were Beaufort’s one silly, beloved indulgence), lingered a while in the library lined with Spanish leather and furnished with Buhl antiques and malachite pieces, where a handful of other men chatted and pulled on their dancing gloves, before he joined the line of guests waiting to greet Mrs. Beaufort at the crimson drawing room threshold.

He was distinctly nervous. He hadn’t gone back to his club after the opera like most young bachelors usually did; the night was mild, so he’d walked a good stretch up Fifth Avenue first before turning back toward the Beauforts’ house. He was terrified the Mingotts were pushing things too far — that Granny Mingott had ordered them to bring Countess Olenska right to the ball. From the way everyone in the club box had reacted back at the opera, he knew that would be a catastrophic social misstep. Even though he was more determined than ever to stand by his promise to support his betrothed’s cousin, he no longer felt that bright, chivalrous eagerness he’d had before their short conversation at the opera.

Wandering into the gold bouton d’or drawing room, where Beaufort had shockingly hung that controversial Bouguereau nude *Love Victorious*, Archer spotted Mrs. Welland and her daughter standing right by the ballroom doors. Couples were already gliding across the floor past them, soft candlelight catching the swirling tulle skirts, the circlets of small white blossoms on the young girls’ heads, the sparkling aigrettes and jewels in the young married women’s updos, the crisp glitter of starched shirt fronts and brand new white glacé gloves.

Miss Welland, her hand full of white lilies of the valley (she carried no other bouquet), was clearly about to join the dancers, but lingered on the threshold. Her cheeks were a little pale, her eyes bright with soft, unguarded excitement. A little circle of young men and girls had gathered around her, laughing, clasping hands, trading light pleasantries, and a little distance away Mrs. Welland watched them, beaming with the carefully calibrated air of mild parental reluctance that was considered perfectly proper for the occasion. It was obvious they were announcing their engagement right then, and Archer had been the one to insist they do it — but this was not at all how he’d imagined his happiest news being shared. Proclaiming it in the noise and crush of a packed ballroom stripped it of that soft, private bloom that ought to wrap around the most precious things in a person’s life. His joy was so deep that this small public blur didn’t touch its core, but he still ached to keep that surface sweetness untouched. To his relief, he saw May Welland felt the exact same way. Her eyes flew to his, beseeching, as if to say *We’re only doing this because it’s the right thing*.

No plea could have reached him faster. But he wished there was some grand, noble reason they had to rush this announcement, not just the messy, awkward business of poor Ellen Olenska’s unspoken scandal. The crowd around May stepped aside for him with knowing, warm smiles, and after he accepted all their congratulations, he drew his betrothed right out onto the ballroom floor, settled his arm around her waist, and they drifted away to the soft, swelling waltz of *The Blue Danube*.

She didn’t say a word at first, just smiled, her lips trembling a little, her eyes soft and faraway, as if she was looking at some perfect, unnameable vision just out of sight. “My dearest,” he whispered, pressing her closer. For the first time it hit him: even in the middle of a crowded ballroom, the first hours of being betrothed felt grave, almost sacramental. What a beautiful new life stretched out ahead of him, with this bright, pure, gentle goodness at his side.

When the dance ended, the two of them, as was proper for an engaged couple, wandered off into the conservatory. They sat behind a thick screen of tree ferns and camellias, and Newland lifted her gloved hand to his lips.
“I told you I’d do what you asked,” May said.
“Yes, I couldn’t bear to wait any longer,” he laughed softly. After a beat, he added, “I just wish we didn’t have to make the announcement in the middle of a ball, that’s all.”
“I know,” she said, meeting his gaze perfectly, as if she understood every single thought in his head. “But after all — even here, we’re alone together, aren’t we?”
“Oh my love, we always will be,” he swore.

It was clear she was always going to get it, always say exactly the right thing. The thought made his cup of bliss overflow, and he said gaily, “The worst part is I want to kiss you properly, and I can’t.” He glanced quickly around the conservatory to make sure no one was watching, pulled her close, and pressed a soft, fleeting kiss to her lips. To make up for that bold, unseemly little moment, he led her to a bamboo sofa in a less shadowed part of the room, sat down beside her, and plucked one small lily of the valley from her bouquet. She sat quiet, and the whole world outside felt like a sunlit, perfect valley stretched out at their feet.

“Did you tell my cousin Ellen about our engagement?” she asked after a little while, as if she was speaking half in a dream.

Archer jolted back to reality. He hadn’t. Some strange, unshakable reluctance to talk about something so intimate to that odd, foreign, out-of-place woman had frozen the words on his tongue before he could say them.
“No — I never got the chance, after all,” he lied quickly.
Her face fell a little, but she was gentle and determined to make him understand. “Then you have to tell her. I didn’t get a chance to tell her either, and I’d hate for her to think —”
“Of course not. But shouldn’t you be the one to tell her?”
She thought about it for a moment. “If I’d done it right away, yes. But now that there’s been a delay, you have to explain that I asked you to tell her at the opera before we told everyone else here. Otherwise she might think I forgot her. She’s family, you know, and she’s been gone for so long, she’s very sensitive.”

Archer looked at her, glowing with adoration. “You are my sweet, perfect angel. Of course I’ll tell her.” He glanced a little nervously toward the packed ballroom. “But I haven’t seen her anywhere. Did she come?”
“No, at the very last minute she decided not to,” May said.
“At the last minute?” he echoed, surprised that she’d ever even considered coming.
“She loves dancing so much,” the girl said simply. “But all of a sudden she decided her dress wasn’t nice enough for the ball, even though we all thought it was lovely. So my aunt took her home instead.”
“Oh, well,” Archer said, completely happy and unbothered.

Nothing about his new betrothed delighted him more than the way she was fully committed to the quiet ritual of pretending all unpleasant things simply didn’t exist, the same ritual they’d both been raised on. She knew perfectly well why Ellen Olenska had stayed away, he thought. But he would never, ever let the smallest sign cross his face that he was even aware there was the faintest shadow hovering over poor Ellen’s reputation.
