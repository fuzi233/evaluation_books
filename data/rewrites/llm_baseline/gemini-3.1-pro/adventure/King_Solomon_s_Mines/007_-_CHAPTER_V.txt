The harvest of the nine elephants occupied us for two full days. We carved the massive tusks from the skulls, the ivory smelling intensely of dense, marrow-rich bone and the dry savanna dust. We laid these gleaming treasures into a deep trench beneath a sprawling tree, an ancestral cache waiting in the earth for a future season. For poor Khiva, we dug an ant-bear hole. I packed the soil around him with solemn care, placing his broad assegai by his side and scattering a handful of dried grain over his chest, that he might not arrive at the great hearth of his ancestors with an empty stomach or idle hands. Even in death, a man’s dignity is tethered to the promise of sustenance.

On the third day, we marched again, tracing a wide, circular path across the veld until the rhythmic, resonant *thwack-thwack* of wooden pestles crushing millet announced Sitanda’s Kraal near the Lukanga River. It was a traditional agrarian outpost where the acoustic ecology remained deeply pure, unspoiled by the grinding gears of modern industry. Here, the hum of bees in the thatch and the lowing of cattle formed a symphony of domestic routine. To the right lay cultivated plots smelling of damp earth, turned soil, and fermenting grain; to the left stretched the arid, scentless silence of the great desert. I listened to the women singing as they ground their scanty supply of grain, their work-songs preserving melodic native dialects that I feared were slowly suffocating beneath the blunt, utilitarian lexicon of colonial trade.

That evening, as the sun roasted the horizon like a slab of meat over open coals, rendering the sky in streaks of rendered fat and blazing copper, Sir Henry and I stood on the stony slope overlooking the wasteland. The air was crisp, carrying the faint, mineral tang of the distant Suliman Berg. 

"There," I said, pointing to the faint blue outlines. "There is the wall round Solomon’s Mines."

"My brother should be there, and if he is, I shall reach him somehow," Sir Henry replied, his voice carrying the quiet, steady timber of absolute resolve.

I turned back toward the scent of our campfire and found we were not alone. Umbopa stood behind us, gazing toward the peaks.

"Is it to that land that thou wouldst journey, Incubu?" the Zulu asked, addressing Sir Henry by his native title.

I sharply reprimanded him for this familiarity. I knew, even as I spoke, that my imposition of English titles was a subtle exercise in power dynamics—an attempt to force his vast, untranslatable identity into my dominant, narrow lexicon. Yet Umbopa only laughed, a rich, baritone vibration that harmonized with the evening wind.

"How dost thou know that I am not the equal of the Inkosi whom I serve?" he challenged, his voice steady. "Be my mouth, O Macumazahn, and say my words to the Inkoos Incubu, my master, for I would speak to him and to thee."

Though irritated by his swagger, I acted as translator. Sir Henry confirmed he sought his brother, a man of his own blood, who had vanished into the wastes two years prior with a Bechuana hunter named Jim. 

"It is a far journey, Incubu," Umbopa noted, the syllables of his dialect rich with the cadence of ancestral memory. Sir Henry answered that no desert could stop a man if love led him, if he held his life as nothing before the will of Heaven.

Umbopa’s response, when I attempted to translate it, lost much of its profound physical truth in my English. He spoke of life and death not as sterile abstractions, but through the visceral sensations of the earth. "What is life?" he asked, his words carrying the acoustic weight of cracking marrow-bones and the hiss of fat on the fire. "It is the seed of the grass, blown and eaten. It is the breath of the oxen white in the winter frost; it is the little shadow that runs across the grazing lands and loses itself at sunset. Out of the dark we came, into the dark we go. We are the storm-driven bird momentarily illuminated by the cooking fire."

He pledged to cross the desert with us, hinting at a strange land beyond the snowy peaks—a place of beautiful things, of ancient trees, and sweet, running streams. His vocabulary was steeped in generations of geographical and culinary heritage that I could barely parse. "But Death sits upon them," he warned, lifting his spear. "Be wise and turn back. Go and hunt elephants." Then he returned to the camp to clean a rifle, seamlessly blending the mythic weight of his ancestry with the domestic chore.

The next day, we made our arrangements. We could not drag our heavy elephant rifles across the sands, so I sought out a local agrarian elder to store them. His eyes lingered greedily on the fine steel. To warn him, I loaded my eight-bore. The moment he touched the trigger, the resulting explosion violently shattered the kraal’s acoustic balance—a brutal, deafening mechanical crack that blew a hole straight through one of his oxen. The elder, furious and deeply frightened by this sudden intrusion of industrial violence, begged me to put the "live devils" away in the thatch.

I leaned close and delivered a curse in his native tongue, exploiting his deepest fears. I swore that if our weapons were stolen, his cattle would go mad, his maize would rot on the stalk, and his milk would turn to sour, foul-smelling curds until his people starved. The threat to his ancestral food supply terrified him into absolute compliance. 

We reduced our gear to forty pounds a man. The centerpiece of our provisions was twenty-five pounds of biltong. This sun-dried game flesh was a masterpiece of preservation—deeply scored, massaged with coarse salt and crushed coriander seeds, and air-cured until it held the rich, smoky umami of the veld itself. We packed our medicines, a pocket filter, tobacco, a bottle of brandy, and five Cochrane’s water-bottles. With the promise of hunting knives, I also hired three villagers to carry heavy gourds of water for our first twenty miles.

At sunset, we partook of a final, hearty meal of fresh, spit-roasted beef, the charred fat melting on our tongues, washed down with heavily steeped tea. It was a profound communion, the last true taste of domestic comfort we would know. At nine o'clock, beneath the silver sheen of the rising moon, Sir Henry removed his hat. In the profound quiet, we prayed to the Maker who shapes the interwoven destinies of men, asking His blessing on our path.

Then we stepped into the wasteland. 

We had only old José da Silvestra’s three-hundred-year-old chart to guide us—a fragile map drawn in blood by a starving man, looping our present journey into the tragic timeline of a past generation. The acoustic shift of the desert was jarring. Gone was the rich chorus of the kraal; here, only the dry crunch of our veldtschoons against the karoo scrub interrupted a profound, oppressive silence. Good tried to whistle a tune, but the shrill, artificial notes were instantly swallowed by the vast, unfeeling acoustics of the wasteland, and he abandoned it.

Around midnight, this silence was violently ruptured. Good, leading by compass, vanished with a shout. Suddenly, the darkness exploded with snorts, the drumming of hooves, and a cacophony of panicked grunts. The hired bearers threw themselves to the earth, wailing about ancestral ghosts, their native cries blending with the chaos. Good had stumbled onto a sleeping herd of quagga, landing squarely on a beast's back. He careered past us in the dust before thudding heavily into the fragrant scrub. He was shaken, but uninjured.

We marched on until the eastern horizon blushed like a perfectly ripe peach, the light piercing the mist. The heat soon intensified, the air taking on the suffocating warmth of a sealed oven. We found a small overhang of boulders, creeping into its cool, sandy shade. There, we shared a spartan breakfast, gnawing the tough, spiced biltong and rationing the tepid, earthy water from our bottles. 

At three in the afternoon, the bearers departed, taking their rhythmic language and empty gourds back to the fertile lands. We set out at half-past four. The desert was devoid of game, offering no sustenance. Instead, we were assaulted by the common house fly. Their incessant, jagged buzzing was an acoustic torture, a stark contrast to the harmonious sounds of the village. They swarmed our faces, an ancient pestilence perfectly preserved through millions of years, indifferent to our suffering.

We trudged through another night, the cyclical rhythm of our footsteps mirroring the endless cycles of those who had perished here before us. By morning, without a rock shelter to protect us, the sun became a devastating physical force. The air danced like heat waves over a fiercely stoked cast-iron stove; we were being roasted, the moisture drawn from our flesh like juices from an overcooked joint of meat. Our mouths went dry, stripping us of our ability to speak, suffocating our words in parched throats.

"We must dig a hole," Good croaked.

With a trowel and our bare hands, we excavated a shallow trench in the baked earth, pulling the bitter-smelling karoo scrub over us. It was a domestic space born of desperation, a crude subterranean hearth where we lay panting. Every so often, we allowed ourselves a meticulously measured drop of water, rolling the precious liquid over our cracked tongues, holding fast to the memory of sustenance as we endured the suffocating, silent heat of the day.
