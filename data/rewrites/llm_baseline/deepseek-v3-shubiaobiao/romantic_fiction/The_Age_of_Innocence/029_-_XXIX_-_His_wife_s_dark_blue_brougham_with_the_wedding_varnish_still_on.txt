The dark blue brougham—still gleaming with its wedding varnish—awaited Archer at the ferry, carrying him in quiet luxury toward the Pennsylvania station in Jersey City. The afternoon was heavy with snow, the gas lamps already lit in the cavernous station, their glow reflecting against the damp walls as he paced the platform. The Washington express was late, and his mind wandered to the wild speculations of men who spoke of tunnels beneath the Hudson, ships that could cross the Atlantic in days, flying machines, electric light—fantasies spun like threads from some fable. *Let them have their dreams,* he thought, *so long as the tunnel remains unbuilt.*

His own dream was simpler, more immediate: Ellen stepping from the train, her face emerging from the crowd, her gloved hand finding his arm. He imagined the slow journey to the ferry through the chaos of carts and shouting drivers, then the sudden hush as they settled into the carriage, the world slipping away beneath them as the boat glided across the black water. There were so many things he needed to say to her—words that even now arranged themselves in perfect order on his tongue.

Then the train roared in, shuddering to a halt like some great beast retreating to its den. Archer pushed forward, scanning each window until—there. Her face, pale and startled, so close he could almost touch it. For a disorienting moment, he couldn’t remember her features at all. Then their hands met, and he drew her to him.

“This way,” he murmured. “I have the carriage.”

The rest unfolded as if he had willed it into being. He helped her inside, spoke reassuringly of her grandmother, of Beaufort’s troubles—though her soft *“Poor Regina!”* lingered in his mind. The brougham lurched forward, weaving through the tangle of wagons and horses, past an empty hearse that made her flinch.

“If only it doesn’t mean—poor Granny!”

“No, no—she’s well, truly. Look, we’ve passed it.”

Ellen’s hand stayed in his, and as the carriage jolted onto the ferry, he bent and pressed his lips to her palm, as though it were something sacred. She pulled away with a faint smile.

“You didn’t expect me today?”

“Oh, no.”

“I nearly crossed you on the train. I was going to Washington.”

Her breath caught. “How close that was.”

“Do you know,” she said slowly, “I hardly remembered you?”

He stiffened. “Hardly remembered me?”

“Not in the way you think. It’s just—*each time you happen to me all over again.*”

The words seared him. “And I to you?”

She nodded, her gaze fixed on the window.

“Ellen,” he whispered. But she didn’t answer.

The silence stretched, thick with all the things he’d meant to say but now couldn’t grasp. Four months apart, yet they might have been strangers. The carriage creaked, the snow streaking the glass beside her, her profile blurred against the dusk.

“What a pretty carriage,” she said suddenly, turning. “Is it May’s?”

“Yes.”

“Then it was May who sent you?”

His jaw tightened. “Your husband’s secretary came to see me.”

The words were out before he could stop them, a petty retaliation for her mention of May. But Ellen didn’t flinch.

“M. Rivière went to see you?”

“You didn’t know?”

“No.”

“And that doesn’t surprise you?”

She hesitated. “Should it? He told me he knew you.”

Archer leaned closer. “Ellen—I have to ask. Was it Rivière who helped you leave your husband?”

Her voice was steady. “Yes. I owe him a great deal.”

The calm of her answer undid him. “You’re the most honest woman I’ve ever met.”

“Or the least fussy,” she said, amused.

“You see things as they are.”

“I’ve had to. I’ve looked at the Gorgon.”

“And she didn’t blind you.”

“No. But she dries up tears.”

The ferry bumped against the dock, throwing them together. Archer’s arm circled her before he could think.

“If you’re not blind, then you see this can’t last.”

“What can’t?”

“This—being together, yet not.”

Her voice changed. “You shouldn’t have come today.” Then, abruptly, she turned, her arms around his neck, her mouth on his.

A gas lamp flared outside, casting light into the carriage. She drew back, and they sat frozen as the brougham lurched into motion again.

Archer spoke first, low and urgent. “I don’t want stolen moments. I want more than this—more than scraps of time between endless waiting.”

She was silent for a long moment. “What do you mean?”

“You know. A life. Together.”

Her laugh was sharp. “You choose a fine place to say it—in your wife’s carriage.”

“Then let’s walk. Unless you fear the snow?”

“No. I’m going to Granny’s. And you’ll sit beside me, and we’ll look at realities, not visions.”

“What realities?”

“The ones where I can’t be your wife.”

The bluntness struck him like a blow. “I want a world where those words don’t exist. Where we’re just two people who love each other.”

She sighed. “Where is that world, Newland? Have you ever been there?”

He had no answer.

“Others have tried,” she said softly. “They always end up at places like Boulogne or Monte Carlo—smaller, dingier versions of the world they left.”

The bitterness in her voice was new. “The Gorgon *has* dried your tears,” he said.

“And opened my eyes. There’s no blessed darkness left.”

The carriage rolled on, the horse speeding toward the north as if it could outrun the tension between them.

“Then what *is* your plan?” Archer demanded.

“There is no *us* in the way you mean. We can only be ourselves if we stay apart.”

He recoiled. “I can’t accept that.”

“You must. I’ve been beyond where you are, and I know what lies there.”

His hand found the carriage bell. He pulled it sharply, and the brougham halted.

“Why are we stopping?” Ellen asked, startled.

“I’m getting out.” He flung open the door, stepping into the icy wind.

She leaned forward, her face pale in the lamplight. For a heartbeat, he thought she might call him back. But he shut the door, spoke the order to drive on, and watched the carriage disappear into the night.

The snow had stopped, but the wind cut like glass. He stood motionless, feeling the cold sting of frozen tears on his cheeks. Then he shoved his hands into his pockets and walked, fast and alone, down the empty avenue toward home.
