The happenings of that Carnival day seemed, at first, to leave Romola nothing to carry in her hands but the same plain burden she had taken up before: cousin Brigida’s wavering endurance of age, her peevish shame at grey hairs, her frightened pride that needed steadying as one steadies a pot on the fire when it threatens to boil over. Yet those bright follies of Carnival had opened upon a Lent that held Romola as if the city itself had become a great kitchen at dawn, all hearths lit, all vessels set to simmer, and every hour demanding watchfulness lest something unseen should scorch, sour, or spill.

Bernardo del Nero was Gonfaloniere. The Medicean party had strained hard and gained that triumph; and with it Romola’s inward foreboding thickened, like a broth darkening when the lid is lifted and one sees how much has reduced away. She felt some scheme in the air—prepared quietly, as men salt meat before a journey, meaning it for a season of scarcity—something that might ripen into success or into betrayal while her godfather held the staff of office.

Each morning, when the first weak light crept into her room, it seemed not merely daybreak but that fear returning—touching her temples, sitting on her chest, making even the familiar sounds in the house—wood shifting, a distant footstep—seem like furtive signals. She carried it with her through the streets on her way to the early sermon; yet in the Duomo, as men forget the dread of death when steel rings against steel, she forgot the chill, because there was a larger tumult to enter.

Savonarola was preaching—his last course of Lenten sermons he would ever be allowed to end in that vast, echoing place. He knew excommunication hung close, and he had come to the point of defying it. His words, sharp as a knife that will not spare sinew because the butcher is tender-hearted, laid bare the Church’s condition; he called things by their names, refusing those polite seasonings that disguise rancidness. He proclaimed renovation with a confidence that mounted, phrase by phrase, as if he were piling faggots and setting fire to them beneath the old, damp timbers of corruption.

Of his own destiny he spoke as one who sees two roads open and cannot tell, at each step, which his foot is taking. At times he seemed to behold himself as part of the coming revolt, his voice blown through Christendom like a trumpet that makes old walls tremble; he spoke of the Church as a dead body that might yet quiver into new life, as Lazarus quivered when the Divine voice pierced the sealed darkness. Then, with a sudden turn—as if a draught had entered and made the flame waver—he spoke as if there were nothing before him but persecution and martyrdom: this life a vigil; dawn, only after death.

For any mind not utterly dull, such a position had its own dreadful impressiveness, even for those—like Macchiavelli—ready to interpret the Frate’s fervour by a key that allowed no loftiness. To Romola, whose kindred ardour made her believe in the genuine greatness of Savonarola’s purpose, the crisis stirred her like something that belonged to her own lot. It sank into her as an exalting memory, and the heat of it went with her into her daily labours.

Those labours asked for more than patience; they asked for courage, the kind that is used up and must be renewed as bread is, day by day. Famine had not yet loosened its grip from Florence. Distress, long continued, grows heavier not because it is worse each hour, but because it is always there: the same hollow cheeks, the same hands tremoring over an empty bowl, the same dull eyes waiting for what cannot be promised. Disease spread among the crowded lanes; the Plague was expected, spoken of as men speak of a storm they smell in the air. Romola went among the sick and hungry and murmuring. The sour odour of close rooms, of damp straw, of unwashed bodies, clung to her clothes; the thin gruel she stirred in poor households had a smell of burnt grain and too much water; yet she took pains all the same—rinsing a pot, scraping what remained at the bottom into a child’s mouth, coaxing a fevered woman to sip warmed wine with herbs when she could procure them. And when her limbs were weary and her pity felt like a mere handful thrown into a sea, it seemed good to be inspired by something besides pity—to believe in a heroism struggling toward sublime ends, toward which her small acts might tend, as dew tends toward an unseen harvest.

Yet that mighty music which rolled through the Duomo did not keep its harmonies unbroken. Since the first days of glowing hope—when the Frate, seeing the near triumph of good in the reform of the Republic and the coming of the French deliverer, had preached peace and charity and the oblivion of political differences—conditions had changed. Intrigue had been too stubborn for oblivion; the French deliverer, turning his back on what men had named his mission, seemed to have wrought harm by disappointing faith. Hostility, petty and grand, attacked Savonarola with new weapons and new determination.

So contention and self-vindication sounded more and more in his sermons. The popular demand pressed him not only for insistence and detail about visions and private revelations, but for a tone of defiant confidence against objectors. He who had denounced the thirst for miracles and declared them unrelated to true faith, had come to say that at the right moment the Divine power would attest the truth of his prophetic preaching by a miracle. And as the vision of triumphant good receded behind the felt predominance of evil, his threats of coming vengeance against vicious tyrants and corrupt priests gained impetus—no longer purely from indignant zeal, but with a sting that could only come from personal exasperation.

In a great public orator who gives himself up to the moment, the conflict of selfish and unselfish emotion—hidden in most men’s secret chambers—stands out terribly clear: the language of inner voices is written, as it were, in letters of fire where all may read. If the jar of exasperation pained Romola, there was another hearer in that crowd for whom those harsh notes were the only thrilling ones, as deep bass vibrations are the only thing that pierces certain deafness.

Baldassarre had learned that the wonderful Frate was preaching again. As often as he could, he came to drink in the threats of a voice that sounded to him like justice made audible. He came the more because he had seen Romola come; he was waiting and watching for the hour when not only outward chance but his own shifting mind would make it possible to seek speech with her.

Twice Romola had caught sight of his face in the Duomo—once when its dark glance fixed itself upon hers. She wished not to see it again, and yet she looked for it, as men look for a portent’s return. But whatever revelation might lie in that old man belonged, she told herself, to the past; and her anxiety was drawn almost wholly into the present.

The stirring Lent passed. April—the second and last month of her godfather’s authority—drew near its close, and nothing had happened to fulfil her foreboding. In the public mind too there had been fears; rumours had travelled from Rome of menacing activity on the part of Piero de’ Medici; but in a few days Bernardo would be out of power, and suspicion might prove only smoke. Romola tried to gather courage from the review of her own futile tremblings, as one tastes a sauce and thinks, Perhaps it was only my palate that was wrong, not the pot.

Then, on the twenty-seventh, as she went on her usual errands of mercy in the afternoon, a messenger met her from Camilla Rucellai—chief among the feminine seers of Florence—praying her to come at once on matters of the highest moment.

Romola shrank. There was in her an unconquerable repulsion from the shrill volubility of those illuminated women, whose excitability seemed to her like froth rising where there should be clear substance. And she had, just now, a special reluctance toward Camilla, for a report had reached her that the woman had announced revelations hostile to Bernardo del Nero. Romola’s first impulse was to send back a refusal. The message might concern public affairs; and she felt, with something like bodily nausea, the wish to shut her ears against knowledge that might only heap weight upon her already strained mind.

But it had become her habit—painful, drilled into her by sorrow and responsibility—to reject the first prompting and obey outward claims. Cowardice and selfishness, she reproved herself; was she to let her presentiments make her refuse a call? So she complied, and went straight to Camilla.

She found the nervous grey-haired woman in a chamber contrived to resemble a convent cell. The bareness was a kind of stage-bareness, as if someone had swept away ordinary living not for cleanliness but for effect. Camilla’s thin fingers clutched Romola as she sat; and the eager voice, beginning in a loud whisper, made Romola feel a physical shrinking, as if a sharp smell had rushed into her nostrils—too much of some pungent herb bruised under a careless hand.

Camilla had a vision to relate. It had been revealed to her by Romola’s Angel—so she said—that Romola knew certain secrets about her godfather, Bernardo del Nero, which, if disclosed, might save the Republic from peril. As Camilla narrated, her voice rose in pitch and volume, leaping upward like a kettle whose lid begins to chatter. She ended by exhorting Romola to obey her Angel’s command and separate herself from the enemy of God.

Romola’s impetuosity belonged to a massive nature; unless deeply stirred, her manner was calm, held-in. She had a constitutional disgust for the shallow excitability of women like Camilla, whose faculties seemed wrought into fantasies until there was no steady remainder for thought or true emotion. Before Camilla had finished, Romola started up and tried to wrench her arm free. Camilla’s grip tightened.

It was useless. The prophetess held on like a crab; and Romola’s resistance, instead of sobering her, drove her into wilder assertion. She burst into shriller statements of other visions meant to corroborate the first. Christ Himself had appeared to her, she said, and ordered her to send His command to certain citizens in office: they were to throw Bernardo del Nero from the window of the Palazzo Vecchio. Fra Girolamo knew of it—and this time, Camilla declared, he had not dared to say the vision lacked Divine authority.

“And since then,” Camilla cried, her excited treble straining upward as her eyes strained too, “the Blessed Infant has come to me and laid a wafer of sweetness on my tongue, in token of his pleasure that I had done his will.”

“Let me go!” Romola’s voice came deep with anger, like something forced up from under stone. “God grant you are mad! else you are detestably wicked!”

Her effort was too strong now even for Camilla. Romola wrenched her arm free and rushed out. She did not pause until she had hurried far along the street and found herself near the church of the Badia.

To pass behind the curtain under the old stone arch was to step into a hush that shut out the street’s jostling. The city’s sounds—barrows rattling on stones, voices bargaining, a hammer striking—fell away, and what remained was the faint, living acoustics of sanctuary: a footfall softened, a breath, the distant murmur that might have been prayer or only the building’s own ancient settling. It was as if the very air had been strained clean, leaving a quiet that could nourish the mind the way clear water nourishes a tired palate.

She went in and sank on the altar-step before Filippino Lippi’s serene Virgin appearing to Saint Bernard, hoping the inward tumult would subside in that place where every object suggested peace persisting in the midst of turmoil.

The sharpest pressure on her was Camilla’s claim that Savonarola had countenanced such wicked folly. Romola did not believe for a moment that he had sanctioned the throwing of Bernardo del Nero from a window as a Divine command; she felt sure there was falsehood or mistake. Savonarola had grown more severe against malcontents; yet strict law and order were fundamental in his political teaching.

Still—since he knew the possibly fatal effects of visions like Camilla’s, since he distrusted such spirit-seeing women and kept away from them as much as possible—why, with his readiness to denounce wrong from the pulpit, did he not publicly denounce these pretended revelations that cast darkness, not light, across the idea of a Supreme Will?

Why?

The answer came with painful clearness, as when one tastes bitterness and knows at once it has entered the whole dish. He was fettered inwardly by the consciousness that such revelations were not, at their base, distinctly separable from his own visions. He was fettered outwardly by the consequence he foresaw: a cry raised even among his own party that he wished to suppress all Divine inspiration of which he was not the vessel—he, or his confidential supplementary seer, Fra Salvestro.

Kneeling with her face buried against the stone of the altar-step, Romola endured one of those sickening moments when the enthusiasm that had come to her as the only energy making life seem worth living appeared bound up, inevitably, with vain dreams and wilful shutting of eyes. She thought, with a sudden pull of longing, of her godfather’s strong worldly sense, his dignified prudence, his untheoretic virtues—virtues like well-leavened bread, not dazzling but sustaining. Was he to be treated as an Agag because he believed a more restricted government better than the Great Council, and because he would not pretend to forget old ties to the banished family?

But with that thought rose again her presentiment of some plot to restore the Medici; and again she felt the popular party was half justified in its fierce suspicion. Again she felt the sacredness of keeping Florence’s government pure, of keeping out a vicious rule. There, the Frate was right; he had carried her understanding irrevocably with him.

Yet at this moment the assent of her understanding came alone, unwillingly. Her heart recoiled from a right allied to so much narrowness—a right that seemed to entail a hard, systematic judging of men by assents and denials that lay like thin labels upon the surface of manhood. Her affection and respect clung with new tenacity to her godfather; and with him clung the memories of her father—memories allied to the refusal to divide men into sheep and goats by some easy political or religious mark, as one sorts grain by a sieve that cannot measure nourishment.

After all that can be said about the widening power of ideas, it remains true they would not act so strongly if they were not taken into a solvent of feeling. The great world-struggle of developing thought is foreshadowed again and again in the struggle of the affections, seeking justification for love and hope. If Romola’s intellect had been less able to discern complexity, the early loving associations of her life would have forbidden her to accept implicitly Savonarola’s denunciatory exclusiveness. She had felt simply that his mind offered deeper, more efficacious truth than any other; and the breathing-room she found in his large view of human duty had made her patient toward what she could not absorb—so long as its practical effect did not strike against any strong force within her.

Now a sudden insurrection of feeling had brought the collision. Her indignation, once roused by Camilla’s visions, could not stop there; it ran like flame along kindred facts in Savonarola’s teaching. For the moment she felt what was true in the scornful sarcasms flung against him more keenly than what was false. It was an illumination that made all life look ghastly.

Where were the beings to whom she could cling—work with—endure with—believing she was working for the right? On the side whence moral energy came lay a fanaticism from which she shrank with newly startled repulsion. On the side to which she was drawn by affection and memory lay the presentiment of secret plotting—something her judgment told her would not unfairly be called crime.

And surmounting every other thought was the dread inspired by Tito’s hints: lest that presentiment should become knowledge in such a way that she would be torn by irreconcilable claims.

Calmness would not come even here. The serene faces in the picture—the saint writing in rocky solitude, visited by celestial peace—did not bring peace to her. That rocky solitude was too far away; Romola was pressed in the hard crush of human difficulty, where the loud world intruded even into prayer.

She rose, thinking to hasten back to her sick people in the courtyard—thinking that by some immediate beneficent act, as by giving a thirsty mouth a cup held steady, she might revive in herself that sense of life’s worth which at this instant was unfed by any wider faith.

But when she turned, she found herself face to face with a man standing only two yards off.

It was Baldassarre.
