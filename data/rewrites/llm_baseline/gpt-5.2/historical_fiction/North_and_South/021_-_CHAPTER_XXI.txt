Margaret and her father went home through a night washed clean, the flagstones still holding the day’s warmth, but the air turned lively and sharp, as if the town had loosened its collar after the heat. She had lifted her white silk a little from the dust and damp, the hem gathered in her hand in the old, half-childish way, and walked with a step that might have been a dance if dread had not kept time with it.

“I rather think Mr. Thornton is not quite easy in his mind about this strike,” she said. “He seemed very anxious to-night.”

“I should wonder if he were not,” Mr. Hale answered. “Yet he spoke with his usual coolness when they were proposing different plans, just before we came away.”

“So he did after dinner. It would take a good deal to stir him from that manner of speaking; but his face strikes me as anxious.”

“I should be, if I were he. He must know of the growing anger—of the hatred that is only half smothered—among his people. They look upon him as what Scripture calls a hard man; not unjust, perhaps, but unfeeling; standing upon his rights as if any rights of ours were much, in the sight of God. I am glad you think he looks anxious. When I remember Boucher’s half-mad words and ways, I cannot bear to think how coolly Mr. Thornton spoke.”

“In the first place, I am not so convinced as you are of Boucher’s utter distress. Badly off for the moment, no doubt. But there is always that mysterious supply of money from the Unions; and, from what you told me, he is of a passionate nature—demonstrative; he gives voice to everything he feels.”

“Oh, papa!”

“Well! I only want you to do justice to Mr. Thornton. He is, I suspect, a man of the opposite kind—too proud to show his feelings. Just the character I should have fancied beforehand you would admire, Margaret.”

“So I do—so I should,” she replied quickly, and then, because her conscience was exacting, she added, more slowly, “but I don’t feel quite so sure as you do of the existence of those feelings. He has great strength of character—unusual intellect, considering the few advantages he has had.”

“Not so few. He has lived a practical life from early youth; he has been obliged to use judgment and self-control. That develops one part of the mind. He needs, to be sure, more knowledge of what has gone before—the past gives the truest ground for guessing the future; but he knows the want—he perceives it. And that is something. You are prejudiced against him, Margaret.”

“He is the first manufacturer I ever had the opportunity of studying, papa. He is my first olive: let me make a face while I swallow it. I know he is good of his kind, and by and by I shall like the kind. I rather think I am already beginning to do so.” She tried to smile, with that brave habit she had of snatching at whatever looked like common daylight. “I was very much interested by what the gentlemen were talking about, though I did not understand half of it. I was quite sorry when Miss Thornton came to take me to the other end of the room, saying she was sure I should be uncomfortable as the only lady among so many gentlemen. I had never thought of it, I was so busy listening; and the ladies were so dull, papa—oh, so dull! Yet it was clever, too. It reminded me of our old game of having each so many nouns to put into a sentence.”

“What do you mean, child?” he asked.

“Why, they took nouns that were signs of things that give evidence of wealth—housekeepers, under-gardeners, extent of glass, valuable lace, diamonds—and each one made her speech so as to bring them all in, as if by accident, and prettily.”

“You will be as proud of your one servant when you get her, if all Mrs. Thornton says is true.”

“To be sure I shall. I felt like a hypocrite to-night, sitting there in my white silk, with my idle hands before me, when I remembered all the good thorough house-work they had done to-day. They took me for a fine lady, I’m sure.”

“Even I was mistaken enough to think you looked like a lady, my dear,” said Mr. Hale, with that quiet smile which softened his face more than any laughter could have done.

But the smile died and left them blanched when Dixon opened the door.

“Oh, master!—oh, Miss Margaret! Thank God you are come! Dr. Donaldson is here. The servant next door went for him, for the charwoman is gone home. She’s better now; but—oh, sir!—I thought she’d have died an hour ago.”

Mr. Hale caught at Margaret’s arm as if the floor had tilted under him. His look went to her face for guidance; and what he saw there was surprise and extremest sorrow—an awe that had no sharp terror in it, as if she had been living for weeks in the shadow of this very moment.

“Oh! I should not have left her—wicked daughter that I am!” Margaret murmured; and though she supported her father, she felt as if she were the one who needed holding up, so heavy was the thought of what waited above.

Dr. Donaldson met them on the landing, speaking low, as if any loudness would jar some fragile thread.

“She is better now. The opiate has taken effect. The spasms were very bad; no wonder they frightened your maid; but she’ll rally this time.”

“This time!” Mr. Hale’s voice broke upon the words. “Let me go to her!”

Half an hour ago he had been a man in his strength; now he moved with dim sight and wavering sense, old in an instant. Dr. Donaldson took his arm, and led him into the bedroom. Margaret followed close.

There lay Mrs. Hale—sleeping, quiet enough to deceive any heart that did not know the handwriting of that stillness. She might be better: she was not recovered. Death had set his seal upon her face, faint but unmistakeable, like wax taking a signet. She breathed; yet the room already felt as if something had been taken away from it, as if the familiar household air had lost a note.

Mr. Hale stood looking long, without speech. Then his whole frame began to shake. He turned away from the bed and groped, blindly, as if he could not remember where the door stood, though several candles—brought up in sudden fright—burned and flared with shaken flames. He staggered into the drawing-room and felt for a chair. Dr. Donaldson wheeled one to him and placed him in it, then took his pulse with firm fingers.

“Speak to him, Miss Hale. We must rouse him.”

“Papa!” Margaret cried. The word came out wild with pain. “Papa! Speak to me!”

The old speculative look—the one that belonged to him when he was far off in thought—came back into his eyes, and he made a great effort.

“Margaret, did you know of this? Oh, it was cruel of you!”

“No, sir, it was not cruel,” said Dr. Donaldson quickly, as if he would not let injustice settle anywhere in that room. “Miss Hale acted under my directions. There may have been a mistake, but it was not cruelty. Your wife will be a different creature to-morrow, I trust. She has had spasms, as I anticipated—though I did not tell Miss Hale of my apprehensions. She has taken the opiate I brought; she will have a good long sleep; and to-morrow that look which has alarmed you will have passed away.”

“But not the disease?” Mr. Hale asked, with a dreadful quietness.

Dr. Donaldson glanced at Margaret. Her head was bent; her face, when it lifted, made no entreaty for a reprieve bought by any kindly lie. The doctor, who understood quick, did not pretend.

“Not the disease. We cannot touch it, with all our vaunted skill. We can only delay its progress—alleviate the pain. Be a man, sir—a Christian. Have faith in the immortality of the soul, which no pain, no mortal disease, can assail or touch!”

But the answer came, choked and human: “You have never been married, Dr. Donaldson; you don’t know what it is.” And then the sobs—deep, manly, helpless—went through the stillness of the house, pulse after pulse, like heavy blows in the dark.

Margaret knelt beside him and put her arms about him, kissing his cold hand, smoothing it, holding him as if by touch she could keep him from falling into some abyss where she could not follow. No one—not even Dr. Donaldson—noticed how time went by, for time is a thing one only hears when the heart is not in torment.

At last Mr. Hale, by some effort that was almost duty rather than strength, spoke of what must be done.

“What must we do?” he asked. “Tell us both. Margaret is my staff—my right hand.”

Dr. Donaldson gave clear directions: there was no fear for the night—nay, even peace for to-morrow, and for many days yet, perhaps—but no enduring hope. He advised Mr. Hale to go to bed and leave only one watcher, that the sleep might not be disturbed by restless moving and anxious whispering. He promised to come early in the morning; then he took their hands with a warmth that was more comfort than any words, and went away into the outer darkness.

They said little after that. Terror leaves one exhausted, like hard labour. Mr. Hale was resolved to sit up all night; Margaret could only prevail upon him to lie down on the drawing-room sofa. Dixon, stout and blunt, refused to go to bed at all; and as for Margaret, she could no more leave her mother than she could leave her own breath, let doctors speak as they pleased of “husbanding resources,” and “one watcher only.”

So the watch began: a domestic vigil, plain and terrible, made up of small familiar things—candles guttering, a jug of water, folded cloths warmed and re-warmed, the faint medicinal sweetness of laudanum hanging in the air and clinging to the back of the throat; and under it all the sound of the house itself, which she had never listened to so intently before. A house has its own acoustic life: boards that creak with temperature, a distant drip, a coal settling in the grate. In Milton, beyond their walls, there was always the town’s heavy breathing; even when machinery was stilled, the habit of it seemed to remain, like a pulse you miss only when you put your ear to the pillow and cannot find it.

Dixon sat and stared and winked and drooped, then jerked herself upright with offended pride, as if sleep were a betrayal; but at last her head fell forward and she gave up the struggle and snored, the sound rough and homely, almost comforting in its vulgar certainty.

Margaret had taken off her gown and tossed it aside with impatient disgust, as if the white silk had been a costume borrowed for an hour from some other life. She put on her dressing-gown, and sat where she could see her mother’s face by the shaded candlelight. She felt she could never sleep again. Her senses seemed sharpened for watching: every change of breath, every twitch of eyelid, every pause in the wind outside pressed on her like a finger. Even thoughts were not silent; they came with edges.

For more than two hours she heard her father moving restlessly in the next room. Again and again he came to the door of the sick chamber and paused there to listen, afraid to enter and afraid to go away. Margaret, not hearing his unseen presence at first, would rise and open the door and tell him, softly, how all went on, answering questions his dry lips could hardly shape.

At last he too fell asleep, and the house grew still.

Margaret sat behind the curtain and thought. Far away in time, far away in space, seemed all that had filled yesterday. Not more than six-and-thirty hours ago she had cared for Bessy Higgins and her father; her heart had been wrung for Boucher; she had gone out into the streets with purpose and returned with plans. Now all that was like a dream remembered from some former life—unreal because it was not her mother. Everything that happened beyond these walls seemed separated, and therefore untrue.

Harley Street appeared more distinct than Milton: she remembered, as if it were yesterday, tracing her mother’s features in Aunt Shaw’s face; letters arriving, making her ache for home. Helstone itself lay in a dim past, a green silence she had once thought secure. The dull grey days of winter and spring, so monotonous, so uneventful, now seemed richer than any festival, because her mother had been there through them, alive, complaining, loving, disappointed—alive.

She would have caught at the skirts of that departing time and begged it to return, to give her back what she had not valued enough while she possessed it.

Life looked to her like a vain show—unsubstantial, flickering, flitting. The thought came with a sound, not an idea: as if from some airy belfry, high above the jar of earth, a bell were always tolling, and the words it tolled were untranslatable into comfort—only into knowledge: All are shadows. All are passing. All is past. No dialect she knew could soften it; it was a dominant lexicon, pitiless in its plainest terms, making everything else—love, duty, pride—seem like smaller words.

When morning came, cool and grey, like many a happier morning before, she looked one by one at the sleepers—Dixon heavy in her chair, her father on the sofa with his hand fallen open—and it seemed, for an instant, as if the terrible night had been unreal; as if it too were a shadow, and now it was past.

Mrs. Hale herself, when she awoke, did not know how ill she had been. She was surprised at Dr. Donaldson’s early visit, perplexed by the anxious faces bending over her, and consented to remain in bed that day because she admitted she was tired. The next day she insisted on getting up; and Dr. Donaldson consented to her returning to the drawing-room.

But comfort would not stay in any posture. She shifted and sighed, the restlessness not of impatience but of a body that could not find ease within its own boundaries; and before night she grew feverish. Mr. Hale, after that first shock, seemed emptied of decision, listless as if all his will had run out in tears.

“What can we do to spare mamma such another night?” Margaret asked on the third day, when the very thought of darkness made her hands cold.

“It is, to a certain degree, the reaction after the powerful opiates I have been obliged to use,” Dr. Donaldson answered. “It is more painful for you to see than for her to bear, I believe. But I think, if we could get a water-bed, it might be a good thing. Not but what she will be better to-morrow—pretty much like herself as she was before this attack. Still, I should like her to have a water-bed. Mrs. Thornton has one, I know. I’ll try and call there this afternoon. Stay—” His eye caught Margaret’s face, blanched with watching, her mouth set as if she had forgotten the taste of food. “I’m not sure I can go; I have a long round to take. It would do you no harm to have a brisk walk to Marlborough Street and ask Mrs. Thornton if she can spare it.”

“Certainly,” said Margaret. “I could go while mamma is asleep this afternoon. I’m sure Mrs. Thornton would lend it to us.”

The doctor’s experience proved right in this: Mrs. Hale brightened that afternoon more than Margaret had dared hope. After dinner she sat in her easy-chair, her hand lying in her husband’s; and though Mr. Hale looked more worn and suffering than she, he could smile now—slowly and faintly, it is true; but two days ago Margaret had never thought to see him smile again.

It was about two miles from Crampton Crescent to Marlborough Street. At three o’clock an August sun beat straight down into the street, turning the air thick, as if it had been stirred with a spoon and left to stand. Margaret went along without noticing much for the first mile and a half; her mind, trained by these days of nursing, moved as if it were carrying a covered dish that must not be jarred. She threaded her way through the irregular stream of Milton people with an instinct she had learnt here—step, pause, sidestep—like passing through a kitchen at full work without upsetting pans or scalding oneself.

But by and by she became aware of an unusual heaving among the crowd. It was not a crowd that went anywhere; it was a crowd that gathered, talked, listened—buzzing with excitement while scarcely shifting its feet, as if the street itself held them. They made way for her; and because she was bent upon her errand and upon the necessity that suggested it, she observed less quickly than she might have done if her mind had been at ease.

Only when she had almost reached Marlborough Street did the full conviction force itself upon her: there was an oppressive irritation abroad, a thunderous atmosphere—moral as well as physical—pressing down upon the town. From every narrow lane opening into Marlborough Street came up a low, distant roar, like myriads of voices kneaded into one sound. It was not speech so much as a soundscape of anger, the kind that enters the body through the ear and sets the heart beating out of time.

The inhabitants of each poor, squalid dwelling were gathered at doors and windows, or standing in the middle of the narrow ways, all looking towards one point. And Marlborough Street itself was the focus of those eyes—some fierce with wrath, some lowering with threats, some widened with fear or beseeching entreaty.

Margaret, reaching the small side-entrance by the folding doors in the great dead wall of Marlborough mill-yard, waited for the porter to answer the bell. She looked round, and heard—far off—the first long roll of the coming tempest; saw the first slow-surging wave of the dark crowd rise at the far end of the street, lift its threatening crest, tumble forward, and then retreat. A moment ago the street had seemed full of repressed noise; now it was ominously still, as if the town held its breath.

All these circumstances forced themselves on her notice, but did not sink down into her preoccupied heart. She did not know what they meant; she could not read their deep significance. She only knew the keen, sharp pressure of the knife that was soon to stab her through by leaving her motherless, and she was trying to make herself ready—ready to bear it, ready to comfort her father when it fell.

The porter opened the door cautiously, not nearly wide enough to admit her.

“It’s you, is it, ma’am?” he said, drawing a long breath, and widening the entrance—still not flinging it wide, as if even a doorway might be a weakness.

Margaret went in. He hastily bolted it behind her.

“Th’ folk are all coming up here, I reckon?” he asked.

“I don’t know. Something unusual seemed going on; but this street is quite empty, I think.”

She crossed the yard and went up the steps to the house door. There was no near sound—no steam-engine at work with beat and pant, no click of machinery, no mingling and clashing of many sharp voices; the mill’s usual din, that industrial chorus which made Milton’s days, had fallen away. Yet far off the ominous gathering roar deepened—clamouring, approaching—like thunder heard through walls.
