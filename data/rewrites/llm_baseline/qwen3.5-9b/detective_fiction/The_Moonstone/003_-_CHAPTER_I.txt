In the opening pages of *Robinson Crusoe*, specifically around page one hundred and twenty-nine, there is a passage that reads: "Now I saw, though too late, the Folly of beginning a Work before we count the Cost, and before we judge rightly of our own Strength to go through with it."

It was only yesterday that I happened to open my copy of *Robinson Crusoe* to exactly that spot. Then, this very morning—the twenty-first of May, eighteen hundred and fifty—my lady's nephew, Mr. Franklin Blake, called upon me. We engaged in a brief exchange of words, which ran something like this:

"Betteredge," said Mr. Franklin, "I have just visited the solicitor regarding various family affairs. Among other topics, we discussed the disappearance of the Indian Diamond from my aunt's residence in Yorkshire, an event that occurred two years ago. Mr. Bruff shares my conviction that the entire affair should be committed to writing, set down in full detail, and without delay, purely for the sake of historical truth."

Not immediately grasping the depth of his intention, but recognizing that aligning oneself with the lawyer's wishes is generally prudent for maintaining domestic harmony, I readily agreed. Mr. Franklin continued, however.

"In this matter concerning the Diamond," he explained, "the reputations of several innocent individuals have already been tarnished by suspicion, as you are well aware. Furthermore, their memories might suffer irreparable harm in the future were there no official record of the facts to which subsequent generations could refer. It is undeniable that this peculiar saga of our family demands to be told. Indeed, I believe Mr. Bruff and I have devised the most appropriate method for narrating it."

While this arrangement seemed perfectly satisfactory to both gentlemen, I still struggled to discern precisely what role I was expected to play.

"We have specific events to recount," Mr. Franklin proceeded, "and we have specific witnesses capable of describing them. Proceeding from these fundamental premises, our plan is for each of us to contribute to the chronicle of the Moonstone, detailing only those portions within our direct personal knowledge. Our narrative shall commence by tracing how the Diamond initially came into the possession of my uncle Herncastle during his military service in India half a century ago. I have already secured a preliminary account of this period, derived from an elderly family document based on eyewitness testimony. Next, we must describe the Diamond's arrival at my aunt's house in Yorkshire two years past, and its mysterious vanishing within barely twelve hours thereafter. No one possesses a deeper understanding of the household dynamics at that critical juncture than yourself, Betteredge. Therefore, you must take up the pen and initiate the story."

Thus was I formally apprised of my involvement in the Diamond affair. Should you wish to know how I responded to this summons, let me assure you that I acted as any sensible person would in your stead. With characteristic humility, I professed myself entirely incapable of undertaking the assigned task. Privately, however, while speaking these words, I harbored the confident belief that I possessed ample ability to execute it successfully, provided I were granted a fair opportunity.

Mr. Franklin, I suspect, detected the discrepancy between my spoken modesty and my facial expression. Dismissing my feigned inadequacy, he insisted that I demonstrate my capabilities.

Two hours have elapsed since Mr. Franklin departed. Immediately upon his leaving, I retreated to my writing desk to commence the narrative. Yet, despite my professed talents, I remain seated there utterly paralyzed. I find myself staring at the blank page, contemplating the same realization that befell Robinson Crusoe: the futility of embarking on a laborous enterprise without first calculating the costs or assessing one's true capacity to endure it.

Please note that I opened the book to that particular passage merely by accident, just one day prior to rashly accepting this burden. Might I inquire, then—is this not a case of prophecy? What else could it be? I confess no superstition; having devoured countless volumes throughout my life, I consider myself a scholar in my own right. Although I have reached the age of seventy, my memory remains sharp, and my legs are as steady as ever. Do not mistake my remarks for the ravings of an uneducated mind when I assert that a book like *Robinson Crusoe* has never truly existed and likely never will again. For years, I have tested this volume—usually accompanied by a pipe of tobacco—and found it an indispensable companion in every necessity of mortal existence. Whether my spirits are low, whether I seek counsel, whether my late wife vexed me in days gone by, or whether I have indulged slightly too heavily in drink in recent times, *Robinson Crusoe* has invariably restored my equilibrium. I have worn through six sturdy editions of this classic through diligent use. On my lady's last birthday, she gifted me a seventh. My celebration involved a glass or two of wine, and once again, *Robinson Crusoe* guided me back to sobriety. It is a fine volume, priced at four shillings and sixpence, bound in blue leather, complete with an illustration.

Still, does this rambling reflection truly constitute the commencement of the Diamond's story? Am I not drifting aimlessly toward some unknown destination? Let us discard this sheet and begin anew, presenting my highest regards to you.
