“Bear up, brave heart! we will be calm and strong;
Sure, we can master eyes, or cheek, or tongue,
Nor let the smallest tell-tale sign appear
She ever was, and is, and will be dear.”

It was a sweltering summer evening when Edith entered Margaret’s bedroom—first in her usual attire, then again, fully dressed for dinner. At first, the room was empty. On her second visit, she found Dixon carefully arranging Margaret’s gown upon the bed—but no Margaret herself. Edith lingered, restless, hands fluttering about as if to tidy something that didn’t need tidying.

“Oh, Dixon!” she exclaimed, “not those dreadful blue flowers with that dull gold dress. What taste! Wait just a moment—I’ll fetch you some pomegranate blossoms.”

“It’s not dead gold, ma'am,” Dixon corrected, stiffly. “It’s straw-colour. And blue always goes with straw-colour.”

But before Dixon could finish, Edith had already returned with the vivid scarlet blooms, tossing them down with an air of triumph.

“Where is Miss Hale?” Edith asked, stepping back to assess the effect of the flowers now tucked into the dark hair of the absent woman.

“I can’t say,” she continued, pettishly. “I simply don’t understand how my aunt allowed her to fall into such careless habits in Milton. I’m always expecting news of some dreadful accident—some scrape among those wretched alleys she insists on wandering through. I’d never venture down half those streets without a servant. They’re not fit for ladies.”

Dixon, still smarting from the insult to her sense of fashion, replied curtly: “It’s no wonder, if you ask me, when ladies talk so much about other ladies—and such delicate, dainty ladies at that. I say it’s no wonder there are no saints left on earth.”

“Oh, Margaret! Here you are!” Edith cried, catching sight of her cousin at last. “I’ve been longing for you. But look at your cheeks—how flushed you are, poor thing! Only think what Henry has done—really, he’s gone beyond brother-in-law limits. Just when I’d arranged my party so perfectly—everything fitted just right for Mr. Colthurst—along comes Henry, apologizing, of course, and using your name as an excuse, asking if he may bring Mr. Thornton from Milton—your tenant, you know—who’s in London on some legal business. It ruins my numbers completely.”

“I don’t care about dinner,” Margaret murmured, her voice low. “I don’t want anything. Dixon can bring me tea here. I’ll be in the drawing-room by the time you come up. I’d really like to lie down.”

“No, no—you mustn’t do that,” Edith insisted. “You do look terribly pale, true enough, but it’s only the heat. We simply can’t manage without you. (Lower those flowers a bit, Dixon. They look like flames in your black hair, Margaret.) You remember—we planned for you to talk about Milton to Mr. Colthurst. Oh! Of course—this man is from Milton too. I believe it might turn out quite well after all. Mr. Colthurst can quiz him thoroughly on everything that interests him, and it’ll be delicious fun to see how your experiences compare with Mr. Thornton’s wisdom in his next speech in Parliament. Honestly, I think Henry hit upon a stroke of genius. I asked him if he was a man one would be ashamed of, and he said, ‘Not if you’ve any sense in you, my little sister.’ So I suppose he can sound his h’s—which isn’t common in Darkshire, is it? Eh, Margaret?”

“Did Mr. Lennox mention why Mr. Thornton came to town?” Margaret asked, her tone strained. “Was it legal business related to the property?”

“Oh! He’s failed, or something like that—the very thing Henry told you about the day you had such a headache. What was it again? (There—that’s perfect, Dixon. Miss Hale does us credit, doesn’t she?) I wish I were as tall as a queen and as brown as a gipsy, Margaret.”

“But about Mr. Thornton?” Margaret pressed.

“Oh, law matters—such a dreadful subject for me. Henry will enjoy telling you all about it. The impression he made on me was that Mr. Thornton is in bad straits, but still a respectable man, and that I should be particularly civil to him. Since I didn’t know how, I came to you to ask for help. Now, come downstairs with me and rest on the sofa for a few minutes.”

The privileged brother-in-law arrived early. Margaret, blushing faintly as she spoke, began questioning him about Mr. Thornton.

“He came up regarding the sub-letting of the property—Marlborough Mills, the house, and the premises adjacent. He’s unable to maintain it himself. There are deeds and leases to review, agreements to draft. I hope Edith will receive him properly, though I could see she was slightly annoyed by the liberty I took in securing an invitation for him. But I thought you’d appreciate some attention being paid to him. One would be especially careful to show respect to a man who’s falling from fortune.”

He had spoken quietly, seated beside her, but at the end of his words, he rose abruptly and introduced Mr. Thornton—just as he stepped into the room—to Edith and Captain Lennox.

Margaret watched him closely as he engaged in conversation. More than a year had passed since she had seen him, and time had visibly altered him. His tall, commanding figure still set him apart from ordinary men, lending him an air of distinction through natural grace and ease. Yet his face bore the marks of age and worry. Still, a noble composure rested upon it, a quiet dignity that struck others—especially those who had heard of his decline—as evidence of inner strength and moral fiber.

From the moment he entered, he had noticed her. He saw her absorbed expression as she listened to Mr. Lennox and approached her with the composed manner of a long-standing friend. The moment he spoke, a sudden flush swept across her cheeks—a color that remained throughout the evening.

She did not speak much to him. Her questions were brief, practical—about familiar faces in Milton—what seemed to him merely formal inquiries. But others joined in, more intimate figures within the household, and he slipped into the background, exchanging occasional remarks with Mr. Lennox.

“You think Miss Hale looks well,” said Mr. Lennox, “don’t you? Milton didn’t agree with her, I imagine. When she first came to London, I thought I’d never seen anyone so changed. Tonight, she’s radiant. And stronger too. Last autumn, a two-mile walk tired her out. On Friday evening, we walked to Hampstead and back. Yet Saturday, she looked as fresh as she does now.”

“We?” Who? They alone?

Mr. Colthurst, a sharp-minded rising politician, was keenly observant. He caught a remark Mr. Thornton made during dinner and, curious, turned to Edith. “Who is that gentleman?” he asked.

To her surprise, the tone of his “Indeed!” suggested Mr. Thornton of Milton was not as obscure a name as she had assumed. Her dinner proceeded smoothly. Henry was in high spirits, his dry wit sparkling. Mr. Thornton and Mr. Colthurst discovered shared interests, briefly touched upon them, then saved deeper discussion for after dinner.

Margaret looked exquisite in the pomegranate blossoms. Though she leaned back in her chair and spoke little, Edith was content—conversation flowed effortlessly without her. Margaret, however, kept her gaze fixed on Mr. Thornton. He never looked at her, which allowed her to study him unobserved, noting the changes even this short time had wrought. Only once—when Mr. Lennox made an unexpected joke—did the old spark return: a flash of laughter in his eyes, a hint of the brilliant smile from days past. For a fleeting instant, his glance sought hers, as if craving her recognition. But their eyes met, and instantly, his expression hardened. He became grave, distant, and deliberately avoided looking near her again.

Only two other ladies were present, both engaged in conversation with Edith and her aunt. When the guests moved into the drawing-room, Margaret sat quietly, occupied with her embroidery. Soon, the gentlemen followed, Mr. Colthurst and Mr. Thornton deep in discussion. Mr. Lennox drew near to Margaret and whispered:

“I really think Edith owes me thanks for my contribution to her party. You have no idea how agreeable and sensible your tenant is. He’s been exactly the person Colthurst needed to get the facts he wanted. I can’t imagine how he managed to ruin his affairs.”

“With his talents and opportunities,” Margaret replied, “you would have succeeded.”

He did not entirely like the tone, though the sentiment echoed thoughts he had himself entertained. Silence followed, then they heard a swell in the voices near the fireplace—Mr. Colthurst and Mr. Thornton speaking.

“I assure you,” Colthurst said, “I heard your name mentioned frequently during my brief stay in the area. With great interest—perhaps curiosity about its outcome.”

Then the words faded. When they could hear again, it was Mr. Thornton speaking.

“I have not the qualities for popularity—if they spoke of me that way, they were mistaken. I take time to embrace new ventures, and find it difficult to reveal myself, even to those I wish to know, and with whom I desire no reserve. Yet, despite these shortcomings, I felt I was on the right path. Starting from a kind of friendship with one individual, I began to grow acquainted with many. The benefits were mutual—we were teaching each other, unconsciously and consciously.”

“You say ‘were.’ I trust you intend to continue?”

“I must stop Colthurst,” Henry Lennox interrupted hastily, redirecting the conversation with a clever, timely question to spare Mr. Thornton the embarrassment of admitting his failure and change in status.

But as soon as the new topic ended, Mr. Thornton resumed where he had left off.

“I have failed in business and had to relinquish my position as a mill owner. I am now seeking employment in Milton—any role where I may work under someone willing to let me pursue my own methods in certain matters. I can rely on myself not to impose reckless theories. My only desire is the chance to foster genuine relationships with the workers—beyond mere transactions. But it might be the point Archimedes sought to move the world, given how seriously some manufacturers regard it. They shake their heads and grow solemn whenever I mention even one or two experiments I’d like to try.”

“You call them ‘experiments,’ I notice,” Colthurst remarked, his manner subtly respectful.

“Because I believe them to be such. I am uncertain of the outcomes. But I am certain they ought to be tried. I’ve come to realize that no institution, however wise or meticulously designed, can truly unite classes unless its implementation brings individuals into direct personal contact. Such interaction is life itself. A worker cannot grasp how much effort his employer has spent in his study planning for the workers’ benefit. A plan emerges polished, mechanical, seemingly perfect. But the hands accept it like machinery—without understanding the mental labor behind it. Yet, if I were to introduce an idea requiring personal involvement—messy at first, perhaps—each obstacle would draw more men into interest. Eventually, success would become everyone’s desire, because all had helped shape it. Even then, I know it would lose vitality the moment it ceased to be driven by shared concern, by the instinctive ways people find to meet, to know each other’s characters, quirks, and speech. We would understand each other better—and, I dare say, grow to like each other more.”

“And you think this might prevent strikes?”

“Not at all. My highest hope is only that they cease to be bitter, venomous sources of hatred. A more hopeful man might dream that closer ties could abolish strikes altogether. But I am not a hopeful man.”

Suddenly, as if struck by a new thought, he crossed to where Margaret sat and began, without preamble, as if he knew she had been listening:

“Miss Hale, I received a round-robin from some of my men—I suspect written by Higgins—stating their willingness to work for me again, should I ever be in a position to employ them. That was good, wasn’t it?”

“Yes,” she said softly, lifting her eyes to meet his—clear, steady, full of meaning. “Just right. I’m glad of it.”

She lowered her gaze beneath the weight of his eloquent stare. He looked back at her for a long moment, as if unsure of his own purpose. Then he sighed.

“I knew you would like it,” he said quietly, and turned away. He did not speak to her again until he bid her a formal “good night.”

As Mr. Lennox prepared to leave, Margaret, her face flushing uncontrollably, hesitated, then said:

“Can I speak to you tomorrow? I need your help with—something.”

“Certainly,” he replied, his voice warm. “I’ll come at any time you choose. Nothing would give me greater pleasure than to be of use. Eleven o’clock? Very well.”

His eye lit with quiet exultation. How she was learning to depend on him! It seemed every day brought him closer to certainty—the certainty he had vowed never to seek again without.
