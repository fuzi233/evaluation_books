An Early Fright

In the shadowed valleys of Styria, we dwell within a fortress of stone and silence, a *schloss* that seems carved from the gloom itself. Here, wealth is measured not in gold but in isolation; a mere eight or nine hundred florins a year stretches our lives to the breaking point, yet in this primitive land where labor costs nothing and fear is currency, it suffices. My father, an Englishman retired from the Austrian service, bought this feudal relic and the desolate acres clinging to it for a song. He is a kind man, growing brittle with age, and I am his only child, bearing an English name though I have never seen the land of my birth. To us, this lonely eminence, surrounded by a forest that stretches fifteen miles to the right and twelve to the left, is the entire world.

The castle sits atop a slight rise, overlooking a moat choked with white water-lilies and swans that glide like ghosts over the dark water. A narrow, ancient road passes beneath a drawbridge that has never risen in my lifetime, leading to a gate framed by a steep Gothic bridge spanning a stream that winds through the deep shadows of the woods. We are utterly alone. The nearest village is seven miles away, a mere speck on the horizon, while the nearest seat of power belongs to General Spielsdorf, twenty miles distant. Between us lies only ruin: three miles west, a dead village with a roofless church, its aisles filled with the moldering tombs of the Karnsteins, an extinct family that once ruled a chateau now swallowed by the trees. Legends speak of why they fled, but I know none of them yet.

Our household is a tiny circle of souls. My mother, a Styrian, died when I was an infant, a memory erased before it could take root. In her place stood Madame Perrodon, a Swiss woman of benevolent bulk and broken English, who became my surrogate mother. Beside her was Mademoiselle De Lafontaine, a "finishing governess" who spoke French and German, while my father and I insisted on speaking English daily to keep the tongue alive. We formed a babel of languages that amused strangers, accompanied occasionally by young ladies of my own age who visited for fleeting terms. Yet, despite these few companions, my life was one of profound solitude.

It was in this quiet isolation that the first true terror of my life took root, an incident so trivial in retrospect that it should perhaps remain unrecorded, yet one that haunts my dreams to this day. I was no more than six years old when the nightmare began. The nursery, a vast chamber with a steep oak ceiling, belonged solely to me. One night, I awoke to find the room empty. The maid was gone, the nurse absent. I was not afraid, for I was shielded from the stories of ghosts and fairies that make grown women tremble at a creaking floorboard. Instead, I felt neglected, insulted by the abandonment. I began to whimper, preparing for a tantrum, when a figure emerged from the shadows beside my bed.

A young woman knelt there, her face solemn yet strangely beautiful, her hands hidden beneath the heavy coverlet. She looked at me with a strange, placid wonder, and as she reached out to caress me and drew me close, a wave of inexplicable delight washed over me, silencing my tears. I drifted back to sleep, lulled by her presence. Then, without warning, a sharp, stinging pain pierced my breast, as if two needles had driven deep into my flesh. I screamed.

The woman recoiled, her eyes wide and fixed on me, before slipping silently to the floor and vanishing beneath the bed.

For the first time, true fear seized me. I yelled until my voice broke. Soon, the nurse, the maid, and the housekeeper rushed in. They soothed me with gentle words, but their faces were pale, etched with an anxiety I had never seen. They searched frantically—under the bed, behind the tables, inside the cupboards. The housekeeper leaned close to the nurse and whispered, "Feel along that hollow in the bed. Someone *did* lie there. The warmth remains."

They examined my chest, searching for the wound I claimed to feel, but found nothing but smooth skin. That night, the three servants sat vigil in the nursery, their eyes never closing. From that moment until I was fourteen, a servant was always present in the room.

The days that followed were a blur of terror. A doctor, an elderly man with a saturnine face pitted by smallpox and a chestnut wig, arrived every second day to prescribe bitter medicines I despised. I could not bear to be left alone, even in the broad daylight.

One morning, my father entered the room. He stood by my bedside, laughing cheerfully at something the nurse said, patting my shoulder and kissing my forehead. "It was only a dream, my dear," he assured me, his voice bright. "It cannot hurt you."

But I knew better. The woman in the bed was not a dream. The cold touch of her fingers and the sudden, needle-like pain were real. I was awfully frightened.

Only the nursery maid offered a sliver of comfort, insisting that it was she who had knelt beside me, and that I must have been half-asleep to fail to recognize her. But this explanation did not satisfy the dread coiling in my stomach. Later that day, a venerable old man in a black cassock entered the room. His face was sweet and gentle, and he took my hands, guiding me to join them as he and the women prayed aloud.

"Lord hear all good prayers for us, for Jesus' sake," he intoned, his voice quavering with earnestness.

I remember the stark contrast of his white hair against the black fabric, the clumsy furniture of three centuries surrounding us, and the scanty light filtering through the small lattice window. He knelt with the women, praying for what seemed an eternity. I forget everything before that moment, and for some time after, my memories dissolve into obscurity. Only the scene of that prayer remains vivid, an isolated picture in a phantasmagoria of darkness, marking the beginning of a life forever altered by the unseen.
