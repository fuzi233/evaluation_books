We woke as men do who have trusted their bones to open ground and a night sky that never quite stops breathing. Even here in Grand Canary, where the day comes on like warm bread drawn from an oven, the dark has its own bite: a dew that beads on hair and sleeve-cuff, and a chill that creeps up through the hips and makes the joints complain when you stand.

Down in the barranca the island was already tuning itself. Water ticked over stones with the thin, constant voice of a kitchen clock; a few early goats somewhere above knocked their bells together in sleepy argument; and from far off—too far to name—the first cart on a road gave a wooden groan that seemed to drag behind it the whole notion of commerce. Between those sounds the air held a hush of thyme and sun-heated rock waiting to happen.

For my own part, when I’ve slept out, I like to set blood moving with a run. But the middle of the island is no gentleman’s track. There wasn’t three yards of level ground to be found; every step was a negotiation with lava that bristled like broken crockery. So when Coppinger—portly, determined, and pious about his own body—began going through dumb-bell exercises with two lumps of prickly stone as if the cliff were his private gymnasium, I followed suit, heaving and stretching until the stiffness was driven from the shoulders and into sweat.

Coppinger had roughed it a good deal in his time. He was a doctor of medicine amongst other things, and he took out a new degree every other year as some men take out new boots. Health theories with him were not opinions; they were a religion, practised with an earnestness that would have been admirable if it hadn’t been so tyrannical.

There had been rain two days before, and a thin trickle still ran along the bottom of the barranca. We went down to it and washed. The water was cold enough to make the gums smart when it touched the mouth, but it carried with it that clean, stony taste that makes you feel—however briefly—that you’ve been rinsed of the town. We brushed our teeth there too. On this sort of expedition a toothbrush is the greatest luxury imaginable—more civilised, in its way, than a bed.

“Now,” said Coppinger when we had emptied our pockets, “there’s precious little grub left, and it’s none the better for being carried in a local Spanish newspaper.”

“Yours is mostly tobacco ashes.”

“It’ll get worse if we leave it. We’ve a lot more bad scrambling ahead of us.”

That was plain enough. So we sat beside the stream where the rock formed a natural table, and ate all that remained: a few crushed odds that tasted of paper, heat, and yesterday’s handling. It wasn’t food so much as a reminder of food, but it took the edge off. I could not help thinking, even as I chewed, of the fonda at Santa Brigida—ten hard miles away—and of the PATRON’S old country wine, dark and honest and smelling faintly of cask and sun. If the English hotels in Las Palmas knew what splendid vintages could be had with a little diplomacy in the mountain villages, their grand old “cellars” would be laughed out of fashion inside a week.

To tell the truth, the two mummies Coppinger had already gathered would have satisfied my small ambition. Sewn into goatskins that had gone brittle as paper, they gave off dust like puffballs when disturbed—as if the dead were trying to return to the cliff. But you know what Coppinger is. He fancied he had come upon traces of an old Guanche university, or sacred college—something of that kind—and he would not be satisfied until he had ransacked every cave that pocked the whole face of the precipice. He had plenty of stuff left for the flash apparatus, twenty-eight more films in his Kodak, and argued that we might as well do the job now as make another journey on purpose.

So he took the crowbar; I shouldered the rope; and away we went up to the ridge where the sun had baked us the day before as if we were loaves set too near the fire.

These caves were not easy to reach—otherwise they would have been looted long ago. Coppinger, who on principle makes out he knows all about these matters, said that in old Guanche days they had ladders of goatskin rope which they could haul up after them, keeping out undesirable callers. No better plan occurred to me, and the cliff certainly looked as though it had been designed to discourage curiosity.

The mouths of the caves were in a more or less level row, thirty feet below the ridge and fifty above the bottom. Spanish curiosity, I had noticed, does not go far where it cannot walk. To ladder such openings from below would have been cumbersome; a light knotted rope is easily carried. Our plan was to descend to each mouth from above, slip down to the foot of the cliff, and begin again ab initio for the next.

Coppinger was plucky enough, and he had a good head on a height, but there is no disguising that he was portly and nearer fifty than forty-five. For such work he must have been keen indeed. I went first each time, easing myself over the edge and “walking” down the face in that fly-fashion you have to adopt when the rock gives you no decent hold and your whole trust is in a thin bootlace of rope. From below there’s little help a man can offer—only his best wishes shouted up. My hands were soon cut raw; the rope bit, warmed, slipped; each grip was a bargain with gravity.

I tried to save him where I could. The first three caves were small and empty, mere dens—store-places, seemingly—and I asked him to take them for granted. But he insisted on clambering down to each one in person. When he decided that one of my “granaries” was a prison, another a pot-making factory, and another a schoolroom for young priests, he naturally said he had no reliance on my judgment. Thorough-going archaeologists have a fatal imagination, and Coppinger’s was in fighting trim.

As the day rose higher, the heat began to press with the steady weight of a hand on the back of the neck. The air in the cliff-side grew close, smelling of old bat, dry stone, and an ancient dust that puffed up at the slightest disturbance and clung to the tongue. Even the island’s sounds altered: the stream below seemed thinner and farther away; the wind in the scrub made a tired whisper rather than a song; and, once, some distant hammering drifted up from a village—metal on metal—thin as a nail and twice as unpleasant, like a promise of modernity that didn’t belong in such a place.

Coppinger began to have had enough, though he would not admit it. He was game, and he insisted on going on longer than was safe. I disliked it. The drop from the ridge was seldom less than eighty feet. But at last he was forced to give up.

I suggested marching for Santa Brigida forthwith, before hunger became a kind of rage. He wouldn’t do it.

“There are three more openings,” he said, peering along the cliff as if the rock itself owed him an answer. “And if you won’t do them, I must make another effort myself.”

He tried to make out he was conferring a favour on me by offering to accept a report from my untrained observation. I refused to see it in that light. I was tired; the sweat had soaked me through; my head ached from the sun’s violence; my hands stung as though rubbed with salt.

Coppinger might be tired, but enthusiasm held him up like a stiff drink.

“Look here,” he said, “there’s no knowing what you may find, and if you do lay hands on anything, remember it’s your own. I shall have no claim whatever.”

“Very kind of you,” I said, “but I’ve got no use for any more mummies done up in goatskin bags.”

“Bah! That’s not a burial cave up there. Don’t you know the difference yet in the openings? Be a good fellow. It doesn’t follow that because we have drawn all the rest blank, you won’t stumble on a good find for yourself.”

“Oh, very well,” I said at last, because he was set on it and I was too wearied to keep refusing. I stumbled over fallen rock, along a ledge, and scrambled up by the fissure in the cliff that saved us the two-mile detour of the first approach. I wrenched out the crowbar, jammed it into a new purchase, and went over the side again, hands smarting worse at every rope-burned grip.

Swinging into the cave mouth was awkward. The rock above overhung—or else it had broken away below, which came to the same thing. I managed it, though I landed with an ugly thump on my back and very nearly lost the rope. Had it slipped away then, Coppinger could not have flicked it into my reach from where he waited below.

At first glance I knew this cave differed from the others. Those had been mere rounded hollows, dens scooped out anyhow. This had been faced up with cutting tools: angles clean, sides smooth and flat. The walls inclined inward toward the roof in a fashion that reminded me of some architecture I had seen before but could not at once place. There were several rooms connected by passages. I was pleased—purely for the sake of labour saved—to discover that the other openings Coppinger wanted were merely windows or doorways into adjoining chambers.

I searched. I ran my fingers along the smoothed stone, feeling for scratches, for the faint rise of markings. No inscriptions. No wall-painting. Nothing but bats—dry leathered bodies that shifted and whispered above me, their small squeaks stitching the dark to the dark—and the powdery litter of ages.

To keep from seeming too quick—Coppinger always suspects a man of slurring his work if it’s done briskly—I lit a cigarette and smoked it through, the smoke hanging low and tasting of stale mineral air. Then I leaned out at the rope and shouted down what I had found.

He turned up an anxious face. “Have you searched it thoroughly?” he bawled.

“Of course I have. What do you think I’ve been doing all this time?”

“No, don’t come down yet. Wait a minute. I’m making fast the Kodak and the flashlight apparatus on the end of the rope. Pull them up and make me half a dozen exposures—there’s a good fellow.”

“Oh, all right,” I said, and hauled the gear up into the cave.

The photographs would be dull enough, but Coppinger liked dullness. He trusted it. One has to be careful of halation in such interiors. There was a ledge like a seat beside each doorway, and I lodged the camera on it for steadiness, snapping the flash from behind and above. The sudden white flare made the bat-wings beat like thrown cloth and left a hot smell in the air, sharp and metallic.

I got four chambers that way and came to one where the ledge was higher and wider. I put down the camera, wedged it level with scraps of stone, and sat to recharge the flash.

The moment my weight came onto that ledge there was a sharp crackle, like the breaking of old sugar, and down I went half a dozen inches. I was up at once, snatching the Kodak as it began to slide.

And then I confess I felt pleased. Here, at any rate, was a cupboard of sorts—a hollow hermetically sealed with cement. If they had taken the trouble to close it up so, the odds were there was something inside worth hiding.

At first there was nothing but dust and rubble. I lit a candle end, its flame breathing in the stale air, and cleared away the loose stuff. Presently my knife began to strike something that did not chip like cement. It came away in regular layers, thin sheets. When I took a fragment toward the entrance where daylight licked the darkness, I saw each layer had two faces: one side a shiny pale stuff like talc; on it smeared a coating of dark toffee-coloured matter that might have been wax. The toffee surface was worked over with a pattern—scratched lines, purposeful, repeated.

I do not pretend to knowledge in these matters. Coppinger had impressed on me often enough that the Guanche could not write, and with that in my mind I did not guess—though I might have—that the patterns were letters in some obsolete character. I only knew that anything so carefully made, so carefully hidden, was worth taking.

So I set to work quarrying it out with the heel of my boot and a pocket-knife. The sheets were more or less stuck together; I did not try to separate them. They fitted the cavity exactly, but by smashing down its front I could reach the lower edges, and then I hacked through the bottom layers until I got the bulk out in one solid piece. It measured perhaps twenty inches by fifteen by fifteen. It looked heavy, but it was not as weighty as its size promised; it handled oddly, as if the material remembered heat and held it.

When I had taken the remaining photographs, I lowered the lump down to Coppinger on the end of the rope. Then I descended myself. The cliff gave me back to sun and wind and the sound of my own breath.

The bundle lay on the ground. Coppinger was on all fours beside it, as reverent as a cook bending over a broth he has watched all night. His eyes were shining; his fingers hovered as though touch might ruin everything.

“What is it?” I asked.

“I don’t know yet,” he said, almost snarling with delight. “But it is the most valuable find ever made in the Canary Islands—and it’s yours, you unappreciative beggar; at least what there is left of it. Oh, man, man, you’ve smashed up the beginning and you’ve smashed up the end of some history that is probably priceless. It’s my fault. I ought to have known better than set an untrained man to do important exploring work.”

“If anything’s gone wrong, it’s your fault,” I said. “You told me there was no such thing as writing among these ancient Canarios. I took your word for it. For anything I knew, the stuff might have been something to eat.”

“It isn’t Guanche work at all,” he snapped. “You ought to have known that from the talc. Great heavens, man, have you no eyes? Haven’t you seen the general formation of the island? There’s no talc here.”

“I’m no geologist,” I said. “Is this imported literature, then?”

“Of course. It’s Egyptian—that’s obvious at a glance. Though how it’s got here I can’t tell yet. It isn’t stuff you can read off like a newspaper. The character’s a variant on any we’ve discovered so far. And this waxy stuff spread over the talc—it’s unique. Some sort of mineral, I think; perhaps asphalt. It doesn’t scratch like animal wax. I’ll analyse it later. Why anyone invented it and then let such a splendid notion drop out of use is a marvel. I could stay gloating over this all day.”

“Well,” I said, “if it’s all the same to you, I’d rather gloat over a meal. It’s ten miles hard going to the fonda, and I’m hungry as a hawk. Look—do you know it’s four o’clock? It takes longer than you think, climbing down to each cave and up again.”

He spread his coat on the ground and wrapped the lump with tender care, refusing to tie it with rope for fear of breaking more edges. He insisted on carrying it himself for most of the way to Santa Brigida. Only when fatigue had him within an ace of dropping did he allow me to take my turn.

“I suppose you may as well carry the stuff,” he snapped, “seeing that after all it’s your own.”

The fonda, when we reached it, smelled like salvation: garlic crushed into oil; stewed peppers; bread with a crust that crackled when broken; the faint sweet-sour of wine sleeping in wood. I ate as well as could be procured, drank a bottle of the Patron’s old Canary, and turned into bed after a final pipe, feeling the body settle into itself as if the mattress were a warm dough. Coppinger dined too, but I have reason to believe he did not sleep much. The house had its own night noises—murmured voices, a door-latch, an occasional cough—but his wakefulness seemed to sit on top of them, alert and hungry.

In the morning I found him still poring over the find, heavy-eyed, unshaven, brimming with enthusiasm as if he had been living on nothing but the idea of it.

“Do you know,” he said, “that you’ve blundered on the most valuable historical manuscript the modern world has ever seen? Of course, with your clumsy way of getting it out, you’ve done an infinity of damage. Those top sheets you shelled away and spoiled contained probably an absolutely unique account of the ancient civilisation of Yucatan.”

“Where’s that, anyway?”

“In the middle of the Gulf of Mexico. It’s all ruins to-day, but once it was a very prosperous colony of the Atlanteans.”

“Never heard of them. Oh—yes, I have, though. They were the people Herodotus wrote about, weren’t they? But I thought they were mythical.”

“They were very real,” he said, and struck the sheets lightly with his knuckles as though to make them answer, “and so was Atlantis—the continent where they lived, which lay just north of the Canaries here.”

“What’s that crocodile sort of thing with wings drawn in the margin?”

“Some beast that lived in those days. The pages are full of them. That’s a cave-tiger. And that’s some sort of colossal bat. Thank goodness the author had the sense to illustrate fully, or we should never have been able to reconstruct the tale—at any rate we could not have understood half of it. Whole species have died out since this was written, just as a whole continent has been swept away and three civilisations quenched. The worst of it is, the man was highly educated and writes a very bad fist. I’ve hammered at it all night and only managed a few sentences here and there.” He rubbed his hands, delighted. “It will take me a year’s hard work to translate properly.”

“Every man to his taste,” I said. “I’m afraid my interest wouldn’t last as long as that. But how did it get there? Did your ancient Egyptian come to Grand Canary for his lungs and write it because he felt dull in a cave?”

“I made a mistake there,” he said testily. “The author was not Egyptian. The similarity of the character misled me. The book was written by one Deucalion—priest or general, perhaps both—and he was an Atlantean. How it got there I don’t know yet. Probably that was told in the last pages, which a certain vandal smashed up with his pocket-knife in getting them away from where they were stowed.”

“That’s right,” I said. “Abuse me. Deucalion, you say? There was a Deucalion in Greek mythology—one of the two who escaped the Flood. Their Noah.”

“The swamping of Atlantis might very well correspond to the Flood.”

“Is there a Pyrrha then? She was Deucalion’s wife.”

“I haven’t come across her yet. But there’s a Phorenice, who may be the same. She seems to have been the reigning Empress, as far as I can make out at present.”

I looked more closely at the margin drawings. They were perfectly intelligible though the perspective was wrong. A cave-tiger, enormous, seemed to be trying to puff down a mammoth; elsewhere a bat like a nightmare spread its wings across the page. There was something in that crude line-work—its confidence, its untranslatable immediacy—that made me think of all the voices that vanish when a language dies: the taste-words, the sound-words, the names for winds and breads and beasts that cannot be carried whole into another tongue.

“Weird beasts,” I said. “And big. By Jove, I shouldn’t care to have lived in those days.”

“Probably they had some way of fighting them,” he said. “That will show itself as I get along with the translation.”

He looked at his watch and yawned. “I suppose I ought to be ashamed, but I haven’t been to bed. Are you going out?”

“I shall drive back to Las Palmas,” I said. “I promised a man a round at golf this afternoon.”

“Very well. See you at dinner. I hope they’ve sent back my dress shirts from the wash. O Lord! I am sleepy.”

I left him going up to bed, and went outside to order a carriage. The street had begun its day-noise: children calling, hooves striking stone, a radio somewhere making a tinny imitation of music. It struck me then how quickly a place’s true sound can be overwritten—how easily the old acoustic fabric is torn and patched with cheap modern racket, until even memory begins to hear wrong.

There we parted for a considerable time.

At the hotel in Las Palmas a cable waited for me: home for business forthwith. A Liverpool boat lay in harbour, and I just managed to catch her as she was steaming out. The boatmen made a small fortune out of my hurry.

Now Coppinger was only an hotel acquaintance. When I got back to England I was up to the eyes in work, and I’m afraid I did not think much more of him. One doesn’t, with people one meets casually abroad. It must have been at least a year later that I saw, in a paragraph of a paper, that he had given the lump of sheets to the British Museum, and that their estimated worth was ten thousand pounds at the lowest valuation.

That was a revelation. He had so repeatedly impressed upon me that the things were mine by right of discovery that I wrote him a pointed note, mentioning that he seemed to have been making rather free with my property.

Back came a stilted letter beginning, “Doctor Coppinger regrets,” and with it the English translation of the wax-upon-talc manuscript. He “quite admitted” my claim, and “trusted that the profits of publication would be sufficient reimbursement for any damage received.”

I had not expected him to take that tone, and wrote back a warm reply to that effect. The only answer I got was through a firm of solicitors, who stated that all further communications with Dr. Coppinger must be made through them.

I regret publicly the line he has taken; but since the affair has gone so far, I am disposed to follow out his proposition. Accordingly the old history is here printed. The credit—and the responsibility—of the translation rests with Dr. Coppinger; and whatever revenue accrues from readers goes to the finder of the original talc-upon-wax sheets: myself. If there is a further alteration in this arrangement, it will be announced publicly at a later date. But at present that appears most unlikely.
