M. Paul Emanuel possessed a sensitivity to interruption that bordered on the pathological. To cross his classroom, regardless of the cause, was deemed by the faculty and students alike a peril comparable to walking through a minefield. Madame Beck, if compelled, would skurry past, her skirts retrenched, coasting the raised platform with the terrified caution of a ship fearing the breakers. But Rosine, the portress, bore the heavier burden. Every half-hour, she was tasked with fetching pupils from the very heart of the divisions to attend music lessons in various halls. On her second or third attempt, she would frequently become tongue-tied, paralyzed by the unspeakable glares sent through M. Paul's dart-dealing spectacles.

One morning, I sat in the *carré*, working on a piece of embroidery begun by a pupil but left unfinished. While my fingers moved over the frame, my ears were assaulted by the crescendo of a voice haranguing in the neighboring class. The tone grew ominously varied, a gathering storm separated from me only by a sturdy partition wall and a glass door leading to the courtyard. I derived more amusement than alarm from the thickening symptoms. Poor Rosine was not safe; she had already made the perilous passage four times that morning. Now, for the fifth time, it fell to her to snatch a brand from the burning—a pupil from under M. Paul's nose.

"Mon Dieu! Mon Dieu!" she cried. "What shall I become? Monsieur will kill me, I am sure; he is so angry!"

Nerved by the desperation of her situation, she opened the door.

"Mademoiselle La Malle au piano!" she called out.

Before she could retreat or close the door, the voice erupted:

"Dès ce moment! The class is forbidden. The first to open this door or pass through this division shall be hanged—even if it were Madame Beck herself!"

Less than ten minutes after this decree, Rosine's French pantoufles shuffled again along the corridor.

"Mademoiselle," she said, "I would not go into that class for a five-franc piece. Monsieur's spectacles are truly terrible. A commissionaire has come with a message from the Athénée. I told Madame Beck I dare not deliver it, and she says I must charge you with it."

"Me? No, that is too bad! It is not in my line of duty. Come, Rosine, bear your own burden. Be brave—go once more!"

"I, Mademoiselle? Impossible! Five times I have crossed him today. Madame must hire a gendarme for this service. Ouf! I cannot endure it!"

"Bah! You are only a coward. What is the message?"

"Precisely the kind Monsieur hates most: an urgent summons to go directly to the Athénée. An official visitor—an inspector, I know not what—has arrived, and Monsieur *must* meet him. You know how he revolts against a *must*."

Yes, I knew well enough. The restive little man detested any spur or curb; against the urgent or obligatory, he was sure to revolt. However, I accepted the responsibility—not without fear, but fear blended with curiosity. I opened the door, entered, and closed it behind me as quickly and quietly as a trembling hand would permit. To be slow, bustling, or to leave a door gaping were aggravations of crime often more disastrous than the crime itself.

There I stood, and there he sat. His humor was visibly bad, almost at its worst. He had been giving a lesson in arithmetic—a dry subject that invariably disagreed with him; not a pupil trembled when he spoke of figures. He sat bent above his desk. To look up at the sound of an entrance, a direct breach of his will and law, was an effort he could not bring himself to make immediately. It was fortunate, for I thus gained time to walk up the long class. It suited my nature better to encounter the near burst of anger like his than to bear its menace at a distance.

At his estrade, I paused just in front. I was not worthy of immediate attention, so he proceeded with his lesson. Disdain would not suffice; he must hear and answer my message. Not being tall enough to lift my head over his elevated desk, I suffered an eclipse in my position. I ventured to peep around, intending merely to get a better view of his face, which had struck me upon entry as bearing a close, picturesque resemblance to a black and sallow tiger. Twice I enjoyed this side-view with impunity, advancing and receding unseen. The third time, my eye had scarce dawned beyond the obscuration of the desk when it was caught and transfixed through its very pupil—transfixed by the "lunettes." Rosine was right; these utensils held a blank, immutable terror beyond the mobile wrath of the wearer's unglazed eyes.

I now found the advantage of proximity. These short-sighted spectacles were useless for inspecting a criminal under Monsieur's nose; accordingly, he doffed them, and we stood on more equal terms.

I am glad I was not really much afraid of him. Indeed, close in his presence, I felt no terror at all. Upon his demanding the cord and gibbet to execute the sentence recently pronounced, I was able to furnish him with a needleful of embroidering thread with such accommodating civility that it could not but allay some portion of his surplus irritation. Of course, I did not parade this courtesy publicly; I merely handed the thread round the angle of the desk and attached it, ready noosed, to the barred back of the Professor's chair.

"Que me voulez-vous?" he said in a growl confined to his chest and throat, his teeth clenched, seemingly registering an inward vow that nothing earthly should wring a smile from him.

My answer commenced uncompromisingly: "Monsieur," I said, "I want the impossible, unheard-of things." Thinking it best not to mince matters, I administered the "douche" with decision, delivering the Athenian message in a low but quick voice, floridly exaggerating its urgency.

Of course, he would not hear a word. He refused to leave his present class, let all the officials of Villette send for him. He would not put himself an inch out of his way at the bidding of king, cabinet, and chambers together.

I knew, however, that he *must* go. Both duty and interest commanded immediate compliance. I stood, therefore, waiting in silence, as if he had not yet spoken. He asked what more I wanted.

"Only Monsieur's answer to deliver to the commissionaire."

He waved an impatient negative.

I ventured to stretch my hand to the *bonnet-grec* lying in grim repose on the window-sill. He followed this daring movement with his eye, no doubt in mixed pity and amazement at its presumption.

"Ah!" he muttered. "If it came to that—if Miss Lucy meddled with his *bonnet-grec*—she might just put it on herself, turn *garçon* for the occasion, and benevolently go to the Athénée in his stead."

With great respect, I laid the bonnet on the desk, where its tassel seemed to give me an awful nod.

"I'll write a note of apology—that will do!" he said, still bent on evasion.

Knowing well it would *not* do, I gently pushed the bonnet toward his hand. Thus impelled, it slid down the polished slope of the varnished desk, carrying before it the light steel-framed "lunettes," and, fearful to relate, they fell to the estrade. A score of times ere now had I seen them fall and receive no damage—but this time, as Lucy Snowe's hapless luck would have it, they fell so that each clear pebble became a shivered, shapeless star.

Now, indeed, dismay seized me—dismay and regret. I knew the value of these "lunettes"; M. Paul's sight was peculiar, not easily fitted, and these glasses suited him. I had heard him call them his treasures. As I picked them up, cracked and worthless, my hand trembled. Frightened through all my nerves was I to see the mischief I had done, but I think I was even more sorry than afraid. For some seconds I dared not look the bereaved Professor in the face. He was the first to speak.

"Là!" he said. "I am now a widower of my spectacles! I think Mademoiselle Lucy will now confess that the cord and gallows are amply earned; she trembles in anticipation of her doom. Ah, traitress! traitress! You are resolved to have me quite blind and helpless in your hands!"

I lifted my eyes. His face, instead of being irate, lowering, and furrowed, was overflowing with a smile, colored with the bloom I had seen brightening it that evening at the Hôtel Crécy. He was not angry—not even grieved. For the real injury, he showed himself full of clemency; under the real provocation, patient as a saint. This event, which seemed so untoward and which I thought had ruined my chance of successful persuasion, proved my best help. Difficult of management so long as I had done him no harm, he became graciously pliant as soon as I stood in his presence a conscious and contrite offender.

Still gently railing at me as "une forte femme—une Anglaise terrible—une petite casse-tout," he declared that he dared not disobey one who had given such an instance of her dangerous prowess. It was absolutely like the "grand Empereur smashing the vase to inspire dismay." So, at last, crowning himself with his *bonnet-grec* and taking his ruined "lunettes" from my hand with a clasp of kind pardon and encouragement, he made his bow and went off to the Athénée in first-rate humor and spirits.

After all this amiability, the reader will be sorry for my sake to hear that I was quarreling with M. Paul again before night; yet so it was, and I could not help it.

It was his occasional custom—and a very laudable one—to arrive in the evening, always *à l'improviste*, unannounced, bursting in on the silent hour of study, establishing a sudden despotism over us and our occupations. He caused books to be put away, work-bags brought out, and, drawing forth a single thick volume or a handful of pamphlets, substituted for the besotted "lecture pieuse" drawnled by a sleepy pupil some tragedy made grand by grand reading, ardent by fiery action. Some drama, whereof, for my part, I rarely studied the intrinsic merit; for M. Emanuel made it a vessel for an outpouring, filling it with his native verve and passion like a cup with a vital brewage. Or else he would flash through our conventual darkness a reflex of a brighter world, showing us a glimpse of current literature, reading passages from enchanting tales or the last witty *feuilleton* that had awakened laughter in the saloons of Paris, taking care always to expunge, with the severest hand, whatever passage, phrase, or word could be deemed unsuited to an audience of "jeunes filles." I noticed more than once that where retrenchment without substitute would have left an unmeaning vacancy, he could, and did, improvise whole paragraphs no less vigorous than irreproachable; the dialogue and description he engrafted were often far better than that he pruned away.

Well, on the evening in question, we were sitting silent as nuns in a "retreat," the pupils studying, the teachers working. I remember my work; it was a slight matter of fancy, interesting to me, having a purpose. I was not doing it merely to kill time; I meant it, when finished, as a gift. The occasion of presentation being near, haste was requisite, and my fingers were busy.

We heard the sharp bell-peal we all knew, then the rapid step familiar to each ear. The words "Voilà Monsieur!" had scarcely broken simultaneously from every lip when the two-leaved door split (as it always did for his admission—such a slow word as "open" is inefficient to describe his movements), and he stood in the midst of us.

There were two study tables, both long and flanked with benches; over the center of each hung a lamp. Beneath this lamp, on either side of the table, sat a teacher; the girls were arranged to the right and left; the eldest and most studious nearest the lamps or tropics; the idlers and little ones towards the north and south poles. Monsieur's habit was politely to hand a chair to some teacher, generally Zélie St. Pierre, the senior mistress, then to take her vacated seat, thus availing himself of the full beam of Cancer or Capricorn, which, owing to his near sight, he needed.

As usual, Zélie rose with alacrity, smiling to the whole extent of her mouth, displaying her upper and under rows of teeth—that strange smile which passes from ear to ear, marked only by a sharp thin curve failing to spread over the countenance, neither dimpling the cheek nor lighting the eye. I suppose Monsieur did not see her, or he had taken a whim that he would not notice her, for he was as capricious as women are said to be. Then his "lunettes" (he had got another pair) served him as an excuse for all sorts of little oversights and shortcomings.
