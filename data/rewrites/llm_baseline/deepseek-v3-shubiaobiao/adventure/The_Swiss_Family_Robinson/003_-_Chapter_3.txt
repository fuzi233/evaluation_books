My wife greeted me with weary relief when we returned. "I won’t describe our first day’s struggles," she said. "Most of it was spent watching for your signals, my heart leaping at every movement on the horizon. This morning, when I saw your sign that all was well, I nearly wept with joy. But as the boys slept, I sat and thought—how can we endure this scorching rock any longer? The sun bakes us mercilessly, and the tent is little better than an oven. Why shouldn’t we, too, seek a better place?"

Her eyes gleamed with determination. "Fritz spoke of those shaded woods—surely there’s a spot among them where we might settle. Somewhere cool, safe, and green. I mean to find it."

By then, the boys had stirred. Jack, ever resourceful, was already at work skinning the jackal Fritz had shot. With careful strokes, he cut long strips of hide, scraped them clean, and studded them with nails, points outward. Then, doubling strips of sailcloth, he pressed them over the raw side to cushion the sharp metal.

"Mother," he said, grinning, "would you stitch these together?"

I handed him needle and thread instead. "The labor is yours to finish, my dear."

But watching his clumsy fingers wrestle with the task, I relented and took over, though the smell of the fresh hide turned my stomach. Soon, two spiked collars for the dogs lay finished, and Jack proudly fastened a belt of the same material around his waist, sliding his pistols into it like a young adventurer.

With the boys armed and our provisions packed, we set out toward the stream, the dogs trotting ahead. Turk, remembering the path from Fritz’s earlier trek, led with confident strides. My heart swelled as I watched Ernest and Jack, rifles in hand—so young, yet already our protectors.

Crossing the stream, we climbed the rise my husband had described. The view stole my breath—rolling woodlands, open meadows, and in the distance, a small, inviting grove. "There," I murmured. "That’s where we belong."

But the tall grass near the grove was a fortress, its blades thick as reeds and towering over the boys. We turned toward the beach, skirting the dense growth until we found easier passage inland.

Then—chaos.

A thunderous flapping erupted at our feet as a massive bird launched into the sky. Ernest and Jack fumbled with their guns, but it was gone before they could fire.

"An eagle!" little Franz gasped.

Ernest scoffed. "Not every large bird is an eagle, Franz."

A second bird burst upward moments later, leaving us stunned. I couldn’t help laughing. "Such mighty hunters! Our larder will surely overflow."

Ernest flushed, but Jack swept off his cap and bowed. "Farewell, noble bird! Until we meet again!"

Examining the nest—a crude heap of grass—we found broken shells nearby. "Bustards," Ernest declared, ever the naturalist. "Ground-nesting birds. See the white bellies? And those moustache-like feathers? Classic traits of the Great Bustard."

I ruffled his hair. "At least your eyes are sharp, even if your trigger finger lags."

At last, we reached the grove—but "grove" was too modest a word. These trees were giants, their trunks lifted high on arching roots, forming natural cathedrals of shade. Jack scaled one of the roots and measured the trunk at eighteen yards around. No thorns or underbrush cluttered the soft earth below—only lush grass, perfect for resting.

We ate our midday meal there, the boys sprawling in contentment. Even the dogs, after scavenging crabs along the shore, curled up without begging—a miracle in itself.

The longer we lingered, the more certain I became: *This* was our new home. If we could build a dwelling among these branches, we would want for nothing.

Back at the shore, we salvaged what we could—rolling casks above the tide line, noting how the dogs feasted on turtle eggs buried in the sand. Then, spotting a distant sail, we hurried back, hearts pounding until we recognized my husband’s raft.

That evening, as we rested by the fire, I pleaded my case. "Let us move to the grove. We could build a platform in the trees—safe from jackals, cool in the heat. In Switzerland, I’ve seen arbors high in the lindens, reached by stairs. Why not here?"

My husband chuckled. "So we’re to live like birds now?"

But I saw the spark of consideration in his eyes.

The next morning, plans took shape. We would fortify the rocky outpost as a storehouse but make the grove our home. A bridge was needed first—no simple task.

As the boys clamored to start, my husband and Fritz sailed back to the wreck, returning with planks and an odd prize: strips of shark skin, which Fritz nailed to dry in the sun. "For polishing wood," my husband explained. "Rough as shagreen."

Ernest, ever the scholar, mused, "I thought shagreen came from donkeys."

"In Turkey, yes," my husband replied. "But angel-fish skin serves the same purpose."

Fritz wrinkled his nose. "An *angel*-fish? That sounds beautiful."

Ernest smirked. "Not all angels are kind."

By day’s end, we had lumber, a plan for the bridge, and a bundle of crawfish Franz had discovered near the jackal’s carcass. As we ate, the boys chattered about our "journey to the Promised Land," impatient to begin.

But first—the bridge. And then, at last, our home among the trees.
