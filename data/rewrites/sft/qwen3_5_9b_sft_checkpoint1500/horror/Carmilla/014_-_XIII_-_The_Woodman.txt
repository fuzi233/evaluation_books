"Yet, alas, there proved to be serious drawbacks. First, Millasca grew lethargic, plagued by a lingering weakness from her recent illness. She rarely left her chamber until well past noon. Worse still, though she invariably locked her door from within and kept the key hidden until admitting her maid, it was discovered that she vanished regularly. In the pale, pre-dawn grey, and again at unexplained hours during the day, she slipped away before anyone knew she stirred. Witnesses from the windows of the *Schloss* reported seeing her wandering eastward through the trees, moving with the vacant, trance-like gait of a somnambulist. I concluded she walked in her sleep, yet this offered no solution. How could she exit a securely barred room? How could she leave the estate without unlocking a single door or window?

"My anxieties deepened into a terrifying dread when I observed her physical decline. Her beauty and health faded with a mystery that bordered on the horrific. First, she was haunted by nightmares of appalling vividness. Then, she claimed visions of a specter—a shifting form, sometimes resembling Millasca, sometimes bestial and indistinct—that paced the foot of her bed. Finally, the sensations began. She spoke of a cold, icy stream flowing against her breast, followed by the sharp, needle-pointed agony beneath her throat. Nights later, a convulsive sense of strangulation seized her, leading inevitably to unconsciousness."

I listened, frozen, to the words of the kindly old General. By now, our carriage rolled over the short grass flanking the road, approaching the roofless village that had known no chimney smoke for fifty years. Every description he uttered mirrored my own symptoms with chilling precision. I shuddered as he detailed the habits of our beautiful guest, Carmilla—who, save for the tragedy that befell her, would have been visiting my father's château at this very moment.

Suddenly, the forest parted. We found ourselves beneath the crumbling chimneys and gables of the ruined village, while the towers and battlements of the dismantled castle loomed above us, shadowed by giant trees on a slight eminence.

In a nightmare state, I dismounted from the carriage. In silence, for both of us were burdened by terrible thoughts, we climbed the ascent and entered the vast, echoing chambers, winding staircases, and dark corridors of the castle.

"And this was once the palatial residence of the Karnsteins!" the old General declared, peering from a high window across the sweeping forest. "A wicked line, and here their bloodstained history was written." His voice held a grim weight. "It is a cruelty that, after death, they should continue to plague mankind with their vile lusts. Down there lies their chapel." He pointed to the grey, Gothic ruins partially obscured by foliage. "And I hear the axe of a woodman ringing through the trees surrounding it. Perhaps he holds the knowledge I seek, and can direct me to the grave of Mircalla, Countess of Karnstein. These common folk preserve the legends of great houses long after the titles and wealth have vanished."

"We possess a portrait of Mircalla at home," my father offered politely. "Would you care to view it?"

"Not yet, dear friend," the General replied. "I suspect I have already seen the original. My true reason for hastening to you was to investigate the chapel we approach."

"Why, she has been dead for over a century!" my father gasped, incredulous.

"Not so dead as you imagine, I am told," the General answered softly.

"I confess, Sir General, you leave me utterly baffled," my father murmured, glancing at the old man. For a fleeting second, I thought I saw the suspicion return to his eyes—the same suspicion I had felt before—but beneath his anger and hatred, there was no madness, only a feverish intensity.

"There remains for me," the General said, guiding us beneath the massive arch of the Gothic church—"one final purpose to occupy me in these remaining years on Earth. To exact upon her the vengeance which, thank God, a mortal arm can still inflict."

"What vengeance do you speak of?" my father asked, his voice rising in alarm.

"To decapitate the monster!" the General roared, his face flushing with a fierce, violent red. He stamped heavily, the echo reverberating mournfully through the hollow ruin, and raised a clenched fist, mimicking the grip on an axe handle, shaking it ferociously. "To sever her head!"

"Cut her head off?" my father stammered, bewildered.

"Aye! With a hatchet, with a spade, with any iron that can cleave through her murderous throat. You shall hear," he growled, trembling with rage. Hurrying forward, he gestured to a square block of wood lying on the grass-grown pavement. "That beam will serve as a seat; your daughter is weary. Let her sit, and I shall conclude this dreadful tale in a few sentences."

I gratefully sank onto the rough timber. Meanwhile, the General summoned the woodman, who had been clearing branches leaning against the ancient walls. The sturdy, weather-beaten man stepped forward, axe in hand.

"He knows nothing of these tombs," the General noted, "but there is an older man, a ranger of these woods, currently staying with the priest two miles hence. He can identify every monument of the old Karnstein line. For a small fee, he will guide us there in less than half an hour."

"How long have you served in this forest?" my father asked the old woodman.

"I have been a woodman here," the man replied in his rough dialect, "under the forester all my life. So did my father, and his father before him, for generations I cannot count. I could show you the very cottage in this village where my ancestors dwelt."

"Why was the village abandoned?" the General inquired.

"It was tormented by revenants, sir," the woodman explained. "Several were tracked to their graves, identified by the traditional signs, and put to an end by decapitation, the stake, or fire. Yet, despite these lawful executions, the village remained cursed."

"But surely, with so many vampires destroyed, the evil must have ceased?"

"No," the woodman shook his head. "A Moravian nobleman, passing through, heard of our plight. Being skilled in such matters, he offered to free us. On a night of a bright moon, shortly after sunset, he climbed the chapel towers. From there, he watched the churchyard below. He saw the vampire emerge from his grave, placing the linen wrappings he wore as a corpse beside it, before gliding silently toward the village to torment its people.

"The stranger waited. When the vampire returned, missing his clothes, he screamed furiously at the Moravian, who sat atop the tower. The nobleman beckoned him upward, inviting him to retrieve his garments. Trusting the lure, the vampire began to climb. As soon as he reached the battlements, the Moravian struck him down with a single blow of his sword, splitting his skull in two. The body fell to the churchyard below. Descending the spiral stairs, the stranger followed, severed the head, and delivered both the head and the body to the villagers the next day. They impaled and burned the remains."

"This nobleman possessed authority from the head of the family to remove the tomb of Mircalla, Countess of Karnstein," the General continued, his eyes gleaming. "He did so effectively, and soon the site was forgotten."

"Do you know where it stood?" the General asked eagerly.

The forester smiled, shaking his head. "No living soul can tell you that now. Besides, rumors say her body was removed, though no one is certain."

With that, dropping his axe, the woodman departed, leaving us alone to listen to the rest of the General's extraordinary narrative.
