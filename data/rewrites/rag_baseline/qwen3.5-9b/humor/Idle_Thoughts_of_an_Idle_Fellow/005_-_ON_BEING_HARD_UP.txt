It is a peculiar irony of fate that I sat down with the solemn resolve to compose something witty and unprecedented, yet my mind, deprived of its usual fuel, offers nothing but the stark, unvarnished reality of being hard up. I suspect the culprit is my posture; I invariably keep my hands buried in my trouser pockets, a habit I maintain with religious fervor unless I find myself in the presence of my sisters, cousins, or aunts. These kinfolk, possessing a vocal agility that borders on the theatrical, launch into a chorus of indignant exhortations regarding the impropriety of such behavior. They argue, with the eloquence of saints, that it lacks gentility. I confess, I cannot fathom the logic. While I readily concede that placing one's hands in *another* person's pocket is a breach of social decorum, I fail to see how occupying one's *own* pockets diminishes a man's inherent nobility. Perhaps the critics possess a vision I lack. Indeed, upon reflection, I recall hearing elderly gentlemen mutter with savage disdain whenever caught in this state. Yet, we young men, seemingly ill-equipped for the rigors of polite society without our pockets filled, remain awkward and shifty creatures, much like a music-hall comedian stripped of his signature top hat.

Let us assume, however, that we stuff our pockets with a modest coinage and a cluster of keys, and proceed to confront a female post-office clerk. Even then, the task of managing one's limbs remains a trial, especially when the pockets are empty save for the void. In days gone by, when my entire fortune might dwindle to the sum the townsfolk derisively termed "a bob," I would recklessly squander a single penny solely to secure the joy of receiving change in copper coins, eager to hear them jingle. There is a distinct psychological weight to currency; one does not feel truly destitute holding eleven pence, whereas a solitary shilling induces a palpable sense of scarcity. Had I been that caricature of the "la-di-da" impecunious youth, mocked by the upper classes, I would have diligently exchanged my penny for two half-pennies. My authority on this subject stems from a life spent as a provincial actor and later as a journalist connected to the press. I have subsisted on fifteen shillings a week, survived a single week on ten while owing the balance, and endured a fortnight living literally on the back of a greatcoat. This experience grants a profound, albeit painful, insight into domestic economics. If one wishes to ascertain the true value of a pound, one need only attempt to survive on fifteen shillings weekly, calculating exactly how much can be saved for attire and amusement. One discovers that waiting for a farthing's change is a worthy endeavor, walking a mile to save a penny is a noble sacrifice, that a glass of beer is a luxury reserved for rare occasions, and that a stiff collar can endure four days of service. I urge any prospective husband to test this regimen prior to tying the knot; it serves as excellent preparation. Similarly, a father should subject his heir to this austerity before dispatching him to university; he will cease to grumble at a hundred pounds a year thereafter.

There are specific types of souls for whom such trials would be transformative. Consider the delicate flower who cannot tolerate claret below ninety-four degrees and would rather dine on cat meat than plain roast mutton. Though such creatures appear occasionally, to the credit of humanity, they are largely confined to the fantastical societies invented by lady novelists. Whenever I encounter someone obsessively debating a menu card, a fierce impulse takes hold to drag them to a common East End pub and force a sixpenny meal down their throat—beefsteak pudding for fourpence, potatoes for a penny, and half a pint of porter for another. The lingering memory of that savory combination, mixed with the intoxicating aromas of beer, tobacco, and roasted pork, might eventually cause them to lower their noses slightly when presented with food. Then there is the generous soul, the cadger's paradise, who dispenses small change freely but forgets to settle debts. Such a lesson in common sense might benefit even him. "One simply cannot give the waiter less than a shilling," explained a young government clerk recently during a lunch in Regent Street. I concurred with his assessment of the impossibility of offering eleven shillings and sixpence, yet I resolved to lure him one day to a haunt near Covent Garden where the waiter, clad in shirt-sleeves that are notoriously filthy by month's end, performs his duties with bare arms. I know this man intimately; if the clerk offers more than a penny, the waiter will immediately demand a handshake as a token of supreme esteem.

Much has been written humorously about the plight of the hard-up, but the reality holds no comedy. Bargaining over pennies is not amusing. Being labeled mean or stingy is not funny. Being shabby and ashamed of one's address is certainly not a source of laughter. Poverty, to the impoverished, is devoid of humor; it is a hellish existence for a sensitive spirit. Countless brave gentlemen, capable of facing the labors of Hercules, have had their hearts shattered by its petty agonies. The physical discomforts are trivial; who would mind roughing it if that were the extent of the hardship? Did Robinson Crusoe care for patches on his trousers, or whether he wore them at all? Did it matter if his toes protruded from his boots or if his umbrella was made of cotton, provided it kept the rain away? His shabbiness troubled him not, for no friends stood nearby to sneer. The sting of poverty lies not in the deprivation itself, but in the exposure. It is not the cold that drives a coatless man to hasten his pace, nor is it the shame of lying—which he knows will be disbelieved—that turns his face crimson when he claims greatcoats are unhealthy and umbrellas are unnecessary principles. Poverty is not a crime, for if it were, men would not feel ashamed of it. It is a blunder, punished accordingly. A poor man is despised universally, equally by Christians and lords, demagogues and footmen. No number of schoolboy maxims can earn him respect. Appearance dictates human judgment; a man will stride down Piccadilly arm-in-arm with the city's most notorious rogue, provided the rogue is impeccably dressed, only to slink into a back alley to whisper to a seedy gentleman. The seedy gentleman, acutely aware of this dynamic, will detour a mile to avoid recognition. Those who knew him in his prosperity need not bother looking the other way; he is a thousand times more anxious to evade them than they are to assist him. All he desires is oblivion, a wish he is often fortunate enough to achieve.

One grows accustomed to being hard up, as one adapts to any circumstance, aided by that venerable homeopathic physician, Time. The distinction between the veteran and the novice is immediate. The hardened individual, seasoned by years of shifting and struggling, approaches the pawnbroker with the same composure he would show a tailor, perhaps even greater civility. The assistant treats him with efficiency, to the chagrin of the lady in the adjacent stall, who ironically remarks she doesn't mind the delay if it involves a regular customer. The transaction resembles a brisk purchase of government bonds. Conversely, a man's first visit to the pawnshop is a spectacle of panic. Like a boy asking his first question, he is brimming with confidence. He loiters outside, drawing the attention of every loafer and arousing the suspicion of the beat constable. Finally, after scrutinizing the window displays to convince onlookers he intends to buy a diamond bracelet, he enters with a feigned careless swagger, attempting to project the aura of the swell mob. Inside, he whispers so softly he is inaudible, repeating himself frantically. When his rambling tale of a "friend" inevitably reaches the word "lend," he is swiftly directed to the court on the right, up the stairs, to the first door around the corner. He exits with a face bright enough to ignite a cigarette, convinced the entire district watches his every move. Upon finally arriving at the correct location, he has forgotten his name and address, reduced to a state of hopeless confusion. When asked sternly how he acquired the item, he stammers and contradicts himself, risking a confession of theft on that very day. He is then informed that they have no dealings with his type and urged to leave immediately. He departs, retaining no memory of the event until he finds himself three miles away, utterly unaware of his journey.

By the way, relying on public houses and churches for timekeeping presents its own dilemmas. Public houses are generally too fast, churches too slow. Furthermore, peering through the swing doors of a pub draws contemptuous glares from the barmaid, categorizing one instantly with area sneak-thieves and cadgers, while agitating the married patrons. The clock is hidden behind the door, and attempts to withdraw quietly often result in jamming one's head. Jumping up and down outside the window is another option, though unless accompanied by a banjo and a song, the gathering youths will be disappointed. There exists a mysterious law of nature ensuring that before one has left a watch "to be repaired" for half an hour, someone stops in the street to ask the time. Curiosity vanishes the moment the watch is on one's wrist.
