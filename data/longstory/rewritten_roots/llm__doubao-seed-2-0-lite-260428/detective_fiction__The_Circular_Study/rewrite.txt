Ebenezer Gryce was in the doldrums. He’d hit that quiet stretch of old age where his usual sharp investigative spark fizzled out unless a truly unusual case prodded it awake, and lately the precinct’s dockets had been nothing but petty thefts, routine domestic disputes, and run-of-the-mill assaults that solved themselves before he even laced up his boots. He’d even started seriously weighing handing in his badge, ready to retire to the little Westchester farm he’d picked up a few years prior—though the thought of endless quiet days of weeding and mending fences made his skin crawl; a man built for mental puzzles could not survive on fresh air alone, no matter how pretty the sunset over the pasture.

He never got to test that quiet life that day. Mid-afternoon, when the gray fall light was already leaking out of the precinct windows, a telephone call came through from Carter’s, the well-known neighborhood drugstore, that jolted every last one of his creaky old joints back into sharp, familiar alertness. The message was odd: a strange lady had flagged down a random street kid outside, paid him two shiny coins to run in and tell the police that a *strange* crime had been committed in the old century-old mansion around the corner. The boy didn’t know her name, couldn’t even describe her face clearly, but he was white as a sheet, and the drugstore clerk swore his story wasn’t a prank. They were holding the kid there until an officer came to collect him.

*Strange*. That one word lodged in Gryce’s chest, and suddenly he forgot every ache, every half-formed retirement plan. The whole precinct knew the old house—one of the last surviving 1800s aristocratic landmarks left wedged between busy shops, bustling restaurants, and glass-fronted clubs that had crept up on either side of it over the decades. It was a local oddity, famous even to people who had no idea who its original owners had been. Now there was a *strange* crime inside its walls? All he had to do was catch the inspector’s eye to get full authorization to lead the investigation, and he was out the door before the inspector could even finish signing the order.

First stop: Carter’s drugstore. He collected the shivering street kid, walked him the two blocks to the mansion, and pumped him for details the whole way. The boy had nothing more useful to add. He’d been lingering by a toy shop window when the lady had grabbed his arm. She was “a total stunner,” he babbled, dressed in shiny, fancy clothes dripping with jewels, and he’d been too overawed to look at her face long enough to make out any distinct features. She’d pressed the first coin in his palm, promised the second as soon as he delivered her message to the drugstore telephone. The second he’d heard she was telling him to call the police about a crime, he’d tried to bolt, but she’d shaken him hard enough to rattle his teeth until he agreed to run the errand. Gryce sighed, recognized the limits of a street kid’s powers of observation, and turned his full attention to the house looming just ahead.

It looked completely, unnervingly normal. A polished oak door, scrubbed marble stoop, heavy lace curtains drawn tight over every window, with thick shades pulled down over half of them. The brick facade with its brownstone trim stood still and quiet, a calm, respectable island surrounded by the noise and bustle of the storefronts pressing in on either side. For half a second, Gryce was convinced he’d been sent on a wild goose chase.

Then a uniformed officer stepped out of the sunken area under the stoop, his face crumpled with confusion. “Rang every bell three times,” he told Gryce, touching his cap. “Place seems empty, but I swear I saw movement behind an upstairs window a minute ago. Was gonna ask if I could cut through the back storage of Knapp & Co.’s store next door to break in through the back yard.”

“Go,” Gryce nodded, passing the kid over to him. “Stash the boy in one of their empty offices first, then get in through the back. Easy enough, no need to make a loud scene.” The officer vanished with the kid, and Gryce leaned against the stoop for five minutes, fighting off the growing crowd of curious passersby that had already caught wind of trouble, until the front door clicked open silently from the inside. He slipped in before anyone else could push past him, and the door snicked shut behind him, swallowing all the street noise.

The basement hall was spotless, the kitchen at its end so neat and well-kept it looked like no one ever did any messy housework there. No blood, no overturned furniture, no sign of anything amiss. He followed the officer up the main stairs, who filled him in as they reached the parlor floor: the neighbors said the man who rented the mansion, one Mr. Adams, was a total hermit. Rich, bookish, impossibly handsome, and so violently allergic to other people that he’d lived there six months and no one had ever seen a single visitor cross his threshold. The only other person who ever came and went was a grim, silent old servant, just as much of a recluse as his master.

Gryce knew all that. The whole city had whispered about Adams when he moved in, the mysterious millionaire who’d bought the old aristocratic homestead to shut himself away from the world. The thought of stepping over that threshold, on a murder investigation no less, made his old detective blood hum.

The front hall was wide, old-fashioned, sparsely furnished with heavy 19th century antiques. Two doors stood open, and they stepped through the one closest to the stairs first—only to find a room as bare as a doctor’s waiting room, no decor, no personal effects, nothing that told them anything about Adams. They would have turned right back around and headed for the second door, if they hadn’t spotted a flash of rich, rare silk peeking out from behind a half-drawn velvet portiere strung across the far end of the room.

They stepped through the portiere, and both men froze, every bit of breath caught in their throats.

It was a circular room, curved walls hung with ancient faded tapestries, every empty shelf below the tapestries crammed with row after row of leather-bound books, little priceless curios tucked into niches between the volumes. A single great oil painting hung on one curved wall, but no one was looking at it. All the light in the room, a concentrated jet of deep rose-colored red, was focused dead in the center, on a man splayed across a thick polar bear rug. A dagger was driven straight through the center of his chest, its hilt glinting. A heavy solid gold cross lay flat over the dark, flowing robe wrapped around his body, its lines sharp against the black fabric, making him look almost like a saint laid out for holy burial. But his face—

It was not the rictus of surprise, not the grimace of searing pain, not the wide-eyed terror Gryce had seen on a hundred other murder victims. The anguish written across his features was something deeper, something quiet and unnameable, so alien to the usual shock of a sudden stabbing that the old detective, with decades of crime scenes under his belt, could not for the life of him put a name to the emotion reflected in that upturned, still face.

For one long, weighty beat—even seasoned Officer Gryce hesitated, awed. The dim stained-glass light, the hush thick enough to press against his eardrums, the curio-cluttered circular room straight out of some old romance, and the still figure stretched at its center like a corpse laid out for the tomb, the cross on his chest pressed directly over his fatal stab wound: all of it was enough to rattle even the veteran detective, who had solved dozens of brutal cases in his decades on the force, but never one that defied logic this thoroughly.

He recovered fast. Before the uniformed officer trailing behind him could even sputter out a word of bewilderment, Gryce stepped past him and crossed to the prostrate man. There was no question he was dead: his pulse was gone, his eyes stared open with such a look of quiet, unspoken reproach that Gryce had to dig deep for his professional sangfroid to meet that gaze. The body was still warm, rigor not yet set in, and though he did not dare pull the ornate dagger free of the wound, he studied it closely first. It was no common household blade, but a carved oriental curio you might pick up in a specialty import shop. At first glance that pointed straight to suicide—until he noted the angle of entry, deep and tilted sharply inward and upward, impossible for a man to inflict on his own chest.

There were almost no other clues, not that you would expect from a violent attack. The entire well-appointed room was free of scuff marks, knocked over furniture, or ripped fabric. The only thing out of place on the floor was a scattering of crumpled rose petals. The massive oak table dominating the center of the room, where the victim had clearly been standing when he was struck, was spotless, its surface crowded with pipes, fountain pens, miniatures, ancient Roman oil lamps, Venetian glass, rare porcelain, war medals, half-finished manuscripts, a scroll of sheet music, a small potted rose bush, and—oddest of all—a full row of small electric buttons set into its polished edge. Gryce brushed one by accident, and the overhead fretted iron lamp immediately shifted its glow from soft amber to a sickly, dripping green, painting every wall and every shadow in ghastly, unnatural tints. He fumbled wildly for another button, and sighed in relief when warm, neutral white light flooded the room again.

“Silly parlor tricks for a man his age and standing,” he ruminated, until he glanced back at the dead man’s sharp, hard-jawed face. There was nothing childish about that lined, purposeful countenance. The colored lights were no gimmick, he realized, they tied into the scientific research clearly stacked on the shelves of reference books and lab gear lining the walls.

He turned his gaze to the tapestry-draped walls next, and spotted it at once: a bent, wrenched-loose nail set in the fabric, the very nail the cross had hung on before someone tore it down to lay it on the dead man’s chest. The red silk suspension cord still tangled in the threads of the cross, knotted into the dark blood seeping from the victim’s chest wound. The empty nail set six full feet away from where the body lay. The victim could never have reached it after being stabbed. Some other hand had ripped that symbol of pardon from its wall, and laid it over his heart as he died—either a last act of mercy, or a sign of the killer’s rabid remorse.

Further along the wall hung the room’s only large portrait: a young woman, soft, sad-eyed, painted sometime right after the end of the Civil War. She was fair, the dead man dark, but they shared the exact same distinct, sharp facial structure that marked them as close kin, likely his daughter. Below the portrait were hung a dented cavalry saber, a set of Union officer’s epaulettes, and a Medal of Honor awarded for battlefield valor.

Then he spotted a narrow velvet curtain, so dark and perfectly matched to the adjacent tapestry pattern he had nearly overlooked it. It did not cover a window—all the room’s small windows were set high near the ceiling. It hid the door to a tiny side closet, converted into a sparse but immaculate bachelor’s bedchamber. The narrow cot was perfectly made, the dresser polished to a high shine, save for a silver-backed comb knocked off its surface lying on the far floor, and a pearl-handled woman’s parasol propped against the stand by the door. A woman had been here, very recently.

Gryce’s eye caught the glint of something small on the threshold between the bedroom and the main study: a tiny black sequin, the kind sewn onto formal gowns. He spotted a second halfway across the study, and a third right next to the dead man’s feet. He knew the pattern well: sequins are strung on a single thin thread stitched to the dress fabric. If that thread snags, they pop free one by one, leaving a glowing, dainty breadcrumb trail. Following the line of sequins back, he realized the gold filigree edge of the cross must have caught on the woman’s dress the moment she tore it off its wall hook, snagging the thread to leave that trail of glittering evidence right back to the crime.

He returned to the center table then, running his finger lightly over its polished surface near the heavy brass inkstand. He found a faint, smudged streak of fresh blood he had missed at first, then lifted the inkstand carefully to check the space beneath it, setting it back precisely where he had found it so no trace of his search was visible.

A quick, heavy footfall behind him made him turn. Officer Styles, who had been sent to search the rest of the house, was standing in the doorway, bouncing with barely contained excitement, pointing emphatically at the staircase visible through the antechamber. “Go up,” he mouthed.

Gryce hesitated, casting a glance back at the study he had barely begun to unpick. Styles grew urgent, breaking his hush to whisper: “Come on! I found the guilty party. He’s locked in a room upstairs.”

“He?” Gryce blinked, surprised by the pronoun.
“Yes, no doubt about it. Once you see him—wait, do you hear that? He’s coming down the stairs. There’s nobody else in the whole house. Hide, quick! He’ll walk right into the room and incriminate himself before he notices we’re here.”

Gryce dragged Styles behind the dark velvet curtain of the small side bedroom, letting the fabric fall almost shut behind them, leaving only a narrow crack to peek through. The footsteps grew louder, and they could hear the approaching man’s breath, coming in ragged, panicky gasps. Styles shook his head when Gryce whispered to ask if the suspect had seen him upstairs.

A second later, the main study portière pulled back, and a lean, pale valet stepped into the room, a silver tray balanced in his hands holding only a single glass of water. Styles looked as stunned as Gryce—he had expected a violent, obvious intruder, not the household’s quiet, mild-mannered servant. Before either could move, the valet caught sight of his employer’s body sprawled on the floor. The glass slipped out of his fingers, shattering loudly against the hardwood, and he let out a half-cry, half-animal howl.

Then the man froze, staring at the empty air in front of him. He stretched one hand far back behind his body, fingers crimped tight as if wrapped around the hilt of an invisible dagger, then lunged forward, driving his arm hard in a vicious upward thrust that looked so physically real the two watching men almost flinched. Slowly, his fist unclenched, as if he was letting go of the knife still buried in his imaginary victim. He staggered backward, eyes blown wide with unthinkable horror, staring at nothing as if he was watching the life drain out of the man he had just killed. Seconds later, he crumpled in a twitching, sobbing heap on the floor, his whole frame wracked by the aftermath of his own private breakdown.

“That’s exactly what I caught him doing, locked in his room upstairs,” Styles whispered, awed. “He’s gone completely mad from the shock of what he did.”

Gryce said nothing, his gaze fixed on the twitching figure on the floor. After forty years of solving cases, he had never run into a mystery quite this confounding, one that left every bit of his hard-won experience useless before it had barely even begun.

The man who had just reenacted the full horror of the recent study tragedy before their very eyes stumbled unsteadily to his feet, his expression softening into a blank, bewildered daze that bore no trace of the ferocious, contorted fury he’d worn only seconds before. He stooped to retrieve the glass he’d dropped, and was already halfway to the door with it in hand when Mr. Gryce called out to stop him.
“Hold on. You don’t need to take that glass anywhere. We’ve got questions first—how does your master end up lying there, dead on his own study floor?”
The demand would have jolted any sane man to attention. This one didn’t so much as flicker a brow, and kept moving for the doorway, until Styles clamped a firm hand to his shoulder to hold him back.
“Hey, you—what’s your hurry?” Styles barked. “Didn’t you hear the man talking to you?”
This time the appeal landed. The glass slipped clean out of the butler’s fingers, shattering against the hardwood floor with a sharp, tinkling crash that tangled with the odd half-moan, half-shriek that tore out of his throat. He’d finally registered the men surrounding him, and his haggard, sunken eyes and cringing hunched shoulders made plain he’d only just grasped the mortal danger of his position. He lunged for the exit a split second later, but Styles’ grip only tightened, dragging him unresisting back to the spot where Mr. Gryce stood beside the bearskin rug holding his master’s corpse.
The second the butler’s gaze landed on the still, recumbent figure, a transformation no one present could have predicted washed over him. Joy—raw, unholy, the kind of glee that curdles your blood when it shows up in a room thick with the stench of fresh death—lit up his wandering, bloodshot eyes and twisted his mouth into a grim rictus. He made no move to hide the sick satisfaction rolling off of him, letting out a low, thrumming laugh that echoed off the high ceiling, ringing through the silent room like something from a nightmare.
Mr. Gryce, a seasoned detective who thought he’d long ago grown immune to every flavour of human horror, felt a prickle of unnameable abhorrence crawl up his spine. He waited until the last faint echoes of that diabolical mirth died away in the shadowed rafters above, then leaned in, locking his own rarely met gaze on the glittering, shifting eyes of the strange creature before him.
“Enough of that. Speak. Who killed this man? You were the only one in the house with him. You must know.”
The butler’s lips parted. Not a single intelligible word came out—only a stream of thick, guttural, meaningless noises. He tapped violently at his ears, then his lips, shaking his head so hard his scraggly grey hair flew around his face. There was only one possible conclusion. The man was deaf and dumb.
Styles’ jaw dropped. His hand fell slack from the butler’s arm, and the man slipped away unharmed, retreating to his usual spot in the corner of the room. There, he proceeded to reenact the whole scene again, with even more wild, jerky vigour—first the blows of the murder, then the hunching over to mourn, the same routine that had sent chills down their spines minutes earlier. When he finished, he stood still, waiting, that same foul, gloating glint burning in his restless eyes, making his very presence feel like a sacrilege inside that room of death.
Styles couldn’t take it. “Can’t you hear me? Can’t you talk?” he yelled at the top of his lungs.
The butler only smiled, a slow, evil, gloating curve of the lips. Mr. Gryce cut it short.
“Take him away. Search every inch of him for blood traces. I’m heading up to the attic quarters you first found him in. He’s tied too close to this murder not to be carrying some small trace of the act on him somewhere.”
But for once, the veteran detective hit a dead end. The search turned up no stains, no fibres, no stray marks of any kind on the old servant’s clothes or skin. The rooms upstairs were equally clean, no clues linking the only remaining resident of the house directly to the brutal killing.
Gryce fell uncharacteristically quiet, turning back to comb every inch of the two connected rooms that had to hold every last clue to this bizarre, baffling crime. The new search yielded almost nothing. He was leaning over the body, staring at the victim’s upturned face—its fixed, tight mouth and that haunting expression of mingled agony and iron resolve—when a sharp, clear cry cut through the silence from the shadowed rafters above his head, splitting into two distinct words that echoed sharp as a knife:
“Remember Evelyn!”
Remember Evelyn? Who on earth was Evelyn? And whose voice was that, when they’d already torn the whole house apart searching for other hidden occupants and found no one? It sounded like it came right from the roof, and when Gryce tilted his head back to look, he spotted it: an English starling, bobbing in a cage strung almost all the way to the top of one of the tall study windows. The bird craned its neck the second it caught Gryce staring, and squawked the line again, louder, sharper, more insistent than before: “Remember Evelyn!”
It was the final uncanny touch in a long string of surreal, nightmare-inducing discoveries. Gryce leaned forward, bracing a hand on the study table to get a better look at the bird, and his elbow brushed a switch he hadn’t noticed. The bright white electric light that had filled the room until then flooded abruptly, without warning, a sick, vivid shade of green. He’d tripped the wrong button, activating another slide in the odd custom lamp built into the ceiling above.
Annoyed—he didn’t have time right now to untangle the puzzle of the strange colour-shifting lights—he stepped away from the table and made for the door, when Styles’ voice carried from the adjacent antechamber, where he’d been posted to guard the mute butler.
“Mr. Gryce? Should I let him move? He’s fidgeting something awful. Not dangerous, far as I can tell—just restless, like he forgot some chore he’s supposed to do, only just remembered it.”
“Let him go,” Gryce called back immediately. “But don’t take your eyes off him. Follow every step he takes. Half the time, folks like him spill the best clues without even realising they’re doing it.”
He walked over to the doorway to watch for himself, and the starling squawked that uncanny line again: “Remember Evelyn!” Gryce’s gaze snapped back up, unbidden. Who was the bird talking to? Him? Or the dead man splayed on the floor, whose ears would never hear another sound? It could be nothing. It could be the very key that unlocked the whole murder. But there was no time to dig into it right then—the butler, who’d hurried out to the front hall the second Styles unlocked him, was walking back towards Gryce now, one hand holding his dead master’s hat, the other holding his folded umbrella.
Gryce paused, curious what this new, strange gesture meant. The mute’s face had smoothed back into the blank, respectful immobility of a perfectly trained household servant. He held out the hat and umbrella for Gryce to take, no fuss, no oddness, then retreated noiselessly back to his usual chair in the antechamber, and within seconds he’d sunk back into that same blank, dazed stupor he’d been in when they first arrived.
Gryce frowned, hanging the dead man’s hat and umbrella back on the hall rack. “Hmph. Mind half gone, memory all shot, can’t give a usable statement to save his life,” he muttered to himself. “Did the shock of walking in on his master’s murder break him? Or was he already half unhinged, and this whole tragedy started because of that?”
He might have chewed over the question for hours more, but the faint, impatient clanging of the front door bell—the crowd of curious onlookers stacked on the porch outside had been yanking it for ten minutes straight—was cut short by a heavy, sharp, authoritative knock. The coroner had arrived. His quiet, unobserved window to hunt down clues was over.

For the first time in years, veteran detective Mr. Gryce found himself out of his depth on a murder case.

The victim, the reclusive Mr. Adams, had lived a life of almost total isolation. His only household member was a deaf, mute handyman who could offer no meaningful testimony. His home sat in a stretch of the city far enough from other dwellings that no nosy neighbors lingered nearby, and for all the attention his death had drawn, no friends, associates, or acquaintances had turned up at the door with questions or information hours after the body was discovered. It was an odd detail, even for a man as famously peculiar as Adams, who had been a noticeable, if unfriendly, fixture of the neighborhood for months. The total absence of usual witnesses didn’t deflate Gryce’s interest, though – it amplified it.

So he breathed a quiet sigh of relief when, minutes before midnight, the crowd of coroner’s staff, medical examiners, reporters, and hangers-on tramped out the front door, leaving him free of interruptions to wrap up two small loose ends before he allowed himself to rest.

His first order of business was the small boy, Jake, dozing in a hall chair. Gryce nudged him awake. “C’mon, lad, time to move. I need you to show me the exact spot that lady grabbed you earlier today.”

Jake blinked blearily, fumbling for his cap. “Can I see my ma first? She’ll be half out of her mind worrying. I never stay out this late.”

“Your mother’s been notified for hours,” Gryce assured him. “She says you’re a good boy, never tell a lie.”

Jake’s face softened. “She’s the best. I’d turn out no better than my no-good pa if I didn’t treat her right.” He tugged his cap on. “I’m ready.”

The pair stepped out into the quiet late night streets, and soon arrived at the small shop window where Jake had been lingering earlier. The boy pointed eagerly at two stuffed frogs propped behind the glass, duelling with tiny toy swords.

Gryce watched the boy far more closely than he watched the frogs. “Was that lady pretty?”

Jake scratched his head, frowning. “She scared me a little. But her clothes – oh, they were gorgeous, nothing I’ve ever seen before.” He shook his head when Gryce asked if she was young, if she carried flowers, if she looked all trembling and upset. “She was troubled, sure, but not crying. She gripped my arm harder than she spoke, just dragged me straight to the drugstore. I only went ‘cause she handed me a coin first – I don’t get much pocket money, so I wasn’t about to run.”

Gryce nodded to himself as they walked toward the drugstore. “Not young… some old flame of Adams’s, then. Those are the dangerous ones, far sharper and more ruthless than any girl half their age.” He gestured to the curb. “Show me where she waited for you while you ran inside.”

Jake pointed to a spot right at the edge of the sidewalk. “When I came out, she was gone. I peeked out the shop door, right? My arm was still sore from her grabbing me, so I was keeping an eye on her. She stepped to the curb and held up her hand – but she wasn’t waiting for a streetcar. None came by for ages after she left.”

Gryce’s scowl softened a little. “She hailed a cab, then. You didn’t catch which direction it drove off?”

Jake shook his head. That was the end of what he’d seen. Gryce handed the boy off to his posted assistant, then turned back to the grim, ill-omened Adams house.

“Anyone waiting for me?” he asked Styles, the officer on guard duty, as he stepped through the door.

“A young man named Hines, sir. Electrician, like you asked for.”

“Perfect. Where is he?”

“In the front parlor. And no one else came in. No one’s upstairs except the covered-up body, like you ordered.”

“Head up there, keep an eye on the landing. I’ll hold the door,” Gryce said. Styles nodded and vanished up the stairs.

Hines, a sharp, neatly dressed young man, stood up when Gryce stepped into the parlor. After a few quick verification questions to confirm he was the right man for the job, Gryce led him back into Adams’s study, where the dead man’s body had been laid on an upper cot earlier that night. A single candle burned on a side table near the door.

“Blow that out for me, will you?” Gryce said, resting his hand on a line of small buttons set into the big central desk.

Hines blew the candle out. For half a second the room was totally dark – then a flood of deep, vivid violet light exploded across every wall, bright enough to make both men blink in surprise.

“First setting,” Gryce said, pressing a second button. The violet melted into soft pink. “Third button gives green. Fourth gives white. This last one,” he added, tapping the final button, “gives that awful yellow, which makes both of us look like we’re coming down with jaundice. Now – take a look at the wiring. Is it professional work?”

Hines knelt to trace the wires along the wall, shaking his head. “Amateur, all the way. Messy, but shockingly well-functioning, like the guy who rigged it spent ages tinkering.”

“Adams built it himself, then?” Gryce asked, staring at the shelf of basic electrical textbooks in the corner. Hines confirmed even a hobbyist with steady hands and a knack for mechanics could have built the whole rig from those books alone. Gryce’s face fell – he’d been hoping the wiring was a professional’s work, a clue that someone else had been in the house messing with the system before the murder.

“Adams clearly wasted half his time on useless mechanical toys,” Gryce grumbled, glancing up at a birdcage perched almost at the ceiling, well out of anyone’s reach without a ladder. “Look at that. There’s no way he dragged a ladder over every time he wanted to feed the thing. Find the hidden switch that lowers it, would you?”

Hines hunted for two minutes, then found a tiny button hidden in the wall tapestry. When he pressed it, a quiet mechanism whirred, and the birdcage glided slowly down to Gryce’s waiting hand. Inside was a plump English starling, a rare sight in the States.

“Leave it down,” Gryce said, setting the cage on the floor. “I don’t want to make the poor bird go back up there. Wait – he’s about to speak, isn’t he? The neighbors said he squawks ‘Remember Evelyn’ all day long.”

But the starling fluttered around his cage once, then tucked his beak under his wing, feigning sleep like he knew better than to make a sound in front of a detective.

Gryce huffed a quiet laugh, and on impulse pressed the one button on the desk he hadn’t tested yet, curious what new color the light would shift to. The ugly yellow glare stayed put – the wires must have jostled loose during Hines’s inspection. He shrugged, ready to head out, when he froze.

The study’s only door – the heavy wooden one leading out to the rest of the house – was sliding shut.

One second it was a yard wide. The next, a foot. Then an inch. Then it clicked solidly into place.

“Lazy man’s dream,” Hines laughed, before the smile died on his face. “Wait a second – that’s not wood. That’s steel. There’s no handle, no way to slide it back open from this side.”

Gryce’s blood ran cold. The man who built this wasn’t just playing with tricks for colored lights. The whole two-room study was lined with brick, every window bricked shut just below the roofline. There was no other exit.

They fumbled every inch of the walls and the desk for the opening button for five minutes, and found nothing. Gryce blew his loudest police whistle, banged the heavy silver alpenstock he carried against the solid steel plate as hard as he could, shouting for Styles upstairs. It took a full hour for the guard to hear the faint thuds through the reinforced wall. It was five more long, claustrophobic hours before a crew with crowbars and a cutting torch managed to saw a hole big enough for the two men to squeeze through.

In all his decades as a detective, Mr. Gryce had never been trapped before. He had not expected the dead man’s final, secret mechanical toy to turn his own crime scene into a locked prison.

The bizarre, unexplained details of his on-scene investigation at the Adams murder house had only sharpened Mr. Gryce’s already ironclad resolve to pry every last secret from the tangled, seemingly impenetrable case. When he finally trundled back to police headquarters, where his long, unaccounted-for absence had left his colleagues wondering what had detained him, his first order of business was a check on Bartow, the deaf-mute butler arrested at the scene.

The news was not good. The attending lunacy expert brought in to evaluate the butler’s catatonic state had confirmed no recovery was expected any time soon. Gryce’s expression turned grim, and he summoned the officer who had overseen Bartow’s arrest to ask after his behavior in custody.
“Surprised first, sir,” the officer reported bluntly. “Shook his head, made a bunch of weird gestures, then fell back into that same pantomime routine. The poor fool keeps holding his right hand out behind him, like this, over and over.”
Gryce noted the gesture — identical to the one Bartow had made the second he realized he was being watched back at the murder house. Its meaning was still opaque. The butler was not left-handed, so the repeated motion clearly was meant to draw attention to a fact that he believed would clear his name.
“His mood swings, sir,” the officer went on. “One minute he’s half-asleep, vacant as a wall. The next he flies into a rage, pounding the cell walls so hard his knuckles bleed. He’ll need 24/7 watching.”
“Leave him be, and log every single tiny shift in his behavior,” Gryce ordered. “His testimony may never hold up in court, but every twitch of his tells a story. I’ll go visit him myself tomorrow.”

Alone at his desk, Gryce sat for a long stretch in quiet thought. He pulled a crumpled little paper package from his coat pocket, tipped five tiny, glittering spangles out onto his blotter, and stared at them. The old detective had a habit of fixing his gaze on some trivial, small object while he puzzled over a case — it cleared the clutter in his head faster than any other trick he knew. After squinting at the sequins, turning them over one by one with the tip of his pencil, he swept them back into their envelope, checked his watch, and stepped out into the main office. He beckoned to a young rookie who’d been lingering by the door, watching his movements with obvious, hungry eagerness.
“You’ve been on the force six months, Sweetwater,” Gryce said, shutting his office door behind them. “You haven’t gotten a real chance to prove your mettle yet. I’ve got a nearly impossible task for you. Do you know this city well enough to dig a needle out of the biggest haystack on the East Coast?”
Sweetwater’s plain face lit up so bright he looked almost handsome. “I’ve been spoiling for a shot at something hard, sir. You won’t regret giving me this chance.”

Gryce laid out the bare bones of the search. The woman they were hunting was last seen wearing a dress heavily trimmed in black spangles, visiting Adams’s house right before or at the time of the murder, and carrying a brand new, never-used parasol made of gray chiffon, with a pearl handle and tiny pink ribbon bows — no maker’s mark, no name tag sewn inside. She had hailed a ride at the curb outside Hudson’s fruit store on 14th Street at exactly 4:30 PM, the same minute the anonymous warning phone call had come through to headquarters. “I don’t know where she came from, or where she went,” Gryce said. “That’s what you’re here to find out. My gut says she’s not one of the city’s known questionable characters — she’s a respectable woman who’s never had a run-in with the police before. Don’t let that gut feeling box you in, though. Hunches are more of a hindrance than a help half the time.” He paused, then added, quiet, “One more thing: I have a sneaking suspicion her first name is Evelyn. But that’s all it is — a hunch.”

Sweetwater didn’t even ask to see the parasol. He laughed and said he’d rather hear it described, so the other rookies wouldn’t rag on him if he struck out. He slipped out of the office bright with confidence, while Gryce was left staring at the stack of incoming reports, no less foggy on the case than he’d been 12 hours prior. By all rights, the front-page coverage of the millionaire recluse’s murder should have dragged dozens of his old acquaintances out of the woodwork. But the whole day ticked by, and not a single friend or distant relative stepped forward. His banker and lawyer came in to confirm his finances were immaculate and he had no living next of kin, no will, no personal business files stashed anywhere. His landlord recalled only that Adams had rented the years prior through a now-dead Philadelphia lawyer, had never invited anyone past his front parlor, and had paid to brick up half the windows in his study and install the massive steel plate bolted across the study’s doorway, for no reason anyone could name. No one at the local pension office or G.A.R. posts recognized the military insignia found hanging on Adams’s study wall. No New York artist claimed credit for the giant portrait that took up most of the wall space in the murder room. The tradesmen who’d supplied Adams’s household for years had only ever met Bartow, the deaf-mute butler — they all said he’d grown jittery, snappish, and tight with a barely contained temper in the weeks leading up to the murder, as if he were holding a furious grievance back by main force.

Gryce was fully ready to write the entire day off as a total loss, when Sweetwater blew through the headquarters doors, cheeks flushed, barely able to contain himself.
“Sir, I don’t need more leads — I just need to borrow that gray chiffon parasol for an hour. I’ve got a test to run in a respectable uptown neighborhood, it’ll only take a minute.”
Gryce handed it over without a single question. The hour he waited for Sweetwater to return dragged on forever, until the rookie finally burst back in, nearly tripping over the threshold.
“My test didn’t work,” he panted, “but the parasol still got me lucky. I found her, sir. The woman we’re looking for.”

He’d skipped the wild goose chase of canvassing every hack driver in the city, he explained. He’d remembered the spangles: that specific black net spangle trim was brand new on the market, wildly expensive, sold only at a handful of high-end fabric and dress shops on Broadway, 23rd Street, and Sixth Avenue, sewn in elaborate scroll patterns. Any woman who lost five of the spangles would notice the obvious gap in the design, and would immediately run out to buy the tiniest possible sliver of the exact same net to repair the dress. He’d tipped off the clerks at every one of those fancy stores to flag any customer buying a tiny cut of that specific spangled net, hung around Arnold’s emporium for an hour, and watched a rosy young maid walk out with exactly that tiny package. He tailed her to a stately brownstone in Gramercy Park, one of the most exclusive residential blocks in the city. He’d run back to grab the parasol, rang the bell a second time, and the maid had tried to shut the door on him, saying her mistress only carried black parasols — before a sharp, commanding voice from the top of the stairs called out to hold the parasol up. That single soft, shocked “ah!” from the landing told him he’d found his mark. She knew exactly who he worked for: Mr. Gryce at Police Headquarters. Ten minutes later, she was dressed for the street, stepping into a cab to come down to headquarters of her own free will. She was sitting in the outer office right now.

Gryce clapped the excited rookie on the shoulder, his own curiosity too ravenous to hold back. He stepped out to greet their mysterious guest — then froze. The woman waiting for him on the bench, cool, sharp, and perfectly composed, was no stranger. She was none other than Miss Amelia Butterworth, the most well-respected amateur sleuth in Manhattan, someone he’d collaborated with on multiple tricky closed cases for years.

The look Amelia Butterworth fixed on Mr. Gryce as he stepped through his office door was one no stranger could have begun to parse. Even the seasoned detective found himself at a loss—he had never before seen the amiable, ever self-complacent spinster flustered so thoroughly.

“Miss Butterworth! What is the meaning of this? Have you—”
“There!” she cut in, sharp and flustered. “You’ve caught me red-handed at my old meddling tricks, I’m properly ashamed, and I suppose you’re quite triumphant. That gray parasol you sent over to my house? It’s not mine. But you were right to guess I was in that circular study before you arrived, and I see no point evading your famous perspicacity any longer. I’ve come to confess.”

Gryce sat up straight, all casual interest sharpening to full focus. He pulled a chair close to her side. “You were there?” he echoed. “When? I won’t even dare to guess your purpose.”
“And I shall not hide it, not if I want to save myself from looking a greater fool than I already do,” she said, grim and determined. “I never meant to dip into another murder case, you know. I’ve shown I have a knack for this kind of work, earned your praise once or twice, but I swore after the Lost Man’s Lane fiasco that I would stick strictly to my own quiet life from then on. I thought I had the willpower to turn down any temptation you might dangle to pull me back into police business. But the temptation never came from you, or I might have resisted. It snuck up on me all unawares. I was passing that old, famous Adams house on my way to the art exhibition yesterday, and something impossible to ignore played out right in front of my eyes.”

She went on to describe the moment the vestibule door burst open. A tall, dapper young man in a crisp check suit stumbled out, half-carrying a blonde young woman who hung limp in a dead faint. His face, she said, was the most vivid mask of warring mortal passion and desperate, fraying restraint she had ever seen, even on a theater stage. Before any passersby could stop and stare, he had set her on her feet, dragged her down the stoop and vanished into the crowd. The front door swung open behind them, left hanging unlatched, and Miss Butterworth heard a muffled, distinct cry of terror from deep inside the old house.
“Curiosity wasn’t what drove me in,” she insisted, cheeks pink. “At least not the trivial kind. I could not shake the sight of that man’s face, the look on that poor girl’s, and before I knew it I was up the steps, inside, shutting the door behind me to keep random strangers from piling in. I followed the cry to the circular study, and there he was—Mr. Adams, stretched on the floor, a dagger in his chest, that heavy metal cross laid across his breast. His right hand was still trembling when I knelt to lift his head. He shuddered once, and drew his last breath right there in front of me. No one else but me saw that last moment.”

Gryce nodded, faintly amused. “I tracked you through five tiny black sequins dropped from your spangled gown, and the faint smudge of dust left by the inkstand you moved on the table. For hours I was convinced the unknown woman who’d left those traces was the killer, not the deaf-mute butler everyone was immediately ready to pin the whole crime on. I’ve been searching high and low for you to apologize for that absurd assumption.”

Miss Butterworth waved off the apology, still red-faced at the thought of the five sequins giving her away. She recounted how she’d heard the parrot shrieking from its cage above the corpse, squawking “Lovely Eva, maddening Eva! I love Eva! Eva! Eva!” — not Evelyn, she swore, no matter what Gryce had thought he heard. She remembered the single panicked line the blonde girl had mumbled as her companion dragged her into the street: You left that line of writing behind! It was for that half-sentence scrap of paper she had hunted the study, moving the inkstand carefully, searching every nook she dared touch. She’d found nothing.
“When no one answered my shouts to call for a doctor, I crept upstairs to try and find help,” she went on. “That’s where I saw the deaf-mute butler going through that wild, wordless pantomime all the papers wrote about later. I was so shaken I just ran, I sent a street boy off to the drugstore to call the police anonymously, and I thought I’d gotten away clean—until you turned up with that parasol.”

Then she laid out the puzzle no one else had spotted. She’d heard the cry that drew her to the study coming from that exact room. But when she went looking for help, the butler was already on the second floor. There were no back stairs. No possible way he could have climbed two flights faster than she could walk down the hall from the study door. “And that’s not the only loose end,” she added, leaning forward, eyes sharp with the old detective spark Gryce had not seen from her in years. “If that butler was standing in the study doorway and saw the murder happen, why didn’t that young man and his girl spot him? They were right there, seconds after the blow. No one could stand in that line of sight and go unseen.”

When Gryce admitted his men had turned the entire study upside down and never found that fabled scrap of writing the two young people had left behind, Miss Butterworth’s face settled into a quiet, satisfied, determined look. Gryce noticed her gaze fixed on his, her old customary intensity burning bright again. “I stayed up all last night turning this case over in my head,” she said softly. “I think I know what happened at the very moment Adams died. Something no one else has thought to look for.”

She leaned in, and whispered a single short sentence right in his ear.
Gryce jumped back, staring at her, his face going from shock to unmasked, unreserved admiration in less than a heartbeat. He shot to his feet, bowing so low his forehead nearly brushed his lapel.
“Madam!” he exclaimed. “That idea is brilliant, worthy of the sharpest amateur detective I have ever had the honor to work with. We act on it this very instant. Wait here just a moment while I give my men their orders. I will not keep you long.”

He turned and left the room at a sharp, uncharacteristic hurry, the whole course of the Adams murder investigation reoriented entirely by the quiet spinster who had stumbled into the scene of the crime no one else could crack.

Miss Butterworth had been raised on a code of manners so rigid that fidgets struck her as a moral failing as much as a social one. When she sat, her spine stayed straight; when she moved, it was with quick, purposeful steps that never stirred a curtain or jostled a candlestick. She had spent fifty years scolding her flightier younger cousins for their petty nervous tics, so it was a shock to find herself, left alone after their long interview, barely able to resist the urge to pluck at her glove seam or tap her foot in tiny, restless rhythms. She was steeped in unease, and when Mr. Gryce finally stepped back into the room, smiling almost cheerily, it was an unmistakeable relief.

“That’s all sorted out,” he said. “All we have to do now is wait for results. Madam, have you any other thoughts to share? I’d be glad of the benefit of them.”

Her self-possession snapped right back into place. “Would you?” she said, eyeing him a touch doubtfully. “I’d rather be sure the theory I already gave you holds water before I venture another. Let’s compare notes instead—let’s start by saying why neither of us thinks Bartow the mute is the killer.”

“I thought we’d exhausted that topic,” Gryce said. “Your suspicions were roused by the young couple you saw fleeing the house, and mine? Well, to you I can admit it: something in the mute’s pantomime makes it clear he’s rehearsing a scene he witnessed, not one he committed. I’ve seen no reason yet to doubt that impression.”

“I felt the exact same way,” she agreed, “even before my suspicions turned elsewhere. Besides, a man is far more likely to be driven mad by shock at another man’s crime than guilt over his own—if he loved the victim.”

“Wait, but Bartow doesn’t mourn Mr. Adams,” Gryce pointed out. “And look at that infernal spark of joy that lights his face at the end of his whole reenactment. It’s as if he’s rejoicing over the deed, or drinking in some enormous satisfaction from it.”

“Maybe he’s copying what he saw the murderer feel,” Miss Butterworth countered, then shook her head. “No. There was no joy on the face of that young man I saw rushing away from the violence. Quite the opposite. Mr. Gryce, we’re in deep water here. I feel half-submerged already.”

“Hold your head up,” Gryce chided, half-teasing. “Every flood has an ebb. If you go under, what will become of me?”

“You’re being humorous,” she said, brightening. “That’s a good sign. You’re never playful when you’re stumped. You must have spotted a crack of daylight somewhere.”

They turned back to dissect Bartow’s gestures line by line, until Miss Butterworth brought up a point that made Gryce laugh outright, caught unawares: the mirror effect. If Bartow stood face to face with the killer, his reenactment would be a perfect mirrored copy. The doctors had confirmed Mr. Adams was killed with a left-handed blow, so if Bartow mimicked that movement with his right, it meant the killer was actually right-handed—and had been positioned *behind* Bartow, not across from him. It was a small, critical flaw in their whole working theory, and Miss Butterworth grew bolder, suggesting they return to the sealed study after the funeral to observe Bartow at his ease, to map the exact shape of the murder scene properly.

It was Gryce’s turn to look thoughtful. “That can be arranged,” he said. “Once the funeral’s over and the swarm of officials clears out. By the way—are you worried the coroner will summon you to testify?”

The question made her fidget, even with all her poise. She admitted she had been, and that there was a whole piece of her adventure in the dead man’s house she had not yet told him. She hadn’t only seen the young couple. A third person had arrived at the door while she was still there: an elderly gentleman, well-dressed, hale, looking almost gay with expectation as he reached for the doorbell, there for a pre-arranged appointment with Mr. Adams in the study.

She had not turned him away. She had ushered him down the hall, to spare him the shock of stumbling on the scene alone. And that shock had been far stranger than anything she could have predicted: he had not just been horrified to find Adams dead on the floor under the cross. His expression shifted, fast and unreadable: from surprise, to curiosity, to blazing fleeting hate, to terror, ending in a wild, uncanny near-laugh that would have broken out loud if the parrot in its high cage had not woken up at that exact second, shrilling the words she would never forget: “Remember Evelyn!”

The squall made the old man jump back, fist raised like he was fending off an attacker. His eyes landed on the portrait hanging over the antechamber, and he cringed so hard it was painful to watch. He stared back at Adams’s body on the floor, muttered a line that sent a chill down her spine: *“It’s Amos’s son, not Amos! Is it fatality, or did he plan this meeting, thinking—”* That was when he spotted her lurking in the antechamber. He smoothed his demeanor back to polished ease, bowed, and fled without a word, letting out a raucous, uncontrolled cackle on his way out the front door. By the time the latch clicked shut, he was wearing the same easy, self-satisfied smile he had arrived with.

“Remarkable,” Gryce breathed, leaning forward. “There’s a whole secret history buried there. ‘Amos’s son’—Adams’s first name was Felix. Felix Amos’s son. That’s an uncommon name. We can run it in the papers, it’s bound to turn up a lead. You certainly got more out of that house than any of the officers did, Miss Butterworth.”

By then the hour was late, and she rose to take her leave. But Gryce stopped her before she could reach the door, one last question on his mind: had she worn her jet bonnet with the long feather boa the day before, when she was in the study?

Confused, she said yes, the boa had been draped around her neck that afternoon.

“Then the faint streaks of blood we found smudged on the edge of the study table are yours,” Gryce said. “If the tips of that boa are stained even faintly, we’ll know you leaned over the body close enough for it to brush the wood. If they’re clean? Then we know that young lady in the dove-colored silk was the one who stood there, not the figure we saw carried out unconscious later.”

Miss Butterworth flushed, half-amused, half-mortified. “So my little secret search for clues left my own trace behind. I’ve proven the fallibility of the inquisitive woman, haven’t I?” She took her leave then, with a show of humility that was not entirely sincere.

Mr. Gryce made no move to stop her. He had extracted every scrap of useful information she held, and his conscience—well, Mr. Gryce’s detective conscience had grown nicely calloused in certain spots, after years of solving murders. He felt no qualms at all.

The next morning, two small notes crossed paths the moment they were delivered: Mr. Gryce received a missive from Miss Butterworth even as hers was handed off by his own messenger.

Her note was crisp, no frills: *You were quite correct. As far as any evidence shows, I was the only person to lean over Mr. Adams’s study table after his unfortunate death. I’ve had to clip the frayed ends off my feather boa.*

His reply was even shorter: *My compliments, madam. Mr. Adams’s jaws were forced apart yesterday. A scrap of paper was found clinched tight between his teeth. We recovered it intact, and it will be read aloud at the inquest. A handful of trusted people will get to read it beforehand—you, notably.*

Naturally, the second note sent a far bigger jolt through Miss Butterworth than the first. She carried herself with a quiet, unshakable complacency all day, a secret no one else in the household could pry out of her, sparking no end of wild speculation about what she could possibly know of the murder.

Over at Police Headquarters, the staff was run ragged sorting through a flood of amateur informers, the kind that always pours out after a sensational crime hits the papers. The useless, conflicting stories that hadn’t trickled in the first day came pouring in nonstop the second: dozens of people swore they’d seen a stranger slip out of Adams’s house the afternoon he died, but none of them could describe the stranger beyond the most useless generalities. A garrulous old woman rambled about a genteel lady opening the front door for an elderly man in a thick greatcoat. A breathless fashionable young woman insisted a pale young man with a deathly pale lady on his arm had run straight into her outside the house at the exact estimated time of the murder—she couldn’t pick the man out of a lineup, couldn’t say if the woman was blonde or brunette, only that she had a beautiful gray feather stuck in her hat. None of these tales added a single new detail to what the police already knew. By nightfall, they were no closer to untangling the mystery than they had been the day before.

Adams’s funeral was held the next day. No relatives or close friends had stepped forward to claim the body, so his landlord arranged the rites, and his banker acted as chief mourner. Half a dozen detectives mingled in the thick crowd blocking the street as the casket was carried out, and followed the procession all the way to the cemetery—but their surveillance turned up nothing. There were no suspects lingering, no secret mourners slipping flowers on the grave, no outbursts of grief or panic that might tip them off. The case would have to be solved using the clues they already had.

That evening, Mr. Gryce excused himself from Headquarters, slipped out alone on foot, and tramped straight back to the dark, shuttered house where the murder had happened. He let himself in with a key, lit every gas lamp in the building, and surveyed the scene: the cross had been rehung on the study wall, the birdcage strung back up under the ceiling, almost everything back to its usual order—save the broken, gaping casings on either side of the study doorway, the steel plate still shoved back into its slot, the faint glint of its blue surface peeking through the hole in the antechamber wall. Gryce, neat as any housewife, dragged two heavy rugs off the parlor floor and strung them over the damaged openings to hide the unsightly mess. He checked his watch: it was nearly nine. He took his post behind the parlor window curtains, and sure enough, a carriage pulled up seconds later, exactly on schedule. He hurried to the front door to greet his guest.

It was Miss Butterworth.
“Your punctuality matches my own, madam,” he said. “Did you send your coachman off?”
“Only to the end of the block,” she replied, stepping inside behind him. “He’ll wait there until you whistle for him.”
“Perfect. Will you be all alone for five minutes? I need to watch from the window for the other guest we’re expecting. There’s no one else in the house right now.”
“If I were prone to fear in matters of justice, I never would have turned up at all. Besides, the whole place is brightly lit. I see you chose a crimson bulb for the study.”
“Because a crimson light was burning the exact second Adams died.”
A high, sharp voice cut through the quiet: “Remember Evelyn!”
“Oh! You brought the parrot back,” Miss Butterworth exclaimed. “That’s not the cry it greeted me with before. It was squawking *Eva! Lovely Eva!* Do you think Eva and Evelyn are the same person?”
“Madam, we’ve got enough riddles on our hands without untangling that one right now. The other guest I’m expecting is Adams’s deaf-mute valet, Bartow. He’s got a key, and he’ll walk in as if this is still his normal shift, no one watching. We just need to observe what his old routines make him do.”
“Sir, you’ve no idea how relieved I am. I thought I wouldn’t get to be here for this test.”
“You more than earned your seat. But we can’t let him spot us. We can’t startle him, distract him, break the muscle memory that might lead us to the clues we’re missing. I’m tucking you behind the parlor portieres—this room was almost never opened while Adams was alive, so he’ll never think to check for anyone here.”
“Then we need to turn off the gas in here. If the light leaks out, he’ll notice it’s wrong.”
“Already on it. I’ll draw the curtains tight, leave two tiny peepholes: one for your eye, one for mine. Nothing fancy, but it works.”

Miss Butterworth huffed a little, settling into the dark space beside the old detective. She wasn’t driven by idle curiosity—she was there out of a stubborn sense of duty, and she would have blushed at the absurdity of her position if the front door hadn’t creaked open just then. A soft “Hush” from Gryce confirmed it: Bartow had arrived.

He set his hat down on a table near the study door, and moved as if he was about to yank back the familiar portiere he’d lifted twenty times a day for years. But he never touched it. Some instinct, some half-memory, made him stop dead. He turned away with that docile, blank look of his, picked his hat back up, and shuffled softly up the stairs.
“I’m going to follow him,” Gryce whispered. “This whistle will bring a backup in from the street in two seconds flat, if you get scared.”
“I’m not scared, I’d be ashamed—” Gryce was already gone before she could finish. He came back a minute later, frowning. “Looks like the experiment’s a bust. He’s in his room upstairs, getting ready for bed.” He led her into the study, and half-absently tapped a button on the tabletop. The whole room flooded with soft violet light, gilding the edges of Evelyn’s portrait and the rest of the rich furnishings.
Before either of them could speak, they heard soft, descending footsteps from upstairs. They barely had time to scramble back behind the parlor curtains before Bartow stepped into the hall, moving with that same busy, purposeful air he’d had before. He paused half a second on the study threshold, as if he could sense something was off, then stepped inside. He didn’t so much as glance at the rug laid over the spot where Adams’s body had been found. He went straight about his usual nightly routine: pulled a bottle of wine out of the cupboard, set a glass down on the table after wiping it spotless, fetched his master’s dressing gown and slippers and laid them within arm’s reach, then stepped into the bedroom. The two watchers crept close enough to see him bend down to pick up the comb Gryce had left out on the floor earlier. He shook his head, set it back on the dresser, then turned down Adams’s empty bed.
“Poor soul,” Miss Butterworth murmured, as they slipped back to their peephole. “He’s forgotten everything but his old routine. We’re not going to get a single clue from him.”

But then they both froze. Bartow was standing dead still in the middle of the study, staring at his master’s empty armchair with a wide, questioning look that said far more than words ever could. He turned, stared straight at the spot on the rug where he’d seen Adams lying dead, and all at once his blank docility fell away. He began reenacting every single movement of the murder, exactly as he had the night Adams died, the same sharp, stabbing motions. Then he went soft again, and crept quietly back up the stairs.

Gryce and Miss Butterworth stepped back out into the light, staring at each other, equally discouraged. Then her eyes lit up.
“Turn on the next light color,” she said quickly. “I’ve got an idea. It’ll bring him back down, I know it will.”
Gryce raised an eyebrow. “You think he can see through floors?” But he tapped the next button, and the violet glow was replaced by a rich, deep blue. Nothing happened at first.
“Madam, I respect your theories, but if you’re implying this deaf old man can see through solid walls, and follow light signals from the top floor that no one ever knew existed—”
“Hush!” she said, holding up a finger, triumphant.
They heard his slow, shuffling steps coming down the stairs. This time he moved feebly, drifted straight into the study, held out his hands over the table like he was expecting to pick up a stack of books he was supposed to reshelve. When he found nothing there, he glanced up at the bookshelves in confusion, shook his head, and sank into the nearest armchair, falling fast asleep.
“Can you make sense of that?” Miss Butterworth said, breathless. “I can. He came down expecting to find books to put back, the same chore he did every night when the blue light came on.”
“But how the hell did he know the blue light was on? There’s no vent, no window, no visible connection between the study and the top floor.”
Miss Butterworth hesitated, almost sheepish. She smiled, half-apologetic. “The last time I was here, when I was heading upstairs after running into Bartow, I saw a tiny thing you must have missed. Something that explains how he can see those lights from the landing.”
“I searched every room upstairs,” Gryce said, surprised. “Checked every inch of the halls. Nothing got past my eyes.”
“Maybe you were looking up, not down,” she said. “Look at the floor. You can tell where people walk most often by the wear marks. That little circular landing on the staircase, the round one? The wood around the edge of the carpet there is all scuffed and worn thin, like someone’s been shuffling back and forth over it for years.”
“On the round of the staircase?”
“Exactly.”

Gryce didn’t waste time arguing. He hurried upstairs, Miss Butterworth right on his heels, and stopped on that worn landing, staring down at the scuffed floorboards. His gaze drifted up to the line of pictures hanging along the stairwell wall, and he reached past her to yank one that hung lopsided off its nail. Behind it was a perfectly round, hidden hole, and a stream of bright blue light poured straight through it, right up from the study far below.
“No window,” he said, stunned. “But a loophole straight into the high back wall of the study. Miss Butterworth, your eyes are sharper than mine. I don’t know how I missed this.”
“It wasn’t hanging crooked before,” she said. “And I was in a hurry, still rattled from running into Bartow. I just noticed the scuffed floor on my way up.”
“I see now,” Gryce said. “Bartow didn’t see the murder from the antechamber that night. He was right here, on this landing, watching through the hole. He saw the whole thing from behind the killer’s shoulder. That’s why when he reenacted the stabbing, he used his left hand instead of his right—he was mirroring the killer’s movements, not facing them, so the man’s right hand strike looked like a left-hand strike to him.”
A sharp, angry squawk echoed up from below, and Miss Butterworth jumped. “That parrot! Its cage must be right under this hole.”
“Just off to the side, close enough to startle you. Let’s peek down at Bartow now, he’s still dozing.”
Miss Butterworth pressed her eye to the loophole, and let out a very decisive, surprised grunt. “He’s shaking his fist at the parrot,” she said. “He looks furious, like he wants to kill the thing right now. No wonder Adams hung the cage so high up. All his weird little rules had a reason behind them, didn’t they?”
“Looks that way,” Gryce said, smiling. “And don’t worry about going back downstairs. Bartow’s old and feeble. You’re perfectly safe with me.”

The hush of the old brownstone settled thick around them the moment Bartow’s footsteps vanished out the front door. At the foot of the stairs, Mr. Gryce had called in two waiting plainclothes men to escort the shaken valet away after their little staged experiment, before he guided Miss Butterworth back across the threshold of the dead man’s strange circular study. The faint, lingering scent of jasmine from Adams’s lapel boutonniere still hung in the air, as if the murdered man’s ghost lingered still.

“One critical truth our test just settled,” Gryce said, his voice low. “Bartow is completely cleared of any part in this crime.”

“Then we can turn all our focus to the two young people who fled the scene. You’ve had no word of them yet, I suppose?”
“None.”
“Nor of that odd old man who laughed as he let himself in that night?”
“Not a whisper.”

Miss Butterworth’s face fell. “I’d hoped that scrap of paper you pried from between his teeth would give us their names. If a dying man fought to swallow it to keep it from his killer, it has to hold the key.”
“Unfortunately, it doesn’t name his assailant. It’s nothing but a frantic scrawl, written in a shaking hand: ‘I return your daughter to you. She is here. Neither she nor you will ever see me again. Remember Evelyn!’ Signed, *Amos’s son*.”

“Amos’s son? That’s Mr. Adams himself.”
“Every handwriting sample we’ve found on his book flyleaves confirms it, yes.”
“How strange. How unaccountable. He held onto that scrap so fiercely even in his death throes he stuffed it between his teeth, the only hiding place he could be sure his killer would never think to search. And that girl you carried out—do you remember her first words when she woke? ‘You left that line of writing behind.’ Those few odd, unexplainable words hold the whole case wrapped up in them. Could you repeat the note for me, one line at a time?”

“Gladly. I’ve run it through my head a hundred times. First: *I return your daughter to you*.”
“So Adams was holding some man’s daughter, and meant to give her back. Not the killer’s daughter, obviously, that would make her a small child. And if he wrote the note for his assailant, why go to such absurd lengths to hide it from him?”
“Exactly the thought I’ve turned over myself.”
“Then if it wasn’t for the young man who stabbed him, who was it for? That old gentleman who showed up after the attack?”
“It’s very possible. Ever since we heard of him, that theory has gained ground, especially paired with the next line: *She is here*. The only woman in this house minutes before that old man’s arrival was the fair-haired girl you saw carried out unconscious.”
“True—but what makes you so sure the note wasn’t years old, some scrap referencing an old secret he kept on hand?”
“It was written that very afternoon. The paper was torn straight from the top sheet of the writing pad on his desk, and the pen he used—you must have spotted it, teetering on the far edge of the table, nowhere near its usual rest by the inkwell.”

“Of course I saw it, I just never guessed how important it was. So the note is tied directly to the girl we found. And she can’t be Evelyn, or he wouldn’t have scrawled that refrain, ‘Remember Evelyn’ at the end. Wait—could that girl be the woman in that big melancholy portrait hanging on the far wall?”
“We can call her Evelyn for the time being. That face, heavy with unspoken grief, certainly hints at a terrible old family secret. But where does the violent young killer fit into this? He’s not mentioned anywhere in these rushed, scribbled lines. It’s all one thick mystery, madam, and we’re wasting time chasing loose ends right now.”

“I hate to give up before we’ve turned every stone.” Miss Butterworth leaned forward, eyes sharp. “You skipped a line of the note, earlier.”
“Right before the Remember Evelyn line: *Neither she nor you will ever see me again*. It’s a promise, a resolve.”
“Those words are more significant than you think, Mr. Gryce. Could he have planned to take his own life? Might this whole thing be a suicide, not a murder?”

“Madam, you’re free to entertain the thought—but Bartow’s pantomime made it perfectly clear the wound wasn’t self-inflicted. It was a wild, enraged thrust from a man acting out of raw outrage. We can’t rule out that Adams had prepared for death, though. His desk is cleared of every last family paper, every document a man of his wealth usually keeps locked away. He expected to die soon, even if the blow came earlier, and from a hand he never expected. And—there’s a secret about this study I haven’t told you yet. A very strange one. I stumbled on it by accident a few nights back. This room can be sealed off completely from the rest of the house, turned into a tomb. Look at this steel plate set in the doorframe. A hidden mechanism slides it across the entire entrance. I got trapped behind it once, and had to drill these small holes along the edge just to breathe long enough to work the lock.”

“Goodness—those are the kind of adventures no one would sign up for voluntarily. This is no ordinary modern New York murder, is it? I half feel like we’re digging up some medieval tragedy, even with the cable cars clattering past three streets over and telegraph wires humming above the roofs.”
“Some men carry the Middle Ages right into Wall Street. Adams was one of them. Romance and mystery colored every single thing he did, even his death. That cross we found resting on his chest when we moved his body? That wasn’t his doing. Someone else placed it there—a woman, or a priest, standing over him the moment he died.”
“He was no mere dreamer, though,” Gryce added, tapping the steel plate. “He was a mechanical genius. All his weird little contraptions worked perfectly.”

“Odd combination. I half expect to feel his ghost lingering in the tapestries, pointing us toward the answer.”
“The only way to get that answer is the one you already pointed out. We have to track down that young couple who were in this room during his death struggle. If we can’t find them, this case will never be solved.”
“Which means we’re right back where we started,” Miss Butterworth said, rising from her chair. She’d run the last of her leads dry for the moment.

Gryce made no move to stop her. He looked just as eager to leave the oppressive circle of the study as she was. But before they stepped over the threshold, they both paused for one last sweeping look around the room. That was when Miss Butterworth’s finger lifted, slow and steady, pointing high up the far wall.
“Look. You can barely see the seam in the tapestry where the hidden passageway opens out, on this side of the wall. The other side of that opening is behind the painting hanging on the stair landing, isn’t it?”
“Right. Hang that painting back up, and the whole passage entrance vanishes completely, invisible to anyone not actively looking for it.”
“People never look anywhere but straight ahead, do they? That painting must have been drawn aside half a dozen times while we stood in this very room, and we never spotted a thing. High and low, right? Everything that sits dead at eye level jumps out at you, but you miss what’s above your line of sight, or below your feet, if you’re not careful.”

Mr. Gryce smiled back at her as he moved to escort her to the front door, no trace of his usual dry irony in the expression. Only a quiet, faint sadness. For a split second, he wondered if the years were finally catching up to the sharpest detective on the force. A dozen years prior, he would have spotted that seam in the tapestry on his first walk through the room. He never would have missed what was hidden high above eye level.

Late that golden afternoon, a well-dressed woman of indeterminate middle age sat tucked in the far corner of the Belleville hotel piazza, ostensibly buried in a novel, but her sharp, kind, hazel eyes never lingered on the printed page long enough to make sense of the plot. She was clearly not reading: her gaze darted constantly over the blue, sail-dusted stretch of ocean, the beach crowded with young people in bright holiday finery, the tennis courts where couples wandered rackets in hand, and the hotel steps beyond. Children held no interest for her at all—this was no idle tourist spinster, no matter how convincingly the part was played, and the way she held her book inches too close to her face made that obvious to any observant onlooker.

No such criticism could land on the spruce, seemingly frail octogenarian camped at the opposite end of the piazza. He was so absorbed in his own open novel that small smiles tugged at his placid, benevolent face every few minutes, until he suddenly snapped the book shut, pulled out a full broadsheet newspaper, and spread it wide across his lap to read.

At that exact moment, the woman on the far end of the piazza pushed her spectacles up her nose to stare at two men stepping out of the hotel doors between them. She recognized them. She banged her book shut so hard the pages fluttered, half rose, then froze, her face flushing with awkward embarrassment. Instead of approaching them, she hurried out onto the lawn, bent down to fuss over a small stray terrier, and turned her back entirely on the new arrivals—her caress so stiff, obvious, and un natural that the dog squirmed as if it could feel how terrified she was of dogs in general.

The two men wandering the piazza never so much as glanced her way. They paced slowly to the end of the colonnade, then turned to retrace their steps, bringing their faces fully into the line of sight of the old man pretending to read his newspaper. He shifted the sheet a bare inch, as if to block the sun from his eyes, but in truth to get a clear look at them. They stopped three feet from his chair, talking low enough that their unassuming neighbor could not help but overhear.

The older of the two was straight, hale, and tough as old Scotch pine, his calm, cold glance sliding over the beach crowds without a flicker of emotion. His far younger companion, equally broad-shouldered, kept his gaze fixed on the ground, wincing away from every passing stranger like a man raw with unspoken misery.
“I don’t think this is doing me any good,” the young man said, his voice thick with gloom. “I’m wretched when I’m in the room with her, and twice as wretched when I leave to try to forget for a minute. I think we should go back. I’d rather sit where she can see me than have her wondering—oh, I’ll be careful, but you have no idea how nerve-wracking it is, holding my tongue about the secret that’s eating us all alive. I’m almost as sick as she is.”
They moved off before his companion could reply, and the old man behind his newspaper turned the page. Five minutes later they circled back, the older man doing all the talking now, his voice so low most of his words were lost.
“Escape… south coast… she’ll stop watching you long enough… clinging, impetuous, foolishly affectionate… no danger if we lay low… no one finds out… time, a quiet cheerful home, courage, all of this forgotten…”
The young man shook his head sharply, his gesture making plain he did not share those hopes.

Back on the lawn, Miss Butterworth—for the sharp-eyed spinster was no one else—still knelt over the squirming terrier, her full attention fixed on two giddy teen girls chattering on a nearby bench. She perked up the second she heard the name “Adams.”
“…I swear she’s sick enough to have a full-time nurse and a doctor,” the first girl gushed. “I passed her open hotel room door yesterday and caught a glimpse of her, and I’ve never seen eyes that big or cheeks that pale. And look at him, her husband! He dotes on her so hard he won’t speak to another woman, just coops himself up in that stuffy little suite with her all day. Do you think they’re newlyweds? They’re young enough, and have you seen her clothes—”
“Oh, don’t talk about clothes! The first day they got here I saw her in that pale blue walking suit and I felt like a total dowdy until she got sick and stopped wearing it.”
“Blue looks good on blondes. You’d look terrible in it anyway. And she’s not even a classic beauty, you know—bad nose, nothing special eyes, skin not even that clear. It must be her mouth, it’s lovely, or that floating way she used to walk before she fainted dead away out on the piazza. No one did anything to her, not even her husband. She tried to get his attention two or three times, he was staring so hard at something serious, and then she just toppled right out of her chair. That woke him up, and he hasn’t left her side since.”
“They don’t look happy for a couple that rich and handsome, do they?”
“Maybe he’s a drunk. I’ve noticed that old gentleman with him never leaves their side, like he’s keeping an eye on him.”
The rest of the girl’s gossip was cut off by a sharp, yelping bolt. Miss Butterworth had unconsciously pinched the poor terrier in her rising indignation at their careless, cruel speculation. The girls laughed and wandered off, leaving her alone—until a young man slipped past her, pausing half a second to whisper under his breath.
“You recognize them? No mistake?”
“None. The younger one is the man I saw leave Mr. Felix Adams’s house that night, and the older one is the man who went in after him.”
“Mr. Gryce says for you to go home. He’s moving to arrest the young man now.”
Sweetwater was gone before she could reply.

Miss Butterworth stumbled to a nearby bench and sat down, her knees weak. She could picture that sick young wife, Eva, pale and terrified, locked in that hotel room with no other woman nearby to comfort her. The older man, Poindexter, had that icy polish to him that repelled her, she knew he had no softness for a girl on the edge of a breakdown. A wild, urgent impulse seized her: she had to be near Eva the second the blow fell, if only to hold her, to keep her from collapsing entirely. But when she looked up, Gryce was gone from the piazza, and the two men had vanished around the corner toward the hotel’s tiny, secluded smoking lounge. She could not follow, so she headed upstairs instead, stopped a maid in the hall, and asked for Mrs. Adams’s room. The maid pointed her down the long corridor, where a sofa sat tucked under the window at the far end, right opposite Eva’s door. She sank down onto it, settling in to wait.
A soft, shaky voice drifted through the open transom above Eva’s door.
“Where is Tom? Oh where is Tom? Why did he leave me? I’m so afraid of what he might do or say out there on that piazza all alone.”
“Mr. Poindexter is with him,” a steady, gentle nurse’s voice replied. “Mr. Adams was getting so restless and tired, your father persuaded him to go down for a quick smoke.”
“I have to get up. I have to—oh, the camphor—”
There was a sudden clatter of glass, a gasp. Eva had fainted again. Miss Butterworth stared at the closed door, her chest tight with helpless misery.

Down in the smoking lounge, Tom Adams and Poindexter sat staring at the unlit cigars they had taken out of mere politeness to each other, neither smoking a single puff, when the door swung open and the seemingly feeble old man from the piazza stepped inside, shutting it firmly behind him.
“Gentlemen,” he said, his voice suddenly sharp, authoritative, no trace of the mild, forgetful old pensioner left, “I am Detective Gryce of the New York Metropolitan Police, and I am here—though I see at least one of you already knows why.”
Both of them knew. The truth crashed down on them so hard Tom crumpled in his chair like a puppet with its strings cut. Poindexter only stiffened, his icy mask cracking for half a second to show how utterly the arrest had destroyed every last one of their plans to run.
Gryce wasted no time on pretense, his voice softening a little at the sight of the young man’s raw, unmasked misery. “You are wanted in New York by Coroner D, to stand as a witness at the inquest into the sudden death of Mr. Felix Adams. Multiple witnesses saw you and your wife leave his house only minutes before he died. We need to confirm whether his death was suicide… or murder.”
Shame flushed dark across Tom’s face, chasing away the ashen terror that had made him look like a ghost minutes before. He could not speak, could barely breathe, and Poindexter was just about to step forward to protest when a voice called through the locked door, panicking: “Mr. Adams! Your wife has fainted again, she needs you right away.”
Tom jolted to his feet, threw a wild, desperate look at Poindexter, pressed one hand flat against the door as if to hold the bad news out, then sank to his knees at Gryce’s feet.
“She suspects! She already knows something’s wrong! She’s so sensitive, she’ll break,” he gasped, the words tearing out of him. “Don’t tell her. Don’t let anyone tell her. She’s an angel. My brother Felix was the man who died—let me take all the blame. I’ll go with you quietly, right now, just keep her in the dark for as long as you can.”
Gryce, who had built his career on quiet fairness, rested a hand on his arm. “I will not ask you for a statement right now. My warrant does not name your wife at all. If you promise to come peacefully, I will even let you step inside to say goodbye to her, as long as I stand right beside you.”
Tom’s head snapped up, a flicker of raw courage lighting his haggard face. He got slowly to his feet, stepped to Poindexter’s side, and fixed Gryce with a steady stare. “Tell me straight. What exactly am I accused of? I can’t leave here in peace until I know.”
Poindexter made a sharp warning movement, but Tom ignored him. Gryce pulled the official warrant out of his coat pocket.
“Lock it,” Tom said hoarsely, nodding at the door. “Don’t let anyone through that door, even if they come to tell me my wife is dead.”
Gryce turned the key in the lock. Tom tried to read the lines of the warrant through his bloodshot, tear-blurred eyes, while Poindexter leaned in, fixed Gryce with his cold stare, and mouthed three silent words: *For murder? Him?*
Gryce gave a short, solemn nod. Tom let out a long, ragged sigh that shook his whole frame.
“Thank you. Now I know where I stand.” His voice was steady now, no trace of the earlier breakdown. “I’m ready to go back to New York, to face whatever the courts have in store for me.” He turned on Poindexter, his tone sharp, commanding. “You will watch over Eva. Not like a distant, strict father—like a mother. Be at her side the second she wakes. Stay with her until she sleeps. No newspapers. No gossiping maids, no curious tourists. Don’t let her suffer more than she has to. Watch over her just like I would. When I come back—*I will* come back, won’t I?” he appealed to Gryce, “my whole life will be yours for that.”
A sob caught in his throat, and he turned away for a minute to get control of himself. When he faced Gryce again, he was calm. “I can’t go in to see her. I can’t bear to look at her unconscious face. Just get me out of here as fast as possible. I’ll tell the whole truth the second anyone asks me for my statement. Maybe then I’ll be brave enough to write to her. Take me away now. The whole world can know my shame tomorrow.”
Gryce tapped twice on the glass of the smoking room window that faced the piazza. Sweetwater stepped into view outside.
“This gentleman needs to rush back to New York on urgent family business,” Gryce called through the glass. “Get him to the station and onto the next express train, no fuss, no questions, no one noticing. I’ll bring his bags and follow right behind. Mr. Poindexter—you are free to go to your daughter now.”
He turned the key in the lock, and the quietest, most painful arrest of his long career was over before any of the vacationers laughing on the piazza had even noticed a single thing was amiss.

Mr. Gryce never balked at using a little gentle finesse when it served his case. He’d told everyone he intended to tail the man calling himself Mr. Adams, and tail him he did—so close on his heels that he caught the exact same train, slid unobtrusively into a seat in the very same car. He wanted the leisure to study the young man at length, for this prisoner had caught his interest in a way he never could have anticipated: something about him pricked at the old detective’s quiet conscience, and made him feel every one of his advancing years, even fresh off wrapping a long, brutal, intricate pursuit that should have left him feeling sharp and triumphant.

He sat a dozen feet away, watching, his curiosity growing with every flicker of expression that crossed the young man’s handsome face. The boy he’d seen less than an hour before, writhing in unmasked, searing agony, was now almost preternaturally calm. Gryce frowned, wondering what well of strength he’d drawn on to sit so stoically under the casual glances of other passengers, when not long ago he’d flinched from the very idea of public scrutiny like a man seared by fire. Was it despair that forged this courage? Or was he so far lost in his own private hell that he didn’t even notice the shadow of grief clinging to him? His brow was knotted in a permanent brood, his eyes glassy and unfocused on the scenery slipping past the window, his mind entirely withdrawn from the present. What dark, twisting depths of remorse was he drowning in? Not even Gryce, with a lifetime of brushing shoulders with criminals of every class and education, could say.

When the train pulled into the Brooklyn station, Gryce walked over to join his prisoner. The young man barely lifted his head to acknowledge him. An hour later, they were through the doors of Police Headquarters, and the formal, serious interrogation of the man the city knew as Mr. Adams began.

He did not fight it. He did not evade a single question. If anything, he seemed almost desperate to speak, to heave the heavy burden he’d carried for days off his chest. But that burden was nothing like what the police had expected. He did not confess to stabbing his brother to death. He confessed to being the unwitting, incidental cause of that violent end. This was the story he told them, in a low, unwavering voice that only broke twice:

“My real name is Cadwalader, not Adams. My father was a Scottish immigrant who naturalized as a Pennsylvania citizen, settling in a small town called Montgomery when he was newly married. He had two children back then; one died in infancy. The other is my brother Felix—the man who died under the name of Adams, the man you’ve brought me here to explain. I was born much later, from my father’s second marriage, years after he’d left Montgomery behind. We were living in Harrisburg when I was born, but we left before I turned three, so I don’t have a single memory of that city. All my earliest memories are across the ocean, in the Old World my father came from. My mother died when I was barely a toddler, so I was sent to Europe as a small child to be raised.

“I didn’t come back to the States until this very year. When I stepped off the boat, I found my father on his deathbed, and Felix fully grown, sitting on a mountain of family money that I’d been told he was ready to split evenly with me. But no sooner was my father buried than Felix left for New York, and I was left drifting, no solid prospects, no definite promise of any income to build a future or start a career on. While I was wandering, I stumbled back to that old town of Montgomery where my father had lived as a young man. I tracked down his old friends from back home, and in the house of a man who’d emigrated from Scotland with my father, I met a young woman” — his voice shook so hard he had to pause, the name he spoke thick with a pain so raw it made every man in the room go still — “Evelyn. I fell in love with her immediately, and I knew I had to marry her. But I had no money, no trade, no home I could offer her, no real prospects at all, and I knew it. Still, I couldn’t give up on her. So I wrote to my brother Felix Cadwalader—Felix Adams, as he chose to call himself later, for silly family reasons that have nothing to do with his death—and told him I’d fallen for a good, well-off young woman, and asked him to settle the sum of money on me that would let me marry her with some small shred of self-respect.

“All he wrote back was the same vague, empty promise he’d thrown at me a dozen times before. But young love is reckless, you know. I decided to marry her anyway, no more waiting, hoping that once he met her, with all her grace and goodness, he’d do the right thing and give me what he’d denied me for years. That was my greatest mistake. I married her without telling her or her father the truth about how little I had. They thought I was comfortable, maybe even wealthy. Mr. Poindexter is a well-off man himself, and he’s ambitious for his daughter, who deserves far better than a penniless drifter—she deserves a millionaire, and she is worth every penny. If he’d known I had nothing but the clothes on my back and a handful of change in my pocket, he would never have let the wedding go ahead.

“Felix got word that we’d married. He didn’t show up, didn’t stop it, didn’t say a word either way. We had a quiet ceremony in a hotel parlor, with only strangers as witnesses. Then, half full of hope, half full of quiet dread, I brought my new bride to his house—the house that’s now so infamous, the circular study house. I thought even a hardened old bachelor like him couldn’t be so cold that he’d ignore Evelyn’s charm, that he’d welcome us. I was wrong. Dead wrong. Two days after our wedding, the second I stepped through his door, I realized I’d only ever seen the softest, least unyielding side of his nature.

“He was sharp as steel, and just as hard. He not only didn’t care for Evelyn at all, he sneered at the very idea that I thought love and marriage mattered at all. Called them silly pastimes for empty-headed people, unworthy of two men of our supposed talent and breeding. Then he turned his back on us. I lost my temper entirely. I screamed at him, raged at him for being a greedy, selfish, heartless monster, and he flinched like I was hitting him. Second by second, he lost his own self-control. He reached for the dagger lying on the table right beside him, screamed at the top of his lungs, ‘You want my money? Fine—take it!’ and drove it straight through his own heart with one single, desperate blow.

“I know you probably won’t believe me. But that is the full, true story of what happened in that house, gentlemen.”

The room hung heavy with unspoken distrust. Unpremeditated tragedies like this did happen, after all; impulsive, uncharacteristic acts of violence had broken every rule of human nature since time began. But not a single man in that room, even those who had not witnessed Bartow’s bizarre pantomime at the crime scene — a performance that had never once lined up with this story of Felix Adams’s sudden, unprovoked suicide — failed to show clear, unmistakeable disbelief at the wild, voluble tale the young man had spilled in front of them.

Thomas Adams, sharp, sensitive to every shift in mood, felt that distrust settle over him like a cold shroud. His head drooped. “I won’t add a word to what I said, or take a word away,” he muttered doggedly. “Make of it what you will.”

The inspector leading the inquiry glanced dubiously over at veteran detective Mr. Gryce as the words left his lips. “We’re sorry you’ve taken that position,” Gryce said, leaning forward slow. “There are far too many loose ends left to tie up, Mr. Adams. For one thing: if your brother stabbed himself entirely out of nowhere, why did you bolt the house the second he fell, no call for help, no announcement that you were even related to him?”

“You don’t have to answer that,” the inspector cut in. “No man in this country is required to incriminate himself.”

The saddest smile any of them had ever seen tugged at the corners of the prisoner’s pale mouth for a heartbeat. He lifted his head, eyes blazing with raw desperation. “Incrimination is the last thing I fear right now. I can read the way all of you look at me. I’m lost, and I have no way at all to prove my story is true.”

The admission sounded like the final, despairing cry of an innocent man trapped by circumstance, and both interrogators paused. “You forget,” Gryce said, “your wife was there with you. She can corroborate every word, no doubt — she’d make an invaluable witness for your side.”

“My wife!” Thomas choked on the words, they were barely audible. “Must you drag her into this? She’s sick, she’s weak, it would kill her. She loves me, she —”

“Was she in your brother’s study? Did she see him lift that dagger to his own chest?”

“No.” The denial seemed to jolt a flicker of new courage into him. “She fainted minutes before the final act, right in the middle of our fight. She never saw him die. Gentlemen, I might as well tell you the full truth — I have nothing to gain by lying now. She finds it just as impossible to believe Felix stabbed himself as you all do. I’ve tried everything to make her understand the truth, but what hope do I have of the world believing me, when the woman I love more than anything — an angel, who would do no harm — she can’t bring herself to pretend what she saw wasn’t real. She’ll never testify for me. She’s too honest, too true to what she knows. I’d lose her, it would kill her —”

Mr. Gryce tried to interrupt, to calm him, but Thomas would not stop. “Spare her, sirs! Spare my wife! Write me down as guilty, call me whatever you want, just don’t force that poor girl to speak —”

The inspector cut his ragged, heart-rending pleas short. “Have you read the newspapers over the last few days?”

“I? Yes, of course I have,” Thomas snapped. “Blood is blood. He was my brother, I left him dying on that floor. I knew how much danger I was in, I couldn’t not read the papers.”

“Then you know he was found with a large wooden cross torn off the wall resting on his chest. Who tore it down? Who laid it there?”

“I did. I’m not Catholic, but Felix was. When I saw him dying without absolution, without his last rites, I thought of that cross on the wall, tore it down, and laid it in his arms.”

“Pious act. Did he recognize it?”

“I couldn’t tell you. My fainted wife was all I could think about.”

“I see. You carried her out, so focused on tending to her that you never noticed Mr. Adams’s valet —”

“He’s innocent! Whatever you all think, he had nothing to do with this death —”

“I say you never noticed him standing in that doorway watching you?” The inspector knew full well Bartow had not been in the doorway at all, but watching from the loophole high above the study, but the chance to trap the witness was too good to pass up.

Beads of cold sweat popped out on Thomas’s pale forehead, and he fumbled to keep his story straight. “That man is crazy, and deaf and dumb, like every paper says! His testimony is worthless. You wouldn’t really make me face him, would you?”

“We’re not confronting you with anyone. We asked a question. You never saw the valet, then?”

“No, sir.”

“Or the mystery of the colored lights strung around the study walls?”

“No, sir.”

“Or the steel plate in the floor, all the other strange contraptions your brother built to amuse himself in his solitude?”

“I don’t know what you’re talking about, sir.” But his tone had gone tight, defensive.

“Fine,” the inspector said. “None of the complications that have slowed our investigation down are part of the story you’ve told us. That’s possible, technically. But there’s one last small, critical detail you haven’t explained — the detail your wife mentioned to you the second she woke up after fainting. I’m talking about the half dozen words your brother scrawled on a scrap of paper right before he died. We found that scrap, and we know it was the root of your fight that night — your wife was even upset that you’d left it behind when you fled. Do you know where we found that paper?”

The eyes Thomas lifted to the inspector’s face were completely blank, all the fight drained right out of him. The mere mention of that scrap of paper had stripped every last shred of willpower he’d used to keep his lies afloat. He shook his head, reached out a hand to grab for the scrap the inspector held up, but his fingers went weak. He burst out in a raw, broken cry.

“Enough! I can’t keep living behind a wicked lie. I killed my brother — for reasons as good as any man has ever had to take another’s life. I won’t tell you what they are. You can try me for murder, hang me, I don’t care.”

It was a total, pitiful breakdown, all the more gutting against his broad, herculean frame and fine, sharp features. Mr. Gryce’s brow furrowed deep, and he stood slow to move beside the young man, who looked ready to collapse to the floor. The inspector stayed seated, drumming his fingers on the table, when Thomas suddenly froze, his whole frame shaking violently.

“What is that?” he gasped, staring hard at the door.

“Nothing,” the inspector started to say — but the door flew open.

A woman, pale as a wraith, glowing with a fierce, almost holy passion, tore herself free of the man who’d been trying to hold her back, and stumbled into the room. She was tiny, frail, her face made for soft smiles, but raw, unmistakeable human agony blazed in her eyes. It was Thomas’s young wife, the invalid he’d left three hours earlier at their home in Belleville.

“What is he saying?” she gasped, her voice trembling. “I know exactly what he’s saying. He’s trying to tell you he killed Felix, that he drove that dagger into a monster no one but us knew how wicked he was. But you can’t believe him. You can’t. He’s innocent. I rode twenty miles from my sick bed to tell you that. He —”

Thomas clapped a hand over her mouth, wild, frantic appeal in his eyes. She looked back at him, a look so full of love every man in the room felt it settle deep in his chest, then she gently pried his hand away.

“It’s too late, Tom. I have to speak. Not my father, not my own weakness, not your strictest orders could keep me locked up at Belleville when I found out they’d arrested you. I won’t be a coward now, not when these men have heard your false confession and might hang you for a crime you didn’t commit. That would be unworthy of the love we share, of the life we were supposed to build together.”

She turned to the group of men, every one of whom was holding their breath, waiting. “Sirs — I know my husband is innocent. Because the hand that drove that dagger into Felix Cadwalader’s chest was mine. I killed him.”

No one moved. The horror of the moment settled over the room slow, bleaching the color from every man’s face. Lips curved for soft words, faces built for gentle kindness, had just admitted to a brutal stabbing. No one stirred until a shout rang out from the doorway: “Catch her! She’s fainting!”

Thomas lunged forward, all the despair that had weighed him down for hours gone, all new strength in his limbs. He caught her as she crumpled, carried her gently to a nearby couch and laid her down. Mr. Gryce, who had watched every second of the spectacle with rapt attention, suddenly understood everything — that was why Thomas had panicked so hard when the cops showed up to arrest him, not him, the poor boy had thought they were coming for his wife, the center of his whole world.

Thomas stood over her, one hand brushing the hair back from her unconscious face. “Don’t you dare think this girl is evil,” he cried, his voice cracking. “There is no purer soul on this earth than hers. She did not kill him out of malice. I worship her — I would kiss her hands, her forehead, her feet, if it meant no harm ever came to her, even after what she did. Now — sirs — you have to listen. There is a terrible, unbelievable story I have to tell you.”

"You will find what I’m about to tell you matches and contradicts every half-truth I fed you in my first confession," Thomas Adams began, all the evasive softness draining from his voice. "I tangled lies and facts together in that earlier account, but if I’m going to explain exactly what pushed my wife to act as she did, I can’t hold anything back. I have to start entirely fresh, as if I’ve never spoken a single word about that night before."

"Wait one moment," Mr. Gryce cut in, sharp and authoritative, holding up one hand to stall him mid-sentence. "We will hear you out soon enough." Leaning across the inspector’s broad shoulder, he murmured a quick, quiet string of instructions, then pulled a stub of pencil from his waistcoat pocket and scribbled a numbered list of points on a scrap of official notepaper, sliding it across the desk to the inspector.

It was clearly a checklist of every loose end they needed the young man’s full unvarnished story to tie up — one the inspector found himself glancing at over and over as the man who was not really Adams at all, but Thomas Cadwalader, at last began laying out the full truth of that night’s murder. The memorandum reads word for word as follows:
1. The woman we know as Mrs. Felix Adams was calm enough to pause mid-interview, stop, and rearrange her hair — per the discarded comb we found on the floor of that locked bedroom. What pushed her from that poised, collected state to a frenzy so violent she would stab a man to death with her own two hands, when no prior grudge between them had ever come to light?
2. Explain the meaning of the note Felix wrote mere minutes before that fatal interview, the one half chewed to pulp when we pulled it from his jaws: *"I return you your daughter. Neither you nor she shall ever see me again. Remember Evelyn!"*
3. Why did Felix use the first-person "I" in that message? What standing did he have, as the newlywed young woman’s brother-in-law, to speak of returning her to her father, as if he had been her custodian before she married his brother?
4. If he had time to grab that note, crumple it, and force it into his mouth to chew it to shreds even as he was dying, why didn’t he use that exact same strength to reach for the light switch? The signal code we’ve cracked for his fancy multi-colored lamp means the crimson glow that was still blazing over his desk when we broke in translates to: *"Nothing more required just now. Keep away."* Why would he deliberately signal his staff to stay away while he was under attack?
5. His wife called him a monster, and claimed she stabbed him in a fit of unbridled rage. Why, then, was his face not twisted with fury or pain when we found him? His expression held only quiet sorrow, and a fierce, deliberate resolve — nothing like the shock and frenzy a man would show if he was blindsided by a mortal blow from someone he never expected to harm him.
6. What was that heavy steel plate we found wedged between the two casings of his study doorway? It was built to slide shut and seal the room completely, but it never moved from its resting socket even at the very peak of the violence that killed him. Why?
7. Old Poindexter showed up at the house barely minutes after the stabbing. The first words we overheard him gasp when he saw the body were *"It is Amos's son, not Amos!"* Did he not know who he was supposed to meet that night? Was the sight of the victim lying there with a cross on his chest and a dagger buried in his heart less of a shock to him than the realization of exactly who that victim was?
8. Remember what we worked out from Bartow, the deaf-mute servant’s, pantomime: the fatal stab was a left-handed thrust. Watch for confirmation that the young woman is left-handed, and make sure he explains why she stood there, frozen, her right arm stretched stiff behind her back for minutes after the murder, before anyone else broke into the room.
9. The parrot that hangs in the conservatory, the one that squawks *"Remember Evelyn!"* at all hours of the day, sometimes breaks off that cry to wail *"Poor Eva! Lovely Eva! Who would strike Eva?"* If that bird learned those phrases from Felix, that means he knew his brother’s bride long before she ever walked through the door of this house as a newlywed. The full story of the tragedy has to account for that hidden history.
10. We’re fairly certain Bartow, the deaf-mute servant, is nothing more than a witness to the crime, with no hand in the stabbing itself. Why, then, did he make no move to call for help, and looked almost secretly glad when he saw the dead body? That secret joy he can’t hide, the thing that drove him half mad with relief — we may not get that answer from Thomas’ confession, but we can’t let it slip our minds.
11. Cross-reference this list with the full test results we ran with Bartow to decode the lamp signal system, the one that controls the colored lights in Felix’s study:
- White light = Bring water
- Green light = Bring my hat and overcoat
- Blue light = Reshelve all the books on my desk
- Violet light = Lock up the house for the night
- Yellow light = Wait and watch for the next signal
- Red light = I need nothing. Stay away completely.
Confirm that the red light was indeed lit in the final moments of Felix’s life, and make sure Thomas’ full account lines up with why he would send that exact signal at that exact time.

With every one of these unanswered questions hanging heavy in the air around them, the small knot of detectives settled back in their chairs, ready to hear the full, tangled history of the crime, and the long-buried, devastating secrets that led to that single, fatal act of violence.

Thomas Cadwalader—who went by the name Adams for most of his life—could only hint at his story when he spilled it, breathless and frayed by terror and urgency. We cannot afford to mumble it as he did, not when every thread of this tangled tragedy stretches back decades before Felix Adams’s violent death, before even the woman who struck the fatal blow was born. The first link in the chain leading to that New York street murder was forged in half-forgotten days, and to understand the passions that spawned such horror, we must tell the story exactly as the two surviving Cadwalader brothers lived it.

Thomas never knew his mother; she died in his earliest infancy. He barely knew the shape of his father, raised in isolation in France by an old Scottish lawyer related to his late mother. The guardian never spoke a single word about the boy’s father until Thomas turned fifteen, at which point he pressed a stack of romances into his hands, with a strange, sharp injunction: “None of these, no matter how thrilling, will ever equal the story of your father’s bitterly wronged and suffering life.”

When Thomas begged for more details, the old man said nothing more that day. But the seed was planted, and the lonely, sensitive teen could not stop digging through the farthest corners of his memory for some half-forgotten fragment of his father’s face. The few blurry impressions he dragged up were not of a man, but of uncanny, unplaceable sensory flashes: a stretch of total darkness, two fixed bars of orange light, the black silhouettes of distant gables against them, a cold prickle of unnameable dread, a great wind swooping at him like a living thing. No one, not even his guardian, could explain the visions. All the old man would do was drop careful, deliberate hints, over months and years: there was a great duty waiting for him across the Atlantic, a duty that justified his long exile from his family, and he must master every skill, every charm, every strength that could make a young man a force to be reckoned with, on both sides of the ocean.

That sense of a secret, important purpose lit every hour of Thomas’s study. By eighteen, he had graduated the Sorbonne, was a master-level musician, a champion fencer and rider, and could outperform almost any peer in tests of both physical and mental skill. Only one flaw stuck out: he lacked easy social grace, was too brusque and self-assured around other men, and far too timid around women. He did not realize how obvious that flaw was until his father’s first ever letter arrived, praising his accomplishments, and calling that exact defect out by name. Stunned, he demanded to know how the father he had never met could know such a private detail of his behavior. The answer hit him like a blow: he was not an only child. He had an older brother, twenty years his senior, who had visited Paris only months before, and almost certainly crossed paths with him without Thomas ever knowing.

Determined not to fail the two men watching his every move from across the ocean, Thomas spent the next year immersing himself in the company of European high society, refining his demeanor until he carried that effortless, unplaceable charm that makes women flush when he enters a room and earns the quiet respect of every man of his class. When he was fully a polished man of the world, his guardian paid him a final visit.

“Your father’s summons will come in weeks,” he warned. “Do not form any ties you cannot break easily. You will never return to France. You will have money, a fine home, all the family affection you have ever craved. All you need to do is carry comfort to a heart that has waited twenty years for you.”

Thomas sailed for New York six months before the fatal street tragedy, burning with feverish anticipation. Waiting for him at the dock was not his brother Felix, but a stranger whose face screamed bad news before he even spoke: Amos Cadwalader, his father, was dying. He was rushed onto a train for a tiny remote village in northern Pennsylvania, still seasick, the motion of the Atlantic thrumming in his bones, stumbling through a stormy night that felt like a nightmare. He climbed down onto a rain-soaked platform, was bundled into a waiting carriage pulled by a steaming, panting horse under a guttering lantern he would see in his nightmares for decades, and was driven down pitch-black country lanes until the doors of a ramshackle farmhouse swung open, lit by a wild old Scottish woman holding a flaming candle. “Is that you, laddie? Come in. The master’s been crying for you all day.”

The grand, luxurious home he had been promised vanished into smoke the second he stepped over the threshold. He saw bare scratched floors, stained peeling walls, a rickety pine staircase, and the old woman crooning over him, overjoyed, that he was the spitting image of his beautiful mother, no trace of his father at all. A storm was howling outside, rain lashing the windows, great tree boughs scraping against the siding, wind shrieking down the chimney, until he picked out a thin, reedy voice calling over the din, “Thomas! Thomas!” He ignored the old woman’s frantic pleas for him to eat first, tore up the stairs, and found his father propped up in a huge bed pushed against the far wall, eyes blazing with fever and a fire sharper and more ferocious than any mere physical illness.

Thomas dropped to his knees, clutching the wasted, thin hands stretched out to him, and the years of hunger for family washed away all his disgust at the squalid surroundings. But Amos did not embrace him. He pushed his son back, made him stand in the glow of a guttering oil lamp on the rickety pine table beside the bed, and inspected him head to toe, muttering through groans of pain, until a savage, triumphant look spread across his dying face.

“Thank God,” he gasped. “Our work did not fail. Handsome, strong, noble. My revenge will succeed. John Poindexter will learn he has a heart that can be torn right out of his chest. I do not need to live to see it.”

Thomas had never heard that name before. His father stared at him, shocked that his guardians had kept the secret so well. With the hand of death already curled around his heart, he forced himself to speak. “John Poindexter was my best friend once. We grew up together, came to America to make our fortunes. He got rich, cold, ruthless, with a grand house and horses. I had nothing, after my wife died, but two children. You are not my only child. There was Evelyn, a sister, born before you, dead before you ever drew breath. And a son, Felix. When the Civil War broke out, I sent my two kids, 15-year-old Felix and 18-year-old Evelyn, to live with Poindexter—he had no children of his own, I thought he would protect them—then I enlisted. I spent four years fighting, locked in Libby Prison, starved almost to death. When I came home, there was a funeral procession in the street. Evelyn’s. Dead. Walking between her coffin and Poindexter was Felix, her little brother, like a guard. Poindexter betrayed her. Destroyed her. Let her die of shame and anguish, and never showed a flicker of remorse.”

“Cease.” The voice was smooth, quiet, and unyielding, sounding right over Thomas’s shoulder. “Let me tell the rest. No man can tell the rest as I can.”

Thomas spun, shaken to his bones. It was Felix, the brother he had never met, standing in the doorway, his face carved with decades of unrelenting grief. “Evelyn had a voice like a bird. She filled Poindexter’s whole house with song, back then, because she was in love with a good, honest young man named Edward Kissam. One day she found me, her eyes wild with terror, begging me to pray our father died in prison instead of coming home to see her like this. She would not speak Edward’s name. She said she was dead to every person in the world but me. I didn’t understand, not at first, until she started wasting away, her life seeping out of her like a flame with no fuel to feed on. I sat outside her bedroom for seven days and seven nights, listening to every breath she took, making sure Poindexter never stepped through her door. The day she died, I found him pacing the hall, pale and shaking, and told him: Amos is coming home. What are you going to say to him, when he asks where his little girl went?”

The words barely left Felix’s lips when Amos Cadwalader heaved a ragged, shuddering sigh from his fevered, panting chest. It spoke louder than any speech: a grief still raw, an agony that had never dulled, even after thirty long years had dragged by since that cursed, fateful night they still refused to name aloud. Felix echoed the sigh, his own face etched with the same unhealed scar, and hurried on, his voice low and sharp, like a knife scraping over old bone.
“It was dusk when I finished telling our story. From full dark to the first streak of dawn we sat, eyes locked on each other’s faces, no sleep, no respite, the whole wretched truth hanging thick between us. Then we went to find John Poindexter.
“If he’d hidden from us, we might have found mercy to temper our rage. But he met us openly, calmly, with the icy indifference of a man who cannot fathom anyone else’s pain because he has never felt a shred of it himself. His very first sentence laid that cold heart bare. ‘Spare yourselves, spare me all useless recriminations. The girl is dead. I cannot call her back. Enjoy your lives—your eating, drinking, getting, spending—you’ve only got a few more years left anyway. Why harp on old griefs?’ His last line was a victory lap. ‘When a man cares for nothing and nobody, cursing him is a waste of breath.’
“Ah, that was it! That was the secret of his untouchable power. He cared for nothing and no one, not even himself. We reeled under the blow, bent under its weight. But before we left his house, before we left the whole cursed town, your father and I swore an oath: we would make that cold heart feel. Some day, some way, we would make that impassive brute suffer exactly as he had made us suffer, no matter how high he climbed, no matter how unassailable his prosperity seemed. That was thirty years ago. And that oath has never been fulfilled.”
Felix paused. Thomas lifted his head to speak, but the dying old man at his bedside cut him off before he could utter a word.
“Some men forget a wrong in a month, others in a year. I never forgot. Neither did Felix. When you were born— I’d remarried, foolishly hoping I could outrun my grief and find new joy— I felt, I don’t know why, that Evelyn’s avenger had finally come. Then, a year or so later, we heard that God had either forgotten John Poindexter’s sins or had decided to settle them at last: after eighteen years of marriage, he’d been given a child of his own. I looked at your bright, bonny little face, and I saw— I thought I saw— a way to fulfill the vengeance Felix and I had dedicated our whole lives to.
“You grew. Your ardent nature, generous temper, quick, sharp mind promised a fine, strong manhood. When your mother died, leaving me a widower for the second time, I stopped hesitating. I devoted you entirely to the purpose you were born for. Thomas, do you remember that day? The start of the journey that would drag you far from home? How I carried you on my shoulder down that dusty road, until we were within sight of his grounds? How I lifted you up from among the old gravestones at the edge of the cemetery, pointed to those distant gables looming black against the twilight gold, and dedicated you to destroy every last scrap of happiness that would ever grow under his new baby’s smile? You do? I never thought you could forget. Now the time has come to make good on that promise. I call on you now, Thomas. Avenge our griefs. Avenge your sister. Poindexter’s little girl has grown to womanhood.”
At those words, Thomas recoiled, horror coiling hot in his gut. But the old man misread his revulsion as cowardice. He clutched Thomas’s arm, his fingers like talons, and raged on, passion burning through the rattle in his chest.
“Woo her. Win her. They don’t know you. You’ll be Thomas Adams to them, not Thomas Cadwalader. Pluck that budding flower right into your bosom, and then— Oh, he must love his child! Through her we have our hand clamped straight over his heart. Make her suffer. She’s nothing but a soft country girl, and you’ve lived years in Paris. Make her hurt, and if, in doing it, you make that iron man blench? Know that I’ll be looking down on you from the grave I’m about to climb into. Be happy. You won’t have lived for nothing. I won’t have died for nothing.”
He paused to gasp for failing breath, but his indomitable will fought off the pull of death, wrapping Thomas in a spell that muddled his instincts, turning him into a puppet stitched through with the hate two men had nurtured for thirty years, suddenly condensed into a single hour.
“I’m dying now,” Amos panted, the words scraping out of his throat. “I’ll never live to see your triumph. I’ll never see John Poindexter’s eyes glaze over with that gut-wrenching agony that makes a man scream that there can’t be a God in heaven. But I’ll know it, where I’m going. No six feet of dirt can hold my spirit down when John Poindexter feels his doom. I’ll taste his anguish. I’ll rejoice. And when he comes faltering down that dark road I’m about to walk, trailing after me—”
“Boy, boy, you were reared for this. God made you handsome. Paris made you polished and strong. You made yourself sharp, accomplished, every inch a gentleman. You only have to show that pretty face to that country girl, and you’ll own her will and her heart. Once they’re yours? Remember me. Remember Evelyn!”
Thomas had never been swept up in a storm of passion like this. It carried him away in a burning tide he couldn’t fight. He lifted his hand, meaning to answer the plea, but no sound came out. His father misread his silence, and cried out, bitter and raw:
“You’re dumb. You don’t like the task. You’re feeling virtuous, maybe— you who lived all those free, unshackled years in Paris. Or maybe you’ve got those silly instincts of honor, that generosity that blinds you to the wrongs that have cried out to heaven for vengeance for longer than you’ve been alive. Thomas, Thomas— if you fail me now—”
“He will not fail you,” Felix cut in, his voice calm, smooth, unshakable. “I’ve watched him. I know him. He will not fail you.”
Thomas shuddered. He’d forgotten Felix was in the room. He turned to look at the man who’d just volunteered to stand surety for the unholy crime his father was demanding of him. The man he saw would draw eyes in any room, anywhere: he was tall as Thomas, not broad, but the same iron will that raved on Amos’s face sat quiet, inflexible, in Felix’s gray eyes, locked on Thomas’s with a power he knew he could never escape. A steel band seemed to clamp tight around his heart. Even so, Felix was as elegant, as perfectly trained in all the graces of gentlemanly life as Thomas himself. None of the shabby poverty clinging to Amos and his surroundings was visible here: Felix was well-dressed, sharp, and could hold his own against any Parisian gentleman without effort. The long, mysterious dark-green serge curtain stretched from floor to ceiling behind him threw his pale features into sharp, stark relief, and for a split second Thomas wondered if it had been hung there on purpose for that exact effect.
But the demand in Felix’s face dragged his attention back. He bowed his head, stammering: “I am at your command, Felix. At your command, father. I can’t say more. Only remember I never saw Evelyn. She died before I was even born, and I—”
Felix’s soft, measured voice cut him off. “Perhaps there is an obstacle we haven’t accounted for. You may already love some woman, mean to marry her. If that’s the case, it need be no impediment—”
Thomas’s indignation burst out before he could stop it. “No,” he snapped. “My heart is completely free. A few trivial old fancies, nothing more, already fading to nothing.” He felt like he’d lived a dozen years since he stepped foot in that room.
“Your heart won’t get in the way,” Felix said, matter-of-fact. “I saw the girl. I went to their house a year ago on purpose. She’s pale as a snowdrop, quiet, half-lost in her own little world. You won’t need to summon up the memory of all the bright Parisian ladies you knew to be immune to her charms.”
Thomas shrugged, numb.
“She has to taste every last drop of the intoxication of hope first,” Felix went on, his voice clear and sharp as a blade. “To know despair, she has to know every single joy that comes with being loved completely. Have you the skill as well as the stomach to play this role all the way through? A role that takes patience, dissimulation, courage, subtlety— that union of will and unforgiving resolve that feeds on tears, that grows stronger, not weaker, when its victim suffers?”
“I have the skill,” Thomas murmured, “but—”
“You lack the incentive,” Felix finished. “Well, we’ll be patient with your doubts. Our hate was fed by memories of Evelyn, who you never met, as you say. So look. Thomas. Look at your sister, as she was, as she will always be for us. Look at her, and think of how Poindexter despoiled her, killed her, threw her away and forgot her. Have you ever seen a face more full of grief? A face where beauty fights so hard against the awful, unescapable prophecy of woe?”
Thomas followed Felix’s outstretched hand, half-convinced he was about to see a ghost standing there. Where the dark, drab curtain had hung a second before, a bright light blazed. At its center was a figure so lovely, so tragic, it made every painting he’d ever seen in every gallery in Europe feel flat and dead. It didn’t look like a portrait. It looked like Evelyn herself, gentle, loving, breathing all the soft warmth he’d never found in the living relatives around him. He fell to his knees at its feet, crying out: “Don’t look at me like that, all reproach, sweet Evelyn. I was born to avenge you. I will. John Poindexter will never go down to his grave in peace.”
A sigh of utter, relieved contentment drifted from the bed.
“Swear it!” Amos cried, holding his arms out in front of him in the shape of a cross.
“Swear it!” Felix repeated, laying his own hand across Amos’s crossed wrists.
Thomas stepped forward, laid his own hand beside Felix’s. The storm outside howled, gust after gust slamming against the walls of the house, as he raised his voice above it.
“I swear. I swear to win Eva Poindexter’s whole heart. And then, when it is entirely mine, I will cast her back in anguish and shame straight into the arms of John Poindexter.”
“Good,” came Amos’s faint voice, sounding like it was coming from an immeasurable distance. Then the dark fog that had been creeping over Thomas’s senses since he spoke the oath closed in completely, and he sank insensible to the floor at the feet of his dying father.
* * * * *
Amos Cadwalader died that night, but not before one last awful scene. Around midnight, he woke from the heavy sleep that had followed the chaotic, brutal events of the evening. He glanced from Thomas to Felix, sitting on either side of his bed, then fixed his eyes on the door, a strange, wild gleam in them.
“Ah!” he gasped. “A visitor. John Poindexter. He’s come to beg my forgiveness before I set out on that dark journey.”
The bitter sarcasm of his tone, the cold, mocking courtesy of his manner, made the hair stand up on both his sons’ heads. Neither of them doubted for a second he saw his enemy standing there as plainly as he saw them.
“Does he dread meeting me on the other side? Does he want to placate me before I join that poor, grieving ghost of his victim? He won’t be disappointed. I forgive you, John Poindexter! I forgive you my daughter’s shame, my whole blighted life. I’m dying. But I leave one behind who will never forgive you. I have a son, an avenger of the dead, who lives to— to—”
He fell back against the pillows, dead. The unfinished words hung in the cold air, sealing Thomas to his terrible fate forever.

Felix had never inherited his father’s knack for self-sabotage when it came to building wealth. In the twenty years since Thomas had been living abroad, he’d amassed a considerable fortune he could never convince their reclusive, bitter father to touch—though the old man had been only too happy to see every spare dollar funneled toward the long-planned family revenge. So there was no empty flourish in the final injunction Felix gave his brother the day Thomas set out for Montgomery:
“I’ve more than enough money for this. Spend whatever you need, no questions asked. By the time the job is done, there will still be plenty left for whatever amusements you want.”
Thomas nodded, dryly thinking the laborer deserved his wage. “What about you?” he asked, glancing around the sparse, bare walls of their family home that had felt entirely pointless ever since their father died. “Are you staying here?”
Felix’s answer was sharp, uncompromising. “No. I leave for New York tomorrow. I’ve already leased a house under the name Felix Adams—you will address me by that name from now on. The name Cadwalader never leaves your lips in Montgomery. You can’t forget half the town already knows my face, which is why we couldn’t send me to do this job in the first place.” He smiled, well aware he was the far more striking of the two brothers. “Now—where are those Paris introductions I told you to bring back?”

The events of the next few weeks unfold with perfect clarity when you read two sets of documents side by side: short, formal letters Thomas sends to Felix, and a private, unshared diary he keeps only for his own unraveling peace of mind. The letters are cold, curated summaries of facts to keep his brother satisfied. The diary spills every messy, conflicting emotion he would never dare put in a letter home. Both are necessary to understand the trap closing around all of them.
---

Dear Felix,
I’ve arrived. I’ve seen her. She is exactly as you described, a pale blonde. Tomorrow I will present my credentials to John Poindexter. From what I’ve already seen, I expect he will welcome me warmly.
Yours affectionately,
Thomas.

I could never write Felix the real story of today. Why not? And why do I have to write it here, hidden where no one will ever read it? To stop myself from replaying it on loop, maybe. I don’t even understand my own feelings right now. I’m still pushing as hard as ever to finish the task we swore to do, but for the first time I find myself dreading it.
I saw her, that much I told Felix. But I couldn’t tell him where, or how. How could I? He’d never believe that a child of Poindexter’s could do something so gentle, or that I—whose heart has been as dark as hell for years, ever since I bound myself to this revenge—could feel that heart swell and my sworn purpose crumble the very first second I laid eyes on the face we planned to drown in tears and agony. Surely Felix would be stronger. Wouldn’t he?
I went straight from the train to the cemetery. I didn’t check into a hotel, I didn’t even wash up before I sought out Evelyn’s grave, to renew my oath on the spot where nineteen years earlier our father had held four-year-old me over the grass and pointed his rage straight at John Poindexter’s house. I found the old, half-buried neglected stone marking her resting place, and I was bending down to brush moss off the carved name in the sunset when I heard the soft rustle of a woman’s skirts. A young girl was walking toward me, wrapped in the rosy glow of the sinking sun that made her look almost ethereal, her delicate frame and bright, soft face lifted like she wasn’t quite touching the ground. She carried a huge bouquet of pure white lilies, and I saw her eyes weren’t on me—they were fixed on the very grave I’d just been standing over. I stepped back into the shadow of some bushes to watch her scatter the flowers, symbols of innocence, over the long-forgotten mound.
What did this mean? Who was this girl honoring my long-buried little sister with such tender care? When she stood up to leave, I couldn’t hold back my shock or my curiosity any longer. I stepped out and greeted her with all the respect her bearing demanded, and when she lifted her face to speak I found it even more beautiful than it had been when she was bent over the flowers. I asked her, in the casual tone of a passing stranger, who was buried there, and why a girl her age would leave flowers on a grave so old the moss on the stone proved the person under it had died decades before she was born.
Her answer hit me like a blow, even though I’d braced for surprises. “I leave flowers here,” she said, “because the girl buried under that stone had the exact same birthday as me. I never met her, but she died in my father’s house when she was no older than I am right now. Now that I’m grown, and I understand how terrible it is to die so young, I make a point of bringing her a share of my own birthday flowers every year. Everyone says she was just like a lily, in looks and in character, so I bring her lilies.”
It was Eva Poindexter. The girl we came here to destroy. And she was scattering lilies on Evelyn’s grave.

Dear Felix,
I shook John Poindexter’s hand today. To win his daughter’s trust, I first have to win his good opinion, or at least catch his favorable notice. I think I’ve already succeeded.
Very truly,
Thomas.

I don’t feel like a whole man anymore. John Poindexter seems cold, sharp-edged, unyielding in his opinions—but he doesn’t fill me with the loathing I expected. He doesn’t make me think of our sister, not once. Is it because he’s Eva’s father? Has her beauty cast a halo over the man we came to punish? If that’s true, Felix has every right to curse me, and our father’s ghost—

Dear Felix,
The introductions you gave me have gotten me welcomed everywhere in town. Montgomery is full of wealthy families with fine houses, so I’ve slipped easily into local society. She is the undisputed star of every gathering. Did I tell you John Poindexter is still the most powerful, respected man in the county?
Yours truly,
Thomas.

She is beautiful. She has the delicate softness of a lily and the faint warm flush of a rose, but that’s not what moves me. It’s the strange, sweet gentleness of her nature—no weakness in it, though, not at all. There’s a quiet, unshakable strength in her that shows the second duty calls. Could Felix ever have imagined a Poindexter like this? I can’t look at that pure, lovely face and link it to the heinous sin we’re here to avenge. I can’t even think about the dark, secret, revenge-fueled life I lived before I came here. All that hatred slips right off me the second her eyes land on me. I start dreaming of things that are pure, true, satisfying, good—and it’s all from her smile, not anything she’s ever said to me. Did Heaven send me an angel to ruin? Or am I so blind I’m seeing a saint where Felix would only see a pretty, charming girl?

Dear Felix,
We’ve been riding, dancing, playing every silly social game there is. Everyone in town has started to notice how much time I spend with her. Only Eva seems completely oblivious. Sometimes I wonder if our whole scheme will fall apart because of her quiet, almost childlike innocence. I worry she’ll never fall in love with me. But I’ve worked as hard as I possibly could to win her favor. Today I rode twenty-five miles out of town just to track down a silly little trinket she mentioned liking once.
Yours affectionately,
Thomas.

This won’t be as easy as Felix thinks. Eva Poindexter might be a small-town girl, but she has her own standards. Charm and good manners alone will never be enough to win her. Do I have the other qualities she’s looking for? I have one secret she can never guess. Will the shadow of that secret overwhelm every other good thing about me, making her pure soul shrink back from me the second I think I’ve won her? I don’t know, and that doubt is hell inside my chest. Something deeper, stronger, more unignorable than my oath of revenge has made winning Eva’s heart the only thing that matters to me. I’ve forgotten our whole purpose. I’ve forgotten everything except that she is the only woman I will ever want, and I’ll never rest until her heart is entirely mine. Good God—am I a slave here, when I thought I would be the one in control? Did I, Thomas Cadwalader, give my soul into the keeping of this innocent girl? I don’t even stop to question it. Winning her is the only reason I get up in the morning.

Dear Felix,
She might not love me yet, but she has eyes for no one else. John Poindexter told me so himself, and he’s clearly very eager to help me win his daughter. People like her, who are quiet and soft on the surface, love all the harder and deeper once they give their heart away. That pale blonde has a heart to fight for.
Yours affectionately,
Thomas.

If all I felt for her was passion, I could fight it, force myself to turn back. But this is something more, something deeper. I catch myself seeing the world through her eyes, hearing things the way she would hear them, feeling my heart beat in time with hers. It’s my soul that’s enthralled, not just my body. I don’t want to win her just to satisfy my own desire. I want to make her happy. I want to prove to her that there is real goodness in the world—me, the man who came here to rot her whole life apart. Me, who still swore to do it. Oh Felix, Felix. You should have picked an older, harder man for this job. Or you should have remembered that anyone who ever felt the weight of family love carries a heart too soft for this kind of infamy.

The name Evelyn is never spoken in John Poindexter’s house. Sometimes I think he’s forgotten her entirely, and that’s the last little spark that still pushes me to remember our revenge. He forgot her! It’s absurd that his own child, born years after our sister died, remembers to bring her lilies, and he has erased her from his memory. And we planned to make the daughter pay for that. Shouldn’t I pray that that never happens? That she will loathe me instead of love me? Distrust me instead of giving me her whole heart? But no man prays against his own happiness. Eva Poindexter has to love me. Even if I tear myself apart with remorse after she gives me her heart, even if I ruin everything for the revenge we swore, it has to happen.

Dear Felix,
Could you send me a few fine, tasteful pieces from Tiffany’s? John Poindexter clearly expects me to give his daughter proper gifts now that my intentions are clear. I think she will accept them. If she does, we can both be sure her feelings for me are real.
Very truly,
Thomas.

I can’t stand to spend even a whole day away from her side. If Felix saw how hard I’m working to court her, he’d praise me in that cold, unyielding heart of his, say I was single-mindedly pursuing the original plan. He’d quote that line from one of his old letters: “You are not disappointing us.” Us. Like our father’s ghost is still hovering nearby, sharing our revenge and our hopes. But if he’s here, he must see deeper than Felix does. He must see that every single day that gets me closer to Eva also pushes me further away from the cruel end they planned. I’ve betrayed my oath. Because I know now I will never, ever break Eva’s faith in me. I couldn’t. I’d rather face my father’s accusing ghost on the other side of death, or Felix’s fury here on earth, or even my own self-contempt for being weak enough to turn away from the dark revenge I promised to deliver.

Dear Felix,
This morning I walked past the window you told me was Evelyn’s old bedroom. I did it on purpose, to test my own feelings, to see how much pain it would stir up. It was enough. Eva accepted the brooch you sent. It was the simplest, most delicate one in the package.
Affectionately,
Thomas.

I hate John Poindexter. I do. But I will never hate his daughter. Only Felix could be so stupidly rigid as to punish the child for the father’s sins, to unleash his rage on this gentle, honest, fascinating girl. But wait—am I required to hate her? Am I not falling in love with her, even now? I should ask Felix. No. I can never ask Felix. He’ll never understand what makes Eva so special, or the hold she has over me. He’ll get suspicious and come running to Montgomery to check on me. Good God—am I so much of a traitor to my own blood that I can’t stand the thought of Felix even looking at Eva, secret and unseen, when she has no idea who he is?

Dear Felix,
We went on a mountain picnic today. I was the one assigned to escort Miss Poindexter down a steep, dangerous rocky slope. We never exchanged a single word of romance, she’s still not ready for that, but I know for a fact I made huge progress in her good graces.
Yours,
Thomas.

I touched her hand. I felt her soft, warm form pressed close against mine as we climbed down the ledges together! No other people were nearby, no eyes on us. For minutes at a time we were so alone on those wooded, sunlit slopes it felt like there was no one else breathing on the whole planet. But I would no more dare to squeeze her hand, or pour out all the mad, worshipful love in my heart to her innocent ears, than if every person in Paris was watching us. I love her so much. The old, dead crime we swore to avenge feels so far away, so faint, like it happened in another lifetime. And that awful hour, when I knelt at my father’s deathbed and swore the oath… that oath. That damn oath.

The thing I dreaded, the thing I should have seen coming, has happened. Felix is in Montgomery. I got a note from him today ordering me to meet him at Evelyn’s grave at midnight, the witching hour, when no one else is out on the streets. I don’t like it. I’ve always been a little afraid of Felix, and I can tell he’s leaning on cheap dramatic tricks to try to force me back in line. But those days are over. I’ll show him I can’t be bullied, not there, not at that hour, not anymore. I can’t be swayed, not when I have the little kid glove I stole from Eva the other day—surely that’s not a sin?—lying on my hotel room table, soft proof of what I’m fighting for. Evelyn is a sacred memory. But the dead can’t be allowed to ruin the living. Eva will never be sacrificed to Evelyn’s ghost. Not even if John Poindexter lives out the rest of his life in perfect peace, not even if Felix—well, I have to stand my ground. Felix is a terrifying man to meet alone, in that spot full of all our old hatred, at an hour when ghosts are supposed to walk the graves.

I wouldn’t have recognized Felix if I’d passed him on the street. He was a stranger in the faint moonlight pouring over the shaded cemetery, his voice even different than I remembered, and there was a hesitation in his manner I never thought him capable of. He didn’t say any of the things I expected. He didn’t yell at me, he didn’t throw accusations in my face. He only told me, cold as ice, that I had to cut my courtship short. The end we both planned had to be hurried up, and he gave me exactly two weeks to bring everything to a climax. Then he turned to Evelyn’s grave, bent down, and tried to make out her name under the moss on the stone. He stayed there so long I leaned down beside him and put my hand on his shoulder. He was trembling, his body as cold as the stone he was leaning against. Was it the memory of our little sister that made him break like that? It made sense—no one ever loved Evelyn half as much as Felix did, when she was alive. I was about to step back out of respect for that decades-old grief when he grabbed my hand, hard, the strength in the grip totally at odds with his quiet, measured tone.
“Don’t you dare forget the end we agreed to. Don’t you forget John Poindexter. His sin, how he ignored our father’s grief, all the years of suffering that turned Amos Cadwalader into a total hermit. I’ve seen the girl. She’s changed—girls her age change fast—and some men, not me, but some men, might think she’s beautiful. But no amount of beauty can be allowed to blind you. Your eyes are supposed to be fixed on our goal. Ignore her. You’ve done that, haven’t you? Pale little blondes can’t sway a man who’s spent years in the company of the most dazzling women in Paris. Keep your eyes on John Poindexter, the debt he owes us, the suffering we promised to make him pay. The fact that Eva is sweet, gentle, nothing like we expected her to be only makes it easier to strike at his heart. The more she matters to him, the harder his fall will be.”
The old spell he used to cast over me when we were kids settled back on me. I couldn’t speak, couldn’t argue. He let go of my hand and stood up, his back turned to the village the whole time—I noticed he never once faced the town since he stepped into the cemetery. “I’m heading back to New York tomorrow. In two weeks you telegraph me when you’re ready to come live at my house. I’ve got a home there more than big enough for both of us, and soon enough it will all be yours.”
He clamped a hand over his chest, and even in the dark I could see a strange, sharp convulsion cross his face

Beneath the half-unravelling surface of the Cadwalader household mystery, a second, far more intimate secret struggle raged, locked deep inside a man’s nature that had long sealed itself off from all human sympathy—shaped as much by his own unyielding temperament as by the grim, vengeful circumstances that had ruled every day of his adult life. No polished, full accounting of it can ever be made; some tragedies fester too dark and private for human ken, and this one only came to light long after Felix Cadwalader’s death had been deemed fully explained, pieced together from tattered scraps of torn, unaddressed letters tucked away under the loose floorboards of servant Bartow’s room. No one knows where Felix hid them, or how Bartow came across them. Their only signature is the sharp, slanted, fevered handwriting that could belong to no one but Felix himself.

Madness.
I saw Eva Poindexter again today, and heaven and hell have been tearing me apart every minute since. Eva! Eva! The girl I once thought of as nothing more than our prey, the girl I deliberately handed over to my brother as part of our plan. She is too lovely for him. Too lovely for any man who is not untouched by all prior passion, who has never thrilled to a woman’s voice, or looked on a face capable of stirring deep, ungovernable feeling in his chest. Is this love? Can I—Felix, who for years had only one single thought, only one burning enthusiasm—hold a hidden, tiny crack in this iron breast that any woman, least of all John Poindexter’s daughter, could slip through? Impossible. I am caught in a frenzy, or the devils are making sport of me. And yet, every time I picture her half-lifted eyes, her soft, unguarded parted lips, her innocent bearing, that strange mix of divine tenderness and quiet strength that makes everyone who meets her admire her, I swear the air around me brightens like the threshold of heaven.

I have to flee. I will never see her again. Thomas’s resolve is unshakable. He must never guess that mine has shattered like an idol struck dead by a thunderbolt.

If Thomas had not been raised in Paris, he too—but no, I am the only weak one here. Curses on my—

Did I say I would run? I can’t. Not yet. One more glimpse of her face, just to prove to myself this madness is justified. Maybe yesterday I was just shocked to find celestial loveliness where I expected nothing more than pale, vapid dullness. If this all comes from my own weakness, not some actual magic in her, I can face that folly before it destroys me. I will stay, and—Thomas will not, shall not—Poindexter’s daughter—hatred, burning hatred for Thom—

My pride is back. I saw her again today. Saw them together. There was real, unfeigned love in Thomas’s eyes. How could I ever have expected him not to fall for her? And I managed to hide every twist of my agony, managed to remind him of his duty to the plan. She loves him, or he is convinced she does. The work moves forward. But I will not stay here to watch it play out. No. No, I cannot.

I told him my secret tonight, wrapped up as if it was some old story from my past. The devils must laugh at men like us. They have every right. Sometimes I wonder if my father, watching us from the clear sight of the afterlife, is laughing right alongside them.

I walked home with that wretched secret clamped to my chest. Home, where nothing can distract me from the gnawing grief, the thoughts that are almost too heavy to bear. I pace the floors at all hours. I scream her name out loud. I scream it even underneath the portrait of Evelyn. There are moments I am half-tempted to write to Thomas, to forbid him—

Eva! Eva! Eva! Every fibre of my miserable body is screaming that one word. No man will ever know. Thomas will never find out that the thought of her fills every hour of my days and nights, turns my life to torment, and makes the future—

I wait for his letters (sparse, cold little things they are) like a condemned man waits for his executioner. Does she truly love him? Or will that exquisite, soulful nature of hers crave a stronger mate, a more fierce, unyielding temperament, a—

I swore I saw my father rise from his grave in one of my dark, fevered hours to curse me. He can curse me all he wants, if only—

What was I saying about no man ever knowing? Bartow knows. Mute, deaf Bartow, who has no voice to tell anyone anything, snuck up on me and caught my secret. I can tell by the way he peers at me from doorways, his sullen dissatisfied glares, that jealous rage in his eyes that I would laugh myself sick at if I was not half ready to shriek aloud from torment. That dumb serving man, picked up goodness knows where, jealous that I am weak for John Poindexter’s daughter! He never once cared how much I ranted about Evelyn. But for years before I dared to go near Eva, her name echoed through every room of this house, and my eyes never strayed from the spot on the wall where Evelyn’s portrait hangs, its sad, woeful beauty a permanent reproach to me.

I had a great shock today. The starling, the one I trained for years to murmur Evelyn’s name, suddenly shrieked “Eva! Eva!” out loud at the top of its little lungs. My first impulse was to wring its neck. My next was to pull it out of the cage and tuck it against my chest under my coat. I did neither. I am still a man.

Bartow will wring that bird’s neck if I don’t stop him. This morning I caught him standing by the cage, hand reached through the bars, a murderous glint in his eye I understood instantly. He can’t even hear the word the wretched bird keeps repeating. He only knows it’s a new name, a secret name, and he is determined to stamp it out. Should I hang the cage high out of his reach? His deafness, his inability to speak to anyone else, the way he follows every order I give him by reading my little colored cue cards, the dog-like devotion he has for me that makes him tend to my every wish—all that is precious to me right now. But the bird—there it goes again. Eva! Eva!

Is this hate I feel, or love? Abhorrence, or passion? Love would make me want to save her, but I have no thought of saving her, not now that she has confessed her love for Thomas, not now that he—this isn’t revenge for Evelyn anymore. It’s revenge for me! These months of endless torture, the ugly revelation of my own weakness, the fact I was forced to give my blessing to their marriage that will be all bliss for them and all unendurable pain for me—all of it cries out for vengeance. They will not escape. Not the two of them. I have planned deep. I have accounted for every possible contingency. It took time, thought, every penny I had to spare. But the trap is perfect. If they dare step across the threshold of my circular study, they will bow to my will, or they will perish there. And I will perish right alongside them. They will never live happy lives. Thomas can forget that dream. John Poindexter can forget that dream. I might have forgotten the old oath we swore on my father’s crossed arms, but I will never forget the unthinkable grief of these past months, the humiliation my own weakness brought me. My weakness will be avenged—this unheard of, unendurable weakness. Remember Evelyn? Remember Felix! Ah, there it goes again. Eva! Eva! Eva!

A thick, thrumming silence hung over the room in the wake of that final, wrenching appeal, every person gathered there still reeling from the extraordinary story they had just heard. Small, breathless whispers broke out across the crowd as they exchanged disbelieving half-sentences, while Thomas Adams turned to his wife, his face softening with equal parts tender love and raw, unspent anxiety. The inspector passed the crumpled, annotated memorandum he had received from Mr. Gryce back across the table to the veteran detective. The sheet was covered in scrawled questions, almost every one marked in the margin with the same sharp, handwritten note: *Answered*.

The full list of riddles the police had been chasing since the murder read:
1.  Why would a woman calm enough to pause mid-interview to fix her hair (the comb found discarded on the floor of Adams’s bedroom was proof of this) spiral so fast into ungovernable frenzy that she killed a man she held no known prior grudge against? *Answered*.
2.  What did Felix Adams mean by the note he wrote right before that fatal interview: “I return you your daughter. Neither you nor she will ever see me again. Remember Evelyn!” *Answered*.
3.  Why did Felix use the first-person “I” in that note? What standing did he hold with his brother’s new bride that gave him the right to speak of her that way, after she had married Thomas? *Answered*.
4.  After that note was written, why did Felix, when attacked, fight to swallow the very paper he had written it on, dying with it still clamped tight between his teeth? *Answered*.
5.  If he had been killed in a blind, unplanned burst of rage, as the young woman had claimed, why did his face show nothing but quiet sorrow and deliberate resolve, no trace of the furious shock a man would feel when struck a mortal blow by an attacker he never saw coming? *Answered*.
6.  If he had the strength to grab that note and drag it to his mouth, why did he not use that same strength to flip on the bright white light that would have called for help? Instead he left the crimson lamp glowing, a signal they had decoded to mean: *“Nothing more required just now. Keep away.”* *Answered*.
7.  What was the purpose of the huge steel plate hidden between the two casings of the study doorway, and why did it stay locked motionless in its socket at the exact climax of Felix’s life? *Answered*.
8.  Why did old Poindexter show up on the scene barely minutes after the murder, muttering the overheard line “It is Amos' son, not Amos!”? Had he not known who he was supposed to meet at the house? Was the identity of the victim more of a shock to him than the sight of a dead man with a cross on his chest and a dagger in his heart? *Answered*.
9.  Remember the conclusions we drew from Bartow’s silent pantomime: Mr. Felix Adams was killed by a left-handed thrust. Watch for proof the young woman is left-handed, and do not forget we still need an explanation for why she held one arm stretched rigid behind her for so long in the immediate aftermath of the murder. *Not Answered*.
10. What did it mean that the parrot that screamed “Remember Evelyn!” on repeat would sometimes alter its cry to wail “Poor Eva! Lovely Eva! Who would strike Eva?” The full truth of the tragedy had to prove that Felix Adams had known his brother’s bride for a very long time, far better than anyone had guessed. *Answered*.
11. If Bartow the deaf-mute servant was only a witness to the crime, and no accomplice, why did he show unmistakeable, wild joy the second he saw Felix dead? The scraps of Felix’s handwriting the police had not yet found would later reveal the servant was consumed by a bitter, unspoken jealousy, and he’d been overjoyed to see the master he hated struck down by the woman Felix had obsessed over. *Answered*.
12. This schedule was confirmed after repeated tests with Bartow and the strange multi-colored lamp in Felix’s study: White light = Water wanted, Green light = Bring overcoat and hat, Blue light = Put back books on shelves, Violet light = Prepare study for the night, Yellow light = Wait for next signal, Red light = No help needed, stay away. A red light was glowing in the study at the exact moment of the murder. *Answered*.

Only two small threads had not been fully untangled, the most critical being point 9, the riddle of the left-handed stab. Mr. Gryce conferred for a quick, quiet moment with the inspector, then approached Thomas Adams and asked if he had the strength to reenact exactly how he had seen his wife deliver the fatal blow. The shocked young man stared back, too stunned to speak at first. He knew rumors had spread that the deaf-mute servant had mimed the crime for the police, but no one had told him the specific details of that pantomime—information the detectives had deliberately held back from all witnesses.

He did not answer right away, but sat lost in thought for a long minute, then lifted his left hand, grabbed a heavy paperweight off the inspector’s desk, and lunged across his own body, driving the makeshift weapon toward an imaginary figure standing at his right side. He looked up at the faces of the men around him, his expression tight with unspoken terror, searching for any sign of leniency.

“You just demonstrated that your wife struck the blow left-handed,” Mr. Gryce said. “But I have watched her every second since she arrived here, and I have not seen a single sign that she is left-handed.”

“She is not,” Thomas said, his shoulders slumping in relief. “But she could not use her right hand, because I had clamped my own hand over hers the second she lunged for the dagger. Our hands were locked together, knotted so tight I still bear the bruise, until the horror of what she had done crashed over her and she fainted, collapsing to the floor sobbing, ‘Tear down the cross and lay it on your brother's breast. I would at least see him die the death of a Christian.’”

A wave of unmistakeable relief passed over both men. The riddle of her strange, rigid pose, holding one arm stretched out behind her for minutes after the stabbing, was explained perfectly: she had still been trapped in that iron-clasped grip with her husband. Mr. Gryce picked up the blue pencil the inspector had laid down, and with a quiet, satisfied smile, scrawled a thick, decisive *Answered* straight across paragraph number 9. The last major puzzle piece of the circular study murder had clicked into place.

Minutes later, Mr. Gryce drifted into the outer waiting room of Police Headquarters, his sharp, seasoned gaze drifting over the huddle of officers, witnesses, and half-distracted onlookers milling between the oak desks. He was chasing the last stray thread of a question that had niggled at the back of his mind through the entire unraveling of this strange, tangled case, and he was certain he would find his answer here.

He was not disappointed. Tucked in a quiet, unobtrusive corner, perched on the edge of a hard wooden bench, sat Miss Butterworth, her amiable, composed features betraying not a flicker of the chaos that had unfolded over the last five hours. She waited calmly, as if fully assured the outcome she had quietly worked to secure would land exactly as she hoped. Gryce stepped over to her with a half-smile, and laid a gentle, playful accusation at her feet for having nudged events along this exact path.

She protested, with a small, uncharacteristic flash of heat. "I was merely present when it mattered," she insisted. "When the crisis broke, when that poor young creature learned her husband had been spirited off to New York in the custody of two men, it was plain as day someone who understood her situation, who grasped the full horror of the straits she had been dragged through, needed to be at her side. And I was that someone."

"You are always that someone," Gryce replied, with his usual quiet gallantry. "If that phrase means being in the exact right place at the exact right critical moment. So you have already heard the full of young Mrs. Adams's story?"

"I pieced the core of it together from her half-delirious, raving outburst the moment the truth came to light: she was the one whose hand closed around the dagger that slew Mr. Adams. Once she had calmed enough to explain the full, terrible chain of events that led to that blow, I did not hesitate another second over what was her duty, or what was mine. Mr. Gryce—surely they will not make her answer for that act? She will not be dragged through a trial, convicted of a crime that was never a crime at all?"

"Madam, there are not twelve men in the whole of New York City so devoid of common sense and basic humanity as to label that act anything but unassailable self-defense. No grand jury will return a true bill against her. You may rest easy."

The shadow of dread that had clung to the edges of Miss Butterworth's eyes lifted at once. "Then I may go home in peace," she exclaimed. "These have been the most desperate five hours of my life, and I am quite thoroughly shaken. Would you be so kind as to escort me to my carriage?"

To Gryce's eye, she looked less shaken than she ever had—never more serene, never more quietly satisfied. But he knew no one could judge the toll of the last hours better than she herself. He paused a moment to confirm that the object of her care, Evelyn Adams, was reviving fast in the next room, her color returning as her husband knelt beside her and murmured quiet assurances, then offered her his arm.

As he helped her step up into her waiting carriage, he paused to ask after Mr. Poindexter, Evelyn's father. "Why is he not here? Did he let his daughter set off on an errand as terrible as this one without offering to stand at her side?"

Her reply was sharp, clipped, and bitter. "Mr. John Poindexter is a man without a spark of heart. He traveled with us all the way from our home to New York, but refused to set one foot over the threshold of Police Headquarters. Sir, you will soon learn that the full, burning, decades-long passions of three souls, and the most deeply rooted revenge I have ever heard of in all my days, were all lavished on a man who is entirely incapable of suffering a single flicker of remorse, pity, or care. Do not speak of old John Poindexter to me. Now, if you would be so good, tell my coachman to drive me to my home in Gramercy Park. I have dipped my fingers into police business for the very last time, Mr. Gryce—positively the very last."

She sank back against the carriage velvet cushions, her face set in an unyielding, final line, though it could not quite hide that small, unmistakeable glint of quiet complacency: she was far from displeased with the part she had played, or the outcome she had helped secure, even if it was the sort of meddling no refined, respectable lady was supposed to indulge in. Gryce suppressed a smile, bowed lower than his usual customary mark of respect, and stepped back as the carriage rumbled off down the cobblestone street.

He walked slowly back toward the station doors, a soft, quiet sigh escaping him. He could not help but wonder—would another case half as strange, half as tangled, half as unforgettable, ever bring the two of them back together to compare notes again?
