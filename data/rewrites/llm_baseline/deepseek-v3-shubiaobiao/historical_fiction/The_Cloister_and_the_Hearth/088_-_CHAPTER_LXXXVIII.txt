The supper table was laid, and Eli’s household had gathered—all but Margaret. Catherine frowned, puzzled that Eli insisted on waiting.

“I told her you wouldn’t wait for the duke himself,” she said.

“She’s not the duke,” Eli replied gruffly. “She’s a good lass who’s waited years, not minutes, for a thankless son of mine. Put the food out—we’ll eat the moment she comes.”

The rich aroma of the meal proved too tempting. “She’ll come once we start,” Eli conceded. “Mistress Joan, sit you down—you’re a guest here, not a servant.”

No sooner had the knives been raised than the door burst open. But it was not Margaret who entered—it was a Dominican friar, his face twisted with fury.

In an instant, he loomed over Cornelis and Sybrandt, his hands clawing the air above their heads like talons. Then came the curse—a torrent of damnation so vivid and venomous it seemed to scorch the very air.

*“Cursed be the lying lips that swore Margaret dead—may they rot before the grave and sear on hell’s iron! Cursed be the hands that forged those letters—may the hangman strike them off to clutch eternal fire! Cursed be the hearts that schemed to sunder love—may they wither joyless, loveless, hopeless, and burn beyond the grave!”*

He cursed their flesh from scalp to sole, then spun toward Eli, flinging a letter onto the table.

*“Read, you hard-hearted fool! See what monsters you’ve bred!”* His voice cracked like a whip. *“We meet again at Judgment—never on earth!”*

And like a storm, he was gone. The household sat frozen, the feast untouched before them.

Margaret arrived then, Jorian at her side. Her eyes darted over the scene—no blood, no violence—only stricken faces. She gasped in relief.

Catherine shrieked and turned away. Eli, scanning the false letter, roared to his feet, sword drawn.

The brothers scrambled beneath the table, howling. Eli lunged, but Margaret and Jorian seized his arms, holding him back just long enough for the pair to flee into the night.

Eli hacked down the shop’s signboard and flung it into the fire. Catherine sobbed into her apron, while Margaret, pale and trembling, tried in vain to comfort her.

*“Oh, my poor wicked boys!”* Catherine wailed. *“Better he’d stayed in Italy than come to curse his own!”*

*“Hold your tongue!”* Eli snapped. *“Your coddling made them what they are!”* Then, gentler, he beckoned Margaret. *“Come here, lass. You’re my daughter now.”*

She stumbled to him, resting her head against his chest, too shattered for tears.

*“Poor lamb,”* he murmured.

With a ragged breath, Eli turned to Jorian and Joan. *“Eat, friends. Trouble’s no excuse for starving guests.”* But Jorian recoiled.

Eli’s face fell. *“Aye. My own son cursed my table.”* He sighed. *“God’s will be done.”*

The supper was tossed to the yard. The house sank into silence, heavy with grief.

And Clement? He lay prostrate before the altar, forehead pressed to the cold stone, torn between rage, remorse, and a fragile, dawning joy—*she lived*.

But then—a sharper sting. He had abandoned a dying man.

Groaning, he rose. Rain lashed outside; wolves howled in the dark. Yet he strode into the storm, beating his breast.

Duty called, and penance was never done.
