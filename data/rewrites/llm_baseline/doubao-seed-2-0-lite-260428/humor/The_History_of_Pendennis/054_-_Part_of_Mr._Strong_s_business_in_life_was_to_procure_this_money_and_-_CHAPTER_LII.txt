Doctor Portman posted his earnest, panicky letter off to London, then spent the rest of the afternoon patting Mrs. Pendennis’s hand and lying through his teeth that he was dead sure Pen’s so-called “wickedness” was just a silly, harmless misunderstanding. Helen was all set to hop a carriage for London the second she could stand, determined to march up and yell some sense into her wayward son personally, but the local apothecary nixed that plan entirely. He forbade her from traveling even the short distance to Fairoaks for the whole first day, and it wasn’t until the next morning that she was settled back on her own sofa, with quiet, faithful Laura hovering at her side to nurse her through the worst of her anxiety.

As luck would have it, Pen never even cracked that homily Portman wrote for him for weeks. Day after day, the widow waited for her son’s reply to the charges stacked against him, and every 24 hours that passed without a word made her fever and distress worse. Laura bore the brunt of the whole mess: she watched the woman she loved like a mother waste away with worry, dealt with the cold, sad distance Helen put between them in her grief, and never once complained. That girl was a pro at quiet duty, powered by the kind of gentle, unshowy prayers she said only in her own locked bedroom, far from any prying eyes. Those kinds of quiet, kind virtues don’t belong on public display any more than a delicate rose belongs bloomed right in the middle of a raucous ballroom. We’ll just say this much: a good woman is the loveliest bloom heaven ever grew, all soft grace, sweet fragrance, and quiet beauty. It’s a crying, unfair shame that grief, sickness, or cruel fate can mow those lovely things down in their prime, but apparently Heaven only puts its favorite souls through the wringer to polish them up that little bit more.

So that urgent letter from Portman sat ignored in Pen’s Lamb Court letterbox, dutifully dropped off by the postman, carried in by his laundress Mrs. Flanagan along with his rest of his post (and we all remember that same dear lady had previously been caught mid-swig of Pen’s hidden gin stash, so no one’s shocked his mail sorting was a total disaster).

All the readers who’ve followed Pen’s nonsense this far have probably already figured out his biggest, craftiest enemy, the rascal that’s been landing him in trouble since page one. Surprise! It’s Pen himself. Half of us have that exact same no-good scoundrel living rent-free in our own heads: a rogue who leads us into fights, idleness, bad company, and every kind of silly mess. Pen had spent his whole life coddling, coaxing, and spoiling that internal rascal, so naturally the guy turned into a spoiled, insolent servant who threw a full-on raging tantrum the second Pen tried to make him do anything unpleasant. People like Laura, who’ve spent years practicing giving up their own fun for others? That kind of self-denial is no sweat for them. But Pen? The boy had never said no to himself a single time in his life, so when he decided he was going to stop seeing Fanny to be responsible? It went about as well as telling a toddler they can’t have any more lollipops.

He tried everything to chase her out of his head! Worked 12 hour days, walked and rode 10+ miles at a stretch, drank more punch than a sailor on shore leave, smoked so many cigars his whole chambers reeked of tobacco for a week. None of it worked. Little Fanny’s face was stuck in his overheated brain no matter what he did. A week later, he was flat on his back in bed with a raging fever. If you’ve never been deathly sick all alone in tiny London bachelor chambers? Count yourself very lucky. Some kind soul ought to hire a cartoonist like Cruikshank or Leech to draw a whole series illustrating the horrors of bachelor living to scare every single unattached man straight down the aisle to get married immediately. The cold, smokeless bachelor breakfast, the dinner shirt you pull on only to find it has zero buttons, the empty room you walk into after a cozy country Christmas where the only thing waiting for you is a stack of Christmas bills spread out on your desk? That’s all bad enough when you’re healthy. But being sick in those tiny rooms, stuck tossing and turning through long sleepless nights, no one to hand you a sip of water when you’re parched, no one to fluff up your lumpy, crumpled hot pillow? That’s a special circle of hell no one should wish on their worst enemy.

That’s exactly the mess Pen landed in. He went to bed feeling off one night, woke up way worse the next day. The only visitor he got besides Mrs. Flanagan was the printer’s devil from the *Pall Mall Gazette*, who Pen dragged himself out of his fever fog to appease by writing half his usual quota of copy. Shandon was off partying peacefully in Boulogne, Warrington was out of town, so the paper’s political and editorial pages looked so empty you could drive a horse and cart right through them. Good-natured Irish sub-editor Finucane ran up to check on Pen, saw he was delirious, and decided to save the day. The next few issues of the paper were full of wildly effusive praise for Ireland, rants about how the Royal Academy was stupidly petty for refusing to make painter Rafferty an RA, rumors that Irish MP Rooney was about to be appointed governor of Barataria, and random unprompted odes to the Giant’s Causeway. Shandon got the forwarded paper in France, cackled, and tossed it to his wife Mary saying “Look love, Jack’s at it again, our readers are getting a very heavy dose of Irish patriotism this week.” Finucane even offered to skip his whole weekend holiday to nurse Pen, but Pen insisted he’d do far better toughing out his fever alone.

That Friday night, after the paper went to print, Finucane was chowing down at the Back Kitchen and mentioned Pen was sick to Captain Costigan. The Captain remembered the fact two days later, and trundled over to Lamb Court on Sunday afternoon to check on his young friend.

He found Mrs. Flanagan sobbing in the sitting room, half sloshed on brandy she’d chugged to cope with how terrible Pen looked. Pen had snapped at her to stop hovering over his bed a few times, which only made her cry harder and reach for the anodyne bottle even more. The Captain gave her a very firm talking-to about her intemperance before heading in to see Pen.

Pen was so feverish he lit up the second he heard Costigan’s familiar voice. He made the old man sit down, tossing and turning in his lumpy unmade bed, chattering about the Back Kitchen, Vauxhall trips they’d take soon, and asking over and over how little Fanny was doing.

Well, how was Fanny? She’d wandered home heartbroken the previous Sunday night, after watching Pen light his lamp in his chambers while he spoke to Bows. Bows trundled past her mom’s lodge a little later, looking as glum as a rain-soaked bank holiday. Fanny tossed and turned all night, disturbing the little kids she shared a room with over and over. She couldn’t sneak a reading session of her new Walter Lorraine book because her dad was home and wouldn’t let anyone light a candle, so she kept patting it under her pillow in the dark to make sure it was still there. She was up at dawn, and even though she was furious at Bows for what he’d said to Pen, she trundled to his room at her usual time to confront him.

“I saw Mr. Pendennis last night, Fanny,” Bows said gently.
“ I knew you did,” Fanny snapped, glaring daggers at the melancholy old musician.
“ I’ve cared about you ever since we moved here, you were a tiny little kid back then, you used to like me right up until you met this gentleman a few days ago,” Bows said.
“Oh I bet you’re about to start saying horrible things about him,” Fanny huffed. “Go on, it’ll make me like you less, which is impressive.”
“ I won’t say a bad word about him at all,” Bows told her. “He’s a very good, honest young man.”
Fanny clenched her tiny fist, paced up and down the room, cheeks flaming. “If you say one single mean thing about him I will never speak a word to you again ever! You want to trash talk him but you’re too scared, aren’t you?”
“I knew him years ago,” Bows went on, “back when he was almost as young as you are, and he was head over heels in love with Captain Costigan’s daughter, who’s Lady Mirabel now. He wanted to marry her, but they were totally wrong for each other, no money, totally different stations, it would’ve made them both miserable. Mr. Pendennis has his whole career to build, he has to marry a lady from his own world. No honest girl would want to ruin his prospects, make him fight with his family, drag him into poverty just to feed her own silly little crush.”

Fanny’s fiery anger melted straight into sobbing dismay. “What are you even talking about? No one ever said a word about marriage! We met at Vauxhall because the Captain took me and Ma there, he rescued us when we got separated, he was just being kind! We walked in the Temple Gardens once, that’s all! I wish I was dead, I wish I was buried right now, I never should’ve laid eyes on him!”
Bows told her Pen had said the exact same thing, explained that Pen could never possibly marry her, and that she owed it to her own good reputation to try to forget him. Fanny agreed to stay away from him, even though she was still head over heels in love, and ran straight home to tell her mom everything. She rambled about how their story was just like the Lady of Lyons, why was everyone allowed to cheer for Pauline staying faithful to poor working Claude, but a girl like her couldn’t even get to be a servant to Pen’s mom out in the country, just so she could catch a tiny glimpse of him once in a while?

Her mom stroked her hair and told her to stop thinking about him, then immediately turned around and assured her he’d come crawling back soon, just like Emily Budd’s old beau did, the one Emily’s dad the cellist chased off before they ended up happily married. Then surly Mr. Bolton, the undertaker’s assistant, trundled in for his supper, and Fanny wiped her tears in two seconds flat, sat demurely in the corner with her needlework, and the poor man never suspected a single thing was wrong.

Everyone around Fanny was accidentally making her crush worse! Her mom encouraged it, Bows’s whole “Pen is too noble to ruin your life” speech just made him sound even more like a dreamy tragic poet hero, with his pretty scented auburn hair and grand gentleman manners. Every respectable grown-up would tell you Pen was 100% right to nip that romance in the bud: marrying a poorly educated working class girl would wreck his whole career, right? Everyone says no one ever dies of a broken heart, you just get over it, move on, pick someone more suitable. But maybe there’s something to be said for that dumb, all-consuming love that makes you willing to throw all your sensible worldly plans out the window for someone? We’ll leave that debate up to the armchair moral philosophers to fight over later.

Pen, being the snob he was at this point in his life, had zero intention of ever marrying a penniless working girl anyway. He thought he was doing the noble, honest thing by crushing his little crush for both their sakes. But Fanny sat and waited for him day after day, week after week, sure he’d come visit. That’s when Costigan mentioned to her that poor Pen was stuck in his chambers deathly ill with a fever.

That same night, Pen’s uncle the Major rolled back into town from Buxton, all cured of his aches, and sent his valet Morgan over to Lamb Court to invite Pen to breakfast, before they both headed off to Lord Steyne’s country estate to shoot partridges. Morgan came back white as a sheet. “Sir, Mr. Arthur’s in bed with a raging fever, he’s very bad, he hasn’t even called a doctor yet!” The Major panicked. He couldn’t skip the trip to Lord Steyne’s, that’d be social career suicide! Also, he never had the measles, what if Pen’s fever was measles? That’s deadly for grown men! He demanded to know if anyone was looking after Pen, and Morgan said yeah, someone was trying to nurse him, but he still hadn’t gotten any proper medical care.
