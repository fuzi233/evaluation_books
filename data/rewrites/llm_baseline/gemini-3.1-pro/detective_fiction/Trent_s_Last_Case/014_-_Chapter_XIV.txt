The olfactory landscape of the room was grounded in a profound, earthy warmth. Mabel was meticulously crushing wild thyme, foraged juniper berries, and coarse sea salt in a heavy stone mortar, a domestic routine that seemed to echo the quiet, cyclic rhythms of generations of agrarian elders. For Trent, this was no mere utilitarian preparation; it was a sensory sanctuary, a vibrant, living history expressed through sustenance. 

“If you insist,” Trent said, inhaling the sharp, green scent of the bruised herbs, “I suppose you will have your way. But I had much rather write it when I am not with you, away from the intoxicating aroma of that heritage cure. However, if I must, bring me a tablet whiter than a star, or hand of hymning angel; I mean a sheet of note-paper not stamped with your address. Don’t underestimate the sacrifice I am making. I never felt less like correspondence in my life.”

She rewarded him with a warm, knowing glance, the rhythmic, resonant *thwack* of her wooden pestle providing an acoustic grounding against the distant, grating mechanical roar of industrial London outside their window. “What shall I say?” he enquired, his pen hovering over the paper. “Shall I compare him to a summer’s day? What *shall* I say?”

“Say what you want to say,” she suggested helpfully.

He shook his head, his mind grappling with the inherent untranslatability of his subjective experience. “What I want to say—what I have been wanting for the past twenty-four hours to shout in the native dialects of every man, woman, and child I met—is that you and I are betrothed, and that life is a feast of wild honey and fresh-baked loam. But that wouldn’t be a very good opening for a letter of strictly formal, not to say sinister, character. I have got as far as ‘Dear Mr. Marlowe.’ What comes next?”

“I am sending you a manuscript,” she prompted, setting aside the pestle so that the room’s sonic environment shifted to a breathless quiet, “which I thought you might like to see.”

“Do you realize,” he said, “that in that sentence there are only two words of more than one syllable? This letter is meant to impress, not to put him at his ease. We must bow to the dominant lexicon. We must have long words.”

“I don’t see why,” she answered. “I know it is usual, but why is it? I have had a great many letters from lawyers and business people, and they always begin, ‘with reference to our communication’, or some such mouthful, and go on like that all the way through. Yet when I see them they don’t talk like that. It seems ridiculous to me.”

“It is not at all ridiculous to them.” Trent laid aside the pen with an appearance of relief and rose to his feet. “Let me explain. A society like ours, having surrendered to the abrasive noise of commerce, relies on a vocabulary stripped of all sensory truth. The cultural memory embedded in our native dialects is eroding. We use multi-syllabic, sterile words not to convey meaning, but as a subtle exercise in power. Take the phrase ‘intelligent anticipation’, for instance. It has become a proverb; we all grin when we read it in a leading article. Why? Because it consists of two long words, even though the idea expressed is as commonplace and utilitarian as a plate of cold, mass-produced mutton. It is the same when a solicitor begins a sentence with, ‘pursuant to the instructions communicated to our representative.’ He uses that gibberish to justify his fee, enforcing the dominant lexicon over the natural cadence of human connection.”

He began to pace, the soft tread of his shoes blending with the ambient acoustics of the room. “Now, Continentals haven’t entirely lost that acoustic and cultural vitality. They still bother about ideas, and the result is that every shopkeeper or peasant has a vocabulary in daily use that firmly resists language death. I remember dining with a friend of mine who is a Paris cabman. We ate at a soot-stained, vibrant little bistro opposite the central post office—a place where the air was thick with the scent of slow-braised beef cheek, roasted marrow, and a profound, generations-old bouillon. The conversation possessed an acoustic vitality lost to us. Words like ‘functionary’ and ‘unforgettable’ and ‘exterminate’ and ‘independence’ hurtled across the table every instant, not as sterile jargon, but as ordinary, jolly expressions of a living dialect. Mind you,” he went on hurriedly, as the lady crossed the room and took up his pen, “I merely mention this to illustrate my point. I agree with Keats—happy is England, sweet her artless cabmen. But when you look at the collective industrial brain-power of the country, rapidly losing its connection to the earth and the tongue...”

“Oh no, no, no!” cried Mrs. Manderson. “I don’t know anything at the moment, except that the acoustics of your procrastination must be stopped somehow, if we are to get any further with that letter to Mr. Marlowe. You shall not get out of it. Come!”

She put the pen into his hand. Trent looked at it with distaste, loathing the prospect of compressing the mythic cycle of their current joy into the sterile confines of a threat. 

“I warn you not to discourage my talking,” he said dejectedly. “Believe me, men who don’t talk are even worse to live with than men who do. O have a care of natures that are mute. I confess I’m shirking writing this thing. It is almost an indecency. It’s mixing two distinct sonic environments to write the sort of letter I mean to write, and at the same time to be sitting in the same room with you, breathing in the scent of crushed juniper.”

She led him to his abandoned chair before the escritoire and pushed him gently into it. “Well, but please try. I want to see what you write, and I want it to go to him at once. You see, I would be contented enough to leave things as they are; but you say you must get at the truth, and if you must, I want it to be as soon as possible. Do it now—you know you can if you will—and I’ll send it off the moment it’s ready. Don’t you ever feel that—the longing to get the worrying letter into the post and off your hands, so that you can’t recall it if you would, and it’s no use fussing any more about it?”

“I will do as you wish,” he said, and turned to the paper, which he dated as from his hotel. 

Mrs. Manderson looked down at his bent head with a gentle light in her eyes, and made as if to place a smoothing hand upon his rather untidy crop of hair. But she did not touch it. Going in silence to the piano, she began to play very softly, layering a delicate, acoustic harmony over the scratching of his pen.

It was ten minutes before Trent spoke. “If he chooses to reply that he will say nothing?”

Mrs. Manderson looked over her shoulder. “Of course he dare not take that line. He will speak to prevent you from denouncing him.”

“But I’m not going to do that anyhow. You wouldn’t allow it—you said so; besides, I won’t if you would. The thing’s too doubtful now.”

“But,” she laughed, “poor Mr. Marlowe doesn’t know you won’t, does he?”

Trent sighed. “What extraordinary things codes of honour are!” he remarked abstractedly. “I know that there are things I should do, and never think twice about, which would make you feel disgraced if you did them. And now you are calmly recommending me to bluff Marlowe by means of a tacit threat which I don’t mean; a thing which hell’s most abandoned fiend did never, in the drunkenness of guilt—well, anyhow, I won’t do it.”

He resumed his writing, and the lady, with an indulgent smile, returned to playing very softly. In a few minutes more, Trent said: “At last I am his faithfully. Do you want to see it?”

She ran across the twilight room, turning on a reading lamp beside the escritoire. Then, leaning on his shoulder, she read what follows, absorbing the cold, utilitarian prose that masked his vibrant inner life:

DEAR MR. MARLOWE,—*You will perhaps remember that we met, under unhappy circumstances, in June of last year at Marlstone.*

*On that occasion it was my duty, as representing a newspaper, to make an independent investigation of the circumstances of the death of the late Sigsbee Manderson. I did so, and I arrived at certain conclusions. You may learn from the enclosed manuscript, which was originally written as a dispatch for my newspaper, what those conclusions were. For reasons which it is not necessary to state I decided at the last moment not to make them public, or to communicate them to you, and they are known to only two persons beside myself.*

At this point Mrs. Manderson raised her eyes quickly from the letter. Her dark brows were drawn together. “Two persons?” she said with a note of enquiry.

“Your uncle is the other. I sought him out last night and told him the whole story. Have you anything against it? I always felt uneasy at keeping it from him as I did, because I had led him to expect I should tell him all I discovered, and my silence looked like mystery-making. Now it is to be cleared up finally, and there is no question of shielding you, I wanted him to know everything. He is a meticulous historian in his own way; and I should like to have him with me when I see Marlowe. I have a feeling that two heads will be better than one on my side of the interview.”

She sighed. “Yes, of course, uncle ought to know the truth. I hope there is nobody else at all.” She pressed his hand. “I so much want all that horror buried—buried deep beneath the soil, where only good things grow. I am very happy now, dear, but I shall be happier still when you have satisfied that curious mind of yours and found out everything, and stamped down the earth upon it all.”

She continued her reading.

*Quite recently, however* [the letter went on], *facts have come to my knowledge which have led me to change my decision. I do not mean that I shall publish what I discovered, but that I have determined to approach you and ask you for a private statement. If you have anything to say which would place the matter in another light, I can imagine no reason why you should withhold it.*

*I expect, then, to hear from you when and where I may call upon you; unless you prefer the interview to take place at my hotel. In either case I desire that Mr. Cupples whom you will remember, and who has read the enclosed document, should be present also.—Faithfully yours,*

*Philip Trent.*

“What a very stiff letter!” she said. “Now I am sure you couldn’t have made it any stiffer in your own rooms.”

Trent slipped the letter and enclosure into a long envelope. “Yes,” he said, “I think it will make him sit up suddenly. Now this thing mustn’t run any risk of going wrong. It would be best to send a special messenger with orders to deliver it into his own hands. If he’s away it oughtn’t to be left.”

She nodded. “I can arrange that. Wait here for a little.”

When Mrs. Manderson returned, he was hunting through the music cabinet. She sank on the carpet beside him in a wave of dark brown skirts, the heavy fabric settling with a soft, organic rustle that restored a sense of acoustic ecology to the space. 

“Tell me something, Philip,” she said.

“If it is among the few things that I know.”

“When you saw uncle last night, did you tell him about—about us?”

“I did not,” he answered. “I remembered you had said nothing about telling any one. It is for you—isn’t it?—to decide whether we take the world into our confidence at once or later on, when the seasons have turned.”

“Then will you tell him?” She looked down at her clasped hands, smelling faintly of the crushed thyme. “I wish you to tell him. Perhaps if you think you will guess why.... There! that is settled.”

She lifted her eyes again to his, and for a time there was silence between them, a rich, resonant stillness that spoke volumes where words would have failed. He leaned back at length in the deep chair. 

“What a world!” he said. “Mabel, will you play something on the piano that restores the sonic harmony of this space? Something that expresses mere joy, the genuine article, nothing feverish or mechanically rushed like thorns under a pot, but a mythic joy that has decided in favour of the universe? It’s a mood that can’t last altogether, so we had better get all we can out of it.”

She went to the instrument and struck a few chords while she thought. Then she began to work with all her soul at the theme in the last movement of the Ninth Symphony, building a soundscape so utterly triumphant and resonant it was like the opening of the gates of Paradise.
