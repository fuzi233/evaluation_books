"Land!" The cry tore through the air, raw and desperate. "There, far to the south and west, beside the dying red of the sun!" It lay there, a long, azure ribbon stretching between the blood-red sea and the burning gold of the sky. After months of fever-dreams and salt-stink, flesh had touched soil again. Fresh streams promised to cool the parched throats; cooling fruits offered relief to tongues numb with scurvy; free space awaited limbs cramped by weeks of swaying motion. And perhaps, hidden within this green mystery, lay gold and jewels, the boundless wealth of the Indies. Who could say? The old world of facts and grim prose lay a thousand miles behind them, swallowed by the past. Before them spread a realm of wonder and fable, a landscape of boundless hope.

Sick men dragged themselves from their stifling hammocks, gasping for air; strong men fell to their knees, pressing foreheads to the warm deck, giving thanks to the heavens. Every eye strained toward the far blue haze, watching it fade as the sun dipped lower, yet growing broader, brighter as the ship surged forward, caught in the grip of the rich trade-wind. It whispered softly around brow and canvas, a loyal companion to the daring. *"Blow steadily, blow harder still, O good trade-wind, of whom it is written that He maketh the winds His angels, ministering breath to the heirs of His salvation. Blow on, and save me, if not from the grave, then from a fate worse than death. Carry me on, and set me down at her feet, that I may call the lost lamb home and finally rest!"*

Thus Frank muttered to himself, his gaze fixed with straining intensity on that first outpost of the New World, which held his entire existence. His cheeks were hollow, wasted by illness, and the feverish flush on either cheek burned crimson in the sunset light. Minutes passed, and the western rainbows dissolved; emeralds, topazes, amethysts, rubies—all faded into a silver-gray twilight. Above, through the deep sapphire vault, the Moon and Venus reigned supreme over the churning sea.

"That must be Barbados, your worship," said Drew, the master, his voice steady despite the tremor in his hands. "Unless my reckoning is wildly astray—which Heaven knows it has no business being, after such a journey, and praise God, it isn't."

"Barbados?" Amyas replied, looking around at the stunned faces. "I've never heard of it."

"Aye, sir, it is very likely," said Yeo, stepping forward. "Yeo and I sailed with Captain Drake here, and I followed shortly after with poor Captain Barlow. I recall good harborage lying to the south-west. A haven, free from Spaniards, cannibals, or any other fiendish beast. A true Garden of the Lord, hid away in the deeps, reserved as an inheritance for those who love Him. I heard Captain Drake speak of planting a colony there, should fortune ever smile upon him."

"I remember now," Amyas mused, his brow furrowed. "Some words I exchanged with poor Sir Humphrey regarding an island hereabouts. Oh, that he had gone thither instead of to Newfoundland!"

"Nay, then," interjected Yeo gently. "He is now in bliss with the Lord, and you would not have withheld him from that glory, would you?"

"He would have waited as gladly as he departed, if service to his Queen allowed it," Amyas admitted, though a shadow of regret crossed his face. "But what say you, my masters? Is there not wisdom in spending a few days here, letting the sick recover their strength, before we press on to the Main and begin our labors?"

The council voiced approval, a murmur of agreement rippling through the gathered men. Yet Frank remained silent, a statue amidst the moving crowd.

"Come, fellow-adventurer," Cary called out, nudging him. "We need your voice, too."

"To my impatience, Will," Frank whispered back, his eyes distant. "There is but one place on earth for me, and I burn with a desire to fly there. Still, the counsel is sound. I approve."

So the decision stood, met with a hearty cheer from the crew. Long before dawn, the vessel had skirted the southern shore of the island, threading carefully into the bay where Bridgetown now stands. Every eye locked onto the low, wooded hills sleeping under the moonlight, dotted with millions of dancing fireflies like fallen stars. Every nostril drank greedily the fragrant air sweeping from the land, heavy with the perfume of a thousand blooming flowers. Every ear welcomed the chorus of insects, the snores of tree-toads, and the plaintive cries of shore-fowl, a noisy, vibrant life that replaced the monotonous whisper of the open water.

Then, slowly, the ship shuddered to a halt. The cable rattled through the hawsehole. Then, disregarding the danger of lurking Spaniards or fierce Caribs, an instinctive roar erupted from every throat. Poor souls! Amyas had much ado to stop them from leaping straight onto the dark sand, reminding them that daylight was but two hours away.

"Never did two hours feel so long," complained a young lad, pacing anxiously up and down.

"You've never known the Inquisition, lad," Yeo retorted dryly. "If you had, you'd understand how time stretches when you're waiting. Stand fast and give thanks that you are where you are."

"Gunner!" a voice cried out. "Is there gold on that island?"

"Never heard of any," Yeo answered sharply. "And so much the better for us."

"But, Gunner," spoke another, a cripple racked by scurvy, licking dry lips, "are there oranges and lemons there?"

"Not that I've seen," Yeo said, "but plenty of fine fruit down to the beach, thank the Lord. Behold, the dawn breaks at last."

Up flushed the rose of morning; up rushed the sun, casting level rays that glittered on the smooth trunks of palm trees, throwing rainbows across the white foam of coral reefs, and gilding lonely uplands far away where, in centuries to come, stately estates and bustling factories would rise. Long lines of pelicans clanged their wings outward to the sea; the insect hum quieted, then a thousand birds burst into jubilant song. A thin blue mist crept upward toward the inner downs and vanished, leaving the hills to quiver in the burning glare. The land-breeze, which had breathed fresh from the ocean all night, died away into glassy calm, and the tropic day began.

The sick were lifted over the side, landing boat-load after boat-load onto the beach to stretch themselves in the shade of the palms. Within half an hour, the whole crew was scattered across the shore, save for a dozen brave men who had volunteered to keep watch and ward on board until noon.

Now, the first instinctive cry of nature rang out: *Fruit! Fruit! Fruit!* The poor, lame wretches crawled from place to place, plucking greedily at the violet grapes of the creeping shore-vines, staining their mouths and blistering their lips on the prickly pears, ignoring Yeo's entreaties and warnings against the thorns. Some of the healthy began hacking down cocoa-nut trees to reach the nuts, doing little more than blunting their hatchets against the hard wood. Till Yeo and Drew, having mustered half a dozen reasonable men, ventured inland and returned within an hour laden with the dainties of that primeval orchard—with acid juniper-apples, luscious guavas, and crowned ananas, the queen of all fruits, found by hundreds on the broiling ledges of the low tufa-cliffs. Then all, sitting on the sandy turf, defying galliwasps and jack-spaniards, and all the weapons of the insect host, partook of an equal banquet. Old blue land-crabs sat in their house-doors, brandishing their claws in defiance at the invaders, while solemn cranes stood in the shallow waters, heads tilted to one side, meditating on how long it had been since they had seen feather-less bipeds break the solitude of their isle.

Frank wandered up and down, silent, moved more by wonder than by sorrow, while Great Amyas walked after him, mouth full of juniper apples, playing the part of a showman with a patronizing air, as if he had already seen all wonders and was above being astonished.

"New, new! Everything new!" Frank murmured, meditatively. "Oh, awful feeling! All things changed around us, even to the tiniest fly and flower; yet we remain the same, the same forever!"

Amyas, to whom such utterances sounded altogether sibylline and unintelligible, answered simply:

"Look, Frank, that's a colibri. Have you heard of colibris?"

Frank stared at the living gem hanging, humming loudly, over some fantastic bloom. Then it dashed away, seemingly calling its mate, whirring and dancing round and round the flower-starred bushes, flashing fresh rainbows with every shift of light.
