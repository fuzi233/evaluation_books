That evening, Oleron huddled close to his crackling hearth, low fire painting the walls in streaks of honey and amber, and seethed a quiet, fuming resentment at Miss Elsie Bengough’s afternoon prognostication. Difficulties waited for him here, she’d said; he would never finish his life’s work, the novel *Romilly*, within these walls. What utter nonsense. No man’s confidence thrives when someone snuffs it out before he’s even struck the first spark of the day’s writing, and wasn’t there an old, dark superstition that to speak a warning out loud was to summon the very thing you warned of? Some unnameable, hostile little seed had been planted in the room that afternoon, all because Elsie could not keep her foolish, unasked-for opinions to herself. Worse, she had tied that hostile influence not to some external bad luck, but to the house he’d only just moved into, the quiet, sunlit flat he’d fallen in love with at first viewing.

He snorted, shifting his chair to squint around the space that seemed to positively glow, soft and welcoming, in the firelight. Even that faint, sallow, drained tinge he’d half-noticed in the cold light of midday was gone, washed away by the orange flames. The flowered chintz curtains, printed with trellises and wicker baskets and little carved oaten pipes, fell in long, still folds to the window seats, the spines of his old leather-bound books glowed rich as melted honey, and for the life of him he could not see a single thing amiss. It was Elsie, he realized with a jolt, who had looked out of place that afternoon. The quiet, sparse, unassuming room had made her look garish, overfull, all florid pink skin and overripe laughter, like a prize rose left out too long in a damp summer storm, oozing moisture from every petal, already half-soured at the core.

He was horrified at the thought as soon as it landed. He and Elsie had been close friends for ten whole years, she was the most loyal, steadfast companion he’d ever had, the one person he would have run to if every other door in the world slammed shut. The hot flush of shame burned up his neck and over his cheeks, he sat staring into the fire for ten long minutes, mortified that he could dissect his oldest friend so cruelly in the privacy of his own head, even briefly.

All was dead quiet in the house save for one tiny, tinkling sound from the direction of the kitchen: a tap he’d failed to twist fully shut, dripping, plink-plunk, into a chipped earthen bowl beneath it. He began tapping his index finger on the arm of his chair in perfect time to the drops, the little regular motion slowly chasing the shameful heat from his face, and before he knew it, his unkind train of thought picked right back up where he’d dropped it.

Elsie’s mind, he realized now, had never lined up with his, not really. For years he’d read his rough draft chapters to her, and she’d nodded along, spoken like she understood every line of his vision, but now he saw it for what it was: empty, performative praise, no actual comprehension behind it. And if Elsie was that shallow, then so was the protagonist of his novel, Romilly Bishop, the woman he’d modeled entirely on her.

The realization hit him like a cold fist to the chest. He hated her. That character he’d spent three years nurturing, the woman who was supposed to be his Beatrice, his perfect ideal of all the soft, glorious things a woman could be, was coarse-fibred, over-saturated, rank, bleeding Elsie’s overripe, common traits through every one of the fifteen chapters he’d finished. He’d been stuck on the sixteenth for weeks, had no idea why he couldn’t bring himself to write a single line further, and now he saw it all: the whole thing was rotten at the root.

He dragged himself to his feet, fetched the thick stack of manuscript pages from his desk, and held them over the flames to burn them all to ash. But before he could drop the pages, the dripping tap caught his attention again. The little plinking sound had shifted, ringing out four or five distinct dulcimer notes in irregular little phrases, so faint he almost thought he was imagining it. He found himself waiting, holding his breath, for the little trilling four-note phrase to repeat, and his eyelids grew heavy, the fire shrinking lower and lower in the grate, the candle flames guttering and licking at the edges of their Sheffield silver holders. He stumbled to his bed half-asleep, locked the doors and windows on autopilot, and dropped into a deep, unshakable slumber.

The oddity hit him the very next morning. His landlady, Mrs. Barrett, knocked the usual gentle tap on the thin wooden wall beside his bed, and he called her in for his breakfast tray. The moment he opened the door, he realized he was humming something soft, under his breath. Mrs. Barrett paused, hand still on the doorknob, her face turned away, a strange, dimpling little smile playing across her lips.
“Dear me, that’s a very old tune you’re humming there, Mr. Oleron!” she trilled in her soft falsetto. “I haven’t heard that one in near forty years.”
Oleron froze, his thumb stuck halfway into the seal of an unopened letter. “I was humming? Sing it for me, please.”
Mrs. Barrett protested that she had no voice, that her sister Ann Pugh was the family singer, but she hummed the air anyway, thin and wobbly. “They do say it was only ever played on a harp, hundreds of years back,” she added. “They call it *The Beckoning Fair One*.”
Oleron stood stock still, the little tune looping in his head. It was exactly the four-note phrase he’d heard dripping from the tap the night before. He’d never heard the song in his life. He’d never even known it existed. But the house already knew it, and it had taught it to him, while he sat half-dozing by the fire.
