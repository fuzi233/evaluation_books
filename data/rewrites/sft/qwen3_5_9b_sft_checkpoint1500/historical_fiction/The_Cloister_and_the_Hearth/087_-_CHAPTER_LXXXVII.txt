As Jorian adjusted his doublet and jerkin, preparing to depart for Peter Brandt's grave, his thoughts raced ahead of his steps. "They used to whisper of him as a sorcerer in Sevenbergen," he mused aloud, pacing the churchyard. "And it is said he bestowed a final token of his craft upon them. He claimed to have glimpsed Margaret's son descending the Rhine in splendid attire, laden with wealth, though his visage bore the scars of a foreign blade and his limbs were incomplete. Yet, alas, nothing materialized from such visions. Margaret still mourns her lost boy, and Peter rests beneath his neighbors, silent and still, save for the stone slab she commissioned to seal his fate."

He placed both hands upon the cold edge of the mound, poised to lift himself from the earth, when the Friar's hand, trembling with a sudden, strange intensity, settled heavily upon his shoulder. A hushed, urgent whisper cut through the air.

"How long since Peter Brandt passed?"

"Two months past, Father. Pray, why do you ask?"

"And was it his daughter who laid him to rest?"

"Nay, I interred him, though she funded the stone and oversaw the work."

"Then... but he had but one daughter, did he not? Margaret?"

"No fewer, at least, that he acknowledged."

"Do you believe, then, that Margaret lives?"

"B Believe? Good heavens, I would be dead without hope!" Jorian laughed, the sound harsh in the quiet air. "Riddle me that. You speak of love, Father?"

"Not more than reason dictates, considering I am a husband and father of four sturdy lads. No, the truth is far stranger. She saved my life scarcely six weeks hence. Had she perished, I surely would have followed. I could not swallow food; physicians could do naught for me. My wife, Joan, muttered, 'Time to buy thee a shroud.' I replied, 'Ay, perhaps, but let us borrow one first.' Then in stepped this Lady Margaret—though she is dead, by your account—and she opened the windows, shifted me from my bed, and healed me in the blink of an eye. With what? With the scurviest herbs, plucked from my own garden, sweet feverfew. An herb? Nay, 'tis a weed, until it cures me. Now, whenever I pass my humble cottage, I doff my bonnet and cry, 'Fly service to thee!' Why, Father, you grow pale, then red, then white again? What troubles you?"

"The surprise—the joy—the wonder—the terror," gasped Clement, clutching at his throat.

"Why, what concern is it to thee? Art thou of kin to Margaret Brandt?"

"Nay, but I knew one who loved her deeply, so profoundly that her death nearly shattered his spirit, body and soul. And yet, thou speakest of her living. And I believe thee."

Jorian stared, the silence stretching taut between them. After a long pause, he spoke with solemn gravity. "Father, I have answered your inquiries truly. Now, for the sake of our Lady, answer me but two. Did you truly know one who loved this poor maiden? And where?"

Clement stood on the brink of confession, yet Jerome's letter haunted him, shrinking him from uttering the name he had worn in the world.

"I knew him in Italy," he murmured.

"If thou knewest him, thou canst tell me his name," Jorian pressed, his tone cautious.

"Gerard Eliassoen."

"Oh, but this is strange. Stay, what caused thee to claim Margaret Brandt was dead?"

"A letter arrived from Margaret Van Eyck," Clement confessed, sinking onto a nearby bench. His strength seemed to fail him. "It told Gerard that the woman he loved lay buried. Let me sit, for my spirit falters. Foul play! Foul play!"

"Father," Jorian said, kneeling beside him, "I thank Heaven for sending thee. Sit, thou looketh like a specter; thou hast fasted too much. Methinks I hold the key to this riddle. If I do, there be two knaves in this town whose heads I would gladly crush against the soil like this very dust." He ground his teeth and lifted his heavy spade high above his head, bringing it down furiously upon the loose earth. "Foul play? Thou hast spoken truer words than any man in his life. If thou knowest where Gerard dwells now, lose no time in showing him the trap. Mine is a dull brain, yet sometimes the slow hound discerns the scent. Go, and I believe we have seized two ends of the same twisted rope, and a most malignant one."

Jorian then stripped away the pleasantries customary to his station and delivered the core of his tale. Employed by the Burgomaster of Tergou to mend an upper floor, he had grown curious during the removal of his tools. Loud voices below had drawn him down; lying prone, he had overheard the Burgomaster discussing a letter Cornelis and Sybrandt intended to swap for one destined for Gerard by Hans Memling. "Their will was noble, but their stomachs were small," the old man had boasted, displaying a drawer brimming with silver to embolden the thieves. "If they performed the deed, they might take as much as they could grasp."

"Well, Father," Jorian continued, his voice dropping, "I paid little heed then, save for the profit that kept me sleepless. Next dawn, ere the sun peeked, who should I encounter emerging from their homes but Cornelis and Sybrandt, each sporting a blackened eye. 'Oho,' I muttered, 'what mark hath Hans left upon thee? I trust that is all thou hast gained.' Did they not hasten to the Burgomaster's residence? I hid."

At this juncture, the monk's nostrils flared, his restless eyes betraying a suspended breath.

"Well, Father," Jorian persisted, "the Burgomaster summoned them to that same chamber. He held a letter, though I am no scholar; yet I possess as many eyes as the Pope, and I saw the drawer open. Those two rogues plunged their hands in, drawing out the silver. Saints preserve us, how they strove to hoard more, and more, and more! Sybrandt, moreover, had smeared his hand in some sticky substance, likely glue, carrying away one or two pieces adhered to his palm. 'Tis a sin to laugh. Luck, as usual, sided against them. They completed the trick, yet how they did it remains unknown until Judgment Day. They left their eternal treasures in that drawer. A mere handful of silver for their trouble; the Devil won the bargain. There, Father, that weighs heavily on my mind. Often I longed to confide, but dared not speak to the women; otherwise, Margaret would have found no friend left in this world. Those two black-hearted villains are the favorites of fortune. Behold, they have just acquired a grand new shop in Hoog Straet. Their sign is a gilt sheep and a lambkin; a pair of wolves suckling their dam would be nearer the truth. There, the entire family feasts today. Oh, 'tis a fine world. What, not a word, holy Father? Thou sittest like stone, unable to cast even a curse upon them, the stony-hearted miscreants. Was it not enough that the poor lad stood alone in a strange land? Must his own flesh and blood conspire to bury the sole blessing those enemies left him? Consider her pining, yearning for years, gazing down the street for Gerard! So constant, so tender, so true: my wife swears no woman ever loved a man with greater devotion than she does the boy whom these villains have torn from her. Not a day passes without her weeping salt tears. And when I reflect that, but for those two greedy, lying knaves, that winsome youth, whom I rescued, might walk beside her today, the happiest soul in Holland; and that sweet lass, who saved my life, might rest her cheek upon her sweetheart's shoulder, the happiest in Holland instead of the saddest; oh, I thirst for their blood! The nasty, sneaking, lying, cunning, cowardly, heartless, bowelless—"

How now?"

The monk leaped upward, livid with fury and despair, rushing headlong from the spot, both hands clenched and raised heavenward. So terrible was this inarticulate eruption of wrath that Jorian's petty anger died instantly, leaving him standing in stunned dismay behind the tempest he had unleashed.

Absorbed in his shock, Jorian felt a small, tremulous hand seize his arm. It was Margaret Brandt.

He startle d; her presence there at that moment seemed utterly inexplicable. She had waited long upon Peter's tombstone, but the friar had not returned. Seeking him within the church, she had failed to find him.

Suddenly, a gigantic shadow, belonging to a friar, swept rapidly across the floor, passing through her as if she were invisible. Fear seized her lips, threatening a scream, but she recalled Jorian's spectral form emerging from the churchyard and managed to scramble out the nearest exit with difficulty. Just as she fled, Clement vanished down the street, yet her gaze caught the wild agitation etched upon his features. Jorian and she locked eyes, both watching the frantic figure of the distant monk.
