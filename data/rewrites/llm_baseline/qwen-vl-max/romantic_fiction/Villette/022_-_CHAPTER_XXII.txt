When silence had fallen over the house—after dinner was done, after the clamor of the evening hour had faded, and darkness had settled in like a soft blanket—I slipped away to the kitchen. I asked for a candle, just for half an hour, for a very special purpose. Goton, my kind friend, smiled and said, “Of course, chou-chou—two candles if you want them.” With that small flame in hand, I crept upward, quiet as a shadow, toward the dormitory.

But there, tucked beneath her nightcap’s lace trim, lay Mistress Ginevra Fanshawe—her figure rumpled, her breath shallow. She seemed asleep, but I knew better. Her eyelids flickered, betraying a watchful stillness. She’d be awake soon enough, and then—oh, the chatter! The endless talk that would flood in at the worst possible moment. I couldn’t stay. I longed for solitude—just one quiet moment—to read the letter I’d waited so long to open.

So I turned away. I went to the classrooms, hoping for peace there. But no—candles lit, benches stacked, dust swirling through the air, coffee grounds scattered across the floor like dark rain. The rooms were being cleaned, and all was chaos. Frustrated, I did not give up. I would find somewhere—anywhere—where I could be alone.

I took a key from its hiding place and climbed three flights of stairs. At last, I reached a narrow, silent landing, opened a warped, worm-eaten door, and stepped into the garret—a cold, black, forgotten space high above the world. No one would come here. Not even Madame Beck. I shut the door behind me, placed the candle on a dusty chest, wrapped myself in a shawl against the chill, and pulled out the letter.

My hands trembled—not with fear, but with longing. I broke the seal slowly, as if afraid to wake something too precious. *Would it be long? Short? Kind? Cool?* I brushed my fingers across my eyes, clearing the silver haze of emotion that clung to my vision like a gentle shower.

It was long. And kind. So kind—more than I had dared hope. To my guarded heart, which had braced itself for disappointment or worse, it felt like mercy. To my hungry soul, starved for warmth and meaning, it felt like salvation. I had feared so much, wished so little—yet here was abundance. This quiet joy, this simple pleasure of reading words meant for me, carried a sweetness I had never known before. In that frosty attic, lit only by a guttering candle, I was happier than queens in gilded halls. It was brief, yes—but while it lasted, it was real. A bubble, yes—but a honey-sweet one.

Dr. John had written with such warmth. He spoke of our walks, our talks, the places we’d seen together—the way sunlight fell on the water, the laughter shared under trees. He wrote as though he, too, remembered them fondly—not just for my sake, but because they brought him joy. That thought pierced me: he had poured his heart out not merely to please me, but because he wanted to. And that—though it might never happen again—was enough for now. For this moment, nothing marred the peace. It felt as though a seraph had paused beside me, rested a wing upon my beating heart, and breathed healing into my spirit.

Dr. John, you wounded me later. But forgive me—I forgive you—for the sake of that one moment of grace.

Then—something stirred. A sound. A whisper of movement. Was it the wind? Or footsteps?

I froze. My candle flickered. From the far corner, near the pile of cloaks where the malefactors’ garments hung like ghosts, I heard it again—a soft glide across the floor.

I turned. The room stretched long and dark. And there—in the center—stood a figure. Black skirt, straight and narrow. White veil wrapped tightly around the head. The face hidden. But the eyes—glinting, fixed, cold.

A nun.

I gasped. My blood turned to ice. If she had moved closer, I would have fainted. Instead, she retreated. I bolted for the door.

How I descended the stairs, I do not know. Instinct guided me. I avoided the refectory and rushed to Madame Beck’s sitting room. I burst in, breathless, trembling.

“There is something in the garret!” I cried. “I saw it. Go—look for yourselves!”

“Everyone,” I insisted, though only four were present—Madame Beck, her mother, her brother M. Victor Kint, and another man speaking quietly to the elder woman. They rose, startled. I pointed toward the stairs, pleading for them to follow. Men were there—hope, strength. I needed them.

But then—my letter. Left on the chest. Alone. Without it, I could not breathe.

I ran back, calling them after me. When I reached the garret, the candle was gone—snuffed out. Darkness swallowed everything. But someone had brought a lamp. Light cut through the gloom.

The candle stood extinguished. But the letter—where was it?

“Oh! They’ve taken it!” I sobbed, searching wildly, clawing at the floor. “My letter! My letter!”

“What letter, Lucy?” a voice asked—familiar, close.

I looked up.

And there he was—Dr. Bretton himself. John Graham. He had been called that evening to attend Madame Kint. He had been in the room when I entered.

“Was it *your* letter?” he asked gently.

“Yours,” I whispered. “You wrote it. I came here to read it—alone. I saved it all day. I didn’t open it until now. I barely glanced at it. I can’t bear to lose it.”

He held my hand—warm, steady—led me down to the salon, where fire burned. We sat before the stove.

“You don’t need to cry,” he said softly. “What does it matter? Come—let’s go. They’re sending for the police. We don’t need to stay.”

But I could not stop. “I can’t help it,” I murmured. “It was everything.”

He watched me, calm, kind. Then, quietly—“Here. Your letter.”

I stared.

He had found it on the floor. Hidden in his pocket.

A trick? A test? A kindness?

Tears welled again—not from grief, but from relief. I couldn less. I could not speak.

“Are you satisfied now?” he asked.

“Yes,” I whispered. “Happy.”

“Good. Now—how do you feel? You’re still trembling.”

“I’m calm,” I said. “No longer afraid.”

“Then tell me what you saw. You said ‘something.’ But what?”

“I won’t say,” I replied. “Unless someone else sees it too. Otherwise, I’ll be accused of dreaming.”

He leaned forward. “Tell me,” he urged. “As your doctor. I see more in your eyes than you think. Your cheek is pale. Your hand won’t still. Speak.”

“You’ll laugh,” I warned.

“If you don’t tell me, you’ll get no more letters.”

I laughed weakly. “You’re laughing now.”

“I may laugh again—only to help you. Lucy, I care for you. I want to understand.”

His smile vanished. His expression softened. He became serious, almost tender.

So I told him—everything. About the legend of the house, the stories I’d once told him during a ride through Bois l’Etang. About the figure in black and white, standing still, watching.

He listened, thoughtful. Then, as others passed downstairs, he sighed.

“They’ll talk about thieves,” he said. “Let them. But you—keep your secret. Don’t speak of the nun.”

“I don’t know if it was real,” I confessed. “Or if it came from my mind.”

“It was a spectral illusion,” he said gently. “A product of prolonged mental strain. Your nerves are unsettled.”

“Can it happen again?” I asked, chilled.

“Possibly. But happiness is the cure. A cheerful mind, the preventive.”

“Cultivate happiness?” I echoed bitterly. “Is that how you do it?”

He smiled. “I’m naturally cheerful. Adversity touched us once—my mother and I—but we laughed at it. It passed.”

“And you never feel sorrow?”

“Only once—about Ginevra Fanshawe.”

“She made you suffer?”

“Pooh! Nonsense. I’m better now.”

And he was. His eyes sparkled with health, his posture strong, his voice light.

“Why can’t you be like me?” he asked. “Buoyant, fearless? Try to snap your fingers. Just once.”

I hesitated.

“If Miss Fanshawe came now—would you flinch?”

“I’d let her try to move me. Only by true love—passionate, real love—would I yield. I’d demand forgiveness at no lesser price.”

“Once, her smile was worth gold to you.”

“Transformed,” he said. “I am free now.”

He stood tall, radiant, unshackled.

“Miss Fanshawe led me through a phase,” he continued. “Now I ask for passion in return. Love for love. And plenty of it.”

“Ah, Doctor,” I murmured, “you once loved under difficulty—charmed by pride and indifference.”

He laughed. “My nature changes. One mood mocks the next.”

He pulled on his gloves. “Will the nun come again tonight?”

“I don’t think so.”

“Tell her—Dr. John sends his compliments. Ask her to wait for a visit.”

“She had a white cloth over her face,” I said. “But her eyes—glittered.”

“Cold and fixed,” I added.

“Confusion to her ghostly dress!” he said, laughing. “But those eyes—bright, soft.”

“No,” I said. “Cold. Unfeeling.”

“Then she shall not haunt you,” he declared. “Give her a firm shake of the hand if she returns. Will she stand that?”

I smiled. I believed she wouldn’t.

And had there been anything in the garret?

They searched. Found nothing. The cloaks were undisturbed. The broken pane in the skylight? Common. Hail had fallen days before.

Madame Beck questioned me closely. I described only a shadowy figure in black. I never said “nun.” I knew she’d dismiss it as fantasy.

She praised my discretion. Told me not to speak of it to anyone.

So the tale ended.

But I—left alone in my thoughts—wondered still.

Was it real?

Was it dream?

Or was it born of my own troubled heart?

And if it was, could it return?
