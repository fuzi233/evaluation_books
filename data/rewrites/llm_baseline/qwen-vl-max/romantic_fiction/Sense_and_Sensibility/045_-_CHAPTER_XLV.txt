Elinor sat motionless for a long while after Willoughby had gone, even after the sound of his carriage had faded into silence. Her mind was overwhelmed—by sorrow, by confusion, by thoughts that tangled and clashed within her. The man she had despised only moments before now stirred a strange compassion in her heart. Despite all his faults, despite the pain he had caused, she found herself mourning his loss—not because of what he deserved, but because of what he had once meant to Marianne. She admitted to herself, with quiet honesty, that her feelings toward him were shaped not by reason, but by memory: by his charm, his warmth, the way he had once lit up Marianne’s world. And though she knew it was wrong to still care, she could not yet let go.

At last, she returned to Marianne’s room. Her sister was just waking, her face calm and soft from deep sleep. Elinor’s chest tightened. So much had happened—the visit, the fear, the hope—her emotions swirled too fiercely for rest. Yet she kept her face composed, afraid to show how deeply she felt. But her caution lasted only a short time. Within half an hour, another carriage rolled up to the house.

She rushed downstairs, driven by a need to spare her mother every second of dread. Just as the door opened, she caught her mother stepping in—pale, trembling, nearly fainting. Mrs. Dashwood had been convinced, as they approached the house, that Marianne was dead. She had no words, no breath to speak. But Elinor did not wait. In one swift moment, she told her—Marianne was alive, recovering, safe. The relief burst forth like sunlight through storm clouds. Mrs. Dashwood clung to her daughter, sobbing with joy, her arms wrapping around Elinor again and again. Between them stood Colonel Brandon, silent, steady, his presence a quiet strength. She turned to him, her eyes full of gratitude and shared joy, pressing his hand as if to say, *You helped bring this back to me.*

When Mrs. Dashwood regained her composure, her first wish was to see Marianne. In minutes, she was at her bedside, weeping softly as she looked upon her child—now more precious than ever, after the terror of losing her. Elinor watched, her heart swelling with happiness, though she worried that the emotion might disturb Marianne’s fragile peace. But her mother, strong in love and duty, held herself in check. Marianne, weak but content, needed only quiet and rest—and she gave it willingly, knowing her mother was near.

Mrs. Dashwood insisted on staying awake all night, watching over her daughter. Elinor, reluctantly obeying her plea, finally went to bed. But sleep refused to come. The long hours of anxiety, the endless thoughts of Willoughby—“poor Willoughby,” she now allowed herself to whisper—kept her mind restless. She wished she had heard his story before judging him so harshly. She blamed herself, then forgave herself, over and over. And yet, the thought of telling Marianne what he had said filled her with dread. What would it do to her sister? Could she ever truly be happy again? For a fleeting moment, Elinor almost hoped Willoughby would never marry—only to stop herself, remembering Colonel Brandon, and the quiet dignity of his love. She pulled back from such a thought. No—she wished nothing but health and happiness for Mrs. Willoughby.

The shock of Colonel Brandon’s sudden arrival at Barton had been softened by Mrs. Dashwood’s own terror. Her fears for Marianne had already pushed her to plan a journey to Cleveland, without waiting for news. The Careys were expected any minute to take Margaret away—her mother unwilling to risk infection. But now, all was changed.

Each day, Marianne grew stronger. Her recovery brought radiant joy to Mrs. Dashwood, whose spirits shone with unshakable happiness. She declared herself, again and again, the happiest woman alive. Elinor listened, touched—but sometimes wondered if her mother ever remembered Edward. But Mrs. Dashwood, swept up in her own joy, had forgotten everything else. Marianne had been saved from death—a danger, she now saw, partly of her own making, for encouraging her daughter’s hopeless attachment to Willoughby. And now, there was more: a new kind of joy, one Elinor had not foreseen.

As soon as they were alone, Mrs. Dashwood leaned forward, her eyes sparkling. “My dear Elinor—you don’t know all my happiness yet. Colonel Brandon loves Marianne. He told me himself.”

Elinor listened, her heart fluttering between surprise and tenderness. She said nothing, but her attention was complete.

“You’re never like me, dear,” Mrs. Dashwood continued, “or I’d marvel at your calmness. If I had asked for one thing above all for our family, it would have been for Colonel Brandon to marry one of you. And I believe Marianne will be the happiest of the two.”

Elinor wanted to ask why—why her mother thought so, when their ages, temperaments, lives were so different. But she knew better. Her mother’s imagination ran wild when love was involved. So instead of questioning, she smiled gently.

“He spoke to me yesterday, as we traveled,” Mrs. Dashwood went on. “It came out naturally, without planning. I could talk of nothing but Marianne—my poor child! He couldn’t hide his pain. I saw it matched my own. Perhaps he thought friendship wouldn’t justify such feeling… or perhaps he didn’t think at all. But he confessed—his love for Marianne has been constant, deep, true. Ever since the first moment he saw her.”

Elinor listened carefully. She knew her mother’s words were colored by emotion—by longing, by hope. She heard not just facts, but dreams dressed as truth.

“His love,” Mrs. Dashwood continued, “is far beyond anything Willoughby ever felt. It is warmer, truer, steadier. And it endured through knowing how deeply Marianne loved that boy—without selfishness, without hope. Can you imagine such nobility? Such honesty? No one can mistake Colonel Brandon’s heart.”

“Colonel Brandon’s character,” Elinor said quietly, “is already well known. He is respected by all who know him.”

“I know it,” Mrs. Dashwood replied seriously. “And after what we’ve seen, I’d never encourage such affection unless I believed in him. But his coming for me—so quickly, so kindly—proves him one of the finest men I’ve ever met.”

“And yet,” Elinor said, “his worth isn’t built on one act. He’s known and admired by Mrs. Jennings, the Middletons. Even I, though I’ve known him only recently, hold him in high regard. If Marianne can find happiness with him, I’ll welcome it as warmly as you do. But what did you say to him? Did you give him hope?”

“Oh, my love,” Mrs. Dashwood sighed, “I couldn’t speak of hope then. Marianne might have died. But he didn’t ask for encouragement. It was a confession—not a request. Still, later, when I could speak, I told him—if she lived, my greatest joy would be to see them married. Since we’ve returned, I’ve repeated it. I’ve given him every comfort I can. A little time,” she said firmly, “and all will be well. Marianne’s heart cannot stay fixed on such a man forever. Colonel Brandon’s merits will win it.”

“But from his expression,” Elinor said gently, “he doesn’t seem as certain.”

“No,” Mrs. Dashwood admitted. “He thinks Marianne’s feelings are too deeply rooted to change quickly. And even if her heart were free, he doubts himself—worries about the difference in age, in temperament. But he’s mistaken. His age is an advantage—it means stability, wisdom. And his nature—gentle, sincere, thoughtful—is exactly what Marianne needs. And his manner—though not as dazzling as Willoughby’s—he has a quiet charm. I never liked the look in Willoughby’s eyes sometimes. Do you remember?”

Elinor could not recall it—but her mother didn’t wait. “And the Colonel’s manners—so natural, so kind—are more suited to Marianne than Willoughby’s flashy ways. I’m certain that even if Willoughby had been good, Marianne would never have been truly happy with him. But with Colonel Brandon—she will be.”

She paused. Elinor did not agree fully, but she said nothing. Her mother’s joy was too great to challenge.

“At Delaford,” Mrs. Dashwood added, “she’ll be close to me—even if I stay here. And surely, in such a village, there must be a small cottage nearby—just right for us.”

Poor Elinor. Another plan to move to Delaford. But her spirit remained firm. She said nothing.

“And his fortune!” Mrs. Dashwood went on, smiling. “At my age, everyone cares about that. Though I don’t know the exact sum, I’m sure it’s substantial.”

They were interrupted by a third person entering the room. Elinor slipped away, alone at last. She sat in silence, her thoughts swirling. She wished for Colonel Brandon’s happiness. She wanted Marianne to find peace. But as she wished it, a quiet ache stirred within her—for Willoughby, for what might have been, for the past that could never be undone.
