Before the great mud wall was erected, the prison known as the House of Stone in Omdurman existed only as a cage of thorns. It stood on the eastern edge of a town that was already the most wretched capital the world had ever known. There was not a flower, not a blade of grass, nor a leaf to break the blinding glare of the sun. It was a landscape of brown stone and dust, where a straggling city of hovels crawled with vermin and rotted with disease.

Between the prison and the Nile, the ground was bare and broken. During the day, the prisoners were permitted to drag themselves in chains along the half-mile slope to the riverbank. For the local tribesmen, escape was a tangible possibility; the banks were lined with moored dhows, bustling markets, and friendly crowds. One could slip into the throng, find a sympathetic blacksmith, and purchase freedom. Yet, in Omdurman, chains meant nothing to the locals; slaves walked freely in fetters, and the clinking of metal was a mundane soundtrack of daily life.

For Europeans, however, the path to liberty was a different kind of hell. There were not enough white prisoners to make them invisible. Escape required armies of camels, vast sums of money, intricate planning, and servants willing to sacrifice their lives—resources that rarely materialized. Colonel Trench watched his hopes erode. Friends whispered encouragement from afar, sometimes nudging his camel or dropping a secret coin, but nothing ever coalesced into action. He simply endured, watching the sun rise over Khartum's palms and burn down, month after agonizing month.

One evening in late August, as the heat of the day finally gave way to a suffocating twilight, the guards arrived. Idris es Saier, a giant of the Gawaamah tribe, led the charge, followed by his fellow jailers.

"Into the House of Stone!" Idris shouted.

The prisoners surged forward, praying and cursing as the pitiless whips cracked against the backs of the stragglers. The massive wooden doors groaned open, revealing a chamber no larger than thirty feet square, dominated by a central pillar and devoid of windows save for tiny, useless vents near the ceiling. Two hundred souls were herded inside, screaming and fighting for breath. Within moments, the door slammed shut, plunging the interior into absolute, crushing darkness.

Colonel Trench fought with a ferocity born of desperation. He sought a specific corner near the door, a spot that offered a sliver of protection from the stampeding feet and the crushing weight of neighbors' shackles. Without it, he feared he would be trampled into oblivion. If a man fell in that swirling mass, he never rose again. Driven by a madness akin to his physical torment, Trench kicked, shoved, dove, and clawed, tearing at clothing and striking heads with his heavy iron chain. At last, he secured his position, panting, sweating, and trembling.

"If I were to fall!" he gasped, the thought echoing like a curse. "O God, if I were to fall!"

He shouted across the gloom, "Is it you, Ibrahim?" and received a muffled reply, "Yes, Effendi."

Relief washed over Trench. Ibrahim, a towering Arab of the Hadendowas, was his anchor. They had formed a bond of necessity, sharing scraps of bread smuggled in by friends or surviving on the charity of converts. Standing shoulder to shoulder against the rotting walls, they were the only lifeline each possessed.

"Aye, Effendi, I am here," Ibrahim rasped, reaching out in the dark to steady Trench.

Nearby, a violent struggle raged, causing the entire packed hall to sway like a ship in a storm. Amidst the clatter of chains and the guttural curses, a faint, human sobbing cut through—the sound of someone giving up. Suddenly, a missile flew from the darkness, and heads collided as men ducked blindly.

Two hours passed in this infernal silence and noise. Eight more hours remained before the door would open. Trench stood on his toes, lungs burning, throat parched, his tongue swollen and dry as leather. He felt the imagination of God devising no torture worse than this August night in Omdurman.

"If I were to fall!" he screamed.

As the cry left his lips, the door burst open. Idris appeared, wielding torches.

"Make room!" Idris roared.

He tossed burning tufts of dry grass into the crush, igniting the panic. The flames licked at shoulders and faces; the crowd could not move, let alone extinguish them. Whips fell in unison, carving a jagged path near the threshold. A man was flung into the gap, and the door slammed shut again.

Standing close to the opening, Trench caught a glimpse of the intruder. He was slight, heavily ironed, and bent double with suffering.

"He will fall tonight," Trench muttered, a cold dread seizing him. "God! If *I* were to..."

Suddenly, the crowd surged. The new prisoner, clinging desperately to the doorframe, was plucked away, jostled backward, and driven like a wedge between the ranks. He crashed directly into Trench.

Kindness had no place in the nightmare of the House of Stone. Survival was the only law. Trench braced himself, ready to push the man aside.

"Back!" he cried violently. "Back, or I strike!"

As he raised his arm to blow the man away, he heard a voice, broken and incoherent, speaking English.

"Don't fall!" Trench yelled, catching the stranger's arm. "Ibrahim, help! God, if he falls!"

Deafened by the surrounding chaos, Trench struggled to focus, but the accent was unmistakably his own. It civilized him instantly, washing away the filth of the prison. Shadows of words rose in his mind—images of grass, cool skies, and clear rivers. For a fleeting second, the stench and the heat vanished.

But the stranger wavered.

"If he falls!" Trench cried again.

Together with Ibrahim, they fought the crowd, wrestling the man clear. The onlookers, seeing the absurdity of the scene, cried out, "Shaitan! They are mad!"

They cleared a circle around the newcomer and set him down. Behind him, amidst the lulls of the shouting, the stranger's voice drifted out, a rambling tale of the sea.

"I saw the riding lights of the yachts... reflections shortening and lengthening... there was a band, too," he murmured, laughing with a crazy chuckle. "Bray... a little fairyland of lights... I don't think that was quite right... it is not right that one should suffer so much pain..."

Trench listened, entranced. The man spoke of sailing out of Dublin Bay, of the engines throbbing relentlessly, of a "fairyland" dwindling to a golden blot. Then the stranger's voice grew frantic, clutching at Trench's sleeve.

"It is funny that those three faces were always the same... they get quite big, too, just when you are going to sleep... they rather frighten one..."

Trench, usually pragmatic, found himself moved to tears by the sheer poetry of the delirium. "It's all right, old man, it's all right," he soothed, trying to calm the boyish terror in the man's grip.

The stranger then shifted, speaking of the future, of returning something. "Will you take it back?" he asked hesitantly. "Really? The others have, all except the man who died at Tamai."

Curiosity sparked within Trench. Who was this man? Why did he speak of taking something back? And what did he mean by "the afterwards"?

Then the name struck Trench like a blow.

"Durrance stood at the window," the stranger whispered, addressing a woman named Ethne. "After I had told them about you... he looked out across the park... I fancied he blamed me. But I think now he was making up his mind to lose you."

Trench gasped. "Is he dead?"

"No, he lives, he lives."

Impossible. Trench recalled the telegram, the silence that followed, the betrayal. But the voice continued, weaving a tapestry of the past.

"Durrance stood at the window... All the time when I was proposing to send in my papers... I wonder."

Trench erupted. "Ethne!"

Silence fell over the pair, broken only by the distant, fading moans of the prison.

"Three little white feathers," the stranger repeated, his voice trembling. "This afternoon we were under the elms down by the Lennon River... do you remember, Harry? Just you and I. And then came three little white feathers; and the world's at an end."

Trench recoiled. The pieces clicked together with terrifying clarity. Feversham. The man who had returned his feather. The man who had fled the squadron, become a spy, wandered the deserts, and survived the brutality of the Mahdist captors.
