Newland Archer sat at his writing desk in the library of his East Thirty-ninth Street home, the quiet hum of the city beyond the windows a distant murmur.

He had just returned from the grand opening of the new galleries at the Metropolitan Museum, where the cream of New York society had circulated beneath vaulted ceilings, past relics of empires long crumbled. The air had been thick with murmured admiration, the rustle of silk, the occasional clink of champagne glasses. Yet amid the glitter, something—an offhand remark, the curve of a familiar corridor—had snagged on the edge of his memory, pulling him abruptly into the past.

And just like that, the present dissolved. He was no longer a man in his sixties, surrounded by the trappings of a well-ordered life, but a younger version of himself, perched on a stiff divan in the old museum, watching a slender figure in a sealskin cloak retreat down the dimly lit hall.

The vision lingered, sharp as a blade. He exhaled slowly, his gaze drifting over the library—this room that had borne witness to the quiet milestones of his existence. Here, nearly three decades ago, May had told him, with the hesitant propriety of their generation, that she was expecting their first child. Here, their son Dallas had taken his first unsteady steps, shouting *"Dad!"* while May laughed behind the door. Here, their daughter Mary had announced her engagement to the most dependable of the Chivers boys, and here, Archer had kissed her forehead beneath her wedding veil before sending her off into a world that had changed beyond recognition.

This room had been the heart of his life.

The telephone’s sharp ring jolted him back to the present. He lifted the receiver, still half-lost in thought.

Dallas, no doubt. His son had been dispatched to consult on some grand Lakeside estate for a client with more money than taste. The firm always sent Dallas—his easy charm and sharp eye made him indispensable.

*"Hullo, Dad—Yes: Dallas. Listen—how do you feel about sailing Wednesday? Mauretania. Our client’s insisting I take a look at some Italian gardens before we finalize the plans. I’ve got to be back by the first of June—"* A pause, then a laugh, warm and self-aware. *"—so you see the urgency. Come with me."*

The invitation was casual, but beneath it lay something unspoken. This would be the last trip just the two of them, before Dallas married Fanny Beaufort and his life shifted irrevocably.

Archer hesitated, though he knew his answer already.

*"Yes,"* he said.

---

Paris unfolded before him like a dream he had long stopped believing in.

Standing at the window of his hotel, Archer watched the sunlight gild the Place Vendôme, the city alive in a way New York never was—soot and grandeur, history layered thick as the perfume in the air.

Dallas clapped him on the shoulder, grinning. *"Something, isn’t it?"* Then, offhand: *"Oh—the Countess Olenska’s expecting us at half-past five."*

Archer went very still.

Dallas, blissfully unaware, carried on. *"Fanny made me promise to see her. Apparently, she was kind to her when she first arrived in Paris. You knew her, didn’t you?"*

The past rose up, vivid as the scent of lilacs.

*"Yes,"* Archer said quietly. *"I knew her."*

Dallas studied him, his expression shifting into something knowing. *"What was she like?"*

Archer turned back to the window. *"Different."*

A laugh. *"That’s always the answer, isn’t it?"*

---

He walked the streets alone that afternoon, letting the city blur around him. The past was no longer a closed book but a living thing, breathing against his ribs.

Somewhere in Paris, Ellen waited.

And for the first time in decades, so did he.
