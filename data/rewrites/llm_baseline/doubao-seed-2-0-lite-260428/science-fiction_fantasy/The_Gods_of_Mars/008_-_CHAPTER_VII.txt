For one suspended, breathless heartbeat, the black pirate and I hung frozen, our eyes locked, the thousand-foot void of Barsoom’s sunken Lost Sea yawning beneath my single-handed grip on the anchor chain. A grim, sharp smile curved the handsome ebony lips hovering inches above my face, as a dark hand slipped over the deck’s edge, snout of a revolver glinting in the faint moonlight to press dead-centre to my forehead.

The hollow black muzzle of the weapon bore directly between my eyes. Simultaneously, my free hand shot out to clamp tight around his exposed throat, just as his finger curled tight on the trigger. His hissing snarl of *“Die, cursed thern”* was half-choked off in his own windpipe under my bruising grip. The hammer fell with a useless, sharp click on an empty chamber. Before he could adjust to pull the trigger again, I hauled him so far over the rail that he was forced to drop his gun and cling white-knuckled to the wooden slats with both hands. My hold on his throat strangled every potential cry for help, and we struggled in grim, total silence: he fought to tear free and drag himself back onto the deck, I fought to yank him over the edge to his quiet death.

His face purpled to a livid hue, eyes bulging almost out of their sockets. He knew he was seconds from death if he didn’t tear my steely fingers off his throat. With one last, heaving effort, he threw his weight hard back onto the deck, releasing his death-grip on the rail to scrabble frantically at my wrists to pry me loose. That single split second of slack was all I waited for. With one mighty downward surge, I swept him clean off the deck’s surface. His falling weight nearly tore my one remaining hand loose from the anchor chain, yanking me half over the rail to plunge with him into the dark waters below.

I didn’t let go of him, though. I knew even one sharp scream tearing from his throat as he plummeted would rouse every one of his crewmates above to hunt me down before I could blink. I held fast, choking him, never easing the pressure, even as his flailing, spasming limbs dragged me lower and lower down the length of the rusted chain. Bit by bit his convulsions slowed, until they stopped entirely. Only then did I release my grip, and his body vanished in an instant into the inky, unlit shadows thousands of feet beneath us.

I hauled myself back up the chain to the cruiser’s rail, and this time I managed to hoist my eyes level with the deck to scan the situation fully. The smaller, nearer moon had already dipped below the horizon, but the soft, brilliant glow of the further satellite bathed the entire deck in silver, throwing the sprawled forms of six or eight sleeping black pirates into sharp, clear relief. Huddled against the base of the rapid-fire gun, bound tight with heavy silk cords, was a young white girl. Her eyes were wide, brimming with horrified anticipation, and locked directly on my face the second I peeked over the rail. Unutterable, sharp relief flooded her features the instant her gaze fell on the glowing mystic jewel set in the centre of the stolen thern headpiece I still wore. She did not speak, only flicked her eyes sideways to warn me of the sleeping men all around her.

I stepped noiselessly over the rail onto the deck, and she nodded me over to her. When I bent low, she breathed the words right against my ear. “Free me. I can help you. You’ll need every hand you can get when they wake.”
“Half of them will wake already in the bosom of Korus before they even know what’s happening,” I murmured, smiling. She caught my meaning at once, and the cruel, sharp curve of her answering smile made my skin go cold. You don’t recoil from cruelty on a hideous, twisted face — but when that same savagery shows on the features of a goddess, whose perfectly carved lineaments were made for softness and beauty, the contrast is breathtaking, appalling.

I cut her bonds in two quick swipes of my sword. “Give me a revolver,” she whispered. “I’ll pick off the ones your blade doesn’t get fast enough.” I handed her one of the pirates’ discarded weapons, then turned to the grim, necessary work ahead of me. This was no time for fine scruples, no time for chivalry these sadistic demons would never understand or repay.

I crept up on the nearest sleeper. He was half over the rail, halfway to Korus before he even stirred. His shriek of shock as he woke to the fall came faint, thin, drifting up from the black depths below. The second pirate woke the exact moment my hand touched his shoulder, and though I managed to hurl him over the edge before he could lunge, his wild cry of alarm wrenched all five of his remaining crewmates to their feet.

Phaidor’s revolver barked in sharp, staccato bursts before they could fully draw their blades, and one of them dropped back to the deck, dead, never to rise again. The rest charged at me with drawn long-swords, and I saw Phaidor hang back, not firing for fear of hitting me, moving with a silent, catlike grace to circle around to their unguarded flank. Then they were on me, and for minutes I fought in some of the hottest, closest quarters combat I’d ever experienced on any world. There was no room to dodge, no room to manoeuvre — only stand your ground, parry, strike, and take what came back. At first I took more than I gave, but soon I slipped under one pirate’s guard and sent him crumpling to the deck. The rest redoubled their attacks, the clang of steel on steel ringing loud enough to carry for miles across the silent Barsoom night, sparks flying off the blades to die instantly in the thin, cold air, until a dull, sickening crack echoed out as my sword bit clean through a man’s shoulder bone.

Three foes still faced me, but Phaidor was almost in position to take at least one of them out with a clean shot. Then everything happened so fast, I still can barely untangle the sequence even now. The three charged at me all at once, clearly intending to force me back the three short steps that would send me tumbling over the rail into the void. At the exact same second, Phaidor fired, my sword arm moved twice: one man dropped with a bullet through his brain, a second’s sword clattered across the deck and sailed over the edge into the Lost Sea as I disarmed him, and the third went down with my blade buried hilt-deep in his chest, three feet of steel protruding out his back — his falling weight wrenched the sword right out of my grasp.

I was completely unarmed now, facing my only remaining foe, who had no weapon left either, his sword lost thousands of feet below in the dark sea. He smiled, baring his gleaming white teeth, clearly pleased to find he could rush me bare-handed. The huge, rippling muscles rolling under his glossy black skin clearly convinced him I would be easy prey, not worth the effort of drawing the dagger from his harness. I let him get almost within arm’s reach, ducked under his outstretched arms, sidestepped sharp to the right, pivoted on my left toe, and swung a brutal right hook straight to his jaw. He dropped like a felled ox, out cold before his body hit the deck planks.

A low, silvery laugh rang out behind me. “You are no thern,” the sweet, clear voice of my companion said, “not with that golden hair and Sator Throg’s harness. No man alive on all Barsoom ever fought like that before. Who are you?”
“I am John Carter, Prince of the House of Tardos Mors, Jeddak of Helium,” I told her. “And whom have I had the honour of serving tonight?”

She hesitated a long moment before answering, staring at me intently, as if she was trying to read every corner of my soul, measure my code of honour and my standard of chivalry with that long, searching gaze. Apparently she liked what she saw. “I am Phaidor, daughter of Matai Shang, Holy Hekkador of the Holy Therns — Father of Therns, Master of Life and Death across all Barsoom, Brother of Issus, Prince of Life Eternal.”

It was right then I noticed the black pirate I’d knocked out was starting to stir, consciousness seeping back. I crossed over to him, stripped off his harness, bound his hands tight behind his back, tied his ankles together, and lashed him to the heavy gun carriage to make sure he couldn’t move.
“Why not the simpler way?” Phaidor asked, tilting her head.
“What simpler way?” I said, confused.
She shrugged her lovely shoulders, made a small flicking gesture with her hands, the motion of throwing something heavy over the side of the ship.
“I am no murderer,” I said. “I only kill in self-defence.” She stared at me narrowly, puckered her perfect, fine brows, and shook her head. She could not understand. Even my own Dejah Thoris had once failed to see the logic of what she’d called my foolish, dangerous leniency to enemies. On Barsoom, no quarter is ever asked, no quarter ever given — every dead man leaves a little more of the dying planet’s dwindling, precious resources for the survivors to split between them. But there was a sharp, strange difference here: Phaidor wasn’t regretting the death of a living soul, she was regretting the thrill of seeing it thrown over the rail.

The pirate had fully woken now, and lay still, watching us intently. He was strikingly handsome, clean-limbed, powerful, his features so perfectly chiselled Adonis himself might have envied their symmetry. The unguided cruiser had been drifting slow across the death valley, but now I stepped to the helm to set a proper course. I could only guess roughly where the Valley Dor lay: far south of the equator, the constellations made that clear, but without the precision charts and delicate instruments I’d used as a Heliumite Navy officer, I couldn’t pin down our exact location. A northerly course would take us straight back towards the settled, populated parts of the planet, so I turned the cruiser’s nose north at once.

The ship swung gracefully under my hand, I pressed the button that activated the repulsive rays, and we soared high, arcing out into open sky. I cranked the speed lever all the way to its final notch, and we raced north, climbing higher and higher above that terrible valley of the dead. Far below us, the flash of gunfire winked across the Thern borderlands, proof of the brutal battle still raging on that cruel frontier, but no sound of it ever reached us. At that high altitude, the rarefied Barsoom air was too thin to carry sound waves, they dissipated long before they could climb up to where we flew.

The air grew bitterly cold. Breathing became a struggle. Phaidor and the bound pirate both stared at me without speaking, until she said, evenly, as casually as if warning me of rain, “Unconsciousness comes very fast at this height. If you are not trying to kill all three of us, you had better drop altitude, right now.”
I brought the cruiser plummeting downwards fast, not a second too soon. Phaidor had already fainted. The pirate was fully unconscious, and I only stayed awake myself by sheer force of will — the one carrying all the weight of responsibility always hangs on the longest. We levelled off low, flying above the foothills of the Otz range, the air warm enough again, thick enough to fill our starved lungs. The pirate woke first, and a moment later Phaidor’s eyes fluttered open.
“That was a close call,” she said.
“It taught me two things,” I answered.
“What are they?”
“That even Phaidor, daughter of the Master of Life and Death, can be mortal,” I said, smiling.
“Immortality only belongs to the therns, granted to us by Issus,” she replied. “So I cannot truly die.” I caught a fleeting, knowing grin flicker across the bound pirate’s face when she said that. I did not understand the meaning of that smirk then — later, both her and I would learn the truth in the most horrible way imaginable.
“The other thing I learned,” I went on, “is that our dusky friend here is not a native of the nearer moon Thuria. He would have frozen to solid ice thousands of feet above the surface. If we’d kept climbing the five thousand miles all the way to Thuria, he’d be nothing but a frozen statue of a man by now.”
Phaidor stared at the pirate, shocked. “If you are not from Thuria, where are you from?” she demanded. He shrugged, turned his eyes away, and refused to answer. She stamped her small foot, imperious. “The daughter of Matai Shang does not let queries go unanswered. You, lesser breed, should feel honoured a member of the holy race born to eternal life would even deign to look at you.”
The pirate smiled that same wicked, knowing smile. “I am Xodar, Dator of the First Born of Barsoom. I give commands, I do not take them,” he said. Then he turned to me. “What do you intend to do with me?”
“I am taking both of you back to Helium,” I said. “No harm will come to you. The red men of Helium are a kind, magnanimous people — and when we make the truth known, no more pilgrims will ever set foot on the banks of the river Iss. The absurd myth they have worshipped for ages will shatter into a thousand pieces.”
“You are from Helium?” he asked.
“I am a Prince of Tardos Mors’ house,” I told him, “but I am not native to Barsoom. I come from a different world.”
Xodar studied me for long moments. “I can well believe you are not from Barsoom,” he said at last. “No man born on this world could beat eight of the First Born single-handed. But how is it you wear the golden hair and jewelled circlet of a Holy Thern?” He spat the word holy with sharp, obvious irony.
“I had forgotten I was even wearing it,” I laughed. “It’s plunder, taken from an enemy.” I swept my hand over my head and pulled the thern disguise free, revealing my own close-cropped black human hair. His eyes went wide in shock, he had clearly expected to see the bald, hairless pate of a true thern.
“You truly are from another world,” he said, a faint touch of awe in his voice. “With skin the colour of a thern, hair the colour of a First Born, and the strength of a dozen Dators, there is no shame even for Xodar to admit you defeated me. A thing I would never, ever do if you were born on Barsoom.”
“You are running far ahead of me,” I cut in. “Your name is Xodar, but who are the First Born? What is a Dator? And why could you never acknowledge defeat to a Barsoomian native?”
“The First Born are the oldest race on Barsoom, the line of black men I was born to lead as Dator — what the lesser red races would call a prince,” he explained. “Our lineage traces unbroken straight back to the Tree of Life that grew in the centre of the Valley Dor twenty-three million years ago. For countless ages, the fruit of that tree evolved, shifting slowly from pure plant life to a hybrid of plant and animal. At first, the fruit only had independent muscular movement, the stem still fixed to the parent tree. Later, the fruit grew a brain, so they hung from their long stems, thinking, moving, as individual beings.
“With the first capacity for perception came the ability to compare those perceptions, make judgements — and that is how reason was born on Barsoom. Ages passed, dozens of forms of life sprouted and died on the Tree of Life, until the tree sprouted the small, tiny plant men we now see grown to giant size roaming the Valley Dor, still hanging from the tree branches by stems growing out of the tops of their heads. The buds that grew those plant men were large, foot-wide nut-like structures, split down the middle by double partitions into four separate sections. One section held a plant man, the next a sixteen-legged worm, the third the ancestor of the great white apes, and the fourth the very first original black man of Barsoom.
“When the buds burst open, the plant man stayed dangling on his stem, but the other three creatures fell to the ground, their frantic efforts to break free sending them hopping across the whole planet. Countless billions of them died before the first black man finally broke out of his shell and stepped out into the light. Curious, he broke open the rest of the unhatched shells — and that is how Barsoom first became populated.
“The pure bloodline of that first black man has never mixed with any other creature, in the race I belong to. Every other form of life on Barsoom — the red men, the white apes, the plant men, the therns — all of them descend from that sixteen-legged worm, that first ape, and the early renegade black men. The therns?” He smiled maliciously. “They are nothing more than the evolved descendants of those ancient pure white apes. A low, false race. We are the only true, immortal humans on Barsoom.
“The Tree of Life died long ago, but before it did, the plant men learned to detach their stems and roam the whole planet alongside the rest of us. Their dual plant-animal biology lets them reproduce like true plants, but they have barely evolved in all those millions of years. Their brains are no bigger than the tip of your smallest finger, their actions are ruled by pure instinct, no reason. They eat vegetation and drink blood, no sense of self-preservation at all, no concept of fear — that is why they are such terrifying enemies to fight.”
I wondered why the proud Xodar would ramble on at such length about Barsoom’s origins to a man holding him prisoner, bound to a gun carriage. Until I caught his eyes flicker, for the barest fraction of a second, over my shoulder. He was facing the stern of the cruiser as he spoke, and for one instant, that dark gaze locked on something behind me, a flash of sharp triumph brightening his eyes.
