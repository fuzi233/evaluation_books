In the beginning, when Harry and I were mere unruly children who scorned our tutor, who would have guessed that Mr. Esmond Warrington of Virginia would one day rise to command a nation? Yet, here I stand, the "Bearleader" of a continent. My mother, when we reunited, could never speak plainly of those wild, terrible years; instead, she whispered of "that dreadful time, my love," and "those years when difference tore us apart." She referred to my former pupil, the worthy man who shipped me barrels of the spirit that built his fortune, not as a friend, but as "your wealthy Lambeth acquaintance," refusing ever to utter his name or touch a drop of his beer. Even at Warrington Manor, where we brewed our own ales, the good Mr. Foker faithfully sent a couple of butts to Ipswich annually. His son, a dashing youth wedded to an earl's daughter, and his father, a kindly soul, were the architects of the most welcome guineas I ever held.

But it was not merely the gold that sustained me; it was the office of Governor, a burden I embraced with trembling hope. Mr. Foker's brewery stood near Pedlar's Acre in Lambeth, close to the sanctuary where a Bavarian surgeon, originally named Voelker, attended my wife during her confinement. This gentle physician, having met me through the Lancashire surgeon who cared for the brewer's kin, developed a deep attachment to my family. He smoked his pipes in my garden, regaled us with songs of "Prinz Eugen," and introduced me to his patron. When Mr. Foker discovered I could sing the German ballads and understand his tongue, a profound friendship blossomed. The brewer's wife placed her carriage and chair at Mrs. Warrington's disposal; his daughter adored our infant—the Captain, now a disfigured man, was once a beauty—and his son, Master Foker, a victim of bullying at Westminster due to his father's trade, was entrusted to my care.

Mr. Foker was a shrewd businessman, yet his heart was warm. I laid bare my finances to him, and his respect for me grew. He laughed at the paltry aid offered by my noble relations—a moldy jelly from Lady Castlewood, a contemptuous glance—but his eyes filled with tears hearing of little Miles's moidore. "I vow," he declared, "they shall drink as much beer as they can!" He treated Lady Maria with obsequious deference, housing her when Hagan traveled to Dublin and funding his studies. When he learned the extent of my destitution, he viewed my refusal to drain my brother's funds for Harry's promotion not as prudence, but as "exalted virtue." Privately, he lauded me and my wife to the stars, and even Madame de Bernstein, whom I deemed selfish, began to treat us with a gentleness that startled me. Later, I learned that our distant relation, the Baron, knew Foker from his apprenticeship in Munich, a connection that likely smoothed the Baroness's path to favor.

Thus, a life that ought to have been bleak was softened by kindness and fortune. My "bear," the colony I led, was a docile cub, dancing to my piping. Better to guide him than to wait in the antechambers of bookshops for the whims of managers. Yet, the shadow of Lady Castlewood loomed large. Her invitations to tea and supper were rare, for the household was ruled by the iron fist of her father, Old Van den Bosch. The Countess confessed to my wife that her husband, Lord Castlewood, was a slave to her father, who guarded the keys to the strong-box. "Law!" she cried, "if he had access to the money, he would melt the plate! We keep him respectable, or he would vanish into debt." It was a gilded cage, and the victim, my Lord, fled to clubs whenever his father's grip slackened. Would I have traded my poverty for such ignominy? No. I earned my wages, however meager, and I remained, in their eyes, intolerably sulky.

Into this world stepped a figure who seemed to tame Fortune itself: my wife. Her poverty was worn with a smile so sweet, a grace so easy, that the niggard goddess melted before her. Tradesmen bowed to her as if she were a duchess; the landladies adored her. She moved through the chaos of life with the serene purity of the lady in *Comus*. We borrowed the Fokers' carriage for drives, once stopping at the Protestant Hero for a syllabub where the hostess insisted on addressing my wife as "Ladyship." We visited Mr. Johnson, drank tea with the ingenious Mr. Goldsmith, and sought the refuge of Aunt Bernstein.

My aunt, the Dowager Countess, was a complex figure. To the world, she was a tyrant, a hoarder of resources who mocked the young Countess's attempts to emulate her. "Scraping and bowing!" she would scoff. "She ain't no more fine than any other lady!" Yet, behind closed doors, she was lonely. She spoke of her fading beauty with a tragic candor, lamenting how quickly time consumed youth. "Why have we such a short time?" she asked, comparing her brief span to Adam's millennia. "It were better not to live at all, than to live without love." And yet, beneath the veneer of arrogance lay a terror of solitude. When the Countess visited, the Baroness trembled, fearing the scrutiny of the young generation. She would bawl at the Countess, accusing her of seeking her money, only to retreat when the door closed, leaving the young lady to depart with a confused sense of being both reviled and needed.

As autumn deepened, the shadows lengthened. The Baroness, frail and paralyzed, sank deeper into a fever dream. The chaplain, Sampson, tried to comfort her with sermons, but she recoiled, demanding only her cards. "I love cards, and play, and court," she whispered, her mind drifting to a bygone era. Suddenly, she saw me not as Henry Esmond, but as the Prince Henry of old France, the lover she had betrayed. "Il a juré de m'épouser," she shrieked, "O yes! They keep their promises! Si non, tu le tueras, cousin!" The hysterical laughter, the frantic delusions, shattered the fragile peace of the room.

Her end came swiftly. A priest, a Catholic stranger, entered her chamber, and the light faded from her eyes. When the will was read, it revealed a modest fortune—pensions dead, a few hundred pounds, and jewels. Harry inherited the bulk, a sum of four thousand pounds that transformed his fate, allowing him to buy an estate and secure a future. I, too, touched a portion, a hundred pounds that buoyed me when my stock was low. But the greatest gift was not money; it was the news from Jamaica. My brother-in-law, the General, and my sister Hetty wrote of their joy, of the children's love, and of the endless stream of half-crowns that flowed from their pockets to George. They begged me to join them, promising a life of ease.

Yet, tragedy struck before the journey could be taken. Young Miles, the little friend who had once dragged his gun through a hedge, was killed by a snapped trigger. The boy returned home to die in agony, leaving a vault beneath the yew trees where I hoped one day to lie. My children would spell the epitaph, a monument to a heartbroken father's love. As we ate the turkey sent by the surviving boy, choked by Lady Warrington's stern letter condemning his disobedience, I realized the circle was closing. The troubles and consolations of life swirled around us, a chaotic dance of love, loss, and the relentless march of time.
