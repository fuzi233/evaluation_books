Twelve years had passed since I had watched the last slant of twilight slide across the old cemetery at Richmond and had seen my great-uncle laid away from the commerce of men in that singular mausoleum of his own devising. There were evenings—thick with August heat and the rasp of cicadas—when the very air above those stones seemed to hold its breath, as though the city itself listened for a sound it could no longer name.

In those years I had turned his instructions over and over like a cook worrying a recipe that will not yield its secret: the insistence upon an open casket, the heavy, old-fashioned mechanism of bolts and levers built so that the vault’s tremendous door could be fastened and unfastened from within, and from within only. It was an arrangement at once domestic and uncanny—like setting a supper table for a guest who has already been mourned, yet leaving the latch unbarred because the household remembers older customs than grief allows.

And always, beneath my puzzling, lay the manuscript he had left me: the handwriting of a man who spoke of Mars—Barsoom—as if it were a neighbor’s farm beyond the river, and of miracles as if they were chores. He was a man who remembered no childhood, who could not even offer the blurriest guess at his own age; always young in limb and face, and yet he had once dandled my grandfather’s great-grandfather upon his knee. He had spent ten years upon that dying planet beneath two moons, had fought for the green men and against them, for the red men and against them, and had won Dejah Thoris of Helium for his wife. For nearly ten years he had been a prince in the house of Tardos Mors, Jeddak of Helium—titles that sounded, on Earth, like words from a language that our mouths could shape but our minds could not truly taste.

When his body was found later upon the bluff above his cottage on the Hudson, the world had accepted the simple, blunt vocabulary of death; but my own thoughts would not stay obedient. Had he gone again to the dead sea bottoms? Had he returned to Barsoom in time—had he thrown open the frowning portals of that mighty atmosphere plant and saved the countless millions who were choking in thin air on that far-gone day before he was flung back across forty-eight million miles of void to Earth? Had he found his black-haired Princess in the royal gardens, and the slender son he had dreamed of standing there among the flowers, waiting with the grave patience of Barsoom? Or had he come too late, and so walked again into a living death upon a dead world? Or—simplest and hardest—was he merely dead, as the stones said, never to return to either his mother Earth or his beloved Mars?

So I drifted in those speculations on a sultry August evening when old Ben—my servant, steady-handed and quiet-footed—entered with a telegram. The house had its usual sounds: the faint tick of a clock, the distant roll of a wagon over cobbles, the low hush of summer wind in leaves. Even these small noises seemed suddenly precious to me, as though I were learning to listen the way a man listens when he fears silence.

I tore open the yellow paper and read:

“Meet me to-morrow hotel Raleigh Richmond.
JOHN CARTER”

For a moment the words had the same effect as the first sharp scent of vinegar when a lid is lifted from a jar you thought sealed forever—an undeniable proof that something within has been alive all along.

Early the next morning I took the first train for Richmond. The journey felt both swift and endless: the clatter of wheels like an insistent drumbeat, the shrill cry of the whistle cutting through the countryside, each station a brief breath. I found myself noting, without meaning to, how the modern world had grown louder in the twelve years since his tomb had closed—more iron, more steam, more harsh advertisement of motion. The noise seemed to press upon the mind, flattening thought, as if it were the language of a place that had forgotten the softer dialects of wind and water.

Within two hours I was being ushered into a room at the Hotel Raleigh.

He rose as I entered.

The same straight, clean-limbed fighting-man of thirty stood before me, as though time had been something he had declined to taste. His keen gray eyes were undimmed. Only the lines of iron character and determination—those deep-set marks that seemed less like age than like the grain in seasoned wood—remained as I remembered them from nearly thirty-five years before. He smiled, and it was the old-time cordial smile of welcome that made the room, for an instant, feel like a kitchen with a fire on and bread in the oven.

“Well, nephew,” he said, “do you feel as though you were seeing a ghost, or suffering from the effects of too many of Uncle Ben’s juleps?”

“Juleps, I reckon,” I answered, because my tongue reached for the nearest homely word to steady itself; “for I certainly feel mighty good. But maybe it’s just the sight of you again that affects me. You have been back to Mars? Tell me. And Dejah Thoris? You found her well and awaiting you?”

His gaze shifted, not away from me, but as though it traveled briefly through distance—through thin, cold spaces that our Earth language names too simply. When he spoke again, the certainty in his voice was like a knife laid flat upon a board: controlled, familiar, intended for work.

“Yes, I have been to Barsoom again,” he said, “and—but it’s a long story, too long to tell in the limited time I have before I must return. I have learned the secret, nephew, and I may traverse the trackless void at my will, coming and going between the countless planets as I list; but my heart is always in Barsoom, and while it is there in the keeping of my Martian Princess, I doubt that I shall ever again leave the dying world that is my life.”

He said it without ornament, yet the words carried a weight our speech can indicate but not translate. In them I heard, oddly, the notion of home not as a place on a map but as a cadence—the particular way a land sounds at night, the way its silence fits the ear. Perhaps that is why his longing struck me so sharply: I had begun, in those years, to suspect that a man can be exiled by sound as surely as by border.

“I have come now,” he continued, “because my affection for you prompted me to see you once more before you pass over for ever into that other life that I shall never know, and which though I have died thrice and shall die again to-night, as you know death, I am as unable to fathom as are you.

“Even the wise and mysterious therns of Barsoom—that ancient cult which for countless ages has been credited with holding the secret of life and death in their impregnable fastnesses upon the hither slopes of the Mountains of Otz—are as ignorant as we. I have proved it, though I near lost my life in the doing of it; but you shall read it all in the notes I have been making during the last three months that I have been back upon Earth.”

As he spoke he laid his hand upon a swelling portfolio that rested on the table at his elbow. The leather looked newly handled; the corners were softened by use, as if the thing had traveled in saddle-bags and been opened by lamplight more often than by day. It struck me then that his wonders were not presented as parlor tricks; he had recorded them like a careful steward recording stores—measured, exact, with a soldier’s discipline and a farmer’s respect for what must be remembered if the household is to survive.

“I know that you are interested,” he said, “and that you believe, and I know that the world, too, is interested, though they will not believe for many years; yes, for many ages, since they cannot understand. Earth men have not yet progressed to a point where they can comprehend the things that I have written in those notes.

“Give them what you wish of it, what you think will not harm them, but do not feel aggrieved if they laugh at you.”

There was in that warning an understanding of power—not the kind that draws blood, but the kind that decides whose words become “truth” and whose are dismissed as quaint dialect, old wives’ talk, savagery. I thought of how quickly a dominant lexicon can strip meaning from a thing by renaming it, and how easily men, made deaf by the din of their own inventions, mock what they cannot hear.

We spoke a little longer—too little, and yet enough to leave me with the taste of separation already on my tongue. The day passed with the bright, indifferent noises of the city outside the windows: carts, footsteps, shouted greetings, the bell of a streetcar. Under it all I seemed to hear another sound—thin, far, persistent—like the whisper of a wind that had crossed a dead sea.

That night I walked down to the cemetery with him.

The air was heavy and warm, and the insects sang as if they were the only creatures left who remembered the old music of the world. The city’s clamor fell away behind us, muffled by distance and stone. Here the soundscape was older: leaves shifting, a low owl call, the soft grind of our shoes on gravel. It felt as though we had stepped into a pocket of quiet where even time moved with a different gait.

At the door of his vault he turned and took my hand in his hard, familiar grip.

“Good-bye, nephew,” he said. “I may never see you again, for I doubt that I can ever bring myself to leave my wife and boy while they live, and the span of life upon Barsoom is often more than a thousand years.”

He released my hand and entered the vault.

The great door swung slowly to, with the patient inevitability of a barn door closing at dusk. The ponderous bolts grated into place—metal on metal, a sound as final as a period. The lock clicked, small but absolute.

I stood there listening until even the echo of that click had been swallowed by the night.

I have never seen Captain John Carter, of Virginia, since.

But here is the story of his return to Mars on that other occasion, as I have gleaned it from the great mass of notes which he left for me upon the table of his room in the hotel at Richmond. There is much which I have left out; much which I have not dared to tell; but you will find the story of his second search for Dejah Thoris, Princess of Helium, even more remarkable than was his first manuscript which I gave to an unbelieving world a short time since, and through which we followed the fighting Virginian across dead sea bottoms under the moons of Mars.

E. R. B.
