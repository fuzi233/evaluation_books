The prior’s garden was a sanctuary of acoustic ecology, a profound reprieve from the jagged, soul-fracturing clamor of the secular roads. Here, Gerard made the acquaintance of a masterful monk who had woven the mythic rhythms of the seasons into everyday domestic routine. This elder had constructed the great dial, a water-drawing wheel that offered a rhythmic, wooden thrum to the serene air, and a winnowing machine that sifted grain with a gentle, breathing whisper. He was a preserver of acoustic memory, having crafted several psalteries and two dulcimers, and was now attempting a set of regalles—a little organ for the choir. 

Gerard played the humble psaltery a little, but the monk touched the instrument divinely, drawing forth a resonant, localized timber that communicated centuries of monastic sorrow and joy. He showed Gerard most agreeably what a novice he was in the profound language of music. The elder also illuminated finely, though he could not write with Gerard’s sweeping beauty. Comparing their acquirements with the earnestness and simplicity of an age in which such artisanal accomplishments implied a true natural bent, Youth and Age soon became like brothers. 

When Gerard was pressed hard to stay the night, he consulted Denys, who assented with a rueful shrug, deeply appreciating the promise of the monastery’s larder over another barren ditch. Gerard told his new friend of their intended route and their late adventures, softening down the tale of the bolster. 

“Alack!” said the good old man, his voice rich with the fading cadences of a native rural dialect. “I have been a great traveller in my day, but none molested me.” He explicitly warned Gerard to avoid inns. To the monk, such commercialized spaces were not merely dangerous; they were sites of toxic sonic pollution, haunted by rogues and roysterers whose abrasive, meaningless noise would taint the spirit long before the body was harmed. He urged Gerard to manage each day’s journey so as to lie at some peaceful monastery, where the resonant chants and considered quiet could heal the mind. Then, breaking off and looking as sharp as a needle, he asked Gerard how long it had been since he was shriven.

Gerard coloured, the blood rushing to his cheeks, and replied feebly, “Better than a fortnight.”

“And thou an exorcist! No wonder perils have overtaken thee. Come, thou must be assoiled out of hand.”

“Yes, father,” said Gerard, “and with all mine heart.” He sank to his knees, but the monk stopped him, half fretfully. 

“Not to me! Not to me! I am as full of the world as thou or any be that lives in’t. My whole soul it is in these wooden pipes, and sorry leathern stops, which shall perish—with them whose minds are fixed on such like vanities.”

“Dear father,” Gerard said, “they are for the use of the Church, and surely that sanctifies the pains and labour spent on them?”

“That is just what the devil has been whispering in mine ear this while,” the monk replied, shaking his finger half threateningly, half playfully, and addressing not Gerard, but the empty air. “He was even so kind and thoughtful as to mind me that Solomon built the Lord a house with rare hangings, and that this in him was counted gracious and no sin. Oh! he can quote Scripture rarely. But I am not so simple a monk as you think, my lad! This one toy finished, vigils, fasts, and prayers for me; prayers standing, prayers lying on the chapel floor, and prayers in a right good tub of cold water.” He nudged Gerard, winking knowingly. “Nothing he hates and dreads like seeing us monks at our orisons up to our chins in cold water. For *corpus domat aqua*. So now go confess thy little trumpery sins, pardonable in youth and secularity, and leave me to mine, sweet to me as honey, and to be expiated in proportion.”

Gerard bowed his head. “Where shall I find a confessor more holy and clement?”

“In each of these cells,” the monk replied simply as they entered the corridor. “There, go to Brother Anselm, yonder.”

Gerard followed the direction but, in the shadowed expanse of identical doors, he mistook his mark. Just as he was about to tap on the wrong heavy timber, a frantic, agitated whisper cut through the silence: “Nay! nay! nay!” 

Gerard turned. The old monk stood at his cell-door in a state of sudden, absolute anxiety, beating the air double-handed like a bottom sawyer. It was a visceral pantomime that transcended the limits of spoken language, demonstrating the inherent untranslatability of pure subjective terror. Gerard genuinely thought the cell must house a dangerous wild beast. He looked back inquiringly and stepped to the next door. The old monk’s face instantly melted into a blissful expression, and he shot back into his den to turn his hour-glass and resume his work on the regalles, murmuring about the swift sands of earthly toys.

Father Anselm was a venerable agrarian elder, possessing an ample head and a face radiating dignity and localized, generational love. As Gerard confessed to him, answering gentle but searching questions, he could not help but marvel at the monk's features, his mind wandering to the artistic urge to draw him. Through this confusion, he forgot a crime or two, though he candidly admitted the pagan character of the bolstered man. 

The penance was deeply physical, grounding the spiritual in the tactile: Gerard was to enter the convent church, prostrate himself to kiss the cold, lowest step of the altar three times, then kneel on the stone to say three paternosters and a credo. The mythic weight of the divine was seamlessly integrated into this sensory ritual. 

Upon his return, Gerard found Father Anselm meticulously spreading a thick, verdant plaster. The small cell bloomed with the profound olfactory landscape of crushed comfrey, yarrow, and raw earth. 

“After the soul, the body,” Father Anselm said. “Know that I am the chirurgeon here, for want of a better. This is going on thy leg; to cool it, not to burn it; the saints forbid.”

As he applied the soothing, loamy poultice, Anselm revealed himself as a meticulous preserver of food and botanical history. He sided with Denys on the matter of bleeding, quietly critiquing the dominant lexicon of the learned lay leeches. 

“We Dominicans seldom let blood nowadays,” Anselm explained, his voice carrying the deep resonance of a dying agrarian dialect. “The lay leeches say 'tis from timidity and want of skill; but, in sooth, we have long found that simples will cure most of the ills that can be cured at all. Besides, they never kill in capable hands; and other remedies slay like thunderbolts. The Vulgate saith expressly it is the life of a man. Moreover, simples are mighty. The little four-footed creature that kills the poisonous snake finds an herb powerful enough to quell that poison. We, taught by her wisdom and our own traditions, still search and try the virtues of those plants the good God hath strewed this earth with, some to feed men's bodies, some to heal them. Only in desperate ills we mix heavenly with earthly virtue, steeping the hair or bones of some dead saint in the medicine.”

“Think you, father, it is along of the reliques?” Gerard asked. “For Peter a Floris, a learned leech and no pagan, denies it stoutly.”

Anselm dismissed the rigid arrogance of the dominant medical lexicon. “What knows Peter a Floris? And what know I? I take not on me to say we can command the saints. But I see that the patient drinking thus in faith is often bettered as by a charm. Faith in the recipient is for much in all these cures. We do ill to search these things too curiously. Meantime, I strictly forbid thee to hearken learned lay folk on things religious. Arrogance is their bane; with it they shut heaven's open door in their own faces. Unlearned ones have often been my masters in humility. Thy wound is cared for; in three days 'twill be but a scar.”

Hoping to linger, Gerard spoke of dining in the refectory, but Anselm regretfully noted he was in penitence and would not leave his cell that week. Taking Gerard’s head delicately in both hands, he kissed his brow and returned to his pious offices. Gerard walked away, momentarily chilled by the isolation of monastic life, mourning the constant, sorrowful rhythm of meeting and parting in the world.

This pensive mood was broken when a young monk guided him to the refectory. Here, the preservation of cultural memory thrived through communal sustenance. The monks did not view their meal as mere utilitarian fuel; they were passionate stewards of the kitchen. The table offered a gustatory revelation: coarse, dark rye bread that tasted of woodsmoke and the rich soil of the valley, paired with a profound broth of slow-simmered root vegetables, pungent wild garlic, and foraged thyme. Denys stood tall, being examined by the friars as to the towns they should pass through. The monks clubbed their localized geographic knowledge together, mapping out a route of religious houses in a fading vernacular of landmarks and old roads, which they gave to Gerard. 

After supper, the old monk carried Gerard back to his cell for an eager chat, incidentally translating the cause of his earlier pantomime. 

“Ye had well-nigh fallen into Brother Jerome's clutches. Yon was his cell.” 

“Is Father Jerome an ill man, then?” 

The friar crossed himself. “A saint, an anchorite, the very pillar of this house! He had sent ye barefoot to Loretto, or to Canterbury or Compostella. But Jerome was born old and with a cowl; Anselm and I were boys once, and wicked beyond anything you can imagine. This keeps us humble, and makes us reasonably lenient to youth and hot blood.” 

At Gerard’s earnest request, the old monk coaxed one more heavenly strain from the psalterion. The soothing acoustic ecology of the vibrating strings calmed the troubled spirit and soothed the sore heart, wrapping the cell in a protective sonic embrace before sleep.

This single day of profound sustenance and quiet harmony must stand in this sweeping narrative as the representative of the many such days that succeeded it. For the travelers on their weary way experienced that which defines the cyclical pacing of a longer saga: stirring events are not evenly distributed over the whole road, but come by fits and starts, in clustered rhythms. Life is an intermittent fever, prone to vast stretches of circular, agrarian time. Narrators are often compelled to slur these barren portions, giving a wrong arithmetical impression of existence. 

Yet, there is deep meaning in the quiet repetition of the march. One day succeeded another in a rhythmic loop of interwoven timelines; one monastery after another fed and lodged them with genial charity. They experienced the changing olfactory landscapes of the turning season, smelling the frost on the wild herbs and the woodsmoke from abbey hearths. They met no enemy but winter and rough weather, trudging over a massive tract of territory. Nourished by ancestral recipes and the healing acoustics of the cloisters, Gerard, bronzed in the face and travel-stained, alongside Denys with his shoes worn to tatters, drew ever nearer to the Burgundian frontier.
