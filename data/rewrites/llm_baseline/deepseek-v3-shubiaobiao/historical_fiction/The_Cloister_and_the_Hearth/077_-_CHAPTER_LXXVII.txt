Not long after, as the family in Tergou gathered for their midday meal, Luke Peterson burst into the room, dust clinging to his clothes. “Good folk! Mistress Catherine is needed at once in Rotterdam.”

“*I* am Catherine,” said the elder woman, while Kate added softly, “She must mean Margaret.”

“Aye, mistress,” Luke nodded. “She said to me, ‘Good Luke, ride swift to Tergou, find Eli the hosier, and beg his wife Catherine to come to me, for the love of God.’ I didn’t wait for dawn.”

Catherine’s hands flew to her cheeks. “Holy saints! Has he returned, Kate? No—she would have said so. What could it be?” Her mind raced with possibilities.

“Perhaps the lad knows more,” Kate ventured timidly.

“Aye, that I do,” said Luke. “The babe is failing. And she’s wrapped her heart around it.”

Catherine stood so quickly her chair scraped the floor. “What ails the child?”

“I cannot say. But it’s been wasting away these past weeks.”

A sly glance passed between Cornelis and Sybrandt, unnoticed by Catherine, whose fierce gaze was fixed on her husband. “Now, Eli,” she warned, voice sharp, “if you dare breathe a word against my going, we’ll quarrel after all these years.”

“Who’s stopping you, foolish woman? Argue with the wind if you must, while I fetch Peter’s mule.”

“Bless you!” she cried, relief softening her face. “You’ve never failed me when it mattered.” Then, to the others: “Eat if you can—I must ready myself.”

She took Luke with her in the cart, plying him with questions—sometimes coaxing, sometimes stern—until she had wrung every scrap of knowledge from him.

Margaret met her at the door, pale and trembling, and flung her arms around Catherine’s neck, her eyes desperate.

“He lives, thank God,” Catherine said after studying her face. She examined the frail child, then the hollow-eyed mother. “You were right to send for me. The babe is poisoned.”

“Poisoned? By whom?”

“By *you*. You’ve been fretting.”

“I *must* fret! How can I not?”

“Nonsense, girl. A nursing mother has no right to sorrow. She must turn her mind from grief to the blessing in her arms. Do you not know a child fades when its mother grieves? This is what comes of your books and letters—fit for men, but they steal a woman’s sense. The child must be weaned.”

Margaret’s face twisted in fury. “Cruel! I regret sending for you. Would you rob me of my last comfort? Nursing him, I forget I am the most wretched soul alive.”

“Yet you do *not* forget,” Catherine retorted, “or he would not be as he is.”

“*Mother!*” Margaret pleaded.

Catherine relented slightly. “It’s hard, I know. But think—would it not be harder to see his sweet face in a coffin?”

Margaret gasped as if struck.

“And how will you bear your other sorrows with your heart full and your arms empty?”

Margaret crumpled. “I’ll do anything. Only save him.”

“Good lass. Trust me—I see clearer than you.”

But the babe himself proved stubborn. Margaret, torn between pride and misery, said, “See? He loves me too well to leave me.”

Catherine was ready. On her journey, she had noticed a healthy young mother nursing a child outside her home. She went to her now, explained the need, and asked if she would nurse the failing infant until he could be weaned. The woman agreed with a smile, setting her own child aside and following Catherine inside.

With a quick curtsey, she took little Gerard into her arms, murmuring softly, examining him with gentle pity before settling to nurse him as if he were her own.

Margaret, frozen in shock, stared—then burst into ragged sobs.

The nurse looked up, startled. “What’s amiss? Wife, you said the mother was willing.”

“She *is* willing—just a fool. Pay her no heed.” Catherine turned on Margaret. “For shame! Here’s a woman leaving her own to save yours, and you weep in her face?”

Margaret pressed her hands to her mouth. “I *hate* her,” she whispered fiercely.

Catherine laughed. “Better said aloud than stewed in silence. Here—hate *this* if you can.” She placed the child in Margaret’s lap.

Margaret turned her face away. “He’s not mine now. She should have taken him—why leave him here? Oh!” She wept bitterly.

Catherine let her cry, offering no empty comfort.

When Joan’s aid was needed, Catherine took Gerard outside for fresh air. Margaret never protested—but upon their return, her cheeks were always wet.

The arrangement was brief. Soon, Margaret was as eager as Catherine to wean him. His health returned—only for teeth to trouble him next. But here, Catherine’s wisdom shone. Amidst her grief for Gerard, Margaret found moments of joy as each tiny tooth emerged.

“Teeth?” she marveled. “No—pearls. Each one a wonder.”

Catherine, too, was caught in the illusion. Had someone called a field of wheat as fine, they’d have been scorned from the house. Each new tooth was hailed with triumph.

Through Joan, Catherine learned that her husband, Jorian, had once served Ghysbrecht Van Swieten but fallen from favor. Now sexton and carpenter, he scraped by. In turn, Catherine shared Margaret’s plight and Gerard’s silence.

Joan sighed. “Trouble fills the world.”

One day, she hinted, “My man knows more of Gerard than he says. But he’s close as a clam. Come at sunset—draw him out. Only, for pity’s sake, don’t say I sent you. The one beating he ever gave me was for babbling his affairs.”

Catherine visited. Jorian gladly boasted of aiding Gerard—old news to her. But when pressed on why Gerard neither returned nor wrote, he shot his wife a dark look (met with blank innocence) and clammed up.

“What should I know?” he grunted. “Think the burgomaster shares secrets with the likes of me?”

“Then Ghysbrecht knows something?” Catherine pounced.

“Likely. Who else would?”

“I’ll ask him.”

“Do. Tell him *I* said so,” Jorian sneered. “Go make an enemy for me. A poor man gets naught but trouble from women’s tongues.” He withdrew, prickly as a hedgehog.

His evasion tormented them. Ghysbrecht had been Gerard’s foe—stopping his marriage, imprisoning him, hunting him. And now his former servant hinted the burgomaster held the key to Gerard’s silence.

Catherine urged Margaret to try. “You’re young, fair—perhaps he’ll speak to you.”

Margaret, child in arms, knocked timidly at Jorian’s door at sunset.

“Come in,” he called.

She entered. He took one look, snorted, and stormed out.

“Is this for me?” Margaret asked Joan, flushing.

Joan’s tone cooled. “Forgive him. He blames you for his poverty. Something about a missing parchment—he got nothing from Ghysbrecht because of it.”

Margaret paled. “Gerard took it.”

“Likely. But Jorian says you swore to keep them safe. I don’t fault his anger—so near fortune, only to lose it through those he helped.” She softened. “Still, I tell him: folk like us, free of trouble, shouldn’t judge those deep in it. Must you go?”

“I won’t stay where I’m unwelcome.”

Joan kissed the bairn. “He’s blameless, poor lamb.”

Stung, Margaret resolved to act—but kept even Catherine in the dark.

When Catherine returned to Tergou after six weeks, Margaret marked the child’s birthday by dressing in her finest, carrying her son to church, and praying long and fervently for Gerard’s return.

That same hour, far off in Basle, Father Clement offered mass for Margaret’s departed soul.
