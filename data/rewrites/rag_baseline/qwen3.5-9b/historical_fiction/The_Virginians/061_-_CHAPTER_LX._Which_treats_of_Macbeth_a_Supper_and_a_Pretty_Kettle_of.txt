The theater emptied into the cool, damp air of the street. The performance was done, the artificial passions extinguished, and the friends piled into the coach for Mr. Warrington's lodging. There, a supper awaited—an elegant arrangement funded by the Virginian's generosity. Mr. Warrington sought to impress; the General and his wife, pleased to extend kindness to their younger charges, accepted. The party assembled: the General, his wife, their son from college, two blooming daughters, Mr. Spencer of the Temple—a new acquaintance from the coffee-house—and the hosts. They ate the landlady's fare with cheerful indifference to the menu. The seating arrangement remains a mystery to the narrator, save for one detail: Miss Theo sat beside the chickens, while Mr. George Warrington found himself paired with Miss Hetty and a ham, sharing the attention of Mr. Harry. Mrs. Lambert presumably occupied George's right hand, leaving the General, his son, and the Templar to fill the remaining spaces.

Conversation drifted back to the play. Some insisted it was delightful; others, more skeptical, held a different view.

"As for what our gentlemen say," Mrs. Lambert interjected sharply at Mr. Spencer, "believe none of it. 'Tis a delightful piece, and my husband and Mr. George behaved as ill as possible."

"We laughed in the wrong place, and when we ought to have cried," the General admitted. "That is the truth."

"You caused the people in the boxes to turn and cry 'Hush!' You made the pit demand silence!" Mrs. Lambert continued, her voice rising. "Such behavior I have never witnessed, and I blushed for you, Mr. Lambert!"

"Mamma thought it was a tragedy, and we thought it was fun," the General replied, turning to his daughter. "George and I behaved perfectly well, didn't we, Theo?"

"Not when I was looking your way, Papa!" Theo retorted.

"Was there ever such a saucy baggage seen?" the General demanded.

"I spoke only when bid," Theo said, lowering her eyes modestly. "I was moved by the play, by the beauty and acting of Mrs. Woffington. I was sorry for the mother losing her child. I am sorry, Papa, if I ought not to have been sorry!" she added, offering a small, knowing smile.

"Women are not so clever as men," Hetty chimed in from across the table, casting a sly glance at Harry. "Next time, Brother Jack, pinch us when we must cry, or nudge us when we must laugh."

"I wish we'd seen the fight," the General mused. "Norval versus the gigantic Norwegian—that would have been rare sport. Write to Jack, suggest it to Mr. Rich."

"I haven't seen that," Harry said gravely, wondering if his comment landed with wit as the company chuckled. "But I saw Slack and Broughton at Marybone Gardens! It requires no giant to knock over the little fellow in the red boots. I could throw him over my shoulder."

"Mr. Garrick is a little man," Mr. Spencer observed. "Yet there are moments when he appears a giant. Remember his Macbeth, Mr. Warrington? That dagger scene! You should have seen our host, ladies! I introduced you in the greenroom to Mr. Garrick and Mrs. Pritchard. Lady Macbeth even took a pinch from his box."

"Did the wife of the Thane of Cawdor sneeze?" the General asked, adopting a theatrical, awful tone.

"She thanked Mr. Warrington in tones so hollow and tragic he nearly dropped his rappee, for Macbeth sneezed thrice."

"Macbeth, Macbeth, Macbeth!" the General cried.

"And the great philosopher standing by Mr. Johnson said, 'Mind, Davy, lest thy sneeze awaken Duncan!' Who, by the way, was conversing with the three witches as they sat against the wall."

"What! Behind the scenes?" Theo exclaimed. "I would give worlds to go behind the scenes!"

"And see the ropes pulled, smell the tallow candles, examine the pasteboard gold and tinsel jewels, and study the painted old women?" the host replied, demurely lifting a glass of hock. "No. Do not look too close."

"Nay, George!" Theo protested.

"Nay? I say yes! You were angry with your papa and me," George corrected gently. "If I may speak for you, sir, as well as myself," he bowed to the General, "I think we were not inclined to weep like the ladies because we stood behind the author's scenes. Looking close up at the young hero, we saw how much was rant and tinsel. As for the pale, tragical mother, her pallor was white chalk, and her grief merely a pocket-handkerchief. Admit it, Theo—you thought me unfeeling?"

"If you find it out without my confession, what is the point of admitting it?" Theo countered.

"Suppose I died," George pressed. "And you saw Harry in grief? You would witness genuine affliction, a real tragedy, and grieve too. But you would not be affected if you saw the undertaker in weepers and a black cloak!"

"Indeed, I should," Mrs. Lambert declared firmly. "Any daughter of mine would."

"Perhaps we might find weepers of our own, Mr. Warrington," Theo suggested.

"Would you?" George asked, his cheeks flushing alongside hers. Hetty's bright eyes watched them both.

"The elder writers understood little of the pathetic," Mr. Spencer remarked, the Temple wit dispensing snuff to the right and left.

"Sophocles and Antigone?" John Lambert called out.

"Our wits trouble themselves little about him unless an Oxford gentleman reminds us," Spencer replied. "I meant to stop at Shakespeare, who, as you agree, does not grasp the elegant and pathetic as well as the moderns. Has he approached Belvidera, Monimia, or Jane Shore? Can you find the elegance of Congreve in his comic females?"

"Must Mr. Spencer have tried his hand?" someone asked.

"Many gentlemen of leisure have. Mr. Garrick returned a piece of mine."

"And I confess I have four acts of a play in one of my boxes," George said.

"It's as good as any of theirs," Harry whispered to his neighbor.

"A tragedy or comedy?" Mrs. Lambert asked.

"Oh, a tragedy, with two or three dreadful murders," George answered.

"Let us play it! Let the audience guard their eyes! Yet my chief humor is for a tyrant," the General stated.

"The tragedy! Fetch it, Gumbo!" Mrs. Lambert commanded the black servant.

Gumbo bowed low. "Tragedy? Yes, madam."

"In the great cowskin trunk," George instructed gravely.

"Yes, sir," Gumbo replied with superior gravity.

"But my tragedy is buried under linen, packages, books, and boots," Hetty noted.

"Fling the linen out the window!" Hetty cried.

"The trunk is at our agent's in Bristol. Gumbo must secure post-horses; we can wait until he returns the day after tomorrow," George decided.

The ladies groaned a comical "Oh!" The General, perhaps more seriously, proposed gratitude for their escape. "Let us think of going home. Our young gentlemen have treated us nobly. We will drink a parting bumper to Madam Esmond Warrington of Castlewood, in Virginia. Suppose, boys, you find a tall, handsome stepfather when you arrive? Ladies as old as she have married before now."

"To Madam Esmond Warrington, my old schoolfellow!" Mrs. Lambert toasted. "I shall write to tell her what a pretty supper her sons gave us. And, Mr. George, I won't say how ill you behaved at the play!" With this final toast, the company dispersed. The General's coach, accompanied by a servant with a flambeau, waited to carry his family home.

After such an entertainment, a visit from the host seemed natural, a courtesy to ensure his guests had reached home safely. Their coach might easily have been waylaid in the open country behind Montague House; the ladies might have caught cold or slept poorly after the emotional exertion of the tragedy. There was no excuse needed for the inquiry. Mr. Warrington closed his books early at the Sloane Museum, perhaps realizing he remembered little of what he had read, and walked away.

What drove this eagerness? This hesitation? This knocking at the door of Mr. Lambert's lodgings in Dean Street, surveying the footman summoned to answer? Did any young man read? Did any old man remember? Did any weary, pulseless heart recall the time of its full beat? It had been hundreds of years since some were young; we forget, but we do not all forget. No, madam, we remember with advantage, as Shakespeare's Harry promised his soldiers at Agincourt. Worn old chargers turned to grass, yet if the trumpet sounds over the hedge, may we not kick up our old heels and gallop briefly in the paddock? I care not for clowns or fairies now; I like to see children at a pantomime. I do not dance or eat supper anymore, but I like to watch Eugenio and Flirtilla twirl, or Lucinda and Ardentio pull crackers. Burn your little fingers! Blaze kindly flames from each other's eyes!
