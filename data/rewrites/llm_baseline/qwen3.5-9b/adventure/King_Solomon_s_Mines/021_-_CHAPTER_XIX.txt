Ten days after that harrowing morning, we returned to our familiar quarters at Loo. Miraculously, we emerged from our ordeal scarcely the worse for wear, save for two distinct changes: my stubble had emerged from the treasure cave three shades grayer than it entered, and Good was irrevocably altered by Foulata's death, a loss that weighed heavily upon his spirit. From the perspective of a seasoned traveler, I must admit that her passing was, in fact, a blessing; had she lived, the delicate balance of our group would inevitably have tipped into chaos. She possessed a stately beauty and a refined intellect rare among her people, yet no amount of grace could bridge the chasm between her station and ours. As she herself poignantly asked, "Can the sun mate with the darkness, or the white with the black?" Thus, we resolved never to revisit Solomon's treasure chamber.

After forty-eight hours of recovery, we descended into the great pit seeking the exit we had used to flee the mountain. Our hopes were dashed immediately; heavy rains had washed away every trace of our tracks, and the cavern walls were pockmarked with the burrows of ant-eaters and other creatures, making it impossible to discern our path. Furthermore, the day prior to our departure, driven by a restless curiosity, we revisited the stalactite cave and ventured once more into the Chamber of the Dead. Standing beneath the spear of the White Death, we gazed in awe at the colossal slab of rock that had sealed our fate, pondering the unimaginable wealth beyond, the crushed remains of the ancient hag, Gagool, and the fair maiden whose tomb served as the portal. Despite our best efforts, we found no seam, no joint, and no mechanism to operate the sliding door—a marvel of engineering in its massive, inscrutable simplicity that likely stands unmatched in the world. Disheartened, we abandoned the quest. Though the temptation to breach five feet of solid rock for unlimited diamonds was overwhelming, logic prevailed; only dynamite could succeed, and we chose to leave the riches undisturbed. Perhaps centuries hence, a luckier explorer might discover the "Open Sesame," but I harbor a grim conviction that those tens of millions of pounds' worth of jewels will remain cold companions to Foulata's bones forever.

Yet, to complain was ungrateful. Before sealing ourselves in our prison, I had prudently stuffed the pockets of my shooting coat and trousers with gems, alongside Foulata's basket, which held even more despite being partially filled with a water bottle. Although several large stones tumbled out during our perilous descent, we retained an immense hoard, including ninety-three magnificent stones weighing between seventy and over two hundred carats each. Even after sharing the load, we possessed enough to become the wealthiest men in Europe, if not millionaires by American standards.

Upon reaching Loo, we were greeted with immense warmth by King Ignosi, who was robust and busy consolidating his power and reorganizing his regiments after the conflict with Twala. He listened intently to our tale, his expression growing somber when we recounted Gagool's gruesome demise. Summoning an ancient counselor, Ignosi confirmed that the witch-doctor had been feared since the days of his great-grandfather, a figure of ugliness and wickedness whose death lifted an ancient curse from the land. "Black Puppy, who tore out the old dog's throat," he declared, signaling the end of her reign. He rejoiced that she would not have allowed us to perish in the dark or plotted his murder as she had his father's, setting up Twala instead.

As we prepared to depart, I addressed Ignosi regarding our imminent departure from Kukuanaland. "Behold, Ignosi," I said, "you came to us as a servant, and we leave you as a mighty king. Remember your promise: rule justly, uphold the law, and spare no life without cause. Tomorrow, at first light, provide an escort to guide us across the mountains."

Ignosi covered his face with his hands, overcome with emotion. "My heart is torn apart!" he cried. "What have I done to you, Incubu, Macumazahn, and Bougwan, that you should abandon me in my hour of triumph? You fought beside me in rebellion and battle; why leave me alone in peace? Take wives from my maidens! Take my homes! Take cattle, game, and the loyalty of my Impis! If you require war, my armies await your command!"

"Nay, Ignosi," I replied gently, "we seek only our own homeland."

His bitterness flared instantly. "Then I learn that you love the bright stones more than your friend! You possess the stones and intend to sell them in Natal, crossing the moving black waters to become rich, as is the nature of a white man's heart. Cursed be the white stones, and cursed be he who seeks them! Death awaits anyone who steps into the Place of Death to find them. I have spoken. Go, white men."

Placing a hand on his arm, I appealed to his nostalgia. "Ignosi, when you wandered in Zululand and among the whites of Natal, did not your heart yearn for the land your mother spoke of—the place of your birth, where you played as a child? In the same way, our hearts turn to our own lands."

Silence fell upon the court. Breaking it, Ignosi's voice softened. "I see now, as I always have, that your words are wise, Macumazahn. That which flies does not love to run on the ground; the white man does not wish to dwell among the black man's kraals. Yet, you must go, and my heart breaks knowing you will be as dead to me, for news cannot reach me from afar."

He turned to us with renewed resolve. "Listen, and let all your brothers hear my decree: No other white man shall cross these mountains. No traders with guns and gin shall pass. My people shall fight with spears and drink pure water, as our ancestors did. I will banish the praying-men who sow fear and incite rebellion against the king. If a single white man approaches my gates, I will send him back; if a hundred, I will push them back; if armies come, I will crush them with all my strength. None shall ever seek the shining stones—not even an army. Should they arrive, I will fill the pit, collapse the caves, and bury them under rocks, ensuring none can reach the door you speak of, the key to which is lost.

"But for you three—Incubu, Macumazahn, and Bougwan—the path remains open forever. You are dearer to me than all that breathes. Your uncle, Infadoos, and my Indunas will guide you with a regiment via another pass to the north, avoiding the treacherous road of Solomon. Farewell, my brave brothers. See me no more, for I lack the heart to endure it. Hear my final decree: Your names, Incubu, Macumazahn, and Bougwan, shall be *hlonipa*—forbidden—as are the names of dead kings. To speak them is death. Thus, your memory shall be preserved in the land eternally."

Tears streamed down his face. "Go now, before my eyes overflow like a woman's. In the future, when you look back on life or gather around the fire in your twilight years, recall how we stood shoulder to shoulder in that great battle planned by your wisdom, Macumazahn; how you were the point of the horn that gored Twala's flank, Bougwan; how you stood in the ring of the Greys, Incubu, felling men like corn before a sickle; and how you broke the wild bull Twala's strength, reducing his pride to dust. Farewell forever, my lords and my friends."

Ignosi rose, covering his face with the corner of his cloak, hiding his sorrow from us. We departed in silence.

At dawn the following day, we left Loo, escorted by our beloved friend Infadoos, who was heartbroken, and a regiment of Buffaloes. The entire town lined the streets to offer us a royal salute, while women threw flowers in our path, blessing us for liberating the land from Twala. Amidst this solemnity, a moment of levity occurred near the town's outskirts. A lovely young girl approached Good, holding lilies, and requested a boon: that he allow her to gaze upon his famous white legs, which had traveled four days' journey to become legendary.

"I'll be hanged if I do!" Good protested excitedly.

"Come, my dear fellow," Sir Henry urged. "You cannot refuse a lady."

"It is positively indecent!" Good insisted stubbornly.

Eventually, yielding to the pressure and the rapturous cheers of the crowd, Good drew up his trousers to his knees and walked in this exposed state until we cleared the town. His legs, I fear, would never again receive such adoration, though the Kukuanas eventually tired of his melting teeth and transparent eye.

Our guide, Infadoos, informed us of a safer alternative: a northern pass leading down a sheer cliff separating Kukuanaland from the desert, broken by the towering "Sheba's Breasts." Two years prior, Kukuana hunters had used this route to chase ostriches, discovering a vast, fertile oasis along the way. This path promised to spare us the rigors of the mountain passes.
