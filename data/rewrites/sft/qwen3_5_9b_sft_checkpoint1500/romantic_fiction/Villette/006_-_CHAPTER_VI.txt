The dawn broke on the first of March, a pale and struggling light fighting through a thick, suffocating fog. As I raised my curtain, the heavy, solemn mass of THE DOME hovered above the rooftops, nearly touching the low-hanging clouds, a dark blue orb in the gloom. Yet, as I gazed upon it, a stirring within my soul seemed to loosen the chains that had held me captive. For the first time, I felt a terrifying, exhilarating impulse to live—to breathe the air of existence rather than merely survive its shadows. That morning, my spirit expanded with the rapidity of Jonah's gourd.

"I acted wisely in coming," I told myself, moving quickly to dress with a newfound efficiency. "There is a vitality in this vast London, a spirit that demands to be engaged. Is it not the cowardice of a timid soul to spend a lifetime in a hamlet, allowing one's faculties to rot in the silence of obscurity?"

Dressed and composed, I descended. I was not travel-worn, but refreshened. When the waiter entered with my breakfast, I greeted him with a cheerful civility. We engaged in ten minutes of conversation, a polite exchange that transformed a stranger into a familiar acquaintance. He was an elderly man with grey hair, a veteran of this specific post for twenty years. Learning this, I realized he must remember my uncles, Charles and Wilmot, who had frequented this establishment fifteen years prior. Mentioning their names, I invoked a bond of respect. His demeanor shifted instantly from wary uncertainty to warm, obliging courtesy; he assured me I resembled Uncle Charles, a compliment I accepted with grace. Suddenly, the path ahead was clear, paved with a sense of belonging.

Stepping out into the street, I found it narrow, quiet, and impossibly clean. The few pedestrians were ordinary, provincial types, nothing to fear. A surge of elation filled me; to wander alone through London felt like an adventure in itself. Soon, I found myself in Paternoster Row, the classic thoroughfare of booksellers. Entering a shop kept by a Mr. Jones, I purchased a small volume—a frivolous extravagance I could barely afford, yet one I hoped to gift to Mrs. Barrett someday. Mr. Jones, a dry man of business, stood behind his counter, appearing as contented as I felt.

Then, I ascended the steps to St. Paul's Cathedral and climbed to the dome. From that height, the city unfolded beneath me: the winding river, the bridges, the ancient spires of Westminster, the verdant Temple Gardens bathed in sunlight, and above it all, a sky of hopeful blue pierced by a thin veil of haze. Descending, I allowed chance to guide my footsteps, swept up in a still ecstasy of freedom. I plunged into the heart of the city, ventured onto the Strand, walked up Cornhill, and mingled with the throngs. I dared the chaos of the crossings, stepping into the flow of the crowd entirely alone. There was a raw, irrational pleasure in this immersion. The West End, with its parks and squares, offered amusement, but the City offered excitement. Here, life was earnest; the business, the rush, the roar—they were vital, serious things. The City was working; the West End was merely playing.

By two o'clock, faint and starving for the first time in years, I returned to my dark, silent inn. I dined on a plain joint and vegetables, which tasted infinitely superior to the dainty, insipid meals Miss Marchmont's cook used to send to my late mistress and me, meals that left us with no appetite whatsoever. Exhausted and delighted, I lay across three chairs, as the room lacked a sofa, and slept. Upon waking, I sat in contemplation for two hours.

My mind was clear, my resolve hardened. The circumstances favored a bold, perhaps desperate, new course. I had nothing left to lose. A loathing so profound for my desolate past that returning to it was impossible. If I failed, if I died far from home—who would weep? I was inured to suffering; the terror of death had lost its power over me. Prepared for any consequence, I devised a plan.

That evening, I sought the waiter's advice regarding voyages to Boulogne-sur-Mer. Time was short; I must depart that very night. Though I could wait until morning, I chose not to risk delay. "Take your berth at once, madam," he counseled. I agreed. After settling my bill at a rate that seemed princely to me but absurd to him, acknowledging his service with generous thanks, I summoned a coach. The driver received instructions to take me directly to the wharf, sparing me the watermen. However, the coachman departed immediately upon receipt of his fare, abandoning me to the mercy of the river.

A dark night enveloped the scene. Watermen swarmed around me, shouting oaths that cut through the chill air. They fought for my trunk, their voices shaking my composure. One seized the luggage; I watched calmly. But when another reached for me, I spoke firmly, shook off his hand, and stepped into a waiting boat. I commanded the rower sternly to place my trunk beside me—"Just there"—and he obeyed instantly. An alliance formed, and I was rowed away.

The river was a torrent of black ink, illuminated only by the glimmer of buildings and rocking ships. Lantern light revealed the names of passing vessels: *The Ocean*, *The Phoenix*, *The Consort*, *The Dolphin*. Finally, *The Vivid* appeared, lying further down. As we glided toward her, the black water evoked thoughts of the Styx and Charon ferrying souls to the realm of shades. Amidst the rude rowers, their insane oaths, and the biting wind, I questioned whether I felt wretched or terrified. Neither emotion held sway. Instead, I felt animated, alert, strangely free. "Methinks," I mused, "I am alive, not depressed."

"The Vivid" emerged from the darkness, a white glare against the night. "Here you are!" shouted the waterman, demanding six shillings. "You ask too much," I replied. He pulled away, swearing he would not embark me without payment. A young steward, grinning with anticipation at the coming dispute, leaned over the side. To avoid disappointing him, I paid the sum. Three times that afternoon, I had paid crowns where shillings would suffice, but I comforted myself with the thought, "It is the price of experience."

"They've cheated you!" the steward exclaimed triumphantly as I boarded. I answered phlegmatically, "I knew it," and went below.

In the ladies' cabin, a stout, handsome, and insolent woman awaited me. She scrutinized me with a mix of curiosity and disdain, muttering about the unusual hour of my arrival. Her face was comely, yet her manners were brazen and selfish. "Since I am on board, I shall certainly stay," I declared firmly, requesting my berth. She complied, though sullenly. Arranging my things, I lay down. Difficulties had been overcome; a victory was won, granting my homeless, anchorless mind a brief respite. Until the ship reached harbor, no further action was needed. Yet, beyond that point, the future remained a blank, terrifying void. Harassed and exhausted, I drifted into a half-trance.

Throughout the night, the stewardess chattered incessantly, not to me, but to her young steward, her son and mirror image. They argued, quarreled, and reconciled twenty times in an hour. She pretended to write a letter home to her father, reading passages aloud with feigned secrecy, referring constantly to a younger sister, Charlotte, seemingly poised to make a romantic, imprudent match. The elder lady protested loudly; the dutiful son scoffed. She was a strange creature, perhaps thirty-nine or forty, yet blooming with the vigor of a twenty-year-old, hard, loud, vain, and vulgar. Her mind and body seemed equally indomitable. Born to public life, she might well have started as a barmaid.

Towards dawn, her topic shifted to "the Watsons," a wealthy family party whose fees amounted to a small fortune for her. Boisterous was her welcome to them; great was the bustle in their honor. Four in number, two male and two female, they stood in stark contrast to the other passengers. Rich and confident, the two young women were dressed in garish silks, velvet cloaks, and bonnets adorned with bright flowers—attire suited for parks, not a damp packet deck. Their husbands, short, plain, fat, and vulgar, stood by them. Deep amazement gripped me at this union, especially when I saw the beautiful bride-to-be not despairing, but gay to the point of giddiness. "Her laughter," I reflected, "must be the frenzy of despair."

Before I could process this, she approached me, an utter stranger, holding a camp-stool and offering it to me with a smile of bewildering levity. Declining politely, she danced off, lightsome and heedless. She must have been naturally good-natured, yet one wondered how she could marry a man resembling an oil-barrel.

The other young lady, accompanied by a languid-looking gentleman, presented a different picture. Dressed simply in print and straw, she looked like a Quaker's child, yet appropriate for her station.
