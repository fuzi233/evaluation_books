The sea crashed violently against the base of the cliff under a strong breeze, while sunlight poured over the land from a sky streaked with soft blue clouds. In this perfect English weather, Trent—restless after a poor night’s sleep—descended before eight o’clock to a secluded rock pool, guided by directions he had been given. He dove into the clear water and swam out past massive grey boulders into the churning open sea, pushing himself forward against the current before turning back, battered but invigorated. Ten minutes later, he climbed the cliff again, his mind momentarily freed from the grim weight of the case he was investigating. As he ascended, thoughts turned to the morning ahead.

It was the day of the inquest—the first full day since his arrival. Since parting ways with the American on the road to Bishopsbridge, Trent had made little progress. That afternoon, he had walked into town with Mr. Cupples, stopping at a chemist’s to make purchases, speaking privately with a photographer, sending off a reply-paid telegram, and making an inquiry at the telephone exchange. He had said little about the case to Mr. Cupples, who appeared uninterested; nothing at all about what he had discovered or what steps he planned to take. After returning from Bishopsbridge, Trent had written a detailed report for the *Record* and sent it via the local representative. He dined with Mr. Cupples and spent the evening alone on the veranda, lost in quiet contemplation.

Now, as he scaled the cliff once more, Trent reflected that he had never taken on a case he disliked so deeply—and yet one that held him so completely. The more he considered it under the golden light of the new day, the darker and more compelling it seemed. Every suspicion, every half-known truth, had consumed his thoughts through the night, robbing him of sleep. Even now, cleansed by the sea’s fierce purity, he saw more clearly the darkness he believed lay beneath the surface—and felt even stronger repulsion toward the motive he suspected. But his resolve had returned. The hunt stirred within him. He would not hesitate, nor show mercy. There could be no hesitation here. By the end of the day, he hoped, his net would be complete. He had work to do, and he waited with keen anticipation—though not much hope—for the reply to the telegram he had dispatched the previous day.

The path back to the hotel ran along the cliff top. As he neared a spot he had marked earlier from below, where the cliff face had long ago eroded away, he paused at the edge, hoping to catch sight of the most delicate movement of water—a gentle wash over broken rock. But there was no rock. Instead, a broad ledge jutted out, a rough platform the size of a large room, covered in wiry grass and bounded steeply on three sides. On the very edge, where the cliff dropped sheer, sat a woman. Her arms were wrapped around her drawn-up knees, her gaze fixed on the distant smoke trail of a passing liner, her face lost in thought.

To Trent, trained to observe with precision and depth, she formed the most striking image he had ever seen. Her southern pallor was touched with color from the wind, giving her cheeks a soft glow. Her profile was delicately regular, free of harshness, though her dark brows arched downward, almost meeting at the bridge of her nose, lending her a look of quiet severity—yet softened by the gentle curve of her mouth. Trent mused that whether a lover might write sonnets to a woman’s eyebrow depended entirely on the quality of that eyebrow. Hers was straight and fine, perfectly proportioned—neither too long nor too sharp, avoiding the pitfall of the overly prominent tip-tilted nose that sometimes drew admiration despite its excess. Her hat lay pinned to the grass beside her, and the breeze played with her thick, dark hair, lifting the wide bands meant to cover her forehead and stirring tiny curls from the mass gathered at her nape. Everything about her was black—from her suede shoes to the discarded hat—dull black covering her down to her bare throat. Her clothes were finely tailored, impeccably worn. Though her demeanor suggested a dreamy, delicate spirit, there was no mistaking her mastery of dress, a skill honed through years of practice. She wore her body with pride, its elegant curves emphasized by the way she sat, knees clasped close. With touches of French style in her attire, she appeared modern—until one looked at her face and saw the vibrant life, the radiant strength of someone fully alive, facing sun, wind, and sea in their prime. She was neither purely English nor American, but something more vivid, more complete.

Trent, who had stopped only briefly in surprise at seeing her, continued along the path above her, absorbing everything: the grace of her posture, the play of light on her features, the subtle details of her appearance. His vision and mind worked swiftly, effortlessly—his ability to absorb detail without staring a marvel to those less gifted. Now, the sensation of beauty heightened his awareness, doubling his perception. In those fleeting moments, a picture burned into his memory—one that would never fade.

As he passed unseen on the turf, the woman suddenly moved. She released her hands from her knees, stretched her limbs with feline elegance, then slowly raised her head and extended her arms, fingers open and curved, as if reaching to embrace the brilliance of the morning. It was unmistakable—a gesture of freedom, of resolution. A soul choosing to live, to claim, to move forward. For a single instant, Trent saw her—then he did not turn back. In that moment, he knew who she must be. And it felt as though a shadow had fallen across the day.

At breakfast, Mr. Cupples found Trent withdrawn, offering a tired night as an excuse. Cupples, however, was alert, almost animated. The prospect of the inquest excited him. He launched into a lecture on the history of the coroner’s court—its ancient roots, its once-busy role, and its freedom from legal rigidity. Then he turned to the case at hand.

“Young Bunner mentioned to me last night,” he said, “the theory he’s formed about the crime. A remarkable young man, Trent. His ideas are sometimes obscure, but I believe he possesses a rare clarity of mind for one so young. Manderson’s decision to appoint him as his principal lieutenant speaks volumes. He’s taken charge of the business affairs here with confidence, advised me wisely about Mabel’s interests, and outlined the best course for her until the will is executed. That makes me less inclined to dismiss his idea of an industrial vendetta as far-fetched. When I questioned him, he cited several cases where labor organizations had attacked—or successfully killed—individuals who had angered them. We live in terrible times, my dear boy. I can’t think of any period in history when the imbalance between material wealth and moral values has been so great, so threatening to society’s stability. And nowhere, in my view, is the outlook bleaker than in America.”

“I thought,” Trent replied listlessly, “that Puritanism was just as strong there as the pursuit of money.”

“Your remark,” Cupples answered, with a hint of dry humor, “is not exactly a compliment to what you call Puritanism—a term more convenient than accurate. It was originally used to describe Anglicans who sought to purify church rituals. But your point holds merit. Consider Manderson himself—he embodied purity, self-restraint, abstinence. Yet still, we see deeper moral failures. The more we focus on the complex machinery science gives us, the less energy remains for cultivating our inner lives. Harvest festivals have vanished with farm mechanization. Inns have died with rail travel. These are just two examples. My view may be controversial among those who share my concerns, but I stand by it.”

“It needs a pithy phrase,” Trent said, rising. “If only it could be reduced to a slogan—like ‘No Popery’ or ‘Tax the Foreigner’—you’d find crowds willing to die for it. But you’re going to White Gables before the inquest, aren’t you? You should leave soon if you want to make it back in time. I’ve some matters to attend to there myself. Let’s walk together. I’ll just get my camera.”

“By all means,” Cupples replied, and they set off into the warming morning.

The roof of White Gables, a dull red patch against the dark trees, matched Trent’s mood. He felt heavy, uneasy, burdened. If a blow must fall upon the radiant being he had seen that morning, he did not wish it to come from his own hand. An old sense of chivalry, nurtured by his mother, stirred in him—but now, the horror of harming such beauty felt more like an artist’s recoil than a gentleman’s duty. At the same time, could the hunt end in nothing? The nature of the case made inaction unbearable. No other case had ever held such power, and he was certain he alone grasped the truth. He resolved that the day would reveal whether his belief was illusion or fact. He would suppress any compunction until he was absolutely sure it was needed. That morning, he would know.

As they approached the gate, they saw Marlowe and the American standing in conversation near the front door. In the shadow of the porch stood the woman in black. She saw them and stepped forward with calm dignity, moving with the poised balance Trent had expected. When Mr. Cupples introduced her, she welcomed Trent with kind eyes—golden-flecked brown, steady and composed. Her pale face, masked in grief, showed no trace of the emotional glow he had seen on the cliff. She spoke in a low, even voice, exchanging polite remarks. After a few words with Cupples, she turned to Trent.

“I hope you succeed,” she said earnestly.

“Do you think I will?” he asked.

He made up his mind as she spoke. “I believe I shall, Mrs. Manderson. Once I’ve gathered enough evidence, I’ll ask to speak with you—explain what I’ve found. It may be necessary to consult you before the facts are published.”

She looked puzzled, and a flicker of distress crossed her eyes. “If it’s necessary, of course,” she said.

On the verge of speaking, Trent hesitated. He remembered she had refused to repeat her account to him—or to be questioned at all. He wasn’t blind to his own desire to hear her voice, to watch her face a little longer. But the matter he wanted to raise troubled him—it didn’t fit neatly into the pattern he’d begun to piece together. She might explain it instantly; no one else likely could. He summoned his courage.

“You’ve been generous,” he said, “allowing me access to the house and every chance to investigate. So I’ll ask a few questions—nothing you’d rather not answer. May I?”

She glanced at him wearily. “It would be foolish to refuse. Ask your questions, Mr. Trent.”

“It’s this,” he said quickly. “We know your husband recently withdrew a large sum of cash from his London bankers and kept it here. It’s still here. Do you have any idea why?”

Her eyes widened. “I cannot imagine. I didn’t know he’d done so. I’m surprised to hear it.”

“Why is it surprising?”

“I thought he had very little money at home. On Sunday night, just before he left in the car, he came into the drawing-room where I was sitting. He seemed agitated and asked if I had any notes or gold I could lend him until the next day. I was startled—he always carried a hundred pounds or so in his note-case. I unlocked my escritoire and gave him what I had—nearly thirty pounds.”

“And he didn’t tell you why he needed it?”

“No. He put it in his pocket, then said Mr. Marlowe had convinced him to go for a moonlight drive, which might help him sleep. He’d been having trouble sleeping, as you may know. Then he left with Mr. Marlowe. I thought it odd he’d need money that night, but I forgot about it. I didn’t remember until now.”

“That is curious,” Trent murmured, staring into the distance.

Cupples began discussing arrangements with his niece. Trent moved toward Marlowe, who was pacing the lawn. The young man seemed relieved to talk about the day’s events. Though still weary and tense, he displayed a quiet wit in describing the pompousness of the local police and Dr. Stock’s solemn air. Trent steered the conversation toward the crime.

“Bunner told me his theory,” Marlowe said when Trent brought it up. “I’m not convinced. It doesn’t explain some of the strangest facts. But I’ve lived in America long enough to know such acts—secret, dramatic revenge—are not unheard of. They’re characteristic of certain labor factions. Americans have a taste for theatricality in such matters. Have you read *Huckleberry Finn*?”

“Do I know my own name?” Trent retorted.

“Well, the most American thing in that great novel is Tom Sawyer’s elaborate plan—taking days—to rescue Jim, when it could’ve been done in twenty minutes. They love secret societies. College clubs have signs and grips. You’ve heard of the Know-Nothings, the Ku Klux Klan. Look at Brigham Young’s rule in Utah—real bloodshed. The Mormons were pure Yankee stock. That’s all part of the same mindset. Americans mock it themselves. But I take it seriously.”

“It can be horrifying,” Trent agreed, “especially when tied to crime or vice. But I have a grudging respect for the drive to make life exciting despite civilization. Back to the case—has it occurred to you that Manderson might have been affected by this threat Bunner believes in? For instance, sending you off at midnight was unusual.”

“About ten o’clock, actually,” Marlowe corrected. “But if he’d woken me at midnight, I wouldn’t have been surprised. It fits what we’ve discussed. Manderson had a flair for drama. He liked sudden moves, direct actions. He decided he needed word from a man named Harris—”

“Who is Harris?” Trent interrupted.

“Nobody knows. Bunner never heard of him. All I know is that when I went to London last week, I booked a deck cabin for Mr. George Harris on the Monday boat—at Manderson’s request. He suddenly needed news from Harris—something too sensitive for telegraph. No train was available, so he sent me.”

Trent glanced around to ensure they weren’t overheard, then faced Marlowe gravely. “There’s one thing I can tell you,” he said quietly, “that you probably don’t know. Martin the butler caught the end of your conversation with Manderson in the orchard. He heard him say, ‘If Harris is there, every moment is of importance.’ Now, Mr. Marlowe—you know my role here. I’m investigating. Don’t take offense. I need to ask: in light of that, will you still say you know nothing about the purpose of the trip?”

Marlowe shook his head. “I know nothing. I’m not easily offended. Your question is fair. I’ve already told the detective everything. Manderson said plainly he couldn’t tell me. He just wanted me to find Harris, tell him he wanted to know how things stood, and bring back a message. Harris might not appear. If he did, ‘every moment was of importance.’ That’s all I know.”

“The conversation happened *before* he told your wife about the moonlight ride. Why hide it from her?”

Marlowe shrugged. “Why? I can’t guess better than you.”

“Why,” Trent muttered, gazing at the ground, “did he hide it—from Mrs. Manderson?”

“From Martin too,” Marlowe added coolly. “He was told the same.”

With a swift motion, Trent dismissed the subject. He pulled a letter-case from his pocket and took out two small sheets of clean paper. “Look at these, Mr. Marlowe,” he said. “Have you seen them before? Any idea where they came from?”

Marlowe examined them closely. “They seem cut from a small diary—October pages. No writing. Nobody here has such a diary, as far as I know. What are they?”

“There may be nothing,” Trent said doubtfully. “Someone in the house might have one without you knowing. But I didn’t expect you to recognize them. I’d have been surprised if you had.”

He paused as Mrs. Manderson approached.

“My uncle thinks we should go,” she said.

“I’ll walk with Mr. Bunner,” Cupples announced, joining them. “There are urgent business matters. Will you join us, Mabel? We’ll wait.”

Trent turned to her. “Mrs. Manderson, I hope you’ll forgive me. I came up this morning to search for clues. I hadn’t intended to attend the court just yet.”

She looked at him with open honesty. “Of course, Mr. Trent. Please do as you wish. We rely on you. Mr. Marlowe, please wait a moment—I’ll be ready.”

She entered the house. Cupples and the American had already walked toward the gate.

Trent met Marlowe’s gaze. “That is a remarkable woman,” he said softly.

“You say so without knowing her,” Marlowe replied. “She is more than that.”

Trent said nothing. He stared out over the fields toward the sea.

A sound broke the silence—a rapid clatter of hobnailed boots. A boy appeared, running from the direction of the hotel, holding an orange envelope. Trent watched impassively as he passed the others. Then he turned to Marlowe.

“Just wondering,” he said, “were you at Oxford?”

“Yes,” Marlowe replied. “Why?”

“Just checking if I was right. It’s one of those things you can often tell about a man, isn’t it?”

“Suppose so,” Marlowe said. “We’re all marked in some way. I’d have guessed you were an artist—if I hadn’t known.”

“Why? Does my hair need cutting?”

“Oh, no. It’s the way you look—at people, at things. Steady, from detail to detail. Not just looking at them, but *over* them.”

The boy arrived, panting. “Telegram for you, sir,” he said. “Just come.”

Trent tore it open with a brief apology. His eyes lit up visibly as he read. Marlowe’s tired face softened into a smile.

“It must be good news,” he murmured.

Trent looked at him, expression unreadable. “Not exactly news,” he said. “It only confirms another guess of mine was correct.”
