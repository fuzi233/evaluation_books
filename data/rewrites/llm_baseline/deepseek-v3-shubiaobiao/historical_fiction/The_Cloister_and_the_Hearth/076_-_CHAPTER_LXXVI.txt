Little Gerard was nearly three months old when a messenger arrived from Tergou, breathless and urgent, summoning Catherine home.

"Go back and tell them I won't come," Catherine said sharply. "They've got Kate."

The messenger left, and she turned back to Margaret, sighing. "Well, child, I suppose I must go. They’re all in chaos—this is the third time they’ve sent for me. If I don’t go now, my man will drag me back by the ear tomorrow."

She spent the rest of the day instructing Margaret on every possible ailment an infant might suffer, pressing coins into her hand despite Margaret’s refusals.

"Come now," Catherine said, growing impatient. "Speak your heart. We’re parting, and who knows if we’ll see each other again?"

"Mother, don’t say such things!"

"Ah, girl, I’ve lived long enough to know how often partings are forever. When I was your age, I thought we’d all live eternally. Now, out with it—what troubles you?"

Margaret hesitated, then confessed. "Your Cornelis said to me, *‘So you’ve come to share with us, eh?’* I told him I’d sooner starve than take what’s begrudged."

Catherine scoffed. "Bother his spiteful tongue! What does it matter? He’d never know."

"But *I* would know," Margaret said firmly. "Unless my child were starving, I’ll take no charity laced with scorn. Your love is enough—I treasure that more than silver."

Catherine relented, though not without grumbling. But when the moment of parting came, both women clung to each other, tears spilling freely.

After Catherine left, the child wailed inconsolably. Suspecting a pin, Margaret searched his clothes—only to find a gold angel pressed against his back.

"So that’s why she gave in so quick," Margaret murmured, wrapping the coin in linen and tucking it away. "We’ll not touch it unless we must, little one."

Days passed, then weeks. Gerard did not return. No word came. Margaret’s heart grew heavy, her mind clouded with uneasy thoughts. Her savings dwindled, and soon the cupboard stood bare.

With a sigh, she retrieved Catherine’s gold piece—only for fate to intervene. A Frenchman, sent by his princess to collect fine illuminations, admired her work and paid handsomely. Margaret knelt by the cradle, showering her son with kisses and gold, whispering prayers of gratitude.

But fortune was fickle. Martin, her faithful lodger, fell ill and died. The cost of his burial drained her purse. Now his chair sat empty by the hearth, and Margaret could not bear to see another fill it.

She needed help—someone to carry her laundry. A young cooper named Luke Peterson offered his services, blushing as he spoke.

"I’ll carry your basket before or after my work," he said earnestly. "No need for payment—we’re neighbors."

Margaret hesitated. "At least let me give you supper when you bring the linen home."

Luke agreed, his cheeks flushing deeper.

For a time, life steadied. But Gerard’s silence gnawed at her. Each day without word twisted her heart tighter, until she cried aloud, "Would that I knew the worst!"

Then came a heavy footstep on the stair—unfamiliar, ominous. The door opened, and Eli stood there, grave-faced.

"The skipper has returned," he said.

Margaret’s breath caught. "Is he alive?"

"He must be. No one has seen him dead."

"But has anyone seen him alive?"

Eli shook his head. "Not in Rome. Kate thinks he’s gone elsewhere."

Margaret’s hands trembled as she prepared a meal for him, her mind adrift in dread. Before leaving, Eli laid a hand on her head.

"Whether he lives or not, you are my daughter now. If there’s aught I can do..."

"Pray for him," she whispered. "And for me."

When the door closed behind him, Margaret sank beside the cradle, clutching her son.

"Oh, my poor fatherless boy," she wept. "My fatherless boy!"
