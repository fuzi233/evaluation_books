CHAPTER XII. MORNING CALLS. “Well—I suppose we must.” FRIENDS IN COUNCIL.

Mr. Thornton had found it no easy task to persuade his mother into making a social call. She was not one for visiting, and when she did, it was with all the solemnity of a ceremonial duty. Her son had provided her with a carriage, but she refused to keep horses for it—only hiring them for rare occasions such as morning or evening calls. Just three days earlier, she had used horses to visit all her acquaintances in Milton, leaving them to reciprocate in kind if they wished. Yet now, the Hales lived too far away for walking, and so she had repeatedly questioned her son whether his desire for her to call on them was strong enough to justify the expense of hiring a cab.

“I’d be glad if it weren’t,” she said, “for I see no reason to make friends with every schoolteacher and master in town. Next thing you’ll want me calling on Fanny’s dancing-master’s wife!”

“And so I would, mother,” he replied firmly, “if Mr. Mason and his wife were strangers in a strange place, like the Hales.”

“Oh, don’t speak so quickly,” she retorted. “I’m going tomorrow. I only wanted you to understand exactly what I meant.”

“If you’re going tomorrow,” he said, “I shall order the horses.”

“Nonsense, John,” she snapped. “One would think you were made of money.”

“Not quite yet,” he answered. “But about the horses, I’m determined. The last time you went out in a cab, you came home with a headache from the jolting.”

“I never complained,” she insisted.

“No,” he said, with quiet pride, “my mother is not given to complaints. But that makes me all the more careful. As for Fanny—well, a little discomfort might do her good.”

“She isn’t built like you are, John,” she said softly. “She couldn’t bear it.”

Mrs. Thornton fell silent. Her words touched upon a subject she deeply resented—her daughter’s weakness. She had an unconscious disdain for frailty, and Fanny embodied it in ways that contrasted sharply with her own strength and resolve. Mrs. Thornton did not reason much; her sharp instincts and firm will guided her without need for prolonged debate. She sensed instinctively that nothing could make Fanny endure hardship patiently or face adversity bravely. Though this realization stung her, it also stirred a gentle, almost maternal tenderness toward her daughter—much like how mothers often treat their sickly children. A casual observer might have thought Mrs. Thornton loved Fanny more than John. But such a judgment would have been profoundly wrong. It was precisely the fearless honesty between mother and son—the way they spoke plainly, even painfully, to each other—that revealed the deep trust between them. The hesitant affection she showed Fanny, the shame she felt over her daughter’s lack of strength in qualities she valued so highly—these betrayed not love, but a lack of confidence in where her heart could truly rest.

She never called her son by anything but “John.” Terms like “love” or “dear” were reserved for Fanny. Yet every day, in silence, she gave thanks for him. And among women, she walked with quiet pride because of him.

“Fanny dear,” she said, turning to her daughter, “I’ve ordered horses for the carriage today—we’re going to call on the Hales. Shouldn’t you go and visit Nurse? It’s in the same direction, and she always enjoys seeing you. You could go there while I’m at Mrs. Hale’s.”

“Oh, mamma,” Fanny sighed, “it’s such a long way, and I’m so tired.”

“Tired of what?” Mrs. Thornton asked, her brow tightening slightly.

“I don’t know—the weather, perhaps. It’s so draining. Couldn’t you bring Nurse here instead? The carriage could fetch her, and she could stay the rest of the day. I know she’d like that.”

Mrs. Thornton said nothing. She set down her work and seemed to consider. After a moment, she murmured, “It would be a long walk for her to return at night.”

“Oh!” Fanny exclaimed, “I’ll send her home in a cab. I never thought of her walking.”

At that moment, Mr. Thornton entered the room just before heading to the mill. “Mother,” he said, “I need hardly say that if there’s any small comfort you can offer Mrs. Hale as an invalid, I’m certain you’ll do so.”

“If I can discover what it is,” she replied, “I will. But I’ve never been ill myself, so I don’t know much about what invalids desire.”

“Well,” he said, “here’s Fanny—she’s rarely without some complaint. Perhaps she can suggest something. Won’t you, Fan?”

“I don’t always have an ailment,” Fanny muttered pettishly. “And I’m not going with you. I have a headache today. I shan’t go out.”

Mr. Thornton looked annoyed. His mother kept her eyes fixed on her stitching, working steadily. “Fanny,” he said firmly, “I wish you to go. It will do you good, not harm. You will oblige me by going—without my saying another word.”

He left abruptly. Had he stayed a moment longer, Fanny would have burst into tears at his tone—even though he had said, “You will oblige me.” As it was, she merely grumbled. “John always speaks as if I pretend to be ill. I never do! Who are these Hales, anyway, that he’s making such a fuss over?”

“Fanny,” Mrs. Thornton said sternly, “don’t speak so of your brother. He has reasons, or he wouldn’t insist. Now hurry and get ready.”

But the brief exchange between son and daughter did little to warm Mrs. Thornton’s feelings toward “these Hales.” A quiet jealousy stirred within her, echoing Fanny’s question: *Who are they, that he insists we pay them such attention?* The thought lingered long after Fanny forgot it entirely, absorbed in admiring her new bonnet in the mirror.

Mrs. Thornton was shy by nature. Only recently had she found time to enter society—and even then, she did not enjoy it. She took pleasure in hosting dinners and critiquing others’ meals, but meeting strangers was altogether different. She felt ill at ease. When she stepped into the Hales’ modest drawing-room, she appeared more stern and severe than usual.

Margaret was busy embroidering a delicate piece of cambric for Edith’s expected baby—a task Mrs. Thornton dismissed inwardly as “flimsy, useless work.” She preferred Mrs. Hale’s double knitting; that was practical. The room was cluttered with trinkets that would take hours to dust—time, to someone of limited means, was money. These thoughts passed through her mind as she spoke in her formal manner to Mrs. Hale, uttering the usual polite phrases most people recite without thinking.

Mrs. Hale, however, was more engaged than usual. She was captivated by the old lace adorning Mrs. Thornton’s dress—lace, she later told Dixon, “of that ancient English point which hasn’t been made in seventy years and cannot be bought. It must be an heirloom. That shows she has ancestors.” So the woman who wore such a relic deserved more than the usual half-hearted effort to be pleasant.

Soon, Margaret, struggling to find conversation with Fanny, noticed her mother and Mrs. Thornton diving into the endless topic of servants.

“I suppose you’re not musical,” Fanny remarked, noticing no piano in the room.

“I enjoy listening to good music,” Mrs. Hale replied, “but I don’t play well myself. Papa and mamma aren’t much interested either, so we sold our old piano when we moved here.”

“I can’t imagine living without one,” Fanny said. “It seems almost essential.”

“Fifteen shillings a week,” Margaret thought to herself, “and three saved from it. But she must have been very young. She probably doesn’t remember. Still, she must know of those times.”

Margaret’s voice grew colder when she next spoke. “You have good concerts here, I believe.”

“Oh, yes!” Fanny replied. “Delightful! Too crowded—that’s the worst. The directors admit anyone. But one hears the newest music there. I always give a large order to Johnson’s the day after.”

“Do you like new music simply because it’s new?” Margaret asked.

“Oh, one knows it’s fashionable in London,” Fanny said. “Otherwise, the singers wouldn’t bring it down here. You’ve been to London, of course.”

“Yes,” Margaret said quietly, “I’ve lived there for several years.”

“Oh! London and the Alhambra are the two places I long to see!”

“London and the Alhambra?”

“Yes!” Fanny whispered. “Ever since I read the Tales of the Alhambra. Don’t you know them?”

“I don’t think I do,” Margaret said. “But surely, it’s a simple journey to London.”

“Yes,” Fanny said, lowering her voice, “but somehow—mamma has never been to London herself, and she doesn’t understand my longing. She’s very proud of Milton—though I feel it’s dirty and smoky. I believe she admires it all the more for those very qualities.”

“If it has been Mrs. Thornton’s home for years,” Margaret said clearly, “I can well understand her attachment to it.”

“What are you saying about me, Miss Hale?” Mrs. Thornton asked sharply.

Margaret, caught off guard, hesitated. But Fanny stepped in. “Oh, mamma! We were only trying to explain why you’re so fond of Milton.”

“Thank you,” Mrs. Thornton said coldly. “I do not feel that my natural affection for the place where I was born and raised—where I’ve lived for many years—requires explanation.”

Margaret was irritated. While Fanny had spoken innocently, it did seem as though they had been prying into Mrs. Thornton’s feelings. But she also resented the lady’s offended tone.

After a pause, Mrs. Thornton continued: “Do you know anything of Milton, Miss Hale? Have you seen our factories? Our magnificent warehouses?”

“No,” Margaret said honestly. “I haven’t seen anything of the sort yet.”

Then she felt compelled to add truthfully, “I dare say papa would have taken me sooner if I’d cared. But I truly don’t find pleasure in touring manufactories.”

“They are curious places,” Mrs. Hale said gently, “but so noisy and dirty. I once went in a lilac silk to watch candles being made—and ruined my gown completely.”

“Very likely,” Mrs. Thornton said curtly. “I only thought, since you are newcomers to a town that has risen to prominence through its industrial character, you might wish to see where this progress takes place—places unique in the kingdom, I’m told. If Miss Hale ever decides to show interest in Milton’s manufactures, I can arrange access to print-works, reed-making, or even the simpler spinning operations in my son’s mill. Every advancement in machinery, I believe, can be seen there in its fullest form.”

“I’m so glad you don’t like mills and manufactories,” Fanny whispered as she rose to leave with her mother, who stood up with dignified rustle.

“I think I should like to know all about them,” Margaret replied calmly, “if I were you.”

As they drove away, Mrs. Thornton said, “Fanny, we’ll be civil to the Hales—but don’t form one of your hasty friendships with the daughter. She won’t do you any good. The mother looks unwell, but she seems a decent, quiet woman.”

“I don’t want to be friends with Miss Hale, mamma,” Fanny said, pouting. “I only tried to talk to her and amuse her.”

“Well,” Mrs. Thornton said, “at least John will be satisfied now.”
