The future sometimes whispers its warnings in hushed tones, like a storm gathering far off—its breath stirring the air, its clouds tearing across the sky, hinting at winds that may scatter ships or bring pestilence on silent fog. At other times, it bursts forth without warning, as if a mountain split open and revealed a grave from which a sleeper rises—unseen, unprepared, yet unmistakably present. One moment you are unaware, the next you stand face to face with a sorrow wrapped in silence—a new Lazarus returned.

Caroline Helstone returned home from Hollow's Cottage feeling well, or so she believed. The following morning, however, she woke with a strange heaviness in her limbs, a weariness unlike any she had known before. At breakfast, then at every meal through the day, appetite fled. Food that once pleased her now tasted of ash and dust.

"Am I ill?" she asked, gazing into the mirror. Her eyes sparkled, their pupils wide; her cheeks were flushed, fuller than usual. "I look well—then why can I not eat?"

She felt her temples throb with a quick pulse, her mind stirred by thoughts that danced too fast to follow—bright, scattered, brilliant images flickering behind her eyelids. A warmth seemed to glow within her, mirroring the flush on her skin.

Then came a night of feverish heat, dry throat, restless sleep. Toward dawn, a dream seized her—so vivid, so terrifying, it clung like a tiger’s grip. When she awoke, she knew: she was sick.

How she caught the illness—fever, they would soon call it—she could not say. Perhaps it was the breeze that carried her home, sweet with honeydew and something darker, invisible, poisonous. It slipped into her lungs, into her veins, finding already within her a fire of mental strain and long-held sorrow, and fanned the embers until they blazed.

Yet this fire burned gently at first. After two days of heat and troubled nights, no violent signs appeared. Her uncle, Fanny, the doctor, even Miss Keeldar when she called, saw nothing alarming. All agreed: a few days’ rest would restore her.

But the days passed, and still no recovery came. Though everyone still hoped, the improvement never arrived. Mrs. Pryor, who had visited daily, watched closely one morning—Caroline had been ill for nearly two weeks. She took her hand, felt her wrist, then quietly left the room. She went straight to Mr. Helstone’s study, where she remained for much of the morning. When she returned, she removed her bonnet and shawl, stood beside the bed, hands folded, rocking slightly—a motion familiar to her. Then she said softly, “I’ve sent Fanny to Fieldhead to fetch a few things I’ll need while staying here. I wish to remain with you until you’re better. Your uncle has kindly allowed me to stay. Will it please you, Caroline?”

“I’m sorry you should take such trouble,” Caroline murmured. “I don’t feel very ill—though I cannot refuse. It will be comfort just to know you’re near, to see you sometimes. But don’t confine yourself for my sake, dear Mrs. Pryor. Fanny cares for me well enough.”

Mrs. Pryor bent over her, smoothing her hair beneath the cap, lifting the pillow gently. Caroline smiled, raising her face to press a kiss upon her cheek.

“Are you free from pain? Are you at ease?” Mrs. Pryor asked, voice low and earnest, yielding to the touch.

“I think I am almost happy.”

“You wish to drink? Your lips are dry.”

She held a glass of cool liquid to her lips.

“Have you eaten today, Caroline?”

“I cannot eat.”

“But soon your appetite will return—it _must_ return… if God grants it.”

As she laid her back upon the couch, Mrs. Pryor drew her close, cradling her briefly against her chest, as though holding a fragile thing.

“I shall hardly wish to get well,” Caroline whispered, “if only to keep you always.”

Mrs. Pryor did not smile. A tremor passed over her face, and for moments she fought to still it.

“You’re more used to Fanny than to me,” she said after a pause. “My presence must seem strange, perhaps intrusive?”

“No,” Caroline replied. “It feels natural—so soothing. You must have cared for many sick people, ma'am. You move so quietly, speak so gently, touch me so lightly.”

“I am clumsy at most things, my dear,” Mrs. Pryor said. “You’ll find me awkward often—never careless.”

Careless, indeed, she was not. From that hour, Fanny and Eliza became shadows in the sickroom. Mrs. Pryor claimed it as her own. She lived there, day and night, performing every task with quiet grace. The patient protested—lightly at first, then not at all. Loneliness vanished. Instead, peace and care settled around her. They became inseparable—two souls bound in tenderness. Caroline, usually proud and self-reliant, accepted help without resistance. Mrs. Pryor, though not naturally skilled in small services, performed them effortlessly, as if born to the role. No trace of weariness crossed her face, no sign that the burden weighed upon her. There was little hardship in the work—but a hired nurse might have found it so.

Still, despite all care, Caroline did not improve. She faded like snow under spring sun, like a flower withered by drought. Miss Keeldar, who rarely entertained fears, began to notice the changes—the thinning frame, the pale eyes, the sinking breath. Her visits turned anxious. She spoke to Mr. Helstone with urgency, and he, though reluctant, finally admitted the truth: his niece was more than merely unwell. When Mrs. Pryor calmly requested a physician, he said she might send for two if she wished.

One doctor came. He spoke with solemn authority, delivered a dark pronouncement—words whose meaning would unfold only in time—wrote prescriptions, gave instructions, pocketed his fee, and departed. Likely he knew healing was beyond him, but he would not admit it.

No news of serious illness spread. At Hollow’s Cottage, it was assumed Caroline had only a bad cold—she had written Hortense as much—and mademoiselle responded with jam, a tisane recipe, and gentle advice.

Mrs. Yorke, hearing of the doctor’s visit, scoffed. “Rich idle people,” she said, “with nothing to occupy their minds, must summon doctors at the least ache. Hypochondria is their pastime.”

Meanwhile, Caroline grew weaker, her strength slipping away swiftly—except to one who understood how quickly a foundation undermined by sorrow could collapse.

Sick people often hold secrets even from those closest to them. Caroline had one such secret—one Mrs. Pryor could not at first decipher. Every Tuesday, at noon, she would insist on being dressed and seated by the window. Whether weak or stronger, she refused to rest until the church clock struck twelve. Then, as the final chime rang, she would grow calm, allow herself to be laid down, bury her face in the pillow, and draw the coverlets close—as if shutting out the world.

More than once, a faint tremor shook the bed, and a soft sob broke the silence. Mrs. Pryor noticed each time.

On a Tuesday morning, Caroline again asked to rise. She sat in her white dressing gown, leaning forward in the chair, staring out the lattice. Mrs. Pryor sat behind her, knitting, but watching. A light touched Caroline’s face—her eyes brightened, her expression softened. She half rose, looking intently outward. Mrs. Pryor crept closer, peering over her shoulder. Below, the churchyard stretched out, then the road. A rider approached—swift, clear. The figure was not yet distant enough to hide. Mrs. Pryor’s sight was sharp. She recognized Mr. Moore. Just as the hill concealed him, the clock struck twelve.

“May I lie down again?” Caroline asked.

Mrs. Pryor helped her to bed, drew the curtain, and stood nearby, listening. The bed trembled. A stifled sob stirred the air. A pang twisted Mrs. Pryor’s features. She wrung her hands. A groan escaped her lips. She remembered—Tuesday was market day. Mr. Moore passed the rectory every week on his way to Whinbury, just before noon.

Caroline wore a slender silk braid around her neck, attached to a small locket. Mrs. Pryor had seen the glint of gold but never glimpsed what lay inside. Her patient never parted with it. When awake, it rested hidden in her bosom; when asleep, it lay clasped in her hand.

That afternoon, a brief doze—more like lethargy—overtook her. The weather was hot. As she shifted in restless sleep, the coverlets slipped aside. Mrs. Pryor bent to adjust them. The small, frail hand lay limp on her chest, fingers curled around the treasure. Gently, she disengaged the chain, revealing a tiny locket—small, plain, suited to a modest purse. Beneath the glass, a curl of black hair—too short, too crisp, to have come from a woman’s head.

A sudden movement made the chain twitch. The sleeper stirred, woke. Her thoughts were scattered, her gaze wandering. Half-sitting, as if frightened, she gasped, “Don’t take it from me, Robert! Don’t! It is my last comfort—let me keep it. I never tell anyone whose hair it is. I never show it.”

Mrs. Pryor had already stepped behind the curtain. Seated in a deep armchair near the bed, she was out of sight. Caroline looked around, thinking herself alone. As her thoughts slowly returned—like weary birds landing on a desolate shore—she believed she was unattended. She was not fully herself; perhaps she would never be again. Perhaps the world of health and strength had already slipped from beneath her feet.

In health, she had never spoken aloud her thoughts. Now words slipped from her lips unbidden.

“Oh, I _should_ see him once more before all is over!” she cried. “Heaven might grant me this! God, give me a little comfort before I die!”

“But he won’t know I’m ill until I’m gone. He’ll come when I’m laid out—cold, stiff, senseless.”

“What can my soul feel then? Can spirits see or know what happens to the body? Can the dead return? Can they come through wind, water, fire? Will the elements carry me to Moore?”

“Why does the wind sometimes sound almost like speech—sing, as I’ve heard it sing lately at night—or pass the casement sobbing, as if mourning what’s to come? Does nothing haunt it? Nothing stir it?”

“It once brought me words—music I could have written down, if I’d dared rise in the dim watch-light to find pen and paper.”

“What is that electricity they speak of—whose shifts make us well or ill, whose absence or excess destroys, whose balance revives? What are all these unseen forces in the air—playing over our nerves like fingers on strings, calling forth joy or sorrow, swelling or weeping?”

“Where is the other world? What will life beyond consist of? Why do I ask? Do I not know the hour draws near—too near—when the veil must tear for me? Do I not know the Great Mystery may burst upon me too soon? O great Spirit, in whose mercy I trust, whom I have called Father since childhood, help this weak creature of Thy hands! Sustain me through this trial I dread! Give me strength! Give me patience! Give me—oh, _give me_ FAITH!”

She sank back onto the pillow. Mrs. Pryor slipped quietly from the room. She returned shortly, appearing calm, as if she had not heard the prayer.

The next day, visitors came. Word had spread that Miss Helstone was worse. Mr. Hall and his sister Margaret arrived. Both left the room in tears—shocked by how changed she had become. Hortense Moore came. Caroline seemed brighter in her presence. She smiled, assured her she was not seriously ill, spoke cheerfully in a low voice. During her visit, color returned to her cheeks. She looked better.

“How is Mr. Robert?” Mrs. Pryor asked as Hortense prepared to leave.

“He was well when he left.”

“Left? Is he gone from home?”

Hortense explained that police matters—about the rioters he was pursuing—had summoned him to Birmingham. He might not return for two weeks.

“He doesn’t know Miss Helstone is very ill?”

“No. Like me, he thought she only had a cold.”

Afterward, Mrs. Pryor avoided the bedside for an hour. She heard Caroline weep—and dared not watch.

As evening fell, she brought tea. Caroline opened her eyes from a brief sleep, glanced at her nurse with confusion.

“I smelt honeysuckles in the glen this summer morning,” she said, “standing at the counting-house window.”

Words like these, spoken from pale lips, pierce the heart deeper than steel. Romantic in books, they wound the soul in reality.

“My darling, do you know me?” Mrs. Pryor asked.

“I went in to call Robert to breakfast. I’ve been with him in the garden. He asked me to go. A heavy dew has refreshed the flowers. The peaches are ripening.”

“My darling! My darling!” the nurse repeated, over and over.

“I thought it was daylight—long after sunrise. It looks dark. Is the moon set?”

The moon, newly risen, gazed full and mild upon her. Floating in blue space, it watched her, unclouded.

“Then it’s not morning? I’m not at the cottage? Who is this? I see a shape at my bedside.”

“It is I—your friend—your nurse—your…” Mrs. Pryor paused, then whispered, “Lean your head on my shoulder. Collect yourself.” Lower, almost to herself: “O God, have mercy! Give _her_ life, and _me_ strength! Send me courage! Teach me words!”

Minutes passed in silence. The patient lay still, resting in the trembling arms, on the beating heart of the nurse.

“I am better now,” Caroline whispered. “Much better. I know where I am. This is Mrs. Pryor. I was dreaming. I talk when I wake from dreams—people do in sickness. How fast your heart beats, ma'am! Do not be afraid.”

“It is not fear, child—only anxiety. It will pass. I’ve brought you tea, Cary. Your uncle made it himself. He says he makes better tea than any housewife. Taste it. He worries about your eating so little. He’d be glad to see your appetite return.”

“I am thirsty. Let me drink.”

She drank eagerly.

“What time is it, ma'am?” she asked.

“Past nine.”

“Not later? Oh! I have a long night ahead. But the tea has strengthened me. I will sit up.”

Mrs. Pryor raised her, arranged the pillows.

“Thank Heaven,” Caroline said, “I am not always this miserable. The afternoon was hard after Hortense left. Perhaps the evening will be better. It’s a fine night, I think? The moon shines clearly.”

“Very fine—a perfect summer night. The old church-tower gleams white, almost silver.”

“And the churchyard—does it look peaceful?”

“Yes. And the garden. Dew sparkles on the leaves.”

“Can you see many weeds and nettles among the graves? Or do they look turfy and flowery?”

“I see closed daisy-heads gleaming like pearls on some mounds. Thomas has cut the dock-leaves and cleared the grass.”

“I always like that done. It soothes the mind to see order. And I suppose the moonlight shines softly inside the church now. It falls through the east window, full on the Helstone monument. When I close my eyes, I see poor papa’s epitaph—black letters on white marble. There’s room below for more names.”

“William Farren came this morning to tend your flowers. He feared, now you cannot care for them, they’d be neglected. He took two of your favorites home to nurse.”

“If I were to make a will,” Caroline said, “I’d leave William all my plants; Shirley my trinkets—except one, which must never leave my neck; and you, ma'am, my books.”

After a pause: “Mrs. Pryor, I have a longing—for something.”

“For what, Caroline?”

“You know I love to hear you sing. Sing me a hymn. That one—‘Our God, our help in ages past…’”

Mrs. Pryor sang.

No wonder Caroline loved her voice. Even in speech, it was sweet, clear as silver. In song, it was nearly divine—purer than flute or harp. But the tone was secondary to the emotion beneath—a tender quiver from a heart deeply moved.

Servants in the kitchen stole to the stairs to listen. Old Helstone, walking in the garden and pondering the fragility of women, stopped among the borders, drawn by the mournful melody. He could not say why it reminded him of his lost wife, nor why it stirred greater concern for Caroline’s fading youth. He was glad he had promised to visit Wynne, the magistrate, that evening. Gloomy thoughts he disliked. When they came, he forced them away. The hymn followed him faintly across the fields. He quickened his pace, trying to escape its echo.

“Thy word commands our flesh to dust,
‘Return, ye sons of men.’
All nations rose from earth at first,
And turn to earth again.

“A thousand ages in Thy sight
Are like an evening gone—
Short as the watch that ends the night
Before the rising sun.

“Time, like an ever-rolling stream,
Bears all its sons away;
They fly, forgotten, as a dream
Dies at the opening day.

“Like flowery fields, the nations stand,
Fresh in the morning light;
The flowers beneath the mower’s hand
Lie withering ere ‘tis night.

“Our God, our help in ages past,
Our hope for years to come,
Be Thou our guard while troubles last—
O Father, be our home!”

“Now sing a song—a Scottish song,” Caroline suggested. “‘Ye banks and braes o’ bonnie Doon.’”

Mrs. Pryor tried. At the end of the first verse, she stopped. She could not go on. Her heart overflowed.

“You are weeping,” Caroline said gently. “Come here—I’ll comfort you.”

Mrs. Pryor came. She sat on the edge of the bed, letting the thin arms wrap around her.

“You often soothe me,” the girl murmured, kissing her cheek. “Let me soothe you. I hope,” she added, “it is not for me you weep?”

No answer.

“Do you think I shall not recover? I don’t feel _very_ ill—only weak.”

“But your mind, Caroline—your mind is broken. Your heart is shattered. You’ve been so neglected, so cast aside, left so alone.”

“I believe grief has always been my worst illness. I sometimes think—if happiness came in abundance—I might revive yet.”

“Do you wish to live?”

“I have no purpose.”

“You love me, Caroline?”

“Very much—truly—inexpressibly. Just now, I feel as if I could grow into your heart.”

“I will return directly, dear,” Mrs. Pryor said, laying her down.

She slipped to the door, turned the key softly, locked it, and returned. She leaned over, drew back the curtain to let in the moonlight, and studied Caroline’s face.

“Then,” she said, voice changed, swift and fierce, “if you love me—if you feel you could ‘grow to my heart’—you will not be shocked to know that _this_ heart is the source of yours—that from _my_ veins flows the blood in _yours_; that you are _mine_—my daughter—my own child.”

“Mrs. Pryor…”

“My own child!”

“That means—you adopted me?”

“It means that, if I gave you nothing else, I gave you life. I bore you. I nursed you. I am your true mother. No other woman can claim the title. It is _mine_.”

“But Mrs. James Helstone—my father’s wife—whom I never saw—she is my mother?”

“She _is_ your mother. James Helstone was _my_ husband. I say you are _mine_. I have proved it. I thought perhaps you were entirely his—that would have been cruel. But it is not so. God allowed me to be the parent of your mind. It belongs to me. It is my right. These features—James’s. He had a fine face when young, unmarred by sin. Papa, my darling, gave you your blue eyes, your soft brown hair, the oval of your face, the symmetry of your features—the outside, he gave. But the heart and the mind are _mine_. The seeds are from _me_, and they have grown—developed—to excellence. I love my child, and I honor her.”

“Is this true? Is it no dream?”

Mrs. Pryor did not answer. She only held her, and the moon watched silently above.
