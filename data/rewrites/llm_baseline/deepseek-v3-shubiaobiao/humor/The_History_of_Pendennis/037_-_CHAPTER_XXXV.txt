On the appointed evening, our two heroes presented themselves at Mr. Bungay’s residence in Paternoster Row—not the bustling trade entrance, where hopeful authors lurked like spectral petitioners, but the private door through which the resplendent Mrs. Bungay made her daily exits, sweeping into her carriage under the envious gaze of Mrs. Bacon across the street.

Mrs. Bacon, still tragically carriage-less, had perfected the art of passive aggression. Whenever Flora Bungay’s chaise rolled into view, she would fling open her drawing-room window and station her four cherubs on full display, as if to say, *"Behold the fruits of my virtue—you, madam, are but a gilded husk!"* And thus, armed with maternal artillery, Emma Bacon volleyed silent triumph at her childless rival.

As Pen and Warrington arrived, a cab and carriage pulled up to the Bacon residence. Out tumbled Dr. Slocum, his bulk as ponderous as his prose, followed by two waistcoats so blindingly white they could’ve been mistaken for beacons of literary pretension.

"Ah, Bacon’s assembled his own circus," Warrington muttered. "That’s Hoolan from Bungay’s stable—though you’d hardly recognize him without his habitual ink stains." Indeed, Messrs. Hoolan and Doolan emerged from a cab, bickering over the fare like schoolboys. Doolan, clad in funereal black, sported gloves so large they might’ve doubled as dinner napkins, which he admired with the pride of a man who’d just invented hands.

Inside, a squadron of stiff-backed footmen (gloved in Berlin’s finest) announced the guests with all the subtlety of town criers. Mrs. Bungay, resplendent in red satin and a turban that defied gravity, performed cursory welcomes before retreating to her window to tally the carriages arriving at Mrs. Bacon’s—each one a dagger to her social aspirations.

The guest list was a veritable menagerie of ink-stained oddities:

- **Mr. Bole**, the magazine’s true editor, lurking in the shadow of the nominal figurehead, Mr. Wagg.
- **Mr. Trotter**, a poet who’d traded sonnets of despair for the steady wage of a publisher’s reader.
- **Captain Sumph**, a professional name-dropper whose entire personality was a footnote to Lord Byron’s biography. ("*I once wrote Shelley’s verses for him at Eton—poor chap couldn’t rhyme to save his life!*")

Mrs. Bungay hung on Sumph’s every word, for nothing thrilled her more than secondhand aristocracy. Had he arrived in his own carriage, she’d have commissioned a trilogy on the spot.

Then—**the Honourable Percy Popjoy** arrived, lacquered boots first, his face a perfect study in cheerful vacuity. Nature, having gifted him a chin-tuft, abandoned further efforts, leaving the rest of his countenance as smooth as a billiard ball.

"*Quiz?* Me? Never made a joke in my—hullo! Pendennis! Warrington! What the devil are *you* doing here?" Percy exclaimed, promptly forgetting his hostess existed.

The grand finale was the entrance of **Mr. Wagg** (a walking pun factory) and **Mr. Wenham** (a man so unctuous he could’ve lubricated a steam engine). Wagg, ever the showman, regaled the room with tales of Baymouth and Clavering Park, while Wenham murmured flattery with the precision of a Swiss watch.

**Miss Bunion**, the evening’s poetical spectacle, was not the ethereal muse Pen had imagined. Instead, she was a formidable woman who creaked into the room like a ship under full sail, trailing straw from her omnibus ride. ("*Sixpence all the way from Brompton—what economy!*") She ate with gusto, ignored Pen entirely, and later confessed she’d mistaken him for "one of those Mayfair prigs."

Dinner was a parade of pretension:

- **Wagg** declared the sherry "filthy" (it was).
- **Popjoy** praised the *cordon bleu* chef (the dishes were from Griggs’ catering).
- **Shandon**, outshone and over-policed by Bungay’s footmen, drank in grim silence while his wife watched him with quiet despair.

Conversation veered from Popjoy’s "novel" (ghostwritten from an old magazine serial) to the launch of Bungay’s new paper, the *Pall Mall Gazette*—a title Wagg insisted should be called *The Small Beer Chronicle*.

"*Why Pall Mall?*" Wagg sneered.

"*Because the editor’s Irish, the publisher’s in Paternoster Row, and the office is in Catherine Street,*" Shandon snapped. "*Must everything make sense?*"

And so, amid clinking glasses and clashing egos, the feast unfolded—a masterclass in ambition, envy, and the eternal quest for a second helping of praise.
