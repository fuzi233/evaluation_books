*"Trust in that veiled hand, which leads
None by the path that he would go;
And always be for change prepared,
For the world’s law is ebb and flow."*

Dr. Donaldson arrived the following afternoon to examine Mrs. Hale, but Margaret found herself shut out—excluded while Dixon was permitted to stay. The old distance, which she had hoped was bridged, had returned. She paced her mother’s adjoining bedroom, her ears straining for any sound. A stifled moan sent her hands clenching, her breath catching. Then silence again—until the scrape of chairs, murmured voices, the doctor’s departure.

She intercepted him in the hall.

"Father is away just now. Would you speak with me in his study?"

Dixon protested, but Margaret swept past, assuming an authority she did not feel. The brief amusement at her own severity faded as she faced the doctor, her voice steady but her pulse wild.

"Tell me plainly—what is wrong with my mother?"

Dr. Donaldson hesitated, then sighed. "Your mother wished you kept ignorant, but you are not a child to be sheltered from truth."

Margaret whitened, her grip on the chair tightening. "Then say it."

Two sentences. That was all it took. Her vision darkened at the edges, her lungs empty of air. When she could speak again, her voice was low.

"Thank you for your honesty. I have feared this for weeks." A tremor ran through her. "Will she suffer?"

"Science grants us some relief," he said gently. "But your father—"

"I will tell him when he is ready. Not yet."

The doctor nodded. "Then I leave her in your hands. You are stronger than you know."

Margaret did not weep until he was gone. Then, alone in the study, she pressed her hands to her face. *Oh, God, let her pain be brief. Let me bear it for her.*

Upstairs, Mrs. Hale sat wrapped in shawls, her weariness lending her an unnatural calm.

"Margaret," she chided softly, seeing her daughter’s face, "you’ve spoken to him."

Margaret fell to her knees, clutching her mother’s hands. "Let me care for you. Let me try."

Mrs. Hale shuddered. "You don’t know what you ask."

"I know enough. And I will learn the rest."

A faint pressure returned Margaret’s grasp. "Then you may stay. Dixon thought—"

"Dixon underestimates me," Margaret said fiercely. "Do not let her doubts stand between us."

They spoke of Helstone then—its makeshift comforts, its lost joys. But when Frederick’s name arose, Mrs. Hale shattered.

"Frederick! Come to me—" Her cries were raw, frantic. Margaret called for Dixon, who scolded but relented, bringing coffee afterward with grudging care.

"You’ll not tell your father?" Dixon muttered.

"No. He could not endure it." Yet even as she said it, Margaret’s composure broke.

Dixon softened. "Ah, child. You’ve her spirit, even if you lack her beauty."

Margaret kissed the old servant’s cheek before stepping outside, the weight of the future heavy upon her.

Dixon watched her go. "Three people I’ve loved in this world," she murmured. "Missus, Master Frederick, and her. The rest can hang."
