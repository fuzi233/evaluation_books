To appease the ghosts painted upon the walls, the living must perform a ritual of restoration. In the quiet house of Broad Place, where dust motes danced in shafts of pale light, Lieutenant Sutch was already awake, vibrating with a kinetic energy that threatened to tear the fabric of the domestic sphere apart. Though he retired late, dawn found him thrashing through the corridors, a whirlwind of frantic motion that doubled the duration of every mundane task. Thirty years of stagnation on the world's half-pay list had calcified his existence into a hollow routine, a static tableau of inaction. Now, miraculously, the dam had broken. Something vital demanded his presence, his vigor, his decisive will. He was to be employed again, and the sheer terror of returning to obscurity fueled a boyish fever of anticipation that manifested as chaos. His servants scrambled like startled birds, unable to keep pace with his insatiable demand for the forgotten artifacts of his past travels, but Sutch cared little for their exhaustion; he was feverish to begin, dreading the brief interlude before his mission commenced.

"I shall be ready this afternoon," he declared to Durrance as they broke their fast, his voice sharp with urgency. "I shall catch the night mail to the Continent. We might ascend to London together; London lies on your path to Wiesbaden."

"No," Durrance replied, the weight of a secret pressing against his ribs. "I have one final obligation in England. It occurred to me only last night, tucked beneath the covers. You planted the seed in my mind."

Sutch paused, his knife hovering mid-air, curiosity warring with his haste. "And whom do you intend to visit?"

"General Feversham," Durrance said, the name hanging heavy in the air.

Sutch dropped his utensil, the clatter echoing sharply. Surprise rippled across his face. "Why? What compels you to seek him out?"

"To inform him of Harry's redemption," Durrance explained, his tone steady despite the turmoil within. "To tell him that honor is being reclaimed, hour by hour. Last night, you swore a vow of silence regarding Harry's intentions or successes until the son returned. Yet I am bound by no such oath. Such a pledge feels like a cruel yoke upon the General's shoulders. Nothing could wound him more profoundly than the confirmation that his son was a coward. Harry might have stolen or slaughtered, and the old man would have welcomed those sins as preferable to this stain of cowardice. I shall cross into Surrey today and declare that Harry was never a coward."

Sutch shook his head, a gesture of weary disbelief. "He will not comprehend. Naturally, he will be grateful, relieved that the family disgrace has been washed away. But he will never grasp the mechanism of his son's fall. Ultimately, he will rejoice merely because the armor of the Feversham name is intact."

"I dissent," Durrance countered, his eyes hardening. "I suspect the old man loves his son, though he would bury that sentiment under layers of stoicism. I hold a certain regard for General Feversham myself."

Sutch had seen little of the General in the intervening five years, harboring a grudge born of shared guilt. He could not forgive the General for his role in Harry's ruin. Had the General possessed the capacity for empathy, understanding the unique texture of the boy's soul, the white feathers would never have stained the letterbox at Ramelton. In Sutch's imagination, the General sat rigidly upon his terrace at Broad Place, oblivious to his own culpability, posing instead as a martyr to a tragedy he had inadvertently authored. He saw the old man surrounded by the darkened portraits of ancestors in the hall, a statue of stubborn pride refusing to acknowledge the fracture in his lineage. Sutch believed patience was a futile currency here; arguments would only harden the General's resolve. Consequently, he had not considered the propriety of relaying Durrance's news to Broad Place.

"You possess a rare consideration for others," Sutch remarked dryly.

"It is not a virtue of mine," Durrance admitted, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. "I practice thoughtfulness solely as an instinct of self-preservation. Selfishness is the inevitable erosion of the blind; knowing this, I guard against it vigilantly."

Thus, Durrance traveled that morning via the branching lines from Hampshire into Surrey, arriving at Broad Place bathed in the golden glow of the afternoon sun. General Feversham, nearing his eightieth year, bore the marks of time with a severity that defied mere aging. While his spine remained as straight as it had been decades prior, presenting the silhouette of a soldier, his frame had shrunk, and his face appeared diminished, etched with the topography of solitude. Durrance recalled walking this very terrace two years ago; even blindness could not obscure the transformation. The General's stride was now labored, weighted by invisible burdens, and a childlike fragility had crept into his voice.

"You have enlisted among the veterans prematurely, Durrance," the General noted, his tone flat. "I chanced upon the report in a newspaper. I intended to correspond, had I known your whereabouts."

If he sensed the purpose of Durrance's arrival, he offered no indication. He summoned tea, which was served in the grand hall where the ancestral portraits watched silently from the shadows. The conversation drifted to officers of the Sudan campaign, to the perceived injustices of the War Office, and to a pervasive dread that the nation was sliding into chaos.

"All things succumb to misfortune or incompetence," the General exploded, irritation cracking his composure. "Even you, Durrance, are not the man who strolled beside me on this terrace two years ago."

Feversham lacked the gift of tact, and his isolation had yielded no wisdom. Durrance could have retorted with a *tu quoque*, pointing out the General's own rigidity, but he held his tongue.

"But I arrive bearing the same burden," Durrance stated firmly.

The General sat upright, his joints protesting. "Then I offer the same response. I have no discourse for Harry Feversham. I will not engage in discussion." His voice was hard, devoid of emotion, as if addressing a stranger. Even the name was spoken without a tremor of sorrow. Durrance began to fear that the wellsprings of affection within the General had run completely dry.

"It would bring you no pleasure, then, to learn where Harry Feversham has wandered and how he has sustained himself over the last five years?"

A pause stretched between them—not prolonged, yet heavy enough to crush steel. Finally, the General answered: "Not in the least, Colonel Durrance."

The reply was absolute, yet Durrance leaned into the silence that preceded it. "Nor concerning the enterprise he undertook?" he pressed.

"I harbor no interest whatsoever," the General declared. "I ensure he does not starve; my solicitor confirms he receives his allowance. That suffices for my peace of mind, Colonel Durrance."

"I shall risk your wrath, General," Durrance said, his voice rising slightly. "There are moments when disobedience to a superior officer becomes a moral imperative. This is such a moment. Naturally, you may eject me from your home. Should you choose that path, I shall recount the chronicle of your son and my friend, from his disappearance in England to this present day."

General Feversham laughed, a brittle sound. "Of course, I cannot expel you from my house," he conceded, adding with severe gravity, "Yet I caution you that you exploit your status as my guest improperly."

"There is no denying it," Durrance replied calmly. And so, he unveiled the narrative—the retrieval of the Gordon letters from Berber, his encounter with Harry Feversham at Wadi Halfa, the imprisonment at Omdurman. He carried the tale down to the precise moment of Lieutenant Sutch's departure for Suakin.

General Feversham absorbed the entire account without interruption, unmoving in his chair. Durrance could not decipher the spirit of his listening, yet he found solace in the fact that the General listened at all, offering no immediate rebuttal. For a considerable time afterward, silence reigned. The General raised a hand to shade his eyes, as if attempting to pierce the veil of darkness that cloaked his vision, searching for the speaker who had just dismantled his fortress of denial. He remained thus, a statue of conflicted emotion. When he finally spoke, his hand did not lower. Pride forbade him to reveal to the silent witnesses on the walls that he was capable of such a primal vulnerability as joy at the reclaiming of his son's honor.

"What remains opaque to me," the General murmured slowly, "is why Harry ever resigned his commission. I failed to comprehend it previously; I understand it even less now, armed with knowledge of his valor. It is one of those peculiar, inexplicable anomalies. They occur, and that is the extent of explanation possible. Nevertheless, I am exceedingly glad you compelled me to listen, Durrance."

"I acted with a specific objective," Durrance replied. "Whether you approve is your prerogative. Personally, I struggle to conceive why Harry should not return home and reclaim all that was forfeited."

"He cannot recover everything," Feversham insisted, his voice tightening. "It is unjust that he should. He committed the sin, and he must pay the price. He cannot restore his career, at the very least."
