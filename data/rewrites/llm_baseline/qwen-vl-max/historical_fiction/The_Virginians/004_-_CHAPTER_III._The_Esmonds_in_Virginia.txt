Henry Esmond, Esq., a man who had served as Colonel during the wars of Queen Anne’s reign, found himself at the close of that era entangled in efforts to restore the Stuart line to the English throne. Though the nation ultimately chose a different dynasty, several loyalists fled beyond the seas, and among them was Colonel Esmond, advised by friends to seek refuge abroad. Yet his regrets over past actions were sincere, and when the new sovereign—renowned for his mildness—ascended the throne, reconciliation proved swift. Through the influence of trusted allies, Esmond’s name was cleared, and he was once again welcomed into the fold.

He belonged to an ancient English family whose ancestral seat stood at Castlewood, in Hampshire. It was widely known that both King James II and his son had offered the title of Marquis to Colonel Esmond and his father. Indeed, the elder Esmond might have claimed an Irish peerage passed down through generations, had not a technicality prevented it—a matter he never sought to correct. Wearied by political strife and burdened by personal matters across Europe, Henry Esmond turned his gaze toward the New World. He settled in Virginia, where his ancestor had been granted vast lands by King Charles I. There, on this distant shore, his daughter and grandsons were born, and his wife passed away.

She had been the widow of the Colonel’s cousin, Viscount Castlewood, who died in a duel with Lord Mohun near the end of William III’s reign. When she married Henry Esmond, she brought with her not only grief but a legacy of noble blood. In honor of his homeland, Esmond named his Virginia estate Castlewood, mirroring the ancestral home left behind. Life here mirrored English customs in nearly every way. The colony remained steadfastly loyal to the Crown. Virginians proudly claimed that King Charles II had reigned in their midst long before he ruled in England. The Church of England was upheld with reverence, and the gentry prided themselves on their ties to aristocratic families across the sea.

They looked down upon the Dutch merchants of New York and the Puritan settlers of Pennsylvania and New England, who pursued wealth with little regard for tradition or grace. Nowhere in the colonies was there less inclination toward republicanism than in Virginia—the very province that would one day lead the charge against British rule. Yet in those days, the land was governed by a patriarchal order. Plantation owners lived in a manner almost feudal, their great estates worked by scores of servants—some indentured, others enslaved—who labored under the authority of their masters. The soil yielded food, livestock, and game; rivers teemed with fish; and ships carried tobacco from private wharves along the Potomac and James Rivers to London and Bristol, returning with English goods and manufactured wares.

Hospitality was a sacred duty. No guest was ever turned away. The gentry visited one another freely, traveling between mansions as if bound by ancient custom. Slavery, though deeply entrenched, was not yet debated as a moral issue. To own black servants was considered natural and proper. The treatment of enslaved people, while far from humane by modern standards, was often less harsh than elsewhere—food was abundant, labor was grueling but not always cruel, and many slaves seemed content enough in their lot. To suggest emancipation to Madam Esmond of Castlewood would have been as absurd as asking her to release her horses from their stables. She believed firmly in discipline and order—both for men and beasts.

Her father, however, harbored doubts. A man of skeptical mind, he questioned many things, yet never acted on his misgivings. Something in his past had cast a shadow over his spirit—a quiet melancholy that lingered even in his later years. He was kind, affectionate, and deferential, especially to women, whom he rarely contradicted. But there was a sense of resignation about him, as though life had been a duty rather than a joy. He submitted to its course without complaint, and perhaps found peace only in his final hours.

After losing his wife, he entrusted the management of his household and affairs to his daughter. He accepted her guidance without resistance. As long as he had books and solitude, he desired nothing more. Guests were received with warmth and wit—he enjoyed their company, yet felt no sorrow when they departed. “My love,” he once told his daughter, “I shall not be sorry to go myself. And you, though the most devoted of daughters, will console yourself in time. Why should I, so old, be romantic? You may still be young enough for such fancies.” He spoke lightly, though not entirely truthfully—for his daughter was a practical woman, lacking any trace of sentimentality.

Fifteen years passed in Virginia, and prosperity flourished on the Esmond estate. At last, the Colonel agreed to his daughter’s plan to build a grander mansion than the modest wooden house he had lived in. Several neighbors had already erected fine homes, and perhaps ambition stirred within her. While the Colonel cared little for architecture or heraldry, his daughter took pride in her lineage. She believed fiercely in the nobility and antiquity of her bloodline, and her father, now mellowed by age, indulged her whims with gentle humor.

He even assisted her, using his knowledge of genealogy and his skill in painting. Heraldry was once a vital part of noble education, and during her travels in Europe, Miss Esmond had studied her family's history with fervor. She returned to Virginia armed with documents, pedigrees, and learned treatises on nobility. These convinced her beyond doubt that the Esmonds descended not only from Norman warriors who followed William the Conqueror but also from native English royalty. Two magnificent family trees, painted by her father’s hand, illustrated this dual origin: one branch reached back to Emperor Charlemagne, depicted in full armor with imperial mantle and crown; the other to Queen Boadicea, portrayed in flowing robes, adorned with a golden crown and blue-tinted tattoos—her image crafted with care and imagination.

From these illustrious roots, the family tree rose until it converged in the thirteenth century upon a fortunate ancestor who claimed descent from both lines.

Of the Warringtons, her husband’s family, she held little regard. Though she styled herself Esmond Warrington, she was universally known as Madam Esmond of Castlewood after her father’s death. It is possible that disputes over rank occasionally disturbed her temper. Though her father had once possessed a marquisate patent from King James—only to burn it and disown it—she often behaved as if the title were valid. She considered the English Esmonds inferior to her own branch, and among colonial society, she made no secret of her superiority.

This led to quarrels, sharp words, and even minor scuffles at Governor’s assemblies in Jamestown. But why dwell on such trifles? The individuals involved are long gone, and the Republic has swept away the old hierarchies. Before independence, Virginia was arguably the most aristocratic region in the world. Its people were raised to revere English institutions and remain loyal to the Crown. The twin sons of Castlewood, George and Harry, were no exception—they were taught from childhood to honor the king and uphold their family’s dignity.

When their grandfather died, their mother, in solemn ceremony, declared George, the elder by half an hour, her heir and successor. Harry was instructed to show obedience to his brother. The entire household, including the numerous enslaved people and indentured servants, was ordered to recognize George’s authority. Only two figures in the family resisted: Madam Mountain, a loyal friend of Mrs. Esmond, and Harry’s foster-mother, a devoted Black woman who could not understand why her child should not be first—especially since, in her view, he was handsomer, stronger, and cleverer than his brother. In truth, the twins were nearly identical in appearance, differing only in hair color. When wrapped in nightcaps, they were indistinguishable even to strangers.

Yet in temperament, they were worlds apart. George was calm, thoughtful, and quiet. Harry was bold, energetic, and loud. He learned quickly once engaged, but resisted study until provoked. Threats of punishment failed to move him, while George often helped him with lessons. Harry had a military bent—he drilled the younger slaves like soldiers, caned them playfully, and fought spirited boxing matches with them, never holding grudges when defeated. George, by contrast, avoided conflict and treated all with gentleness.

Each boy had a personal servant assigned to him. Once, George found his young black servant asleep on his bed and sat beside him, brushing flies from the child’s face with a feather fan. Old Gumbo, the servant’s father, discovered the scene and cried out in horror. Madam Esmond, furious, ordered the child whipped. George pleaded desperately, wept openly, and begged for mercy—but his mother refused. The boy was taken away, begging his master not to cry.

A fierce argument erupted between mother and son. George insisted the punishment was unjust—“a shame,” he said—and declared that as master of the boy, no one—not even his mother—had the right to touch him. He vowed, shouting an oath that shocked everyone present, that on the day he came of age, he would free young Gumbo. That night, he visited the slave quarters and gave the boy one of his toys.

The child was indeed impudent and lazy, but no worse for the beating, as the Colonel likely thought. He quietly approved of the punishment when his daughter insisted, then laughed good-naturedly when Harry exclaimed, “You let mamma rule you in everything, grandpapa.”

“Why, so I do,” replied the old man. “Rachel, my love, the way I am petticoat-ridden is so evident that even this baby has noticed it.”

“Then why don’t you stand up like a man?” demanded little Harry, always ready to support his brother.

Grandpapa blinked. “Because I like sitting down best, my dear. I’m an old gentleman, and standing fatigues me.”

Harry, with his playful nature and affinity for his grandfather’s interests, became his favorite companion. He would chatter endlessly, laughing and storytelling, while George remained silent and reserved. George thrived in the library, where he read books long before he could comprehend them. Harry, meanwhile, was alive in the stables, woods, and fields—eager for hunts and fishing trips, showing early promise as a sportsman.

Once, when the Colonel’s ship sailed for Europe, the boys were asked what gifts Captain Franks should bring them. George hesitated between books and a fiddle. Harry instantly demanded a small gun. Madam Warrington (as she then was) was dismayed that her eldest son showed such low tastes, but praised Harry’s choice as fitting for a gentleman of noble birth.

“Books, papa, I can see are a reasonable choice,” she said to her father, who tried to defend George’s preference. “Though I suppose you already possess every book in existence. But I cannot imagine—though I may be wrong—I cannot imagine—that my son, grandson of a Marquis, should become a fiddler.”

“Should be a fiddlestick, my dear,” the Colonel replied gently. “Remember, Heaven’s ways are not ours. Each soul has its own realm of thought, and it is a sin to intrude. Suppose George loves music? You cannot stop him any more than you can command a rose not to bloom or a bird not to sing.”

“A bird sings by instinct,” Mrs. Warrington retorted. “George did not come into the world with a fiddle in his hand. I hated the harpsichord as a girl at Kensington School, and only learned it to please my mother. Say what you will, dear sir, I simply cannot believe fiddling is suitable for a person of fashion.”

“And King David played the harp, my dear?”

“I wish my papa would read him more, and not speak of him in that manner,” Mrs. Warrington interrupted.

“Nay, my dear,” the father replied softly, “it was only by way of illustration.”

It was true, as the Colonel confessed in his memoirs, that he was always guided by women. With his wife gone, he doted on his daughter—laughing at her quirks, indulging her prejudices, making jokes of her opinions while allowing them full sway. He nurtured her imperious nature, though he believed strongly that one could not reshape character through force, and that excessive control bred hypocrisy.

At last, the time came for Mr. Esmond to leave this world. He laid down his burdens as if relieved. We need not open our story with tolling bells or mournful sermons. Those who heard Parson Broadbent’s eulogy in Jamestown wondered at his eloquence and Latin fluency. Perhaps Mr. Dempster, the boys’ Scottish tutor, knew the truth—he had corrected the proofs of the speech, which was printed at Benjamin Franklin’s press in Philadelphia at the request of the Governor and many distinguished citizens.

No funeral in the colony had ever matched the splendor arranged by Madam Esmond for her father—one who would have smiled at such extravagance. The young boys, draped in mourning black, led the procession, followed by Lord Fairfax of Greenway Court, the Governor of Virginia in his coach, the Randolphs, Careys, Harrisons, Washingtons, and others. All respected the departed man—for his kindness, intellect, generosity, and quiet dignity.

Upon hearing of his passing, the family of Colonel Esmond’s stepson, Lord Castlewood of Hampshire, offered to pay for the marble slab commemorating his mother and husband. After due preparation, the monument was erected, bearing the Esmond arms and coronet, flanked by cherubic figures in mourning, and inscribed with an epitaph that, for once, spoke only truth.
