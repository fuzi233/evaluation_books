CHAPTER VII. Barton Park stood about half a mile from the cottage, nestled in the valley they had passed earlier on their way home. Though it was hidden from view by a rise in the land, its grandeur was unmistakable once seen. The house was spacious and elegant, reflecting the refined tastes of the Middleton family. Sir John and Lady Middleton lived with a warmth and polish that made them the heart of social life in the district. Their hospitality knew no bounds—guests were always welcome, and their gatherings outnumbered those of any other household nearby. It was not merely a matter of fashion; for both husband and wife, such connections were essential to their contentment. Though their personalities could not have been more different—Sir John bold and energetic, Lady Middleton gentle and maternal—they shared one thing: a lack of deeper interests beyond what society offered. His passion was the hunt, hers the care of her children. He roamed the fields with gun and hound, while she nurtured her brood with tender indulgence. These were their only true pursuits. Yet, thanks to constant company and lively engagements, neither felt the emptiness of limited talents. Sir John thrived on noise and numbers, especially among the young; he delighted in filling his home with laughter and music, often organizing outdoor feasts in summer or private dances in winter—events so plentiful they would satisfy even the most eager young lady. A new family arriving in the neighborhood brought him joy, and the Dashwood sisters were no exception. They were young, lovely, and unpretentious—qualities that won him over instantly. To be genuine, he believed, was all a woman needed to make her charm as radiant as her beauty. His kind nature also made him glad to help those less fortunate than himself. In welcoming his cousins, he found real satisfaction in generosity; and in housing a household of ladies at his cottage, he took quiet pleasure as a sportsman might in preserving a peaceful retreat—though he cared little for others who shared his passions.

At the door of Barton Park, Mrs. Dashwood and her daughters were greeted by Sir John, who welcomed them with open-hearted warmth. As he led them into the drawing room, he repeated his regret from the previous day—that he had been unable to secure any charming young men to join them. “You’ll see only one gentleman besides myself,” he said cheerfully, “a dear friend staying here—but I’m afraid he’s neither very youthful nor particularly sprightly.” He hoped they wouldn’t mind the small gathering and promised never again to let such a thing happen. He’d visited several homes that morning seeking additional guests, but everyone was occupied—engagements filled the evening, and the moonlight seemed to draw people indoors. Fortunately, Lady Middleton’s mother had arrived just hours before, and though she wasn’t young, she was bright and full of spirit. “She’ll keep things lively,” Sir John declared, “and I trust you’ll find the company quite agreeable.”

The Dashwoods, though strangers to the group, were content with this modest assembly. Mrs. Jennings, Lady Middleton’s mother, was a robust, cheerful woman with a round face and a voice that rang through the room. She spoke freely, laughed often, and by dinner’s end had already teased the girls about love and marriage. “I hope you didn’t leave your hearts behind in Sussex!” she joked, pretending to catch a blush on Elinor’s cheeks. Marianne winced—not for herself, but for her sister—and glanced toward Elinor, whose quiet composure belied the discomfort beneath.

Colonel Brandon, Sir John’s guest, seemed an unlikely companion. Unlike the boisterous host or the gentle Lady Middleton, he sat quietly, reserved and solemn. To Marianne and Margaret, he appeared an old bachelor—well past thirty-five, perhaps older. Yet despite his plain features, there was something dignified in his bearing. His manner was calm, thoughtful, and thoroughly gentlemanly. Among all present, none truly matched the Dashwoods’ sensibilities—but compared to Lady Middleton’s dullness, even Colonel Brandon’s reserve seemed appealing. Lady Middleton came alive only when her four energetic children burst into the room after dinner, tumbling around her and pulling at her dress, reducing conversation to matters of feeding and clothing.

In the evening, when Marianne’s musical talent was discovered, she was invited to play. The piano was unlocked, and the room fell silent in anticipation. Marianne sang with grace and feeling, performing the songs Lady Middleton had once favored—melodies that may have gathered dust since her wedding day, when she had given up music entirely. Though her mother claimed she had played beautifully, Lady Middleton now declared she had never cared much for it. Still, Marianne’s voice moved the listeners. Sir John clapped loudly after each song, talking animatedly throughout. Lady Middleton scolded him gently, exclaiming how anyone could possibly talk during music, then asked Marianne to sing a piece she had just finished.

Only Colonel Brandon listened without extravagance. He gave no outburst of praise, only steady attention. And in that moment, Marianne felt a quiet respect for him—something she couldn’t extend to the others, whose lack of appreciation seemed almost cruel. His enjoyment of music may not have reached the heights of her own passion, but it was sincere, measured, and deeply respectful. Compared to the indifference of the rest, it was noble. And though she acknowledged that a man of his age might naturally lose some of life’s finer sensitivities, she allowed him every kindness human nature demanded. He was not perfect, but he was real—and that, in a room full of pretense, was rare indeed.
