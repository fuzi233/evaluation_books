There was a certain charm in watching Daniel arrange fresh garments for Mordecai, a quiet satisfaction akin to an artist sketching a vision. He imagined the crisp gray flannel shirts and a dressing gown that evoked the humble robes of a Franciscan monk, framing Mordecai's slender frame. A significant part of his delight stemmed from seeing Mirah's brother anew, hoping to shield her from any jarring reality and preserve the intensity of her joy. Yet, as he finished preparing everything, a shadow of uncertainty crept in. Was he perhaps misreading her nature? Did he mistake the superficial appeal of poverty for something deeper? Had he not recognized that Mirah, like himself, possessed a capacity to find profound dignity even within the starkness of deprivation, viewing it not merely as a barrier but as a sacred testament to her brother's history? There was, however, the practical necessity of appeasing the Meyricks, a family prone to skepticism regarding Mordecai's Jewish background. Daniel found himself acutely aware of the need to hide anything that might trigger their immediate aversion, craving instead to bathe every object in the solemn, golden light of a sunset born from deep, reverent love.

His doubts would have grown stronger had he listened to the conversation around Mrs. Meyrick's hearth later that evening, after Mirah retired. Hans, now residing in Chelsea, had stayed on, and as Mrs. Meyrick stoked the fire into a warm blaze, she announced, "Now, Kate, extinguish your candle, and let us gather close. Hans, dearest, please cease your laughter at those poems for the ninety-ninth time and join us. I have something marvellous to share."

Kate, watching the others climb onto the fender, retorted, "As if I didn't know already, Mother! I've caught glimpses of it in your eyes and noticed your feigned errands for weeks."

"Well, since you're so perceptive," Mrs. Meyrick declared, "perhaps you realize that Mirah's brother has been found!"

"Oh, bother!" Hans exclaimed instantly.

"Hans, that is cruel," Mab chided. "What if we had lost you?"

"I simply cannot help feeling somewhat relieved," Kate admitted. "And where is her mother?"

"She is deceased."

"I trust the brother is not a bad man," Amy offered cautiously.

"Nor a dandy wrapped in jewels, a 'Crystal Palace Assyrian' with a top hat," Hans muttered in a sour mood.

"Were there ever such unfeeling children?" Mrs. Meyrick pressed, emboldened by their lack of sympathy. "Do you not care at all about Mirah's happiness?"

"You know, Ma, Mirah barely remembers her brother," Kate noted.

"People missing for twelve years should never return," Hans insisted, pacing the room with his hands deep in his velvet pockets. "They only stand in the way."

"Hans!" Mrs. Meyrick scolded gently. "If I had been absent for twenty years, I imagine—"

"I spoke of twelve years," Hans interrupted sharply. "Any relative lost for roughly that duration belongs to the shadows."

"But it is lovely to find people again; there is so much to say," Mab interjected, clutching her knees. "Did Prince Camaralzaman discover him?"

Mrs. Meyrick then recounted everything she knew without pause, concluding, "Mr. Deronda holds the utmost respect for him. He seems almost to worship him, and he believes Mirah is precisely the kind of sister needed to understand this brother."

"Deronda is becoming utterly absurd about these Jews," Hans sneered, rising and banging his chair aside. "He intends to indulge Mirah in whatever delusions she harbors."

"Oh, shame on you, Hans!" Mab cried. "To speak of Mr. Deronda that way!" Mrs. Meyrick's face betrayed a subtle, suppressed emotion beneath her composed exterior.

"Now we shall never be a complete circle again," Hans rambled, "yet we must entertain this Prophet Elijah, and Mirah will spend all her time dwelling on the ruins of Jerusalem. She'll be ruined as an artist—you mark my words, she'll become as narrow-minded as a nun. Everything will be undone—our home, our peace. I shall resort to drinking."

"Oh, truly, Hans," Kate snapped, exasperated. "Men are the most contemptible creatures. They demand the world bend to their whims, otherwise they become insufferable."

"Oh, oh, oh, it's dreadful!" Mab wailed, mimicking ancient Nineveh collapsing.

"If all that knowledge from university means you remain so childish, Hans, what was the point?" Amy reasoned. "You should learn to accept kindness from those Providence sends you. *We* will have to accept him."

"I pray you all enjoy the new *Lamentations of Jeremiah*—'to be continued in our next,'" Hans dismissed, grabbing his wide-awake. "There is no virtue in being superior if you must tolerate men with fixed ideas staring blankly at you, expecting your entire conversation to serve as mere footnotes to their texts. If you are trapped behind a petrifying wall, you'd best be an old boot. I refuse to be an old boot." With a hurried, desperate gesture, he kissed his mother's brow and offered a condescending farewell to the girls before departing.

"Suppose Mirah knew how you behave," Kate murmured, though the answer was merely the slamming of the door. "I should like to witness Mirah when Mr. Deronda tells her," she told her mother, "for I know she will look beautiful."

However, upon reflection, Daniel had decided against a direct confrontation. Instead, he wrote a letter for Mrs. Meyrick to deliver the following morning. He requested she reveal the news rather than waiting for him, omitting his true motive—he dreaded revisiting a narrative where he appeared as the central benefactor—preferring to stay with Mordecai while Mrs. Meyrick brought Mirah ostensibly on a visit. This arrangement would create a brief respite before the inevitable relocation Mirah would soon propose.

Secretly, Daniel worried about Mordecai. After years of solitary contemplation, his physical frailty had sharpened his mental focus, potentially rendering him exclusive in his views. Would Mordecai allow space for Daniel's tenderness toward his sister, beyond the obligatory piety? While Mordecai's feelings for the Cohens, particularly young Jacob, remained active and evident, Daniel observed a strange, heavy silence from Mordecai regarding Mirah. The old man had accepted the new clothes with obedient quietude, only to whisper afterward, with a faint smile, "I must keep my old garments by me for a remembrance." Seated together, awaiting Mirah, Mordecai said nothing, his eyelids lowered, yet his face and hands betraying a restless agitation. It was a specific nervous disturbance known to those whose minds, driven long by a single powerful current, are suddenly forced into a new or reopened channel. For susceptible souls whose strength has been drained by dormant biases, an interview that violently resurrects the past feels like a terrifying illness. Even joy, in this context, carries a terrible weight.

Daniel caught the contagion of this excitement. When the bell rang, he stepped outside, unsure why, simply wishing to greet Mirah before the formal introduction. To his startlement, she wore the hat and cloak in which he had first encountered her—the memorable cloak that had once served as a makeshift shroud, wetted by rain. She had descended prepared for this encounter. When Mrs. Meyrick asked, "You wish to go in that dress, dear?", Mirah replied, "My brother is poor, and I want to resemble him, lest he feel distant from me"—presuming she would meet him in his workman's attire. Daniel could offer no comment, feeling a secret shame for his own fastidious preparations. They shook hands silently; Mirah's countenance was pale and awestruck.

As Daniel held the door open, Mordecai rose, his gaze turning eagerly toward the entrance. Mirah took only a few steps before halting. They gazed at one another, frozen in time. It was less their physical presence that overwhelmed them than a spectral presence, a meeting of spirits prior to the touch of flesh. Mirah broke the silence first.

"Ezra," she called out, using the exact tone she employed when recounting her mother's summons.

With a sudden movement, Mordecai stepped forward, placing his hand gently on her shoulder. He towered over her, looking down with tender regard. "That was Mother's voice. Do you recall her calling me?"

"Yes," Mirah whispered, tears welling. "And how you answered, 'Mother!' I knew then that you loved her." She threw her arms around her brother's neck, claspng her small hands tightly behind it, drawing his face down to hers with a lavish, childlike kiss. Her hat tipped backward, revealing her tousled curls.

"Ah, the dear head, the dear head!" Mordecai murmured, his thin hand caressing the curls in a low, loving hum.

"You are very ill, Ezra," Mirah said, her expression softening with sorrowful observation.

"Yes, my dear child, I shall not long remain in this body," he answered quietly.
