As the full moon cast its silvery glow over the tranquil waters of the Nile, three officers gathered on a grassy knoll above Wadi Halfa's mess room, their lounge chairs softly creaking in the stillness of the night. The air was heavy with the sweet scent of blooming acacia trees, and the distant calls of bull-frogs echoed across the river. Below, the water's glassy surface reflected the starlight like a burnished mirror, while the opposite bank stretched out into the desert, a seemingly endless expanse of white sand dotted with rocky hummocks that glinted like diamonds.

Colonel Dawson, a man of middle age with a rugged face etched by the harsh realities of life in the Soudan, puffed on his cigar, his brow furrowed in concern. His voice was low and measured as he spoke, "I fear for his safety, gentlemen. Eight weeks have passed since Durrance departed, and I dare not think what may have befallen him."

Lieutenant Calder, a seasoned engineer with a shock of grey hair and a face that seemed ageless, nodded thoughtfully. His eyes, though lined with the weight of experience, still shone with a hint of optimism. "He left us eight weeks ago, to be precise," he said, his voice tinged with a note of doubt.

Major Walters, a tall, lean major of the Army Service Corps, spoke up, his words dripping with a hint of warmth. "We mustn't jump to conclusions, sir. Durrance is a man who thrives on the simple things in life – a campfire under the stars, the camaraderie of sheiks gathered 'round. He'll find his way back to us, though perhaps not as swiftly as we'd like."

Colonel Dawson's expression remained grave, his moustache twitching as he stroked it thoughtfully. "He is three weeks overdue, gentlemen. I fear for his safety."

Walters pointed out into the desert, where the moonlight cast an ethereal glow on the endless dunes. "If he had taken the route west, toward the Red Sea, I might share your concerns.
