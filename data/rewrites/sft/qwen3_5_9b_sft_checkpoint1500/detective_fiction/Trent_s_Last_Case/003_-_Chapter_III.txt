It was shortly after eight in the morning of the following day when Nathaniel Burton Cupples stood upon the veranda of the Marlstone Hotel. His thoughts were singularly focused on the prospect of sustenance. For Mr. Cupples, the colloquialism "thinking about breakfast" was literal; he engaged in deliberate contemplation of every conscious act afforded by time, including the immediate necessity of nourishment. He recalled that the previous day's excitement surrounding the discovery of the corpse had thoroughly disrupted his appetite, leaving him with a deficit he intended to rectify this morning with a third piece of toast and an extra egg, the remainder of his daily ration to be saved for luncheon.

Having settled this culinary strategy, Mr. Cupples devoted a few moments to admiring the view before summoning his meal. Through the lens of a connoisseur, he surveyed the rugged coastline where a massive, pierced rock jutted from a glassy sea, contrasting with the orderly beauty of the vast, sloping fields of pasture and woodland that climbed gently from the cliffs toward the distant moors. Mr. Cupples possessed a deep delight in landscapes.

He was a man of medium height and spare frame, nearing sixty, constitutionally delicate yet wiry and active for his age. A sparse, straggling beard and moustache failed to hide a thin but kind mouth; his eyes were keen and pleasant, framed by a sharp nose and narrow jaw that lent him a distinctly clerical air. This impression was reinforced by his commonplace dark attire and soft black hat. The overall effect was undeniably priestly. He was a man of an unusually conscientious, industrious, and orderly mind, devoid of significant imagination. His father's household had historically recruited domestic staff via advertisements describing them as belonging to a "serious family." Having escaped that fortress of gloom, Mr. Cupples retained two saintly gifts unspoiled by his environment: an inexhaustible kindness of heart and a capacity for innocent gaiety that owed nothing to humor. Had he lived in an earlier era with formal clerical training, he might have ascended to the scarlet hat. Instead, he was a highly respected member of the London Positivist Society, a retired banker, and a widower without issue. His austere yet content life was spent largely among books and museums. His profound, patiently accumulated knowledge of various disconnected subjects—which had stirred his interest at different junctures—had secured him a place in the quiet, half-lit world of professors, curators, and research devotees. At their amiable, unconvivial dinner parties, he was most himself. His favorite author was Montaigne.

Just as Mr. Cupples was concluding his meal at a small table on the veranda, a large motor car turned onto the hotel drive. "Who is this?" he queried the waiter. "Id is der manager," the young man replied listlessly. "He have been to meed a gendleman by der train."

The vehicle halted, and the porter hurried from the entrance. Mr. Cupples exclaimed with pleasure as a long, loosely built man, significantly younger than himself, descended from the car and mounted the veranda, flinging his hat onto a chair. The newcomer's high-boned, quixotic face bore a pleasant smile; his rough tweed clothing, hair, and short moustache were tolerably untidy.

"Cupples, by all that's miraculous!" cried the man, lunging forward before Mr. Cupples could rise and grasping his outstretched hand in a firm clasp. "My luck is serving me today," the newcomer continued spasmodically. "This is the second slice within an hour. How are you, my best of friends? And why are you here? Why sit'st thou by that ruined breakfast? Dost thou its former pride recall, or ponder how it passed away? I *am* glad to see you!"

"I was half expecting you, Trent," Mr. Cupples responded, his face wreathed in smiles. "You are looking splendid, my dear fellow. I will tell you all about it. But you cannot have had your own breakfast yet. Will you join me at this table?"

"Rather!" said the man. "An enormous great breakfast, too—with refined conversation and tears of recognition never dry. Will you get young Siegfried to lay a place for me while I go and wash? I shan't be three minutes." He vanished into the hotel, and Mr. Cupples, after a brief pause, proceeded to the telephone in the porter's office. Upon returning, he found his friend already seated, pouring tea and displaying an unaffected interest in the selection of food.

"I expect this to be a hard day for me," Trent remarked, speaking with the curious, jerky cadence that seemed habitual to him. "I shan't eat again till the evening, very likely. You guess why I'm here, don't you?"

"Undoubtedly," said Mr. Cupples. "You have come down to investigate the murder."

"That is rather a colorless way of stating it," Trent replied as he dissected a sole. "I prefer to put it that I have come down in the character of avenger of blood, to hunt down the guilty and vindicate the honor of society. That is my line of business. Families wait on at their private residences. I say, Cupples, I have made a good beginning already. Wait a bit, and I'll tell you." A silence ensued, during which the newcomer ate swiftly and abstractedly while Mr. Cupples watched him with happy satisfaction.

"The manager here," the tall man finally said, "is a fellow of remarkable judgment. He is an admirer of mine. He knows more about my best cases than I do myself. The *Record* wired last night to announce my arrival, and when I disembarked from the train at seven this morning, there he was awaiting me with a motor car the size of a haystack. He is beside himself with joy at having me here. It is fame." He sipped his tea and continued, "Almost his first words were to ask if I wished to view the body of the murdered man—he believed he could arrange it for me. He is as keen as a razor. The body lies in Dr. Stock's surgery, down in the village, exactly as it was found. It is to undergo a post-mortem examination this morning, hence my timely arrival. Well, he rushed me down to the doctor's, providing full particulars of the case en route. By the time we arrived, I was reasonably *au fait*. I suppose the manager of an establishment like this possesses some influence with the doctor. Anyhow, he made no difficulties, nor did the constable on duty, although he was careful to insist that I not reveal his identity in the press."

"I viewed the body prior to its removal," Mr. Cupples noted. "I would not say there was anything remarkable about it, save that the shot to the eye had scarcely disfigured the countenance and caused minimal effusion of blood. The wrists were scratched and bruised. I presume that, with your trained faculties, you discerned other details of a suggestive nature."

"Other details, certainly; but I know of no specific suggestion they offer. They are merely odd. Consider the wrists, for instance. How could you perceive bruises and scratches there? I wager you encountered Manderson here prior to the murder."

"Certainly," Mr. Cupples affirmed.

"Well, did you ever observe his wrists?"

Mr. Cupples reflected. "No. Now that you raise the point, I am reminded that when I interviewed Manderson here, he was wearing stiff cuffs extending well over his hands."

"He always did," Trent stated. "My friend the manager confirms this. I pointed out to him that you failed to notice a crucial detail: there were no cuffs visible. Indeed, they had been dragged up inside the coat sleeves, as yours would be if you hurried into a coat without pulling your cuffs down. That was why you saw his wrists."

"Well, I call that suggestive," observed Mr. Cupples mildly. "One might infer, perhaps, that upon rising, he hastened over his dressing."

"Yes, but did he? The manager asserts precisely what you imply. 'He was always a bit of a swell in his dress,' he told me, and inferred that when Manderson arose in that mysterious fashion before the house stirred and ventured into the grounds, he was in a great hurry. 'Look at his shoes,' he said to me: 'Mr. Manderson was always specially neat regarding his footwear. Yet those shoe-laces were tied in a hurry.' I agreed. 'And he left his false teeth in his room,' said the manager. 'Doesn't *that* prove he was flustered and hurried?' I conceded it appeared so. But I countered, 'Look here: if he was so exceedingly pressed, why did he part his hair so carefully? That parting is a work of art. Why did he don so much? For he wore a complete outfit of underclothing, studs in his shirt, sock-suspenders, a watch and chain, money and keys in his pockets. That is what I said to the manager. He could find no explanation. Can you?"

Mr. Cupples considered. "Those facts might suggest that he was hurried only at the conclusion of his dressing. The coat and shoes would naturally come last."
