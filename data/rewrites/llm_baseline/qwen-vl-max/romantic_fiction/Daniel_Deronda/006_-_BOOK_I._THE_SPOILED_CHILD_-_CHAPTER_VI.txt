“Do you think you have humbled me by teaching me that the earth revolves around the sun? I swear I esteem myself no less.” —FONTENELLE: _Pluralité des Mondes_.

Gwendolen felt a new kind of ache in her heart after reading those words. She would never have admitted how deeply she regretted not having had Miss Arrowpoint’s musical training—how much she envied the ease with which that quiet, unassuming girl could question Herr Klesmer’s judgment with the confidence of true mastery. And even more, she couldn’t confess—least of all to herself—that each time they met, Miss Arrowpoint stirred something unfamiliar within her: not jealousy over wealth or beauty, but irritation at the very idea that such an ordinary-looking woman—slight, average height, small features, pale eyes, sallow skin—could possess such a rare depth of understanding, such refined taste and precision in music, such unwavering standards that made admiration impossible to force, and respect hard to avoid.

This twenty-four-year-old young lady, who might have passed unnoticed in any crowd if not for her name, seemed to carry a silent conviction that Gwendolen’s talents were merely conventional—something that, though always masked by kindness, only added to the sting. Yet Gwendolen refused to dwell on anything that cast doubt on her own worth.

Herr Klesmer, the great musician who had so unexpectedly expanded her world, was not always near. His frequent trips between London and Quetcham soon became a hope rather than a fact—a chance, perhaps, to win his deeper admiration. In the meantime, the pleasure she brought to gatherings at Brackenshaw Castle, the Firs, and other places helped restore her composure. She found comfort in approval, not criticism, and unlike those few souls who crave perfection beyond what others demand, she was content with recognition as it came. It might have been rash to claim she was inwardly exceptional—but then again, wasn’t she? Her grace, her boldness, the sharp edge she gave to common ambition—all these set her apart. Still, she had no grand theories about life or society. She mocked reformers and intellectual women alike, distancing herself from them with irony, yet she craved to be seen as different.

She longed to feel herself extraordinary—yet her vision of greatness was shaped by genteel romance: a heroine whose journal brims with vague rebellion and originality, while her real life dances within the limits of fashion. If she ever stumbled into danger, the tragedy would lie partly in her satin shoes—because nature and society had woven invisible nets around her dreams. A soul burning with longing for meaning, ready to consume the world as fuel, remained trapped by the delicate wires of propriety, doing nothing truly daring.

And now, even in the fresh winter at Offendene, she sensed this fate closing in. She knew she didn’t want the same life as most young ladies. But she had no clear plan for what else she might do. What acts would prove her freedom? Offendene offered a lovely backdrop—if only something remarkable happened there. But mostly, the neighborhood failed her.

Beyond the initial dazzle of her beauty, her early social outings offered little excitement. She returned home from brief forays into wit and satire—like the ones that had offended Mrs. Arrowpoint—and filled the days with childish pastimes. The most defiant thing she could do was skip Alice’s lessons (reasoning that Alice would likely thrive on ignorance), and instead employ her with Miss Merry and the maid to help prepare elaborate costumes for future charades or plays—costumes she insisted on having ready, even if she had to make them happen by will alone.

She’d never acted before—only posed in *tableaux vivants* at school—but she was convinced she could perform with power. Having seen Rachel at the Théâtre Français and heard her mother speak of her, Gwendolen often dreamed of becoming an actress like her—especially since she believed herself more beautiful than the thin Jewish star. For now, the rainy days before Christmas were spent crafting Greek, Oriental, and hybrid costumes, in which she dramatically posed and declaimed lines before a domestic audience, including even the housekeeper, whom she once pressed into service to swell the applause. But when the housekeeper dared say, “Miss Harleth looks far more like a queen in her own dress than in that baggy thing with her arms bare,” she was never invited back.

One day, after showing off her Greek costume to Anna and acting out fragments of tragic scenes, Gwendolen asked, “Do I look as well as Rachel, mamma?”

“You have better arms than Rachel,” replied Mrs. Davilow. “Your arms would suit any role, Gwen. But your voice isn’t as deep or tragic.”

“I can make it deeper if I choose,” said Gwendolen, half-serious. Then, decisively: “I believe a higher voice is more tragic—it’s more feminine. And the more feminine a woman is, the more tragic it seems when she does desperate things.”

“There may be something in that,” Mrs. Davilow murmured, unconvinced. “But I don’t see the point in making one’s blood creep. And if anything terrible must be done, I’d rather leave it to men.”

“Oh, mamma, you’re dreadfully dull! As if all the great poetic criminals weren’t women! Men are just cautious creatures.”

“Well, dear,” her mother said gently, “and you—who are afraid to be alone at night—I don’t think you’d be very bold in crime, thank God.”

“I’m not talking about reality, mamma,” Gwendolen snapped. Then, as her mother left the room, she turned quickly to Anna. “Ask Uncle to let us put on some charades at the rectory. Mr. Middleton and Warham could join us—just for practice. Mamma says it wouldn’t do to have Mr. Middleton rehearsing here. He’s stiff, but we could give him good parts. Do ask—or I’ll do it myself.”

“Oh, not until Rex comes,” Anna replied. “He’s so clever, and such a dear old thing—he could play Napoleon looking across the sea. He really looks like Napoleon. Rex can do anything.”

“I don’t believe a word of it,” Gwendolen laughed. “He’ll turn out like those awful blue-and-yellow watercolors you hang in your room and worship.”

“Very well, you’ll see,” Anna said firmly. “It’s not that I know what clever means, but he already has a scholarship, and papa says he’ll get a fellowship. Nobody’s better at games. He’s smarter than Mr. Middleton, and everyone but you thinks Mr. Middleton is clever.”

“So he may be in a dark-lantern sort of way,” Gwendolen said. “But he *is* a stick. If he had to say, ‘Perdition catch my soul, but I do love her,’ he’d say it exactly as if he were reciting, ‘Here endeth the second lesson.’”

“Oh, Gwendolen!” Anna gasped, shocked. “And it’s unkind to talk like that—especially since he admires you so much. I heard Warham tell mamma once, ‘Middleton is regularly spooney upon Gwendolen.’ She was furious, but I know what it means—it’s what they say at college for being in love.”

“How can I help it?” Gwendolen said, dismissive. “Perdition catch my soul if I love *him*.”

“No, of course not,” Anna said softly. “Papa wouldn’t want it. And he’s leaving soon. But it makes me sad when you make fun of him.”

“What would you do if I made fun of Rex?” Gwendolen teased.

“Oh, Gwendolen, dear—you *won’t*, will you?” Anna pleaded, tears welling. “I couldn’t bear it. There’s nothing in him to mock. But you might find things out. No one ever laughed at Mr. Middleton before you. Everyone said he was handsome, polite, proper. I’ve always been afraid of him because of his learning, his square coat, and his being the bishop’s nephew. But you won’t ridicule Rex—promise me.” Her eyes begged.

“You’re a dear little coz,” Gwendolen said, gently touching Anna’s chin. “I never want to hurt you. Especially if Rex helps bring everything to life—charades and all.”

When Rex finally arrived, he brought light and energy to Offendene and the rectory. His cheerful cooperation with Gwendolen’s plans left no room for mockery—only playful teasing, which he enjoyed. He was a fine, open-hearted young man, with a face like his father’s and Anna’s, but softer and broader. Full of healthy joy, he found vice boring, not tempting. To him, bad habits were “what some fellows did”—stupid stuff best avoided. He loved Anna deeply, though his pleasures outside her were greater than hers. And he had never known a stronger affection.

The cousins were inseparable—mostly at Offendene, where Gwendolen reigned freely. Whatever she wanted became their shared purpose. The charades took place as planned, along with impromptu scenes she hadn’t imagined. Rehearsals and performances happened at Offendene, Mrs. Davilow now allowing Mr. Middleton to join—especially since Rex was there, and Middleton’s presence was essential. Warham, busy preparing for the Indian Civil Service exams under a strict tutor, had no time and was too depressed to participate.

Mr. Middleton played solemn roles, flattered by Gwendolen’s praise of his stillness. At first uneasy about her closeness to Rex, he soon reassured himself that such friendship meant no serious attachment. In fact, he began to think her formal treatment of him was a sign of favor—encouraging him to act before leaving Pennicote, though he’d planned to wait until his position was secure. Gwendolen, aware of his devotion, cared little—she simply liked being admired. She looked at him with calm indifference, feeding his hopes by avoiding dramatic interaction, knowing that meaning depends entirely on interpretation.

Some might have thought it odd that a reserved Anglican clergyman, sensitive to sacred things, rarely laughing, and uncomfortable with plain language, would consider marrying a girl so bold in mockery and lacking the quiet grace expected of a clergyman’s wife. Or that a man steeped in theology would expect harmony with a restless spirit like Gwendolen’s. But why must we explain why people behave differently from what others predict? The fault lies with those who assume too much.

As for Rex, who might have pitied poor Middleton if he’d known his inner struggle, he was too lost in first love to notice anything else. He didn’t observe Gwendolen—he felt her presence. Her words, her movements, even her absence—he registered them all. Within two weeks, he was utterly devoted, seeing no obstacles. His love was proof enough of hers, as natural as breathing. She sang for him whenever he wished, rode with him gladly—even if his borrowed horses were absurd—and appreciated Anna. Nothing seemed missing. That she should make a grand match had never crossed his mind. He had no vanity—only the certainty that Gwendolen was perfect, and that her perfection was part of the goodness he’d always known.

One moment during their rehearsals revealed a side of her he hadn’t expected—a depth of feeling hidden beneath her usual courage and high spirits.

After many practices, they decided to invite a select group to Offendene to watch their performance. Anna surprised everyone with her charm—her simple roles were perfectly played, hinting at quiet intelligence beneath her innocence. Mr. Middleton, though awkward, succeeded by not trying to be funny. The main challenge was Gwendolen’s wish to wear her Greek costume. No charade theme came to mind that allowed her to strike a statue-like pose. Racine was out—no one could speak French. Improvisation risked burlesque. And Mr. Gascoigne forbade acting from actual plays, though he permitted charades in his sister-in-law’s home.

Everyone wanted to please her. Rex suggested ending with a tableau where her dignity wouldn’t be disrupted by speech. Gwendolen agreed—now only the choice remained.

“Something pleasant, children, I beg of you,” Mrs. Davilow said. “No Greek wickedness.”

“It’s no worse than Christian wickedness, mamma,” Gwendolen replied, recalling her earlier mention of Rachel-like heroines.

“And less scandalous,” Rex added. “Besides, it’s all history. How about Briseis being taken away? I’ll be Achilles, and you’ll look back at me—after the print at the rectory.”

“That would suit me well,” Gwendolen said, accepting.

But later, she changed her mind. “No. It won’t do. We need three men in proper costume, or it’ll be ridiculous.”

“I’ve got it,” Rex said, thinking. “Hermione as the statue in *The Winter’s Tale*? I’ll be Leontes, Miss Merry Paulina—each on one side. Our clothes won’t matter,” he laughed. “It’ll be more romantic if Leontes looks like Napoleon and Paulina like a modern spinster.”

They chose Hermione. All agreed age didn’t matter. Gwendolen insisted, however, that instead of just a tableau, there should be a brief scene—enough to signal the music, then she would step down and advance. Leontes, instead of embracing her, would kneel and kiss the hem of her robe—then the curtain would fall. The antechamber with folding doors made an ideal stage, and the whole household, plus Jarrett the carpenter, worked to prepare for what promised to be a success—after all, imitation often exceeds the original.

Gwendolen was thrilled by the prospect. She knew Herr Klesmer was back at Quetcham, and she had made sure he was invited.

Klesmer arrived. Calm and silent, he sat quietly, responding to questions with gentle, indistinct sounds—as if carrying his cross patiently in a world overrun with amateurs, or careful not to crush a noisy mouse with his lion’s paws.

Everything went smoothly—until the moment that revealed Gwendolen’s hidden vulnerability. At first, no one understood what had happened.

The tableau of Hermione stood out sharply from the rest. It worked perfectly, and a soft murmur of approval grew quiet as Paulina (Mrs. Davilow, in a white burnous) said, “Music, awake her, strike!”

Herr Klesmer, seated at the piano, struck a thunderous chord.

At the same instant, before Hermione could move, the panel beside the stage—aligned with the piano—flew open, revealing a chilling image: a dead face and a fleeing figure, starkly lit by wax candles.

Everyone jumped—but all eyes turned back to Gwendolen, who stood motionless, yet transformed. Her expression was terrifying: pallid lips parted, eyes wide and fixed. She looked like a statue possessed by fear. Her mother rushed forward, and Rex followed instinctively. But her mother’s touch sent her collapsing to her knees, hands covering her face. She trembled, silent, yet seemed aware enough to try to control herself. She was gently lifted and led away while the guests exchanged hushed comments.

“A magnificent bit of *plastik*!” Klesmer said to Miss Arrowpoint. A ripple of whispered questions spread.

“Was that part of the play?”

“Oh, no. Miss Harleth was too affected. A sensitive creature!”

“Dear me! I didn’t know there was a painting behind that panel.”

“Neither did I. Probably some oddity from the Earl’s family.”

“How painful! Please shut it.”

“Was the door locked? It’s mysterious. Must be spirits.”

“But there’s no medium.”

“How do you know? When such things happen, we must conclude there is.”

“Oh, the door wasn’t locked. Likely the piano’s vibration opened it.”

Mr. Gascoigne spoke up, asking Miss Merry to fetch the key. But Mrs. Vulcany thought it unseemly for a clergyman to rush to rationalize such things. Still, the key was found, and the rector locked the panel with a firm click—“so it won’t start again,” he said, pocketing the key.

Soon, Gwendolen returned, her usual spirit restored, determined to ignore the incident.

But when Klesmer said, “We must thank you for the perfect climax—you couldn’t have chosen a finer moment of *plastik*,” a flush of pleasure warmed her cheeks. She liked believing it was real talent, not fear. He saw through her mortification and meant to comfort her—by praising her performance. Gwendolen clung to the idea that he now admired her skill as much as her beauty. Her unease melted into pride.

Yet only Klesmer tried to soothe her. Others knew what had been rehearsed and what hadn’t—and no one else bothered. The event was quietly dropped.

But the memory lingered—not just in the minds of those present, but in the quiet corners of Gwendolen’s soul, where the line between art and terror had blurred, and the truth of her own nature had, for a moment, shown through.
