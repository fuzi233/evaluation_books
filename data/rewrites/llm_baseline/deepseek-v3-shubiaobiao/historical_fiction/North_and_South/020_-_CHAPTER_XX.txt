Margaret returned home burdened by the weight of what she had witnessed—the suffering in the Higginses' cottage lingered in her mind, making the thought of dressing for a dinner at the Thorntons' seem frivolous. Yet duty called; her mother, confined to her bed, would expect every detail upon her return.

"Did your factory friend agree to see you in your finery?" Mrs. Hale asked, her voice tinged with the fretfulness of prolonged illness.

"I scarcely thought to ask," Margaret admitted, her voice heavy. "She was too ill for such trifles."

Mrs. Hale sighed, a flicker of guilt crossing her face. "Perhaps some of Mr. Thornton’s port wine might help?"

Margaret shook her head. "Consumption does not yield to wine, Mama. Though I might take her some of our Helstone preserves—if only to offer comfort."

The contrast between her evening’s obligations and the poverty she had just seen gnawed at her. How could she adorn herself for a dinner while others starved? Yet her mother, in a rare burst of energy, insisted on packing a basket for the Higgins family—only to fret afterward whether such charity prolonged the workers' defiance.

Mr. Hale, caught between his wife’s impulsive kindness and Mr. Thornton’s stern practicality, wavered. He would visit Boucher in the morning, he decided, though Milton’s hardships still felt foreign to his pastoral sensibilities.

---

Margaret dressed mechanically, the ritual of pinning up her hair and fastening coral beads feeling hollow. Her reflection—pale against the black silk, her coiled hair like a crown—drew admiration from Dixon, but her mother’s wistful praise only sharpened her melancholy.

"I wish I were going with you," Mrs. Hale murmured, her eyes bright with memory. "You look every inch the lady."

Margaret kissed her, swallowing the urge to stay.

---

The Thorntons' home was a spectacle of opulence. The drawing room blazed with yellow damask and floral carpets, a deliberate assertion of wealth that bordered on ostentation. Mrs. Thornton presided with pride, her disdain for the striking workers barely veiled.

"Does the mill’s proximity trouble you?" Mr. Hale ventured.

"Not at all," she replied coolly. "We do not forget the source of our prosperity."

Fanny Thornton wrinkled her nose. "The noise is dreadful."

Margaret, silent, noted the irony—the masters dined in splendor while the workers starved.

When Mr. Thornton entered, his presence commanded the room. Margaret observed the ease with which he moved among his peers, his authority unchallenged. Yet his gaze, when it flickered to her, held an intensity that unsettled her.

---

Dinner was a lavish affair, the excess almost vulgar to Margaret’s London-trained eye. The conversation, dominated by the men, buzzed with trade disputes and market strategies. She listened, surprised by her own interest in their fierce debates—until Mr. Thornton’s voice cut through, decisive and calm.

Later, he approached her. "You sided with us tonight," he remarked.

"On principle," she said. "Though I wonder at the morality of some tactics."

"A ‘gentleman’ might hesitate," he countered. "A *man* acts."

The distinction intrigued her. Before she could reply, he was summoned away, leaving her to ponder the unyielding resolve in his words—and the cost of such conviction.

---

As the evening waned, Margaret overheard fragments of talk about the strike—Thornton’s unwavering stance, the threats of violence. His voice, steady as a drumbeat, dismissed concerns: *"I take the risk."*

She caught curious glances from the other guests, their whispers branding her an outsider. Fanny’s flustered explanations only deepened her isolation.

Yet amid the stifling grandeur, Margaret found herself reluctantly admiring the Milton men’s vigor—their unapologetic ambition, their vision of a future they might never see. It was a far cry from London’s polished ennui.

And when Mr. Thornton’s eyes met hers across the room, she sensed a challenge—one that unsettled and exhilarated her in equal measure.
