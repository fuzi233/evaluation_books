The Colonel Esmond, maternal grandfather of our Virginians and a man whose name has echoed through these pages, had spent many quiet years in the New World after leaving England behind. In the solitude of his American estate, he devoted part of his long leisure to writing down the story of his earlier life. These memoirs, though never published, were rich with personal recollections—and among them, Madame de Bernstein, known in her youth as Beatrice Esmond, held a prominent place. George Warrington had read these writings over and over again, so that by the time he met his aunt in person, he already knew her—knew her not as she was now, but as she once had been: young, radiant, and fiercely independent, a woman who had once stirred hearts and commanded attention.

Now, however, her beauty had faded; her eyes, once bright and bold, were dimmed by time; her spirit, once so lively, seemed dulled by the weight of years. And yet, as George watched her return from her evening gathering, rouged and adorned with diamonds, he could not help but think of what she must have been in her prime. Was it sorrow or satisfaction, he wondered, for such a woman to look back upon those days? When the heart grows cold, does it ache to remember how it once burned? When strength wanes, do we still find joy in recalling the fire of youth, the ease of laughter, the eagerness with which life was lived?

So it is with all things—roses bloom and fall, summer gives way to winter, and even the grandest palaces become ruins. “And that,” George thought, “was a beauty once.” He saw her now—this woman who had once been the center of admiration—surrounded by relics of her former glory. Crowds of lovers had once courted her; men had fallen silent before her presence. But now, only shadows remained.

He remembered a fireworks display at Williamsburg on the King’s birthday—the dazzling bursts of light, the sudden silence after the last spark died out, the hollow framework of the wheel left standing like a skeleton. That was what Aunt Beatrice’s past felt like to him now: brilliant, fleeting, and gone.

Harry had read the manuscripts too, but unlike George, he had no taste for reflection. Books did not interest him; introspection was not his nature. If he had pondered the words of his grandfather, he would not have drawn the same conclusions as his brother—nor would he have found any moral lesson in the tale of a woman’s lost splendor.

George said nothing about how intimately he knew his aunt’s history. There was no need. The truth stood plainly before him.

After dinner, the Baroness pointed to her escritoire with her cane and instructed her maid to fetch the letter beneath the inkwell. George recognized the handwriting immediately—it was the letter he had carried from Virginia.

“From this,” she said, fixing her nephew with a sharp glance, “it seems your return has brought some disagreement between you and my sister.”

“I did not know Madam Esmond mentioned it,” George replied.

She adjusted her spectacles—once famed for their fiery gaze—and scanned the letter before handing it to him.

It began:

> RICHMOND, VIRGINIA, December 26th, 1756
> HONOURED MADAM! AND SISTER!—I have received, and thankfully acknowledge, your ladyship’s letter of October 23rd, sent via the Rose packet. I reply at a season meant for peace and goodwill, though Heaven has seen fit to burden us still with sorrow and trial. My answer will be delivered by my eldest son, Mr. Esmond Warrington, who returned to us miraculously from the Valley of the Shadow of Death—as our previous correspondence has informed my poor Henry. He wishes, and I consent, to visit Europe, though he has been with us only briefly.

> I grieve deeply that my dear Harry should have presented himself in England under false pretenses—as if he were the rightful heir—when my son George, though dead to us, is still alive. Ah, madam! For eighteen months of captivity, what anguish have his mother’s and brother’s hearts endured! Harry’s heart is the tenderest of any man living. In the joy of seeing Mr. Esmond Warrington restored, he will forget the worldly loss he suffers. He will return to (comparative) poverty without regret. The most generous, the most obedient of sons, he willingly yields the inheritance that was his by birth—except for fate’s cruel twist—and the providential return of George.

> Your kindness toward my dear Harry will be doubly welcome now that he must live on a younger brother’s modest share. Many years ago, an excellent opportunity arose to secure his future here in Virginia—he might now own a noble estate, slaves, and stand well among the gentry, had his mother’s wishes been followed and his father’s small fortune invested wisely. But legal formalities, and—I regret to say—my elder son’s scruples, prevented it. This chance was lost. Yet Harry’s savings have been carefully preserved—may he long enjoy them! May Heaven bless you, dear sister, for whatever aid you may offer!

> From your letter, I understand the sum allotted to him has not covered his expenses among fine company. And rightly so—being the grandson of the Marquis of Esmond, one who nearly succeeded to his lordship’s title, he deserves equal footing with any nobleman in Britain. I have a sum set aside, always intended for his establishment. I entrust it to my eldest son, who, to his credit, holds deep affection for his brother, to use as best suits Harry’s needs.

At this point, George interrupted: “It took him out of prison yesterday, madam. I think that was the best use we could make of it.”

“Nay, sir!” she retorted sharply. “Why not buy him a commission? Or help him acquire land and slaves, if he fancies home? Besides, I had a mind to pay that debt myself.”

“I hope you’ll let him handle it,” George said calmly. “I ask leave to act as my brother’s banker. I’ve borrowed much from my mother—let me repay it to Harry.”

“Do you indeed?” she said, raising an eyebrow. “Give me a glass of wine. You’re a spendthrift, nephew! Read on—you’ll see your mother agrees. Here, drink to your health. Good Burgundy. Your grandfather never cared for Burgundy—he drank claret, when he drank at all.”

George continued:

> This remittance should cover any expenses Harry may have incurred due to the misunderstanding of his status. I wish I could trust my elder son’s prudence as fully as I do my Harry’s! But I fear Mr. Esmond W. has learned little humility during his captivity—though I have always taught it to my children. Should you show him these lines, when the Great Ocean divides us, he shall know that a mother’s prayers and blessings follow both her sons. No act I have ever done, no desire I have ever expressed—however little he may have obeyed—has not been for their welfare.

There was a scratch on the paper, made with a penknife, and a large stain, as if water had fallen upon it.

“Your mother writes well,” the Baroness remarked. “Did you two quarrel?”

“Yes, ma'am,” George answered quietly. “Many times. The last was over money—ransom promised to the old lieutenant who helped me escape. I told you he had a mistress, a poor Indian woman who aided me. Six weeks after my return, she arrived in Richmond, having traveled the same path I did. She brought me the token Museau had promised—a sign he’d assisted my flight. A new commander and reinforcements had reached Duquesne. Charges—of corruption, though unclear—were laid against Museau. He was arrested, tried to flee, but failed. Shot on the ramparts, he sent the woman to me with my grandfather’s watch and a note written in blood: *Send ce que je scavais* to a notary in Havre de Grace, France, to be forwarded to his family in Caen. Silverheels, the hunter, helped her reach safety. Without him, she might have been scalped.

“But at home, they received her coldly. They gave her no real welcome. I won’t say what suspicions they harbored—about her and me. She fell into drinking whenever possible. I ordered food and shelter for her, but she became a joke among the servants, a subject of gossip in town. Fortunately, the Governor was reasonable. I persuaded him to grant her passage back to her people. Her grief at parting only confirmed their doubts. A preacher condemned me from the pulpit—I believe. I had to cane another man. And I argued fiercely with Madam Esmond—our quarrel remains unresolved—because I insisted on paying the ransom to Museau’s heirs, as I had promised.

“You see,” George concluded, “scandal thrives at the edge of the wilderness, just as it does in the Old World.”

“Ah,” said Madame Bernstein softly, “I have suffered from it myself, my dear. Fill your glass. A touch of cherry brandy—’twill do you good.”

The letter continued:

> As for my poor Harry’s marriage—though I know, from bitter experience, the dangers youth faces, and would keep my boy from them at any cost, even if I wished him to marry a woman of rank, as befits his birth—Lady Maria Esmond is entirely out of the question. Her age is nearly mine. And I know my brother Castlewood left his daughters with the smallest portions. My Harry is so obedient that a word from me will be enough to end this imprudent match. Some once believed I myself considered a second marriage—with someone far above our station. No! I knew what was due to my children. Mr. Esmond W., as successor to this estate, is amply provided for. Now, my task is to save for his less fortunate brother. And since I do not wish to live alone, let him return soon to his loving mother.

> Your account of Harry fills my heart with gratitude. He is everything a mother could desire. A year in Europe will refine him beyond what Virginia can offer. Mr. Stack, our minister in Richmond, has a letter from Mr. Ward—my children’s tutor—who knows Lady Warrington and her admirable family. He reports that Harry has been with his cousins lately. I am thankful he has such good influence. May he heed their counsel and walk the path of true wisdom. Adieu, dear madam and sister! Accept the heartfelt thanks of a mother’s love. Though we have been apart, may these ties grow stronger. I am grateful you spoke kindly of my dear father—he truly was one of the finest men. He, too, thanks you for your kindness to one of his children. His daughter,
> With sincere thanks, your ladyship’s most dutiful and grateful sister and servant,

> P.S.—I have spoken with Lady Maria, but there is no need to tell her or Harry that either you or I hope to increase his fortune. The match is wholly unsuitable.

When George finished reading, he laid the letter down.

“As for myself, madam,” he said, “your letter reveals nothing new. I always knew Harry was the favorite son—deservedly so. He possesses qualities I lack. He has better looks—”

“Nay,” the Baroness interjected, glancing at him slyly, “that is not your fault. Were it not for his fair hair and your dark skin, one might pass for the other.”

George bowed slightly, a faint blush rising on his pale cheek.

“His disposition is bright, mine dark,” he went on. “Harry is cheerful; I am not. He wins affection easily; I have few friends.”

“My sister and you have your little disputes,” the Baroness observed. “We had such in our family. And if Madam Esmond takes after our mother—”

“My mother always called hers an angel upon earth,” George broke in.

“Eh!” she snapped. “That’s what people say when they’re dead! Rachel Castlewood was an angel, if you like—but angels are often inconvenient to live with. Perhaps they’re too pure for our sinful world. The air down here doesn’t agree with them. My poor mother was so perfect she couldn’t forgive me for being otherwise. Ah, mon Dieu! How she oppressed me with her heavenly airs!”

George lowered his eyes, remembering his own lonely youth. He did not wish to reveal more of his family secrets to this worldly woman, who seemed to read his thoughts despite his silence.

“I quite understand you, sir,” she said, “even when you speak not a word. A sermon every morning, one at night, and three on Sundays—that’s what people call goodness. Every pleasure forbidden. Balls are abominations. Theater is sin. Cards are damnation! What a life! Mon Dieu, what a life!”

“We played cards every night when we pleased,” George smiled. “And my grandfather loved Shakespeare so much, my mother never objected to his favorite author.”

“I recall,” she tapped her fan, “he could quote whole passages. Though I prefer Mr. Congreve. And then there was that dreadful Milton—whom he and Addison pretended to admire!”

“If you don’t care for Shakespeare,” George said gently, “you won’t quarrel with my mother for being indifferent to him. Indeed, I believe you misjudge her. Wherever there are the poor, she helps them. Wherever there are the sick—”

“She poisons them with her foul purges and pills!” the Baroness exclaimed.

“Of course,” George said, “just as your mother did.”

“She does her best!” he insisted. “She acts in good faith, doing what she believes is right.”

“I don’t blame you,” she said, “for keeping your counsel. But there is one thing we all agree on—this foolish marriage must be stopped. Do you know how old the woman is? I can tell you, though she tore the first page from the family Bible at Castlewood.”

“My mother knows her cousin’s age,” George replied. “She finds the difference shocking. A city-bred woman of that age, used to London society, would find our plantation a dismal place. And the house—such as it is—is not Harry’s. He is welcome, Heaven knows—perhaps more welcome than I, who inherit it by right. But I doubt his wife would—would like our colony.”

The Baroness laughed sharply.

“He, he! Nephew Warrington! Speak freely. I’ll tell no tales to your mother—though I know she has a temper and loves her own way. Harry has kept quiet, but it needed no wizard to see who ruled the household. I love my niece, Lady Molly, so dearly I’d wish her three years in Virginia under your mother’s rule. You look alarmed! Harry has said enough to show me who governs.”

“Madam,” George said, smiling, “I may say this: I don’t envy any woman coming into our house against my mother’s will. And my brother knows this well.”

“What? You two have discussed it? Of course you have. And the fool thinks himself bound by honor—of course he does, the gaby!”

“He says Lady Maria behaved nobly,” George explained. “When he was imprisoned, she brought him her jewels and every guinea she owned. That touched him deeply—he feels more obligated than ever. But I confess, he seems bound by duty rather than love—such is his present feeling.”

“My good creature,” the Baroness said, “don’t you see? Maria brings trinkets and a few guineas to Mr. Esmond, the heir of a great Virginia estate—not to the second son, who is penniless and has squandered every shilling. I swear, on my honor, that knowing Harry’s stubbornness and the misery awaiting him, I tried to bribe Maria to break off the engagement. I failed—because I couldn’t bribe high enough. When he was in prison, I sent my lawyer to him, offering to pay his debts—if he would leave her. But Maria had already acted. Harry refused to go back on his word.”

Then, with a wicked twinkle, she recounted the affair in full—the cynical terms, the sharp truths, the kind of language that passed among the elite in her day, though we no longer utter such things in polite company. And perhaps that is for the better. We may not be more virtuous, but we are more decent. We may not be purer, but we are cleaner.

She talked so long, so cleverly, that she was delighted with herself and her audience. As Mrs. Brett helped her retire, she declared she had changed her opinion of George—now he was handsome, witty, far superior to poor Harry (whose intellect, she admitted, was sadly limited), and possessed a certain melancholy dignity that reminded her of the Colonel.

Had she ever told Brett about the Colonel? Many times, no doubt. And tonight, she told her again.

Meanwhile, George, walking home late, was not so pleased. What a strange picture of life he had seen! How bitterly she mocked everyone—yet revealed, in her scorn, the selfishness of her own family, the petty ambitions, the grasping desires, the endless pursuit of trivial rewards. The scenes of faded pleasures, hollow triumphs, and wasted lives—how they painted the world around her!

“Are these the heirs of noble blood?” he asked himself, passing houses where guests were leaving, chairmen yawning over dying torches. “Are these the proud bearers of ancient names and ancestral honors? Our family tree bears coats-of-arms and titles older than the Conquest. Did knights of old turn away from a friend in need? Did noble lords deceive defenseless women? Did chivalry cheat at dice or lie about horses? Can it be that this worldly woman has deceived my poor Harry? That her tears are false, and she will abandon him when she learns he is poor? Shall we pack our trunks and sail home?”

He reached his door, still turning over these thoughts. Gumbo came forward, yawning, candle in hand. Inside, Harry sat asleep before the cold hearth, his pipe ashed out beside him.

He woke suddenly, eyes clouded by sleep—then lit with joy at the sight of George.

“There he is, flesh and blood, thank God!” he exclaimed, throwing an arm around his brother. “I was dreaming of you—Ward giving us our lesson! Remember the ruler, Georgy? Bless my soul—it’s three o’clock! Where have you been, Mr. George? Supped? I ate at White’s, but I’m hungry again. I didn’t play—no, no! Not for younger brothers! And Lord March paid me fifty pounds he lost to me. I bet against his horse and backed the Duke of Hamilton’s! They raced this morning—Lord March was under weight. He lost. Paid up. And was as sour as a bear. Let’s have one pipe, Georgy—just one.”

And after the smoke, the brothers went to bed. May they rest well—for there is no greater comfort than to see brothers who love one another.
