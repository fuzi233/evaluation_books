Waiting for an earnest letter rarely leaves the mind unchanged. When Margaret finally received hers, she poured out her energy and her faith—whether justified by her dying father's vision or merely his delusion. Once the letter was read and Luke departed, she recoiled in horror at her own credulity. Her conscience pricked sharply regarding Luke, and shortly after, Catherine arrived to scold her. For paying the price of false hopes and elevated spirits, Margaret was forced to sink into a depth of despondency even darker than before.

She was discovered in this state by a steadfast friend she had recently made, Joan Ketel. This good woman entered radiating with an idea. "Margaret, I know the cure for your illness!" Joan exclaimed. "There is a hermit in Gouda, a wondrous holy man. In his mood, he can foretell what is coming."

"I have heard of him," Margaret replied hopelessly.

With some difficulty, Joan persuaded her to walk as far as Gouda to consult the hermit. They carried a basket containing butter and eggs and made their way to his cave. How had these two become such close companions? Six weeks prior, Jorian had fallen violently ill with a bowel disease. It began with raging pain; when that subsided, leaving him weak, a terrible symptom followed: neither liquid nor solid food could remain in his stomach for a single minute.

The doctor declared, "If this continues for many hours, he must die. Therefore, boil a chicken with a golden angel floating in the water, and let him eat that!" Alas, the gilt-chicken broth shared the fate of the simpler dishes that preceded it. Next, the healer soaked the thumb of Saint Sergius in beef broth, yielding the same result. Desperate, Joan ran weeping to Margaret to borrow linen for a shroud.

"Let me see him," Margaret commanded.

She entered the room, felt his pulse, and gasped, "Ah! You have not struck at the root. Open the window! You are stifling him. Change all his linens immediately."

"Alack, woman, why?" the medieval wife protested. "Why soil more linen for a dying man?"

"Do as I bid you," Margaret said dully, turning away.

Joan, compelled by force of habit, obeyed. Margaret returned with her apron full of a flowering herb. She prepared a decoction and brought it to the bedside. Before administering it, she took a spoonful herself, smacking her lips hypocritically.

"That is fair," Jorian managed with a feeble attempt at humor. "First it tastes sweet, now bitter."

Once he had swallowed the mixture, she engaged him in conversation. This "bitter-sweet" tonic became his constant companion. Observing its effect, she built upon it like cards stacked high: she mixed a drop of Schiedam gin into the third spoonful and a bit of beaten egg yolk into the seventh. With the patience characteristic of her sex, she coaxed his body back from Death's grasp. Finally, Nature, having been patted rather than kicked under the bed, restored Jorian Ketel to his feet. Yet, the doctress made them both swear eternal silence on her guilty deed. "They would imprison me, separating me from my child," she whispered.

The simple remedy that saved Jorian was known as sweet feverfew, which Margaret had gathered from his own garden. Her keen eyesight had spotted it growing through his window.

Thus, Margaret and Joan arrived at the hermit's cave and placed their offering of butter and eggs on the small stone platform. Margaret pressed her mouth to the aperture designed for speaking and prayed aloud: "Holy hermit, we bring thee the finest butter and eggs. I, a poor deserted girl, technically a wife though no longer one, and mother of the sweetest babe, come to beg you tell me: Is he quick or dead? True to his vows or false?"

A faint, rasping voice drifted from within the cave: "Trouble me not with earthly matters. Send me a holy friar; I am dying."

"Alas!" cried Margaret. "Is it truly so, poor soul? Then let us enter to help you."

"Saints forbid! That is a woman's voice. Send me a holy friar."

They turned and walked back as they had come. Joan could not suppress her indignation. "Are women imps of darkness, then, that we cannot approach a dying bed?"

But Margaret was too deeply dejected to respond. Joan offered rough consolation, which was ignored until she spoke again: "And Jorian will speak out soon enough. He is just on the boil. He is very grateful to you, believe it."

"Seeing is believing," Margaret replied with quiet bitterness.

"Not that he thinks you might have saved him with something more extraordinary than that," Joan continued. "'A man of my size cured with feverfew?' he says. 'Why, if there is such a sorry herb...' He was even thinking of pulling up all his feverfew. I told him remedies aren't better because they are rare. You and the feverfew cured him when the grand medicines failed completely. So I said, 'Go down on your four bones to the feverfew.' But indeed, at bottom, he is grateful. You are his only thought and his only talk. However, he sees Gerard's people coming around, good friends... and he said only last night—"

"Well?"

"He made me vow not to tell you."

"Prithee, tell me."

"Well, he said: 'If I reveal what little I know, it won't bring him back, and it will set everyone by the ears. I wish I had more sense,' he muttered. 'I am sorely perplexed. But least said is soonest mended.' That is his favorite phrase; he returns to it from a mile away."

Margaret shook her head. "Yes, we are wading in deep waters, my poor babe and I."

It was Saturday night, and there was no sign of Luke.

"Poor Luke!" Margaret murmured. "It was very kind of him to go on such an errand."

"He is one out of a hundred," Catherine responded warmly. "Mother, do you think he would be kind to little Gerard?"

"I am sure he would. So please be kinder to him when he returns! Will you promise?"

"Yes."

Brother Clement, guided by the nuns, navigated a bend in the river, striding vigorously toward a station miles closer to the coast than where Luke was lying in wait for Gerard Eliassoen. The following morning, he started early and arrived in Rotterdam by noon. He headed straight for the port, eager not to keep Jerome waiting.

He observed several monks of his order on the quay and approached them, but Jerome was nowhere to be seen. He asked one if Jerome had arrived.

"Surely, brother," the monk replied.

"Prithee, where is he?"

"Where? Why, there!" the monk pointed to a ship setting sail. Clement noticed that all the other monks were gazing seaward.

"What? Gone without me! Oh, Jerome! Jerome!" he cried in a voice of anguish.

Several friars turned to stare.

"You must be Brother Clement," one said eventually. On hearing this, they kissed him and greeted him with warm brotherly affection, handing him a letter Jerome had entrusted to them. It was a hasty scrawl. The writer informed him coldly that a ship was about to depart for England and that he was loath to waste time. He noted that Clement might follow if he wished, but advised him to stay behind and preach to his own countrymen.

"Give glory to God, brother. You possess a wonderful power over Dutch hearts, but you are no match for those haughty islanders; you are too tender."

"Know that on my journey, I met someone who asked for you under the name you bore in the world. Be on your guard! Do not let the world catch you again in any silken net. Remember: Solitude, Fasting, and Prayer are the sword, spear, and shield of the soul. Farewell."

Clement was deeply shocked and mortified by this contemptuous abandonment and the icy tone of the message. He promised the good monks he would sleep at the convent and preach wherever the prior appointed, for Jerome had once raised him to the skies as a preacher. Now, stung to the quick, he withdrew abruptly, needing solitude.

He questioned himself: Was there some incurable fault in him, repulsive to such a true son of Dominic? Or was Jerome himself devoid of the Christian Love that St. Paul held above faith itself? Shipwrecked together, saved on the same fragment of wreckage—his pupil, his penitent, his son in the Church—and now, for four hundred miles, his fellow traveler in Christ, only to be shaken off like dirt at the first opportunity with harsh, cold disdain.

"Why are worldly hearts no colder nor less untrustworthy than this," he lamented. "The only one who ever truly loved me lies in a grave nearby. Fly me, fly to England, man born without a heart. I shall go and pray over a grave at Sevenbergen."

Three hours later, he passed Peter's cottage. A troop of noisy children played near the door, and the house had been repaired, a new outhouse added. He turned his head hastily away, unwilling to disturb a picture cherished in his memory, and proceeded to the churchyard.
