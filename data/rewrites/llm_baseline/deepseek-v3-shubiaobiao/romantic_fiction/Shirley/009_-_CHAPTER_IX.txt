The evening air carried a biting chill as frost crept over the gardens of Briarmains, settling like a silent intruder upon the budding shrubs and the stone path leading to the Yorke residence. The house itself, sturdy and unyielding, stood as it had for generations—older than the road that now passed it, a relic of quieter times when the only approach was a winding lane through open fields.

Inside, warmth and light spilled from the windows, a stark contrast to the cold outside. The clamor of Briar Chapel’s prayer meeting echoed nearby, hymns rising with fervor into the night. The words, disjointed yet impassioned, reached even the ears of those within Briarmains:

But the house had its own subdued liveliness. In the back parlor, the Yorke family gathered, the fire blazing high despite the season—Mr. Yorke insisted on it. He sat beside the hearth, a book idle in his hands, his gaze fixed not on the pages but on his children. Opposite him, his wife maintained her habitual solemnity, her presence a weight in the room.

The children were a study in contrasts. Rose, the eldest daughter, quiet and thoughtful, bore her father’s sharp features softened by youth. Jessy, bright and restless, was all charm and chatter, her laughter a defiance of her mother’s gravity. The boys—Matthew, Mark, and Martin—were engrossed in their game, their expressions revealing more than they knew. Matthew’s temper simmered beneath the surface, Mark’s calm held an edge of cynicism, and Martin, though unremarkable now, carried the promise of something brighter.

A knock at the door went unnoticed until Mrs. Yorke’s voice, always pitched to funereal solemnity, called, *"Come in."*

Robert Moore entered, greeted with her customary lack of ceremony.

*"Can a single man be said to have a home?"* he countered.

She scoffed. *"Nonsense. Your sister keeps house for you, doesn’t she?"*

*"Not enough to tie him down,"* Mr. Yorke remarked with a dry chuckle. *"I had sisters too at his age, but that didn’t stop me from looking for a wife."*

*"And see where it’s brought him,"* Mrs. Yorke added, casting a glance at their brood. *"Four sons and two daughters—think on that, young man, before you leap into matrimony."*

Moore’s reply was grim. *"These aren’t times for leaping at all."*

She nodded approvingly, but Jessy, indignant, scrambled from her father’s lap to Moore’s.

*"He’s already married!"* she declared. *"He promised me last summer, when I wore my new white frock. Didn’t you, Father?"*

The room’s tensions eased, if only for a moment, as laughter broke through—even Mrs. Yorke’s stern facade wavered. Yet beneath it all, the night pressed on, cold and unrelenting, as though waiting to see what warmth, what fire, might yet endure.
