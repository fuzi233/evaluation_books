I yield to a custom I have long despised, yet lack the stubborn spirit to resist, lingering on the final stage of this tale to gather up the small, scattered fragments of human life I wove through the story: not all of them, for the passing wayfarers who crossed Gerard’s path along his journey have no further claim on me. Their purpose was served the moment they convinced you that the young pilgrim traveled from Holland to Rome through a world of breathing, contradictory real people, not a population of stiff, lifeless dolls.

Eli and Catherine lived to a remarkably great old age, so long that even the memory of their son Gerard and his beloved Margaret softened and dimmed to faint, hazy echoes in the twilight of their years. Giles too was long-lived: he journeyed to take service at the court of Bavaria, and was still living there at ninety, though by then he had weathered down to little more than bone and tanned, leathery skin, his voice still blaring out loud and brassy as a military trumpet.

Cornelis, meanwhile, had no rivals left standing between him and the family shop, and was long ago fully forgiven by his mother, who clung to him all the tighter now that every other one of her children had scattered to far-flung corners of the world. He waited, and waited, and waited for his elderly parents to draw their last breath. But Catherine’s old shrewd prediction came true long before the two of them wore out their days: this man, who had done nothing but loiter and covet what was not yet his, rusted away himself first. At sixty-five, he lay dying of creeping old age in the arms of his mother, still hale and sharp at eighty-six. He drifted unconscious for a while, then woke clear in the very last moments before death, saw her leaning over him, and babbled eagerly of all the grand renovations he would make to the shop and the whole premises the very second they became his. “Yes, my dearest boy,” the poor old woman soothed him gently, and a minute later he was still as clay, and that clay was borne to his grave by every pair of feet whose shoes he had spent decades idly waiting for.

Denys, brokenhearted by the death of his dearest comrade Gerard, was glad to turn his face for home to Burgundy. A small royal pension the court granted him kept him housed and fed for years, until he unexpectedly came into a sizable inheritance left by a distant relation. For decades after that, he was a well-known fixture in his hometown: a crusty, gruff old soldier who could spin rousing, vivid tales of war and foreign roads when he chose, and who railed against the whole fairer sex with a bitter, unshakable edge no one could ever talk him out of.

Jerome, disgusted by the loose, lax morals he’d seen across the northern lands, retired to Italy, and thanks to his powerful high-placed connections, rose to become a mitred abbot by the time he was seventy. He clamped down hard on discipline in his abbey, turning the screw of monastic rule tight until his monks both revered and resented him. He ruled his community with an iron rod for ten full years. Then one night he died, all alone—for he had spent his whole life carving rules and enforcing order, and had never once found the way to unlock a single human heart. The Vulgate Bible rested on his pillow, a crucifix was clasped tight in his hand, and on his lips was a faint soft smile no one had ever seen grace his face while he lived. So I like to think, in that terrible, solemn hour, he was not quite as alone as he seemed. May he rest in peace. The Master he served has many servants, and they carry many different natures; even the faithful ones can be gruff and unyielding, as is often the case in our mortal lives.

The yellow-haired little laddie, Gerard Gerardson, belongs not to fiction, but to recorded history. History has written of his birth in far grander terms than mine. Carved over the doorway of the tailor’s house on Brede Kirk Straet are the words: *“HAEC EST PARVA DOMUS NATUS QUA MAGNUS ERASMUS”* — This is the little house that gave birth to great Erasmus. The world has written dozens of biographies of him in the centuries since, but there is still work left for history to do. No scholar has ever truly comprehended the great Erasmus, not any more than a pygmy can wrap his mind around a giant, or a biased partisan can fairly judge a man of perfect impartiality. He was the foremost scholar and divine of his entire age, and he was also a heaven-born dramatist, unmatched across his century. Some of the brightest, most memorable scenes in this very tale are drawn directly from his medieval pen, and they shine bright wherever they appear on our pages. For the words of a genius as towering as his are never born to die. Once their immediate work on their own generation is done, they may seem to slumber, forgotten in dark library stacks—but with every fresh wave of intelligence Time pours over the generations that study him, they prove their immortal nature. They rise again, spring up from the dust of old great libraries, bud, bloom, bear fruit, and scatter seed, generation after generation, age after uncounted age.
