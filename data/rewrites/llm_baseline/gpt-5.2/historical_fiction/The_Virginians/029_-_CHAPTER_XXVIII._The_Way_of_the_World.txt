After two or three days at Tunbridge Wells, the young Virginian found himself, to his own wonder, promoted from stranger to spectacle, as though he were a rare dish newly set upon the board and every eye must taste him first. No coroneted lord drew half so much staring. Even the Bishop of Salisbury, grave as a winter pew, scarce commanded such bows. Folks wheeled about to look after Harry as he went by; farmers in from the lanes paused in market with their baskets and their brown eggs, gaping as if he were an Indian prince. At the Rooms, stout matrons beckoned him close with hands that smelt of lavender-water and orange-peel, contrived a vacancy upon the bench, and left him, by accident of motherly providence, alone with daughters who smiled like china shepherdesses.

The Wells had measured him to the acre and the shilling. At every tea-table where the spoons clinked against saucers and the kettle sang, his Virginian lands were cast up like sums, his income weighed as carefully as sugar. His winnings at play were bandied about with as much relish as a new receipt for syllabub; wonderful, the quickness with which neighbours season our names with their own conjectures. The interest ran so high that even his servant profited by it. People smiled upon Gumbo, drew him aside to the alehouse door, and treated him to cold meat and a foaming mug, as though truth might be coaxed out of him with beef and beer. Gumbo, who had always possessed a genius for enlargement, grew visibly rounder under this diet, became a principal person among the valets’ fraternity, and lied more enormously than ever—fed, as it were, upon other men’s curiosity.

No party, it seemed, was complete unless Mr. Warrington made one of it. Harry was amused, a little astonished, and carried his new consequence with tolerable ease. He had been bred in his own country to think well of himself; the sudden English homage did not lessen that natural opinion, though it did not wholly intoxicate him. He did not swagger about his victories, nor crow like a cock upon a dung-hill; yet he had a queer colonial notion of honour which bound him to meet any summons as if Old Virginia herself had been challenged. If a gentleman called him to a horse-match, or a drinking-bout, or a game at cards, Harry felt he must not draw back. And indeed they were ready enough to try him at all these trials. He had a strong head for wine, a steady hand for play, a firm seat, and nerves that did not flutter; and the representative of his province came off very handsomely in his friendly rivalry with the mother-country.

Madame de Bernstein—who had her fill of cards nightly, and no doubt repaired those losses of which we heard before—was delighted with her nephew’s credit. He shot with Jack Morris and beat him; rode a match with Mr. Scamper and won; took up tennis against Captain Batts, and though he had never tried the game, learned its springing, stinging strokes in a few days, as a good cook learns a new sauce by tasting it once. He played with the famous gamesters, my Lords Chesterfield and March, and both bore witness to his coolness, gallantry, and breeding.

At books Harry was no prodigy; but he wrote as well as many men of fashion, and the naïveté of his ignorance diverted the old lady. She had read in her time, and could talk learnedly with learned folk; she relished humour, had a taste for Molière and Mr. Fielding; yet she loved the world far better than the library, and would leave the best chapter for a rubber with the same promptness with which she would quit a dish of stewed prunes for a plate of hot fritters. She took a fond, tyrannical pleasure in Harry’s toilette—rummaged old laces for his ruffles, found him a pretty diamond brooch for his frill—dressing him as one garnishes a handsome capon with lemon and parsley, not for nourishment but for admiration. He became the prime favourite among her nephews and kin. Lady Maria, I fear, was only too well pleased at his successes, and did not grudge him his superiority over her brothers; but those gentlemen must have quaked when they heard of Harry’s astonishing luck and the advance he had made in their rich aunt’s affections.

After a fortnight at Tunbridge, he was a personage. He knew the best company in the place. Was it his fault if he grew acquainted with the bad likewise? Was he very wrong in taking the world as he found it, and drinking from that bright cup of pleasure which was held brimming to his lips? The old aunt enjoyed his triumphs, and bade him pursue his enjoyments. She was no rigorous moralist, nor perhaps a wholesome instructress for youth. Had the saucy Cattarina written him billets-doux, Aunt Bernstein would likely have told him to go; but Harry had brought from his thinly-peopled colonial home a stock of modesty that still sat upon him like honest homespun linen. Libertinism was not the fashion of those rough towns and wide plantations. The vices of great cities were scarcely known there, much less practised. Harry blushed at the daring talk of his new associates. Even Aunt Bernstein’s jests and freedom astonished him, so that the worldly old woman would call him Joseph, or simpleton.

Yet, innocent as he was, the world gave him credit for being as bad as the rest. How was he to guess there was harm in keeping company with Cattarina, when he had seen my Lord March driving her in his phaeton? Harry thought no sin lay in giving her his arm and walking openly with her in the public walks, where the gravel crunched beneath their feet and the band, in the distance, scraped out its fashionable airs. She took a fancy to a trinket in a toy-shop; and his pockets being full of money, he was pleased to buy her the locket she coveted. Next day it was lace; again he gratified her. Next day something else; Madame Cattarina’s desires were like a table that is never cleared. At last she asked for diamond drops for her pretty ears, and here Harry stopped—turned her off with a laugh and a jest. He was shrewd enough, not reckless nor prodigal, though generous. But who was to give him credit for his modesty? Old Bernstein would insist that her nephew played Don Juan and supplanted my Lord March; and she insisted the more when poor Maria was by, loving to stab that tender heart and enjoy her niece’s piteous silence.

“Boys will be boys, my dear,” the Baroness would say, “and I don’t want Harry to be the first milksop in his family!”

The bread Maria ate at her aunt’s expense choked her sometimes. O me! how hard and indigestible some women can make the very loaf.

Mr. Wolfe was forever coming from Westerham to pay court to the lady of his love; and as Harry knew the Colonel’s mind was wholly in that pursuit, he did not look to see much of him, though he liked the officer’s conversation. It was different from the ribald talk around Harry. Mr. Wolfe never spoke of cards, or horse pedigrees, nor bragged of his prowess in the hunting-field, nor boasted of women’s favours, nor retailed the innumerable scandals of the time. And it was not a good time. That old world was more dissolute than ours. There was an old King with mistresses openly in his train, whom great people honoured. There was a nobility mad for pleasure; there was a looseness in words and acts which we, as faithful historians, must note without particulars that would needlessly offend honest readers. Harry had fallen among some of the wildest of these wild people, and had found an old relative who lived in the thick of the rout.

So, at first, Harry did not remark how Colonel Wolfe avoided him, nor, when they met by chance, notice the coldness in the other’s manner. He did not know what stories were told of him. Who ever does know the stories that are told of him? Who makes them? Who fathers those wondrous lies? Poor Harry did not know the reputation he was acquiring; that whilst he rode and played and took his frolic, he passed among respectable folk for the most abandoned and profligate of young men. Alas, alas, to think that the lad we liked—gentle, quiet, easily pleased—should be, in report, a hardened spendthrift, a confirmed gamester, a frequenter of abandoned women! Such tales reached honest Colonel Lambert at Oakhurst: first one, then another, then a crowd of them, until his kind heart was full of care. His family saw plainly that something gnawed him.

At first Lambert would not speak. He put aside his wife’s fond questioning. Mrs. Lambert’s imagination ran to misfortune: ruin, dangerous orders, one of the boys ill or dead—an anxious woman can invent tragedies faster than a kitchen can boil water. Who can resist the cross-examination of the conjugal pillow? Lambert was forced at last to tell part of what he knew. His wife was as grieved and amazed as he had been. From their chamber the grief, stifled awhile under bed-pillows, came downstairs. Theo and Hester caught it from their parents, and had it very bad. Kind little wounded hearts! Hester at first flew into a passion, clenched her small fists, grew red, and vowed she would not believe a word of the wicked tales; but she ended by believing them. Scandal almost always masters people—especially the good and innocent. Oh, the serpent they had warmed at their fire! Oh, the wretched, wretched boy! To think of his walking about with that horrible painted Frenchwoman, giving her diamond necklaces, parading his shame before all society at the Wells!

The three ladies, after crying over it, and Lambert too deeply moved to bear it alone, took the parson into their confidence. In vain he preached next Sunday his favourite sermon against scandal and our propensity to think evil. We repent, we promise to do so no more; and when the next ill story comes we swallow it, as if it were physic and must be taken. So did those kindly Oakhurst people believe what they heard of poor Harry Warrington.

Meanwhile Harry was far too pleased with himself to guess how ill his friends were thinking of him. He pursued an idle, pleasant, unprofitable life, with no notion of the hubbub he made, nor of the dreadful repute in which many good men held him.

One day, coming out from a match at tennis with Mr. Batts—his limbs warmed, his spirits high, the very air at the Wells sounding, in his ears, like applause—Harry overtook Colonel Wolfe, returning from one of his visits to the lady of his heart. Harry held out his hand. Wolfe took it; but his salutation was so cold that Harry could not but observe it. And when Mr. Batts made a fine bow, the Colonel scarcely touched his hat with his forefinger. The tennis captain walked away, looking disconcerted, leaving Harry behind with his friend.

Mr. Wolfe strode on, very erect, silent, and chill as a north wall.

“I have not seen you these many days,” said Harry.

“You have had other companions,” Wolfe returned curtly.

“But I had rather be with you than any of them,” cried the young man.

“Indeed I might be better company for you than some,” said the other.

“Is it Captain Batts you mean?” asked Harry. “He is no favourite of mine, I own; he had a rascally reputation in the army, and I doubt he has not mended it since he was turned out. You certainly might find a better friend than Captain Batts. Pardon the freedom.”

“Friend? He is no friend: he only teaches me tennis. He is hand-in-glove with my lord and the people of fashion who play.”

“I am not a man of fashion,” said Mr. Wolfe.

“My dear Colonel, what is the matter? Have I angered you? You speak as if I had, and I am not conscious of having done aught to forfeit your regard.”

“I will be free with you, Mr. Warrington,” said Wolfe gravely. “I don’t like some of your friends.”

“Why, they are men of the first rank and fashion in England,” Harry answered, not choosing to take offence at bluntness.

“Exactly—too high for a hard-working poor soldier like me. If you continue to live with such, you will find numbers of us humdrum people can’t afford to keep such company. I am here paying my addresses to an honourable lady. I met you yesterday openly walking with a French ballet-dancer, and you took off your hat. I must tell you plainly, I had rather you would not take off your hat when you go out in such company.”

“Sir!” Harry cried, the blood rushing up under his skin till his face burned. “Do you mean that I am to forgo the honour of Colonel Wolfe’s acquaintance altogether?”

“I shall request you to do so when you are in company with that person,” said Wolfe angrily; and he used a word not fit to be written here, though Shakespeare puts it into Othello’s mouth.

“Great heavens! what a shame to speak so of any woman!” Harry burst out. “How dare any man say that poor creature is not honest?”

“You ought to know best, sir,” Wolfe said, looking at him with surprise, “or the world belies you very much.”

“What ought I to know best? I see a poor little French dancer come hither with her mother and ordered by the doctors to drink the waters. I know people of my rank don’t keep company with hers; but are you so squeamish? Have I not heard you say you did not value birth, and that all honest people ought to be equal? Why should I not give this little unprotected woman my arm? There are scarce half a dozen people here who can speak a word of her language. I can speak a little French, and she is welcome to it; and if Colonel Wolfe does not choose to touch his hat to me when I walk with her—by George he may leave it alone!”

“You don’t mean to say,” Wolfe answered, studying him, “that you do not know the woman’s character?”

“Of course she is a dancer, and I suppose no better or worse than her neighbours. But I mean to say, had she been a duchess—or your grandmother—I could not have respected her more.”

“You don’t mean to say you did not win her at dice from Lord March?”

“At what?”

“At dice. Everybody knows the story. Not a soul at the Wells is ignorant of it. I heard it but now in the company of that good old Mr. Richardson, and the ladies were saying you would be a character for a colonial Lovelace.”

“What on earth else have they said about me?” Harry asked; and then Wolfe, who was fair enough to speak out, told him. One charge after another—alarming accounts of his wickedness and profligacy—were laid before him. He was said to corrupt virtue, to drink habitually, to game like a professional, to blaspheme, to sneer at religion; to be a fitting companion for my Lord March and that society into which he had fallen.

“I tell you these things,” Wolfe said, “because it is fair you should know what is said of you; and because, from the way you met the last charge, I do heartily believe you are innocent of most of the rest. I feel I, for one, have been doing you wrong; and I ask your pardon sincerely.”

Harry, eager and honest, took his hand at once; and this time their grasp had warmth in it. As for most accusations, Harry rebutted them easily. Play he owned to; and he defended it with the same plain logic with which a hungry man defends a good supper. A gentleman, he thought, should not refuse a fair challenge from other gentlemen if his means allowed; he never would play beyond those means. After winning considerably at first, he could afford large stakes, since he was playing with other men’s money. Play was fair—certainly pleasant. Did not all England, except the Methodists, play? Had he not seen the best company at the Wells over the cards—his aunt among them?

Mr. Wolfe did not at once comment upon Harry’s notion of “best company,” but spoke frankly, because Harry’s own frankness had won him, and warned him that the life he led might be the pleasantest, but was not the most profitable.

“It can’t be, sir,” Wolfe said, “that a man is to pass his days at horse-racing and tennis, and his nights carousing or at cards. Every man was made to do some work; and a gentleman, if he has none, must make some. Do you know the laws of your country, Mr. Warrington? Being a great proprietor, you will doubtless one day be a magistrate at home. Have you travelled your own land, made yourself acquainted with its trades and manufactures? These are fit things for a gentleman to study, and may occupy him as well as a cock-fight or a cricket-match. Do you know anything of our profession? That, at least, you will allow is noble; and there is plenty in it to learn, and suited, I should think, to you. I speak of it rather than of books and learned professions, because, as far as I can judge, your genius does not lie that way. But honour is the aim of life; and every man can serve his country one way or another. Be sure, sir, idle bread is the most dangerous bread that is eaten. Cards and pleasure may be taken after work, but not instead of work, and all day. And do you know—though the world calls you the Fortunate Youth—I think you are rather Warrington the Unlucky; for you are followed by daily idleness, daily flattery, daily temptation; and the Lord send you deliverance out of your good fortune.”

Harry did not like to tell his aunt that afternoon why he looked grave. He meant not to drink; but at the ordinary there were jolly fellows and the bottle went round, the glass clinked, the room grew loud with laughter that did not so much answer the musicians outside as drown them. He meant not to play; but a fourth was wanted at his aunt’s table—how resist? The cards made their little dry music as they were dealt; gold slid and rang; phrases of fashionable wit fell like crumbs. He was the old lady’s partner again and again; he had Somebody’s own luck, to be sure; and once more he watched the dawn creep in, pale as watered milk, and feasted at sunrise on chickens and champagne—rich flesh torn hot from the bone, the wine sharp and yeasty on his tongue—while outside, the waking world began its daily murmur, and the Wells, already busy with talk, began to cook his name anew.
