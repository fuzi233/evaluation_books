Tito possessed ample justification for claiming his safety. Over the preceding three months, anticipating the likely exposure of the Medicean conspirators, he had meticulously prepared his defenses. He had fortified his standing in both Rome and Milan, positioning himself as the conduit for clandestine intelligence and indirect maneuvers against the Friar and the popular faction. Simultaneously, he had cultivated the favor of that very popular party with unprecedented diligence, projecting a subtle alignment with their political convictions. Yet, throughout this double-dealing, he had not withdrawn his active service from the Medici; rather, he had sought greater employment and exclusive trust from them. Maintaining this triple game proved effortless. The Medici, admitting duplicity as a principle of statecraft, lacked the moral compass to judge a colleague who, unlike themselves, possessed no hereditary ties, alliances, or prejudices deeply rooted in their cause. To them, deceiving the enemy was a fair strategy; deceiving their own party was a baseness they felt no urge to commit. In employing Tito's agile talents, they remained oblivious to the fact that his lack of traditional attachment—which made him a convenient tool—was also the absence of the primary guarantee of mutual honor among themselves.

Furthermore, the *Arrabbiati* of Rome and Milan, the aristocratic opponents of Savonarola, operated a network of espionage and covert correspondence where profound hypocrisy was the highest currency and the largest payment the greatest merit. Suspecting an agent merely because he played a role convincingly would have been a logical absurdity. Conversely, the *Piagnoni*, driven by the direct energy of fervent conviction, were prone to believe Tito's professed loyalty to their cause, largely because he feigned no religious sympathy. Under these conditions, the last quarter had been a period of dazzling success for Tito. His primary ambition was to secure a permanent post in either Rome or Milan. He harbored a growing resolve to leave Florence once the opportune moment arrived, seeking the ease of capital cities where talent and learning were lavishly rewarded. Currently, the scales tipped toward Milan; if he could deliver decisive services to Duke Ludovico Sforza within the year, the prospects at the Milanese court outweighed those of Rome.

The revelation of the Medicean conspiracy had indeed been within Tito's foresight, though not the specific manner of its occurrence. The arrest of Lamberto dell'Antella, found carrying incriminating letters and fueled by bitter hatred for the Medici, was an unforeseen variable. Despite the careful pretexts guarding his agency, Tito could not evade implication in a broad disclosure of Medicean plots. However, his sharp mind quickly charted a path to safety with minimal collateral damage. While preserving one's integrity is pleasant, the skin remains sensitive to the air; Tito's calculations had not failed. That evening, before returning home, he secured the three outcomes he desired: exemption from prosecution regarding his alleged complicity with the Medici, retention of his secretaryship for another year (unless he chose to resign), and the strict confidentiality of the price exacted for these guarantees.

To most, the cost seemed exorbitant, and Tito himself would have preferred not to pay it. His first move was to win over Francesco Valori, one of the Ten holding immediate authority over him and a member of the special council investigating the plot. As the leader of the *Piagnoni*, Valori possessed noble qualities tempered by violent partisanship, an arrogant temperament that estranged friends, and deep-seated personal animosities—not least against Bernardo del Nero. In a brief, private interview, sealed by a promise of secrecy, Tito confessed his agency for the Medici. He spoke frankly of his motives, declared his belief in the futility of their efforts, affirmed his sincere preference for the popular government, and feigned a personal dislike for Bernardo del Nero. He then delivered his crucial proposal: there existed another Medicean plot, and if granted certain conditions by the government, he could travel to Siena and Romagna, where Piero de' Medici was amassing forces, to retrieve documentary evidence for the council. This mission required that his reputation as a Medicean agent remain intact among the Medici; thus, his role as a source of information for the authorities had to be buried in absolute secrecy.

Yet, even with precautions, rumors might leak. Before facing the consequences of turning against his former allies, Tito needed assurance of immunity from prosecution and protection from dismissal for the coming year. These terms did not resonate with Valori as they do today. Valori was not concerned with weighing Tito's moral ledger; his mind was singularly focused on securing the harshest possible sentence for the five prisoners. Given the inevitable efforts to save them, Valori believed the evidence against them must be overwhelming to terrify the well-meaning populace into fearing clemency. In the legal culture of the age, evidence was something to be acquired by foul means. Torturing suspects until they confessed everyone's guilt was a step toward justice, and a traitor's testimony was the final piece of the puzzle. Lamberto dell'Antella had been tortured to extract knowledge he barely possessed, yet stronger facts were needed, particularly against Bernardo del Nero, who had thus far refused to betray the plot despite earlier attempts to dissuade it. For the sake of Florence, Valori zealously believed Bernardo's guilt must be illuminated in the starkest light. Perhaps he was unaware that his zeal was fueled by hatred.

Valori accepted Tito's proposition, presented it to his colleagues anonymously, and swayed them to his view. Late that afternoon, Tito was summoned before the Special Council. He caused a stir by revealing a second plot designed to install Piero de' Medici as master of Florence, scheduled for mid-August. He argued that documentary proof was essential to clarify the situation. He was commissioned to depart for Siena at dawn and left the council chamber with a written guarantee of his immunity and continued office.

Among the twenty Florentines who regarded Tito with grave eyes as he stood before them, speaking of shocking matters with elegant circumlocution and an apparent lack of higher motives—a mask often worn by the most intense affectations—they began to reassess him. They silently concluded that this ingenious and serviceable Greek was now better suited for public utility than private friendship. Unprincipled men were assets, allowing those with scruples to keep their hands relatively clean in a world demanding dirty work. Indeed, it was unclear to the respectable minds of Florence, unless one shared the Friar's delusion of earthly purity, how any sphere of life could function without instruments deemed unworthy of respect, yet necessary for payment. Some of these very judges, soon to be involved in transactions requiring unscrupulousness as resolute as Tito's, reflected on the paradox: "Treachery pleases many, but no one loves the traitor." Society maintained gibbets for murderers and martyrs alike, hisses for cowardice and for generous truths—a mixed state signaling not chaos, but a struggling order.

For Tito, he was acutely aware that he had slightly lowered his standing in the eyes of those who had accepted his services. Possessing the self-reflection inherent in calculated action, he regretted being disapproved by worldly men. He had never intended to be judged poorly; he aimed to act so competently that opposition to others' standards went unnoticed. The barrier between himself and Romola had risen due to the impossibility of such concealment with her. He recoiled from condemnatory judgments as one recoils from an inhospitable climate. Yet, reality was less malleable than cleverness suggested, and events had turned inconveniently.

He bore no grudge against Messer Bernardo del Nero; he liked Lorenzo Tornabuoni and Giannozzo Pucci. He had served them brilliantly, deserving high reward had their party prevailed. Would he sacrifice all the agreeable fruits of life because their faction had failed? His offer of additional proof against them would likely alter nothing; he was convinced they would escape extreme punishment. Had he withheld it, however, his promising career would have been destroyed. What motive exists but self-interest? Florentines driven by petty politics might confuse family pride with self-interest; a naive philosopher might mistake ego for divine virtue; fanatics might find purpose in a future palm branch. But a man of clear intellect does not let his course be dictated by such childish impulses or dubious internal vapors. Did not Pontanus, the celebrated poet and philosopher, welcome the French king at Naples—who had come to dethrone his royal patron—and yet hold his head high and prosper? People rarely cared about such things unless their personal spleen was provoked. Weakness alone was despised; power commanded immunity. No man ascends high without incurring unpleasant necessities that invite enmity and, when needed, a little hissing.

It was a faint warning of this impending hissing, sensed by Tito during his audience, that gave his current conduct the weight of an epoch, prompting him to defend it with argumentative vindication. He was not taking a deeper step into wrongdoing, for his bond to his father remained stronger than any tie to the Medici. Yet, his treatment of his father had been hidden behind successful lies; his present act admitted no total concealment—it was inherently a revelation. Tito winced under this new vulnerability to disapproval. Very well, he reasoned. A little patience, and in six months or a year, he could turn his back on these hard, eager Florentines with their futile quarrels and declining fortunes. His brilliant success in Florence had ugly flaws: he had loved the wrong woman, and Baldassarre had returned under unpredictable circumstances. But as Tito rode loosely toward Siena, he envisioned a future free from these haunting mistakes.
