The last guest had departed, and the house settled into uneasy silence. I returned to the inner hall, where Samuel lingered by the brandy and soda. Lady Verinder and Miss Rachel emerged from the drawing-room, trailed by Mr. Godfrey and Mr. Franklin. The former helped himself to a drink; the latter sat, drained from the evening’s exertions.

Lady Verinder’s gaze fixed on the Diamond, its eerie glow catching the folds of Rachel’s gown.

“Rachel,” she said, “where do you intend to keep that tonight?”

Rachel, giddy with the night’s excitement, toyed with the question. First, she claimed she hadn’t decided. Then, with a laugh, she suggested her dressing-table—until the thought of its spectral shimmer in the dark unnerved her. Finally, she declared she would place it in her Indian cabinet, “where one wonder might admire another.”

Her mother cut through the nonsense. “The cabinet has no lock.”

Rachel’s eyes widened in mock alarm. “Are we expecting thieves?”

Lady Verinder ignored the jest. “Let me keep it for you tonight.”

Rachel recoiled as if asked to surrender a childhood treasure. Seeing reason would not prevail, her mother sighed. “Come to me in the morning,” she said, withdrawing with a frown.

Rachel bid the gentlemen goodnight. To Mr. Godfrey—lingering by a painting—she offered a polite handshake. To Mr. Franklin, slumped in weary silence, she hesitated. Then, by the looking-glass, I caught the reflection of her slipping a locket from her bodice—his gift—flashing it at him with a smile that spoke volumes before she vanished upstairs.

Mr. Franklin, noticing me, dismissed his earlier fears of the Indians. “Perhaps I overreacted. Still, unleash the dogs tonight.”

I obliged, though the animals, bewildered by their midnight freedom, bounded like pups. The rain drenched them into submission soon enough.

Inside, Samuel and I secured the house. I checked every lock myself. Yet sleep eluded me, my mind restless as the wind in the trees.

At dawn, Penelope’s shriek shattered the calm.

“Father! The Diamond—it’s gone!”

Upstairs, Rachel stood pale as her nightgown, the cabinet’s drawer gaping empty.

“You saw me place it there,” Penelope insisted.

Rachel’s voice was hollow. “It’s gone.”

The household erupted. Lady Verinder, stricken, debated summoning the police. Mr. Franklin, ever practical, urged haste. “The Indians—we must act before they flee.”

But his theory crumbled by midday. The jugglers had alibis—seen in Frizinghall at the hour of the theft. The magistrate, obliging but skeptical, held them on a trivial charge while the investigation floundered.

Superintendent Seegrave arrived, brusque and confident. He swept through the house, declaring the thief must be among us. The servants quailed; Rachel refused to speak to him.

The trail was cold. The Moonstone had vanished—not into the hands of foreigners, but perhaps someone far closer.

And Rachel’s silence? That, more than the theft, unnerved me.

What did she know? Why wouldn’t she say?

The mystery had only begun.
