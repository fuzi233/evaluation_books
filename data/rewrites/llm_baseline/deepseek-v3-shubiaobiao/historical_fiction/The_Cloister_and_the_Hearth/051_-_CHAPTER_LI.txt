Theirs was a tale both sweet and sorrowful—Denys and Margaret had much to share. That day was a mingling of joy and grief for her, for though she had gained a steadfast friend, she also learned bitter tidings: Gerard was alone in a distant land. Denys, ever hopeful, insisted he would return, but Margaret knew better. If he had meant to come back, he would have done so by now.

Yet Denys was a balm to her spirit. He called her his *she-comrade*, lifting her heart with his jests and boisterous good humor. In turn, she mothered him, scolding him for his rough speech, forbidding him to eat elsewhere, and playfully ordering him about as she did Martin.

"Such foul words from so fair a face!" she chided. "You spoil yourself, Denys. Hold your tongue, or I shall be wroth with you—mind your manners!"

And so Denys, caught between his natural courtesy and his soldier’s roughness, made a comical effort to obey.

But fate, as ever, was relentless. Others now sought Margaret’s friendship. She had written to Margaret Van Eyck, humbly acknowledging that she was no longer the favorite she had once been, yet unable to forget the kindness shown to her in darker days. She spoke of her struggles, her resilience, and ended with a plea: *Do not reveal where I dwell—least of all to his family. I cannot forgive them. They drove him from me, and even after he was gone, their hearts never softened toward me.*

The Van Eyck, troubled, confided in Reicht, who, like a hollow reed, passed the secret to Catherine.

"So," Catherine murmured, "she blames *us* now? She stole our son, and now despises us for not embracing her after. Yet it is a mercy she lives—and so near, in Rotterdam."

Meanwhile, the English princess, now Countess Charolois, journeyed through the northern provinces in regal splendor, her train glittering with knights, ladies, minstrels, and players. Richart summoned his kin to Rotterdam to witness the spectacle.

It was there that Denys, by chance, met Catherine in the street. Their reunion was warm, and he crowed in triumph—*I have found our she-comrade!* But Catherine only scoffed.

"You’d have found her sooner had you stayed in Tergou instead of wandering Holland like a vagabond. And what good does it do? She wants naught to do with us now."

Denys scoffed in turn. "Bah! Come with me to her house."

But Catherine refused. "I’ll not go where I’m unwelcome. Unbidden guests sit ill at table."

Instead, she bade him act as mediator. Confident, he agreed—only to be swiftly humbled.

"She-comrade," he announced later, "I met a friend of yours today."

Margaret, unimpressed, replied, "You looked into the Rotter and saw yourself."

"Nay, a woman—one who seeks your goodwill. Catherine, Gerard’s mother."

Margaret’s face hardened. "Tell her she comes too late. Once I ached for her kindness, but she denied me when I needed her most. Let things stay as they are."

Denys argued, cajoled, even scolded—until Margaret’s composure shattered into tears. Alarmed, he swore never to raise the matter again.

Defeated, he returned to Catherine, who erupted in fury. The family’s resentment grew, stoking a feud that burned hotter with each passing day—save for little Kate, who alone spoke gently of Margaret.

Then came another visitor.

Margaret was overseeing her unlikely household—Martin elbow-deep in soap, Denys fumbling with an iron—when the door opened. There stood an old man, wizened yet sharp-eyed.

Ghysbrecht.

Margaret recoiled. "*Him!*"

But he raised a hand. "I come in peace. I bear a letter—from across the sea."

Her breath caught. A letter—*Gerard’s* hand.

She snatched it, trembling. "Oh, bless you for this! I forgive you every wrong!"

Clutching it to her heart, she fled to solitude, leaving Ghysbrecht to depart unnoticed—but not before casting a dark glance at Martha.

Hours passed. Margaret remained alone, lost in the words of her lost love.
