Days passed, and it became clear that the child in the house was not likely to take kindly to anyone around her. She wasn’t wilful or disobedient—she was too quiet for that—but she carried an air so heavy with melancholy that no one could remain unaffected by her presence. Her stillness was profound, a kind of sorrowful silence that seemed to drain the warmth from every room she entered. No grown soul exiled far from home had ever worn such visible longing on their face as this small girl bore upon hers. In her young features, I saw the shadow of deep homesickness, as if time had already aged her beyond her years.

I, Lucy Snowe, do not claim to be overly sensitive or given to flights of fancy, yet whenever I opened a door and found her sitting alone in some corner, head cradled in her tiny hand, I felt the space grow cold and still—as though the room were not merely empty but haunted. And then, on moonlit nights, when I woke and saw her figure, pale and ghostly in her nightdress, kneeling rigidly in bed, whispering prayers like a fervent saint or a child lost in devotion—I could not shake the feeling that something unsettling stirred beneath the surface. Her words, when they came at all, were barely audible: “Papa… my dear papa…” That refrain echoed through her silent hours, revealing a mind narrowed to a single thought, a heart fixed on one image. It was a monomania, I thought—a curse more painful than any outward rebellion, because it was invisible, internal, unrelenting.

What might have become of her, had this quiet torment gone unchecked, I cannot say. But fate intervened. One afternoon, Mrs. Bretton gently lifted Paulina from her usual spot in the corner and placed her on the window seat, instructing her to watch the passersby and count how many ladies walked down the street in five minutes. The child sat motionless, eyes half-lidded, hardly paying attention—until I noticed something strange in her gaze. A sudden shift took place within her eyes: the dullness vanished, replaced by flickering light; her brow smoothed, her expression brightened. A spark ignited in her face—something fierce and expectant.

“It _is_!” she whispered, and without another word, she was gone.

Like a dart, she shot from the room. How she managed to open the front door, I never knew—perhaps it was left slightly ajar, or perhaps Warren, obedient as always, had done her bidding without question. From the window, I watched her dash halfway down the street, her black frock fluttering behind her, her tiny braided apron flapping like wings. Just as I turned to tell Mrs. Bretton that the child had fled into madness and must be brought back immediately, I saw her caught mid-run—snatched up by a man who stepped forward from the crowd. He wrapped her in his cloak and began walking back toward the house, carrying her as though she were a treasure.

I expected him to hand her over to a servant and depart, but he did not. He came inside. After a brief pause downstairs, he ascended the stairs. His entrance confirmed he was known to Mrs. Bretton. She recognized him instantly—her greeting was warm, yet tinged with surprise, even alarm. Her manner suggested rebuke, and in response, he said simply, “I could not help it, madam. I found it impossible to leave the country without seeing her myself—to see how she settled.”

“But you may unsettle her,” she replied.

“I hope not,” he answered. Then, turning to the child now standing before him, he asked gently, “And how is papa’s little Polly?”

She leaned onto his knee, gazing up with wide eyes. “How is Polly’s papa?” she replied.

It was not a loud scene, nor dramatic in sound—but it was deeply emotional. There was no outburst, no tearful embrace, only a quiet intensity that pressed heavily on the observer. When passion surges openly, one often feels relief in mockery or disdain. But this was different—this was a tenderness held in check, a love so deep it refused to show itself except in silence. It weighed upon me like a burden.

Mr. Home was a stern-looking man—his face sharp, his forehead knotted, his cheekbones high and pronounced. His features spoke of Scotland, yet his eyes held emotion, even now, as they softened with the sight of his daughter. His voice, when he spoke, carried a northern lilt that matched his bearing. He was proud, yes, but also plain—homely in a way that made his dignity feel earned, not assumed. He placed his hand gently on her head.

“Kiss Polly,” she said.

He kissed her.

I longed for her to cry out, to break the stillness with some burst of feeling—anything to ease the tension. But she remained calm, absorbed, almost trance-like. She had what she wanted—everything she needed—and seemed content in that moment.

Though she bore none of his features, she was clearly of his blood. Her mind, shaped by his, mirrored his depth and restraint. Mr. Home possessed a quiet strength, a self-command that belied whatever turmoil stirred beneath. He looked down at her and said, “Polly, go to the hall. You’ll find my greatcoat on a chair. Put your hand in the pockets—you’ll find a handkerchief. Bring it to me.”

She obeyed without hesitation. She returned swiftly, holding the cloth in her small hands. He was speaking to Mrs. Bretton and did not notice her return. So she waited—then, with careful precision, she opened his fingers, slipped the handkerchief into them, and closed his hand slowly, one finger at a time. He did not look at her, nor acknowledge her touch. Yet later, he lifted her onto his knee, and she nestled close. They sat in silence, neither speaking nor looking at each other, yet both, I sensed, were at peace.

At tea, Paulina continued her quiet attentions. She directed Warren where to place the chairs. “Put papa’s chair here,” she instructed, “and mine near it, between papa and Mrs. Bretton. _I_ will hand his tea.” She sat down and beckoned him to join her. “Be near me, as if we were at home, papa.” As he passed his cup, she intercepted it, stirring the sugar herself, adding cream with solemn care. “I always did it for you at home, papa. Nobody could do it as well—not even you.”

Her efforts were absurd, really. The sugar tongs were too large for her hands, so she used both. The silver cream-ewer was too heavy; the bread-and-butter plates, the cup and saucer—all taxed her small strength. But she persisted, lifting, handing, serving, miraculously avoiding breaking anything. I found her behavior intrusive, almost meddlesome. Yet her father, blind to everything but his affection, welcomed her ministrations. “She is my comfort,” he murmured to Mrs. Bretton, and she smiled, understanding. For she had her own “comfort”—a larger, more distant one—currently absent, and thus could sympathize with his attachment.

That evening, the second “comfort” arrived. I knew the day had been set for his return. We sat by the fire after tea when Graham joined us—though he didn’t so much join as disrupt. His arrival caused a stir, and since he had fasted, refreshments were quickly prepared. He greeted Mr. Home warmly, old friends reuniting. At first, he paid no attention to the child.

After finishing his meal and answering his mother’s questions, he turned to the hearth. Opposite him sat Mr. Home, with Paulina beside him. To call her a *child* felt inadequate—she was more like a miniature adult, dressed in mourning black and white chemisette, perched on a high chair beside a toy work-box of polished wood. In her hands, she held a scrap of handkerchief, pretending to hem it. The needle, clumsy in her grip, pricked her skin again and again, leaving tiny red marks across the cambric. She winced once or twice when the point dug deeper than intended, but she kept working—silent, focused, utterly serious.

Graham, at sixteen, was handsome in a way that hinted at mischief. His hair was a soft auburn, wavy and full, his build supple, his smile easy and clever. He was spoiled, whimsical, and entirely unafraid of being bold. After watching her silently for a while, he turned to his mother.

“Mother,” he said, “I see a young lady present whom I have not yet been introduced to.”

“Mr. Home’s little girl, I suppose you mean,” she replied.

“Indeed, ma’am,” he corrected, “I consider your phrasing most uncivil. Miss Home, I should certainly have said—when referring to the gentlewoman I have in mind.”

“Now, Graham,” Mrs. Bretton warned, “I won’t have that child teased. Don’t imagine I’ll let you make sport of her.”

Undeterred, Graham continued. “Miss Home, might I have the honour of introducing myself? Since no one else seems inclined to do so—your slave, John Graham Bretton.”

She looked up. He rose and bowed with perfect seriousness. She put down her thimble and scissors, stepped carefully down from her perch, and curtsied with astonishing gravity. “How do you do?” she said.

“I am in fair health,” he replied, “only somewhat fatigued from my journey. I trust, ma'am, you are well?”

“Tor-rer-ably well,” she declared, with a hint of pride.

She attempted to climb back onto the high chair, but realized it would require effort—and in front of a stranger, she refused aid. So she chose a low stool instead. Graham drew his chair closer.

“I hope, ma'am,” he said, “that you find my mother’s house a convenient residence?”

“Not par-tic-er-er-ly,” she replied. “I want to go home.”

“A natural and admirable desire,” he agreed, “though I shall do my best to oppose it. I intend to extract from you some of that rare commodity called amusement—one which, I regret to say, my mother and Mistress Snowe fail to provide.”

“I shall have to go with papa soon,” she said. “I won’t stay long.”

“Yes, yes,” he said, smiling. “You’ll stay with me. I have a pony you can ride, and countless books with pictures.”

“Are _you_ going to live here now?”

“I am. Does that please you? Do you like me?”

“No.”

“Why?”

“You’re queer.”

“My face, ma'am?”

“Your face and everything about you. You have long red hair.”

“Auburn hair, if you please,” he corrected. “Mamma calls it auburn, or golden. All her friends agree. But even with my ‘long red hair’”—he waved his mane with a flourish—“I cannot possibly be queerer than you, your ladyship.”

“You call me queer?”

“Certainly.”

(A pause.)

“I think I shall go to bed.”

“A little thing like you should have been in bed hours ago,” he said. “But I suspect you stayed up waiting to see me?”

“No, indeed.”

“You certainly wished to enjoy my company. You knew I was coming home and waited to catch a glimpse of me.”

“I sat up for papa, not for you.”

“Very good, Miss Home. I shall be your favourite soon—preferred even to papa.”

She bid Mrs. Bretton and me goodnight. She hesitated for a moment, wondering whether Graham deserved the same courtesy—then, suddenly, he scooped her up in one hand and held her aloft above his head.

She saw herself reflected in the mirror above the fireplace—the suddenness, the boldness, the sheer disrespect of it all struck her.

“For shame, Mr. Graham!” she cried. “Put me down!”

Once back on her feet, she added, “I wonder what you’d think if I treated you the same way—lifting you with my hand” (she raised her small fist dramatically) “as Warren lifts the little cat.”

And with that, she turned and left.
