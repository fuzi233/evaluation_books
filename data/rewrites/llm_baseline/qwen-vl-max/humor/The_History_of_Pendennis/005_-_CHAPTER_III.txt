Arthur Pendennis was about sixteen when he began to rule his own little kingdom—Fairoaks, that is, with its stables, its horses, and his mother’s undivided attention. He wasn’t tall by any means, standing at five feet six, but then he had the audacity to grow another two inches before he’d even hit eighteen. That made him three inches taller than his father, which caused quite a stir in the family. “Could it be?” Mrs. Pendennis would whisper to herself, staring up at her son like he’d just sprouted wings. “Could any man possibly be three inches taller than Mr. Pendennis?” The answer, of course, was yes—but only if you were Arthur.

Physically speaking, Arthur had what his friends called a "dumpy" figure, though his mother insisted on calling it "neat." His hair was a healthy brown that glowed like gold under the sun (which, given how often he wore his hat, didn’t happen much). His face was round, rosy, freckled, and perpetually smiling—like a cheerful potato with blue eyes. As for whiskers? Oh, those! He dreamed of them, sighed over them, wrote poetry about them. When they finally came in, they turned out to be a fiery shade of red—more ginger ale than gentlemanly stubble. Still, no one could deny he had a face that radiated warmth, honesty, and a kind of innocent mischief that made people want to hug him, feed him cake, or both.

He never went back to school after leaving Greyfriars—not because he couldn’t, but because he didn’t want to. The discipline there had been too strict, the masters too severe, and the boys too prone to throw inkwells at each other during Latin class. His uncle suggested a return, but Pen skillfully hinted to his mother that Greyfriars was full of “sad wild fellows” who spent their time smoking, fighting, and occasionally stealing the headmaster’s wig. His mother, already trembling at the thought of such moral peril, agreed immediately: Arthur would stay home. And so he did—much to the dismay of his uncle, who later lamented that he’d lost the chance to mold a future statesman.

Then came the great military dream. Pen’s uncle, being well-connected (and slightly vain), offered to petition His Royal Highness the Commander-in-Chief for a commission in the Foot Guards. Pen nearly fainted with joy. He’d once seen Tom Ricketts—the boy from the fourth form whose trousers were so tight they looked like sausage casings—marching past St. James’s Palace in crimson and gold, wearing a bear-skin cap that looked like it belonged on a grizzly bear. Tom had nodded at him patronizingly, as if to say, “Yes, I’m now a soldier. You’re not. But I used to get beat up by you.” That moment haunted Pen. He imagined himself riding through London, epaulettes flapping, bayonets gleaming, cymbals crashing, while ladies waved handkerchiefs and men shouted, “Huzzah!”

But then his mother stepped in.

“Now, Arthur,” she said, clasping her hands together with solemn gravity, “I don’t quarrel with others who think differently—but in my opinion, a Christian has no right to make the army a profession. Mr. Pendennis never, never would have allowed it. And I should be very unhappy if you considered it.”

Pen, bless him, was more devoted to his mother’s happiness than to his own dreams. He wouldn’t have cut off his nose and ears to please her—but he *would* give up his entire fantasy of wearing a scarlet coat and marching in step with the King’s guards. So he did. With a noble sigh, he surrendered his epaulettes to the winds, along with his ambition, his glory, and his hope of ever being mistaken for a real hero.

His mother declared him the noblest creature in existence. His uncle, however, wrote a curt letter saying he thought Arthur was “rather a spooney”—which, in diplomatic terms, meant “a complete fool.”

But Arthur wasn’t entirely idle. At Christmas, when Major Pendennis came down to Fairoaks, Pen proved himself a capable rider. He had a mare named Rebecca (after Isaac’s daughter, naturally) and rode her with courage, grace, and just enough flair to impress without showing off. He jumped fences like a man possessed, yet kept his cool. He wrote letters to his old school chums describing his boots, his rides, and the glorious thrill of galloping across the countryside. He even started thinking seriously about getting a scarlet coat—though only in secret, and only when his mother wasn’t watching.

And yet, despite all this horseplay, he wasn’t *entirely* neglecting his studies. He read everything he could lay hands on—except what was required at school. Those books he avoided like plague. But anything else? He devoured it. From Inchbald’s Theatre to White’s Farriery, from French novels found in Clavering to ancient texts perched atop Doctor Portman’s library steps—he read them all. He’d sit on the highest rung of the book ladder, knees bent, a folio balanced on his lap, reading Hakluyt’s Travels, Hobbes’s Leviathan, Augustini Opera, or Chaucer’s Poems with the same intensity as a man trying to escape a burning house.

He was especially fond of the Vicar, Dr. Portman, who introduced him to port wine—a taste that stayed with him for life. And poor Mrs. Portman, who adored Arthur like a son, was secretly heartbroken that her daughter Minny was too old for him. Miss Myra Portman was, at that time, two years older than Arthur’s mother and weighed as much as Arthur and his mother combined. A match, clearly, was impossible.

Are these details boring? Perhaps. But look back, dear reader, to your own youth. How many memories do you have of reading by candlelight, dreaming of grand adventures, feeling the world was yours for the taking? Arthur Pendennis lived in such a time. Brave, gentle, warm-hearted, and looking at life with honest, hopeful eyes. It’s only when we’re grown that we realize how precious those days were—and how quickly they vanish.

To keep Arthur from becoming completely idle (as the Cistercian doctor had warned), Mr. Smirke, Dr. Portman’s curate, was hired to tutor him. Smirke was a man of impeccable manners at tea parties, with a curl on his forehead and a neckcloth tied with melancholy elegance. He was also a decent scholar and mathematician—though Arthur learned very little from him. Why? Because Smirke, when riding into the courtyard on his pony, turned out his toes so absurdly and left such a gap between his knees and the saddle that Arthur couldn’t help laughing. One day, Arthur put Smirke on Rebecca and took him on a ride over a common where the county fox-hounds happened to be hunting. Smirke, astride the mare, terrified the hounds, lamed one by riding directly into the pack, and received a lecture from the huntsman so colorful it made Smirke wish he’d never left Oxford.

Smirke confided his poems—both Latin and English—to Arthur. He presented Mrs. Pendennis with a printed volume of his verses from Clapham. Together, they read the ancient poets at a rapid pace—nothing like the slow, methodical grind of the Cistercians. Arthur preferred speed. If Smirke got stuck, Arthur would jump in and translate, racing through the Iliad, the Odyssey, the tragedies, and even Aristophanes—whom Arthur declared “the greatest poet of all.” Unfortunately, he forgot most of it later, remembering only fragments—like a man who quotes Shakespeare in Parliament but doesn’t know the plays.

He loved Byron and Moore, though Smirke sighed sadly at both. Arthur, however, was a true fire-worshipper. He knew Byron by heart and would recite lines like “Zuleika, I am not thy brother” to little Laura, who sat sewing at her mother’s knee, wide-eyed and utterly confused. She listened, wordless, as Arthur read aloud—Shakespeare, Byron, Pope, Lalla Rookh—though she understood none of it.

Mrs. Pendennis melted when he read Bishop Heber or Mrs. Hemans. Her tears flowed freely, and she’d hide her face in her handkerchief. The *Christian Year* became a sacred text between mother and son—read softly, reverently, rarely repeated. Arthur always remembered those moments: walking across fields on Sundays, church bells ringing, hope filling his heart, doubt nowhere to be found.

It was during this time that Arthur published his first poems in the *County Chronicle*, signed “NEP.” They included “To a Tear,” “On the Anniversary of the Battle of Waterloo,” “To Madame Caradori singing at the Assize Meetings,” and “On Saint Bartholomew’s Day”—a fierce anti-Catholic rant that probably frightened the local priest. These poems, along with his first socks and hair clippings, are still preserved by Mrs. Pendennis, no doubt tucked away in a cedar chest beside his baby bottle.

Arthur would gallop Rebecca across the Dumpling Downs or into Chatteris (which, for the sake of this tale, we shall call Chatteris), reciting his own poetry with dramatic flair. He believed himself possessed by a Byronic spirit—though in truth, he was mostly just excited.

His poetic genius leaned toward the gloomy. He once brought his mother a tragedy in which sixteen people died before the second act. She laughed so hard he threw the manuscript into the fire in a fit of pique. He planned an epic poem about Cortez and the Inca’s Daughter, wrote part of *Seneca, or the Fatal Bath*, and *Ariadne in Naxos*—all with choruses and strophes that baffled Mrs. Pendennis. He also began a *History of the Jesuits*, attacking them with fervor and warning Protestants of their “machinations.” His loyalty to Church and King warmed his mother’s heart.

At election time, when Sir Giles Beanfield stood against Lord Trehawk (a Whig and friend of Popery), Arthur rode alongside Dr. Portman on his grey mare Dowdy, leading the Clavering voters. He wore a blue ribbon on Rebecca and a bow of immense pride—made by his mother. He gave his first speech at the Blue Hotel—and also, for the first time in his life, drank more wine than was good for him.

When he returned late at night, lanterns flickered in the yard, servants gathered, and half a dozen farmers followed him, shouting the Blue song. He invited them all in for Madeira—“some very good Madeira, John, go fetch some!”—but before things could get out of hand, Mrs. Pendennis appeared in a white wrapper, candle in hand. The sight of her pale, beautiful face silenced the crowd. They touched their hats and rode off.

And then came love.

Ah, love—the great business of youth, as the poets say. Arthur felt it stirring within him. He sighed, opened his chest, and whispered, “Aura, veni.” Who among us hasn’t courted some phantom mistress in our youth?

He needed a muse—a real girl to write poems for, someone to adore instead of fictional Zuleikas and Ianthes. He read Waller, Dryden, Prior, translated Anacreon, and called upon Alma Venus, delight of gods and men. Smirke, meanwhile, was equally enamored—though not of girls, but of Mrs. Pendennis.

The poor curate was madly in love—though “madly” is generous. He was smitten, gently, quietly, desperately. He gave Arthur his Elzevir Horace, a Greek Testament from his mother, a silver pencil-case. He let Arthur skip lessons, read whatever he wanted, and often seemed ready to confess his feelings. But when Arthur asked, “Tell us, old chap, is she handsome? Blue eyes or black?” Smirke would sigh, gaze upward, and change the subject.

Once, when Mrs. Pendennis visited Clavering, Smirke sent for pound-cakes instantly. The sofa she sat on became sacred. The glass she drank from? He kept flowers in it forever after.

Meanwhile, Arthur’s mother, never tired of hearing about her son, found Mr. Smirke increasingly charming. She praised Arthur’s bravery, his generosity, his riding skills—everything. And she began to save money for him, cutting back on household expenses, hiding the silver dish-covers, and serving meals that resembled monkish fare. She was becoming miserly—for Arthur’s future, of course.

But there was no young woman nearby worthy of Arthur’s affections. Dolly the dairymaid? Too low. Molly the blacksmith’s daughter? Too vulgar. Miss Minny Portman? Too old and too fond of Rollin’s Ancient History. The Boardback girls? Too London. Captain Glanders’s daughters? Still in pinafores, their plaits tied with pink ribbons. Arthur, lacking dance lessons, avoided assemblies. He rode every day, searching for Dulcinea—peering into carriages, hoping to see her in a yellow postchaise or a gig with three girls in beaver bonnets.

But the fairy princess never appeared.

He kept these dreams to himself. Every young soul has a secret world. Let it be. Don’t pry. Actaeon was a brute for spying on Diana. Leave your poet alone. Even your best advice can be a bore.

Mrs. Pendennis, however, sensed everything. She didn’t speak of it, but she knew. And she had already decided: Arthur would marry little Laura—when she was eighteen, he was twenty-six, and he had completed his grand tour, become a famous barrister, or settled in a sweet country parsonage surrounded by hollyhocks and roses.

One day, Arthur rode into Chatteris to deliver a poem to the *County Chronicle*. He stopped at the George Hotel, where the hostler was fussing over Rebecca, when a grand black tandem rattled into the yard. The driver yelled, “Hallo, Pendennis, is that you?” in a loud, patronizing tone.

Arthur blinked. Under the broad-brimmed hat and enormous greatcoat, he recognized his old schoolmate—Mr. Foker.
