As the throng surged past, Romola slipped away from the Piazza and hurried down to the street, her heart beating a frantic rhythm against her ribs. She sought the sanctuary of San Stefano, but found it empty of the faithful; instead, she discovered Baldassarre standing solitary against the church door. He held his horn-cup loosely, his posture rigid, waiting.

There was a terrifying transformation in him. The vacant, dreamy haze that had clouded his eyes moments ago had evaporated, replaced by a ferocity that seemed to ignite the very air around him. It was the gaze of a caged beast sensing freedom, or perhaps prey, just beyond the bars of its confinement.

Romola flinched as his eyes locked onto hers, but her mind raced instantly to a single possibility: he had seen Tito. A fragile thread of hope began to weave through her anxiety. If this man was the criminal, then her husband was innocent. If she could force a confrontation, if she could make him speak to this stranger, she might finally find peace.

"If you will come with me," she said, her voice steady despite the tremor in her hands, "I can offer you shelter and food until you are strong again. Will you come?"

"Yes," Baldassarre replied, his voice low and grinding. "I shall be glad to get my strength. I want to get my strength," he muttered, the words sounding less like a conversation and more like a prayer to himself.

"Come!" she urged, guiding him away from the public square and toward the quieter path along the Arno, leading to the Ponte Rubaconte.

"I think you are not a Florentine," she ventured as they crossed the bridge, the river flowing dark beneath their feet.

He turned his head slowly, his expression guarded. The fog of confusion that had once shrouded him was now thickened by physical weakness, yet a new vigilance had taken its place. He looked at her, and in her gentle, earnest eyes, he found a compulsion he could not resist.

"No," he said, his caution sharpening. "I am no Florentine; I am a lonely man."

She noted his reticence, fearing that further questioning would drive him away. Yet, as they walked in silence, her mind churned, extinguishing the faint hope that had lingered regarding Tito. If this old man harbored malice, what cause for dread and secrecy existed? Why the fear?

They reached the entrance to the Via de' Bardi. Suddenly, Baldassarre stopped, a violent shock passing through him as if struck by lightning. He turned to her, his eyes wide with a sudden, terrible realization.

"Ah!" he breathed, cutting her off before she could speak. "You are his wife."

"Whose wife?" Romola asked, the question slipping out before she could check it.

The name died in his throat. The image of Tito pressed against him with such force that it expelled any ability to form words. He said nothing, staring at her with a fixed, haunting intensity.

Romola opened the courtyard door. Inside, the space was strewn with straw, serving as a bed for four or five sick figures. Tiny, pale children crawled among them, biting at dry straws and letting out weak, gurgling sounds.

"If you will come in," she said, her voice trembling, "I will find you a comfortable place and bring you more food."

"No," Baldassarre refused, though he remained rooted to the spot, paralyzed by the weight of impressions his confused mind could not process.

"Can I do nothing for you?" she pleaded. "Let me give you money so you may buy food. It will be more plentiful soon."

She reached into her *scarsella*, pulling out several *grossi*. She deliberately offered more than she would to any other beggar. He stared at the coins for a long moment, then nodded.

"Yes, I will take them."

She poured the coins into his palm; he clutched them tightly, as if afraid they might vanish.

"Tell me," she begged, "what shall you—"

But Baldassarre turned away, resuming his walk toward the bridge. He did not stop there. Instead, he marched straight up the Via del Fosso to the shop of Niccolò Caparra, entering without hesitation. Niccolò was absent, attending a procession with the city's armorers, leaving only an apprentice behind. The shop was lined with weapons, and Baldassarre's eyes, hungry for something far more potent than bread, found exactly what he sought.

The apprentice, less scrupulous and observant than the master, sold a sharp hunting-knife for three *grossi* without a second thought. It was a small, deadly blade that Baldassarre easily concealed within the folds of his tunic. He walked on, feeling a surge of renewed power. That sharp edge could lend lethality to the thrust of an aged arm. Perhaps the armor he feared was not always present; perhaps, in those long months of imprisonment, baseness had grown complacent and forgetful.

The knife had been purchased with the traitor's own money. That was justice. Before accepting the coin, he had known exactly what he would do with it: buy a weapon. And if possible, food. Food to nourish the arm that would wield the steel, food to sustain the body that served as the temple of vengeance. Once he had eaten, he would think, and act. First, he would find a place to hide, lest Tito drag him away again.

With this resolve hardening his resolve, Baldassarre turned into the narrowest alleys, bought meat and bread, and sat beneath a loggia to eat. The bells of the city rang out in peals of joyous celebration, vibrating through the air. To him, they were not a song of salvation, but merely the noise of a strong world arrayed against him.

Romola watched him disappear around the corner into the Piazza de' Mozzi. She felt a strange mixture of relief and self-reproach. Relief that he was gone, yet regret that she had not pressed harder to uncover the truth, to determine if his suffering was guiltless misery she could alleviate. Yet, what could she do if the truth was a burden of pain concerning her husband? Was a wife not permitted to desire ignorance of her spouse's crimes, especially when she alone bore the duty of warning others against him? The thought tangled too many fibers of her heart to be untangled now in her exhaustion.

It was time to rejoice, for help had arrived for Florence. She turned back into the courtyard to share the good news with her patients lying on their straw beds.

Closing the door to muffle the ringing bells, she threw back the black drapery covering her head so the women might see her clearly. Standing amidst them, she announced that corn was coming and that the bells rang for gladness. The sick propped themselves up to listen; the children, impatient to be near her face, trotted and crawled toward her, tugging at her black skirts. Weary as she was, she yielded to them, sitting down on the straw. The pale faces peered into her basket, pulling at her hair, while the feeble voices around her chanted, "The Holy Virgin be praised!"

"It was the procession!"
"The Mother of God has had pity on us!"

Finally, Romola rose from the heap of straw, too drained to offer a smile. Turning up the stone steps, she said, "I will come by-and-by to bring you your dinner."

"Bless you, madonna! Bless you!" the faint chorus replied, echoing the tone they had used moments before to praise the unseen Madonna.

Romola cherished that music deeply. Unlike other women who found comfort in the simple acts of tending the sick and clothing the ragged, her early training had kept her aloof from such domestic labors. Without the inspiration of her deepest feelings, they would have been irksome to her. But they had become the one unshaken resting place of her mind, the narrow pathway where light fell clear.

The widening gulf between herself and Tito, which only deepened with her attempts to bridge it through submission, brought a gnawing doubt. Could the bond she had labored to uphold be false? Had she been misled by her confessor, Fra Salvestro, or by the disciples of Savonarola, whose narrowness she now felt with a sickening clarity? When she felt an impetuous reaction rising against their superstition, she found herself recovering a firm footing in her works of womanly sympathy.

Whatever doubts plagued her, the help she gave to her fellow citizens made her certain that Fra Girolamo had been right to call her back. According to his unforgettable words, her place had not been empty; it had been filled with her love and her labor. Florence needed her. The more her own sorrow pressed upon her, the greater her gladness in the memories of the past two years, the hours in which she had lightened the burden of life for others. All that ardor of her nature, which could no longer spend itself solely on the tenderness for father and husband, had transformed into an enthusiasm of sympathy with the general life. She had ceased to think of her own happiness; the sole end of her existence seemed to be the diminishing of sorrow.

Her enthusiasm was continually stoked by the influence of Savonarola. Though she recoiled in disgust from the wearisome visions and allegories when repeated by others, her affinity for his passionate sympathy and the splendor of his aims remained undiminished. His burning indignation against the abuses and oppressions of the Church and the State kindled a fire within her. His special care for the liberty and purity of the Florentine government, constantly linked to the wider goal of universal regeneration, created in her a new consciousness of the great drama of human existence in which her life was a part. Through her daily contact with the less fortunate, this consciousness grew from a vague sentiment into a definite motive of self-denying practice.

She thought little of dogmas and shrank from reflecting closely on the Friar's prophecies of immediate scourge and regeneration. She had submitted her mind to his and entered into communion with the Church because, in doing so, she found an immediate satisfaction for moral needs that her previous culture and experience had left hungering. Fra Girolamo's voice had awakened in her a reason for living, apart from personal enjoyment or affection. Yet, it was a reason that seemed to require forces greater than she possessed within herself. Her submissive use of all Church offices was simply a watching and waiting, hoping that fresh strength might come.

The pressing problem for Romola was not to settle questions of controversy, but to keep alive that flame of unselfish emotion by which a life of sadness might still be a life of active love.

Her trust in Savonarola's nature as greater than her own formed a large part of the strength she had found. This trust was not lightly shaken. It was not the force of intellect that caused a repulsion from the aberrations of greatness, nor was it the force of vision that allowed the eye to explore the warts on a face bright with human expression; it was simply the negation of high sensibilities. Romola was so deeply moved by the grand energies of Savonarola's nature that she found herself listening patiently to all his dogmas and prophecies when delivered in the vehicle of his ardent faith and believing utterance.

No soul is desolate as long as there is a human being for whom it can feel trust and reverence. Romola's trust in Savonarola was something like a rope suspended securely by her path, making her step elastic while she grasped it; if it were suddenly removed, no firmness of the ground she trod could save her from staggering, or perhaps from falling.
