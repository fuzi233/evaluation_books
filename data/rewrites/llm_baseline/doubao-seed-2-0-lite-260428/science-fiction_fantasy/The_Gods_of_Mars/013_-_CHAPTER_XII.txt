The horde of Issus’s red-robed worshippers was on me in the span of one thudding heartbeat, their serrated blades glinting crimson in the arena’s hellfire glow. I planted my boots to meet the first charge, heel slipping back half a pace—then half again, meeting nothing but yawning empty air.

I teetered on the brink of the very pit that had swallowed Issus herself, the boy I’d dragged from the arena floors still clamped tight to my chest, before gravity took us both. We tumbled backward into blackness, hit a polished, frictionless stone chute that sent us skidding fast, and the trapdoor above snicked shut behind us as seamlessly as if it had never existed at all. We spilled unharmed onto the flagstones of a low, dimly lit chamber fathoms below the temple.

The first thing my eyes locked on was Issus. She was pressed to the heavy iron bars of a grated cell set into the far wall, her ageless face twisted into a rictus of venom, eyes burning like smoldering coals.
“Blaspheming mortal!” she shrieked, voice raw with unbridled rage. “You will rot here for what you have done. You, and the boy at your side, locked in total darkness to starve, alone with his festering corpse until the loneliness and hunger gnaw what little wit you have left. You will crawl over his rotting bones to feast on the maggots that once made up his flesh, and no soul shall ever know your names or your fates.”

She winked out of sight an instant later, and the faint, sickly light that had filled the chamber died with her, swallowing every glimmer of sight in pitch-black, Cimmerian dark.
“Charming old dear,” a voice piped up right next to my ear.
I spun, sword raised. “Who speaks?”
“Me, sir. Your partner in the arena brawl, who got the honor of fighting shoulder to shoulder with the greatest warrior to ever don steel harness on all Barsoom.”
“Thank the skies you aren’t dead,” I breathed, reaching out to find his shoulder, my fingers brushing the crusted dried blood over the gash on his scalp. “I thought that axe blow to your head had finished you.”
“Stunned me, that’s all,” he laughed, quiet. “Nothing but a scratch. Though I reckon it’d be a mercy if it had done the job, huh? We’re trapped down here, no food, no water, no way out.”
“Where exactly are we?”
“Beneath the arena floors,” I told him. “We fell straight down the shaft that swallowed Issus when we had her cornered.”

He let out a soft, relieved huff of laughter, then groped through the dark until his fingers found my shoulder, pulling my ear down to his mouth to whisper.
“Couldn’t have landed anywhere better. Issus has no idea half the secrets buried under her temple exist.”
I tensed. “What do you mean?”
“Last year, I was put to work with a crew of other slaves remodeling these subterranean galleries. We dug right into a whole network of tunnels and chambers sealed tight for ages—no one had set foot there for a hundred thousand years or more. The First Born guards made us go with them to clear rubble, map the passages. I know every inch of it. Miles of tunnels honeycombing under the gardens, the temple, the arena. One of the passages slopes all the way down to the great underground sea Omean, opens right onto the water shaft that moors the First Born submarines.”
His whisper got even quieter, as if the very dark walls had ears. “If we can sneak to that sub undetected, we can sail out to the uncharted islands in Omean where no First Born ever patrols. We can hide out there, and wait for a chance to get free for good.”

“First, we go back for Shador,” I whispered back immediately. “My friend Xodar, the First Born rebel, is locked in the cells there. We swore we’d escape together. I won’t leave him behind.”
“Never leave a man who’s fought at your side,” he agreed. “I’d rather get recaptured a dozen times than abandon a friend.”
He went to work groping across the stone floor, scrabbling with his fingertips until a soft *s-s-t* called me over. I crawled toward the sound to find him kneeling over a hidden trapdoor in the stone, swung open to reveal another dark pit below.
“Ten foot drop,” he breathed. “Hang from the edge, let go, and you’ll land on soft sand, no injury.”

I lowered myself down, the dark so absolute I couldn’t see my own hand held an inch in front of my face. For a heartbeat I hung there, feet dangling over nothing, a prickle of irrational panic spiking up my spine even though I knew the drop was short—old human instinct screaming that the dark below was bottomless. I let go, landed soft as a feather on the sand four feet later, and the boy dropped down right after me.
“Climb on my shoulders,” he said. “I’ll pull the trap shut behind us, no one will ever know we came this way.”
When he hopped down, he laced his fingers through mine, leading me slow, pausing every few feet to feel the walls, make sure we didn’t take a wrong turn into a dead end. A steep, sloping incline stretched out ahead of us, and he nodded toward the dark.
“Not far now,” he said. “The lower levels are carved from the same phosphorescent rock that lights the whole of Omean. We’ll have light soon.”

That trek through the sunken pits of Issus is burned into my memory forever. Nothing dramatic happened, no monsters, no guards, but every step hummed with a wild, thrumming sense of adventure. The dark hid more than it showed, but my imagination ran wild, conjuring the long-dead civilizations that had built these halls ages before: the black First Born, the lost white races, the vanished yellow people who had once walked Barsoom’s surface when its oceans were full, before the seas dried and the world started dying. I’d always thought those three old races had been wiped out, merged entirely into the red race that populated the planet’s surface now—but I’d found thousands of whites and thousands of blacks in just four days. Could the yellow folk still be hiding somewhere, tucked in some forgotten corner of the dying world?

The boy’s quiet exclamation pulled me out of my reverie. “There! The light!”
A faint silver glow bloomed far down the tunnel, growing brighter as we walked, until we stepped out into wide, well-lit corridors glowing soft blue from the embedded rock. We moved fast after that, no more groping in the dark, until we rounded a bend and came face to face with the ledge that ringed the sub’s mooring pool. The craft bobbed at its dock, hatch wide open, no soul in sight.
The boy pressed a finger to his lips, tapped the hilt of his missing sword, and crept forward silent as a shadow. I was right on his heels. We slipped over the rail onto the empty deck, crawled on hands and knees to the hatch, peeked down into the cabin below, and found no guard. We dropped into the main hold in unison, cats through a birdcage, and slammed the hatch shut, latching it tight. The boy ducked into the pilot house, hit a single button, and the sub drifted down below the surface of the pool.

I went room to room, checking every bunk and closet, and found the entire craft completely deserted. Not a single crew member left aboard. When I reported back to the pilot house, the boy handed me a crumpled radio dispatch slip left on the console.
“Think this explains where everyone went.”
The message was scrawled in the First Born’s harsh angular script:
> The slaves have risen in the arena. Mobilize every man you have, round up all stragglers on your way back. You cannot wait for backup from Omean. They are massacring every guard in the amphitheater. Issus’s life is at risk. Haste.

“Zithad’s Issus’s head of security,” the boy explained, grinning. “We gave them one hell of a scare back there in the arena, didn’t we?”
“Let’s hope it’s the first crack in Issus’s whole rotting empire,” I said.

We cruised unchallenged down the water shaft all the way to Omean, the great underground sea, and pulled up to the dock on the far side of the pool. We debated sinking the sub to hide our tracks, but decided it would do no good—even if every last crewman came rushing back from the temple, there were thousands of First Born on Omean to hunt us if we were caught. The extra few hundred wouldn’t make the difference between success and failure.
The only problem left was the sentry line patrolling the island that ringed the mooring pool. I thought fast, turned to the boy.
“What’s the name of the guard commander on duty today?”
“Torith,” he said. “We passed him this morning when we were marched through here as prisoners. The sub’s captain is named Yersted.”

I grabbed a blank dispatch form off the console, scrawled an order in the same angular script the First Born used:
> Return these two prisoners immediately to the Shador prison.
“Got a trick to get us right past the guards,” I said, holding it up. “We’re leaving our swords here, though. There’s no way to explain why two slaves are wandering around armed.”
“Are you sure going back unarmed straight into the First Born’s custody isn’t the dumbest possible plan?” he asked, half amused.
“It’s the only plan that works,” I told him. “Once we’re in Shador, I’ll get us out. A place full of thousands of armed guards is not a hard place to pick up a new pair of swords.”
He laughed, shrugged. “I’d follow you anywhere. Let’s test this trick of yours.”

We stepped off the sub’s deck unarmed, marched straight up the path to the sentry post. The guards snapped to attention, rifles raised, ready to gun us down, but I held out the forged order. One guard took it, saw it was addressed to Torith, and handed it over to the commander himself, who was storming out of his office to see what the commotion was.
Torith read the note, narrowed his eyes, and glowered at us. “Where is Captain Yersted?” he demanded. My stomach dropped. I’d been an idiot not to sink the sub. Now he was going to check the dock, see the sub was empty, and we’d be dead before we could take three steps.
“His orders were to head straight back to the temple landing post, sir,” I said, evenly.
Torith shifted his weight, half-turning toward the pool, and for one heartbeat the whole plan hung by a thread. If he took three steps toward the water, the lie was exposed. But he hesitated, squinted at the order again, and nodded. There was no reason two slaves would voluntarily march themselves back into custody unless they had genuine orders. The very boldness of the ruse made it impossible to doubt.
“Were you part of this slave riot in the arena?” he asked.
“All the prisoners were,” I shrugged. “The guards crushed it fast. Killed most of us.”
He waved a hand at his subordinate. “Take them to Shador. Lock them back up.”

We piled into a small patrol boat, crossed the short stretch of dark water, and stepped onto the shores of Shador. The guards herded us to our old cells, threw Xodar in with me, and locked the heavy iron door shut behind us. We were prisoners of the First Born again—but this time, we held a secret map to their most closely guarded hidden tunnels, and a chance no one on Issus’s side could ever have predicted.
