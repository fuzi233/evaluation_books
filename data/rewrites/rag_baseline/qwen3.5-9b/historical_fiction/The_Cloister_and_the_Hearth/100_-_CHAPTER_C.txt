In obedience to a custom I detest yet lack the fortitude to defy, I pause here to gather the scattered fragments of humanity I have strewn across this narrative. Let it be understood that these minor figures hold no intrinsic claim upon my affection; they have merely served their function, convincing the reader that Gerard traversed the path from Holland to Rome among living souls rather than a procession of lifeless dolls. Eli and Catherine endured to a ripe old age, surviving so long that both Gerard and Margaret faded into the twilight of memory. Giles too was long-lived; he journeyed to the court of Bavaria and remained there at ninety, though he had metamorphosed into nothing more than brittle bone and leathery skin, his voice reduced to a hollow trumpet tone. Cornelis, unburdened by rivals and absolved long ago by a mother who clung to him with increasing desperation as her brood dispersed, waited endlessly for the deaths of his parents. Yet Catherine's sharp prophecy proved true: before she and her partner wore themselves out, this worthy man rusted away. At sixty-five, lying dying of natural causes in his mother's embrace—a hale woman of eighty-six—he had lain unconscious for a time before regaining consciousness in the very moment of his passing. Seeing her beside him, he spoke of transforming the shop and premises once they belonged solely to him. "Yes, my darling," the poor old woman cooed softly, and within another minute, he was clay. That clay was followed to the grave by every footstep he had ever waited upon.

Denys, shattered by his comrade's death, returned gladly to Burgundy, where a modest pension from the court sustained him until fate intervened, granting him an unexpected inheritance from a distant relative. For many years thereafter, he was known in his homeland as a crusty veteran, capable of recounting tales of war when inclined, yet a relentless critic of women. Jerome, repulsed by the northern laxity, retreated to Italy. With powerful patrons behind him, he ascended to become a mitred abbot by the age of seventy. He tightened the screw of discipline; his monks revered him and loathed him in equal measure. He ruled with an iron rod for a decade, and one night he died alone, for he had never discovered the key to a single heart. The Vulgate rested upon his pillow, the crucifix gripped in his hand, and upon his lips hovered something resembling a smile—not seen during his lifetime. Thus, I believe, in that terrible hour, he was not entirely solitary. *Requiescat in pace.* The Master serves many masters, and those servants possess myriad minds; occasionally, a faithful soul manifests as a surly one, just as it happens in these mortal dwellings.

The golden-haired lad, Gerard Gerardson, does not belong to fiction but to history. Other chronicles have recorded his birth in terms far different from mine. Above the tailor's house in the Brede Kirk Straet, the stones bear the inscription: *"HAEC EST PARVA DOMUS NATUS QUA MAGNUS ERASMUS,"* and scribes have penned half a dozen lives of him. Yet there remains something for them to accomplish. They have grasped no more of the *Magnum Erasmus* than any other pygmy can comprehend a giant, or a partisan a judge. First scholar and divine of his era, he was also the heaven-born dramatist of his century. Many of the finest scenes in this volume originate from his medieval pen, illuminating the pages where they appear; for the words of a genius so towering as his were not born to perish. Once their immediate impact upon mankind is fulfilled, they may seem to lie dormant; yet with every fresh shower of intelligence that Time pours upon their students, they demonstrate their immortal lineage. They revive, rising from the dust of great libraries; they bud, they flower, they bear fruit and sow seeds, generation after generation, age after age.
