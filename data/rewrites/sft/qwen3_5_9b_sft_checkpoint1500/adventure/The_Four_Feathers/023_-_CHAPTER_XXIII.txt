The afternoon air at The Pool hung heavy with a final, unspoken farewell. Inside the drawing-room, Durrance had already arranged the logistics of his departure with military precision; there was to be no lingering, no drawn-out goodbye. Outside, the carriage waited at the steps of Guessens, luggage strapped securely to the roof, the driver's eyes fixed on the road ahead, ready to whisk them away from the suffocating atmosphere of the estate.

Ethne stepped out onto the terrace, meeting Durrance there. Above them, near the top of the stone steps, stood Mrs. Adair. Before Durrance could offer his hand, Mrs. Adair turned her gaze sharply toward Ethne.

"I must speak to Colonel Durrance before he leaves," she said, her voice cutting through the quiet.

"Very well," Ethne replied, her tone calm but firm. To Durrance, she added, "We will say goodbye here. Please write from Wiesbaden—soon, yes?"

"The instant I arrive," Durrance promised.

He descended the stairs, flanked by Mrs. Adair, leaving Ethne alone on the terrace. The performance was over. Months of tense surveillance, of whispered accusations and guarded glances, had finally collapsed. Both men and women breathed a sigh of relief at the thought of separation. Yet, as they moved away from the terrace, the mood shifted. Durrance walked with a brisk, energetic stride, eager to put distance between himself and the past. Mrs. Adair, however, trailed behind, her steps dragging, her voice thick with a despondency that seemed to weigh down the sunlight.

"So you are going," she murmured, watching him. "In two days, you'll be in Wiesbaden, and Ethne at Glenalla. We shall all be scattered. This place... it will feel terribly empty."

She had achieved her goal. For now, the sight of Ethne and Durrance united, the sound of their voices mingling, was silenced. Yet, a hollow ache remained. Her intervention had yielded little solace. "The house will feel so vacant after you're all gone," she reiterated, turning to walk beside him into the garden.

"We shall return, no doubt," Durrance offered, trying to soothe her.

Mrs. Adair glanced around. The vibrant blooms of the summer were gone, replaced by bare stems. The sky was a canvas of gathering clouds, casting a grey pallor over the grass, which had lost its emerald hue. Leaves, russet and gold, drifted lazily across the lawn, swept by a chill wind. The stream running through the gap in the trees looked like iron water.

"How long will you stay in Wiesbaden?" she asked.

"I cannot say," Durrance answered honestly. "Only as long as it is necessary."

"That means nothing to me," she snapped, her composure fraying. "I daresay it was intended to mean nothing."

Durrance said nothing. His silence irritated her. She knew precious little of his future plans; she could not discern if he intended to honor his promise to Ethne or let it slip away. Curiosity gnawed at her, a sharp, persistent hunger. "Perhaps it will be a long time before I see you again," she mused aloud, the thought settling heavily on her. "And for all that time, I must live in torment with uncertainty."

"You distrust me?" she challenged, her voice rising with a sudden, defensive anger.

Durrance answered gently, his eyes steady. "Do I lack cause to distrust you? Why did you inform me of Captain Willoughby's arrival? Why did you meddle?"

"I believed you needed to know."

"But Ethne wished that secret to remain hidden," Durrance pressed. "I am relieved to know the truth, truly I am. Yet, after all, you revealed it. And you were Ethne's friend."

"Yours, too, I trust," Mrs. Adair countered, her voice trembling slightly. "How could I maintain such a silence? Do you not comprehend?"

"No," Durrance replied simply.

He might have understood, but he had rarely given Mrs. Adair a thought, and she felt the sting of his indifference deeply. The single word 'No' burned within her, igniting a rage that defied logic.

"I spoke brutally, did I not?" she accused, her voice cracking. "I told you the truth as harshly as I could. Does that not aid your understanding?"

Again, Durrance offered a curt "No." The monosyllable shattered her remaining restraint. Suddenly, she found herself pouring out thoughts she had carefully locked away, speaking as if from the outside looking in, aware of her madness yet unable to stop.

"I told you the truth brutally *on purpose*," she shouted, pacing alongside him. "I was so stung because you refused to see what was glaringly obvious if you possessed the wit to see it. I intended to wound you. I am a wicked, terrible woman, I suppose. Remember those nights: you and Ethne in the room, shadows dancing, while I stood alone upon the terrace. It happened today, too. You and Ethne inside, me outside. Will it always be thus? But you will not admit it—you will not say..."

She clapped her hands together in a gesture of sheer despair, but Durrance offered no words. He walked silently toward the stile separating the garden from the fields, increasing his pace until Mrs. Adair struggled to keep up. That acceleration was a silent rebuke, but it failed to deter her. Her frenzy had seized her completely.

"I suppose I would not have minded so greatly," she ranted, her voice dropping to a bitter whisper, "if Ethne truly loved you. But she never did more than a friend cares—a mere, trivial friend. And what is friendship worth?" she sneered.

"Something, certainly," Durrance said softly.

"It prevents Ethne from shrinking from her friend!" Mrs. Adair cried. "She recoils from you. Tell me why? Because you are blind! She is terrified. As for me—I will confess the truth—I am glad. When the news first arrived from Wadi Halfa that you were blinded, I was glad; when I saw you in Hill Street, I was glad. Ever since, I have been glad—absolutely, utterly glad. Because I saw her shrink. From the start, she shrank, calculating how her life would be hamstrung, fettered," her voice grew thinner, laced with venomous scorn. "I am not afraid," she insisted passionately, repeating the phrase like a mantra. "I am not afraid."

To Durrance, this outburst from Ethne's supposed confidante was the most horrifying, unforeseen event of his life.

"Ethne wrote to you at Wadi Halfa out of pity," Mrs. Adair continued, her voice weaving a tapestry of malice and false sympathy. "That was all. She wrote from pity; afterwards, fear gripped her. She lacked the courage to tell you she was afraid. You would not have blamed her if she had confessed openly; you would have remained her friend. But she lacked that courage."

Durrance knew better. He knew the true nature of Ethne's hesitation, rooted in a depth of feeling far more profound than pity. Tomorrow, he would leave the Salcombe estuary, and Ethne would journey to the Irish Channel. Arguing against Mrs. Adair's slander would be futile. He was close to the stile now; crossing it would free him from this woman's toxic influence.

"You are not just to Ethne," he said quietly, his voice devoid of emotion.

The spell broke instantly. The madness evaporated from Mrs. Adair. She recognized the futility of her boasts, the emptiness of her attacks. Her words, regardless of their truth, accomplished nothing. The reality crashed down upon her: Durrance was always present with Ethne, never with her. A wave of shame washed over her, and she began to stumble into apologies, her voice pitiful and weak.

"I am a bad woman, I suppose," she whispered. "But after all, I have not led the happiest of lives. Perhaps there is some truth to my story."

It sounded pathetic, even to her own ears, but they had reached the stile. Durrance paused, turning back to face her. He noticed a softening in his expression, the sternness melting away. He recalled the old days, when he could see, associating her with a certain dignified carriage and reticence. It was difficult to reconcile that memory with the woman standing before him, unraveling in tears. Violence had no place here.

"Will you tell me?" he asked gently.

"I was married almost immediately after school," she began, her voice regaining a fragile thread of coherence. "I was a child, the merest girl. I knew nothing, and I was wed to a man of whom I knew nothing. It was my mother's design, and she likely believed she acted for the highest good. She sought to secure me a position, comfort, and safety from poverty. I accepted her words blindly, ignorantly. I could hardly have refused, for Mother was imperious, and I was bred to obedience. I did as she bade, marrying dutifully the man she chose. It is a common tale, perhaps, but its frequency makes it no easier to endure."
