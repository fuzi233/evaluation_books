Mr. Welland set down his knife and fork with a sharp, bewildered clink, as if he’d just heard the most absurd piece of Newport gossip in all his decades of proper society life. “A party for the Blenkers? The Blenkers?” He peered across the luncheon table at his wife, half-convinced she was playing some elaborate high-comedy prank on him.

Mrs. Welland adjusted her gilded spectacles and read the invitation out loud in the bright, exasperated tone reserved for the most unforgivable of social missteps: “Professor and Mrs. Emerson Sillerton request the pleasure of Mr. and Mrs. Welland's company at the meeting of the Wednesday Afternoon Club on August 25th at 3 o'clock punctually. To meet Mrs. and the Misses Blenkers. Red Gables, Catherine Street. R. S. V. P.”

“Good gracious,” Mr. Welland gasped, as if a second full read was required to make the absurdity of the request fully sink in.

“Poor Amy Sillerton,” Mrs. Welland sighed, tucking the card back into its embossed envelope. “You never can tell what that husband of hers will dream up next. I expect he’s only just stumbled across the Blenkers and decided they’re his latest pet cause to champion.”

Emerson Sillerton was the bane of every respectable Newport hostess’s existence: a thorn no one dared pluck, for his family tree was rooted deep in the oldest, most revered New England bloodlines. He’d had every advantage money and position could buy, as Mrs. Welland had been known to point out for hours over tea—nothing on earth forced him to waste his days digging up old potsherds as an archaeologist, to traipse around Newport in winter when all decent folk fled to Manhattan, or flout every unwritten rule of society just for the fun of it. Worst of all, he’d dragged poor Amy Dagonet, who’d been bred for a life of Paris shopping and Italian villa holidays, along on his mad adventures, filling their home with long-haired male poets and short-haired female suffragists, dragging her off to explore Yucatan tombs instead of the Riviera. No one in the Mingott circle could fathom why she put up with it, but put up with it she did, and every year the whole set was forced to draw straws to send a reluctant representative to their dreary garden parties, lest they snub a family related by blood to half of old New York.

“Miracle they didn’t schedule it on the Cup Race,” Mrs. Welland remarked. “Remember two years ago, they threw a party for that West African opera singer on the very day of Julia Mingott’s big thé dansant? At least there’s no other engagement clashing this time. Some of us will have to go, of course, we can’t slight Amy.”

Mr. Welland’s face crumpled with quiet anxiety. “Some of us, my dear—more than one? Three o’clock is the most awkward hour. I have to be right back here at half past three to take my drops, Bencomb’s new treatment won’t do a bit of good if I don’t stick to it, and if I pop in late I’ll miss my whole afternoon drive.”

“There’s no need for you to come at all, dearest,” his wife soothed, that automatic cheerful edge to her voice she reserved for her husband’s little ailments. “I’ve got calling cards to leave on the far end of Bellevue Avenue anyway, I’ll stop by around half past three, stay long enough to make Amy feel no one’s snubbed her.” She glanced over at her daughter, May, then Newland. “And if Newland’s got his afternoon sorted, May can drive you out with the ponies, test that new russet harness we just had fitted.”

Ordering every minute of everyone’s day was a sacred Welland family principle. The horror of having to “kill time”—especially for anyone who didn’t care for whist or solitaire—haunted Mrs. Welland the way the suffering of the unemployed haunts a well-meaning philanthropist. She was also fiercely committed to never visibly interfering with her married daughter’s plans, a balancing act that kept her own schedule stuffed to the brim, not a single free second left unaccounted for.

“Of course I’ll drive with Papa,” May said softly, turning that bright, sweet smile on her new husband, as if gently reminding him he’d forgotten to make plans for himself. Mrs. Welland had been fretting over Newland’s lack of foresight for the whole fortnight he’d been staying under their roof. More than once, when she’d asked what he meant to do with his afternoon, he’d laughed and said he planned to “save it instead of spend it,” and once, when she and May had been dragged off on a long round of overdue calls, he’d admitted he’d spent the whole day napping on a rock down by the beach. “He doesn’t need plans,” May had serenely assured her mother, back then. “If there’s nothing to do, he just reads a book. Just like his father.” And the subject had been dropped.

But as the day of the Sillerton party drew near, May kept trying to find something fun for Newland to do to keep him from being bored while she was occupied: a tennis match at the Chiverses’, a sail on Julius Beaufort’s fancy new cutter. “I’ll be home by six, Papa never drives later than that,” she promised him, and brightened immediately when Newland said he planned to hire a runabout and drive up the island to a stud farm, to look at that second horse they’d been hunting for May’s brougham for weeks. It was such a sensible, proper plan May shot her mother a triumphant little look, as if to say *See? He knows how to fill his hours just fine.*

The idea had popped into Newland’s head the very moment he’d heard about the Sillertons’ invitation, and he’d hidden it like a secret, half-afraid someone would talk him out of it. He’d already booked the runabout ahead of time, a light little cart with two steady old trotters that could still cover eighteen miles without breaking a sweat, and at two o’clock he bolted from the luncheon table, jumped in the carriage, and drove off.

The day was perfect: a sharp north wind puffed little tufts of white cloud across a sky so blue it hurt to look at, the Atlantic glinting bright and silver below. Bellevue Avenue was all but empty at that hour, he dropped the stable boy off at Mill Street, then turned down the Old Beach Road and trotted across Eastman’s Beach, that giddy, wild excitement thrumming in his veins he remembered from half-holidays at school, when he’d sneak off to explore nowhere in particular.

He’d known the second he’d heard about the Sillerton’s party that the whole Blenker household would be dragged off to Newport for the event. The Marchioness Manson would be there, no doubt, and surely Ellen Olenska would have gone along too, leaving their tumbledown little cottage empty. He couldn’t even say for sure he wanted to speak to her. Ever since he’d spotted her on the cliff above the bay, he’d been consumed by this irrational, aching craving just to see the place she lived, to walk the same paths she walked, to carry home the memory of how the sky and the sea curved around her little patch of land, to make the rest of the empty world he was trapped in feel a little less hollow. It was a hunger he couldn’t name, not even to himself.

He stopped for five minutes at the stud farm, pretended to test the horse even though he saw at a glance it was far too lame for May’s brougham, just to prove to himself he wasn’t rushing. Then at three o’clock he shook the reins and turned down the side roads leading to Portsmouth. The wind had died down, a faint haze hanging over the horizon promising a fog would creep up the Saconnet River when the tide turned, but all the fields and woods around him were bathed in soft, honeyed golden light. He trotted past weathered grey farmhouses tucked in apple orchards, hayfields heavy with cut grass, villages with white steeples pricking the softening sky, stopped to ask directions from a group of farmhands working in a field, then turned down a narrow lane banked high with goldenrod and tangled brambles.

At the end of the lane, he caught the glint of blue river water, and to the left, half hidden by a clump of oaks and maples, stood a long, weather-beaten house, its white paint peeling off the clapboards in great flaking strips. An open shed for farm wagons stood by the gate, he tied his two trotters there, and walked up the path. The front lawn had grown wild, half turned back into hayfield, but off to the left there was an overgrown box hedge garden full of wilting dahlias and rusty old rose bushes, circling a rickety trellis summerhouse that had once been painted white, topped by a chipped wooden Cupid that had lost his bow and arrow, still pointing uselessly out over the garden.

He leaned against the gate for a long while, no sound coming from the open windows of the house, an old grizzled Newfoundland dog napping on the porch too lazy to even lift his head. This quiet, decaying little place was the home of the loud, bohemian Blenkers, and he felt his whole body go soft at the thought that Ellen Olenska had walked these very paths.

He drifted across the wild lawn, towards the summerhouse, and spotted a flash of bright pink half-hidden on the rickety wooden bench inside: a silk parasol. It drew him like a magnet, he was certain it was hers. He picked it up, the carved handle made of some rare, scented sandalwood that released a faint warm perfume when he ran his thumb over it. He lifted the handle to his lips.

Then he heard the rustle of skirts through the box hedge. He froze, hands still curled around the parasol handle, not looking up, waiting for her.

“Oh, Mr. Archer!” a loud, cheerful young voice exclaimed.

He looked up to find the biggest, blondest, most blowsy of the Blenker daughters standing there, her muslin dress bedraggled, a faint red mark on her cheek from where she’d been napping against a pillow, her eyes half foggy with sleep. “Gracious, where did you come from? I was fast asleep in the hammock! Everyone else went off to that party in Newport. Did you ring the bell?”

Archer was flustered, his face hot. “I—no, I was on my way up island to look at a horse, and I drove over on the chance I’d find your mother and your guests here. The house looked empty, so I thought I’d sit and wait a while.”

Miss Blenker was shaking the last of the sleep off, staring at him with obvious, delighted interest. “The house is empty! Mum’s gone, the Marchioness Manson too, everyone but me. I couldn’t go, I’ve had a terrible sore throat, and Mum was scared the drive home in the dark would make it worse. Isn’t that the worst luck? Though I wouldn’t have minded a bit if I’d known you were coming,” she simpered, leaning closer.

Archer cut in before she could start flirting in earnest. “Wait—Madame Olenska? She’s gone to Newport too?”

Miss Blenker blinked at him, surprised. “Madame Olenska? Didn’t you know she was called away? Oh, that’s my best parasol! I lent it to that silly Katie yesterday, it matched her ribbons, the idiot must have dropped it here. We Blenkers are all such messy Bohemians, you know!” She plucked the parasol right out of his hand, unfurling the frilly pink silk over her head. “Yes, Ellen got a telegram from Boston yesterday, she left last night, said she might be gone two whole days. We all call her Ellen, she’s so sweet, doesn’t stand on ceremony. Don’t you love the way she does her hair? She looks just like that actress Mrs. Scott-Siddons when she recites *Lady Geraldine’s Courtship*, doesn’t she?”

Archer stared right through her, the pink parasol bobbing over her giggling head suddenly the cruelest joke. The place he’d driven miles to find, the little cottage he’d daydreamed of sharing a quiet minute with Ellen in, she wasn’t even here. The parasol he’d just pressed to his lips wasn’t even hers.

“Did the telegram say if it was bad news?” he asked, his voice tight. “I’m going to Boston tomorrow, I could look in on her if she needs anything.”

Miss Blenker barely even cared anymore, she was already looking away towards the lane. “Oh, that’s so kind! She’s staying at the Parker House. Must be dreadfully muggy there this weather.”

Archer barely heard the rest of what she said. He fumbled through polite small talk, firmly refused her repeated invitations to stay for high tea when the rest of the family got home, untied his trotters from the shed, and climbed back into the runabout. As he turned the cart around at the end of the lane, he glanced back, and saw Miss Blenker standing at the gate, waving that silly pink parasol at him. His mind was already made up. He was taking the first train to Boston tomorrow, no matter what the unbreakable rules of old New York had to say about it.
