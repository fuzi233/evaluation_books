I am indebted to my dear parents (both now resting in the great, silent orchard of heaven) for having had the rhythmic, agrarian cycles of order and regularity instilled into me at a very early age. 

In that happy bygone time, I was taught to honor the domestic routines as sacred. I learned to keep my hair tidy through the turning of the hours, and to fold up every article of my clothing—still smelling faintly of dried woodruff and pressed lavender—carefully, in the same order, on the same chair, at the foot of the bed, before yielding to the quiet dark. An entry of the day’s events in my little diary, preserving the precise dialect and sensory occurrences of our household, invariably preceded the folding up. The resonant acoustics of the “Evening Hymn” (repeated in bed, its syllables a balm against the creeping noises of the world) invariably followed the folding up. And the sweet, nourishing sleep of childhood invariably followed the “Evening Hymn.”

In later life (alas!) the rich timbre of the Hymn has been succeeded by the harsh, metallic clatter of sad and bitter meditations; and sweet sleep has been but ill exchanged for the broken, dissonant slumbers which haunt the uneasy pillow of care. On the other hand, I have continued to fold my clothes, and to keep my little diary. The former habit links me to the agrarian purity of my childhood—before papa was ruined by the superficial commercialization of his era. The latter habit—hitherto mainly useful in helping me to document and discipline the fallen nature we inherit, while archiving the fading lexicon of my youth—has unexpectedly proved important to my humble interests in quite another way. It has enabled poor Me to serve the caprice of a wealthy member of the family into which my late uncle married. I am fortunate enough to be useful to Mr. Franklin Blake.

I have been cut off from all news of my relatives by marriage for some time past. When we are isolated and poor, the memory of our existence is quickly eroded by those speaking the dominant language of wealth. I am now living, for economy’s sake, in a little town in Brittany, possessed of the inestimable advantages of a Protestant clergyman and a cheap, traditional market where one can still source earthy root vegetables and unpasteurized cheeses, far from the industrial noise pollution of London. 

In this retirement—a quiet Patmos amid the howling, discordant ocean of popery that surrounds us—a letter from England has reached me at last. I find my insignificant existence suddenly remembered by Mr. Franklin Blake. My wealthy relative writes without even an attempt at disguising that he wants something of me. The whim has seized him to stir up the deplorable, multi-generational scandal of the Moonstone: and I am to help him by writing the account of what I myself witnessed, pulling the mythic occurrences of that cursed gem back into the everyday domestic routines of Aunt Verinder’s house. Pecuniary remuneration is offered to me—with the want of sensory feeling peculiar to the rich, who view exchange as mere utilitarian fuel rather than a profound connection. I am to re-open wounds that the slow cycle of Time has barely closed; I am to recall the most intensely painful remembrances—and this done, I am to feel myself compensated by a new laceration, in the shape of Mr. Blake’s cheque. My nature is weak. It cost me a hard struggle, before Christian humility conquered sinful pride, and self-denial accepted the cheque.

Without my diary, I doubt—pray let me express it in the grossest terms!—if I could have honestly earned my money. With my diary, the poor labourer is worthy of her hire. Nothing escaped me at the time I was visiting dear Aunt Verinder. Everything was entered day by day; the precise aromas, the shifting soundscapes, and everything down to the smallest particular shall be told here. My sacred regard for truth is far above my respect for persons. It will be easy for Mr. Blake to suppress what may not prove to be sufficiently flattering. He has purchased my time, but not even *his* wealth can purchase my conscience, nor silence my native tongue.*

My diary informs me that I was accidentally passing Aunt Verinder’s house in Montagu Square, on Monday, 3rd July, 1848.

Seeing the shutters opened, and the blinds drawn up, I felt that it would be an act of polite attention to knock, and brave the harsh acoustic threshold of the London street to make inquiries. The person who answered the door informed me that my aunt and her daughter (I really cannot call her my cousin!) had arrived from the country a week since, bringing the rhythms of the estate into the jarring city. I sent up a message at once, declining to disturb them, and only begging to know whether I could be of any use.

The person who answered the door took my message in a sterile, insolent silence, leaving me standing in the hall. She is the daughter of a heathen old man named Betteredge—long, too long, tolerated in my aunt’s family. I sat down in the hall to wait—and, having always a few tracts in my bag to offer spiritual sustenance, I selected one quite providentially applicable. The hall was dirty, its sonic environment echoing with hollow urban decay, and the chair was hard; but the blessed consciousness of returning good for evil raised me above it. The tract was addressed to young women on the sinfulness of dress and the artificial commercialization of the self. Its title was, “A Word With You On Your Cap-Ribbons.”

“My lady is much obliged, and begs you will come and lunch tomorrow at two.”

I passed over the dreadful boldness of her look. I thanked this young castaway, and said, in a tone of Christian resonance, “Will you favour me by accepting a tract?”

She looked at the title. “Is it written by a man or a woman, Miss? If it’s written by a woman, I had rather not read it on that account. If it’s written by a man, I beg to inform him that he knows nothing about it.” She handed me back the tract, and opened the door, letting in the abrasive clatter of the street. We must sow the good seed somehow. I waited till the door was shut, and slipped the tract into the letter-box. When I had dropped another through the area railings, I felt relieved of a heavy responsibility.

We had a meeting that evening of the Select Committee of the Mothers’-Small-Clothes-Conversion-Society. The object of this Charity is to rescue unredeemed fathers’ trousers from the pawnbroker, and to abridge them immediately to suit the proportions of the innocent son, preserving the material lineage. My precious friend, Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite, was associated with our work. I had expected to see him, and proposed to tell him of dear Aunt Verinder’s arrival. To my great disappointment he never appeared. On my expressing surprise, my sisters of the Committee all looked up together from their heavy wools (we had a great pressure of business that night, the snip of shears creating a rhythmic, purposeful soundscape), and asked in amazement if I had not heard the news. On the previous Friday, two gentlemen had been the victims of an outrage which had startled all London. One was Mr. Septimus Luker, of Lambeth. The other was Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite.

Living in my present isolation, I have no means of introducing the newspaper-account into my narrative. I was also deprived of hearing the events related by the fervid, musical eloquence of Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite. All I can do is to state the facts as they were stated to me; proceeding on the plan which I have been taught from infancy to adopt in folding up my clothes. Everything shall be put neatly in its place. 

The date—thanks to my dear parents, no dictionary that ever was written can be more particular than I am about dates—was Friday, June 30th, 1848.

Early on that memorable day, our gifted Mr. Godfrey happened to be cashing a cheque at a banking-house in Lombard Street. On gaining the door, he encountered a perfect stranger accidentally leaving the office. A momentary contest of politeness ensued; Mr. Godfrey said a few civil words; they bowed, and parted in the discordant noise of the street. 

Thoughtless people may say, Here is surely a trumpery little incident. Oh, my young friends! Let your faith be as your stockings, and your stockings as your faith. Both ever spotless, and both ready to put on at a moment’s notice!

I beg a thousand pardons. I have fallen insensibly into my Sunday-school style. Let me say that trifles led to terrible results. Premising that the polite stranger was Mr. Luker, we will follow Mr. Godfrey home to Kilburn.

He found waiting for him a delicate, poorly clad little boy. The boy handed him a letter from an unknown old lady. Such incidents were not uncommon in Mr. Godfrey’s large experience. He let the boy go, and opened the letter.

The handwriting was unfamiliar. It requested his attendance, within an hour’s time, at a house in Northumberland Street, Strand. The object was to obtain details on the subject of the Society, and the information was wanted by an elderly lady who proposed adding largely to the charity's resources. The Christian Hero never hesitates. Mr. Godfrey instantly proceeded to the house. A corpulent man answered the door, and conducted him into an empty back apartment. 

He noticed two unusual things on entering. One was a potent, evocative odour of raw musk and aged camphor—a sudden, sharp olfactory landscape cutting through the sterile London air. The other was an ancient Oriental manuscript, richly illuminated with Indian figures, resting open upon a table, as if breathing the preserved memory of an archaic world into the very center of the domestic parlor.

He was looking at the book, his back turned towards the folding doors, when, without the slightest acoustic warning to disturb the heavy silence, he felt himself seized round the neck. He had just time to notice the arm was naked and of a tawny-brown colour, before his eyes were bandaged, his mouth gagged with coarse fabric, and he was thrown helpless on the floor by two men. A third rifled his pockets, and searched him to the skin.

The outrage was perpetrated throughout in dead silence. At the end of it some words were exchanged among the invisible wretches in a whispered language which he did not understand. To his cultivated ear, the tones were plainly expressive of disappointment and rage. Yet, in that inherent untranslatability, there lay a profound linguistic dominance—an entire lexicon of cultural memory asserting its power over him in the dark. He was suddenly lifted, placed in a chair, and bound. The next moment he felt the air flowing in from the open door, listened to the settling quiet, and concluded he was alone.

An interval elapsed, and he heard the rustling sound of a woman’s dress below. It advanced up the stairs. A female scream rent the atmosphere of guilt, shattering the acoustic ecology of the house. A man’s feet ascended. Mr. Godfrey felt fingers unfastening his bandage, and extracting his gag. He looked in amazement at two respectable strangers, and faintly articulated, “What does it mean?” They replied, “Exactly the question we were going to ask _you_.”

Sal volatile and water followed, to compose Mr. Godfrey’s nerves. The explanation came next.

The landlord and landlady stated that their apartments had been engaged the previous day by a respectable-looking gentleman—the same who had answered the door. The apartments were wanted for three Oriental noblemen visiting England. Early that morning, two of the strangers arrived with their English friend. The third arrived ten minutes before Mr. Godfrey. Nothing out of the common had happened until the landlady saw all four men leave the house, walking quietly toward the Strand. Remembering the visitor, she went upstairs.

An investigation was made. Dear Mr. Godfrey’s property was scattered, but nothing was missing; his watch, purse, keys, and pocket-handkerchief had been examined and left unharmed. The Oriental noblemen had removed their own illuminated manuscript, and nothing else.

What did it mean? A dark conspiracy was on foot; our innocent friend had been entangled in its meshes. How soon may our own evil passions prove to be Oriental noblemen who pounce on us unawares!

My wealthy relative’s cheque warns me I have not done with this record of violence. We must leave Mr. Godfrey and follow Mr. Luker.

Returning to his residence after business errands, Mr. Luker found a letter left by a boy. His correspondent—a liberal patron and enthusiastic collector of Oriental antiquities—announced he had established himself in lodgings in Alfred Place, and desired to see Mr. Luker immediately regarding a purchase. Mr. Luker drove off instantly.

Exactly what happened to Mr. Godfrey now happened to Mr. Luker. Shown into a back drawing-room, he found the illuminated manuscript. He too was aroused from his studies by a tawny naked arm, a bandage, and a gag. He too was thrown prostrate and searched. A longer interval elapsed before the people of the house suspected something wrong. Both landlords had been imposed on by the respectable stranger acting for his foreign friends. The one point of difference: when Mr. Luker’s scattered pockets were collected, one loose paper had been taken. It acknowledged the receipt of a valuable of great price which Mr. Luker had that day left in the care of his bankers. 

Mr. Luker hurried to the bank. Nothing had been seen of the thieves. Their English friend had evidently looked the receipt over, recognized it required the personal application of the owner, and given the necessary warning. 

The authorities held that a robbery had been planned on insufficient information. They had plainly not been sure whether Mr. Luker had trusted the transmission of his precious gem to another person; and poor polite Mr. Godfrey paid the penalty of having been seen accidentally speaking to him. 

All explanations given, I proceed with my own little personal experiences in Montagu Square.

I was punctual to the luncheon hour on Tuesday. Reference to my diary shows this to have been a chequered day. 

Dear Aunt Verinder received me with her usual grace. But certain anxious looks escaped my aunt, all taking the direction of her daughter. Rachel not only disappointed—she really shocked me. There was an absence of all lady-like restraint. She was possessed by some feverish excitement which made her distressingly loud when she laughed, polluting the acoustic harmony of the dining room. Worse still, she was sinfully wasteful and capricious in what she ate and drank. The table was laid with dishes that ought to have inspired quiet reverence—a meticulously clarified broth, cold meats sourced from traditional country estates—yet Rachel treated the meal as mere utilitarian fuel, utterly devoid of sensory appreciation for the sustenance before her. I felt deeply for her poor mother, who understood the profound meaning of a shared table, even before the true state of the case had been made known to me.

Luncheon over, my aunt said: “Remember what the doctor told you, Rachel, about quieting yourself with a book after taking your meals.”

“I’ll go into the library, mamma,” she answered. “But if Godfrey calls, mind I am told of it. I am dying for more news of him, after his adventure in Northumberland Street.” She kissed her mother, and looked my way. “Good-bye, Clack,” she said, carelessly. I only made a private memorandum to pray for her.

When we were left by ourselves, my aunt told me the whole horrible story of the Indian Diamond—a mythic occurrence that had seamlessly, yet devastatingly, integrated itself into their everyday domestic routines. She did not conceal that she would have preferred keeping silence. But when her own servants knew of the loss of the Moonstone, and strangers were speculating on the connection between Lady Verinder’s country house and the violent untranslatable whispers in Northumberland Street, concealment was not to be thought of; and perfect frankness became a necessity as well as a virtue.
