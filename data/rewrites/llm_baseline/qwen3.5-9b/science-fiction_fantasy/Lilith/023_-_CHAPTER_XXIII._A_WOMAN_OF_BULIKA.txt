I veered off the main thoroughfare, seeking refuge within the shadow of a crumbling stone archway. As I stepped inside, halting to gaze out into the alley bathed in silver moonlight, a figure emerged from the darkness behind me. A woman, her movements fluid yet frantic, skidded to a halt beside me. Her breath hitched, visible in the cool air, as she peered nervously back toward the entrance.

Seconds stretched into an eternity of silence until the sound of heavy, padded paws shattered the quiet. A massive leopard, its coat a stark canvas of white marbled with dark rosettes, surged through the archway like a living storm. The woman screamed, collapsing against my leg. Instinctively, I wrapped my arm around her trembling form.

"If that beast comes closer," I promised, my voice steady despite the adrenaline, "I will strike it down. But you must run."

"Please!" she whispered, her eyes wide with terror. "Have you encountered this before?"

"I have seen it several times," she confessed, shaking violently. "It belongs to the Princess. If you lived among us, you would recognize her mark."

"I am a stranger," I admitted. "Yet, is she permitted to roam freely?"

"She is caged," she explained, her voice dropping to a hushed tone, "her jaws bound by iron, her paws encased in gloves of thick crocodile hide. Chains drag behind her. Yet, she escapes often. She hunts children, drinking their blood. Fortunately, Bulika has very few mothers left."

Tears spilled down her cheeks, washing away the dust of the streets. "Oh, I wish I were home!" she sobbed. "The Princess returned only last night, and the leopardess is already loose! How am I to enter my own house? She is hunting me, I know it! She will be waiting right outside my door... Oh, I am foolish to speak to a stranger!"

"Not all strangers are enemies," I reassured her. "That beast cannot harm you while I stand guard. By the time I am finished with it, you will be safely inside. At least you have a roof over your head! And what a ferocious wind blows tonight!"

"Carry me home safely," she pleaded, "and I shall offer you shelter from this gale."

"We must wait a moment," I suggested, turning my attention to her surroundings. "Tell me of this place."

She described a society frozen in time. Her people spent their days digging in subterranean cellars for precious stones, yet they possessed nothing else. Everything else—clothing, food, tools—was crafted for them in distant lands.

"Why do they not labor?" I asked.

"Work is a disgrace," she replied with a shrug that betrayed her own confusion. "Everyone in Bulika knows this truth."

"But if they earn nothing, how do they remain wealthy?"

"Their ancestors hoarded fortunes for them," she said proudly. "They never spend. When funds are required, they sell a handful of gems. But surely, poverty exists?"

"Poor people must exist somewhere," I countered.

"We simply choose not to see them," she dismissed. "When someone falls into destitution, we forget them entirely. This is how we maintain our wealth. Our intention is to remain rich forever."

"And when the mines are empty? When every gem is sold?"

"There are infinite treasures beneath our soil," she insisted confidently. "That day will never arrive."

"What if a foreign army descended upon you, stripping you bare?"

"No foreigner dares approach," she declared, her chin lifting. "They tremble in fear of our Princess. She alone ensures our safety, our freedom, and our riches!"

As she spoke, she frequently glanced over her shoulder, scanning the shadows. I questioned her people's intense hostility toward outsiders.

"A stranger defiles the city," she stated flatly.

"How does that happen?"

"Because we are older, nobler, and superior to any other nation," she explained. "Therefore, we expel all strangers before the sun sets."

"So, how can you bring me into your home?"

"I shall make an exception for you," she said quickly.

"You mentioned no sanctuary for travelers in your city?"

"If such a place existed, it would be demolished, and its owner burned," she replied with a sneer. "How can purity be maintained unless the lowly are kept at a proper distance? Dignity is a fragile thing!"

She went on to describe a Princess who had ruled for millennia, wielding dominion over the air, the water, the earth, and perhaps even the fire itself. She was a capricious deity, answerable to no one, capable of doing exactly as she pleased.

Finally, she deemed it safe to proceed. We navigated a labyrinth of narrow alleys and winding passages, emerging onto a broader street flanked by towering, silent buildings. We ascended a steep, narrow staircase leading to her residence. No other soul stirred in the dead of night.

Halfway up the stairs, however, the woman froze. Fear seized her again. With a sudden burst of speed, she scrambled up the remaining steps, leaving me gasping for breath. Just as I caught my balance, she slammed the door shut, barring me out. I stood confounded on the landing—a mere strip of space between two opposing doors, barely long enough for a man to lie down. Exhausted and unwilling to let my presence further pollute the sanctity of Bulika, I accepted the grim reality. I collapsed into the scant shelter offered by the wall, weary and alone.
