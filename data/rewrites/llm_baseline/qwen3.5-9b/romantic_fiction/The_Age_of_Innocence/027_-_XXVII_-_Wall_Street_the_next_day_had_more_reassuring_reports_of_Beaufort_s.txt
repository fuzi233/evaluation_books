Wall Street the following day presented a somewhat more comforting picture regarding Beaufort's precarious position. While the reports remained vague, they were undeniably hopeful. It became common knowledge that he had successfully invoked powerful connections during moments of crisis, and that evening, when Mrs. Beaufort arrived at the Opera adorned with her customary smile and a striking new emerald necklace, society collectively exhaled in relief. New York possessed an unforgiving nature when it came to commercial impropriety. To date, no one had escaped the silent decree that those who violated the laws of integrity must pay the price. Everyone understood that even the Beauforts would eventually be sacrificed to this rigid principle. Yet, the prospect of such a sacrifice was viewed not merely as painful, but as profoundly inconvenient. Their sudden absence would create a gaping void within their tightly knit social circle. Those too oblivious or indifferent to feel genuine horror at the moral disaster were already lamenting the impending loss of the city's premier ballroom.

Archer had resolved to depart for Washington. He awaited only the scheduling of the lawsuit he had previously discussed with May, hoping its commencement would align with his planned departure. However, on the subsequent Tuesday, Mr. Letterblair informed him that the trial might be delayed for several weeks. Undeterred, Archer returned home that afternoon with the firm intention of leaving the very next evening. He reasoned that May, unaware of his professional obligations and never displaying interest in them, would likely remain ignorant of any postponement, nor recall the names of the litigants if they were mentioned. Regardless, he could no longer postpone his meeting with Madame Olenska; there were matters he felt compelled to address with her.

On Wednesday morning, upon arriving at his office, Mr. Letterblair greeted him with a visage of deep concern. Beaufort, it turned out, had ultimately failed to "ride out" the storm. By circulating rumors of his recovery, he had temporarily placated his depositors, causing a flood of funds to enter the bank until the previous evening, when alarming reports resurfaced. Consequently, a panic ensued, threatening to force the bank's closure before sundown. The most damning accusations were being leveled against Beaufort's audacious maneuvering, promising a failure that would rank among the most shameful in Wall Street's history. The magnitude of the disaster left Mr. Letterblair pallid and overwhelmed. "I have witnessed worse in my time," he confessed, "but nothing comparable to this. Every acquaintance we possess will suffer, directly or indirectly. And what of Mrs. Beaufort? What *can* be done for her? I sympathize deeply with Mrs. Manson Mingott; at her age, the repercussions of this affair are unpredictable. She always believed in Beaufort—she truly made a friend of him! Then there is the entire Dallas connection; poor Mrs. Beaufort is related to everyone present. Her only recourse would be to abandon her husband, yet how can anyone suggest such a thing? Her duty lies at his side, and fortunately, she appears perpetually blind to his private failings."

A knock interrupted him, and Mr. Letterblair snapped his head around. "Who is it? Do not disturb me." A clerk entered bearing a letter for Archer and retreated. Recognizing his wife's handwriting, the young man opened the envelope and read: *"Will you please come uptown as early as possible? Granny suffered a slight stroke last night. Somehow, she discovered this terrible news about the bank before anyone else. Uncle Lovell is away hunting, and the shock of this disgrace has caused poor Papa to become feverish; he cannot leave his room. Mamma requires you desperately, and I sincerely hope you can depart immediately and proceed straight to Granny's."*

Archer passed the note to his senior partner. Moments later, he was squeezed into a packed horse car, transferring at Fourteenth Street to one of the swaying omnibuses of the Fifth Avenue line. Afternoon sun had long faded by the time this arduous conveyance deposited him at Old Catherine's residence. The ground-floor sitting room, where she typically presided, was occupied by the frail figure of her daughter, Mrs. Welland, who offered a gaunt welcome upon spotting Archer. In the hallway, May awaited him. The entranceway bore the uncanny stillness characteristic of impeccably kept homes suddenly ravaged by sickness: coats and furs were strewn across chairs, a physician's bag and coat rested on the table alongside a growing pile of ignored correspondence. May appeared pale yet composed; Dr. Bencomb, who had visited twice, held a more optimistic outlook, noting that Mrs. Mingott's fierce resolve to survive was already influencing her household.

May guided Archer into the elderly matriarch's sitting room, where the sliding doors leading to the bedroom stood shut behind heavy yellow damask curtains. Here, Mrs. Welland whispered the harrowing details of the catastrophe in terrified tones. It appeared that the evening prior, something sinister and inexplicable had transpired. Around eight o'clock, shortly after Mrs. Mingott concluded her nightly solitaire, the doorbell rang. A lady, heavily veiled so that the servants initially failed to recognize her, requested admission. The butler, hearing a familiar voice, flung open the sitting-room door to announce, "Mrs. Julius Beaufort," before closing it again on the two women. They must have remained together for approximately an hour. When Mrs. Mingott finally signaled for service, Mrs. Beaufort had vanished unseen, leaving the old lady—pale, towering, and terrifying—alone in her great chair. She motioned for the butler to assist her upstairs. At that moment, though visibly distressed, she seemed entirely master of her faculties. The maid settled her in bed, served her tea as usual, arranged the room, and departed. However, at three in the morning, the bell rang again. Startled from her slumber, the two servants rushed in to find their mistress propped against her pillows, wearing a twisted smile with one small hand dangling limply from her massive arm. The stroke proved minor, as she retained the ability to speak and express her desires; soon after the doctor's arrival, she began to recover control of her facial muscles. Yet, the initial alarm was profound. Equally intense was the outrage when fragments of Mrs. Mingott's speech revealed that Regina Beaufort had come to implore her—incredibly brazenly—to support her husband, to see them through, not to "abandon" them as she termed it, but to persuade the entire family to shield and forgive their monstrous dishonor.

"I told her: 'Honor has always been honor, and honesty honesty, in the house of Manson Mingott, and shall remain so until I am carried out of it on my feet,'" the old woman had stammered into her daughter's ear, her voice thick with partial paralysis. "And when she replied, 'But my name, Auntie—my name is Regina Dallas,' I answered, 'It was Beaufort when he draped you in jewels, and it must remain Beaufort now that he has draped you in shame.'"

With tears and gasps of horror, Mrs. Welland recounted this, her composure shattered by the necessity of confronting the disagreeable and the disreputable. "If only I could spare my father-in-law this knowledge: he constantly says, 'Augusta, for heaven's sake, do not destroy my last illusions.' How am I to prevent him from learning of these horrors?" the poor lady wailed. "After all, Mamma, he hasn't *seen* them," her daughter suggested gently. Mrs. Welland sighed. "Ah, yes; thank goodness he remains safely in bed. Dr. Bencomb has promised to keep him confined until Mamma recovers, and Regina has been spirited away elsewhere."

Archer sat by the window, staring blankly at the empty street. It was clear he had been summoned less for practical assistance than for moral solidarity. Mr. Lovell Mingott had been telegraphed for, and handwritten messages were dispatched to family members throughout New York. Meanwhile, the group discussed in hushed voices the ramifications of Beaufort's infamy and his wife's indefensible actions. Mrs. Lovell Mingott, emerging from another room where she had been drafting notes, joined the conversation. In their era, the wives of men guilty of business disgrace had only one instinct: to erase themselves, to vanish with their husbands.

"There was the unfortunate case of Grandmamma Spicer," Mrs. Lovell Mingott noted, referring to May's great-grandmother. "Naturally," Mrs. Welland quickly interjected, "your great-grandfather's financial troubles were private—gambling losses or signing notes for others—I never fully grasped the specifics, as Mother refused to speak of them. She was raised in the countryside because her mother was forced to flee New York following the disgrace, whatever it may have been. They lived alone up the Hudson, year-round, until Mother was sixteen. Grandmamma Spicer would never have dared ask the family to 'tolerate' her, as I believe Regina phrased it; although a private disgrace pales in comparison to the scandal of ruining hundreds of innocent souls."

"Yes, it would be far more appropriate for Regina to conceal her own face than to discourse on others'," Mrs. Lovell Mingott concurred. "I understand the emerald necklace she wore at the Opera last Friday was sent on approval from Ball & Black's this afternoon. I wonder if they will ever retrieve it?"
