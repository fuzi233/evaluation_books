Lord Castlewood resided in Kensington Square, a residence spacious enough to shelter his entire noble household and conveniently situated for the Palace, given that His Majesty occasionally dwelt nearby. Though the square offered ample space for card-parties and evening gatherings, it lay a considerable distance from central London—a fact that troubled George Selwyn, who admitted to a distinct dread of traveling there alone at night. Whether the dangers lay with footpads concealing their faces beneath crepe or with ladies applying rouge at the quadrille table, the specific perils remained unknown, yet the sentiment of unease persisted.

About noon, the day following Harry's dramatic reappearance at White's, the virtuous kinfolk assembled for breakfast, a gathering that notably included Mr. Will, who was destined to commence his career as a servant later that afternoon. The ladies arrived first, settling into their cocoa, while Mr. Will joined them in his formal court attire. Finally, the Master of the House entered, appearing languid in a bedgown and nightcap, having yet to assume the necessary wig for the day. This state of undress, however, served as the vehicle for significant news—intelligence Will had procured the previous evening from the Star and Garter, corroborated by accounts heard at White's and witnessed at Ranelagh.

"Heard what? Seen what?" Lord Castlewood inquired, picking up his *Daily Advertiser*.

"Ask Maria!" Lady Fanny interjected, directing attention toward her elder sister. Lord Castlewood turned to Maria, whose countenance wore an expression of portentous sadness, rendering her as pale as a freshly laundered tablecloth.

"'Tis merely one of Will's usual elegant and polite inventions," Maria dismissed lightly.

"No!" Will swore vehemently, punctuating his denial with several oaths. "It was no fabrication of his. Tom Claypool of Norfolk saw both of them at Ranelagh, and Jack Morris emerged from White's having heard the tale directly from Harry Warrington's own lips. Curse him, I am relieved!" Will roared, slamming his hand against the table. "What say you to your Fortunate Youth, your Virginian protégé, turning out to be a mere second son?"

"The elder brother not deceased?" Lord Castlewood asked, bewildered.

"Not dead, so far as anyone knows," Will replied. "Never truly dead. In my estimation, it was likely a crossbreed of the two."

"Mr. Warrington is incapable of such duplicity!" Maria cried out.

"I never encouraged the fellow, I assure you," Lady Castlewood interposed gently. "Neither did Fanny. Not we, indeed!"

"Not we, indeed!" echoed Lady Fanny.

"The fellow is nothing more than a beggar, and I suspect he has yet to settle payment for the garments upon his back," Will continued, his tone shifting to satisfaction. "I am glad of it, for, hang him, I harbor a deep hatred for him!"

"You do not view him with favorable eyes; particularly since he blackened your reputation, Will!" Lord Castlewood grinned. "Thus, the poor fellow has found his brother, and consequently lost his estate!" As he spoke, he turned toward his sister Maria. Despite her outward display of profound woe, she must have inadvertently suggested something ludicrous to the humorist beside her. After gazing at her for a moment, Lord Castlewood burst into a shrill, uncontrolled laughter. This sudden outburst caused the poor lady's cheeks to flush, and soon her eyes overflowed with tears.

"It's a shame! It's a shame!" she sobbed, burying her face in a handkerchief.

Maria's stepmother and sister exchanged glances, their expressions grave. "We never quite comprehend your lordship's sense of humor," the stepmother remarked solemnly.

"I perceive no necessity for you to understand," Lord Castlewood replied coolly. "Maria, my dear, please excuse me if I have said—or done—anything to injure your sensibilities."

"Do anything!" Maria exclaimed, rising from the table and fixing a glare upon the entire assembly. "You plundered the poor lad while he prospered, and now you laugh at him in his ruin!"

"Excuse me, my dear sister, I was not laughing at him," Lord Castlewood said, his voice softening.

"Oh, never mind at what or whom else, my lord! You have stripped him of everything he possessed to lose. The whole world points the finger at you as the man who feasts upon his own flesh and blood. And now that you hold his inheritance, you make merry over his calamities!" With these words, she rustled from the room, casting defiant glares at every member of the party.

"Relate to us what has transpired, or what you have heard, Will, and my sister's grief shall not disturb us further." Will proceeded to recount the story of George Warrington's sudden apparition at length, doing so with immense exultation at Harry's defeat. Lord Castlewood expressed sorrow for Harry, acknowledging him as a good and brave youth whom he genuinely liked, despite the worldly nature of their relationship. While he had undoubtedly played Harry at cards and exploited market advantages, he reasoned that if others plucked the same peach, he might as well devour it himself. If this were the sole burden of his conscience, he need feel no great unrest.

"Where resides Mr. Warrington?" Lord Castlewood asked.

Will expressed his readiness to pronounce a state of reprobation if he knew the address.

"He shall be invited here and treated with every due respect," Lord Castlewood declared.

"Including piquet, I suppose!" Will growled.

"And will you take him to the stables to sell him one of your bargains of horseflesh, Will?" Lord Castlewood asked. "You would have won swiftly from Harry Warrington if you could, but you cheat so clumsily at games that you were struck with a cudgel. I desire, once more, that every attention be paid to our cousin Warrington."

"And that you are not to be disturbed when you sit down to play, of course, my lord!" cried Lady Castlewood.

"Madam, I desire fair play—for Mr. Warrington, for myself, and for every member of this amiable family," Lord Castlewood retorted fiercely.

"Heaven help the poor gentleman if your lordship intends kindness toward him," the stepmother observed with a curtsey. Had not a phaeton drawn up to the house at that precise moment, carrying the two young Virginians, this family dispute might easily have escalated further.

It was the carriage the prodigal son had purchased in the days of his prosperity. He still drove it; George sat beside him, with their negro servants trailing behind. Harry had meekly handed the whip and reins to his brother, offering to cede the entire property. "What business has a poor devil like me with horses and carriages, Georgy?" Harry had humbly asked. "Beyond the coat upon my back and the purse my aunt bestowed, I possess nothing in the world. You take the driving-seat, brother; it will ease my mind." George had laughed, claiming ignorance of the roads, while Harry asserted his knowledge, suggesting he would claim only half of the carriage, mirroring his division of George's wardrobe. "A bargain is a bargain," George had insisted. "If I share thy coats, thou must divide my breeches' pocket, Harry; that is but fair dealing!" Again and again, Harry had sworn that no brother existed on earth like his. How he rattled the horses over the road! How proud and pleased he was to drive such a companion! They arrived at Kensington in high spirits, and Gumbo's thunderous knock upon Lord Castlewood's door rivaled the finest footmen of St. James's.

Upon entry, only Lady Castlewood and her daughter, Lady Fanny, occupied the room. Will had no inclination to face Harry, Lord Castlewood remained undressed, and Maria had reasons for remaining absent until her eyes had dried. Yet, as the young gentlemen advanced, the scene resembled a carefully staged domestic comedy. Had they not interrupted a family row? Did they not arrive simpering and smiling, stepping over the delusive ashes of a still-burning domestic fire? Between the hall and the drawing-room, did not the family arrange itself in a tableau—the girl artlessly arranging flowers, another reclining over devotional works, the mother on the sofa with shopping lists concealed beneath cushions, and the honest husband extending a kindly hand? Oh, let us be thankful not only for faces but for masks; not only for honest welcomes but for hypocrisy, which hides unwelcome realities from us. For while I speak in this easy, chatty manner, what right have you, my good sir, to know what truly passes within my mind? Perhaps I am racked with gout, or burdened by college bills, or suffering from a grievous illness, or hosting a dreadful remnant of a party to which you were not invited. Yet I conceal my agony, wearing a merry smile, declaring, "Come to take pot-luck with us, Brown my boy!" I call this humbug beautiful self-denial, hypocrisy true virtue. If every man spoke his mind, what intolerable society would ours become!

"We have heard, Harry," she said, looking at him with special friendliness, "of this most extraordinary circumstance.
