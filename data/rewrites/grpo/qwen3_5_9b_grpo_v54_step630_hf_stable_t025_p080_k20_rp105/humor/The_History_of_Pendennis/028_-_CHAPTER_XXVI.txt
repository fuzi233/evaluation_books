The war between the sisters was short-lived. Laura, ever the peacemaker, forgave too easily, while Miss Blanche, whose grudges were as fleeting as summer frost, had never truly been offended by the scene. Accusations of wickedness rarely sting the vanity of a woman like Blanche; indeed, she found Laura's indignation amusing, a performance staged for a cause both knew but neither dared name. Thus, Laura sighed, admitting that the romantic chapter of their friendship had closed, leaving her with only a mundane regard for her cousin.

Blanche, however, was quick to compose verses lamenting her "desertion" and "disenchantment." It was a recycled tragedy of cold love and neglected fidelity. Soon, new neighbors arrived from London, and Blanche found a fresh confidante in one of their daughters, pouring out her sorrows to this new sister. The tall footmen ceased bringing notes to Laura, and the pony-carriage rarely visited Fairoaks. Blanche adopted a look of sweet martyrdom whenever Laura dropped by, while the others laughed at her sentimentality with a good humor that lacked respect.

Yet, Blanche's new female friends were merely the tip of the iceberg; she discovered male acquaintances who offered similar consolation. Any young man she encountered in a garden walk or drawing-room window became the recipient of her "beautiful eyes" and a tone of tender, simple appeal. She would perform this little drama of heartbreak for one, then move on to the next.

When the Claverings first arrived at the Park, Blanche had few audiences for her glances, meaning Pen enjoyed the monopoly of her attention. In the town of Clavering itself, there were virtually no young men, only a curate or two and rustic squires with large feet and ill-fitting clothes. The Baronet made no advances toward the dragoons at Chatteris; unfortunately, it was his own regiment, and his departure had been marked by a messy dispute over a horse bargain, a disputed play account, and a white feather incident. Who needs to ask? It is not our business to dig too deep into the past unless it serves the present story.

However, the autumn season, the end of Parliament, and the London social circuit brought county families down to their houses, filling the watering-place of Baymouth and opening Mr. Bingley's Theatre Royal at Chatteris. For the first time, the old county families—the Fogys of Drummington, the Squares of Tozely Park, the Welbores of The Barrow—ceased their gossip about the "new neighbors." Stories circulated endlessly about Sir Francis, his Lady, Miss Amory, and Captain Strong. The family of Clavering Park had been in the county for three months before the great folk finally took notice.

The turning point came when the Earl of Trehawk, Lord Lieutenant of the County, visited Eyrie Castle, and the Countess Dowager of Rockminster occupied a mansion on the Marine Parade at Baymouth. These magnates came publicly and in state to call upon the Claverings, and the carriages of the county families swiftly followed in their wake.

It was then that Mirobolant found his art useful, distracting him from the pangs of love. The footmen were too busy to ferry messages between the Park and Fairoaks. Blanche found new friends and new walks, leaving Pen to fish alone by the river. He came day after day, whipping the stream, but the fish wouldn't bite, nor did the elusive "Peri" appear. Here, in strict confidence, we must mention a delicate matter hinted at before: a certain hollow tree where Pen once stationed himself for his passion for Miss Fotheringay. He later repurposed this cavity into a post-office. Under a piece of moss and a stone, he placed little poems or letters addressed to an "Undine" or "Naiad" who frequented the stream. Occasionally, these were replaced by a receipt in the shape of a flower or a modest word or two in French or English on pink scented paper. Miss Amory, we know, walked by this stream and used pink paper. But after the great folks invaded Clavering Park, evening after evening, nobody came to fetch Pen's letters. The white paper remained undisturbed under its stone and moss while the Brawl rolled by. The letters contained little—merely half-jocular, half-sympathetic words suitable for any young lady. Oh, silly Pendennis, if you wanted this one, why didn't you speak? Perhaps neither party was in earnest; you were merely playing at being in love, and the sportive Undine was humming along.

When a man is baulked in this game, he often loses his temper. When nobody came for Pen's poems, he took them seriously again, feeling tragic and romantic once more. He demanded an explanation. One day he found the Hall full of visitors; another, Miss Amory was asleep after a ball; a third, she was walking in state with the Bishop and Bishopess of Chatteris, who scowled at him and drew up in great dignity when his name was presented. The Right Reverend Prelate knew of the little transaction in the Dean's garden.

"The Bishop says you're a sad young man," good-natured Lady Clavering whispered. "What have you been doing? Nothing to vex such a dear Mar as yours? How is your dear Mar? Why doesn't she come and see us? We aren't seeing any neighbors now. Give my love to her and Laura, and come to dinner tomorrow."

Mrs. Pendennis was too unwell to come, but Laura and Pen attended. At the great party, Pen managed only a hurried word with Miss Amory. "You never come to the river now," he said.

"I can't," Blanche replied. "The house is full of people."

"Undine has left the stream," Pen continued, choosing to be poetic.

"She never ought to have gone there," Miss Amory answered. "She won't go again. It was very foolish, very wrong. It was only play. Besides, you have other consolations at home," she added, looking him full in the face for an instant before dropping her eyes.

If he wanted her, why didn't he speak then? She might have said "Yes." But as she mentioned other consolations at home, he thought of Laura—so affectionate, so pure—and his mother, who had bent her fond heart on uniting him with her adopted daughter.

"Blanche!" he began in a vexed tone. "Miss Amory!"

"Laura is looking at us, Mr. Pendennis," the young lady said. "I must go back to the company," and she ran off, leaving Mr. Pendennis to bite his nails in perplexity and gaze out into the moonlit garden.

Laura was indeed looking at Pen. She was conversing with Mr. Pynsent, Lord Rockminster's son and grandson of the Dowager Lady, who sat in state receiving Lady Clavering's bad grammar and patronizing the vacuous Sir Francis, whose interest in the county she sought to secure. Pynsent and Pen had met at Oxford, where the latter, in his heyday of fashion, had been the superior of the young patrician. They exchanged that exceedingly impertinent and amusing demi-nod of recognition practiced only by University men—a gesture saying, "Confound you, what do you do here?"

"I knew that man at Oxford," Mr. Pynsent said to Miss Bell. "A Mr. Pendennis, I think."

"Yes," said Miss Bell.

"He seems rather sweet upon Miss Amory," the gentleman continued. Laura looked at them, perhaps thinking so too, but said nothing.

"A man of large property in the county, isn't he? He used to talk about representing it. He used to speak at the Union. Whereabouts do his estates lie?"

Laura smiled. "His estates lie on the other side of the river, near the lodge-gate. He is my cousin, and I live there."

"Where?" asked Mr. Pynsent, laughing.

"Why, on the other side of the river, at Fairoaks," answered Miss Bell.

"Many pheasants there? Cover looks rather good," said the simple gentleman.

Laura smiled again. "We have nine hens and a cock, a pig, and an old pointer."

"Pendennis doesn't preserve, then?" continued Mr. Pynsent.

"You should come and see him," the girl said, laughing, greatly amused by the notion that her Pen was a great county gentleman.

"Indeed, I quite long to renew our acquaintance," Mr. Pynsent said gallantly, with a look that fairly said, "It is you that I would like to come and see." To which Miss Laura vouchsafed a smile and a little bow.

Here Blanche stepped up with her most fascinating smile and ogle, begging Laura to sing a duet. Laura, ready to please, went to the piano. Mr. Pynsent listened until Miss Amory began for herself, then strode away.

"What a nice, frank, amiable, well-bred girl that is, Wagg," Mr. Pynsent said to a gentleman from Baymouth—"the tall one, I mean, with the ringlets and red lips—monstrous red, ain't they?"

"What do you think of the girl of the house?" asked Wagg.

"I think she's a lean, scraggy humbug," said Mr. Pynsent with great candor. "She drags her shoulders out of her dress, she never lets her eyes alone, and she goes simpering and ogling about like a French waiting-maid."

"Pynsent, be civil," cried the other, "somebody can hear."

"Oh, it's Pendennis of Boniface," Mr. Pynsent said. "Fine evening, Mr. Pendennis; we were just talking of your charming cousin."

"Any relation to my old friend, Major Pendennis?" asked Mr. Wagg.

"His nephew. Had the pleasure of meeting you at Gaunt House," Mr. Pen said with his very best air. The acquaintance between the gentlemen was made instantly.

The next afternoon, the two gentlemen stayed at Clavering Park were found by Pen on his return from a fruitless fishing excursion, seated in his mother's drawing-room in comfortable conversation with the widow and her ward. Mr. Pynsent, tall and gaunt with large red whiskers, was striding over a chair in the intimate neighborhood of Miss Laura. She was amused by his talk, which was simple, straightforward, humorous, and interspersed with homely expressions of a style sometimes called slang. It was the first specimen of a young London dandy Laura had seen.

Mr. Wagg, entering the Fairoaks premises with his companion, eyed everything. "Old gardener," he said, seeing Mr. John at the lodge—"old red livery waistcoat—clothes hanging out to dry on the gooseberry-bushes—blue aprons, white ducks—gad, they must be young Pendennis's white ducks—nobody else wears 'em in the family. Rather a shy place for a sucking county member, ay, Pynsent?"

"Snug little crib," said Mr. Pynsent, "pretty cosy little lawn."

"Mr. Pendennis at home, old gentleman?" Mr. Wagg asked the domestic. John answered, "No, Master Pendennis was agone out."

"Are the ladies at home?" asked the younger visitor. Mr. John answered, "Yes, they be." As the pair walked over the trim gravel and neat shrubberies to the hall-door, Mr. Wagg noted everything: the barometer, the letter-bag, the umbrellas, the ladies' clogs, Pen's hats and tartan wrapper. Such minutiae attracted Wagg instinctively.

"Old fellow does all the work," he whispered to Pynsent. "Caleb Balderstone. Shouldn't wonder if he's the housemaid." The next minute they were in the presence of the Fairoaks ladies, whom Pynsent recognized as perfectly well-bred. Mr. Wagg made his obeisance with florid bows and an occasional knowing leer. Mr. Pynsent ignored these signals except by extreme haughtiness toward Wagg and particular deference to the ladies. If there was one thing laughable in Wagg's eyes, it was poverty. He had the soul of a butler brought from the pantry to make fun in the drawing-room. His jokes were plentiful, his good nature genuine, but he didn't understand that a gentleman could wear an old coat or that a lady could be respectable without a carriage or a French milliner.

"Charming place, ma'am," said he, bowing to the widow. "Noble prospect—delightful to us Cocknies, who seldom see anything but Pall Mall." The widow simply replied she had never been in London but once, before her son was born.

"Fine village, ma'am, fine village," said Mr. Wagg. "Increasing every day. It'll be quite a large town soon. Not a bad place to live in for those who can't get the country, and will repay a visit when you honour it."

"My brother, Major Pendennis, has often mentioned your name to us," the widow said. "And we have been very much amused by some of your droll books, sir," Helen continued, who detested their tone thoroughly.

"He is my very good friend," Mr. Wagg said with a low bow. "One of the best known men about town, and where known, ma'am, appreciated—I assure you appreciated. He is with our friend Steyne at Aix-la-Chapelle. Steyne has a touch of the gout, and so, between ourselves, has your brother. I am going to Stillbrook for the pheasant-shooting, and afterwards to Bareacres, where Pendennis and I shall probably meet." He poured out a flood of fashionable talk, introducing scores of peers, rattling on with breathless spirits while the simple widow listened in silent wonder. *What a man,* she thought. *Are all the men of fashion in London like this? I am sure Pen will never like him.*
