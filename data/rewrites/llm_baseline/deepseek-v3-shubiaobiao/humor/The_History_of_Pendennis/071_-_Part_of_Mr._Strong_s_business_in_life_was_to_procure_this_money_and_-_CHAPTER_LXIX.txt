The next morning, Major Pendennis’s shutters were thrown open by none other than Morgan—his soon-to-be-former valet—who entered with the same grave, unreadable expression as ever, bearing the Major’s freshly pressed clothes, shaving water, and the gleaming silver implements of his daily toilette.

“Ah, it’s you,” the Major observed from his bed. “Just so we’re clear—you’re not being taken back.”

Morgan, with the dignity of a deposed monarch, replied, “I ’ave no intention of returning to your service, Major Pendennis, nor to any man’s. But as you are still under my roof, I shall see to your comfort as befits a gentleman.”

And with that, he laid out the Major’s dressing-case with ceremonial precision, stropped the razor to a murderous edge, and performed his duties with the air of a man attending a funeral—his own, perhaps.

Once finished, he cleared his throat with ominous gravity. “Considerin’ you may require a respectable replacement, I took the liberty of engaging a young man last evening. ’E is ’ere.”

The Major, still horizontal, arched an eyebrow. “How very thoughtful.”

“He ’as moved in the first circles,” Morgan assured him. “Valeted for young Lord Cubley abroad. Delicate chest—can’t abide Scotland. Name’s Frosch.”

“Charmed, I’m sure,” the Major drawled.

Morgan stiffened. The Major’s politeness was somehow more infuriating than outright abuse. “Shall I send ’im in?”

“By all means. And Morgan—be a good fellow and *leave*, would you?”

Morgan withdrew, bristling. The Major’s excessive civility was worse than a slap.

Breakfast was a silent affair. The Major, now armed with a cane, oversaw the packing of his belongings—few enough, for a man who lived as lightly as a dandelion seed. His library consisted of:

- One Bible (his mother’s).
- A road book.
- A calf-bound copy of *Walter Lorraine* (his nephew’s novel, which he had never read).
- The Duke of Wellington’s *Despatches*.
- Various portraits of generals, sovereigns, and consorts (mostly gifts he had forgotten to return).

The Slavey—so called by Morgan, though known to Mrs. Brixham as “Betty”—dusted and packed under Morgan’s baleful eye. The man was a silent storm cloud, withholding his thunder but promising rain.

When the moment came, Morgan presented his accounts with the solemnity of a tax collector. The Major paid without comment.

Then, leaning in, Morgan lowered his voice. “Now, between men—I ’ave information.”

The Major feigned indifference. “Have you indeed?”

“Valuable information,” Morgan pressed. “About a certain *baronet* who ain’t as married as ’e lets on. And ’is lady? Married to another.”

The Major’s pulse ticked up, but his face remained impassive. “Ah. Eavesdropping, were we?”

Morgan smirked. “A servant ’as ’is ways, same as a gentleman. And this information—well, it could be worth somethin’.”

The Major’s jaw tightened. “Blackmail, then.”

“Call it *insurance*,” Morgan corrected. “Five hundred a year, and I stay quiet. Otherwise—”

“Otherwise?”

“Otherwise, I smash Clavering. Smash ’is marriage. Smash your nephew’s prospects. And you along with ’em.”

The Major’s hand drifted toward his desk drawer. “You forget yourself, Morgan.”

Morgan’s eyes flickered to the drawer. “Try it. I’ve a policeman below.”

The Major laughed—a dry, rattling sound. “This old thing?” He drew out a petite double-barreled pistol. “Hasn’t been loaded since the Siege of Seringapatam.”

Morgan shrieked like a scalded cat and bolted for the window. “MURDER! POLICE!”

The Major sighed and beckoned the constable upstairs. “This drunken fool’s been stealing from me. We’ll search his trunks.”

Morgan paled. Among his possessions were:

- Several fine shirts (borrowed indefinitely).
- A gold-headed cane (misplaced by the Major in ‘42).
- An opera-glass (conveniently forgotten).

The Major let him squirm, then relented. “Sign this confession, and we’ll call it even.”

The document was a masterpiece:

> *I, James Morgan, having enriched myself under Major Pendennis’s employ, confess to theft, lies, and general villainy. I return Mrs. Brixham’s extorted funds and declare myself a scoundrel.*

Morgan balked. “I’d sooner die.”

“Then die,” the Major said pleasantly. “Or sign.”

Morgan signed.

With his trunks loaded, the Major tipped his hat to Mrs. Brixham, who blessed him through tears. Morgan, glowering from the window, received a mocking salute in return.

And so the Major departed—not with a whimper, but with the quiet satisfaction of a man who had just checkmated a blackmailer without spending a penny.

That evening, Arthur Pendennis arrived at the house to find Morgan lounging like a landlord—which, in fact, he now was.

“Uncle out?” Arthur asked.

“Major Pendennis ’as left,” Morgan intoned. “Permanently.”

Arthur blinked. “Where’s he gone?”

“Cox’s Hotel. Jermyn Street.” A pause. Then, with eerie calm: “Might I ’ave a word with you, sir? On a matter of… mutual interest?”

Arthur, amused, agreed.

And thus did Morgan, outmaneuvered by the Major, turn his gaze upon the nephew—with schemes yet untold.
