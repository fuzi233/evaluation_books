During the first days of my sojourn at the Terrace, Graham never drew his chair into the little province where I sat with my work. He traversed the room in long, steady measures, as if keeping time to some inward march; he spoke to his mother with his usual ease, laughed when she smiled, and yet—under all that—there was a restraint, fine as the film that skins cooling broth, telling you the pot has been watched and worried over.

I watched him too. I listened—not only with the ear, but with that sharper sense which catches what is going to be said before it is spoken. I expected one name to break from him as naturally as steam from a kettle: Miss Fanshawe’s. I kept myself ready—sympathy set out like cups and saucers upon a tray, patience standing by like a maid in an orderly kitchen, waiting the bell.

At last, after a struggle I saw and could not but respect, he ventured on the subject as if it were a dish to be offered with some ceremony, not flung down.

“Your friend is travelling, I hear?”

Friend. The word, in his mouth, had the softening of butter on hot bread. In mine, it stuck like an unrisen crust; yet I let it pass. It was not my business to contradict him in his chosen seasoning of things.

“Friend?” I asked, as if I did not know whom he meant.

He had sat down at my work-table. His fingers, restless as if they must be doing something, took up a reel of thread and began to unwind it with a heedless extravagance—drawing out yards as one might spill flour upon a clean board. It was a small waste, but it betrayed him: he could not be still.

“Ginevra—Miss Fanshawe—has accompanied the Cholmondeleys, they say, on a tour in the south of France?”

“She has.”

“Do you correspond?”

“It will astonish you to hear that I never once thought of applying for that privilege.”

“You have seen her letters?”

“Yes; several to her uncle.”

“They will not be deficient in wit and naïveté,” he said, as if tasting the phrase. “There is so much sparkle, and so little art in her soul.”

“She writes comprehensively enough when she writes to M. de Bassompierre: he who runs may read.”

He did not hear—would not hear—the plainness under my words; he was intent on the pleasing part, as if choosing only the candied fruit out of a cake and leaving the currants of truth behind.

“And her handwriting? It must be pretty—light—ladylike?”

It was. I said so.

“I verily believe,” he went on, “that all she does is well done.”

I did not echo him quickly enough, so he added, almost eagerly, “You, who know her—could you name a point in which she is deficient?”

“She does several things very well.” In thought, I subjoined: flirtation among the rest; the art of making a man’s peace depend upon her smile, and his hunger upon her caprice.

“When do you suppose she will return to town?” he asked next, as if his question could summon her like a bell calls to the supper-table.

“Pardon me, Dr. John, I must explain. You honour me too much in ascribing to me an intimacy with Miss Fanshawe I have not the felicity to enjoy. I have never been the depositary of her plans and secrets. You will find her particular friends in another sphere than mine: amongst the Cholmondeleys, for instance.”

He understood me—wrongly. He thought I spoke as one slighted speaks: that I had been put aside, and that pride had taken the tone of indifference.

“Excuse her,” he said, in that tone of generous excuse with which he would cover any fault in her, as one lays a white cloth over a stain. “Judge her indulgently. The glitter of fashion misleads her; but she will soon find these people hollow, and return to you with augmented attachment and confirmed trust. I know something of the Cholmondeleys—superficial, showy, selfish people. Depend on it, at heart Ginevra values you beyond a score of such.”

“You are very kind,” I answered briefly.

A denial burned on my tongue, but I quenched it. I submitted—reader, I submitted—to be taken for the discarded confidante of Miss Fanshawe, humbled, cast off, and pining. It was a bitter posture; the very air in that room seemed to taste of it.

“Yet,” he pursued, “while I comfort you, I cannot take the same consolation to myself. I cannot hope she will do me justice. De Hamal is most worthless, yet I fear he pleases her. Wretched delusion!”

Then it was as if some hand, long and steadily pressing down on a lid, slipped—only for a second—and the steam of all my pent endurance rushed out. Illness and weakness had made me brittle; the least tap broke me.

“Dr. Bretton,” I cried, “there is no delusion like your own. On all points but one you are a man—frank, healthful, right-thinking, clear-sighted: on this exceptional point you are but a slave. I declare, where Miss Fanshawe is concerned, you merit no respect; nor have you mine.”

I rose and left the room, heated and ashamed even while I was hot.

That little scene occurred in the morning. In the evening I met him again, and at once knew mischief had been done. He was not made of common clay. His nature had breadth and strength in its main lines; but in the finishing there was workmanship almost feminine in delicacy—such fineness as you do not suspect in a man until you have brushed, too sharply, against the hidden nerve and felt him shrink. He was not quick at reading another’s feeling by instinct; but he could feel his own with an exquisite exactness. The sympathetic faculty—divining another’s pain before it is spoken—was not his predominant gift; yet he was kind, generous, open-handed: show him need, and his palm offered itself; put grief into words, and he listened. Only do not demand miracles of intuition.

That night, when he entered and the lamplight met his face, I saw the whole mechanism of him as plainly as if a cover had been lifted. To be called “slave,” and told that, on a point, he merited no respect, had struck him as a sharp knife strikes under the rib. He did not deny it—indeed, I think his candid mind turned it over and over, as if looking for the part in him that had earned the name. His gravity—perhaps his coldness—showed itself to me and to his mother; but on his countenance there was neither malice nor rancour nor littleness. Even depressed, it had the best beauty of a man: not the beauty of smiling, but of being able to bear.

When I placed his chair at the table—hastening to do it myself, before the servant could—when I gave him his tea with trembling care, he said, “Thank you, Lucy,” in as kind a tone as ever my ear had owned for comfort.

For me, there was only one course. I must expiate my vehemence, or I should not sleep. I could not endure a household where the air between two people is cut into by a quarrel: it is like trying to eat in a kitchen where a knife keeps striking the board—tap, tap—till appetite turns to nausea. School solitude, conventual silence, any stagnation seemed preferable to living at odds with Dr. John.

As for Ginevra—let her take her silver wings, let her mount where she pleased, let her set up her little throne among the stars of his imagination; never again, if I could help it, would I dispute the constellation he chose to worship.

Long I tried to catch his eye. Again and again it met mine, and then withdrew—no defiance in it, only that quiet withdrawal of one who waits to be approached, and will not force himself. After tea he sat reading, sad and still. I wanted to go and sit near him, as one draws close to the hearth when the room is chilly; yet I feared, absurdly, that he would show hostility if I ventured.

His mother left the room. The absence of her presence made the silence louder, as the hush after a door shuts makes you hear the ticking of a clock. At last regret grew insupportable, and I murmured, “Dr. Bretton.”

He looked up from his book. His eyes were not cold; his mouth had no curl of scorn. He was ready to hear me. His spirit was too mellow—of too generous a vintage—to sour in one thunder-clap.

“Dr. Bretton, forgive my hasty words: do—do forgive them.”

He smiled the moment I spoke.

“Perhaps I deserved them, Lucy. If you don’t respect me, I am sure it is because I am not respectable. I fear I am an awkward fool: I must manage badly in some way; for where I wish to please, it seems I don’t please.”

“Of that you cannot be sure; and even if such be the case, is it the fault of your character, or of another’s perceptions? But now—let me unsay what I said in anger. In one thing, and in all things, I deeply respect you. If you think scarcely enough of yourself, and too much of others, what is that but an excellence?”

“Can I think too much of Ginevra?”

“I believe you may; you believe you can’t. Let us agree to differ. Let me be pardoned: that is what I ask.”

“Do you think I cherish ill-will for one warm word?”

“I see you do not and cannot; but just say, ‘Lucy, I forgive you!’ Say that, to ease me of the heart-ache.”

“Put away your heart-ache, as I will put away mine; for you wounded me a little, Lucy. Now, when the pain is gone, I more than forgive: I feel grateful, as to a sincere well-wisher.”

“I am your sincere well-wisher: you are right.”

So our quarrel ended.

If, as you read on, you should find my opinion of Dr. John shifting, pardon the seeming inconsistency. I can only give the feeling as I felt it then, and show the character as it appeared when discovered. He proved his fineness by being kinder to me after that misunderstanding than before. The very incident which, by my fears, should have estranged us, changed our relation otherwise than I had anticipated.

An invisible coldness—very slight, very transparent, but very chill—had long glazed the medium through which we spoke. It was like a thin screen of ice over water: you could see through it, yet it kept you from touching. Those few warm words—warm only with anger—breathed on the frostwork. About that time it gave its little crackling note of dissolution.

From that day, so long as we continued friends, he no longer held to ceremony in discourse with me. He seemed to know that if he would talk of himself—above all, of what most absorbed him—my expectation would always be answered, my will—strangely—always ready to yield.

It follows, of course, that I continued to hear much of “Ginevra.”

“Ginevra!” He thought her so fair, so good; he spoke so lovingly of her charms, her sweetness, her innocence, that, in spite of my plain, prose knowledge of the reality, a reflected glow began to settle on her image even for me—as lamplight makes common china look like porcelain. Still, I confess, he often talked nonsense; but I strove to be unfailingly patient. I had learnt my lesson. I had learnt how severe the pain was to me of crossing him, grieving him, disappointing him. In a strange, new sense, I grew selfish—selfish for the pleasure of indulging his mood, of being pliant to his will, as one gives way in kneading dough: press it too roughly and it resists; work with it, and it becomes smooth beneath your hands.

He seemed to me most absurd when he obstinately doubted, and desponded, about his power to win at last Miss Fanshawe’s preference. The fancy rooted itself in my mind more stubbornly than ever that she was only coquetting to goad him, and that, at heart, she coveted his looks and words as greedily as she coveted anything that glittered.

Sometimes, in spite of my resolution to bear and hear, he harassed me. There was a gall-and-honey pleasure in the bearing; yet now and then he struck so on the flint of what firmness I owned that it emitted fire.

One day, to still his impatience, I asserted with decision that I felt positive Miss Fanshawe must eventually intend to accept him.

“Positive!” he echoed. “It was easy to say so; but had I any grounds for such assurance?”

“The best grounds.”

“Now, Lucy—do tell me what!”

“You know them as well as I; and, knowing them, Dr. John, it amazes me you should not repose the frankest confidence in her fidelity. To doubt, under the circumstances, is almost to insult.”

“Now you are beginning to speak fast and breathe short,” he said; and there was eagerness under his banter. “Speak a little faster and breathe a little shorter, till you have given an explanation—a full explanation. I must have it.”

“You shall, Dr. John. In some cases you are a lavish, generous man: you are a worshipper ever ready with the votive offering. Should Père Silas ever convert you, you will give him abundance of alms for his poor; you will supply his altar with tapers; the shrine of your favourite saint you will do your best to enrich. Ginevra, Dr. John—”

“Hush!” he interposed quickly. “Don’t go on.”

“Hush, I will not: and go on I will. Ginevra has had her hands filled from your hands more times than I can count. You have sought for her the costliest flowers; you have busied your brain devising gifts the most delicate—such as one would have thought only a woman could have imagined; and, besides, Miss Fanshawe owns a set of ornaments to purchase which your generosity must have verged on extravagance.”

A flush—sudden, deep—ran over the face of her admirer; a modesty she herself had never shown in the matter rose in him for her, as if he would veil what she had paraded. He snipped at a skein of silk with my scissors, cutting too sharply, as though the blades could shear off my words.

“Nonsense!” he said. “I offered them to please myself. I felt she did me a favour in accepting them.”

“She did more than a favour, Dr. John: she pledged her honour that she would make some return; and if she cannot pay you in affection, she ought to hand out a business-like equivalent—in the shape of some rouleaux of gold pieces.”

“But you don’t understand her,” he persisted, with the obstinacy of a man defending what he loves against the very evidence of his senses. “She is far too disinterested to care for my gifts, and too simple-minded to know their value.”

I laughed out—could not help it. I had heard her price each jewel as neatly as a tradesman counts coins; I knew her embarrassments, her schemes, her perpetual measuring of worth, her efforts to realise supplies—money, its want and its pursuit, had been, young as she was, the most frequent and favourite stimulus of her thoughts for years.

He went on, as if my laugh had not sounded.

“You should have seen her whenever I have laid on her lap some trifle—so cool, so unmoved. No eagerness to take, not even pleasure in contemplating. Just from amiable reluctance to grieve me, she would permit the bouquet to lie beside her, and perhaps consent to bear it away. Or, if I achieved the fastening of a bracelet on her ivory arm—however pretty the trinket might be (and I always carefully chose what seemed to me pretty, and what of course was not valueless)—the glitter never dazzled her bright eyes; she would hardly cast one look on my gift.”

“Then, of course, not valuing it, she would unloose and return it to you?”

“No; for such a repulse she was too good-natured. She would consent to seem to forget what I had done, and retain the offering with ladylike quiet and easy oblivion. Under such circumstances, how can a man build on acceptance of his presents as a favourable symptom? For my part, were I to offer her all I have, and she to take it—such is her incapacity to be swayed by sordid considerations—I should not venture to believe the transaction advanced me one step.”

“Dr. John,” I began, “love is blind—”

But at that instant a blue, subtle ray shot sideways from his eye—quick as the glint on a knife lifted from the board. It reminded me of old days; it reminded me of his picture. It half led me to think that part, at least, of his professed belief in Miss Fanshawe’s naïveté was assumed; it made me doubtfully conjecture that, in spite of his passion for her beauty, his appreciation of her foibles might possibly be less mistaken—more clear-sighted—than his general language implied.

After all, it might have been only a chance look—at best the token of a momentary impression. Chance or intention, real or imagined, it closed the conversation.
