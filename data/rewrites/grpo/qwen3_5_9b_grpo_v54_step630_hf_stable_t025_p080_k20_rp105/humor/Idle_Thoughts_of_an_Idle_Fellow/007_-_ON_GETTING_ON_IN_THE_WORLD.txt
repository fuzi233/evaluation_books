Now, I admit it: this isn't exactly the kind of deep, soul-crushing philosophy an idle fellow usually broods over. But then again, the best view of the game is often from the sidelines, isn't it? Picture me, tucked away in my private arbor by the roadside, puffing away on my hookah of pure contentment and nibbling on sweet lotus-leaves of indolence. From this lofty perch, I get a front-row seat to the absolute circus unfolding on the Great High-Road of Life.

And what a circus it is! The procession never ends. Day and night, you can hear the frantic *thump-thump-thump* of millions of feet—some sprinting, some shuffling, some limping like broken toys—but all of them moving with a feverish urgency. It's a race where everyone is straining every muscle, every nerve, every drop of blood just to catch up with a horizon that keeps running away faster than they can jog.

Take a look at the crowd as they surge forward. It's a riot of humanity: men and women, ancient and young, kings and clowns, the rich and the ragged, the cheerful and the miserable. They are all hustling, busting, scrambling. The strong shove the weak out of the way; the clever ones sneak past the slow ones; those in the back elbow those in front; and those in front are so desperate they're actually kicking the people behind them as they run.

Peep closely at the show. There's an old man gasping for air, looking like he's seen a ghost; there's a shy young lady being dragged along by a hard-faced matron who looks like she'd rather be anywhere else; there's a studious youth clutching a book titled *How to Get On in the World*, stumbling blindly while everyone else zooms past him; there's a bored-looking chap with a fashionably dressed woman bumping his elbow; there's a boy staring wistfully back at the sunny village he'll never see again; and there, striding with a confident swagger, is a broad-shouldered giant. Right next to him, a thin, stooped fellow tiptoes stealthily, dodging and shuffling like a cat in a room full of rocking chairs. Then there's the artful rogue, eyes glued to the dirt, weaving side-to-side, pretending he's making progress; and right there, a noble-looking youth stands frozen, torn between the distant dream and the sticky mud under his boots.

Suddenly, a pretty girl appears, her face already lined with worry; then a weary man; then a hopeful lad.

It's a motley mess—a motley mess! Princes and beggars, saints and sinners, butchers and bakers and candlestick makers, tinkers and tailors, plowboys and sailors—all jostling shoulder to shoulder. Here comes the lawyer in his wig and gown; here's the old Jewish clothes-man under his dusty cap; here's the soldier in his bright red coat; here's the undertaker's mute in his streaming hatband and threadbare gloves; here's the musty scholar fumbling with his faded books; and here's the scented actor dangling his flashy seals.

Here's the slick politician shouting his magic cures for the world's problems, and right beside him, the traveling huckster holding up his fake remedies for human misery. Here's the fat capitalist and there's the wiry laborer; here's the scientist and there's the shoe-shiner; here's the poet and there's the water-rate collector; here's the cabinet minister and there's the ballet dancer. Here's a red-nosed publican bragging about his beer vats, and right next to him, a temperance lecturer charging fifty pounds a night to tell you not to drink. Here's a judge and there's a swindler; here's a priest and there's a gambler. Here's a jeweled duchess, smiling like an angel; and right next to her, a thin lodging-house keeper, irritable and hungry; and there, wobbling and strutting, is a creature covered in tawdry paint and finery.

Cheek by jowl, they struggle onward. Screaming, cursing, and praying, laughing, singing, and moaning, they rush past each other in a blur. Their speed never slows, the race never stops. There is no rest stop, no cool fountain, no shady tree to pause under. On, on, on—through the heat, the crush, and the dust. On, or they'll be trampled flat and forgotten. On, with throbbing brains and shaking legs—on, until the heart turns sick, the vision blurs, and a gurgling groan tells the folks behind that someone else has finally dropped out of the race.

And yet, despite the killer pace and the rocky road, who but a lazybones or a complete idiot stays away? Who, like the late traveler who watches a fairy party, drinks a goblin cup, and suddenly finds himself spinning in the middle of the whirlwind, can watch this mad chaos and not get sucked right in? Not me, for one. I have to admit, my arbor, my pipe, and my lotus leaves sound lovely and philosophical on paper, but I'm afraid I'm not the type to sit in a garden smoking when there's actual fun happening outside. I think I'm more like the Irishman who saw a crowd gathering and sent his little girl out to ask, "Is there going to be a row?" because, as he said, "If there is, Father would like to be in it."

I love the fierce fight. I love watching it. I love hearing stories of people battling their way up—bravely and fairly, meaning they didn't just slip through by luck or cheating. It stirs up that old Saxon fighting blood inside us, just like the tales of knights fighting against impossible odds used to thrill us when we were schoolboys.

And fighting the battle of life? That's fighting against truly terrifying odds. There are giants and dragons in this nineteenth century, and the golden treasure chest they guard isn't as easy to steal as the storybooks make it seem. In those books, Algernon takes one last look at the ancestral hall, wipes a tear from his eye, and heads off—to return three years later, rolling in riches. The authors never tell us *how* he did it, which is a shame, because it would surely be thrilling.

But then, not one novelist in a thousand ever tells the real story of their hero. They spend twelve pages describing a tea party, then summarize a whole lifetime with a line like, "He had become one of our merchant princes," or "He was now a great artist, with the world at his feet." Come on, there's more real life in one of Gilbert's patter-songs than in half the biographical novels ever written. He tells us every single step by which his office boy rose to become the "ruler of the queen's navy," and explains exactly how a lawyer with no cases managed to become a great and good judge, "ready to try this breach of promise of marriage."

The real interest of existence lies in the petty details, not the big results. What we really want is a novel that shows us the hidden under-current of an ambitious man's career—his struggles, his failures, his hopes, his disappointments, and his victories. It would be a massive hit. I'm sure the story of wooing Fortune would be just as fascinating as the story of wooing any flesh-and-blood maiden, though, by the way, it would read almost exactly the same; for Fortune is, indeed, as the ancients painted her, very much like a woman—not quite so unreasonable and inconsistent, but nearly so—and the pursuit is much the same in both cases.

Ben Jonson nailed it in his couplet:

"Court a mistress, she denies you;
Let her alone, she will court you."

It puts both situations in a nutshell. A woman never truly cares for her lover until he stops caring for her; and it's not until you snap your fingers in Fortune's face and turn on your heel that she starts smiling at you.

But by that time, you don't really care if she smiles or frowns. Why couldn't she have smiled when her smiles would have filled you with ecstasy? Everything comes too late in this world.

The good people say it's perfectly right and proper that it should be this way, and that it proves ambition is wicked.

Nonsense! The good people are completely wrong. (They always are, in my opinion. We never agree on anything.) What would the world do without ambitious people, I should like to know? Why, it would be as flabby as a Norfolk dumpling. Ambitious people are the yeast that raises it into wholesome bread. Without them, the world would never get out of bed. They are the busybodies who are up early in the morning, hammering, shouting, and rattling the fire-irons, making it generally impossible for the rest of the house to stay in bed.

Wrong to be ambitious, forsooth! The men are wrong who, with bent backs and sweating brows, cut the smooth road over which humanity marches forward from generation to generation! They are wrong for using the talents that their Master has entrusted to them—for toiling while others play!

Of course, they are seeking their reward. Man is not given that godlike unselfishness that thinks only of others' good. But in working for themselves, they are working for us all. We are so bound together that no man can labor for himself alone. Each blow he strikes in his own behalf helps to mold the universe. The stream, struggling onward, turns the mill-wheel; the coral insect, fashioning its tiny cell, joins continents together; and the ambitious man, building a pedestal for himself, leaves a monument to posterity. Alexander and Caesar fought for their own ends, but in doing so, they put a belt of civilization half around the earth. Stephenson, to win a fortune, invented the steam-engine; and Shakespeare wrote his plays in order to keep a comfortable home for Mrs. Shakespeare and the little Shakespeares.

Contented, unambitious people are all very well in their way. They form a neat, useful background for great portraits to be painted against, and they make a respectable, if not particularly intelligent, audience for the active spirits of the age to play before. I have not a word to say against contented people so long as they keep quiet. But do not, for goodness' sake, let them go strutting about, as they are so fond of doing, crying out that they are the true models for the whole species. Why, they are the deadheads, the drones in the great hive, the street crowds that lounge about, gaping at those who are working.

And let them not imagine, either—as they are also fond of doing—that they are very wise and philosophical and that it is a very artful thing to be contented. It may be true that "a contented mind is happy anywhere," but so is a Jerusalem pony, and the consequence is that both are put anywhere and are treated anyhow. "Oh, you need not bother about him," is what is said; "he is very contented as he is, and it would be a pity to disturb him." And so your contented party is passed over, and the discontented man gets his place.

If you are foolish enough to be contented, don't show it, but grumble with the rest; and if you can do with a little, ask for a great deal. Because if you don't, you won't get any. In this world, it is necessary to adopt the principle pursued by the plaintiff in an action for damages, and to demand ten times more than you are ready to accept. If you can feel satisfied with a hundred, begin by insisting on a thousand; if you start by suggesting a hundred, you will only get ten.

It was by not following this simple plan that poor Jean Jacques Rousseau came to such grief. He fixed the summit of his earthly bliss at living in an orchard with an amiable woman and a cow, and he never attained even that. He did get as far as the orchard, but the woman was not amiable, and she brought her mother with her, and there was no cow. Now, if he had made up his mind for a large country estate, a houseful of angels, and a cattle-show, he might have lived to possess his kitchen garden and one head of live-stock, and even possibly have come across that *rara-avis*—a really amiable woman.

What a terribly dull affair, too, life must be for contented people! How heavy the time must hang upon their hands, and what on earth do they occupy their thoughts with, supposing that they have any? Reading the paper and smoking seems to be the intellectual food of the majority of them, to which the more energetic add playing the flute and talking about the affairs of the next-door neighbor.

They never knew the excitement of expectation nor the stern delight of accomplished effort, such as stir the pulse of the man who has objects, and hopes, and plans. To the ambitious man, life is a brilliant game—a game that calls forth all his tact and energy and nerve—a game to be won, in the long run, by the quick eye and the steady hand, and yet having sufficient chance about its working out to give it all the glorious zest of uncertainty. He exults in it as the strong swimmer in the heaving billows, as the athlete in the wrestle, the soldier in the battle.

And if he be defeated, he wins the grim joy of fighting; if he loses the race, he, at least, has had a run. Better to work and fail than to sleep one's life away.

So, walk up, walk up, walk up. Walk up, ladies and gentlemen! Walk up, boys and girls! Show your skill and try your strength; brave your luck and prove your pluck. Walk up! The show is never closed and the game is always going. The only genuine sport in all the fair, gentlemen—highly respectable and strictly moral—patronized by the nobility, clergy, and gentry.
