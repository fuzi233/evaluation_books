Miss Keeldar and her uncle were like two forces in nature that could never find balance—opposites, always at odds. He was quick to anger; she, spirited beyond restraint. He ruled with an iron hand; she craved freedom like air. He lived by the world’s rules; she, perhaps, by dreams of something higher.

He had not come to Yorkshire on a whim. His purpose was clear: to settle his niece’s future once and for all. He wanted her married—to someone suitable, respectable, well-placed. A husband who would take charge of her, secure her fortune, and let him forget her entirely.

But from the moment she could speak, Shirley had challenged his definitions. What he called “suitable,” she found dull. What he deemed “proper,” she saw as stifling. And now, as she stood at the threshold of marriage, it was uncertain whether she would yield even to him.

The test came swiftly.

Mr. Wynne proposed for his son, Samuel Fawthrop Wynne.

“Decidedly suitable! Most proper!” declared Mr. Sympson. “A fine estate, no debts, excellent connections. _It must be done._”

He summoned his niece to the oak parlour, shut the door behind them, delivered the offer, gave his opinion, and demanded her consent.

She refused.

“No,” she said firmly. “I will not marry Samuel Fawthrop Wynne.”

He stared. “Why? I must have a reason. In every way, he is more than worthy of you.”

She stood before the hearth, pale as the marble behind her, eyes wide and unblinking.

“And _I_ ask—what makes this man worthy of _me_?”

“He has twice your fortune, twice your sense, equal standing, equal respectability.”

“If he had my money multiplied fifty times, I would never vow to love him.”

“Then state your objections.”

“He has lived a life of petty, vulgar excess. That alone is reason enough to reject him.”

“Miss Keeldar, you shock me!”

“That conduct sinks him into a depth I cannot reach. His mind does not rise to anything I can honor. There is another barrier—he thinks narrowly, feels little, tastes poorly, behaves with vulgarity.”

“The man is wealthy and respected! To refuse him is arrogance!”

“I refuse, plainly. Do not bring this up again. I forbid it.”

“Do you intend to remain unmarried? Or do you choose celibacy?”

“I deny you the right to demand such a question.”

“Then do you expect some nobleman—a peer—to claim your hand?”

“I doubt if any peer exists whom I would give it to.”

“If madness runs in our blood, then you are mad. Your pride and whimsy border on frenzy.”

“Perhaps,” she said quietly, “by the time I finish speaking, you’ll see me leap over it.”

“You will!” he cried. “Foolish, impractical girl! Take warning! I challenge you to disgrace our name with a _mésalliance_!”

“Our name?” she echoed coldly. “Am _I_ called Sympson?”

“God be praised, you are not! But beware—I will not be mocked!”

“What power do you have over me? Why should I fear you?”

“Beware, madam!”

“I shall take scrupulous care, Mr. Sympson. Before I marry, I mean to esteem—to admire—to _love_.”

“Absurd! Indecent! Unfitting for a woman!”

“To love with all my heart. I know I speak a language you do not understand—but I care little if you do.”

“And if your love falls upon a beggar?”

“It will never fall upon one. Poverty is not noble.”

“Upon a clerk, an actor, a writer—perhaps—?”

“Have courage, Mr. Sympson. Or what?”

“A literary scribbler, a poor, whining artist?”

“I have no taste for the shabby or weak. But I value literature and art. And there, I wonder how your Mr. Wynne could ever suit me. He cannot write a letter without spelling errors; he reads only horse racing papers; he was the laughingstock of Stilbro’ grammar school!”

“Unladylike! Good God—where will she end?” He raised his hands in horror.

“Never at the altar with Sam Wynne.”

“Where will she end? Why aren’t the laws stricter so I might force her to listen?”

“Console yourself, uncle. Even if Britain were a serfdom and you the Czar, you could not _force_ me into this. I will write to Mr. Wynne myself. Leave the matter to me.”

* * * * *

Fortune, though often fickle, sometimes repeats its kindness. Miss Keeldar—her reputation, her spirit—had stirred the countryside. Wherever she went, whispers followed. After Mr. Wynne’s proposal, three others arrived, each with merit. Each was pressed upon her by her uncle—and each was rejected. Among them were men of character and wealth. Many wondered what she expected, what hidden prize she hoped to win.

At last, gossip claimed to have uncovered her secret—and her uncle believed it. The discovery changed everything. He saw his niece not as a reckless girl, but as a woman of rare judgment. His manner toward her softened, then deepened into reverence.

Fieldhead had grown too hot for both of them. Their aunt tried to mediate, but failed. The daughters avoided their arguments, whispering together in fear. Gertrude and Isabella dreaded being left alone with their bold cousin. But now, peace returned. Mr. Sympson was appeased. The household calmed.

Nunnely village had been mentioned—the old church, the forest, the ruins of a monastery. It also held the priory, a grander, older house than Briarfield or Whinbury. And it had a baronet—something neither of those places could boast. But for years, this title had been merely nominal. The young baronet, Sir Philip Nunnely, had lived far away, unknown in his own land.

During Miss Keeldar’s stay at Cliffbridge, she met Sir Philip. They crossed paths on the beach, along the cliffs, at dances. He seemed quiet, almost shy. His manner was simple—not arrogant, not proud, but gentle. He did not lower himself to their company; he seemed glad to be included.

Shirley, who loved honesty and sincerity, quickly warmed to him. She walked with him, sailed with him and his family, laughed with him. She liked him—for his kindness, his modesty, and the pleasure he took in her presence.

There was one flaw—though what friendship lacks this? Sir Philip wrote poetry. Sonnets, ballads, stanzas. Perhaps Shirley thought he clung too closely to his verses; perhaps she found the rhythm flat, the imagery stale. At any rate, when he spoke of them, she turned away. She tried, gently, to steer the conversation elsewhere.

He would coax her into moonlight walks across the bridge, just to recite his longest ballad. He’d lead her to secluded seats where the waves lapped softly, and when they were alone, with the sea before them and cliffs behind, he’d pull out his latest poems and read them with trembling voice. He didn’t seem to realize they were rhyme, not poetry. From Shirley’s downcast eyes and uneasy silence, it was clear she did.

She tried to guide him away from this obsession. It was his passion, but on other subjects he was sensible. She welcomed his questions about Nunnely. She delighted in describing the ancient priory, the wild park, the old church. She urged him to return, to gather his tenants beneath ancestral halls.

To her surprise, he obeyed. By late September, he arrived at the priory.

He soon visited Fieldhead—and stayed. After touring the neighborhood, he said no roof had offered him greater comfort than the gray manor of Briarfield. Modest compared to his own, yet he cherished it.

Soon, sitting beside Shirley in the panelled parlour wasn’t enough. He needed solitude—time to share his newest verses. So he invited her to his grounds, to forests, to valleys watered by the Aire. He led her through peaceful pastures, to still waters.

Her reputation grew. Her uncle foresaw greatness. He imagined himself casually mentioning “my nephew the baronet” with a nonchalant leg crossed. Now, he saw his niece not as a mad girl, but as a “most sensible woman.” He called her, in private, “a truly superior person—unusual, but brilliant.” He bowed before her, opened doors, stooped to pick up her gloves, suffering headaches from bending so often. He joked about women’s wit surpassing men’s wisdom, apologized for misjudging “a certain personage near Fieldhead.” In short, he strutted like a cock in polished shoes.

Yet Shirley remained calm. She seemed half-aware of his efforts. When accused of favoring Sir Philip, she replied simply: “I believe he likes me. And I like him. I never thought a man of rank—the only son of a loving mother, the only brother of devoted sisters—could possess so much goodness and sense.”

Time proved she was right. Sir Philip did like her. Perhaps he found in her the same “curious charm” Mr. Hall had noted. He sought her more and more—until her presence became essential to him. Around Fieldhead, strange emotions stirred. Hopes fluttered, anxieties haunted the halls. Some wandered restlessly through the fields. A silent expectation filled the air.

One thing was certain: Sir Philip was no man to scorn. He was kind. Though not deeply intellectual, he was thoughtful. Shirley could not say of him what she had of Sam Wynne—that his feelings were blunt, his tastes coarse, his manners vulgar. He had sensitivity. He cared for art, though not perfectly. He carried the grace of an English gentleman. His lineage and wealth far surpassed hers.

At first, her humor had mocked his appearance—boyish, slight, sandy-haired, small in stature. But she soon stopped. If anyone else made fun, she would defend him fiercely. “He has a pleasing face,” she insisted. “And there’s something in his heart worth more than three Roman noses, more than Absalom’s hair or Saul’s height.” She still teased gently about his poetry—but only allowed herself to do so.

In short, matters had reached a point where Mr. Yorke felt compelled to remark to Louis Moore:

“Your brother Robert seems either a fool or a madman. Two months ago, I’d have sworn he had the game in his hands. Now he’s running the country, spending weeks in London, and by the time he returns, he’ll find himself checkmated. Louis, ‘there is a tide in the affairs of men which, taken at the flood, leads on to fortune, but once missed, never returns.’ I’d write to Robert and remind him.”

“Robert had hopes for Miss Keeldar?” Louis asked, as if stunned.

“I suggested it to him. And he might have succeeded—she liked him.”

“As a neighbor?”

“As more. I’ve seen her flush and change color at the mere mention of his name. Write to him, I say. Tell him to come home. He’s a finer gentleman than this little baronet.”

“Doesn’t it seem presumptuous—for a penniless man to dream of a rich woman’s hand?”

“Oh, if you want lofty ideals, I’ve nothing to say. I’m plain and practical. If Robert chooses to surrender a royal prize to a pampered aristocrat, I’m content. But at _his_ age, in _his_ position—with _his_ reasons—I’d have fought. No baronet, no duke, no prince should steal my sweetheart without a battle. But tutors are solemn creatures—talking to you is like speaking to a parson.”

* * * * *

Though flattered and courted, Shirley was not spoiled. Beneath the attention, her true self remained. People no longer linked her name with Moore’s. Silence had fallen, seemingly approved by her own apparent forgetting. Yet she had not forgotten him—not entirely. She still cared, if not with love, then with interest. This was evident in the sudden tenderness she showed Louis Moore when illness struck him.

Moore may have caught the fever during visits to poor cottages with Mr. Hall and his pupil. Whatever the cause, he fell ill. After resisting for days, he was confined to bed.

One evening, Henry watched by his side, when a soft tap came at the door—too light for Mrs. Gill or the maid.

“How is Mr. Moore tonight?” asked a low voice from the dark gallery.

“Come in and see for yourself.”

“Is he asleep?”

“I wish he could sleep. Come talk to him, Shirley.”

“He wouldn’t like it.”

But she stepped in. Henry, seeing her hesitate, took her hand and led her to the bed.

The dim light barely revealed her—yet she was dressed elegantly. A party waited below, including Sir Philip. The ladies were in the drawing room. She had slipped away to visit the tutor.

Her white dress, fair arms, golden chain around her throat, glistened in the shadows. Her expression was quiet, thoughtful. She spoke gently.

“Mr. Moore, how are you?”

“I’ve not been very ill. I’m better now.”

“I heard you were thirsty. I brought grapes. Can you taste one?”

“No. But thank you for remembering.”

“Just one.”

She plucked a berry and offered it. He shook his head, turning his flushed face away.

“But what can I bring instead? You don’t want fruit—but your lips are dry. What would you prefer?”

“Mrs. Gill gives me toast-and-water. That’s best.”

Silence.

“Do you suffer? Any pain?”

“Very little.”

“What caused this?”

Silence.

“I wonder what sparked this fever? What do you think?”

“Miasma, perhaps. Autumn brings fevers.”

“I know you visit the sick often, with Mr. Hall. Be careful—recklessness isn’t wise.”

“That reminds me, Miss Keeldar—you should not enter here. I don’t believe my illness is contagious. I scarcely fear _you_ catching it. But why risk even a shadow?”

“Patience. I’ll go soon. But I’d like to do something for you—any small service.”

“They’ll miss you downstairs.”

“No. The gentlemen are still at table.”

“They won’t stay long. Sir Philip doesn’t drink wine. I heard him move from dining to drawing room.”

“It’s a servant.”

“It’s Sir Philip. I know his step.”

“Your hearing is sharp.”

“It’s never dull. And it seems sharper now. Sir Philip was here yesterday. I heard you sing him a song he brought. I heard him call you outside at eleven to look at the evening star.”

“You’re overly sensitive.”

“I heard him kiss your hand.”

“Impossible!”

“No. My room is above the hall. The window faces the front door. The sash was slightly open—I felt feverish. You stood ten minutes with him on the steps. I heard every word. And I heard the kiss.”

“Henry, bring me water.”

“Let me give it.”

But he rose slightly to take the glass from Henry, declining her help.

“And can I do nothing?”

“Nothing. You can’t guarantee me a night of peace. That’s all I need.”

“You don’t sleep well?”

“Sleep has abandoned me.”

“Yet you said you weren’t seriously ill?”

“I am often sleepless—even when healthy.”

“If I had the power, I’d wrap you in deep, quiet slumber—without dreams.”

“Blank oblivion? I don’t desire that.”

“With dreams of all you wish.”

“Monstrous fantasies! Then sleep would be delirium, waking—death.”

“Your wishes aren’t so unreal. You’re no dreamer.”

“Miss Keeldar, I suppose you think so. But my character isn’t as clear to you as a new novel.”

“That’s possible. But this sleep—I’d like to coax it to your pillow. Let me read aloud. I can spare half an hour.”

“Thank you. But I won’t keep you.”

“I’ll read softly.”

“It won’t help. I’m too feverish to bear a soft, soothing voice near me. Please leave.”

“All right. I’ll go.”

“And no good-night?”

“Yes, sir. Mr. Moore, good-night.” (Exit Shirley.)

“Henry, go to bed. It’s time.”

“Sir, I’d gladly watch all night.”

“Not necessary. I’m improving. Go.”

“Give me your blessing, sir.”

“God bless you, my best pupil.”

“You never call me your dearest.”

“No. And never shall.”

* * * * *

Perhaps Miss Keeldar resented the tutor’s refusal of her courtesy. She never repeated it. Though her footsteps often passed his door, she never paused. Her soft voice never disturbed the quiet again. Soon, the sickroom was no longer needed—Moore’s strong constitution overcame the illness. In a few days, he returned to duty.

Yet “auld lang syne” still held sway. Sometimes, he crossed the distance she kept between them, laying aside her reserve with quiet authority.

One afternoon, the Sympsons had gone out. Shirley, pleased to escape their company, stayed behind, saying she had business—letter-writing. Once finished, she walked to the garden.

It was a tranquil autumn day. The golden light of Indian summer bathed the fields. Woods stood ripe, leaves still clinging. Purple heather bloomed faintly on hills. The beck flowed silently to the Hollow. No wind stirred. The gardens bore signs of gentle decay—yellow leaves drifted onto swept paths. Flowers and fruit were gone, save a few apples. One blossom, pale and delicate, clung to a branch.

Shirley gathered these last blooms, threading them into a scentless bouquet. As she walked, Henry called from the house.

“Shirley, Mr. Moore wants to see you in the schoolroom. He’d like you to read a bit of French—if you’re free.”

He delivered the message plainly, as if it were ordinary.

“Did Mr. Moore tell you to say that?”

“Of course. Why not? Come now. Let’s be as we were at Sympson Grove. We used to enjoy school hours.”

Shirley may have thought things had changed. But she said nothing, only paused, then followed Henry quietly.
