Gerard had undertaken a peculiar penance: to welcome Lodovico with kindness, though he harbored inward thoughts akin to those directed toward a slippery serpent. Yet, in the economy of self-denial, there is rarely true suffering; what begins as a rigorous trial often dissolves into mere habit. Initially, the assassin's gaze upon the single life he had spared was a grotesque mixture of arrogance and pathos, bordering on the ridiculous. However, once a virtuous opening is made within a hardened heart, a river of grace tends to flow through it. Having discovered the sweetness of preservation, Lodovico gradually grew repulsed by the act of killing. From this aversion sprang remorse, and from remorse, a shadow resembling repentance. Here, Teresa played her part, issuing threats—not for the first time—to depart should he refuse to live honestly. Supported by the benevolent Fathers of the convent, Lodovico and Teresa were dispatched by sea to Leghorn, where Teresa possessed acquaintances. There, the former murderer settled into the mundane trade of a porter.

At first, the labor seemed intolerably drab to him, and he voiced his discontent. "It is a miserable, dull existence," he would complain. Yet, reflection suggests that this quiet, grinding toil served him far better than the fleeting, bloody thrill of being torn apart by Princess Claelia's minions. His exile preserved him from that fate, leaving the Princess frustrated and bereft of vengeance. She descended into a profound, brooding melancholy, dismissing her former spiritual guide and seeking out a new confessor renowned for his sanctity—a man named Fra Jerome. It proved to be providential timing, for she could scarcely have landed in kinder hands.

Fra Jerome listened to her harrowing tale with grim composure before turning upon her, shaking the illusions from her mind with a roughness reserved for a scullery maid. To give credit to this stern monk, he feared neither the wrath of kings nor the fury of the seas when standing in the path of divine duty. In a few moments, delivered with the voice of thunder and lightning, he laid bare her guilt, declaring her the criminal among criminals.

"You are the Devil," he proclaimed, "who, through your wealth, tempted a man to slaughter his brother. Blinded now by selfish love, instead of condemning and punishing yourself, you thirst for the blood of others, yet they are not half so guilty as you!"

Initially, she resisted, protesting that she was unaccustomed to such severe rebuke from her confessors. But Jerome overpowered her arguments. He invoked the Church's curses, both temporal and eternal, tearing away the scales from her eyes until she sank to the floor, groveling in terror and remorse before the towering figure of this Boanerges-like friar.

"Oh, holy Father," she cried, "have pity on this poor, weak woman and aid me to save my soul. I was lost in darkness, lacking guidance like yours, good Father. I awake now as from a nightmare."

"Doff thy jewels," commanded Fra Jerome sternly.

"I shall," she replied quickly.

"Strip off thy silks and velvets. Go instantly to Loretto clad in garments humbler than those worn by thy lowest servant."

"I will," the Princess whispered, her strength failing.

"No shoes, but a single bare sandal."

"No, Father!"

"Wash the feet of every pilgrim, both arriving and departing. Confess thy sins to any holy friar who hears thee, and await his judgment."

"Oh, holy Father, allow me to keep my mask!"

"Hmph!"

"Oh, mercy! Consider! My face is known throughout Italy."

"Aye. Beauty is a curse to many of your kind. Very well, thou mayst veil thy eyes, but no further."

With this concession, she lunged forward to seize his hand, intending to kiss it. He snatched it away with a rude, abrupt motion.

"What would you do?" he asked sharply. "That hand touched the Eucharist but an hour ago. Is it fit for one of thy condition to lay it upon such holiness?"

"Oh, no. But pray, do not depart without blessing your penitent child."

"There is ample time to seek that blessing when thou returnest from Loretto."

Thus, that miraculous event by the banks of the Tiber left an indelible mark upon all involved, much like the legends surrounding ancient prodigies. The assassin, softened by the act of saving the life he was hired to end, abandoned the stiletto for the knot of porters. The Princess journeyed barefoot to Loretto, weeping her crimes and washing the feet of men born to humble stations. As for Gerard, having been brought from the Tiber into the convent as a suicide, he now passed within those walls for a young saint.

Under the watchful gaze of loving, experienced eyes, he underwent a probation shorter than usual before being admitted to Holy Orders. Soon thereafter, he took the monastic vows, becoming a friar of St. Dominic. Dying to the world, the monk severed ties with his past, discarding even the name by which he had lived, thereby breaking the final chain linking him to earthly affections.

Here ended Gerard, and Brother Clement began.
