"By the soft green light in the woody glade,
On the banks of moss where thy childhood played..."

The journey home was quiet, a gentle drift from the bustling hubbub of Harley Street back to the hushed solitude of Helstone. Margaret sat beside her father, wrapped in the comfortable silence of the carriage, though her mind churned with the whirlwind of the last forty-eight hours. They had traveled to assist at a wedding, a joyous occasion for the groom, yet for Margaret's family, it was merely a transit point. Her mother, Mrs. Hale, had stayed behind at home, a decision shrouded in "half-reasons" that baffled everyone save Mr. Hale himself. He knew the truth with painful clarity: he lacked the funds to purchase a fresh ensemble of silks and bonnets for his wife, and thus, the grey satin gown she wore to the wedding was a garment of compromise, caught between the fading of fashion and the demands of propriety. It was a grievance, however trivial, that Mrs. Shaw, her wealthy sister-in-law, could not fathom. To Mrs. Shaw, the tragedy was not financial; it was the sorrow of a disparity in ages, the lament of a woman who marries a man eight years her senior, possessing a temper as sweet as his blue-black hair. To the Shaw girl, marriage was a romantic triumph; to Mrs. Hale, it was a ledger of unfulfilled desires for finer fabrics and domestic splendor.

Margaret, however, did not dwell on the material lack. She viewed her mother's absence as a blessing. Returning to the Vicarage meant avoiding the chaotic theater of the wedding preparations, where she had played the frantic Figaro, juggling requests and farewells. As the train rattled onward, the physical ache of exhaustion gave way to a deeper, sadder emotion. The hurried goodbyes of the last few days, the faces of those she had known so intimately, pressed upon her with a weight of loss. The times were gone, irrevocably severed, and the grief of what was lost settled heavily in her chest. Yet, as the carriage slowed, her gaze fell upon her father, asleep.

Frederick Hale leaned back, his blue-black hair now streaked with a thin, premature grey that lay starkly over his brow. The bones of his face, usually softened by youth, were laid bare; the lines around his mouth and eyes spoke not of serenity, but of a weary, habitual depression. It was a repose earned through struggle, not born of contentment. Margaret watched him, her heart tightening. *Poor Frederick,* she thought, a sigh escaping her lips. *If he had become a clergyman, if he had not sailed away into the navy!* A shadow hung over his return, a "terrible affair" that Aunt Shaw had alluded to vaguely, a mystery that had cost them all so dearly. He looked so fragile, so burdened, and the thought of returning home, of being close enough to comfort both his father and his mother, filled her with a fierce, protective resolve.

When the carriage halted, Margaret greeted him with a bright, practiced smile, masking the fatigue that gnawed at her. He smiled back, faintly, as if the effort required more energy than he possessed. His lips parted slightly, a nervous habit that unsettled his features, giving him an air of indecision. Yet, beneath the worry, there lingered the same large, soft eyes that belonged to his daughter—eyes that moved slowly, grandly, veiled by pale lids. Margaret was his reflection, not in her beauty, which some whispered was lacking compared to her parents' handsomeness, but in her spirit. Her mouth, wide and curved in a soft line of rich red lips, framed a face that held a dignity rarely seen in one so young. When she spoke to her father, however, the reserve melted away, replaced by a brightness akin to morning, dotted with dimples and glances that burned with childish gladness and boundless hope.

They arrived in Helstone in the latter part of July. The air was thick, brooding, and the forest trees stood in a dark, dusky green, while the ferns below caught the slanting rays of the sun. For Margaret, this landscape was paradise. She walked with her father, crushing the ferns with a cruel, joyful glee, inhaling their unique fragrance. Out on the commons, amidst the warm, scented light, she saw a world teeming with wild, free creatures. Here, in the green shade, she found her true self. She was not the prim, well-mannered girl of Harley Street; she was a friend to the villagers, learning their dialect, nursing their babes, and carrying dainties to the sick. She dreamed of teaching at the school, treating education as an appointed task, though the temptation to wander into nearby cottages to visit individual friends often drew her away.

Yet, the idyll of the outdoors masked a discord within the walls of the Vicarage. Her mother, usually so kind, was plagued by a restless discontent. She fretted that the Bishop neglected his duties by failing to grant Mr. Hale a better living, and she subtly reproached her husband for his inability to resign his current parish to seek a larger charge elsewhere. Every time Mrs. Hale urged him to pursue preferment, Frederick shrank further, overwhelmed by a sense of duty and bewilderment that only deepened with time. Margaret, witnessing this friction, strove to reconcile her mother to the humble surroundings, trying to prove that the trees did not harm her health, tempting her onto the upland commons. For a time, the fresh air helped, but as autumn deepened and the weather turned fickle, Mrs. Hale's complaints resurfaced with renewed vigor. She railed against the isolation, wishing they lived closer to the Stansfields or the Gormons.

"Gormons!" Margaret exclaimed, wrinkling her nose. "Those shopkeepers who made their fortune in Southampton? Oh, I'm glad we don't visit them! I despise 'shoppy' people. We are far better off knowing only cottagers and laborers, folks without pretense."

"You must not be so fastidious, Margaret," her mother chided, secretly recalling a handsome young Mr. Gorman she had once glimpsed at a party.

"No!" Margaret insisted, her tone ringing with youthful conviction. "My tastes are comprehensive. I like those who till the soil, soldiers, sailors, and the learned professions. I certainly do not care to admire butchers, bakers, or candlestick-makers."

"But the Gormons were coach-builders, respectable tradesmen."

"Coach-building is a trade nonetheless, and a far more useless one than baking bread," Margaret retorted, her mind drifting to the endless drives in Aunt Shaw's carriage, a life she had longed to escape. "How I used to tire of it! How I longed to walk!"

And walk she did, whenever the heavens permitted. She danced across the heaths, the soft violence of the west wind bearing her forward like a fallen leaf, light and easy. But the evenings offered little solace. After tea, the silence descended. Mr. Hale retreated to his small library, leaving his wife and daughter alone. Books, once a treasure trove for young Margaret, offered scant amusement now. Only the well-bound classics remained, weeded from her father's shelves to fill the drawing-room racks. Thomson's *Seasons*, Hayley's *Cowper*, Middleton's *Cicero*—light, perhaps, but devoid of the intellectual fire that once animated her father's nights.

These quiet hours were spent listening to her mother recount tales of London life, stories of ease and comfort that served only to highlight the narrow means of Helstone. On such evenings, Margaret would fall silent, drawn to the rhythmic *drip-drip* of rain on the leaded windows. Twice, the urge rose within her to ask the question that haunted her: Where was Frederick? What was he doing? Why had they heard nothing? But then, the memory of her mother's delicate health and her profound dislike for Helstone—which stemmed directly from the trauma of Frederick's departure—would make her pause. The full account of his involvement in the Mutiny remained buried, a secret doom destined for sad oblivion.

Instead, she turned to her father, hoping he might offer answers. But the news was dry, skeletal. In a letter prior to their departure, he had written that Frederick was still at Rio, in good health, sending his love. It was a fact, but it lacked the living intelligence Margaret craved. Frederick was spoken of only as "Poor Frederick." His room, untouched since his departure, was meticulously dusted and arranged by Dixon, the maid. Dixon, who had once served the Beresford girls, considered Mr. Hale the blight that had ruined her young mistress's prospects. Loyal to a fault, Dixon treated Frederick's empty chamber as if he might return tonight, arranging his linens with a tenderness that betrayed her devotion to her fallen employer.

Margaret sensed a shift in the atmosphere, a tension that her mother failed to perceive. Her father's spirits, usually tender and responsive to the smallest tidings of others, had grown distant. He seemed preoccupied by a subject that no daily action could relieve. He stopped venturing into the garden, preferring to stand dreamily by the study window, watching the lane. He waited for the postman, his head bowed in a half-respectful, half-confidential nod as the carrier passed, disappearing into the room with the visible weight of a heavy heart and an occupied mind.
