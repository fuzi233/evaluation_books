To the man who listens to that inner voice warning him that twilight and peril are encroaching upon his very soul, fear tends to manifest as an absolute, monolithic force—a wall against which his heart must brace itself instantly, lest the entire architecture of his nature crumble. Fortunately, Oleron rarely needed to search far for defenses. Against the creeping powers of darkness, he constructed formidable ramparts using the mundane, the habitual, and the conventional: the small, immediate rituals of daily life. He was even willing to categorize not just terror, but joy itself, as absolute entities to be guarded. The final betrayal he feared was the dissolution of these boundaries—not merely for himself, but for the collective safety of every soul around him.

Yet, in his own person, Oleron began to commit this treason. He started by granting the inexplicable and the horrific an increasing familiarity. It happened insensibly, unconsciously, marked only by a growing disdain for the rules Elsie Bengough had once imposed. Two months prior, the label "haunted house" applied to his beloved home would have frozen his blood; now, his sensitivity dulled, he could casually ask, "Haunted by what?" remaining blissfully unaware that horror, once proven relative, loses its true sting. He was erasing the landmarks. Mist and confusion wrapped around him, yet he felt only a ravenous curiosity. He needed *to know*. He was resolved to find the truth, and with cunning desperation, he searched for a method.

He need not have searched. The solution was deceptively simple. Just as in his youth, during those rare bursts of creative flow where words seemed to materialize fully formed, so now his questions appeared to answer themselves the moment they were spoken. There was a thrilling exhilaration in this effortless clarity, a joy in his own intellect unmatched since the days when writing was a daily delight. It felt as though the path ahead was being dictated to him. Naturally, the first step was to define the problem mathematically. Given that he lacked privacy, given that the old house had captured his spirit, and given that this unknown co-tenant shared a common denominator with him, what remained? Clearly, the nature of the other variable—the "other numerator"—must be identified. How? Usually complex, to Oleron it was now crystal clear. The key lay in his unfinished novels—or rather, in both *Romillys*, the original and the proposed replacement.

A short while ago, Oleron would have deemed such a theory madness; now, he accepted the dizzying hypothesis without a tremor. He opened the pages of the first *Romilly* and the progress accelerated with violent speed. He recalled vividly finding the character insufficient on the very first morning he worked in the new house. Memories of his aversion flooded back: the night he nearly threw the manuscript into the fire, the dawn when he began plotting the new *Romilly*. It was then that Mrs. Barrett, catching him humming a tune suggested by a dripping tap, informed him he was singing an air he had never heard before: "The Beckoning Fair One."

"The Beckoning Fair One!" he whispered mentally. The first *Romilly* had been definitively abandoned, only for the second to instantly seize him, clamoring for birth in his brain. Looking back, he fancied he sensed a passion, even hatred, in that usurpation. More than once, a stray thought toward his discarded creation seemed to offend the new occupant. Astonishingly, Oleron found the idea credible—that a malignancy almost homicidal in its intensity could extend from fiction to its mortal prototype. Despite his hardening resolve to treat the horrible as a tangible object to be handled, a gasp escaped him. The intrusion of the first *Romilly*'s real-life counterpart shattered his abstract inquiry; the mere thought of Elsie was fatal to logic. Furthermore, he could not recall Barrett's letter or the subsequent confrontation without a surge of heat rising in his cheeks and a tightening of his brow. He had argued with the man immediately.

Striding across the square the next morning, Oleron had confronted Barrett on his own doorstep. Returning minutes later, convinced he had only worsened the situation, he found the neighbor a study in vagueness. Barrett offered nothing but a muttering jumble of phrases: "Certain things... Mrs. Barrett... respectable house... if the cap fits... proceedings that shall be nameless." "Not that I make any charge—" Barrett had concluded. "Charge!" Oleron had shouted. "I 'ave my idears of things, as I don't doubt you 'ave yours—" "Ideas—mine!" Oleron had roared, instantly lowering his voice as neighbors appeared at the windows. "Listen here, my man; you've an unwholesome mind, which you probably can't control, but a tongue you can—and shall! If there is a whisper of this repeated..." "I'll not be talked to on my own doorstep like this by anybody," Barrett had blustered. "You shall, and I'm doing it..." "Don't you forget there's a Gawd above all, Who 'as said..." "You're a low scandalmonger!" And so the exchange deteriorated rapidly.

Oleron retreated to his house in a rage. From then on, watching from his window, he spotted Barrett's face at irregular intervals, lifting blinds or peering through curtains, seemingly hunting for evidence. The incident necessitated changes in Oleron's domestic routine. Barrett's tongue had already done its damage; the residents of the square eyed him askance. Until he could secure other assistance, Oleron decided to shop for provisions elsewhere, avoiding the local stores. Otherwise, housekeeping was familiar territory, and he intended to revert to his old bachelor ways. Moreover, he was immersed in abstruse investigations requiring solitude.

One midday, weary and unwell, grateful for the chance to stay indoors, Oleron gazed out his window and saw Elsie Bengough crossing the square toward his house. The weather had turned foul; a raw, gusty wind battered her, forcing her wide skirts to billow around her opulent figure while her veil spun and streamed behind her. Oleron moved with instinctive panic. Snatching his hat, he sprinted downstairs. A dread seized him: she must not enter the house. Running along the alley, he felt his gaze drawn upward to the eaves, unaware that a loose slate might fall.

He intercepted her at the gate, speaking with frantic verbosity. "This is truly terrible, Elsie! Just as I'm urgently summoned away! I fear it cannot be helped, and you must forgive me for acting like an inhospitable beast." He spilled his lies as they formed. When she asked if he was heading to town, he confirmed it eagerly. "Yes—to town. I must see Chambers. You know Chambers, don't you? No? A tall man I once introduced you to... I should have gone yesterday, and—" (he felt this was a brilliant improvisation)—"he leaves for Brighton today. I received a letter this morning." Taking her arm, he guided her up the square. She gently reminded him that the route to town lay the opposite way. "Of course—how foolish of me!" he laughed loudly. "I'm so accustomed to walking the other way with you... naturally, that's the direction of the bus. Will you join me? I am terribly sorry this has happened..."

Seeing her composure, Oleron slowed his reckless speech, filling the silences cautiously. By the time they reached the stop, no observer would have guessed that the pale man without an overcoat and the girl in ample skirts were both praying silently that they had saved each other from an unimaginable fate. They climbed to the top deck; Oleron protested forgetting his coat and claimed the day felt oppressively hot. They settled onto a front seat.

Now that their encounter was unavoidable, Oleron faced a new dilemma, one he had been circling for some time. Remembering the success of his excuse regarding the urgent trip to town, he untangled his predicament with another fabrication. "I'm considering leaving for a bit, Elsie," he said. "Oh?" she replied simply. "Somewhere for a change. I need a break. I think I'll leave tomorrow, or the day after. Yes, tomorrow." "Yes," she murmured. "I don't know how long I'll be," he continued. "I'll let you know when I return." "Yes, let me know," she said, her tone unnervingly even.

"You don't ask where I'm going," he ventured, trying to rally her. She stared straight ahead, past the driver. "I know," she stated flatly. Startled, he asked, "How do you know?" "You aren't going anywhere," she replied. Silence stretched between them. After a minute, she continued in the same controlled voice. "You aren't going anywhere.
