Desire trims the sails, but it is circumstance alone that furnishes the wind to fill them. Outside, Grandcourt rode his sleek black yacht *Yarico*, the groom trailing behind on the *Criterion*, cutting a path of pleasure from Diplow to Offendene. Inside, the air was stiller, charged with the quiet tension of a decision hanging in the balance. Gwendolen sat before the mirror, her mother gathering the cascading mass of her light-brown hair, coiling it with practiced care.

"Only gather it up easily, Mamma," Gwendolen instructed softly.

When the hair was secured, Mrs. Davilow reached for a pair of earrings. "Let me bring you some, Gwen?" she asked, her voice brightening as she caught her daughter's reflection. It was impossible to miss the transformation; the eyes burned with a renewed fire, a shadow seemingly lifted from the face to reveal lines smoothed back into placid youthfulness. The mother drew a hopeful inference, her tone buoyant. "Do you want your earrings?"

"No, Mamma," Gwendolen replied, offering one of her characteristic, enigmatic smiles before rising to shed her dressing gown. "I shall wear none. I'll put on my black silk. Black is the only attire for refusing an offer."

"Suppose the offer isn't made after all?" Mrs. Davilow suggested, a sly note threading her words.

"Then that will simply be because I refused it beforehand," Gwendolen countered, her chin lifting in a proud, defiant toss. When she descended the stairs in her long black robes, she possessed that firm poise and elastic vitality that had recently evaporated from her, like moisture from a parched plant. Her mother watched her pass, thinking, *She is quite herself again. It must be the joy of his coming. Can her mind truly be resolved against him?*

If those words had been spoken aloud, Gwendolen would have bristled with anger, perhaps all the more sharply because for the last twenty hours, with only a fleeting slumber, she had been trapped in a perpetual oscillation of images and arguments regarding her potential union with Grandcourt. The conclusion she had initially drawn had lost its grip; the counterbalancing tides of doubt and desire had washed away any solid ground, leaving her adrift. She intended to refuse him, yes, from the moment the letter arrived, yet this resolve had become hollow, drained of blood, no more vital to her quivering existence than the phrase "God's will be done" spoken by someone eagerly awaiting fate.

She did not mean to accept him. From the first glance at his letter, she had meant to reject him. Still, she could not help but confront the unwelcome truths head-on, stripping them of their awe-inspiring power. Her imagination, fueled by necessity, filled the gaps in her knowledge with various scenarios, reshaping the facts until they seemed to support her stance. By staring at a dubious object with a constructive mind, one can conjure twenty different forms. The vague hesitations she had felt before the meeting at the Whispering Stones now counted for nothing, submerged beneath a final, overwhelming repulsion. If not for that day at Cardell Chase, she told herself, there would have been no barrier to her marrying Grandcourt. On that day, and in its wake, she had not reasoned or weighed options; she had acted with an impulsive force against which all questioning was merely a whisper against a roaring torrent. This impulse stemmed not only from maidenly pride and jealousy, nor solely from the shock of another woman's calamity thrust violently into her vision, but from a profound dread of wrongdoing. It was a vague, distant fear, removed from the daily textures of her life, yet undeniably potent. Whatever aligned with the code of a lady required no scrutiny from her conscience; but from the dim, shadowed realm of the disgraceful, the wrong, the guilty, she shrank with a mixture of pride and terror. Apart from shame, her instinct dictated that any deliberate injury inflicted upon another belonged squarely within the category of guilt.

Yet, did she truly grasp the extent of the case concerning Mrs. Glasher and her children? She had offered a sort of promise, saying, "I will not interfere with your wishes." But would another woman marrying Grandcourt actually obstruct those wishes, or cause genuine harm to the mother and son? Could it not be preferable, even beneficial, for Grandcourt to marry? After all, what could not a woman achieve once wedded, provided she knew how to assert herself? Here, too, lay the domain of constructive imagination. Gwendolen possessed as precise a concept of marriage—the intricate web of mutual influences, demands, and duties between man and woman—as she did of magnetic currents or storm laws.

"Mamma managed badly," was her summation of her mother's experience; she vowed to handle matters quite differently. The trials of matrimony were the final topic Mrs. Davilow wished to explore with this daughter.

*I wonder what Mamma and Uncle would say if they knew about Mrs. Glasher!* Gwendolen debated silently. Not that she could envision confessing it, even if silence hadn't bound her lips tighter. *I wonder what anyone would say; or what they would say to Mr. Grandcourt marrying someone else and fathering other children!* To consider the judgment of "anyone" liberated her from the burden of discerning where clarity ended and obscurity began, since feeling had ceased to be the arbiter. She merely collected her memories, which confirmed that "anyone" viewed illegitimate children as deserving of greater social ostracization and deprivation than illegitimate fathers. The verdict of the public seemed clear: she had no compelling reason to champion Mrs. Glasher and her offspring.

However, there was another dimension to their presence that troubled her. Public opinion could not extinguish a feeling best described as indignation and loathing—to be expected to unite herself with an exhausted life, laden with backward secrets that must have been acutely felt by the woman herself, yet foreign to her own experience. True, the question of her own love for Grandcourt had occupied her mind scarcely at all. The desirability of marriage had always seemed rooted in sentiments other than love; to be enamored was the province of the man, upon whom the advances rested. Gwendolen had found no fault with Grandcourt's manner of being enamored until she glimpsed his past, a revelation she resented as a deliberate affront. His advances toward *her* had been calculated and deliberate, and now she felt a retrospective disgust for them. Perhaps the lives of other men were similarly constructed—filled with secrets that reduced the ignorant assumptions of the women they sought to marry to a farce, performed with laughter hidden in their sleeves.

These feelings of disgust and indignation had sunk deep. Though subsequent troubling experiences in recent weeks had dulled them from passionate fire to mere memory, it was chiefly their lingering resonance that kept her steadfast in her understanding with herself: she would not accept Grandcourt. She had never intended to form a new determination; she had only been contemplating what might be thought or said. If anything could have swayed her, it would have been the prospect of making life easy for "poor Mamma"; that, she admitted, was a temptation. But no! She was going to refuse him. Meanwhile, the anticipation of his rejection invigorated her; she held the white reins in her hands once more. A new current surged through her frame, reviving her from the beaten-down consciousness left by her interview with Klesmer. She was no longer going to crave validation of her capabilities; she was going to exercise her power.

Was this what caused her heart to palpitate anxiously when she heard the horse's hooves crunching on the gravel? When Miss Merry opened the door to announce that Grandcourt was in the drawing-room, the hours of preparation and the triumph of the situation seemed futile. She might as well have encountered him suddenly amidst her despair. Walking into the drawing-room required her to concentrate every ounce of energy into a self-control that rendered her gravely gracious. She extended her hand to him, answering his inquiry about her health with a voice as low and languid as his own.

A moment later, seated on two wreath-painted chairs, the tableau was striking. Gwendolen sat upright with downcast eyelids; Grandcourt remained about two yards distant, leaning one arm over the back of his chair, his gaze fixed upon her, his hat held loosely in his left hand. Any observer viewing them as a painting would have concluded they were in the throes of romantic suspense. Indeed, the courtship had commenced. She already felt herself being wooed by this silent man seated at an agreeable distance, enveloped in a subtle atmosphere reminiscent of attar of roses, his attention bent wholly upon her. He, too, considered himself the suitor; he was not a man to believe his presence carried no weight, and he was precisely the type to derive the utmost piquancy from a girl he had not yet deemed fully calculable.

"I was disappointed not to find you at Leubronn," he began, his usual broken drawl tinged with a shade of amorous languor. "The place was intolerable without you. A mere kennel of a location. Do you not agree?"

"I cannot judge what it would be without myself," Gwendolen replied, turning her eyes upon him with a recovering sense of mischief. "*With* myself, I like it well enough to have stayed longer, if I could. But I was obliged to return home due to family troubles."
