Before Deronda was summoned to a second audience with his mother, a day slipped by during which she sent only a single missive, stating she was not yet well enough to receive him. Yet on the third morning, a fresh note arrived: "I leave to-day. Come and see me at once."

He was ushered into the same chamber as before, though now it was shrouded in gloom, the windows choked by heavy blinds and drawn curtains. The Princess was absent initially, but she soon appeared, draped in a loose wrap of soft, dusky-orange silk. Black lace floated loosely around her head, framing a face that seemed even more formidable in the somber light; her eyes appeared larger, the lines of her visage more vigorous. One might have imagined her a sorceress, stretching forth a wonderful arm to brew youth-potions for others while scorning to mix them for herself, having drunk her fill of youth already.

She placed her arms instantly around her son's shoulders, kissing him on both cheeks, before settling among her cushions with an air of assured firmness and dignity that stood in stark contrast to the fitfulness of their first meeting. She gestured for Deronda to sit beside her. He complied, asking, "You are quite relieved now, I trust?"

"Yes, I am at ease again. Is there anything more that you would like to ask me?" she replied, speaking with the detached matter-of-factness of a queen rather than a mother.

"Can I find the house in Genoa where you used to live with my grandfather?" Deronda asked.

"No," she answered, making a deprecating gesture with her arm. "It is pulled down—not to be found. But regarding our family, and where my father dwelt at various times—you will discover all that among the papers in the chest, far better than I can recount. My father was a physician. My mother was a Morteira. I used to hear all those things without truly listening. You will find them all. I was born amidst them without my will, and I banished them as soon as I could."

Deronda struggled to mask his pained feeling, saying, "Anything else that I should desire to know from you could only be what it is some satisfaction to your own feeling to tell me."

"I think I have told you everything that could be demanded of me," the Princess said, her expression turning coldly meditative. It seemed she had exhausted her emotional reserves in their previous interview. In truth, she had reasoned to herself, "I have done it all. I have confessed all. I will not go through it again. I will save myself from agitation." And she was enacting that plan.

Yet to Deronda's nature, the moment was cruel; it transformed the filial yearning of his life into a disappointed pilgrimage to a shrine stripped of its sacred symbols. It seemed that all the woman lacking in her was present in him. With a tremor in his voice, he whispered, "Then are we to part and I never be anything to you?"

"It is better so," the Princess said, her voice suddenly softer, mellower. "There could be nothing but hard duty for you, even if it were possible for you to take the place of my son. You would not love me. Don't deny it," she interrupted abruptly, raising her hand. "I know what is the truth. You don't like what I did. You are angry with me. You think I robbed you of something. You are on your grandfather's side, and you will always harbor a condemnation of me in your heart."

Deronda felt himself under a ban of silence. Rising from his seat beside her, he preferred to stand if he had to obey that imperious prohibition against any tenderness. But his mother now looked up at him with a new admiration in her glance.

"You are wrong to be angry with me. You are the better for what I did." After a pause, she added sharply, "And now tell me what you shall do?"

"Do you mean now, immediately," Deronda clarified, "or as to the course of my future life?"

"I mean in the future. What difference will it make to you that I have told you about your birth?"

"A very great difference," Deronda said emphatically. "I can hardly think of anything that would make a greater difference."

"What shall you do then?" the Princess asked, her tone sharpening. "Make yourself just like your grandfather—be what he wished you—turn yourself into a Jew like him?"

"That is impossible. The effect of my education can never be undone. The Christian sympathies in which my mind was reared can never die out of me," Deronda replied, his tone growing increasingly tenacious. "But I consider it my duty—it is the impulse of my feeling—to identify myself, as far as possible, with my hereditary people. And if I can see any work to be done for them to which I can give my soul and hand, I shall choose to do it."

His mother watched him with wondering speculation, examining his face as if believing that close scrutiny could decipher a difficult language. He met her gaze firmly, sustained by a resolute opposition that was the expression of his fullest self. She leaned toward him slightly, speaking with decisive emphasis, "You are in love with a Jewess."

Deronda flushed and protested, "My reasons would be independent of any such fact."

"I know better. I have seen what men are," the Princess stated peremptorily. "Tell me the truth. She is a Jewess who will not accept anyone but a Jew. There *are* a few such," she added with a touch of scorn.

Deronda faced the objection we all know when speaking to those too certain of their own fixed interpretations to be enlightened by anything we may say. Besides this, the immediate point was one on which he felt a repugnance either to deny or affirm. He remained silent, and she presently continued, "You love her as your father loved me, and she draws you after her as I drew him."

Those words struck at Deronda's filial imagination, and some tenderness in his glance was taken by his mother as an assent. She proceeded with rising passion: "But I was leading him the other way. And now your grandfather is getting his revenge."

"Mother," Deronda remonstrated, "don't let us think of it in that way. I will admit that there may come some benefit from the education you chose for me. I prefer cherishing the benefit with gratitude to dwelling with resentment on the injury. I think it would have been right that I should have been brought up with the consciousness that I was a Jew, but it must always have been a good to me to have as wide an instruction and sympathy as possible. And now, you have restored me my inheritance—events have brought a fuller restitution than you could have made—you have been saved from robbing my people of my service and me of my duty: can you not bring your whole soul to consent to this?"

Deronda paused in his pleading; his mother listened intently, as if the cadence of his voice were taking her ear, yet she shook her head slowly. He began again, even more urgently.

"You have told me that you sought what you held the best for me: open your heart to relenting and love toward my grandfather, who sought what he held the best for you."

"Not for me, no," she said, shaking her head with absolute denial and folding her arms tightly. "I tell you, he never thought of his daughter except as an instrument. Because I had wants outside his purpose, I was to be put in a frame and tortured. If that is the right law for the world, I will not say that I love it. If my acts were wrong—if it is God who is exacting from me that I should deliver up what I withheld—who is punishing me because I deceived my father and did not warn him that I should contradict his trust? Well, I have told everything. I have done what I could. And *your* soul consents. That is enough. I have after all been the instrument my father wanted. 'I desire a grandson who shall have a true Jewish heart. Every Jew should rear his family as if he hoped that a Deliverer might spring from it.'"

In uttering these final sentences, the Princess narrowed her eyes, waved her head up and down, and spoke slowly with a new kind of chest-voice, as if quoting unwillingly.

"Were those my grandfather's words?" Deronda asked.

"Yes, yes; and you will find them written. I wanted to thwart him," the Princess said, with a sudden outburst of the passion she had shown in their former interview. Then she added more slowly, "You would have me love what I have hated from the time I was so high"—here she held her left hand a yard from the floor—"That can never be. But what does it matter? His yoke has been on me, whether I loved it or not. You are the grandson he wanted. You speak as men do—as if you felt yourself wise. What does it all mean?"

Her tone was abrupt and scornful. Deronda, in his pained feeling and under the solemn urgency of the moment, had to maintain a clutching remembrance of their relationship, lest his words become cruel.
