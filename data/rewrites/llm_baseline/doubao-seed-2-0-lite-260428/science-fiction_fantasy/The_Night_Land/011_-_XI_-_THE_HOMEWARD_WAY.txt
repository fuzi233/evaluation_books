The air felt almost bright after the suffocating, lonesome gloom of the ancient seabed, and I quickly realized I had emerged onto the dark landscape east of the point where I first stepped down onto the old sea floor during my outward trek to find Naani. Fire-holes dotted the ground all across my field of vision, their warm glows a welcome sight that made my heart light – though I moved with deliberate care toward them, for I knew well every uncanny, predatory thing that drew to their heat in the cold of the Night Land.

I glanced down at Naani, who looked back up at me and drifted closer, soft and sweet, the most wondrous thing I had ever seen. But her face was pale, and she moved with the heavy drag of utter exhaustion, and I berated myself for overpushing her: my own frame had been hardened by years of wandering the Pyramid’s outer dark, as if forged from iron, while she was a tender, sheltered maid of the Lesser Redoubt. She would not let me chide myself long, standing close to gaze up at me with luminous eyes, and I wrapped my arms around her to kiss her, before lifting my head to scan the horizon and plot our path home.

Far ahead, I spotted the broad blue radiance I had first glimpsed from the mouth of the Upward Gorge – the faint, constant glow I had steered by all the way across the pitch black seabed, the only fixed marker that had kept me from wandering in blind, endless circles through the dark. After a long moment of careful orientation, I worked out that the Gorge must lie to my left, a fair distance off: far enough that I could not see it, but close enough that I could make out the smoldering red glare of the giants’ great fire-pit glowing over the horizon in that direction. I knew we must steer well clear of both that pit and the unsettling blue Shine burning across the far western stretch of the land, which every instinct in me warned was a place of unnameable evil.

I pointed left over the dark, empty expanse and told Naani the Gorge lay somewhere out that way, hidden from sight, located only by the landmarks I had memorized on my journey outward. She had been studying the landscape quietly as I oriented myself, and asked after my planned route. I told her we would go as straight as we could, staying far from both the Shine and the giants’ fire pit. She gestured to the direct path I had named, pointing out distant fire-holes rimmed with a sickly, bilious green glow. That marked a vast band of fatally poisonous gas, she told me – the people of the Lesser Redoubt had mapped it centuries prior with their instruments, and no living thing could breathe it in and survive. The gas zone stretched all the way to the northwest, where the blue Shine burned, and she warned me the Fixed Giants lurked hidden in that light, only visible when the roiling glowing mists parted to reveal their forms. I recognized them immediately as kin to the Great Watchers that ringed the Mighty Pyramid, and thought of the monstrous, colossal face I had glimpsed in the Shine’s smoke the day I first stepped out of the Gorge: a force of ancient, unthinkable cruelty.

For a long minute I was stumped how to reach the Gorge without straying too close to the Shine, until I remembered my own outward path: I had wandered past the far edge of the gas band by pure chance, never even knowing it existed. Naani proposed the perfect plan: we would drop back down onto the ancient seabed, trek several hours southwest along the coast far below the poison gas’s reach, then climb back up onto the land once we had cleared the zone, to make straight for the Gorge’s mouth while staying safely distant from the giants’ fire pit. The idea was so sound it made me smile, and a soft, warm feeling swelled in my chest to watch her think and plan aloud, every part of her sharp, curious mind open to me with no pretense.

It had been nearly 17 hours since either of us last slept, and Naani was dead on her feet, so we made for a moderately distant fire-hole a little north of our position, to thaw our frozen bones before we rested. It took a full hour to reach it, far larger than it had looked from far off: a great rocky hollow holding a gulf of roaring red flame that cast a wide warm glow across the dark land. I signaled Naani to stay dead silent, drew my Diskos from my hip, and crawled on hands and knees to the lip of the hollow, gesturing for her to follow. We peered down at the fire, and saw no immediate large monsters lingering nearby – but I did not let my caution slip, for I knew too well what horrors lurked close to heat sources.

Naani was shivering so hard her teeth chattered, begging to climb down to the fire just long enough to chase the bitter chill out of her bones. My head told me it was unwise, but my heart could not resist: the constant distant dangers of the Night Land felt small and unreal next to the sharp, biting cold that was turning her lips blue. I searched the hollow thoroughly first, and found no large threats, only three small snakes and two scorpion-like creatures that hid motionless in rock crevices, neither advancing nor retreating. But I knew those venomous, heat-loving things would creep toward us while we slept, so I said nothing of them to Naani, letting her enjoy the warmth until she spotted a snake slither past the rocks herself, and begged me to find a secure spot far from any crawling things.

We found a tiny, dry cave set 30 feet up the face of a tall rock spire rising from the middle of the hollow, no crannies or hiding nooks for small creatures to lurk in. We climbed inside, the fire’s soft red glow spilling over us, a tiny haven cut off from the vast, menacing dark of the Night Land. My heart stayed tense, listening for every faint distant sound, but Naani trusted me utterly, no flicker of fear in her that I would not keep her safe – a trust so sweet it almost eased the constant, heavy dread I carried for our journey home. We curled together wrapped in our shared cloak, and slept seven full hours, 24 full hours from our last rest, jolting awake at the exact same instant.

Screams ripped through the night, raw, human, thick with terror. My heart clenched: it was a band of survivors from the Lesser Redoubt, running for their lives. I burned to leap to their aid, but I could not leave Naani unguarded. Thunderous hoarse roars echoed across the dark, voices like rolling boulders, and the ground shook under the heavy thud of massive, running feet. Naani trembled hard in my arms, for she had heard those exact sounds every night during her month lost and wandering in the Night Land. A young woman’s scream cut through the dark, abruptly snuffed out, followed by more cries, the chase drawing closer. A ragged, half-naked band of men and women ran past the mouth of the hollow, torn and bleeding from rocks and thorns, wild with exhaustion, all hope gone from their faces. Four giants lumbered past after them: three shaggy, brutish, dark-skinned, the fourth a livid, blotched white thing, a monster stitched together from unholy life. The chase faded off into the dark, and a final distant wail marked the end of the last of the fleeing survivors.

A hot, bitter anger burned in my chest – I thought they should have turned to fight, even if every last one of them died, for they were doomed anyway, and might have at least taken one giant down with them. But I knew the truth: the people of the Lesser Redoubt had been starved of the life-giving Earth Current for a hundred thousand generations, robbed of some of that sharp, feral courage that lived in those raised near the Mighty Pyramid. Then I spotted a young naked woman stumble over the lip of the hollow, cowering under a low rock ledge below our spire. A squat, bull-broad shaggy giant slipped silently down after her, moving as quiet and deadly as a stalking cat.

I did not hesitate. I leaped the full 20 feet from the cave mouth down to the hollow floor, my Diskos screaming as it spun in my hand. I could not reach her in time: the brute ripped her apart before my eyes, her final cry ringing out across the fire-glow. The enraged monster lunged at me, leaping silent, and I swung the Diskos hard, slicing half its monstrous arms clean off, before it hurled me across the hollow, my armour clanging loud against the rocks. I was unhurt, back on my feet in an instant, and swung the whirling blade again, shearing straight through the giant’s head and shoulder, dropping it dead.

I carried the dead girl’s torn body and laid it gently in the fire, so no scavenger would ever feed on her. I turned back to the cave, and Naani was running toward me, the belt knife I had given her for defense held tight in her hand, ready to fight at my side. She was pale, but unshaking, staring at the dead monster’s hulking bulk in silence. The look in her eyes was so full of soft respect and unspoken love that it humbled me, a memory I would carry for every remaining day of my life. Only once I pulled her close did the trembling set in, the full horror of what we had both just seen crashing down on her.

We climbed back up to the cave, ate two nutrient tablets each and drank our fill of water, and waited a full hour listening intently for any sign of returning giants, before climbing back down, gathering our gear, and slipping out of the hollow. We moved slow, our ears pricked for every faint sound, taking two full hours to reach the edge of the ancient seabed. No threat drew near, and the vast, silent dark of the Night Land stretched unbroken all around us, as we set foot once more on the path of our homeward journey.
