Buck did not read the newspapers, or he would have known that a storm was brewing—not just for himself, but for every strong, thick-furred dog from Puget Sound to San Diego. Men, searching in the frozen dark of the Arctic, had uncovered gold. News spread fast, and steamships and railroads began ferrying thousands into the Northland. These men needed dogs—big, powerful dogs with muscles built for labor and coats thick enough to survive the cold.

But Buck lived far from such troubles. He lived at Judge Miller’s estate in the sun-drenched Santa Clara Valley, where life moved slowly and gently beneath blue skies. The house stood back from the road, half hidden by trees, with wide verandas and shaded walkways winding through lawns and poplar groves. Behind it sprawled stables, vine-covered cottages, orchards, pastures, and berry patches. A cement tank held cool water for the Judge’s boys, and over all this land, Buck ruled.

He was no ordinary dog. Born here, raised here, he had lived four years as a prince among animals. There were other dogs—terriers who yapped from windows, pugs and hairless Mexican dogs who stayed indoors—but they were mere shadows in his world. Buck was neither kennel dog nor house pet. He swam in the tank, hunted with the Judge’s sons, walked beside the daughters on twilight strolls, and slept before the library fire on winter nights. He carried the grandsons on his back, rolled them in grass, guarded their steps down to the stable fountain, and even beyond. Among the terriers, he moved like a king; Toots and Ysabel, the odd little pets, barely registered in his awareness. He was lord of the land—over every creature, human included.

His father, Elmo, had been a great St. Bernard, the Judge’s constant companion. Buck, though smaller—just 140 pounds—carried himself with equal dignity. His mother, Shep, a Scotch shepherd, had given him strength and endurance. But more than size or breed, it was his life that shaped him: good food, open spaces, hunting, swimming. He had never become soft. His muscles were hard, his spirit proud. He knew no fear—only loyalty, pride, and a quiet confidence born of being respected.

And so, in the fall of 1897, when the Klondike gold rush pulled men from around the world into the frozen north, Buck remained blissfully unaware. He didn’t know that Manuel, one of the gardeners’ helpers, had a secret weakness: gambling. He played Chinese lottery, believing in systems that always failed. And now, with money tight and debts growing, Manuel saw a chance.

The Judge was away at a meeting. The boys were busy starting an athletic club. That night, no one noticed when Manuel led Buck through the orchard—not for a walk, but for something darker. No one saw them arrive at College Park station, except one man. They spoke briefly. Money changed hands.

“You might wrap up the goods before you deliver ’em,” the stranger said gruffly.

Manuel tied a thick rope around Buck’s neck under his collar. “Twist it, and you’ll choke him plentee,” he warned.

Buck accepted it calmly. It was unusual, yes, but he trusted men—he believed they knew better than he did. But when the stranger took the ends of the rope, Buck growled. Not out of fear, but defiance. He thought his growl was command.

It wasn’t.

The rope tightened. Breath left his body. In rage, he lunged—only to be grabbed by the throat, thrown over, and pinned. The rope bit deeper. He fought wildly, tongue lolling, chest heaving. Never had he been treated so cruelly. Never had he felt such fury.

Then darkness.

When he came to, he was moving—jolted along in a train car. The shriek of a whistle told him where he was. He remembered riding with the Judge. But this was different. This was stolen time. He opened his eyes—and saw only betrayal.

The man rushed for his throat. Buck snapped—teeth clamped down on the hand. The man screamed, but Buck didn’t let go until unconsciousness swallowed him again.

“Yep, has fits,” the man muttered, hiding his bleeding hand from the baggageman. “I’m takin’ ’m up for the boss to ’Frisco. A dog doctor there thinks he can cure him.”

That night, the man sat in a dingy shed behind a saloon on the San Francisco docks. “All I get is fifty,” he grumbled. “And I wouldn’t do it over for a thousand.”

His hand was wrapped in bloodstained cloth. His pants ripped from knee to ankle.

“How much did the other guy get?” the saloon-keeper asked.

“A hundred. Wouldn’t take a sou less.”

“That makes a hundred and fifty. And he’s worth it—or I’m a fool.”

The kidnapper unwrapped his hand. “If I don’t get hydrophoby…”

“It’ll be because you were born to hang,” laughed the saloon-keeper. “Here, lend me a hand before you pull your freight.”

Buck woke again—dazed, throat burning, tongue raw. He tried to rise, but was knocked down repeatedly. Finally, they removed his heavy brass collar. Then the rope. Then he was shoved into a cage-like crate.

There he lay, trapped and furious. What was happening? Why were these strangers holding him? He didn’t know, but a deep dread settled over him. Each time the door rattled, he hoped for the Judge, or the boys. But only the saloon-keeper appeared—ugly face, flickering candlelight. Each time, the joy in Buck’s heart turned to snarling hatred.

They left him alone. But in the morning, four rough-looking men lifted the crate. They laughed at him, poked sticks through the bars. He bit them—until he realized that was what they wanted. Then he lay still, sullen, as they carried him into a wagon.

From there, he passed through many hands: clerks, wagons, ferries, trains. For two days and nights, he rode in an express car, bound for Seattle. He refused food. He refused water. The messengers teased him—growling, barking, crowing, mocking. It was childish. But to Buck, it was an insult. His pride burned hotter than hunger.

Thirst gnawed at him. His throat swelled. His eyes turned red. He became a monster—feral, wild, unrecognizable. Even the Judge wouldn’t have known him. But the express men breathed easier when they dropped him off in Seattle.

Four men carried the crate into a small yard walled high. A stout man in a red sweater stepped forward, signed the book, and smiled grimly. He brought a hatchet and a club.

“You ain’t going to take him out now?” the driver asked.

“Sure,” the man replied, driving the hatchet into the wood.

The four men scattered to the wall, watching.

Buck raged inside—tearing at the splintering wood, snapping at every blow from outside. When the opening was wide enough, the man dropped the hatchet and gripped the club.

“Now, you red-eyed devil,” he said.

Buck launched himself—140 pounds of fury, teeth bared, eyes blazing. Midair, the club struck. He crashed to the ground, jaw snapping shut painfully. He rose again. Again, the club broke his charge. Again and again, he charged. Again and again, he was crushed.

After one brutal blow, he staggered, blood flowing from nose and mouth. Still, he roared—a lion’s roar—and charged once more.

This time, the man caught him by the jaw, twisted downward, and swung him in a full circle before slamming him onto the ground.

For the last time, Buck rushed.

The man delivered the final blow—sharp, clean, decisive.

Buck crumpled. Unconscious.

“He’s no slouch at dog-breakin’,” one man shouted.

“Druther break cayuses any day,” the driver replied, climbing into his wagon.

Buck regained consciousness slowly. Weak. Wounded. But awake.

He watched the man in the red sweater.

“‘Answers to the name of Buck,’” the man murmured, reading from a letter. “Well, Buck, my boy. We’ve had our little fight. Best let it go at that. You’ve learned your place. I know mine. Be a good dog, and all will go well. Be bad, and I’ll beat the stuffing out of you. Understand?”

As he spoke, he patted the dog’s head—the same head he’d beaten mercilessly.

Buck bristled—but didn’t move.

Later, when the man offered water, Buck drank greedily. Then raw meat, chunk by chunk, from the man’s hand.

He was beaten. He knew it.

But not broken.

He had seen the truth: a man with a club was law. The club was a revelation. It marked the beginning of a new world—one ruled by force, instinct, and survival.

From then on, Buck watched as other dogs arrived—some calm, some raging. All were subdued by the man in the red sweater. Some fawned. Some begged. One refused. That dog was killed.

Buck learned quickly: obedience was expected. Respect was earned. But submission? Not required.

Then came the stranger—a wiry man who spat broken English and strange words.

“Sacredam!” he exclaimed, seeing Buck. “Dat one dam bully dog! How moch?”

“Three hundred,” the man replied. “And a present at that.”

Perrault grinned. The price was fair. The dog was exceptional.

Money changed hands.

Curly, a gentle Newfoundland, and Buck were led away.

That was the last Buck saw of the red sweater. From the deck of the *Narwhal*, he looked back at Seattle—the warm Southland fading into memory.

Below, Perrault handed them over to François, a black-faced giant with sharp eyes and a calm voice. They were French-Canadians—different from any men Buck had known. Not kind, but fair. Not soft, but wise.

In the ’tween-decks, Buck met two others. One was a white dog from Spitzbergen—friendly, but deceitful. He stole from Buck’s food. When Buck lunged, François cracked his whip—swift, precise—striking the thief first. Buck recovered the bone. He respected that.

The other dog—Dave—was silent, morose, indifferent. He ate, slept, yawned. When the ship rocked violently across Queen Charlotte Sound, Buck and Curly panicked. Dave merely glanced at them, yawned, and went back to sleep.

Day after day, the ship hummed with the engine’s pulse. The air grew colder. The sky grayed.

One morning, the engine stopped. A hush fell. Then excitement.

François leashed them and brought them on deck.

Buck stepped onto the cold surface—and sank into something white and mushy. He snorted, leapt back.

More fell from the sky.

He shook himself. More landed on him.

He sniffed. Then licked it.

It burned. Then vanished.

He tried again. Same result.

The others laughed.

Buck felt ashamed—though he didn’t know why.

It was snow.

And it was the beginning of everything.

Buck’s first day on the Dyea beach was like a nightmare—each hour a fresh shock, each moment a new surprise. He had been ripped from the quiet comfort of civilization and thrown into the raw, untamed heart of the wild. There was no rest here, no peace, no safety. Only chaos, constant motion, and the ever-present threat of danger. To survive, he had to stay alert at all times. These dogs and men weren’t like those back home. They were wild, lawless creatures bound by no rules but the brutal code of club and fang.

He’d never seen dogs fight like this—savage, swift, and merciless. His first lesson came not from experience, but from watching. Curly, a gentle giant among the team, had approached a husky dog as large as a wolf, though half her size. There was no warning—just a flash of movement, a metallic snap of teeth, and a sudden leap away. In an instant, Curly’s face was torn open from eye to jaw. It was the way wolves fought: strike and vanish. But that wasn’t all. A circle of thirty or forty huskies formed around them, silent, intent, their mouths watering as they licked their chops. Buck didn’t understand their silence—or their hunger.

Curly charged again. Her opponent struck once more, leaping aside. Then, with a strange twist of his chest, he knocked her off balance. She fell—and never rose. That was the signal. The pack surged forward, snarling, yelping, burying her beneath a writhing mass of fur and teeth. She screamed in agony, then went still. It happened so fast, so violently, that Buck froze. He saw Spitz flick out his scarlet tongue—a gesture of mockery—and François rushing in with an axe, swinging it wildly to scatter the dogs. Three men with clubs joined in, driving the attackers back. Within two minutes, it was over. But Curly lay dead in the blood-soaked snow, her body nearly shredded, while the half-breed stood over her, cursing.

That scene haunted Buck in his dreams. So this was how it worked. No mercy. Once you fell, you were finished. He vowed he would never fall.

Spitz laughed again with that cruel tongue, and from that moment, Buck hated him—deeply, fiercely, without end.

Before he could recover from Curly’s death, another blow struck. François fastened a harness onto him—straps and buckles, like those used on horses back home. And just as horses pulled sleds, so now Buck was made to pull one. He hauled François to the forest edge and returned with firewood. Though pride stung him at being reduced to a beast of burden, he knew better than to resist. He focused, worked hard, and learned quickly. François was strict—demanding obedience, enforcing it with a whip. Dave, the experienced wheeler, nipped him when he slipped up. Spitz, the leader, growled sharp warnings or threw his weight in the traces to correct his path.

Buck adapted fast. By the time they returned to camp, he already understood “ho” for stop, “mush” for go, how to swing wide on curves, and how to keep clear of the wheeler when the sled rushed downhill. François praised him to Perrault. “T’ree vair’ good dogs,” he said. “Dat Buck—heem pool lak hell. I tich heem queek as anyt’ing.”

Later that afternoon, Perrault arrived with two more dogs—Billee and Joe. Brothers, both true huskies, yet opposite in every way. Billee was endlessly cheerful; Joe was sour, brooding, always snarling, eyes burning with hostility. Buck greeted them warmly. Dave ignored them. Spitz, however, attacked both. Billee tried to appease him, wagging his tail, but when that failed, he fled, crying softly as Spitz’s teeth tore his flank. Joe, though, met the challenge head-on—mane bristling, ears flat, lips curled in a snarl, jaws snapping like a trap. His eyes glowed with fury. So terrifying was he that even Spitz hesitated—and then turned on the harmless Billee, driving him to the edge of the camp.

That evening, Perrault brought in a fifth dog—an old husky named Sol-leks, meaning “The Angry One.” Lean, gaunt, with a scarred face and one glowing eye, he carried himself with quiet strength. Like Dave, he asked nothing, gave nothing, expected nothing. Even Spitz left him alone. But Buck soon discovered a weakness: Sol-leks disliked being approached from his blind side. Buck, unaware, stepped too close—and felt a searing pain as Sol-leks turned and slashed his shoulder, leaving a deep gash. From then on, Buck avoided that side—and never bothered him again.

That night, Buck faced a new problem: sleep. The tent, lit by candlelight, seemed warm and inviting. But when he entered, Perrault and François erupted in curses, hurling pots and pans at him. Startled, he fled into the cold. A biting wind lashed his wounded shoulder. He tried to lie down in the snow, but frost drove him shivering to his feet. Miserable, he wandered between tents, only to find everywhere was equally freezing. Some dogs lunged at him, but he bristled, snarled, and held his ground—learning fast. Finally, he decided to check on his teammates. But they were gone.

He circled the camp, confused, until he stumbled into a hole in the snow. Something moved beneath him. He leapt back, growling, afraid of the unknown. But then a soft yelp reached his ears—friendly, pleading. He crept closer. A warm breath rose from under the snow. There, curled tightly in a ball, was Billee. He whined, wriggled, and licked Buck’s face in peace.

Another lesson. This was how they survived. Buck chose a spot and dug his own hole. In seconds, his body heat warmed the space. He slept deeply, though his dreams were filled with growls and battles.

When he woke, he didn’t know where he was. Snow had fallen during the night, burying him completely. The walls pressed in. Panic surged through him—the primal fear of being trapped. Though he was a civilized dog, born in luxury, this instinct was ancient, buried deep in his blood. His muscles tensed, his fur rose, and with a fierce snarl, he burst upward, snow flying in a blinding cloud. As he landed, he saw the camp spread before him—and remembered everything: Manuel, the journey, the fight, the hole he’d dug.

François shouted. “Wot I say?” he called to Perrault. “Dat Buck for sure learn queek as anyt’ing.”

Perrault nodded. As a government courier, he needed strong dogs—and Buck was proving invaluable. Within hours, three more huskies joined the team, bringing the total to nine. Before long, they were harnessed and moving up the trail toward the Dyea Cañon.

Buck was glad to be gone. The work was hard, but he found he didn’t hate it. He was surprised by the team’s eagerness—it infected him. More astonishing was the change in Dave and Sol-leks. They were transformed by the harness. All passiveness vanished. They were alert, driven, furious at any delay. Pulling the sled was their purpose, their joy, their reason for living.

Dave was the wheeler, pulling ahead of him—Buck, then Sol-leks. The rest stretched out behind, led by Spitz. Buck was placed between Dave and Sol-leks to learn. And learn he did. They corrected him swiftly, with sharp teeth. Dave was fair—only nipping when necessary, never otherwise. With François’s whip backing them, Buck quickly learned to obey rather than fight.

Once, during a halt, Buck tangled in the traces and delayed the start. Both Dave and Sol-leks attacked, delivering a harsh beating. The mess was worse after, but Buck never made the same mistake again. By the end of the day, he was doing well. The whip cracked less. Even Perrault stopped to examine his paws—respectful approval.

It was a grueling run—up the cañon, through Sheep Camp, past the Scales, beyond the timberline, across glaciers and snowdrifts hundreds of feet deep, over the Chilcoot Divide, the great barrier between salt and fresh water, guarding the lonely North. They traveled fast through the chain of lakes formed in volcanic craters, and late that night reached the vast camp at Lake Bennett—thousands of gold seekers building boats for the spring thaw.

Buck dug his hole and slept deeply, exhausted. But dawn came too soon. He was dragged from sleep and harnessed again. That day, they covered forty miles—trail packed. But the next days were harder. They broke their own trail, working longer, going slower. Perrault often walked ahead, wearing webbed shoes to pack the snow, making it easier for the dogs. François guided the sled, sometimes switching places—but rarely. Perrault was in a hurry, and he knew ice better than anyone. Thin fall ice meant danger. Swift currents meant no ice at all.

Day after day, Buck labored in the traces. They left camp in darkness, hitting the trail at dawn. They pitched camp after dark, ate fish, and crawled into the snow to sleep. Hunger gnawed at Buck. His ration—pound and a half of sun-dried salmon—seemed to vanish instantly. He was never full. The others, lighter and born to the life, got only a pound and stayed strong. Buck lost his finicky ways. He watched as his mates finished first and stole what remained. He couldn’t defend it. While he fought two or three, the food disappeared.

So he ate faster. And when hunger drove him, he stole. He saw Pike, a clever thief, sneak a slice of bacon when Perrault turned. The next day, Buck copied him—taking the whole chunk. Chaos followed, but he was not suspected. Dub, clumsy and careless, took the blame.

This theft marked Buck as fit to survive. It showed his adaptability, his ability to change—not just physically, but mentally. His moral sense, once strong, began to fade. In the Southland, stealing was wrong. Here, under the law of club and fang, it was survival. To care about fairness was folly. Buck didn’t think it through. He simply did what was easiest. He didn’t steal for pleasure, but because his stomach demanded it. He didn’t rob openly—he stole quietly, carefully, out of respect for the law of the wild.

His transformation accelerated. His muscles hardened like iron. Pain became trivial. He could eat anything—rotten, bitter, foul—and his stomach extracted every ounce of nourishment. His blood carried it to every part of his body, building stronger flesh. His senses sharpened. He heard faintest sounds in sleep, knowing if they meant danger or calm. He bit ice free from his paws. When water holes were frozen over, he reared and smashed the ice with stiff legs.

But most remarkable was his ability to sense wind. No matter how still the air, when he dug his nest, the wind that came later always found him sheltered, leeward. And instincts long buried stirred awake. The generations of domestication fell away. Vaguely, he remembered the youth of his breed—the wild dogs who ran in packs, hunted in forests, killed as they chased. Fighting with slash and snap came naturally. It was how his ancestors fought. Their memories quickened within him, their tricks becoming his.

And on silent nights, when he raised his nose to the stars and howled long and low, it was not just Buck speaking. It was his ancestors—dead, dust, and forgotten—howling through him, across centuries. Their sorrow, their fear of cold and dark, their longing for freedom—all echoed in his voice.

Life, he now understood, was a puppet show. The ancient song surged through him, and he came into his own. He came because men had found gold in the North. And because Manuel, a gardener’s helper, could not feed his family on his wages.

Buck was changing—deeply, silently. The ancient instinct that slept in his blood stirred and grew stronger with every mile of trail, every gust of wind, every bitter night under the stars. But it was not a wild, reckless force; it was cunning, deliberate, patient. He moved through the harsh world of the northern wilderness with quiet control, never rushing into conflict, never showing weakness. Though he felt the hatred burning between him and Spitz, he held back. There was no foolishness in Buck now. He watched, waited, and learned.

Spitz, on the other hand, could not hide his disdain. He saw Buck as a threat—a strange dog from the South, too large, too strong, too silent. And so he tested him constantly, snarling, snapping, trying to provoke a fight. But Buck refused to rise. Not yet.

Then came the night at Lake Le Barge.

Snow fell like knives. The wind bit through fur and bone. They made camp on the ice, sheltered only by a sheer rock wall. No tent, no firewood enough for warmth. François thawed fish over a small blaze that melted through the ice and left them eating in darkness. Buck curled up close to the rock, digging a nest where the cold could not reach him. When François brought the fish, Buck ate quickly and returned to his spot—only to find Spitz already there.

A low growl rumbled in Spitz’s throat. Buck froze. Then something inside him broke.

He lunged.

It wasn’t rage—it was instinct. A roar rose from deep within, older than memory, older than fear. Spitz was stunned. He had always thought Buck timid, a brute who survived only because of size. But this was different. This was a challenge.

They circled, eyes locked, muscles coiled. Just as they were about to clash, chaos erupted.

An oath from Perrault. A crack of a club. A yelp of pain.

The camp was swarming with starving huskies—dozens of them, drawn by the smell of food. They had crept in while the two dogs fought. Now they surged forward, teeth bared, eyes wild with hunger. One had its head buried in the grub-box. Perrault struck it hard. The box tumbled. In an instant, the wolves tore into the food, fighting over scraps, ignoring the clubs that swung down upon them.

The team-dogs were caught off guard. They burst from their nests and found themselves surrounded. These huskies were nothing like the ones they knew—gaunt, half-dead, driven mad by hunger. Their bones showed through skin, their jaws dripped saliva, their eyes burned with frenzy. They attacked without mercy.

Buck was set upon by three. His head and shoulders were ripped open. Blood ran warm over his muzzle. He fought back, tearing one’s throat, feeling the spray of life in his mouth. It stoked his fury. Then, from the side, fangs sank into his neck.

Spitz.

He had turned traitor, striking while Buck was occupied.

Perrault and François rushed in, swinging clubs. The wave of beasts recoiled. Buck shook free—but only for a moment. The men had to return to save the food, and the attack resumed.

Billee, usually gentle, bolted. Pike and Dub followed. The rest fled after them. As Buck prepared to leap, he saw Spitz charge—intent on knocking him down into the pack. He braced himself, absorbed the impact, and then sprinted across the ice, joining the others.

Later, they gathered in the woods. Every dog was wounded. Dub limped badly. Dolly had a torn throat. Joe had lost an eye. Billee whimpered all night, her ear shredded. At dawn, they limped back to camp. The marauders were gone. But the damage was done: half the food was eaten, the sled lashings chewed, even François’s whip cord gnawed. The men were furious.

François looked over the dogs, voice soft.

“Ah, my friends,” he said. “Maybe these bites make you mad dogs. Maybe all of you are mad now. What do you think, eh, Perrault?”

Perrault frowned. With four hundred miles still ahead, madness among the dogs was dangerous. They worked fast—two hours of swearing and mending—and the team was back on the trail, struggling through the hardest stretch yet.

The Thirty Mile River was open. Water roared beneath thin ice. They crossed it in agony. Perrault broke through twelve times, saved only by his long pole. Each time, he had to build a fire to dry his clothes. The temperature dropped to fifty below zero. Once, the sled broke through, dragging Dave and Buck into the freezing current. They were half-drowned before being pulled out. They stood around the fire, covered in ice, forced to run until they sweated and thawed, so close to the flames they were singed.

Another time, Spitz went through, pulling the entire team with him. Buck strained backward, claws digging into the ice edge. Behind him, Dave pulled. Behind the sled, François hauled with cracked tendons.

Then the rim ice gave way. There was no escape but up the cliff. Perrault climbed, miracle-like. François prayed. They tied every rope, harness, and lashing together and hoisted the dogs one by one. The sled came last. Then they searched for a path down—using the rope again. Night found them back on the river, barely a quarter-mile ahead.

By the time they reached the Hootalinqua, Buck was exhausted. His feet hurt. They were softer than the huskies’, worn down by generations of domestication. All day he limped, collapsed at camp, refusing food unless François brought it to him. Every night, the dog-driver rubbed his paws for half an hour, then cut the tops off his own moccasins to make boots for Buck.

One morning, François forgot. Buck lay on his back, legs waving in the air, refusing to move. Even Perrault grinned.

Soon, Buck’s feet hardened. The moccasins were discarded.

At the Pelly, something terrible happened.

Dolly—always quiet, unremarkable—suddenly went mad. She let out a long, heart-wrenching howl that sent chills through every dog. Then she charged straight at Buck.

He had never seen such madness. He ran—not from fear of death, but from the horror of what she had become. He fled, terrified, with Dolly panting, frothing, just one leap behind. He raced across islands, through rivers choked with ice, desperate to escape. He didn’t look back—he could hear her snarling, closing in.

François called from a quarter-mile away. Buck turned, still one leap ahead, gasping, trusting the man would save him.

The axe came down—crash—on Dolly’s skull.

Buck staggered, breathless, sobbing, helpless.

And then Spitz struck.

He leapt on Buck, teeth sinking into flesh, ripping deep. But François’s lash snapped down. Buck watched as Spitz received the worst beating any dog had ever endured.

“One devil, that Spitz,” Perrault muttered.

“Dat Buck? Two devils,” François replied. “I watch him. I know. Some fine day, he’ll go mad as hell—and he’ll chew Spitz up and spit him out on the snow. Sure.”

From that moment, war began.

Spitz, the lead-dog, felt his rule threatened. Buck was unlike any Southland dog he’d known. Most failed—died under frost, work, starvation. But Buck endured. He matched the huskies in strength, cunning, and savagery. And he was smarter. The club had broken his old recklessness, leaving only cold, calculating will.

This was inevitable—the clash for leadership.

Buck wanted it. Not for power alone, but for pride. The pride of the trace—the same pride that drove Dave to pull until he dropped, Sol-leks to strain with every ounce, Spitz to punish the weak. It was the pride that made dogs live for the trail, die in the harness, break their hearts when removed.

And Buck had it.

He challenged Spitz openly. He stepped between him and the dogs he punished. Once, Pike hid under a foot of snow. Spitz raged, digging, snarling. When Pike was found, Spitz lunged to strike. But Buck flew in—just as fast, just as fierce. Spitz was thrown back. Pike, trembling, jumped on him. Buck joined in.

François laughed—then cracked the lash. Buck wouldn’t yield. The butt of the whip hit him. He fell, dazed, but stayed. Spitz beat Pike mercilessly.

After that, Buck kept interfering—only when François wasn’t watching. A quiet rebellion spread. The team unraveled. Dave and Sol-leks stayed loyal. But the others grew bold. Pike stole half a fish. Dub and Joe fought Spitz. Billee whined less. Buck never passed Spitz without snarling, swaggering past like a bully.

Discipline broke. The camp became a battlefield. François cursed, stamped, tore his hair. His lash sang, but it did no good. When his back turned, the dogs were at it again.

He backed Spitz. Buck backed the rest.

But Buck was too clever to be caught. He worked hard—because the trail had become joy. Yet greater joy was stirring trouble. He loved seeing fights start, traces tangled, chaos reign.

At the Tahkeena, one night, Dub flushed a snowshoe rabbit. It vanished into a creek. The whole team chased it—sixty dogs, including fifty police huskies from a nearby camp.

Buck led the pack. He ran low, muscles flashing, breath coming in eager whines. Moonlight painted the snow white. The rabbit danced ahead—light, swift, a ghost of frost.

This was more than hunting. This was primal ecstasy—the bloodlust, the joy of the chase, the thrill of killing with your own teeth. It was deeper than any man’s hunt. Buck was running with the pack, chasing living meat, driven by instincts older than language.

There is a peak of life—an ecstasy beyond words. It comes when one is most alive, yet forgets being alive. It strikes the artist in flame, the soldier in battle, the animal in flight.

And it came to Buck—leading the pack, howling the old wolf-song, straining after prey that fled through moonlit snow. He was touching the depths of his soul, diving back into time itself. He was ruled by the surge of life—the tidal wave of being—the pure joy of every muscle, every sinew, expressing itself in motion, flying under stars, over dead earth.

But Spitz, colder, sharper, cut across the bend. He leapt from the bank—into the rabbit’s path.

The rabbit couldn’t turn. Teeth snapped. A shriek echoed—loud as a man’s cry.

Life falling from life’s height into death.

The pack roared—hell’s chorus of triumph.

And Buck, at the front, felt the echo of the wild song rise in his chest. He had been born again—not as a pet, not as a servant—but as a beast of the wild, answering the call.

“Eh? What did I say? I told you—Buck’s got two devils in him!” François exclaimed the next morning, his voice sharp with surprise as he discovered Spitz missing and Buck covered in wounds. He pulled the dog close to the firelight, pointing out the deep gashes and torn fur. “Spitz fought like hell,” Perrault said, scanning the damage, “but Buck? He fought like two hells.”

“And now we’ll make good time,” François declared. “No more Spitz. No more trouble. Sure thing.”

As Perrault packed the camp gear and loaded the sled, François began harnessing the dogs. Buck trotted forward to claim the lead position—Spitz’s old spot—but François didn’t see him. Instead, he brought Sol-leks forward, believing him the strongest dog left. But Buck lunged at Sol-leks, driving him back and stepping into the leader’s place.

“Eh? Eh?” François shouted, slapping his thighs with delight. “Look at that Buck! He killed Spitz and thinks he can take the job!”

“Get away, Chook!” he barked, but Buck refused to move. François grabbed him by the scruff, and though Buck growled fiercely, he was dragged aside. Sol-leks was repositioned, but the older dog looked uneasy, clearly afraid of Buck. François stood firm, but when he turned his back, Buck leapt forward again, pushing Sol-leks aside without resistance.

François roared, “Now, by Gar, I’ll fix you!” He returned with a heavy club raised. Buck remembered the man in the red sweater—the one who had beaten him—and stepped back slowly. He didn’t charge when Sol-leks was brought forward again. Instead, he circled just beyond the club’s reach, snarling with bitter rage. But he watched the club closely, ready to dodge if it came flying. He’d learned how clubs worked.

The driver went about his tasks, then called Buck to return to his old spot behind Dave. Buck retreated. François followed. Buck retreated again. This dance continued for some time, until François finally dropped the club, thinking Buck feared punishment. But Buck wasn’t running from pain—he was refusing to yield. The leadership was his. He had earned it. And he would not settle for less.

Perrault stepped in. Together, they chased Buck around the camp for nearly an hour. They threw clubs. He dodged. They cursed him—his ancestors, his bloodline, every hair on his body—and he answered only with snarls, always staying just out of reach. He never tried to flee. He simply circled, retreating in a wide arc, signaling plainly: *Give me what I want, and I’ll come in.*

François sat down, scratching his head. Perrault checked his watch and swore. Time was slipping away—they should have been on the trail an hour ago. François scratched again, shook his head, and grinned sheepishly at Perrault, who shrugged in defeat.

Then François walked over to Sol-leks and called to Buck. Buck let out a low, doggy laugh—but stayed where he was. François unhooked Sol-leks’s traces and moved him back to his old place. The team stood ready, harnessed in a straight line, waiting for the trail. Only one spot remained open—up front.

François called again. Buck laughed once more and kept his distance.

“Throw down the club,” Perrault ordered.

François obeyed.

At once, Buck trotted forward, laughing triumphantly, and took his place at the head of the team. His traces were fastened, the sled broken out, and with both men running beside it, they surged onto the river trail.

François had praised Buck’s strength, but even he hadn’t realized the full measure of the dog’s power. Before the day was young, Buck proved himself far more than a strong animal—he was a master.

He took command instantly. Where judgment and quick decisions were needed, he surpassed even Spitz, whom François had considered unmatched. But it was in enforcing order that Buck truly excelled.

Dave and Sol-leks cared little about the change. Their purpose was to pull, to endure, to work. As long as the traces held, they didn’t mind who led. Billee, gentle and easygoing, could have led them all—so long as he kept things orderly.

But the rest of the team had grown wild under Spitz’s rule. Now, under Buck, discipline returned swiftly.

Pike, who lagged behind and gave no more effort than he had to, was shaken hard and repeatedly. By the end of the first day, he was pulling harder than ever before.

That first night, Joe—the sour, snapping one—was punished severely. Spitz had never managed this. Buck simply overwhelmed him with his weight, tearing at him until Joe stopped fighting and began whining for mercy.

The whole team changed. Unity returned. They moved as one, powerful and synchronized.

At Rink Rapids, two native huskies—Teek and Koona—were added. Buck broke them in so quickly that François was stunned.

“Never seen such a dog as this Buck!” he cried. “No, never! He’s worth a thousand dollars, by Gar! What do you say, Perrault?”

Perrault nodded. They were ahead of schedule, gaining ground each day.

The trail was excellent—packed hard, snow-free. Temperatures dipped to fifty below zero and stayed there. The men alternated riding and running, keeping the dogs moving with few stops.

They crossed the Thirty Mile River in one day—a journey that had taken ten on the way in. In one stretch, they raced sixty miles from Lake Le Barge to White Horse Rapids.

Over Marsh, Tagish, and Bennett—seventy miles of lakes—they traveled so fast that the runner who was supposed to run behind the sled was towed along on a rope.

On the last night of the second week, they crested White Pass and descended toward Skaguay, the lights of the town and the ships below glowing in the darkness.

It was a record run—fourteen days, forty miles a day.

For three days, Perrault and François paraded through Skaguay’s main street, tossing mailbags in the air, showered with invitations to drink. The dogs were the center of attention, surrounded by admirers—dog handlers, mushers, legends of the trail.

Then, a few rough westerners tried to stir up trouble. They were gunned down like rats, and public interest shifted.

Next came orders.

François called Buck to him, embraced him tightly, and wept. That was the end of their journey together.

Like all men, they passed from Buck’s life forever.

A Scotch half-breed took over. With twelve other teams, Buck and his mates began the long trek back to Dawson.

This was no longer a race. It was hard labor. A heavy load—the mail—dragged behind them. Each day was grueling, relentless.

But Buck bore it well. He took pride in the work, as Dave and Sol-leks did. He made sure his mates pulled their share, whether they liked it or not.

Life became routine. One day blended into the next.

Each morning, cooks rose early, fires were lit, breakfast eaten. Some broke camp while others harnessed the dogs. They set off hours before dawn.

At night, camp was built. Flies were pitched, wood cut, beds laid with pine boughs, water carried. Then the dogs were fed.

That was the highlight of their day. After eating, they lounged with the others—over fifty dogs in total. There were fierce fighters among them, but after three fights, Buck had established dominance. When he bristled and showed his teeth, they scattered.

Best of all, he loved lying near the fire—hind legs tucked beneath him, front legs stretched forward, head raised, eyes blinking dreamily at the flames.

Sometimes he thought of Judge Miller’s house in California—the sun-drenched valley, the swimming pool, Ysabel the hairless Mexican dog, Toots the Japanese pug. But more often, he remembered the man in the red sweater, Curly’s death, the fight with Spitz, and the food he’d eaten—or dreamed of.

He wasn’t homesick. The Sunland felt distant, faint. These memories meant nothing.

Far stronger were the echoes of his ancestors—the instincts buried deep within him, waking now, alive and vivid.

As he blinked at the fire, sometimes it seemed the flames belonged to another world. He saw a different man—a short-legged, long-armed figure with stringy muscles, matted hair, and a head sloping backward. This man clutched a stone-tipped stick, naked except for a ragged, fire-scorched hide. He moved bent forward, springy and alert, as if always afraid. He squatted by the fire, head between his knees, hands clasped above his head like a shield.

And beyond the fire, in the dark, Buck saw gleaming coals—two by two—eyes of great beasts stalking through the woods. He heard their crashing, their growls in the night.

Dreaming there, by the Yukon bank, these visions made his fur rise along his spine, his neck, his shoulders. He whimpered softly, growled low. Then the half-breed cook would shout, “Hey, you Buck, wake up!”

And the dream vanished. Reality returned. He’d rise, yawn, stretch—as if he’d been asleep.

The trip was brutal. The mail weighed them down. The dogs grew thin, weak.

When they reached Dawson, they were in poor shape. They should have rested for a week. But within two days, they were loading letters again, descending the Yukon from the Barracks.

The dogs were exhausted. The drivers complained. And it snowed every day—softening the trail, increasing friction, making the pull heavier.

Yet the drivers treated the dogs fairly. Every night, the dogs ate first. No man slept until he’d checked his dogs’ feet.

Still, their strength faded. Since winter began, they’d traveled eighteen hundred miles—pulling sleds the entire way. Even the toughest couldn’t endure that.

Buck held his team together. He enforced discipline, though he too was weary.

Billee whimpered in his sleep each night. Joe was worse than ever. Sol-leks remained cold and distant.

But Dave suffered most.

Something was wrong. He grew moody, irritable. Once camp was set, he made his nest immediately, and waited for his driver to feed him.

Once out of harness, he didn’t rise again until morning.

Sometimes, when the sled jerked or he strained to start, he screamed in pain.

The driver examined him. Found nothing broken. Nothing clear.

All the drivers talked about it—over meals, over pipes. One night, they held a meeting.

Dave was brought to the fire, pressed, prodded—until he howled.

Something was inside, but they couldn’t find it.

By the time they reached Cassiar Bar, Dave was falling constantly in the traces.

The half-breed halted the train and removed him from the team. He attached Sol-leks to the sled instead, intending to let Dave run free behind.

But Dave resisted. He grunted, growled as the traces were unfastened. When he saw Sol-leks take his place, he whimpered broken-heartedly.

The pride of the trace—the honor of the trail—was his. Even dying, he couldn’t bear for another to do his work.

When the sled moved, Dave floundered alongside in the soft snow, attacking Sol-leks, rushing at him, trying to push him into the deep snow, striving to leap into his traces, to stand between him and the sled. All the while, he howled with grief and agony.

The half-breed tried to drive him off with the whip. But Dave ignored the lash. The man couldn’t bring himself to strike harder.

Dave refused to run quietly behind. He kept struggling in the deep snow, exhausting himself. Then he fell, lying still, howling mournfully as the train passed.

With his last strength, he staggered forward until the train stopped again. Then he crawled past the sleds to his own, standing beside Sol-leks.

His driver paused to light his pipe from the man behind. Then he returned and started the dogs.

They swung out—but the sled didn’t move.

The driver stared. His comrades gathered. Dave had bitten through both of Sol-leks’s traces. He stood directly in front of the sled, pleading with his eyes to stay there.

They were silent. They spoke of dogs who broke their hearts when denied their work. Of old dogs, injured ones, who died because they were cut from the traces. And they agreed—it was mercy to let him die doing what he lived for.

So Dave was harnessed again. Proudly, he pulled as before—though he groaned in pain. He fell several times, dragged in the traces. Once, the sled ran over him, leaving him limping.

But he held on until camp was reached. His driver made a space for him by the fire.

Morning came. Dave was too weak to move.

At harness-up time, he struggled to crawl forward. He pushed himself up with convulsive efforts, stumbled, fell. Then he began to inch forward—forelegs advancing, body dragged forward in jerks, then forelegs again—slowly, painfully—toward his mates.

His strength failed. The last thing his companions saw was him lying in the snow, gasping, yearning toward them.

They could hear his mournful howl until they disappeared behind a belt of river trees.

There, the train stopped.

The Scotch half-breed walked back to the camp they’d left.

The men fell silent.

A revolver cracked.

The man returned quickly.

The whips snapped. The bells rang cheerfully. The sleds churned forward.

But Buck knew. And every dog knew.

What had happened behind the trees was clear.

Dave had died—not in fear, not in pain, but in the traces, where he belonged.

Thirty days after leaving Dawson, the Salt Water Mail reached Skaguay, with Buck and his team leading the way. They arrived in terrible condition—exhausted, broken down, and hollow-eyed. Buck, once a sturdy one hundred and forty pounds, had shriveled to just over a hundred and fifteen. His teammates, though smaller, had lost even more weight. Pike, the chronic malingerer who’d faked injuries before, now limped for real. Sol-leks dragged one leg, and Dub bore a painful wrench in his shoulder blade.

Every paw was raw and sore. There was no spring left in their steps. Each stride thudded heavily on the trail, jolting their bodies and doubling the toll of every mile. They weren’t just tired—they were spent. Not from sudden strain, but from months of relentless labor that had drained them to the last drop of strength. Their muscles, fibers, cells—all were deadened by fatigue. Recovery was impossible; they had nothing left to give. In less than five months, they’d traveled over two thousand five hundred miles, resting only five days during the final eighteen hundred. When they reached Skaguay, they seemed barely alive. The traces hung loose, and on downhill stretches, they could barely keep out of the sled’s way.

“Mush on, poor sore feets,” the driver urged as they stumbled through the main street. “Dis is de las’. Den we get one long res’. Eh? For sure. One bully long res’.”

The drivers expected a long break. They’d covered twelve hundred miles with only two days off—and by rights, they deserved rest. But the Klondike rush had flooded the region with people, and letters from those left behind piled up like snowdrifts. Official orders came down: fresh Hudson Bay dogs would replace the worn-out ones. The old dogs were to be sold—cheaply, quickly, without mercy.

Three days passed. Then, on the fourth morning, two men from the States bought the entire team—harness included—for next to nothing. They called themselves Hal and Charles. Charles was middle-aged, pale, with weak eyes and a mustache that curled upward defiantly, betraying the droop of his lips beneath. Hal was young—nineteen or twenty—with a Colt revolver strapped at his hip and a hunting knife beside it. His belt, crammed with cartridges, was the most striking thing about him—a badge of reckless youth. Both looked out of place. Why such men would venture into the North was a mystery no one could solve.

Buck watched the exchange, saw the money change hands, and knew the Scotch half-breed, the mail drivers, and all who’d come before were gone. When he was driven to their camp, he saw disorder everywhere—half-raised tent, unwashed dishes, chaos. And there was a woman. “Mercedes,” the men called her. Charles’s wife, Hal’s sister. A family unit, ill-suited to the wild.

Buck eyed them warily as they began packing. They moved with effort, but not with purpose. The tent was rolled clumsily, three times too big. Dishes stayed dirty. Mercedes flitted around, interfering, chattering advice. She argued when they placed a clothes-sack on the front of the sled—then insisted it go on the back—then found things that had to go inside it again. They reloaded twice.

Three neighbors from a nearby tent watched, grinning.

“You’ve got a right smart load already,” one said. “And I wouldn’t tote that tent if I were you.”

“Undreamed of!” Mercedes gasped, throwing her hands up. “How could I possibly manage without it?”

“It’s spring,” the man replied. “No more cold weather coming.”

She shook her head firmly. Charles and Hal stacked the final items on top of the mountainous heap.

“Think it’ll ride?” another asked.

“Why shouldn’t it?” Charles snapped.

“Oh, that’s all right,” the man said hastily. “Just wondering. Seems a bit top-heavy.”

Charles turned away and tightened the lashings—poorly.

“And the dogs can haul this thing all day,” a third man said.

“Certainly,” Hal replied, grabbing the gee-pole and cracking his whip. “Mush! Mush on!”

The dogs lunged forward, strained against the breastbands, then collapsed. They couldn *t move the sled.

“The lazy brutes,” Hal yelled, raising the whip.

But Mercedes grabbed it, tears in her eyes. “Oh, Hal, you mustn’t!” she pleaded. “Poor dears! Promise me you won’t hurt them, or I won’t go a step.”

“Precious little you know about dogs,” Hal sneered. “They’re lazy. You have to whip them to get anything out of them. Ask anyone.”

She looked at the onlookers, pleading, horror etched on her face.

“They’re weak as water,” one said. “Plum tuckered out. They need rest.”

“Rest be blanked,” Hal shot back.

Mercedes winced at the curse.

But she stood by her brother. “Don’t listen to him,” she insisted. “You’re driving our dogs. Do what you think best.”

Hal cracked the whip again. The dogs threw themselves forward, dug in, lowered their bodies—everything they had. But the sled didn’t budge. After two tries, they stopped, panting. The whip whistled through the air—then Mercedes dropped to her knees before Buck, arms around his neck.

“You poor, poor dears,” she sobbed. “Why don’t you pull harder? Then you wouldn’t be whipped.” Buck didn’t like her, but he was too weary to resist. He took it as part of the day’s misery.

One of the watchers, clenching his teeth, finally spoke: “I don’t care what happens to you—but for the dogs’ sake, break out that sled. The runners are frozen. Push on the gee-pole, side to side. Break it free.”

Hal tried again. This time, following the advice, he freed the runners. The overloaded sled lurched forward. The dogs struggled under a storm of blows. A hundred yards ahead, the path curved sharply downward into the main street. It would have taken an expert to keep such a top-heavy load upright—and Hal wasn’t one. As they turned, the sled tipped over, spilling half its cargo through loose lashings. The dogs kept running. The lighter sled skidded along on its side. Angry at the abuse and unfair burden, Buck surged forward. The others followed. Hal shouted “Whoa! Whoa!” but they ignored him. He tripped. The overturned sled crushed him. The dogs raced up the street, scattering the rest of the gear across Skaguay like confetti.

Kind-hearted citizens caught the dogs and gathered the mess. They gave advice: “Half the load, twice the dogs—if you ever want to reach Dawson.”

Hal, Charles, and Mercedes listened reluctantly. They pitched a new tent, sorted their gear. Canned goods were pulled out—laughed at. “Blankets for a hotel!” one man joked. “Half as many is too much. Toss the tent. Those dishes—who’s going to wash them? Good Lord, do you think you’re riding a Pullman?”

So it went—the ruthless purge of excess. Mercedes cried as her bags were emptied. She wept over everything, each item discarded. She rocked back and forth, clutching her knees, declaring she wouldn’t move an inch. She appealed to everyone, then wiped her eyes and threw out even essential clothing. Her fury grew—she attacked her men’s belongings next, sifting through them like a storm.

Still, the load remained massive. That evening, Charles and Hal bought six Outside dogs. Added to the original six, plus Teek and Koona—two huskies picked up at Rink Rapids—the team swelled to fourteen. But these new dogs were useless. Three were short-haired pointers, one a Newfoundland, two mongrels. They didn’t know trace or trail. Buck and his mates looked at them with contempt. He taught them what not to do, but not what to do. Most were confused, broken by the harshness of the north. Only the two mongrels had no spirit at all—just bones that could be shattered.

With the newcomers helpless and the old team wrecked by twenty-five hundred miles, the future looked bleak. Yet Charles and Hal were proud. Fourteen dogs! They’d never seen a sled with so many. They didn’t understand that one sled couldn’t carry food for fourteen dogs. They’d figured it out with a pencil—so much per dog, so many days, Q.E.D. Mercedes nodded, convinced it was simple.

The next morning, Buck led the team down the street. No energy. No fire. They started dead-tired. Buck had made the journey four times. Now, battered and spent, he faced it again. His heart wasn’t in it—and neither was any other dog’s. The Outsiders were afraid. The Insiders doubted their masters.

Buck sensed these people couldn’t be relied on. They didn’t know how to do anything. As days passed, it became clear: they couldn’t learn. They were careless, disorganized. It took them half the night to pitch a shabby camp, half the morning to pack it—so poorly that they spent the whole day stopping and rearranging. Some days, they barely made ten miles. Other days, they didn’t start at all. Never did they cover more than half the distance they’d planned.

It was inevitable they’d run out of dog food. But they rushed it by overfeeding. The Outside dogs, unused to famine, ate greedily. When the huskies pulled weakly, Hal doubled the ration. Then, when Mercedes begged for more food—tears in her eyes—he refused. So she stole fish from the sacks and fed the dogs secretly.

But what Buck and the huskies needed wasn’t food—it was rest. And though they moved slowly, the heavy load sapped their strength.

Then came underfeeding. One morning, Hal realized half the dog food was gone, and only a quarter of the trip completed. No more food could be found. He cut the rations—again, below standard. His sister and brother-in-law agreed. But they couldn’t make the dogs go faster. Their own incompetence kept them from starting earlier or traveling longer. They didn’t know how to work dogs—or themselves.

Dub was the first to fall. Poor, clumsy thief though he was, he’d always worked hard. His injured shoulder worsened until Hal shot him with the Colt. The country says an Outside dog dies on a husky’s ration. These six Outside dogs died on half that. The Newfoundland went first. Then the three pointers. The mongrels held on longest—but not forever.

By then, the soft ways of the South were gone. Arctic travel had stripped away romance, revealing a brutal truth. Mercedes stopped crying for the dogs. She wept for herself, quarreled with Charles and Hal. They fought constantly. Their irritation grew with their suffering, feeding on itself. The patience born of hardship—kindness forged in pain—never came to them. They were stiff, aching, bitter. Words were sharp—first thing in the morning, last at night.

Charles and Hal fought whenever Mercedes let them. Each believed he did more than his share—and each said so. Sometimes she sided with her husband, sometimes with her brother. The quarrel grew monstrous—dragging in ancestors, cousins, distant relatives, some long dead. Hal’s taste in art, his aunt’s social habits—anything—could spark a fight. Mercedes vented her rage on her husband’s family, accusing them of gossip and weakness. Meanwhile, the fire stayed unlit, the camp unfinished, the dogs unfed.

Mercedes nursed a deep grievance—of being treated like a woman. She was soft, pretty, used to being protected. Now, her husband and brother treated her roughly. She was used to helplessness. They complained. And so she made life unbearable. She no longer cared for the dogs. She rode the sled, despite her weight—120 pounds—adding the final burden to the starving animals. She rode for days until they collapsed. Charles and Hal begged her to walk. She wept, accused them of cruelty.

Once, they pulled her off by force. They never did it again. She went limp like a spoiled child and sat down on the trail. They walked on. She didn’t move. After three miles, they turned back, unloaded, carried her, and forced her onto the sled.

In their own suffering, they ignored the dogs’ agony. Hal preached that one must harden. He failed to teach his family—so he beat the dogs with a club. At Five Fingers, the food ran out. An old squaw offered frozen horse-hide in trade for Hal’s revolver. It was worse than nothing—stripped from starved horses months before. Frozen, it felt like galvanized iron. Thawed, it became thin, leathery strings and hair—indigestible, irritating.

Through it all, Buck trudged at the front—like a dream. He pulled when he could. When he couldn’t, he fell. Blows from whip or club brought him back to his feet. His beautiful coat was gone—matted, torn, stained with blood. Muscles had wasted to cords. Flesh vanished. Every rib, every bone showed beneath wrinkled skin. It was heartbreaking—except Buck’s heart was unbreakable. The man in the red sweater had proven that.

Same with his mates. Seven dogs remained—walking skeletons. They no longer felt the lash or club. Pain was dull, distant. Sight and sound blurred. They were not half-alive. They were empty shells—bags of bones with faint sparks still flickering. At stops, they dropped like dead dogs. The spark dimmed. Then, when struck, it flared weakly, and they rose, tottered, and staggered on.

One day, Billee—the good-natured one—fell and couldn’t rise. Hal had traded his revolver. He took the axe and killed Billee where he lay. Cut the body from the harness, dragged it aside. Buck saw. His mates saw. They knew it was coming for them.

Next day, Koona fell. Five remained: Joe, too weak for anger; Pike, crippled, half-conscious; Sol-leks, one-eyed, still loyal, mournful; Teek, fresher but beaten harder; and Buck—still at the head, but blind half the time, feeling the trail only by instinct, by the touch of his paws.

Spring was glorious. The sun rose early, set late. Dawn came at three. Twilight lingered till nine. The world blazed in light. Winter’s silence was gone. The land hummed with life waking up. Sap rose in pines. Willows burst into buds. Shrubs turned green. Crickets sang at night. Creeping things stirred in the sun. Partridges boomed. Woodpeckers knocked. Squirrels chattered. Birds sang. Overhead, geese honked in sharp V-formations, slicing the sky.

From every slope, water trickled—music from unseen springs. Everything thawed, bent, cracked. The Yukon strained to break free. Ice melted from below. Sun burned from above. Air holes formed. Fissures spread. Thin ice crashed into the river.

And through it all—under the blazing sun, in soft breezes—like travelers toward death, stumbled the two men, the woman, and the huskies.

When John Thornton froze his feet in December, his partners had seen to his comfort and left him behind while they pushed upriver to harvest a raft of logs for Dawson. He was still limping slightly when he found Buck half-dead by the riverbank, but as the weather stayed warm, even that limp faded away. Lying there through the long spring days, watching the water flow and listening to birdsong and the hum of nature, Buck slowly regained his strength.

After traveling three thousand miles, rest was a gift. And as his wounds healed, his muscles swelled, and flesh returned to cover his bones, Buck grew lazy—content, even. The same could be said of John Thornton, Skeet, and Nig. They waited together for the raft that would carry them down to Dawson, drifting into a quiet rhythm of stillness and ease.

Skeet was a small Irish setter who quickly befriended Buck, though he was too weak then to resist her affection. She possessed a gentle instinct, like a mother cat cleaning her young—she washed and tended to Buck’s wounds each morning after breakfast, until he came to expect her care as much as he did Thornton’s. Nig, larger and quieter, was a massive black dog, part bloodhound, part deerhound, with kind eyes and an endless patience. He never challenged Buck, nor did he seek attention. He simply existed in harmony.

To Buck’s surprise, neither dog showed jealousy. They shared the warmth and generosity of their master. As Buck grew stronger, they pulled him into playful games—games so joyful that even Thornton couldn’t help joining in. Through these moments, Buck passed from recovery into a new life. For the first time, he knew love—not just loyalty or duty, but something deeper, fiercer, more alive. It was not the polite companionship he’d known at Judge Miller’s estate in the sun-drenched Santa Clara Valley. There, with the Judge’s sons, it had been work; with the grandsons, a sort of solemn guardianship; with the Judge himself, a dignified friendship. But this—this was devotion without measure. This was madness born of trust. And only John Thornton could awaken it.

Thornton had saved his life—that alone meant everything. But more than that, he was the perfect master. While others treated dogs as tools or responsibilities, Thornton cared for them as if they were children—because he couldn’t help it. He remembered every kindness, offered words of encouragement, and loved nothing more than sitting quietly with them, talking in low tones (“gas,” he called it), sharing thoughts and stories. He had a way of gripping Buck’s head in both hands, pressing his own forehead against Buck’s, shaking him gently back and forth, muttering curses that to Buck were songs of affection. Each motion sent waves of ecstasy through him, as if his heart might burst from joy. When released, Buck would leap up, mouth open in silent laughter, eyes bright, throat humming with unspoken sound. Then Thornton would look at him reverently and whisper, “God, you can almost speak.”

Buck had his own way of expressing love—one that seemed almost painful. He would grab Thornton’s hand in his jaws and clamp down hard enough to leave marks, teeth sinking deep. But Thornton understood. He saw it not as aggression, but as a tender gesture, a bond sealed in bite.

Mostly, though, Buck’s love was silent. Unlike Skeet, who nudged insistently for pets, or Nig, who rested his great head on Thornton’s knee, Buck preferred to watch. He would lie at Thornton’s feet for hours, gazing up, studying every flicker of expression, every shift in posture. Or he’d rest farther off, observing the man’s silhouette, the rise and fall of his chest, the quiet movements of his body. Sometimes, their mutual gaze would draw Thornton’s head around, and they would lock eyes—no words needed, no need for speech. In those moments, their hearts shone through, speaking plainly across silence.

For weeks after being rescued, Buck couldn’t bear to let Thornton out of sight. From the moment the man stepped outside the tent until he returned, Buck followed closely behind. He feared what he had seen before: masters passing into memory, slipping away like shadows. Perrault, François, the Scotch half-breed—all gone. Even in sleep, the fear haunted him. He’d wake, trembling, and crawl to the edge of the tent flap, listening for the steady breaths of his master.

Yet despite this fierce love, the wild remained within him. Civilization had touched him, but not tamed him. Faithfulness and devotion were now part of him—gifts shaped by fire and shelter—but the ancient instincts lived on. He was not a domesticated dog of the Southland, bred soft by generations of comfort. He was a creature of the wild, drawn in by firelight, not forged by it. Because of his love for Thornton, he would never steal from him. But from any other man, in any other camp, he would take what he wanted—and vanish unseen, clever and swift.

His face bore scars from many fights, and he fought as fiercely and wisely as ever. Skeet and Nig were too gentle to quarrel, and anyway, they belonged to Thornton. But any strange dog—regardless of breed or courage—either acknowledged Buck’s dominance or faced a fight to the death. And Buck was merciless. He had learned the law of club and fang: there was no mercy, no hesitation. To show weakness was to invite death. Mercy didn’t exist in the old world. It was mistaken for fear—and fear meant failure. Kill or be killed. Eat or be eaten. That was the law, and Buck obeyed it without question.

He was older than his years, older than the breaths he’d drawn. He carried the echoes of ancestors—half-wolves, wolves—within him. They stirred in his blood, urging him, guiding him. They tasted the meat he ate, drank the water he sipped, smelled the wind with him, heard the forest’s secrets, and dreamed through him. When he slept, they walked beside him, shaping his dreams.

And so, day by day, the call of the wild grew louder. Deep in the forest, a voice echoed—mysterious, powerful, impossible to ignore. Whenever he heard it, he felt the urge to turn from the fire, to flee into the trees, to run without knowing where or why. Yet each time he reached the green shade and soft earth, the pull of Thornton brought him back. Only Thornton could hold him.

No one else mattered. Travelers might praise or pet him, but he remained cold. If someone grew too familiar, he would rise and walk away. When Hans and Pete arrived on the long-awaited raft, Buck ignored them until he saw they stood near Thornton. Then, reluctantly, he accepted their presence, taking their offerings as if granting them a favor.

Hans and Pete were men of the earth—simple, clear-sighted, strong. Before the raft reached the mill at Dawson, they understood Buck. They respected his ways, and never forced closeness.

But for Thornton, Buck’s love grew deeper every day. No one else could command him as Thornton could. Only Thornton could put a pack on his back during summer travels. Nothing was too difficult when Thornton asked.

One afternoon, they sat atop a cliff overlooking a sheer drop of three hundred feet. Thornton leaned near the edge, Buck beside him. A sudden impulse seized Thornton—he turned to Hans and Pete. “Jump, Buck!” he shouted, sweeping his arm over the chasm.

The next instant, Buck lunged forward, leaping toward the void. Thornton grabbed him just in time, wrestling him back from the edge as Hans and Pete dragged them both to safety.

“It’s uncanny,” Pete said, catching his breath.

Thornton shook his head. “No. It’s splendid. And terrible. Sometimes… it frightens me.”

“I wouldn’t want to be the man who tried to touch you while he’s near,” Pete declared, nodding at Buck.

“Py Jingo!” Hans added. “Not me either.”

That fear became reality later, at Circle City. “Black” Burton, a cruel and angry man, was arguing with a newcomer at the bar. Thornton stepped in, trying to calm things. Buck lay nearby, watching every move his master made. Without warning, Burton swung a fist—straight from the shoulder. Thornton spun backward, clutching the bar rail to keep from falling.

Then came a sound—not a bark, not a growl, but a roar. Buck rose from the floor, launching himself at Burton’s throat. The man threw up his arm instinctively, blocking the first strike, but Buck drove in again—this time, tearing open his throat. The crowd rushed in, driving Buck back. He prowled the room, snarling, refusing to stop, until surgeons bound Burton’s wound. A miners’ meeting decided Buck had cause to act—and he was cleared. But his name spread across Alaska.

Later, in autumn, Buck saved Thornton’s life in another way. The three men were guiding a narrow poling boat through treacherous rapids on Forty-Mile Creek. Hans and Pete worked along the bank, using a rope to control the boat, while Thornton stayed aboard, pole in hand, shouting directions. Buck ran alongside, eyes fixed on his master.

At one point, a submerged rock jutted into the river. Hans cast off the rope, letting the boat drift past, then ran down the bank to catch it again. But he pulled too sharply—the boat flipped over, and Thornton was thrown into the current, swept downstream toward the worst stretch of water.

Buck jumped in instantly. Three hundred yards downstream, amid swirling chaos, he caught up to Thornton. When the man grabbed his tail, Buck turned toward shore, swimming with all his strength. But progress was slow—the current was too fast. Below, the water roared over jagged rocks like teeth.

Thornton scraped over rocks, bruised, battered. He clung to one slippery stone, releasing Buck. Above the thunder, he shouted: “Go, Buck! Go!”

Buck couldn’t stay. He was swept downstream, fighting hard but unable to turn back. When he heard the command again, he reared up, then obeyed—turning toward shore. He swam powerfully, and Pete and Hans hauled him ashore just before the water became deadly.

They knew Thornton couldn’t last long. They ran upstream, attached a rope to Buck’s neck, and launched him again. He struck out boldly—but not far enough into the current. By the time he reached Thornton, he was already being carried past.

Hans tightened the rope, jerking Buck under the surface. He stayed submerged until he hit the bank, half-drowned. Hans and Pete pounded air into him, bringing him back. He staggered, fell. Then, faintly, Thornton’s voice reached them—weak, desperate. Hearing it, Buck sprang up, racing ahead of the men to the starting point.

Again, the rope was fastened. Again, Buck plunged into the water—but this time, straight into the current. He had learned from his mistake. Hans paid out the line carefully, keeping it taut. Buck swam to a point directly above Thornton, then turned and charged downward like a train.

Thornton saw him coming. When Buck slammed into him with the full force of the current, he wrapped his arms around the dog’s thick neck. Hans snubbed the rope around a tree. Together, Buck and Thornton were yanked under—dragged, choked, smashed against rocks, scraping along the bottom—until they veered to shore.

Thornton woke up belly-down, being shaken violently across a log by Hans and Pete. His first glance was for Buck. Nig howled over his limp body. Skeet licked his wet face, closed eyes. Thornton checked Buck’s injuries—three broken ribs.

“That settles it,” he said. “We camp here.” And they did, until Buck recovered.

That winter, in Dawson, Buck earned another legend. Not as heroic, perhaps—but one that raised his fame even higher. It began in the Eldorado Saloon, where men bragged about their dogs. Buck, because of his record, became the target. Thornton defended him fiercely.

One man claimed his dog could start a sled with five hundred pounds. Another said six hundred. A third, seven hundred.

“Pooh! Buck can start a thousand,” Thornton said calmly.

“And break it out? Walk off with it for a hundred yards?” Matthewson, a Bonanza King, challenged.

“And break it out. And walk off a hundred yards,” Thornton replied.

“Well,” Matthewson said slowly, “I’ve got a thousand dollars that says he can’t.” He slammed a sack of gold dust onto the bar.

Silence. Thornton had been trapped. He didn’t know if Buck could do it. Half a ton—impossible. And he had no money. Neither did Hans or Pete.

“I’ve got a sled outside with twenty fifty-pound sacks of flour,” Matthewson added bluntly.

Thornton looked around, lost. Then his eyes met Jim O’Brien’s—a friend, a Mastodon King. “Can you lend me a thousand?” he whispered.

“Sure,” O’Brien said, slamming down his own sack. “Though I don’t believe it’ll happen.”

The saloon emptied into the street. Men gathered around the sled, frozen solid in the sixty-below-zero cold. Odds were two to one against Buck. A dispute arose over “break out”—did it mean freeing the runners or pulling from a dead stop? Most ruled in Matthewson’s favor. Odds climbed to three to1.

No one believed Buck could do it. Thornton doubted. But the challenge lit a fire in him—the warrior spirit that ignores odds, sees no limits, hears only battle cries.

He gathered Hans and Pete. Their combined savings totaled two hundred dollars. They laid it against Matthewson’s six hundred.

The team was unhitched. Buck was harnessed. He sensed the moment—something great must be done for Thornton. Murmurs rose: “Look at him!” “Magnificent!” He was lean, powerful, 150 pounds of muscle and vitality. His coat gleamed like silk. His mane bristled with energy. His chest and legs were massive, muscles coiled beneath skin like iron.

A man offered eight hundred dollars for him before the test.

Thornton shook his head. He knelt beside Buck, took his head in his hands, cheek to cheek. He didn’t shake him or curse softly. He whispered: “As you love me, Buck. As you love me.”

Buck whined—eager, controlled.

The crowd watched. It felt like magic.

Thornton stood. Buck gripped his mittened hand between his jaws, pressing in, then releasing slowly—like a promise.

“Now, Buck,” Thornton said.

Buck tightened the traces, then let them slacken. He knew the ritual.

“Gee!” Thornton commanded.

Buck swung right, then lunged—taking up the slack, jerking his weight forward. The sled trembled. Cracks snapped under the runners.

“Haw!”

Buck turned left. The sled moved. Then, slowly, steadily, it began to roll.

The crowd gasped. Then roared. Buck pulled it forward—fifty yards, then a hundred. The impossible had happened.

And the legend grew.

When Buck earned sixteen hundred dollars in five minutes for John Thornton, it gave his master the means to settle debts and set out with his companions—Pete and Hans—on a journey into the eastern wilderness in search of a legendary lost mine. This mine was as old as the country itself, whispered about in tales passed down through generations. Many had searched for it; few had found it; and some had vanished without a trace. The story was steeped in sorrow and shadow. No one knew who first built the ramshackle cabin that marked its site. Dying men had sworn to its existence, clutching nuggets of gold unlike any ever seen in the Northland, yet no living soul had ever plundered its riches.

So, with Buck and half a dozen other dogs, they ventured eastward on an unknown trail, hoping to succeed where others had failed. They traveled seventy miles up the Yukon, turned left into the Stewart River, passed the Mayo and the McQuestion, and pressed on until the river narrowed into a stream winding between towering peaks—the spine of the continent.

John Thornton asked little of man or nature. He was fearless of the wild. With just a rifle and a handful of salt, he could vanish into the wilderness and live off it for as long as he pleased. Moving at a slow, deliberate pace like the Indigenous people he admired, he hunted his meals along the way. If he missed, he simply kept going, trusting that food would come when it was meant to. On this journey, their diet was meat—raw and unseasoned—and their sled carried mostly ammunition and tools. Time stretched ahead like an endless plain.

To Buck, it was pure joy—hunting, fishing, wandering through strange lands under open skies. For weeks, they moved steadily forward; then, for weeks more, they paused, camping here and there while the dogs rested and the men dug through frozen earth, panning gravel by the firelight. Sometimes they went hungry; sometimes they feasted wildly, depending on the game and luck. Summer arrived, and they crossed blue mountain lakes on rafts, paddled through rivers carved from standing trees, and climbed or descended valleys hidden from all maps.

Months slipped by. They wandered back and forth across vast, unmapped territories—places untouched by modern hands, though traces of ancient travelers lingered. They crossed snow-swept passes in summer blizzards, stood beneath the midnight sun on bare mountains between timberline and eternal ice, plunged into valleys choked with gnats and flies, and picked strawberries and wildflowers beside glaciers—fruits as sweet as any southern land could offer.

In autumn, they entered a silent lake region—still and mournful, once home to birds but now lifeless. Only wind blew, ice formed in sheltered coves, and waves rippled softly against lonely shores.

Another winter passed as they followed ghost trails of men who had gone before. Once, they found an ancient path cut through the forest—a trail with no beginning and no end. It remained a mystery, like the man who made it and why. Another time, they stumbled upon the ruins of a hunting lodge. Among rotting blankets, Thornton discovered a long-barreled flintlock rifle—an old Hudson’s Bay Company gun from the early days of the Northwest. That was all—no name, no clue to the man who had built the lodge or left the weapon behind.

Spring returned. At last, after all their travels, they did not find the Lost Cabin—but instead, a shallow placer in a wide valley where gold gleamed like butter in the bottom of a pan. They stopped searching. Each day brought thousands in dust and nuggets. The gold was packed into moose-hide sacks, fifty pounds each, stacked like firewood outside their spruce-bough shelter. They worked relentlessly, day after day, as if dreams were chasing real time.

The dogs had nothing to do except haul meat when Thornton killed it. Buck spent long hours sitting by the fire, drifting into visions. More often than before, the image of the short-legged, hairy man came to him—this creature from another world, one he dimly remembered. He watched him sleep by the fire, head tucked between knees, hands clasped above—sleeping fitfully, jerking awake, peering into darkness, adding wood to keep the flames alive. When they walked along the shore, gathering shellfish, the man’s eyes darted everywhere, ready to flee at the first sign of danger. Through the woods they crept silently, Buck close behind. Both were alert—ears twitching, nostrils flaring. The man heard and smelled as keenly as Buck.

He could climb trees with ease, swinging from limb to limb like a monkey, leaping gaps of ten feet without missing a grip. He slept high in branches, gripping tight. Buck remembered nights spent watching him from below, waiting, listening.

And always, echoing in the depths of the forest, was the call—the sound that stirred something deep within Buck. It filled him with unrest, longing, and a strange sweetness. It woke him at night, drew him into the woods, where he barked softly or defiantly, as if challenging it. He’d bury his nose in moss or dark soil, snorting with pleasure at the rich scent of earth. He’d crouch behind fallen logs, wide-eyed, ears pricked, waiting—perhaps hoping to catch the call, though he didn’t know what it was.

Then, suddenly, he’d rise from rest, head lifting, ears erect, and dash into the forest—running for hours through thickets and clearings, drawn by invisible forces. He loved running down dry creek beds, spying on birds, lying in brush for days watching partridges strut. But most of all, he loved running during the dim twilight of summer nights, listening to the hushed murmurs of the woods, reading signs and sounds as if they were words in a book—searching for the mystery that called him, waking or sleeping, day or night.

One night, he woke with a start—eyes wide, nostrils flaring, mane bristling. From the forest came the call—clearer than ever before—a long, mournful howl, unlike any dog’s cry, yet familiar. He knew it. He knew it from before. Without a sound, he bolted through the camp and raced into the trees.

As he neared the sound, he slowed, every movement cautious. Then he saw it: a lean, long-bodied timber wolf, standing on hind legs, nose raised to the sky, howling into the night.

Buck had made no noise. Yet the wolf stopped, sensing him. Buck stepped into the clearing, half-crouched, body tense, tail stiff, feet falling carefully. His posture mixed threat and invitation—just as wild animals do when meeting equals. But the wolf fled.

Buck chased, leaping wildly, driven to catch him. He cornered the animal in a dead-end creek bed blocked by fallen logs. The wolf spun around, pivoting on hind legs like Joe, snarling, bristling, snapping teeth in rapid succession.

But Buck did not attack. Instead, he circled, offering peace. The wolf was wary—Buck was three times his weight, his head higher than the wolf’s shoulders. After a moment, the wolf darted away. The chase began again. Again and again, the wolf was trapped, only to escape when he could. Though weakened, he was fast—yet Buck was faster.

Finally, the wolf sensed no harm. He sniffed noses with Buck. Then they played—nervous, half-shy, like fierce creatures pretending gentleness. After a while, the wolf started moving at a steady lope, clearly leading the way. He showed Buck he was expected to follow. So they ran side by side through the twilight—up the creek bed, into the gorge, over the divide.

On the far slope, they entered a vast, flat land of forests and rivers. They ran hour after hour, the sun rising, warming the air. Buck felt wild joy. He was answering the call at last—running beside his brother of the woods, heading toward the place the call surely came from. Memories flooded back—of this very journey, of this same land, of a time long forgotten. He had done this before. And now, he was doing it again—free, unbound, the earth beneath him, the sky above.

They stopped by a stream to drink. Then Buck remembered John Thornton. He sat down. The wolf looked back, sniffed his nose, nudged him gently—as if urging him onward. But Buck turned and walked slowly back the way they came.

For nearly an hour, the wild brother ran beside him, whining softly. Then he sat, lifted his nose, and howled—a sad, long cry. As Buck continued, the sound faded into the distance.

Thornton was eating dinner when Buck burst into camp, leaping onto him in a frenzy of affection—overturning him, licking his face, biting his hand. “Playing the general tom-fool,” Thornton laughed, shaking him violently, cursing him lovingly.

For two days and nights, Buck never left the camp. He followed Thornton everywhere, watched him eat, saw him to bed and woke him in the morning. But then the call returned—stronger than ever. Restlessness seized him. He dreamed of the wild brother, of the land beyond the divide, of running through endless forests. He wandered again, but the wolf did not return. No mournful howl echoed through the night.

He began sleeping outside, staying away for days. Once, he crossed the divide and entered the land of timber and streams. There, he wandered for a week, searching for signs of the wolf, killing his own food, moving with a long, effortless stride that never tired. He fished for salmon in a broad river flowing toward the sea. There, he met a massive black bear, blinded by mosquitoes while fishing, raging through the woods helpless and furious. The fight was brutal, but Buck won—unleashing the full fury of his blood and instinct.

Two days later, he returned to the kill to find a dozen wolverines fighting over the carcass. He scattered them like chaff. Two fell dead.

The hunger for blood grew stronger. He was a killer—alone, unaided, surviving by strength and cunning in a world where only the strong lived. This pride filled him—radiating from every move, every muscle, every step. His fur seemed even more glorious, his body more powerful. Except for the brown streaks on his muzzle and above his eyes, and the white patch down his chest, he might have been mistaken for a giant wolf—larger than any of his kind.

From his St. Bernard father, he inherited size. From his shepherd mother, form. His muzzle was long, like a wolf’s—but broader, larger. His head, massive and broad, bore the shape of a wolf magnified.

His mind was sharp—wild, cunning, intelligent. His experience, forged in the fiercest trials, made him unmatched. A carnivore on raw meat, he thrived—full of life, energy, virility. When Thornton touched his back, sparks crackled along his coat—each hair discharging static at the contact. Every part of him—nerve, muscle, brain—was tuned to perfection. He reacted instantly to sight and sound—perceiving, deciding, acting in a single breath. He saw, he responded—faster than any dog could dream.

His muscles snapped into action like steel springs. Life surged through him, radiant and untamed, so full it seemed it might burst from his body and flood the world.

“Never was there such a dog,” Thornton said one day, watching Buck leave camp.

“When he was made, the mould was broke,” Pete added.

“Py jingo! I t’ink so mineself,” Hans agreed.

They saw him march out. But they did not see the change that happened the moment he entered the forest. He no longer marched. He became a shadow—silent, cat-footed, slipping between trees. He used every cover, crawled like a snake, struck like one. He stole ptarmigan from nests, killed rabbits asleep, snapped chipmunks mid-flight. Fish in pools couldn’t escape him. Beavers mending dams were too slow.

He killed to eat—not for sport—but he preferred his own kills. Often, he teased squirrels, almost catching them, then letting them go—watching them chatter in terror up the trees.

As fall approached, moose appeared in greater numbers, moving south to warmer valleys. Buck had already taken a young calf, but he craved a greater challenge. One day, on the divide, he found a herd of twenty—led by a great bull, wounded by an arrow. The bull stood over six feet tall, antlers branching to fourteen points, seven feet across. His eyes burned with rage. He roared at Buck.

Guided by ancient instincts, Buck began to separate the bull from the herd. It was hard. He barked and danced just out of reach—too close for safety, too far for attack. The bull charged, hooves smashing the ground. Buck retreated, luring him forward. But each time, younger bulls rushed to defend the leader, forcing Buck back.

There is a patience in the wild—deep, tireless, older than time—that holds the spider still in its web, the snake coiled, the panther hidden. This patience belonged to Buck. He clung to the herd’s flank, slowing their progress, irritating the young bulls, tormenting the cows and calves, driving the wounded bull mad with frustration.

For half a day, it continued. Buck attacked from all sides, a whirlwind of menace. He wore down the herd’s endurance. As the sun sank westward and night fell, the young bulls hesitated more and more to help. Winter was pushing them downward. Their lives mattered more than one wounded bull’s. In the end, they accepted the toll.

At dusk, the bull stood alone, head lowered, watching his herd disappear into the fading light. He could not follow. Before him leaped the fanged terror that would not let him go. Three hundredweight more than half a ton—he had lived a long, strong life. Now, death waited at the teeth of a creature whose head barely reached his knees.

From then on, Buck never left his prey. He denied it rest, water, food. The bull grew weaker, slower. He stood for hours, nose to the ground, ears drooping. Buck took advantage—resting, drinking, growing stronger.

At last, on the fourth day, he pulled the great moose down. For a day and night, he stayed by the kill, eating and sleeping in turns. Then, refreshed, he turned toward camp.

He broke into his easy lope, traveling straight through unfamiliar territory with uncanny certainty—his sense of direction surpassing compass and map.

As he journeyed, he felt a new stir in the land. No longer subtle, it was clear now. Birds sang of it. Squirrels chattered. The breeze carried whispers. He stopped, breathed deeply—reading messages in the air. Something terrible was happening—or had already happened. He quickened his pace.

Crossing the final ridge, he dropped into the valley toward camp—cautious, alert, senses wide open.
