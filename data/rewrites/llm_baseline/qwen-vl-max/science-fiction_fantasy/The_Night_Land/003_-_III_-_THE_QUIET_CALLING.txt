For many years, I had heard whispers—stories carried on the breath of memory and passed from mouth to mouth in the great cities that climbed the thousandth floor of the Pyramid. Across the one thousand, three hundred and twenty tiers of our Last Redoubt, there was a tale that would not die: somewhere beyond the endless night, far out in the desolation of the Night Lands, another refuge existed—a second stronghold where the last remnants of humanity had gathered, fighting against oblivion.

I heard this story everywhere. From the time I turned seventeen, I journeyed through each city for one day, as was the custom of every youth’s training. Three years and two hundred twenty-five days I spent in that pilgrimage—each city a brief pause in a life too short to hold more than fleeting moments of connection. I met many souls along the way—some whose names I still carry like sacred embers in my heart—but none I could keep. Life is measured not by years but by duty, and each of us must return to the work of preserving the Redoubt. Still, even now, I remember their faces, their laughter, the warmth of their hands—though they are gone.

And always, wherever I went, the same legend echoed: *There is another place*. In the libraries of those cities, I found countless writings—ancient records, fragmented histories, fevered speculations—all speaking of a second Redoubt. Once, long ago, these texts were accepted without doubt. But time has worn faith thin. Now, only scholars read them, their eyes skeptical even as they turn the pages. Such is the fate of truth when it lives too long in silence.

Yet I never doubted. Not after the first time I heard the story from Master Monstruwacan himself, who stood atop the Tower of Observation, his mind a beacon among the stars of knowledge. He and I had always been bound by something deeper than mere rank or age. Though he was full-grown and I but a boy, our bond was unbroken. When I reached twenty-one, he granted me a post within the Tower—the highest honor in all the Redoubt. For here, amid the instruments and the silent hum of the aether, we strained to listen to the world beyond the dark, seeking answers no machine could give.

And there was reason for my appointment. I possessed a gift rare among millions: Night-Hearing. It was not a name born of science, but of myth—yet it described what I could do. I could perceive the invisible vibrations of the aether, the faintest echoes of thought sent across the void. Without instruments, I could hear messages that danced through the eternal night—messages clearer, purer, than any device could catch.

And so, over the years, I listened—not just for signals, but for *her*. A voice from before time, a melody woven into the fabric of my soul. Mirdath—beautiful, lost, remembered. She slept within me, whispering in dreams that felt more real than waking. Her voice came not from memory alone, but from longing, from love that had survived the death of the sun and the fall of worlds.

Then, one evening at the fifteenth hour—when Sleep-Time began—I stood alone in the observation chamber, lost in thought. I pondered her, that woman who had lived in an age before light, whose face I could not see but whose voice rang in my bones. And as I dreamed, I fancied she spoke to me—softly, gently, as if from the edge of eternity.

And then—*it came*.

A whisper, trembling through the darkness, brushing against my inner hearing like a breath upon skin. It was not sound, but sensation—an echo from a world buried beneath aeons of silence. I knew it instantly. The voice, the rhythm, the sorrow behind it—it was hers.

But I did not speak. Not yet. I was trained in wisdom, and I knew the danger of false calls. The Evil Forces, the monstrous things that dwelled beyond the walls of the Redoubt, sometimes mimicked voices. Or worse—the House of Silence, that unseen power said to twist minds and steal souls. Only one thing could prove it was truly her: the Master-Word.

So I sent it—not with lips, but with my mind, with every fiber of my being, driving it into the night.

And then—silence.

Then, a throb. A slow, steady pulse vibrating through the aether, echoing around me like a heartbeat in the void. The Master-Word. Answered.

I trembled. My spirit soared. With all the joy I could summon, I called again, not with words, but with thought: *"Mirdath! Mirdath! Mirdath!"*

At that moment, the door opened. Master Monstruwacan stepped into the chamber, his face pale, his eyes wide. He had not heard the call—he lacked Night-Hearing—but he had sensed the disturbance. He had come from the receiving instrument, thinking he’d caught a flicker of the Master-Word—too faint to register, but strong enough to make him search for me.

He saw my face—my eyes alight, my body rigid with awe—and he said nothing. Instead, he waited, listening, as I told him everything: the dream, the memory, the awakening, the call.

He listened. And though he could not hear the aether, he understood. In that age, men spoke of things now deemed madness. They believed in spirits, in dreams that shaped reality, in voices that crossed time. Our ancestors had known such things. We, today, have forgotten. But he—wise, gentle, open—believed.

As I spoke, I kept listening. And then—faint, sweet, almost playful—a laugh. Like wind through flowers. Then nothing.

No more that day.

Yet the memory stayed. And I found myself arguing with scholars, disputing their doubts about the old stories—the Sun, the Days of Light, the world before the Night. They scoffed, calling my tales fairy fables. But I remembered. I *knew*. And I told them anyway.

Master Monstruwacan always listened. Even when I spoke for hours, drawing from the deep well of my Memory-Dreams, he sat in silence, absorbed. Sometimes, when I returned to the present, I found the entire Tower gathered around me—Monstruwacans, assistants, observers—all captivated, forgetting their duties. He would snap back into awareness, chide me softly, and send them away—only to find them later, still whispering, still wondering.

Even beyond the Tower, my words spread. Downward, through the thousand cities, until I reached the Underground Fields—hundred miles beneath the surface, where the ploughboys worked the soil under artificial light. There, I once found a group of them gathered, talking about "the man who remembers the sun."

And indeed, those Fields were a marvel. A pyramid of land, stacked one beneath the other—three hundred and six levels, each smaller than the one below, until the topmost field, four miles square, lay beneath the lowest floor of the Redoubt. Each level was encased in the grey metal of the fortress, pillared, floored, protected. No monster could dig in.

The Earth-Current powered it all—light, heat, life. It ran through the soil, feeding the crops, making the poppies bloom, the corn rustle in the breeze. It had taken a million years to build. The waste from its creation had been dumped into the Crack—the source of the Current—where it vanished into depths no probe could reach.

And in that underground world, air flowed, winds stirred, and people walked, loved, laughed—just as they might in any garden of the old days. I had seen it. And sometimes, in the quiet corners, I thought I heard her voice again—faint, distant, like a sigh on the edge of sleep.

But there was a law: no man could venture into the Night Lands before twenty-two. No woman ever. Those who wished to go must endure three lectures on the horrors of the dark—on the mutilations, the monstrosities, the fates of those who had tried. Only if they remained sane and resolute could they be allowed.

And to each, a capsule was implanted beneath the skin of the left forearm. If trapped, they were to bite it open—so that their spirit might escape in death, preserved by the sanctity of their soul.

This was the price of courage.

And I? I thought of it often. For always, I listened. Twice, I sent the Master-Word into the night—solemnly, carefully. But no more. The Word must not be wasted. Yet I called her name—"Mirdath!"—with my mind, sending it into the dark. Sometimes, I thought I felt a reply—weak, fragile, as if from a dying star.

Then, one afternoon at the thirteenth hour, I stood beside the instruments. Suddenly, the aether thrummed. Not a single note—but a vibration, a ripple, spreading through the void. I made the Sign for Silence. All movement ceased. The men bowed over their breathing-bells, still as stone.

Then—again. A soft thrill. And then, clear and low, a voice in my mind:

My name. The old-earth name. Not the name of this age.

It struck me like lightning. Memories flooded—faces, scenes, a sunlit sky, a hand holding mine. I sent the Master-Word again.

And then—silence.

Then, far off, a beat. Faint, but unmistakable. And then—another throb. Stronger. Closer.

Only I could hear it.

And then—full clarity. A voice, young, clear, trembling:

I turned to the instruments. "Mirdath," I said aloud.

And from the darkness, a reply—swift, tender, perfect:

Not Mirdath. Naani.

And then came the truth. She had read a book. An ancient record. Of a man named Alec, who loved a girl named Mirdath. When she first called, she heard a voice cry *"Mirdath!"*—and it reminded her of the story. So she answered, as if she were the one from the tale.

The romance—my lifelong dream—shattered.

Yet, even in sorrow, I felt a spark. She said something strange: *"Your voice... it should be deeper."*

I froze. In my time, my voice had been deep. But now—after centuries of change—was it different? Could it be that the man in the book was not me? Or was it possible... that I was the man? That someone else had written of me?

I asked her. She hesitated. Said no. But I pressed. And in her confusion, I saw something else: *She didn’t know.* She didn’t know the truth. She only knew the story.

And in that moment, I realized: perhaps the story wasn’t fiction. Perhaps it was memory. Perhaps the past was not dead.

And then, she told me of her world.

Far out in the Night Lands, a second Redoubt stood—a small, three-sided pyramid, barely a mile high. Built long ago, on the shore of a sea that no longer existed. Its founders had fled the chaos, the monsters, the half-human tribes. One man—once exiled from the Great Redoubt for rebellion—had designed it. He had seen our tower, once, and now built a smaller version, aided by four million weary wanderers.

They found the Earth-Current in a valley leading to the sea. They dug a shaft—ten years of labor—and tapped into it. Enough to sustain them.

They built. They guarded. They created instruments. And for ages, they spoke with us—daily, through the aether.

But then—the Current failed. Slowly, steadily. The instruments died. The messages stopped.

And for a million years—maybe more—there was silence.

The people dwindled. Generations passed. The Current weakened. The world grew cold.

Until—recently—the Current surged again. Youth lasted longer. Children were born anew. Hope returned.

And then—Naani, daughter of their Master Monstruwacan, became Sensitive. She heard vibrations. She spoke of them.

They rebuilt the instruments. And now—she had called.

And now—she was speaking to me.

And though she was not Mirdath, and though I was not the man of the book—something deeper had happened.

The silence had broken.

And the Night Land had begun to speak.
