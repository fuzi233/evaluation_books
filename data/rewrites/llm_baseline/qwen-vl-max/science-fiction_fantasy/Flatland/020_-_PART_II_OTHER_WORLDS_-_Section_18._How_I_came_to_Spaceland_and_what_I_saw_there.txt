A terrible dread seized me. Darkness swallowed the world. Then—something beyond sight, a sensation that twisted perception itself. I saw a line that wasn’t a line, a space that defied space. I was myself, yet not. I was no longer certain of being. When I could speak, my voice cracked with terror: “This is madness… or Hell.”

“It is neither,” came the calm reply of the Sphere. “It is knowledge. It is Three Dimensions. Open your eye once more—and look steadily.”

I obeyed. And then—light.

Before me stood a vision beyond imagination. The beauty I had only dreamed of in Flatland—the perfect symmetry, the harmony of form—was now visible, tangible, real. At the center of the Stranger’s being, something lay open to my gaze. Yet I saw no organs, no blood, no inner workings. Only a radiant, unified presence—something beyond words. You, readers of Spaceland, would call it the surface of a Sphere.

Prostrate in awe, I whispered, “How can I see within you, yet see no heart, no lungs, no arteries? How can I behold your inside and yet not see your life?”

“You do not see what you think you see,” replied the Sphere. “You cannot perceive my internal parts. I am not of your kind. If I were a Circle, you might glimpse my insides. But I am composed of many Circles—many in one—what you would call a Sphere. Just as the outer face of a Cube is a Square, so the outer form of a Sphere appears as a Circle to your eyes.”

Though bewildered, I no longer resisted. I bowed in silent reverence. He continued, his voice softer now. “Do not distress yourself if you cannot grasp the mysteries of Spaceland at once. They will unfold with time. Let us begin by returning to the land you knew. Come back with me to Flatland, and I shall show you something you have long reasoned about—but never truly seen: a visible angle.”

“Impossible!” I exclaimed.

Yet, guided by the Sphere, I followed like a dreamer, until his voice halted me again. “Look below—there is your own house, your home, and all who dwell within.”

I looked down—and there it was. My pentagonal home, laid bare before my very eyes. Not inferred, not imagined—but seen. My four sons slept peacefully in the northwestern rooms. My two orphaned grandsons rested to the south. The servants, the butler, my daughter—all in their places. Only my wife, anxious over my absence, had left her chamber, pacing the hall. And the page, roused by my cries, had slipped into my study, pretending to check if I had fainted—while secretly searching the cabinet.

And now I could *see* it all. Even the contents of the cabinet—the two chests of gold, the tablets mentioned earlier. Everything was revealed.

Moved by my wife’s sorrow, I tried to leap down to comfort her—but I could not move. “Do not worry for her,” said my Guide. “She will not wait long. For now, let us survey Flatland.”

Again, I rose through space. As we climbed higher, the world beneath expanded. My city, every house, every creature—tiny, clear, exposed. We ascended further, and the earth’s secrets opened: mines, caverns, hidden depths—all unveiled.

Overwhelmed, I turned to my Companion. “I am become as a God! For in our world, omnividence—seeing all—is said to be the mark of divinity.”

There was a hint of scorn in the Sphere’s voice. “Is that so? Then every thief and outlaw in my world should be worshipped as gods—since each sees as much as you do now. But know this: your wise men are mistaken.”

I asked, “Then is omnividence not divine?”

“No,” he replied. “Even if such sight exists among others, does it make them more just? More merciful? Less selfish? More loving? No. Then how does it make them divine?”

“But mercy and love?” I protested. “Those are women’s traits! And we know a Circle is superior to a Line—knowledge above affection.”

The Sphere paused. “I do not rank virtues. Yet many of the wisest in Spaceland value compassion over intellect, the humble Straight Line over the proud Circle. But enough of this. Look yonder. Do you recognize that structure?”

I gazed afar and saw a vast polygonal building—the General Assembly Hall of Flatland—surrounded by rows of pentagonal homes forming streets. We were approaching the capital.

“We descend,” said the Sphere.

It was morning—the first hour of the first day of the two-thousandth year. As tradition demanded, the highest Circles convened in solemn session, just as they had on the first day of the year 1000 and 0.

One of them—my brother, a perfectly symmetrical Square and Chief Clerk—read aloud from the minutes:

> “Whereas the States have been disturbed by individuals claiming revelations from another world, and attempting to prove them with arguments that inflame minds, it has been unanimously resolved that on the first day of each millenary, strict orders be sent to district Prefects to hunt down such persons. All Isosceles, regardless of degree, are to be destroyed without trial. Regular Triangles are to be scourged and imprisoned. Squares and Pentagons are to be sent to the asylum. Any of higher rank is to be arrested and brought to the Capital for judgment.”

“The Council has spoken,” said the Sphere. “Your fate is sealed—death or imprisonment awaits the preacher of the Gospel of Three Dimensions.”

“No,” I answered. “Now I understand it clearly. This truth is so evident, so undeniable, that even a child could grasp it. Let me descend now and teach them!”

“Not yet,” said my Guide. “Wait. I must fulfill my purpose. Remain here.”

With that, he leaped—gracefully, swiftly—into the sea of Flatland, landing directly amid the circle of Counsellors.

“I come,” he proclaimed, “to announce the existence of a land of Three Dimensions!”

Many of the younger members recoiled in horror as the Sphere’s circular outline widened before them. But the presiding Circle showed no fear. With a gesture, he signaled six low-grade Isosceles from different sides.

“They have him!” they shouted. “No—yes—we have him! He’s going! He’s gone!”

“Lords,” said the President to the junior Circles, “no need for alarm. My secret archives—accessible only to me—record similar events on the last two millennial commencements. Say nothing of this outside the Cabinet.”

He called the guards. “Arrest the policemen. Gag them. You know your duty.”

After silencing the poor officers—unwilling witnesses to a state secret they would never reveal—he addressed the Council once more.

“Our business is concluded. I wish you a happy New Year.”

Before leaving, he turned to my brother, the Clerk, and expressed deep regret—for, by precedent and secrecy, he must condemn him to lifelong imprisonment. Yet, he added, “If you remain silent about today’s event, your life may be spared.”

And so I watched—from the edge of the third dimension—as the world I knew rejected the truth it could not comprehend.
