Mrs. Dashwood listened to Elinor’s account of their former favourite with a heart that could not help but soften at the clearing away of part of his disgrace. She was glad that some portion of the dark was proved not so black as rumour had painted it; she was sorry for him; she even wished him a quieter life than he had earned. Yet no warmth of present pity could re-kindle what the past had burned down. Nothing could give him back to Marianne with faith unbroken, with honour whole. Nothing could sweeten the knowledge of what Marianne had endured through him, nor rinse away the stain of his conduct to Eliza. Whatever compassion might be spared for him could not, for that reason, lessen Colonel Brandon’s claims.

Had Mrs. Dashwood, like Marianne, heard the story from Willoughby’s own lips—had she been met by the tremor of his voice, the forced composure, the expressive misery of a face that can plead when words fail—her compassion might have risen higher. But Elinor neither possessed the power, nor the inclination, to rekindle in another the very agitation she had herself felt on first hearing it. Reflection had cooled her; it had not excused him, only arranged him in his true proportions. She told her mother the plain substance—facts without ornament, tenderness without seduction—because she would not have imagination season the tale into indulgence.

That evening, when the small household sat together, the cottage settled into its familiar sounds: the tick of the clock that measured their uneasy quiet; the low scrape of a chair; the wind worrying the thatch and worrying, too, the mind; and, beneath it, the domestic murmur that always returned—Margaret’s half-whispered questions, the distant clink of a dish being set away, the hearth’s soft crackle. In such ordinary music, which should have soothed them, Marianne, after long sitting with that restless, unquiet thoughtfulness that made stillness itself oppressive, spoke of him again of her own accord.

It was not without effort. Colour rose and fell in her cheek, as if the very name brought heat and then shame; her voice, when she began, wavered like a flame in a draught.

“I wish to assure you both,” said she, “that I see every thing—as you can desire me to do.”

Mrs. Dashwood would have stopped her at once with those tender interruptions by which she tried to wrap pain in gentleness; but Elinor—who truly wished to hear Marianne’s unborrowed judgment, and to know it steadied—made an eager sign that held their mother silent.

Marianne went on slowly. “It is a great relief to me—what Elinor told me this morning—I have now heard exactly what I wished to hear.” For a few moments her voice failed her altogether; only the breath came, uneven, as though the words had lodged in her throat. Then, gathering herself again with more calmness than before, she added, “I am now perfectly satisfied. I wish for no change. I never could have been happy with him, after knowing, as sooner or later I must have known, all this. I should have had no confidence, no esteem. Nothing could have done it away to my feelings.”

“I know it—I know it,” cried Mrs. Dashwood, at last unable to keep back her ardour. “Happy with a man of libertine practices!—With one who so injured the peace of the dearest of our friends, and the best of men!—No—my Marianne has not a heart to be made happy with such a man!—Her conscience, her sensitive conscience, would have felt all that the conscience of her husband ought to have felt.”

Marianne sighed, as if even agreement cost her, and repeated only, “I wish for no change.”

“You consider the matter,” said Elinor, “exactly as a good mind and a sound understanding must consider it; and I dare say you perceive, as well as myself, not only in this, but in many other circumstances, reason enough to be convinced that your marriage must have involved you in many certain troubles and disappointments, in which you would have been poorly supported by an affection, on his side, much less certain.

“Had you married, you must have been always poor. His expensiveness is acknowledged even by himself, and his whole conduct declares that self-denial is a word hardly understood by him. His demands and your inexperience together, on a small—very small income, must have brought on distresses which would not be the less grievous to you, from having been entirely unknown and unthought of before. Your sense of honour and honesty would have led you, I know, when aware of your situation, to attempt all the economy that would appear to you possible: and, perhaps, as long as your frugality retrenched only on your own comfort, you might have been suffered to practice it—but beyond that—and how little could the utmost of your single management do to stop the ruin which had begun before your marriage? Beyond that, had you endeavoured, however reasonably, to abridge his enjoyments, is it not to be feared, that instead of prevailing on feelings so selfish to consent to it, you would have lessened your own influence on his heart, and made him regret the connection which had involved him in such difficulties?”

Marianne’s lips quivered; it was not the picture of poverty that most shook her, but the blunt word Elinor had used, like a knife laid cleanly on a truth she still did not want to feel. She repeated it, as though tasting it and finding it bitter.

“Selfish?” The tone implied, do you really think him that?

“The whole of his behaviour,” replied Elinor, “from the beginning to the end of the affair, has been grounded on selfishness. It was selfishness which first made him sport with your affections; which afterwards, when his own were engaged, made him delay the confession of it, and which finally carried him from Barton. His own enjoyment, or his own ease, was, in every particular, his ruling principle.”

“It is very true,” Marianne said, and the admission seemed to hollow her voice. “My happiness never was his object.”

“At present,” continued Elinor, “he regrets what he has done. And why does he regret it?—Because he finds it has not answered towards himself. It has not made him happy. His circumstances are now unembarrassed—he suffers from no evil of that kind; and he thinks only that he has married a woman of a less amiable temper than yourself. But does it follow that had he married you, he would have been happy? The inconveniences would have been different. He would then have suffered under the pecuniary distresses which, because they are removed, he now reckons as nothing. He would have had a wife of whose temper he could make no complaint, but he would have been always necessitous—always poor; and probably would soon have learned to rank the innumerable comforts of a clear estate and good income as of far more importance, even to domestic happiness, than the mere temper of a wife.”

“I have not a doubt of it,” said Marianne; “and I have nothing to regret—nothing but my own folly.”

“Rather say your mother’s imprudence, my child,” cried Mrs. Dashwood; “she must be answerable.”

Marianne would not let her proceed; and Elinor, satisfied that each felt her own error, and unwilling to send them turning their minds over old griefs till they bruised themselves anew, resumed the first subject.

“One observation may, I think, be fairly drawn from the whole of the story—that all Willoughby’s difficulties have arisen from the first offence against virtue, in his behaviour to Eliza Williams. That crime has been the origin of every lesser one, and of all his present discontents.”

Marianne assented with a feeling that did not spend itself in words; and Mrs. Dashwood, taking fire from the thought, began to enumerate Colonel Brandon’s injuries and merits with all the warmth that friendship and design could dictate together. Yet Marianne did not look as if much of it reached her. Her attention seemed to drift, caught by the same inward current that had so long carried her away from whatever was said beside her.

In the two or three days that followed, Elinor saw, as she had expected, that Marianne did not go on gaining strength as she had done. Her resolution remained; her effort to appear easy and cheerful continued; and that was enough, for Elinor could trust time to do what argument could not. The cottage life settled itself again into a routine—quietly taken up, quietly laid down. Margaret returned, and the family were restored to their usual completeness, like a table reset after disturbance: everything in its place again, though the appetite was changed. They were not pursuing their studies with quite the vigour of their first weeks at Barton, but they planned a vigorous prosecution of them in future, as people do who mean to believe tomorrow will be steadier than today.

Elinor, however, grew impatient for tidings of Edward. Since leaving London she had heard nothing—nothing new of his plans, nothing certain even of his present abode. Letters had passed between her and her brother on Marianne’s illness; and in the first of John’s there had been this sentence: “We know nothing of our unfortunate Edward, and can make no enquiries on so prohibited a subject, but conclude him to be still at Oxford;” which was all the intelligence Edward’s own name afforded her, for in the later letters it was not even mentioned.

She was not, however, doomed to long ignorance of his measures.

One morning, their man-servant had been sent to Exeter on business; and when, waiting at table, he had answered his mistress’s questions about the errand, he added, with the careless certainty of one who thinks himself the bearer of ordinary news:

“I suppose you know, ma’am, that Mr. Ferrars is married.”

The words, dropped like a stone into still water, struck every face at once. Marianne started violently, her eyes fastened on Elinor; she saw Elinor turn pale; and then Marianne fell back in her chair in hysterics.

Mrs. Dashwood, who had looked the same way at the same instant, was shocked by what she read in Elinor’s countenance; and then, in the very next moment, equally distressed by Marianne’s condition, she scarcely knew on which child she ought to fix her care. The servant, seeing only that Miss Marianne was taken ill, had sense enough to call one of the maids; and with Mrs. Dashwood’s help they supported Marianne into the other room. The passageway carried their hurried footsteps sharply, the house’s usual hush cut through by the sudden rustle of skirts and the quick, anxious instructions—sound itself turning, for a moment, into a kind of pain.

By the time Marianne was rather better, Mrs. Dashwood left her to Margaret and the maid and returned to Elinor. Elinor, though still much disordered, had recovered enough command of herself to be beginning, in a voice that scarcely sounded like her own, to question Thomas about his intelligence. Mrs. Dashwood immediately took the trouble on herself, sparing Elinor the effort of asking what she dreaded to hear answered.

“Who told you that Mr. Ferrars was married, Thomas?”

“I see Mr. Ferrars myself, ma’am, this morning in Exeter, and his lady too—Miss Steele as was. They was stopping in a chaise at the door of the New London Inn, as I went there with a message from Sally at the Park to her brother, who is one of the post-boys. I happened to look up as I went by the chaise, and so I see directly it was the youngest Miss Steele; so I took off my hat, and she knew me and called to me, and inquired after you, ma’am, and the young ladies, especially Miss Marianne, and bid me I should give her compliments and Mr. Ferrars’s, their best compliments and service, and how sorry they was they had not time to come on and see you, but they was in a great hurry to go forwards, for they was going further down for a little while; but howsever, when they come back, they’d make sure to come and see you.”

“But did she tell you she was married, Thomas?” Mrs. Dashwood pressed, as if there were still some small hope of mistake, some misheard word to undo what had been said.

“Yes, ma’am. She smiled, and said how she had changed her name since she was in these parts. She was always a very affable and free-spoken young lady, and very civil behaved. So I made free to wish her joy.”

“Was Mr. Ferrars in the carriage with her?”

“Yes, ma’am, I just see him leaning back in it, but he did not look up;—he never was a gentleman much for talking.”

Elinor’s heart could easily account for his not putting himself forward; and Mrs. Dashwood, by the sudden fixedness of her daughter’s eyes, probably found the same explanation without being told.

“Was there no one else in the carriage?”

“No, ma’am, only they two.”

“Do you know where they came from?”

“They come straight from town, as Miss Lucy—Mrs. Ferrars told me.”

“And are they going farther westward?”

“Yes, ma’am—but not to bide long. They will soon be back again, and then they’d be sure and call here.”

Mrs. Dashwood looked at Elinor; but Elinor knew better than to expect them. She recognized the whole of Lucy in the message—the airy civility, the careful display, the triumph dressed up as kindness—and was very confident Edward would never come near them. In a low voice, meant only for her mother, she observed that they were probably going down to Mr. Pratt’s near Plymouth.

Thomas’s intelligence seemed over, yet Elinor’s face, as if compelled to drink what harmed her, still looked as though it wished to hear more.

“Did you see them off, before you came away?” asked Mrs. Dashwood.

“No, ma’am—the horses were just coming out, but I could not bide any longer; I was afraid of being late.”

“Did Mrs. Ferrars look well?”

“Yes, ma’am, she said how she was very well; and to my mind she was always a very handsome young lady—and she seemed vastly contented.”

Mrs. Dashwood could think of no other question. Thomas and the tablecloth, now alike needless, were dismissed.

Marianne sent word that she should eat nothing more. Mrs. Dashwood’s and Elinor’s appetites were equally gone—as if the very notion of food, of comfort taken in at the mouth, had become ridiculous while grief sat heavy in the chest. Margaret, with the straightforward hunger of youth and the baffled resilience of one who cannot live on sorrow, might think herself fortunate that, with all the uneasiness her sisters had lately suffered and all the reason they had had to neglect their meals, she had never yet been obliged to go without her dinner before.

When dessert and wine were arranged and then left untouched, and Mrs. Dashwood and Elinor were alone together, they remained long in a sameness of thoughtfulness and silence. The house’s ordinary sounds—clock, fire, the faint creak of settling boards—seemed suddenly too loud, as if every small domestic noise insisted on the world continuing, when all feeling demanded it stop. Mrs. Dashwood feared to hazard any remark; she ventured not to offer consolation. She now found that she had erred in relying on Elinor’s representation of herself, and concluded—justly—that everything had been softened before, to spare her mother additional unhappiness while she suffered for Marianne. She saw she had been misled by Elinor’s careful, considerate attention into thinking the attachment—which she had once understood so well—much slighter than she had been wont to believe, or than it was now proved to be. She feared that, under this persuasion, she had been unjust, inattentive—nay, almost unkind—to her Elinor; that Marianne’s affliction, because more acknowledged and more immediately before her, had too much engrossed her tenderness, and led her away to forget that in Elinor she might have a daughter suffering almost as much—certainly with less self-provocation, and greater fortitude.
