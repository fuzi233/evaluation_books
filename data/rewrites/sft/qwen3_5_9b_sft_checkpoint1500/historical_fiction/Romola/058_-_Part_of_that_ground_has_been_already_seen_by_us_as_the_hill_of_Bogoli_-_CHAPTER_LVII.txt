There was no room for doubt in Tito's mind regarding his security; indeed, his safety was the direct fruit of three months of meticulous calculation. During this interval, he had anticipated the inevitable exposure of the Medicean conspirators and had spent every moment fortifying his position. He had expanded his network of influence across Rome and Milan, positioning himself as the vital conduit for clandestine intelligence and subtle counter-moves against both the friar and the popular faction. Simultaneously, he had cultivated an image of unwavering loyalty to the popular cause, presenting subtle proofs that his political convictions aligned perfectly with theirs. Yet, paradoxically, he had not withdrawn his services from the Medici; rather, he had become more indispensable to them, seeking exclusive trust through a masterful display of utility.

Maintaining this triple deception was effortless for Tito. The Medici, by admitting the principle of duplicity into their own conduct, had stripped themselves of any moral standard by which to judge a colleague lacking their hereditary bonds, alliances, and intense prejudices. To deceive an enemy was considered honorable strategy; to deceive a friend was viewed as a base betrayal they felt no urge to commit. By exploiting Tito's agile abilities, they remained oblivious to the fact that his lack of traditional attachment—their greatest convenience—was also the absence of the primary guarantee of mutual honor found within their own circle. Furthermore, the *Arrabbiati*, the aristocratic enemies of Savonarola, operated a web of espionage where deep hypocrisy was the currency of the realm, demanding exorbitant fees for silence. Suspecting an agent merely because he played a dual role would have been a failure of logic on their part. Conversely, the *Piagnoni* of the popular party, driven by the directness of energetic conviction, were inclined to believe Tito's professed adherence to their cause precisely because he feigned no religious sympathy.

Consequently, the last three months had been a period of flattering triumph for Tito. His ultimate ambition, however, was not the immediate victory of his allies but the securing of a future post in either Rome or Milan. A growing resolve took hold in him to abandon Florence for one of those great capitals where life was less arduous and the rewards of talent and learning more splendid. Currently, the scales tipped toward Milan; if he could render specific services to Duke Ludovico Sforza within the year, the prospects at the Milanese court would eclipse the allure of Rome.

The revelation of the Medicean conspiracy, therefore, was a scenario Tito had foreseen, though not the manner of its execution. The arrest of Lamberto dell'Antella, carrying a damning letter and harboring bitter rancor against the Medici, was an incalculable variable. Despite the careful pretexts guarding his agency, Tito could not escape implication in such a wide discovery. However, his sharp mind quickly charted the course that would ensure his safety with minimal discomfort. While it is pleasant to remain unblemished, even intact skin remains sensitive to the atmosphere.

His calculations proved accurate. That night, before returning home, Tito had secured the three outcomes he desired: freedom from all proceedings regarding his alleged complicity with the Medici, the retention of his secretaryship for another year, and, crucially, the classification of the price he had paid for these guarantees as a state secret. This price would have seemed exorbitant to many, and Tito himself would have preferred not to pay it.

First, he targeted the mind of Francesco Valori. As one of the Ten under whom Tito held his secretaryship and a member of the special council investigating the plot, Valori was the key. Known as the head of the *Piagnoni*, Valori possessed fine qualities incompatible with nothing, including violent partisanship, an arrogant temper that alienated friends, and a fierce personal animosity toward Bernardo del Nero. In a brief, private interview, sealed by a pledge of secrecy, Tito confessed his agency for the Medici. He spoke frankly of his motives, declaring that he had always deemed their efforts futile and that he sincerely preferred the maintenance of the popular government. He feigned confidence in Valori regarding his personal dislike for Bernardo del Nero. Finally, he presented the critical offer: there existed another Medicean plot, centered on Piero de' Medici gathering forces in Siena and Romagna, for which he could procure documentary evidence by traveling there, provided he obtained certain conditions from the government. For this to succeed, his reputation as a Medicean agent had to remain unshaken, necessitating that his role as a source of information to the authorities be buried in profound secrecy. Before he could incur the unpleasant consequences of acting against his friends, he required absolute assurance of immunity from prosecution and protection from the loss of his office.

To Valori, these propositions did not sound as they do to us today. Valori's mind was not bent on weighing Tito's conduct with nuance; it was intensely focused on procuring an extreme sentence against the five prisoners. He foresaw immense efforts to save them and believed that, for public grounds, the evidence against them needed to be overwhelming enough to alarm all well-meaning citizens to the dangers of clemency. In the legal spirit of the age, evidence was a commodity accessible only through foul means. Torturing a few to confess everyone's guilt was a step toward justice, and the appearance of a traitor was often necessary to validate the next step. Lamberto dell'Antella had been tortured to extract what he already knew willingly, yet additional, stronger facts were desired, particularly against Bernardo del Nero, who had thus far refrained from betraying the plot despite failing to discourage it. The welfare of Florence, Valori zealously believed, demanded that Bernardo's guilt be cast in the starkest light. Perhaps Valori was not fully aware that the fervor of his zeal was fueled by hatred. He resolved to accept Tito's proposal, presenting it to his colleagues without revealing Tito's name, and successfully swayed them to his view. Late in the day, Tito was granted an audience with the Special Council. By revealing another plot designed to insure the mastery of Florence for Piero de' Medici, scheduled for mid-August, he caused a deep sensation. He argued that documentary evidence on this subject would clarify the right course above all else. He received a commission to depart for Siena at break of day and, departing from the council chamber, carried with him a written guarantee of his immunity and the retention of his office.

Among the twenty Florentines who lowered their grave eyes upon Tito as he stood gracefully before them, speaking of startling matters with easy periphrasis and an apparently unaffected admission of motives falling short of the highest, several minds were not wholly preoccupied. They silently revised their judgment, concluding that this ingenious and serviceable Greek was henceforth to be utilized for public necessities rather than private intimacy. Unprincipled men were assets, allowing those with scruples to keep their hands relatively clean in a world saturated with dirty work. Indeed, it was unclear to the respectable brains of Florence, unless they shared the Friar's extravagant belief in earthly purity, how life could be conducted in any sphere without employing human instruments whom it would not be unbecoming to kick or spit upon while paying their wages. Some of these same men, passing a tacit verdict on Tito, were shortly to engage in a memorable transaction impossible to carry through without a level of unscrupulosity as decisive as his. Yet, as the poet Pulci had noted for them, "Betrayal pleases many, / But no one likes the traitor ill." The same society erected a gibbet for the murderer and a gibbet for the martyr, offered execrations to dastardly acts and equally loud hisses to words of generous truth or just insight—a mixed condition signaling not hopelessness, but struggling order.

For Tito himself, he was not unaware that he had sunk slightly in the estimation of those who had accepted his services. He possessed a degree of self-contemplation inherent to the habit of acting on well-considered reasons, regardless of their quality. If he could have chosen, he would have declined to see himself disapproved by men of the world. He had never intended to be disapproved, conducting himself so ably that if he opposed others' standards, they would remain unaware. The barrier between himself and Romola had been raised by the impossibility of such concealment with her. He shrank from condemnatory judgments as from a climate to which he could not adapt. However, reality was not as plastic as the hands of cleverness wished, and events had turned inconveniently. He harbored no rancor against Messer Bernardo del Nero; he held a personal liking for Lorenzo Tornabuoni and Giannozzo Pucci. He had served them ably, and if their party had won, he would have deserved high reward. Was he to relinquish the agreeable fruits of life solely because their party had failed? His proffer of additional proof against them would likely have no influence on their fate; in fact, he felt convinced they would escape extreme consequences. Yet, had he not given it, his own fortunes, which promised a magnificent fabric, would have been utterly ruined. What motive could any man possess but self-interest?
