As Harry Warrington recounted his tales of adventure to his newfound kin, Lady Bernstein—possessed of a sharp wit and a worldly wisdom—formed her judgments upon the characters and events described. If her verdict was not wholly favorable, it is a testament to the imperfection of mankind that human life is rarely entirely pleasant or profitable. The court-lady recoiled at the mere idea of her American cousin's rustic existence; such a life would prove wearisome to any bred in the city. Yet little Madam Warrington knew no better, content with her lot as she was with herself. While epicures disdain coarse fare, it does not follow that Hodge suffers misery over his bread and bacon. Madam Warrington led a life of duties, humdrum yet pleasing to her spirit. A brisk woman of business, she kept the affairs of her vast estate under her immediate gaze. No pie was baked at Castlewood without her little finger stirring the pot; she directed the maids, supervised the kitchen wenches, rode afield on her pony, and oversaw the overseers and enslaved hands in the tobacco and corn fields. If a slave fell ill, she visited his quarters in any weather, administering remedies with great resolve. She possessed a tome of old-fashioned receipts, a distillery for waters and elixirs, and a medicine chest that terrified her neighbors, who trembled at the prospect of her decoctions and pills.

A century prior, Virginia held scarce any towns; the gentry's establishments were little villages inhabited by themselves and their vassals. Rachel Esmond ruled Castlewood like a miniature queen, while neighboring "princes" governed their domains. Many were needy potentates, living plentifully yet roughly, with domestics whose liveries were often ragged. They kept open houses, welcoming every stranger, and were proud, idle, and fond of field sports becoming to gentlemen of noble lineage. The Widow of Castlewood was as hospitable as her neighbors and a better economist than most. Though many would have shared her life-interest in the estate to serve as fathers to her boys, where lay a man worthy of a ladyship of her exalted birth? Gossip spoke of installing the Duke of Cumberland as viceroy or even King of America. Madam Warrington's gossips laughed, suggesting she waited for him. With grave dignity, she remarked that persons of his royal blood had already offered alliance to the Esmond family.

Under her command served an officer's widow, previously named, who had been Madam Esmond's school companion, just as her late husband had been the regimental friend of the late Mr. Warrington. At the Kensington Academy, where Rachel Esmond received her education, English girls teased and tormented the little American stranger, mocking the princified airs she adopted from youth. Fanny Parker, however, defended and befriended her. Both married ensigns in Kingsley's Regiment and became tenderly attached, exchanging letters filled with "my Fanny" and "my Rachel." Tragically, Fanny's husband died in sad straits, leaving no provision for his widow or infant child. Later, during one of Captain Franks's annual voyages, he brought Mrs. Mountain aboard the *Young Rachel* to Virginia.

There was ample room at Castlewood House, and Mrs. Mountain served to enliven the place. She played cards with the mistress, possessed some musical knowledge to assist the eldest boy, laughed with guests, attended to strangers' chambers, and presided over the presses and linens. A kind, brisk, jolly-looking widow, more than one unmarried colonial gentleman sought to change her name for his own. Yet she chose to retain "Mountain," perhaps because it had brought her no fortune. "One marriage was enough," she declared, noting that Mr. Mountain had amiably spent her small fortune and his own. Her final trinkets paid for his funeral, and as long as Madam Warrington would keep her, she preferred a home without a husband to any offer made to her in Virginia. The two ladies quarreled plentifully yet loved one another deeply; they made up differences only to fall out again, reconciled once more. When either boy fell ill, each lady vied with the other in maternal tenderness. In his final days, Mrs. Mountain's cheerfulness and kindness were greatly appreciated by the Colonel, whose memory Madam Warrington cherished above all living persons. Thus, whenever Captain Franks asked if she intended to return on his next voyage, she declined, proposing to stay another year.

When suitors approached Madam Warrington, she received their compliments kindly, often inquiring if it was Mrs. Mountain they sought. She used her best offices for Mountain, declaring that Fanny—a girl of good English family—would make any gentleman happy. Should a suitor insist he courted her daughter rather than her dependent, she would curtsy gravely, confess her utter mistake, and remind him that the daughter of the Marquis of Esmond lived for her people and sons, showing no intent to alter her condition. Had we not read how Queen Elizabeth, a sensible woman of business, inspired both terror and love? So the little Virginian princess had her favorites, accepted their flatteries, grew weary of them, and treated them with cruelty or kindness suited to her wayward imperial humor. Compliments, however slight, she graciously received as her due. Her foibles were so well known that wags practiced upon them. Rattling Jack Firebrace of Henrico County held free quarters for months, a favorite because he versified stolen from pocket-books. Tom Humbold of Spotsylvania wagered fifty hogsheads against five that he would induce her to institute an Order of Knighthood, and he won.

The elder boy observed these freaks and chafed at them. From early days, he revolted against flattery, striving to expose it with juvenile satire. Consequently, his mother would say gravely, "The Esmonds were always of a jealous disposition, and my poor boy takes after my parents." George hated Jack Firebrace and Tom Humbold, whereas Harry sported with them, engaging in fowling, fishing, cock-fighting, and enjoying the rural fun.

One winter, after dismissing their first tutor, Madam Esmond took the boys to Williamsburg for education. There, it proved fortunate for the family to hear the famous Mr. Whitfield preach. Unlike many neighboring provinces, Virginia was a Church of England colony; clergymen were state-paid with glebes, yet lacking a bishop, colonists imported divines from the mother country. These arrivals were often noblemen's hangers-on or insolvent parsons seeking a living, bringing stained cassocks to the colony. It is no wonder Whitfield's great voice stirred those whom harmless Mr. Broadbent, the Williamsburg chaplain, could never awaken. Initially, the boys were as excited as their mother, singing hymns and listening with fervor. If Whitfield had stayed longer, Harry and George probably would have worn black coats instead of epaulettes. The simple boys communicated their experiences, constantly watching for the sacred "call," thrilled by the fervor sweeping Protestant England.

However, Mr. Whitfield could not remain with the small Williamsburg congregation forever. His mission was to enlighten the benighted church from east to west, trumpeting truth to slumbering sinners. Nevertheless, he comforted the widow with precious letters, promising a tutor capable of teaching not only profane learning but also strengthening the faith. Soon, a chosen vessel arrived from England. Young Mr. Ward possessed a voice as loud as Whitfield's and talked almost as readily and at length. Nights and evenings echoed with his exhortations; domestic negroes crept to doors to listen, darkening porch windows with their crisp heads. Most notably, Mr. Ward influenced the "black sheep" of the Castlewood flock. These woolly lambs were immensely affected by his words, and when he gave out a hymn, a negro chorus rang out across the Potomac—a sound unheard in the Colonel's time, for the worthy gentleman suspected all cassocks and refused controversy with clergymen save over backgammon. Where money was needed for charity, no man was more ready. The good Virginian clergyman, who loved backgammon, asserted that the Colonel's charity covered his shortcomings.

Ward was handsome, and his preaching pleased Madam Esmond immediately, satisfying her as much as Whitfield's. Women a hundred years ago were credulous, eager to admire and believe, imagining excellences in objects of their devotion. For weeks, nay months, Madam Esmond never tire of hearing Mr. Ward's glib voice and voluble commonplaces. As was her wont, she insisted neighbors attend and be converted. Her young favorite, Mr. Washington, she eagerly sought to influence, pressing him repeatedly to stay at Castlewood and benefit spiritually. Yet the young gentleman found particular business calling him home or away, ordering his horse every evening when Mr. Ward's exercises commenced. And what boys are just to their pedagogue? The twins quickly grew tired, even rebellious.

They found him a bad scholar, a dull fellow, and ill-bred. George knew more Latin and Greek than his master, catching him in perpetual blunders and false quantities. Harry, taking greater liberties, mimicked Ward's manner of eating and talking until Mrs. Mountain and Madam Esmond were forced to laugh, and little Fanny Mountain crowed with delight. Madam Esmond might have exposed him as a vulgar quack but for her sons' opposition, which she countered with indomitable will.
