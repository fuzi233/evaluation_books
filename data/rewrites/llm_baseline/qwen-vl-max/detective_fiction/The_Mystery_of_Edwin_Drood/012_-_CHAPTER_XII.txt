As evening settles over Cloisterham, Mr. Thomas Sapsea—now Mayor of the town—often takes a stroll through the Cathedral Close, where he enjoys the illusion of authority. He walks with hands clasped behind his back, puffing out his chest as if ownership of the churchyard were a personal achievement. He particularly likes to pass by the railings where his own inscription stands proudly: “In memory of Mrs. Sapsea, who was publicly awarded a prize for her meritorious conduct.” He imagines strangers reading it and blushing in retreat, as though they’ve been caught in some moral transgression. To him, this is proof that society functions best when led by men like himself—men of vision, influence, and public service.

His new office has elevated his status, but not his wisdom. He believes mayors are essential pillars of civilization, even claiming credit for inventing the metaphor. He dreams of knighthood for delivering speeches—especially those that elevate English grammar into explosive rhetoric. He is certain he could "go up" with an address, and so might be knighted. Sir Thomas Sapsea! The salt of the earth, indeed.

He has grown fond of Mr. Jasper since their first meeting at the gatehouse, where they shared port, conversation, backgammon, beef, salad, and music. Jasper played the piano, singing old patriotic tunes—songs about George the Third and Britain’s divine right to conquer every island and continent under heaven. It was not mere entertainment; it was a declaration of national superiority, delivered with fervor. Mr. Sapsea admired this: youth who respects elders, who sings no foreign nonsense, but only the true spirit of the realm.

Tonight, walking slowly near the churchyard, Sapsea turns a corner and finds the Dean conversing with the Verger and Jasper. He bows deeply, instantly transformed into a man of ecclesiastical gravity—more reverent than any archbishop.

“You’re clearly writing a book about us,” says the Dean, smiling. “We are ancient, and we ought to make a good story. We lack wealth, but perhaps you’ll mention our misfortunes.”

Mr. Tope chuckles at the suggestion.

“I have no such intention,” replies Jasper. “It’s merely a whim. And even that is more due to Mr. Sapsea than to me.”

“How so?” asks the Dean, nodding toward the Mayor.

“I’m not sure,” says Sapsea, looking around as if searching for clues. “To what does Your Reverence refer?”

“Durdles,” murmurs the Dean.

“Ay!” echoes the Dean. “Durdles, Durdles!”

“It’s true,” explains Jasper. “My interest in him began when Mr. Sapsea introduced us. His knack for drawing out oddities, for uncovering hidden depths—well, it made me look at Durdles differently. You wouldn’t be surprised if you’d seen Sapsea handle him in his own parlour.”

“Oh!” says Sapsea, catching the compliment with delight. “Yes, yes. That’s what you mean? I brought them together. I consider Durdles a Character.”

“And one you turn inside out with just a few strokes,” adds Jasper.

“Not quite,” says Sapsea, puffing slightly. “I may have some influence. Some insight. But remember, I’ve seen the world.” He steps behind the Dean to examine his coat buttons closely.

“Well,” says the Dean, glancing about, “I hope you’ll use your understanding of Durdles to advise him not to break our Choir-Master’s neck. We can’t afford it. His voice—and his head—are too valuable.”

Mr. Tope laughs respectfully, then falls into quiet murmurs, implying that any gentleman would welcome such a compliment—even if it came with a broken neck.

“I shall answer for Mr. Jasper’s safety,” declares Sapsea loftily. “I’ll tell Durdles to be careful. He listens to me. How is he in danger now?”

“Only because I’m planning a moonlight walk with him through the tombs, vaults, towers, and ruins,” says Jasper. “You suggested it when you brought us together—remember? You said, as a lover of the picturesque, it might interest me.”

“I remember!” exclaims Sapsea, though he clearly does not.

“Taking your advice,” continues Jasper, “I’ve walked with him during the day. Tonight, we go at night.”

“And here he is,” says the Dean.

Indeed, Durdles appears, slouching forward with a bundle in hand. Seeing the Dean, he removes his hat, then begins to move away. But Sapsea stops him.

“Mind you take care of my friend,” he commands.

“What friend o’ yourn is dead?” asks Durdles. “No orders come in for any friend o’ yourn.”

“My live friend,” says Sapsea.

“Oh, him?” Durdles shrugs. “He can take care of himself, Mister Jarsper.”

“But you must take care of him too,” insists Sapsea.

Durdles glares, then surveys him from head to toe. “With all due respect to the Dean, if you’ll mind your own business, Mr. Sapsea, Durdles will mind his own.”

“You’re out of temper,” says Sapsea, winking at the others as if to say: *See how I handle him.*

“I admit it,” replies Durdles. “I don’t like liberties.”

Sapsea winks again, satisfied. Then he turns and stalks off.

Durdles gives the Dean a good evening, puts his hat back on, and mutters, “You’ll find me at home, Mister Jarsper, as agreed. I’m going home to clean myself.” Then he disappears into the shadows.

This claim—to clean himself—is one of Durdles’ strange rituals. Yet he remains perpetually dusty, his clothes unchanged, his boots caked with grime. No trace of cleanliness ever shows.

The lamplighter arrives, climbing his ladder with quick steps, lighting the lamps along the Close. His little ladder, a fixture under the cathedral’s shadow for generations, stands as a symbol of continuity. The Dean goes to dinner, Mr. Tope to tea, and Jasper to his piano.

There, in dim firelight, he sings choir music softly, beautifully, for hours—until darkness falls and the moon begins to rise.

Then he closes the piano, changes into a pea-jacket, slips a wicker bottle into his pocket, and dons a low-crowned hat. He leaves quietly. Why so silently? There’s no obvious reason. But something within him stirs—something dark and deep.

He makes his way to Durdles’ unfinished house—a hole in the city wall. A light glows inside. He moves cautiously among the gravestones, monuments, and rubble, already touched by the rising moon. Two saws remain stuck in stone blocks, like silent sentinels. In the shadows, two skeleton figures seem ready to carve the next tombstones—perhaps for the next two souls destined to die in Cloisterham. One wonders which of them it might be.

“Ho! Durdles!”

The light shifts. Durdles appears at the door. He seems to have been cleaning himself—with a bottle, jug, and tumbler. No soap or water in sight. Only alcohol.

“Are you ready?”

“I am ready, Mister Jarsper. Let the old ones come out if they dare. My spirit is ready.”

“Animal spirits or ardent?”

“The one’s the t’other,” says Durdles. “I mean both.”

He takes a lantern from a hook, pockets matches, and they leave—dinner-bundle and all.

A strange expedition. Durdles, always prowling among graves like a ghoulish wanderer, has no need of company. But that the Choir-Master should join him—seeking moonlit effects in such company—is puzzling indeed.

“Watch out for that mound by the yard-gate, Mister Jarsper.”

“I see it. What is it?”

“Lime.”

Jasper stops. Durdles lags behind.

“Quick-lime?” asks Jasper.

“Ay,” says Durdles. “Quick enough to eat your boots. With a little stirring, quick enough to eat your bones.”

They continue, passing the red windows of the Travellers’ Twopenny, then stepping into the moonlit Monks’ Vineyard. They reach Minor Canon Corner, half in shadow, half in light.

A house door shuts. Two men emerge: Mr. Crisparkle and Neville.

Jasper freezes. A sudden smile crosses his face. He places a hand on Durdles’ chest, stopping him.

At this end of the corner, the shadow is deep. There’s a low dwarf wall—the last remnant of an old garden—blocking their path. Jasper and Durdles halt behind it.

“They’re just walking,” whispers Jasper. “They’ll come into the moonlight soon. Stay quiet. If they see us, they’ll stop us—or want to join us.”

Durdles nods. He pulls out fragments from his bundle and munches. Jasper folds his arms atop the wall, chin resting on them, watching.

But he does not watch the Minor Canon. He watches Neville—intently, fixedly—as if his gaze were a trigger, ready to fire.

A sense of deadly power radiates from Jasper’s face. Even Durdles pauses mid-chew, staring at him.

Meanwhile, Crisparkle and Neville walk back and forth, talking quietly. Their words are fragmented, but Jasper catches his name several times.

“This is the first day of the week,” Crisparkle says clearly. “And the last day of the week is Christmas Eve.”

“You may be certain of me, sir.”

Echoes carry the words. Then, as they approach, the sound becomes muffled. But the word “confidence” breaks through—shattered, yet recognizable.

Later, a reply: “Not deserved yet, but shall be, sir.”

Then, as they turn away, Jasper hears again: “Remember that I said I answered for you confidently.”

The voices fade. They disappear into the moonlight at the far end.

Only when they’re gone does Jasper move. Then he bursts into laughter—loud, uncontrollable laughter.

Durdles, still chewing, stares in confusion. Jasper buries his face in his arms, laughing wildly. Finally, Durdles swallows the food in his cheek, as if surrendering to indigestion.

After dark, these secluded corners are nearly lifeless. Even during the day, there’s little activity. At night, the silence is profound. The High Street runs parallel, lively and well-lit, while the Cathedral grounds lie hushed—haunted, almost. People avoid them after dusk. Ask a hundred citizens if they believe in ghosts, and ninety-nine will say no. But ask them to choose between the eerie precincts and the busy street at night, and ninety-nine will pick the longer route.

Why? Not superstition alone—though some swear they’ve seen a ghostly woman with a child and a rope dangling from her neck—but something deeper: the instinctive fear of the living encountering the dead. A feeling that if spirits ever appear, this place would be their natural haunt. So people flee.

Now, Jasper and Durdles pause before descending into the crypt through a small side door—Durdles holding the key. The moonlight bathes the scene. No one else is in sight. The tide of life seems held back by the gatehouse. Beyond, the murmur of life continues. But no wave passes the archway—only the red glow of Jasper’s lamp, like a lighthouse warning.

They enter, lock the door, descend the rough steps, and step into the crypt.

No lantern is needed. Moonlight streams through bare windows, casting patterns on the floor. Heavy pillars cast deep shadows, but between them run lanes of silver light.

They walk slowly. Durdles talks of the “old uns” still buried beneath the walls, slapping a stone as if addressing an old family. His usual silence fades under the influence of the wicker bottle, which circulates freely—into Durdles’ system, while Jasper sips only once, then spits it out.

They climb the great tower. On the dark steps, Durdles stops for breath. Jasper sits beside him. The smell of the bottle drifts through the air. Neither can see the other, yet they talk as if facing one another.

“This is good stuff, Mister Jarsper!”

“It is very good stuff. I bought it especially.”

“They don’t show, you see—the old uns don’t.”

“It would be a more confused world if they did.”

“True,” says Durdles. “But do you think there might be ghosts of other things—things not human?”

“What things? Flower beds? Horses?”

“No. Sounds.”

“What sounds?”

“Cries.”

“What cries?”

“Screeches. Now I’ll tell you, Mr. Jarsper. Wait till I fix the bottle.” The cork pops, then clicks back. “There! _Now_ it’s right. This time last year, just a few days later, I was doing what was proper for the season—giving it the welcome it deserved—when the town boys attacked me. I escaped, came here, and fell asleep. What woke me? The ghost of a scream. Then the ghost of a dog’s howl—long, sad, woeful—like when someone dies. That was my last Christmas Eve.”

“What do you mean?” Jasper snaps, suddenly tense.

“I mean I asked everywhere. No one else heard it. So I say they were ghosts. Though why they came to me, I don’t know.”

“I thought you were a different sort of man,” Jasper says coldly.

“So I thought,” replies Durdles calmly. “Yet I was chosen.”

Jasper rises abruptly. “Come. We’ll freeze here. Lead the way.”

Durdles complies, unsteadily. He opens the door at the top with his key, and they emerge onto the Cathedral level—into a passage beside the chancel.

The moonlight is bright again. Colors from the stained glass fall across their faces. Durdles, standing still, holding the door open, looks like a figure risen from the grave—purple hand across his face, yellow splash on his brow. Yet he bears Jasper’s scrutiny without flinching.

Jasper fumbles in his pockets for a key to unlock an iron gate leading to the tower staircase.

“That and the bottle are enough for you,” he says, handing the bottle to Durdles. “Give me your bundle. I’m younger. I can carry it.”

Durdles hesitates—between bundle and bottle—then chooses the bottle. The bundle goes to Jasper.

Up they go, winding through the tower stairs, lowering their heads to avoid the stones above. Durdles lights the lantern, striking a spark from the cold wall. Guided by its flicker, they climb through cobwebs and dust.

They pass through narrow galleries, where Durdles waves the lantern—making the angels on the corbels seem to watch them. Then into steeper staircases, where wind whips through, startling birds whose wings beat against the walls, showering dust upon their heads.

Finally, they reach the top. Leaving the lantern behind, they look down upon Cloisterham—bathed in moonlight. Ruins at the base of the tower, moss-covered roofs beyond, the river winding from mist to sea, already restless with the knowledge of its journey.

Another inexplicable journey. Jasper gazes at the scene—especially at the part shaded by the Cathedral. But he watches Durdles just as closely. And Durdles, at times, feels his eyes.

Only at times. For Durdles is growing drowsy. Like an aeronaut lightening his load to rise, he has drained the bottle on the climb. Sleep ambushes him—snatches of it catch him mid-step. He hallucinates the ground below as level with the tower, and would walk off into the air if not stopped.

When they begin to descend, he drinks more—from the bottle, to weigh himself down, like an aeronaut preparing to land.

They reach the iron gate—locked only after Durdles tumbles twice and cuts his eyebrow once.

Back in the crypt, they aim to exit as they entered. But Durdles is now unsteady—both in step and speech. He collapses against a pillar, muttering for “forty winks of a second each.”

“If you insist,” says Jasper, “I won't leave you. Take your rest. I’ll walk.”

And so he does—while Durdles sleeps, dreaming of ghosts, of screams, of a world where the dead speak in silence.
