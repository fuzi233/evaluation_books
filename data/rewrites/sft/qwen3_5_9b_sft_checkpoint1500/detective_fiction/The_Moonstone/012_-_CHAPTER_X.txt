The remainder of the household trailed behind the Ablewhites until the entire retinue was assembled. Counting the servants, the number totaled twenty-four. It was indeed a magnificent tableau when they were finally seated around the dinner table, and the Rector of Frizinghall, delivering grace with a voice of exquisite elocution, rose to begin the benediction.

There is no necessity to catalogue the guests for you. You are unlikely to encounter most of them again within the scope of my narrative, save for two exceptions. These two individuals occupied the seats flanking Miss Rachel, who, reigning as the sovereign of the evening, naturally drew every gaze. Indeed, she was the focal point of the gathering, though this fact caused a quiet irritation to my mistress. Her annoyance stemmed from the fact that she wore her magnificent birthday present, the Moonstone, which overshadowed all other adornments. Although the diamond arrived unmounted, the ubiquitous Mr. Franklin, utilizing his nimble fingers and a scrap of silver wire, had ingeniously fashioned it into a brooch pinned to the bodice of her white gown. Everyone, of course, marveled at the prodigious size and beauty of the gem. However, the only two guests who ventured remarks beyond the ordinary were those seated at her immediate right and left.

To her left sat Mr. Candy, our local physician. He was a pleasant, affable little man, albeit with a distinct flaw: an excessive propensity for jesting, both in season and out, coupled with a tendency to plunge abruptly into conversation with strangers without first gauging the waters. In social settings, he frequently erred, inadvertently setting people at odds. Yet, in his medical practice, he proved far more prudent, seemingly guided by an instinct for discretion that saved him from the errors plaguing more cautious colleagues. Regarding the diamond, his comment, delivered as per his custom, was intended as a mystification. He gravely implored Miss Rachel, citing the interests of science, to permit him to remove the stone home and incinerate it. "First, we shall heat it, Miss Rachel," the doctor declared, "to a specific temperature; then, exposing it to an airflow, we shall gradually, puff! evaporate the diamond, thereby sparing you the torment of guarding such a valuable treasure!" My lady, observing him with a somewhat distressed countenance, appeared to hope that the doctor was speaking seriously and that Miss Rachel possessed sufficient zeal for science to sacrifice her gift.

On the young lady's right sat an eminent public figure: the renowned Indian traveler, Mr. Murthwaite. He had previously risked his life to penetrate regions where no European foot had tread. He was tall, lean, wiry, and brown, possessing a weary demeanor yet a steadiest, most attentive eye. Rumors suggested he grew weary of the mundane life surrounding us and yearned to return to the wild tramps of the East. Beyond his singular observation regarding her jewel, I doubt if he uttered six words or consumed a drop of wine throughout the entire meal. The Moonstone was the sole object of his interest; the legend of it had evidently reached him during his perilous wanderings. After gazing at it silently for so long that Miss Rachel grew confused, he addressed her in his cool, immovable tone: "If you ever journey to India, Miss Verinder, do not carry your uncle's birthday gift. A Hindoo diamond may serve a religious function. I know a certain city and a temple therein where, dressed as you are, your life would not suffice for five minutes' purchase." Safe in England, Miss Rachel was delighted to learn of her hypothetical danger. The footmen, known as the Bouncers, were equally thrilled, dropping their cutlery with a clatter and exclaiming, "O! how fascinating!" My lady shifted uncomfortably in her seat and deflected the topic.

As the dinner progressed, I became increasingly aware that this celebration was failing to flourish as previous festivities had. Looking back from the vantage point of subsequent events, I am half convinced that the cursed diamond cast a blight over the entire assembly. I circulated wines generously, acting as a privileged figure to ensure the unpopular dishes were tried, whispering confidentially, "Please reconsider trying this; I assure you it will benefit you." Nine times out of ten, they relented, ostensibly out of respect for their old steward, yet to no avail. Gaps of silence widened as the meal advanced, rendering me personally uneasy. When speech resumed, it was employed innocently but with disastrous intent. Mr. Candy, the doctor, for instance, uttered unluckier words than I had ever witnessed before. Consider this example of his misfortune: One of our ladies, the worthy Mrs. Threadgall, widow of the late Professor, habitually discussed her deceased spouse without acknowledging his death to strangers. She presumably believed every able-bodied adult in England should know this fact. During a lull in conversation, someone broached the dry and disagreeable subject of human anatomy. Whereupon, true to form, Mrs. Threadgall introduced her late husband without mentioning his demise, describing anatomy as the Professor's favorite pastime. By ill fortune, Mr. Candy, sitting opposite and ignorant of the gentleman's status, overheard her. Being the epitome of politeness, he seized the opportunity to further the Professor's anatomical pursuits. "They have acquired remarkably fine skeletons recently at the College of Surgeons," announced Mr. Candy, loudly and cheerfully across the table. "I strongly recommend the Professor, madam, to visit them whenever he finds an hour." You might have heard a pin drop. The company, respecting the memory of the dead, sat in stunned silence. Standing behind Mrs. Threadgall, pouring her hock, I watched as she lowered her head and murmured, "My beloved husband is no more." Unluckily, Mr. Candy, deaf to the truth and miles removed from suspicion, continued louder and more politely. "The Professor may not realize," he stated, "that membership in the College admits him, on any day but Sunday, between ten and four." Mrs. Threadgall buried her face in her cap, repeating the solemn words in a voice barely audible, "My beloved husband is no more." I blinked furiously at Mr. Candy across the table; Miss Rachel touched his arm; my lady stared at him with unutterable expressions. It was futile! Onward he went with a cordiality impossible to stop. "I shall be delighted," he proclaimed, "to send the Professor my card, if you will kindly inform me of his present address." "His present address, sir," cried Mrs. Threadgall, suddenly losing her temper with an emphasis that rang the glasses, "is the grave! The Professor has been dead ten years." "Oh, good heavens!" exclaimed Mr. Candy. Save for the Bouncers, who laughed aloud, a profound blank descended upon the company, as if everyone were departing in the wake of the Professor, hailed from the direction of the tomb.

Such was Mr. Candy. The others were nearly as irritating in their respective ways. When they should have spoken, they remained silent; when they did speak, they were perpetually at cross-purposes. Mr. Godfrey, though eloquent in public, declined to exert himself privately. Whether sullen or bashful following his discomfiture in the rose-garden, I could not determine. He reserved his discourse exclusively for the lady seated beside him, a committee-woman of spiritual disposition, distinguished by a prominent collarbone and a refined taste for dry champagne. Being stationed directly behind them at the sideboard, I testify that the company lost a significant amount of edifying conversation, which I intercepted while drawing corks and carving meat. Their discussion transcended charity work; they debated serious subjects. Religion, according to Mr. Godfrey, equated to love, and love equated to religion. Earth was heaven, slightly worn, and heaven was earth, refurbished to appear anew. Earth harbored objectionable elements, yet to compensate, all women in heaven belonged to a prodigious committee free from quarrels, attended by men serving as ministering angels. Beautiful! Beautiful! Yet, why did Mr. Godfrey hoard these sentiments to himself and his companion?

And what of Mr. Franklin? Surely, you would assert, Mr. Franklin endeavored to stir the company into a pleasant evening? Nothing of the sort! Having fully recovered his composure, likely informed by Penelope of Mr. Godfrey's reception in the garden, he displayed wonderful force and spirits. However, nine times out of ten, he selected the wrong subject or addressed the wrong person, offending some and puzzling all. His foreign training—that French, German, and Italian aspect of his nature I have noted—manifested in a bewildering fashion at my lady's hospitable table. Consider this: he discussed the extent to which a married woman might admire a man who was not her husband, articulating it in his clear-headed, witty French to the maiden aunt of the Vicar of Frizinghall. Then, shifting to German, he told the lord of the manor, while the latter quoted his expertise in breeding bulls, that such experience counted for nothing and that the proper method was to introspect, evolving the ideal bull from one's mind. What say you when our county member, heated by cheese and salad, lamented the spread of democracy in England, asking, "If we lose our ancient safeguards, Mr. Blake, what remains?" To which Mr. Franklin replied, from the Italian perspective: "Three things remain, sir—Love, Music, and Salad"? He not only terrified the company with such eruptions but, when the English side of him resurfaced, lost his foreign smoothness.
