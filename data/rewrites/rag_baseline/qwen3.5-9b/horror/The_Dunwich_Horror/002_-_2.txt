Wilbur Whateley entered the world at five in the morning on Sunday, February second, nineteen thirteen. The clock struck the hour in the farmhouse perched against the hillside, four miles from Dunwich and a mile and a half from the nearest neighbor. It was Candlemas, though the villagers called it something else, a date marked by the persistent barking of the countryside dogs and the low, grinding noises rising from the hills. These sounds had begun the night before, a rhythmic, unsettling percussion that preceded the birth.

Less significant, yet noted in the local record, was the lineage. The mother was Lavinia Whateley, a thirty-five-year-old albino of the decaying branch of the family, her skin pale and her features distorted by a deformity that rendered her unattractive to the eye. She lived with her father, Old Whateley, a man of advanced age and fractured sanity, surrounded by whispers of wizardry that had haunted his youth. Lavinia had no husband, yet she made no move to deny the child, accepting him with a strange, possessive pride. To the townsfolk, who speculated wildly about the father's identity, she offered no defense. Instead, she murmured prophecies regarding the infant's unusual powers and a terrible future, her voice carrying the weight of a woman who understood the gravity of what she held.

Lavinia was a solitary figure, accustomed to wandering the hills amidst thunderstorms and poring over the great, odorous books inherited by her father—a collection of rotting volumes, eaten by worms and time, containing the fragmented lore of centuries. Uneducated and devoid of conventional order, she kept a home that mirrored her own decay, free from the standards of cleanliness that governed the rest of the county. Yet, on the night Wilbur was born, a hideous scream tore through the silence, echoing above the wind and the dogs. No doctor attended; no midwife was present. Only the raw, primal act of creation remained.

Neighbors learned of the child a week later, when Old Whateley drove his sleigh through the snow into Dunwich Village. He stopped at Osborn's general store, addressing a crowd of loungers with an incoherent fervor. There was a shift in him, a new layer of furtiveness in his clouded mind that transformed him from a passive observer into a source of dread. He spoke of the child's paternity with a mixture of defiance and pride, dismissing the locals' expectations. "I dun't keer what folks think," he declared, his voice rough. "Ef Lavinny's boy looked like his pa, he wouldn't look like nothin' ye expeck. Ye needn't think the only folks is the folks hereabouts. Lavinny's read some, an' has seed some things the most o' ye only tell abaout. I calc'late her man is as good a husban' as ye kin find this side of Aylesbury... Let me tell ye suthin'—some day yew folks'll hear a child o' Lavinny's a-callin' its father's name on the top o' Sentinel Hill!"

For the first month, Wilbur was seen only by Old Whateley and Mamie Bishop, the common-law wife of Earl Sawyer. Mamie visited out of curiosity, her stories confirming the oddities she observed. Zechariah Whateley, of the undecayed line, came to lead a pair of Alderney cows purchased by Old Whateley from his son Curtis. This purchase initiated a pattern of cattle acquisition that would continue until the horror of 1928, yet the ramshackle barn never seemed overcrowded. People grew curious enough to climb the steep hillside and count the herd grazing precariously near the farmhouse, but they could never find more than ten or twelve specimens. They were anemic, bloodless, afflicted by a mysterious blight. Some bore wounds resembling surgical incisions, and occasionally, observers swore they saw similar sores around the throats of the old man and his daughter.

In the spring following his birth, Lavinia resumed her walks in the hills, carrying the swarthy child in her misproportioned arms. Once the townsfolk had seen the baby, public interest waned, and no one commented on the rapid development he exhibited. His growth was phenomenal; within three months, he possessed the size and muscular power of an infant nearing a full year. His movements were deliberate, restrained, lacking the chaotic energy typical of babies. By seven months, he walked unassisted, stumbling briefly before mastering the motion completely.

It was shortly after this, on Hallowe'en, that a great blaze erupted at midnight atop Sentinel Hill, illuminating the ancient table-stone amidst its mound of bones. Silas Bishop, of the undecayed Bishops, claimed to have seen the boy running sturdily up the hill an hour prior, leading his mother. Silas was rounding up a stray heifer when he spotted the two figures in the dim light of his lantern. They moved almost noiselessly through the underbrush, appearing entirely unclothed to the startled watcher. Later accounts were vague regarding the boy's attire, suggesting perhaps a fringed belt and dark blue trousers, but thereafter, Wilbur was never seen alive and conscious without complete, tightly buttoned clothing. The disarray of his garments seemed to trigger immediate anger and alarm in him.

His contrast with his squalid mother and grandfather was striking, particularly in his dress, a distinction that would later acquire a grim significance. The following January, gossip turned to the fact that "Lavinny's black brat" had begun to speak at the tender age of eleven months. His speech was remarkable, differing from the regional accent and free of the infantile lisping common to children of three or four. Though not talkative, when he spoke, he projected an elusive quality absent from Dunwich and its inhabitants. The strangeness lay not in his words, but in his intonation and the internal mechanics of his voice. His face also betrayed a premature maturity; sharing his mother's and grandfather's lack of a chin, his firm, precociously shaped nose combined with large, dark, almost Latin eyes to create an aura of quasi-adulthood and preternatural intelligence.

Yet, beneath this brilliance lay an extreme ugliness. Something animalistic clung to his thick lips, his large-pored, yellowish skin, his coarse, crinkly hair, and his oddly elongated ears. He was soon despised more fiercely than his parents, and all speculation about him became seasoned with references to the bygone magic of Old Whateley. Tales circulated of the hills shaking when the child shrieked the dreadful name of Yog-Sothoth in the midst of a circle of stones, holding a great book open in his arms. Dogs abhorred him, forcing him to adopt constant defensive measures against their relentless barking.
