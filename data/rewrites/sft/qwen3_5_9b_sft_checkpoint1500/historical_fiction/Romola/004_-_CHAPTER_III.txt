The Barber's Shop.

"To tell you the truth," the young stranger remarked to Nello as they pushed their way past the tangle of carts and mules, "I am relieved to be delivered from that patron of mine to someone with a less barbarous accent and a less puzzling trade. Is it customary among you Florentines for a traveling peddler of shattered glass and rags to boast of selling lutes and swords?"

"Customary? By no means. Our Bratti is no ordinary man. He operates on a theory, and adheres to it with a rigor I cannot claim for any philosopher I have ever had the honor of shaving," replied Nello, whose verbosity flowed like an overfilled vessel, refusing to dispense merely a drop. "Bratti intends to extract the maximum possible pleasure—that is to say, the greatest profit—from this life, concluding his existence with a bargain for the easiest passage through purgatory by surrendering his winnings to the Holy Church. He has drafted his last will on the cheapest terms a notary could secure. Yet I have often told him, 'Bratti, your bargain is a limp one, and you stand on the lame side of it. Does it not sadden you to gaze upon the paintings of Paradise? You shall never there haggle over rags and rusty nails; the saints and angels require no pins or tinder. Save perhaps with San Bartolomeo, who carries his skin about in a cumbersome fashion, I see no opportunity for trading in secondhand garments.' But God forgive me," Nello added, shifting his tone and crossing himself, "such levity is unfit for a morning when Lorenzo lies dead and the Muses rend their hair—an image painfully familiar to a barber's mind. And you yourself, Messere, are likely under a cloud; when a gentleman of your speech and bearing accepts such humble lodgings, it betokens some calamity."

"What Lorenzo do you speak of whose passing causes such commotion?" the stranger inquired, appearing so preoccupied with this point that he missed the indirect query trailing behind it.

"What Lorenzo? There is but one Lorenzo," Nello asserted, "whose demise could stir the Mercato into turmoil, make the lantern of the Duomo leap in despair, and compel the lions of the Republic to devour one another. I mean Lorenzo de' Medici, the Pericles of our Athens—if I may draw such a parallel in the hearing of a Greek."

"Why not?" the other laughed. "For I suspect Athens, even in the days of Pericles, could not have produced a learned barber so profound."

"Yes, yes; I felt sure I was not mistaken," said the rapid Nello. "Otherwise, I have shaved the venerable Demetrio Calcondila to little purpose. But forgive me, I am lost in wonder: your Italian surpasses his, though he has resided in Italy for forty years—even better than that of the accomplished Marullo, who may be said to have wed the Italic Muse in senses beyond number, having married both our learned and lovely Alessandra Scala."

"It will assuage your surprise to learn that I descend from Greek stock planted in Italian soil far older than the mulberry trees, which have flourished so readily here. I was born at Bari, and raised by an Italian. In fact, I am a Greek, very much as your peaches are Persian. The Greek hue has been subdued in me, I presume, by prolonged residence and extensive travel in the land of gods and heroes. And, to confess a private matter, this same Greek dye, along with a few ancient gems I possess, constitutes the sole fortune shipwreck has left me. But—when towers fall, you know it is ruin for the small nest-builder—the death of your Pericles makes me regret that I did not turn my steps toward Rome, as I should have, but for a deceptive Minerva in the guise of an Augustinian monk. 'At Rome,' he said, 'you will vanish into a throng of starving scholars; but at Florence, every corner bathes in the sunshine of Lorenzo's patronage: Florence is the fairest market in Italy for commodities like yours.'"

"_Gnaffè_," said Nello, "and so it shall remain, I trust. Lorenzo was not the sole patron and arbiter of learning in our city—heaven forbid! Because he was a large melon, every other Florentine is not a pumpkin, I assure you. Have we not Bernardo Rucellai, Alamanno Rinuccini, and countless others? And if you seek information on such matters, I, Nello, am your man. It feels like a millennium before I can serve a _bel erudito_ like yourself. First, however, the matter of your hair. That beard, my fine young man, must go, were it as dear to you as the nymph of your dreams. Here in Florence, we dislike a man whose nose projects over a waterfall of hair. Remember, you have crossed the Rubicon once you are shaven: if you repent and allow your beard to regrow after it has hardened in battle with the razor, your mouth will eventually reveal not the divine prerogative of lips as Messer Angelo termed it, but appear like a dark cavern fringed with brambles."

"That is a dreadfully ominous prophecy," the Greek observed, "particularly if your Florentine maidens are as lovely as the little Tessa I kissed this morning."

"Tessa? She is a rough-handed peasant girl. You will ascend to the favor of ladies who bear no scent of the mule-stables. To achieve that end, you must not carry the air of a _sgherro_ or a man of ill repute; you must resemble a courtier, a scholar of the most refined sort, such as our Pietro Crinito—one who sins among the well-bred and well-fed, rather than sucking down vile _vino di sotto_ in a tavern."

"With all my heart," the stranger agreed. "If the Florentine Graces demand it, I am willing to sacrifice this trifling matter of my beard, yet—"

"Yes, yes," Nello interjected. "I foresee what you would say. It is the _bella zazzera_—the hyacinthine locks you refuse to part with. There is no need. A little trimming—ecco!—and you will look not unlike the illustrious Prince Pico di Mirandola in his prime. And behold, we are timely arrived in the Piazza San Giovanni, before the door of my establishment. Yet you pause; naturally, you wish to view our wonder of the world, our Duomo, our Santa Maria del Fiore. Very well, a fleeting glance suffices; I beseech you reserve a closer scrutiny for after you have been shaved. I am trembling with the inspiration of my art, quite to the edge of my razor. Ah, then, follow this way."

The mercurial barber grasped the stranger's arm and guided him to a spot on the south side of the square, offering an immediate view of the massive dark shell of the dome, the slender, soaring grace of Giotto's bell tower, and the quaint octagon of San Giovanni facing them, displaying its unique gates of storied bronze, which still retained a somewhat dimmed splendor of their original gold. The inlaid marbles appeared fresher then, in hues of pink, white, and purple, than they do today when four centuries of winter have bleached their white to the rich ochre of well-aged pipestone; the façade of the cathedral did not stand in ignominy of faded stucco, but bore the magnificent promise of the half-finished marble inlays and statue niches Giotto had conceived a century and a half prior. As the campanile, in all its harmonious variety of color and form, lifted the eyes upward into the clear air of this April morning, it seemed a prophetic symbol, declaring that human life must, somehow and sometime, align itself with that pure, aspiring beauty.

Yet this was not the impression it evoked in the Greek. His eyes were drawn irresistibly upward, but standing with folded arms and curls falling backward, a faint trace of scorn lingered on his lip. When his gaze returned, it swept the scene with a scanning coolness that rather pricked Nello's Florentine spirit.

"Well, my fine young man," the Greek said, with some impatience, "you seem to regard our Cathedral as lightly as if you were the Angel Gabriel descended straight from Paradise. I should like to know if you have ever beheld finer craftsmanship than Giotto's tower, or any dome that would not look a mere mushroom beside Brunelleschi's there, or any marbles more exquisite or cunningly worked than those our Signoria procured from distant quarries at a price sufficient to purchase a dukedom. Come, have you ever witnessed anything to equal them?"

"If you asked me that question with a scimitar at my throat, in Turkish fashion, or even with your own razor," the young Greek smiled cheerfully, stepping toward the gates of the Baptistery, "I dare say you might extort a confession of the true faith. But with my throat free from peril, I venture to tell you that your buildings smell too much of Christian barbarism for my palate. I harbor a shuddering intuition of what lies within—hideous, smoke-blackened Madonnas; fleshless saints in mosaic, staring down with idiotic astonishment and rebuke from the apse; skin-clad skeletons hanging on crosses, speckled with arrows, or stretched on gridirons; women and monks with heads twisted aside in eternal lamentation.
