The journey downriver from Wadi Halfa to Assouan unfolded before Captain Calder as a study in stark indifference. He viewed the Sudan not merely as a landscape, but as a testament to a "callous country inhabited by a callous race." Leaning heavily against the iron railing of the steamer's upper deck, Calder gazed downward at the flat-bottomed barge lashed securely to his ship's side. Upon the barge's lower deck, nestled among the local passengers, sat an *angareb*—a rough framework of mats woven over four legs. Stretched upon this primitive bed lay a figure shrouded entirely in a heavy black veil, perfectly still.

Two Arabs, having ferried the burden aboard at Korosko just hours prior, now lounged in the stern of the barge, their laughter and chatter rising in a jarring contrast to the silence of their passenger. To them, the occupant of the *angareb* was no more than cargo, likely a corpse or a dying wretch, yet they paid it no heed. Calder scanned the horizon: to his right and left stretched vast, glaring expanses of sand and rock, sculpted by time into the rigid, geometric shapes of ancient pyramids. Life here existed only in a narrow, meagre strip of green clinging to the water's edge—the sole response of the desert to spring rains and summer heat.

His gaze returned to the *angareb*. Beneath the black fabric, features were obscured, save for the outline of a nose, deep hollows around unseen eyes, and a mouth. It was impossible to tell if a mustache or beard lay hidden beneath. As the sun climbed higher, casting its slanted, burning rays toward the boat, the natives nearby shifted into the cool shade cast by the steamer's upper deck. Yet the *angareb* remained untouched. The two men in the stern showed no concern for their charge.

Calder watched the golden light advance relentlessly over the dark form, creeping upward from the feet until it blazed pitilessly upon the face. Still, the figure beneath the veil did not stir. The fabric did not flutter over moving lips; the legs stayed stretched in rigid extension; the arms lay pressed tight against the sides.

Driven by a sudden urgency, Calder shouted to the pair in the stern. "Move the *angareb* into the shadow!" he commanded, his voice cutting through the hum of the engine. "And be quick!"

The Arabs rose with visible reluctance, grumbling, and dragged the heavy frame toward the shade.

"Is it a man or a woman?" Calder asked immediately.

"A man," one replied, wiping sweat from his brow. "We are taking him to the hospital at Assouan, but I fear he will not survive. He fell from a palm tree three weeks ago."

"And you give him neither food nor water?"

"He is too ill," the Arab dismissed.

To Calder, this was the tragic logic of the region, a narrative where fate dictated the end before the body succumbed. A man who had likely suffered only a bruise three weeks prior—a wound healable in days with basic care—had been abandoned to the elements. Left unwashed, unfed, and exposed to the relentless sun and swarms of flies, the initial trauma had festered into a festering sore, which in turn had gangrened. Now, the Mudir of Korosko, discovering the accident only after it was too late, had dispatched the man downriver on the steamer. Yet, despite the grim familiarity of the tale, the man's absolute immobility fascinated Calder. When a strong wind sprang up near sunset, blowing upstream against the current, Calder found a strange comfort in knowing the patient might finally find some respite.

That evening, at the dinner table, a neighbor with a distinct German accent struck up conversation. On a sudden impulse, Calder turned to him. "Are you a doctor by any chance?"

"Not a doctor," the man smiled, adjusting his eyeglasses. "A student of medicine at Bonn. I came from Cairo to view the Second Cataract, but was barred from proceeding further than Wadi Halfa."

Calder cut in quickly. "Then I beg to intrude on your holiday. I require your professional assistance."

"For yourself? With pleasure, though I wouldn't have guessed you were ill," the student chuckled.

"Nor am I. I need help for an Arab."

"The man on the bedstead?"

"Yes, if you will oblige. Let me warn you—he was injured three weeks ago. In these parts, no one has tended to him since. The sight... it will not be pleasant. This is hardly a hospitable land for neglected wounds."

"All experience is valuable," the German shrugged. "Shall we go?"

They left the table as the wind had freshened, raising choppy waves that tossed the steamer and splashed the water onto the decks. Night had fallen, turning the river into a churning void illuminated only by a single lantern swinging from the center of the upper deck. Its feeble glow cast uncertain, dancing shadows over a small circle of the lower deck, leaving everything else swallowed by darkness, save for the bow where white sheets of spray whipped up like driven snow.

"He has been moved," the German observed, peering intently over the rail. "There is no one in the bows."

Calder nodded slowly, staring into the abyss below. "I believe the *angareb* is there. I am certain."

Following his companion, Calder hurried down the steep stairs to the lower deck. Confirming his suspicion, he spotted the *angareb* standing in the wash of water exactly where it had been placed earlier that day. The figure beneath the black covering lay as motionless as stone, utterly devoid of expression, drenched continuously by the cold, stinging spray of the river.

"I thought it would be so," Calder murmured.

He retrieved a lantern and, accompanied by the German student, climbed across the bulwarks onto the swaying barge. Summoning the two Arabs, he ordered, "Move the *angareb* from the bow." Once they complied, he pointed to the covered figure. "Now remove that covering. My friend, who is a physician, needs to inspect the wound."

The Arabs hesitated, exchanging glances. One spoke with insolent defiance. "There are doctors in Assouan, whither we are taking him. Why trouble ourselves?"

Raising the lantern high, Calder stepped forward and pulled the black veil away from the wounded man's face. "Do as I say," he instructed calmly.

The German student approached cautiously, examining the wounded thigh while Calder kept the light steady overhead. As predicted, the scene was gruesome. The wound was raw and crawling with infection. The student recoiled slightly, glad when Calder signaled him to cover it up again.

"I can do nothing," the student declared, shaking his head. "Perhaps in a proper hospital, with baths and dressings, some relief might be offered. But here? I cannot even begin. Do these men speak English?"

"No," Calder answered.

"That changes things," the student noted. "He did not fall from a palm tree. That story is a lie. The injury was inflicted by the blade of a spear or some similar weapon."

"Are you sure?"

"Yes."

Calder knelt suddenly beside the Arab on the *angareb*. Though the man never moved, Calder sensed consciousness. The man's eyes had tracked his movements, following his gaze.

"Do you understand English?" Calder asked softly.

The Arab could not speak, but a flicker of comprehension lit his eyes.

"Where do you come from?" Calder pressed.

Lips strained to form words, but no sound emerged. Instead, the eyes pleaded silently, radiating a desperate eagerness to communicate. Calder dropped to his knees closer to the man's head, holding the lantern beam directly onto the eyes. He began listing towns.

"Dongola?" Nothing.
"Metemmeh? Berber? Omdurman?"

At the mention of "Omdurman," the Arab's eyelids closed tightly, a silent affirmation. Calder continued, his heart racing with mounting excitement.

"You were wounded there? No. Where then? At Berber? Yes. Were you in prison at Omdurman and escaped? No. Yet you were wounded."

Calder sank back on his heels, his mind working furiously. Excitement surged through him. He leaned close to the Arab's ear, lowering his voice.

"Were you assisting someone to escape? Yes. Who? Colonel Trench? No." He listed the other white captives known to be in Omdurman. To each name, the Arab's eyes responded with a negative shake. Then Calder spoke the final name. "Effendi Feversham?"

The eyes assented with unmistakable clarity, as if the lips had spoken.

"But this is all I can learn," Calder sighed, standing up. "I am pledged to help Effendi Feversham, yet the truth remains locked inside a silent man. Those two Arabs know what they must not meddle in. They cling to a convenient lie about a fall from a palm tree to absolve themselves of responsibility. Their kinsmen in Korosko simply asked them to transport the stranger, and they agreed. Despite my pressing questions, I cannot extract a single more explicit word. I have the information Durrance waits for, buried in a locked book within this man."
