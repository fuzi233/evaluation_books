The physician returned home, groaning about a "bad burn"—for while we are quick to cauterize another’s wound, our own pain is ever a burning misery. His housekeeper, with the gentle confidence of old remedies, applied a salve as mild as milk. He endured it meekly, his only aim being relief. Yet even as he suffered, his mind turned to vengeance: he drew up a bill for his shattered phials and swore before a magistrate that two strangers, indebted to him, plotted to flee the town.

Fate, however, was unkind. For once, the truth spilled from his lips—*in an affidavit*, no less!—and when the officers reached *The Silver Lion*, the birds had flown. Down to the river they rushed, gathering word of the fugitives’ path, and gave chase.

Denys, ever sharp-eyed, had seen the danger the moment the physician left in silence. "That old fool’s quiet was unnatural," he muttered. "We are strangers here; the law favors its own. Five minutes more, and we’ll be clapped in irons for striking a town worthy. Can you walk to the river? Once there, a boat is as good as a bed—better, for it moves."

Gerard’s eyes lit with feverish excitement. "The boat! The Rhine! The very breeze will cool this trifling heat in my blood. To Rome—to Sevenbergen—to home! Let us fly!"

Swiftly, Denys settled their dues, and they hurried to the water’s edge—only to learn the public boat had long since departed. But where questions gather, a crowd follows, and soon an old boatman with his two sons offered passage. A bystander scoffed. "The current’s too strong for three oars."

"Then my comrade and I will row," said Gerard, though he swayed where he stood.

"No need," laughed the old man. "The fool owns the other boat."

With a stout wind at their backs and oars in hand, they set off at a gallant pace. Gerard, breathing deep, sighed with relief. "The river air is healing already. Though, in truth, the doctor did me some good."

Denys chuckled. "The doctor? You refused his cures."

"Aye, but—" Gerard hesitated, then grinned. "Knocking him down soothed me."

Denys roared. "Amiable dove! What a fierce little soul unfolds! I misjudged you at first."

"Nay," Gerard protested. "I’d have borne his threats, but he was near an infidel—denying the hand of God in our shaping! Such men I was taught to abhor. Had it been a thunderbolt, it would have been justice—yet all he got was a pillow from this feeble arm."

"*Feeble?*" Denys scoffed. "I’ve seen arms in my day, but never one that struck like a catapult. The bolster wrapped his nose, the ends met behind his head, and his skull rang like a bell. Had he been Goliath, down he’d have gone. Saint Denys spare me from such *feeble* foes!"

The river twisted, and the wind shifted, tilting the boat. All but one small figure—a child of five, the boatman’s grandson—scrambled to the high side. The boy, who had slipped aboard unseen, sat unconcerned, too light to heed the laws of balance.

As they sailed, Gerard suddenly recalled: this path led to Rome—*not* Burgundy. "Denys," he said, troubled, "this is not your road."

"I know," said Denys quietly. "But I’ll not leave you while fever grips you. You shift from red to white even now. I must go on to Cologne, then strike across."

Gerard brightened. "Then thank Heaven! For how could we pass so near and not see it? A city ancient and vast—founded by a Roman lady, sacked by Attila, scarred by Lothaire! A church for every week, chapels beyond count, and the great minster, unfinished yet glorious! There lie the Three Kings—Melchior, Gaspar, Balthazar—in their shrine of shining brass, jeweled and radiant. And St. Ursel’s, where rest eleven thousand virgins, martyred by Moors! Their bones fill the crypts; their skulls gaze from the church. And the Maccabees, boiled for refusing swine’s flesh—"

Denys laughed. "Peremptory king! I’d have eaten the bacon and kept my skin."

Gerard scowled. "It was their *faith*."

"Peace! Are there no knights in this city of priests?"

"Aye! Herman Gryn, who slew a lion with cloak and sword—his statue stands there, lion and all. Few have done the like—Samson, Lysimachus, Benaiah—"

Denys grinned. "A supper with those four! What a night that would be."

Gerard shuddered. "Not I. But tell me, when did *you* see Cologne?"

"Never. But the monks paint it bright in words."

Denys shook his head. "No dead saint draws me there—only you, my living friend."

Gerard fell silent.

Then the youngest boatman cried, "What ails the brat?"

The child, who had sat mute, now burst into tears. Oh! Oh! Oh! No injury, no fright—just sudden wailing.

"Who can fathom children?" snapped the boatman. Then his eye caught movement ashore. "Look yonder—who are those men?"

Four figures strode along the eastern bank. The boatmen huddled, whispering, glancing between passengers and pursuers.

But before they could act—*disaster*. A squall struck. The boat heeled; the men lunged to balance it—only to see water rushing across the deck. A heartbeat later, the Rhine swallowed them whole.

The officers fumbled for their ropes. Gerard, a strong swimmer, surfaced gasping, shaking water from his eyes. He saw the capsized boat, figures clinging—then heard a voice, low and despairing:

"Adieu, comrade. Adieu."

Denys was sinking, dragged down by his arbalest. His pale face, wide-eyed, turned once to Gerard—then vanished beneath the dark water.

With a cry of terror, Gerard dove after him.

The officers hurled a rope into the churning river.
