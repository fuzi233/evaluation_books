"Calvin C. Bunner, at your service," the newcomer corrected himself, adding a touch of stiff formality as he withdrew an unlit cigar from his mouth. He was accustomed to finding Englishmen slow and ceremonious with strangers, yet Trent's brisk introduction plainly threw him slightly off balance.

"You are Mr. Trent, I presume?" Bunner continued. "Mrs. Manderson mentioned you a moment ago. Good morning, Captain."

Mr. Murch acknowledged the somewhat odd salutation with a curt nod. "I was heading up to my room and caught a strange voice inside, so I thought I'd drop in."

Bunner chuckled easily. "You suspected I might be eavesdropping, perhaps?"

"No, sir," Bunner replied smoothly. "I overheard a word or two regarding a pistol—this one, I imagine—and that was all."

Mr. Bunner was a slender, somewhat short young man possessing a shaven, pale, and bony face that bore a nearly girlish quality, framed by large, dark, intelligent eyes. His dark hair waved and was parted in the center. When his lips were free of a cigar, they remained perpetually half-open in an expression of eager curiosity; however, once he began smoking or chewing tobacco, this vanished, replaced by the image of the consummately cool and sagacious Yankee he truly was. Born in Connecticut, he had entered a brokerage firm upon graduating college, soon attracting the attention of Manderson, whose business dealings with the firm he frequently managed. The Colossus observed him for some time before offering him the position of private secretary. Bunner was the model businessman: trustworthy, far-sighted, methodical, and precise. While Manderson could have secured many men with these virtues, he chose Bunner specifically for his swiftness, secrecy, and a singular, innate instinct regarding the movements of the stock market.

Trent and the American surveyed one another coolly. Both seemed content with what they perceived.

"I was having it explained to me," Trent said pleasantly, "that my discovery of a pistol capable of shooting Manderson amounts to very little. I'm told it is a favorite weapon among your people and has become quite popular here."

Bunner extended a bony hand and retrieved the pistol from its case. "Yes, sir," he said, handling it with practiced familiarity. "The Captain is correct. Out home, we call this a 'Little Arthur,' and I daresay there are duplicates of it in a hundred thousand hip pockets right now. Personally, I find it too light in the hand."

Mechanically reaching under the tail of his jacket, Bunner produced a grim-looking weapon. "Feel this, Mr. Trent—it's loaded, by the way. Now, this 'Little Arthur'... Marlowe purchased it just before we arrived here this year to please the old man. Manderson declared it ridiculous for a gentleman to exist in the twentieth century without a pistol. So, he procured whatever was offered him, I suspect, without consulting me. Not that it's a bad gun," Bunner conceded, squinting along the sights. "Marlowe struggled with it initially, but I've given him lessons over the past month or so, and he has practiced until he is fairly competent. Yet, he never developed the habit of carrying it. Why, it is as natural to me as wearing trousers. I have carried one for several years now, simply because someone was always likely to be waiting for Manderson. And now," Bunner concluded with a sigh, "they have gotten him while I was absent. Well, gentlemen, you must excuse me. I am proceeding to Bishopsbridge. There is much to attend to these days, and I must dispatch a series of cables large enough to choke a cow."

"I must depart as well," said Trent. "I have an appointment at the 'Three Tuns' inn."

"Allow me to give you a lift in the automobile," Bunner offered cordially. "I pass directly by that establishment. Say, Captain, are you traveling my way? No? Then come along, Mr. Trent, and assist me in retrieving the car. Our chauffeur is incapacitated, and we shall have to perform nearly all tasks ourselves, save for cleaning the dust from her."

Still talking in his measured drawl, Bunner led Trent downstairs and through the house to the garage at the rear. It stood at a slight distance from the main building, providing a cool refuge from the midday sun's glare. Bunner showed no haste to extract the vehicle. He offered Trent a cigar, which was accepted, and for the first time, lit his own. Seating himself on the footboard of the car, his thin hands clasped between his knees, he regarded the other intently.

"Here is a matter, Mr. Trent," he said after a pause. "There are certain details I can share that may prove useful. I know your record; you are a sharp man, and I enjoy dealing with sharp individuals. I cannot say if I have sized up Detective Murch correctly, but he strikes me as a mutt. I would answer any questions he possessed the sense to ask—I have done so, in fact—but I feel disinclined to offer him any of my theories unless he asks. Understood?"

Trent nodded. "That is a sentiment shared by many in the presence of our police force," he remarked. "It is merely the official manner, I suppose. However, let me inform you that Murch is anything but what you assume. He is one of the shrewdest officers in Europe. He lacks rapidity of mind, but he possesses certainty. His experience is immense. My strength lies in imagination, but I assure you, in police work, experience outweighs it significantly."

"Outweigh nothing!" Bunner retorted crisply. "This is no ordinary case, Mr. Trent. Allow me to state one reason. I believe the old man knew something was coming for him. Furthermore, I believe it was something he felt he could not evade."

Trent pulled a crate opposite Bunner's seat and settled himself. "This sounds like business," he said. "Tell me your thoughts."

"I express what I do because of the change in the old man's demeanor over the last few weeks. You likely know, Mr. Trent, that he was a man who kept himself strictly in hand. That was true. I have always regarded him as the coolest and hardest-headed figure in commerce. That man's composure was deadly—I have never witnessed anything comparable. I knew Manderson better than anyone else. I worked alongside him in the endeavors he truly loved. I suspect I knew him far better than his wife, the poor woman; better than Marlowe, who rarely saw Manderson in his office during critical times; and certainly better than any of his friends."

"Did he possess any friends?" Trent interjected.

Bunner glanced at him sharply. "Someone seems to be placing you next to others, I see," he noted. "No: properly speaking, I should say not. He had numerous acquaintances among the powerful figures, people he met daily; they would even yacht or hunt together. But I do not believe there was ever a single individual to whom Manderson opened a corner of his heart. However, what I intended to convey is this. Several months ago, the old man began to act unlike anyone I had ever known—gloomy and sullen, as if eternally brooding over some evil, some predicament he could not resolve. This persisted without interruption, identical downtown as it was at home; he acted as if a heavy burden weighed upon his mind. Yet, it was not until a few weeks prior that his self-restraint began to falter. Let me tell you this, Mr. Trent"—the American placed his bony claw upon the other's knee—"I am the only man who knows this. With everyone else, he would appear merely morose and dull; but when alone with me in his office, or wherever we worked together, if the slightest error occurred, by George! he would fly off the handle to beat the Dutch. In this very library, I have seen him open a letter containing something displeasing, and he would tear around and behave like an Indian, declaring he wished the writer were there, that he wouldn't harm him, and so forth, until it became pitiful. I have never seen such a transformation. Here is another point. For a week preceding his death, Manderson neglected his work, for the first time in my experience. He refused to answer letters or cables, despite matters appearing to collapse completely overseas. I assumed this anxiety, whatever its nature, had exhausted his nerves. Once I advised him to consult a physician, and he told me to go to hell. But no one saw this side of him but me. If he was having one of these rages in the library, for instance, and Mrs. Manderson entered the room, he would instantly revert to being calm and cold."

"And you attribute this to some secret anxiety, a fear that someone had designs on his life?" Trent asked.

The American nodded.

"I suppose," Trent resumed, "you have considered the possibility of a mental breakdown due to overstrain. That is the first conclusion your account suggests to me.
