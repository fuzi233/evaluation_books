Mrs. Adair watched the scene from a distance with a growing, gnawing anxiety. She had spilled secrets to Captain Durrance, and now she feared the fallout. She worried that he would finally strip away the mask Ethne wore, revealing the painful truth: that Ethne's devotion to him was a fiction, while her heart beat only for Harry Feversham. If Durrance were to speak plainly, to lay bare the error of his judgment and the reality of Ethne's misplaced love, he would struggle to hide her complicity in his own realization. The moment Ethne's steady gaze met his, demanding an answer, would be unbearable. Yet, for now, the surface remained calm. To the outside eye, Ethne and Durrance moved through the day as if the chaotic days surrounding Willoughby's arrival and Ethne's violin performance had never happened. This outward stability brought Mrs. Adair a fleeting relief, but soon it curdled into anger and confusion. Why did nothing change? Had her meddling achieved nothing?

Durrance, however, had already resolved the internal storm. Knowing Ethne wished to remain ignorant, he found solace in the belief that he was still the source of her happiness. He saw no reason to shatter this illusion yet. Before taking action, he needed absolute certainty regarding the rift between Ethne and Feversham, and crucial information about Harry's fate. So, he adopted a new persona. He abandoned his usual vigilance, feigning a contentment he did not feel. He minimized his own suffering and exaggerated the joy Ethne's company brought him.

"You see," he told her, his tone light, "one learns to live with blindness eventually. It becomes part of the landscape. But you, Ethne—you never grow old. Every time I meet you, you reveal a new facet of yourself that delights me. Besides, there is always the hope of a cure."

His strategy worked. Ethne, sensing his retreat from suspicion, countered her own sorrow with his relentless cheer. Her grief, however, had deepened. While she had briefly known a lighter spirit before the white feathers arrived at Ramelton, she now knew a heavier misery. Though her pride had been steeled and a fragile hope sparked by Durrance's words—that Harry might yet be saved—she was trapped in a cycle of sleepless nights and a dull, burning pain in her head. She could no longer pretend Harry was merely a lost friend. He was alive, somewhere in terrible straits, and she thirsted for knowledge. Occasionally, her impatience broke her restraint.

"One assumes escape is possible from Omdurman," she said, forcing a tone of detached curiosity.

"Possibly," Durrance replied, keeping his smile bright. "Though it is fraught with difficulty and takes time. There have been attempts to extract Colonel Trench and others, but none have succeeded yet. The bottleneck is the intermediary."

Ethne's eyes narrowed slightly. "The intermediary?" she asked, pulling herself upright. "Do you mean the Arab who travels between Omdurman and the Egyptian border?"

"Yes," Durrance confirmed. "Usually a Dervish trader dealing with Sudanese tribes. He slips into Wadi Halfa, Assouan, or Suakin to arrange things. The risk is immense; if caught, he faces swift execution. Naturally, many shy away from the final leap. Furthermore, some are rogues. You arrange the deal in Egypt, hand over funds, and wait six months or a year. Often, he returns alone with excuses—the season was wrong, guards tightened, he was suspected—and demands more money. Sometimes his story is true, sometimes not. He returns, asks for more, and vanishes again."

Ethne nodded slowly. "Exactly."

In that moment, Durrance had unlocked a key piece of the puzzle for her. Until now, she had wondered how Harry intended to aid Colonel Trench. Now it clicked: Harry was planning to become his own go-between. This knowledge fueled her hope; if Harry was orchestrating a rescue, he would be meticulous. He would desperately want the second feather returned, and if he succeeded in fetching Trench, he would not stay behind.

They sat on the terrace as the sun bled crimson into the water below.

"Prison life in Omdurman sounds dreadful," Ethne murmured, maintaining her affectation of indifference.

"Dreadful?" Durrance laughed softly. "Impossible. A hovel crammed with Arabs, suffocating heat, no air, a roof barely two feet above your head. Locked inside from dusk until dawn. Likely standing in that filth all night, shoulder to shoulder. Imagine it here, in England, on a night like this. What would it be like in the Sudan? Especially if you have a memory of a place like this to torment you."

Ethne gazed out over the cool garden. Right now, Harry Feversham might be gasping for air in that dark, stinking cell, parched and fever-blasted, haunted by visions of Ramelton's green slopes and the liquid music of the Lennon River.

"One prays for death," Ethne said slowly, "unless..." She hesitated, cutting off the thought, *"unless one goes there willingly with a purpose."*

Durrance finished the sentence. "Unless there is a chance of escape." And if Feversham was in Omdurman, there was a chance.

Immediately, Durrance regretted painting such a grim picture. "Please understand," he hastened to add, "this is all hearsay. We know nothing for certain. Prisoners' conditions vary." He dropped the subject, and Ethne did not press it.

A mystery lingered between them: Durrance's sudden silence regarding her abrupt exit from the drawing room on the night he revealed his meeting with Harry. He never broached it, and Ethne wisely mirrored his silence. The shift in his demeanor, the lack of his former scrutiny, soothed her fears. It appeared he had concocted a simple, natural explanation for himself. She also wondered why he had volunteered the details of Harry's journey south, but she found a satisfying, albeit strange, answer: he was merely a vessel for a higher power's message, designed specifically for her understanding.

For the month of September, they maintained the charade. Every morning, when Durrance crossed the fields to visit Ethne at The Pool, Mrs. Adair watched them laughing and talking, free of any trace of awkwardness. Anger swelled within her, making it harder to endure the lie. It was a month of taut nerves for all three. Finally, a reprieve came when Durrance scheduled visits to an oculist in London. These trips became frequent and lengthy, offering Ethne brief respites where she could shed her mask and rest. But despair still seized her in lonely hours. *Could I sustain this pretense when we marry?* she asked herself, pushing the terrifying thought down.

After the third trip, Durrance addressed her seriously. "Do you recall mentioning a renowned oculist in Wiesbaden? It seems prudent for me to consult him."

"You recommend it?"

"Yes, and I must go alone."

Ethne looked at him sharply. "You fear I would be tedious?"

"Not at all. I may require a period of solitude, perhaps entering a home. It is best to avoid distractions for a time."

"Are you certain?" Ethne asked, her voice trembling slightly. "If I thought you chose this because you felt I would be happier elsewhere, it would wound me deeply."

"No," Durrance assured her, speaking the truth. "We must separate. Both of us suffer under the burden of constant acting. Only because I respect your determination to honor our arrangement did I refrain from shouting the truth. I am returning to London next week and will decide then whether to proceed."

Ironically, Durrance was fortunate to raise this topic before receiving a telegram from Calder in Wadi Halfa. Ethne, unaware of the news, could not link his impending departure to any update on Feversham. That afternoon, the telegram arrived. Durrance carried it to The Pool in the evening, handed it to Ethne, and stepped away immediately, joining Mrs. Adair in the drawing room. He folded the paper so thoroughly that by the time Ethne opened it, she stood alone on the terrace.

Four stark words stared back at her: **"Feversham imprisoned at Omdurman."**

Her mind leaped to the horrors Durrance had described. The quiet September air, the rising mist over the creek, the closing prison gates in the heat of the Sudan—it all coalesced into a nightmare. "Tenfold justice!" she cried aloud, her voice breaking in protest against the cruelty. "And the fault was shared by my father and me as well. Neither of us truly understood."

She cursed herself for sending the fourth feather. Leaning on the stone railing, eyes closed, she wondered if Harry would survive the night. The cool stone beneath her hands felt like a bitter slap. Then, a resolve hardened within her. "Something must be done."

Durrance emerged from the house, anticipating her return. "He was and remains my friend. I cannot abandon him. I will write to Calder tonight. No expense will be spared. He is my friend, Ethne. You will see. From Suakin or Assouan, we shall act."

He framed every offer of aid as an expression of his loyalty, ensuring Ethne believed she held no stake in the rescue other than her gratitude.
