Elinor’s chest ached with quiet, unshakable unease as she watched Edward Ferrars drift through their cottage days. His visit should have been pure joy, but no matter how kind his words, no light of true, unburdened warmth shone in his eyes. It was impossible to miss how unhappy he was—yet try as she might, Elinor could not be certain his old, tender affection for her had not faded entirely. One moment he would look at her with that soft, lingering gaze she’d once thought only she could draw out of him, and the next he would draw back into cold, formal reserve, leaving her to wonder if she’d imagined the warmth at all.

The next morning, before the rest of the household had roused for breakfast, he slipped into their little parlour to find Elinor and Marianne already sipping their tea. Ever eager to give the pair every chance to nurture their quiet attachment, Marianne made a hasty excuse to run up to her room, her feet barely tapping the stairs before she heard the parlour door swing open again. She spun around to find Edward standing in the entryway, flustered, stammering that he meant to walk down to the village to check on his horses before they were all ready to sit for breakfast, that he would be back before long.

When he returned an hour later, he was brightened, full of praise for the valley spread out below their cottage. From the higher ground of the village, he’d looked over every winding wooded path and sun-dappled meadow, and the view had delighted him entirely. The subject was catnip for Marianne, who immediately leaned in, ready to gush over every crag and wild patch of gorse she loved most, and press him for every detail of what had caught his eye on the walk. But Edward laughed and held up a hand to stop her.

“You must not quiz me too far, Marianne,” he said. “I know nothing at all of this ‘picturesque’ craze everyone speaks of. I shall offend you with my ignorance, I promise. I would call a steep, dramatic crag just steep, a wild, uneven thicket strange and uncouth, and any distant half-hidden hill simply out of sight, instead of attributing its soft blur to the magic of hazy atmosphere. You must be content with my honest admiration: this is a very fine country. The hills are steep, the woods full of strong, tall timber, the valley snug and welcoming, dotted with rich meadows and neat little farmhouses. It is exactly what I want beautiful scenery to be—equal parts lovely and useful. I have no doubt it counts as picturesque by your standards, full of rocks and grey moss and brambles, but all of that is quite lost on me.”
“I am afraid that is far too true,” Marianne said, frowning. “But why on earth would you boast of such a lack of taste?”
“I suspect,” Elinor cut in, smiling softly, “that Edward is running from one kind of affectation straight into another. He is sick of all the people who pretend to adore wild, untamed nature far more than they actually do, just to sound clever and refined—so he affects to care far less for the beauty around him than he really does. He is as stubborn about his own little brand of pretension as any of them.”
“Exactly so,” Marianne agreed, nodding vehemently. “Admiring landscape has become nothing but empty jargon now. Everyone parrot the exact turns of phrase the first writer of picturesque beauty coined, no matter what they actually feel. I detest that kind of empty talk so much I often bite my own feelings back, for fear I will have no words to describe them that have not been worn thin of all meaning.”
“And I swear to you,” Edward said, turning back to Marianne, “that you feel every bit of that deep delight in a fine prospect you claim to, and I would never doubt it. But in return, you two must allow me to feel no more than I admit. I do not care for crooked, gnarled, dying trees—give me tall, straight, flourishing oaks any day. I do not want tattered, half-ruined cottages, or fields full of nettles and thistles and wild heather. A snug, warm farmhouse makes me far happier than a distant medieval watchtower, and a crowd of laughing, well-fed villagers pleases me far more than any band of romantic banditti you could describe.”

Marianne stared at him, astonished, then turned to look at Elinor with pity, as if her sister could ever tolerate such an unromantic perspective. Elinor only laughed, and the topic was dropped. Marianne fell silent, lost in thought, until a small detail right in front of her caught her eye. Edward was sitting beside her, and as he took his cup of tea from Mrs. Dashwood, his hand passed directly in front of her face. On one of his fingers, he wore a small plain gold ring, its center set with a neatly plaited lock of hair.
“I never saw you wear a ring before, Edward!” she blurted out. “Is that Fanny’s hair? I remember her promising you a lock of hers once. But I could have sworn her hair was far darker than that.”

The second the words left her mouth, Marianne knew she had blundered. A deep, burning flush rose all the way up Edward’s neck to his ears, and he flickered a quick, guilty glance over at Elinor before answering. “Yes, it is my sister’s hair. The setting always casts a different shade over it, you know.”
Elinor had met that fleeting, flustered look, and her heart swelled soft and warm. She knew at once the hair was hers, not Fanny’s—Marianne assumed it had been a free gift from Edward’s sister, but Elinor knew full well he must have snipped it from her without her noticing, some quiet moment she had never caught. She did not take it as an insult, not for a second. She pretended not to have noticed anything amiss at all, immediately striking up a conversation about their jar of homemade plum jam to distract the rest of the room, while resolving to find an excuse to lean close to that ring later, to confirm the color was exactly the soft chestnut of her own locks.

Edward’s embarrassment lingered long after that, settling into a quiet, distracted gravity that hung over him for the rest of the morning. Marianne berated herself for her carelessness over and over, and would have felt far worse if she had known how little Elinor was actually pained by the incident.

By midday, Sir John and Mrs. Jennings arrived at the cottage, having heard news that a gentleman had come to stay, and eager to get a look at their new guest. It did not take Sir John long, helped along by his mother-in-law’s well-honed talent for prying, to connect the name Ferrars to the F they had heard young Margaret mention in relation to Elinor. A whole mine of raillery was already building in his mind, only held back by the fact that they barely knew Edward well enough to tease him about it openly. Even so, Elinor could read every one of his significant, winking looks, and knew perfectly well how much of the secret their little household had already let slip to their neighbors.

Sir John never visited the Dashwoods without pressing them to an invitation, and this time he was determined to entertain their new guest properly. “You simply must come drink tea with us tonight,” he insisted. “We shall be all alone otherwise, and tomorrow you must absolutely come dine with us—we’ll have a full, merry party.”
Mrs. Jennings chimed in at once. “And who knows? We might even push the tables back and have a dance, that’s sure to tempt you, Miss Marianne!”
“A dance?” Marianne exclaimed, blinking in surprise. “Impossible! Who is there even to dance with?”
“Who? Why you, the Careys, and the Whitakers, of course!” Mrs. Jennings laughed. “You did not think we’d have no one to dance with just because that certain unnamed gentleman is off visiting his aunt, now did you?”
“God, I wish Willoughby was here right now,” Sir John sighed loudly.
The comment made Marianne’s cheeks flame bright crimson, and new little sparks of suspicion lit up in Edward’s eyes. He leaned in to Elinor, quiet, and asked her who Mr. Willoughby was. She gave him a short, vague explanation, but Marianne’s glowing, flustered face told him far more than words ever could.

As soon as Sir John and Mrs. Jennings had taken their leave, Edward wandered over to Marianne, a faint, playful glint in his eye. “I’ve been guessing,” he whispered. “Shall I tell you what I’ve guessed about Mr. Willoughby?”
Marianne tilted her head, confused. “What on earth do you mean?”
“Shall I tell you?” he repeated, smiling.
“Certainly.”
“Then I guess that Mr. Willoughby is very, very fond of hunting.”
Marianne stared at him, half scandalized, half helpless with laughter at his quiet mischief. After a moment of flustered silence, she swatted lightly at his arm. “Oh Edward! How can you tease me so? But I hope one day soon, I am certain of it, you will meet him—and you will love him, I promise.”
Edward blinked, a little surprised at how earnestly she spoke. “I have no doubt I will,” he said. He would never have dared to make the little joke at all, if he had not assumed Willoughby was just some casual new friend of the Dashwoods, a silly little running joke between the whole household.
