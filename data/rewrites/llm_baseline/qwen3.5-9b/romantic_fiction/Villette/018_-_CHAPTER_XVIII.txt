During the early days of my residence at the Terrace, Mr. Graham never seated himself near me. Even when he paced the room, his steps deliberately avoided the corner where I sat. He appeared preoccupied, his demeanor heavier than usual, and I immediately associated this change with Miss Fanshawe, expecting her name to tumble from his lips at any moment. I held my ears and mind in a state of perpetual alertness, waiting for the tender subject to arise. My patience was kept on permanent guard, and my sympathy remained stocked and ready to pour itself out.

Finally, after a brief internal struggle—which I observed and respected—he broached the topic. He introduced it delicately, almost anonymously. "I hear your friend is spending her vacation traveling?"

*Friend, forsooth!* I thought to myself. Yet, contradiction was impossible; he must have his way. I accepted the soft impeachment: *friend* it would be. Still, driven by curiosity, I asked whom he meant. He had taken a seat at my worktable and was recklessly unwinding a reel of thread.

"Ginevra—Miss Fanshawe," he said, "has accompanied the Cholmondeleys on a tour through southern France?"

"She has."

"Do you and she correspond?"

"It will astonish you to learn that I never once considered applying for that privilege."

"Have you seen letters written by her?"

"Yes; several addressed to her uncle."

"They lack neither wit nor *naïveté*; there is so much sparkle and so little art in her soul?"

"She writes comprehensively enough when addressing M. de Bassompierre. Anyone running can read them." (Indeed, Ginevra's epistles to her wealthy kinsman were typically business documents, straightforward requests for funds.)

"And her handwriting? One imagines it must be pretty, light, and ladylike?"

It was, and I agreed.

"I verily believe that everything she does is done well," said Dr. John. Seeing that I showed no inclination to echo this sentiment, he added, "Since you know her intimately, could you name a single point in which she is deficient?"

"She accomplishes many things very well." (*Flirting, among other things,* I added mentally).

"When do you suppose she will return to town?" he inquired shortly thereafter.

"Pardon me, Dr. John, but I must clarify. You honor me too greatly by ascribing to me an intimacy with Miss Fanshawe that I do not possess. I have never been the keeper of her plans or secrets. You will find her truest friends elsewhere, among the Cholmondeleys, for instance."

He genuinely believed I was stinging with a jealousy akin to his own!

"Excuse her," he said. "Judge her indulgently. The glitter of high society deceives her, but she will soon discover these people to be hollow. She will return to you with increased attachment and confirmed trust. I know the Cholmondeleys well: they are superficial, showy, and selfish. Depend on it, at heart, Ginevra values you above a dozen such individuals."

"You are very kind," I replied briefly.

A protest against the sentiments attributed to me burned on my lips, but I extinguished the flame. I allowed myself to be viewed as the humiliated, discarded, and now pining confidante of the distinguished Miss Fanshawe—but, dear reader, it was a bitter pill to swallow.

"Yet, you see," continued Graham, "while I comfort *you*, I cannot find the same solace for myself. I cannot hope she will render me justice. De Hamal is utterly worthless, yet I fear he pleases her. Wretched delusion!"

My patience finally snapped. Without warning, it crumbled completely. Perhaps illness and weakness had rendered it brittle.

"Dr. Bretton," I burst out, "there is no delusion like your own. On every point except one, you are a man: frank, healthy, right-thinking, and clear-sighted. On this singular exception, you are merely a slave. I declare that regarding Miss Fanshawe, you deserve no respect, nor do I grant you any."

I rose and left the room, thoroughly agitated. This confrontation occurred in the morning; I was destined to encounter him again that evening, and I realized immediately that I had caused damage.

He was not fashioned from common clay, nor assembled from vulgar materials. While the broad outlines of his nature were shaped with strength and vigor, the details possessed a workmanship of almost feminine delicacy—finer, far finer, than one could expect to find in a man, even after years of acquaintance. Indeed, until some sharp contact with his nerves exposed their acute sensitivity through its effects, this elaborate construction had to remain unseen. Especially since the faculty of sympathy was not prominent in him. To feel, and to instantly grasp another's feelings, are distinct properties; few possess both, and fewer still possess neither. Dr. John possessed the former in exquisite perfection. Since I acknowledged he lacked the latter to an equal degree, the reader should refrain from concluding he was unsympathetic or unfeeling. On the contrary, he was a kind and generous man. Make your need known, and his hand opens wide. Pour your grief into words, and he turns no deaf ear. Do not, however, expect miracles of intuition or refined perception; disappointment awaits those who do.

That night, when Dr. John entered the room and met the evening lamp, I perceived his entire mechanism at a glance. Having called him a "slave" and banned him from respect on a specific point, he must harbor peculiar feelings. Whether the epithet was apt and the ban justified, he offered no denial; his mind candidly entertained the possibility of his own inadequacy. Seeking in this accusation the cause of the distress that had gripped his mental peace, his demeanor appeared grave, perhaps cold, to both me and his mother.

Yet, there was no malice, no rancor, no pettiness in his countenance. It remained beautiful with a man's finest beauty, even in its depression. When I hurried to place his chair at the table, anticipating the servant, and handed him his tea with trembling care, he said, "Thank you, Lucy," in a kindly tone of his full, pleasant voice as my ear had ever welcomed.

For my part, I had only one path forward: I must expiate my rash vehemence, or I could not sleep that night. This was intolerable; I made no pretense of being able to sustain such a footing. Solitude, school isolation, conventual silence—anything seemed preferable to remaining entangled with Dr. John. As for Ginevra, she might take the silver wings of a dove—or any other flying creature—and ascend directly to the highest stars, where her lover's fanciful imagination fixed the constellation of her charms. Never again would it be mine to dispute such an arrangement.

Long did I try to catch his eye. Again and again, his gaze met mine, but having nothing to say, it withdrew, leaving me baffled. After tea, he sat, sad and quiet, reading a book. I wished I dared approach and sit near him, but it seemed that if I ventured such a step, he would inevitably display hostility and indignation. I longed to speak aloud yet dared not whisper.

His mother left the room. Then, moved by an insupportable regret, I merely murmured the words, "Dr. Bretton."

He looked up from his book. His eyes were not cold or malevolent; his mouth was not cynical. He was ready and willing to listen to whatever I had to say; his spirit was of vintage too mellow and generous to sour in a single thunderclap.

"Dr. Bretton, forgive my hasty words: *do, do* forgive them."

He smiled the moment I spoke.

"Perhaps I deserved them, Lucy. If you do not respect me, I am certain it is because I am not respectable. I fear I am an awkward fool; I must manage poorly in some way, for where I wish to please, it seems I fail to please."

"That you cannot be sure of; and even if such were the case, is it the fault of your character or of another's perception? But now, let me unsay what I said in anger. In one thing, and in all things, I deeply respect you. If you think too little of yourself and too much of others, what is that but an excellence?"

"Can I think too much of Ginevra?"

"*I* believe you may; *you* believe you cannot. Let us agree to differ. Let me be pardoned; that is what I ask."

"Do you think I cherish ill-will toward one warm word?"

"I see you do not and cannot; but simply say, 'Lucy, I forgive you!' Say that to ease me of this heart-ache."

"Set aside your heart-ache, as I will set aside mine; for you wounded me slightly, Lucy. Now, when the pain is gone, I forgive more than I was hurt—I feel grateful, as to a sincere well-wisher."

"I *am* your sincere well-wisher; you are right."

Thus, our quarrel ended.

I present the feeling exactly as I felt it at the time, describing the view of character as it appeared when discovered.
