Two more hours of careful descending, and I stepped clear of the great Gorge’s shadow out into the wide, blazing country beyond. For all the sharp, bewildered unease thrumming under my skin, I cannot put to words the rush of joy that hit me at the sight of its wild, uncurbed splendour.
Before I had left the Gorge’s high mouth, I had paused long enough to survey the vast expanse spread out below me, and counted twenty-seven massive volcanoes rising from the land. That tally did not include two monstrous distant ranges of fire-hills burning far off to my right, nor the hundred thousand smaller vents and steaming seeps scattered across every mile of the landscape.
This was truly a land of fire and water. A small active fire-hill jutted from a stretch of open sea no more than a mile from where I stood, and a score more dotted its waters behind it. I had counted three smaller inland seas, plus one vast ocean that stretched unbroken all the way to the red haze of the far volcanoes, vanishing completely from sight with no visible horizon end. Islands rose from the waves, each crowned with its own smoking crater, while elsewhere fire-hills jutted straight up from the surf. Steam hung thick over the near waters, as if the sea itself boiled at random points across its surface.
A low, constant muttering thunder hummed in the red-tinted air, shifting its source from near to far, rolling through the ground. I knew that noise for the voices of the fire-hills, the raw, living energy of the world breathing out loud. This was the old world’s unfiltered roar, sharper and far more present than the faint, muffled world-noise of my own age.
Let me set down what I saw before me as clearly as memory holds it. To the left of the Gorge mouth loomed a huge, blackened mountain, rising fifteen to twenty miles straight up into the perpetual dark overhead. A mighty peaked volcano jutted from its flank at a height I guessed to be five miles, and a second sat further up, nine or ten miles above the ground. As if that were not wonder enough, two more giant fire-hills smouldered on its far left crest, perched so high they glowed like smouldering twin suns hanging in the night sky. Below them, vast mountains of ash and cinder had poured down the slopes for uncountable eons, building grey, sombre monuments to the unhurried, dreadful glory of deep time.
To my right, sea stretched on endlessly, glowing red with the light of the distant burning hills. To my left, vast ancient forests rolled out, broken here and there by more monstrous fire-hills rising above the treeline. That was the first imprint the land left on my raw, astonished senses.
Once I had descended fully out of the Gorge, I halted, suddenly uncertain which path would lead me to Naani’s hidden refuge. I wandered back and forth for long minutes before I cursed my own foolishness, and climbed back up into the Gorge mouth to map my route first.
There, the obvious, only viable path lay clear before me. The endless sea blocked all routes to my right, but along the shoreline where land met water to my left, a clear, passable stretch ran as far as my eyes could see. It felt sure enough that if I followed it, I would find a way forward. I named this red-shining, fire-wreathed expanse the Country of the Seas, and descended back down to its floor.
By the time I reached the shore, twenty-four full hours had passed since I last slept. I hunted out a sheltered nook, finding a dry, warm rock basin ringed by three thick, gnarled trees. I ate three of my ration tablets, sipped from my water skin (my belly still ached for real, hot food), spread a soft bed of pine needles in the basin, and lay down. My thoughts drifted to Naani for only a few moments before I slipped over the edge of sleep.
I woke abruptly, half-submerged in hot, steamy water. By some mercy I had not drowned in my rest. I stood up fast to find the basin brim full of pungent, bubbling hot water, pouring in through a smoothed crevice on its far side. I realized then this was a periodic geyser vent that filled the basin every hour, then drained slowly back through fissures in the rock’s floor.
Shaking the water off, I stripped off my armour, fished all my supplies out of the rising pool, and spread my clothes and gear across a flat, sun-heated rock to dry. While they dried, I waded back into the almost empty warm pool for a pleasant soak, keeping my Diskos propped within arm’s reach on the edge. I sensed none of the crawling, horrid malevolence that had hung thick over every mile of the Night Land. All the evil forces of that dark realm, I reasoned, had clustered around the Mighty Pyramid, drawn to the concentration of millions of living human souls there just as sharks trail a ship carrying slaughtered cattle. I did not pause to puzzle over how those dark powers had crossed the boundary to this land, for such speculation was useless vanity.
My clothes dried quickly. I climbed out of the pool, dressed, strapped my armour back on, and felt far lighter in spirit. I curled up beside the now-empty basin again, and slept another six full hours, woken only once by the gurgling roar of the geyser refilling the basin.
When I woke, I was sharp, rested, and ready to push on. I ate my rations, drank, and set off at a fast, steady pace. The vast nameless forests stayed to my left, the shoreline to my right. The trees grew right down into the surf in many places, so I often cut a path through the woods. I had never known a wild, uncurbed mystery like that of these old forests, nothing like the solemn, quiet groves of the Underground Fields. Their sharp, sweet scent lifted my whole spirit.
Life moved in those dark woods all around me. I caught glimpses of glinting eyes peering out from the underbrush now and then, retreating back into shadow before I could make out their owners. None came close enough to harm me. Three times that day I came to small red spitting fire-hills, their roars carrying through the trees long before I reached them. A dead, desolate zone of blackened bare ground ringed each crater, but tiny fast-growing plants crept close to their edges, sprouting and living their whole short lives between eruption bursts. I passed thirty-seven steaming springs that day, some roaring so loud in the distance I mistook them for the cries of strange wild beasts.
When the eighteenth hour of travel rolled around, I sat down against a tree trunk as I had at the sixth and twelfth hour marks, ate two ration tablets, and sipped from my fizzing, mineral-rich water skin. I chose a bed spot that had a solid boulder at my back, so no creature could sneak up on me from behind. I drifted toward sleep, my mind lingering on Naani, as it had all day, half-convinced her spirit hovered close, trying to speak to me. I swore to push faster, to cover more ground, to reach her small hidden refuge in the dead world faster, and fell asleep instantly.
I woke sharp all at once. The bright red volcanic glow of the country showed me my danger in a heartbeat, no confusing shadows or half-dark to leave me suspended in terror as the Night Land always did. I was propped on one elbow, staring right at six squat, hunch-shouldered men crouched at the treeline no more than twenty paces off. Their necks and shoulders were thick as human bulls, their eyes glowing like wild beasts’ in the red light. My hand closed around the hilt of my Diskos. I stood up ready to fight, and in the blink of an eye, they were gone. I had kept my gaze fixed on that exact patch of shadow the whole time, and never saw them move. I knew they had been there, no illusion, but they had vanished completely.
I checked my time marker: I had slept five hours. I ate two rations standing, eyes scanning the treeline, drank a mouthful of water, and set off at a dead run. I was lean, hard, and covered in muscle after weeks of hard travel, and I was sure I could outpace any of those humped men. For thirty straight hours I pushed onward at that hard run, eating two rations every sixth hour, never slowing, never taking my eyes off the treeline. Twice or thrice I sensed things moving parallel to me through the woods on my left, keeping pace with my speed, always hidden, never breaking cover. I did not dare stop to sleep until I found somewhere safe.
Near the end of the thirtieth hour, I saw open water stretching ahead of me, separate from the endless sea on my right. I thought the sea curved inland at that point, but when I reached it, I found a wide, fast river pouring out of the interior forests to meet the ocean. A small island sat right at the river’s mouth. I thought for a second it would make a perfect refuge from the men tracking me, but I needed to get across to the far shore to keep following the sea path that stretched on ahead.
I could not swim, and even if I could, there was no telling what monstrous things lurked in that warm, fast-flowing river. I turned and followed the riverbank upstream, hoping to find a narrower crossing, and barely a mile inland I hit a second tributary pouring into the first. Now I was trapped: the sea to my south, one river ahead of me, the second river to my north.
The threat of the humped men spurred my wits awake. I hunted for fallen logs, dragged two long light ones down to the water, lashed them together tight with my spare armour belts and straps, and hacked a rough pole from a young sapling to make a rickety raft. All the while I never stopped scanning the treeline, terrified the humped men would break cover before I could push off. It took me half an hour of clumsy paddling to get across to the small island. I ate my rations, collapsed on the mossy ground, and slept nine full straight hours, thirty-three hours after my last rest. I slept so deep I might as well have been dead, the island feeling perfectly safe.
When I woke, I finished the paddle across to the far shore, retrieved my belts and straps from the logs, and set off again. I was sure I had left the humped men trapped on the near bank, though the thought later crossed my mind that there could be others on this side too. I moved fast all day, passing two giant fire-hills whose eruptions shook the whole surrounding landscape four times, ducking for cover four times to avoid passing monstrous beasts that never noticed me. My thoughts kept drifting back to Naani, the whole of my journey one long, unspoken promise to her, even when constant perils kept me from lingering on that thought. I passed thousands of acres of lush green forest, rich sweet air thick with life, and daydreamed for a moment that one day, when the Night Land’s last refuge grew too cold and airless to support human life, survivors might flee here and build a new home, if they could ever find a way past the evil things clustered around the Mighty Pyramid.
A little before the eighteenth hour of travel that day, I emerged from the woods and climbed a long low cliff that rose above the shoreline. A strange, tall pointed rock a hundred feet high stood directly ahead, and I moved toward it carefully, curious. What I had first thought was a single spire turned out to be a narrow flat platform perched on top of the spire, covered in small trees and moss, while the rest of the spire’s bare rock looked as if it had been scoured clean by some ancient blast. It looked like the perfect, defensible place to rest. I began climbing, and halfway up, I found a recessed ledge set a little back from the cliff face. I stopped there, ate and drank, and fell asleep before I could even think of Naani, heavy with the accumulated weariness of days of nonstop travel.
I woke abruptly seven hours later, my inner warning instinct screaming of immediate danger. I grabbed my Diskos, crept to the edge of the ledge, and looked down. Two humped men were scaling the spire below me, climbing fast and silent, their thick bull necks glistening with sweat. They had smelled me, and they were coming to kill me. I stepped back from the ledge edge, Diskos raised, ready to cut the first one down the second he pulled himself within reach, so there was no chance they could flank me while I fought the first.
