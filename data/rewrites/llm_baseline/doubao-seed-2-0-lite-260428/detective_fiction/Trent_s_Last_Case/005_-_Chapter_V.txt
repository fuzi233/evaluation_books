There are quiet, unaccountable moments in life when the hidden, half-busy part of ourselves slips a flash of foreknowledge into our waking minds: that unshakable, unforced certainty that everything is about to break right, not the desperate bravado of a man staring down fate, not the daft persistent optimism of a fool, but a little wild spark of conviction that rises unbidden like a bird flushing out of heather. The general knows at dawn his battle will turn to victory; the golfer on the green knows before he swings that the long putt will drop. As Trent climbed the narrow staircase to the second floor outside the library, that exact certainty settled over him, thick and warm. A jumble of stray observations and half-formed inferences he’d not yet been able to weave together suddenly hummed in his head; the tiny, seemingly meaningless details he’d noted earlier now felt like pieces of a puzzle that were about to slot straight into place. He did not know what the full truth was yet, but he knew, beyond any doubt, that the light was about to break.

The bedrooms lined both sides of a wide, carpeted upper hall, lit by a tall sash window at the far end, where the corridor turned sharp right to lead to the servants’ wing. The butler Martin’s room was a quirk, tucked off a tiny half-landing halfway to the attic floor. Trent glanced in as he passed: a cramped, spotless, completely unremarkable little room. The rest of the stairs he climbed with exaggerated, deliberate care, pressing close to the wall and setting each foot down slow, but the old dry timber still let out a series of loud, unapologetic creaks that echoed down the empty hall.

He made straight for Manderson’s room, the first door on the right when he reached the landing. He tested the latch and lock, both working smoothly, ran a finger over the key’s wards, then turned to take in the space. It was shockingly small, almost bare, a far cry from the opulence most plutocrats surrounded themselves with. The bed was unmade, sheets and blankets tangled and thrown back as if the man had torn himself out of it in a hurry that morning the body was found. Bright sunlight spilled through the window over the rumpled bedding, glinting off the gold work of a set of dentures soaking in a shallow glass bowl on the plain little bedside table, next to a dented wrought-iron candlestick. A few items of discarded clothing lay draped over one of the two rush-seated chairs, and the top of the chest of drawers pressed into service as a dressing table was strewn with the half-ordered mess of a man who’d dressed in a terrible rush. Trent’s eye picked out at once that the dead man had never washed, never shaved before he left the room at dawn. He poked the dental plate gently with a fingertip, frowning at the odd, unexplainable detail of it left half-submerged there, while the sun-warmed empty air of the room began to hum with a faint, gruesome weight. He could almost see the man there, haggard, moving by the dim first light of day, glancing over his shoulder every few seconds at the connecting door behind which his wife slept, his eyes wide with some nameless terror.

Trent shivered, and turned to yank open the two tall wall cupboards flanking the bed to ground himself in hard facts. They were stuffed full of fine clothing, a huge, varied selection that was clearly one of the only small luxuries the grim millionaire had ever allowed himself. The same ran to his shoes: rows and rows of them, carefully treed and polished, ranged along two low shelves against the far wall, not a single heavy work boot among the lot. Trent, an amateur of fine leather footwear himself, ran an appreciative eye over the collection: it was clear Manderson had been proud of his small, neat foot, every pair narrow, round-toed, exquisitely stitched, all cut from the exact same last.

Then his eyes narrowed, locking on a pair of scuffed patent-leather shoes on the top shelf. These were the exact pair Inspector Murch had described earlier, the ones Manderson had been wearing the night he died. They were well worn, but they’d been polished not long ago. Something about their uppers snagged his attention. He leaned in, comparing their finish to the pairs on either side, then lifted them to run a finger along the seam where the uppers joined the soles.

Unconsciously, he began to whistle, soft and precise, a short melodic phrase Murch would have recognized instantly. Most men who are fiercely self-controlled have a tiny, involuntary tell for when they’re holding back excitement: Murch had long ago noticed that whenever Trent picked up a hot new scent in a case, he whistled that exact bar of Mendelssohn’s *Songs Without Words* in A Major.

He turned the shoes over, measured them with a marked tape he pulled from his pocket, then squinted hard at the soles. Tucked in the narrow angle between the heel and the instep on each shoe was a faint, smudged trace of the same red gravel that lined the drive and the paths near the copse where Manderson’s body had been found.

Trent set the shoes down on the floor, paced over to the window, and stared out at the lawn, still whistling under his breath, his eyes unfocused. Once his lips parted and he muttered a sharp, soft expletive of pure, sudden enlightenment. When he turned back, he moved fast, checking every single pair of shoes on the shelves one after the other, every last one.

That done, he picked up the clothes draped over the chair, examined every seam, then folded and replaced them. He hunted through both wardrobes a second time, pored over the mess on the dressing table for a second long stretch, then sank down onto the empty chair, dropped his head in his hands, and sat staring at the carpet for several full minutes. Finally he stood, and pulled open the inner connecting door that led straight to Mrs. Manderson’s bedroom.

One glance told him the big room had been stripped bare in a tearing hurry, all traces of the lady’s personal life cleared out. The usual array of cosmetics, perfumes, and trinkets that crowded a woman’s dressing table was gone. No hats, gloves, veils, or half-hidden handkerchiefs peeked out from under pillows or tumbled out of drawers. The space looked as neat and impersonal as an unused guest room, but every detail of the furniture and soft, muted colour scheme spoke to an unconventional, exacting artistic taste. Trent, his eye trained to pick up on small clues about a person’s character, could easily picture the quiet, lonely woman who’d lived here, and his brow drew tight as he thought of the quiet burdens laid on her life, and the story of the crime that was slowly taking solid, unexpected shape in his head.

He walked over to the tall French window facing the door, opened it, and stepped out onto a small iron-railed balcony. Below him a stretch of broad lawn ran straight down, only separated from the house wall by a narrow flower bed, sloping sharply at the far end toward the old orchard. The room’s other sash window looked out directly above the library’s garden entrance, and a second door in the far corner of the room opened onto the main upper hall, the door the maid used to enter each morning.

Trent sat down on the empty bed, pulled his notebook out, and quickly sketched a rough floor plan of both bedrooms. The bed sat tucked in the corner between the connecting door and the sash window, its headboard pressed up against the shared wall between the two rooms. He stared at the pillows for a long moment, then lay down deliberately full length on the bed, and looked straight through the open connecting door into Manderson’s empty room.

Satisfied with that observation, he stood, noted down on his plan that each side of the bed held a small covered side table, the one furthest from the door holding a graceful copper electric lamp strung on a long loose wire to the wall. He studied the lamp thoughtfully, then checked the wall switches for the rest of the room’s lights, all mounted by the main door, far out of reach of anyone lying in bed. He confirmed every fixture was working, then turned on his heel, walked straight back into Manderson’s room, and rang the bell pull by the door.

“I need a favour, Martin,” he said, as the impassive butler appeared in the doorway. “I’d like you to send Mrs. Manderson’s maid up to speak with me.”

“Certainly, sir.” Martin nodded.

“What sort of woman is she? Sharp, on the ball?”

“She’s French, sir,” Martin said succinctly, pausing for a beat before adding, “She hasn’t been with us long, but I’d say the girl has more than enough worldly sense to look after herself.”

Trent grinned. “Good, I’m not easily flustered. I just have a few questions for her.”

“I’ll send her up at once, sir.” Martin withdrew, and Trent wandered the small room, hands behind his back.

The maid, Célestine, appeared sooner than he expected, a tiny neat figure in a black uniform. She’d spotted Trent crossing the lawn from her window earlier that day, and had been desperately hoping the famous mystery-solver—whose reputation was as legendary in the servants’ quarters as it was anywhere else—would send for her. Her nerves were frayed to breaking point after the murder; the stuffy Inspector Murch had chilled her into silence with his official gruffness, but Trent had not struck her as the sort of policeman who crushed dramatic scenes, and from a distance he’d seemed perfectly *sympathique*.

Still, instinct told her coquetry would be out of place here. She entered with an air of open, amiable candour. “Monsieur wished to speak to me?” she said, adding helpfully, “My name is Célestine.”

“Naturally,” Trent said, all business. “Tell me, Célestine: when you brought your mistress her tea at seven yesterday morning, was the door between these two bedrooms open?”

Célestine lit up like a lamp at once. “Oh oui! It was open as it always is, monsieur, and I shut it as I always do! But you must understand, listen—come with me, I will show you, it is simple!” She tugged gently at his arm, pulling him through the connecting door into the big bedroom. “See! I come in from the hall door there, carrying the tray, I walk toward the bed. Before I reach the bed, the door to Monsieur Manderson’s room is right here on my right, wide open—but the door swings in toward the bed, not toward me! I never see into his room at all when I push it shut. It is madame’s strict order to shut it first thing, every morning. Yesterday it was exactly the same! Madame was still fast asleep, she saw nothing, I shut the door, set down the tray, opened the curtains, laid out her things, and left—that is all!” She paused, breathless, and spread her hands wide.

Trent nodded, his face grave. “I see exactly how it was. Thank you, Célestine. So Mr. Manderson was supposed to still be in his room the whole time your mistress was getting dressed and eating her breakfast in her sitting room, right? No one noticed he was gone at all?”

“Oui, monsieur.”

“Nobody missed him, in fact,” Trent said. “Thank you, you’ve been a great help.”

Célestine turned at the outer door, her hand on the knob, and a sudden fierce passion flooded her small dark face. “It is nothing, monsieur. I hope you catch the man who killed him. But I do not regret Monsieur Manderson, not at all!” She switched into rapid, fiery French, her words spilling out. “I would throw myself into the fire for madame, she is so sweet, so gentle! But that man—sour, silent, cold! I could not bear him one minute longer! The very idea that people like him walk the earth—!”

“Enough of that racket, Célestine!” Trent cut in sharply, his own fluent student’s French coming back to him in a rush. “That’s quite the scene you’re putting on, it’s getting tedious, you know. Pull yourself together, mademoiselle, it’s terribly reckless. If the inspector downstairs heard you talking like that you’d find yourself in real trouble. And stop waving your fists around, you’ll knock something over.” He softened a little as she calmed down under his steady gaze. “I see you’re far more relieved than the rest of us that Mr. Manderson is gone. I almost suspect he never gave you so much as a second glance, did he?”

“He barely ever looked at me at all!” Célestine said simply.

“Now that is truly the last straw,” Trent said, grinning. “You’d be quite the entertainment at a small tea party, that’s for sure. Well, I’m very busy. Good day, Célestine. You are quite a beauty, you know.”

Célestine took the line as an unexpected compliment, her balance restored. She flashed a quick bright smile over her shoulder and slipped out the door, the lock clicking softly behind her.

Left alone, Trent muttered a couple of equally fiery French curses under his breath, then turned back to his problem. He fetched the pair of patent leather shoes he’d examined earlier, set one on each of the two chairs, and sat down opposite them, hands in his pockets, eyes fixed unblinkingly on the two dumb, leather witnesses. Now and then he whistled a few almost inaudible bars of that Mendelssohn tune. The house was dead quiet outside, save for the faint chirp of birds in the trees beyond the open window, and the soft rustle of the climbing ivy on the sill. Trent’s face had gone hard and sombre with thought, and he did not move for half an hour straight.

Finally he stood fast, replaced the shoes carefully back on their shelf, and stepped out onto the landing. Directly opposite him were two more bedroom doors. He opened the first one he came to, and stepped into a messy, lived-in space that was clearly not Manderson’s. Fishing rods and golf clubs leaned tangled in one corner, a stack of books teetered in another. The dressing table and mantelpiece were heaped with the jumble of a man who never bothered to tidy: pipes, penknives, golf balls, old letters, photos, tins of tobacco. Fine etchings hung on the walls, a row of shoes and boots lined up under the far window. Trent studied the shoes intently for a long minute, measured several of them with his tape, whistling very softly all the while. Then he sank down on the edge of the unmade bed, his gaze roving gloomily over the room.

His eyes landed on the photos on the mantel first. He picked up one of Marlowe and Manderson out riding, two more of famous Alpine peaks, a faded old print of three ragged young men dressed in costume as 16th century foot soldiers, one of them unmistakeably the sharp-eyed secretary Marlowe. A framed portrait of a stately old woman, who looked a little like Marlowe, sat in the corner. Trent pulled a cigarette out of an open box on the mantel, lit it, then picked up a flat leather case sitting right next to the box.

It flipped open easily, revealing a small, exquisitely made light revolver, and a loose handful of cartridges. The initials *J.M.* were engraved deep into the stock.

A footstep sounded on the stairs. Trent had just swung open the revolver’s breech to peer down the barrel when Inspector Murch appeared in the doorway. “I was wondering where you’d got to—” he began, then stopped short when he saw what Trent was holding. “Whose is that pistol, Mr. Trent?” he asked, mild as ever.

“Looks like it belongs to the man who lives in this room, John Marlowe,” Trent said lightly, tapping the engraved initials. “Found it on the mantel there. Seems like a handy little thing. Looks like it was very carefully cleaned not too long ago, but I don’t know much about firearms.”

“Oh, I do,” Murch said quietly, taking the pistol gently from his hand. “Firearms are a bit of a speciality of mine, as you know. And you don’t need an expert to spot what’s important here.” He set the revolver back in its case on the mantel, fished a cartridge out of the box, laid it on his big palm, then pulled a small leaden bullet out of his waistcoat pocket and set it next to the cartridge. The bullet was slightly dented at the nose, scratched with bright new fresh marks across its sides.

“That’s the one they pulled from Manderson’s head?” Trent murmured, leaning in.

“That’s him,” Murch nodded. “Lodged in the bone at the back of his skull. Dr. Stock dug it out less than an hour ago, sent it right up to me. The bright new scratches are from his surgical tools. The rest of the marks? Those are from the rifling grooves of a gun barrel—exactly like the one you’re holding. Same make, same calibre. No other gun makes that exact pattern on a bullet.”

For a long quiet moment, the two men stared at each other across the case that held the revolver. “This whole case is backwards,” Trent said finally. “It makes no sense. Let’s lay out what we know for certain. We were both sure Manderson sent Marlowe off in the car to Southampton, and that Marlowe didn’t get back until very late last night, hours after the murder happened.”

“There’s no doubt at all about that,” Murch said, putting sharp emphasis on the verb.

“And now,” Trent went on, “this neat little pistol is asking us to believe the most ridiculous story possible. That Marlowe never went to Southampton at all. That he snuck back to the house in the dead of night, somehow convinced Manderson to get dressed and go out into the grounds without making a sound that would wake his wife or any of the servants, shot him with his own gun, then carefully cleaned the gun, snuck back in to leave it sitting right out on his mantelpiece where the police would find it, then hid somewhere for the rest of the day with a whole large motor car, and drove back up to the house pretending he’d just arrived from Southampton at what, nine last night?”

“A little after nine,” Murch said, staring glumly at Trent. “And as you say, that theory sounds totally wild—until you realize it falls apart completely before it even gets started. Marlowe was fifty to a hundred miles away when that shot was fired. He definitely did go to Southampton.”
