Pushing past the last of the tangles of braying mules and overloaded market carts, the young stranger drew a breath of cleaner Florentine air and turned to Nello with a faint, relieved smile. “To tell you the truth, I am not sorry my former guide Bratti the itinerant peddler passed me off to you. His accent is rougher than a ploughboy’s, and his whole business was a riddle I had no wish to solve. Is it common among you Florentines for a man who trades in rags and broken glass to talk as if he keeps a shop stocked with fine lutes and polished swords?”

“Common? No! Our Bratti is no ordinary man,” Nello answered, his loquacity spilling out as freely as wine from an overfilled skin. “He has a whole theory for his life, and he lives up to it—more than I can say for any philosopher I have ever had the honour of shaving. Bratti’s plan is to squeeze every drop of joy out of his years on earth, and joy for him is the thrill of hard bargaining, haggling over every last scrap of old iron or tattered cloth. When his days are done, he’ll leave every last penny he’s won to Holy Church, to bargain for the easiest possible passage through purgatory. He even had his will drawn up for the lowest fee a notary would agree to, to save every coin for that final trade. But I have told him to his face, ‘Bratti, your bargain limps, and you are the lame side of it. Does it not sadden you a little to look at paintings of Paradise? You will never get to chaffer over rags or rusty nails there. The saints and angels want neither pins nor tinder, and except for San Bartolommeo, who carries his flayed skin slung over his arm like an inconvenient old cloak, I see no chance for you to strike a deal for secondhand clothing.’” He crossed himself abruptly, his cheery tone softening to gravity. “But God pardon me for this light talk! It ill befits a morning when Lorenzo lies dead, the whole city’s in an uproar, and the Muses are tearing their hair—an agony any barber who serves our learned men feels deep in his bones. And you yourself, Messere, seem to carry a shadow over you too: a man of your speech and fine bearing would never put up with a shabby roadside inn like the one you left last night if some misfortune had not befallen him.”

“What Lorenzo is that whose death you speak of?” the stranger asked, so fixed on the news that he did not even notice the quiet inquiry into his own circumstances.

“What Lorenzo? There is only one Lorenzo whose death could throw the Mercato into chaos, make the lantern atop the Duomo look like it leaps in despair, and set the Republic’s caged lions to looking as if they might tear each other apart. I mean Lorenzo de’ Medici, the Pericles of our Florentine Athens—if I may dare make that comparison to a Greek.”

“Why not?” the other laughed. “I doubt Athens even in Pericles’ prime ever produced a barber half so learned as you.”

“Ah, I knew I could not be mistaken!” the rapid, grinning Nello clapped his hands lightly. “Else I have shaved the venerable Demetrio Calcondila for forty years to no purpose. But pardon my wonder: your Italian is smoother than his, even smoother than that of the accomplished Marullo, who wedded the Italian Muse in more ways than one when he married our brilliant, lovely Alessandra Scala.”

“It will ease your wonder to know I come of a Greek stock planted in Italian soil longer than the mulberry trees that grow so thick around our lands,” the stranger explained. “I was born at Bari, raised by an Italian guardian, and only reconnected fully to my Greek heritage when I travelled long across the old lands of gods and heroes. My Greek learning, and a handful of ancient gems sewn into the lining of my doublet, are all the fortune shipwreck has left me. And when the great towers fall, you know, it is ill luck for the small nest-builders: your Pericles’ death makes me half wish I had turned my steps to Rome, as I almost did. A fallacious Augustinian monk persuaded me otherwise. ‘At Rome,’ he said, ‘you will be lost in a crowd of hungry scholars begging for scraps of patronage. But at Florence, every corner basks in Lorenzo’s sunshine of favour. Florence is the best market in Italy for a man of your talents.’”

“Gnaffè, and so it will remain, I hope!” Nello waved off his worry. “Lorenzo was not the only patron of learning in our city—heaven forbid! Because he was a great, sweet melon, that does not make every other Florentine a worthless pumpkin. Have we not Bernardo Rucellai, Alamanno Rinuccini, and a dozen other wealthy men who adore wit and letters? And if you want guidance on all such matters, I, Nello, am your man. It feels like a thousand years until I can be of service to a bel erudito like yourself. First, though, we must attend to your hair. That beard, my fine young man, has to go, even if it was as dear to you as the nymph of your dreams. Here at Florence, we do not love to see a man’s nose peeking out over a cascade of unkempt hair. But mark this: once you have been shaven, you have crossed the Rubicon. If you repent later and let your beard grow back after it has fought the razor, your mouth will not long show what Messer Angelo calls the divine prerogative of lips, but will look like a dark cavern fringed with wild, horrent brambles.”

“That is a terrible prophecy,” the Greek laughed, “especially if Florentine maidens are half as pretty as the little contadina Tessa I stole a kiss from this morning.”

“Tessa? She is a rough-handed farm girl who reeks of the mule stables!” Nello scoffed. “You will rise to far better favour with ladies who carry no such scent. But to earn that, you cannot look like a sgherro, a ruffian of ill repute: you must look like a polished courtier-scholar, the sort of man who sins gently among well-bred, well-fed people, not one who guzzles vile sour vino di sotto in a random roadside tavern.”

“With all my heart,” the stranger agreed. “If the Florentine Graces demand it, I will give up my beard. But—”

“Yes, yes!” Nello interrupted, already herding him along. “I know what you mean. You will not part with your hyacinthine curls, and there is no need. Just a little pruning—ecco!—and you will look the very image of the illustrious Pico di Mirandola in his prime. And here we are, just in time, at the edge of Piazza San Giovanni, at the door of my shop. But I see you pausing: naturally, you want to gawk at our wonder of the world, our Duomo, Santa Maria del Fiore. Well, a single glance, but I beg you save the closer survey for after your shave. I am quivering with the inspiration of my art right to the edge of my razor! Come this way.”

The mercurial barber seized his arm and led him to the south side of the piazza, where the full view spread out before them: the huge dark, soaring shell of Brunelleschi’s cupola, the slender, graceful line of Giotto’s campanile piercing the clear April sky, and the quaint octagon of the Baptistery of San Giovanni, its unique bronze doors glowing still with the dimmed glory of their original gilding. The pink, white, and purple inlaid marbles were far fresher then than they would be four centuries later, when long winters had bleached their bright whites to the warm ochre of well-mellowed meerschaum. The Duomo’s façade was not yet a disgrace of faded stucco, but held the magnificent half-finished promise of the marble inlay and statued niches Giotto had designed 150 years before. The campanile, with all its harmonious blend of colour and form, drew the eye upward high into the bright air, seeming a prophetic symbol that human life could some day be shaped to match that pure, aspiring beauty.

But that was not the impression it made on the Greek. His eyes were drawn irresistibly upward, but he stood with his arms folded, curls falling back over his shoulders, a faint hint of scorn tugging at the corner of his lip. When his gaze fell again, he glanced around the piazza with a cool, scanning curiosity that pricked Nello’s Florentine pride.

“Well, my fine young man,” he said, with a touch of impatience, “you look at our Cathedral as if you were the Angel Gabriel fresh straight from Paradise. I would like to know if you have ever seen finer work than Giotto’s tower, or a cupola that would not look like a common mushroom next to Brunelleschi’s there, or marbles more cunningly wrought than these our Signoria paid a fortune for—enough to buy an entire dukedom. Come, now: have you ever seen anything to equal them?”

“If you asked me that question with a scimitar at my throat, Turkish fashion, or even your own razor at my neck,” the young Greek said, smiling gaily as he moved toward the Baptistery gates, “I daresay you could force me to confess whatever faith you wanted. But with my throat safe, I must tell you these buildings smack a little too much of Christian barbarism for my taste. I shudder thinking what hides inside: smoke-stained hideous Madonnas, fleshless mosaic saints staring down idiotic astonishment and rebuke from the apse, skin-clad skeletons nailed to crosses or stuck full of arrows or stretched across gridirons, women and monks with their heads tilted in perpetual lamentation. I have seen enough of those wry-necked favourites of heaven in Constantinople. But what is this bronze door, rough with carved imagery? The women’s figures here are moulded in a different spirit from those gaunt, staring saints. The high relief heads speak of a living human mind behind them, not like signs pointing to perpetual colic and spasms.”

“Yes, yes!” Nello said, triumphant. “I will show you soon enough our Florentine art is no barbarism. These gates were cast half a century ago by Lorenzo Ghiberti, when he was hardly any older than you are now.”

“Ah, I remember,” the stranger said, turning away as if his appetite for contemplation was already sated. “I have heard your Tuscan sculptors and painters have been dabbling a little in antique styles. But with monks for models and the legends of mad hermits and martyrs for subjects, the very vision of Olympus itself would be of little use to them.”

“I understand,” Nello shrugged significantly as they walked toward his shop. “You are of the same mind as Michele Marullo, ay, and Angelo Poliziano himself, for all his canonicate. He slips into my shop after his lectures sometimes, rants about the old pagan gods waking from their long sleep, making the woods and streams alive again. But he rails against the Roman scholars who want to force everyone to speak Latin again: ‘My ears are already flayed raw by the barbarians of the learned world,’ he says. ‘If the vulgar have to speak Latin too, I would as soon be in Florence the day they took to beating every kettle in the city, for fear the bells alone were not enough to appease the wrath of the saints.’ Ah, Messer Greco, if you want to taste the full flavour of our Florentine scholarship, you must frequent my shop. It is the very focus of all our city’s wit, and in that sense the navel of the earth, as my great predecessor Burchiello said of his own shop—though he made the more frivolous claim that his street of Calimara was the centre of all Florence. And here we are, under the sign of ‘Apollo and the Razor.’ Apollo, you see, is bestowing the razor on the Triptolemus of our craft, the first reaper of beards, the sublime Anonimo, whose mysterious identity is marked by a shadowy hand.”

“I see you have had customers already, Sandro,” Nello continued, addressing a solemn, dark-eyed youth who made way for them on the threshold. “Clear a chair for this signor, and prepare your finest scented lather, for he has a learned and a handsome chin.”

“You have a charming little inner sanctum there, I see,” the stranger said, peering through a latticed screen that divided the shop from a room of equal size, which opened onto a tiny walled garden where a few bay and laurel bushes circled a small stone statue of Hermes. “I suppose your conclave of eruditi meets there?”

“There, and no less often right out here in the shop,” Nello said, leading the way in. Benches lined the walls, a table held one open manuscript and one printed book with large capital letters, a lute, a few oil sketches, and a pair of small plaster models of hands and ancient masks. “My shop is no less a haunt of the Muses, as you will agree when you feel the sudden flood of clear understanding and serene inspiration that comes with a newly shaved chin. Ah, I see you can make that lute sing! I have some small skill at it too, though serenatas are useless when daylight shows my face, no fresher than a winter-worn apple. But look at that sketch over there: it is a fancy of Piero di Cosimo’s, that strange, whimsical painter. He says he dreamed the whole thing staring for hours at a mouldy old wall.”

The sketch showed three masks: one a drunken, laughing Satyr, another a grieving Magdalen, and the third, set between them, the rigid, cold face of a Stoic. The three masks rested tilted on the lap of a small child, whose cherubic features lifted above them with that soft, supernal glow painters had just begun to give to depictions of the Divine Infant.

“A symbolic picture, I see,” the young Greek said, plucking lightly at the lute to pull a soft murmur of music from its strings. “The child, perhaps, is the Golden Age, wanting neither forced worship nor harsh philosophy. The Golden Age can always come back, so long as men are born as babies, not popped into the world already wearing a cassock or a furred magistrate’s robe. Or the child may mean the gentle wisdom of Epicurus, equally removed from gross indulgence, bitter sorrow, and rigid severity.”

“Ah! Everyone has their own interpretation for that sketch,” Nello laughed. “If you ask Piero himself what he meant by it, he will tell you his paintings are an appendix the Good Lord was pleased to add to the universe, and if any man is confused about their meaning he had best go ask Holy Church. People have begged him to turn the sketch into a full painting, but he claps his fingers to his ears and shakes his head. The fancy has passed, he says—what a strange creature our Piero is! Now, all is ready for your initiation into the mysteries of the razor.”

“Mysteries they may well be called,” the barber went on, his spirits rising at the prospect of a long monologue, as he tucked the stranger snugly into the shroud-like shaving cloth. “Mysteries of Minerva and the Graces both. I get the very flower of men’s thoughts, because I catch them in the very first moment after their shave, when their heads feel light as air. (Ah, you wince a little at the lather! It tickles the very edge of the nose, I admit.) That is what makes a barber’s shop so perfect for wit and learning. Look at the druggist’s shop over by the sign of the Moor: that dull conclave pretends to rival mine, but what inspiration can you possibly get from the stench of nauseous boiled vegetable decoctions? Not to mention the second you cross their threshold you see a doctor of physic, all wrapped in fur and scarlet like a giant disguised spider, waiting to pounce on his prey—sometimes even blocking the doorway on his bony old nag, inspecting a vial of his patient’s saliva. (Lift your chin a little, if you please. Stare up at that angel blowing a trumpet on the ceiling. I had it painted expressly to keep my clients’ chins in the right position.) Besides, your druggist, who tramps the hills to pick herbs and boils his noxious brews, is a man full of prejudices. He has poisoned people according to his system, so he has to defend that system to justify what he has done. Now a barber can be entirely dispassionate: the only thing he owes loyalty to is his razor, as long as he is not also an author. That was the flaw in my great predecessor Burchiello: he was a poet, and so he was hopelessly biased in favour of his own verses. I escaped that trap. I learned very young that authorship is a narrowing business, that clashes directly with the liberal art of the razor, which demands impartial affection for every man’s chin. Ecco, Messere! The outline of your chin and lip is as clear as a young maiden’s now. Fix your mind on a knotty question: ask yourself whether you are supposed to spell Virgil’s name with an i or an e, and tell me if you do not feel a sudden unwonted clarity on the point. Only, if you decide for the i, keep it to yourself until your fortune is made. The e has the far stronger following in Florence. Ah! I think I see a glint of sharper wit in your eye. I have it on the authority of our young Niccolò Macchiavelli, sharp enough to spot il pelo nell’uovo, the tiniest flaw in an egg, and a great lover of fine shaves even though his beard is barely two years old, that no sooner do the hairs start sprouting on a man’s face than a certain grossness of apprehension creeps over his mind.”

“Suppose you let me look at myself,” the stranger laughed. “The happy effect on my intellect is probably being blocked by a little doubt about how the shave has changed my appearance.”
