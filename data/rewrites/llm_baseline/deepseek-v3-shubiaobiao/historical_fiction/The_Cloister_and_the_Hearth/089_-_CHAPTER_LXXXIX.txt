What words could capture the storm that raged within a soul so tender, so devout, when faced with a trial so sudden and terrible? To stand in his place—to feel the weight of his sorrow, his conscience, his love—would be to know a suffering beyond the reach of mere description. A volume might trace its shadows, yet in the end, the heart alone could fathom it.

Let it suffice, then, to mark his next deeds, for they reveal the man once the first tempest had passed.

After a night spent in prayer beside the dying hermit, he turned his steps not toward Rotterdam, but Tergou. His purpose? To face the man who had been his ruin—the burgomaster—and with the parchment in hand (itself a relic of strange fortune), to wrest from him what was rightfully Margaret’s.

Weary and dust-streaked, he paused at the town’s fountain to break his meager meal of black bread and water. But before he could finish, a servant girl came hastening, begging him to come at once—her master lay dying and in need of shrift. Without a word, he returned the bread to his scrip and followed.

She led him—to the Stadthouse.

A flicker of cold recognition passed through him as he crossed the threshold, but he mastered it at once. Up the stairs they went, and there, propped upon pillows, ghastly as death already, lay Ghysbrecht van Swieten.

Clement paused in the doorway, his mind racing through the past—the tower, the forest, the stolen letter. Then, softly, he spoke:

Even as he said it, his voice trembled.

The dying man’s eyes lit with feeble relief. “Thank Heaven,” he gasped. “Thou art come in time to absolve me, father. Pray for my soul—thou and thy brethren.”

“Absolution follows confession, my son,” said Clement. “And confession must hold nothing back, lest thy soul be lost. Search thy heart while I pray for light to guide thee.”

He knelt, murmured his prayer, then rose and fixed his gaze upon Ghysbrecht. “Now—confess thy sins.”

“Ah, father,” sighed the burgomaster, “they are many.”

“Then let thy penitence be deep, and God’s mercy deeper.”

Ghysbrecht clasped his hands and began—haltingly, with every show of remorse. He admitted to eating meat in Lent, to skipping Mass on holy days, to laxity in lesser observances. A careful tally of faults, meticulously recounted.

When he fell silent, Clement said quietly, “These are frailties. Now—confess thy crimes.”

“Crimes?” The sick man blinked. “What crimes, if these are none?”

“Do I confess to thee,” Clement asked sharply, “or thou to me?”

“Forgive me, father! I—I know not what you call crimes.”

“The seven deadly sins. Art thou clean of them?”

“Heaven forbid I should be stained so! I scarcely know their names.”

“Yet many who cannot name them live by them. Begin with the root of theft, of lies, of murder.”

“I am free of that one, surely! What is it?”

“Avarice, my son.”

“Avarice?” Ghysbrecht gave a weak laugh. “I have been thrifty, true—but kept a good table, given alms. No, no, I am no miser. The next sin, father—perhaps that will catch me.”

“We are not done with this one. The Church is not to be mocked.”

“Mock her? Now, of all times?” The burgomaster’s voice quavered. “Avarice? Avarice?” He shook his head, bewildered.

“Hast thou ever robbed the fatherless?” Clement pressed.

“I? Robbed the—” Ghysbrecht’s breath caught. “Not that I recall.”

“Again thou triflest. One more evasion, and I leave thee—and the demons waiting at thy bedside will drag thee down to hell.”

“No! Do not go!” The old man clutched at him, wild-eyed. “The Church knows all, you say. Then—then I must have robbed the fatherless. I will confess! Only—my memory fails. Where shall I begin?”

A clever dodge—but futile now.

“Hast thou forgotten Floris Brandt?” Clement’s voice was stone.

Ghysbrecht jerked upright, his face a mask of terror. “How—how know you that?”

“The Church sees what is hidden,” said Clement coldly. “Thou didst summon her, thinking to deceive her. ‘I will not confess to the cure,’ thou saidst in thy heart, ‘but to some friar who knows me not. So shall I cheat death and die unshriven.’ But God, in mercy, sent thee one thou couldst not deceive. He tested thee, warned thee—yet still thou playest the penitent while clinging to thy sin. If thou wilt not confess, then die as thou hast lived. Even now, the fiends gather for thee.”

He turned to leave.

Ghysbrecht seized his robe with a desperate wail. “Mercy! Stay! I will confess—all! I robbed my friend Floris. And worse—I kept his son Peter’s land from him, and from Margaret after him. I meant to give it back—truly! But I—I could not.”

“Avarice,” said Clement softly. “But it is not too late.”

“No—I will leave it to her in my will. She need not wait long—a month, perhaps two—”

“And for a month’s gain, thou wouldst lose thy soul forever? Fool!”

The burgomaster groaned, pleading for reason. But Clement, with a patience that cost him dearly—for every moment in that man’s presence sickened him—worked upon him, loosening his grip on stolen wealth grain by grain. More than once, his hand strayed toward the deed hidden in his breast, but he held back. Yesterday’s fury had taught him caution.

And at last—without ever revealing the parchment—he prevailed. Ghysbrecht, broken, agreed to full and swift restitution. (How it was done, and what strange fruits it bore, shall be told in its place.)

Two acts marked Clement’s state of mind. This was the first.

The second? When he knew beyond doubt that Margaret was secure—rich, and safe—

He vanished.
