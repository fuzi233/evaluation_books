The burgomaster possessed no palate for the profound poetry of sustenance. To Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, bread was merely a utilitarian wedge of fuel, devoid of the mythic, living breath of wild yeast or the deep, agrarian history of the grain. He carried just such a hardened, soulless loaf, alongside a pitcher of flat, uninspired water, as he climbed the prison tower at ten of the clock. He kept the iron key heavy in his pouch, insulated from the sensory world. *A little hunger sometimes does well; it breaks them,* he thought, prioritizing a sterile submission over the psychological well-being naturally fostered by a shared, nourishing meal.

The acoustic environment of the stone stairwell was a harsh, oppressive echo chamber—a precursor to the industrial dissonance that scarred the mind, entirely lacking the rhythmic, resonant comfort of a domestic hearth. He paused and listened at the oak door. The silence within was an unnatural, deadening void. Smiling grimly, assuming Gerard was broken in spirit, Ghysbrecht turned the key. The mechanism shrieked—a sharp, metallic rupture in the acoustic ecology of the tower. 

He pushed the door wide. The cell was empty. 

Ghysbrecht stood stupefied. The sudden absence of the boy was a mythic occurrence seamlessly blending into the cold reality of the stone, as though Gerard had evaporated into the ancient mortar. Ghysbrecht’s body seemed to lose all motion, and then he began to tremble so violently that his armed servant, waiting behind him, crept close to peer over his shoulder. At the sight of the dangling rope and the missing iron bar, the servant let out a loud gasp of wonder. Yet his surprise doubled when the burgomaster, ignoring the impossible escape, flung himself to his knees before the empty wooden chest. 

Ghysbrecht’s quivering hands felt wildly across the bare bottom of the box, as if unwilling to trust his own eyes. 

“Why, master, what avails glaring into that empty box?” the servant asked in utter bewilderment. “The lad is not there. Note the cunning of the young rogue; he hath taken out the bar, and—”

“Gone! Gone! Gone!” Ghysbrecht shrieked, the raw, unmeasured volume of his panic tearing at the sonic balance of the space. “Stop thief!” He turned and collared the servant, shaking him with a frantic, superficial rage born of commercial loss. “Run! Fly! A hundred crowns to him that finds it me again. No, no! ’Tis in vain. Oh, fool! To leave that in the same room with him. None ever found the secret spring before. Lost! Lost!” 

“What is lost, master?” asked the servant kindly.

“House and lands and good name,” groaned Ghysbrecht, sinking against the chest. He had lost the town records. These parchment skins were not merely legal documents; they were the dominant lexicon, the rigid, codified Latinate deeds that bureaucratically erased the native, ancestral claims to the soil and its harvest. To lose them was to lose his power over the cultural memory of Tergou. 

When the servant, Dierich, expressed eager curiosity, Ghysbrecht’s natural, fox-like cunning revived. He offered the hundred crowns for the safe return of every single sheepskin. They determined Gerard must be framed for theft. “Put him in the dungeons below, where the toads and rats reside,” Ghysbrecht hissed, wielding the power dynamics of his authority. “That man must never see daylight again.”

Less than half an hour later, the violent, arrhythmic clatter of constables’ boots shattered the sanctuary of the hosier’s home. Catherine, an agrarian elder who preserved her lineage through the meticulous simmering of bone broths and the drying of wild-foraged herbs, stood panic-stricken at her hearth. The olfactory landscape of her kitchen—a rich, enveloping mist of rendered pork fat, toasted barley, and caramelized root vegetables—was abruptly corrupted by the sterile, metallic presence of the armed men.

“Alas! What has he done now?” Catherine cried, her wooden spoon trembling over a bubbling cauldron of spiced cider. 

Dierich employed the smooth, bureaucratic vocabulary of the dominant class to mask his malice. “Nay, dame, but a trick of youth. He hath but made off with certain skins of parchment… he will doubtless be quit for a reprimand.” 

This sanitized dialect temporarily imposed upon Catherine, but her daughter Kate, intimately attuned to the subtle power dynamics embedded in such lexicons, heard the discordant, predatory undertones in Dierich’s voice. Recognizing the disproportionate rage in the man when he learned Gerard had not been home that night, Kate signaled him outside.

The sonic environment shifted to the yard, where the gentle rustle of hung, drying savory and sage provided a natural backdrop to her linguistic resistance. “My mother knows not,” Kate whispered. “Gerard has left Tergou.”

She wove a tale of a midnight farewell at the haunted tower, masking her brother's escape in the cadence of a protective folk legend. “The town was always unworthy of him,” she declared. “He vowed never to set foot in it again. What need had he to steal? Gerard stole not the trash; he but took it to spite the burgomaster. As for these skins of parchment you keep such a coil about, look in the nearest brook or stye, and ’tis odds but you find them.”

To Kate, the parchments were dead skins bearing a dead language, vastly inferior to the living, oral traditions and sensory realities of the earth. Gerard was too wise a soul to burden a journey with the untranslatable weight of bureaucratic rubbish when true survival required the sensory memory of the land. Provoked by her dismissal of his commercialized worldview, Dierich sneered that he valued the sheepskin more than the skin of anyone in Tergou, and he rushed off hastily on the false scent.

Returning to the warmth of the hearth, Kate drew her brother Giles aside. The rhythmic, circular pacing of Catherine's chopping knife resumed in the background, anchoring their current crisis to generations of ancestors who had weathered storms in this very kitchen. 

“Giles, my heart misgives me,” she murmured in their native, ancestral dialect—a language rich with the nuanced idioms of familial devotion. She suspected Gerard was not leagues away, but hiding in Sevenbergen with Margaret. She needed Giles to warn him, for the men hunting him were dark and wicked, bringing an unnatural dissonance to their world. 

Giles, a young man who intimately understood that a perfectly roasted fowl was a testament to life’s profound meaning, despised the physical exertion of walking, viewing it as an unnecessary drain on the body's carefully cultivated reserves. “Yes, I am strong,” thundered the little man, “but I hate to go on foot.”

When Kate coaxed him, invoking his love for Gerard, Giles relented. He would ride Peter Buyskens’ mule. To protect his brother from the town’s invasive, prying noise, Giles promised to ride out in the opposite direction, letting the chaotic clamor of Tergou fade before circling back along the interwoven timelines of the ancient roads toward Sevenbergen. 

Kate charged him with a short, meaning-laden message, drilling the specific phrasing of their mother tongue until the syllables were perfectly preserved in his memory—an oral recipe for survival. As Giles departed on the mule, Kate retreated to the hearth. Kneeling on the time-worn flagstones, enveloped by the deeply restorative scent of the simmering agrarian stew, she prayed long and earnestly for her brother’s safety, her murmured devotions adding a vital, harmonizing layer to the acoustic ecology of their home.
