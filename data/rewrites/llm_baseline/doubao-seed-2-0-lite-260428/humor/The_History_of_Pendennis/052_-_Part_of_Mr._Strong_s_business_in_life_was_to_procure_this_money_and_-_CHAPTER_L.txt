Fashion bailed on pretty old Temple Gardens so long ago, even the toughest, most weed-resistant rose that Shakespeare’s York and Lancaster plucked for their silly civil war badges would take one whiff of the London smog and refuse to grow a single bud, as every stuffy old Handbook of London will gleefully confirm. Most of the lawyers and clerks milling about the surrounding buildings don’t know or care a fig if roses ever bloomed there, they only use the old gate as a shortcut to their chambers. The solicitors’ runners don’t tuck posies under their arms while they sprint to counsel’s offices, and the barristers who take constitutional walks are far too busy panicking over railway merger paperwork to spare a single thought for the Wars of the Roses. The only folks who get starry-eyed over the gardens are dusty antiquarians and literary nerds, who like to daydream about Sir Roger de Coverley waddling down the path, the Spectator squinting like he’s got a bad case of hayfever, or Oliver Goldsmith camped out in the summerhouse toggling between three equally urgent thoughts: finishing his next *Citizen of the World* essay, stressing that his new suit from tailor Mr. Filby won’t have lumpy shoulders, and avoiding the dunning letter his publisher Mr. Newbery sent that says he’s three quid behind on rent.

Next thing you know, Dr. Johnson comes trundling along the gravel in a snuff-stained suit and a wig that looks like a sparrow’s nest, his Scottish valet trailing three sheets to the wind on port they drank at the Mitre Tavern, yelling that Goldsmith has to come over for tea because Miss Williams already burnt the muffins twice. Those old literary ghosts feel more real than half the living people hanging around Temple, and Arthur Pendennis, being a big old romantic literary nerd who couldn’t care less about the boring legal work everyone else in the quarter does, was definitely indulging in exactly those daft daydreams on the Sunday evening right after the chaos of the last chapter.

Sunday at the Temple is dead quiet, especially during vacation. All the fancy top-tier lawyers are off at dinner parties in Belgravia swilling claret so good it makes their toes tingle, the charming young barristers are tagging along to those parties to flirt with the judge’s accomplished daughters, and the poor sods who didn’t get an invite are camped out at the local pub splitting a sad overcooked roast joint and a half-pint of bitter, telling circuit jokes that no one else in the common room finds even slightly funny. The only people left in chambers are sick Mr. Cockle whose laundress is making him gruel that tastes like wallpaper paste, Mr. Toodle on the second floor who murders *Rule Britannia* on his flute at 9pm sharp every Sunday, and law student Tiger whose windows billow cigar smoke, with a stack of half-eaten chippy plates from Dick’s or the Cock stacked by his door. That’s it, no one else is around.

Pen, who’d dined alone at a tavern down the road, decided to pop into the gardens for a bit, enjoy the fresh air, and stare at the Thames which was surprisingly not murky that evening. After wandering for ten minutes he plopped down in one of the summerhouses flanking the main path, and got all sappy: the sky was cloudless, the chimneys on the south bank weren’t smoking at all, the church bells were ringing for evening service, and he found himself thinking of his mom, back home, walking arm-in-arm with him on their lawn terrace at sunset. He shook himself out of the mope, thumped his boot on the bench and announced out loud “Nah, that’s enough moping. I’m going home.”

He stepped straight out of the summerhouse and nearly flattened two tiny toddlers, who barely reached his knee, trotting along the gravel with their long blue shadows stretching east. One yelped “Oh!” and the other cackled like a tiny gremlin and yelled “Missa Pendennis!” It was none other than his new little friends, Ameliar-Ann and Betsy-Jane. Pen blushed bright red, grabbed the one he’d nearly tripped over, tossed her in the air and kissed her, which made Ameliar-Ann shriek like she was being kidnapped by a circus bear.

That shriek immediately summoned two ladies all dolled up for their Sunday walk: Mrs. Bolton wrapped in a fancy scarlet cashmere shawl, wearing her best black silk dress, and Fanny Bolton in a sweet sprigged muslin dress, yellow scarf, and a frilly parasol, looking every inch the proper lady. Fanny didn’t say a single word, but her eyes shone brighter than the blazing gas lamps on Paper Buildings. “Lor sir!” Mrs. Bolton gasped. “How ever did we all end up here? Ain’t that the most unplanned, extraordinary coincidence ever, Fanny?”

And of course it was a coincidence! You’ve all had those, right? When you’re strolling the country house shrubbery with your friend and the rich young heir you’ve been gushing over just happens to be there? When you’re loitering on Brighton pier listening to the band and the two fancy captains you fancied just clink past you? Pen had only mentioned in passing the day before that Temple Gardens was the nicest spot for a Sunday stroll, and the place was full of perfectly respectable people! Nothing suspicious at all, 100% artless good fun.

Pen was extra fancy that day, dressed in white duck trousers, a crisp white hat, a bright multi-colored neckcloth, gold chains dangling off his waistcoat, and fancy shirt studs, looking like he thought he was at least a minor royal prince. Fanny’s heart eyes were so obvious you could spot them from the other side of the river. The two little girls had played “be Mr. Pendennis” all night the day before, Ameliar-Ann shoving her chubby hands through the armholes of her pinafore to copy Pen’s habit of adjusting his waistcoat, and Fanny had laughed so hard she cried. Fanny looked pale and exhausted, and when Pen asked why, she blushed and mumbled “I was up all night.” “I told her to blow that candle out and go to sleep!” her mom interjected. “But she wouldn’t listen, not for love nor money.”

“Reading what, that was so interesting?” Pen asked, amused.
“Oh it’s the most beautiful book ever written!” Fanny sighed. “Walter Lorraine! I hate that stupid Neaera so much, and I love Leonora, and Walter, oh he’s the sweetest man who ever lived!”

Turns out Fanny had spent all night working it out that Pen was the author: she’d cozied up to grumpy old Captain Costigan, helped him mend his shirts that had holes big enough to stick your whole head through, mixed him his perfect whisky tumbler, got him to sing a wobbly old Irish ballad, then popped the question about what book Arthur had written before he could launch into a second encore. She ran straight to every library she could find, but they were all out of copies or wouldn’t lend one without a shilling. She ran home sobbing, her mom handed over the shilling, and she’d sprinted back to the library terrified someone would check the last copy out before she got there.

Pen was so flattered his chest felt like it was going to burst. “Oh you sweet little thing!” he babbled. “I’ll get you a copy of my own, wait no—no, I can’t, I really have to go, good night, thank you Fanny, god bless you!” He squeezed her hand once, nodded at her mom, and bolted out of the garden, muttering to himself all the way to the gate: “Dear little thing, she’s worth all the stuck-up fancy people I hang around. I need to get home to my mom. I won’t stay, I won’t let this go wrong, I swear—”

He was running so fast he barrelled straight into a tiny old man: it was Mr. Bows, the fiddler. “Your very humble servant, sir,” Bows said, doing the most sarcastic little bow you’ve ever seen, tipping his ratty old hat. “Off somewhere very urgent?”

“Good evening!” Pen snapped, grumpy. “Quit following me around spying, I’m in a huge hurry.”
“Spying nothing,” Bows said, frowning. “Where are the Boltons? They said they were going to Temple Church, they weren’t there. You haven’t hidden them in your chambers, have you? That’d be a right disaster.”

“For crying out loud!” Pen yelled. “Come up to my rooms and search the whole place if you don’t believe me! The court’s right here, the door’s unlocked, come on in.”

Bows followed him up three flights of creaky stairs. Pen’s chambers were as scruffy as ever: Warrington’s beat-up old bookcase full of tattered books, Pen’s writing desk stacked with half-finished essays, not a single Fanny, toddler, or Bolton in sight. “You wanna check under the beds?” Pen said, bitter. “Dig through the coal cellar? Did I hide the kids in the coal scuttle for fun?”

“Your word’s good enough for me,” Bows said, his voice soft and sad. “You say they’re not here, and I know they’re not. I just hope they never are here, ever.”

Pen got huffy immediately. “Who do you think you are, picking my friends for me? I’ve always been kind to you, I don’t deserve to be followed around like I’m a common thief.”

Bows sighed, all the anger draining out of him. He was a lonely old man, he said, all his life he’d wanted to write plays and music and be somebody, but no one ever gave him a chance. No one cared about the deformed old fiddler, playing tunes in alehouses for pennies. Fanny was the only thing he had, he’d taught her everything, and he didn’t want some fancy young dandy to break her heart, toy with her for fun, then toss her aside when he got bored.

Pen softened up immediately. “You’ve got me all wrong,” he said, gentle. “I swear I never meant to hurt her. I was literally running away just now, I didn’t even realize she had such a big crush on me until tonight. I was thinking about my mom back home, all the kind, good women I grew up around, and I was hurrying home to avoid doing anything stupid that I’d regret forever.”

Bows stared at him for a second, then his face relaxed. He apologized for being so hostile, walked over and shook Pen’s hand, the two of them making up like the old friends they’d once been. “I believe you,” Bows said, as he trundled slowly down the stairs. “I’ll pray you keep that promise, young man.”

“Amen to that,” Pen called after him, closing the door behind the old fiddler.
