Harry Warrington recounted his adventures to his newfound relative, and Madam Bernstein, a woman of sharp wit and worldly experience, formed her judgment upon the tale. Though her opinion was not entirely favorable, she recognized the imperfection of human nature; a city-bred lady like herself would indeed recoil at the thought of such a countrified existence, finding it wearisome. Yet, little Madam Warrington knew no better. She was content with her life and with herself, for she understood that while epicures demand fine dining, the simple man named Hodge finds joy in his homely bread and bacon. Madam Warrington was a brisk woman of business, the unseen architect of every affair at Castlewood. No pie was baked without her little finger in the dough. She directed the maids, supervised the kitchen wenches, rode afield on her pony to oversee the overseers and the negro hands in the tobacco and corn fields, and personally tended to the sick slaves in any weather, armed with a book of old-fashioned receipts and a medicine chest that terrified her neighbors into health.

A century prior, Virginia held few towns; the gentry's estates were little villages where masters and vassals dwelt together. Rachel Esmond ruled Castlewood like a queen, while neighboring potentates governed their lands with varying degrees of neediness. Some lived plentifully but roughly, with ragged liveries and open houses that welcomed all strangers. Madam Warrington was as hospitable as her peers but a sharper economist. Many would have gladly shared her life-interest in the estate to serve as a father to her boys, yet none seemed good enough for a lady of her exalted birth. Gossips joked that she waited for the Duke of Cumberland to be made viceroy or king of America, to which she responded with grave dignity that persons of such high rank had already offered alliance to the Esmond family.

Her household included an officer's widow, a former school companion and the late husband's regimental friend. At the Kensington Academy, while English girls teased the American stranger for her princified airs, Fanny Parker had defended and befriended her. Both married ensigns in Kingsley's and remained tenderly attached, exchanging letters signed "my Fanny" and "my Rachel." Tragically, Fanny's husband died in sad circumstances, leaving her a widow with an infant. Later, Captain Franks brought Mrs. Mountain to Virginia aboard the *Young Rachel*.

Mrs. Mountain enlivened Castlewood House. She played cards with the mistress, assisted the eldest boy with music, greeted guests warmly, and managed the household linens. A kind, brisk, and jolly widow, she declined proposals from unmarried gentlemen, preferring to keep her name Mountain. Her first husband had amiably spent her fortune and his own, and she valued her home at Castlewood over any new suitors. Though the two ladies quarreled frequently, they loved each other deeply, reconciling quickly whenever illness struck the boys. In the Colonel's final days, Mrs. Mountain's cheerfulness was greatly appreciated, and Madam Warrington cherished her memory above all living persons. Consequently, when Captain Franks asked if she would return, she always declined, promising to stay another year.

When suitors approached Madam Warrington, she received them kindly but often asked if they sought Mrs. Mountain. She would graciously accept compliments, grow tired of them, and treat them with her wayward imperial humor, much like Queen Elizabeth. She had favorites, accepted flatteries, and grew bored easily. Rattling Jack Firebrace of Henrico county enjoyed free quarters for months, addressing stolen verses to her, while Tom Humbold of Spotsylvania wagered fifty hogsheads that she would institute an order of knighthood—a bet he won.

The elder boy, George, chafed at these oddities. From an early age, he revolted against the flatteries paid to his mother, exposing them with juvenile satire. His mother noted gravely that the Esmonds were always of a jealous disposition and that her poor boy took after his parents. George hated Jack and Tom, whereas Harry, his younger brother, enjoyed sporting, fowling, fishing, and cock-fighting with them.

One winter, after dismissing their first tutor, Madam Esmond took the boys to Williamsburg for education. There, they heard the famous Mr. Whitfield, whose great voice stirred the colony unlike the harmless Mr. Broadbent. Virginia was a Church of England colony, yet lacking bishops, they imported divines who were often noblemen's hangers-on or insolvent parsons seeking a living. Whitfield's fervor excited the boys; they sang hymns with fervor, dreaming of the sacred "call." Though Whitfield could not stay, he promised to send a tutor capable of teaching profane learning and strengthening them in spiritual science.

Soon, a chosen vessel arrived with Young Mr. Ward. He possessed a voice as loud as Whitfield's and spoke endlessly. The hall echoed with his exhortations night and evening, drawing even the domestic negroes to the doors. He had the most influence over the "black sheep" of the flock, inspiring a negro chorus unheard in the Colonel's time. Ward was handsome, and Madam Esmond, credulous and eager to admire, found his glib voice and voluble commonplaces delightful. She insisted neighbors attend and ordered them to be converted. She was especially anxious to influence her young favorite, Mr. Washington, pressing him repeatedly to stay at Castlewood. But Washington always ordered his horse when the time came for Mr. Ward's exercises.

The twins, however, grew tired and rebellious under their new teacher. They found him a bad scholar, dull, and ill-bred. George knew more Latin and Greek than his master, catching him in perpetual blunders. Harry, taking greater liberties, mimicked Ward's manner of eating and talking until Mrs. Mountain and Madam Esmond were forced to laugh. Madam Esmond, defending Ward against her sons' opposition, argued that profane learning mattered less than spiritual truth. "What matters whether he has more or less of profane learning?" she asked. "In that which is most precious, Mr. W. is able to be a teacher to all of us... It is your wicked pride—the pride of all the Esmonds—which prevents you from listening to him." Ward's discourse that evening concerned Naaman the Syrian and his pride in his native rivers, with the moral that Ward was the keeper of the healing waters of Jordan and the boys must submit or perish.

George adopted a wicked sarcastic method, perhaps inherited from his grandfather. He mocked Ward's pompous remarks, causing the young divine to choke over his meals. He made Madam Esmond angry, doubly so when he sent Harry into fits of laughter. Her authority was defied, her officer scorned, and her youngest child perverted by the obstinate elder brother. She made a desperate attempt to maintain her power.

The boys were fourteen, Harry taller and more advanced, while George was delicate and childlike. The "baculine method"—the use of the cane—was common among sergeants and schoolmasters. Harry was accustomed to it, but since the Colonel's death, the cane had been laid aside, and the youths were allowed their own way. With authority spurned, the mother considered coercion. Consulting Mr. Ward, she found biblical justification for the practice, which was deemed wholesome in those days.

But the boys laughed at his flattery, scorned his bad manners, and yawned at his sermons. The more their mother favored him, the more they disliked him; tutor and pupils cordially hated each other. Mrs. Mountain, the boys' friend and George's confidante, warned them of a conspiracy. "Ward is more obsequious than ever to your mamma," she warned. "It turns my stomach to hear him flatter... You must be on your guard... A mischief will come." She mentioned Major Washington, whom she disliked, noting that Washington had advised the mother that "Discipline is discipline, and must be preserved. There can be but one command in a house." Harry confirmed that Washington had urged him to press George to obey. George replied haughtily, "Let him manage his own house, not mine," becoming only more supercilious.

The next day, the storm broke. During morning study, George was insubordinate. Even Harry admitted his brother was in the wrong. Mr. Ward kept his temper, promising to speak to Madam Esmond. At dinner, Ward laid the subject before her. He asked Harry to confirm his statements, which Harry did. George, standing under his grandfather's portrait, agreed haughtily that Ward was correct.

"To be a tutor to such a pupil is absurd," Ward declared, interspersing Scripture phrases that George mocked with scornful smiles. Ward asked to retire.

"Not before you have punished this wicked and disobedient child," Madam Esmond commanded, her anger gathering.

"Punish!" cried George.

"Yes, sir, punish! If means of love and entreaty fail... other means must be found... I punish you now, rebellious boy, to guard you from greater punishment hereafter. The discipline of this family must be maintained. There can be but one command in a house, and I must be the mistress of mine." She demanded Ward punish the boy, threatening that her servants would aid him if there was resistance.

In her harangue, filled with vehement Scriptural allusions, Madam Esmond revealed her schooling from her impetuous spiritual guide. George suddenly felt he had been wrong. "There can be but one command in the house, and you must be mistress—I know who said those words before you," George said slowly, looking very white. "And—and I know, mother, that I have acted wrongly to Mr. Ward."

"He owns it! He asks pardon!" cried Harry. "That's right, George! That's enough: isn't it?"

"No, it is not enough!" cried the little woman. "The disobedient boy must pay the penalty... I desire you will do your duty, Mr. Ward."

"Stop, mother—you don't quite know what you are doing," George said, agitated.

"I know that he who spares the rod spoils the child, ungrateful boy!" Madam Esmond retorted with more references. George looked pale and desperate.

Upon the mantelpiece stood a china cup, cherished by the widow as her father had used it. George suddenly took it, a strange smile passing over his pale face. "Stay one minute. Don't go away yet," he cried to his departing mother. "You—you are very fond of this cup, mother?" Harry looked on, wondering. "If I broke it, it could never be mended, could it? All the tinkers' rivets would not make it a whole cup again. My dear old grandpapa's cup! I have been wrong. Mr. Ward, I ask pardon. I will try and amend."

The widow looked at her son indignantly. "I thought," she said, "I thought an Esmond had been more of a man than to be afraid, and—" She screamed as Harry exclaimed and dashed forward.

George raised the cup, opened his hand, and let it fall on the marble slab. Harry tried in vain to catch it.

"It is too late, Hal," George said. "You will never mend that again—never. Now, mother, I am ready, as it is your wish. Will you come and see whether I am afraid? Mr. Ward, I am your servant. Your servant? Your slave! And the next time I meet Mr. Washington, madam, I will thank him for the advice which he gave you."

"I say, do your duty, sir!" cried Mrs. Esmond, stamping her foot. George bowed to Mr. Ward and asked him to lead the way to the study.

"Stop! For God's sake, mother, stop!" cried poor Hal. But passion boiled in the little woman's heart. "You only abet him, sir!" she cried. "If I had to do it myself, it should be done!" Harry, with sadness and wrath, left the room.

The widow sank onto a great chair, staring vacantly at the broken cup fragments. Silence held for a moment, then a loud outcry made her start. Mr. Ward emerged bleeding from a great wound on his head, followed by Harry, eyes flaring, brandishing a little couteau-de-chasse from the library wall.

"I don't care. I did it," Harry shouted. "I couldn't see this fellow strike my brother; and, as he lifted his hand, I flung the great ruler at him. I couldn't help it. I won't bear it; and, if one lifts a hand to me or my brother, I'll have his life."

The widow gasped, then sighed. She suffered terribly during the boys' absence, imagining the stripes inflicted on the elder had smitten her own heart. She was not angry now; perhaps she was delighted with the younger's prowess. "You are a very naughty disobedient child," she said in a peaceable voice. "My poor Mr. Ward! What a rebel, to strike you! Papa's great ebony ruler, was it? Lay down that hanger, child. 'Twas General Webb gave it to my papa after the siege of Lille. Let me bathe your wound, my good Mr. Ward, and thank Heaven it was no worse." She called for Mrs. Mountain to fetch court-plaster. "Put on your coat and waistcoat, child! You were going to take your punishment, sir, and that is sufficient. Ask pardon, Harry, of good Mr. Ward... And guard against your passionate nature, child—and pray to be forgiven. My son, O my son!" With a burst of tears, she threw herself on her eldest-born's neck. Harry laid down the hanger and apologized feebly.

The widow looked up at George's pale face. In reply to her eager caresses, he coldly kissed her on the forehead and separated from her. "You meant for the best, mother," he said. "and I was in the wrong. But the cup is broken; and all the king's horses and all the king's men cannot mend it. There—put the fair side outwards on the mantelpiece, and the wound will not show."
