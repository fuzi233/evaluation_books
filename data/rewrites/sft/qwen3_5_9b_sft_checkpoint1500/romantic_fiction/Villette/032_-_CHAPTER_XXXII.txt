The First Letter.

It became necessary, at this juncture, to ascertain the whereabouts of Paulina Mary and to evaluate the status of my acquaintance with the opulent Hôtel Crécy. That association had previously been interrupted by absence, as Monsieur and Mademoiselle de Bassompierre had spent several weeks traversing the French provinces and the capital. Fortunately, fortune informed me of their return almost immediately upon their arrival.

One mild afternoon, strolling leisurely along a tranquil boulevard, I enjoyed the benevolent rays of an April sun and indulged in certain pleasant reveries. Suddenly, I observed ahead a cluster of horsemen pausing as if they had just encountered one another, exchanging salutations upon the wide, smooth avenue lined with linden trees. On one side stood a middle-aged gentleman and a young lady; on the other, a young and handsome man. The lady's demeanor was exceptionally graceful, her attire impeccable, her entire presence delicate yet stately. Yet, as I watched, a sense of familiarity seized me. Drawing closer, I recognized them instantly: Count Home de Bassompierre, his daughter, and Dr. Graham Bretton.

What animation lit Graham's countenance! What warmth and sincerity, tempered by a modest reserve, radiated from the joy he expressed! Here was the precise combination of circumstances designed to captivate and bind Dr. John. The "pearl" he admired possessed immense value and purest quality, yet he was not the sort to appreciate a gem while ignoring its setting. If he had encountered Paulina with equal youth and beauty, but unattended, alone, clad in simple garb as a dependent worker, he might have deemed her merely a charming little creature, loving her with his eye for her motions and bearing. But it required more than this to vanquish him as he now stood subdued. To bring him safely under her dominion, without loss and indeed with gain to his masculine honor, one saw that he was utterly reduced. About Dr. John, there was everything of the man of the world. Satisfying himself was insufficient; society must approve, the world must admire his actions, or he counted his endeavors false and futile. In his victress, he required everything visible here: the stamp of high cultivation, the consecration of careful and authoritative protection, the adjuncts decreed by Fashion, purchased by Wealth, and adjusted by Taste. For these conditions, his spirit stipulated before surrendering; they were fulfilled to the utmost. And now, proud, impassioned, yet fearful, he paid homage to Paulina as his sovereign. As for her, the smile of feeling, rather than conscious power, slept softly in her eyes.

They departed. He passed me at a swift pace, scarcely feeling the earth beneath his skimming boots, seeing nothing on either hand. He looked remarkably handsome; spirit and purpose were fully roused within him.

"Papa, there is Lucy!" cried a musical, friendly voice. "Lucy, dear Lucy—*do* come here!"

I hastened toward her. She threw back her veil and stooped from her saddle to kiss me.

"I was coming to see you tomorrow," she said, "but now, tomorrow you will come and see me."

She specified the hour, and I promised compliance.

That evening found me with her, she and I sequestered within her private room. I had not seen her since the occasion when her claims were weighed against those of Ginevra Fanshawe and so signally prevailed. She had much to recount regarding her travels in the interim. She was a most animated, rapid speaker in *tête-à-tête*, a lively describer; yet, with her artless diction and clear, soft voice, she never seemed to speak too fast or say too much. My attention, I thought, would not soon waver, but eventually, she seemed to require a change of subject. She hastened to wind up her narrative briefly. Yet, why she concluded with such a concise abridgment did not immediately become apparent. Silence followed—a restless silence, not devoid of signs of abstraction. Then, turning to me in a diffident, half-appealing tone—"Lucy—"

"Well, I am at your side."

"Is my cousin Ginevra still at Madame Beck's?"

"Yes, your cousin remains there; surely you must be longing to see her."

"No—not particularly."

"You wish to invite her to spend another evening?"

"No... I suppose she still discusses marriage?"

"Not to anyone you care for."

"But, of course, she still thinks of Dr. Bretton? She cannot have altered her mind on that point, given how fixed it was two months ago."

"Why, you know, it matters not. You saw the terms on which they stood."

"There was a slight misunderstanding that evening, certainly. Does she seem unhappy?"

"Not she. Let us change the subject. Have you heard or seen anything of, or from, Graham during your absence?"

"My father received letters from him once or twice regarding business, I believe. He undertook managing some affairs requiring attention while we were away. Dr. Bretton appears to respect papa and takes pleasure in obliterating him."

"Yes: you met him yesterday on the boulevard; judging from his aspect, you would conclude that his friends need not anxiously worry about his health?"

"Papa seems to agree with you. I could not help smiling. He is not particularly observant, you know, because he is often thinking of other things than what passes before his eyes; but he said, as Dr. Bretton rode away, 'Really it does a man good to see the spirit and energy of that boy.' He called Dr. Bretton a boy; I believe he almost considers him so, just as he regards me as a little girl. He was not speaking to me, but dropped that remark to himself. Lucy..."

Once more fell the appealing accent, and simultaneously she left her chair and seated herself upon the stool at my feet.

I liked her. It is not a declaration I have often made concerning my acquaintances in the course of this book: the reader will bear with it for once. Close intimacy and scrutiny revealed in Paulina only what was delicate, intelligent, and sincere; therefore, my regard for her ran deep. An admiration more superficial might have been more demonstrative; mine, however, was quiet.

"What do you wish to ask of Lucy?" I said. "Be brave, and speak out."

Yet there was no courage in her eye; as it met mine, it fell. There was no coolness on her cheek—not a transient surface blush, but a gathering inward excitement that raised its tint and temperature.

"Lucy, I *do* wish to know your thoughts of Dr. Bretton. Do, *do* give me your real opinion of his character, his disposition."

"His character stands high, and deservedly high."

"And his disposition? Tell me about his disposition," she urged, "you know him well."

"I know him pretty well."

"You know his home-side. You have seen him with his mother; speak of him as a son."

"He is a fine-hearted son; his mother's comfort and hope, her pride and pleasure."

She held my hand between hers, and with each favorable word she gave it a little caressing stroke.

"In what other way is he good, Lucy?"

"Dr. Bretton is benevolent—humanely disposed toward all his race. Dr. Bretton would have benignity for the lowest savage or the worst criminal."

"I heard some gentlemen, friends of papa, talking about him, say the same. They say many poor patients at the hospitals, who tremble before some pitiless and selfish surgeons, welcome him."

"They are right; I have witnessed as much. He once took me over a hospital; I saw how he was received: your father's friends are correct."

The softest gratitude animated her eye as she lifted it for a moment. She had yet more to say, but seemed hesitating regarding time and place. Dusk was beginning to reign; the parlor fire already glowed with twilight ruddiness; yet I thought she wished the room dimmer, the hour later.

"How quiet and secluded we feel here!" I remarked, to reassure her.

"Do we? Yes; it is a still evening, and I shall not be called down to tea; papa is dining out."

Still holding my hand, she played with the fingers unconsciously, dressing them now in her own rings, now circling them with a twine of her beautiful hair. She patted the palm against her hot cheek, and finally, having cleared a voice naturally liquid as a lark's, she said:—

"You must think it rather strange that I should talk so much about Dr. Bretton, ask so many questions, take such an interest—but—"

"Not at all strange; perfectly natural; you like him."

"And if I did," she said, with slight quickness, "is that a reason why I should talk? I suppose you think me weak, like my cousin Ginevra?"

"If I thought you one whit like Madame Ginevra, I would not sit here waiting for your communications. I would rise, walk at my ease about the room, and anticipate all you had to say with a round lecture. Proceed."

"I mean to proceed," she retorted; "what else do you suppose I intend to do?"
