Hurry, coachman! Drive fast through the dim lanes of London—Strand and Fleet Street—bearing George Warrington to his brother’s rescue. Any observer familiar with Hogarth’s depiction of a London hackneycoach and the city’s roads in that age might well imagine how wearisome such a journey must have been: few lights save those carried by link-boys; coaches poorly hung, jolting over uneven pavements; great holes in the road, and deep sloughs of winter mud. To young George, the ride from Piccadilly to Fleet Street felt as endless as the long trek from Marlborough to London he had made earlier that very day.

He had written to Harry, announcing his arrival at Bristol. Before that, he had sent word of his own return—news of his survival after months of captivity, of his miraculous escape from death beneath Braddock’s command. At the time, war raged between England and France, and French privateers prowled the seas, seizing British merchant vessels even within sight of port. The letter carrying news of George’s return must have been aboard one of the many American ships captured by the French. It never reached Harry. Instead, it lay unopened in his lodgings, delivered on the third morning after Harry’s imprisonment—just as Mr. Ruff, furious at the loss of his lodger’s belongings, refused to surrender a single item.

George’s first stop upon arriving in London was Harry’s apartment in Clarges Street. He asked for his brother. The maid who answered the door screamed at the sight of him—so strikingly like her master—and fled to her mistress. The woman, unwilling to admit the truth—that poor Harry was imprisoned by her husband’s suit—said only that Mr. Warrington had left, and she did not know where he had gone.

But George knew Clarges Street was near Bond Street. He had studied London maps often. Aunt Bernstein would know where Harry was. Perhaps she was with her now. George remembered Harry’s letters to Virginia, full of gratitude for Aunt Bernstein’s kindness. Even Madam Esmond had been moved by it—especially by the letter from the Baroness that had prompted George to rush across Europe without delay. She had hoped, sincerely, that Madam Beatrix had found cause to repent her past ways. At her age, it was long overdue. Heaven knows, she had much to regret!

Rachel Esmond never spoke of her eldest daughter. Madam Esmond Warrington never mentioned her sister. And though the order for remission had been issued, though the words were written on the floor of the Temple—there remains a sin some will never forgive. Especially among women. Female virtue, once wounded, can be implacable.

Perhaps the Virginians’ agent at Bristol had painted a grim picture of Harry’s downfall. But when George reached his aunt’s door, Gumbo—the faithful negro servant—stood there, trembling. As the boy recovered from his shock at seeing the master he believed dead, he managed to stammer out a few words about Harry’s whereabouts and his pitiful state. That was enough to harden George’s manner when he approached his aunt.

To him, it seemed natural that family should come to a brother’s aid in distress. Oh, George, how little you know of London and its ways! How many poor men do you pass in your walks? If every philanthropist tried to free them all, even the wealth of America would not suffice. Yet as George rattled through the darkened streets toward the bailiff’s house, he began to feel a pang of remorse. The old lady’s agitation, her quiet sorrow—it touched him. “She does care,” he thought, “for my poor Harry’s plight. I judged too quickly.” Again and again in life, George would find himself rebuked for this same fault—haste in judgment. How many of us are not guilty of it? And alas, once the harm is done, no repentance can mend it.

“Quick, coachman!” he called. “We move as slowly as you do.”

Gumbo knew the way well. Once more, the bell rang. The outer door opened, then shut tightly behind them. They stood in a narrow passage, lit by a flaring tallow torch held by a small Jewish janitor. A second door creaked open, and they entered the house.

The porter stared, startled, as the torchlight revealed a second Mr. Warrington. “Come to see the gentleman?” he asked. “Yes. But wait—this is Mr. Warrington’s brother from America.” Gumbo was sent to prepare the master. “Step into this room,” said the porter. “There’s a gentleman already here about Mr. W.’s business. Another upstairs with him. No end of people have been calling.”

The room into which George was ushered was small, known as Mr. Amos’s office. By the flicker of a guttering candle, a stout man in a cloak and laced hat sat talking with the bailiff. The young porter carried his candle ahead, so the space was well lit.

“We’re not angry anymore, Harry!” said the stout gentleman, rising cheerfully and stepping forward with an outstretched hand. “Thank God, my boy! Mr. Amos says there’ll be no trouble—James and I will stand bail, and we’ll settle everything by breakfast tomorrow. Why… Angels and ministers of grace! Who are you?”

He stepped back, surprised, as the newcomer grasped his hand. But the stranger held tighter. “God bless you, sir!” he said. “I know who you are. You must be Colonel Lambert—whose kindness to my brother Harry he wrote of. And I am the brother you’ve heard of—left for dead in Braddock’s battle, living again after eighteen months among the French. I live to thank God—and you—for your kindness to my Harry.”

“James! James! News!” cried Mr. Lambert, turning to a gentleman in red who entered the room. “Here are the dead risen! Harry Scapegrace’s brother has returned—and with his scalp still on his head!” (George had removed his hat and stood in the light.)

“This is my brother-bail, Mr. Warrington,” said Mr. Lambert. “This is Lieutenant-Colonel James Wolfe, at your service. There’s been a little difference between Harry and me, Mr. George. Is he pacified, James?”

“He is full of gratitude,” replied Mr. Wolfe, bowing to George. “Harry wrote home about me, sir,” said the young man, “and I hope my brother’s friends will be kind enough to welcome me as their own.”

“I wish he had none but us, Mr. Warrington,” said Mr. Wolfe. “Poor Harry’s fine folks have been too fine for him—and ended by bringing him here.”

“Nay, gentlemen,” interjected Harry’s landlord, “I’ve done my best to keep the young gentleman comfortable. Knowing your honour—when you came before to bail Captain Watkins, and knowing your security is good—if you wish, the young gentleman may go free tonight. I’ll settle with the lawyer in the morning.”

“The debt is five hundred and odd pounds?” asked Mr. Warrington.

“With a hundred thanks,” said the landlord, “I can pay it now into the officers’ hands—take the usual acknowledgment and caution. But I shall never forget, gentlemen, that you helped my brother in his need. For that, I say thank you—and God bless you—in my mother’s name and mine.”

Meanwhile, Gumbo had hurried upstairs to Harry’s chamber. The young man would likely have scolded him for returning so late—but he was already softened by the conversation he’d just had with the friend who had left him. He sat smoking a pipe of Virginia tobacco, lost in thought. Even Maria’s kindness and devotion, shown just moments before, had not fully comforted him. He had begun to dread the consequences of that very love—how it bound him, how it shaped his future.

Then the door opened, and Mr. Wolfe entered—his homely face bright with warmth, his hand outstretched. “Lambert’s below,” he said. “And he’s come to help you.”

In spite of himself, Lambert would be kind. In spite of Harry’s ill temper and unjust anger, the good man meant to help him—even against Wolfe’s own advice. “For you were wrong, Mr. Warrington,” said the Colonel plainly. “You wouldn’t listen. You used harsh words to your senior—and one of the finest men alive. See what your pride has brought you. Will you bear with a friend who speaks honestly?”

“You have acted as always,” said Harry, “as the best Christian, the best friend, the most generous man. Nay, if you want proof of his goodness—here it is: He has turned me around. I was angry with you, but he brought me down here to be your bail. Let us both cry *Peccavimus*, Harry—and shake hands with our friend. He waits below. He would not come up until he knew how you’d receive him.”

“I think he’s a good man,” groaned Harry. “I was wild and angry when we last met, Colonel Wolfe. Perhaps he was right to send back those trinkets—though it hurt me. Go down to him, will you? Tell him I’m sorry—ask his pardon—and—God bless him for his generosity.”

And he turned his face away, rubbing his eyes.

“Tell him yourself, Harry,” said the Colonel, taking his hand. “No deputy will say it half so well. Come with me now.”

“You go first—I’ll follow,” said Harry. “On my word, I will. See? I’m in my morning gown! Just let me put on a coat. Give him my message first—prepare him for me!”

Wolfe smiled as he left—knowing well the young man’s hesitation. As he opened the door, Gumbo burst in—so excited he nearly forgot to bow. His eyes rolled, his mouth grinned, his hands trembled. “What, Gum? What’s happened?” asked Harry.

“No new sweetheart, mas’r,” said Gumbo, grinning wildly. “I’ve seen a ghost!”

“A ghost? Whose? Where?”

“Whar? Saw him at Madame Bernstein’s house. Came with him in the coach! He’s downstairs now—with Colonel Lambert!”

As Gumbo spoke, putting on Harry’s coat, his head wagged, his lips twitched, his whole body quivered with joy.

“What ghost?” demanded Harry, agitated.

“Is anyone—is—my mother come?”

“No, sir! No, Master Harry!” Gumbo’s head spun nearly off. Harry looked at him oddly, then flung open the door and raced down the stairs.

He reached the bottom just as a voice from within the little office—its door wide open—said, “And for doing so, I say thank you, and God bless you, in my mother’s name and mine.”

“Whose voice is that?” shouted Harry, his own voice trembling.

“It’s the ghost’s, mas’r!” yelled Gumbo from behind.

Harry ran forward—into the room.

There, if you please, we pause for a moment—before we enter.
