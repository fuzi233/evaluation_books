I rose, my heart a chaotic drum against my ribs, and scanned the darkness. For a heartbeat, she was gone. The solitude crashed back in, heavy and suffocating, like a storm cloud returning after the rain. The woman I had pulled from the jaws of death had vanished, leaving me in a void of absolute desolation. I could not endure this hideous isolation another second. Had I truly committed a crime against her? Was I destined to spend the rest of my existence carrying the weight of a burden I had forced upon her?

I spotted her moving quickly across the grass, heading away from the river. I took a final plunge into the water, a desperate restorative, and launched myself in pursuit. The bite of the white leech and the strike of the woman had drained me, yet my vitality was already surging back. I kept her in my sights with ease.

"Is this the end?" I whispered, a mournful tune playing in my chest. Her eyes, burning with anger and hatred, haunted my vision. I understood her resentment; I had forced life upon her, a cruel gift. But how else could I have hurt her? Why did she loathe me? Could the very concept of modesty rebel against genuine service? How could the proudest soul, who knew every movement I made, harbor even a shred of feeling that I had dishonored her? With what reverence had I touched her! Like a father tending a motherless child, I had borne her and cared for her. Had all my labor, all my desperate hope, been redeemed only by ingratitude? "No," I told myself, forcing conviction into my trembling voice. "Beauty must possess a heart! No matter how deeply buried, it exists! The deeper the tomb, the more powerful and true its awakening will be in its beautiful grave! To rouse that heart is a greater gift than the happiest life; it is to offer her a nobler, higher existence!"

She climbed a gentle slope ahead of me, walking with the straight, steady purpose of one who knows her destination. Suddenly, I realized she was widening the gap between us. I summoned every ounce of strength, and it flooded my veins like a fresh tide. My body felt ethereal, light as wind, and I rapidly closed the distance.

She never glanced back. She moved swiftly, like a Greek goddess fleeing danger, yet without panic. When I was merely three yards away, she turned sharply, her grace unbroken, and halted. She showed no sign of fatigue or heat. Her pallor was not sickness, but a pure, luminous whiteness. Her breathing was slow and deep. Her eyes seemed to fill the heavens, casting light upon the world. It was nearly noon, yet the sensation was akin to a great night where invisible dew makes the stars appear enormous.

"Why do you follow me?" she asked, her voice quiet but stern, as if she had never seen me before.

"I have lived so long," I answered, "on the mere hope of your eyes, that I must want to see them again!"

"You WILL not be spared!" she said coldly. "I command you to stop where you stand."

"Not until I see you in a place of safety will I leave you," I replied.

"Then take the consequences," she said, resuming her swift, gliding walk.

But as she turned, she cast a glance my way, and I stood frozen, as if pierced by a spear. Her scorn had failed; she would kill me with her beauty!

Despair restored my will; the spell shattered. I ran, overtaking her.

"Have pity upon me!" I cried.

She ignored me. I followed her like a child whose mother pretends to abandon him. "I will be your slave!" I shouted, laying my hand on her arm.

She turned as if a serpent had bitten her. I cowered before the blazing intensity of her eyes, unable to look away.

"Pity me," I begged again.

She resumed walking.

The entire day I trailed her. The sun climbed to its zenith, paused, and descended. She never paused, never slowed her pace. I never ceased to follow.

As the sun dipped below the horizon and night crept up, I stayed close. If I lost sight of her for a single moment, it would be forever!

We had walked all day over thick, soft grass. Abruptly, she stopped and threw herself onto it. There was still enough light to see she was utterly exhausted. I stood behind her, gazing down.

Did I love her? I knew she was not good! Did I hate her? I could not leave her! I knelt beside her.

"Go away! Do not dare touch me," she screamed.

Her arms lay on the grass by her sides, paralyzed.

Suddenly, they snapped shut around my neck, rigid as the limbs of a torture-maiden. She pulled my face down to hers, and her lips clung to my cheek. A sharp sting of pain shot through me, pulsing violently. I could not move a hair's breadth. Gradually, the pain faded. A slumberous weariness, a dreamy pleasure, stole over me, and then I knew nothing.

Suddenly, I woke. The moon hung a little way above the horizon, spreading no radiance; it was merely a bright object set in blackness. My cheek stung; I touched it and found a wet spot. My neck ached; there was another wet spot there! I sighed heavily, feeling incredibly tired. I turned my eyes listlessly around—and saw what had happened to the moonlight. It had gathered about the lady! She stood in a shimmering nimbus! I rose and staggered toward her.

"Down!" she cried imperiously, as if addressing a rebellious dog. "Follow me a step if you dare!"

"I will!" I murmured, with an agonized effort.

"Step within the gates of my city, and my people will stone you: they do not love beggars!"

I was deaf to her words. Weak as water, half-awake, I did not know I was moving, but the distance between us shrank. She took one step back, raised her left arm, and with a clenched fist struck me on the forehead. I felt as though hit by an iron hammer and fell.

I sprang to my feet, cold and wet, but clear-headed and strong. Had the blow revived me? It had left neither wound nor pain—but how was I wet? I could not have been lying long, for the moon was no higher!

The lady stood some yards away, her back to me. She was doing something I could not distinguish. Then, by a sudden gleam, I knew she had thrown off her garments and stood white in the dazed moonlight. She stood for a moment—and fell forward.

A streak of white shot away in a swift, drawn line. The same instant, the moon recovered itself, flashing fully, and I saw that the streak was a long-bodied creature rushing in great, low-curved bounds over the grass. Dark spots seemed to run like a stream down its back, as if it had been fleeing along under the edge of a wood, catching the shadows of the leaves.

"God of mercy!" I cried, "is the terrible creature speeding to the night-infolded city?" And I seemed to hear from afar the sudden burst and spread of outcries, as the pale savage bounded from house to house, rending and slaying.

While I gazed after it, fear-stricken, a second large creature, pure white, shot past me from behind like a swift, almost noiseless arrow. Its path was straight for the spot where the lady had fallen, and, as I thought, lay. My tongue stuck to the roof of my mouth. I sprang forward to pursue the beast, but the spot I aimed for was already far behind it.

"It was well," I thought, "that I could not cry out: if she had risen, the monster would have been upon her!"

But when I reached the place, no lady was there; only the garments she had dropped lay dark in the moonlight.

I stood staring after the second beast. It tore over the ground with even greater swiftness than the first—in long, level, skimming leaps, the very embodiment of wasteless speed. It followed the line the other had taken, and I watched it grow smaller and smaller until it disappeared into the uncertain distance.

But where was the lady? Had the first beast surprised her, creeping upon her noiselessly? I had heard no shriek! There had not been time to devour her! Could it have caught her up as it ran and borne her away to its den? So laden, it could not have run so fast! And I should have seen that it carried something!

Horrible doubts began to wake in me. After a thorough but fruitless search, I set out in the track of the two animals.
