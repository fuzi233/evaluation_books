It’s a curious thing. I sat down fully intending to write something brilliantly original, but my mind has stubbornly fixated on one singular, unglamorous topic: being broke. Perhaps it’s because my hands are jammed in my pockets—a habit of mine, except when in the company of certain female relatives who launch into passionate sermons on how this is "not gentlemanly." Frankly, I fail to see how rummaging in one’s own pockets diminishes gentility. If I were pilfering from *someone else’s* pockets, sure, that’s a scandal. But my own? Absurd.

Though, now that I think of it, I *have* heard men grumble bitterly while doing it—usually older gentlemen. We younger chaps, however, find pockets indispensable. Without them, we’re as awkward as a music-hall dandy without his top hat. Trousers pockets, mind you—right one jingling with a few coins, left one weighed down by keys—are the armor with which we face the world, even a steely-eyed post office clerk.

But empty pockets? A tragedy. In my leaner years, when my fortune dwindled to a solitary shilling, I’d recklessly squander a penny just for the luxury of jingling eleven coppers. Oddly, eleven pence feels richer than twelve. If I were truly pretentious, I’d have exchanged that penny for *two* ha’pennies—maximal jingle, minimal sense.

I speak on this matter with authority. I’ve been a provincial actor. I’ve been a "gentleman of the press." I’ve subsisted on 15 shillings a week. I’ve stretched 10 shillings over a fortnight while owing the other five. I’ve even lived off the contents of a greatcoat—though what those contents were, I’d rather not recall. Poverty is a ruthless tutor in domestic economy. When every farthing counts, you learn to:

1. Wait for your ha’penny change.
2. Walk a mile to spare a penny.
3. Treat beer as a rare sacrament.
4. Wear a collar four days straight (turning it judiciously).

I recommend this education to all prospective spouses and university-bound heirs. Nothing cures extravagance like counting pennies.

Take the delicate souls who shudder at claret under ninety-four shillings or roast mutton unadorned by French adjectives. I long to drag them to an East End pub and force upon them a sixpenny feast: beefsteak pudding (4d), potatoes (1d), half a pint of porter (1d). The aroma alone—beer, tobacco, and questionable grease—might teach them humility.

Then there’s the Generous Cadger, scattering small change like confetti while dodging his tailor’s bills. "One *can’t* tip less than a shilling," sniffed a young clerk I once lunched with. I nodded gravely while plotting to steer him to a Covent Garden eatery where the waiter’s sleeves doubled as napkins. Tip him a shilling? The man would weep with gratitude—possibly on your shoulder.

For all the jokes about empty pockets, there’s nothing funny about it. Haggling over pennies isn’t droll. Being labeled "stingy" isn’t a lark. Poverty isn’t hell because of discomfort—Robinson Crusoe didn’t fret over patched trousers (assuming he wore any). No, the sting is in being *seen* as poor. It’s not the cold that makes a coatless man hurry; it’s the lie he’ll tell about "health principles."

"Poverty is no crime," they say. Correct. Crimes are at least *interesting*. Poverty is merely a blunder—punished by universal contempt. The world scorns a shabby man, whether Christian, lord, or demagogue. Appearances reign supreme. The same man who’ll parade Piccadilly with a well-dressed scoundrel will duck into an alley to avoid a threadbare acquaintance. And the threadbare man? He’d rather crawl into a sewer than be spotted.

Time, that indifferent physician, numbs the shame. The veteran pauper pawns his watch with the calm of a man ordering tea. The novice? He lurks outside the shop like a criminal, sweats through a fabricated story about a "friend," and leaves convinced the entire neighborhood is pointing.

And let’s not forget the agony of telling time without a watch. Public houses are too fast; churches, too slow. Peering into a pub for the clock earns you the barmaid’s scorn. Jumping outside the window? That just draws a crowd expecting a song.

The uninitiated view pawnshops as the final descent into disgrace. The seasoned know better—you’ll find half your acquaintances there eventually. I maintain it’s nobler than borrowing from friends, though some protest "on principle." (Translation: They object to 25% interest.)

There are degrees of destitution. Some lack thousands; some lack shillings. Currently, I lack a fiver—temporarily, of course. Should any charitable reader wish to remedy this (forward to Messrs. Field & Tuer, *sealed*), I’d be eternally obliged. I’d even furnish an I.O.U., though I can’t promise it’d be worth the paper it’s scrawled on.
