“Here we go up, up, up; And here we go down, down, downee!” — NURSERY SONG

All across Milton, the mill chimneys puffed out plumes of unbroken gray smoke, while the endless roar of looms, the thunderous beat of steam engines, and the dizzying whirr of spinning spindles churned on without rest. Wood, iron, and steam labored on in their endless, senseless cycles, but their tireless, monotonous motion was matched by the crowds of mill workers, merchants, and traders who thronged the streets, every one of them driven by sharp, urgent purpose. No one loitered for pleasure: every face was etched with lines of eagerness or gnawing anxiety, men fought to grab every scrap of news they could find, and jostled each other aside on the Mart floor and the Exchange just as they jostled through life, caught in the cruel, selfish grind of cutthroat competition.

A heavy gloom hung over the entire town. Hardly any buyers came calling, and the few that did were eyed with deep suspicion by every seller, for credit had turned fragile overnight, even the most established firms at risk of being dragged down by the wave of collapses sweeping through the great port a few miles away, where shipping houses had toppled like dominoes. No Milton mill had failed yet, but everyone knew that the string of catastrophic losses that had come to light from bad speculations in America, and closer to home too, would cripple at least a handful of local trading houses. Day after day, every man’s face asked, if not his tongue: *What news? Who’s gone under? How will it hurt me?* When two or three men huddled to talk, they spoke first of the names of firms they knew were safe, no one daring to whisper out loud which houses they suspected might topple next—for idle, careless rumor could drag down a house that might otherwise have weathered the storm, and one collapsing firm would drag a dozen more after it.

“Thornton’s safe,” everyone said. “His business is huge, growing bigger every year—sharp as a tack, daring but prudent, that man is.” Then old Mr. Harrison, the lifelong croaker who had inherited his father’s fortune and never dared risk a single penny to expand his trade, would tug a neighbor aside, lean in to mutter in his ear: “Thornton’s business is big, right enough—but he poured every penny of his profits straight back into expanding it. No reserve capital stashed away. That new machinery of his, installed in the last two years? Cost him a fortune. A word to the wise, eh?”

But the truth was, Mr. Thornton was stretched thinner than he had ever been in his life, and the pain of it bit at the most vulnerable part of him: the fierce pride he held in the commercial reputation he had built entirely from scratch. He had always believed commerce gave every brave, honest, persevering man the chance to climb high, to build a name so solid it rang like gold across far-flung ports in the East and the West, where no one would ever know his face, but everyone would honor his word. His mother had read the old verse aloud to him as a boy, “Her merchants be like princes,” and it had felt like a trumpet call summoning him to the fight. He had spent years chasing that distant glory, barely noticing the people who lived and worked right alongside him in his own mills—until that day he had met Higgins, entirely outside the usual rigid master-and-workman dynamic, and the two men had looked each other eye to eye, and realized for the first time that “we have all of us one human heart.” It was the thin, sharp edge of a wedge that had split open his whole world: now, the fear of losing the connection he had built with the men he had only lately learned to see as full, complicated human beings, of having the quiet, tender experiments in better working conditions he had dreamed of nipped off before they could even be tested, had seeped into every quiet corner of his anxiety.

He traced all the trouble back to the strike a year and a half prior. Back then he had rushed to fulfill huge, high-value contracts, imported unskilled Irish labor to replace the striking hands, only to find their work clumsy and full of flaws, totally unfit to ship out from a firm that prided itself on selling nothing but top-quality goods. He had locked nearly all his capital up in new, expensive machinery, and bulk cotton he had bought at a premium for those unfulfilled contracts. Even after the strike ended, the losses lingered, and for a long time he had caught himself snapping irritably at Higgins for no cause at all, out of old, leftover resentment—until he forced himself to curb that anger, to seek Higgins out whenever business allowed, until all that resentment melted away, replaced by a quiet, mutual respect that let them disagree without malice, and see each other as more than just master and hand.

Now the full weight of the trade crash had arrived. The market had plummeted, the value of all his massive stock of cotton fell to nearly half what he had paid for it. No new orders came in, he could not even collect payment for orders he had already fulfilled, and the bills for that old bulk cotton purchase were coming due. Money was impossibly scarce, he could only borrow at usurious, ruinous interest rates, and there was no way he could sell off any of his assets to free up cash. But he did not despair. He worked day and night, planning for every possible emergency, stayed gentle and calm with the women of his household at home, spoke few words at the mill—though his hands knew him well enough now, saw the weight pressing down on him, and greeted his short, sharp orders with sympathy, not the old smoldering resentment they had once held for him.

“Th’ measter’s got a world o’ trouble on his plate,” Higgins said one night, after he heard Thornton snap a sharp demand to know why a neglected work order had not been followed through, and caught the soft, suppressed sigh Thornton heaved as he walked past the workroom. That night, Higgins and a fellow hand stayed hours after their shift, no one the wiser, to finish the neglected work, and Thornton never knew it was them, assuming the overlooker had seen to it himself.

A few days later, he ran straight into Thornton striding fast down Marlborough Street, deep in thought. “Measter,” he said, stopping him short, and Thornton looked up with a sharp, annoyed jolt, his mind far away. “Have yo’ heerd aught of Miss Marget lately? Miss Hale, th’ old parson’s daughter.”

“Miss Hale—oh, yes,” Thornton said, and suddenly all that wintry, careworn frost melted right off his face, as if a soft summer gale had blown all his anxieties away. His mouth was still set firm, but his eyes softened, warm and gentle as he looked at Higgins. “She’s my landlord now, you know. I get updates from her agent here every so often. She’s well, staying with friends—thank you for asking, Higgins.” That quiet, warm “thank you” lit up a new thought in Higgins’s sharp, observant head: he thought he saw a little glimmer of something deeper than he’d guessed, and he meant to follow it.

“And she’s not got married yet, measter?”
“Not yet. There’s talk of a match with a relation of her family, as far as I’ve heard.” Thornton’s face clouded over again.
“So she won’t be coming back to Milton, I reckon?”
“No.”
“Stop a minute, measter.” Higgins leaned in, confidential. “Is th’ young gentleman cleared, then? Master Frederick, her brother that came by when her mother died. Mary and me knew all about it, Mary worked in their house back then, we kept quiet, never breathed a word. I just wanted to know if he’s got his name cleared at last.”

Thornton stared at him, all the old uncertainty he had carried around about that night, about Margaret’s odd evasions, melting away like snow in spring. It had been her brother, all along. Of course it had. He had never for a second doubted her honor, but now he had his proof, a tiny little golden thread of comfort running through the dark, tangled web of all his misfortunes. He broke off from Higgins soon after, hurrying back to the pressing business he had been on his way to see, leaving Higgins baffled but not unhappy.

That very week, the final, crushing blow landed: his largest agent, the one handling all his American trade, went down with a whole string of other firms, toppling like a pack of cards. Thornton’s entire fortune was gone. Night after night he brought ledgers and papers up to his private study, sitting up long after everyone else in the house had gone to bed, none of them knowing he never slept at all. One dawn, gray light seeping through the cracks in the shuttered windows, the door opened and his mother walked in. She had not taken off her clothes or laid down to rest either. Their faces were wan and rigid, both worn thin from hours of silent, sleepless worry.

“Son John,” she said, her voice steady, even as it trembled. “Do you think I could sleep easy while you sit up here, eaten through with care? Tell me what it is.”

“Trade’s bad,” he said, lifting his head, holding it high and proud. “I won’t fail. I’m going to pay every last penny I owe. Not a single creditor will lose a farthing to me. But I’ll have to give up the business. I could save it, if I took the huge, high-risk speculation Watson offered me. If it succeeded, no one would ever know how close I came to ruin, I’d be richer than I ever was. But if it fails—”

“Go on,” she said, stepping forward, clutching his arm, her eyes bright with eager hope.

“If it fails, I’d be gambling all my creditors’ money just to save my own name. Honest men would be ruined for my petty pride. Mother, I’ve decided.” His voice was quiet, but unshakable. “It’s hard. I built this mill from nothing. I wanted to do such good by these men, give them better lives. Now all of it’s over, and I’m too old to start all over again with the same fire. But I’ll do it. You won’t grieve too much to leave this big house, will you, dear?”

“Not the house!” she cried, and burst into sobs, throwing her arms around his neck. “But to see you, my good, honest, hard-working boy, brought low when every scoundrel who gambles on other people’s luck gets rich—what kind of justice is there in that?”

“Mother,” he said, holding her gently, his voice soft. “You told me once, when father died, and we had nothing at all, to be brave, to trust. Don’t let the world have hardened us that much. Say those old brave words to me again.”

For a long minute she said nothing, sobbing convulsively, until she looked up and saw him sitting there, quiet, his face calm, and all her bitter rebellion melted away. She thanked God, fervent, that he was still hers, whole and unbroken. He went over and wrenched open the shutters, letting the pale, ruddy dawn light flood the room. The east wind was blowing sharp and cold, the unseasonable late spring frost lingering on, no one would be buying light summer cotton goods that year. All last hopes of a market rebound were dead.

Thornton’s brother-in-law Watson, furious that Thornton had refused to join his speculation or even borrow money from him, took the huge, high-risk gamble all on his own. He made a fortune, a massive, staggering sum that made him the talk of the Milton Exchange for nine whole days. Success turned him into a local legend, everyone calling him the wisest, most far-sighted man in the whole town. No one stopped to wonder that the very same gamble Thornton had refused to risk his creditors’ money on had been the path to sudden, glittering wealth. Thornton, meanwhile, quietly set to work hunting for a post as a mill manager, ready to start again from the bottom, steady, unbroken, the pride of his good name still intact.
