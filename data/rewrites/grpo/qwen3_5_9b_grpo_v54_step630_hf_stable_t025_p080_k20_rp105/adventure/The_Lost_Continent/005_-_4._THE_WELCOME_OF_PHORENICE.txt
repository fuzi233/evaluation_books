By the time the rival navy intercepted us at the mouth of the gulf, I had ceased to view myself merely as a recruit for the Empress. The ambush, the ferocity with which those ships fought solely to capture me, were omens written in fire and steel that even the blind could read: I was destined to play a central role in the nation's fate. Yet, if our arrival was watched by enemies, Phorenice had her own eyes. Scouts perched upon the mountains saw us approach and carried the news to the capital.

The sea-arm leading to the vast city of Atlantis is treacherous and winding. Where the mountains have collapsed, sending molten rock to harden the seabed, the channel narrows to a river's width before widening into a lake-like expanse. Overland, a skilled runner navigating the flats, swamps, and smoking hills could outrun any galley, provided he survived the fire-streams, floods, and beasts. Phorenice, however, spared no expense for speed. She dispatched twenty couriers to the headland overlooking the straits. Seven returned safely with their tidings; three more were known to have survived the journey.

At the time, we remained unaware of this frantic relay. The slaves rowing our galley were savages from Europe, their presence a foul odor that soured the voyage, compounded by the grit of an erupting volcano carried on the wind. We pushed forward with urgency, driven by a mixture of discomfort and a human curiosity I could not suppress despite my priestly training in stoicism. What awaited me at Atlantis? Would I be a formal prisoner awaiting trial for my actions in Yucatan? Or would I step ashore unnoticed, summoned later by the Empress? The reality remained a mystery.

As dawn broke, the peak of the sacred mountain, crowned with eternal fires, loomed ahead. The boatswains' whips cracked violently, driving the exhausted slaves to a final spurt. Against the adverse wind, we made steady progress under oars alone. The city began to rise from the water—pyramids and towers floating on the gleaming surface. Twenty years had passed since I last saw Atlantis, and my heart burned with the memory of its paving stones.

The city expanded around us as we neared. I recognized the temple of my manhood initiation, the pyramid of my secret rites, and finally, the house where I was raised. Tears blurred my vision as the memories surged.

We pulled up outside the white harbor walls, adhering to the law. The slaves slumped over their oars, panting. Usually, a port-captain's uncertainty about his own dignity leads to delays, but today, a ten-oared boat raced out from the castle, dropping the chain between the harbor gates.

"That is Dason's galley?" the captain asked.

"It was," Tob replied.

"I saw Dason's head on your bow," the captain said grimly. "You are Captain Tatho?"

"And remain so. Tatho's fleet was sunk by Dason's traitors; we took this rotting vessel to finish the voyage."

The captain scanned our deck. "Captain, I fear a dangerous reception. I see no Lord Deucalion. Has he perished under your charge? If you have let him die, the Empress will flay you alive."

"With Phorenice and Tatho both concerned for his safety," Tob interjected, "Lord Deucalion is a hazardous passenger. But I shall save my skin." He jabbed a thumb toward me. "He is here to vouch for me."

The captain stared, then bowed deeply. "I trust my Lord will pardon my ignorance of his nobility. I expected more... suitable attire."

"Pish," I said. "If I choose simplicity, I cannot object to being mistaken for a commoner. I do not wish to advertise my status with gaudy finery. End this inquiry."

The captain became instantly obsequious. "It is my honor to pilot your galley to the appointed berth."

As our slaves resumed rowing, Tob watched me with a dry smile. "I fear," he said, "that when you step among this fine gentility, Lord Deucalion will remember me only as a rude fellow."

"You think nothing of the kind," I assured him.

"Then I must prove my refinement," Tob declared, seizing my hand in his massive fist. "By the Gods, Deucalion, you may be a great prince, but I have known you as a man. You are the finest fighter of beasts and men alive today. That spear-stroke on the lizard will be sung in taverns for generations."

We entered the harbor, trumpets blaring from the castle. The port-captain had hoisted my banner to his masthead, and from the quays, the sounds of music and the glow of welcoming fires drifted across the water. It was clear the Empress felt a profound interest in a returning subject. Nothing was to be done by halves.

Our galley was moored to the royal quay, secured by rings of gold sacred to the throne.

"If Dason had foreseen this honor," Tob joked grimly, "he would have used silk rope instead of hemp. He would be frowning this very minute if the sun hadn't scorched his face stiff." He gestured to the quay. "Will you pick your way carefully over this common ship and tread the carpet they've spread for you?"

The port-captain looked horrified at Tob's rudeness. I sighed inwardly; colonial freedom had no place in Atlantis. I prepared myself for the weight of ceremony.

I consented to enter a crimson pavilion erected to shelter me until the Empress arrived, but my compliance ended there. Three gorgeously dressed chamberlains presented me with robes bedecked in every conceivable frippery, demanding I transform into a peacock.

I refused curtly. When one stammered about the Empress's tastes, I asked plainly if she had issued specific commands regarding my clothing. There were none.

"Phorenice commanded Deucalion the man to attend her," I retorted. "She sent no word on his outer casing. This dress suits my temper; it shields me from the heat and is clean. Your interference smells of impertinence."

In unison, the chamberlains drew their swords, pushing the hilts toward me.

"It would be a favor," their spokesman said, "if Lord Deucalion took his vengeance now, rather than delivering us to tormentors later."

"Poof," I dismissed them. "The matter is forgotten. You make too much of a little thing."

Their action, however, revealed a chilling truth: this was a different Atlantis, one where a man feared torture for a difference in coat cut.

Inside the pavilion, I bathed, though the water was tainted with a cheap scent. I waited with outward composure. The chamberlains, too well-bred to disturb my calm, stood silent while the Sun beat down on the scarlet roof. Outside, music blared and fires danced in the square.

It is said that the great maintain dignity by keeping lesser ones waiting. Phorenice disagreed. As soon as Tob's galley touched the royal quay, she departed the palace. Her procession, already marshalled, waited only for her. Trumpets sounded, and the advance began.

From the doorway, I watched the soldiers march straight to the square to salute, then range themselves aside. Followed by the Mariners' Guild, more soldiers, merchants, tanners, and spear-makers, all attired for a pageant. Some rode beasts tamed into servitude.

Then came the wonders. First, a huge bull mammoth emerged from the shadows of the houses. I had hunted these monsters, led raids against villages, and known their savage fury. Yet here was a giant among giants, tame as a whipped slave, bearing a golden half-castle stamped with the outstretched hand and adorned with silver snakes. Its tusks were gilded, its neck garlanded with flowers. It was a symbol of masterful strength.

Simultaneously appeared the mammoth's mistress, Empress Phorenice. While the beast drew my eye with its uncouth power, the lady on its back held my gaze immovably.

I stood as the crowd shouted, remaining in the pavilion until her steed halted in the center of the square, then I advanced across the open space, head erect, meeting her gaze.

"On your knees, my lord," whispered a chamberlain.

"At least with bent head," urged another.

I ignored them, marching forward with self-respect intact. She surveyed me, and I surveyed her. In those few seconds, I saw a woman unlike any I had imagined.

Throughout my official life, women had held no sway over me, though I had studied their influence on policy. This woman baffled me. Her body was slight, supple, and beautifully molded. Her face was a book of cleverness, fair beyond belief, with hair of lovely ruddiness cut short in the new fashion. Her eyes! Who could plumb their depths or find a trace of their heaven-made color?

She scrutinized me down to my soul, and her scrutiny seemed satisfied. She nodded as I approached. When I performed the required obeisance permitted to my rank, she spoke in a voice loud enough for all to hear.

"For others," she declared, "it is fitting to kneel, once, twice, or more, according to their station. But you are Deucalion, my lord. Though I knew you only from pictures drawn with tongues until today, I have now seen you and judged for myself. Therefore, I decree: Deucalion is above all other men in Atlantis. If one does not render him obedience, that man is an enemy of Phorenice and shall feel her anger."

A stair was brought, and I mounted to sit beside her in the golden half-castle under the canopy of royal snakes. An attendant fanned us with perfumed feathers. As the mammoth turned back toward the royal pyramid, flanked by heavily armed troops, Phorenice turned to me with a smile.

"You piqued me," she said.

"Your Majesty overwhelms me with such notice."

"You looked at my steed before you looked at me. A woman finds it hard to forgive a slight like that."

"I envied you the greatest of your conquests, and do still. I have fought mammoths and killed them, but I never dared think of taking one alive and taming it."

"You speak boldly," she smiled, "and yet you can turn a pretty compliment. Faugh! Deucalion, the way these people fawn on me gives me nausea. I am not of the same clay as they; just because I am the daughter of Gods, they must feed me on the pap of insincerity."

She was right, and the swineherd was forgotten. If she chose to maintain the fiction, it was not my place to contradict.

"I have pined long enough for stronger meat than they can offer," she continued. "I have procured my tongue-pictures of you, Deucalion. Though you do not know me, I knew you thoroughly before we met. I can admire a man with a mind great enough to forego silly gauds, excesses of feasts, or the pampering of women." She glanced down at her silks and jewels. "Women like to carry colors, but that is different. I sent for you to be my minister and bear the burden of ruling."

"There should be better men in broad Atlantis."

"There are not, my lord. I know them all by heart. They are enamored of my person, wearying me with empty phrases. Their cries of service are hollow; their own advancement and treasuries come first. So I sent for you, Deucalion, the one strong man in the world. Will you not sigh to be my lover?"

She watched for my answer.

"The Empress," I said, "is my mistress, and I will be an honest minister. With Phorenice the woman, I shall likely have little to do. Besides, I am not the sort to sport with this toy they call love."

"And yet you are personable enough," she mused. "That proves your strength, Deucalion. You will not lose your head through weak infatuation for my looks." She signaled to the attendant. "Ylga, fan not so violently."

Our conversation paused as I looked about. We passed through the chief street of the fairest city the world had ever seen. Having left it twenty years prior, I noted its growth: new temples, pyramids, palaces, and statuary. Its imperial magnificence surpassed even my memory of Yucatan's princely cities.

Yet, most striking was the poverty clinging to this splendor. Gaunt bodies and hungry faces lined the streets. Some stood naked as savages, dull to shame. Even traders, draped in tawdry gauds aping fashion, wore scared, uneasy expressions, hiding frantic hearts behind outward vauntings of prosperity.

Phorenice read my gaze.

"The season has been unhealthy," she explained lightly. "These lower people will not build fine houses to adorn my city. Living in their squalid kennels, they suffer calentures and sickness, making them disinclined to work. Trade has nearly stopped for half a year as rebels hammer at my gates."

I was startled out of my decorum. "Rebels! Who attacks Atlantis? Is the city under siege?"

"Of their condescension," she said, "they give us holiday today. Happily, my welcome to you comes undisturbed. If they were fighting, your ears would tell you. They are noisy enough. My spies say they prepare new engines for the walls, which you may break tomorrow if it amuses you. But for today, Deucalion, I have you, you have me, and there is peace. If you ask for more, I will give it."

"I did not know of this rebellion," I said. "Since you have made me your minister, I should know its scope immediately. This requires seriousness."

"Do you think I cannot take it seriously?" she retorted. She turned to Ylga. "Set loose my dress at the shoulder."
