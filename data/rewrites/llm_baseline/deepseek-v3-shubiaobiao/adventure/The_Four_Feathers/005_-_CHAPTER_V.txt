Habit carried them through the evening—the effortless banter, the practiced smiles, the illusion of joy woven so seamlessly that no one in Lennon House suspected the rift between them. Harry Feversham watched Ethne move through the crowd, her laughter light, her steps effortless, as though no shadow had fallen upon her heart. Yet beneath the masquerade, she bore a weight that threatened to crush her, just as he felt the four feathers searing against his chest, a brand of shame he was certain must be visible to all.

It astonished him that the room did not turn on him in unison, that no whisper of *coward* hissed through the music. But he no longer feared exposure. What did it matter now? He had lost Ethne. And as he watched her—her proud bearing, the quiet command in her grey eyes, the way she stood apart from every other woman in the room—he wondered, with a dull ache, how he had ever been fool enough to believe he deserved her.

Only once did her composure falter. They were dancing together when her gaze drifted toward the windows, where the first pale light of dawn crept behind the blinds.

"Look," she murmured, and suddenly her weight sagged against his arms. Her face drained of color, exhaustion etching lines where joy had been. Her eyelids fluttered, then opened again, weary and resigned.

"The morning," she whispered, her voice frayed at the edges. "Is it right that one should suffer so much pain?"

"Hush," Feversham urged, stepping closer, shielding her from curious eyes. "Just a little longer. Only a few minutes more."

She steadied herself, nodded, and let the dance carry her away.

The irony of it—him, of all people, offering her strength—was lost on them both. For Feversham, her moment of vulnerability had laid bare the depth of the wound he’d dealt. Her words clung to him, sharper than any blade: *Is it right that one should suffer so much pain?* He knew they would haunt him long after this night, long after everything.

When the last guests had departed and the house settled into silence, Feversham lingered in the hall with Ethne.

"You needn’t wait," she said, her back to him as she lit a candle. "I’ve already told my father everything."

Feversham nodded but did not move. "I must see him."

She turned then, studying him with a frown, as though she could not reconcile this quiet resolve with the man she thought she knew.

Dermod Eustace emerged from his study, his steps slow, his face aged by more than years. He stared at Feversham for a long, wordless moment, his mouth working soundlessly before he finally managed, "Well—" He stopped, adjusted the candlewick with unsteady fingers, and tried again. "Well—"

No other words came.

At last, he sighed, picked up his candle, and muttered, "If you want whiskey, stamp twice. The servants will hear you."

Then he trudged upstairs, leaving silence in his wake.

His restraint, his inability to voice the condemnation Feversham expected, was perhaps the harshest punishment of all.

---

Alone in her room, Ethne stood at the open window, breathing in the cool dawn air. The world outside was colorless, drained of life—a reflection, she thought, of the days ahead.

She had work to do.

Methodically, she gathered every token Harry Feversham had ever given her, determined to erase him from her life. But as she held each one—a book, a trinket, a letter—memories rose unbidden, and her resolve wavered.

In the end, she kept only one thing: his photograph, locked away where she would not have to see it. The rest she packed carefully into a box, sealed it, and left it in the hall to be sent away.

Then she burned his letters.

One by one, they blackened and curled in the flames, ink dissolving into ash, words vanishing like whispers on the wind. She watched until the last ember died, leaving only feathers of grey dust.

A sound outside her window startled her—a footstep on the gravel. Instinctively, she snuffed out her candle and sat motionless, listening. The birds sang; the river murmured. Then, faintly, the footsteps retreated.

Loneliness settled over her like a shroud.

Her gaze fell upon a violin case. Without thinking, she lifted the instrument—Durrance’s gift, though she did not remember it—and drew the bow across the strings.

Somewhere in the house, a guest stirred at the sound of music drifting through the halls. But Ethne played on, softly, as though the notes could fill the emptiness around her.

She did not yet know that the violin in her hands was the one Durrance had sent. She only knew that, for now, it was her sole companion in the silence.
