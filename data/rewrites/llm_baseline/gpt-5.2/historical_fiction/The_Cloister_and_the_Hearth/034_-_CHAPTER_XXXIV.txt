“Where be the true men?”

“Here be we. God bless you all! God bless you!”

The cry, half prayer and half battle-call, burst into the upper room and seemed to shake the very rafters. There was a rush for the stairs; torchlight leaped and wavered like a storm of fireflies; and half-a-dozen hands—hard with bow and bill, yet friendly as hearth-stones—caught at Denys and Gerard and hauled them safe among the archers.

“Y’have saved our lives, lads,” Denys cried, breathless, with that loud gratitude a man spends the more freely when his skin is still upon him. “Y’have saved our lives this night.”

A wild sight met the rescued pair: torches flaring in pitchy gusts, the glitter of breastplates slicked with soot, bronzed faces shining with sweat, and the bound thieves with cheeks turned paper-white under rope. In the midst lay the giant’s great body, bleeding out upon the boards; and these seasoned men left it there in its own gore as if it were no more than a butchered ox, already beyond reckoning. The soundscape of the room was a harsh music: boots scuffing, cords rasping, steel rings clinking, men speaking over one another, and underneath it all the thin, insect-like hiss of torches eating tallow—an ugly chorus that made the safe, domestic noises of a true inn (spoons against bowls, a kettle’s homely mutter) feel like a story told in some other language.

Gerard went round the archers and took them each by the hand, his eyes bright and wet. They, moved by that frank clasp, kissed him; and for once, shy no longer, he kissed them in return, tasting smoke and salt on their cheeks as if war itself had a flavor.

Then he turned to one handsome archer of his own years. “Prithee, good soldier, have an eye to me. A strange drowsiness overcomes me. Let no one cut my throat while I sleep—for pity’s sake.”

The archer laughed, thinking him merry with relief, and promised. Gerard’s lids sank as if a heavy cloth had been laid upon them. He went off into a sleep so sudden it looked like a swoon; his breath deepened at once, and his body slackened where he sat, as if all the fear and fasting of the night had been a debt called in at last.

Denys stared at him a moment, surprised; but the surprise did not ripen into care. It served Denys’s purpose that the lad was out of hearing.

Two of the archers were already prodding at the Abbot’s huge corpse with their feet, turning it half over to marvel at the weight of him and the manner of his fall. They asked which of the two had flung that enormous rogue down from an upper storey, for they would fain have the trick of his arm.

Denys at first pished and pshawed, and in his heart feared the sleeping youth might wake and speak of Providence and miracles, and so make him look a braggart. But with Gerard sunk like a stone in water, Denys found his courage swelling into a tale.

“Well then, you see, comrades,” he began, drawing their eyes as neatly as a cook draws diners to the steaming pot, “I had run my sword through this one up to the hilt, and one or two more of ’em came buzzing about me; so it behoved me have my sword or die. So I just put my foot against his stomach, gave a tug with my hand and a spring with my foot, and sent him flying to kingdom come! He died in the air, and his carrion rolled in amongst you without ceremony: made you jump, I warrant me. But pikestaves and pillage! what avails prattling of these trifles once they are gone by? Buvons, camarades, buvons.”

The archers answered that it was easy to say buvons when there was no liquor, but not so easy to do it.

“Nay, I’ll soon find you liquor,” said Denys, with a sniff that might have belonged to a hound or a vintner. “My nose hath a natural alacrity at scenting out the wine. You follow me; and I my nose. Bring a torch!”

They went, leaving the upper room with its ropes and blood, and found a short flight of stone steps that went down into the belly of the house. The air thickened as they descended: damp, stale, and close, with that cold mineral taste old walls keep. The cellar was large and low, and the torch’s yellow light stroked the stones to a greasy shine. Here and there the walls were crusted with what looked like cobwebs until the flame showed them to be saltpetre oozed from the wet stone and crystallized like hoarfrost in a cave.

“Oh! the fine mouldy smell,” said Denys, drawing it into his lungs as some men draw in a roast’s first scent when the joint is turned. “In such places still lurks the good wine. Advance thy torch. Diable! what is that in the corner? A pile of rags? No—’tis a man.”

They gathered around the torch, and there indeed crouched the landlord on a heap, pale as ashes, shivering as if the damp had got into his bones.

“Why, it is the landlord,” said Denys. “Get up, thou craven heart!”

One of the archers shouted at him. “Why, man, the thieves are bound, and we are dry that bound them. Up! and show us thy wine; for no bottles see here.”

“What, be the rascals bound?” stammered the landlord, his teeth knocking as if in a fever. “Good news. W-w-wine? That will I, honest sirs.” He rose on unsure joints and offered to lead them to the wine.

But Denys slid in like a knife under a joint. “You are all in the dark, comrades. He is in league with the thieves.”

“Alack, good soldier, me in league with the accursed robbers! Is that reasonable?” The landlord’s voice tried for injured honesty; it came out thin as sour ale.

“The girl said so anyway.”

“The girl! What girl?” The man’s eyes darted. “Ah! Curse her, traitress!”

Another archer, cooler, cut in. “The girl is not here, but gone on to the bailiff. So let the burghers settle whether this craven be guilty or no: for we caught him not in the act. And let him draw us our wine.”

“One moment,” said Denys, shrewd as a man who knows when a pot has been watered. “Why cursed he the girl? If he be a true man, he should bless her as we do.”

“Alas, sir,” said the landlord, licking his lips, “I have but my good name to live by, and I cursed her to you because you said she had belied me.”

“Humph!” Denys gave him a look like a cook gives a piece of meat that smells fair but feels wrong under the thumb. “I trow thou art a thief, and where is the thief that cannot lie with a smooth face? Therefore hold him, comrades: a prisoner can draw wine an if his hands be not bound.”

The landlord offered no objection. He even said, with a wretched politeness, he would show them where his little stock of wine lay, and only begged they would pay for what they drank, for his rent was two months due. The archers smiled grimly at his simplicity as they took him—one hand firm on his shoulder, another man leading with the torch.

They had reached the threshold when Denys halted them with a sharp “Halt!”

“What is’t?”

“Here be bottles in this corner; advance thy light.”

The torch-bearer, a man called Philippe, turned. Denys had slipped off his scabbard and was prodding at the very heap the landlord had crouched upon.

“Nay, nay,” cried the landlord, sudden in his fear. “The wine is in the next cellar. There is nothing there.”

“Nothing is mighty hard, then,” said Denys, and put his hand into the heap. He drew out something and held it up. It was only a bone. He tossed it down; it rattled on stone.

“There is nought there but the bones of the house,” said the landlord quickly, as if words could salt away suspicion.

“Just now ’twas nothing. Now that we have found something ’tis nothing but bones.” Denys rummaged again. “Here’s another. Humph? Look at this one, comrade; and you come too and look at it—and bring you smooth knave along.”

Philippe took the bone into the torchlight and turned it about, measuring it with a soldier’s eye.

“Well?” said Denys.

“Well—if this was a field of battle, I should say ’twas the shankbone of a man; no more, no less. But ’tisn’t a battlefield, nor a churchyard; ’tis an inn.”

“True, mate,” said Denys; and his voice had changed, growing colder, like a pot taken off the fire and left to stand. “But yon knave’s ashy face is as good a light to me as a field of battle. I read the bone by it. Bring yon face nearer, I say. When the chine is amissing, and the house dog can’t look at you without his tail creeping between his legs, who was the thief? Good brothers mine, my mind it doth misgive me. The deeper I thrust the more there be. Mayhap if these bones could tell their tale they would make true men’s flesh creep that heard it.”

“Alas! young man,” the landlord whined, “what hideous fancies are these! The bones are bones of beeves, and sheep, and kids, and not, as you think, of men and women. Holy saints preserve us!”

“Hold thy peace! Thy words are air.” Denys’s contempt cracked like dry wood. “Thou hast not got burghers by the ear, that know not a veal knuckle from their grandsire’s ribs; but soldiers—men that have gone to look for their dear comrades, and found their bones picked as clean by the crows as these I doubt have been by thee and thy mates. Men and women, saidst thou? And prithee, when spake I a word of women’s bones? Wouldst make a child suspect thee. Field of battle, comrade! Was not this house a field of battle half an hour agone? Drag him close to me, let me read his face. Now then—what is this, thou knave?”

He thrust a small object into the landlord’s sight so suddenly the man jerked back as if struck.

“Alas! I know not.”

“Well, I would not swear neither; but it is too like the thumb bone of a man’s hand.” Denys swallowed, and for all his bravado there was a shiver in him now, as if the cellar’s damp had found his spine. “Mates, my flesh it creeps. Churchyard! How know I this is not one?”

He drew his sword and began to rake the heap apart: earth, broken crockery, and bones scraped out onto the floor with a sound like a shovel in a midden. The landlord protested that Denys wasted his time.

“We poor innkeepers are sinners,” he babbled, clutching at common, petty guilt as if it might hide the greater. “We give short measure and baptize the wine—we are fain to do these things; the laws are so unjust to us; but we are not assassins. How could we afford to kill our customers? May Heaven’s lightning strike me dead if there be any bones there but such as have been used for meat. ’Tis the kitchen wench flings them here: I swear by God’s holy mother, by holy Paul, by holy Dominic, and Denys my patron saint—ah!”

Denys held up a bone under his eye and said nothing.

It was a bone no man, however ignorant, however lying, could confound with those of sheep or oxen. The sight stopped the man’s mouth as if a hand had clapped it. His hair rose visibly, bristling; his knees gave way as if the stones had slid out from under him.

But the archers dragged him upright and held him under the torch. Denys had raked free a skull—white as chalk, and yet no whiter than the living cheek set opposite it. Its empty sockets stared, and in that stare the murderer’s courage collapsed. His lips opened and opened, but no sound came.

“Ah!” said Denys, and now rage shook him harder than fear. “Look on the sockets out of which thou hast picked the eyes, and let them blast thine eyes, that crows shall pick out ere this week shall end. Now hold thou that while I search on. Hold it, I say, or here I rob the gallows—”

He threatened the quaking wretch with his naked sword until, with a groan, the landlord took the skull and held it, swaying, almost fainting—forced into a dreadful parody of a host offering, bearing not bread nor wine but the very proof of his own slaughterhouse.

The heap scattered wider. Alas—there were not one nor two. Many skulls came to light; many bones, too many for any kitchen’s honest leavings. Each time Denys uncovered another, the landlord moaned, and each moan sounded like the creak of a door in an empty house, a noise with no comfort in it.

Then Denys gave a cry stranger still, a note of distress that seemed to tear itself out of him against his will. He had drawn up, from the foulness, a mass of human hair.

It was long, glossy, golden—woman’s beautiful hair, still shining even in that cellar’s sour air, as if the dead carried a stubborn memory of sunlight.

At sight of it the archers shook the landlord by instinct, as men shake a sack to learn what foul thing lies inside. He only whined.

“I have a little sister with hair just so fair and shining as this,” Denys said hoarsely, and the words came not in French bravado now but in something deeper and more native, where feeling clung to the consonants like steam clings to a kitchen ceiling. “Jesu! If it should be hers! There—quick—take my sword and dagger, and keep them from my hand, lest I strike him dead and wrong the gibbet. And thou, poor innocent victim, on whose head this most lovely hair did grow, hear me swear this, on bended knee, never to leave this man till I see him broken to pieces on the wheel even for thy sake.”

He dropped to his knee upon the wet stone. The torches hissed above him; somewhere overhead the inn settled with a creak as if it listened.

He rose. “Ay, had he as many lives as here be hairs, I’d have them all, by God,” and he put the golden hair into his bosom as tenderly as a man stows away a relic.

Then fury took him like a fever. He seized the landlord by the neck and forced him down, grinding his face among the bones where they lay thickest. The assassin first yelled, then whined and whimpered, as a dog yells and then whines when its nose is forced into the body of some innocent it has killed.

“Now lend me thy bowstring, Philippe!”

He passed the string through the eyes of a skull and hung the ghastly thing round the man’s neck like an accursed collar. Then he hauled him up and kicked him—kicked him steadily, industriously—toward the kitchen, where an alderman of the burgh had arrived with constables and was taking an archer’s deposition.

The grave burgher started at the sight: the landlord driven in bleeding from a dozen scratches—scratches made, as it were, by the very dead—carrying his horrible ensign.

Denys came panting after and in a few hot words made the matter plain.

“Bind him like the rest,” said the alderman sternly. “I count him the blackest of them all.”

While they bound his hands, the poor wretch begged, as if begging for a cup of water, that “the skull might be taken from him.”

“Humph!” said the alderman, eyeing it. “Certes, I had not ordered such a thing to be put on mortal man. Yet being there, I will not lift voice nor finger to doff it. Methinks it fits thee truly, thou bloody dog. ’Tis thy ensign, and hangs well above a heart so foul as thine.”

He asked Denys whether they had secured the whole gang or only part.

“Your worship,” said Denys, “there are but seven of them, and this landlord. One we slew upstairs, one we trundled down dead, the rest are bound before you.”

“Good! Go fetch the dead one from upstairs, and lay him beside him I caused to be removed.”

Here a voice broke in—thin, peevish, like a guinea-fowl’s cry in a farmyard, an officious little sound amid so much blood and judgment.

“Now, now, now, where is the hand? That is what I want to see.”

It was a little pettifogging clerk, eyes bright with the hunger not for justice but for documentation. The world to him was a ledger; even horror must be translated into items that could be counted, labeled, and displayed.

“You will find it above,” said someone, “nailed to the door-post by a crossbow bolt.”

“Good!” squeaked the clerk, and leaned to whisper his master: “What a goodly show will the pièces de conviction make!” He began to write them down, enumerating each in separate squeaks as his quill scratched: Skulls—Bones—A woman’s hair—A thief’s hands—1 axe—2 carcasses—1 crossbow bolt. His words made a strange dominion: Latin and French tags turned lived terror into official tokens, as if a change of language could drain the warm, untranslatable weight from what men had seen and smelled.

He itched to search the cellar himself; there might be other valuable morsels of evidence—an ear, or even an earring. The alderman assented. The clerk seized a torch and hurried toward the stair—

—and an accident stopped him and carried him, in a heartbeat, a step or two the opposite way.

The constables had gone up the stair in single file. But the head constable no sooner saw the phosphorescent corpse seated by the bedside than he stood stupefied. Next he began to shake like one in an ague, terror gaining on him until he uttered a sort of howl and recoiled swiftly. Forgetting the steps in his recoil, he tumbled backward over his nearest companion. The second, shaken by that shout and catching a glimpse of something horrid, was already staggering back and could not sustain the head man’s weight—who, like most head constables, was ponderous. The two carried away the third, and the three the fourth, and they streamed into the kitchen and settled on the floor overlapping each other like a sequence laid out on a card-table.

The clerk, coming hastily with his torch, ran into the fourth man and was knocked flat on his back—the ace of that fair quint. There he lay kicking and waving his torch, seeming for a moment to flourish it in triumph, but in truth convulsed, wind and sense driven out together by the blow.

“What is to do now, in Heaven’s name?” cried the alderman, starting up in alarm.

Denys explained with a quick, contemptuous brevity, and offered to accompany his worship.

“So be it,” said the alderman. His men picked themselves up, rueful and red, and he put himself at their head and examined the premises above and below, the torches’ hiss and the men’s uneasy footsteps making a sour accompaniment in every room.

As for the prisoners, their questioning was postponed until they could be confronted with the servant.

Before dawn the thieves—alive and dead—and all the relics and evidences of crime and retribution were swept away into the law’s net. The inn, which should have been a place of bread-scent and clinking cups, fell silent and almost deserted, as if the stones themselves held their breath to forget what they had heard.

There remained but one constable, and Denys and Gerard—the latter still sleeping heavily, as if the night, with all its smoke and shouting, had been muffled for him into a single deep, wordless sound.
