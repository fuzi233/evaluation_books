There was Mr. Harry Warrington of Virginia, settled in sun-faded Bond Street lodgings in London, eating of the land’s richest fat and quaffing bumper drafts of its finest claret, by the winter of 1756. The ton had universally dubbed him the Fortunate Youth: young, wealthy, broad-shouldered and handsome, easy of temper and quick to laugh, he was taken to the bosom of fashionable society as if he had been born to a London pewter and a coronet. Do not be too quick to cry out against the world’s selfish fondness for the fair and fortunate, my dear friends. If in my own club I may choose the company of a lively, well-bred young fellow who diverts me over that of a slouching, unwashed misanthrope or a ceaselessly prating coxcomb, may not all of society claim the same small liberty?

Harry was liked because he was eminently likeable. Over a bottle with roistering topers he would roar out a hunting song and drain his glass as deep as any man. With sporting gentlemen he would ride a twenty-mile chase after a fox or tramp through muddy fields after partridge as keenly as the oldest English squire. With ladies he carried a soft, blushing shyness that rendered him endlessly charming. To those of lower rank he was magnificently, unthinkingly liberal, slipping a guinea to a footman who fumbled a tray or sparing a poor groom a scolding for spooking his horse. He carried himself with the grand high air of a gentleman of rank, no different from any other man of quality in that day, when lines of hierarchy were drawn sharp and clear: the Secretary of State knelt to the King every morning with his despatches, and the Under-Secretary dared not sit down in his chief’s presence. Even I, for all my modern liberal notions, confess I bristle a little when a man whose only connection to letters is through the parish constable slaps my back and calls me “old boy” uninvited, so who are we to grudge Harry his proper dignity?

Every pleasure the 1750s London winter had to offer was his for the asking. He attended the Italian operas that all the wits pretended to decry as foolish, Popish, and unmanly – though every last one of them crowded the boxes night after night to gawk at the singers. He saw Garrick thunder through tragedy at Drury Lane, and Mrs. Clive steal the show in farce at Covent Garden. He drifted through masked masquerades and crowded ridottos, but found the private “drums” and card assemblies of the nobility tedious compared to White’s Club, where the play was higher, the stakes fairer, and no prattling old dowager tapped his wrist for betting too deep.

One day his kinsman Lord Castlewood dragged him to St James’s Palace, and presented him to King George II, newly come back from Kensington. The gruff little sovereign was in a black, ill humour, for he either disliked Castlewood or had heard tales of Harry’s gaming from his mistress Lady Yarmouth. “Oh, heard of you from Lady Yarmouth,” he grunted, and turned to Castlewood to snap a rebuke in rapid German that Harry could not follow. “Tell him he plays far too much!” And with that, the Defender of the Faith turned his royal back on the two of them.

Castlewood shrank back, pale as wax. “His Majesty thinks the play at White’s runs too high, and he is displeased,” he whispered.

Harry frowned. “If he does not want us here, we need not come again. For my part, I never thought that German fellow was a proper King of England.”

“Hush! For Heaven’s sake hold your cursed colonial tongue!” Castlewood hissed, clutching Harry’s arm. “The walls here have ears!”

Sure enough, every courtier who had been bowing and simpering at Harry ten minutes before was now shrinking away from him as if he carried the plague. But before Harry could laugh at the absurd spectacle, a broad, broad-shouldered man in a scarlet-laced waistcoat and a great old-fashioned full-bottomed wig stepped forward and clapped a heavy brown hand on his shoulder. “Shake hands, nephew. I heard every word you said. The walls may have ears, but so do I. If all the rest of them give you the cold shoulder, I’ll give you my hand.” He grinned. “You’ve got the very same eyes your father had, though I suppose you Virginians grow a little wiry out there on your plantations. I’m Sir Miles Warrington, your father’s brother. Castlewood knows me well enough.”

Castlewood looked positively yellow with fright. “Yes, my dear Harry, this is your paternal uncle, Sir Miles Warrington.”

“Might as well have come up to Norfolk to visit us instead of dangling about playing the fool at Tunbridge Wells, Mr. Warrington – or Mr. Esmond, whichever you choose to call yourself.” The baronet chuckled. “The old lady your mother still calls herself Madam Esmond, doesn’t she?”

Harry lifted his chin a little, proud. “My mother is not ashamed of her father’s name, nor am I, uncle.”

“Well said, lad. Come back to Hill Street with me at three o’clock, and eat a bit of roast mutton with Lady Warrington – that is, unless you cannot bear to go without your fancy White’s kickshaws.” He nodded at the still trembling Castlewood. “Don’t look so frightened, my lord. I shan’t go blabbing tales out of school.”

On their walk out to St James’s Street later, Castlewood begged Harry not to listen to any ill rumours Sir Miles might spread about him, explaining the two men had old family quarrels dating back years, and Harry ought to take all the baronet’s words with a very large grain of salt.

By the time Harry stepped into White’s an hour later, the story of the king’s snub had already spread far and wide. The tale had already warped past recognition: everyone was saying the king was jealous of Harry’s favour with Lady Yarmouth, that Harry was on the closest possible terms with the royal favourite, that his victories at the gaming table were the talk of every club in town. Half a dozen gentlemen clapped him on the back that night to congratulate him on his “conquest” of the court, and by dawn it was settled fact that the Fortunate Youth had added the royal household to his long list of triumphs.

Harry turned up at Sir Miles’s Hill Street house at three sharp, and found Lady Warrington stiff and stately, her two teenaged daughters curtseying so primly they barely spoke a word to him, and the little heir Master Miles propped up at the dinner table beside his tutor. Halfway through the meal, the small boy stared hard at Harry for a long minute and blurted out, “Are you wicked, cousin Harry? You don’t look very wicked!”

His tutor Mr. Walker turned bright red and tried to hush him, but the boy cried louder, “You said he was! And mamma said too that Lady Yarmouth is a wicked woman!”

Sir Miles roared with laughter. “We are all miserable sinners, Miley, haven’t you heard the parson say so every Sunday? He’s no more wicked than the rest of us. Run off with your tutor now, and you shall have a ride on your little pony in the park this afternoon.” He turned to Harry once the boy was gone, grinning. “My wife here has taken to Mr. Whitfield’s preaching these last few years, ever since our poor eldest son died, and that chaplain of theirs Ward that came over to Virginia brought back a whole sack full of wild tales about you and your brother George.”

Harry’s face flamed. “Say what you like of me, but if any man dares breathe a word against my brother George I will break his bones! That Ward was not fit to buckle my brother’s shoes, and if I ever catch him repeating the lies he told in our house I’ll cane him soundly.”

Sir Miles clapped him on the back so hard he nearly spilled his wine. “There’s the Warrington spirit! I always said you weren’t half as bad as you were painted.” That night Harry stopped off at a jeweller’s in Tavistock Street on his way to White’s, bought three pretty trinkets for his girl cousins, and told the shopkeeper to send them over with a note saying they were a gift from their aunt Madam Esmond in Virginia. The girls were overjoyed, and wrote a collective thank-you note that made its way across the Atlantic to Virginia that spring. Madam Esmond read it, smiled sadly, and knew full well the gifts were Harry’s, given in a fit of his usual generosity while his luck was still high. She immediately tightened the household purse strings, vowing to save every spare penny for when his luck turned – though the poor pinched servants on her estate began to steal and skirmish all the more to make up for their short rations.

A few weeks later Harry was lounging in his Bond Street lodgings in a brocade dressing gown, sipping chocolate, his face pale and haggard. His luck had turned sharply. Two nights running he had lost heavily at White’s, and owed Mackreth the club proprietor nearly a thousand pounds in counters. Parson Sampson crept in, fumbling with a crumpled piece of paper, and humbly begged Harry to settle the two hundred and forty three pounds of debts he had asked his patron to cover. Harry had laughed grimly, promised he would sell out a portion of his Virginia stock that afternoon to cover it, and sent Sampson away, his own head sinking into his hands as he prodded the coals in the fire with his poker, muttering oaths under his breath.

His old friend Colonel Lambert knocked not long after, fresh from the Captain-General’s levee, his military cloak still dusted with road grime from the country. Harry greeted him, and heard that the entire Lambert family was in London for a spell, staying with their old friend Lord Wrotham. But Harry’s face fell, for he knew well what was coming.

Sure enough, Lambert sighed and said, “Every man at the levee was gossiping about your great winnings at play. They say you have won six, eight thousand pounds in the last month alone. I did not undeceive them.” He pulled a small velvet packet out of his coat pocket, and held it out. “Harry, those pretty trinkets you sent my Hetty and Theo – they cannot be paid for with gaming house winnings. I cannot let my girls wear jewellery bought with money won off the turn of a card. Mrs Lambert kept the small locket you gave her, and sends you all her love and a God bless you. I would not bring these back to grieve you, lad, but I have no other choice.”

Pride, and the sharp sting of his recent losses, and the endless prying eyes of all London all came surging up in Harry’s breast. He stood up, bristling, and refused to take the older man’s outstretched hand. “I have had quite enough of unsolicited advice from you, sir. You make it your business to pry into my gains, my company, my amusements, when I never once asked for your counsel. I tried to show my gratitude for your past kindness by sending your family those small gifts, and you cast them back in my face. This is an insult I will bear from no man alive. My servants will show you to the door, sir!” And rustling in his brocade dressing gown, he turned on his heel and stormed off to his bedroom, leaving the good colonel standing alone in the empty parlour, the little velvet packet still in his hand.
