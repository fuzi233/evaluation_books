To understand the architecture of this northern landscape, one must first confront the suffocating heat of the present era—a season of dust, sunburn, and arid stagnation that demands we flee the noonday glare into the cooling shadows of siesta. We shall leave behind the feverish present, burying it in slumber, and retreat to the dawn of the nineteenth century, specifically the years between eighteen hundred and twelve. If you anticipate from this prelude a romance steeped in sentiment, poetry, or the intoxicating vapors of melodrama, you are grievously mistaken. Do not seek passion or stimulus here; calm your expectations and lower them to a humble, unromantic standard. What lies before you is something real, cool, and solid—unlike the glittering illusions of today. It is a narrative as stark as Monday morning, when the working class awakens with the crushing consciousness of duty. While excitement may arrive later, perhaps midway through the meal, the opening course is deliberately austere: a dish fit for a Catholic, nay, an Anglo-Catholic, to consume on Good Friday during Passion Week. It consists of cold lentils and vinegar without oil, unleavened bread with bitter herbs, and no roast lamb. No feast awaits; only the bare necessities of survival.

In recent decades, a deluge of curates has swept across the north of England, blanketing the hills until every parish is choked with them. Young, vigorous, and ostensibly destined for great deeds, they should be agents of profound good. Yet, we are not speaking of this bloated present. In the early eighteen hundreds, this affluent rain had not yet fallen. Curates were a rarity then. There was no Pastoral Aid, no Additional Curates' Society to extend a helping hand to worn-out rectors, providing the funds to hire a vigorous young colleague from Oxford or Cambridge. The successors of the apostles, disciples of Dr. Pusey and instruments of the Propaganda, were merely being hatched under cradle blankets or regenerated by nursery baptisms in wash-hand basins. One could not have looked upon any of them and recognized the Italian-ironed double frills of their net caps as the crowning glory of a preordained successor to St. Paul, St. Peter, or St. John. Nor could anyone foresee, hidden within the folds of their long nightgowns, the white surplices they would later wield to cruelly exercise the souls of their parishioners, or the strange, alien garment they would brandish from the pulpit—a shirt-like raiment never before waving higher than the reading desk.

Even in those days of scarcity, however, the precious plant could be found. A favored district in the West Riding of Yorkshire boasted three rods of Aaron blossoming within a circuit of twenty miles. You shall witness them now. Enter this neat garden-house on the skirts of Whinbury, step forward into the little parlour, and behold them at dinner. Allow me to introduce you: Mr. Donne, curate of Whinbury; Mr. Malone, curate of Briarfield; Mr. Sweeting, curate of Nunnely. These are Mr. Donne's lodgings, the habitation of John Gale, a small clothier. Mr. Donne has graciously invited his brethren to regale with him. Join us, observe what is to be seen, and hear what is to be heard. For now, they are merely eating, and while they eat, we shall speak aside.

These gentlemen are in the prime of youth, possessing the frenetic energy of that age—an energy their moping, elderly vicars vainly attempt to channel into pastoral duties, urging them toward diligent school supervision and frequent visits to the sick. But the youthful Levites find such work dull. They prefer to lavish their energies on a peculiar routine that, to outside observers, appears heavy with ennui and cursed with monotony, far worse than the toil of a weaver at his loom. This routine yields them, however, an unfailing supply of enjoyment and occupation.

It is a rushing back and forth among themselves, a triangular circuit of visits maintained relentlessly through winter, spring, summer, and autumn. Season and weather hold no sway; with unintelligible zeal, they brave snow, hail, wind, rain, mire, and dust to dine, drink tea, or sup with one another. What attracts them remains elusive. It is not friendship, for whenever they meet, they quarrel. It is not religion—the subject is never named among them; theology may be discussed occasionally, but piety is absent. It is not the love of food and drink, for each could enjoy equally good joints, puddings, potent teas, and succulent toast at his own lodging as is served at his brother's. Mrs. Gale, Mrs. Hogg, and Mrs. Whipp—their respective landladies—assert that "it is just for naught else but to give folk trouble." By "folk," the good ladies naturally mean themselves, for indeed, they are kept in a perpetual fry by this system of mutual invasion.

Mr. Donne and his guests are currently at dinner. Mrs. Gale waits upon them, though a spark of the hot kitchen fire burns in her eye. She considers the privilege of inviting a friend to a meal occasionally, without additional charge—a perk included in the terms of letting her lodgings—to have been sufficiently exercised of late. The week is only Thursday. On Monday, Mr. Malone of Briarfield came to breakfast and stayed for dinner. On Tuesday, Mr. Malone and Mr. Sweeting of Nunnely arrived for tea, remained for supper, occupied the spare bed, and favored her with their company at breakfast on Wednesday morning. Now, on Thursday, they are both here at dinner, and she is almost certain they will stay all night. "C'en est trop," she would say if she could speak French.

Mr. Sweeting is mincing slices of roast beef on his plate, complaining of its toughness; Mr. Donne declares the beer is flat. Indeed, that is the crux of the matter: if they would only be civil, Mrs. Gale would not mind so much. If they seemed satisfied with what they received, she would not care. But "these young parsons are so high and so scornful, setting everybody beneath their feet." They treat her with less than civility simply because she does not keep a servant but performs the household work herself, as her mother did before her. Furthermore, they constantly speak against Yorkshire ways and Yorkshire folk. By that very token, Mrs. Gale believes none of them to be a real gentleman or of gentle kin. "The old parsons are worth the whole lump of college lads," she mutters. "They know what belongs to good manners and are kind to high and low alike."

Mr. Malone's father termed himself a gentleman; he was poor, in debt, and besottedly arrogant, and his son was precisely like him.

Mrs. Gale offered the loaf.

"Cut it, woman," said her guest, and the "woman" cut it accordingly. Had she followed her inclinations, she would have cut the parson as well; her Yorkshire soul revolted absolutely from his manner of command.

The curates possessed good appetites. Though the beef was "tough," they consumed great quantities of it. They swallowed a tolerable allowance of the "flat beer," while a dish of Yorkshire pudding and two tureens of vegetables disappeared like leaves before locusts. The cheese received distinguished marks of their attention, and a "spice-cake," which followed as dessert, vanished like a vision, never to be found again. Its elegy was chanted in the kitchen by Abraham, Mrs. Gale's son and heir, a youth of six summers. He had reckoned upon the reversion of the cake, and when his mother brought down the empty platter, he lifted up his voice and wept sore.

The evening had descended into a suffocating obscurity, a pitch-black void where the stars and moon were extinguished beneath a canopy of slate-gray clouds. Even by day, these clouds wore a somber hue, but under the cover of night, they transformed into a sable shroud, swallowing the light entirely. Malone, however, was not a man inclined to scrutinize the subtle dialectics of nature; the shifting moods of the earth and sky passed him by, largely unnoticed. He could traverse miles on a variable April morning without witnessing the celestial dance between soil and atmosphere—the moment a sunbeam kisses a hilltop to reveal a verdant smile, or when a shower weeps over the landscape, veiling its crests in the disheveled tresses of passing clouds. Consequently, he felt no compulsion to contrast the current sky—a muffled, streaming vault of ink, broken only by the tremulous, lurid shimmer of Stilbro' ironworks furnaces glowing toward the east—with the serene clarity of an unclouded frosty night. The main highway now had to be abandoned, for the remaining distance to Hollow's Mill could be significantly shortened by cutting directly across the fields. To understand the architecture of Mr. Yorke's household, one must first grasp the absolute duality of his spirit, a paradox that defied the simple categorization of his neighbors. Cheerfulness, it appears, is a volatile compound, dependent as much on the internal landscape of the self as on the external weather of the world. Thus, when Mr. Helstone and Mr. Moore emerged from the mill-yard gates, leading their diminutive procession, they moved with a vitality that seemed entirely disconnected from the drizzling night and the perilous expedition awaiting them. As the lanterns they carried swung low, casting fractured light upon their faces, a peculiar spark ignited in Mr. Moore's eyes—a lively, defiant flame—and a newfound vivacity bloomed across his dark countenance. Simultaneously, the rector's visage, usually etched with severity, softened into a mask of agrin and ashine glee. Yet, to any observer attuned to the logic of survival, such elation amidst the rain and the threat of mob violence appeared irrational. Had the workers of Stilbro' Moor glimpsed this party, they would have found immense satisfaction in taking aim from behind the walls, for the leaders knew this danger intimately. And yet, possessing steely nerves and steady hearts, they were intoxicated by the thrill of the confrontation.

I am acutely aware, reader, that a parson should ideally be a man of peace, a figure dedicated to reconciliation rather than conflict. I recall clearly the sacred commission entrusted to the clergyman, the servant of Christ whose message demands gentleness. Nevertheless, if you harbor a hatred for the priesthood, do not expect me to walk your dismal, downward path of anathema. Do not expect me to join your narrow, sweeping condemnations against "the cloth," nor to inflame my lungs with the venom of a Supplehough or a Barraclough in horror at the diabolical rector of Briarfield.

For he was not diabolical. The tragedy lay solely in his misplaced vocation; he was born a soldier, forged by circumstance into a priest. In truth, he was a conscientious, hard-headed, hard-handed man—brave, stern, implacable, and faithful. He lacked sympathy, was ungentle, prejudiced, and rigid, yet he remained true to his principles, honorable, sagacious, and sincere. Yet Helstone was cursed, vilified by many of his own flock, even as he was adored by others—a frequent fate for those who display partiality in friendship and bitterness in enmity, clinging equally to principles and prejudices.

United in purpose and buoyed by excellent spirits, one might assume Mr. Helstone and Mr. Moore would converse amicably as they rode side by side. Alas, these two men, both of hard, bilious constitutions, chafed against one another whenever their paths crossed. Their perpetual bone of contention was the war. Helstone stood as a high Tory, a loyalist of the old order, while Moore was a bitter Whig—or at least, a Whig insofar as his opposition to the war-party served his own interests. On this singular issue, he claimed any allegiance to British politics. He delighted in infuriating Helstone by proclaiming the invincibility of Bonaparte, taunting England and Europe with the impotence of their defenses, and arguing coolly that submission was preferable to destruction, for the French eagle must eventually crush every antagonist and reign supreme.

Helstone could scarcely endure these sentiments. To truly grasp the architecture of Hiram Yorke, one must first dismantle the illusions of the polite society that surrounds him. He stands before us as a Yorkshire gentleman *par excellence*, yet this title masks a visage that defies aristocratic grace. At fifty-five, though appearing older, his silver-white hair frames a face that is fresh and hale, yet carved from the harsh granite of the North. Every trait screams England—not the Norman lineage of the elite, but an inelegant, unclassic, unaristocratic mold. To the fine folk, it is vulgarity; to the sensible, mere character; but to the shrewd observer, it radiates a rude, real originality, a pithy sagacity latent in every furrow. It is the face of a man who resists leadership and evades domination, a stately integrity of port in a wiry frame, utterly devoid of clownish affectation.

Yet, to sketch his body is trivial compared to mapping his mind. Do not expect perfection here, nor a benevolent patriarch. While he speaks with occasional sense and good feeling to Mr. Moore, let this not deceive you into believing his thoughts are consistently just or his heart consistently kind. Yorke lacks the organ of veneration—a fatal defect that misaligns him wherever reverence is due. He is equally deficient in the organ of comparison, stripping him of genuine sympathy, and he possesses scant benevolence or ideality, draining the divine softness from his nature.

This absence of veneration renders him intolerant of hierarchy. Kings, nobles, priests, dynasties, parliaments, and establishments are to him nothing but rubbish, an abomination. He believes the world would be cleared of damage if these high places were razed and their occupants crushed. This lack of awe dries up the electric delight of admiration; it withers a thousand vivid pleasures. Though a member of no sect, he is not irreligious; he believes in God and Heaven, but his deity is the god of a man devoid of awe, imagination, and tenderness.

His inability to compare breeds inconsistency. While professing doctrines of mutual toleration, he harbors a bigoted antipathy toward specific classes. He speaks of "parsons" and "lords" with a harshness bordering on insolence, unable to place himself in their shoes, unable to weigh their errors against their temptations. He cannot realize how circumstances warp the soul of another similarly situated, leading him to express ferocious, tyrannical wishes against those he deems tyrants. By his own threats, he would employ arbitrary cruelty to advance freedom. Equality! Yes, he talks of it, yet at heart he is a proud man—friendly to his workers, generous to those beneath him, and submissive to his own station, but haughty as Beelzebub toward anyone he considers superior. Revolt courses in his blood; he cannot bear control, nor could his ancestors, nor will his descendants.

His lack of general benevolence makes him impatient of imbecility and any fault that grates on his shrewd nature, leaving no check to his cutting sarcasm. Unmerciful, he wounds repeatedly, oblivious to the depth of his thrusts. As for his paucity of ideality, it is hardly a fault; he retains a fine ear for music and a correct eye for color, possessing taste. Who cares for imagination? Many dismiss it as a dangerous, senseless attribute, akin to frenzy—a disease rather than a gift. Those who claim to possess it speak of an elixir that warms the heart and refines the vision, a strange companion that prevents loneliness. But this is an illusion for the fanatics who cling to the dream. Since Yorke lacks poetic imagination, he views it as superfluous in others. He tolerates painters and musicians because he can relish their results, seeing the charm of a picture or the pleasure of music. But a quiet poet, burning with internal fire, would live despised and die scorned under Hiram Yorke's gaze unless he could play the role of the tradesman in the Piece Hall.

There are many Hiram Yorkes in the world, and it is fortunate that the true poet, though outwardly placid, often harbors a truculent spirit and measures the stature of those who look down on him. He maintains a festal brightness in his bosom, independent of the world's cold indifference, laughing in his sleeve at misguided sympathizers who wail over his wrongs. Even when utilitarians pronounce his art useless, he meets their judgment with hard derision and merciless contempt for the Pharisees who deliver it. These, however, are not Yorke's reflections; they belong to the poet he scorns. We remain with Yorke.

You have heard of his faults; his virtues are that he is one of the most honorable and capable men in Yorkshire, commanding respect even from his detractors. The poor love him for his thorough kindness and fatherly demeanor. To his workmen, he is considerate and cordial. When dismissing a worker, he strives to redirect them or assist their relocation. If insubordination arises, Yorke—who knows how to control with vigor—crushes rebellion in the germ, eradicating it like a bad weed. Secure in his own affairs, he feels free to speak severely of others, blaming their plight entirely on their own faults, severing ties with masters, and advocating freely for the operatives.

Yorke's family is the oldest in the district, and though not the wealthiest, he is highly influential. Educated well, he traveled the Continent before the French Revolution, becoming adept in French and Italian. During two years in Italy, he amassed paintings and tasteful rarities adorning his residence. When inclined, his manners are those of a finished gentleman of the old school; his conversation is singularly interesting and original. Usually speaking in the Yorkshire dialect, he prefers his native Doric to refined vocabulary, asserting that a Yorkshire burr is as superior to a Cockney lisp as a bull's bellow to a rat's squeak.

Known by everyone for miles, yet intimately acquainted with few, Yorke rejects the ordinary. A racy, rough character, high or low, finds acceptance; a refined, insipid personage, however exalted, is his aversion. He spends hours talking freely with a shrewd workman or a sagacious old cottager, grudging a moment to a commonplace fine gentleman or a fashionable, frivolous lady. At eighteen, the grandiose theater of youth collapses; the stagehands withdraw the velvet curtains, and the audience is forced to confront the stark, unforgiving proscenium of reality. Until this threshold, existence was a curated fantasy, a marvellous fiction wherein the world was painted in hues of impossible gold and verdant myth. The woods were darker, the hills stranger, the skies a burning heaven populated by demi-gods. We gaze upon a moon of exaggerated luminescence, believing our hearts tremble at its mere aspect because it *must* be beautiful, ignoring the fact that such perfection exists only in the realm of the unreal.

But at eighteen, the shores of Reality rise before us, blue and soft, luring us with the promise of a green meadow beneath the azure. We imagine silver lines of living water and forget the treacherous currents that lie ahead. We are deceived into thinking that every joy is a birthright, unaware that true bliss requires the grueling payment of suffering. We believe that love arrives like a lost angel, unburdened by the quiver of pain, its arrows piercing us not with wounds but with the electric thrill of new life. The surface of the nation donned a deceptive garb of renewal: fields swelled with verdant life, hills breathed freshness, and gardens burst into bloom. Yet, beneath this pastoral veneer, the rot remained untouched. At her core, England was no better than before; her poor remained wretched, her employers perpetually harried. They convened meetings, delivered impassioned speeches, and drafted petitions designed to extort this boon, caring naught for the conditions attached. All men, considered individually, possess a degree of selfishness; taken collectively, they become intensely so. The two girls emerged into a world that had momentarily ceased to breathe, finding no living soul on their return to the rectory. They moved through the corridors as ghosts, slipping inside without a sound, ascending the stairs unheard by the waking house. It silenced her senses, washing away the tremors of the storm.

Perfect health was Shirley's enviable portion. Warm-hearted and sympathetic though she was, she possessed a constitution that refused to be broken by passion. Powerful emotions could rouse and sway her spirit without draining it dry. The tempest had shaken her while it raged, but it had left her elasticity unbent, her freshness unblighted. For every day that brought stimulating emotion, every night yielded recreating rest. Caroline, observing her sleeping form, read the profound serenity of Shirley's mind in the beauty of her happy countenance.

For herself, however, being of a different temperament, Caroline could not sleep. The mere commonplace excitement of the tea-drinking and school-gathering would have sufficed to keep her restless all night; the effect of the terrible drama enacted before her eyes was unlikely to leave her for days. Life wastes fast in such vigils as Caroline had lately kept too often—vigils during which the mind, having no pleasant food to nourish it, no manna of hope, no hived-honey of joyful memories, tries to subsist on the meagre diet of wishes. Failing to derive either delight or support from these empty cravings, and feeling itself ready to perish with a gnawing want, the mind turns to philosophy, to resolution, to resignation. It calls upon all these gods for aid, calls vainly—is unheard, unhelped, and languishes.

Caroline was a Christian; therefore, in trouble, she framed prayers according to the Christian creed, offering them with deep earnestness, begging for patience, strength, relief. This world, however, is the scene of trial and probation; and, seeing no favorable result from her petitions, it seemed to her that they were unheard and unaccepted. She believed, sometimes, that God had turned His face from her. At moments she was a Calvinist, and sinking into the gulf of religious despair, she saw darkening over her the doom of reprobation.

Most people have periods in their lives when they feel thus forsaken—when, having long hoped against hope, they see the day of fruition deferred until their hearts truly sicken within them. This is a terrible hour, yet it is often the darkest point which precedes the rise of day—that turn of the year when the icy January wind carries over the waste at once the dirge of departing winter and the prophecy of coming spring. The perishing birds, however, cannot thus understand the blast before which they shiver; and as little can the suffering soul recognize, in the climax of its affliction, the dawn of its deliverance. Yet, let whoever grieves still cling fast to love and faith in God. God will never deceive, never finally desert Him. "Whom He loveth, He chasteneth." These words are true, and should not be forgotten.

To truly grasp the dual architecture of Moore's soul, one must witness not merely the defensive shield he raised for his mill, but the relentless, terrifying spear he wielded against its architects of chaos. While the mob—the blind, starving followers driven by false counsel and gnawing privation—he spared with an innate, almost judicial mercy, refusing to stain his hands with the blood of the misled, he turned his full fury upon the leaders. These were strangers, emissaries from the great cities, not of the operative class but "down-draughts": bankrupts, perpetual debtors, men intoxicated by circumstance and ready to gamble their souls for a fleeting thrill. They were men with nothing to lose and everything to gain in the currency of character, cash, and cleanliness. Moore hunted these predators with the fervor of a sleuth-hound, deriving a peculiar, exhilarating pleasure from the pursuit that surpassed the mundane rhythm of weaving cloth.

His horse, weary of the constant strain, seemed to share the rider's hatred of these times. Moore lived on the road, inhaling the crisp, cleansing air of the open country with a hunger that dwarfed the stifling steam of the dye-houses. The magistrates of the district trembled in their seats; they were men of timidity and slowness, easily rattled by the shadow of the man who sat astride their fears. Moore delighted in this, forcing them to reveal the primal terror of assassination that had paralyzed every manufacturer and public figure in the region save for the old Cossack himself. Moore knew the risk; he knew he could be shot. Yet, such a death held no terror for his nerves—it would have been his chosen end, had fate allowed a choice.

Conversely, Moore understood the danger emanating from the shadows he chased. This awareness acted as a spur to his high-mettled temper, fueling an unquenchable scorn for the source of his peril. Fear was alien to him; too proud, too hardened by necessity, too phlegmatic to succumb to dread. Often, riding late over the moors under a moonless or silvered sky, he felt more elated, his faculties sharper and more refreshed than when trapped in the stagnation of the counting-house. Four leaders remained to be accounted for. Two had been cornered near Stilbro' within a fortnight; the remaining two haunted the outskirts of Birmingham, their lairs unknown but their presence felt.

Yet, amidst this whirlwind of justice and commerce, Moore did not entirely abandon the battered shell of his mill. He existed within a microscopic empire of two, subjugated by a woman whose authority was absolute and whose presence was suffocating. She was a square-built, brutal artifact of a world that valued order over empathy, roughly thirty years of age, though time in the sickroom seemed measured only by the decay of hope. Her dark hair fell in matted, feral clusters, and a vast, flannel-wrapped bulk obscured her humanity, rendering her less a nurse than a beast draped in the ruin of domestic tyranny. She possessed a terrifying latent power, yet she spent her days in a state of stagnant lethargy, only stirring to exert her dominance over the convalescing youth—displacing bandages, tampering with linens, or demanding obedience with the casual cruelty of a master over a slave.

Their lives were bound by the daily ritual of surveillance, barricaded from the outside world by the rigid protocols of the infirmary. Beyond this, Martin's existence was an enforced void. He was permitted no agency, no privacy, and almost no language beyond the commands of his captor. The Nurse utilized silence as a tool of oppression, rationing her words to scolding and threats, ensuring his mind remained as barren as the rocky crags that caged them. In other respects, she was no woman, but a dragon. Hortense Moore fell effaced before her; Mrs. Yorke withdrew—crushed; yet both these women were personages of some dignity in their own estimation, and of some bulk in the estimation of others. Perfectly cowed by the breadth, the height, the bone, and the brawn of Mrs. Horsfall, they retreated to the back parlour. She, for her part, sat upstairs when she liked, and downstairs when she preferred it. She took her dram three times a day, and her pipe of tobacco four times.

As to Moore, no one now ventured to inquire about him. Mrs. Horsfall had him at dry-nurse. It was she who was to do for him, and the general conjecture now ran that she did for him accordingly. Morning and evening MacTurk came to see him. His case, thus complicated by a new mischance, had become one of interest in the surgeon's eyes. He regarded him as a damaged piece of clockwork, which it would be creditable to his skill to set going again. Graves and young MacTurk—Moore's sole other visitors—contemplated him in the light in which they were wont to contemplate the occupant for the time being of the dissecting-room at Stilbro' Infirmary.

Robert Moore had a pleasant time of it—in pain, in danger, too weak to move, almost too weak to speak, a sort of giantess his keeper, the three surgeons his sole society. Thus he lay through the diminishing days and lengthening nights of the whole drear month of November.

In the commencement of his captivity, Moore used feebly to resist Mrs. Horsfall. He hated the sight of her rough bulk, and dreaded the contact of her hard hands; but she taught him docility in a trice. She made no account whatever of his six feet, his manly thews and sinews; she turned him in his bed as another woman would have turned a babe in its cradle. Martin, having tasted the intoxicating nectar of excitement, found himself loath to surrender the throne of power he had briefly occupied. Miss Helstone—that visage he had once dismissed as merely ugly, yet which now haunted his consciousness with relentless persistence, appearing in the daylight and the shadow alike—had once entered his orbit. The possibility that this fleeting contact might never recur gnawed at him. Though currently a schoolboy, he was no mere apprentice to mediocrity; destiny marked him as an original force. Years hence, he would attempt to sandpaper himself down to conform to the dull patterns of society, but the unique stamp of his nature would remain indelible. For now, he sat idly at his desk in the grammar school, his mind wandering like a restless bird, searching for a plot to expand the unfinished romance brewing in his heart. He remained unaware that countless such life-romances are condemned to perish before they reach their second chapter.

This Saturday half-holiday, he retreated to the woods, clutching his book of fairy legends alongside the unwritten manuscript of his own imagination. Martin harbored a distinct, almost religious aversion to the approaching Sunday. While his parents, though theoretically detached from the rigid structures of the Establishment, dutifully filled the large pew at Briarfield Church with their entire blooming family on the sacred day, Martin viewed the institution with skepticism. Theoretically, Mr. Yorke claimed equality among all sects; Mrs. Yorke, conversely, bestowed the highest praise upon Moravians and Quakers for their crowns of humility. Yet neither was ever known to step foot in a conventicle. Martin despised Sunday primarily because the morning service dragged on interminably and the sermons rarely resonated with his cynical palate.

However, this particular Saturday afternoon revealed a new allure in the impending day. It was a day of profound snowfall—so deep that Mrs. Yorke, during breakfast, declared her conviction that the children, both boys and girls, would be safer indoors. Instead of attending church, she proposed they sit in silent contemplation for two hours in the back parlour, alternating between reading selections from John Wesley's *Sermons*. Wesley, a figure of reform and agitation, commanded respect in both her and her husband's hearts.

"Rose will do as she pleases," Martin murmured, eyes fixed on the page, continuing his customary study over bread and milk.

"Rose will do as she is told, and Martin too," the mother observed firmly.

"I am going to church," Martin replied, embodying the ineffable quietude of a true Yorke—a man who knows his will and the means to enforce it, who may endure crushing pressure if escape is impossible, but who will never capitulate voluntarily.

"It is not fit weather," Mr. Yorke remarked.

"No answer," Martin responded, breaking his bread with studied slowness and sipping his milk.

"Martin hates to go to church, but he hates still more to obey," Mrs. Yorke noted.

"I suppose I am influenced by pure perverseness?"

"Yes, you are."

"Mother, *I* am not," Martin corrected sharply.

"By what, then, are you influenced?"

"By a complication of motives, the intricacies of which I should as soon attempt to explain to you as I should undertake to turn myself inside out to reveal the internal machinery of my frame."

"Hear Martin! Hear him!" cried Mr. Yorke, his voice rising. "I must see and ensure this lad is brought up to the bar. Nature intended him to live by his tongue. Hester, your third son must certainly be a lawyer; he possesses the essential stock-in-trade: brass, self-conceit, and words—words, words, words."

"Some bread, Rose, if you please," Martin requested, his demeanor radiating intense gravity, serenity, and phlegm.

Naturally, the boy possessed a low, plaintive voice; in his dour moods, it rose scarcely above a lady's whisper. The more inflexibly stubborn his humor, the softer and sadder the tone became. He rang the bell and gently asked for his walking shoes.

"But, Martin," his father urged, "there is drift all the way; a man could hardly wade through it."

However, seeing the boy rise as the church bell began to toll, Mr. Yorke relented. "This is a case wherein I would by no means balk the obdurate chap of his will. Go to church by all means. There is a pitiless wind, a sharp, frozen sleet, and the depth underfoot. Go out into it, since thou preferreth it to a warm fireside."

Martin quietly assumed his cloak, comforter, and cap, and deliberately stepped outside.

"My father has more sense than my mother," he pronounced aloud. "How women miss it! They drive the nail into the flesh, thinking they are hammering away at insensate stone."

He reached the church early. In his mind, he rehearsed a scenario: if the weather frightened her, or if Mrs. Pryor objected to venturing out, he would be vexed. She would arrive for the chance of seeing him, just as he awaited the chance of seeing her. She would seek a word regarding her confounded sweetheart, just as he sought another flavor of what he considered the essence of life—a taste of existence with the spirit preserved within, not evaporated. Adventure was to stagnation what champagne was to flat porter.

He looked around. The church was cold, silent, and empty save for one old woman. As the chimes subsided and the single bell tolled slowly, others arrived—elderly parishioners taking humble stations in the free sittings. It was always the frailest, the oldest, and the poorest who braved the worst weather to prove and maintain their constancy to dear old Mother Church. On this wild morning, not one affluent family attended; not a single carriage party appeared. All the lined and cushioned pews remained vacant; only on the bare oaken seats sat ranged the gray-haired elders and feeble paupers.

"I'll scorn her if she doesn't come," Martin muttered, shortly and savagely, to himself.

The rector's shovel-hat passed the porch. Mr. Helstone and his clerk were already in the vestry. The bells ceased, the reading-desk was filled, the doors closed, and the service commenced. Empty stood the rectory pew; she was not there. Martin scorned her.

"Worthless thing! Vapid thing! Commonplace humbug! Like all other girls—weakly, selfish, shallow!" Such was his liturgy.

"She is not like our picture. Her eyes are not large and expressive; her nose is not straight, delicate, Hellenic; her mouth lacks that charm I thought it had, which I imagined could beguile me of sullenness in my worst moods. What is she? A thread-paper, a doll, a toy, a *girl*, in short."

So absorbed was the young cynic that he forgot to rise from his knees at the proper moment, remaining in an exemplary attitude of devotion when, the litany over, the first hymn was given out. To be so caught did not soothe him. He started up red, for he was as sensitive to ridicule as any girl. To make matters worse, the church door reopened, and the aisles began to fill: *patter, patter, patter*, a hundred little feet trotting in. These were the Sunday scholars. According to Briarfield winter custom, these children had until now been kept where a warm stove burned, entering the church only just before communion and sermon. The little ones were settled first, and finally, when the boys and younger girls were arranged—and the organ swelling high, the choir and congregation rising to uplift a spiritual song—a tall class of young women came quietly in, closing the procession. Their teacher, having seen them seated, passed into the rectory pew.

The French-gray cloak and small beaver bonnet were known to Martin; it was the very costume his eyes had ached to catch. Miss Helstone had not allowed the storm to prove an impediment. After all, she had come to church. Martin probably whispered his satisfaction to his hymn book; at any rate, he hid his face for two minutes. Whether satisfied or not, he had time to become angry with her again before the sermon ended. She had never once looked his way; at least, he had not been so fortunate as to encounter a glance.

"If," he said internally, "if she takes no notice of me, if she shows I am not in her thoughts, I shall form a worse, a meaner opinion of her than ever. Most despicable would it be to come for the sake of those sheep-faced Sunday scholars, and not for my sake or that long skeleton Moore's."

The sermon concluded; the benediction was pronounced; the congregation dispersed. She had not been near him. Now, indeed, as Martin set his face homeward, he felt the sleet cutting sharp and the east wind biting cold. His nearest path lay through some fields—a dangerous route, because it was untrodden. He did not care; he would take it.

The winter is over and gone; spring has followed with beamy and shadowy, with flowery and showery flight. We are now in the heart of summer—in mid-June—the June of 1812.

It is burning weather. The air is deep azure and red gold. It fits the time; it fits the age; it fits the present spirit of the nations. The nineteenth century wants in its giant adolescence; the Titan boy uproots mountains in his game and hurls rocks in his wild sport. This summer Bonaparte is in the saddle; he and his host scour the Russian deserts. With him are Frenchmen and Poles, Italians and children of the Rhine, six hundred thousand strong. He marches on old Moscow. Under old Moscow's walls the rude Cossack awaits him. Barbarian stoic! He waits without fear of the boundless ruin rolling on. He puts his trust in a snow-cloud; the wilderness, the wind, and the hail-storm are his refuge; his allies are the elements—air, fire, water. And what are these? Three terrible archangels ever stationed before the throne of Jehovah. They stand clothed in white, girdled with golden girdles; they uplift vials brimming with the wrath of God. Their time is the day of vengeance; their signal, the word of the Lord of hosts, "thundering with the voice of His excellency."

"Hast thou entered into the treasures of the snow? Or hast thou seen the treasures of the hail, which I have reserved against the time of trouble, against the day of battle and war? Go your ways. Pour out the vials of the wrath of God upon the earth."

It is done. The earth is scorched with fire; the sea becomes "as the blood of a dead man"; the islands flee away; the mountains are not found.

In this year, Lord Wellington assumed the reins in Spain. They made him generalissimo for their own salvation's sake. In this year he took Badajos, he fought the field of Vittoria, he captured Pampeluna, he stormed San Sebastian; in this year he won Salamanca.

Men of Manchester, I beg your pardon for this slight résumé of warlike facts, but it is of no consequence. Lord Wellington is, for you, only a decayed old gentleman now. I rather think some of you have called him a "dotard"; you have taunted him with his age and the loss of his physical vigor. What fine heroes you are yourselves! Men like you have a right to trample on what is mortal in a demigod. Scoff at your ease; your scorn can never break his grand old heart.

But come, friends, whether Quakers or cotton-printers, let us hold a peace-congress, and let out our venom quietly.
