The rain lashed against the leaded glass of the Higgins' window, a rhythmic drumming that felt less like weather and more like the frantic, indifferent heartbeat of a city that cared nothing for the quiet suffocation of its working women. Inside, the air was thick with the scent of damp wool and the metallic tang of grief. Margaret stood before the bed, her gaze fixed on the face that had been so recently etched with the weary lines of pain and restless, troubled thoughts. Now, those features bore the faint, soft smile of eternal rest. Slow tears gathered in Margaret's eyes, blurring the room, yet a profound, terrifying calm settled into her soul. This was death. It looked more peaceful than life itself. *They rest from their labours,* the scriptures whispered in her mind; *the weary are at rest.*

Below, in the hallway, Mary sat huddled on the first chair she reached, her apron pulled over her head as she sobbed into the fabric. The sound was raw, tearing through the silence. Nicholas Higgins, resting his hand upon the house-table, stood frozen in the center of the floor. His great eyes, usually bright with a fierce, pragmatic energy, were wide and dry, staring at the reality of his daughter's absence. He struggled to reconcile the woman who had been sickly for so long, whom he had convinced would surely "pull through," with the empty space she now occupied. He had been persuaded by hope; now he faced the brutal arithmetic of loss.

Margaret felt a sudden, crushing sense of intrusion, as if she had no business familiarizing herself with the geography of death while the father, who had only just learned the terrain, stood paralyzed beside it. There had been a momentary hesitation on the steep, crooked stair when she first encountered him, but now she forced herself to glide past his abstracted gaze, leaving him trapped in the solemn circle of his household misery.

When Mary finally threw herself forward, her cries rising to a hysterical pitch, the noise shattered Nicholas's trance. He seized Margaret's arm with a grip that was suddenly desperate and violent, holding her until he could force words past the dryness of his throat. They came up thick, choking, and hoarse.

"Were yo' with her?" he demanded, his voice cracking. "Did yo' see her die?"

"No!" Margaret replied, standing still with a patience that bordered on the supernatural, now that she realized she was the sole witness to his unraveling.

For a long moment, he said nothing, his eyes darting between her and the closed bedroom door. Finally, he spoke with a strange, heavy gravity that suggested intoxication—not enough to cloud his senses, but sufficient to bewilder his thoughts.

"All men must die," he muttered, staring at the floorboards. "But she were younger than me." He paused, grappling with the finality of it, his hand tightening on her arm. Then, his head snapped up, a wild, searching inquiry burning in his glance. "Yo're sure and certain she's dead—not in a dwam, a faint? she's been so before, often."

"She is dead," Margaret answered, feeling no fear despite the bruise forming on her arm from his vice-like grip. Wild, confused gleams crossed the stupidity of his eyes.

"She is dead!" she repeated, her voice firm.

He looked at her, the search fading from his gaze, and then, with a sudden, explosive release, he let go. Throwing his body half across the table, he shook it violently, rattling every piece of furniture in the room with his sobs. Mary trembled toward him, terrified.

"Get thee gone! Get thee gone!" he shrieked, striking wildly and blindly at the air. "What do I care for thee?" Margaret gently took his hand, holding it within hers as he tore at his hair and beat his head against the hard wood. Then, spent and stuporous, he collapsed. Neither Margaret nor Mary moved. Mary trembled from head to foot, her heart hammering against her ribs.

Time lost its meaning; it might have been a quarter-hour, an hour, or an eternity. Slowly, Nicholas lifted himself up. His eyes were swollen, bloodshot, and vacant. He seemed to have forgotten anyone else was present, scowling at the watchers when he registered their existence. He shook himself heavily, giving them one final, sullen glare before turning toward the door.

"Oh, father, father!" Mary cried, flinging herself onto his arm. "Not to-night! Any night but to-night. Oh, help me! he's going out to drink again! Father, I'll not leave yo'. Yo' may strike, but I'll not leave yo'. She told me last of all to keep yo' fro' drink!"

But Margaret stood in the doorway, silent yet commanding. Nicholas looked up at her, defying her presence.

"It's my own house," he growled, shaking off Mary with renewed violence. Her face was already bleeding from her fall against a chair. "Stand out o' the way, wench, or I'll make yo'!" He looked ready to strike Margaret, his gloomy fierceness radiating heat. If she had stirred a muscle, he would have thrust her aside with even greater brutality.

"What are yo' looking at me in that way for?" he asked, daunted and awed by her severe, unmoving calm. "If yo' think for to keep me from going what gait I choose, because she loved yo'—and in my own house, too, where I never asked yo' to come, yo're mista'en. It's very hard upon a man that he can't go to the only comfort left."

Margaret felt the weight of his acknowledgment. What could she do? He had seated himself on a chair close to the door, half-conquered, half-resenting, intending to flee the moment she vacated her position, yet unwilling to unleash the violence he had threatened moments prior. Margaret laid her hand on his arm.

"Come with me," she said, her voice low and solemn, devoid of fear or doubt regarding his compliance. "Come and see her!"

He sullenly rose. He stood uncertain, his face a mask of dogged irresolution. She waited, quietly and patiently, for his time to move. He derived a strange, perverse pleasure in making her wait, but eventually, he turned toward the stairs.

They stood by the corpse.

"Her last words to Mary were, 'Keep my father fro' drink,'" Margaret said softly.

"It canna hurt her now," Nicholas muttered, his voice dropping to a wail. "Nought can hurt her now." Raising his voice again, he poured out his grief: "We may quarrel and fall out—we may make peace and be friends—we may clem to skin and bone—and nought o' all our griefs will ever touch her more. Hoo's had her portion on 'em. What wi' hard work first, and sickness at last, hoo' led the life of a dog. And to die without knowing one good piece o' rejoicing in all her days! Nay, wench, whatever hoo said, hoo can know nought about it now, and I mun ha' a sup o' drink just to steady me again sorrow."

"No," Margaret said, her tone softening with his own crumbling resolve. "You shall not. If her life has been what you say, at any rate she did not fear death as some do. Oh, you should have heard her speak of the life to come—the life hidden with God, that she is now gone to."

He shook his head, glancing sideways at her with a look of painful exhaustion. His pale, haggard face struck her with fresh force.

"You are sorely tried," he said abruptly. "Where have you been all day—not at work?"

"Not at work, sure enough," he replied with a short, grim laugh. "Not at what you call work. I were at the Committee, till I were sickened out wi' trying to make fools hear reason. I were fetched to Boucher's wife afore seven this morning. She's bed-fast, but she were raving and raging to know where her dunner-headed brute of a chap was, as if I'd to keep him—as if he were fit to be ruled by me. The d—d fool, who has put his foot in all our plans! And I've walked my feet sore wi' going about for to see men who wouldn't be seen, now the law is raised against us. And I were sore-hearted, too, which is worse than sore-footed; and if I did see a friend who ossed to treat me, I never knew hoo lay in a-dying here. Bess, lass, thou'd believe me, thou wouldst—wouldstn't thou?" turning to the poor, dumb form with a wild, broken appeal.

"I am sure," Margaret said, "I am sure you did not know; it was quite sudden. But, now, you see, it would be different; you do know; you do see her lying there; you hear what she said with her last breath. You will not go?"

No answer. In fact, where was he to look for comfort?

"Come home with me," she ventured, a bold step that made her tremble internally. "At least you shall have some comfortable food, which I'm sure you need."

"Yo'r father's a parson?" he asked, his mind shifting gears with sudden curiosity.

"He was," Margaret said shortly.
