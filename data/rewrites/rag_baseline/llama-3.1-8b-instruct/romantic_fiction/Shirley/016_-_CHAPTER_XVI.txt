Whitsuntide brought a semblance of calm to the valley, its tranquility forged by the diligent efforts of Miss Keeldar, the rectors, and their stalwart assistants, Mary Ann Ainley and Margaret Hall. As they worked tirelessly to raise a handsome sum for the poor, the neighbourhood began to exhibit signs of a nascent peace. For two weeks, the mills stood untouched, their machinery idle and still, and not a single outrage marred the once-troubled landscape. Shirley's optimism swelled, buoyed by the promise of summer's warmth, which always brought an upsurge in trade. She confided in her tenant, Gérard Moore, as they strolled through the fields, sharing her conviction that the war, though relentless, was finite. "We must hold on to hope," she said, her words carried away by the gentle breeze. Moore listened intently, his eyes narrowing slightly behind his spectacles, his mouth curled into a soft smile.

"I, too, believe in the finite nature of conflict," he replied, his voice low and soothing. "It's the anchor that holds fast my own hopes, and upon which my plans depend." As they walked, he spoke of his mill, Hollow's, which operated on speculation, its goods unsold and its fate uncertain. "I'm waiting for the storm to pass," he said, "and when it does, I'll be ready to seize the opportunity. The loan from a generous benefactor saved me from ruin, and I'm determined to make the most of it." His words were laced with a quiet determination, a resolve that belied the turmoil brewing beneath the surface.

As they conversed, Shirley sensed a subtle undercurrent of unease emanating from Moore. His words, though reassuring, seemed tinged with a hint of foreboding. "I fear, Mr. Moore," she said, her voice barely above a whisper, "that your optimism may be misplaced. The working classes, though grateful for charity, often resent the dependency it creates. And what of the disaffected, those who plot and scheme in secret? They're not to be underestimated, for they have their own networks and leaders, their own strategies and plans." Moore's expression turned grave, his eyes clouding over like the sky on a stormy day. "You are right, Miss Shirley," he said, his voice heavy with a sense of foreboding.
