To capture the essence of Claelia's transformation, one must first witness the deliberate construction of her own mythos. She commissioned a full-length portrait, yet rejected the suggestion of her friend Pietro Vanucci with a sharp, calculated disdain. "'Twill be time to put a slight on the Gerardo, when his work discontents me," she declared, her voice cutting through the air like a blade. Gerard, aware of his own limitations as a colorist despite his mastery of line, proposed an alternative: a Roman statue. He demonstrated his ability to mimic marble on paper, coaxing her into consenting to the pose. However, the moment he insisted she don the tunic, toga, and sandals of antiquity, her medieval frankness surfaced. "Why, I had as lieve be presented in my smock," she retorted, her refusal grounded in a pragmatic rejection of historical alienation.

Gerard, undeterred, appealed to her aesthetic sensibilities, arguing that the ancient garb was free, ample, and noble—a fitting vessel for the Roman features and shapely limbs Heaven had bestowed upon her. "Alack! signorina," he pleaded, "you have surely never noted the ancient habit... most becoming your highness." Her response was a deferral, a strategic pause: "Come o' Saturday, and then I will say ay or nay."

This respite was consumed by the frantic tailoring of costumes that were a millennium out of date, tried on in her chamber to ensure they compensated for their temporal displacement. But Gerard's journey to this interview was abruptly halted by a vision in a shop window. There, lying open, was a copy of Lactantius. His eye, trained to recognize the fluid grace of the human hand, scanned the page. "That is fairly writ, anyway," he thought, expecting the familiar rhythm of ink.

Then, the realization struck with the force of a physical blow. It was not written; it was printed.

Gerard groaned, the sound echoing in the quiet street. "I am sped; mine enemy is at the door. The press is in Rome." He entered the shop, feigning nonchalance, asking how long the printing press had been present in the city. The merchant laughed, dismissing the idea entirely until Gerard pressed him regarding the specific volume. "Oh, the Lactantius; that was printed on the top of the Apennines."

"What, did the printing-press fall down there out o' the moon?" Gerard demanded, his horror palpable.

"Nay, messer," the trader replied, pointing to the title page. "It shot up there out of Germany. See!"

Gerard snatched the book, his eyes devouring the foreign script: *Opera et impensis Sweynheim et Pannartz Alumnorum Joannis Fust. Impressum Subiacis. A.D. 1465.* "Will ye buy, messer? See how fair and even be the letters. Few are left can write like that; and scarce a quarter of the price."

"I would fain have it," Gerard said sadly, clutching the threat of mechanized reproduction. "But my heart will not let me. Know that I am a caligrapher, and these disciples of Fust run after me round the world a-taking the bread out of my mouth. But I wish them no ill. Heaven forbid!" He fled the shop, driven by a singular urgency. "Dear Margaret," he whispered to the empty air, "we must lose no time; we must make our hay while shines the sun. One month more and an avalanche of printer's type shall roll down on Rome from those Apennines, and lay us waste that writers be."

With this dread anchoring his resolve, he rushed to the Princess's residence. He was ushered into an apartment new to him, a space of opulent isolation. A fountain played in the center, its waters masking footsteps on a floor paved with panther skins, dressed with hair to create a surface of absolute silence. An antechamber to her boudoir, separated only by a curtain of gorgeous tapestry, it was a theater staged for performance.

Left alone, Gerard grew uneasy, doubting if he had been led astray. These fears dissolved with the arrival of a light step behind the tapestry. The fabric parted, revealing a figure that transcended mere mortality. It was neither quite Venus nor quite Minerva, but a synthesis of both—nobler than the goddess of love, more womanly than the daughter of Jove. Dressed in toga, tunic, and sandals, she was a living relic, untouched by modernity. As for beauty, it was timeless.

Gerard started, the artist within him flushing with a mixture of shock and ecstasy. "Oh!" he cried innocently, gazing in rapture. This reaction, genuine and unguarded, added the final charm to his model. A light blush tinted her cheeks, her eyes brightened, and her mouth curved in a smile of delicious complacency at this tribute to her own allure.

After a prolonged silence, during which Gerard's eloquence reduced itself to staring, she broke the spell. "Well, Gerardo, thou seest I have made myself an antique monster for thee."

"A monster? I doubt Fra Colonna would fall down and adore your highness, seeing you so habited."

"Nay, I care not to be adored by an old man. I would liever be loved by a young one: of my own choosing."

Gerard settled his pencils, covering his canvas with stout paper, and began to work. Absorbed completely, he showed no mercy to his model. Finally, after nearly an hour in one rigid posture, she spoke, her voice faint. "Gerardo," she said, "I can stand so no more, even for thee."

"Sit down and rest awhile, Signora."

"I thank thee," she replied, sinking into a chair, turning pale and sighing. Gerard, alarmed by her distress and realizing his inconsideration, reached for water from the fountain. He prepared to splash it on her face, but she raised a white hand deprecatingly. "Nay, hold it to my brow with thine hand: prithee, do not fling it at me!"

Timidly, he applied the wet cloth to her forehead. "Ah!" she sighed, "that is reviving. Again." He repeated the action. She thanked him and signaled for a bell. A maid arrived, dispatched to fetch Floretta with orders to bring a large fan.

Floretta appeared, but the moment she drew near, the Princess's highbred nostrils flared like a bloodhorse's. "Wretch!" she shouted, springing to her feet with a sudden resurgence of vigor. Seizing Floretta by the left arm, she twisted it violently into her hair and, with her right hand, delivered three severe boxings to the girl's ears.

Floretta screamed and blubbered, receiving no mercy. The antique toga, now discarded, revealed a bare arm that seemed as powerful as it was beautiful, rising and falling like the piston of a steam engine. Heavy slaps resonated against Floretta's shoulders, driving her sobbing and screaming through the tapestry, where she continued to cry bitterly for some time.

"Saints of heaven!" cried Gerard, horrified. "What is amiss? what has she done?"

"She knows right well. 'Tis not the first time. The nasty toad! I'll learn her to come to me stinking of the musk-cat."

"Alas! Signora, 'twas a small fault, methinks."

"A small fault? Nay, 'twas a foul fault." With an amazing descent from fury to humility, she added, "Are you wroth with me for beating her, Gerardo?"

"Signora, it ill becomes me to school you; but methinks such as Heaven appoints to govern others should govern themselves."

"That is true, Gerardo. How wise you are, to be so young." She summoned the other maid, handing her a purse. "Take that to Floretta, and tell her 'the Gerardo' hath interceded for her; and so I must needs forgive her. There, Gerardo."

Gerard blushed at the compliment, unable to formulate a witty reply, and asked if he should resume the painting.

"Not yet; beating that hussy hath somewhat breathed me. I'll sit awhile, and you shall talk to me. I know you can talk, an it pleases you, as rarely as you draw."

"That were easily done."

"Do it then, Gerardo."

Gerard was taken aback. "But, signora, I know not what to say. This is sudden."

"Say your real mind. Say you wish you were anywhere but here."

"Nay, signora, that would not be sooth. I wish one thing though."

"Ay, and what is that?" she asked gently.

"I wish I could have drawn you as you were beating that poor lass. You were awful, yet lovely. Oh, what a subject for a Pythoness!"

"Alas! he thinks but of his art. And why keep such a coil about my beauty, Gerardo? You are far fairer than I am. You are more like Apollo than I to Venus. Also, you have lovely hair and lovely eyes--but you know not what to do with them."

"Ay, do I. To draw you, signora."
