Yes—accounts must be closed; the hearth must be swept; the last loaf turned from the oven. A story, like a household, cannot go on for ever with its table always laid and its doors always open. We must put by the good linen, tie up the herbs to dry, and speak—briefly, yet truly—of what became of certain persons who have sat with us, nearer or farther from the fire.

Let us begin with the curates: long overlooked guests, yet, in their way, indispensable, as salt is to broth. Modest merit—come forward! Mr. Malone, at least, would step up at any call, quick as a lad when the pot-lid rattles.

No, Peter Augustus; not you. We cannot serve up your history—no, not even minced fine and seasoned cunningly. There are truths like strong spirits: the unaccustomed palate revolts; the genteel stomach will not “digest” them. The squeal of the real pig, they say, is not music for drawing-rooms; but a painted pig, in gilt ribbons, will be patted and praised. Such is the world’s taste: it rejects the honest, rough-born fact and adopts the pretty counterfeit. So stand down, Peter—legitimate, noisy, and none too washed.

Make way for Mr. Sweeting.

Here he comes, with his lady on his arm: Mrs. Sweeting—Dora Sykes that was—plump as a Christmas goose, and weighty enough to make a church pew feel dutiful. They were married under skies as clear as good crystal. He had a comfortable living; her father had comfortable means; and between parsonage pantry and Sykes’s purse there was no lack of bread, no scanty joint, no thin tea. They lived long; they lived peacefully; parishioners liked them, friends clustered round them, and the whole picture takes varnish readily—there, it shines.

Advance, Mr. Donne.

This gentleman, against expectation, turned out well—much better than either you or I might have foreseen. He married a sensible, quiet little woman, and she—by mere steady warmth of household influence, like yeast working in dough—made him what he became: an exemplary domestic character; a parish priest active to restlessness (though as pastor, to the end, he conscientiously refused to act). He loved the outside of things: to him the church was a room to be furnished, the altar a cabinet to be polished, the very devotion a matter of arrangement and cleanliness. His small school, his small church, his small parsonage—each rose by his care and each stood as a model; and if taste in architecture had been religion, Mr. Donne might have been a saint.

He possessed, besides, one master-art—begging. Never did man handle a subscription-list as a cook handles a rolling-pin: with firm pressure and no hesitation. He begged of high and low—of the barefoot child in the lane and the duke with a coronet on his carriage-panel. He sent letters far and wide—to old Queen Charlotte, to the princesses her daughters, to her royal sons, to the Prince Regent, to ministers of state; and, more marvellous still, he screwed money out of all. It is written somewhere that he got five pounds from the close-fisted Queen, and two guineas from her splendid eldest son. When Mr. Donne went collecting, he put on—metaphorically—a suit of brazen mail. That you gave yesterday was no reason why you should not give to-day; he would tell you so, straight, and ten to one he would get the second sum by the sheer clang of his perseverance. People yielded to be rid of him; and, to do him justice, he did some good with what he gained. In his day and generation he was useful.

And since we are speaking of curates, it may be remarked—though without satisfying any appetite for scandal—that on Mr. Malone’s sudden and premature vanishing from Briarfield (you cannot learn how it happened; curiosity must go hungry where taste is dainty), there came another Irish curate, Mr. Macarthey. Of him I can speak, with truth, as of a decent, decorous, conscientious man—just as Peter had been rampant and boisterous and—no: I will not finish the epithet; it would let the cat out, and we are not in a mood for prowling revelations.

Mr. Macarthey laboured faithfully. Under his sway the schools—Sunday and day—flourished like well-watered crops. Being human, he had faults; but they were steady, clerical faults—faults many would call virtues. Invite him to tea with a Dissenter, and his whole week would be disordered as a dairy is disordered when a stranger meddles with the milk-pans. Let him see a Quaker keep on his hat in church, or hear of an unbaptized creature buried with Christian rites, and it shook his body and soul as a wrong note shakes an ear trained to church music. Otherwise he was sane, diligent, charitable.

I dare say a justice-loving public has remarked my remissness in pursuing and punishing the would-be assassin of Mr. Robert Moore. Here was opportunity for a tidy dance—law and gospel, dungeon and dock, the whole set to the grim fiddling of “dead-thraw.” You might have liked it; I should not. Facts, happily, absolve me: the murderer was never punished because he was never caught; and he was never caught because he was never pursued. The magistrates shuffled a little, as if they meant to be spirited; but Moore himself—lying still on his cottage couch, laughing in his sleeve, sneering with every feature of his pale, foreign face—would not urge them now; and they, after performing indispensable forms, prudently let the matter drop.

Mr. Moore knew who had shot him, and all Briarfield knew. It was Michael Hartley, the half-crazed weaver: frantic Antinomian in religion, mad leveller in politics. Poor soul—he died of delirium tremens a year after the attempt; and Robert gave the wretched widow a guinea to bury him.

The winter went away at last—like a harsh tune dying down beyond the hills—and spring came in, beamy and shadowy, flowery and showery, setting the hedges whispering again. Now we are in the heart of summer, mid-June, June of 1812.

It is burning weather. The air looks deep azure and red gold, and one hears the season as well as sees it: the drone of insects in the grass, the far crack of a whip, the lazy clink of a pail-chain in a yard; and over all, as if the whole county had become a single great instrument, the sound of bells—public joy striking metal. Yet the same summer that ripens currants and warms dairy floors has its own harsher music in the world beyond Yorkshire. Bonaparte is in the saddle; his host scours Russian deserts; six hundred thousand march on towards old Moscow. Under Moscow’s walls the rude Cossack waits, trusting not in cannon so much as in a snow-cloud; the wilderness, the wind, and the hail-storm are his allies. Elements—air, fire, water—stand like terrible ministers of judgment.

In this year, too, Lord Wellington took the reins in Spain; Badajos, Vittoria, Pampeluna, San Sebastian, Salamanca—names that ring like drum-beats in history. Men of Manchester, pardon this slight résumé; it is of no consequence. You may call Wellington a dotard now, if you please; your scorn cannot break his grand old heart.

But come—Quakers, cotton-printers—let us hold a peace-congress and draw our breath from war’s din. A triumph came in your own line. On the 18th of June 1812 the Orders in Council were repealed, the blockaded ports thrown open; and you made Yorkshire and Lancashire shake with your shout. The ringers cracked a bell in Briarfield belfry: the note has a jar in it to this day, as if the bronze remembers the violence done it. The Association of Merchants and Manufacturers dined together at Stilbro’, and went home in such a plight as their wives would never wish to see again. Liverpool started and snorted like a river-horse roused among reeds by thunder. Some American merchants, in the first shock of prosperity, had themselves bled—wise men, preparing even in joy to plunge again into speculation’s deep pits. Stocks that had lain dead and dusted were swept away in an instant; warehouses lightened, ships laden, work abounded, wages rose. The prospect might be delusive, but it was brilliant; and to many it proved true. In that one June, more than one solid fortune was made.

When a whole province rejoices, the humblest of its inhabitants tastes something festal; the sound of bells reaches even the most secluded dwelling and seems to call it out of itself. Caroline Helstone felt that summons. She dressed more carefully than usual that day, in her neatest muslin, and went to spend the afternoon at Fieldhead to superintend millinery preparations for a great event—the last appeal in such matters being reserved for her unimpeachable taste. She decided the wreath, the veil, the dress for the altar; chose robes and fashions for common occasions with little reference to the bride’s opinion—Miss Keeldar being, at present, in a mood difficult to consult.

Louis had foretold trouble and found it. His mistress had shown herself exquisitely provoking—putting off her marriage day by day, week by week, month by month: at first coaxing him with soft pretences of delay; in the end rousing his whole deliberate, determined nature to revolt against a tyranny sweet and intolerable. It needed a tempest-shock to bring her to a fixed day; but at last she lay fettered to it, conquered by love, bound with a vow.

Vanquished thus, restricted, she pined like a chained creature born for wide moors and sudden caprices. Her captor alone could cheer her; his society only could make amends for lost liberty. In his absence she sat or wandered alone, spoke little, and ate less—as if even bread, when it tastes of submission, will not go down. She furthered none of the preparations; Louis had to direct all arrangements. He was virtually master of Fieldhead weeks before he became so in name—the least presumptuous, the kindest master; but his lady left him absolute. She abdicated without a struggle. “Go to Mr. Moore—ask Mr. Moore,” was her answer when applied to for orders. Never did wooer of wealthy bride escape the subaltern place more completely, never was he more inevitably forced into the paramount part.

A year afterwards she confessed there had been system in her surrender. “Louis,” she said, “would never have learned to rule if I had not ceased to govern. The incapacity of the sovereign developed the powers of the premier.”

It had been intended that Miss Helstone should be bridesmaid at the approaching nuptials; but Fortune had destined her another part.

She came home in time to water her plants. The last flower she tended was a rose-tree blooming in a quiet green nook at the back of the house—a sheltered corner where the air held the day’s warmth as a kitchen holds the scent of baking long after the loaves are drawn. She gave the roots their drink; the leaves shook off bright drops; the rose itself, full and soft, breathed out that mingled sweetness and spice which makes June seem edible.

Near the wall stood a fragment of sculptured stone—a monkish relic, perhaps once the base of a cross. Caroline mounted it to command the view, watering-pot still in one hand, with the other lifting her dress aside to avoid the trickling drops. She looked over the wall: along lonely fields; beyond three dusk trees standing side by side against the sky; beyond a solitary thorn at the head of a solitary lane far off. She surveyed the moors, where bonfires were kindling—public rejoicing set alight on the high ground. The warm evening held bell-music in its bosom; the blue smoke of the fires looked soft, their red flame bright. Above, where the sun had vanished, a silver point twinkled—Venus, the star of love.

Caroline was not unhappy. Yet she sighed; and as she sighed, a hand circled her waist and rested quietly there. She thought she knew who had drawn near; she did not start.

“I am looking at Venus, mamma,” she said gently. “See—how white her lustre is, compared with the deep red of the bonfires!”

The answer was a closer caress. She turned—and looked, not into Mrs. Pryor’s matron face, but up at a dark, manly visage. The watering-pot slipped from her hand; she stepped down from the pedestal as if the stone had suddenly grown too high for her.

“I have been sitting with ‘mamma’ an hour,” said the intruder. “I have had a long conversation with her. Where, meantime, have you been?”

“To Fieldhead. Shirley is as naughty as ever, Robert. She will neither say Yes nor No to any question. She sits alone. I cannot tell whether she is melancholy or nonchalant. If you rouse her, or scold her, she gives you a look—half wistful, half reckless—which sends you away as queer and crazed as herself. What Louis will make of her, I cannot tell. For my part, if I were a gentleman, I think I would not dare undertake her.”

“Never mind them. They were cut out for each other. Louis—strange to say—likes her all the better for these freaks. He will manage her, if any one can. She tries him, however. He has had a stormy courtship for such a calm character; but you see it ends in victory. Caroline, I have sought you to ask an audience. Why are those bells ringing?”

“For the repeal of your terrible law—the Orders you hate so much. You are pleased, are you not?”

“Yesterday evening at this time I was packing some books for a sea-voyage. They were almost all I felt free to take to Canada—books, clothes, seeds, roots, tools. I was going to leave you.”

“To leave me? To leave me?”

Her fingers fastened on his arm; fear sharpened her voice.

“Not now—not now. Examine my face—look well. Is the despair of parting written there?”

She looked, and in the dusk found light: a countenance beaming, strong in its lines, shedding hope as a hearth sheds warmth.

“Will the repeal do you good—much good—immediate good?” she asked, breathless.

“The repeal of the Orders in Council saves me. Now I shall not turn bankrupt; now I shall not give up business; now I shall not leave England; now I shall be no longer poor; now I can pay my debts; now all the cloth in my warehouses will be taken off my hands, and commissions given me for much more. This day lays for my fortunes a broad, firm foundation—on which, for the first time in my life, I can securely build.”

Caroline drank his words as one drinks at last after thirst—held his hand, drew a long breath that sounded like relief.

“You are saved? Your heavy difficulties are lifted?”

“They are lifted. I breathe. I can act.”

“At last! Oh, Providence is kind! Thank Him, Robert.”

“I do thank Providence.”

“And I also—for your sake!” She looked up, devout.

“Now I can take more workmen, give better wages, lay wiser and more liberal plans, do some good, be less selfish. Now, Caroline, I can have a house—a home I can truly call mine—and now—”

He paused; his deep voice was checked as if the bell-sound out on the moor had struck something within him.

“And now,” he resumed, “now I can think of marriage; now I can seek a wife.”

This was no moment for her to speak. She did not.

“Will Caroline—who meekly hopes to be forgiven as she forgives—pardon all I have made her suffer? all that long pain I wickedly caused her? all that sickness of body and mind she owed to me? Will she forget what she knows of my poor ambition, my sordid schemes? Will she let me expiate these things? Will she suffer me to prove that, as I once deserted cruelly, trifled wantonly, injured basely, I can now love faithfully, cherish fondly, treasure tenderly?”

His hand lay in hers. Her answer came first as a pressure, then as words.

“Is Caroline mine?”

“Caroline is yours.”

“I will prize her. The sense of her value is here—in my heart; the necessity for her society is blended with my life. Not more jealous shall I be of the blood whose flow moves my pulses than of her happiness and well-being.”

“I love you too, Robert, and will take faithful care of you.”

“Will you take faithful care of me? Faithful care! As if that rose should promise to shelter from tempest this hard gray stone! But she will care for me—in her way. These hands will minister every comfort I can taste. I know the being I seek to entwine with my own will bring me a solace, a charity, a purity to which, of myself, I am a stranger.”

Suddenly Caroline was troubled; her lip quivered, and she shrank, then leaned again, as if her heart could not decide between joy and duty.

“What flutters my dove?” Moore asked, feeling the unrest.

“Poor mamma! I am all mamma has. Must I leave her?”

“I thought of that difficulty. I and ‘mamma’ have discussed it.”

“Tell me what you wish—what you would like—and I will consider if it is possible to consent. But I cannot desert her, even for you. I cannot break her heart, even for your sake.”

“She was faithful when I was false—was she not? I never came near your sick-bed, and she watched it ceaselessly.”

“What must I do? Anything but leave her.”

“At my wish you never shall leave her.”

“She may live very near us?”

“With us—only she will have her own rooms and servant. For this she stipulates herself.”

“You know she has an income that, with her habits, makes her quite independent?”

“She told me that, with a gentle pride that reminded me of somebody else.”

“She is not at all interfering, and incapable of gossip.”

“I know her, Cary. But if, instead of being reserve and discretion itself, she were quite the opposite, I should not fear her.”

“Yet she will be your mother-in-law?” Caroline gave an arch little nod. Moore smiled.

“Louis and I are not of the order of men who fear their mothers-in-law, Cary. Our foes never have been, nor will be, those of our own household. I doubt not my mother-in-law will make much of me.”

“That she will—in her quiet way. She is not demonstrative; and when you see her silent, or even cool, you must not fancy her displeased; it is only a manner she has. Let me interpret for her whenever she puzzles you. Always believe my account, Robert.”

“Oh, implicitly! Jesting apart, I feel she and I will suit—on ne peut mieux. Hortense, you know, is exquisitely susceptible—in our French sense of the word—and not, perhaps, always reasonable in her requirements; yet, dear honest girl, I never wounded her deeply, nor had a serious quarrel with her in my life.”

“No; you are most generously considerate—most tenderly indulgent to her; and you will be considerate with mamma. You are a gentleman all through, Robert, to the bone, and nowhere so perfect a gentleman as at your own fireside.”

“A eulogium I like; it is very sweet. I am well pleased my Caroline should view me so.”

“Mamma just thinks of you as I do.”

“Not quite, I hope?”

“She does not want to marry you—don’t be vain. But she said to me the other day: ‘My dear, Mr. Moore has pleasing manners; he is one of the few gentlemen I have seen who combine politeness with an air of sincerity.’”

“‘Mamma’ is rather a misanthropist, is she not? Not the best opinion of the sterner sex?”

“She forbears to judge them as a whole; but she has her exceptions whom she admires—Louis and Mr. Hall, and, of late, yourself. She did not like you once; I knew that, because she would never speak of you. But, Robert—”

“Well—what now? What is the new thought?”

“You have not seen my uncle yet?”

“I have. ‘Mamma’ called him into the room. He consents—conditionally. If I prove that I can keep a wife, I may have her; and I can keep her better than he thinks—better than I choose to boast.”

“If you get rich, you will do good with your money, Robert?”
