When Tito turned away from the Via de’ Bardi that day, he walked as men do when a noose has been loosened from the throat—not with gratitude so much as with a quickened appetite for air. The street sounds came back to him as if they had been muffled during his late suspense: the clop of a mule’s hoof on uneven paving; a distant hammer on bronze; a woman’s voice, sharp as a knife over a market-board, bargaining in the Florentine tongue that bit and clacked in Tito’s ear with its rough, homely certainties. The city had its own breath—smoke from kitchen chimneys, sour wine lees tipped into gutters, warm bread carried under a cloth—and his mind, released from the immediate presence of Romola and her father, began to turn and worry at the little abrasions left by the danger he had escaped.

He had been thoroughly freed, as he told himself, from the peril that had threatened him; and because fortune had done this for him, it seemed only fair that she should. He had not been brought to account; he had not been forced to look too long at the face of his own first deception. Who was hurt? The results to Baldassarre were too doubtful, too entangled in all the “ifs” that make an easy pillow for a man who wishes to sleep. The load was lifted; he could breathe; and it was precisely that lightness—this delight in immunity—that was silently dulling the very sensibility which might have saved him from future snares.

Yet there remained one thing that tugged like a thread caught on a nail: the ring.

In those hours of dread he had not only felt, but acted as he would not have done had he truly foreseen. He would not have parted with that familiar gem. Romola would notice its absence; so would others who had seen it on his hand, that glittering token he had professed to prize. If he could not produce it, he must explain—must feign some noble purpose, some special gift bought with the money. And Tito, for the moment, felt a nausea at the very taste of simulation, as though he had been chewing too long on sweetmeats that had turned cloying.

After all, the ring was a slight matter.

But then—Tessa.

The thought came with an odd domestic vividness, as if he saw again the little round face and the pouting lips, and felt on his palm the warmth of a small hand that trusted. Should he seek her out and undeceive her at once? Or let daylight and the stir of life end happy dreams without his intervention? A dream, after all, is like a dish of milk and honey given to a child: pleasant while it lasts, and when it is done there is no need to scold the tongue for having been sweetened.

The sweet, innocent, pouting little thing—impossible not to think of her. And with the thought came the homelier question that had pricked him even then: did her step-father use her more cruelly now her mother was dead? Tito imagined carrying her some small present—something that would make her eyes widen with that quick gratitude of the poor, who are accustomed to being refused. A scarf? A ribbon? A little gilt image? And then a counter-thought came, cold as water: if he saw her again, if he undeceived her, what then? Tell Romola? Let Romola find some happier lot for her?

No. That unlucky little incident of the _cerretano_ and the marriage—his allowing Tessa to part from him wrapped in delusion—must never come under Romola’s gaze. Not because Romola would fail to pity; rather because Romola’s pity, once awakened, had a force that would tear away coverings Tito meant to keep in place. And besides, no enlightenment could wholly expel what had already planted itself in Tessa’s mind; there would always be a risk—some childish betrayal, some babbled phrase in the wrong dialect to the wrong ear. Even a child’s love, when disappointed, may have a sharpness in it, a little gall. Yes—she must be a little in love with him; and for that very reason it might be wisest that he should not see her again.

It was a trifling adventure, he argued—just such a thing as a country girl might brood over until some ruddy contadino, smelling of sun-warmed fields and crushed fennel, made his acceptable love; then she would loosen her tongue and learn—by chance, by laughter, by some neighbor’s coarse wisdom—that she was free.

_Dunque_—good-bye, Tessa. Kindest wishes.

People are apt to think that resolutions taken for their own ease will hold like iron. Tito made up his mind that the affair should have no further consequence for him; and with that mind made up, he turned to what could be managed, shaped, seasoned—what could be turned into a pleasing dish for another’s eyes.

The fifty-five florins that had come from the ring were not to lie idle. He had already conceived a pretty ingenious thought—one that would help him account for the absence of the gem, and would also serve as a gentle barrier against the recurrence in Romola of those monkish fancies that jarred upon him. He would replace a thing with a thing: trade the personal token for an object that looked like art, like taste, like devotion to the antique—something that could sit in the household as naturally as a carved chest or a painted panel, and silently teach Romola’s mind to play where he wished it to play.

So he went to the Via Gualfonda, to find Piero di Cosimo—the painter then most esteemed for fantastic mythological invention, precisely the sort Tito’s purpose required.

Entering the court on which Piero’s dwelling opened, he saw at once the heavy iron knocker thickly wrapped with wool and cunningly lashed with cords. The absurdity of it had its own rationale; Tito remembered how Piero defended his ears against obtrusive noise, as if the city’s clamour were an invasion of the soul. It struck Tito—who was always quick to profit by another man’s peculiarity—that this painter’s very oddness was a kind of acoustic piety: he would not have his inward images battered by common thunderings and cries.

Tito tapped modestly with his knuckles; then, growing more importunate, shook the door. No answer. He was already moving away—half vexed with himself for wasting time, thinking he might better have waited to encounter Piero again at Nello’s—when a little girl entered the court with a basket of eggs on her arm. She went straight to the door as if it were an everyday altar. Standing on tiptoe, she pushed up a small iron plate that slid in grooves, put her mouth to the opening, and piped, “Messer Piero!”

In a moment came the metallic music of bolts drawn back; the door opened; and Piero stood there in a red night-cap and a loose brown serge tunic, the sleeves rolled up to the shoulder as if he were about to knead bread or butcher game rather than paint gods. He darted one surprised glance at Tito—no more than a brushstroke—then held out his hand for the basket, re-entered, and presently returned with the empty basket.

“How much to pay?”

“Two grossoni, Messer Piero,” said the child. “They are all ready boiled, my mother says.”

Piero drew the coin from the leathern scarsella at his belt. The little maiden trotted away, not without a few upward glances of awed admiration at the handsome young signor who did not belong to her world of eggs and ash and hearthstone.

Piero’s look at Tito was much less admiring.

“What do you want at my door, Messer Greco?” he said. “I saw you this morning at Nello’s. If you had asked me then, I could have told you that I see no man in this house without knowing his business and agreeing with him beforehand.”

“Pardon, Messer Piero,” Tito answered, with a good-humour smooth as well-pressed oil. “I acted without enough reflection. I remembered nothing but your admirable skill in inventing pretty caprices, when a sudden desire for something of that sort prompted me to come to you.”

Piero’s odd manners were too notorious for Tito to take this as a personal affront; and even had there been insult intended, Tito’s instinct was not to resent but to win. He watched Piero’s face—how it distorted itself, as if flattery were a sour fruit in his mouth. The painter grinned, stretching the corners of his lips and pressing down his brows in a way that hid his feelings like a mask.

“And what may that need be?” he asked, after a pause; for the hint of invention called to him like a trumpet muffled by cloth.

“I want a very delicate miniature device, taken from certain fables of the poets, which you will know how to combine for me,” Tito said. “It must be painted on a wooden case—I will show you the size—in the form of a triptych. The inside may be simple gilding: it is on the outside I want the device. It is a favourite subject with you Florentines—the triumph of Bacchus and Ariadne; but I want it treated in a new way. A story in Ovid will give you the necessary hints. The young Bacchus must be seated in a ship, his head bound with clusters of grapes, and a spear entwined with vine-leaves in his hand: dark-berried ivy must wind about the masts and sails, the oars must be thyrsi, and flowers must wreathe themselves about the poop; leopards and tigers must be crouching before him, and dolphins must be sporting round. But I want to have the fair-haired Ariadne with him, made immortal with her golden crown—that is not in Ovid’s story, but no matter, you will conceive it all—and above there must be young Loves, such as you know how to paint, shooting with roses at the points of their arrows—”

“Say no more!” Piero broke in, the words snapping like a twig underfoot. “I have Ovid in the vulgar tongue. Find me the passage. I love not to be choked with other men’s thoughts. You may come in.”

He led the way through the first room. The air within struck Tito with the mixed scent of ashes and old wood, faintly sweetened by the stale, sulphury breath of boiled eggs. The basket the child had brought stood on the open hearth near a heap of broken shells, white and jagged as little skulls, and a bank of ashes that had the soft grey of sifted flour. It was as if the painter’s household fed itself on the simplest country fare—eggs boiled in their own skins, eaten with salt, the shells tossed down where they fell—while his mind feasted on monsters and gods.

In strange keeping with this sordid litter, there stood a low bedstead of carved ebony, covered carelessly with a piece of rich oriental carpet, the kind that might have lain on the steps to a Madonna’s throne. There was a carved _cassone_, its sides and lid painted with devices; and little else in that large room but casts, wooden steps, easels, rough boxes—all webbed over with cobwebs like old bread left too long in a pantry.

The next room was larger and more crowded, and its very disorder seemed alive. The double door beneath the high window was thrown open, and beyond it lay a garden—no, rather a thicket—where fig-trees and vines trailed in tangled wildness among nettles and hemlock. A tall cypress lifted its dark head from a stifling mass of yellowish mulberry-leaves. The outdoor dampness seemed to have crept indoors; in one corner, among carved marble fragments and rusty armour, tufts of long grass and dark feathery fennel had thrust their way up, and a large stone vase, tilted on its side, looked as if it were pouring ivy in a frozen stream across the floor.

All about the walls hung pen and oil sketches: fantastic sea-monsters; dances of satyrs and maenads; Saint Margaret rising out of the devouring dragon; Madonnas with supernal light upon them; studies of plants; grotesque heads. On rough, irregular shelves lay a few books scattered among drooping bunches of corn, bullocks’ horns, pieces of dried honeycomb, stones with rare-coloured lichen, skulls and bones, peacocks’ feathers, and great birds’ wings. It was a storeroom of the world’s fragments, as if Piero collected not things but the very textures of existence—hard horn, brittle comb, papery feathers—so that his painted marvels might taste, as it were, of truth.

Rising from the dirty litter of the floor were lay figures: one in the frock of a Vallombrosan monk, grotesquely crowned with a helmet and barred visor; another smothered with brocade and skins tossed over it in haste.

And among all this heterogeneous still life, living creatures moved with homely unconcern: speckled and white pigeons perched and strutted, too tame to fly at the entrance of men; three corpulent toads crawled near the door-stone with the slow intimacy of accustomed tenants; and a white rabbit—apparently the model for one that frightened Cupid in a picture of Mars and Venus set on the central easel—twitched its nose contentedly over a box of bran. Their small sounds—the soft scrape of claws, the pigeons’ low coo, the rabbit’s faint rustle—made a gentle counterpoint to the city outside, as if here, in this ear-stuffed sanctuary, the world’s louder discourse had been negotiated down to the domestic.

“And now, Messer Greco,” said Piero, signing to Tito to sit on a low stool near the door, while he stood over him with folded arms, “don’t be trying to see everything at once, like Messer Domeneddio, but let me know how large you would have this same triptych.”

Tito indicated the dimensions, and Piero marked them on a piece of paper.

“And now for the book,” Piero said, reaching down a manuscript volume.

Tito found the passage and gave it him. “There’s nothing about Ariadne there,” he said; “but you will remember I want the crowned Ariadne by the side of the young Bacchus: she must have golden hair.”

“Ha!” Piero pursed his lips abruptly, as if tasting a new spice. Then, looking down into Tito’s face, he added, “And you want them to be likenesses, eh?”

Tito laughed, and a blush rose—quick, like wine rising in a glass when it is poured too fast. “I know you are great at portraits, Messer Piero; but I could not ask Ariadne to sit for you, because the painting is a secret.”

“There it is!” said Piero. “I want her to sit to me. Giovanni Vespucci wants me to paint him a picture of Oedipus and Antigone at Colonos, as he has expounded it to me: I have a fancy for the subject, and I want Bardo and his daughter to sit for it. Now, you ask them; and then I’ll put the likeness into Ariadne.”

Tito’s mind ran swiftly over the request—over Bardo’s proud, thorny scholarship, and Romola’s grave reluctances; over how certain words, uttered in the right dialect and tone, could open a door that force would only bar more firmly. “Agreed,” he said, “if I can prevail with them. And your price for the Bacchus and Ariadne?”

“_Baie_! If you get them to let me paint them, that will pay me. I’d rather not have your money: you may pay for the case.”

“And when shall I sit for you?” Tito asked lightly; “for if we have one likeness, we must have two.”

“I don’t want your likeness; I’ve got it already,” Piero answered, with the blunt relish of a man who enjoys having surprised another. “Only I’ve made you look frightened. I must take the fright out of it for Bacchus.”

As he spoke, he laid down the book and went to look among some paintings propped face to the wall. He returned with an oil sketch in his hand.

“I call this as good a bit of portrait as I ever did,” he said, gazing at it as he advanced. “Yours is a face that expresses fear well, because it’s naturally a bright one. I noticed it the first time I saw you. The rest of the picture is hardly sketched; but I’ve painted you in thoroughly.”

He turned the sketch and held it toward Tito.

There Tito was—right hand uplifted with a wine-cup, the attitude of triumphant joy; and yet the face was turned away from the cup with eyes dilated and lips pallid in an expression of such intense fear that Tito felt, absurdly, a cold stream slide through his veins, as if his body had taken instruction from the painted terror. For a moment, the room’s quiet creatures and the rustle of the thicket outside seemed to fall away, and only that silent scream of a face remained—fear pinned in colour.

“You are beginning to look like it already,” Piero said, with a short laugh, moving the picture away again. “He’s seeing a ghost—that fine young man. I shall finish it some day, when I’ve settled what sort of ghost is the most terrible—whether it should look solid, like a dead man come to life, or half transparent, like a mist.”

Tito was ashamed of his sudden sensitiveness—it ran strangely counter to his habitual command of himself. He forced a careless tone, as if swallowing down an unexpected bitterness.

“That is a subject after your own heart, Messer Piero—a revel interrupted by a ghost. You seem to love the blending of the terrible with the gay. I suppose that is the reason your shelves are so well furnished with death’s-heads, while you are painting those roguish Loves who are running away with the armour of Mars. I begin to think you are a Cynic philosopher in the pleasant disguise of a cunning painter.”

“Not I, Messer Greco,” Piero said promptly. “A philosopher is the last sort of animal I should choose to resemble. I find it enough to live, without spinning lies to account for life. Fowls cackle, asses bray, women chatter, and philosophers spin false reasons—that’s the effect the sight of the world brings out of them. Well, I am an animal that paints instead of cackling, or braying, or spinning lies.”

His words fell with a kind of rough honesty, like coarse salt thrown on the tongue—no elegance, but a sting of flavour that stayed.

“And now,” he added, “I think, our business is done; you’ll keep to your side of the bargain about the Oedipus and Antigone?”

“I will do my best,” Tito said, taking the strong hint and rising at once.

“And you’ll let me know at Nello’s. No need to come here again.”

“I understand,” Tito answered, laughingly, and lifted his hand in a friendly sign of parting.

When he passed back into the court, the city’s louder pulse met him—voices bouncing from stone, the clang of a shutter, the call of a hawker. For an instant, those sounds felt like a coarse net flung over finer things. Yet Tito, whose nature was to take each atmosphere as it served him, let the noise bear him along. In his mind the little triptych began already to exist: grapes swelling in painted clusters, ivy curling round a mast, Ariadne’s gold hair catching a light that was not the sun’s—and behind that, like a stain on the palate that sweet wine cannot wash away, the image of himself with the lifted cup and frightened eyes, as if some uninvited ghost had found a way, not into Piero’s picture only, but into the very feast Tito meant to set before Romola.
