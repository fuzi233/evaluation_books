"Early morning," I noted, scanning the horizon where the azure dome of the sky merged seamlessly with the emerald expanse of the plain. Our column cut a bold silhouette against the light, a disciplined force moving with purpose. The strategy was simple yet demanding: traverse the daylight, halt for the afternoon, resume under the cover of darkness, and repeat until we breached the city gates before dawn. We aimed to infiltrate the sleeping capital undetected, turning the element of surprise into our primary weapon.

The migration seemed absolute. Flocks of birds surged ahead, mistaking our advance for the vanguard of a new era, while clouds of butterflies and insects danced in the periphery. Behind us trailed a host of quadrupeds—bears, deer, and others—that abandoned us the moment the sun dipped below the tree line, save for the avian and insectile allies who remained loyal to the end.

We established camp mid-afternoon. It was our first sustained march, yet exhaustion was absent. The cold of the approaching night served as a natural stimulant; many collapsed onto the backs of their mounts, only to rise refreshed at first light. No one tumbled. Riders atop shaggy, lumbering bears maintained a pace that rivaled the elephants, while those mounted on deer were restrained from breaking into a gallop. Above the elephants, riders lost to the height of their platforms engaged in constant chatter with their mounts. One youth, drawing fodder from a bundle, spoke earnestly to his beast: "Nosy dear, I'm digging you out of the mountain. Be patient; I'm coming. Soon you'll raise your trunk to greet me, and we'll kiss like proper elephants!"

His words triggered a cacophony of trumpets, neighs, and childish mimicry that echoed across the silent plains. Uncertain of the city's proximity, I silenced the noise instantly. Silence was our armor.

Then, the sun and the city ascended simultaneously. To the children, the fortifications were merely a monolithic wall of rock. When I explained that within lay a labyrinth of stone nests, apprehension flashed in their young eyes—a primal fear they had never known. Yet, they pressed on, bolstered by faith in Lona and in me, however unworthy I felt of such trust.

Passage through the archway was deafening. Hooves pounded, paws padded, creating a rhythmic assault on the ancient stones. Horses recoiled at the resonance of their own impact, plunging and wheeling before settling. Fear gripped the Little Ones; the bears and butterflies除外，but the humans froze. Lona's face bore a dark, heavy anxiety, mirrored in my own chest. The entire army rested on my command and my conscience; I had led them straight into the shadow of danger. Yet, I drew strength from the vision of the future kingdom—the Little Ones ruling, the giants enslaved, the animals subservient. Alas, I who dreamed of liberation remained a slave to my own stubbornness, a tyrant of innocence. Beside me, Lona sat calm, silent, watchful, utterly fearless.

We entered the city's heart before the populace registered our arrival. Then, windows shattered the gloom as heads popped out. Faces initially displayed a blank, stupefied wonder, which curdled instantly into consternation upon spotting the beasts. Despite their terror, women rushed into the streets, followed by men, though all maintained a safe distance from the animals.

A boy, roughly five years old, clinging to his mother's memory, spotted a woman in the throng. From his antelope mount, he launched himself at her, wrapping his limbs around her neck. She embraced him warmly, but a man's hand seized the boy by the throat. Instinctively, a girl drove a sharp spear into the attacker's arm. He screamed savagely, only to be speared again by two companions before fleeing in hysterical retreat.

"They are just bad giants!" Lona shouted, her eyes blazing as she spurred her horse against a towering giant blocking her path. The man, startled by the charge, slunk aside and was immediately struck down by flying stones. Another brute attempted to intervene between me and the boy riding behind; the boy ordered him to address the king. The giant responded by smashing his hammer into the boy's small steed, sending it crashing. Before the giant could deliver a fatal blow, an elephant from our rear crushed him flat, trampling him repeatedly until the army had passed.

Yet, seeing the women, Lona's expression darkened further. Could her charges find refuge among such figures?

We halted in the central square when messengers arrived reporting that two boys had been snatched by local women. Turning immediately, we discovered the fate of the woman we had befriended: she had vanished with her infant. Simultaneously, a white leopardess bounded down a narrow lane toward us. Memories of the forest battle flickered in the minds of the Little Ones; some faltered, but the order held firm.

Odu, a boy renowned for his speed and courage, remembered my praise of the white leopardess. Leaping from his bear, he sprinted to intercept the predator. The leopardess, fearing to crush him, rolled violently to her feet, only to find the child securely astride her back. The sight of an enemy's urchin riding a creature of terror solidified the subjugation of the populace. We pressed onward.

At the residence designated by our guides, a scream pierced the air. I dismounted and hammered on the door. My horse shoved me aside, then began battering the entrance with his heels. Just then, Odu arrived on the leopardess, trembling at her sight. She, too, hearing the cry, forgot the child on her back and threw herself at the door, slamming the boy against it and rendering him unconscious. Before I could reach him, Lona caught the boy. Upon regaining consciousness, he was placed back on his bear.

When the leopardess assaulted the door a third time, it splintered inward. She darted inside, vanishing into the depths. We followed, searching every room, finding no trace. Descending a hidden passage beneath the stairs, we entered a labyrinth of excavations. Shortly thereafter, we encountered the leopardess carrying the missing child. He revealed that the woman, intending to sacrifice him to the leopardess, had thrown him into a pit—but the beast, proving benevolent, had rescued him.

Seeking the second boy, we moved to the adjacent house. Too late. A savage had just slain the captive. For Lona, this was a mercy; she had feared the boy would mature into a corrupt giant. Death spared him that fate. The leopardess springed upon the killer, seizing his throat and dragging him into the street, following Lona like a cat clutching a rat.

"Leave this horror," Lona commanded. "There are no mothers here. This people is not worth saving."

The leopardess dropped her burden and charged into the densest parts of the crowd. Slaves shrieked and scattered, tripping over one another in chaotic heaps. Returning to the main army, we found them standing in perfect formation, ready for action. But unease settled heavily upon me; Lona showed no sign of intent, and her plans remained opaque. Vigilance was mandatory throughout the night.

The Little Ones possessed a resilience unknown to civilized folk; they could sleep anywhere. We instructed them to lie down with their animals where they stood. Instantly, they descended into a slumber as peaceful as water flowing over grass or wind rustling leaves. Their mounts slept lightly, perpetually on the verge of awakening. Older children patrolled softly among the dreamers. Absolute stillness reigned; the wicked city appeared finally at rest.
