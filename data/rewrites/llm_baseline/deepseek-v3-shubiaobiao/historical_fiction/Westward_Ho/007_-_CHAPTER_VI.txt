*"Far, far from hence,
The Adriatic breaks in a warm bay
Among the green Illyrian hills, and there
The sunshine in the happy glens is fair..."*
—Matthew Arnold

Such were the hidden glens that scored the high moors between Devon and Cornwall, each cleft like a wound in the earth, opening toward the endless Western Ocean. No other place in England held such wild beauty—each valley a twin to the next, yet each utterly its own.

Inland, the steep slopes were draped in oak and furze, dark and rich. Nearer the sea, the cliffs rose black and jagged, their teeth gnashing against the waves. Between them lay slender meadows, threaded by trout-filled streams, gray mills humming beside them, and pools where salmon gathered after autumn rains. The coves were strewn with sand and polished pebbles, but their jaws were cruel—fangs of rock waiting to grind ships to splinters.

One such cove, Marsland, was guarded by a natural seawall, making it the only safe landing for miles. And here, beneath the October moon, two figures stood upon the shingle: Rose Salterne and the White Witch, Lucy Passmore.

Rose had come despite her fear, lured by the thrill of the unknown. The witch had promised her a vision—one that would reveal her future love. Now, as midnight neared, the girl shivered in the salt-laced wind.

"Safe enough here tonight," Lucy muttered. "None comes but the mermaids." She scowled at the boat left too near the tide. "That lazy old villain—leaving it to be stolen!"

Rose clutched the mirror the witch had given her, her pulse quickening. The cliffs loomed above, their shadows alive with unseen things. A snipe’s cry echoed like a lost child’s wail.

"Go now," Lucy urged. "Dip thrice and speak the words. But look *only* in the glass after."

Rose stepped into the shallows. The cold seized her, but worse was the sudden blaze of light—phosphorescent creatures swirling around her limbs, turning the water to liquid fire. Seaweed swayed like beckoning arms, urging her deeper.

She fled back to shore, dripping, and raised the mirror.

Before she could finish, hoofbeats pounded the beach. Four men—two on foot, two dismounting—rushed toward the boat. Lucy sprang up like an avenging spirit.

"Thieves! Rogues! Leave my boat be!"

A Welsh voice protested, but Lucy swung an oar with startling force, cracking it against one man’s shins.

"Ten nobles or I’ll keep ye till dawn!" she demanded.

The money was paid.

As the boat scraped into the waves, another rider galloped down—Eustace Leigh, bloodied, his face twisted in fury.

"Amyas—he took the letter!" he gasped. "Devil take him—*I* dare not curse him! He saved me!"

The men hauled him aboard just as William Cary’s horse thundered into view.

Lucy, watching from the rocks, crowed as Cary drove the fleeing servant into the river.

Rose, hidden in a crevice, had seen Eustace’s glare—those wild eyes passing over her hiding place. Had he *seen* her?

The witch clambered down. "Home now, dearie. You’re shaking like a leaf."

But Rose could not forget that face—haunting, desperate—nor shake the dread that his curse had found her.

By morning, fever held her. For weeks, the phantom lingered in her dreams. And by the time she returned to Bideford, Amyas was already gone—sailing toward battles far away.
