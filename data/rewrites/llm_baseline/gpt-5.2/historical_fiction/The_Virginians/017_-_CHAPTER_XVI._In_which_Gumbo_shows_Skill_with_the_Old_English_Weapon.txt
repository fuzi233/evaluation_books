Harry Warrington, having won money from his cousin Will and from Mr. Sampson the chaplain, held himself—by a queer gentleman’s logic—obliged to offer them the means of winning it back. If Madam Esmond could have heard how that duty was discharged, she would have called it anything but duty. Cards, in that house, were as regular as the bell.

After the early dinner—served at three, with the lids lifted from dishes as solemnly as if they were opening a ceremony—there would be whist or quadrille with the ladies. The table smelled of beeswax and warmed baize; the candles sweated; the air carried the mingled savour of roast, extinguished by tea, and then revived by wine. The women’s laughter and the dry click of counters went on till supper. And then, when the family withdrew, and the long passages settled into their night-sounds—the last footfall of a maid, a door softly complaining on its hinge, a far-off clink from the pantry—Mr. Will would come stealing across to Harry’s quarters with a dice-box and a bottle of rum, as if he were bringing contraband spices.

There, with Hal and the chaplain, he would sit till dawn, the dice rattling like hail on a shutter, the glasses set down with little thuds upon the wood, and the talk rising and falling with that nocturnal boldness which men mistake for wit. The ladies had extracted from Cousin Will a thousand promises—he would not lead the Virginian into mischief, he would keep out of it himself. Will gave his word with the same ease that he shuffled a pack: light, quick, and meant to be handled again.

Harry had not meant to mislead Lord Castlewood when he spoke of his mother’s estate in America. He was honest by nature; but honesty, in a young man, does not always include a clear measure of what others will hear. At home, he had grown up among a rough plenty—horse-jockeys, tavern loungers, sporting men, card-players—such company as a tobacco colony breeds when its gentlemen have land, slaves to work it, and long evenings to fill. Their amusements were the hunt, the punch-bowl, the table where money slid from one hand to another like a dish passed at supper. Hospitality was without fence or lock: a neighbour’s house might as well have been your own, and the same venison, corn, and drink stood ready behind cracked windows and tumble-down rails. Harry had slept on straw mattresses that smelled of summer hay and old sweat; he had watched the sunrise come in through broken shutters while the last bowl of claret-punch sat emptied and sticky, the nutmeg grating still perfuming the air, and outside the hounds already giving tongue, impatient for daylight.

His brother George, he owned with a kind of shame, had been made of soberer stuff—books and tea rather than a gallop and Burgundy. Nature mixes her families so. Harry’s tastes showed themselves quickly in Hampshire. His English cousins did not love Puritan discipline; and if Harry was no milksop, they liked him the better. Manners were looser then; tongues freer; things were named and done that later generations would not bear to hear. The house, in its own way, was as indulgent as any Virginian hall, only the indulgence wore cleaner linen.

And yet Harry, keen-eyed under his good humour, suspected that his cousins meant to draw him out—pump him, as he phrased it—about his affairs. So he kept his counsel. In sport and chance he was no novice. At cards he met them with an easy hand; at backgammon and piquet he learned their English turns without losing his Virginian quickness. Even at billiards—“the noble game,” my lord called it—he held his own after a few days. His grandfather in Virginia had owned one of the few tables in the province; the green cloth, the balls, the chalk, were not foreign mysteries to Harry. Mr. Will, who beat him at first, found he could not keep him under long; and Will confessed, with a grudging respect, that the American was his match in most things and his better in many.

There was one contest, however, in which Harry often came off second—especially with one of the ladies. He was profoundly courteous to any creature in petticoats; that tradition has not yet left his country. Every woman in the Castlewood establishment softened towards him. The grim housekeeper unbent; the fat cook beamed, her hands always smelling of onion and thyme; the waiting-maids, French and English, smirked and giggled when he passed; even the porter’s pretty daughter at the lodge had always a shy, kind word ready. Madame de Bernstein said little, but watched much, and in her silence there was the sharpness of a knife laid aside, ready to be taken up again.

As for Lady Maria Esmond—who could say her age? In those days, peerage-books were fewer, and truth about women’s years was a thing handled like hot sugar. Will said she was five-and-thirty; he was abusive, and liked exaggeration. Maria was Will’s half-sister; she and my lord were children of the late Earl’s first wife, a German lady married in Queen Anne’s wars. Baron Bernstein—who married Maria’s Aunt Beatrix, Bishop Tusher’s widow—was German too, a Hanoverian nobleman and kin to the first Lady Castlewood. If Lady Maria had been born under George the First, and if George the Second had sat thirty years upon the throne, how could she be seven-and-twenty, as she told Harry? Yet she would say to him, when they were alone, in that voice that made every commonplace sound like confidence:

“I am old, child. I am a hundred years old. I am seven-and-twenty. I might be your mother almost.”

And Harry, with a boy’s heat and a man’s awkward daring, would answer, “Your ladyship might be the mother of all the Cupids, I am sure. You don’t look twenty, upon my word you do not!”

Lady Maria looked any age one wished—an easy, dazzling beauty with white-and-red complexion, fair hair in abundance, and round white arms shown to uncommon advantage when she played at billiards with Cousin Harry. When she leaned across the table for a stroke, the green cloth rising like a little field under her, Harry would catch sight of an ankle, a clocked stocking, a black satin slipper with a red heel—details small as spices, yet burning on the tongue of his mind. It filled him with a rapture he could not name without making himself ridiculous. And yet—foolish Harry—his own mother’s foot was slighter, shorter, finer. But boys do not look at their mothers’ slippers with rapture; they look where their blood tells them to look.

Lady Maria could be very kind when they were alone; and before her sister, aunt, stepmother, she made light of him—called him a chit, a simpleton, mimicked his Virginian accent with cruel exactness. Harry would blush and try to mend his intonation, coached by his English monitresses. “We shall make a pretty fellow of you,” his aunt said, as if she were seasoning a dish and knew the proper pinch.

Lord Castlewood, since that Sunday talk between them, became more familiar with Harry. Until the crops were off, there was little sport—an occasional cock-match at Winchester, a bull-baiting at Hexton Fair. Harry and Will rode out to fairs and races; Harry was shown, like a new coin passed from hand to hand, among county families—the Henleys of the Grange, the Crawleys of Queen’s Crawley, the Redmaynes of Lionsden. The neighbours came in their heavy coaches and stayed two or three days, eating, drinking, playing cards, and filling the house with the sound of wheels in the court, servants running, doors opening and shutting, and the constant clatter of plates. More would have come, but for the family’s fear of Madame de Bernstein. She disliked country company; their talk annoyed her; their laughter seemed to her too loud, their compliments too slow.

“We shall be merrier when my aunt leaves us,” the young folks whispered. “We have cause to be civil. You know what a favourite she was with our papa. She got him his earldom—she was very well at Court then, with the King and Queen. She commands here naturally—perhaps too much. We are all afraid of her. Even my elder brother stands in awe of her; and my stepmother is far more obedient to her than ever she was to my papa, whom she ruled with a rod of iron. But Castlewood is merrier when our aunt is not here. At least we have more company. You will come in our gay days, Harry—won’t you? Of course you will. This is your home, sir. I was so pleased—oh, so pleased—when my brother said he considered it your home!”

A soft hand accompanied the speech; blue eyes, well preserved, looked extremely friendly. Harry grasped her hand with ardour. He did not know what privilege cousinship might claim, only he was timid, and timidity made him honest in a way boldness never is. He had thought the English cold and selfish. How mistaken! How kind they were—especially the Earl, and dear, dear Maria. He longed to recall the first letter he had sent to his mother and Mrs. Mountain, in which he had hinted that his welcome was cool. My lord promised to introduce him in London, present him at Court, take him to White’s. Castlewood was to be his English home. Harry wrote all this with contrition in a second despatch to Virginia, adding—since his spelling was not yet the most exact of accomplishments—that “My cousin, the Lady Maria, is a perfect Angle.”

“Ille praeter omnes angulus ridet,” muttered little Mr. Dempster, at home in Virginia, wincing at the word as if it were a bone in the throat.

“The child can’t be falling in love with his angle!” cried Mrs. Mountain.

“Pooh, pooh! my niece Maria is forty!” said Madam Esmond. “I perfectly well recollect her—a great, gawky, carroty creature, with a foot like a pair of bellows.”

Where truth lies in such matters, who knows? Beauty is half the eye that beholds it, and half the tale that the heart repeats until it believes. The same face can be a paragon or a caricature depending on who tastes it—like wine that is nectar to one and vinegar to another.

Not only was Harry a favourite in the drawing-room and among the ladies of the servants’ hall; like master, like man, his valet Gumbo found himself admired in every corner. Gumbo had a hundred accomplishments. He could fish, hunt, shoe a horse, handle iron like a blacksmith. He dressed hair beautifully and improved himself under my lord’s Swiss gentleman, learning the little refinements of powder and ribbon as if they were sauces and garnishes. He was a cook of note in his own fashion—famous for Virginian dishes, and eager to steal new secrets from my lord’s French man. One day he would be roasting and basting with a patience that would shame a priest, the fat hissing and sending up its rich animal scent; another he would be stirring a pot with herbs bruised between his fingers, lifting the lid and sniffing as if the steam were a message. He sang exquisitely at church, and sang secular songs too—often inventing airs and rude words in the manner of his people. He played the fiddle so charmingly that he set the girls dancing in Castlewood Hall, and at the Three Castles in the village he was always welcome to a mug of ale—gratis—if he would but bring the fiddle. He loved to play for the village children, and the sound of his bow in the twilight—bright as a bird-call—made even the yard-dogs cease their barking. In a countryside where most music was measured by church bells and the groan of cartwheels, Gumbo’s fiddle seemed a new weather. It softened the house’s usual noises: the clank of kitchen-pots, the slap of wet linen, the scold of a cook; it made the place, for an hour, feel less like a machine of rank and more like a hearth.

The servants, who were no fools, soon perceived that Gumbo was a liar. The day at church when he pretended to read from Molly’s psalm-book, he sang quite other words than those printed there—he could not decipher a syllable. When he pretended to understand written music, the Swiss valet brought him some; Gumbo turned the page upside down. Such long-bow practice occurred daily and was patent to all; they did not think much the worse of him, because they liked him. But they did not know how great a liar he was—how vast a map of invention he carried in his head—and because they liked to believe, they believed.

Whatever wonder and envy he may have felt at first beholding Castlewood’s English comforts, Gumbo kept his sentiments to himself and examined park, stables, appointments coolly, as if he were judging a market. The horses were very well, he said—what there were of them—but at Castlewood in Virginia they had six times as many, and fourteen—eighteen—grooms to tend them. Madam Esmond’s carriages were much finer than my lord’s; a great deal more gold on the panels. Her gardens covered acres; they grew every kind of flower and fruit under the sun. Pineapples and peaches? So common they were given to pigs. They had twenty—forty—gardeners, not white gardeners, all black gentlemen like himself. In the house were twenty—forty—gentlemen in livery, besides women-servants; fifty women-servants, he thought, all Madam Esmond’s property, worth ever so many hundred pieces of eight apiece. A piece of eight was bigger than a guinea. Madam Esmond had twenty—thirty—thousand guineas a year, rooms full of gold and plate. She came to England in one of her ships; she had ever so many ships, Gumbo could not count them. Estates reached a week’s journey, all tobacco and negroes. Was Master Harry heir to all this? Of course—now Master George was killed and scalped by the Indians. Gumbo had killed ever so many Indians, tried to save Master George, but he was Master Harry’s boy—and Master Harry was as rich as ever he liked. He wore black because Master George was dead; but in Bristol were chests full of gold clothes and lace and jewels. Master Harry was the richest man in all Virginia and might have twenty—sixty—servants; only he liked traveling with one best, and that one was Gumbo.

This tale was not invented at once. It was coaxed out in draughts, like a stew that thickens the longer it simmers. Gumbo contradicted himself a little at first, but after he had told it twice or thrice in the servants’ hall or in the butler’s private room, he knew his story as a cook knows the order of courses. He could name precisely the number of Madam Esmond’s slaves and the amount of her income. There was, after all, a seed of truth: American establishments did swarm with servants, and a Virginian house could hold more negroes than an English one held men and maids.

Gumbo’s account travelled upward, borne like kitchen-smell up a staircase: from servants to valets, from waiting-women to their ladies. It reached my lord and Madame de Bernstein, and, in the retelling, lost nothing—except perhaps modesty. A young gentleman in England is not less liked for being thought heir to vast wealth. When Lady Castlewood heard of Harry’s prodigious expectations, she repented of her first coolness—repented too of having pinched her daughter’s arm black-and-blue for stretching it out too kindly towards the Virginian. Was it too late to coax him back into those fair arms? Lady Fanny was welcome to try; she resumed her dancing lessons, and the Countess would play the music with all her heart. But—how provoking—Lady Maria would always insist on being in the room; and as sure as Fanny walked in the garden or park, so sure would Maria come trailing after her, as if the elder sister’s shadow had learned to move of its own will.

Madame de Bernstein laughed at the rumours of the prodigious fortune. She knew her half-sister’s man of business in London and likely the real state of Madam Esmond’s money; but she did not contradict Gumbo’s splendid inventions. She was diverted by the way they altered the Castlewood family’s behaviour, as if a new dialect had been introduced into the house: the language of deference, sweetened and enlarged when money was supposed to be in the room.

“Hang him! Is he so rich, Molly?” said my lord to his elder sister. “Then good-bye to our chances with your aunt. The Baroness will be sure to leave him all her money to spite us—and because he doesn’t want it. Nevertheless, the lad is a good lad enough; and it’s not his fault being rich.”

“He is very simple and modest in his habits for one so wealthy,” Maria remarked.

“Rich people often are,” my lord said. “If I were rich I often think I should be the greatest miser, live in rags and on a crust. There is no pleasure so enduring as money-getting. It grows on you, and increases with old age. But because I am as poor as Lazarus, I dress in purple and fine linen, and fare sumptuously every day.”

Maria went to the book-room and fetched the History of Virginia, by R. B. Gent, and read of its admirable climate; how fruit and corn grew there; how noble rivers—the Potomac, the Rappahannoc—abounded in fish. She wondered whether the climate would agree with her; whether Harry’s mother would like her. Harry declared his mother would adore her—so would Mountain. When asked of the number of servants at home, he said they certainly had more than in England—he did not know how many; the negroes did not do near as much work as English servants, hence the need of keeping so many. When some of Gumbo’s details were brought to him, he laughed and said the boy was wonderful as a romancer and, in telling such stories, was only trying to speak out for the honour of the family.

His denials, being modest, only served to confirm the others in their belief. More and more Lady Castlewood and the ladies grew affectionate. More and more Mr. Will betted with him and offered him bargains. Harry’s simple dress and equipage—one servant, no horses or carriage of his own—seemed to them not poverty but an almost heroic restraint. When he went to London, of course he would cut a better figure; Castlewood would introduce him at St. James’s; he would appear as he ought. Mr. Sampson, the chaplain, became more lively, wicked, obsequious than ever—so eager to show Harry “a little of London life,” to warn rogues off him and keep him out of harm. Everyone was kind. Harry liked the respect paid to him; as Madam Esmond’s son, he thought it perhaps his due, and, never having seen any place but his own province, took for granted he was the personage his family imagined. So from Castlewood House to Castlewood village, and thence through the county, it became a settled fancy that Mr. Harry Esmond Warrington was heir to immense wealth and a gentleman of great importance—because his negro valet told lies about him in the servants’ hall.

After a week or two, Harry’s aunt, Madame de Bernstein, began to tire of Castlewood, its inhabitants, and the neighbours who visited. She tired of most things sooner or later. She nodded and slept over the chaplain’s stories; dozed at her whist and over her dinner. She grew snappish, sarcastic, hitting out at my lord, at Will the jockey, at the ladies widowed and unmarried, who winced and bore it. The cook, once praised, now gave no satisfaction; the wine was corked; the house damp and draughty; doors would not shut; chimneys smoked. She declared the Tunbridge waters necessary, and ordered the doctor from Hexton to order them for her benefit.

“I wish to heaven she would go!” growled my lord, the most independent of the family. “She may go to Tunbridge, or Bath, or Jericho for me.”

“Shall Fanny and I come with you to Tunbridge, dear Baroness?” asked Lady Castlewood.

“Not for worlds, my dear! The doctor orders me absolute quiet; if you came I should have the knocker going all day, and Fanny’s lovers would never be out of the house,” replied the Baroness, weary of her sister-in-law’s company.

“I wish I could be of any service to my aunt,” said sentimental Lady Maria, demurely.

“My good child, what can you do? You cannot play piquet so well as my maid; and I have heard all your songs till I am perfectly tired of them! One of the gentlemen might go with me—at least make the journey, and see me safe from highwaymen.”

“I’m sure, ma’am, I shall be glad to ride with you,” said Mr. Will quickly.

“Oh, not you! I don’t want you, William,” cried his aunt. “Why do you not offer? Where are your American manners, you ungracious Harry Warrington? Don’t swear, Will. Harry is much better company than you are, and much better ton too, sir.”

“Ton, indeed! Confound his ton,” Will muttered to himself, sour as stale beer.

“I dare say I shall be tired of him, as I am of other folks,” Madame de Bernstein went on. “I have scarcely seen Harry at all these last days. You shall ride with me to Tunbridge, Harry!”

At the direct appeal—more to no one’s wonder than to the Baroness’s—Harry blushed, hemmed, hawed, and at last said, “I have promised my cousin Castlewood to go over to Hexton Petty Sessions with him to-morrow. He thinks I should see how the courts here are conducted—and—and—the partridge-shooting will soon begin, and I have promised to be here for that, ma’am.”

As he spoke, he grew red as a poppy. Lady Maria kept her meek face down and plied her needle with nimble diligence, the small sound of the thread drawn through cloth oddly loud in the sudden hush.

“You actually refuse to go with me to Tunbridge Wells?” cried Madame de Bernstein, her eyes flashing and her face flushing with anger.

“Not to ride with you, ma’am; that I will do with all my heart,” Harry said, striving to be respectful and firm. “But to stay there—I have promised—”
