The shortest route from my lady’s sitting-room to the garden ran along the shrubbery path—a place I need not introduce, as you’ve already seen it. For your better understanding of what follows, I’ll add that this path was Mr. Franklin Blake’s favourite walk. Whenever he was abroad in the grounds and we couldn’t find him elsewhere, he’d usually be discovered here.

I confess I am a stubborn old man. The more Sergeant Cuff kept his thoughts locked away from me, the harder I pressed to pry into them. As we turned onto the shrubbery path, I tried another tactic. “If I were in your shoes,” I said, “I’d be at my wits’ end.”

“If you were in my place,” he replied, “you’d have formed an opinion—and now, any doubt you might have had would be settled once and for all. Don’t worry about what those conclusions are just yet, Mr. Betteredge. I didn’t bring you out here to corner me like a badger. I brought you out to ask questions. You could have answered them indoors, of course—but doors and eavesdroppers tend to gather together. In my line of work, we prefer fresh air.”

Who could outwit *this* man? I gave up—though I waited as patiently as I could to hear what came next.

“We won’t speculate on your young lady’s motives,” he continued. “But it’s a pity she refuses to assist me. Her refusal makes the inquiry harder than it need be. So we must solve the mystery of the smear on the door—which, mark my words, is the same as solving the mystery of the Diamond—in some other way. I’ve decided to speak with the servants, to probe their minds and actions rather than their clothes. But before I begin, I want to ask you a few things. You’re observant—did you notice anything unusual among the servants after the Diamond was lost? Any quarrels? Anyone acting strangely? Out of sorts? Unusually irritable? Or suddenly ill?”

I barely had time to recall Rosanna Spearman’s sudden illness at dinner the day before—when Sergeant Cuff’s eyes darted toward the shrubbery and I heard him mutter under his breath, “Hullo!”

“What’s wrong?” I asked.

“A touch of rheumatism in my back,” he said loudly, as if addressing someone beyond us. “We’ll have a change in the weather soon.”

A few steps further brought us to the corner of the house. We turned sharply right, stepped down the central terrace stairs, and entered the garden below. The Sergeant stopped in the open space where we could see all around.

“About that young woman, Rosanna Spearman,” he said. “She doesn’t look like the kind who’d have a lover. But for her own sake, I must ask: has she taken one on, poor girl, like the rest?”

What on earth did he mean by asking such a thing now? I stared, unable to answer.

“I saw Rosanna hiding in the shrubbery as we passed,” he said.

“When you said ‘Hullo’?”

“Yes—just then. If there’s a sweetheart involved, her hiding isn’t much to worry about. But if there isn’t—given the state of things here—it’s highly suspicious. And I’ll have to act accordingly.”

What could I possibly say? I knew the shrubbery was Mr. Franklin’s usual walk. I knew he’d likely return that way from the station. I knew Penelope had repeatedly caught Rosanna lingering there, always insisting she was trying to attract Mr. Franklin’s attention. If Penelope was right, Rosanna might have been waiting for him when the Sergeant noticed her.

I was caught between two dangers: either revealing Penelope’s theory as my own, or letting the girl suffer the consequences of being suspected by Sergeant Cuff. Out of pure compassion—for God’s sake, out of pure compassion—I told him the truth: that Rosanna had fallen deeply in love with Mr. Franklin Blake.

Sergeant Cuff didn’t laugh. When amused, he only curled the corners of his lips slightly. He did so now.

“Perhaps you should say she’s mad enough to be both ugly and a servant?” he remarked. “Falling for a gentleman like Mr. Franklin Blake doesn’t strike me as the most foolish part of her behaviour. Still, I’m glad it’s clear. It’s a relief to have things sorted. Yes, I’ll keep it secret, Mr. Betteredge. I like to be tender toward human weakness—though I don’t often get the chance in my profession. Do you think Mr. Franklin suspects her feelings? Ah—had she been good-looking, he’d have noticed quickly enough. Ugly women have a rough time in this world. Let’s hope they’re compensated in the next.”

He paused, then added, “You’ve got a fine garden here. See how much healthier the flowers look with grass instead of gravel. No, thank you—I won’t take a rose. It breaks my heart to pull them from the stem. Just as it breaks yours when something goes wrong in the servants’ hall. Did you notice anything odd among the staff when the Diamond went missing?”

So far, I’d managed to keep pace with the Sergeant. But the way he slipped in that last question made me wary. Plainly, I didn’t relish helping him investigate the servants—especially when he was sneaking around like a serpent in the grass.

“I noticed nothing,” I said, “except that we all lost our heads—myself included.”

“Oh,” he said. “That’s all you have to tell me, is it?”

I answered, with what I thought was a steady face, “That is all.”

His dark eyes fixed on mine.

“Mr. Betteredge,” he said, “do you object to shaking hands with me? I’ve taken a peculiar liking to you.”

(Why he chose this moment—when I was lying—to show me such favour, I’ll never understand. Yet I felt a strange pride. I truly did feel proud of having bested the famed Cuff.)

We returned to the house. The Sergeant asked me to give him a room to himself and then summon the indoor servants—one by one, in order of rank.

I led him to my own room and gathered the servants in the hall. Rosanna appeared among them, looking as usual. She was sharp, like the Sergeant, and I suspect she’d overheard his earlier remarks. Still, she stood there as if she’d never heard of the shrubbery.

I sent them in, one at a time.

The cook entered first. She stayed briefly. On leaving, she whispered: “Sergeant Cuff seems downcast—but he’s a gentleman through and through.”

Next came my lady’s maid. She remained longer. Her report: “If Sergeant Cuff doesn’t believe a respectable woman, he might at least keep his opinion to himself!”

Penelope followed. She stayed only a moment. Her verdict: “Sergeant Cuff deserves sympathy. He must have been jilted in love, father, when he was young.”

Then the first housemaid. She stayed as long as the lady’s maid. Her complaint: “I joined her ladyship’s service to serve—not to be doubted to my face by a low police officer!”

Rosanna went next. She stayed longer than anyone. No word when she left—only silence and lips as white as ash.

Samuel, the footman, followed. He lingered a minute. His comment: “Whoever polishes Sergeant Cuff’s boots ought to be ashamed.”

Nancy, the kitchen-maid, was last. She stayed a short while. Her remark: “Sergeant Cuff has a heart. He doesn’t joke with poor working girls like others do.”

Afterward, I went to the room to see if he needed anything. There he sat, gazing out the window, whistling “The Last Rose of Summer.”

“Any discoveries, sir?” I asked.

“If Rosanna asks to go out,” he said, “let her go—but let me know first.”

I might as well have stayed silent about Rosanna and Mr. Franklin. It was clear—the girl was now under suspicion, despite everything I’d done.

“I hope you don’t think Rosanna is involved in the theft?” I ventured.

His lips curled again. He looked me straight in the eye.

“I think I’d better not tell you, Mr. Betteredge,” he said. “You might lose your head—again.”

I began to wonder if I’d truly outwitted him after all.

Our conversation was interrupted by a knock. A message from the cook: Rosanna had requested to go out—her head was bad, she needed fresh air.

At the Sergeant’s nod, I said yes.

“Which is the servants’ exit?” he asked.

I showed him. Then he said, “Lock your door. If anyone asks for me, tell them I’m composing my mind.”

He smiled faintly and vanished.

Left alone, curiosity drove me to investigate. It was obvious the Sergeant had learned something during the interviews. Only two servants—besides Rosanna—had stayed long: the lady’s maid and the first housemaid. Both had been leading the persecution of Rosanna from the start.

I found them in the servants’ hall, tea being served. I joined them—knowing full well that tea loosens tongues as oil feeds a lamp.

In less than half an hour, I knew as much as the Sergeant.

The lady’s maid and housemaid had never believed Rosanna was ill. They’d gone upstairs during the afternoon, tested her door—locked. Knocked—no answer. Listened—no sound. When Rosanna came down for tea and was sent back to bed, they checked again—door still locked. They peered at the keyhole—plugged. They saw light beneath the door at midnight, and heard a fire crackling at four in the morning—unseasonable, even in June.

They’d told the Sergeant. But he hadn’t believed them. Instead, he’d given them cold, suspicious looks. That explained their harsh reports.

And why they were eager to complain about him.

Given my experience with the Sergeant’s methods—and seeing him clearly follow Rosanna when she left—I realized he’d withheld their evidence to avoid inflating their egos. Had he praised them, they might have warned Rosanna.

I walked outside, troubled for the girl. Later, drifting toward the shrubbery, I met Mr. Franklin. He’d just returned from the station and had spoken at length with my lady. She’d told him of Miss Rachel’s refusal to allow her wardrobe searched—putting him in low spirits. The family temper showed plainly on his face.

“Well, Betteredge,” he said, “how does this atmosphere of mystery suit you? Remember that morning I arrived with the Moonstone? I wish to God we’d thrown it into the quicksand.”

He fell silent, then asked what had become of the Sergeant.

I couldn’t lie. I told him everything—including what the two maids had said about Rosanna.

Mr. Franklin’s mind worked swiftly.

“Didn’t you tell me this morning,” he asked, “that a tradesman claimed to have seen Rosanna yesterday on the road to Frizinghall—while we thought she was sick?”

“Yes, sir.”

“If the maids are telling the truth,” he said, “then the tradesman *did* see her. Her illness was a cover. She had reason to go into town secretly. The paint-stained dress is hers. And the fire at four o’clock—lighted to destroy it. Rosanna Spearman stole the Diamond. I’ll go in and tell my aunt.”

“Not yet, if you please,” said a quiet voice behind us.

We turned. Sergeant Cuff stood there.

“Why not?” Mr. Franklin asked.

“Because, sir, if you tell her ladyship, she’ll tell Miss Verinder.”

“Suppose she does. What then?”

Mr. Franklin spoke with sudden heat—as if the Sergeant had insulted him.

“Do you think it’s wise,” the Sergeant replied calmly, “to ask such a question of me—now?”

Silence. Mr. Franklin stepped close. They locked eyes.

He dropped his voice. “I suppose you know, Mr. Cuff,” he said, “that you’re treading on delicate ground?”

“It isn’t the first time,” the Sergeant replied, unmoved. “By hundreds, it’s not.”

“You’re to understand,” Mr. Franklin said, “that I throw up the case if you tell Lady Verinder—or anyone—until I give permission.”

It was settled. Mr. Franklin had no choice but to obey. He turned away, angry, and left.

I stood trembling, unsure whom to trust, what to believe. But two things were clear: First, my young lady was somehow behind the tension between them. Second, they understood each other perfectly—without ever speaking directly.

“Mr. Betteredge,” the Sergeant said, “you’ve done something foolish in my absence. You’ve done detective work on your own. From now on, perhaps you’ll be so kind as to do it *with* me.”

He took my arm and led me back along the path he’d come.

I may have deserved his rebuke—but I wouldn’t help him trap Rosanna Spearman. Thief or not, lawful or not, I pitied her.

“What do you want of me?” I asked, breaking free and stopping.

“Only a little information about the country,” he said.

I couldn’t refuse. “Is there a path from here to the sea-beach?” he asked, pointing to the fir plantation leading to the Shivering Sand.

“Yes,” I said. “There is.”

“Show it to me.”

Side by side, in the twilight of the summer evening, Sergeant Cuff and I set off for the Shivering Sand.
