The fourth night I thought I fell asleep. It was not sleep. It was a kind of long, lightless simmering, like a pot left on dead embers that yet keeps its heat in the clay. And in that same night I woke indeed.

My eyes opened, and though the air lay black as a covered oven, I knew—by scent, by hush, by the very weight of stillness—that I was in the house of death; and I knew, as certainly as one knows the first clean crack of salt on the tongue, that every moment since I lay down there I had been dreaming, and only now was awake.

“At last!” I said inwardly, and my heart sprang like dough answered by yeast.

I turned my eyes; Lona stood by my couch, waiting for me.

I had never lost her—only for a little while lost the sight of her. All my lamentation, all my hunger of grief, had been like a famished man dreaming of famine while the loaf cooled under linen at his elbow.

It was dark, as I say, and yet I saw her: she was not dark. Her eyes held the same steady radiance I had seen in the Mother’s—light not flaring like a lamp fed with oil, but shining as if it were the substance of her being. The brightness issued from her face, and not from her face only: her death-dress, filled now with the light of her body tenfold awake in the power of its resurrection, was white as snow and glittered as if every thread had been washed in morning.

She had fallen asleep a girl; she awoke a woman—ripe, as fruit is ripe, not with show but with inward sweetness, the loveliness of life essential.

I folded her in my arms and knew, with a certainty more bodily than thought, that I lived indeed.

“I woke first!” she said, and the wonder in her smile was like the first taste of water after a long thirst.

“You did, my love—and woke me!”

“I only looked at you and waited,” she answered, as if waiting were a craft she had learned at a hearth, patient and exact.

The candle came floating toward us through the dark, a small obedient star moving without hand or chain. In a few moments Adam and Eve and Mara were with us. They greeted us with a quiet good-morning and a smile: they were used to such wakings, as old housefolk are used to bread rising and children being born.

“I hope you have had a pleasant darkness!” said the Mother.

“Not very,” I answered; “but the waking from it is heavenly.”

“It is but begun,” she returned; “you are hardly yet awake!”

“He is at least clothed-upon with Death, which is the radiant garment of Life,” said Adam.

He embraced Lona his child, put an arm around me, looked a moment or two inquiringly at the princess, and patted the head of the leopardess as one might lay a palm on a great, warm animal that belonged to peace.

“I think we shall meet you two again before long,” he said, looking first at Lona, then at me.

“Have we to die again?” I asked.

“No,” he answered, and his smile was like the Mother’s—quiet as dawn in a valley. “You have died into life, and will die no more; you have only to keep dead. Once dying as we die here, all the dying is over. Now you have only to live, and that you must, with all your blessed might. The more you live, the stronger you become to live.”

“But shall I not grow weary with living so strong?” I said. “What if I cease to live with all my might?”

“It needs but the will, and the strength is there!” said the Mother. “Pure life has no weakness to grow weary withal. The Life keeps generating ours.—Those who will not die, die many times, die constantly, keep dying deeper, never have done dying; here all is upwardness and love and gladness.”

She ceased with a look that said without words, We are mother and son; between us no farewell is possible.

Mara kissed me on the forehead and said, gaily, “I told you, brother, all would be well!—When next you would comfort, say, ‘What will be well, is even now well.’”

She gave a little sigh, the sort that carries its own history the way a remembered song carries a lost dialect—sweet, and half unheard by those who have forgotten the old cadence.

“—You know me now!” she ended, with a smile like her mother’s.

“I know you!” I answered. “You are the voice that cried in the wilderness before ever the Baptist came! you are the shepherd whose wolves hunt the wandering sheep home ere the shadow rise and the night grow dark!”

“My work will one day be over,” she said, “and then I shall be glad with the gladness of the great shepherd who sent me.”

“All the night long the morning is at hand,” said Adam.

A faint agitation moved in the soundless air—an unease like the beginning of a storm far out at sea, heard not by sight but by ear. “What is that flapping of wings I hear?” I asked.

“The Shadow is hovering,” replied Adam: “there is one here whom he counts his own! But ours once, never more can she be his!”

I turned to look on the faces of my father and mother, meaning to kiss them ere we went. Their couches were empty, save for the Little Ones who had, with love’s boldness, appropriated their hospitality and curled themselves where elders had lain. For an instant that awful dream of desolation shadowed me again, and my breath went thin.

“What is it, my heart?” said Lona.

“Their empty places frightened me,” I answered.

“They are up and away long ago,” said Adam. “They kissed you ere they went, and whispered, ‘Come soon.’”

“And I neither to feel nor hear them!” I murmured.

“How could you—far away in your dreary old house! You thought the dreadful place had you once more! Now go and find them.—Your parents, my child,” he added, turning to Lona, “must come and find you!”

The hour of our departure was at hand.

Lona went to the couch of the mother who had slain her and kissed her tenderly. It was the kind of kiss that neither bargains nor boasts—only gives, as bread gives itself when broken. Then she laid herself in her father’s arms.

“That kiss will draw her homeward, my Lona!” said Adam.

“Who were her parents?” asked Lona.

“My father,” answered Adam, “is her father also.”

Lona turned and laid her hand in mine, and the warmth of it was like the familiar grip of a wooden spoon—homely, true, carrying a lifetime of stirring.

I kneeled and humbly thanked the three for helping me to die. Lona knelt beside me, and they all breathed upon us. Their breath was not wind only, but a benediction with a scent in it—clean as rain on warm earth, and strangely intimate, like the smell of a childhood kitchen remembered in a language you can no longer speak aloud.

“Hark! I hear the sun,” said Adam.

I listened.

He was coming with the rush as of a thousand times ten thousand far-off wings, with the roar of a molten and flaming world millions upon millions of miles away. His approach was a great crescendo chord of a hundred harmonies—layer on layer, like the building of an old song in parts, each voice entering in its proper moment. In that sound there was no harsh clang of hammered metal, no grinding of machinery—nothing of the industrial shriek that tears a mind from itself. It was magnitude without violence, power without insult; the acoustic world made whole.

The three looked at each other and smiled, and that smile went floating heavenward—a three-petaled flower, the family’s morning thanksgiving. From their mouths and their faces it spread over their bodies and shone through their garments, as if joy were a fire behind thin paper.

Ere I could say, “Lo, they change!” Adam and Eve stood before me the angels of the resurrection, and Mara was the Magdalene with them at the sepulchre. The countenance of Adam was like lightning, and Eve held a napkin that flung flakes of splendour about the place like shaken flour in sunbeams.

A wind began to moan in pulsing gusts, a troubled rhythm, as if the house itself were breathing against a burden.

“You hear his wings now!” said Adam; and I knew he did not mean the wings of the morning.

“It is the great Shadow stirring to depart,” he went on. “Wretched creature, he has himself within him, and cannot rest!”

“But is there not in him something deeper yet?” I asked.

“Without a substance,” he answered, “a shadow cannot be—yea, or without a light behind the substance!”

He listened, head a little bent, as if catching a faint note under a louder one. Then he called out, with a glad smile, “Hark to the golden cock! Silent and motionless for millions of years has he stood on the clock of the universe; now at last he is flapping his wings! now will he begin to crow! and at intervals will men hear him until the dawn of the day eternal.”

I listened.

Far away—as in the heart of an æonian silence—I heard it: the clear, jubilant outcry of the golden throat. It cut clean through the darkness, not as noise cuts—brutal and meaningless—but as a true word cuts through a lie. It hurled defiance at death and the dark; it sang infinite hope, and coming calm. It was the expectation of the creature finding at last a voice; the cry of a chaos that would be a kingdom.

Then I heard a great flapping, nearer, heavier.

“The black bat is flown!” said Mara.

“Amen, golden cock, bird of God!” cried Adam, and his words rang through the house of silence and went up into the airy regions, as if the very beams had learned to echo.

At his AMEN—like doves arising on wings of silver from among the potsherds—up sprang the Little Ones to their knees on their beds, calling aloud, “Crow! crow again, golden cock!” as if they had both seen and heard him in their dreams.

Then each turned and looked at the sleeping bedfellow, gazed a moment with loving eyes, kissed the silent companion of the night, and sprang from the couch.

The Little Ones who had lain down beside my father and mother gazed blank and sad for a moment at their empty places, then slid slowly to the floor. There they fell each into the other’s arms, as if then first, by the witness of the other’s eyes, they were assured they were alive and awake. In their small voices I heard the fragile music of a people not yet trained into the loudness of dominion—speech still close to breath, and therefore close to truth.

Suddenly spying Lona, they came running, radiant with bliss, to embrace her.

Odu, catching sight of the leopardess at the feet of the princess, bounded to her next. Throwing an arm over the great sleeping head, he fondled and kissed it as if it were the dearest beast of his own hearth.

“Wake up, wake up, darling!” he cried; “it is time to wake!”

The leopardess did not move.

“She has slept herself cold!” he said to Mara, looking up with an appeal that broke the heart by its simplicity.

“She is waiting for the princess to wake, my child,” said Mara.

Odu looked at the princess and saw beside her, still asleep, two of his companions. He flew at them.

“Wake up! wake up!” he cried, and pushed and pulled, now this one, now that. But soon his hands paused, and his face changed. He began to look troubled and turned to me with misty eyes.

“They will not wake!” he said. “And why are they so cold?”

“They too are waiting for the princess,” I answered.

He stretched across and laid his hand on her face. “She is cold too! What is it?” he cried, and looked round in wondering dismay, as if the world had used a word on him that he could not translate.

Adam went to him.

“Her wake is not ripe yet,” he said. “She is busy forgetting. When she has forgotten enough to remember enough, then she will soon be ripe, and wake.”

“And remember?”

“Yes—but not too much at once though.”

“But the golden cock has crown!” argued the child, and fell again upon his companions.

“Peter! Peter! Crispy!” he cried. “Wake up, Peter! wake up, Crispy! We are all awake but you two! The gold cock has crown so loud! The sun is awake and coming! Oh, why won’t you wake?”

But Peter would not wake, neither would Crispy; and Odu wept outright at last—tears new to his own cheeks, as if some ancient spring had opened in him and found a channel.

“Let them sleep, darling!” said Adam. “You would not like the princess to wake and find nobody? They are quite happy. So is the leopardess.”

He was comforted and wiped his eyes as if he had been all his life used to weeping and wiping, though now first he had tears wherewith to weep—soon to be wiped altogether away.

We followed Eve to the cottage. There she offered us neither bread nor wine; there was no breaking of crust, no warmth of cup—only her radiant stillness, desiring our departure as a mother desires the grown child to go and live his own life, not clinging with the greed of loneliness.

So, with never a word of farewell, we went out.

The horse and the elephants were at the door, waiting for us. We were too happy to mount them, and they followed us.
