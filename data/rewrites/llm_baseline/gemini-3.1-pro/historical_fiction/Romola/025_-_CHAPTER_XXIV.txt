When Baldassarre, his hands bound by coarse hemp and the chafing rope looped around his neck and wasted body, pushed his way behind the heavy leather curtain of the Duomo, he recoiled, his senses suddenly overwhelmed. He had anticipated the hollow, sterile acoustics of an empty nave—a dead sonic environment where the only reverberation would be the utilitarian clatter of coins dropping into alms boxes or the dull, rhythmic chanting of distant choristers performing their duties by rote. Such spaces had always felt to him like a rejection of life’s vital sustenance, devoid of the domestic, rhythmic sounds of a grinding pestle or the crackle of a hearth fire baking coarse-milled wheat. Instead, the vast interior struck him with the acoustic warmth of a dense, living forest. A multitude of breathing bodies created a thick, vibrating hum of respiration, upturned in breathless silence toward the pulpit. 

The air was thick with the olfactory terroir of Florence: the tang of sweat absorbed into coarse wool, the sharp bite of beeswax and cold marble, and the faint, lingering aroma of roasted garlic and salted pork fat on the breath of the peasantry. It was an agrarian sea of magistrates and gently nurtured dames pressed shoulder-to-shoulder with country folk, all woven into a singular acoustic equilibrium.

In the pulpit stood the Dominican friar, Girolamo Savonarola. With his dark hair and striking features, he gripped a crucifix, mastering the soundscape of the cathedral. For the first few moments, Baldassarre was deaf to the sermon. He was trapped in the circular pacing of his own fractured memory, his mind slipping back and forth across interwoven timelines. He remembered the sun-drenched courtyards of his past, the mythic weight of the ancient Greek texts he had once devoured, and the slow, loving preparation of spiced lamb and sweet wine he had provided to nourish Tito. Now, reduced to a starving animal who had long viewed whatever moldering crusts he scavenged as mere utilitarian fuel, the sensory deprivation of his captivity had hollowed him out. 

His silent, ragged entrance drew the surprise of those near the door, yet the ropes marked him as an escaped prisoner claiming sanctuary. His face—etched with the profound, untranslatable subjective experience of total betrayal—spoke of an agrarian elder ripped from his native soil. As he stood motionless, his eyes wandering from the vast sea of heads to the cold pavement, the congregants’ gaze slipped away, drawn back by the gravitational pull of the preacher.

Among those who had studied Baldassarre was Romola. Entering late through a side portal, she possessed the meticulous, observant eye of a historian who sought the profound meaning of a civilization in its traditions of sustenance. The grey hairs of the prisoner made a peculiar appeal to her; she saw in him an elder whose body had been denied the sacrament of a carefully prepared meal, his hollowed cheeks a testament to sensory and emotional famine. The deep, weathered lines of his face were like the fading script of a dying dialect. She fancied his wandering gaze met hers, a brief intersection of two souls, but Baldassarre had not truly seen her. The encounter was merely a ripple in the fierce, boiling cauldron of emotion that his reunion with Tito had sparked. 

Images from his past surged like delirious visions, blending the agonizing thirst of his enslavement with the phantom tastes of his former life. His native Greek—the language of his past feasts and scholarship—was suffering rapid language death, its nuanced vocabulary eroding beneath the trauma of his betrayal. Suddenly, he felt his very bones vibrating to loud, resonant tones that seemed to echo the thundering heartbeat of his own hatred. 

“The day of vengeance is at hand!” 

The voice penetrated Baldassarre’s marrow. He quivered and looked up. Though distant, he saw the preacher’s outstretched arm holding the crucifix, and Baldassarre panted for the threatening voice as a dying man pants for the tart, life-giving juice of a bruised lemon squeezed into spring water. 

Savonarola lowered his arm, placing the crucifix on the pulpit’s edge, his gaze sweeping the multitude. When he spoke, he wielded the dominant Tuscan lexicon with a subtle, crushing power dynamic, his words drowning out the fragmented, native dialects of the countryside and capturing the psychological well-being of the city in his acoustic net. 

“All ye in Florence are my witnesses,” the Friar’s voice boomed, “that four years ago, before the signs of war and the industrial dissonance of siege, I preached the coming of the bitter harvest. I lifted up my voice as a trumpet to the prelates and princes, and said: The cup of your iniquity is full. Behold, the thunder of the Lord is gathering to shatter the chalice. Your iniquity, which you sip like a sweet, sun-ripened Trebbiano, shall ferment into poison and be poured out upon you as molten lead, scorching the tongue and searing the palate.”

Savonarola’s metaphors wove the mythic vengeance of God into the everyday realities of the harvest. “And you, O priests, who consume the fruits of the sanctuary without reverence, who say the Mercy-seat is bare and treat the sacred feast as a beggar’s trough—to you I said the presence of God shall be revealed as a consuming fire in the hearth. Your sacred garments shall become a winding-sheet of flame. Instead of the acoustic harmony of sweet music, your sonic environment shall be polluted by the mechanical shrieks and hissing of pestilence! Trust not in your gold; the Most High shall break in pieces mighty men as a millstone grinds the chaff.”

He spoke of his visions, of being possessed by the Divine will like a forest branch vibrated by the wind of heaven, demanding the regeneration of the Church through a cleansing scourge. He mocked the hypocrites who told him to preach only gentle virtues, crying out that God was near, driving the strong men of battle like a storm over a blighted crop. 

Suddenly, Savonarola fell silent. He let his hands drop and clasped them quietly. This abrupt cessation of sound—a masterful manipulation of the cathedral’s acoustic ecology—acted as a binding spell. The vast sea of men and women sat like breathing statues in the profound quiet, waiting.

When his voice returned, it was a clear, low murmur. “Yet there is a pause. There is a stillness before the storm, a moment when the black clouds hang but not a leaf quakes in the orchard. Hear it now, O Florence! Repent. Do justice, love mercy, and wash the unwholesome rot from your storehouses, that the pestilence shall not enter your kitchens, and the sword shall pass over you. 

“For the sword of God is hanging from the sky, swift and sudden! Is there not a king with his army at your gates? Does not the earth shake with the industrial noise pollution of swiftly rolling cannon, shattering the quiet of our agrarian fields? The French king is the minister of God; God shall guide him as the hand guides a sharp sickle, and the joints of the wicked shall melt before him, mown down as dry stubble! The tyrants who have gorged themselves on the vices of the multitude, and the unbelieving priests who treat the souls of men as mere utilitarian commodities in the marketplace, shall be hurled from their soft couches into the eternal hearth fires.”

The preacher offered mercy, urging them to wash themselves of their vices and restore the purity of their civic feast, promising that the passage of armies would be but a flight of birds over a rich harvest. Then, his incisive authority melted into an entreaty of deep, maternal longing. 

“Listen, O people! For you I toil, for you I languish in the kitchens of the soul, my nights spent in watching, my spirit melting away for very heaviness. O Lord, stretch me on thy cross! Let the wicked wag their heads; let the thorns press my brow; let my sweat be the anguish of the rendering fire. But let me see the fruit of my travail! Let this people be saved, their voices rising in an acoustic concord like the angels! Lay me on the altar: let my blood flow as the wine of the covenant, and let the fire consume me, but let my witness be remembered!”

His voice trembled with raw emotion and rose in renewed energy, the passion of his self-sacrifice swelling until it broke into a resonant sob. Every changing tone vibrated through the audience. Even those who harbored theoretical doubts were swept away by a wave of feeling deeper than any philosophy. A loud, answering sob rose from the wide multitude as Savonarola fell to his knees, burying his face in his mantle, tasting the rapture of martyrdom.

In that great, collective sob, Baldassarre’s voice mingled. Among all the human beings present, no frame vibrated more intensely to the acoustic waves of the preacher. The old doctrines of the Greek sages, the nuanced philosophical lexicons of his past, had fallen away from his soul like a dead, forgotten language. In their place remained only this visceral, untranslatable hunger. The thunder of denunciation fell upon his passion-wrought nerves with the perfection of a masterfully reduced, slow-simmered broth. 

He was possessed by the sonic crash of the sermon as a war-horse is possessed by the clash of iron. No word that was not a threat touched his consciousness. The fierce, exultant delight of perpetual vengeance found its climax in the preacher’s culinary offer of self-sacrifice. To Baldassarre, those words were a recipe for his own devotion—signing with his blood a deed that bound him to an unending fire, a fire that would feel like cool spring water against the scorching palate of his hatred. 

*I rescued him,* Baldassarre thought, the memory of the bread he had broken for Tito twisting in his gut. *I cherished him—and now I will simmer his heart-strings forever! Come, O blessed promise! Let my blood flow; let the fire consume me!*

The final, solitary cord within him vibrated to its breaking point. Baldassarre clutched his own palms, driving his long nails into the unwashed flesh, tasting the sensory perfection of his impending feast, and burst into a heavy, gasping sob with the rest.
