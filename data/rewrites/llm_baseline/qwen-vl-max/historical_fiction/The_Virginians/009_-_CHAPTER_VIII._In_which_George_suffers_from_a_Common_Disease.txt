On the day set for Madam Esmond’s reception of General Braddock, Castlewood was transformed into a scene of opulent festivity. The great house stood resplendent in its best attire—flags fluttered above the portico, fresh flowers adorned every window ledge, and the gardens were swept clean and trimmed with care. Madam Esmond herself appeared in a gown of deep crimson silk, embroidered with silver thread and edged with lace that shimmered like moonlight on water. It was a dress far more grand than she usually wore, but she had chosen it deliberately, to honor her guest and to lend dignity to an occasion that weighed heavily upon her heart.

The first arrival was the General’s new aide-de-camp, Mr. Washington, who rode up under a clear blue sky, his horse sleek and well-groomed, his uniform polished to a mirror shine. He dismounted at the steps, bowing low as he handed the reins to the waiting negro servants, who led the animal away to its familiar stall. No young man in the colony rode better or carried himself with greater grace than this one, and Madam Esmond greeted him warmly in the covered gallery before the house.

For several moments, while Major Washington removed his riding boots, he and the widow walked slowly along the gallery, speaking in quiet tones. She had much to say—about the General’s impending visit, about the news of her son’s departure, and about the strange turn of events that brought such men as Franklin of Pennsylvania into the company of the Commander-in-Chief. She spoke with a mixture of pride and unease, for though she admired the General’s rank and power, she could not help but wonder at the world that now surrounded them.

“Mr. Franklin,” she remarked, “was once a printer’s boy, I understand. And yet here he is, traveling in the coach of our most noble commander.”

Mr. Washington smiled gently. “Madam, I have seen many men rise from humble beginnings. But none, I assure you, with such wit, industry, and sense as Mr. Franklin. He may not wear the trappings of nobility, but he carries within him a mind that would command respect in any court.”

Madam Esmond shook her head slightly. “I only hope my son does not find himself among such common folk when he goes off to war. I trust he will be with gentlemen—true gentlemen—of fashion and breeding.”

The Major paused, then said quietly, “There are no perfect companions, madam. Young men must learn from all sorts—the good and the bad. They must hear both wisdom and folly, and judge for themselves.”

“And yet,” she insisted, “he will be with veterans of the finest army in Europe, officers bred in the courts of kings, friends of His Royal Highness the Duke…”

She trailed off, and Washington merely inclined his head. He did not wish to speak too freely of matters that might wound her feelings.

“And with you, dear Colonel,” she added, turning to him with a soft smile, “whom my father always held in highest esteem. You will take care of him, sir? Though you are but five years older, I trust you more than any other officer. My father often told us children to look up to you. I have always done so myself.”

“I would have given anything to win Colonel Esmond’s favor,” replied Washington, bowing with courteous gravity. “And if I could earn your regard, madam, I should consider myself the happiest man alive.”

The widow blushed deeply, and dropped into a curtsey so low and graceful that it was said no woman in the province could match it. “Then I shall always count you among my dearest friends,” she said softly, offering her hand. He kissed it with profound respect.

They walked back into the house together, where George Warrington soon joined them—dressed in his finest clothes, powdered wig neatly arranged, sword at his side, and bearing himself with all the stiff formality of a gentleman just come of age.

He bowed deeply to his mother and again to the Colonel, but there was no warmth in his eyes. A polite smile touched his lips, yet his gaze flickered with something sharper—anger, perhaps, or disappointment. What had passed between them? Nothing overt, nothing improper. Yet both the widow and the Colonel seemed strangely flushed, their expressions strained.

“Have you had good sport today, Mr. George?” asked Washington, trying to ease the tension.

“No,” George replied sharply. “Have you?”

The Colonel, never one for idle talk, began at once to describe his morning’s hunt—a long and detailed account of how he and the king’s officers had pursued birds through the woods, how they had shot game, and how General Braddock, despite his bulk and clumsy boots, had managed to flounder through the forest with a pack of dogs and negroes barking wildly beside him.

“He actually shot a deer,” Washington laughed, “with a fowling-piece in hand, while the dogs were still chasing it!”

“Great God, sir!” cried Braddock, puffing and blowing, “what would Sir Robert Walpole have said to see such a sight in Norfolk?”

Madam Esmond chuckled delicately. “Indeed, Colonel, you are vastly comical this afternoon!”

But George remained silent, his face darkening further.

“You mean Sir Robert Walpole, the Prime Minister?” asked Harry, who had just entered the room, dressed identically to his brother—gold-braided coat, silver-hilted sword, solitaire ring gleaming on his finger.

“Yes,” said Washington. “The General has spoken of visiting him, of the great hunts at Houghton, the palace, the paintings. I confess, I’d give much to see a proper fox-chase in England.”

George sneered. “And yet here we are, hunting elephants and buffaloes, I suppose. Or is it rock-fish and roe-deer? I know not what you call it. But I am no sportsman.”

“You’re a milksop,” said Washington, unable to hide his irritation.

“Indeed,” said George, drawing out a pink ribbon from his apron strings—his mother’s favorite color—and holding it up. “Look at these lovely ties! Who wouldn’t want to be bound by them? See how charming they are! I remember when they were black—for my grandfather.”

“And who would not mourn such a gentleman?” asked Washington, puzzled.

“Ah, Harry!” burst out Madam Esmond, tears filling her eyes as her younger son stepped forward. “Oh, Harry, Harry!”

“What is it, mother?” asked Harry, embracing her.

“I don’t know,” said Washington, biting his lip. “A mere question about ribbons, I believe.”

“Sir,” snapped George, “will you please to speak for yourself?”

The Colonel straightened, then lowered his voice. “He speaks too much for himself,” sighed the widow.

George turned to her. “I protest I do not know the source of these tears any more than the source of the Nile. And if the portrait of my father were to begin to cry, I should wonder at paternal sorrow as much as at maternal.”

He twirled on his scarlet heels with a flourish.

“George Warrington!” exclaimed Harry, “what devil’s dance is that?”

“I do not dance,” said George, “but I can sing.” And without another word, he sat down at the harpsichord and began to play and sing:

The melody echoed through the hall, and Washington, stepping from the balcony, paused to listen.

“I am playing *God save the King*,” said George, smirking, “in compliment to the new expedition.”

“You never know whether you are laughing or serious,” said Washington, “but surely that is not the tune.”

George played a series of rapid trills and quavers, showing off his skill. The Colonel watched, uncertain—was this a man of war or a delicate musician?

At last, Washington checked his watch. “His Excellency’s coach will be here shortly. If you’ll excuse me, I shall retire to prepare.”

“Colonel Washington knows the way to his room,” said George, still at the harpsichord, glancing over his shoulder but making no move to assist.

“Let me show him,” said Madam Esmond, her voice sharp with anger. And with that, she swept from the room, followed by the bewildered Colonel.

George continued to pound the keys, lost in thought.

Harry stepped forward. “For heaven’s sake, George, what does this mean?”

“Why shouldn’t he kiss her hand?” George retorted, pulling out a folded paper from his pocket. “I told you—he did.”

“It was only kindness,” said Harry.

“Kindness!” George shrieked. “Look at this!”

He unfolded the note. It read:

*...is older than myself, but I, again, am older than my years; and you know, dear brother, have ever been considered a sober person. All children are better for a father's superintendence, and her two, I trust, will find in me a tender friend and guardian.*

“Friend and guardian!” George spat, clenching his fists. “Curse him!”

Harry read on:

*...The flattering offer which General Braddock hath made me, will, of course, oblige me to postpone this matter until after the campaign. When we have given the French a sufficient drubbing, I shall return to repose under my own vine and fig-tree.*

“Under my own vine and fig-tree,” George repeated bitterly, pointing to the creeping vines climbing the wall. “That’s Castlewood. That’s home.”

And then came the final line:

And the letter ended.

“Rachel,” George whispered. “Rachel is not weeping for her children. She wants to be comforted.”

He looked at his brother. “Now, Harry. Let us go upstairs at once. Kneel down, as becomes us, and pray: ‘Dear papa, welcome to your house of Castlewood.’”
