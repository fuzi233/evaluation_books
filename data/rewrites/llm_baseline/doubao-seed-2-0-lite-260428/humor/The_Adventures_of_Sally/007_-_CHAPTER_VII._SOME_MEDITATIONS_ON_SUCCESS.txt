If there’s one unifying quirk shared by actors, actresses, and unsupervised seven-year-olds, it’s that they crumple completely at the smallest disaster then bounce right back to delirious joy the second good luck swings their way. Sally could barely wrap her head around the fact that all it took to make every single person in the *Primrose Way* troupe over the moon was her lending her brother Fillmore a measly twenty thousand bucks. If the actual Millennium had rolled into town, the whole company couldn’t have been more chuffed with themselves. The fog of gloom that had hung over them through a whole week of sitting around twiddling their thumbs waiting for the show to fold fell off them faster than a chorus girl’s discarded dance shoe.
The news that the former assistant stage manager — the guy who’d spent his days fetching cold coffee for the cast and ducking flying scripts from temperamental leading ladies — had rocketed up to the fancy new title of show proprietor tickled their theatrical bones perfectly. Most of them had starred in at least three old melodramas where the exact same rags-to-riches beat happened to the persecuted heroine right around the 11pm mark of the final act, so the whole situation felt dramatically foolproof. What’s more, now that the sour, joyless Miss Hobson was gone for good, everyone suddenly agreed she’d been a bigger killjoy than a rainstorm on a beach picnic.
Optimism was contagious, and absurdly cheerful rumors zipped around the cast nonstop. Teddy, the guy who never took his bowler hat off even backstage, had straight from his hotel’s lift boy that the theatre ban would be lifted no later than Tuesday. The actor who played the butler swore he got top-secret intel from the girl working the cigar stand at the Pontchatrain Hotel that Toledo and Cleveland were both opening their theatre doors the very next day. The whole lot of them were fully convinced the sun was blowing through the clouds at last, and Fate could just give up already on its stupid, impossible mission to keep hardworking showfolk down.
Fillmore was fully his ebullient old self again. Everyone has their own silly little way of celebrating a win, and Fillmore’s method involved splurging on a fancy new embroidered waistcoat, a hundred premium half-dollar cigars, and throwing a huge diva-level fuss when his lunch spot informed him they’d run out of his favourite pickled walnuts. It might have been a trick of the light, but Sally swore he packed on at least six whole pounds the first day of his new good fortune regime. Back when he was just a lowly serf in charge of wrangling paper knives and fake teacups for props, he’d been practically svelte for him. Now that he was the big boss, he’d bloomed out into soft, content, billowy curves, and when he loitered on the sidewalk outside the theatre gawking at the new posters blaring *FILLMORE NICHOLAS PRESENTS*, passing pedestrians had to detour around him like he was a newly installed public fountain.
It felt deeply unfair that Sally — the fairy godmother who’d made all this joy possible — couldn’t shake a faint, vague unhappiness, and she puzzled over it for days. The little shadow lingering at the back of her mind refused to step out into the light and name itself, no matter how hard she tried to pin it down. It wasn’t until she was out for a drive in a rented car with Gerald one afternoon on Belle Isle that it all clicked.
Ever since Miss Hobson skipped town, Gerald had been his absolute best self, all warm charm and no of the brooding moodiness that had made him such a drag a few weeks prior. Like Fillmore, he positively bloomed in the sunshine of unlooked-for prosperity. And yet — as the car rolled smoothly past the sun-dappled woods and river-side fields, Sally could feel something small and sharp jarring under all his cheer.
Gerald was chatting a mile a minute, gushing about all the huge plans he had for the future. “Once this show catches on — and it’s definitely going to catch on — I’m gonna show every last snob who ever laughed at me! One single hit, that’s all I need, and then you watch me go! I never got a fair shot before, but —”
His voice rambled on, but Sally had stopped listening. This was the time of year when the soft warm glow of afternoon burned off fast, leaving a sharp evening chill right behind it. The sun had dipped behind the treeline, and a cold wind was blowing straight up off the river. All at once, it felt like that wind had blown the fog clean out of her head, and she finally saw what had been niggling at her for days.
For a second the truth stood bare, no hiding spots left, and the whole world suddenly felt a little forlorn. She’d just stumbled on the huge, fundamental difference between how men and women looked at life. Success! Men worshipped the thing like it was a holy relic, and they barely had a scrap of themselves left over for anything else. The irony hit her that this very obsession was the core theme of Gerald’s play, the one she’d just saved from being shut down for good.
How many of the men she knew viewed life as anything other than a mad race they had to sprint full-tilt to win, no matter what small, nice things they trampled past on the side of the road in their hurry? Fillmore, Gerald, every last one of them. Sure, there might be a woman tucked somewhere in the back of their life, but she always came second — an afterthought, something to fit into their spare time after all the important success work was done.
For her, Gerald was everything. His big flashy success would never be more than a side note. She would have been perfectly happy living in a tiny cramped apartment eating tinned beans with him, no hit shows, no fancy producers lining up to beg for his scripts. But she wasn’t enough for him, not on her own. A stupid, sharp little spasm of useless jealousy shook her, and she shivered.
“Cold?” Gerald said, barely pausing his rant. “I’ll tell the driver to turn back. Honestly, there’s no reason this show doesn’t run a full year in New York, everyone’s saying it’s brilliant. Once it blows up, every producer in town’s gonna be chasing me, I —”
Sally stared out at the bleakening world outside the car window. The sky had turned a flat, heavy lead grey, and the wind off the river blew sharp and dismal, cutting right through her coat.
