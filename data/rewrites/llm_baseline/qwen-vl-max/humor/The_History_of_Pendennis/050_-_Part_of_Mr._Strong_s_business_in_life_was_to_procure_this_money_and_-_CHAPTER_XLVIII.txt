CHAPTER XLVIII. A Visit of Politeness (Revised for Humor)

Arthur Pendennis woke up one morning with the kind of vigor usually reserved for people who’ve just won the lottery, discovered a secret tunnel to Spain, or finally found their missing sock. No hostile messages from Mr. Huxter—no urgent financial demands, no existential dread about the state of his bank balance. Just peace. And a headache. But not the *kind* of headache that comes from overwork or moral decay. This was the sort you get after a night spent at Vauxhall, watching fireworks and thinking deep thoughts while sipping champagne like it’s your birthright.

He stirred in bed, stretched, and immediately began mentally reviewing the evening’s events—specifically, the charming little Fanny Bolton, whom he had met under circumstances so romantic they could have been lifted straight from a novel written by someone who’d never actually left London. “What a lovely creature,” he mused, blinking into the sunlight like a philosopher emerging from a cave. “So natural. So… unartificial. Unlike those ballroom butterflies who spend half their lives practicing how to trip over their own train while pretending not to care.”

He paused, then added, “Though I suppose Miss Blanche isn’t entirely without her charms either. But she does everything with such calculated grace—it’s like watching a clock tick backwards.”

Fanny, on the other hand, was pure spontaneity. She lived in a porter’s lodge, sang under the tutelage of an old man named Bows (who sounded suspiciously like a retired pirate), and had the kind of voice Arthur imagined angels might use when they’re trying to sound casual. “If her singing voice is as sweet as her speaking voice,” Arthur thought, “then I may be in trouble. Or at least in need of a new column for the *Pall Mall Gazette* titled ‘Why I Can’t Stop Thinking About a Girl Who Lives in a Doorway.’”

And so, inspired by both admiration and mild guilt (he didn’t know why he felt guilty—he hadn’t done anything wrong, except perhaps look too much like a prince when he wasn’t one), Arthur decided to pay a visit to Shepherd’s Inn. Not because he wanted to help Fanny learn to sing—though he did think that would be a noble pursuit—but because he wanted to study her, and also possibly write a book about her, which he’d title something dramatic like *The Rose That Grew in a Porter’s Lodge: A Tale of Love, Courage, and Minor Property Disputes*.

Also, he needed a break from genteel society. He’d grown tired of men who spoke only in monosyllables unless they were quoting Shakespeare, and women who giggled at nothing and yet expected you to believe they were witty. “I’m getting too old for this,” he muttered, pulling on his best white hat (which he wore even though it was Saturday, because style doesn’t take weekends off). “Too young for real men, too mature for party animals, and far too well-dressed for anyone who thinks a waistcoat should be worn as armor.”

Thus, armed with curiosity, a slightly-too-large umbrella, and a heart full of literary ambition, Arthur made his way to Shepherd’s Inn—where, if we’re honest, the odds of finding anything resembling high culture were about as good as finding a unicorn in a pub basement.

But lo! When he arrived at the wicket gate, there stood Fanny Bolton—blushing like a sunset, eyes wide as saucers, hands clasped together like she’d just seen God Himself descend from the clouds (and perhaps she had—because Arthur, in his white trousers and shiny boots, did look divine).

She called out, “Mamma! Mamma!” as if announcing the arrival of royalty—and honestly, what else could Arthur Pendennis be but a king of sorts? At least in the hearts of two women and four small children.

Mrs. Bolton, initially wary—“Who is this dandy? Is he here to collect rent? Or worse, ask about the bill-stamps?”—was quickly won over by Arthur’s charm, which was basically the equivalent of handing out free candy while wearing a top hat. He asked after her health, inquired about the undertaker business (which involved quite a few mutes, apparently), and expressed great interest in the Polwheedle and Tredyddlum railway shares—something that probably meant nothing to anyone but him.

He sat down on a child’s chair—because there were no proper chairs available, and because he liked to make a statement. The children laughed. Fanny blushed harder. One little girl, Betsy-Jane, said she wanted to go to the play too, which caused Fanny to snap, “Don’t bother!” and Mrs. Bolton to say, “Git-long, Betsy-Jane, do now,” sending the two youngest off to play near the statue of Shepherd the Great, who looked very stern and probably disapproved of all this nonsense.

Then came old Bows—dressed in clothes so ancient they seemed to have been woven by time itself. He appeared at the archway, saw Arthur sitting like a conquering hero atop a tiny chair, and froze.

“What! Mr. Bows?” Arthur boomed, as if greeting a long-lost uncle. “How d’you do, Bows? I was coming to see you!”

Bows blinked. “You were coming to see me?” he asked, sounding mildly alarmed.

“Yes,” Arthur replied, puffing out his chest. “I’ve decided to cultivate you. You’re a character. A genuine human being. I’ll put you in my next book.”

“Ah,” said Bows, nodding slowly. “That’s nice. I’ve always wanted to be in a book. Though I prefer the kind where you don’t have to wear a wig.”

They shook hands. Arthur offered tickets to the theatre. Mrs. Bolton nearly fainted with joy. Fanny clapped her hands. Even the cat, who had been sleeping on the roof, opened one eye and stared.

And thus ended the most important visit Arthur Pendennis had made all week—not because it changed history, or saved a soul, or even led to a romance—but because it reminded him that sometimes, the best stories aren’t written in ink, but in smiles, blushes, and the occasional awkward sit-down on a child’s chair.

And if that’s not comedy, then what is?
