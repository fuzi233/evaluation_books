Before we delve further into the adventures of Miss Lydia, we must first unravel a mystery hidden within Mr. Warrington's letter to his brother regarding Lady Maria Esmond. To the uninitiated, the notion seems baffling: how could decent people imagine that a woman of noble birth, ancient lineage, and mature judgment—who was ardently in love merely a dozen months prior—could suddenly forget herself? How could innocent youths conceive that she would fall for a man of low station, decades younger than herself, and wed him against the world's wishes? Technically, she did not marry "in the face" of the world, but rather behind its back, secured by the private rites of Parson Sampson in Mayfair.

But pause before condemning her entirely. Did her past affection for her young cousin preclude future love? Do men alone possess the right to change partners while women are scolded for seeking solace? Such judgments are crude and unphilosophical. Recall Hamlet's harsh critique of his mother's remarriage; surely, a widow needs someone to cling to once her first husband passes. History shows women capable of twisting around two men simultaneously. Why forbid marriage tables after funeral feasts? Just as one eats today, hunger returns tomorrow. Nature serves *à la russe*; we consume dishes sequentially. Like the cannibals of Polyphemus's cave, society devours us one by one—young, old, plump, lean, beautiful, or plain—with no escape. Witness Lady Ogresham! Last year she seized, devoured, and discarded poor Tom Kydd; now she holds Ned Suckling captive beneath her gaze, trembling and fascinated. Soon, faint squeaks of pity will rise, followed by silence.

Tragically, I suspected Maria Esmond had lost her heart many times before Harry Warrington claimed it. I fancied she would preserve her love through celibacy, bewailing her misfortunes. Yet, if I fell in love tomorrow, I would assume ownership of my beloved's heart—not as a tenant in a dilapidated flat shared by countless lovers, but as a rightful owner. We declare eternal unity, dismissing rivals like Sir Cresswell Cresswell. But Lady Maria of the last century possessed an ill-regulated mind. As worldly observers know, when an older flirt exhausts one passion, she seeks another. Flirting resembles drinking: once the brandy is gone, one turns to gin. If Maria found a successor for Harry Warrington, establishing a new sovereign in her heart, what could anyone expect? Her emotional territory resembled the Low Countries: accustomed to conquest and perpetually open to invasion.

Her current captor was none other than Mr. Geoghegan (or Hagan), the young actor famed in George's tragedy. With his thrilling voice, bright eyes, noble demeanor, and striking appearance in gilt armor and a large periwig, he enchanted her completely. Even Ariadne surrendered to Bacchus after Theseus departed. The Irishman was deeply moved by her favor. Though he might have preferred a younger lady, few could match her tenderness. She clung to him fiercely, retiring to his modest Westminster lodgings when their marriage became public knowledge, despite her furious relatives cutting ties.

News reached General Lambert at Whitehall, sparking merriment among his family. In those simpler times, jokes could be broad; a fine lady might laugh at Fielding's pages while weeping over Clarissa's letters. The General regaled his kin with waggery about the union, George's role in facilitating it, and Barry's anticipated jealousy. He joked that cousin Hagan hadn't chosen George as best man, suggesting names like "Carpezan" or "Sybilla" for the first child, born amidst theatrical drama. Perhaps they couldn't afford a coach, but a chariot with pasteboard dragons from Mr. Rich's theater would suffice. The baby might be christened in Macbeth's cauldron, with Harry and Harlequin as godparents.

"Why shouldn't she marry him if she loves him?" asked little Hetty innocently. "Why shouldn't he love her for being slightly older? My mother is older, yet you love her dearly. When you married my mother, sir, you mentioned poverty, yet you were happy, and no one mocked you!" Unaware of Lady Maria's past indiscretions, the child spoke purely from youthful naivety.

Had her family truly abandoned her? George recounted their fury: Lady Castlewood's mourning attire, Mr. Will's threat to tear the actor's ears, and Madame de Bernstein's explosive rage. "It insults the family," declared haughty Miss Hett. "Ladies of rank must be indignant at marrying such a man, though desertion is another matter."

"My dear child," her mother replied, "after Lady Maria's behavior, no respectable person visits her."

"What behavior, mama?"

"Never mind. Little girls shouldn't pry into such matters. Suffice it that I am shocked she ever returned here. No honest soul associates with her!"

"Aunt Lambert, then I must be whipped and sent to bed," George said mockingly. "Though I admit, earlier than I confessed, that I visited my cousin the player and his wife. They lived in filthy Westminster quarters, where the wretch supported not only his wife but also his elderly mother and a younger brother attending school. Despite Mr. Hagan's improvidence, I felt a genuine respect for him. Some marriages are simply reckless, aren't they, Theo?"

"If they marry spendthrifts like you," Theo retorted playfully.

"No, not the purse, sir," George smiled tenderly. "That was Theo's doing. Mine came empty. Mr. Rich is furious, fearing riots similar to those surrounding foreign dancers. The macaronis threaten violence, and my cousin Will vows to seize the actor's ears. Without his salary, this couple faces starvation."

"And you gave them aid, sir? Truly generous, George!" Theo admired him.

"It was your purse, dear Theo," he corrected.

"Mama, pray go visit them tomorrow!" Theo begged.

"If she refuses, I demand a divorce!" Papa exclaimed jokingly, adding, "Kiss me, you little wench—with Monsieur le Beau-Fils's permission."

"Monsieur mon beau fiddlestick, papa!" Miss Lambert laughed, complying with the order. This marked the first time George Esmond Warrington was dubbed a "fiddlestick."

Anyone making an imprudent marriage endures familial scorn, pity, and wrath. Whether marrying a curate's daughter or a struggling barrister, the reaction is severe. Imagine the outrage faced by Lady Maria Hagan and her husband. First, consider old Mrs. Hagan, dutifully cared for by her son since his father's death. His earnings sustained his siblings and household comforts. Now, a frivolous lady intruded, sharing their meager provisions. Unless Hagan was spirited and the mother fearful, life would be uncomfortable. Taking a seat at such a small table was selfish, yet love often ignores consequences. Passionate individuals invite guests knowing full well the room cannot accommodate them, trusting that fewer will arrive. Intoxicated by romance, they neglect budgeting for inevitable butcher and fishmonger bills.

Lady Maria's presence strained resources, prompting complaints from the old mother-in-law. Yet, her outcry paled beside the scandal erupting in Kensington. Whispers filled the Princess's court, officers exchanged jests, printers published waggery, and prudes expressed outrage. Madame Bernstein suffered fits, fleeing to Tunbridge Wells. Lord Maria refused to see his sister, while the Countess mourned publicly. Days passed in excitement over the scandal until fresh news—a German victory, American uncertainties, a returning officer awaiting trial, or an Italian soprano—diverted public attention. Lady Maria faded into obscurity, eating hard-earned meals in her garret alongside the actor's family.

Mr. George Warrington continued his letter to Theo:

"My dearest little Theo, you wished to join Mama on this charitable mission, but I deemed Aunt Lambert's solitary visit preferable. Upon arrival, Cousin Hagan was absent. We found her ladyship in casual dress, hair untidy, playing cribbage with a neighbor from the second floor, while good Mrs. Hagan sat by the fire with punch and *The Whole Duty of Man*.

'Maria, your Maria once,' she whispered tearfully upon seeing us. Aunt Lambert offered immediate sympathy. While she comforted Lady Maria, I complimented the elder woman. As conversation lagged, we worked together to lighten the gloom. Discussing you, Wolfe, and war, we hoped you faced the French soon. We imagined you at Cape Breton, studying maps of Quebec and the St. Lawrence. Recalling your joyful expression upon my safe return from a minor skirmish, we prayed for Harry's similar success. Sharing our optimism, we discussed the campaign's conclusion and Captain Warrington's promotion. May our hopes fulfill swiftly.

How strange that the mettlesome one escapes conflict while I, a peaceful youth, find battles thrust upon me! What do you think of my latest entanglement? With no lesser figure than your old enemy, our kinsman, Mr. Will.
