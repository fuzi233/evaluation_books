XXXI. Archer stood frozen, the news from old Catherine still echoing in his mind like a thunderclap. It was natural enough that Madame Olenska had rushed to her grandmother’s side when summoned—she had always been devoted to Mrs. Mingott. But that she would choose to stay, especially now that the older woman was recovering, puzzled him deeply. He knew full well that Ellen’s financial situation hadn’t improved. Her husband had granted her only a meager allowance after their separation, and without Mrs. Mingott’s support, it wouldn’t have sustained even a modest existence by New York standards. And now, with Medora Manson—her companion and confidante—ruined, the two women were left with barely enough to survive.

Yet Archer didn’t believe money had driven her decision. He knew Ellen too well. She carried herself with the careless grace of someone who’d never known want, whose taste for luxury came not from necessity but habit. She could live simply when needed, though her relatives often remarked on how little she cared about appearances or “how things were done.” Still, he remembered: months had passed since her allowance ended, and yet she hadn’t once reached out to reconcile with her family. So why now?

The answer was clear, if painful. On the ferry ride back from Jersey City, she had whispered that they must remain apart—her head resting against his chest, her breath warm on his skin. He knew those words weren’t meant to charm or provoke; they were born of desperation. She was fighting a war no one else could see, clinging to the idea that they could honor their obligations, preserve dignity, and still hold onto each other. But perhaps, in the ten days since her return, she had sensed his silence. The absence of visits, the lack of urgency—these might have signaled to her that he was preparing to act, to make a choice that couldn’t be undone. And fear, cold and sudden, might have seized her then. Not fear of losing him, but of losing herself—of becoming just another woman caught in a secret life, half-truths, and silent betrayals.

Earlier that evening, as he rang Mrs. Mingott’s bell, Archer had felt certain of his path. He intended to speak alone with Ellen, or at least learn when she was leaving for Washington. Then he would follow her—not just to Washington, but wherever she went. His heart leaned toward Japan, a distant dreamland where they could begin anew. He planned to leave a note for May—final, decisive, shutting down any possibility of retreat. He believed himself ready, even eager, for this leap into the unknown.

But when he heard that Ellen had decided to stay, his first emotion wasn’t anger or sorrow—it was relief. A strange, quiet relief. Yet as he walked home through the winter night, that relief curdled into something heavier. He realized he was no longer excited by the idea of running away. The path ahead wasn’t unfamiliar—he’d seen others walk it before, men who played the game of love and duty with practiced hands. They called it “protecting a woman’s honor,” a phrase he’d learned from novels and dinner-table talk. He’d watched Sillerton Jackson do it with quiet pride, watching Mrs. Thorley Rushworth smile through lies, laugh through pretense, maintain a perfect façade while living a double life.

Now, standing in the cold dark, he saw the truth: that role was not one he could wear lightly. It demanded constant deception—a lie in every glance, every touch, every word. A lie that would stretch across years, poisoning joy and turning intimacy into performance. Women could play such roles more easily, society allowed them more leeway. They could claim nerves, moods, exhaustion. Men, however, were judged harshly. In Archer’s world, a man who continued affairs after marriage was despised. There was a season for wild oats—but only one.

He had always believed this. He thought Lefferts contemptible. But loving Ellen Olenska changed everything. She was unlike any other woman. He was unlike any other man. Their situation was unique, unbound by convention. They owed allegiance not to society, but to themselves. And yet… tomorrow he would step through his own door. There would be May, waiting. Habit, duty, honor—everything he’d ever believed in would stand between him and the future he dreamed of.

At the corner of Fifth Avenue, he paused. Ahead loomed a great house, dark and silent, its windows shuttered save for one faint glow upstairs. He remembered it lit up, alive with music and laughter—the place where he’d kissed May for the first time, under a canopy of candles, where she’d appeared like a goddess of silver light. Now it was empty, almost tomblike. And there, at the door, stood Mrs. Mingott’s carriage.

A flicker of irony crossed his mind—what a chance for gossip if Sillerton Jackson happened by! But he pushed it aside. He’d been deeply moved by Catherine’s account of Ellen’s visit to Mrs. Beaufort. It made the moral outrage of New York seem petty, almost cruel. Still, he knew what people would say. That Ellen was flirting with scandal, playing games with the elite. Rumors already whispered that Beaufort had fled to Fanny Ring. But Ellen’s behavior suggested otherwise—she was choosing restraint, not recklessness.

He looked up at the window. Perhaps the two women sat together, talking quietly. Beaufort had likely gone elsewhere. As he approached, the door opened—and she stepped out.

“Ellen,” he said softly, his voice barely above a whisper.

She stopped, startled. Behind her, a dim light guided her down the steps. Two young men approached from the opposite side of the street—Lefferts and Chivers, stylish and polished, heading to a theater party. For a moment, Archer felt a pang of shame—didn’t she know the danger of being seen? But then her hand touched his, warm and real, and all hesitation vanished.

“I shall see you now—we shall be together,” he blurted, his words tumbling out.

“Oh,” she replied. “Granny told you?”

They stood in silence, the city breathing around them. The two young men had turned away, discreetly crossing the avenue. That kind of complicity—men protecting secrets, shielding reputations—was familiar to Archer. But now it repulsed him.

Could they really live like this? Was this all she imagined? He couldn’t bear the thought.

“Tomorrow,” he insisted, “we must meet—somewhere we can be alone.”

She hesitated, glancing toward the carriage. “I’ll be at Granny’s—for now,” she added, as if needing to justify her change of plan.

“Somewhere alone,” he repeated, his voice sharp with frustration.

She laughed faintly, a sound that grated on him. “In New York? There are no churches… no monuments.”

“The Art Museum,” he said quickly. “In the Park. Half-past two. I’ll be at the door.”

She turned without answering, climbed into the carriage, and as it rolled away, he thought he saw her wave—once, briefly, into the dark.

He stared after her, torn between hope and despair. It felt as though he’d spoken to a stranger—a woman who gave pleasure but offered nothing more. He despised the worn-out phrases, the hollow promises. “She’ll come,” he muttered, almost bitterly.

At the museum, they avoided the crowded galleries and wandered into the quiet room housing the Cesnola antiquities—broken shards of glass, clay, bronze, relics of forgotten lives. No one else was there. They sat on the divan beside the radiator, facing the glass cases filled with fragments of Ilium.

“It’s odd,” Ellen said, “I’ve never been here before.”

“Ah, well—some day it may become a great museum.”

She stood and drifted across the room. Archer watched her—how the furs draped her slender figure, the delicate curl framing her cheek, the way the veil framed her face like a mask. He was lost in the details that made her uniquely herself.

Then he rose and joined her at the case.

“It seems cruel,” she murmured, “that after a while nothing matters—just like these little things, once so important to people long dead, now labeled ‘Use unknown.’”

“Yes,” he said. “But meanwhile…”

“Meanwhile…” she echoed.

She stood there—long coat, muff, veil drawn low, violets trembling in her breath. To him, she seemed untouched by time, pure harmony of form and color. Impossible to imagine she could ever fade.

“Meanwhile, everything matters—that concerns you,” he said.

She looked at him, thoughtful, then returned to the divan. He sat beside her, waiting. But soon, a distant footstep echoed through the empty halls. A guard in braided cap passed slowly, ghost-like, through the rooms of mummies and sarcophagi.

“What is it you wanted to tell me?” she asked, as if sensing the same urgency.

“What I wanted to tell you?” he replied. “That I believe you came to New York because you were afraid.”

“Afraid?”

“Afraid of my coming to Washington.”

She lowered her eyes, fingers twitching in her muff. “Well?”

“Well—yes,” she admitted. “You knew?”

“Yes,” he said. “I knew.”

“So… this is better, isn’t it?”

“Better?” he echoed.

“We’ll hurt fewer people. Isn’t that what you always wanted?”

“To have you here—close, but not too close? To meet in secret, on the sly? That’s the opposite of what I want. I told you what I wanted.”

She hesitated. “And you still think this—worse?”

“A thousand times worse.” He paused. “It would be easy to lie to you. But the truth is—I think it detestable.”

“Oh, so do I!” she breathed, relieved.

He sprang up. “Then why—what do you think is better?”

She bowed her head, clenching and unclenching her hands. The guard passed again, vanishing into the shadows.

Instead of answering, she whispered, “I promised Granny I’d stay because it seemed safer here.”

“Safer from me?”

“Safer from loving you?”

Her profile didn’t move. But a tear slipped from her lashes, catching in the mesh of her veil.

“Safer from doing irreparable harm. Don’t let us be like all the others!”

“What others? I’m not claiming to be different. I feel the same wants, the same longing.”

She glanced at him, a flush rising in her cheeks. “Shall I—once come to you… and then go home?”

The blood surged to his forehead. “Dearest,” he whispered, unmoving.

It felt as though he held his heart in his hands, fragile and full. Then her last words struck him.

“Go home? What do you mean by going home?”

“Home to my husband.”

“And you expect me to say yes to that?”

She raised her eyes, troubled. “What else is there? I can’t stay here and lie to those who’ve helped me rebuild my life.”

“But that’s exactly why I ask you to come away!”

“And destroy their lives? When they’ve given me mine?”

Archer stood, staring down at her, speechless with despair. He could have said yes—“Come, just once.” He knew the power she would give him. Persuading her to stay would have been easy. But something stopped him. Her honesty, her refusal to play the game—made it impossible to lure her into the trap of temporary escape.

“If I let her come,” he thought, “I must let her go again.” And that was unthinkable.

But he saw the tear on her cheek, and wavered.

“After all,” he began again, “we have our own lives. There’s no use pretending the impossible. You’re so open-minded about some things, so used to facing the Gorgon—why are you afraid to look at our case honestly? Unless you think the sacrifice isn’t worth it.”

She stood, lips tightening. “Call it that—then I must go.” She pulled out her watch.

As she turned to leave, he caught her wrist.

“Then come to me once,” he said, his voice breaking. “Just once.”

For a moment, they stared at each other—like enemies, yet bound by love.

“When?” he pressed.

“Tomorrow?”

She hesitated. “The day after.”

“Dearest—”

She freed her wrist, but they held each other’s gaze. Her face had grown pale, yet radiated an inner fire. He felt awe—this was love made visible.

“Oh, I shall be late—goodbye. Don’t come farther,” she said, hurrying away.

At the door, she turned, waved once, and disappeared.

Archer walked home alone. Darkness fell as he entered his house. He looked around the hall as if seeing it from beyond death. The parlour-maid ran up to light the gas.

“Is Mrs. Archer in?”

“No, sir. She went out after lunch and hasn’t returned.”

Relief washed over him. He entered the library, sank into his chair, and sat motionless—elbows on knees, chin in hands, eyes fixed on the dying fire.

He sat in silence, unaware of time, consumed by a deep, grave amazement. “This was what had to be… this was what had to be,” he repeated, as if chained to fate.

The dream he’d held—of escape, of freedom—had been shattered. The rapture was tainted with chill.

The door opened. May stepped in.

“I’m dreadfully late—were you worried?”

She placed her hand on his shoulder, rare and tender.

“Is it late?” he asked.

“After seven. I thought you were asleep.”

She laughed, tossed her hat on the sofa. Pale, but animated, her eyes bright.

“I went to Granny’s. Just as I was leaving, Ellen came in from a walk. So I stayed. We talked—really talked. It’s been ages…”

She settled into her chair, smoothing her hair.

“She was so dear—just like the old Ellen. I haven’t been fair to her lately. I’ve sometimes thought—”

Archer rose, leaning against the mantelpiece, out of the lamp’s reach.

“Yes, you’ve thought—?”

“Well, perhaps I haven’t judged her fairly. She’s so different—on the surface. She takes up odd people. She likes to stand out. Probably from her European life. We must seem dull to her. But I don’t want to judge unfairly.”

She paused, breathless. Her cheeks flushed.

He saw the effort in her—like that moment in St. Augustine, when she’d reached beyond herself.

“She hates Ellen,” he thought. “And she’s trying to overcome it. Trying to win me over.”

The thought stirred him. For a moment, he nearly confessed everything.

“You understand, don’t you,” she continued, “why the family has been annoyed? We did what we could. But she never seemed to grasp it. And now—going to Mrs. Beaufort, in Granny’s carriage! I’m afraid she’s alienated the van der Luydens…”

“Ah,” Archer said, with a weary laugh.

The door had closed again.

“It’s time to dress. We’re dining out, aren’t we?”

She rose, lingered near the fire. As he passed, she stepped forward—eyes meeting, blue and swimming, like the ones he’d left behind.

She threw her arms around his neck, pressed her cheek to his.

“You haven’t kissed me today,” she whispered.

And he felt her tremble in his arms.
