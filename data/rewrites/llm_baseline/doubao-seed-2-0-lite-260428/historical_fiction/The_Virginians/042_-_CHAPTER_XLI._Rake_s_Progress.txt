In Harry Warrington’s day, no one in learned or fashionable circles could stop bickering over the relative merits of the ancients and the moderns—and by and large, the verdict landed in favour of the former. The “moderns” of that 18th century are the distant ancients of our 1858 present, just as our grandchildren a hundred years from now will sit in judgment on the morals and wit of our own era. You respectable 1700s ancestors? For all your boasts of Gibbon, you must own yourselves beaten on book-learning: a pair of Cambridge and Glasgow professors today know more Greek than every university in Europe, Athens included, could scrape together in your time. Your science barely outstripped the heathen writers you deemed your inferiors. And as for public and private decency? Is 1858 really a match for 1758? Gentlemen of Disraeli’s Commons, does every last one of you have a price, as under Walpole or Newcastle? Or—far the more delicate question—have most of you already had yours? Ladies, I do not pretend you are a college of Vestal virgins, but the scandalous chronicles of a century back are so lurid you ought to thank heaven you did not live in those perilous times. On my conscience, I believe men and women both are better now: there are far more Susannas among us, and far fewer wicked Elders lurking about.

Have you ever read *Clarissa*, *Tom Jones*, *Roderick Random*, unexpurgated? Those are unvarnished portraits of the men, women, and seething society of that day. If we dared depict the unredacted exploits of a Lovelace, a Lady Bellaston, or that infamous “Lady of Quality” who laid her memoirs bare for the author of *Peregrine Pickle*, our pure, outraged 19th century would shriek, flee the room, yank all its young ladies out by the wrist, and order Mr. Mudie to ban every last book by that odious author forever. You, madam, at fifty-eight, are no doubt far too squeamish, crying out before you are hurt when no one ever intended to offend your ladyship. There is truth to the complaint that the novelist’s art is hobbled by these restraints, just as the fair limbs of many a harmless marble statue in St. Peter’s and the Vatican are spoiled by the frumpy tin draperies pious old churchmen swaddle them in. But that prudery is not entirely foolish, any more than state press censorship is entirely unfounded. If a page risks corrupting good morals, out come the censor’s scissors to snip the prurient line. Society the despot has issued its imperial decree, and we must submit. You have all seen those American pianos tricked out in frilled muslin trousers over their mahogany legs? Depend on it, that muslin muffled half the notes, muddling the music and slowing the player down.

What is this preamble leading us to? Harry Warrington, Esquire, in his Bond Street lodgings, and the wild life he and a thousand other young fashion-mad bucks led in those days—a life I can no more drag my gentlest young readers into than Lady Squeams can take her innocent daughter to an ordinary evening at Cremorne Gardens. My dear Miss Diana—pshaw, I know full well you are eight-and-thirty, however shy you pretend to be, however you try to convince everyone you have just outgrown pinafores and schoolroom dinners—when your grandfather was a young man about town, a member of White’s Club, dining at Pontac’s off feasts laid on by Braund and Lebeck, riding down to Newmarket with March and Rockingham, toasting “the best in England” alongside Gilly Williams and George Selwyn (who made jokes whose flavour has long evaporated since their first bottling), the old gentleman lived a life your noble aunt, author of *Legends of the Squeamses; or, Fair Fruits of a Family Tree*, has never breathed a word of to you. It was long before your grandmother adopted the severe piety for which she was celebrated in her final long residence at Bath, long after Colonel Tibbalt married rich soap-boiler’s heiress Miss Lye, that her ladyship sowed her own wild oats. She was as giddy as the rest of the gentry in her youth: ten card tables set out at her Hill Street house every Wednesday and Sunday evening, save for the short summer season when Ranelagh Gardens was open for Sunday entertainments. She gambled eight, nine, ten hours every single night of her life. Everyone else in society did the same. She won, she lost, she cheated, she pawned her jewels—and who knows what else she did not pledge, just to feed her ravenous hunger for play? What of that after-supper duel at the Shakespeare’s Head in Covent Garden between your grandfather and Colonel Tibbalt, where they drew swords and fought only in front of Sir John Screwby, who was passed out drunk under the table? They were interrupted by Mr. John Fielding’s constables, and your grandfather was carried home to Hill Street wounded in a sedan chair.

Those gentlemen in powder and ruffled cuffs, who turned the toes of their buckled pumps out so daintily, were terrible, ferocious fellows. Swords were perpetually being drawn, bottle after bottle drained dry, oaths roared unceasingly in conversation. Tavern drawers and watchmen were pinked and maimed, chairmen beaten, sober citizens insulted by reeling bands of pleasure-hunters. You have been to Cremorne with proper, vetted vouchers, of course? You remember our great London theatres of thirty years ago? You were too good a person ever to attend a play. Well, you have not the faintest idea what those playhouses, their notorious green boxes especially, were like when Garrick and Mrs. Pritchard trod the boards before them. For my children’s sake, I am grateful to that great retired actor who was the first to banish that shameful licence from the stage. No, madam, I do not plume myself on superior virtue. I do not claim you are naturally better than your rouged, gambling, loud, tearing 18th century ancestress, nor even than poor Polly Fogle just taken up for shoplifting, who would have been hanged for the same crime a hundred years prior. I am only heartily thankful my temptations are far slighter, for I have more than enough to contend with from the vices of our own century.

So if Harry Warrington rides down to Newmarket for the October races, and wins or loses whole sacks of gold there; if he carouses with a party of bucks at the Shakespeare or Bedford Head Tavern; if he dines at White’s ordinary and sits down afterward to macco and lansquenet; if he brawls with the watch and finds himself locked up in the Roundhouse for a night; if he lets himself go for a spell and turns into a wild, dissipated, harum-scarum young Harry, I, who know well the weakness of human nature, will not be shocked. Aware as I am of my own shortcomings, I have no interest in raining fire and brimstone on my neighbour. Mr. Sampson, the clergyman, did that for everyone else: in his Long Acre chapel he lashed vice unmercifully, gave sin no quarter, out-cursed blasphemy with thunderous anathemas, knocked drunkenness down and trampled the prostrate brute wallowing in the gutter, dragged conjugal infidelity out into the light and pelted her with endless stones of rhetoric—then, once service was over, he would turn up for dinner at the Star and Garter, mix a great bowl of punch for Harry and his friends at the Bedford Head, or take a hand at whist in Harry’s Bond Street lodgings, or Lord March’s, or wherever there was a good supper and cheerful company waiting for him.

Oftentimes I worry I may have taken my usual uncharitable, degrading view of Harry’s exploits during this period of his London residence—for I have not uttered a single word of virtuous indignation against his conduct, and if his actions were not actually reprehensible, then I have judged him cruelly indeed. O Truth, O Beauty, O Modesty, O Benevolence, O Pudor, O Ancient Decency, O Blushing Shame, O Namby-Pamby, each with your great honoured capital letters! O Niminy-Piminy! How dare I ever say a young man can be a young man? No doubt, dear young lady, I am slandering Mr. Warrington as my heartless custom dictates. For proof, here is a letter pulled straight from the Warrington family papers, written by Harry to his mother, that has not a single line to suggest he leads anything but a quiet, respectable life:

> **BOND STREET, LONDON, October 25, 1756**
>
>
> I take up my pen to acknowledge your esteemed favour of 10 July, per the *Lively Virginia* packet, which duly arrived forwarded by our Bristol agent, and I rejoice to hear your crops promise so well. ‘Tis Tully who says agriculture is the noblest of pursuits! How delightful when that pursuit is also profitable!
>
> Since my last letter from Tunbridge Wells, one or two incidents have occurred of which it is necessary [this word has been scraped at with a penknife but left uncorrected, no doubt to the writer’s satisfaction] I should advise my honoured mother. Our party there broke up at the end of August, right as partridge-shooting commenced. Baroness Bernstein, whose kindness to me has been unwavering, has departed for Bath, her usual winter retreat, and made me a most welcome present of a fifty-pound bill. I rode back in company with the Reverend Mr. Sampson, whose instruction I find most valuable, and my cousin Lady Maria, to Castlewood. [Could Parson Sampson have been dictating some of these remarks to Mr. Warrington?] On the way I paid a flying visit to my dear kind friends Colonel and Mrs. Lambert at Oakhurst House, who send my honoured mother their most affectionate remembrances. The youngest Miss Lambert, I grieve to say, is unwell, and her parents are some little anxious.
>
> At Castlewood I lament to say my stay was short, owing to a quarrel with my cousin William. He is a young man of violent passions, and alas, addicted to liquor, whereupon he has no control over himself. In a trifling dispute about a horse, high words arose between us, and he aimed a blow at me—or its equivalent—which my grandfather, my honoured mother’s father’s son, could not brook. I returned the blow, and felled him to the ground, whence he was carried almost senseless to bed. I sent to enquire after his health the next morning, but hearing no further news of him, set off for London, where I have remained save for brief intervals of absence.
>
> Knowing you would wish me to see my dear grandfather’s University of Cambridge, I rode thither lately in company with some friends, passing through part of Hertfordshire, and stopping overnight at the famous Bed of Ware. The October meeting was just beginning at Newmarket, near Cambridge, when I arrived. I saw the students in their gowns and caps, and rode over to the famous Heath, where races were being held—my friend Lord March’s horse Marrowbones, sired by Cleaver, won a great stake that day. It was a most amusing spectacle: the jockeys, horses, all very different to our poor races at home, and the betting something awful. The richest noblemen here mix right in with the jockeys, betting on every hand. Cambridge pleased me greatly, especially King’s College Chapel, a rich but elegant work of Gothic architecture.
>
> I have been introduced into the world, and made a member of White’s Club, where I meet gentlemen of the first fashion. My Lords Rockingham, Carlisle, Orford, Bolingbroke, and Coventry are my friends, all introduced by Lord March, of whom I have often written. Lady Coventry is a fine woman, but thin. Every lady paints here, old and young, so if you and Mountain and Fanny wish to be in fashion, I must send you out some rouge pots. Everybody plays cards: eight, ten tables at every house on every reception night. I am sorry to say all do not play fair, and some do not pay their debts. I have been obliged to sit down and do as Rome does, and have actually seen ladies I could name take my counters right out from under my nose!
>
> One day, his regiment the 20th being paraded in St. James’s Park, a friend of mine Mr. Wolfe did me the honour to present me to His Royal Highness the Captain-General, who was most gracious. A fat, jolly prince, if I may say so without disrespect, whose manners remind me of that unhappy General Braddock whom we knew to our sorrow last year. When he heard my name, and how dearest George served and fell in Braddock’s unfortunate campaign, he talked a great while with me, asked why a young fellow like me did not take up arms too, why I did not go to the King of Prussia, who is a great general, and see a campaign or two, and whether that would not be better than dawdling about at routs and card parties in London. I said I would go with all my heart, but was now an only son, on leave from my mother, and bound to tend to our estate in Virginia. His Royal Highness said Mr. Braddock had written home glowing accounts of Mrs. Esmond’s loyalty, and that he would gladly serve me. Mr. Wolfe and I have waited on him since at his house in Pall Mall. The latter, who is still quite a young man, served in the Scottish campaign with his Highness, whom Mr. Dempster so loves at home. Though he was too severe, if anything can be too severe against rebels in arms.
>
> Mr. Draper has had half the Stock, my late Papa’s property, transferred to my name. Until there can be no doubt of that painful loss in our family, which I would give my right hand to undo, the remaining stock must remain in the trustees’ name for the benefit of him who inherited it. Ah, dear mother! There is no day, scarce an hour, when I do not think of him. I wish he were by me often. I feel the better for thinking of him, and would for the honour of my family that he was representing it here instead of—
> Honoured madam, your dutiful and affectionate son,
>
> P.S. I am like your sex, who always, they say, put their best news in a postscript. I had something to tell you of a person to whom my heart is engaged. I shall write more of it later, there is no hurry. Suffice she is a nobleman’s daughter, and her family as good as our own.

Harry’s aunt, the Baroness de Bernstein, wrote separately to his mother in Virginia, her far sharper, more worldly missive penned two days prior to Harry’s own letter:
>
> My good sister, I think we have been all our lives a little more than kin and less than kind, to use the words of a poet whom your dear father loved well. When you were born in our Western Principality, my mother was not as old as Isaac’s wife was when he bore him his sons, but even then I was old enough to be your mother. Though she gave you all she could leave or give, including that small share of love that ought to have been my due, still, if we can hold goodwill toward one another we can do without affection. And you owe me a little kindness, for your son’s sake as well as your father’s, whom I loved and admired more than any man I ever knew in this world. He was greater than almost all, though he made no noise in it. I have seen hundreds of men who did make a noise, and believe me, I have found very few with such good heads and good hearts as Mr. Esmond.
>
> Had we been better acquainted, I might have given you some advice about introducing your young gentleman to Europe that you would have found wise in hindsight. You would have said afterwards, “What she counselled me was right, and had Harry done as Madam Beatrix wished, it had been better for him.” My good sister, it was not for you to know, nor for me to write to you of it when you never wrote to ask, but your boy, coming to England and Castlewood, found very few friends there—save one, his old aunt, of whom all manner of ill has been spoken for these fifty years past, and not without cause, perhaps.
>
> Now I must tell Harry’s mother what will doubtless astonish her: almost everyone who knows the boy loves him. He is prudent of his tongue, generous with his money, as bold as a lion, with an imperious, domineering way that sits very well on him. You know well enough if he is handsome or not. My dear, I like him none the less for not being over-witty or over-wise. I never cared for those “set the Thames afire” gentlemen who think themselves far cleverer than all their neighbours. Your father’s great friend Mr. Addison always struck me as a supercilious prig, and his follower Sir Dick Steele was no more pleasant in his cups than out of them. And returning to Harry, your Master Harry will certainly never set the Thames on fire with his wits. Of book-learning he is as ignorant as any lord in England, and for this I hold him none the worse. If Heaven did not give him a turn that way, there is no use trying to bend him to it.
>
> Considering the station he is to hold in his colony when he returns, and the stock he comes from, let me tell you he has not nearly enough allowance to support his station, and that narrowness of his income will make him all the more extravagant—sheer despair will make him break all bounds, even though extravagance is not natural to his character. He wants to live as well as the rest of his company, and between ourselves, he has fallen in with some of the rakes of the first rank in England. He thinks it a point of family honour not to back down, and many a time calls for ortolans and champagne when he would just as soon dine off a steak and a mug of beer.
