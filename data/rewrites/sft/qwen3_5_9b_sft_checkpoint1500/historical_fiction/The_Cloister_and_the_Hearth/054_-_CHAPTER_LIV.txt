When consciousness returned to Margaret, her head rested upon Catherine's shoulder, surrounded by the sturdy, unpretentious circle of her kin who offered their rough, heartfelt encouragement. Giles, the voice of the household, boomed that Gerard lived and wrote the finest letter humanity had ever seen, a testament to a man who, though missing from his counting house in Amsterdam, remained a beacon of kindness. "Gerard was alive," Margaret whispered, her strength failing her, yet her heart swelling with a mixture of guilt and relief. "But why did he flee to these wild shores? Why did I not keep him closer?" She gently pushed aside Catherine's arm, attempting to kneel, only to collapse back onto her sister-in-law's lap, clinging to her with a desperate, tender embrace.

"Read it, Richart," Eli commanded gently, noting the youthfulness in the boy's gaze. As Richart unfolded the parchment, his admiration for the neat, needle-point script quickly turned to exasperation regarding Gerard's absence from business. But as the reading began, the somber atmosphere lifted, replaced by the vivid warmth of Gerard's journey. He recounted his recovery in the care of a benevolent merchant, the healing of his sprained leg, and the sorrow of his broken psaltery. His voice, through the ink, traveled across the plains of Lombardy, describing a land where winter wore the colors of summer, with olive trees draped in white linen and oxen adorned like living pictures. He spoke of Venice, a city of marble and mystery, viewed from his sickbed as a dream of gold boats and palaces built on water.

Yet, it was Gerard's observations of the human soul that captivated the room. He contrasted the churlish honesty of northern Germans with the sophisticated, wine-soaked gentility of the French, noting how the latter treated their women with a reverence that bordered on idolatry, yet betrayed them with cruelty in times of strife. He described the intricate customs of the Italians—their subtlety, their obsession with cleanliness, and their peculiar morality where sin was taxed and virtue was sometimes masked as vice. Through his eyes, the family saw the breadth of the world: from the red snails of Germany to the black-fleeced sheep of France, from the brutal justice of Venice to the artistic wonders of its churches.

As the narrative reached its climax, the tone shifted abruptly. Gerard, now aboard a ship bound for Rome, described a scene at St. Mark's Square that pierced the hearts of the listeners. Amidst the grandeur of the cathedral and the treasures within, he had seen a garland of amaranths on a Dutch mast—a symbol of courtship. There, in bold letters, burned the name *Richart Eliassoens*. Tears streamed down Richart's cheeks, a surge of brotherly love crashing over him as he realized his sibling's devotion. The room fell silent, save for the soft sobs of Catherine and the gentle, tear-streaked face of little Kate, who sewed baby caps with hands that trembled.

The revelation that followed, however, shattered the fragile peace. Eli, noticing the betrayal in Gerard's account of "marriage lines," confronted Margaret. With a stinging accusation, he revealed that Martin Wittenhaagen knew of a bond between them, a fact hidden until now. The air grew thick with tension as Denys stepped forward, his voice rising in defense of the girl. He recalled how Margaret had bled for Gerard when he was hunted, how she had risked everything to protect him, calling her a traitor to the accusation of loving another. The truth, raw and painful, hung in the air: Gerard had loved her deeply, and she had loved him back, a secret kept in the shadows of their lives.

In the end, the storm cleared, leaving a trail of forgiveness and understanding. Eli, moved by the depth of Margaret's sacrifice, declared that a chair would always be set at their table for her, and for Gerard, wherever his path led. He urged them to write immediately, sending Gerard home to heal, assuring the family that distance would not sever the bonds forged in blood and love. As the evening settled, the room filled with a quiet hum of anticipation. Giles, the dwarf, sat upon a stool, listening intently as Margaret whispered the final, tender lines of Gerard's letter into his ear, her face inches from his, a striking contrast of beauty and grotesquery that seemed to capture the very essence of their intertwined fates.
