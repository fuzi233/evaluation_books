The following morning, Mr. Gryce received a dispatch from Miss Butterworth, timed almost precisely to coincide with a message sent to her by him. Her letter was brief yet definitive: "You were quite correct. So far as appears, I was the only person to lean over Mr. Adams's study table after his unfortunate death. I have had to clip the ends of my boa." His reply was equally terse: "My compliments, madam! Mr. Adams's jaws have been forced apart. A small piece of paper was found clinched between his teeth. This paper has been recovered, and will be read at the inquest. Perhaps a few favored persons may be granted the opportunity of reading it before then, notably yourself."

Between the two correspondences, the latter naturally ignited a far greater storm within the recipient. Miss Butterworth's composure was absolute, a fortress built around a secret she could not share with anyone else, which in turn fueled endless speculation among the servants regarding its source. Meanwhile, at Police Headquarters, the atmosphere shifted from idle rumor to frantic activity. The initial day's leads, gleaned from the public, had yielded little, but the second day brought a flood of information from a motley crowd of would-be informants. They recounted sightings with vivid detail—a lady of genteel bearing opening the door to an elderly gentleman in a heavy coat, a fashionable young woman describing a pair running past her, a pale youth accompanied by a girl with a beautiful gray feather in her hat—but when pressed for descriptions, their accounts dissolved into vague generalities. These stories merely confirmed what was already known, offering no new path through the labyrinth of the murder.

As night fell without resolution, the next day brought Mr. Adams's funeral. Lacking relatives or close friends, his landlord conducted the rites, while his banker stood as the chief mourner. As the body was lowered into the hearse, a contingent of detectives mingled with the throng outside, but their vigil proved futile. The problem required a different approach. Recognizing the impasse, Mr. Gryce excused himself from headquarters later that evening and ventured alone, on foot, to the dark, seemingly sealed house where the tragedy had unfolded.

With a key in hand, he entered and systematically illuminated the interior. After confirming that the study had been tidied—the cross restored to the wall, the birdcage rehung—he focused on the evidence of the violence: the shattered casings flanking the doorway, still gaping in disarray. Though the steel plate covering the vent had been shoved back into its recess, glimpses of the blue light filtering through the breach in the antechamber wall remained an unsettling feature. Demonstrating the practical efficiency of a housewife, Mr. Gryce pulled two rugs from the parlor floor and draped them over the gaps, effectively sealing off the view. Consulting his watch, he noted it was nearly nine o'clock. Taking his position behind the curtains of the parlor window, he waited.

A carriage soon halted, and a lady descended. It was Miss Butterworth. "Madam, your punctuality is equal to my own," Gryce remarked as he ushered her down the hall. "Have you instructed your coachman to wait nearby?" "Only as far as the corner," she replied, her voice steady. "He awaits your signal." "Then you need not fear remaining alone for a moment while I monitor the entrance," Gryce suggested, noting the warm, crimson illumination he had chosen for the study. "It mimics the lighting present when Mr. Adams died."

Suddenly, a voice echoed from within: "Remember Evelyn!" Miss Butterworth reacted instantly. "Oh, you have brought back the bird!" she exclaimed, contrasting it with the previous cry, "'Eva! Lovely Eva!' Do you suppose Eva and Evelyn are the same?" "Madam," Gryce interjected gently, "we face so many riddles that we shall leave this particular thread for the moment. I expect Mr. Adams's valet shortly."

Her relief was palpable. "Sir, you relieve me of an immense weight. I feared the privilege of witnessing your proposed test might be denied." "Miss Butterworth, you have earned a seat at this experiment," Gryce assured her. "Bartow has been given a key and will enter freely, as per his custom. Our role is simply to observe." "In this room?" she hesitated. "I do not think I shall like facing this madman directly." "You will not be required to," Gryce explained. "We must not startle him. To ensure he acts according to his natural impulses, free from distraction, I propose placing you where the chance of detection is nil—in this parlor, a space rarely used during Mr. Adams's lifetime."

"As soon as you extinguish the gas, however," she reminded him, "any unfamiliar light could betray us." "I shall not only extinguish the gas," Gryce declared, drawing the heavy drapes tight, "but create a small aperture for our eyes. A simple expedient, yet effective."

As Miss Butterworth found herself concealed in shadow behind a curtain, her internal reaction was a mixture of professional duty and personal unease. She felt a blush rise, perhaps, at the clandestine nature of their position, until the abrupt creak of the front door signaled the critical moment. The deaf-and-dumb valet had arrived. A hushed warning from Mr. Gryce silenced her, and she strained her ears for the inevitable.

Bartow appeared even faster than anticipated. He laid down his hat and moved with his characteristic busyness toward the portière, only to halt inches from it. Something—perhaps the memory of the scene, or the oddly placed rugs—seemed to block his entry. He turned away with a meek, mechanical expression, picked up his hat, and ascended the stairs without entering the study.

"I will follow him," Gryce whispered, assuring Miss Butterworth not to fear. "This whistle will summon assistance immediately." "I am not afraid," she protested, though her tone lacked conviction. "I would be ashamed—" Before she could finish, Gryce was already moving, returning moments later to report that the experiment had failed; the old man had retreated to his chamber to prepare for bed.

Gryce led the way back into the study, casually pressing a button on the tabletop that bathed the room in a new, violet glow. Miss Butterworth, admiring the effect on the rich furnishings, was cut short by the faint sound of descending footsteps. They scrambled back to their hiding spots just as Bartow burst into the hall, resuming his hurried pace. Crossing the threshold, he paused for a fraction of a second, as if sensing an anomaly, before stepping fully into the room. He ignored the rugs, retrieving a bottle of wine and a meticulously cleaned glass, arranging his master's dressing gown and slippers, and disappearing into the bedroom.

From their vantage point near the curtains, the watchers observed Bartow stoop over a comb Mr. Gryce had inadvertently left on the floor. The object seemed to confuse him; he shook his head, returned it to the dresser, and turned down the bed. "Poor fool!" Miss Butterworth murmured. "He remembers nothing but his routine." Suddenly, she froze. Bartow stood motionless, staring at his master's empty chair with an intensity that spoke of deep disturbance. Then, his gaze shifted to the rug where the body had lain. Awakening to the reality of the crime, his demeanor shifted violently. He began a series of actions that mirrored the struggle, then quietly exited and fled upstairs.

Emerging from concealment, Gryce and Miss Butterworth surveyed each other with shared dismay. Yet, a spark of hope ignited in the older woman. "Change the light," she urged quickly. "Let us try again. I suspect it will draw him down." Gryce raised an eyebrow. "Do you truly believe he can see through solid floors?" Undeterred, Gryce pressed another button, flooding the room with a rich blue hue. Nothing happened initially, but then, holding up a finger, Miss Butterworth cried out, "Hark!" The soft tread of descending steps confirmed her intuition.

Bartow approached with a slow, deliberate gait, reaching for the table where he expected to find a stack of books to shelve. Finding nothing, he glanced upward at the bookshelves, shook his head, and collapsed into a doze on the nearest chair. "Can you interpret this?" Miss Butterworth asked, her voice triumphant. "He came expecting books. How does he know what light burns below?" Her manner betrayed a hesitation, a naive smile breaking across her face. "When I went upstairs previously... I noticed a trifle. A very small thing." "And the halls?" Gryce prompted. "Yes. If you have patience enough to look lower—to examine the floor—you may find the key."

She pointed to the edge of the carpeting on the landing above. "Why is the woodwork so marred here? Why does someone shuffle constantly in this corner, despite the width of the stairs and the lack of a window?" Gryce, whose attention had drifted to the wall, reached over her shoulder. Pulling away a picture hanging slightly askew, he revealed a round opening pouring a beam of blue light from the adjacent study. "No window," he repeated, marveling. "Yet here is an opening answering the same purpose. Your eye, Miss Butterworth, is to be trusted. I wonder why you did not notice this earlier." "It was not hanging crooked then," she admitted.
