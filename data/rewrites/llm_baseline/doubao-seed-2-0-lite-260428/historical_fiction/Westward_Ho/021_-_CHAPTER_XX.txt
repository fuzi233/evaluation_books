The old ballad of brave Lord Willoughby hums true enough to the day:
> Full seven long hours in all men's sight This fight endured sore,
> Until our men so feeble grew, That they could fight no more.
> And then upon dead horses Full savorly they fed,
> And drank the puddle water, They could no better get.
> When they had fed so freely They kneeled on the ground,
> And gave God thanks devoutly for The favor they had found;
> Then beating up their colors, The fight they did renew;
> And turning to the Spaniards, A thousand more they slew.

When the tropical sun burst over the horizon at dawn, flooding the Caribbean with sharp, golden light, Amyas Leigh paced his deck like a caged lion. His hair hung wild and uncombed, his tunic was torn and caked with salt spray, his eyes bloodshot from a night spent alternating between roaring rage and bitter, heaving sobs for his lost brother Frank. No words can rightly sum that agony—you who have lost a kinsman you loved more than your own life will know it already, and you who have not, thank heaven for the mercy of that ignorance.
He strode back and forth, his mind teeming with wild, impossible schemes as the *Rose* heeled hard into the choppy, wind-lashed waves. He would sail straight back to that accursed villa and burn it to ash. He would storm La Guayra, put every man in the garrison to the sword to pay for Frank's life. "We can do it, lads!" he roared, turning to his crew, fist clenched. "If Drake could take Nombre de Dios, we can take La Guayra."
A roar of "Aye!" burst from every throat on deck.
"We'll take the town, and get Frank back yet," Will Cary called out, clapping Amyas on the shoulder—but Amyas shook his head. Some deep, unnameable instinct told him no port in all of New Spain could ever give back the sight of that beloved face he had watched die the night before.
"Aye, he'll have full vengeance, and no mistake. Look yonder!" He pointed out to the shoreline, where three sails bobbed not five miles to windward, between them and the far-off, snow-capped peaks of the Silla. "There are the Spanish bloodhounds we saw off Guayra yesterday, hot on our heels. Back to our stations, lads—welcome them, even if there were a dozen of the curs."
A murmur of approval rippled through the ranks. If any young hand had a moment's qualm at the thought of fighting three enemy vessels at once, it was snuffed out by the roar of older mariners and the stentorian boom of Salvation Yeo's voice.
"If there were a dozen of them, the Lord is with us still, as it is written: One of you shall chase a thousand. Clear the decks, lads, and see the glory of the Lord worked this day."
"Amen!" Cary cried, and the *Rose* was hauled even closer to the wind to meet their pursuers.

The rush of coming battle jolted Amyas out of his fog of grief. He forgot his wounds, forgot the heavy sorrow weighing on his chest; even the memory of Frank's soft, angelic last smile dimmed a little as he bustled about barking orders, and within a quarter hour his voice rang out clear, firm, and cheerful as it had been in the old days, before the loss.
"Now my masters, first we serve God, then we breakfast, and after that we clear for action."
Parson Jack Brimblecombe led the daily prayers, the special litany before a sea fight, his honest voice trembling as he slipped a line into the Prayer for All Conditions of Men, even over Amyas's despair: "And we pray especially for our dear brother Mr. Francis Leigh, held captive among the idolaters, if it be thy will to spare him." The crew rose from their knees together.
"Now to breakfast," Amyas said. "A Frenchman fights best fasting, a Dutchman drunk, an Englishman full, and a Spaniard when the devil is in him—and that's always."
"Good beef and a just cause are a match for any devil alive," Cary laughed. "Come down below, captain—you have to eat too."
Amyas shook his head, pried the tiller out of the steersman's hands, and bade the man go below to fill his belly. Cary vanished below decks, and came back five minutes later with a heaped plate of bread and salt beef and a great jack of ale, coaxing and cajoling till he had got every last bite and drop down Amyas's throat like a nurse feeding a sullen, grieving child, before he darted back out of sight, tears rolling fast down his own cheeks.
Amyas stayed at the tiller, steering. In one single night, his face had aged seven years. A terrible, iron calm had settled over him. Woe to any man that crossed his path that day.
"There are three of them, you see, my masters," he called as the rest of the crew trundled back on deck. "The big galleon up front, two galleys trailing astern of her. The big ship is a fast race-built craft, but if we can just get the wind of her, we'll see if our low, handy build isn't more than a match for her long, unwieldy length. We'll give her the slip, and take the two galleys first."
"I thank the Lord," Yeo boomed, "for giving such wise heart to so young a general—a very David and Daniel, saving your presence, lads. If any man dare not follow him, let him be counted among the cursed men of Meroz and Succoth. Amen! Silas Staveley, go knock that young monkey of a powder boy over the head! What's he doing lingering up here instead of manning the powder room door?"
Yeo set about tending his guns with the sure, steady hand of a man who knew exactly what he was doing, who meant to do it thoroughly, and who had unshakable faith he was doing God's work.
Every man set to his task. Though the *Rose* had been kept in near-perfect fighting order all through the previous night, there was still deck-clearing to do, nettings to lace to catch falling rigging, makeshift bulwarks to rig, waist cloths to string, tops to fortify, pikes to grease, yards to sling, sheets and tacks to double. It was enough preparation to satisfy even the famously pedantic Sir Richard Hawkins himself. Amyas took charge of the poop deck, Cary of the forecastle, Yeo the master gunner of the main deck, and Drew the ship's master took his post in the waist. All was ready, and more than ready, long before the leading Spanish galleon was within two miles of them.

Now, while the mastiffs of England and the bloodhounds of Spain draw ever closer across the rolling swells, both thirsting for each other's blood, let us pause a minute to consider the cause that let the English of that day face and conquer Spanish fleets twice, three times their size, boasting that through the whole of the Spanish war, only one royal queen's ship, the *Revenge*, and (if memory serves) one private man-of-war, Sir Richard Hawkins's *Dainty*, ever struck their colors to the enemy.
What was it that let Sir Richard Grenville's *Revenge*, in that last terrible fight off the Azores, hold off eight Spanish galleons for twelve hours before she struck, two of them three times her size sinking beside her, all her masts gone, repelling three boarding attempts, defying a whole fleet of fifty four ships that circled her like hounds around a dying lion? What let young Richard Hawkins's *Dainty*, half her guns useless thanks to a careless or traitorous gunner, keep up a running fight for three days against two Spanish ships her equal in size, double her firepower, ten times her number of men? What let Sir George Cary's *Content* fight single-handed from seven in the morning till eleven at night against four great galleons and two galleys, her heaviest gun a mere nine-pounder, with only thirteen men fit for duty for half the battle? What let two London Turkey merchantmen, the *Merchant Royal* and the *Tobie*, and their three small consorts cripple an entire Spanish galley fleet sent to intercept them off Pantellaria in the Mediterranean, and sail back triumphant through the Straits of Gibraltar? And what, soon after, let the English fleet shatter the Great Armada of 1588, losing nothing more than one small pinnace and one gentleman of note?
There were many reasons. First, the English ships were far shorter than the Spanish, which with the rig of the day gave them maneuverability so sharp it left the Spaniards completely bewildered. Camden himself wrote of the 1588 fight that the English ships charged the enemy with marvellous agility, fired their broadsides, and darted away into deep water again, their shots striking true every time, while the great heavy Spanish galleons lumbered too slow to escape. Second, the Spanish warships built for the West Indies were flush-decked, or "race-built" to carry more cargo, leaving every man on deck exposed, while the English raised their poop and forecastle as high as possible, added stout bulkheads and "close fight" stockades to turn their decks into a line of separate small forts, loopholed for musket fire and arrows.
But the greatest advantage of all was the men themselves. The Elizabethan sailor was every inch an amphibious jack-of-all-trades, capable of sewing a sail, carpentering a broken spar, manning a cannon, or fighting hand to hand with equal skill. The whole nation was required by law to practice archery from boyhood, to treat swordplay and quarterstaff as a normal part of a man's education, the fiercest free people on earth, every man fighting for his own skin, trusted to use his own wits to finish the job he was given. Officers did not hold themselves above the men: Drake once snapped at a group of prissy gentleman adventurers on his round the world voyage that he would not stand for any man, no matter his rank, refusing to lay a hand to a rope. Elizabeth's court promoted brave, sharp men no matter their birth, and the merchant navy was a nursery for great warriors, where every captain, high born or low, learned to fight for their lives since Spanish crews would fire on English merchant ships on sight to wipe out competition.
No wonder English mutinies were almost unheard of, while Spanish ships saw near constant unrest. The Spaniards had crippled their navy with absurd rules of rank and etiquette that would eventually freeze their whole empire to death. They split their crews into three separate, unequal classes: soldiers who never touched a line, gunners who were almost all foreign hirelings from Flanders or Germany, and mariners treated like slaves, forced to work day and night on the open decks in sun, storm, rain, with no shelter at all, forbidden to rest below. No wonder the free English seamen always beat them.

Back to the fight. The big Spanish galleon is now within two musket shots of the *Rose*, her golden royal standard flapping bright above her poop, her trumpets blaring challenge across the breeze from a dozen brazen throats. The *Rose* answers with a cheer of her own, St George's cross flying high on her poop, the arms of Leigh and Cary side by side on her foremast, above the town arms of their home port of Bideford.
"Silence the trumpets! Waits, play up 'Fortune My Foe'! God and the Queen be with us!" Amyas roared.
Up rose the beloved old Elizabethan ballad, blaring from cornet and sackbut, fife and drum. Parson Jack Brimblecombe had planted himself among the musicians on the poop, sawing away at his violin as fiercely as old Volker the minstrel of the Nibelungenlied.
"Well played, Jack! Your elbow flies like a lamb's tail!" Amyas laughed, forcing a light jest.
"It shall fly to better work than a fiddle bow soon enough, sir, if I get the chance—"
"Steady the helm!" Amyas cut in. "What's the Dago up to now?"
The Spaniard, who had been charging down full sail before the wind, was furling all her light canvas.
"He doesn't know what to make of us waiting for him so bold," the steersman said.
"Aye he does, and he means to fight, too!" another hand called. "See, he's hauling up his mainsail foot. He's trying to keep the wind of us."
"Let him try," Amyas grinned. "Keep her closer still. No one fires till I give the word. Man the starboard guns, all small arms men wait. Pass the word down to Yeo to fire high, take out their rigging."
The Spaniard's bow gun boomed once, the shot flying far wide, then two more in quick succession, while the English crew fidgeted, checking the priming on their muskets, loosening their arrows in their quivers.
"Lie down, men, and sing a psalm. I'll call you when I need you. Closer still, helmsman. We'll see if a short, handy ship can't out-sail a long lumbering one two points closer to the wind."
Amyas was right. The Spaniard wanted to cross the *Rose's* bows to rake her, but dared not, knowing the English would fire into her full length before she could complete the turn. Her only other move was to head up into the wind and wait for the *Rose* to come to her.
Amyas laughed to himself. "Hold on a little longer. There's more ways to kill a cat than choking it with cream. Drew, are your men ready?"
"Aye aye, sir!"
They closed fast, till they were within pistol shot.
"Ready about!"
The *Rose* turned like an eel, slipping onto the opposite tack directly under the Spaniard's stern. The Spaniard gaped in astonishment at the speed of the maneuver, hesitated a heartbeat, then tried to turn about too—but it was too late. Her great lumbering hull hung dead in the eye of the wind, and Amyas's bowsprit almost scraped her quarter as the *Rose* drifted slowly ten yards from her stern.
"Fire! With a will!" Amyas roared. "At them, archers! At them, musketeers!"
A storm of bar shot, chain shot, round shot and canister swept the whole length of the Spanish deck, while through the smoke musket balls and the deadly English cloth-yard arrows whistled through the air. Down went the helmsman, every man on the poop. Down went the mizzen topmast. The stern windows and quarter galleries shattered. When the smoke cleared, the gilded, ornate painting of Our Lady of Sorrows, her heart pierced by seven swords, that had hung framed on the Spaniard's stern, was smashed to splinters. The great golden Spanish standard that had flaunted over the heads of the crew a moment before now trailed in the water. The galleon, her tiller shot away and her helmsman dead, staggered helplessly for a minute, then luffed up dead into the wind.
"Well done, men of Devon!" Amyas shouted, and a roar of cheers split the sky.
"She's struck!" some man cried as the hurrahs died down.
"Not a bit of it," Amyas said. "Hold on, helmsman. Let them patch their tackle while we go settle the two galleys."
The *Rose* shot merrily away, long before the galleon could get her rigging sorted, two miles to windward, where the two galleys were sweeping down on them fast, forty oars to a side.
They were venomous craft, snout pointed like swordfish, crammed full of soldiers in their square forward forecastle, cannon muzzles grinning from portholes facing dead ahead, so they could fire constantly at any ship before them. Their long low waists were packed full of chained slaves, five or six to every oar, and the English could see the overseers pacing the central gangway with whips in their hands. The raised stern quarterdeck was crammed with more soldiers, their armor glinting in the sun. The English could hear the crack of whips, the wild, animal yells of the beaten slaves in response, the rhythmic rattle of oars, the great heave "Ha!" from the slaves with every stroke, the oaths and curses of the overseers, and a sickly, musky stench drifting over the water, like the smell of a kennel of hounds. More than one young sailor shuddered at the sight of these floating hells, whose cruelties they had heard about for years from escaped shipmates who had endured years of captivity chained to the oars.
"Must we fire on the slaves?" more than one man asked.
Amyas sighed.
"Spare them as much as you can, in God's name. But if they try to run us down, we will have to rake their decks, and God forgive us for it."
The two galleys were coming abreast, forty yards apart. Amyas knew he could not outmaneuver their oars the way he had outmaneuvered the galleon's sails. To turn and run would just trap them between the two galleys and the damaged galleon behind.
He made up his mind, as always, to play the desperate hand.
"Lay her head up into the wind, helmsman. We wait for them."
The galleys opened fire from their bow guns at musket shot range, but the choppy, rough sea threw their aim wildly off. Amyas held his fire, as usual.
The crew stood at their stations, lips compressed, not knowing what was coming next. Amyas towered motionless on the quarter deck, giving his orders calm and decisive, and the men saw he did not fear for himself, so they trusted him completely.
The Spaniards cheered for joy, seeing him wait for them—was the Englishman mad? The two galleys converged fast, planning to ram him full on, one on each bow.
