Great black clouds, ominous and heavy, began to gather overhead, heralding the onset of another wet winter. The terrible storms that followed forced us to abandon our daily excursions. Even within the walls of our spacious home, surrounded by varied and engaging tasks, time seemed to drag with oppressive weight. The spirits of everyone were low, and even brief intervals of fair weather, offering opportunities for swift rides, failed to lift our gloom.

Fritz and I both noticed this despondency. "Father," Fritz asked, "why should we not build a canoe? Something swifter and easier to handle than the clumsy vessels we currently possess? I often dream of a light skiff in which I might skim across the water."

The idea sparked enthusiasm among all of us, except for my wife. She, who harbored a deep fear of the sea, argued vehemently against it. "Our chances of drowning with our pinnace and existing canoe are already sufficient," she declared. "There is no necessity for adding another craft to tempt us onto the treacherous waves."

However, her fears were quickly dispelled. I assured her that the boat I planned to build would not be a flimsy shell, but a sturdy, safe craft modeled after the Greenlanders' *cajac*. I resolved not only to keep the children occupied but to create a masterpiece of engineering—a robust and highly serviceable canoe.

The boys were eager, and construction began immediately. We built a skeleton of whalebone, reinforcing the sides and forming a continuous deck from split bamboo canes that spanned the entire length of the boat, leaving only a central square opening for the occupant. The work consumed our full attention, and by the time we finished, the rains had ceased, revealing a brilliant, golden sun.

Our front door was barely wide enough for the boat to pass through, so we completed the final assembly outdoors. We carefully lined the sides and deck with seal-skin, sealing every seam thoroughly with rubberized material (*caoutchouc*). The resulting *cajac* looked peculiar—curved and strange—but it was remarkably light, lifting easily with a single hand. When launched, it bounced upon the water like a giant rubber ball.

Fritz was unanimously chosen as the canoe's captain. Yet, before his mother would allow him to board, she insisted on creating a swimming suit. "If his boat takes a puncture from a jagged rock or a shark's fin and collapses," she reasoned, "he must at least have a chance to stay afloat."

Though I did not share her belief that the canoe was as fragile as a soap bubble, I gladly helped her. The garment she produced was unusual, resembling a double waistcoat made of linen treated with a solution of India rubber. The seams were coated with rubber, rendering the outfit perfectly airtight. We designed a small valve hole through which air could be pumped to inflate the space between the layers.

Meanwhile, I watched with delight as the island's vegetation exploded into life. Seeds we had scattered were sprouting rapidly, promising magnificent crops. The veranda was adorned with fragrant, colorful creepers climbing toward the pillars, and the air buzzed with birds. Life teemed everywhere.

Soon, the swimming suit was complete. One sunny afternoon, Fritz gravely donned the inflated garment and stepped into the water. Amidst roars of laughter from his brothers, he paddled effortlessly across the bay toward Shark Island, where we followed in one of our boats. The experiment was a resounding success. Despite mocking the outfit, Ernest, Jack, and Franz begged their mother to make them similar suits.

On our return journey, we visited the colony of animals we had established the previous autumn. They were thriving; footprints showed that the antelopes had discovered and utilized the shelter we built. Satisfied that we could do no more for them, we scattered handfuls of maize and salt, then crossed to the opposite side of the island.

The shoreline was strewn with beautiful shells and delicate corals, which the boys collected for their museum. Great piles of multicolored seaweed lay at the water's edge. My wife, noting its utility, directed the boys to gather it for storage.

Back on the beach, I noticed my wife selecting specific seaweed leaves with serrated, saw-like edges. At home, she carefully washed and dried them in the oven. Curiosity piqued, I approached her. "Are these leaves intended to replace tobacco?" I asked. "Do you crave its aroma?"

She smiled, knowing her aversion to the herb. "Do you not think a mattress stuffed with these leaves would be wonderfully cool in summer?" Her twinkling eyes suggested a deeper mystery, fueling my intrigue.

Later, after a tiring expedition, the family lounged on the veranda. My wife appeared bearing a porcelain dish filled with translucent, shimmering jelly. "Ambrosia!" Fritz exclaimed upon tasting it. It was exquisite.

"Aha!" she laughed. "Is this not a superior substitute for tobacco? Behold the produce of my mysterious seaweed."

"My dear wife," I said, "where did you acquire such ingredients?"

"While visiting my Dutch friends at the Cape," she replied, "I saw this dish and instantly recognized the leaves from Shark Island. Once you know the secret, it is simple: soak the leaves in fresh water daily for a week, then boil them with orange juice, citron, and sugar."

Delighted by the delicacy, we thanked her profusely. The boys immediately rushed to the island to gather more leaves. I joined them, eager to inspect the plantations we had cultivated. Everything flourished miraculously; palms and mangroves grew rapidly, and seeds scattered randomly among rocky crevices had taken root, beginning to clothe the barren boulders.

High among the rocks, we found a sparkling spring, clearly used by the thirsty antelopes. Turning our attention to Whale Island, we found evidence of the rabbits' voracious appetite. Young tree shoots bore the marks of countless teeth, sparing only the coconut palms. Determined to protect the grove, I and the boys erected a hedge of prickly thorns around each palm stem.

Before returning, I noticed that some seaweed had also been gnawed by the rabbits, prompting me to collect samples for later study. The skeleton of the whale, stripped clean by birds and bleached white by the elements, caught our eye. I decided to salvage ten or twelve vertebrae joints, rolling them down to the boat for future use.

A new project formed in my mind: a crushing machine to process materials efficiently. Stone was impractical, but the whale vertebrae offered a unique solution. The following morning, I sought suitable timber. Upon reaching the stables, I learned the boys had departed early with guns and traps, leaving me the choice between a bull and a buffalo. Content with the horse, Storm, I saddled up.

Crossing the bridge, I found the cassava field devastated. Examining the tracks, which resembled those of pigs, I followed the trail. It led past our old potato patch, where the dogs Floss and Bruno suddenly barked loudly. A whole family of sows stood at bay, snarling and bristling, causing the dogs to hesitate.

Raising my gun, I fired twice. Two pigs fell, and the rest fled, pursued by the dogs. Retrieving the fallen carcasses, I continued into the forest. Soon, I located a tree ideal for my machinery, marked it, and returned home.

Ernest helped me flay the young porkers, handing them to my wife for dinner. Late in the evening, the sound of hooves signaled the boys' return. Jack thundered in on his horse, followed by Fritz and Franz carrying game bags. They unloaded their haul: four birds, a kangaroo, twenty muskrats, a monkey, two hares, and numerous beaver rats. Fritz also tossed down a bundle of thistles.

Jack, exhilarated, described riding an ostrich—"flying" along at terrifying speeds—and requested glass-eye goggles to protect himself from the wind. I gently declined, citing two reasons: my refusal to grant unnecessary demands and his proven capability to provide his own protection. I commended his hard work and sent him to check on the animals before supper.

Dinner was a feast of roasted pork and fragrant mead. Fritz recounted the day's adventures in detail: trapping muskrats with carrots, catching beaver rats and a duck-billed platypus, and hunting the kangaroo. He presented cuttings from cinnamon and apple trees to my wife. Finally, he urged me to examine the thistles, believing them useful for manufacturing wool.

Recognizing them immediately as fuller's teasles, plants used to raise the nap of cloth, we resolved to prepare the harvest the following morning. As exhaustion settled over the boys, I concluded the day with evening prayers.

The sheer volume of skins to remove proved laborious. I recalled a large syringe found in the surgeon's chest during the shipwreck. After modifying it with valves and a perforated stopper, I created a powerful air pump. The boys watched in stunned silence as I hung the kangaroo by its hind legs, made a small incision, and injected air with force.

As I pumped, the skin expanded, distending the creature until it became a shapeless mass. The internal air chambers, stretching the delicate fibers holding the skin to the flesh, eventually snapped under pressure. With a few decisive cuts to sever remaining ligaments, the hide slipped off with perfect ease.
