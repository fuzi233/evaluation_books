Five years had turned their wheel since last we looked upon North Devon; and now it was a clear, hushed morning in November, with such a cold brightness in the air that every sound seemed cut sharp as a knife, and yet carried kindly, as if the world itself were listening.

Bideford church-bells had no mind to keep their accustomed hours. They had been set to ring for the daily service; but instead of falling into sober measure, they broke out again and again into sudden, laughing peals, as though the ropes were pulled by boys who could not help their joy. The very town answered them: streets were hung with garlands and bright cloths; tapestry leaned from windows; the pool was thick with shipping dressed in flags; and the guns, great and small, spoke out in hearty thunder, that rolled away over the water and came back softened along the mud-flats and reeds.

The day smelt of smoke and wet wool and horses—every stable crammed, every yard alive with stamping, jingling tackle, grooms shouting, stirrups clinking against stone. Sir Richard Grenville’s house, hard by, was no longer a gentleman’s quiet dwelling, but a kind of cheerful siege: men running, boots thudding on boards, platters going in and out, drink poured without counting, saddles flung down, reins gathered up again. From the kitchen door there drifted the steady, honest breath of a great household at full stretch—meat roasting, bread just broken, ale warmed by the crush of bodies and the long work of the morning. Even if a man had come deaf, he might have known by that smell alone that the town kept holiday.

Up the little churchyard streamed the gentry of North Devon, and with them their wives and daughters, bright as the banners overhead—faces pale with cold and excitement, cheeks warmed by the walk, voices lowered and quick, shoes whispering through frost-stiff grass. Countess of Bath came in state, Sir Richard bare-headed beside her because her Earl was away with the Queen in London. Bassets from Umberleigh, Carys from Clovelly, Fortescues from Wear and Buckland and every quarter, Coles, Stukelys, St. Legers, Coffins, Coplestones even from Eggesford far off—each family bringing its own train, as if the whole shire had been poured out into that narrow place.

Sir John Chichester of Ralegh came after the old patriarchal manner, with his eight daughters in file behind him and three of his famous sons; and he met at the gate his cousin of Arlington, who seemed to have emptied a nursery into the street—daughters and nineteen sons, the last not yet past the town-hall while the first were already near the lychgate, laughing and making way for the elder branch. Within the church there was the usual busy justice of place and precedence: sour looks between high-born matrons, small pushes masked as courtesies, whisperings that stung more sharply than the November air. The churchwardens and sidesmen—who had never before been asked to marshal so glittering a company—bustled red-faced and breathless, rods in hand, until at last they were driven, almost to pleading, to beg help of Sir Richard himself: who was elder, who younger, who bore eight quarterings and who but four, lest a day of thanksgiving should end by setting half the county at feud. The old men were safely penned in the corporation pews; the young men sought only a corner where they might see the ladies.

And then, as if some hand had laid a cloth over the whole place, there fell a sudden silence. Heads turned. Eyes drew toward the door. Outside, a far-off music approached—flutes and hautboys, drums and trumpets—braying and screaming and thundering up the street with a merry violence which made the very stones seem to thrill; then it ceased at the church doors. Down went the churchwardens in a flutter; through the congregation ran a whisper like wind in dry leaves; and more than one woman’s breath broke into a sob that was half laugh, half prayer.

The wonder of the day entered, and the rector began—not the morning service, but the old thanksgiving appointed after a victory at sea.

Four weather-beaten mariners came first, their hard coats trimmed and knotted with ribbons by hands that had waited and feared and labored; but before them walked a figure at once strange and familiar: a beardless boy, yet built like a young Hercules, towering a head and shoulders above the crowd, golden hair falling loose upon his shoulders as if he had come out of some old song into the plain wooden church. Devon eyes devoured him. He went straight, with the others, as if drawn by a cord, up to the altar; and there the five knelt at the rails.

Then all the gaze of the church turned to one pew, where Mistress Leigh of Burrough hid her face between her hands. Her hood shook with the soundless trembling of joy, the way a tree trembles when the wind has passed through it and left it living.

For this was fellow-feeling, as it had been of old in merry England—county and town; and these were Devon men, and men of Bideford: Amyas Leigh of Burrough, John Staveley, Michael Heard, Jonas Marshall of Bideford, and Thomas Braund of Clovelly; and they, first of all English mariners, had sailed round the world with Francis Drake, and were come home to give God thanks.

It is a long tale how such a thing befell; and to make it plain we must go backward a little, almost to the place where we last set our foot.

A twelvemonth and more after Mr. Oxenham’s going, young Amyas had kept, for the most part, to the promise he had made—quiet enough, save for the sudden fierce outbursts that belong to all young male creatures, and to boys most of all who have any strength of blood and will. His book-learning mended little; his home-learning did. He grew daily more handy with bow and horse and sword—especially in the old buckler-play—when his father, going down upon business to the Exeter Assizes, took the gaol-fever from the prisoners (as too often in those days), sickened in the very court, and died within a week.

So Mistress Leigh was left a widow a little past forty, with God above her and a young lion-cub at her hand to be tamed for this life and the next.

She had loved her husband fervently and holily; and he—though scholar, courtier, soldier—had been a disappointed man. His estate had been pinched by his father’s folly and his own youth’s ambition, which carried him up to Court to spend both heart and purse upon a shadow. He had the gifts of many men, and lacked chiefly the gift of using them; and in him there was a certain shy pride, a delicate honor—call it what you will—which kept him always from playing the winning game in the very world whose prizes he desired; and so he revenged himself upon it by continual grumbling. Yet at last, by good fortune, he met at Elizabeth’s Court a fair young Miss Foljambe of Derbyshire, weary, as he was, of the world’s sins (though she had seen less of them); and they pleased each other so well that, though the Queen grumbled—because the lady dared to marry, and the gentleman dared to adore any one but herself—they were suffered to vanish from that little Babylon at Whitehall and settle at Burrough. In his wife he found a treasure, and knew it.

Mistress Leigh had been from her youth one of those noble old English churchwomen—without superstition, without severity—who are among the fairest features of that hard heroic time. Yet a melancholy had dwelt with her always, for memory carried her back to days when to be Protestant was to live with fire at one’s shoulder. She could remember, five-and-twenty years before, the burning of poor blind Joan Waste at Derby, and of Mistress Joyce Lewis too, a lady born. In dreams, even now, she heard again her mother’s bitter cries to God—either to spare her that torment, or to give her strength to endure it as others had endured.

For her mother had been one of Queen Catherine’s bedchamber women, the friend and disciple of Anne Askew; and she had sat in Smithfield, blood curdled, to see that hapless beauty of Henry’s Court carried in a chair—so broken by the rack—to her fiery doom beside her fellow-courtier Mr. Lascelles; while the heavens themselves, it seemed, spoke wrath and grief in thunder, and heavy drops hissed upon the crackling pile.

Therefore sadness hung about Mistress Leigh all her life, deepening in Queen Mary’s days when, being a known Protestant and heretic, she was forced to hide among the hills and caverns of the Peak, saved only by her husband’s tenants’ love and by his own bold vow that, good Catholic as he was, he would run through the body any constable, justice, priest—aye, bishop or cardinal—who dared serve the Queen’s warrant upon his wife.

So she escaped. Yet the shadow remained; and the hem of that dark mantle fell upon the young girl who had shared the hidings and the wanderings; who, when she came to be a wife, gave herself utterly up to God.

And yet—this is worth the saying—she did not give herself to God in any barren way that forgets hearth and neighbor. She gave herself to her husband and her children, and to the poor of Northam Town; and she was none the less welcome among Grenvilles and Fortescues and Chichesters and all gentle houses round, who honored her husband’s gifts and were glad of his wit. She had her private austerities—often gently rebuked by him—yet without one superstitious thought of buying favor with pain. Her spirit was schooled in the freest and loftiest doctrine of Luther; and that little mystic Alt-Deutsch Theologie was her counsel and her comfort, by day and night.

So now, when she stood alone, she was still lovely in face and figure; lovelier yet for the divine calm that brooded over look and word and gesture, a sweetness ripened by storm as well as sunshine, which this world had not given and could not take away. No wonder Sir Richard and Lady Grenville loved her; no wonder her children worshiped her.

And Amyas—when the first burst of grief had spent itself and he could see where he stood—felt that a new life had begun. His mother was no longer to think and act for him only; he must think and act for her. Therefore, the very day after his father’s funeral, when school-hours were done, he did not come straight home, but went boldly into Sir Richard Grenville’s house and asked to see his godfather.

“You must be my father now, sir,” he said, and stood to it.

Sir Richard looked at the boy’s broad strong face and swore a great and holy oath, “by oak, and ash, and thorn,” that he would be a father to him and a brother to his mother, for Christ’s sake. Lady Grenville took the boy by the hand and walked home with him to Burrough; and there the two fair women fell on each other’s necks and wept—one for the loss that had been, the other, by a prophetic instinct, for the like loss that was to come. Sweet St. Leger knew her husband’s fiery spirit would not leave his body in a quiet bed; death would find him, as he prayed almost nightly, sword in hand upon the field of duty and fame.

They vowed sisterhood and kept it. After that, things at Burrough went on much as before: Amyas rode, shot, boxed, wandered the quay at Sir Richard’s side; for Mistress Leigh was too wise to change one tittle of the training Amyas’s father had judged best for his younger son.

It was enough that her elder son had taken, of his own accord, the very way of life in which she would fain have molded both.

Frank—God’s wedding-gift to that pure love—had won honor early. He went from Bideford school to Exeter College, and there made friendship with Sir Philip Sidney and other young men of rank and hope. In the summer of 1572, traveling toward Heidelberg, he passed through Paris with letters to Walsingham at the English Embassy; by that chance he met Sidney again, and by that chance too he saved his own life (as Sidney did his) in the Massacre of St. Bartholomew. At Heidelberg he stayed two years, winning love and praise, refusing Sidney’s entreaties to follow him into Italy; for he scorned to burden his parents and so became tutor to two young German princes. After a time he took them down to Padua “to perfect them,” as he wrote home, “according to his insufficiency, in all princely studies.”

Friends he had everywhere: princes, magnificos, learned doctors—such letters and diplomas he carried back and forth that a lesser man would have been puffed up by the very weight of them. He tasted Italy as a careful man tastes a rare dish—eager, attentive, resolving to remember. He talked of sonnets with Tasso, of history with Sarpi; he listened, half awed, half unbelieving, to Galileo’s daring; he brought his pupils to Venice to have their portraits painted by Paul Veronese; he watched the merchant princes on the Rialto and the argosies, and saw Palladio’s palaces. He stood before Tintoretto’s huge tempests of color; and by private intercession in high places he was admitted to that sacred room where old Titian, long-bearded and undimmed of eye, lingered amid a pantheon of his own making, telling tales of Bellini and Raphael and Michael Angelo, of St. Peter’s building, Venice’s fires, Rome’s sack, and the kings and poets long gone to their account—showing too the brush which Francis the First had stooped to pick up for him.

Frank even went to Rome—much to the scandal of good Protestants at home—and saw the Pope of that day, whom Sidney (in his worldly frankness) calls a “right good fellow” not yet eaten to death. He saw the Vatican’s frescos; he heard Palestrina beneath St. Peter’s dome, presiding over the performance of his own music, and fell half in love with those luscious strains—till he woke, as from enchantment, by remembering that under that same dome men had given thanks to God for blood in Paris streets and shrieking women and heaps of insulted corpses.

A few months before his father died, he took his pupils back to Germany and was dismissed with rich gifts. Mistress Leigh’s heart beat high at the thought of his return; but within a month after the death came a long letter from Frank, full of the Alps and the Waldensian valleys, where he had talked with the Barbes about late persecutions; and full too of Latin quotations, as was his way. He wrote of Padua, where he had met the learned Stephanus Parmenius—Budaeus from his native place—who persuaded him to go down the Danube to Buda and see the learning for which Hungary was famed. He wrote again and again thereafter to the father whom he believed still living; but no answer reached him for nearly two years. At last, fearing calamity, he hurried home to find his mother widowed—and Amyas gone to the South Seas with Captain Drake of Plymouth.

Even then Frank was not allowed to remain long in Devon. Sir Richard, to whom idleness seemed unrighteous, had him up and doing before six months were out, and sent him off to Court to Lord Hunsdon. There, being as delicately fair as Amyas was huge and strong, he found place in some office of the Queen’s household by Carew’s help and Sidney’s and Leicester’s; and he basked in the full sunshine of favor, ladies’ eyes, chivalries and euphuisms of Gloriana’s fairyland, and in the close friendship of Sidney—returned with honor in 1577 from his delicate mission to Vienna, under color of condoling with the new Emperor Rodolph, while in truth he carried the hopes and fears of Protestant leaders abroad. Frank found him in 1579 as lovely and loving as ever, at five-and-twenty acknowledged among the most remarkable men in Europe—patron of letters, counselor of warriors and statesmen, confidant of William of Orange and Languet and Du Mornay and the rest.

Mistress Leigh, who had long since learned to have no self, but to live in her children, submitted without a murmur. She only said, smiling, to her stern friend—

“You took away my mastiff-pup, and now you must needs have my fair greyhound also.”

“Would you have your fair greyhound, dear lady,” Sir Richard answered, “grow into a tall true Cotswold dog that can pull down a stag of ten, or one of those smooth-skinned poppets which Florence ladies lead about with a ring of bells round its neck and a flannel farthingale over its loins?”

So she yielded; and after a few months she was repaid by a letter sent through Sir Richard—written by none other than Gloriana herself—thanking her for “the loan of that most delicate and flawless crystal, the soul of her excellent son,” praising him beyond my room to set down, and exalting the poor mother above Cornelia: for Cornelia had shown her jewels and kept them; but this woman, having two as precious, had lent both to country and Queen, who held herself indebted and would repay as became both giver and receiver. The sweet mother bedewed the letter with holy tears and laid it in the cedar-box that held her household treasures, beside Frank’s diplomas and recommendations—Latin which she spelled patiently over though she understood not a word, hoping always to light upon that dear phrase excellentissimus noster Franciscus Leighius Anglus, which was all in all to a mother’s heart.

But why did Amyas go to the South Seas?

For two causes—each of which has driven many a lad farther and worse: first, because of an old schoolmaster; secondly, because of a young beauty. Take them in order.

Vindex Brimblecombe—once servitor of Exeter College, Oxford, and commonly called Sir Vindex after the fashion of the time—was master of the grammar-school at Bideford. He was in truth a godly, kind-hearted pedant at root; but like many schoolmasters in those days of the birch, his heart had been toughened by long license to inflict pain upon those weaker than himself. It may be healthful enough for the sufferer, being short and bodily; but for the executioner it is too often a certain way to scrape away chivalry and tenderness, and sometimes even self-command. Sir Vindex felt, in his way, that it was now his duty to take especial care of the fatherless boy whom he strove to cram with qui, quae, quod; but the fruit of that new responsibility was chiefly that Amyas’s floggings increased rapidly—from two a week to one each day—not without consequences to Sir Vindex in the end.

All this while Amyas never once lost sight of his darling desire—the sea. When he could not wander on the quay and stare at the shipping, or go down to the pebble-ridge at Northam and sit there, devouring with hungry eyes the great broad ocean that seemed to beckon him outward into boundless space, he consoled himself in school by drawing ships and imaginary charts upon his slate instead of minding his “humanities.”

It fell out one afternoon that he was deep in a map—a bird’s-eye view of an island—with a great castle on it and at the gate a dragon terrible to see; while in the foreground came what was meant for a gallant ship with a great flag aloft, but which, being crowded with a forest of lances, looked more like a porcupine carrying a signpost. At the roots of those lances he had made many little round o’s for heads, whereby was signified Amyas and his schoolfellows about to slay the dragon and rescue the beautiful princess imprisoned in the enchanted tower. To behold this marvel, the boys at the desk must needs club their heads together, the more boldly because Sir Vindex, after dinner as his custom was, lay back in his chair and slept the sleep of the just.
