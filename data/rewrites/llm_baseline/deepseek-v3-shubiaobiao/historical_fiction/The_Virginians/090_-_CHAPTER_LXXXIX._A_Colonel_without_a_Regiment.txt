Upon concluding my visit to my brother, and seeing my wife and child safely returned to Richmond, I rode to Williamsburg to confer with the Governor on the rising unrest spreading through the colonies. Delegates had been chosen to attend the Continental Congress in Philadelphia, while Massachusetts simmered with rebellion. Though Maryland and Pennsylvania still professed loyalty, and the Carolinas and Georgia held firm for the Crown, Virginia’s ancient allegiance was now in question.

The Governor’s circle brimmed with fervent Loyalists, eager to crush what they called treason. My brother Harry’s name was often spat in contempt—blamed for his radical leanings, influenced, they claimed, by his wife and his friend at Mount Vernon. Yet I knew Harry, like many, sought reconciliation before revolt. He was no traitor, only a man pushed to desperation.

We drank the King’s health with fervor, swore to uphold his rights, and dreamed of a royal prince ruling America. Some even suggested offering Harry a command to lure him back to loyalty. I wrote to him, though with little hope, repeating arguments we had worn thin in past debates. Our mother, ever zealous, assailed him with Scripture and threats, only to receive a cool, well-reasoned rebuttal—likely penned with the help of his Whig-leaning clergyman.

When I rode to Fanny’s Mount, I found only my sister-in-law. Harry had gone west, she said, to inspect horses—a flimsy excuse. We both knew the truth. War was coming.

“If fate deals kindly,” I told her, “our mother’s estate shall pass to Harry, for I’ve land enough in England.” Her eyes brightened at that, though she feigned fairness. “Give my love to Theo,” she said, and with that, I left.

The Governor and I agreed the time had come to arm. I was named Colonel of the Westmoreland Defenders—a grand title for a phantom regiment. Hagan, ever loyal, became our chaplain; my mother stitched our colors; Dempster, my old tutor, scoured the Carolinas for recruits. But men were scarce. Lexington had ignited the colonies. Farmers now stood against British regulars, and Breed’s Hill became a rallying cry.

Then came the Governor’s betrayal. He fled by night to a warship, abandoning his post, leaving us to face the chaos. Worse, he armed runaway slaves and incited the tribes—acts of cowardice that turned even loyalists against him. When I confronted him aboard his ship, he raged, called me a rebel, and threatened arrest. I demanded a court-martial; the officers present knew the truth of his shame.

Somehow, my mother’s estate remained untouched, though rebel troops often quartered there. Fanny, returned from the northern campaign, softened toward her, and whispers suggested her influence kept the Whigs at bay. Perhaps. Or perhaps even rebels saw no gain in harassing an old woman while her son fought for their cause.

As for me, my command dissolved like mist. Some called me a traitor for speaking plainly; others, a schemer clinging to land. Let them talk. I knew my conscience. And if history misjudges me—well, I am in good company.
