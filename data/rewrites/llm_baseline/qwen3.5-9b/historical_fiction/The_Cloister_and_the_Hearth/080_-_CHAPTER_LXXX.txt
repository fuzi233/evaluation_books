Brother Clement had labored in Basel for over a year, teaching and preaching, when one day Jerome appeared before him. Dust clung to his robes, but his eyes burned with triumph. "Glory be to God, Brother Clement!" he declared. "Now you may journey to England with me."

"I am ready, Brother Jerome," Clement replied warmly. "And having anticipated your return for many months, I have spent my intervals between lessons and prayer studying the English tongue with close attention."

"Good thinking," Jerome acknowledged. He explained that he had delayed their departure only until he secured special faculties from the Pope: authority to collect funds for the Church in England and permission to hear confessions within all secular monasteries. "Now, gird up your loins," Jerome urged, his voice hardening. "Let us go forth and strike a decisive blow for the Church, and against the Franciscans."

Thus, the two friars set out, preaching their way down the Rhine toward England. In the larger cities, they shared the pulpit; in the smaller towns, they often split apart, taking opposite banks of the river to meet again at a designated hour. Both possessed the gift of the oratory, yet their styles diverged sharply. Jerome's speech was noble and imposing, though occasionally constrained in its theological scope and prone to a certain monotony in delivery. Clement, conversely, offered a torrent of variety, often slipping effortlessly into the vernacular. His tone flowed tenderly, curling around the listener's reason and heart. Yet, whenever his thoughts ascended, Clement soared far above Jerome, while Jerome remained grounded. In those moments of fervor, Clement became everything history records of inspired prophets and pagan orators alike: *vehement as a storm, excited as a rushing torrent, inflamed as lightning*—he thundered, flashed, and swept away all obstacles with the swift waves of his eloquence.

I could quote passages to prove this, but five reasons stand in my way: the difficulty of translation, the brevity of our time, the fact that I have already written elsewhere, the existence of a superior imitator, and the simple truth that while similarity is a virtue in peas, it is a vice in literature. However, to avoid evading the subject entirely, I shall note that Clement secretly prepared for every town he visited, researching local events and identifying the specific sins plaguing each community. Jerome, however, the unbending scholar, scorned such detours, refusing to tailor his message to the particular vices of any people.

At one great city, several leagues from the Rhine, they climbed the same steps to preach in turn. Jerome addressed the vanity of dress, a favorite topic of his. He spoke with biting satire and eloquence, and the crowd listened with complacent satisfaction, for vanity was not a common fault among them. Clement, meanwhile, attacked drunkenness—a besetting sin in these regions, shielded by popular prejudice and even practiced by the clergy themselves. He struck mercilessly with scripture and worldly evidence. He called it a crime, the breeding ground of theft and murder. He recounted a recent parricide committed by a man overcome by liquor, and described how a gang of drunkards had roasted one of their own companions alive in a neighboring village.

"You claim your late prince died of apoplexy," Clement shouted, his voice ringing clear. "But you know well he died of drink! Last month, one of your aldermen perished miserably, drowned in a puddle while utterly intoxicated! You strip your children's backs bare so you may gorge yourselves on the poison that turns you into the worst of beasts—silly as calves, yet fierce as boars. This drives your families into want and your souls into hell. I tell you, your town, yes, your entire nation, would sink to the bottom of humanity if your women drank as you do. How long will they remain temperate, defying nature to resist the example of their husbands and fathers? Vice never stands still. Amend yourselves, or watch them descend to your level. Already in Bohemia, women drink alongside the men. What does a drunken woman look like? Do you wish to see your wives and mothers reduced to such a state?"

A roar of horror erupted from the audience. Medieval listeners had not yet learned to sit silently during a stirring sermon.

"Aha! That strikes home!" the friar cried. "Do you suppose it shocks the All-Pure God less to see a man, His noblest creation, turned into a drunken beast, than it shocks you, creatures of sin and unreason, to see a woman degraded into something no better than yourselves?"

He concluded with two vivid tableaux: the chaotic ruin of a drunkard's household versus the peace of a sober man's home. So true and dramatic were his descriptions that the women began to exclaim, "Oh!" and "Ah!" and whispered, "By my faith, that is true!" The discourse caused absolute uproar. Hearers clustered in knots; the men grew indignant. Consequently, the women sided openly with them against the preacher. A husband had a right to a drop; he needed it to sustain the labor of his family. They refused to trade their sturdy men for milksops. "Double-faced hypocrites!" they muttered.

That very evening, a group of men intercepted Brother Clement near the market square. They filled his wallet with coins, plucked roses from their own heads to offer him, kissed his habit, and blessed him for undertaking to cure their "sots."

Jerome observed this scene with a frown. "Drunkenness is not heresy, Clement," he remarked coolly. "It is hardly fitting that a whole sermon should be devoted to it."

As they traveled onward, Jerome discovered to his astonishment that Clement's sermons sank deeper into the hearts of his hearers than his own. They made people listen, think, weep, and sometimes even reform. "He possesses the art of descending to their level," Jerome mused, watching the crowds react. "Yet, he can soar high enough at times."

On the whole, the sight puzzled Jerome, who harbored a secret sense of superiority over his tenderer brother. After traveling roughly two hundred miles, the situation began to displease him as much as it confused him. Yet, he strove to suppress this feeling as petty and unworthy. "Souls differ like locks," he reasoned aloud. "Preachers must differ like keys, or the Church will remain closed to God's passing. And certainly, this novice holds the key to these northern souls, being himself a northern man."

Thus, they continued their slow descent down the Rhine, sometimes drifting a few miles downstream, but mostly walking the banks, preaching, teaching, and hearing confessions in the towns and villages. Finally, they reached Düsseldorf.

There lay the small quay where Gerard and Denys had once boarded their boat upstream. The friars stepped onto the stone. The streets stretched out before them, and there stood "The Silver Lion." Nothing had changed in the city, save for him who walked through it barefoot, his heart calm and cold, his hands folded across his breast, his eyes bowed meekly to the ground—a true son of Dominic and the Holy Church.
