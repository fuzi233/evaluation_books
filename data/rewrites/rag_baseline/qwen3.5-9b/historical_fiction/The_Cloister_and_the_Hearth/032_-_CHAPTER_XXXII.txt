In the dim, candlelit expanse of the refectory, where the air hung thick with the scent of stale bread and incense, conversation drifted toward the sudden vacancy at the scriptorium. A monk spoke of the brother who had perished—a victim of fate or fever—who had been entrusted with the monumental task of transcribing the monastery's charter and rule. The news rippled through the assembly until it reached Gerard, whose heart leaped with a mixture of terror and hope. Rising with trembling knees, he offered his services, his voice barely audible above the rustle of habits.

"Nay, not for hire, my lords," he pleaded, his eyes darting nervously to the high table, "but for love. As a trifling return for the countless nights of shelter the brethren of your order have bestowed upon this poor wayfarer."

A murmur of approval rose from the lower ranks, but a senior monk, his face lined with skepticism, shook his head. "The late brother was a master of the pen," he intoned, his tone carrying the weight of institutional tradition. "His work cannot be continued save by a master. Your skill, stranger, is unknown here."

Gerard felt the heat rise in his cheeks. From the folds of his tunic, he drew forth a vellum deed, the back of which he had painstakingly cleaned and inscribed with a flourish of his own making—a specimen of his craft. He held it out, his fingers stiff.

The monk's reaction was instantaneous. His eyes widened, the skepticism evaporating into awe. He snatched the document, his movements jerky with excitement, and hurried up the long hall to the high table. Bending his knee, he brushed against the fifth and tenth steps—the sacred thresholds of the Prior's domain—and presented the deed with a reverence usually reserved for relics.

Instantly, the atmosphere shifted. A dozen knowing eyes locked onto the page, and a low hum of voices erupted, growing into a buzz of speculation. Gerard watched, breathless, as the Prior pointed repeatedly at the script, his finger tracing the elegant curves of the ink. The senior monk returned, his face flushed with triumph, bearing a savory *crustade royale* and a game pie spiced with exotic herbs for Gerard, alongside a silver grace cup brimming with rich *pimentum*.

Gerard accepted the feast, bowed low first to the distant Prior, then to his new companions, and raised the cup. As he quaffed and passed it along, a transformation occurred. The hostility dissolved; the table hailed him as kin.

"Art convent-bred? Deny it not?" the voices demanded in unison.

Gerard acknowledged the truth, giving silent thanks to Heaven. Without this revelation, he would have remained as rough and ignorant as his fellow travelers, Sybrandt and Cornelis.

"But 'tis passing strange how you could know," the Prior remarked, leaning forward.

"You drank with the cup in both hands," two monks chimed in, their laughter ringing clear.

For some time, the lower tables had been the source of raucous noise, but now a burst of mirth so obstreperous and prolonged threatened to tear the roof from the hall. The Prior, losing patience, dispatched the Sub-Prior to quell the uproar and impose penance upon the riotous monks. Yet, amidst the chaos, Gerard's face burned with shame. In the heart of the unruly revelry, his ear had caught a specific word—"courage!"—and the trumpet-toned voice of Denys of Burgundy cutting through the din.

Soon, Gerard was settled in Feu Werter's cell. Wax lights flickered against the stone walls, illuminating a small easel capable of adjusting to any angle, surrounded by the finest materials of calligraphy. Yet, the work proved too arduous for a single evening. The question gnawed at him: how could he ask Denys, the self-proclaimed monk-hater, to remain longer?

He approached Denys, offering to abide by whatever decision the soldier wished. To his delight, Denys responded with unexpected grace. "A day's rest will do neither of us harm. Write thou, and I'll pass the time as I may."

Gerard's work was swiftly admired. The community agreed that the monastery's records had suffered a grievous loss with poor Werter's death. The Sub-Prior pressed a rix-dollar into Gerard's hand, along with brushes and pigments from the convent's ample stores. Warm-hearted and buoyed by this good omen, Gerard believed his fortune—and his recovery of his well-beloved—lay in the power of the pen.

"Come, Denys," he said, his demeanor lightening, "see what the good monks have given me. Now, do try to be fairer to them; for to be round with you, it chilled my friendship for a moment to hear even you call my benefactors 'hypocrites.'"

"I recant," Denys declared immediately.

"Thank you! Thank you! Good Denys."

"I was a scurrilous vagabond."

"Nay, nay, say not so!"

"But we soldiers are rude and hasty. I give myself the lie, and I offer those I misunderstood all my esteem. 'Tis unjust that thousands should be defamed for the hypocrisy of a few."

"Now you are reasonable. Have you pondered what I said?"

"Nay, it is their own doing."

Gerard crowed softly, for everyone delights in being proven right, and listened intently as Denys recounted the genesis of his conversion.

"Well then," Denys began, his voice animated, "at dinner the first day, a young monk beside me opened his jaws and laughed right out, most musically. 'Good,' said I, 'at last I have fallen on a man and not a shorn ape.' So, to sound him further, I slapped his broad back and administered my consigne. 'Heaven forbid!' says he. I stared. For the dog looked as sad as Solomon; a better mime you never saw, even at a Mystery play. 'I see war is no sharpener of the wits,' he replied. 'What are the clergy for but to fight the foul fiend? and what else are the monks for? "The fiend being dead, The friars are sped." You may plough up the convents, and we poor monks shall have nought to do—but turn soldiers, and so bring him to life again.' Then there was a great laugh at my expense. 'Well, you are the monk for me,' said I. 'And you are the crossbowman for me,' quoth he. 'And I'll be bound you could tell us tales of the war should make our hair stand on end.' 'Excusez! the barber has put that out of the question,' quoth I, and then I had the laugh."

"What wretched ribaldry!" Gerard observed, his brow furrowing pensively.

The candid Denys admitted at once that he had witnessed merrier jests hatched with less cacophony. "'Twas a great matter to have got rid of hypocrisy," he continued. "'So,' said I, 'I can give you the chaire de poule, if that may content ye.' 'That we will see,' was the cry, and a signal went round."

Then Denys burst into a tale of glee regarding his bedtime. Instead of the great dormitory, he had been ushered into a private cell, strictly forbidden to sleep. To aid his vigil, a book of pictures depicting a hundred merry adventures of monks pursuing the female laity was lent to him. In due course, he was taken barefoot down to the parlour, where a supper fit for a duke awaited. Twelve jolly friars, the roaringest boys he had ever met in peace or war, sat around the table. The story, the toast, the jest, and the wine-cup circulated freely. Some played cards with a gorgeous pack where Saint Theresa and Saint Catherine, bedizened with gold, stood for the four queens; while black, white, grey, and crutched friars represented the four knaves. They staked their very rosaries, swearing like troopers when they lost.

At midnight, a sly monk stole out, only to be cunningly followed by others into the garden, where he was seen thrusting his hand into the ivy and pulling out a rope-ladder. He scaled the ten-foot wall with such nimbleness that a russet kirtle popped up from the outer world as quickly as he. Thus began a scene of billing and cooing that struck Denys as rather feline than ecclesiastical, drawing from him the appropriate comment of a "mew!" The monks joined the musical chorus, and the lay visitor shrieked in discomfort. But Abelard merely cried, "What, are ye there, ye jealous miauling knaves? Ye shall caterwaul to some tune tomorrow night. I'll fit every man-jack of ye with a fardingale."

This brutal threat had reconciled Denys to staying another day—at Gerard's request. Gerard groaned inwardly. Meanwhile, unable to disconcert such a brazen monk, and the demoiselle beginning to whimper, they had danced caterwauling in a circle before bestowing a solemn benediction on the two wall-flowers and retreating to the parlour. There, they found a pair lying dead drunk and two others affectionate to tears. They had straightway carried off the inanimate bodies, dragged the loving and lachrymose ones kicking and screaming into their cells, and shut them up in measureless content.
