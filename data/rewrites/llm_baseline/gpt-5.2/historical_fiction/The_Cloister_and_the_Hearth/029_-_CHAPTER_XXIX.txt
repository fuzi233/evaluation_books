Gerard reeled as if a hand had struck him behind the ear. The message—so sudden, so plain—drove the blood up into his face and then drained it again, leaving him white and hot by turns. His jaw set; his whole body took the posture of a man who has been hunted too near, too long, till fear itself curdles into anger and he would rather turn and gore than be driven another step.

Then, as swiftly, the heat sank out of him. His shoulders dropped with a sort of miserable childishness, and he said, not to Denys only but to the wet night and the hard earth, “And my axe is in Rhine.”

It was as if he had confessed he had left his right hand behind.

They stood close, heads together, the wind worrying at their cloaks and making a thin, fretful music in the hedges. Prudence spoke first—Prudence that sees a village as a net and every lighted window as an eye. “Avoid it,” said that cautious voice within them. Hunger answered with the belly’s blunt logic: “Buy food.”

Hunger spoke loudest. Prudence argued best.

So they took the third counsel, old as flight itself: not to go through the mouth of danger, but round it, and feed like field-mice while they ran. They struck away from the road, sliding over clods and through wet grass that hissed underfoot. A haystack loomed, darker than the dark, breathing out the warm, sweet smell of summer stored—sun caught and dried, with the faint sharpness of last year’s weeds. They did not so much steal as borrow from it, for men on the road make compacts with the dead generosity of harvest. Two bundles came away with them, prickling their fingers, shedding seeds that clung to their sleeves like small memories.

In a ditch—dry at the bottom though nettles licked the sides—they laid the hay as a poor man’s bed. It crackled softly, a domestic sound, like bedding shaken in a cottage loft, and for a moment that sound did more to steady them than any prayer: the world still knew the old language of comfort, even here. But the nettles were fluent too, and wrote their stinging script along wrist and neck.

By turns they slipped out, low as foxes, and came back with turnips pulled from the field. The bulbs were cold with earth; their skins carried the scent of clay and rain, and when they broke them with their teeth the juice was harsh, peppery, almost bitter—no cook’s dish, no hearth’s kindness, only raw sustenance bitten straight from the ground. Yet even that had its honest flavor: the field speaking in root and fibre, in the old peasant tongue that needs no interpreter. They munched at intervals, sharing without ceremony, chewing slowly to make the most of what little sweetness there was, and listening between bites for any footstep, any call, any dog’s bark that might translate into pursuit.

At sunset the light bled away; the sky shut like a lid. They crept out again, shivering into rain and darkness, and found the road on the farther side of the village. The storm had made a world without edges. The wind drove in gusts that roared and then fell away into a sullen moan; the rain rattled on leaves and whispered over mud. It was so black they could neither see nor, for long moments together, truly hear—only feel the night’s great pressure, as if they were pushing through a slab of black marble. In such weather, men are half-blind and half-deaf, and that dulled sense was a shield: they could not be seen, and their own tread was swallowed. For aught they knew they might have passed close to those they feared—close enough for a man’s breath to mingle with another’s—yet the tempest made all living things ghosts to one another.

They had almost forgotten their pursuers in the presence of the natural terror itself: not the clamor of men, but the huge indifferent noise of sky and water. And still they trudged, one foot after another, the road sucking at their soles, their cloaks heavy, the raw turnip taste lingering like penance.

When the moon rose, it did not bring warmth, only a pale witness. It found them many leagues from Düsseldorf, trudging still, bent into the wind, their shadows broken in the puddles.

At last a vast building bulked up out of the wet night, the stones shining dully where the moon caught them. Denys drew breath as if he had smelled bread. “Courage!” he cried, and his voice rang strangely clear against the wall, a human sound in all that weather. “I think I know this convent. Aye—it is. We are in the see of Juliers. Cologne has no power here.”

And with that one piece of knowledge—place-names like charms, jurisdictions like borders drawn in air—they went in at the gate, and the wind’s howl fell behind them as if a door had been shut on it.

The next moment they were safe within the walls.
