Rattle and plunge, you poor hackney-coach, through the Strand and Fleet Street; stumble, weary wheels, over stones that never learnt kindness; and you, spent horses, drag George Warrington to his brother as if each jolt were a fresh insult added to the night.

London in winter had a sound of its own—iron-rimmed wheels grinding in ruts, hoofs sucking at black mud, an oath cracking like a whip in the dark, and then, just for a breath, the thin rush of wind between the houses, as though the city exhaled. There were few lamps: only the link-boys’ torches, flaring tallow and resin, spitting in the damp, casting greasy halos on puddles and on the faces of men who looked as if they had been carved out of soot. The coach-lamps shook; the springs groaned; the streets answered with their endless, unmusical clatter. To George, that short passage from Piccadilly to the Temple seemed as long as his morning journey from Marlborough—perhaps longer, because now his blood was hot with dread and haste.

He had written to Harry from Bristol, announcing his landing. He had written before that—written the great, incredible news of himself: the brother left for dead at Braddock’s defeat, the brother who had not stayed dead; who had crawled back into the world after eighteen months among the French. But there was war, and the sea was a net of privateers. Somewhere, between ship and shore, a letter had been swallowed. Harry never broke its seal. That other letter—the one from Bristol—came too late as well: it lay, unopened, at Harry’s lodgings, delivered on the third morning after the arrest, when Mr. Ruff, in his anger, refused to yield up one single item more of his lodger’s property, as though paper itself could be held to ransom.

George’s first step in London had been to those apartments, to the very door where his brother’s name still hung in the air like the ghost of tobacco-smoke. A maid opened; seeing the likeness, she screamed and ran, as if the dead had come to collect his due. The mistress, unwilling to confess that Captain Warrington was in a bailiff’s house at her husband’s suit, lied clumsily: Mr. Warrington had gone away; she did not know where.

George did know where to go next. Clarges Street lay close to Bond Street. He had studied London’s map again and again on the far side of the ocean, tracing lanes with a finger as other men trace rivers. Aunt Bernstein would tell him. Harry’s letters to Virginia had spoken of her kindness; even Madam Esmond had been softened by it—softened, too, by a letter from the Baroness, written in a hand which, for all its elegance, carried the weight of urgency that had sent George packing post-haste for Europe. Madam Esmond hoped—heartily hoped—that Madam Beatrix had at last found occasion to repent. At her age, indeed, it was time. Heaven knows there was much to repent of.

And yet in families, as in nations, forgiveness keeps a narrow pantry. Some faults are salted down and kept for ever; the tongue preserves them when it lets all else decay. Rachel Esmond never named her eldest daughter. Madam Esmond Warrington never named her sister. No handwriting on the Temple floor, no remission signed and sealed, can make certain people unhear the old word once spoken. Female virtue, especially, can be inexorable—its lexicon full of harsh finalities, and no translation possible from one heart to another. George, fresh from battlefields and captivity, did not yet understand the London dialect of kinship: the polite, decisive vocabulary by which those nearest in blood can turn away as if the stranger were you.

Still, he went to Aunt Bernstein’s. Gumbo met him at the door—Gumbo, who had mourned his young master as dead, and now stood blinking at him as though at a miracle that had walked in off the road. The lad’s terror passed into a trembling delight; and then, in hurried, broken phrases, he let slip enough about Harry—where he was, and in what pitiable condition—to make George’s face set hard when he was shown in to the old lady.

George had thought it simple: a brother in trouble would be rescued by his relations. Oh, George—how little you knew of London ways! In the streets, you could not walk a furlong without meeting hunger and misfortune, and if a man undertook to redeem them all, the riches of all America would not do it. Help in London was a commodity; sympathy often a performance sold by the hour. Yet the agitation and real feeling in Aunt Bernstein—her quick, frightened breath; her hands working together as if kneading some invisible dough of worry; the way her voice thickened when she spoke of Harry—touched George in spite of himself.

As the coach jolted on towards the Temple, George’s anger cooled into shame. She does feel it, he told himself. I was too hasty. Again and again in life, he would have to scold himself with that same fault. And the worst of it is: once a harsh word has been served, no repentance can take it back and make it fresh again.

“Quick, coachman!” he cried, as if speed could mend what time had already spoilt.

Gumbo knew the way to the bailiff’s house as well as he knew the way to market. The house received them like a trap. First a bell rung sharply; then a door opened; then it was locked behind them with caution, as though they carried plague. They stood in a narrow passage under the eye of a small Jewish janitor holding a flaring tallow-torch. Its smoke carried the rancid smell of cheap fat, and the flame made every shadow jump. A second door was unbolted; they were admitted deeper, as into the belly of a careful beast.

The janitor stared hard when the torchlight fell on George’s face—as if he had already seen that face in the house, and could not make his mind accept two of them.

“Come to see that gentleman?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“But wait.” The man’s eyes slid to Gumbo. “This Mr. Warrington’s brother from America?”

Gumbo nodded, swollen with importance, and was sent upstairs—“to prepare the gentleman,” as the porter put it, for the shock of it.

George was led into a little room called Mr. Amos’s office. A guttering candle stood on a table, and the air was stale with ink, damp wool, and the sour reek of smoke that had no proper chimney. There sat the bailiff, and beside him a stout gentleman in a cloak and laced hat, who, hearing the new arrival, rose with a frank cheerfulness.

“We are not angry any more, Harry!” the stout gentleman cried, thrusting out his hand. “Thank God, my boy! Mr. Amos says there will be no difficulty—James and I will be your bail, and we will do your business by breakfast in the morning. Why—”

He halted as though struck. His hand was taken—held firmly—and he peered into George’s face.

“Angels and ministers of grace! Who are you?”

The stranger’s grip tightened, not in threat but in gratitude that could not help being forceful.

“God bless you, sir,” George said, his voice unsteady. “I know who you are. You must be Colonel Lambert, whose kindness my poor Harry wrote of. And I am the brother you have heard of—the one left for dead at General Braddock’s action; who came to life again after eighteen months among the French; and who lives to thank God, and to thank you, for your goodness to my brother.”

“James! James! Here is news!” Colonel Lambert called, half-laughing, half-crying, to a gentleman in red who entered at that moment. “Here are the dead come alive! Here is Harry Scapegrace’s brother come back—with his scalp on his head too!”

George had removed his hat; the candle caught his hair and the hard travel-lines about his mouth.

“This is my brother-bail, Mr. Warrington,” Lambert continued, recovering his humour as a man does when emotion is too sharp and laughter must cover it. “This is Lieutenant-Colonel James Wolfe, at your service. You must know there has been a little difference between Harry and me, Mr. George. He is pacified, is he, James?”

“He is full of gratitude,” said Wolfe, bowing—plain-featured, brisk, with that air of exactness which makes one think a man even measures his words like rations.

“Harry wrote home about Colonel Wolfe too,” George said quickly. “And I hope my brother’s friends will be so kind as to be mine.”

“I wish he had no other than us,” Lambert returned, the cheer in him edged with something bitter. “His fine folks have been too fine for him, and have ended by landing him here.”

Mr. Amos, who knew a sound security when he saw it—rank, respectability, and ready money—shifted his manner as a cook shifts a sauce when a guest of consequence enters. His voice grew obsequious.

“Nay, your honours, I have done my best to make the young gentleman comfortable; and knowing your honour before, when you came to bail Captain Watkins, and that your security is perfectly good—if your honour wishes, the young gentleman can go out this very night, and I will make it all right with the lawyer in the morning.”

“The debt is five hundred and odd pounds, I think?” George asked.

“Ay, sir.”

“With a hundred thanks to these gentlemen, I can pay the amount at this moment into the officer’s hands, taking the usual acknowledgment,” George said. “But I can never forget, gentlemen, that you helped my brother at his need. For doing so, I say thank you—and God bless you—in my mother’s name and mine.”

While they spoke below, Gumbo had gone up to Harry’s room.

Harry sat there in his morning-gown, with a pipe of Virginia between his fingers. The tobacco’s scent—sweet, grassy, and homely as a summer field—seemed out of place in that hired prison, where the walls smelled of old breath and boiled cabbage from some lower kitchen, and the street outside kept up its hard, sleepless racket. Even Maria’s goodness, shown but now with a tenderness that ought to have been balm, had not wholly consoled him. Her fidelity bound him; and he had looked, with a kind of dismay, at the consequences of being loved so honestly when one is not honest enough to deserve it.

Then Wolfe came in with his homely face lit by good intention, and his hand thrust out as if the gesture could drag a man up from his own sulks.

He told Harry how Lambert was below, and for what errand the two officers had come.

In spite of himself, Lambert would be kind. In spite of Harry’s ill-temper, suspicion, and foolish anger, the good man was resolved to help him—help him even against Wolfe’s advice, as Wolfe now told the prisoner with soldierly frankness.

“For you were wrong, Mr. Warrington,” said Wolfe, “and you would not be set right; and you, a young man, used hard words and unkind behaviour to your senior—and what is more, to one of the best gentlemen who walks God’s earth. You will bear with a friend who speaks plainly? Martin Lambert hath acted in this as he always doth—as the best Christian, the best friend, the most kind and generous of men. If you want proof, here it is: he has converted me, who was angry with you for your treatment of him, and has brought me down to be your bail. Let us both cry Peccavimus, Harry, and shake our friend by the hand. He is sitting in the room below. He would not come here till he knew how you would receive him.”

“I think he is a good man,” Harry groaned, as if the admission cost him physically. “I was very angry and wild when we met last, Colonel Wolfe. Nay, perhaps he was right in sending back those trinkets, hurt as I was. Go down to him, will you be so kind, and tell him I am sorry, and ask his pardon—and—God bless him for his generous behaviour.”

He turned his face away and rubbed his hand across his eyes, ashamed of tears and ashamed of the pride that had earned them.

“Tell him thyself, Harry!” Wolfe cried, seizing his hand. “No deputy will ever say it half so well. Come now.”

“You go first,” Harry pleaded, clinging to the last rag of dignity as a man clings to a blanket in the cold. “I’ll—I'll follow. On my word I will. See—I am in my morning-gown. I will but put on a coat and come. Give him my message first—just—just prepare him for me.”

Wolfe smiled as he went out; he understood the taste of humble-pie, and that it is best swallowed quickly, but that some palates need a moment’s warning.

As Wolfe opened the door, Gumbo came tumbling in, almost forgetting his usual bows and flourishes. His eyes rolled; his mouth split in a vast grin; his hands shook as though he had been set to sift flour in a gale.

“What, Gum?” Harry asked, startled into irritation. “What has happened to thee? Hast thou got a new sweetheart?”

“No, mas’r. No new sweetheart,” Gumbo said, and could hardly get the words out for delight. “I see a ghost, mas’r!”

“A ghost? Whose, and where?”

“Whar? Saw him at Madame Bernstein’s house. Come with him here in de coach. He downstairs now with Colonel Lambert!”

While he spoke, he helped Harry into his coat, fingers clumsy with excitement.

“Ghost—what ghost?” Harry demanded, the pipe forgotten. A strange agitation seized him. “Is anybody—is—my mother come?”

“No, sir. No, Master Harry!” Gumbo’s head wagged so violently it seemed near to rolling off.

Harry stared at him one sharp second, then flung the door open and went rapidly down the stairs, the boards answering his steps with a hollow clack that sounded suddenly like a drum.

He reached the foot just as a voice in the little office—its door ajar—was saying, “…and for doing so, I say thank you, and God bless you, in my mother’s name and mine.”

“Whose voice is that?” Harry cried, and his own voice sounded to him oddly—too high, too bare, as if the air had been knocked out of him.

“It’s de ghost’s, mas’r!” Gumbo whispered behind.

Harry ran forward to the room—and there, before we follow him over the threshold, let the moment hold, like a breath held over a pot lest it boil over.

Inside, the two officers turned their heads away, giving privacy as men do when something sacred has entered the everyday. The lost was found. The dead stood living. The prodigal—if prodigal he had been—fell upon his brother’s heart; and George, whose sternness had been born of fear and love, felt that heart fill with gratitude, repentance, and a warmth so sudden it was almost pain.

“Come away, James,” Colonel Wolfe said softly to Lambert. “I think we are not wanted any more here. Good-night, boys. Some ladies in Hill Street won’t sleep for this strange news. Or will you go home and sup with them, and tell the story?”

No—with many thanks, the brothers would not go to sup that night. They had a tale of their own to eat and to digest, and no table in Hill Street could serve it.

“Quick, Gumbo, with the trunks!” Harry called at last, when speech returned to him in scraps. “Good-bye, Mr. Amos!”

And yet, when he went away from that place—free, with his brother beside him, with the night’s noise suddenly sounding less like a sentence and more like mere weather—Harry felt, oddly, almost unhappy, as though joy itself had its aftertaste, and the sweetest leaf could still carry a hint of smoke.
