"To tell you the truth," the young stranger said to Nello as they navigated the chaotic tangle of carts and mules, "I am relieved to be transferred from that patron of mine to someone speaking a language without the barbarous accent of the East, conducting a trade less obscure than selling lutes and swords alongside broken glass."

"Common?" Nello scoffed, his loquacity spilling forth like an overfilled cask. "Our Bratti is no common peddler. He operates on a rigorous theory, executing it with precision that most philosophers fail to achieve. His strategy is simple: extract the maximum possible utility from existence through aggressive negotiation, securing a favorable exit strategy via the Church once the game concludes. He has drafted his will accordingly, paying minimal fees to ensure his winnings go to the Holy See. Yet, I often warn him: 'Bratti, your contract is flawed; you are positioned on the losing end. Do you not find it melancholy to gaze upon the paintings of Paradise? There, you cannot haggle for rags or rusted nails; the saints require neither pins nor kindling. Unless you attempt to trade with Saint Bartholomew, who carries his own skin in a cumbersome manner, I see no avenue for commerce in the afterlife.' But forgive me," Nello added, his tone shifting abruptly as he crossed himself, "such levity is inappropriate given the morning's gravity. Lorenzo lies dead, and the Muses mourn. This is a source of genuine distress for a barber, and you, Messere, must also be under a cloud. A man of your eloquence and bearing taking refuge in such squalid lodging suggests a calamity has befallen you."

"Who is this Lorenzo whose passing you mention?" the stranger asked, his attention fixated on the name with an intensity that suggested he had missed the earlier implication.

"There is but one Lorenzo capable of throwing the Mercato into chaos, causing the Duomo's lantern to leap in despair, and forcing the Republic's lions to turn upon one another," Nello declared. "I refer to Lorenzo de' Medici, the Pericles of our Athens—if I may draw such a parallel before a Greek."

"Why not?" the other laughed. "I suspect Athens, even in Pericles' zenith, could not have produced a scholar as learned as a barber."

"Indeed," Nello agreed rapidly. "Unless I have wasted my time shaving the venerable Demetrio Calcondila. Forgive me; I am struck by awe. Your Italian surpasses his, despite his forty years in Italy, and exceeds even that of the accomplished Marullo, who effectively wedded the Italic Muse in multiple ways, including his marriage to our learned and beautiful Alessandra Scala."

"It will clarify your surprise to know I descend from Greek stock rooted in Italian soil deeper than the mulberry trees themselves. Born in Bari, I was raised by an Italian. In essence, I am a Greek, much as your peaches are Persian. The Greek hue has faded, submerged by prolonged residence and extensive travel across the land of gods and heroes. However, to confess a private matter: this residual Greek dye, along with a few ancient gems, constitutes the sole fortune shipwreck has left me. But—" he paused, gesturing to the city—"when towers fall, it is ruin for the small nest-builder. The death of your Pericles compels me to regret not heading toward Rome, as I intended, had not a deceitful Minerva in the guise of an Augustinian monk misled me. 'At Rome,' he claimed, 'you will drown in a sea of starving scholars; yet in Florence, every corner bathes in the sunlight of Lorenzo's patronage. Florence remains the premier market in Italy for commodities such as yours.'"

"_Gnaffè_," Nello retorted, "and so it shall remain, I trust. Lorenzo was not the sole arbiter of learning in our city—heaven forbid! Because he was a large melon, every other Florentine is not a pumpkin. We possess Bernardo Rucellai, Alamanno Rinuccini, and countless others. If you seek information, I, Nello, am your resource. It feels like a millennium until I can serve a _bel erudito_ like yourself. First, however, consider your hair. That beard, my fine young man, must be discarded, regardless of how dear it is to the nymphs of your dreams. In Florence, we despise the visage of a man with his nose protruding above a waterfall of hair. Remember, once you cross the Rubicon of the shave, repentance is futile. If you allow your beard to regrow after acquiring strength from the razor's bite, your mouth will cease displaying what Messer Angelo termed the divine prerogative of lips, transforming instead into a dark cavern fringed with vicious brambles."

"That is a dire prophecy," the Greek admitted, "particularly if your Florentine maidens rival the beauty of the little Tessa I kissed this morning."

"Tessa? She is a rough-handed peasant girl," Nello corrected sharply. "You aspire to the favor of ladies who carry no scent of the mule-stables. To achieve this, you must shed the appearance of a _sgherro_ or a man of ill repute. You must project the image of a courtier, a scholar of the refined sort, akin to our Pietro Crinito—one who sins among the well-bred and well-fed, not one who devours vile _vino di sotto_ in a random tavern."

"With all my heart," the stranger conceded. "If the Florentine Graces demand it, I am prepared to surrender this minor issue of my beard, provided—"

"Yes, yes," Nello interrupted. "I anticipate your objection. It is the _bella zazzera_—the hyacinthine locks you refuse to relinquish. Rest assured, there is no need for total destruction. Mere pruning—ecco!—will render you strikingly similar to the illustrious Prince Pico di Mirandola in his prime. We arrive punctually in Piazza San Giovanni, at the threshold of my establishment. Yet you hesitate; naturally, you wish to inspect our world wonder, our Duomo, our Santa Maria del Fiore. Very well, a fleeting glance suffices. I beg you reserve a detailed examination until after your shave. I tremble with the fervor of my art, right to the edge of my razor. Ah, follow me this way."

The mercurial barber gripped the stranger's arm, guiding him to a vantage point on the south side of the piazza offering an unobstructed view of the massive dark shell of the dome, the slender, soaring elegance of Giotto's campanile, and the quaint octagon of San Giovanni ahead, displaying its unique gates of storied bronze, still retaining the faint brilliance of their original gold leaf. The inlaid marbles appeared fresher in their shades of pink, white, and purple than they are today, when four centuries of winters have bleached their white to the rich ochre of aged tobacco pipe. The cathedral's façade did not stand in ignominious faded stucco but displayed the magnificent promise of the half-completed marble inlaying and statue-filled niches Giotto had envisioned a century and a half prior. As the campanile, in its harmonious variety of color and form, drew the eye upward into the clear April air, it seemed a prophetic symbol indicating that human life must eventually align itself with that pure, aspiring beauty.

Yet, this was not the impression formed in the Greek. His eyes were drawn upward involuntarily, but as he stood with arms folded and curls falling back, a distinct note of scorn touched his lip. When his gaze returned to earth, it scanned the surroundings with a cool detachment that rankled Nello's Florentine pride.

"Well, my fine young man," the barber said impatiently, "you treat our Cathedral as lightly as if you were the Archangel Gabriel descending directly from Paradise. Pray tell, have you ever witnessed craftsmanship superior to Giotto's tower, or a dome that would not resemble a mere mushroom beside Brunelleschi's creation? Have you seen marbles finer or more intricately worked than those our Signoria procured from distant quarries at a cost sufficient to purchase a dukedom? Come, admit it; have you ever encountered anything comparable?"

"If you posed that question with a scimitar at my throat, in the Turkish style, or even with your own razor," the young Greek replied, smiling cheerfully as he moved toward the Baptistery's gates, "I dare say you might extract a confession of the true faith from me. But with my throat free from peril, I venture to assert that your structures smell too strongly of Christian barbarism for my palate. I possess a shuddering intuition of what lies within—hideous, smoke-blackened Madonnas; fleshless saints in mosaic, staring down in idiotic astonishment and rebuke from the apse; skin-clad skeletons suspended on crosses, or pinned with arrows, or stretched on gridirons; women and monks with severed heads in eternal lamentation. I have seen enough of those wry-necked favorites of heaven in Constantinople. But what of this bronze door, rough with imagery? These female figures seem molded from a different spirit than the starved, staring saints I described; these heads in high relief suggest a human mind within, rather than serving as an index to perpetual spasms and colic."
