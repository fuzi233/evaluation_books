Only half the iron in Moore had been shown on the night he stood in the mill-yard and beat back the press of hungry men. The other half—harder, colder, and more tireless—came after, when the broken panes were mended, the splintered doors set to rights, and the clatter of danger had settled into a steadier, more ominous undertone: pursuit.

He did not spend his wrath on the multitude. Those he had recognised at the pale edge of morning—faces he might have passed on market-day, hands that had once taken wages from him and bread from their own tables—he met now on lane and street without sign. Whether justice spoke in him, or only a certain pride that scorned to strike what was already bowed by want, the result was the same: the followers went free. It was the leaders he wanted; and these were not of the parish.

Strangers, they were said to be—men blown in from the thick towns like soot on a wind: emissaries with no field, no hearth, no settled name. “Down-draughts,” folk called them—bankrupts, debt-bitten, often drink-sodden; men who had nothing to lose and much to gain if fear and confusion could be sold for coin. Moore hunted them as if the chase were a sport God had made for him alone. He liked it—liked it better than cloth. The road, the open air, the long reach of moorland under a changing sky suited him more than the steamy breath of dye-houses; and if the horse beneath him could have spoken, it might have cursed the days when its master’s temper sharpened and his seat grew harder.

He rode far and late; the magistrates, slow and cautious, were forced to move because he would not let them sleep in their indecision. He even liked to see them blanch—to hear the little crack in their voices when the word was said plain: assassination. That dread had lain over the district like a low thunder; it had made manufacturers whisper and public men keep to their own doors at dusk. Only Helstone had ever met it as if it were merely weather.

The old rector—the old Cossack—had long ago made his terms with violence. If he were shot in a lane, he would have fallen as he had lived: upright, contemptuous of pity. Moore, too, knew there was danger; and because he knew it, he despised it. He rode the moors moonlit or moonless with a kind of fierce exhilaration, his mind clearer than it ever was behind a counting-house desk, his blood fresher than it ever grew amid the rattle of looms. The consciousness that he tracked men who would strike from behind was the spur; fear could not live in him beside pride.

There were four leaders to be accounted for. Two, within a fortnight, were run down and brought to bay near Stilbro’. The other two were said to haunt farther off, nearer Birmingham; and Moore’s roads lengthened accordingly.

Yet he did not neglect the mill. Its repair was almost trivial—carpenters and glaziers, nails and putty, a new beam here, a pane there. The rioters had never broken through; his grim darlings, the machines, stood untouched, smelling of oil and iron, as if they had merely watched and waited. Business, justice, and the relentless rhythm of his days left little sign of gentler concern. He went seldom to Fieldhead; and when he did, it was briefly, as if he feared loitering. At the rectory he appeared only to confer with the rector in the study—two hard heads bent together over hard facts.

Meanwhile the year itself seemed set against peace. War’s long hurricane still swept the Continent; there was no clean break in the clouds, no sweet intermission, no softening rain except that bitter red one men talk of in metaphors and taste in their dreams. And closer to home, ruin worked like a quiet miner under Moore’s feet. Whether he crossed his own hearthstone or galloped over sullen Rushedge, he felt the ground give a hollow echo to his tread, as if it were already loosening.

While summer thus wore him thin with motion and purpose, how did it pass with those he left behind?

Let us go first to Fieldhead.

One might expect Shirley Keeldar, with her heart set where it must not be fed, to droop into the paleness of neglected girls—to sit long over a book as if it were a substitute for warmth, or to stitch with a bowed head and silent mouth, languishing into the pretty martyrdom society loves to praise. But she did nothing of the kind. If there remained about her face a wistfulness, it sat there as a passing shadow might sit on bright grass; her careless smile was not banished. The old house, dark with age and oak, she kept light with her presence, as a kitchen is kept cheerful by the certainty of supper and a good fire.

The long gallery learned new echoes—her footfall, her laugh, the light scolding she gave to herself when she remembered she had left something half-done. The dim entrance-hall with its one window grew accustomed to the frequent swish of silk as she crossed it, now with flowers in her hands to soften the barbarous peach-bloom salon, now to the dining-room to throw wide the casements and admit the scent of mignonette and sweet-briar. She had plants forever on the move: lifted from a staircase window where they had grown pale, set at the open porch-door to drink sun as if sun were milk.

She would take up her sewing sometimes; but it was Shirley’s fate never to sit over it steadily five minutes. The thimble was hardly on before some remembered trifle drove her upstairs: an old ivory-backed needle-book she did not need, a china-topped work-box she could not bear to see out of place, a drawer she must put in order because she had glimpsed that morning a confusion that offended her sense of beauty. And often—often—she went only to a particular window, because from that window Briarfield church and rectory could be seen, lying as if in a nest of trees.

Hardly would she return to her bit of cambric or her half-wrought canvas when Tartar’s scrape and strangled whistle sounded at the porch. She must open to him herself; she must put her hand on the great head, feel the heat of his breath, hear the panting that made the still hall sound alive. It was a hot day; he would want water. Shirley would lead him straight to the kitchen and see—see with her own eyes—that the bowl was filled to the brim.

And the kitchen door stood open on the court, and the court was a kind of bright farm-pageant, all sun and feathers: turkeys with their poults, peahens with their chicks, Guinea-fowl freckled like pearls, pigeons pure white or purple-necked or blue and cinnamon, stepping and cooing with that soft domestic music which is as much a part of a place as the smell of bread. The soundscape there—wings rustling, beaks tapping, the low complaint of a hen, the steady, comforting cluck of a mother—was to Shirley like an old dialect of happiness: not written anywhere, not to be translated, only to be lived.

She would run to the pantry for a roll and scatter crumbs on the step. At once her eager, plump vassals thronged her skirts. John would be about the stables; John must be spoken to; her mare must be looked at, stroked, praised, and teased. She would still be patting the warm neck when the cows came in to be milked—slow, heavy, patient shapes, their hooves dull on the yard-stones. Shirley considered that procession important; she must stand and review them as if she were an admiring historian of her own little kingdom, reading in hide and horn the lineage of comfort.

There might be calves, and little new-yeaned lambs—perhaps twins rejected by their mothers. Then Miss Keeldar must feed them herself, under John’s careful eye: the small mouths searching, the thin tongues warm, the greedy sucking that made a faint, intimate sound like rain on leaves. It is not easy to be purely miserable while one is warming milk, while one is wiping a creature’s damp nose, while the air smells of straw and animal heat and summer.

And then John, foreman and oracle, would begin to moot doubtful questions of crofts and ings and holmes. Shirley must fetch her gipsy-straw garden-hat and go with him over stile and along hedge-row to see the matter on the spot. They would stand in the grass, listening to the wind in the hedges, to distant bells, to the far-off pulse of the district’s new noises—hammers, wheels, that metallic far murmur which never quite died now. Shirley, who loved harmony, heard those sounds too; she could not help it. Sometimes, in a pause, when a lark rose and poured out its clear thread of song, the town’s din seemed for a moment an insult; and then the moment passed, because life at Fieldhead insisted on its own duties—milk to be skimmed, fruit to be gathered, bread to be cut.

So bright afternoon wore into soft evening. She came in for late tea, and after tea she never sewed. After tea she read. In that she had a steadiness she never gave to her needle. Her study was not a desk but the rug; her seat a footstool—or, often, the carpet at Mrs. Pryor’s feet. She had learned there as a child, and habit, like a family recipe, keeps its flavour through years.

Tartar lay beside her, tawny and lion-like, his black muzzle on his paws, his limbs stretched straight and strong as if he had been shaped for mountains. One hand of his mistress rested on his rude head, for if she withdrew it he groaned and fretted, and Shirley could not endure that note of dissatisfaction in the room. While she read, she scarcely lifted her eyes, scarcely moved—unless it were to answer Mrs. Pryor’s quiet, deprecatory cautions.

“My dear, you had better not have that great dog so near you; he is crushing the border of your dress.”

“Oh, it is only muslin. I can put a clean one on to-morrow.”

“My dear, I wish you could acquire the habit of sitting to a table when you read.”

“I will try, ma’am, some time; but it is so comfortable to do as one has always been accustomed.”

“My dear, let me beg of you to put that book down. You are trying your eyes by the doubtful firelight.”

“No, ma’am; my eyes are never tired.”

At last a pale light fell on the page from the window. Shirley looked up: the moon was risen—cool, remote, and sovereign. She closed the volume, rose, and walked the room.

If the book had been a good one, it had fed her—fed her in that strange way words can, when they taste of feeling and smell faintly of worlds we cannot enter. It had refilled her heart as broth refills a chilled body; it had set her mind astir, furnishing it with pictures. The still parlour, the clean hearth, the open window showing the twilight sky and the moon new-throned and glorious, sufficed to make earth seem Eden and life a poem. A deep inborn delight glowed in Shirley then—unmingled, untroubled, beyond human reach because by no human hand bestowed. Nature’s free gift; God’s secret dower. It lifted her as if on green steps up glad hills into a station where she could look down on her life and see it—not as she wished it, for she had no time to wish—only as a swift spreading glory that outran thought and outshone desire.

She did not speak while that trance held. If Mrs. Pryor addressed her then, Shirley answered softly, and went out into the dim gallery upstairs, pacing as if she would wear the splendour into her very bones. Had she been less indolent, less reckless, less ignorant of her own riches, she would have seized such moments with a pen and fixed them; she would have written plainly, in her queer, clear hand, the song and the story that came to her unbidden, and so made them property. But she did not. She let them pass, as some people let a beautiful phrase in a dying dialect pass because it is not written, because it is only heard, because tomorrow’s dominant language will not bother to remember.

Shirley took life easily. It was in her eye. When she was calm, that large gray sphere held softness—humour, indulgence, tenderness. Rouse her, and a red ray pierced the dew; it kindled instantly to flame.

Before July was out, she would likely have started with Caroline on the northern tour they had planned—those imagined miles like promises, those fresh inns, those mountain airs that taste like cold water in a cup of tin. But just then Fieldhead was invaded.

A genteel foraging party descended upon her stronghold and besieged her with politeness until she surrendered at discretion. An uncle, an aunt, and two cousins from the south—Mr. and Mrs. Sympson and the two Misses Sympson of Sympson Grove, ——shire—came down upon her in state. Hospitality is a law with iron under its velvet; Shirley gave in, and gave in so readily that Caroline, who knew her promptness and fertility of expedient when her own will was at stake, could not help questioning her.

“How can you submit so easily?”

“Old feelings have their power,” Shirley answered. She had spent two years of her early youth at Sympson Grove.

“And do you like them?”

“I have nothing in common with them,” she said. Only little Harry Sympson—their only brother—had once been dear to her; he was unlike his sisters. But he was not coming to Yorkshire—at least not yet.

The next Sunday, in Briarfield Church, the Fieldhead pew looked like a small exhibit of correctness. There sat a prim, trim, fidgety elderly gentleman, shifting his spectacles and changing posture every three minutes; a patient, placid elderly lady in brown satin; and two pattern young ladies in pattern attire, with pattern deportment. In the midst of them Shirley had the air of a black swan among domestic geese, or a white crow perched in a row of tame birds; and for all her spirit, her aspect was forlorn.

Having placed her thus in respectable society, we leave her there a little, and turn to Caroline.

With Miss Keeldar fenced off by fine relatives and the bustle their arrival made in the neighbourhood, Caroline could not go to Fieldhead. The visiting commotion frightened her away as a sudden loudness frightens a shy creature from its accustomed cover. She was limited again to the gray rectory; to solitary morning walks in remote by-paths where the hedges held their own quiet talk of insects and leaves; to long, lonely afternoons in a parlour the sun deserted at noon; or to the garden alcove where it shone bright yet sadly on ripening red currants trained over the trellis, and on monthly roses entwined between, and fell chequered through their leaves on Caroline’s white summer dress as she sat still as a garden statue.

She read old books from her uncle’s library. Greek and Latin were dead to her, not only as languages but as nourishment. The lighter shelf had belonged once to Aunt Mary: venerable Lady’s Magazines that had crossed the sea and been in a storm, their pages stained with salt water—as if the very print had learned the taste of brine; Methodist Magazines full of miracles and apparitions, preternatural warnings, ominous dreams, frenzied fanaticism; the equally mad letters of Mrs. Elizabeth Rowe from the Dead to the Living; and a few old English classics. In childhood Caroline had sucked honey from these faded flowers; now they were tasteless.

For change, and for good, she would sew—make garments for the poor under good Miss Ainley’s direction. Sometimes she watched her tears drop slowly onto the cloth and wondered how that excellent woman could be so serenely equal in her own solitude.

“I never find Miss Ainley oppressed with despondency or lost in grief,” Caroline thought. “Yet her cottage is still and dim, and she is without bright hope or near friend. But she told me once she had taught her thoughts to tend upwards to heaven. She allows there has been little enjoyment in this world for her; she looks, I suppose, to the bliss of the world to come. So do nuns in their close cells, with iron lamps and robes strait as shrouds, beds narrow as coffins. She says often she has no fear of death—no dread of the grave; no more, doubtless, had St. Simeon Stylites lifted up terrible on his wild column; no more has the Hindu votary stretched on iron spikes. Such people, violating nature, reverse their natural likings; they grow morbid.

“I do fear death as yet; perhaps it is because I am young. Poor Miss Ainley would cling closer to life if life had more charms for her. Surely God did not create us and cause us to live with the sole end of wishing always to die. In my heart I believe we were meant to prize life and enjoy it while we have it. Existence was not originally meant to be that blank, pale, slow-trailing thing it becomes to many—and is becoming to me.

“Nobody in particular is to blame, that I can see, for the state in which things are; and I cannot tell, however I puzzle over it, how they are to be altered at once. But I feel there is something wrong somewhere. Single women should have more to do—better chances of interesting and profitable occupation than they possess now. And when I speak thus I do not feel I displease God; that I am impious or impatient. My consolation is that God hears many a groan, and has compassion on much grief that man stops his ears against, or frowns on with impotent contempt. I say impotent; for where society cannot readily cure grievances it usually forbids their utterance, and covers its weakness with scorn like tinsel thrown over deformity. People hate to be reminded of ills they cannot or will not remedy. The reminder disturbs their ease; it shakes their self-complacency.

“Old maids, like houseless and unemployed poor, should not ask for a place and an occupation in the world; the demand disturbs the happy and rich—it disturbs parents. Look at the numerous families of girls in this neighbourhood—the Armitages, the Birtwhistles, the Sykeses. Their brothers are in business or professions; they have something to do. Their sisters have no earthly employment but household work and sewing; no earthly pleasure but unprofitable visiting; and no hope in all their life to come of anything better. This stagnant state makes them decline in health. They are never well; their minds shrink to wondrous narrowness.

“The great wish—the sole aim—of every one is to be married, but the majority will never marry. They scheme; they plot; they dress to ensnare husbands. The gentlemen turn them into ridicule; they do not want them; they hold them cheap. I have heard them say it with sneering laughs: the matrimonial market is overstocked. Fathers say so too, and are angry when they observe their daughters’ manoeuvres; they order them to stay at home. But what do they expect them to do at home? If you ask, they answer: sew and cook. Sew and cook—contentedly, regularly, uncomplainingly—all their lives long—as if they had no germs of faculties for anything else. It is as reasonable as holding that fathers have no faculties but for eating what their daughters cook or wearing what their daughters sew.

“Could men live so themselves? Would they not grow weary? And when no relief came—only reproach for the least sign of weariness—would not the weariness ferment into frenzy?

“Lucretia spinning at midnight among her maidens, and Solomon’s virtuous woman, are quoted as patterns. Lucretia, I dare say, was worthy—much like my cousin Hortense Moore—but she kept her servants up very late. I should not have liked to be one of the maidens. Hortense would work Sarah and me so if she could, and neither of us would bear it. The virtuous woman, again, had her household up in the middle of the night—Mrs. Sykes says she ‘got breakfast over’ before one o’clock a.m.—but she did more than spin and give out portions. She was a manufacturer; she made fine linen and sold it. She was an agriculturist; she bought estates and planted vineyards. That woman was a manager—what the matrons here call a clever woman. I like her better than Lucretia; and I don’t believe either Mr. Armitage or Mr. Sykes could have got the advantage of her in a bargain. Yet I like her.

“‘Strength and honour were her clothing; the heart of her husband safely trusted in her. She opened her mouth with wisdom; in her tongue was the law of kindness; her children rose up and called her blessed; her husband also praised her.’ King of Israel, your model is worthy. But are we, in these days, brought up to be like her? Men of Yorkshire—do your daughters reach that standard? Can they? Can you help them? Can you give them a field wherein their faculties may be exercised and grow?

“Men of England, look at your poor girls—many fading around you, dropping off in consumption or decline; or, what is worse, degenerating into sour old maids—envious, back-biting, wretched—because life is a desert to them; or, what is worst of all, reduced to strive by scarce modest coquetry and debasing artifice to gain by marriage that consideration which is denied to celibacy. Fathers, cannot you alter these things? Perhaps not at once; but think when the matter is brought before you. Do not dismiss it with an idle jest or an unmanly insult. You would wish to be proud of your daughters and not blush for them; then seek for them an interest and an occupation that shall raise them above the flirt, the manoeuvrer, the mischief-making tale-bearer. Keep your girls’ minds narrow and fettered, and they will still be a plague and a care—sometimes a disgrace. Cultivate them; give them scope and work; they will be your gayest companions in health, your tenderest nurses in sickness, your most faithful prop in age.”

So thought Caroline, her needle moving while her heart stood still; and outside, beyond the roses and the currants, the district’s mixed music went on—birds and bees in the garden, and, farther away, that hard industrial murmur that was changing the very air people breathed, changing what they could hear, changing what they could remember, and what words they would one day have left to name their own hunger.
