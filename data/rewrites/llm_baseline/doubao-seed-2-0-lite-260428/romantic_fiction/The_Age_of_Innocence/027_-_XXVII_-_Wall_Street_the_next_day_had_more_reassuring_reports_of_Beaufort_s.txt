The very next day, Wall Street hummed with soft, reassuring rumours that Julius Beaufort’s precarious finances were not nearly as dire as the gossip of the weekend had made them out to be. There were no hard facts, no signed statements, but the air thrummed with hope: everyone knew the bold, brash banker had powerful friends in high places, and word spread fast that he’d called on them, and they’d come through. That evening, when Mrs. Regina Beaufort stepped into her usual opera box with her old, bright social smile and a glinting new emerald necklace strung around her throat, the whole of New York’s tight-knit old guard exhaled in unison.

Their unspoken rule was ironclad: anyone who broke the sacred code of financial probity had to be cast out, no exceptions. But no one wanted to be forced to turn the Beauforts away. Their gilded, endlessly festive ballroom was the glittering heart of the season, and losing them would leave a hollow no other household could fill. Plenty of people who didn’t care a fig for moral rectitude were already mourning the loss of their best entertainer before the man had even formally collapsed.

Newland Archer had already made up his mind. He was going to Washington. He’d timed the trip to line up with the patent lawsuit he’d vaguely mentioned to May, the perfect excuse to slip away and see Countess Ellen Olenska, to finally say all the words he’d been swallowing down for months. But on Tuesday, his senior partner Mr. Letterblair told him the case was going to be postponed for weeks. Still, he walked home that afternoon dead set on leaving the very next evening. May knew next to nothing about his work, had never shown the faintest curiosity about his cases, and would never notice a delayed court date, or remember the names of two random strangers litigating over a invention. He couldn’t put off seeing Ellen any longer. There was too much at stake, too much he had to tell her.

When he walked into his office Wednesday morning, Letterblair’s face was ashen. Beaufort hadn’t pulled it off after all. He’d only spread those reassuring rumours to lull his depositors into calm, drawing thousands in new payments to his bank right up until the night before, when the ugly truth finally leaked out. Now there was a full-blown run on the bank, and its doors would shatter and close before the day was out. The tricks he’d pulled were cruel, cowardly, and set to be one of the most shameful failures in Wall Street’s short, rough history.

“I’ve seen scandals in my time,” Letterblair stammered, white as a sheet, “but nothing this bad. Everyone we know is going to get burned. And what on earth do we do about his wife? I pity old Catherine Manson Mingott more than anyone — she always believed in him, made him her favourite, and at her age, who knows what this shock will do to her? And she’s related to all of you, through the Dallas line. Her only chance would be to leave him, but who on earth is going to tell her that? Her place is at his side, and thank goodness she never seemed to notice how rotten his private affairs were.”

A clerk knocked, and handed Archer a note sealed in May’s familiar neat, rounded handwriting.
*“Dearest Newland, please come uptown as soon as you possibly can. Granny had a slight stroke last night. She somehow found out about the bank disaster before anyone else could warn her. Uncle Lovell is away shooting, and Papa was so overcome by the thought of the disgrace he spiked a fever and can’t leave his bed. Mamma needs you dreadfully — please go straight to Granny’s house the second you can get away.”*

Archer passed the note to Letterblair, and ten minutes later was lurching north in a packed horse car, switching at 14th Street for one of the wobbly, high-roofed Fifth Avenue omnibuses that jolted over cobblestones like a storm-tossed ship. It was past noon by the time it dropped him outside old Catherine’s brownstone.

Mrs. Welland was hovering by the ground-floor sitting room window, her face drawn, and waved him in with a haggard, relieved gesture. May was waiting for him in the hall, pale but smiling. The space had that strange, tight hush all normally immaculate houses get the second illness invades: furs and winter wraps were heaped in piles on the hall chairs, the doctor’s bag and overcoat sat on the side table, and half a dozen unopened calling cards were stacked beside them, forgotten. Dr. Bencomb had just left his second visit, May told him, and the outlook was far more hopeful than it had been at dawn — Granny’s ferocious will to live was already pulling her back from the edge.

Behind closed doors and heavy yellow damask curtains, Mrs. Welland whispered the full, horrifying story in shocked undertones. The night before, just after Catherine had finished her usual post-dinner game of solitaire, the doorbell had rung. A lady so swathed in veil the servants didn’t recognise her at first had demanded to see her. The butler realised who it was when she spoke, opened the sitting room door and announced “Mrs. Julius Beaufort”, then left the two of them alone for an hour. By the time Catherine’s bell rang, Regina had slipped out unseen, and the old woman was sitting huge, still and white in her armchair, gesturing to be helped to her bedroom. She’d seemed fully in control then, but at three that morning the servants had found her propped up on her pillows, one small plump hand hanging limp, a lopsided smile on her face.

The stroke was mild, the doctor said. She could talk, make her wishes clear, and was already regaining movement in her face. When they pieced together her slurred, fragmented sentences, they realised what Regina had done: she’d come begging for old Catherine to bail her husband out, to use all her influence to make the entire family cover up his crime, to not “desert” them, to pretend none of the hundreds of ruined depositors existed.
“‘Honour’s honour, and honesty’s honesty, in Manson Mingott’s house, and it’ll stay that way till they carry me out feet first,’” the old woman had stammered to her daughter, thick-tongued from partial paralysis. “And when she said ‘But Auntie, my name’s Regina Dallas’ — I told her it was Beaufort when he hung those jewels round her neck, and it’s Beaufort now he’s covered her in shame.”

Mrs. Welland wept softly, distraught at having to face such unvarnished disgrace. Archer sat by the window, staring blankly out at the empty street. They’d called him more for moral support than anything else: telegrams were out to Lovell Mingott, footmen were running notes out to all the family members in the city, and all anyone could do was sit in the dim, curtained room and murmur about the ruin of the Beauforts and Regina’s unforgivable audacity.

The older ladies traded stories of old scandals, agreeing that any proper wife of a disgraced man would vanish off the face of the earth with him, never dare show her face or beg her family to cover for her. They clucked about Regina’s emerald necklace, rumoured to have been sent over on approval from the jewellers the very afternoon she wore it to the opera, and wondered if they’d ever get paid for it.

Archer listened half-distractedly. His belief in unbreakable financial honesty for old New York families was as deeply ingrained as anyone’s, but his mind kept drifting to the half-packed suitcase waiting for him at home, to the train to Washington he was meant to board that very next evening.

Then Mrs. Lovell Mingott stepped back into the room, frowning.
“She’s asking for Ellen. I already wrote to her and Medora, but no — she says to send a telegram, right now, tell Ellen to come home alone.”

No one spoke for a long beat. Mrs. Welland sighed in resigned acceptance, and May stood up to gather the scattered newspapers off the floor.
“Of course we have to do what Granny wants,” she said, turning back to them with that bright, unshakable smile, her voice clear as two tapped silver bells as she spoke Ellen Olenska’s name. “Newland’s here, he can drop the telegram at the office right away. You were telling me you had to run out for work errands anyway, dearest, right? It’ll be just in time for tomorrow’s morning train.” She sat down at Granny’s tiny rosewood writing desk, scribbled the telegram out in her large, neat, childlike hand, blotted it carefully, and handed it to him.
“What a silly little shame,” she said softly, turning back to her mother and aunt, “that you’ll just miss each other on the road. Newland has to leave for Washington tonight for that Supreme Court patent lawsuit, after all. We can’t ask him to put the whole firm’s important work on hold, not when Granny’s already doing so much better, and Uncle Lovell will be home tomorrow.”

Mrs. Welland agreed at once, of course — Granny would never be the one to ask him to neglect his duties. Archer heard her murmur, as he walked out the door, “But why on earth would she make us telegraph for Ellen?” and May’s clear, steady voice answering, “Perhaps she wants to remind her, once and for all, that her duty is to go back to her husband the Count.”

The front door clicked shut behind Archer. He stepped out into the crisp afternoon air, the telegram crinkling slightly in his hand, and turned toward the telegraph office. Somewhere south of the city, the tracks to Washington stretched out before him. Somewhere north, Ellen was already packing her bags to come home.
