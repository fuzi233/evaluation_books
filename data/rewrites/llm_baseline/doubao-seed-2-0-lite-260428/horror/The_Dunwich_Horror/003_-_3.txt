All the while, Old Wizard Whateley kept buying head after head of cattle, and not a single one ever turned up in his ragged, lowing herd. No hides hung on his fence line, no bones bleached in the pastures, no smatter of dried blood marked the grass where they might have been butchered—they simply vanished, swallowed whole by the dark that clung to his hillside farm like a shroud.

The old clapboard house, with its steep peaked roof and rear half buried deep in the unyielding rocky slope, creaked and hummed under his relentless labor. For decades, only three ground-floor rooms, half-rotted and strewn with trash, had ever been livable enough for him and his hunchbacked daughter Lavinia; now he hammered and sawed from dawn to long after the hollows went dark, repairing long-abandoned upper floors that no one had stepped foot in for two generations. The old man, half bent to the bone and gnarled with splinters and mildew, held reserves of strength no one could explain, babbling demented half-prayers to things in the hills as he worked, his carpentry sharp, precise, no wasted motion at all.

He had started the work the day Wilbur was born, first clearing and nailing tight a windowless toolshed at the edge of the property, slapping a heavy iron lock on its door that no local blacksmith could pick. Now, restoring the upper story, he boarded every single window shut from the inside, nailing planks an inch thick across every pane, and when the local gossips jeered that it was crazy to waste work on a part of the house no one had used in 50 years, he only stared back at them with milky, unblinking eyes.

Downstairs, he fitted out a new room for his grandson, lining every wall with heavy, tall shelves that no one but him and Wilbur were allowed to touch. He dragged out every tattered, rotting grimoire and partial book heaped in the corners of the house for decades—tomes bound in unknown leather, pages printed in black letter script that no local minister could identify—and sorted them with painstaking, reverent care, gluing torn pages back together with paste he stirred over his rusted kitchen stove. “I got some small use out of these,” he’d mumble to anyone who wandered in, fingers black with old ink and mold, “but that boy’s fitten to make far better use. He’ll need ’em all in perfect order, for they’re every scrap of learnin’ he’ll ever need.”

By September of 1914, when Wilbur was a year and seven months old, the whole of Dunwich began to whisper that something was deeply, unforgivably wrong with the child. He stood as tall and broad as a normal four-year-old, his speech fluid, sharp, and eerily old, as if the words were being pulled from a throat far older than his tiny frame. He roamed the hills with Lavinia at all hours, scrambling up cliff faces no other child could climb, chittering back at the crows that circled Sentinel Hill like he was trading secrets with them. At home, he pored over the queer diagrams and unnameable charts in his grandfather’s moldy books for hours on end, while Old Whateley quizzed him in low, rumbling tones through the hushed summer afternoons.

When the upper story renovations were done, the watchers on the village road spotted a thing they could not explain: one of the rear windows on the east gable, pressed right up against the hillside, had been cut out and replaced with a solid, cleated plank door, and a wide, heavy wooden runway ran straight up the bare rock from the ground to its threshold. Around that same time, the old locked toolshed Wilbur’s birth had left sealed was left gaping open, its iron lock hanging snapped off its hasp. When Earl Sawyer stopped by to sell Old Whateley a herd of calves a few days later, he stepped inside the shed out of curiosity, and stumbled back gagging, his throat burning. The stench, he swore later, was not rot, not sulfur, not any smell that belonged to this world—he had only ever caught a whiff of something close to it once, near the standing stone circles hidden deep in the highest hills. The rest of Dunwich folk merely shrugged; none of their homes or outbuildings ever smelled particularly sweet, anyway.

The months that followed brought no visible disasters, but every soul in the valley swore the low, thrumming rumble that had rumbled under the hills for decades was growing louder, steadier, making the mason jars rattle on pantry shelves in the dead of night. On May Eve 1915, the tremors were strong enough to shake windows 20 miles away in Aylesbury, and that Hallowe’en, pale, unearthly flames burst up from the summit of Sentinel Hill, synchronized with a deep underground roar that no earthquake could explain. The locals crossed their thresholds and spat over their shoulders, muttering that it was just those witch Whateleys, calling up their devils again.

Wilbur was growing at an uncanny, impossible pace. By the time he turned four, he looked like a boy of ten, his narrow, goatish face settling into a fixed, hungry look of unspoken evil that made strangers flinch when they met his gaze. He talked far less than he had as a toddler, turning taciturn and secretive, muttering snatches of guttural, unrecognizable jargon under his breath and chanting in bizarre, lurching rhythms that chilled the marrow of anyone who overheard. The local hounds hated and feared him with a ferocity no one could account for—they would snarl, cower, and lunge for his throat the second he came within sight, so he took to carrying a rusted revolver tucked in his belt. Every time he shot a dog that charged him, he sank even further into the black books of Dunwich’s farmers.

Visitors to the Whateley house almost always found Lavinia alone on the ground floor, while heavy, unhuman footsteps and echoing, inhuman cries boomed behind the nailed-shut second story walls. She would never explain what her father and her son were doing up there, not even when a jocular fish peddler tried to rattle the locked stairway door. The second his hand touched the knob, Lavinia went deathly pale, her whole body shaking with a terror so raw it made the peddler stop teasing immediately. Later, he told the loafers at the Dunwich general store that he’d heard something heavy as a team of draft horses stamping and snorting on the second floor, a floor that should never have held a weight half that great. The men on the store porch went quiet, thinking of that strange plank door set into the gable, that heavy runway built to bear impossible weight, all the cattle that had vanished without a trace. They shuddered, recalling old tales of Old Whateley’s youth, of the dark things you call up out of the earth when you slit a bullock’s throat at midnight on the right altar, for the old gods that don’t sleep under the hills. Before long, every dog in the county refused to step within a hundred yards of the Whateley farm, snarling and howling as if there was something worse than the boy himself waiting inside.

In 1917, the Great War pulled the draft boards into the remote valley, and Squire Sawyer Whateley, heading the local board, nearly wept trying to scrape together enough young Dunwich men even fit to send to a development camp, the whole region rotting with inbreeding and deprivation the government could not explain. Alarmed by the scale of the decay, Washington sent officers and medical experts out to survey the area, and the reports made headlines across New England. That publicity dragged city reporters out to Dunwich, and the *Boston Globe* and *Arkham Advertiser* ran flamboyant full-page Sunday spreads detailing young Wilbur’s impossible precociousness, Old Whateley’s rumored black magic, the shelves of unholy books, the sealed second floor of the Whateley farmhouse, and the strange, unearthly noises thrumming under the hills. Wilbur was four and a half years old then, and he looked like a lad of fifteen, coarse dark stubble fuzzing along his jaw and upper lip, his voice already cracking into a deep, guttural rumble that did not sound like any human child’s voice.

Earl Sawyer guided the reporters and photographers out to the farm, pointing out the strange, unearthly stench that now seeped out from under the nailed upper story windows, thick enough to taste on the back of your tongue. It was exactly the same smell, he told them, that he’d encountered in that abandoned tool shed years before, the same faint, alien tang that drifted off the stone circles hidden in the high hills at midnight. The local Dunwich folk grinned at the papers’ obvious, silly mistakes about their customs, but they went quiet when they read that the reporters had noticed Old Whateley always paid for every new head of cattle in ancient, tarnished gold coins so old no historian could name their origin. The Whateleys had greeted the city visitors with sullen, ill-concealed loathing, but they never dared raise a hand to drive them away—they knew drawing any more attention to the things growing behind their nailed-shut walls would be far, far worse than a few silly newspaper stories.
