In the early days of George the Magnificent’s reign—when the world was still figuring out whether to wear cravats or just go with a scarf tied like a pirate—there lived in Clavering, a small town in the west of England, a gentleman named Pendennis. Now, this wasn’t some dashing nobleman who’d been born with a silver spoon and a title already engraved on his forehead. No, sir. This Pendennis had *earned* his dignity, one gritty apothecary appointment at a time.

Back in the day, when he was young and poor (and possibly not quite as dignified), Mr. Pendennis ran a little shop in Bath—a place so modest it might have been mistaken for a storage room for someone’s old boots. Above the door hung a gilt pestle and mortar, gleaming like a warning sign: “Do not enter unless you are prepared to be treated with both medicine and mild disdain.” Inside, Mr. Pendennis dispensed remedies to the sick, plasters to farmers’ wives, and toothbrushes to anyone who looked like they hadn’t brushed their teeth since the Regency began.

And yet! Despite selling toothpaste that probably tasted like regret and powdered chalk, this man was no mere tradesman. He was, in fact, a gentleman of ancient bloodline, whose family tree stretched back so far it likely included a Druid who once cursed the Romans for stealing his best acorn. The Pendennises were Cornish nobility—well, almost. They had intermarried with Normans (a move that, while socially questionable, at least gave them a decent excuse for being perpetually confused about their own heritage). They were related to every important family in Wales and Brittany—though, admittedly, only by marriage, a cousin’s cousin’s aunt’s husband’s dog, or something equally tenuous.

Pendennis himself had studied at Cambridge—briefly. He’d even managed to scrape through two years before his father, in a moment of financial recklessness, declared bankruptcy. That ended any dreams of academic glory. So, instead of becoming a scholar or a poet, young Pen found himself apprenticed to his mother’s brother—a London apothecary of such low birth that even the local pigeons avoided him. It was a fate worse than death, but necessity, as they say, is the mother of all awkward careers.

He eventually broke free from his uncle’s clutches (who, by the way, was more interested in mixing laxatives than in manners) and set up shop in Bath under his own name. His early days were lean—so lean, in fact, that he once considered selling his mother’s hairbrush to pay for a new windowpane. But then came Lady Ribstone, a woman of considerable size, wealth, and temper, who was knocked sideways by an Irish chairman driving a carriage like a man possessed by the spirit of drunkenness itself.

The accident happened right outside Pendennis’s shop. The chairpole speared through the most beautiful pink bottle in the window (a tragedy!), and Lady Ribstone—screaming like a startled badger—collapsed into the doorway. Mr. Pendennis, ever the gentleman, rushed forward with cinnamon and sal-volatile, revived her, and treated her with such soothing grace that she immediately appointed him her personal apothecary. And thus, by sheer luck and excellent bedside manner, Pendennis became the medical man to one of Somerset’s most prominent families.

From there, things improved rapidly. He treated Master Ribstone during a Christmas fever (which involved a lot of hot baths and very little sleep), won over the entire Codlingbury household, and soon found himself patronized by the upper crust of Bath society. His shop, once humble, became stylish; he stopped selling perfumes and toothbrushes (they were “unworthy of a gentleman of ancient lineage,” he declared, though he’d once sold a tube of toothpaste to a goat); he shut the shop entirely and opened a proper surgery attended by a young man who wore a waistcoat and tried very hard not to look like a servant.

Then came the gig—with a man to drive it. Then a one-horse carriage. And finally, just before his mother passed away, he bought a close carriage with the Pendennis coat of arms emblazoned on the panels—proudly, defiantly, like a statement: *I am now a man of consequence.*

“Would Arthur say now?” his mother asked, gazing from her bedroom window, where her chair had been rolled to catch a glimpse of the grand vehicle. “He never once came to see Johnny through all these years of poverty.”

“Captain Pendennis is in India, mother,” said John, adjusting his collar. “And if you please, I wish you would not call me Johnny in front of Mr. Parkins.”

She died soon after. Her voice—the kind that scolded, cajoled, and occasionally sang off-key hymns—was gone. Pendennis changed the night-bell to her room and slept in her big bed, which smelled faintly of lavender and unresolved grievances.

By this time, he was well past forty, and had achieved what every self-respecting provincial doctor dreams of: *being a gentleman.* He’d framed his pedigree and hung it proudly in the drawing room, between a painting of Codlingbury House and another of St. Boniface’s College, Cambridge—where he’d spent exactly two months before being forced into the world of medicinal mixtures and moral compromise.

Around the same time, another patient died—Lady Pontypool, a woman of many marriages, including one to a reverend who apparently believed in the power of divine punishment. She’d been cared for by Miss Helen Thistlewood, a distant relative of the Bareacres family and daughter of a naval officer killed at Copenhagen. Miss Thistlewood was quiet, sweet, and endured her elderly patroness’s tyranny with the patience of a saint—and perhaps a touch of masochism.

It was at Lady Pontypool’s funeral that Pendennis, feeling a sudden surge of courage (or possibly just a strong desire to avoid being alone), decided to ask Miss Thistlewood to marry him. He’d rehearsed the speech in bed, then perfected it in his carriage, whispering lines like, “I am old, yes, but I am good-natured, respectable, and possess a certain amount of property.” He held her hand—cold, pale, and trembling—as he delivered it.

She blushed, curtsied deeply, and said she would “consider” his proposal.

They married during the dull season in Bath—when London was in full swing. Pendennis, having secured lodgings in Holles Street via a professional friend (M.R.C.S., naturally), took his new wife to the theatres, parks, and Chapel Royal. He left cards on Lord Pontypool, the Earl of Bareacres, and Sir Pepin Ribstone—only to receive silence, a polite visit, and a card delivered by a footman, respectively. But the Ribstones invited them to dinner—an event Pendennis bragged about until the day he died.

His dream had always been to become a gentleman. And now, thanks to frugality, a lucky copper mine investment, and a well-timed sale of his apothecary business, he was officially a landowner. He bought a house and small estate near Clavering, where he could walk across fields and pretend he was the lord of the manor. He retired from medicine, sold the shop to Mr. Parkins (who promptly renamed it “Parkins & Sons”), and adopted the title of Squire—much to the delight of everyone except the local postmaster, who thought it was all rather excessive.

Arthur Pendennis, his son, was eight at the time. He remembered nothing of the shop, the pills, or the smell of turpentine. His father never spoke of it. Instead, he wore a bottle-green coat with brass buttons, attended market in a knowing manner, and bowed gravely to coachmen as if he’d been born to it.

He founded the Clavering Book Club (mostly to impress people), started the Samaritan Soup and Blanket Society (to show charity), and rerouted the mail coach away from Cacklefield and through Clavering (because, clearly, Clavering deserved better).

At church, he was a model vestryman. At market, he inspected oats, punched geese in the breast, and negotiated with farmers like a man who’d seen everything. He was now ashamed to be called “Doctor”—that title was for common folk. Everyone who wanted to please him called him “Squire.”

And then there were the portraits. A whole gallery of them appeared overnight—Lelys, Vandykes, and one suspiciously similar to a portrait of Oliver Cromwell, but with different eyes. Pendennis insisted they were ancestors. His wife looked skeptical. His son believed every word. To little Arthur, Roger Pendennis of Agincourt, Arthur Pendennis of Crecy, and General Pendennis of Blenheim were real men—just as real as Robinson Crusoe or Peter Wilkins. In fact, he often argued that the Pendennises were more heroic, because they had actual coats of arms.

Their income—about £800 a year—wasn’t enough to live among the great folks of the county, but it was enough to live near them. They dined each other twice a year under moonlight, sometimes traveling twelve miles for a meal that cost half a crown. They also had the pleasure of hosting Mrs. Pybus, who constantly poked around Helen’s conservatories and intercepted soup tickets. Captain Glanders, a swaggering dragoon officer, was forever trying to get the Squire involved in feuds with the Vicar, the Postmaster, and the Reverend F. Wapshot—whose son, Anglesea Glanders, had allegedly been flogged too hard for wearing mismatched socks.

Fairoaks, their home, stood a mile from Clavering, overlooking the river Brawl. On the other side lay Clavering Park—the decaying estate of Sir Francis Clavering, Bart. The house was empty, shuttered, and haunted by the ghosts of wasted fortune. Arthur used to wander through its halls, imagining the spirits of long-dead nobles whispering secrets. At sunset, the view was magnificent—the park glowed golden, the river ran off into shadow, and the towers of Clavering Abbey rose in purple splendor. Arthur would stand with his mother, recite poetry (“These are thy glorious works, Parent of Good…”), and they’d hug like they were auditioning for a sentimental novel.

But when Mr. Pendennis entered the room, all poetry ceased. He walked in with his newspaper under his arm, his hat perfectly brushed, his meals served at precisely six o’clock, and his nap scheduled for 6:15 sharp. He read the *Gardener’s Gazette*, played picquet, and inspected the stables, henhouses, and pigstye—all at exact times. Little Pen, meanwhile, buried himself in books, reading anything from *Robinson Crusoe* to the *Encyclopædia Britannica*.

Every two years, Major Pendennis—the elder brother—would visit from India. He was the family’s connection to high society. He brought news of lords, dined with dukes, and spoke of them with soldierly respect. “My Lord Bareacres has kindly invited me to shoot pheasants,” he’d say, as if it were a diplomatic mission. The Major was a bachelor, despite offers from Miss Hunkle of Lilybank, who had fifteen hundred pounds a year and a reputation for being “very agreeable.” But the Major declined. “As a bachelor, nobody cares how poor I am,” he explained. “Miss Hunkle, though respectable, lacks the birth and manners required for my sphere. I shall die an old bachelor.”

Time proved him right. Miss Hunkle married a French nobleman and became Baroness de Carambole—though she divorced him within a year, claiming he kept trying to teach her how to ride a horse without using a saddle.

The Major was beloved at Fairoaks. He praised Mrs. Pendennis as “the finest lady in England,” and truly meant it. She was gentle, kind, and so perfect that country ladies with three times her money couldn’t help but resent her. Miss Pybus called her cold. Miss Pierce said she was proud. Mrs. Wapshot, as a doctor of divinity’s wife, felt she should be given the *pas*. But Mrs. Pendennis paid no attention. She prayed, loved her family, helped neighbors, and carried herself with the calm dignity of someone who knew she was doing the right thing—even if no one else did.

Arthur, growing up under such a mother, thought of her as an angel—perhaps slightly less supernatural than the Virgin Mary, but definitely more practical. When his father took her to balls or concerts, he’d walk in with her on his arm and stare boldly at the nobility, as if to say: *Look at her. Can any of you produce a woman like that?*

And really, could they?

Not that it mattered much. For Arthur Pendennis, the future was wide open. His father was a gentleman. His mother was an angel. And somewhere, in the distance, the Major was planning his next visit—and possibly another failed romance. Life, in all its absurdity and charm, had begun.
