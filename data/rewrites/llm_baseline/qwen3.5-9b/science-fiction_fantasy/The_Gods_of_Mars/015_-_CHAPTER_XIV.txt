"My son!" I could scarcely believe my own ears. Rising slowly, I confronted the handsome youth standing before me. Only now, upon close inspection, did I truly comprehend the magnetic pull of his face and demeanor. His features possessed the sharp, clear-cut elegance inherited from his mother, yet tempered with a rugged masculinity that was unmistakably mine. Most striking were his grey eyes—their color, their depth, and the piercing expression within them were pure John Carter.

The boy stood facing me, his gaze a mixture of trembling hope and lingering uncertainty.

"Tell me of your mother," I commanded softly. "Speak of all the years I have been stolen from her by a ruthless destiny."

With a cry of pure delight, he launched himself at me, wrapping his arms tightly around my neck. For a fleeting moment, holding my son close, tears blurred my vision, threatening to choke me—a reaction I might have dismissed as the weakness of a sentimental fool had not a lifetime of experience reminded me that a man may display profound tenderness toward women and children while remaining an iron giant in the harder battles of life.

"Your height, your bearing, the terrifying ferocity of your blade-work," the boy observed, pulling back slightly, "match exactly what my mother has recounted to me a thousand times. Yet, even with such proof, I struggled to accept the truth of what seemed so improbable, despite my desperate desire for it. Is there something else? Did some specific detail convince you?"

"What, my boy?" I asked.

"Your first words," he whispered. "They were of my mother. Only a man who loved her as deeply as my father did would instinctively think of her first."

"For many years, my son, I have rarely known a moment when the radiant image of your mother's face has not haunted my thoughts. Tell me of her."

"The elders who knew her best say she remains unchanged, or perhaps has only grown more beautiful—if such a thing is possible. Yet, when she believes I am unaware, her countenance falls into a deep sadness, filled with longing. She thinks constantly of you, my father. All of Helium shares her grief and her sorrow. Even her grandfather's people cherish her; they loved you too, and they revere your memory as the savior of Barsoom.

"Every year, on the anniversary of the day you raced across this dying world to unlock the secrets of that terrible portal guarding the life-force of millions, a great festival is held in your honor. But amidst the thanks, there are tears—real tears of regret that the giver of this happiness is not here to share the joy of living. Upon all Barsoom, there is no greater name than John Carter."

"And under what name does your mother call you?" I asked.

"The people of Helium requested I bear my father's name, but my mother refused. She insisted that you and she had chosen a name together, and that your wish must supersede all others. So, the name she calls me is the one you desired—a blend of ours: Carthoris."

Xodar, who had been steering the ship during our conversation, interrupted us.

"She is sinking dangerously by the bow, John Carter," he reported. "While climbing steeply, the tilt masked the problem, but now that I'm attempting a level course, it's critical. The wound in her hull has breached one of the forward repulsor tanks."

I verified the damage immediately. It was far worse than anticipated. Maintaining a horizontal trajectory required forcing the bow upward at a severe angle, drastically reducing our speed. Furthermore, with the forward tanks leaking their repulsive energy, we had less than an hour before the ship would flip stern-first, rendering us helpless drifters.

Though we had slowed slightly earlier, driven by a newfound sense of security, I seized the helm again and pushed the engine to its absolute limit. We surged northward at terrifying speeds. Meanwhile, Carthoris and Xodar frantically worked on the gaping rift in the bow, battling a futile war against the escaping energy.

Night still held sway as we crossed the northern edge of the polar ice cap and the cloud layer. Beneath us stretched a quintessential Martian landscape: rolling ochre plains of dried-up seas, low-lying hills, and scattered the grim, silent ruins of a forgotten civilization. Towering piles of ancient architecture stood empty, inhabited only by the ghosts of a mighty race and the great white apes of Barsoom.

Maintaining our precarious balance grew increasingly impossible. The bow dipped lower and lower until I was forced to cut the engines entirely to avoid plunging us earthward.

As dawn broke, sweeping away the darkness, our craft gave a final, violent lurch. It tipped onto its side, then swung in a slow, agonizing circle, the bow dragging deeper below the stern with every passing second. Clinging desperately to rails and stanchions, we eventually unbuckled our safety harnesses. Moments later, the deck tilted to a vertical ninety-degree angle, leaving us hanging by our belts, feet dangling a thousand yards above the barren ground.

Positioned near the controls, I reached for the lever governing the repulsors. The ship responded instantly, gently easing our descent toward the surface.

It took nearly thirty minutes before we touched down. Directly north rose a lofty range of hills, offering the best cover from pursuers we suspected might be tracking us. An hour later, we navigated into the rounded gullies of these hills, surrounded by the vibrant flowering plants that thrive in Barsoom's arid wastes. Here, we discovered vast clusters of milk-bearing shrubs—a vital resource for the Green Men—and a welcome respite for our starving bodies.

Hidden beneath a thick canopy of foliage, concealed from any wandering aerial scouts, we lay down to sleep. For me, it was the first rest in many hours. This marked the beginning of my fifth day on Barsoom, since my sudden translocation from my humble cottage on Earth's Hudson River to Dor, the valley that was once beautiful and now hideous. In all this time, I had slept only twice, including once inside the storage vault of the Therns.

Mid-afternoon brought an awakening. Someone seized my hand, pressing kisses to my palm. Startled, I opened my eyes to behold the beautiful face of Thuvia.

"My Prince! My Prince!" she cried, overflowing with ecstatic joy. "'Tis you whom I mourned as dead! My ancestors have been merciful; I have not lived in vain."

Her voice roused Xodar and Carthoris. The boy stared at the woman in astonishment, but she seemed oblivious to anyone but me. She would have embraced me and buried me in affection had I not gently but firmly withdrawn.

"Come, Thuvia," I soothed. "You are overwhelmed by the dangers and hardships you've endured. You forget yourself, and you forget that I am the husband of the Princess of Helium."

"I forget nothing, my Prince," she replied earnestly. "You have spoken no word of love to me, nor do I expect you ever shall; yet nothing can stop me from loving you. I would not usurp the place of Dejah Thoris. My highest ambition is to serve you forever as your slave. No greater boon exists, no higher honor can be craved, no greater happiness hoped for."

As I have noted before, I am no lover of women, and I must confess I felt more uncomfortable and embarrassed than ever at that moment. Though I understood the Martian custom allowing female slaves to accompany Martian men—who possess the high, chivalrous honor to protect every woman in their households—I had never personally chosen women as my attendants.

"If I ever return to Helium, Thuvia," I said, "you shall come with me, but as an honored equal, not as a slave. There, you will find many handsome young nobles willing to face Issus herself to earn your smile. We will arrange your marriage shortly to one of the finest among them. Cast aside this foolish gratitude-born infatuation, born of your innocence and mistaken for love. I prefer your friendship, Thuvia."

"You are my master; it shall be as you say," she answered simply, though a note of sadness lingered in her tone.

"How did you come to be here, Thuvia?" I asked. "Where is Tars Tarkas?"

"The great Thark, I fear, is dead," she replied mournfully. "He was a mighty fighter, but a multitude of Green Warriors from a rival horde overwhelmed him. The last I saw, they were carrying him, wounded and bleeding, toward the abandoned city from which they had sallied to attack us."

"Are you certain he is dead?" I pressed. "And where is this city?"

"It lies just beyond this range of hills. Your vessel, in which you so nobly yielded your seat to ensure our escape, proved beyond our small skills to navigate properly. Consequently, we drifted aimlessly for two days before deciding to abandon the craft and attempt to reach the nearest waterway on foot. Yesterday, we crossed these hills and encountered the dead city beyond. We ventured into its streets, heading toward the center, when at an intersection we spotted a group of Green Warriors approaching.

"Tars Tarkas was ahead, and they saw him, but they missed me. The Thark rushed back to my side, forcing me into a nearby doorway. 'Remain hidden,' he urged. 'Make your way to Helium if you can.'

"'There is no escape for me now,' he declared. 'These are the Warhoons of the South. Once they see my armor, it will be to the death.'
