The Carnival day had passed, leaving behind no immediate mark on Romola’s life save the quiet burden of caring for her cousin Brigida, whose frailty and fading youth now seemed to weigh upon her like a solemn duty. Yet beneath this surface calm, a deeper current stirred—a Lenten season that brought with it not rest, but relentless mental strain and ceaseless effort. Bernardo del Nero had been chosen Gonfaloniere, a victory for the Medicean faction, though one that only deepened Romola’s unease. She felt a growing certainty—almost a premonition—that some hidden scheme was unfolding, poised between triumph and betrayal during these two months of her godfather’s authority. Each morning, as dawn crept into her chamber, she was met again by the same haunting dread, clinging to her like a shadow. It followed her through the narrow streets toward the Duomo, where she went each day to hear the early sermon. But once inside, amid the throng and the rising voices, the chill began to fade—like fear dissolving in the heat of battle.

For Savonarola was preaching. These were his final Lenten sermons, delivered under the shadow of excommunication, which he now openly defied. With unflinching clarity, he held up the Church to public scrutiny, naming its corruption without disguise or mercy. He spoke of renewal—not merely reform, but a revolution born of divine will. He declared that a time was coming when the world would rise against moral decay, and the dead body of Christendom would tremble back to life, as Lazarus did at the call of God. At times, he saw himself leading that awakening, his voice echoing across Europe, stirring nations from spiritual slumber. At other moments, he foresaw only persecution and death—this life as a vigil, and salvation only beyond the grave.

Even those who doubted his sincerity could not deny the power of his presence. To Romola, who believed deeply in his purpose, the moment was electrifying—less as a political event than as a personal reckoning. His words became part of her daily strength, woven into the labor of tending the sick, feeding the hungry, and comforting the despairing. Famine still clung to Florence, disease spread through crowded homes, and whispers of the Plague grew louder. In such days, pity alone was not enough. She needed something greater—an ideal to sustain her, a vision of justice rising from suffering. And Savonarola gave her that: a belief in a higher struggle, one in which her small acts of mercy were not meaningless, but seeds sown in darkness for a harvest yet unseen.

Yet even in this grand music, dissonance emerged. The early days of hope—when the Frate preached peace, charity, and unity—had given way to sharper tones. Political factions refused to yield; the French deliverer, so long awaited, had turned away from his promise; and enemies, both petty and powerful, now attacked Savonarola with new fury. As the pressure mounted, so did his rhetoric. Once he had scorned miracles, insisting faith needed no supernatural proof. Now he declared that if the truth of his message were not acknowledged, God Himself would intervene with a miracle. And in the fire of his passion, threats of divine vengeance against tyrants and corrupt priests took on an edge sharpened not just by zeal, but by personal anger.

To Romola, this shift was painful. She sensed the conflict within him—the tension between selfless prophecy and human pride—laid bare in every word. But there were others who found in those harsh notes a kind of ecstasy. Baldassarre, the old man whose past remained shrouded in mystery, had discovered Savonarola’s sermons again. He attended them whenever he could, drawn not by piety, but by the fierce justice they promised. He watched Romola too—saw her face among the crowd—and waited for the right moment to speak with her. Twice she had glimpsed him in the Duomo—once, his dark eyes fixed on hers. She wished to avoid him, yet she looked for him, as one might search for a sign.

But now, more pressing than any memory of him was the present. The Lenten weeks passed. April, the final month of Bernardo del Nero’s rule, neared its end. Still, nothing happened to confirm Romola’s fears. Rumors had circulated from Rome—of Piero de’ Medici preparing a return—but Bernardo’s term would soon be over. Romola tried to find comfort in the fact that her anxieties had proven unfounded. Then, on the twenty-seventh, as she walked through the city on her usual rounds of mercy, a messenger from Camilla Rucellai intercepted her. The woman, known throughout Florence as one of its most fervent seers, demanded her presence immediately—on matters of utmost urgency.

Romola hesitated. She detested the shrill, ecstatic manner of such women, and lately had heard that Camilla had claimed visions condemning Bernardo del Nero. The thought of hearing more such nonsense made her recoil. Why should she open herself to knowledge that could only deepen her turmoil? Yet, by now, habit had shaped her responses. She had learned to suppress her impulses, to obey external demands rather than inner inclinations. Chiding herself for cowardice, she agreed to go.

She found Camilla in a room arranged like a monk’s cell—bare, dim, and filled with the scent of incense and anxiety. The woman seized Romola’s arm with trembling hands, speaking in urgent whispers that rose quickly to a shriek. She had seen a vision—revealed by Romola’s own angel—that Romola knew secrets about Bernardo del Nero, secrets that, if revealed, could save the Republic. She must act. She must break from the enemy of God.

Romola, usually calm and composed, reacted with sudden force. She pulled her arm free, but Camilla clutched tighter, like a crab refusing to let go. Her voice soared into frenzy, claiming that Christ Himself had appeared to her and commanded certain citizens to throw Bernardo del Nero from the window of the Palazzo Vecchio. Fra Girolamo knew of it, but dared not say the vision was false. “And since then,” Camilla wailed, eyes wild, “the Blessed Infant came to me and placed a sweet wafer on my tongue—proof that I have done His will!”

“Let me go!” Romola thundered, her voice low but sharp with rage. “God grant you are mad! Else you are abominably wicked!”

Her strength broke Camilla’s grip. Romola fled, rushing down the street until she reached the church of the Badia. There, behind the heavy curtain under the ancient stone arch, she found a space apart—a sanctuary of stillness. She sank onto the step before Filippino Lippi’s painting of the Virgin appearing to Saint Bernard, a scene of serene grace in a world of chaos. For a moment, she hoped the storm within her might subside.

But the question burned brightest: how could Savonarola allow such visions to stand unchallenged? Could he truly condone the idea of murder in the name of divine justice? Romola knew he wouldn’t. He valued law, order, and reason above all. Yet why did he not denounce Camilla’s claims publicly? Why remain silent?

The answer came, clear and painful: because he, too, walked the line between revelation and imagination. He feared that to condemn such visions would be to condemn his own. He was bound not only by conscience but by politics—by the risk of being seen as a suppressor of divine inspiration, especially when he himself was its chief mouthpiece. His silence was not weakness—it was entrapment.

Kneeling there, face buried in her hands, Romola felt the collapse of her faith. The passion that had once lifted her now seemed entangled with fanaticism. She thought of her godfather—his practical wisdom, his dignity, his refusal to pretend loyalty to the Medici. He was no villain. Yet the very people who called him a traitor were fighting for what she believed was right: a pure government, free from tyranny. And yet, in their zeal, they blurred the line between justice and cruelty.

Her heart pulled her toward Bernardo, toward the memories of her father—men who judged not by symbols, but by character. But her mind warned her: there was likely a plot. A secret movement to restore the Medici. Was that crime? Or merely resistance?

And then came Tito’s warnings—the dread that she might one day know the truth, and be torn between loyalty and duty.

Calm did not come. Not even in the hush of the chapel, not even before the peaceful image of the saint receiving heavenly visitors. The rocky solitude of the painting was too distant. She rose, determined to return to her work—to the sick, the poor, the dying. Only in action, in tangible kindness, could she reclaim a sense of meaning.

As she turned, she saw him standing just two yards away.

It was Baldassarre.
