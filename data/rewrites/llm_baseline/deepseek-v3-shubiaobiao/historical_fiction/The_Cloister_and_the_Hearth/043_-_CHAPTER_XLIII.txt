A shadow fell upon Margaret Brandt. She moved through her days as if walking in a dream, her spirit weighed down by some invisible burden. Even the gentlest word from Peter now made her startle, her wide eyes fixing upon him with something like fear. She withdrew from the warmth of Margaret Van Eyck’s company, no longer seeking solace in the old woman’s affection. Where once she had leaned into the embrace of friendship, she now stood stiff and trembling, sometimes even flinching away.

Most strange of all, she refused to step beyond her door while the sun still hung in the sky. If she went to Tergou, it was under the cover of darkness, slipping back before dawn could betray her. She avoided matins, shunned the daylight, until at last even Peter—slow as he was to notice such things—pressed her for an answer.

“The people,” she murmured. “They look at me.”

Margaret Van Eyck, too, saw the change and questioned her gently. “What troubles you, child? Surely you need not fear to confide in me?”

But Margaret could only shake her head, tears spilling down her cheeks. “I have no sorrows but those of my own making. I am treated more kindly than I deserve—especially here.”

“Then why stay away from me, dear heart?”

“I come more often than I should,” she whispered, her voice breaking.

Margaret Van Eyck sighed, puzzled. “There now, Reicht calls for you. Go, child. But I will find the truth of this.”

Turning the matter over in her mind, the old woman concluded that Gerard’s family must be the cause—their scorn driving the girl into this sorrow. Resolved to confront them, she set out the next day, Reicht at her heels, and marched to the hosier’s house.

Catherine received her with stiff respect, thanking her through tears for her kindness to Gerard. But when the conversation turned to Margaret Brandt, the warmth vanished. Catherine’s lips thinned, her eyes hardened, and she stood as immovable as stone. Margaret Van Eyck argued, cajoled, but met only silence and resistance. At last, her patience frayed, she left in a flurry of indignation, her curtsey clipped and cold.

Outside, she found Reicht speaking with a pale girl on crutches—little Kate. The old woman would have swept past, pride still stinging, but then a small, trembling hand slipped into hers. She looked down into Kate’s eyes, brimming with silent pleading and gratitude. The anger melted from her heart. She bent and kissed the girl’s forehead.

“I see,” she murmured. “Very well. I leave it to you.”

Back at home, she mused aloud. “Today I saw two things—one common, one rare. A stubborn, foolish woman… and an angel walking the earth. If only I could paint that face.”

Kate did not disappoint. She waited for the right moment, then told her mother what Reicht had shared—how Margaret had shed her own blood for Gerard in the wood.

“See how she loves him, Mother.”

“Who would not love him?” Catherine muttered.

“Think of it, though. Poor thing.”

“Aye,” Catherine admitted grudgingly. “She has her own troubles, no doubt. I can’t bear the sight of blood, let alone my own.”

It was a small concession, but when Kate pressed further, she was swiftly silenced.

A month later, a soldier returning from Burgundy brought a letter to the hosier’s house. The family sat at supper, the father absent, when the man laid the folded parchment on the table and left without reward.

Though none could read, they knew at once it was Gerard’s hand.

“And your father must be away!” Catherine cried. “Shame on you all—not one who can read your own brother’s words!”

Yet there was something in the letter they *could* understand. Between the lines, Gerard had drawn two clasped hands—a silent farewell. The sight struck them like a blow. The women wept openly; even Giles murmured, “Poor Gerard,” his voice uncharacteristically soft. Cornelis and Sybrandt sat in uneasy silence.

But how to hear the words? They did not wish to bare their ignorance or their grief before a stranger.

“Dame Van Eyck?” Kate suggested timidly.

“Aye, I’ll go to her,” Catherine decided. “She loves Gerard too. She’ll not refuse me.”

She found Margaret Van Eyck at prayer, waiting in a room where a young woman sat pensively by the stove. The stranger rose politely, and Catherine, warming her hands, studied her discreetly. The girl’s gown was trimmed with fur—a mark of wealth—but her face was gentle, unassuming.

“What brings me here, my dear? A letter—from my poor boy, far away in some wild place. And none of us can read it. Can you?”

“Yes.”

“Ah! That’s to your credit. She won’t be long, but every minute weighs on a mother’s heart.”

“I will read it to you.”

“Bless you, child.”

Catherine did not notice the tremor in the hand that took the letter, nor the way the girl’s breath caught.

“The first words are: *‘To my honoured parents.’*”

“Aye, he always did honour us,” Catherine whispered.

*“‘God and the saints have you in His holy keeping, and bless you by night and by day. Your one harsh deed is forgotten; your years of love remembered.’*”

Catherine pressed a hand to her heart, sinking back with a sob.

“Then comes this,” the girl went on softly. *“‘A long farewell.’*”

“Go on, dear. Even sorrow is comfort now.”

*“‘To my brothers Cornelis and Sybrandt—Be content; you will see me no more!’*”

“What does that mean? Ah!”

*“‘To my sister Kate. Little angel of my father’s house. Be kind to her—’*”

“That’s Margaret Brandt, his sweetheart,” Catherine murmured. “I’ve not been kind to her. Forgive me, Gerard.”

*“‘—for poor Gerard’s sake: since grief to her is death to me—’*”

The girl’s voice broke. A gasp, a shudder—then she slid from the chair, insensible, the letter slipping from her fingers as her head came to rest upon Catherine’s knees.
