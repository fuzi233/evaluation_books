The air in Margaret's chamber felt thick with the weight of revelation, a day that offered both solace and sorrow. She had found in Denys a true comrade, a balm to her weary spirit, yet the news he carried was sharp: Gerard was utterly alone in that distant, strange land. Hope, fragile as glass, shattered within her; she could not share Denys's naive belief that he would return soon. Indeed, if he had intended to come home, he would have arrived long ago.

Denys, however, was a relentless source of cheer. He embraced her as his "she-comrade," filling the room with his formulas of comfort and boisterous laughter. Margaret indulged him, doting on his presence just as she did on Martin, though her temper flared when he lingered too long over her provisions. She would not allow him to eat a single meal in her house, nor would she tolerate his rough tongue. "It spoils you, Denys," she chided, her voice trembling with suppressed emotion. "Good lack, to hear such ugly words escape such a comely head! Forbear, or I shall be angry; be civil." Instantly, Denys switched to his best manners, the struggle between his native politeness and his acquired ruffianism playing out as a ludicrous pantomime.

Yet, as the saying goes, when it rains, it pours. Other visitors soon sought Margaret's friendship. Among them was Margaret Van Eyck, who had sent a humble letter confessing her loss of favor and vowing to keep her distance, yet pleading for secrecy regarding her current residence. She begged that her whereabouts not be revealed, especially not to Gerard's people, whom she despised for driving him away. She wrote bitterly of their cruelty, noting that even after his departure, their hearts had not softened toward her as they might have if they had repented.

The Van Eycks were perplexed by her sudden coldness. Eventually, they confided in Reicht, whose quick wit carried the secret like water through a pipe straight to Catherine.

"Ay, and is she turned so bitter against us?" Catherine asked, her tone laced with confusion. "She stole our son from us, and now she hates us for not rushing into her arms. Nevertheless, it is a blessing she lives and is no farther away than Rotterdam."

Meanwhile, the English princess, now Countess Charolois, made a stately progress through the northern duchy, accompanied by her stepdaughter, Marie de Bourgogne, the young heiress. The old Duke, the most magnificent prince in Europe, displayed his full splendor. Troops of dazzling knights and bevy of gorgeously attired ladies attended the procession, followed by minstrels, jongleurs, bards, musicians, actors, and tumblers. Fencing matches, dances, and merriment filled every town they visited. Richart invited all his people to gather in Rotterdam to witness the pageant.

Days passed in Rotterdam before Denys accidentally encountered Catherine in the street. After a warm greeting, he crowed that he had finally found her "she-comrade." But Catherine cooled his enthusiasm, pointing out that he would have found her sooner had he stayed quietly at Tergou rather than vagabonding across Holland. "And being found, what the better are we?" she asked sharply. "Her heart is set dead against us now."

"Oh, let that flea stick," Denys retorted, undeterred. "Come with me to her house."

"No," Catherine refused, sensing the chill of rejection. "Those who come unbidden sit unseated." She insisted that Denys act as mediator, bringing the parties to a good understanding. He accepted the task with great pomp and confidence, trotting off to Margaret.

"She-comrade," he announced, "I met today a friend of yours."

"You did look into the Rotter then, and see yourself?" Margaret replied dryly.

"Nay, 'twas a female, and one who seeks your regard. 'Twas Catherine, Gerard's mother."

"Oh, was it?" Margaret said, her voice hardening. "Then you may tell her she comes too late. There was a time I longed and longed for her; but she held aloof in my hour of most need. So now, we will be as we have been."

Denys tried to coax her, but she remained bitter and sullen, unmoved by his pleas. He then scolded her, and at that, she broke down into hysterics. Frightened by the result of his own eloquence and caught off guard, Denys allowed himself to be trapped into a solemn promise never to mention the subject again. He returned to Catherine crestfallen and recounted the reception of his overtures. Catherine flew into a rage, informing the family how her advances had been rebuffed. The feud ignited, burning fiercer each day, until little Kate alone dared to make excuses for Margaret.

The very next day, another visitor arrived. Margaret found her household in a state of military enslavement and degradation: Martin was elbow-deep in soapsuds, Denys ironing with clumsy ineptitude, and Margaret herself plaiting ruffs, her eyes scanning her raw recruits with the critical gaze of a mistress. Into this scene stepped an old man, venerable at first glance but keen and wizened upon closer inspection.

"Ah!" cried Margaret. Swiftly, she turned her back on him, hiding her face with invincible repugnance. "Oh, that man! That man!"

"Nay, fear me not," said Ghysbrecht gently. "I come on a friend's errand. I bring you a letter from foreign parts."

"Mock me not, old man," she said slowly, turning around.

"Nay, see," he replied, holding out an enormous envelope.

Margaret darted forward, snatching the letter with trembling hands and glistening eyes. It was Gerard's handwriting.

"Oh, thank you, sir, bless you for this," she whispered, forgiving him for all the ill he had ever wrought upon her. Pressing the letter to her bosom with one hand, she glided swiftly from the room, leaving Ghysbrecht behind.

As she did not return, Ghysbrecht departed, casting a scowl at Martha. Margaret was left alone with her letter for hours, the silence of the room amplifying the thunder of her emotions.
