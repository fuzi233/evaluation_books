The following day, Higgins's door remained firmly locked when the sisters sought to pay their respects to the widow Boucher. An officious neighbor, however, revealed that Higgins was indeed at home, though he had visited Mrs. Boucher only briefly before departing on his daily rounds. Their reception proved deeply unsatisfying. Mrs. Boucher viewed herself as a grievously wronged woman, a sentiment born of genuine tragedy yet twisted by a corrosive selfishness. While her "animal" affection for her children existed, it was entirely overshadowed by her preoccupation with her own suffering. She regarded her offspring merely as burdens, her grief consumed by a relentless, querulous focus on her ruined state rather than on them.

Margaret attempted to bridge the gap between mother and children, finding that the latter were far truer mourners than their distracted parent. Through their eager, stammering recollections, they spoke of a kindness and tenderness lost when Daddy fell. Margaret's heart bled watching the mother drag them upstairs to gaze upon their dead, disfigured father. She saw how the coarseness of physical horror was being mingled with the sanctity of natural grief, a mixture that could only deepen their trauma. She tried to redirect their thoughts toward practical duties—how they might care for their mother, how they might honor their father's memory through service. These suggestions resonated more effectively than Mr. Hale's lofty appeals for spiritual reflection. The children, sensing immediate tasks within reach, began to attempt tidying the slatternly room, though Mr. Hale's standards remained too abstract for the indolent widow.

Mrs. Boucher's mind was a fortress of resentment, impervious to reason. She could not conceive of her husband's internal misery leading to the final, terrible step; instead, she saw only the consequences affecting herself. Though she secretly blamed him for his despair, she was inveterate in accusing everyone else—the masters, specifically Mr. Thornton, whose mill had been attacked; the Union, represented by Higgins; the hungry, noisy children—all conspirators in her ruin. She denied any justification for his suicide, yet blamed the world entirely for driving him to it.

Margaret listened to this unreasonableness with growing dismay, leaving her father dispirited when they departed.
"It is the town life," Margaret observed, trying to soothe him. "Their nerves are quickened by the haste and bustle, the constant pressure. Even children, confined in these pent-up houses, suffer from a lack of fresh air and open space. In the country, life is lived outdoors, under the sky."
"But people must live in towns," Mr. Hale countered gently. "And in the country, stagnation sets in, fostering fatalistic habits of mind."
"Yes," Margaret admitted. "Each mode of life breeds its own trials. The townsman struggles to remain patient amidst the chaos, while the country-bred man finds it difficult to summon activity and adapt to sudden emergencies. Both struggle to envision a future: the city-dweller because the present is so overwhelming and immediate, and the rural dweller because the slow pace tempts them to revel in mere animal existence, neglecting the pleasures of ambition and foresight."
"And so, the necessity for engagement in the city, and the dull contentment of the country, produce similar effects of narrow-mindedness," Mr. Hale concluded. "But this poor Mrs. Boucher! How little we can do for her."
"And yet, we dare not abandon her, though our efforts may seem futile," Margaret replied, her voice trembling slightly. "Oh, Papa, it is a hard world to live in!"
"So it is, my child," he said softly. "We feel it acutely now, perhaps, but we have known happiness even in sorrow. What a pleasure Frederick's visit was!"
"Yes, it was," Margaret said, her brightness faltering. "It was so charming, so snatched, so forbidden." She stopped abruptly. The memory of Frederick haunted her, tainted now by her own cowardice. Of all vices, she despised the lack of bravery, the meanness of heart that led to untruth. And she had lied. The shadow of Mr. Thornton's knowledge fell over her. Would he mind detection less if it were anyone else? She imagined telling her Aunt Shaw, Edith, her father, Captain Lennox, and Frederick. With Frederick, the thought was most painful; their mutual regard was still in its first flush. Yet, even falling from his esteem paled before the crushing shame she felt at the prospect of facing Mr. Thornton again.
Still, she longed to see him, to resolve the uncertainty gnawing at her soul. Her cheeks burned as she recalled her earlier defiance regarding trade practices, her implication that commerce inevitably involved deceit. She remembered Mr. Thornton's look of calm disdain, his succinct dismantling of her arguments. He had shown her that in the vast machinery of commerce, dishonesty, however clever, ultimately injured the actor. He had corrected her misuse of "chivalric" with the higher word "Christian," drawing the argument onto himself while she sat silent, harboring a faint, misplaced contempt.
Now, that contempt vanished. No more talk of chivalry. Henceforth, she must feel humiliated in his sight. Her heart leaped at every ring of the bell, only to sink into sickness at each disappointment. Her father clearly expected him, surprised by his absence. They had discussed matters the previous evening that required further time, and it had been understood they would meet again, the first opportunity Mr. Thornton allowed. Mr. Hale, who had paused his teaching due to his wife's illness, grew restless throughout the evening, plagued by the thought that the messenger delivering the book last night might have forgotten a note, or that a message lay waiting today.

"I will go and inquire, Papa," Margaret offered, rising as the bell rang again. "Stay!" she cried, sitting instantly and bending over her work. A footstep sounded on the stairs. It was not a visitor, but Dixon.
Lifting her head, Margaret sighed, a faint gladness warming her.
"It's Higgins, sir," Dixon announced. "He wants to see you, or Miss Hale. Perhaps Miss Hale first, then you, sir; he's in a strange mood."
"He had better come up," Mr. Hale directed. "Then he can choose his audience."
"Oh, very well," Dixon murmured, reluctant to approach the man. "I'd rather not hear what he has to say, sir. If you saw his shoes, you'd think the kitchen floor was cleaner."
"He can wipe them," Mr. Hale said. Dixon hurried away to summon Higgins. When the man arrived, looking at his feet with hesitation, Dixon's irritation softened. Sitting on the bottom stair, Higgins removed his muddy boots and climbed the steps silently.
"Servant, sir!" he muttered, smoothing his hair. "Please forgive me for being in stockings. I've been tramping all day, and the streets aren't exactly clean."
Margaret noted his unusual quietude, the subdued air suggesting great difficulty in speaking. Mr. Hale, ever sympathetic to shyness, stepped in.
"We shall have tea directly, Mr. Higgins. You're tired, surely, after such a wet, relaxing day?"
Margaret hastened the tea, taking the preparation into her own hands despite Dixon's protests. Yet, Martha, like those who eventually touched Margaret's spirit, found it an honor to serve her wishes. Dixon's irritability melted away under Margaret's gentle forbearance.
"Why, Master, and you must always bring the lower classes upstairs since we moved to Milton?" Dixon grumbled. "At Helstone, they never rose above the kitchen. I've told a few they might consider it an honor just to be there."
Finding it easier to speak to one than two, Higgins slipped out after Margaret left to assure himself the door was closed. Standing near Mr. Hale, he lowered his voice.
"Master, you wouldn't guess easily what I've been tramping for today. Remember how I spoke yesterday? I've been seeking work. I told myself I'd keep a civil tongue, let them say what they would. I'd bite my tongue sooner than speak in haste. For *that man's* sake—you understand." He jerked his thumb backward.
"No, I don't," Mr. Hale said, bewildered. "Who?"
"That chap lying there," Higgins whispered. "The one who drowned himself, poor chap! I didn't think he had it in him to lie still and let the water creep over him. Boucher, you know."
"Yes," Mr. Hale said, regaining composure. "Go on. You said you wouldn't speak in haste..."
"For his sake. Yet not for his sake; wherever he is, he'll know no more cold or clammy weather. But for the wife's sake, and the little children."
"God bless you!" Mr. Hale started up, then calmed himself. "What do you mean? Tell me out."
