The morning sun streamed through the open door as I stepped into the hall of Mr. Candy’s house, his pretty housemaid holding it ajar for me. The light fell sharply upon the face of Ezra Jennings as I turned to look at him, and I understood at once why Betteredge had spoken of his appearance with such misgiving.

There was something unsettling about the man—his sun-darkened skin, the hollows beneath his cheekbones, the strange duality of his features that made him seem both youthful and prematurely aged. His eyes, deep-set and contemplative, held an unsettling gleam, and his hair, streaked with incongruous shades, only added to his unsettling presence. And yet, despite it all, I felt an inexplicable pull toward him, a sympathy I could not rationalize. Common sense urged me to answer his question about Mr. Candy’s decline and be on my way, but curiosity rooted me to the spot.

“Are you walking my way, Mr. Jennings?” I asked, noting the hat in his hand. “I’m bound for my aunt’s—Mrs. Ablewhite.”

He replied that he had a patient to attend to but would accompany me part of the distance.

As we left, I observed the housemaid’s reaction to his murmured instructions about his return—her lips tightened, her gaze darting anywhere but his face. The dismissal in her manner was unmistakable. Betteredge had claimed Jennings was disliked everywhere, and here was proof of it. *What a life he must lead,* I thought as we stepped into the street.

Having broached the subject of Mr. Candy’s illness, Jennings now fell silent, leaving it to me to press further. I obliged.

“The change in him is severe,” I remarked. “Was his condition graver than I realized?”

“It’s a wonder he survived at all,” Jennings replied.

“And his memory—is it always so fragmented? He seemed to be struggling to tell me something—”

“—About events before his fever?” Jennings finished for me.

“Yes.”

“His recollection of that time is shattered,” he said. “It would almost be a mercy if he forgot entirely. As it is, he grasps at fragments—plans half-formed, words unspoken—but cannot piece them together. He knows his own deficiency, and it torments him.” His voice softened. “Perhaps we would all be happier if we could forget entirely.”

“Some memories,” I countered, “are worth preserving.”

“For most men, I hope so. But not all.” He studied me. “Do you believe the memory he sought had significance for *you*?”

The question struck at the heart of my own thoughts. My interest in Jennings had been cautious, but his manner—his quiet, unforced dignity—persuaded me he was a man to be trusted.

“I believe it may,” I admitted. “Is there any way to help him recover it?”

Jennings’s dreamy eyes sharpened with sudden focus. “His memory is beyond repair. I’ve tried.”

My disappointment must have shown.

“That isn’t to say the recollection itself is lost,” he added. “It may be possible to trace it without relying on Mr. Candy.”

“How?”

“By reconstructing his delirium.” He paused. “May I explain in detail?”

At my eager assent, he continued. During Candy’s fever, Jennings had recorded his ravings in shorthand, later transcribing them with gaps where the words broke apart. He had then pieced them together like a puzzle, inferring the missing links until the fragments formed coherent thought.

“You see,” he said, “the mind may still reason even when the tongue falters.”

“Did he speak of *me*?” I cut in.

Jennings met my gaze. “He did. Over one long night, his thoughts circled some matter between you and him. I have the fragments—and the connections I’ve drawn. The question is whether this aligns with the memory he sought today.”

“We must look at once!” I exclaimed.

“Impossible.” His tone was firm. “I will not share a man’s fevered words lightly. If you wish to know more, you must first tell me why it matters.”

I hesitated. To confess my suspicion—that I was accused of theft—was unbearable. I dodged with vague explanations, but Jennings listened without satisfaction.

“I’m sorry,” he said at last. “Nothing in his ravings concerned the Moonstone.”

We reached a fork in the road. Jennings took the path toward the village, bowing farewell. But as he walked away, conviction seized me—he held the key I needed.

I called him back.

When he turned, I forced the words out. “I haven’t been honest. My interest isn’t in the diamond. It’s personal—shamefully so.”

A shadow crossed his face. “Then I must be honest in turn,” he said, his voice low and taut. “Before you confide in me, you should know how I came to be in Mr. Candy’s employ. It’s not a long tale, but it may change your mind about trusting me.”

The intensity in his eyes silenced me. I nodded, and we walked on.

After a distance, he paused by a break in the stone wall bordering the moor.

“May we rest?” he asked. “I am not as strong as I appear, and some truths weigh heavily.”
