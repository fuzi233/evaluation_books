August 25th, 1756. The morning light filtered through the windows of Tunbridge Wells, illuminating a letter penned by Mr. Henry Esmond Warrington. Addressed to "My dear Lord March," it bore the satisfaction of a man who had finally secured his wager. "I have won all our three bets," he wrote, detailing his swift journey from Bromley to London in record time. He recounted driving his own horses, guided by a postilion, with Mrs. Betty safely tucked inside. Though two horsemen had shadowed them near Blackheath, their suspicious glances had driven them away, allowing Harry and his charges to arrive at the spa town just after eleven o'clock. With business concluded, Harry resolved to settle his gambling debts to Lord March, eager to offer his opponent a fresh challenge—and perhaps, a measure of revenge.

Back at the Bailiff's residence, the atmosphere had been heavy with dread until the witching hour. Lady Maria Esmond and the Reverend Sampson had played cards in the dim candlelight following a meager meal provided by the bailiff's wife. Then, the sharp *whirr* of approaching wheels shattered the silence. Before the carriage could even halt, the gates swung open. First emerged Mrs. Betty, her face aglow with a joy that bordered on hysteria, followed closely by a tall, dashing young gentleman. As the stranger stepped forward, the Reverend dropped his cards with a triumphant shout, while Lady Maria, drained of color, collapsed into his embrace. It was Harry Warrington, the rescuer. He had ridden through the night to liberate her from the clutches of tyranny and shame. Tears streamed down her cheeks as she buried her face in his shoulder, overwhelmed by the relief of being free. Leaning heavily on his arm, she stumbled home, safe at last, while Madame Bernstein, unaware of the drama unfolding in her house, slept undisturbed.

Inside the safety of the lodgings, Harry immediately issued orders. "You are both free to go home," he declared, his voice ringing with authority. To Betty, he bade her fetch his lady's hat and cloak; to Sampson, he suggested they share a pipe to soothe the jarring effects of the escape. The young clergyman, a man of profound and volatile emotions, was instantly undone. Seizing Harry's hand, he sobbed openly, praying fervently for divine blessing upon his generous patron. Harry felt a warm, swelling pride pulse through his veins. To aid the suffering, to transform sorrow into joy—it was a noble trade, and he wore his success like armor. As he stood beside his rescued princess, the world seemed to spin around him in a kaleidoscope of honor. Beautiful illusions danced before his eyes, crowning him as a conquering hero, a champion hailed by the streets. Yet, deep down, Harry knew the truth: his feat required little more than a kind hand and a silver tongue. He had merely disarmed the dragon guarding the gate and persuaded the tyrant to lay down his axe. Though his vanity swelled with the praise, his nature was gentle enough to accept it.

That evening, the scene shifted to the warmth of a tavern, where the atmosphere was thick with Burgundy and the intoxicating scent of camaraderie. Sampson remained under the spell of his liberation, his spirit soaring with gratitude. With every glass raised, his admiration for Harry climbed higher, painting the young man as the epitome of nobility. Harry, buoyed by his recent triumphs, embraced the flattery with a regal air. "The younger branch of our family," he proclaimed, pouring himself another glass, "has treated you poorly, Sampson—but rest assured, I shall stand by you!" He spoke with the grandeur of a man recounting the glories of his lineage, the House of Esmond. Sensing the chaplain's financial troubles regarding a sister at school, Harry produced a banknote, offering it freely. "Count on me whenever you need me," he urged, passing the money across the table.

Sampson, moved to tears by such unmerited kindness, protested his unworthiness, swearing oaths of devotion. "I do not deserve this!" he cried, his voice trembling. Harry waved off the sentiment with a dismissive laugh, noting the irony of the situation. "There was no money in that pocket-book I lost last week," he reminded him gently. "How do you know?" Sampson asked, his head bowing in shame. "Because I am a villain, sir," the chaplain confessed, revealing that he had discovered Harry's stolen letters and pocket-book the night of the incident at Barbeau's.

Harry's complexion flushed crimson as the truth laid bare. "Did you read the letters?" he demanded, rising abruptly. "They told me nothing I did not already know," Sampson admitted, his gaze fixed on the floor. "You have spies, sir—spies whom you are far too young and naive to detect." Harry's mind raced, connecting the dots about Lady Fanny, his cousin Will, and the crumbling fortunes of the older Esmond branch. "Do these rumors about Lady Maria hold truth?" he whispered, his voice tight with fear. "Oh, not for worlds!" Sampson hastened to assure him, though his tone betrayed a strange hesitation. "Though I admit, she seems older than you... and perhaps ill-suited, though she possesses every virtue."

"She is too old, I know she is," Harry insisted with feigned dignity, masking his turmoil. "But she has my word, and you saw how devoted she is." He pressed Sampson to retrieve the stolen papers, promising forgiveness. "Let me try to forgive myself!" Sampson wept, retreating to his chamber. Moments later, he returned, pale and shaken, holding out the empty pocket-book. "The letters are gone, sir," he confessed, describing how his trunk and belongings had been rifled while he was detained. "Someone else has taken them."

The room fell silent. Who could have entered? The landlady swore no one had approached, yet something vital had vanished. Was it Lady Maria, seeking evidence against the Baroness? Or was it the Baroness herself, the mastermind behind the chaos? The pieces fit together: the Baroness's secret journey to London, her knowledge of the local shoemaker where Sampson lodged, and the mysterious disappearance of the documents. Yet, the motive remained murky. Had the Baroness arrested Lady Maria to hide the theft? Or was this a trap laid by someone else entirely?

For the moment, however, Harry was too intoxicated by his victory to dwell on the shadows. Swollen with self-satisfaction over his winnings, his rescue mission, and the lavish supper sent by Monsieur Barbeau, he accepted Sampson's vows of loyalty with open arms. "I think, Sampson, you would," Harry mused, raising his glass. "My family—the House of Esmond—is accustomed to faithful friends, and we reward them well." They toasted the glory of the Esmond-Warrington line, and as the sun began to rise outside the shuttered window, casting a golden glow into the room, Harry drifted off to sleep, secure in his benevolence.

By noon, a servant arrived bearing a message from Madame de Bernstein. She wished to see her nephew, inviting him for a cup of chocolate. Harry complied, walking to the lodgings where he found the elderly Baroness waiting. Her demeanor had softened, replaced by a mixture of affection and maternal concern. She thanked him profusely for his generosity toward Lady Maria, recounting the terror of the seizure in church and the humiliation of losing three hundred guineas to Lady Yarmouth the previous night. "I could not stay in debt," she explained, explaining how she had sent her steward, Case, to London to raise funds. "Can you lend me some, child?" she pleaded.

Harry, blushing deeply, offered twenty-two pounds, lamenting his own extravagance in buying horses and clothes. But the Baroness refused, producing a note for fifty pounds instead. "Go and spend it, be merry!" she commanded, urging him to enjoy life. Her thoughts then turned sharply to his engagement. "You have engaged yourself to poor Maria, who is nearly twice your age?" she exclaimed, her eyes widening. "What will Mother Esmond say? She may live three centuries, and you will not have wherewithal to support yourselves!" She scoffed at the madness of their gaming habits, warning that Maria would eventually pawn Harry's coat just to keep playing. "It runs in our blood," she admitted, shaking her head. "I warned you! A lad of twenty promising eternal love on his knees, signing his heart's blood..."

"And who showed you my letter?" Harry interrupted, burning with shame. "An accident," the Baroness replied, explaining how Lady Maria, fainting from the stress of arrest, had dropped a sachet containing Harry's letter. "I opened it out of curiosity," she confessed, handing over the case. "And I burned it."

A sharp pang tore through Harry's heart. "Why didn't she destroy it?" he wondered silently. "I will give it back to Maria," he stammered, reaching for the locket. "No, my dear," the Baroness interjected, her voice firm. "I have burned the foolish letter. If you keep that promise, misery awaits you. She has entrapped you, Harry, and I have cut the strings. Go back! Betray me if you must, but do not follow her."
