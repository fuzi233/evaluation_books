"Old and young, boy, let 'em all eat, I have it; Let 'em have ten tire of teeth a-piece, I care not." — *Rollo, Duke of Normandy.*

Margaret returned home so weighed down by the sights and sounds of the day that she struggled vainly to summon the energy required for the duties awaiting her. Her mother, confined to the house, relied upon Margaret's brief excursions as the sole source of news, expecting a constant stream of cheerful chatter.

"And can your factory friend come on Thursday to see you dressed?"

"She was so ill I never thought of asking her," Margaret replied, her voice dripping with sorrow.

"Dear! Everyone seems ill nowadays, I think," remarked Mrs. Hale, a flicker of envy crossing her features, common among those bedridden and dependent on others' health. "But it must be terribly sad to be sick in one of those narrow back alleys."

(Kindness overriding her own discomfort, and the old customs of Helstone resurfacing in her mind.)

"It is hard enough here. What could you possibly send her, Margaret? Mr. Thornton has sent me some of his vintage port since you left. Do you think a bottle would do her any good?"

"No, Mother! I doubt they are truly destitute—they certainly didn't sound like it. Besides, Bessy's ailment is consumption; she wouldn't benefit from wine. Perhaps I might bring her a jar of preserves made from our beloved Helstone fruits. No! There is another family I wish to assist—"

"Oh, Mother, Mother!" Margaret suddenly broke through the self-imposed restraint she had maintained upon entering, spilling the raw details of her encounter at Higgins's cottage. "How am I to appear in my finest attire and attend these fashionable gatherings after witnessing such misery today?"

The revelation distressed Mrs. Hale profoundly. Irritated and restless until compelled to act, she ordered Margaret immediately to pack a basket in the drawing-room to be dispatched to the family at once. She was nearly incensed when Margaret suggested it mattered little if the gift arrived until morning, noting that Higgins had already secured their immediate needs and that she had left funds with Bessy. Accusing her daughter of callousness, Mrs. Hale refused to pause until the basket was delivered.

Only then did she sigh, reflecting, "After all, we may have erred. Last time Mr. Thornton visited, he remarked that true friends do not aid in prolonging struggles by supporting the riots. Wasn't that Boucher involved in a riot?"

When Mr. Hale ascended the stairs later, fresh from a lesson with Mr. Thornton that had devolved into their usual discourse, his wife posed the question. In her current agitation, Margaret cared little whether their charity inadvertently extended the conflict; her thoughts were too clouded. Mr. Hale listened, striving for judicial calm, recalling arguments that had seemed undeniable moments prior. He reached an uneasy compromise. While acknowledging his wife and daughter's correctness in this specific instance, he conceded Mr. Thornton's broader point: if the strike dragged on, masters would eventually recruit labor from afar, or machines would render human hands obsolete. Thus, generally speaking, refusing assistance that propped up foolish stubbornness was the kindest course. Yet, regarding Boucher specifically, he resolved to visit him first thing the following morning to ascertain what aid was feasible.

True to his word, Mr. Hale departed the next day. Finding Boucher absent, he held a lengthy conversation with his wife, promising to secure an infirmary order for her. Observing the abundance Mrs. Hale had provided—which the children, acting as masters in their father's absence, had spent lavishly—he returned with a report far more reassuring than Margaret had dared hope. Indeed, her previous night's grim description had primed her father's imagination to exaggerate the severity of conditions; consequently, he painted a picture of recovery that was rosier than reality warranted.

"I shall return tomorrow to see the man himself," Mr. Hale declared. "I find it difficult to reconcile these dwellings with the cottages of Helstone. Here, furniture exists that our laborers could scarcely dream of purchasing, and foods considered luxuries elsewhere are staples. Yet, with wages halted, these families seem to have no recourse but the pawnbroker. One must adopt a new standard of living here in Milton."

Bessy showed slight improvement that day, though her weakness was such that she appeared to have forgotten her earlier fervent desire to witness Margaret's transformation—unless that impulse stemmed from a feverish delirium. Margaret found herself contrasting this peculiar preparation for a social event she dreaded against the joyful, girlish trousseaus she and Edith had orchestrated barely a year prior. Her only solace in adorning herself now was the knowledge that her mother would delight in viewing her. She flushed when Dixon opened the drawing-room door to solicit approval.

"Miss Hale looks radiant, ma'am," Dixon observed. "Mrs. Shaw's coral couldn't have chosen a finer spot. It provides the perfect splash of color, ma'am. Without it, Miss Margaret would have been far too pale."

Margaret's thick black hair, unsuitable for braiding, was instead coiled tightly around her head in massive silken rings resembling a crown, culminating in a large spiral knot at the nape. Two substantial coral pins, shaped like slender arrows, anchored the weight. Her white silk sleeves were bound with cords of matching material, and heavy coral beads rested beneath the curve of her milky throat.

"Oh, Margaret! How I long to accompany you to one of the old Barrington assemblies," her mother gushed, envisioning herself as Lady Beresford escorting her daughter. "Taking you as she used to take me."

Margaret kissed her mother, suppressing a smile despite her low spirits. "I would prefer staying at home with you, Mother. Much preferred."

"Nonsense, darling! Ensure you observe the dinner closely. I shall love hearing how they manage affairs in Milton. Especially the second course, dear. Notice what they serve instead of game."

Had Mrs. Hale witnessed the opulence of the table and its settings, she would have been less interested and more astounded. For Margaret, possessing a London-refined palate, the array of delicacies felt oppressive; half the provision would have sufficed for a tighter, more elegant presentation. Yet, adhering to Mrs. Thornton's rigid code of hospitality, every guest was entitled to partake of each distinct dish if inclined. Though careless in her own daily habits, setting a feast before her guests was a point of immense pride for Mrs. Thornton. Her son shared this sentiment. Having known nothing but societies revolving around magnificent repasts, he denied himself the trivial expense of a sixpence and regretted sending the invitations, yet he welcomed the chance to witness the old grandeur of preparation.

Margaret and her father arrived first. Mr. Hale was punctually anxious. The drawing-room was empty save for Mrs. Thornton and Fanny. Covers were removed, revealing walls draped in yellow silk damask and carpets blooming with brilliant flowers. Ornamentation filled every corner, creating a visual fatigue that stood in stark contrast to the bare, ugly view of the mill yard outside, where wide gates stood open for carriages. The mill towered to the left, casting a premature shadow over the summer evening.

"My son was detained by business until the last moment. He arrives shortly, Mr. Hale. Please, take a seat."

Mr. Hale stood by a window. Turning away, he queried, "Do you not find such proximity to the mill somewhat disagreeable at times?"

Mrs. Thornton straightened her posture. "Never. I have not grown so refined as to wish to forget the source of my son's wealth and power. Moreover, there is no other factory in Milton. A single room spans two hundred and twenty square yards."

"I meant the smoke and noise—the constant comings and goings of workers might prove irritating!"

"I concur with you, Mr. Hale!" interjected Fanny. "There is a perpetual scent of steam and oil, and the noise is utterly deafening."

"I have heard noises termed 'music' far more deafening," Mrs. Thornton retorted. "The engine-room sits at the street end; we hear it only in summer when windows are open. As for the constant hum of the workforce, it disturbs me no more than the buzzing of a bee-hive. If I ponder it, I associate it with my son, feeling how everything belongs to him, guided by his head. Currently, silence reigns; the hands have proven ungrateful enough to strike, as you likely heard. However, the very business I mentioned concerns the steps he intends to take to teach them their place."

Dark anger settled upon her stern face as she spoke, failing to lift even when Mr. Thornton entered. She instantly perceived the burden of care weighing upon him, though his greeting to the guests remained cheerfully cordial. He shook Margaret's hand—a first meeting between them, though she remained unaware. He inquired after Mrs. Hale, receiving Mr. Hale's optimistic report, and glanced at Margaret to gauge her agreement. Seeing no dissent, he was struck anew by her beauty. Never had he seen her so attired, yet this elegance suited her noble figure and serene countenance perfectly; she seemed destined to wear such splendor always.

He watched his sister, Fanny, fidgeting restlessly with her gown, her eyes wandering aimlessly, contrasting sharply with Margaret's steady gaze fixed on a single point, radiating a gentle repose.
