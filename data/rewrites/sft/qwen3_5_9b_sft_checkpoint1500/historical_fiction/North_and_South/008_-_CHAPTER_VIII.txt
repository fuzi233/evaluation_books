It required the delicate, floral wallpaper to soften the blow of Milton upon Mrs. Hale, yet even that was insufficient. What was truly lacking could not be supplied. By the time Mrs. Hale arrived, the thick, yellow fogs of November had rolled in, swallowing the view of the valley plain cut off by the river's sweeping bend. To the newcomers, the world had vanished behind a curtain of gray.

Margaret and Dixon had labored for two days, unpacking boxes and arranging furniture, yet the interior remained chaotic. Outside, a dense, choking mist pressed against the glass, invading every open door in swirling, unhealthy wreaths.

"Oh, Margaret!" cried Mrs. Hale, her voice hollow with dread. "Are we to live here?"

Margaret's heart mirrored the desolation in her mother's tone. With effort, she managed a calm reply. "Oh, the fogs in London are sometimes far worse!"

"But then you knew London, and friends waited behind it," Mrs. Hale insisted, gesturing wildly at the gloom. "Here... well! We are utterly desolate. Oh, Dixon, what a place!"

"Indeed, ma'am, I'm sure it won't take long to finish you off, and then I'll know who stays! Miss Hale, that is far too heavy a burden for you to carry alone."

"Not at all, thank you, Dixon," Margaret answered coolly, masking her weariness. "The kindest thing we can do for Mamma is to prepare her room for rest. I shall return shortly with coffee."

Her father shared his wife's gloom, seeking sympathy from Margaret. "Margaret, I do believe this is an unhealthy spot. Imagine if Mother's health—or yours—should fail. I wished I'd chosen a cottage in Wales; this is simply terrible." He moved to the window, staring out at the gray void.

"There was no comfort to offer. They were settled in Milton and must endure the smoke and fog for the foreseeable future; indeed, life itself seemed obscured by a fog of circumstances. Only the day before, Mr. Hale had calculated their costs with alarm. The removal, plus their fortnight at Heston, had drained nearly all his modest savings. Here they were, trapped, and forced to remain.

That night, when Margaret realized the permanence of their situation, she felt a stupor of despair settle over her. The heavy, smoky air clung to her bedroom, a long, narrow projection at the rear of the house. Its window faced a blank wall of another similar projection, barely ten feet away, looming through the fog like an impenetrable barrier to hope. Within the room, chaos reigned. All their energy had been devoted to making Mamma's quarters comfortable. Margaret sat on a packing box, her gaze fixed on a direction card that struck her as unmistakably from Helstone—beautiful, beloved Helstone! She sank into dismal thoughts until she resolved to divert her mind. Suddenly, she recalled a letter from Edith, only half-read amidst the morning's rush.

Edith's letter painted a vibrant picture of their arrival at Corfu. It described the voyage across the Mediterranean, filled with music and dance aboard ship. A new, gaudy life awaited them. Edith detailed her villa perched high on precipitous rocks overlooking the sea, sharing it with Captain Lennox and another newlywed officer. Even in late autumn, their days were spent boating or picnicking outdoors. Edith's existence seemed like a vast vault of blue sky, utterly free from flecks or clouds. Her husband's drills and her duty copying popular tunes for the bandmaster were merely trifling, arduous chores compared to the joy surrounding them. She expressed a warm hope that if the regiment stayed another year, Margaret might join them. Edith also referenced the rainy afternoon a year prior in Harley Street, when Edith refused to wear her new gown to a dull dinner, fearing it would get wet in the carriage—a mishap that led to their first meeting with Captain Lennox.

"Yes," Margaret remembered clearly. Edith and Aunt Shaw had dined there; Margaret had joined later. Vivid memories flooded back: the opulent arrangements, the stately beauty of the furniture, the spacious house, and the tranquil ease of those guests. Everything stood in stark, jarring contrast to the present misery. That smooth sea of former life closed over without a trace, leaving no marker of where they had all been. The habitual dinners, the social calls, the dancing evenings—they all continued endlessly, though Aunt Shaw and Edith were no longer there, and Margaret, of course, was missed even less. She suspected none of that old circle thought of her, except perhaps Henry Lennox. Knowing his capacity to banish unpleasant thoughts, she believed he too would strive to forget her, as he often boasted of doing.

Yet, as she dwelt on what might have been, a bitter gratitude arose. Had she loved Henry as a lover and accepted him, only for her father's fortunes to plummet, Mr. Lennox would have received the news with impatient fury. While it was a humiliation to feel the world degrading her father through its coarse judgments, she could bear it patiently, fortified by knowledge of her father's pure intentions. However, the fact that society viewed her father as degraded would have irked Mr. Lennox deeply. Realizing these possibilities, she grew thankful for what *was*. They were at their lowest point now; they could not descend further. Edith's and Aunt Shaw's shock would have to be met with bravery when their next letters arrived. Rising slowly, Margaret began to undress, savoring the rare luxury of leisure after a frantic day. She fell asleep hoping for some inner or outer brightness, unaware of how long the darkness would persist. The season was ill-suited for health or spirits. Soon, Mamma contracted a severe cold, and Dixon, already unwell, struggled to cope. There was no girl available to assist; all were employed in the factories. Those who applied were harshly rebuked by Dixon for daring to trust themselves to work in a gentleman's household. Thus, they relied on a charwoman in near-constant employment. Margaret longed to summon Charlotte, but Charlotte was superior in skill to what they could afford, and the distance was prohibitive.

Meanwhile, Mr. Hale encountered several pupils recommended by Mr. Bell or influenced directly by Mr. Thornton. These students were largely of an age where many peers remained in school, yet prevailing local wisdom dictated that to forge a capable tradesman, a boy must be seized young and acclimated to the rhythm of the mill, office, or warehouse. Sending them to Scotch universities risked unsettling them for commercial pursuits; Oxford or Cambridge were inaccessible until eighteen. Consequently, most manufacturers placed their sons in positions at fourteen or fifteen, ruthlessly pruning any branches leading toward literature or higher mental cultivation, aiming to channel all vigor into commerce. Yet, there were wiser parents, some with enough self-awareness to recognize their own deficiencies and seek remedies. Indeed, a few, no longer youths but men in the prime of life, possessed the stern wisdom to admit their ignorance and learn belatedly what they should have known early. Mr. Thornton was perhaps the oldest of Mr. Hale's pupils and undoubtedly his favorite. Mr. Hale fell into the habit of quoting Thornton's opinions so frequently and reverently that it became a domestic jest to wonder how much actual teaching occurred within the allotted hour, given the sheer volume of conversation consumed.

Margaret subtly encouraged this lighthearted approach to her father's acquaintance with Mr. Thornton, sensing her mother viewed the new friendship with jealous scrutiny. Previously, when Mr. Hale's time was wholly devoted to his books and parishioners at Helstone, she cared little whether she saw much of him. Now, however, his eager anticipation of renewing contact with Mr. Thornton seemed to wound her, as if he were slighting her company. Mr. Hale's excessive praise carried the usual effect of over-praise on his listeners; they were somewhat rebellious against Aristides being constantly hailed as "Always Just."

After a quiet, pastoral existence in a country parsonage for over twenty years, something dazzling about Mr. Hale's new reality was the energy that effortlessly conquered immense difficulties. The machinery of Milton, the power of its men, impressed him with a sense of grandeur that he yielded to without probing the details of its operation. Margaret, conversely, ventured less among machinery and crowds. She witnessed less of power in its public effects. By chance, she found herself drawn close to a few individuals who, in matters affecting the masses, suffered acutely for the sake of the many. The eternal question haunted her: *Has everything been done to minimize the suffering of these exceptions?* Or, in the triumph of the thronging procession, had the helpless been trampled underfoot, rather than gently lifted aside from the conqueror's path, whom they lacked the power to follow?

It fell to Margaret to search for a servant to aid Dixon, who had initially promised to find exactly the right person for the rough work. Yet Dixon's concept of a "helpful girl" stemmed from her recollections of tidy, older scholars at Helstone School, who were so proud they rarely visited the parsonage on busy days, treating Mrs. Dixon with a mixture of profound respect and genuine terror.
