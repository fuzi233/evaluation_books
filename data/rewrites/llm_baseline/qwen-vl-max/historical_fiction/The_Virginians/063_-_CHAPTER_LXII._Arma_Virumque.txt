If Harry Warrington had any passion for war and military life, he was fortunate to live in a time when Europe trembled with conflict and every London drawing-room buzzed with talk of battlefields and kings. Though our own beloved Prince of the House of Hanover had suffered defeat, the Protestant Hero—the King of Prussia—was spreading his name across the continent with dazzling victories. I am grateful, on my own account, that Harry did not take part in those great campaigns; for then I would have been compelled to describe battles already immortalized by another pen. I am truly glad that Harry Warrington was not at Rossbach on that famous Gunpowder Day, November 5th, 1757, nor at Leuthen, where the Prussian king delivered such a crushing blow just a month later. These mighty events shall be told in other volumes—volumes that all the world eagerly awaits. Would this history dare to rival such grand narratives? Could my light-hearted yellow park phaeton challenge the thundering chariot of war? Could my gentle Pegasus meet the charge of a steed with flaming nostrils and bit of fire? Dear reader—kind, patient, dear reader—let us both be thankful that Harry Warrington did not follow the King of Prussia, as he once intended. For then I would have had to recount wars that Thomas Carlyle himself will paint with greater power. And I do not wish you to compare me with such a master.

Yet Harry did not join the King of Prussia—not because he lacked desire, but because fate held him back. Instead, he lingered in idle sorrow, a man without purpose or direction. He wandered through military coffee-houses, reading nothing but newspapers. Books were not his calling. He found novels absurd, and could not understand how ladies wept over Richardson’s tales. He laughed heartily—though often too late, after the joke had passed. Why must every gentleman be a scholar? And do we despise friends simply because they never composed a single verse? Ruined, idle, dependent upon his brother for support, Harry read only to fall asleep. His strong hands had no work to do. How lucky it is that he avoided deeper ruin! Even Achilles, sent by his mother to the court of King Lycomedes to keep him safe from war, found himself stirred by longing and pride among women. And what became of Pyrrhus? A bold young hero should not be bound by women's care, nor kept idle with distaffs and tea-cups. When he is not fighting, such a man is likely to fall into worse mischief still.

The two elder Lambert ladies—gentle, compassionate souls—had deep pity for Harry, a kindness only certain women can give. Who comforts a man in grief? Who soothes him in anger? Who doubles his joy, shares his triumph, and stands beside him when disgrace strikes? Who binds the wounds made by Fortune’s cruel arrows? Only woman, if you please. You who have known hardship—have you not found such tender healers beside you? Thank the gods for their presence. What gentleman has not his rock, his chain, his liver torn by daily pain? But the sea-nymphs come—soft, kind, understanding—they kiss our wounded feet, wet our dry lips with their tears, and comfort us Titans in our downfall. They do not abandon us when we fall.

Theo and her mother felt profound sympathy for Harry. But Hetty’s heart was harder, sharper. She resented his idleness, his lack of glory. The world was at war—why could he not carry a musket? It was harvest season, and thousands reaped with flashing sickles—could he not cut down his own sheaf of honor?

“Why, how fierce she is with him!” said Papa, after one of Hetty’s usual sharp exchanges with Harry, who came daily like a target set up in Mrs. Lambert’s drawing-room.

“Her behavior is abominable!” cried Mamma. “She deserves to be scolded and sent to bed.”

“Perhaps,” Theo ventured gently, “it’s because she likes him more than any of us do. Perhaps she is angry for his sake. If I loved someone deeply, I would want to admire him always—to believe everything he did was right—and to think my gentleman better than all others.”

“That’s true, my dear,” replied Mrs. Lambert. “Your father is so much better than anyone else—he has spoiled us all. Have you ever seen anyone like him?”

“Very few,” Theo admitted, blushing.

“Few indeed. Who is so good-tempered?”

“I think no one, mamma,” Theo said softly.

“Or so brave?”

“Well, Mr. Wolfe, or Harry, or Mr. George, are certainly brave.”

“Or so learned and witty?”

“Oh, Mr. George seems very learned, and witty in his way,” Theo said. “His manners are exquisite—Madame de Bernstein says so, and she has seen the world. He has a noble bearing, unlike others. In books, he chooses the finest, most noble things, even though he mocks them. He is satirical, yes—but only toward the petty and weak. No gentleman has a softer heart. Just yesterday, after he spoke so sharply, I looked out the window and saw him stop to buy apples for a whole crowd of children. And the day before, when he brought me Molière, he gave money to a beggar—so gracefully! And he reads French beautifully. I agree with him about *Tartuffe*, though it is clever and lively—it’s too mean a villain to be the center of a great play. Iago, Mr. George said, is nearly as wicked—but he isn’t the main character; Othello is, with his noble weakness. But Molière’s portraits of gentlemen and ladies—Mr. George thinks them splendid—but oh, I can’t repeat the verses after him.”

“But you know them by heart?” asked Mrs. Lambert.

“Oh yes, mamma—I know them by... Nonsense!”

I imagine here a blush, a flutter, and Miss Theo hurrying away, cheeks glowing like roses. Why had she stopped? Because her mother was watching her oddly. And why did Mamma watch her so? And why had she followed Mr. George with her eyes when he left, and waited for him when he returned? Ah, and why do cheeks flush, and why do flowers bloom? Time still flies. Old spring, old summer, old autumn, old winter—when trees stand bare or white with snow.

A few minutes after George arrived, Theo would descend with a fluttering heart, perhaps, holding a fresh nosegay—just gathered, as it were, in his honor. I suppose she watched the street constantly, eager to see his generosity to the sweep or his purchase from the apple-woman. But if it was Harry who knocked, she stayed in her room, working or reading, sending her sister to greet him—or her brothers, if the elder was home from college, or Doctor Crusius had given the younger leave.

Our friends were welcome at Mr. Lambert’s house, free to come and stay as they pleased. One day, Harry sat on a couch, sunk in a mood of unusual weariness and despair, when who should appear but Miss Hetty? It was curious—though the girls would never admit it—that when Harry came, Hetty appeared; when George arrived, Theo somehow materialized. Thus, according to custom, it was Miss Lambert, junior, who now entered to entertain the younger Virginian.

After polite greetings, she said:

“And pray, sir, what makes your honor look so glum this morning?”

“Ah, Hetty,” he sighed, “I have nothing else to do but look glum. I remember when we were boys—I was a lazy one, you may be sure—I’d always ask my tutor for a holiday, spend it swinging on a gate or making ducks and drakes on the pond. Those idle days were always the most melancholy. What have I to do now, from morning till night?”

“Breakfast, walk—dinner, walk—tea, supper, I suppose,” said Hetty, tossing her head. “And a pipe of your Virginia.”

“I tell you,” Harry went on, “when I returned with Charley to the Chartreux the other night, I almost said to the master, ‘Teach me, sir. Here’s a boy who knows more Latin and Greek at thirteen than I do at twenty-three. I’ve nothing to do all day—I might as well return to my books and try to mend my youth.’ Why do you laugh, Hetty?”

“I laugh at the thought of you at the head of a class, called up by the master!” she exclaimed.

“I wouldn’t be at the head,” Harry said humbly. “George might lead any class. But I’m not a scholar, you see. As a boy, I neglected myself. We wouldn’t let our tutors cane us much at home—but if we had, it might have helped.”

Hetty tapped her foot, looking at the young man—strong, idle, sorrowful.

“Upon my word, it might help you now!” she thought. “What does Tom say about being caned at school? Does his story make you long for it?”

“My brother’s account,” Harry answered simply, “makes me realize I wasted my time when I should have worked. I have no gift for books. So what am I good for? Only to spend my patrimony when I come abroad, or loiter at coffeehouses or racecourses, or chase hounds when I’m home. I’m good for nothing. That’s what I am.”

“What, such a big, brave, strong fellow—good for nothing?” Hetty retorted. “I wouldn’t confess that to any woman, even if I were twice as useless!”

“What am I to do?” Harry asked. “I’ve begged to join the army, but Madam Esmond hasn’t answered. That’s the only thing I’m fit for. I have no money to buy a commission. I’ve spent all mine—and so much of my brother’s. I won’t ask for more. If my mother would send me, I’d go in a heartbeat.”

“Eh!” said Hetty. “A man of spirit doesn’t need a woman to buckle his sword or clean his musket! Remember what Papa told us yesterday about Sir John Armytage?”

“Sir John Armytage? I knew him at White’s and the clubs—a fine, noble young man, heir to a great estate in the North.”

“And engaged to a famous beauty—Miss Howe, Lord Howe’s sister—but surely that’s no obstacle?”

“To what?” asked Harry.

“To glory!” Hetty declared. “I don’t think any woman of spirit would say ‘Stay!’ even if she adored her lover. When her country calls, she lets him go. Sir John volunteered for the coming expedition. Yesterday at court, His Majesty asked when he’d be ready. ‘Tomorrow, please your Majesty,’ he replied. And the king said, ‘That’s a soldier’s answer.’ Papa himself longs to go, though he has us all at home. Oh dear, oh dear! Why wasn’t I born a man? Both my brothers are going into the Church—but I know I’d make a fine little soldier!” And as she spoke, she paced the room, radiating courage, bold as Joan of Arc.

Harry watched her with quiet admiration. “I think,” he said, “I wouldn’t like to see a musket on that small shoulder, or a wound on that pretty face, Hetty.”

“Wounds? Who fears wounds?” she snapped. “Muskets? If I could carry one, I’d use it. You men think we’re only good for puddings and samplers. Why wasn’t I a man? George read Tasso to us yesterday—look, here’s the book, with the mark where we left off.”

“With the mark?” Harry asked.

“Yes. It’s about a woman disappointed because her brother won’t go to war. She says—

‘Alas! why did not Heaven these members frail
With lively force and vigour strengthen, so
That I this silken gown...’”

“Silken gown?” Harry asked, puzzled.

“Well, sir, I know it’s only Calimanco—but so it is in the book—

‘... this silken gown and slender veil
Might for a breastplate and a helm forgo;
Then should not heat, nor cold, nor rain, nor hail,
Nor storms that fall, nor blust'ring winds that blow,
Withhold me; but I would, both day and night,
In pitched field or private combat, fight—’”

“Fight? Yes, that I would!” Hetty declared. “Why must both my brothers become parsons? One of my father’s children ought to be a soldier!”

Harry smiled—softly, kindly—as he looked at her. He couldn’t imagine hurting such a delicate warrior.

“Why,” he said, holding up a finger, “this finger is nearly as thick as your arm. How would you stand against a big, strong man? I’d like to see any rogue try to harm you. You little, tender creature! Do you think any villain would dare touch you?” Excited, he began pacing, imagining a Frenchman daring to offend Miss Hester Lambert.

It was this silent strength he seemed to possess that drew Hetty to him—the courage she believed he had—that made her admire him. Yet Hetty herself was no warrior. She was no more bold than Erminia, whose words she had just recited—whose name Harry had never heard. He may have been present when George read poetry to the ladies, but his thoughts were elsewhere, lost in Clotildas and giants and enchanted forests. No, Miss Hetty—no, I believe—had no trace of the Amazon in her nature. Otherwise, she would have liked a soft, literary man, or one who played the flute. And who has not noticed how great, strong men are drawn to fragile, gentle women? How tender women are drawn to powerful, honest men? How mighty warriors often end up henpecked? If Harry Warrington falls in love with a woman like Miss Lambert—and marries her—without magic, we can all guess the outcome.

So while Hetty fired her sharp remarks at Harry, he scarcely felt them, letting her shoot on without even flicking the tiny arrows from his skin. Did she mean to stir him into action? He was too generous to grasp such subtle hints. Did she mean to shame him by saying she, a weak woman, would wear armor and fight? He either melted at the thought of her in danger—or burst into laughter.

“What good is a strong hand if you only use it to hold silk for my mother?” Hetty demanded. “And what’s the point of being strong in a drawing-room? Nobody wants you to throw someone out the window, Harry! A strong man, indeed! There’s probably a stronger at Bartholomew Fair. James Wolfe isn’t strong. He looks weak and ill. Last time he was here, he coughed all day, pale as if he’d seen a ghost.”

“I never understood why a man should fear a ghost,” Harry said.

“Have you seen one, sir?” asked Hetty, sharp as ever.

“No,” he replied. “I thought I once—at home, when we were boys. But it was only Nathan in his nightshirt. I wasn’t frightened. I think ghosts don’t exist. Nurses lie about them.” He paused. “George was a little afraid—but then, he’s…”

“Then George is what?” Hetty pressed.

“George is different from me,” Harry said. “Our mother is as bold as any woman—but she screams at mice. Can’t help it. It’s her nature. So perhaps George can’t bear ghosts. I don’t mind them.”

“George always says you’d have made a better soldier than he.”

“So I think I would—if I’d been allowed to try. But he can do a thousand things better than anyone. Why didn’t he let me volunteer on Braddock’s expedition? I might have died—and then I’d be as useful as I am now. At least I wouldnーt have ruined myself, brought shame on the Warrington name. Why can’t I go on this new expedition, and volunteer like Sir John Armytage? Oh, Hetty! I’m a miserable fellow—that’s what I am,” he groaned, pacing quickly.

“What a compliment to us! Thank you, Harry!” she said, then softened at his look. “Are you—thinking of going home?”

“And have all Virginia laughing at me? Not a gentleman there would accept me—except one, and my mother doesn’t like him. I’d be ashamed to return. You don’t know my mother, Hetty. I’m not afraid of most things—but somehow, I am of her. What shall I say when she asks, ‘Harry, where’s your inheritance?’ ‘Spent, mother,’ I’ll have to say. ‘What have you done with it?’ ‘Wasted it, mother—and ended up in prison.’ ‘Who got you out?’ ‘Brother George, ma'am. He took me out. Now I’m back—useless, with no profession, no future—only to tend negroes, be scolded at home, or sleep through sermons, or gamble, drink, and cockfight at taverns.’ How can I face the gentlemen of Virginia? I’m ashamed to go home like this. I must do something! What shall I do, Hetty? What shall I do?”

“What did Mr. Wolfe do at Louisbourg?” Hetty asked. “Ill as he was, in love as we knew, he didn’t wait to be nursed by his mother or dally with his sweetheart. He went into the King’s service and returned covered in glory. If there’s another campaign in America, Papa says he’s sure of a command.”

“I wish he’d take me,” Harry moaned. “And that a bullet would finish me.”

“You speak as if it were my fault you’re not in the army,” Hetty said, shrugging. “When you know I’d give—give, what have I to give?—yes! My life—to go into service!”

“Life indeed!” Hetty said, dismissively.

“You don’t seem to value it much,” Harry remarked sadly. “No more it is—anyone’s life. I’m a poor, useless fellow. I’m not even free to throw it away, as I’d like—because I’m under orders here and at home.”

“Orders?” Hetty scoffed. “Why under orders? Are you not tall enough, old enough to act for yourself? Must you have George as your master here, and your mother as your schoolmistress at home? If I were a man, I’d do something great before I was twenty-two. I’d make the world talk of me. I wouldn’t waste time at apron-strings. I wouldn’t curse my luck—I’d make it. I swear I would!”

For the first time, Harry flinched at her words.

“No slave on our plantation is more enslaved than I am,” he said, turning red. “But then, Miss Lambert—we don’t blame the poor man for not being free. That’s not generous. That’s not honor. Perhaps it’s different for women. Or maybe I’m wrong. Maybe I shouldn’t be hurt by a girl telling me my faults. Maybe my faults aren’t faults—only bad luck. You’ve talked so long about this man volunteering, that man winning glory, praising their courage as if I had none. I suppose I’m as well equipped as any gentleman. I don’t brag—but I’m not afraid of Mr. Wolfe, nor Sir John Armytage, nor anyone I’ve ever met. How can I buy a commission when I’ve spent my last shilling? Or ask my brother for more, when he’s already shared half with me? A gentleman of my rank can’t enlist as a common soldier—else, by Jupiter, I would! And if a bullet ends me, I suppose Miss Hetty Lambert wouldn’t mind. It’s unkind, Hetty—I didn’t think you’d say that.”

“What have I said?” she asked. “Only that Sir John Armytage volunteered, and Mr. Wolfe gained honor. And now you scold me! How can I help it if Mr. Wolfe is brave and famous? Is that reason to be angry?”

“I didn’t say angry,” Harry said gravely. “I said I was hurt.”

“Oh!” Hetty exclaimed. “I thought such a tiny creature as I couldn’t hurt anyone! It’s flattering to hear that a girl whose arm is no bigger than your little finger can wound a man as strong as you!”

“I never expected you’d try, Hetty,” he said. “You see, I’m not used to this kind of reception in this house.”

“What is it, my poor boy?” asked Mrs. Lambert, appearing at the door, seeing Harry’s distressed face.

“Oh, we’ve heard it all before, mamma!” Hetty said quickly. “Harry’s complaining again about having nothing to do. He’s unhappy—and keeps saying so over and over.”
