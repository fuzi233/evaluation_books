"To-morrow" is the name joy borrows when life is fairest; "To-morrow" fits sorrow best in young despair. Yet at the rectory, there existed a trouble far more enduring and heavy.

When Rex returned, he threw himself upon his bed, sinking into a state of apparent apathy that lasted unbroken until the following day. Only then did more positive signs of illness begin to interrupt his stillness. No mention was made of his journey to Southampton. Instead, the paramount thoughts of his mother and Anna became how to tend this patient—a boy who seemingly desired no recovery. Once the brightest, most grateful spirit in the household, he had been metamorphosed into an irresponsive, dull-eyed creature who met every affectionate attempt with a murmuring, "Let me alone."

His father looked beyond the immediate crisis, viewing it merely as the shortest exit from an unlucky affair. Still, he felt the weight of the inevitable suffering. Now and then, he would sit by Rex's side in silence for a few moments, parting with a gentle pressure of his hand upon the boy's blank brow and whispering, "God bless you, my boy."

Warham and the younger children used to peep around the door edges to witness this incredible spectacle of their lively brother laid low, but fingers were immediately raised to shoo them back. The constant guardian was Anna. Her little hand was permitted to rest within her brother's, though he never offered a welcoming squeeze. Her soul was torn between anguish for Rex and a sharp reproach directed at Gwendolen. "Perhaps it is wicked of me," she confessed to herself in her recurring inner monody, "but I think I never *can* love her again." Even Mrs. Gascoigne harbored an angry feeling toward her niece, which she could not refrain from expressing (with apologies) to her husband.

"I know, of course, it is better, and we ought to be thankful she is not in love with the poor boy," Mrs. Gascoigne said to Henry. "But really, Henry, I think she is hard. She has the heart of a coquette. I cannot help thinking she must have led him to believe something, or the disappointment would not have seized him so completely. And some blame attaches to poor Fanny; she is quite blind regarding that girl."

Mr. Gascoigne answered imperatively: "The less said on that point the better, Nancy. I ought to have been more awake myself. As to the boy, be thankful if nothing worse ever happens to him. Let the matter die out as quickly as possible; and especially concerning Gwendolen—let it be as if it had never been."

The rector's dominant feeling was that they had escaped disaster. If Gwendolen had loved Rex in return, it would have created a much harder problem, a solution potentially beyond his control. However, he faced further difficulties.

One fine morning, Rex requested his bath and prepared for the day as usual. Anna, filled with excitement at this change, could do nothing but listen for his descent. Finally hearing his step, she ran to the foot of the stairs to meet him. For the first time, he gave her a faint smile; yet, it looked so melancholy upon his pale face that she could hardly restrain her tears.

"Nannie!" he said gently, taking her hand and leading her slowly along to the drawing-room.

His mother was present, and when she came to kiss him, he exclaimed, "What a plague I am!" Then he sat still, gazing out of the bow-window at the lawn and shrubs covered in hoarfrost, across which the sun sent faint, occasional gleams. Anna watched him, thinking of that sad smile on Rex's face. He felt as though he had undergone a resurrection into a new world, unsure of his purpose there, with his old interests left far behind.

Anna sat near him, pretending to work, while truly watching him with yearning looks. Beyond the garden hedge lay a road where wagons and carts occasionally passed during field-work. A railed opening had been cut in the hedge because the upland, bordered by woods and a clump of ash-trees against the sky, presented a lovely sight. Soon, a wagon laden with timber rolled past. The horses strained their grand muscles, and the driver, cracking his whip, ran anxiously alongside to guide the lead horse's head, fearing a swerve.

Rex seemed shaken into attention. He rose and watched until the last quivering trunk of timber disappeared, then paced the room once or twice. Mrs. Gascoigne was no longer there. When Rex sat down again, Anna, sensing a return of speech in her brother's eyes, could not resist bringing a small stool and seating herself against his knee. She looked up at him with an expression that seemed to plead, "Do speak to me."

And he spoke.

"I'll tell you what I'm thinking of, Nannie. I will go to Canada, or somewhere of that sort." (Rex had not yet studied the character of our colonial possessions.)

"Oh, Rex, not for always!"

"Yes, to get my bread there. I should like to build a hut, work hard at clearing, and have everything wild about me, with a great wide quiet."

"And not take me with you?" said Anna, big tears rushing fast.

"How could I?"

"I should like it better than anything; and settlers go with their families. I would sooner go there than stay here in England. I could make the fires, mend the clothes, cook the food; and I could learn how to bake bread before we went. It would be nicer than anything—like playing at life over again, as we used to do when we made our tent with the drugget, and had our little plates and dishes."

"Father and mother would not let you go."

"Yes, I think they would, when I explain everything. It would save money; and Papa would have more to bring up the boys with."

Further talk of this practical nature continued at intervals, ending with Rex consenting that Anna accompany him when he spoke to his father on the subject. Naturally, this discussion took place only when the rector was alone in his study. Their mother would eventually reconcile herself to whatever decision he made, but mentioning the question to her first would have distressed her.

"Well, my children!" said Mr. Gascoigne, cheerfully, as they entered. It was comforting to see Rex about again.

"May we sit down with you a little, Papa?" said Anna. "Rex has something to say."

"With all my heart."

It was a noticeable trio these three creatures formed, each possessing faces of the same structural type: the straight brow, the nose abruptly straightened from an intention of being aquiline, the short upper lip, and the short, strong, well-hung chin. There was even the same tone of complexion and set of the eye. The gray-haired father was both massive and keen-looking; a perpendicular line on his brow deepened whenever he spoke with force of interest, and the habit of ruling gave him an air of reserved authority.

Rex would have seemed a vision of his father's youth, if one could imagine Mr. Gascoigne stripped of distinct plans and command, smitten with heart-sorrow, and lacking the capacity for concealment that a sick animal possesses. Anna was a tiny copy of Rex, her hair drawn back and knotted, her face mirroring his expressions of change as if they shared one soul.

"You know all about what has upset me, Father," Rex began, and Mr. Gascoigne nodded.

"I am quite done up for life in this part of the world. I am sure it will be no use my returning to Oxford. I couldn't do any reading. I should fail, and cause you expense for nothing. I want your consent to take another course, sir."

Mr. Gascoigne nodded more slowly; the vertical line on his brow deepened, and Anna's trembling increased.

"If you will allow me a small outfit, I should like to go to the colonies and work on the land there." Rex considered the vagueness of the phrase prudent; "the colonies" necessarily embraced more advantages and were less susceptible to rebuttal on a single specific ground than any particular settlement.

"Oh, and with me, Papa," said Anna, unable to bear being excluded from the proposal even temporarily.

"Rex would want someone to take care of him, you know—someone to keep house. And we shall never, either of us, be married. And I should cost nothing, and I should be so happy. I know it would be hard to leave you and Mamma; but there are all the others to bring up, and we two should be no trouble to you anymore."

Anna had risen from her seat, employing the feminine argument of moving closer to her father as she spoke. He did not smile, but he drew her onto his knee and held her there, as if to gently remove her from the equation while he addressed Rex.

"You will admit that my experience gives me some power of judging for you, and that I can probably guide you in practical matters better than you can guide yourself?"

Rex was obliged to say, "Yes, sir."

"And perhaps you will admit—though I do not wish to press that point—that you are bound in duty to consider my judgment and wishes?"

"I have never yet placed myself in opposition to you, sir."

In his secret soul, Rex could not feel that he was bound not to go to the colonies, but rather to return to Oxford—that was the true point in question.
