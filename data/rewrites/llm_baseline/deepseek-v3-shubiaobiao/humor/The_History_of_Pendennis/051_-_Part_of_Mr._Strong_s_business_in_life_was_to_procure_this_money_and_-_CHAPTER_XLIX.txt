Arthur Pendennis strode into Shepherd’s Inn with all the swagger of a man who had not a care in the world—or at least, who wished very much to appear that way.

“How d’ye do, Mr. Bows?” he called out, his voice a little too loud, his cheerfulness a touch too forced.

Bows, that gaunt and melancholy figure, responded with a dry nod, while Fanny Bolton—poor Fanny—blushed so violently that one might have mistaken her for a hastily painted tavern sign. The air between them was thick with things unsaid, and the ensuing handshake between Bows and Pen was about as warm as two icicles colliding.

Pen, ever the optimist in his own mind, attempted to lighten the mood. He launched into a spirited retelling of the previous night’s escapades at Vauxhall, complete with an impression of Captain Costigan’s drunken negotiations with the check-taker. Alas, his performance fell flatter than a week-old glass of champagne. Fanny managed a nervous giggle, Mrs. Bolton stared in polite confusion, and Bows—well, Bows looked as though he were mentally composing a funeral dirge.

The silence that followed was so profound one could hear the boot-heels of the loitering ticket-porter clacking against the cobblestones outside.

“You were coming to see me, sir,” Bows remarked at last, his tone drier than a solicitor’s ledger. “Would you care to ascend to my chambers? They’re rather elevated, but—”

“Oh, I’m no stranger to garrets!” Pen interrupted with a breezy wave. “Shepherd’s Inn is positively bustling compared to Lamb Court.”

“Indeed,” Bows said, with the air of a man who had just spotted a particularly stubborn stain on his cravat. “Though I was going to observe—and pray don’t take this as discourteous—that the air three flights up is generally considered more salubrious than that of a porter’s lodge.”

Pen’s candle of indignation flared. “Sir! Am I to understand—”

“You were kind enough to mention an intention to visit my humble abode,” Bows interjected smoothly. “Shall we?” He gestured toward the door with the solemnity of a tragedian delivering his final line.

And so, with a stiff bow to Mrs. Bolton and a lingering glance at Fanny (who blushed anew, as if her cheeks were in some sort of crimson competition), Pen allowed himself to be led away.

---

Upstairs, the atmosphere was no less charged. Captain Costigan, freshly emerged from his quarters and radiating an aroma best described as *eau de whiskey*, greeted Pen with a handshake that threatened to dislocate something.

“Ah, me dear boy!” he boomed, blissfully unaware that he was perhaps the least welcome participant in the room. “Sir Charles and Lady Mirabel have been pressin’ me most urgently to join ’em at Baden-Baden—”

Pen nodded gravely, recalling with some amusement the days when he had regarded the Captain’s tales with something akin to awe. Now, they rang as hollow as a tin drum.

Meanwhile, Bows sat stiffly in his impeccably ordered room—a stark contrast to Costigan’s chaos—his gaze occasionally flickering toward the lithograph of Miss Fotheringay (now Lady Mirabel) above his bed. The irony was not lost on Pen: here was a man who had once pined for an actress, now playing guardian to a girl who had unwittingly caught *his* wandering eye.

The visit concluded with handshakes all around, though Bows—ever the watchful sentinel—followed Pen out, trailing him like a shadow. Pen, irritated, finally turned on him.

“Confound it, must you dog my steps?” he muttered, though he was laughing to himself by the time he reached the Strand—a laugh that tasted faintly bitter in his own mouth.

That evening, in a futile attempt to distract himself, he drifted from the Panorama (too dull) to his club (too quiet) to a comic theatre (too garish). Mrs. Leary’s ankles, which had once seemed the height of allure, now left him cold.

And so, with a sigh and a shrug, Arthur Pendennis returned to his lodgings, his thoughts a tangled mess of guilt, defiance, and the gnawing suspicion that he was making a spectacular fool of himself.
