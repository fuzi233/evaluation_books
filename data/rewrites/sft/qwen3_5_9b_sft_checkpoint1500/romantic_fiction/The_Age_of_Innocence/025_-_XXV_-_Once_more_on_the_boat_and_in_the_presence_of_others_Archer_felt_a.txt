Once again aboard the boat, surrounded by the indifferent chatter of strangers, Newland Archer felt a profound tranquility settle over his spirit. It was a peace that surprised him as much as it sustained him, a quietude that felt almost alien to a man consumed by unfulfilled love and preparing for an indefinite separation from his passion. By any standard measure, the day had been a ridiculous failure; he had not so much as kissed Madame Olenska's hand, nor had he drawn from her a single word promising future encounters. Yet, for someone so sick with desire, leaving behind the object of it, he felt a humbling calm, a strange comfort.

It was the perfect equilibrium she had maintained between their mutual loyalties to duty and their raw honesty with themselves that had stirred and then soothed him. This balance was not the product of careful calculation, as her tears and stumbling words revealed, but sprang naturally from an unashamed sincerity. Now that the immediate danger had passed, it filled him with a tender awe. He thanked the fates that no vain impulse, no desire to perform before the sophisticated witnesses watching them, had ever tempted him to push her further. Even after they had clasped hands goodbye at the Fall River station and he turned away into the solitude of the platform, the conviction remained unshaken: he had saved far more from that meeting than he had lost.

Back at the club, Archer retreated to the deserted library, sitting alone amidst the dust motes dancing in the shafts of light. He turned over every fleeting second of their time together in his mind. The truth became undeniable under close scrutiny: if Ellen were to eventually decide to return to Europe—to return to her husband—it would not be because the old life tempted her, not even on the new terms now offered. She would go only if she felt herself becoming a temptation to him, a lure that might cause him to fall away from the high standards they had both erected. Her choice would remain to stay near him as long as he did not ask her to come closer; and it fell entirely to him to keep her exactly there, safe but secluded.

These thoughts accompanied him onto the train, enclosing him in a golden haze through which the faces of his fellow travelers looked remote and indistinct. To speak to anyone now would feel futile, as if they could not possibly understand the depth of what he carried. Waking the following morning to the stifling heat of a September day in New York, he stared through the window as heat-withered faces streamed past, maintaining that same golden blur until suddenly, a specific visage detached itself from the crowd, forcing its way into his consciousness. It was the face of the young man he had noticed the previous day exiting the Parker House—a boy who did not fit the mold of the typical American traveler, lacking the easy, commercial smile.

As the stranger approached, lifting his hat, the recognition struck Archer anew, stirring a faint echo of former associations. "Surely, Monsieur, we met in London?" the young man asked in accented English, his expression dazed, like a foreigner cast adrift on the harsh mercies of American travel.

"To be sure! In London!" Archer grasped his hand with a mixture of curiosity and sympathy. "Did you actually make it here, after all?" he exclaimed, studying the astute, haggard, little countenance of Count Olenski's French tutor.

"Oh, I got here—yes," M. Rivière smiled, though his lips were drawn tight. "But not for long; I return the day after tomorrow." He clutched his light valise in one neatly gloved hand, gazing into Archer's face with an anxiety that bordered on appeal.

"I wondered, since fate allowed us to cross paths, if I might..." Archer began, offering an invitation to luncheon downtown, suggesting a decent restaurant near his office.

M. Rivière was visibly touched and surprised. "You're too kind. But I was merely asking if you could direct me to some form of conveyance. There are no porters, and no one here seems to listen—"

"Our American stations must be surprising to you," Archer explained gently, noting how porters often produced chewing gum instead of assistance. "But if you'll come along, I'll extricate you. And you really must lunch with me."

After a brief, just-perceptible hesitation, the young man replied with profuse thanks, though his tone lacked complete conviction, stating he was already engaged. However, once they stepped onto the comparative reassurance of the street, he asked if he might call later that afternoon.

Archer, at ease in the mid-summer leisure of his office, fixed an hour and scribbled his address. The Frenchman pocketed it with reiterated gratitude and a wide flourish of his hat before departing. Punctually, at the appointed hour, M. Rivière returned. He was shaved, smoothed-out, yet still unmistakably drawn and serious. Archer sat alone in his office, and before accepting the seat proffered, the young man began abruptly.

"I believe I saw you, sir, yesterday in Boston."

The statement was insignificant enough, and Archer was about to offer an assent when his words were checked by something mysterious yet illuminating in the visitor's insistent gaze.

"It is extraordinary, very extraordinary," M. Rivière continued, "that we should have met in the circumstances in which I find myself."

"What circumstances?" Archer asked, wondering vaguely if the youth needed money.

M. Rivière studied him with tentative eyes. "I have come, not to look for employment, as I spoke of doing when we last met, but on a special mission—"

"Ah—" Archer exclaimed. In a flash, the two meetings connected in his mind. He paused to absorb the situation, newly lit up for him, and M. Rivière remained silent, as if aware that what he had said was sufficient.

"A special mission," Archer repeated at length.

The young Frenchman opened his palms, raising them slightly. The two men continued to look at each other across the office desk until Archer roused himself to say, "Do sit down." Whereupon M. Rivière bowed, took a distant chair, and waited.

"It was about this mission that you wanted to consult me?" Archer finally asked.

M. Rivière bent his head. "Not in my own behalf: on that score I—I have fully dealt with myself. I should like—if I may—to speak to you about the Countess Olenska."

Archer had known for the last few minutes that the words were coming, but when they arrived, they sent the blood rushing to his temples as if he had been caught by a bent-back branch in a thicket.

"And on whose behalf," he said, "do you wish to do this?"

M. Rivière met the question sturdily. "Well—I might say *hers*, if it did not sound like a liberty. Shall I say instead: on behalf of abstract justice?"

Archer considered him ironically. "In other words: you are Count Olenski's messenger?"

He saw the young man's blush darken, reflected in M. Rivière's sallow countenance. "Not to *you*, Monsieur. If I come to you, it is on quite other grounds."

"What right have you, in the circumstances, to *be* on any other ground?" Archer retorted sharply. "If you're an emissary, you're an emissary."

The young man considered. "My mission is over: as far as the Countess Olenska goes, it has failed."

"I can't help that," Archer rejoined on the same note of irony.

"No: but you can help—" M. Rivière paused, turning his hat about in his still carefully gloved hands, looking into its lining and then back at Archer's face. "You can help, Monsieur, I am convinced, to make it equally a failure with her family."

Archer pushed back his chair and stood up. "Well—and by God I will!" he exclaimed. He stood with his hands in his pockets, staring down wrathfully at the little Frenchman, whose face, though he too had risen, remained an inch or two below the line of Archer's eyes.

M. Rivière paled to his normal hue; paler than that, his complexion could hardly turn.

"Why the devil," Archer explosively continued, "should you have thought—that I suppose you're appealing to me on the ground of my relationship to Madame Olenska—that I should take a view contrary to the rest of her family?"

The change of expression in M. Rivière's face was for a time his only answer. His look passed from timidity to absolute distress; for a young man of his usually resourceful mien, it would have been difficult to appear more disarmed and defenseless. "Oh, Monsieur—"

"I can't imagine," Archer continued, "why you should have come to me when there are others so much nearer to the Countess; still less why you thought I should be more accessible to the arguments I suppose you were sent over with."

M. Rivière took this onslaught with a disconcerting humility. "The arguments I want to present to you, Monsieur, are my own and not those I was sent over with."

"Then I see still less reason for listening to them."

M. Rivière again looked into his hat, as if considering whether these last words were not a sufficiently broad hint to put it on and depart. Then he spoke with sudden decision. "Monsieur—will you tell me one thing? Is it my right to be here that you question? Or do you perhaps believe the whole matter to be already closed?"
