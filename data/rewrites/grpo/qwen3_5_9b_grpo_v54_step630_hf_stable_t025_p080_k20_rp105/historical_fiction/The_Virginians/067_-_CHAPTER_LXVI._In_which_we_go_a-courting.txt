It is a custom among my kind readers to visit the celebrated gardens of Regent's Park, where nature's creatures—finned, feathered, and four-footed alike—are housed in exchange for our amusement and instruction. Amidst the bustle of such a place, where business and private thought inevitably mingle with the surrounding life, I found myself gazing into an aquarium, yet my mind remained firmly anchored on our friends, the Virginians.

Among the specimens, one creature particularly caught my eye. A master of motion, he swept through his green bath in harmonious curves, flashing a black, glistening back before revealing a fair, white chest. Every movement was active and graceful. Yet, this was none other than our old, homely acquaintance, the flounder—a beauty we had previously overlooked while gobbling him up at Greenwich, unaware of his potential.

As is often the case with mankind, so it is with flounders. Viewed in their proper element, they appear agile, healthy, and comely. Remove them from their depth, however, and their beauty vanishes; their motions become disgraceful as they flap their tails against the unfeeling ground, gasping out their feeble lives. But take such a creature tenderly and return him to the Thames, and he revives.

This proverb of the "fish out of water" seemed strikingly applicable to Harry Warrington. For a long time, he had been floundering, displaced from his natural habitat. Yet, the moment he found his footing, health, strength, and spirit surged back into him. With the tap of an epaulet on his shoulder, he sprang up an entirely altered being. He delighted in his new profession, mastering the details of military life with eager quickness. Had I possessed the skill of my friend Lorrequer, I would have followed Harry into camp, sharing his carouses under the tents and explaining the maneuvers of war alongside him. Since I cannot, I ask the reader to use his imagination to fill in the colors of this picture: envision Mr. Harry Warrington in his new red coat and yellow facings, radiant with happiness to bear the King's colours and eager to perform the duties of his new trade.

While Harry embraced the excellence of battle, George, his brother, found a different, yet equally fulfilling, path. Proud of Harry's success and rejoicing in his restored fortune, George wrote an affectionate letter to his mother in Virginia. He recounted the praises Harry had earned, noting that his brother's modesty would never allow him to boast of them himself. George described how Harry had secured his first step in the army and declared his intention to ask his mother's permission to share the expense of purchasing a higher rank for him.

Nothing brought George greater delight than the ability to assist his brother, especially since Harry's sudden return to prosperity had inadvertently deprived him of an inheritance he had legitimately considered his own. Having labored under the misconception that he was the heir, Harry had incurred expenses he would never have dared as a younger son. George viewed his contribution not merely as fair compensation, but as a thank-offering for his own deliverance from financial ruin.

Having settled matters regarding Harry, George turned his pen to his own affairs, addressing a point of deep personal concern to his honored mother. He reminded her of the kindness shown to them by Mr. and Mrs. Lambert, true friends who had stood by them when their own blood relations proved worldly and unfeeling. The General was respected throughout the army, and Mrs. Lambert's affection for the Esmond children was unparalleled. Now, George wrote, he had formed an attachment for the elder Miss Lambert, a bond upon which he believed his entire happiness depended. He had made no formal offers yet, but he felt bound to confess that his sentiments were clear, and that both the young lady and her parents appeared favorable. Describing her as a model of perfection and an exemplary daughter, George expressed his firm conviction that his future lay solely in possessing or losing her.

Why, one might ask, does the editor withhold this letter? Why not reveal the secrets of a young man's love, his incoherent vows and wild raptures? Because it is cruel to babble such confidences. To overhear a lover's sighs, to bottle his tears, or to lay a stethoscope upon a delicate breast to feel the throbbing of passion is a violation of sanctity. Love is sacred. Whether it shoots suddenly from two pairs of eyes, glances sadly from one, or descends from a mother to a child, it demands reverence. To kiss and tell is wrong; to tell about kisses is worse. Let us respect the privacy of the heart.

While Harry's love for battle led him to rush toward the reluctant dragons of war, George pursued an amusement far more peaceful: the art of courtship. He penned sonnets to his mistress's eyebrow, paced beneath her window watching the lamp in her chamber, and found endless pretexts to send notes that required no immediate answer but always received one. He culled verses from his favorite poets and gathered flowers from Covent Garden for her adornment. He walked to St. James's Church, singing along with the Prayer-book while ignoring the sermon, his mind elsewhere. He was prodigiously affectionate to Miss Theo's relations—her siblings at school, her brother at college, her witty cousin Hetty, and even her mother, who, according to Martin Lambert, was already half in love with him. For if fathers may grow sulky at the prospect of a son-in-law, mothers often become sentimental, loving their own loves anew.

Gumbo and Sady were perpetually on the move between Southampton Row and Dean Street, devising summer junketings and proposing excursions to Ranelagh, Hampstead, Vauxhall, and Marylebone Gardens. George desired that Miss Theo copy a famous tragedy for the stage, admiring her beautiful Italian hand. As sheets passed between them, accompanied by notes of thanks, interrogation, and admiration, a tender intimacy grew. These virgin words, whispered for one ear alone, were too sacred for public gaze. Years later, a husband might read them with sweet pangs of remembrance, but to expose them now would be sacrilege. Let kind readers trust our word: the young lady's letters were modest and pure, the gentleman's respectful and tender. In these few months, Mr. George Warrington had decided he had found the woman of women. She may not be the most beautiful—Cousin Flora, Coelia, and Ardelia were far more handsome—but her sweet face pleased him more than any other. She may not be the most clever, but her voice was the dearest to hear. In her company, he felt clever, eloquent, and generous; he was so noble and witty that he delighted in her presence. And regarding the young lady, we may be sure, as Heaven knows we do not suppose women to be devoid of virtue, that she endowed her young hero with wit, wisdom, beauty, valour, and goodness in equal measure.

When George's letter reached home, excitement rippled through the circle around Madam Esmond's fireside. He was in love and wished to marry! It was natural, and it promised to keep him out of harm's way. If he intended to unite with a well-bred Christian woman, Madam saw no objection.

"I knew they would be setting their caps at him," said Mountain. "They fancy his wealth equals his estate. He does not say whether the young lady has money. I fear otherwise."

"People would set their caps at him here, I dare say," replied Madam Esmond, grimly regarding her dependent. "They would try to catch Mr. Esmond Warrington for their own daughters, who are no richer than Miss Lambert may be."

"I suppose your ladyship means me!" said Mountain. "My Fanny is poor, as you say; and 'tis kind of you to remind me of her poverty!"

"I said people would set their caps at him. If the cap fits you, *tant pis!* as my papa used to say."

"You think, madam, I am scheming to keep George for my daughter? I thank you, on my word! A good opinion you seem to have of us after the years we have lived together!"

"My dear Mountain, I know you much better than to suppose you could ever fancy your daughter would be a suitable match for a gentleman of Mr. Esmond's rank and station," said the mistress of Castlewood with dignity.

"Fanny Parker was as good as Molly Benson at school, and Mr. Mountain's daughter is as good as Mr. Lambert's!" cried Mrs. Mountain.

"Then you did think of marrying her to my son! I shall write to Mr. Esmond Warrington and say how sorry I am that you should be disappointed!" declared the mistress. And we may suppose that Mrs. Mountain was indeed disappointed, harboring some ambitious views for her daughter, which explains her anger at the notion of Mr. Warrington's marriage to another.

In reply, Madam Esmond wrote that she was pleased with the fraternal love George exhibited. It was right, she noted, to compensate Harry, whose expectations had led him to adopt a costly lifestyle he would not have chosen had he known he was only a younger son. Regarding the purchase of his promotion, she offered to halve the expense with Harry's elder brother, grateful that his own gallantry had won him his first step. She added that this bestowal of George's money was far more satisfactory than other extravagances she would not detail.

The other extravagance alluded to was the payment of the ransom to the French captain's family—a tax George's mother never would have submitted to. Possessing a determined spirit of her own, which her son inherited, she viewed his persistence as pride and obstinacy. What she thought of her own pertinacity, her distant biographer does not pretend to say. Only one can assume that people a hundred years ago resembled their modern descendants in loving to have their own way and making others follow it.

After paying his own ransom, his brother's debts, and half the price for his promotion, George calculated that a significant portion of his private patrimony would be consumed. Nevertheless, he made the sacrifice with a perfect good heart. His good mother always enjoined him to remember his grandfather and support the dignity of his family. She gave him commissions to purchase goods in England, and though she had sent only trifling remittances, she constantly alluded to the exalted rank of the Esmonds. She desired that he do nothing unworthy of that illustrious family, advising him peremptorily to appear in the first society, frequent the court where his ancestors moved, and conduct himself in a manner worthy of his name. George made no doubt that his mother's money would be forthcoming when his own ran short, and he generously obeyed her injunctions regarding his style of life.

In the Esmond papers of this period, I find bills for genteel entertainments, tailors' bills for court suits, liveries for his negro servants and chairmen, horse-dealers' receipts, and the like. Thus, it appears the elder of our Virginians was also living at considerable expense. Yet, he was not wild or extravagant like his brother. There was no talk of gambling or racehorses against Mr. George; his table was liberal, his equipages handsome, and his purse always full. The estate to which he was heir was known to be immense. I mention these circumstances because they likely influenced the conduct of George and his friends regarding the matter of his marriage.

The young heir of Virginia was traveling for pleasure and improvement in foreign kingdoms. The Queen, his mother, maintained daily correspondence with his Highness, constantly enjoining him to act as became his lofty station. There could be no doubt from her letters that she desired him to live liberally and magnificently. He was perpetually making purchases at her order. She had not yet settled the accounts; on the contrary, she had written by the last mail for twelve new sets of waggon harness and an organ capable of playing fourteen specified psalm-tunes. George dutifully ordered these items. She had not paid as yet, and might not today or tomorrow, but eventually, of course, she would. Mr. Warrington never troubled his friends with these calculations or discussed his mother's domestic affairs. They, on their side, took for granted that he was in a state of competence and ease. Without being mercenary, Mr. and Mrs. Lambert were pleased to see an attachment growing between their daughter and a young gentleman of such good principles, talents, family, and expectations. There was honesty in all Mr. Esmond Warrington's words and actions, and in his behavior to the world a certain grandeur and simplicity that marked him as a true gentleman. Somewhat cold and haughty in his demeanor to strangers, especially the great, he was not supercilious; he was perfectly courteous to women and, with those he loved, kind, amiable, lively, and tender.

No wonder that one young woman came to think him the best man in all the world—alas! not even excepting her father. A great love felt by a man towards a woman makes him better, as regards her, than all other men. We have said that George used to wonder how witty, eloquent, and wise he became when talking with the fair young creature whose heart had become all his. We will not again listen to their love whispers. Those soft words do not bear being written down. If you please, good sir or madam, who are sentimentally inclined, lay down the book and think over certain things for yourself. You may be ever so old now; but you remember. It may be all dead and buried; but in a moment, it springs out of its grave, looking, smiling, and whispering as of yore when it clung to your arm and dropped fresh tears on your heart. Is it here, and alive? O far, far away! O lonely hearth and cold ashes! Here is the vase, but the roses are gone; here is the shore, and yonder the ship was moored; but the anchors are up, and it has sailed away forever.
