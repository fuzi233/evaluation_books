The sky above Florence hung heavy with a slate-grey pall, yet the Piazza del Duomo wore a mask of festive azure. Banners unfurled at the corners of the Baptistery caught the faint light, casting shadows over marble steps that were already trampled by the throngs of the faithful. It was the hour for Advent sermons, a solemn duty that demanded the city's attention regardless of the gloom outside. Yet, not all who moved through the square hurried toward the cathedral doors. High and low walked with a restless pace, their movements dictated by errands rather than devotion. While some lingered near the entrance, content to miss the preacher, others stood apart in clusters, their expressions far removed from the lazy enjoyment of a holiday.

At the corner where the Piazza met the Via de' Cerretani, the air grew somber, the artificial brilliance of the square giving way to the dull, grey morning light. There stood a ragged assembly of workers, their clothes stained with the dyes and dust of daily labor, many armed with tools that doubled as weapons. Among them was Ser Cioni, a notary whose voice had once been dismissed with scorn in the Mercato, but now spoke with the complacent humor of a man riding high. To the gathered crowd, he promised a terrifying truth: "Never talk to me of bloodthirsty Swiss or fierce French infantry," he declared, his tone cutting through the damp air. "They might as well be trapped in mountain passes as walking our streets. Peasants have shattered the finest armies of our condottieri when forced into steep precipices. Tell me truly, Florentines, need we fear any army within our own walls?"

A man named Oddo, his hands and forearms permanently tinted crimson by the vats he tended, stepped forward. A small hatchet hung at his belt. "True enough, Ser Cioni," Oddo replied, his voice rising. "Those French cavaliers who paraded in their stiff doublets last week saw little but a meal waiting for them. I was carrying cloth past Ognissanti when I saw my masters looking down on us, pitying our inability to strut like proud roosters. 'Stick out your stomachs,' I told them, 'for I carry a meat-axe that will slide right in.' Then the great bell of the Palazzo Vecchio sounded—the *vacca muglia*—and chaos erupted. We raised a barricade, trapping them, and our *Ciompi*, led by one brandishing a great scythe, cut off the plumes from a nobleman's helmet. It was glorious! Though, alas, Piero de' Medici never returned. What a pity; we might have left him with neither legs nor wings to fly home."

"Aye, and let us hope he stays put," agreed a young butcher, his knife sheathed at his hip. "Piero looked as spooked as a hunted chicken when we pelted him. A coward, unless he has horses to hide behind. But we shall swallow no Medici again, whatever the King of France demands."

"And those cannons?" interjected Goro, a stout man whose grievances had fattened him over two years. "San Giovanni defend us! If Messer Domeneddio truly cares for us, why send the French to Naples instead? By God, they could have marched through Bologna or Arezzo. We wouldn't have minded Arezzo!"

"Fools!" Ser Cioni snapped, attempting to steer the conversation toward the glory of Florence, but his words were drowned out by a sudden cry: "Look there!"

All eyes swiveled toward the Via de' Cerretani. Advancing with a brilliance that clashed with the grey street was Lorenzo Tornabuoni, accompanied by a French nobleman dressed in splendor, his white linen embroidered with gold. With them walked two other Florentines of high birth, but leading the procession on the Frenchman's left was a figure who needed no brocade to command attention. He wore only black, a mantle of mourning that would soon be exchanged for red as he prepared to walk in the Latin Secretary's procession. This was Tito Melema.

Eighteen months had passed since last seen, yet a subtle shift marked him. The final shedding of youthful innocence had settled over him, replaced by the cold, calculated self-possession of a man fully adopted into the machinery of state. His features remained soft, his eyes clear, but beneath the surface lay an indefinable transformation, like the shifting hues of dawn. He smiled, listened, and explained with effortless grace, masking a profound internal change. Beside him, the Frenchman sought instructions on ceremony for his return to Signa, pausing finally to survey the Piazza where the royal procession would halt.

Though the party drew stares, the gaze was not universally warm. Whispers circulated regarding the Frenchman's hoof-shaped shoes, delicate flatteries of royal excess, while sharp, muttered insults directed at the Medici were carefully lowered to avoid detection. Lorenzo Tornabuoni, however, possessed the skill of a master actor, disguising any annoyance with a practiced smile. Tito, aided by his alien perspective, viewed the local tensions with detached amusement, using his fluency in French and familiarity with the South to charm the visitors.

As the group reached a point offering a view of the Duomo, they paused. Festoons adorned the central door, prompting a debate. Tornabuoni summoned Piero di Cosimo, who lounged idly before Nello's shop. An animated discussion ensued, punctuated by Piero's pungent observations, which Tito translated with literal precision. The absurdity of the exchange turned even the grumpiest onlookers into amused spectators, their faces wearing expressions of half-smile, half-humiliation. For Tito, this was a triumph; he reveled in the approving glances, basking in the warmth of the crowd's laughter as the grey light faded around them.

Yet, in the shadowed lanes beyond the square, a darker current flowed. Oncomers, clad in rags and devoid of festive finery, approached with grim determination. Their focus was drawn to the conspicuous French attire, specifically the strange, oversized footwear. Pressing closer, a growing number of non-admiring citizens surrounded the foreigners. Leading this tide were three men in scanty clothing, their hands bound tightly by cord, a rope looped around their necks and bodies to restrain any violent movement. Their guards were French soldiers, who used broken Italian and the threat of strangulation to urge their charges to beg.

Two of the prisoners complied, kneeling and holding out their bound hands with piteous tones: "For the love of God and the Holy Madonna, give us something for our ransom! We are Tuscans; we were captured in Lunigiana."

But the third man, the eldest, remained stubbornly silent. Unlike his younger companions, who appeared sturdy and mendicant despite their poverty, this older man had crossed the threshold of age, likely fifty or sixty. His beard, grown long in neglect, and his straight, thick hair framed a bald crown, both turning nearly white. His frame, though emaciated, stood firm and upright, radiating a strange, isolated energy. Dark eyes and strong brows burned with intensity against a face of yellow, bloodless skin, deeply wrinkled and tangled with grey strands. Yet, a fitfulness haunted his gaze; after flickering with fierce scrutiny of the windows and passersby, his eyes would fall again, lost and wandering. His lips were motionless, his hands clenched resolutely downward. He refused to beg.

The sight sparked growing exasperation among the Florentines. Initially, many had dropped coins, some out of automatic piety, others out of a superstitious awe of the French military, believing payment ensured safety. But as the procession moved deeper into the city, this compliance evaporated. A chorus of angry exclamations rose, intelligible enough to the foreign ears without translation. "French dogs!" "Bullock-feet!" "Snatch their pikes!" "Cut the cords and let them run! They'll flee faster than geese!"

The soldiers, who relished their insolence, found themselves checked by the sheer volume of the crowd. With a strong desire to draw steel, they were hamstrung by the necessity of maintaining a tight grip on their prisoners. Hurrying now toward a nearby inn, hoping for shelter, they were met by the dyer, Oddo. Seeing the tumult, Oddo snatched his axe from his belt and ran forward, followed by the butcher and the rest of the workers, leaving Goro to retreat into a narrow alley.

Oddo's appearance, with his blood-red arms and uplifted axe, acted as a catalyst. He did not strike immediately but stood as a stirring symbol of resistance, a living banner of the street. From the crowd emerged Lollo, a conjuror's impish lad, dancing and jeering before the innocent children who formed the bulk of the mob. Lollo cared little for the prisoners' plight; he coveted the chance to display his dexterity. Seizing a moment as the crowd jostled more violently, he leaped forward. In an instant, he sliced the cord binding the eldest prisoner.

"Run, old one!" Lollo whispered, piping the word into the man's ear. Immediately, he set an example, sprinting away with the frantic agility of a startled bird.

The old man's senses were not dulled by age. The idea of escape had haunted him constantly, and the shifting mood of the crowd gave him fresh hope. He broke free instantly.
