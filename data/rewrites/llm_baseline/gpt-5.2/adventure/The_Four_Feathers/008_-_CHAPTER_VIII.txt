Durrance came into London on a pale June morning when the city already had its summer voice: iron-rimmed wheels over granite, a thousand footsteps stitched together on the pavements, and above it all the ceaseless, thin scraping of commerce—bells, whistles, a distant hammering that never quite stopped. By afternoon he had done what men newly landed from far stations always did, as if to prove the country still held them; he went to Hyde Park and sat beneath a great tree, letting the green canopy soften the hard metallic din.

The grass smelled bruised and sweet where it had been cut; under the leaves the air was cooler, laced with the faint perfume of gloves and powdered hair. Women passed in light dresses that seemed made of summer itself, their laughter scattering like birdsong over the drone of the town. Durrance watched them with a kind of hunger—an exile’s astonishment at small, careful graces—and all the while his sunburnt face, with its set look about the eyes, marked him as one who had lived where horizons were bare and noise came from wind and rifle-fire rather than from streets.

Among the strollers one woman’s glance did not slide away. She smiled faintly, as though at something remembered, and when Durrance rose from his chair Mrs. Adair was beside him.

She was in black.

The colour struck him first—more than her face, more than the quick, almost secret measuring look with which she took him from head to foot, comparing what stood before her with the image she had carried for three years. He saw only the mourning, and the question he did not speak she answered before he could shape it.

“My husband died eighteen months ago,” she said, her voice quiet, as if she were giving a date from an old parish register. “Thrown from his horse during a run with the Pytchley. Killed at once.”

“I had not heard,” Durrance said, awkwardly, because condolences were a language he never commanded. “I am very sorry.”

She took the chair beside his. She did not say she was glad of his pity, nor that she needed it. She had always been a woman of pauses that seemed to contain more than speech could bear; her pale, composed face offered no clue to what those silences held. For a moment the Park’s soft noises—the rustle of skirts, the low hum of talk—seemed to press in on the two of them, and Durrance, remembering Adair as good-humoured and demonstrably fond of his wife, discovered with discomfort that he could not summon up a full grief. Adair had been, in that household, almost a shadow; the sorrow belonged to Mrs. Adair alone, and she wore it with a correctness that made it hard to touch.

He abandoned the attempt and turned instead to the other names that had haunted him all the way from the sea.

“Are Harry Feversham and his wife in town?”

Mrs. Adair’s answer took time, as though she were searching for the proper word and finding none that would not cut.

“Not yet,” she said at last; then, as if the phrase betrayed her into a lie of habit, she corrected herself quickly. “I mean—the marriage never took place.”

Durrance’s surprise, when it came, did not burst out in exclamation. It was a slight stiffening, a stillness. “I don’t think I understand. Why did it never take place?”

She looked sharply at him, as though his deliberate tone hid some accusation. “I don’t know why. Ethne can keep a secret if she wishes.”

Durrance nodded; that, at least, matched what he knew. “The marriage was broken off on the night of a dance at Lennon House.”

He turned fully towards her. “Just before I left England three years ago?”

“Yes.”

“Then you knew?”

“No. Only—” he paused, and in that pause the sea-wind of Dover seemed to blow through the leaves. “You have explained something which happened on the very night I left. What has become of Harry?”

Mrs. Adair lifted her shoulders in a small, indifferent shrug that somehow felt like a sentence. “I do not know. I have met no one who does know. I do not think I have met any one who has even seen him since that time. He must have left England.”

Durrance, staring past her at a carriage glittering by, suddenly saw again the man on the pier at Dover—his face twisted with trouble, despair clinging to him like a fog. So it had been Harry after all. His friend. And he had gone away as completely as if the Channel had swallowed him.

“And Miss Eustace?” Durrance asked after a moment, with a timidity that surprised him. “She has married since?”

Again she delayed, and the delay was its own answer before the word arrived. “No.”

“Then she is still at Ramelton?”

Mrs. Adair shook her head. “There was a fire at Lennon House a year ago.” She paused. “Did you ever hear of a constable called Bastable?”

“Indeed, I did.” The name brought back the sharp smell of wet leather and turf smoke from an Irish night, and the indignation of being hauled up like a criminal. “He was the means of introducing me to Miss Eustace and her father. I was travelling from Londonderry to Letterkenny. I received a letter from Mr. Eustace—whom I did not know, but who knew from friends at Letterkenny that I was coming past his house. He asked me to stay the night with him. Naturally I declined, and Bastable arrested me on a magistrate’s warrant as soon as I landed from the ferry.”

“That is the man,” Mrs. Adair said, and began, in that steady, feelingless way she had, to tell him what had happened.

Bastable’s claim to Dermod Eustace’s friendship, it seemed, rested upon a talent not of law but of the saucepan. There was, in that household, a particular toddy—whiskey warmed and sweetened, the water tempered just so, and a spice added with the confidence of long practice. Bastable had a refinement for it, an old country cunning: to make it perfect he would simmer in the saucepan a single oyster, just one, until its briny sweetness and faint sea-metal melted into the drink and gave it a depth no one could quite name. The thing was absurd and, to those who had tasted it, irresistible. It was as though the ocean itself had been persuaded to lend a whisper to the fire.

On a June night at two in the morning, the spirit lamp beneath the saucepan had been upset. The two men—confederates in drink—had not their wits about them. The small flame, fed too easily, had found wood, curtains, whatever it could catch; the air would have filled first with the sharp reek of burning spirit, then with the bitter, choking smell of charred fabric. By the time help came, half the house was burnt and the rest was ruined by the water that saved it.

“There were consequences still more distressing than the destruction of the house,” Mrs. Adair went on, her eyes fixed straight ahead as if she were reading from a page only she could see. “The fire was a beacon warning Dermod’s creditors, for one thing. Dermod, already overpowered with debts, fell in a day upon complete ruin. He was drenched by the hoses besides, and took a chill which nearly killed him. From the effects he has never recovered. You will find him a broken man. The estates are let, and Ethne is now living with her father in a little mountain village in Donegal.”

She had spoken as though necessity, not sympathy, forced each sentence out of her. Even when she stopped she did not turn her face towards him.

“So she has lost everything?” Durrance said.

“She still has a home in Donegal,” Mrs. Adair returned.

“And that means a great deal to her,” Durrance said slowly, as if tasting the truth of it.

“Yes, I think you are right.”

“It means,” Mrs. Adair said, “that Ethne with all her ill-luck has reason to be envied by many other women.”

Durrance did not answer at once. The Park went on around them, London’s easy chatter and the bright, careless laughter. Above it, always, that undertone of the city’s machinery—wheels, hooves, the faint clatter of harness—like an instrument no one could ever quite silence. In it he heard the difference between comfort and exile: out there, under wide skies, the world could be quiet enough for a man to hear his own thoughts coming; here the noise pressed and pressed, as though it feared what a person might remember in stillness.

At last his slow mind worked toward words.

“Women suffer much more than men when the world goes wrong with them,” he said, and it sounded, even to himself, less like a statement than a question set down carefully. “I know very little, of course. I can only guess. But I think women gather up into themselves what they have been through much more than we do. To them what is past becomes a real part of them, as much a part of them as a limb; to us it’s always something external—at the best the rung of a ladder, at the worst a weight on the heel. Don’t you think so? I phrase it badly. But put it this way: women look backwards, we look ahead; so misfortune hits them harder, eh?”

Mrs. Adair did not agree or contradict him. Her reply came from another place altogether, quiet and exact, like a direction written on the back of an envelope.

“The mountain village at which Ethne is living,” she said in a low voice, “is called Glenalla. A track strikes up towards it from the road halfway between Rathmullen and Ramelton.”

She rose as she finished, and held out her hand. “Shall I see you?”

“You are still in Hill Street?” Durrance asked.

“I shall be for a time in London.”

He took her hand. “Then you will see me.”

Mrs. Adair’s eyebrows lifted very slightly, her face sharpening with the suspicion she always had of motives. She could not understand why he stayed. If his thoughts were already in Donegal, why did he not follow them? She heard, as the weeks went, that he was continually at his Service Club; that he haunted its inner courtyard beneath the great tree, speaking to this man and that, as if he listened for a particular note in the sound of names.

She was more puzzled still when he told her, without emphasis, that he had gone down into Surrey and spent a day with General Feversham.

It was an ineffectual day. The old general kept him steadily to the campaign from which Durrance had returned—troop movements, orders, distances, the tidy arithmetic of war. The general’s house was quiet in a way London never was; but it was a brittle quiet, a silence packed tight around certain rooms. Only once did Durrance edge towards the subject that mattered. He spoke Harry’s name.

The effect was immediate. The general’s face set, as if plaster had been drawn over it. The features became blank; the eyes, which had been attentive, seemed to look past Durrance and see nothing.

“We will talk of something else, if you please,” he said.

Durrance returned to London not an inch nearer to Donegal, though his mind travelled there each night.

So he sat under the courtyard tree at his club through June afternoons and evenings, letting the soft shade make a small country within the city’s clangour. The place had its own soundscape: the muted clink of glasses carried out from the smoking-room, the dry slap of newspapers folded, the low, well-bred rumble of voices that never rose into true laughter. He spoke to whomever passed—any friend of Feversham’s, any man who had ever dined with him, ridden with him, served near him. The words were always the same in the end, turned and turned again like a question asked in different dialects in the hope one would unlock the answer.

But each time he met only shoulders lifted, eyes averted, a “Hanged if I know!” And it was as though Harry Feversham’s very name had begun to die on their tongues, slipping out of common speech; a thing no longer spoken of soon became, in that world, a thing unreal.

Toward the end of June an old naval officer limped into the courtyard. Lieutenant Sutch. Durrance saw him pause when he noticed the man beneath the tree, saw him hesitate, and then, with remarkable speed for his infirmity, attempt to move away as though he had mistaken the door.

Durrance was on his feet in an instant.

“Mr. Sutch,” he said.

The lieutenant’s embarrassment showed itself too plainly. “Colonel Durrance, to be sure,” he replied, forcing a geniality that creaked. “It is some while since we met, but I remember you very well now. I think we met—let me see—where was it? An old man’s memory, Colonel Durrance, is like a leaky ship. It comes to harbour with its cargo of recollections swamped.”

“We met at Broad Place,” Durrance said. He stepped close enough that Sutch could not drift away into safer company. “I wish you to give me news of my friend Feversham. Why was his engagement with Miss Eustace broken off? Where is he now?”

For a moment the lieutenant’s eyes gleamed with a quick satisfaction. He had never been sure whether Durrance knew of the disgrace that had fallen on Harry. Durrance plainly did not.

“There is only one person in the world, I believe,” Sutch said slowly, “who can answer both your questions.”

Durrance did not flinch. “Yes. I have waited here a month for you.”

Lieutenant Sutch pushed his fingers through his beard and stared down at him, as if weighing whether he was being trapped by honour or by curiosity.

“Well, it is true,” he admitted. “I can answer your questions—but I will not.”

“Harry Feversham is my friend.”

“General Feversham is his father, yet he knows only half the truth. Miss Eustace was betrothed to him, and she knows no more. I pledged my word to Harry that I would keep silence.”

“It is not curiosity which makes me ask.”

“I am sure that, on the contrary, it is friendship,” Sutch said warmly, and meant it. “Nor that entirely,” Durrance answered, and the faintest colour rose under his sunburn. “There is another aspect. I will not ask you to answer my questions, but I will put a third one to you. It is harder for me to ask than for you to answer. Would a friend of Harry Feversham be at all disloyal to that friendship if—if he tried his luck with Miss Eustace?”

The question startled Lieutenant Sutch into a frank exclamation. “You?”

He stood considering Durrance, recalling his rapid promotion, the look of him—strong, set, a man who had done things and would do them again—and imagining, in his simple theory of women, what such brute courage might mean to a woman stripped of house and fortune. In that instant the lieutenant felt something like jealousy on Harry’s behalf, fierce as an old nurse’s over a favourite child. He had cherished a secret hope that Harry would come back in the end to all he had lost, like a king who returns from exile; and now he saw the hope threatened by the very man who asked him the question with such restraint.

Sutch was tempted—sorely tempted—to lie. He knew Durrance well enough to believe the lie would do its work. Let him think it disloyal; he would go back to the Soudan and leave Ethne alone with her poor father and her mountain village.

“Well?” Durrance asked, pitiless in its quiet.

Sutch looked up at the sky and down at the flags, as if the answer were written somewhere in the spaces between. He thought of Harry under a burning sun, lost to all maps, and set that image against the comfortable courtyard, the successful soldier waiting under a tree while servants moved silently with trays. He felt, with a sudden anger, like breaking his word and telling all—where Harry had gone, why he had vanished, what shame had driven him.

“Well?” Durrance repeated.

Sutch’s mouth tightened. Honour held him by the throat, and something else held him too—the sense that words, once spoken, could never be taken back; that a name, once shaped in the dominant language of their world, could kill a man’s chance to be remembered any other way.

“No,” he said at last, regretfully. “There would be no disloyalty.”

And on that evening, while London clattered on with its indifferent noise, Durrance went to his rooms, packed, and took the train for Holyhead.
