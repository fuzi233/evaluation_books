When we reached our hut, I gestured for Infadoos to step inside with us.

“Now, Infadoos,” I said, “we wish to speak with you.”

“Let my lords speak,” he replied.

“It seems to us,” I continued, “that Twala, the king, is a cruel man.”

“He is indeed, my lords,” Infadoos answered. “The land groans under his tyranny. Tonight, you will see—tonight is the great witch-hunt. Many will be accused of sorcery and killed. No one is safe. If the king desires a man’s cattle, or his wife, or fears him as a threat to his rule, then Gagool—the old woman you saw—or one of her trained witch-finders will mark him as a wizard, and he shall die. Before the moon fades, many will fall. It always happens this way. Perhaps even I may be among them. I have been spared so far because I am skilled in war and beloved by the soldiers, but I do not know how much longer I shall live. The people suffer under Twala’s cruelty. They are weary of him and his bloodthirsty ways.”

“Then why do they not rise against him?” I asked.

“Because he is the king,” Infadoos said. “If he were slain, Scragga would take his place—and Scragga’s heart is blacker than his father’s. Under Scragga, the yoke upon our necks would be heavier still. If Imotu had not died, or if Ignosi, his son, had survived, things might have been different. But both are gone.”

“How do you know Ignosi is dead?” a voice broke in behind us. We turned sharply, startled. It was Umbopa.

“What do you mean, boy?” Infadoos demanded. “Who gave you leave to speak?”

“Listen,” Umbopa said quietly. “I will tell you a story. Long ago, King Imotu was killed in this land, and his wife fled with their son, Ignosi. Is that not true?”

“It is.”

“They say the mother and child perished on the mountains. Is that not so?”

“It is.”

“But they did not die. They crossed the mountains and were led by a wandering tribe of desert people across the sands until they found water, grass, and trees once more.”

“How do you know this?”

“Listen. They journeyed for many months until they came to a land ruled by the Amazulu—a people of Kukuana descent who live by war. There they stayed for years. Then the mother died. Her son, Ignosi, became a wanderer again. He traveled into a land of wonders where white men dwell, and there he spent many years learning their ways.”

“It sounds like a tale,” Infadoos said, skeptical.

“For years, he lived as a servant and soldier, yet never forgot what his mother told him of his homeland. He waited, planning how he might return before death claimed him. At last, he met white men seeking this unknown land, and joined them. Together, they crossed the burning deserts, climbed snow-capped mountains, and finally reached the land of the Kukuanas—where they found *you*, O Infadoos.”

“You must be mad,” Infadoos said, stunned.

“Perhaps I am,” Umbopa replied. “But watch. I will show you, O uncle.”

“I am Ignosi, rightful king of the Kukuanas!”

With a swift motion, Umbopa removed his *moocha*—his girdle—and stood naked before us.

“Look,” he said, pointing to a blue tattoo circling his waist: a great snake coiled with its tail in its open mouth, just above the junction of his thighs.

Infadoos stared. His eyes widened, then he dropped to his knees.

“Koom! Koom!” he gasped. “It is my brother’s son! It is the king!”

“Did I not tell you, uncle?” Umbopa said. “Rise. I am not yet king—but with your help, and the help of these brave white men who are my friends, I shall be. And Gagool was right—the land will run with blood first. Hers too, if she can die. She killed my father with her words and drove my mother away. Now, Infadoos, choose: will you place your hands in mine and become my man? Will you share the dangers ahead and help me overthrow this tyrant? Or will you not? Choose.”

Infadoos pressed his hand to his head, thinking. Then he rose, stepped forward, knelt before Umbopa, and took his hand.

“Ignosi, rightful king of the Kukuanas,” he said, “I place my hand in yours. I am your man until death. When you were a babe, I held you in my arms. Now, my old arm shall fight for you and freedom.”

“It is well,” Ignosi said. “If I win, you shall be the greatest man in the kingdom after the king. If I fail, you can only die—and death is near enough already. Rise, uncle.”

“And you, white men,” Ignosi said, turning to us, “will you stand with me? What can I offer? The white stones! If I succeed and find them, you shall have as many as you can carry. Will that suffice?”

I translated.

“Tell him,” Sir Henry replied, “that an Englishman does not sell himself for riches. Wealth is good—if it comes our way, we’ll take it. But a gentleman doesn’t trade honor for gold. Still, speaking for myself—I’ve always liked Umbopa. So long as I can, I’ll stand by him. It will be a pleasure to face that cruel beast Twala. What about you, Good? And you, Quatermain?”

“Well,” Good said, “to use their dramatic language—this sounds like a fine row. Warms the cockles of the heart. As for me, I’m his boy. Only condition: I insist on wearing trousers.”

I relayed the message.

“It is well,” Ignosi said. “And you, Macumazahn? Are you with me, old hunter—cleverer than a wounded buffalo?”

I paused, scratching my head.

“Umbopa—or Ignosi,” I said, “I don’t care for revolutions. I’m a man of peace and a bit of a coward”—Umbopa smiled—“but I stand by my friends. You’ve stood by us, played the part of a man. I’ll stand by you. That said, I’m a trader—I need to earn my living. So yes, I accept your offer of diamonds, should we ever reach them. One other thing: we came here to find Incubu’s lost brother. You must help us locate him.”

“I will,” Ignosi promised. “Stay, Infadoos. By the sign of the snake around my middle, tell me the truth: has any white man ever set foot in this land?”

“None, O Ignosi.”

“If one had been seen or heard, would you have known?”

“I would have known.”

“Then hear this, Incubu,” Ignosi said to Sir Henry. “He has not been here.”

“Well,” Sir Henry sighed, “there it is. I suppose he never made it. Poor fellow. All for nothing. God’s will be done.”

“Now, business,” I said, eager to move on. “Fine to be king by right, Ignosi—but how do you plan to become king in truth?”

“I do not know,” he admitted. “Infadoos, do you have a plan?”

“Ignosi, Son of the Lightning,” Infadoo replied, “tonight is the great dance and witch-hunt. Many will be marked and slain. Others will burn with grief and fury at the king. After the dance, I will speak to the great chiefs. If I can win them over, they will rally their regiments. I will speak softly at first, showing them you are truly the king. By tomorrow’s light, I believe you will command twenty thousand spears. But now I must go—think, listen, prepare. After the dance, if I live and you live, I will meet you here. At best, there will be war.”

At that moment, a cry rang out: messengers from the king had arrived. We ordered them in. Three men entered, each bearing a gleaming shirt of chain mail and a magnificent battle-axe.

“The gifts of my lord the king to the white men from the Stars!” announced a herald.

“We thank the king,” I said. “Withdraw.”

They left. We examined the armor with fascination. It was the finest chainwork any of us had ever seen—so tightly linked that the entire coat formed a mass barely large enough to cover both hands.

“Do you make these in this land, Infadoos?” I asked. “They are beautiful.”

“No, my lord,” he said. “They came down from our ancestors. We do not know who forged them. Few remain. Only those of royal blood may wear them. They are magical—no spear can pierce them. Those who wear them are nearly invincible in battle. The king sends them either out of favor—or fear. Wear them tonight, my lords.”

We spent the rest of the day resting, discussing our situation—exciting enough. As dusk fell, the thousand watchfires blazed to life. In the dark, we heard the tramp of thousands of feet, the clash of hundreds of spears—as regiments marched to their posts. Then the full moon rose, brilliant and clear. Infadoos arrived, dressed in his war gear, escorted by twenty men. As he advised, we had already donned the chain shirts beneath our clothes—surprisingly light and comfortable. The armor, clearly made for larger men, hung loosely on Good and me, but fit Sir Henry like a glove. We strapped revolvers at our waists, took up the battle-axes, and set off.

At the great kraal—where we’d been received by the king earlier—we found it packed with some twenty thousand men arranged in regiments. Each regiment was divided into companies, with narrow paths between them for the witch-finders to pass. Never had I seen such an imposing sight—silent, orderly, armed to the teeth. The moonlight bathed their raised spears, their proud forms, their plumed heads, and the colorful shields. Everywhere, rows of dim faces topped by shimmering spear tips.

“Surely,” I said to Infadoos, “the whole army is here?”

“No, Macumazahn,” he replied. “Only a third. Another third stands ready outside in case of trouble. Ten thousand guard the outposts around Loo. The rest watch the kraals. This is a mighty people.”

“They are very quiet,” Good observed. Indeed, the silence among so many men was almost suffocating.

“What says Bougwan?” Infadoos asked.

I translated.

“Those shadowed by Death are silent,” he answered grimly.

“Will many die?”

“Many.”

“It seems,” I said to the others, “we’re attending a gladiatorial spectacle—without mercy.”

Sir Henry shivered. Good wished we could escape.

“Are we in danger?” I asked Infadoos.

“I cannot say, my lords. I hope not. But do not appear afraid. If you survive the night, all may yet go well. The soldiers mutter against the king.”

As we advanced toward the center, where stools were placed, another group approached from the royal hut.

“It is the king Twala, his son Scragga, and Gagool the old,” Infadoos whispered. “And look—those who kill.” He pointed to a dozen enormous, savage men, each armed with a spear and a heavy club.

Twala sat on the central stool. Gagool crouched at his feet. The executioners stood behind.

“Greeting, white lords,” Twala called. “Be seated—time is precious. The night is too short for what must be done. You come at a good hour. You shall witness a glorious show. Look around, white lords—look around!” He rolled his single, wicked eye across the ranks. “Can the Stars show you such a sight? See how they tremble in their wickedness—all those with evil hearts, fearing the judgment of ‘Heaven above.’”

“_Begin! Begin!_” shrieked Gagool, her voice thin and piercing. “The hyenas are hungry—they howl for food. _Begin! Begin!_”

For a moment, silence gripped the place—horrible, expectant.

The king lifted his spear. Instantly, twenty thousand feet rose as one and slammed down on the earth. Three times. The ground shook. Then, from the edge of the circle, a lone voice began a mournful song:

“_What is the lot of man born of woman?_”

The answer echoed from every throat:

“_Death!_”

Gradually, company after company joined in. The song swelled—now love, now war, now a death dirge—ending in a single, heart-wrenching wail that rolled across the darkness like thunder.

Silence returned. Then the king raised his hand.

A pattering of feet. From the ranks, strange figures emerged—women, mostly old, their white hair adorned with fish bladders, faces painted in yellow and white stripes, snake skins trailing down their backs, human bones rattling at their waists. Each carried a forked wand. Ten of them. They halted before us.

One pointed at Gagool and cried:
“Mother, old mother—we are here.”

“_Good! Good! Good!_” Gagool croaked. “Are your eyes keen, _Isanusis_—seers in dark places?”

“Mother, they are keen.”

“_Good! Good! Good!_ Are your ears open, _Isanusis_, who hear words not spoken?”

“Mother, they are open.”

“_Good! Good! Good!_ Are your senses awake? Can you smell blood? Can you purge the land of those who plot against the king? Are you ready to do the justice of ‘Heaven above’—you whom I have taught, who have eaten my bread and drunk my magic?”

“Mother, we can.”

“Then go! Tarry not, ye vultures. The slayers”—she gestured to the executioners—“sharpen their spears. The white men from afar hunger to see. _Go!_”

With a wild yell, the witch-finders scattered like shards from a shell, bones rattling, racing through the crowd.

We watched the nearest one. She danced wildly, spinning faster and faster, screaming:
“I smell him—the evil-doer!”
“He is near—he who poisoned his mother!”
“I hear the thoughts of him who plots against the king!”

Her frenzy grew—foam flew from her jaws, her eyes bulged, her flesh quivered. Then she froze—like a hunting dog scenting prey. With her wand outstretched, she crept forward, low and slow.

The warriors before her stiffened. Their stoicism cracked. They recoiled.

She crept closer—then suddenly lunged, touching a tall warrior with her wand.

Instantly, two comrades seized him—one arm each—and dragged him toward the king.

He didn’t resist. But his limbs dragged as if paralyzed. His fingers, limp as a corpse’s, let the spear fall.

Two executioners stepped forward. They met. The king looked on.

“_Kill!_” he barked.

“_Kill!_” squealed Gagool.

“_Kill!_” echoed Scragga, chuckling.

Before the words faded, the deed was done. One drove a spear into the man’s heart. The other smashed his skull with a club.

“_One_,” counted Twala—like a madwoman tallying lives.

The body was dragged aside.

Another victim came—this one wearing a leopard-skin cloak, a man of rank. Same ritual. Same fate.

“_Two_.”

And so it went—until a hundred bodies lay in rows behind us.

I’ve heard of Roman gladiator games, Spanish bullfights—but none could match the horror of this witch-hunt. At least those spectacles entertained. This was pure terror. Even the most hardened seeker of thrills would flee if he knew he might be next.

Once, we tried to protest. Twala silenced us.

“Let the law run its course, white men. These dogs are wizards and evildoers. It is well they die.”

Around ten-thirty, the hunt paused. The witch-finders gathered, exhausted. We thought it was over.

But no. Gagool rose—leaning on a stick—and staggered into the open space. The sight was grotesque: a bent, ancient hag, slowly gathering strength, until she darted about like her own pupils. She chanted, weaving through the crowd—then suddenly lunged at a tall man in front of a regiment.

A groan rose from his unit.

Officers seized him. He was brought forward.

He was slain. Twala counted: “One hundred and three.”

Then Gagool danced again—closer and closer to us.

“By the gods,” Good muttered, “I think she’s coming for us.”

“Nonsense,” Sir Henry said.

But when I saw that fiend advancing, my heart sank. I glanced at the rows of corpses. I shivered.

Closer she came—waltzing like a twisted stick, her eyes glowing with unholy fire.

Closer. Closer.

The entire assembly watched, breathless.

Then she stopped. Pointed.

“Which one?” Sir Henry muttered.

In a flash, she rushed forward and touched Umbopa—Ignosi—on the shoulder.

“I smell him!” she shrieked. “Kill him! Kill him—he is full of evil! Slay the stranger before blood flows from him! Kill him, O king!”

A pause.

I seized the moment.

“O king!” I shouted, rising. “This man is the servant of your guests. He is our dog. Whoever sheds his blood sheds ours. By the sacred law of hospitality, I claim protection for him!”

“Gagool has marked him,” Twala growled. “He must die.”

“Nay,” I replied. “He shall not die. Any who touch him shall die instead.”

“Seize him!” Twala roared.

The executioners advanced—eyes red with blood.

They hesitated.

Ignosi gripped his spear, ready to fight.

“Stand back, dogs!” I yelled. “If you want to see tomorrow’s sun, stay back. Touch one hair of his head—and your king dies.”

I leveled my revolver at Twala. Sir Henry and Good drew theirs—Sir Henry aimed at the lead executioner, Good at Gag。

Twala flinched as my barrel lined up with his chest.

“Well,” I said, “what is it to be, Twala?”

He spoke.

“Put away your magic tubes,” he said. “You have invoked hospitality. For that reason—and not from fear—I spare him. Go in peace.”

“It is well,” I said calmly. “We are weary of slaughter. May we sleep? Is the dance ended?”

“It is ended,” Twala said sulkily. “Let these dead dogs”—he gestured to the corpses—“be thrown to the hyenas and vultures.”

He raised his spear.

The regiments began to file out in perfect silence. A small party remained to drag away the dead.

The witch-hunt was over.
