As Tito stepped into the Rucellai Gardens' pavilion, the soft glow of candlelight danced across the faces of the assembled guests, casting a warm ambiance over the gathering. The carefully curated selection of company only reinforced his initial suspicion that the evening's purpose lay beyond mere socializing – though perhaps nothing more substantial than a strengthening of bonds forged through camaraderie. In the Renaissance era, a well-set table and fine wine were believed to heighten one's awareness of their convictions, and the ease of conversation that followed supper was thought to reveal even the most deeply held opinions. The Florentines, known for their sobriety and thriftiness, made exceptions when it came to hospitality, and the Rucellai family was renowned for their grandeur and love of fine living.

As Tito took his seat, Bernardo Rucellai greeted him with a hint of extra warmth, placing him between Lorenzo Tornabuoni and Giannozzo Pucci, two esteemed young members of the Medicean party. The air was filled with the sweet scent of fresh damask cloth from France, and the soft clinking of glasses against the gentle hum of conversation created a sense of effortless elegance.

The evening's conversation began with lighthearted remarks, as the company passed the scented water bowl, and rings sparkled on outstretched hands beneath the warm glow of wax candles. A question arose about what Dante Alighieri, the venerable poet, would think if he could return and witness the modern innovations at the table, such as silver forks. The assembly concurred that the customs of future generations would be far removed from those of their illustrious predecessor.

As the meal progressed, the discussion turned to the esteemed guests who had once gathered within these very gardens to pay homage to the ideas of Plato. The bust of the great philosopher, a relic from Lorenzo de' Medici's villa, stood watch over the proceedings, a reminder of the Platonic Academy's storied past. The company spoke of Pico della Mirandola, a brilliant and audacious young thinker, who had grown from a zealous enthusiast to a more measured scholar, his passion for self-perfection driving him to explore the mysteries of the universe. They recalled Marsilio Ficino, a devoted student who had been nurtured on the teachings of Plato, his mind perhaps slightly softened by the exclusivity of his intellectual diet.
