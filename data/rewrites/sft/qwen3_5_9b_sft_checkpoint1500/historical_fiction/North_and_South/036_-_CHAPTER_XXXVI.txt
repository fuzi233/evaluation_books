On the appointed day, Miss Hale and her father set forth to visit Nicholas Higgins and his family. The recent tragedy cast a pall over them both, a somber shadow revealed in their unusual attire and the deliberate nature of their outing, which marked the first time in weeks they had ventured out together without compulsion. Yet, as they approached Higgins's dwelling, a silent, profound sympathy drew them close.

Inside, the atmosphere was heavy with grief. Nicholas Higgins sat in his usual corner by the fireside, though the pipe that usually accompanied him was absent. Leaning heavily on his hand, his arm draped across his knee, he remained seated despite their arrival, though his eyes betrayed a warm, if sorrowful, welcome.

"Sit ye down, sit ye down. Fire's welly out," he muttered, poking the embers vigorously, seemingly attempting to distract attention from his own desolation. He appeared disheveled, a dark, unshaven beard framing a pallid face, clad in a jacket that cried out for repair.

"We thought we'd find you just after dinner-time," Margaret said gently.

"We have shared our sorrows since last seeing you," Mr. Hale replied.

"Ay, ay. Sorrows are more plentiful than dinners these days; I reckon my dinner hour stretches across the whole day. You're likely to find me whenever you seek."

"Are you out of work?" Margaret asked.

"Ay," he answered briefly. After a pause, he looked up, his expression grim. "I'm not lacking for brass. Dunno ye think it. Bess, poor lass, had a little stock under her pillow, ready to slip into my hand at the last moment, and Mary is cutting fustian. But I am out of work nonetheless."

"We owe Mary a debt," Mr. Hale interjected before Margaret could intervene.

"If she accepts it, I'll turn her out of doors!" Higgins snapped, his voice rising. "I'll stay within these four walls, and she must abide outside. That's all."

"But we wish to express our gratitude for her kind service," Mr. Hale persisted.

"I never thanked your daughter for her acts of love towards my poor wench. I never found the words. I must begin now if you start making ado about what little Mary could serve you."

"Is it because of the strike that you are unemployed?" Margaret asked softly.

"The strike is ended. 'Tis over for this time. I am out of work because I never asked for it. And I never asked because good words are scarce, and bad words are plentiful."

His mood was curiously sullen, offering answers that resembled riddles. Yet, Margaret sensed a desire within him for explanation.

"And what are good words?" she pressed.

"Asking for work. I reckon those are nearly the best words men can utter. 'Give me work' implies, 'And I'll do it like a man.' Those are good words."

"And bad words?"

"Ay. Bad words are refusing you work when you ask for it."

"Ay. Bad words are saying, 'Aha, my fine chap! You've been true to your orders, and I'll be true to mine. You did the best you could for those who wanted help; that's your way of being true to your kind: and I'll be true to mine. You've been a poor fool, knowing no better than to be a true, faithful fool. So go and be damned to you. There's no work for you here.' Those are bad words. I am no fool; and if I were, folks ought to have taught me how to be wise in their fashion. I could perhaps have learned, if anyone had tried to teach me."

"It would not be worthwhile," suggested Mr. Hale, "to ask your former master if he would take you back? It might be a poor chance, but it would be a chance."

Higgins lifted his gaze, fixing a sharp, searching look upon the questioner, before letting out a low, bitter titter.

"My master! If it's no offense, I'll ask you a question or two in return."

"You are quite welcome," Mr. Hale said.

"I reckon you earn your bread somehow. Folks seldom live in Milton merely for pleasure, if they can live elsewhere."

"You are quite right. I possess some independent property, but my intention in settling in Milton was to become a private tutor."

"To teach folk. Well! I reckon they pay you for teaching them, do they not?"

"Yes," Mr. Hale smiled. "I teach in order to receive payment."

"And those who pay you... do they dictate to you? Do they tell you what to do, or what not to do with the money given in fair exchange for your labor?"

"No; certainly not!"

"They do not say, 'You may have a brother, or a friend as dear as a brother, who requires this coin for a purpose both you and he deem right; but you must promise not to give it to him.' You may perceive a worthy use for your funds, yet if we disagree, they simply cease dealing with you?' They do not say that, do they?"

"No; certainly not!"

"Would you endure it if they did?"

"It would require some very hard pressure to induce me to submit to such dictation."

"There is no pressure on all the broad earth that would compel me," declared Nicholas Higgins, his voice gaining strength. "Now you have it. You've hit the bull's-eye. Hampers—that's where I labored—requires their men to pledge themselves they shall not contribute a single penny to aid the Union, nor assist in keeping turn-outs from collapsing. They may make pledges, yes, and break them," he continued with scorn, "but they merely create liars and hypocrites. To me, it is a lesser sin to harden men's hearts so that they refuse kindness to those in need, or to obstruct a righteous and just cause, however it opposes the strong hand. But I shall never forsake myself for all the work the King could offer. I am a member of the Union, and I believe it is the sole institution capable of doing any good for the workmen. I have been a turn-out, and I know the sting of dismissal. Therefore, if I secure a shilling, sixpence shall go to them if they ask for it. Consequently, I do not see where I am to obtain a shilling."

"Is that rule regarding non-contribution to the Union enforced at all the mills?" Margaret inquired.

"I cannot say. It is a new regulation at ours, and I reckon they will discover they cannot sustain it. But it is in force now. By-and-by they will find out: tyrants make liars."

A brief silence fell. Margaret hesitated, weighing whether to speak her mind, fearing she might irritate a man already so gloomy and despondent. Finally, the words escaped her, delivered in a soft tone, her manner reluctant, showing her unwillingness to inflict pain. It did not anger Higgins, but perplexed him.

"Do you recall poor Boucher saying the Union was a tyrant? I think he called it the worst tyrant of all. And I remember agreeing with him at the time."

For a long while, he remained silent. Resting his head on his hands, he stared into the fire, shielding his expression from view.

"I shall not deny that the Union finds it necessary to force a man into his own good. I shall speak truth. A man leads a dreary existence who remains outside the Union. But once inside, his interests are safeguarded better than he could manage for himself, or anyone else could. It is the only path by which working men secure their rights, through collective unity. The greater the number of members, the higher the chance for individual justice. The Government tends to fools and madmen; thus, if any man inclines to harm himself or his neighbor, the Union places a check upon him, whether he likes it or not. That is all we do in the Union. We cannot imprison folk, but we can render a man's life so burdensome that he is compelled to enter, becoming wise and helpful in spite of himself. Boucher was a fool throughout, and never a worse fool than at the end."

"He harmed you?" Margaret asked.

"Aye, that did he. We held public opinion on our side until he and his ilk began rioting and breaking laws. Then it was all over with the strike."

"Then would it not have been far better to leave him alone, rather than forcing him to join the Union? He did you no good; you drove him mad."

"Margaret," her father warned in a low, cautionary tone, sensing the storm gathering on Higgins's face.

"I like her," Higgins said suddenly, his demeanor shifting. "She speaks plainly what is in her mind. She does not comprehend the Union for all that. 'Tis a great power; 'tis our only power.
