As far as the chief business of his life—his writing—was concerned, Paul Oleron treated the world with a consideration it did not return. He had long since ceased to keep accounts of injury and recompense. To do so would not have changed the arithmetic, and it might have soured what was left of his temper. At forty-four, he had chosen his way in the years when generosity still looked like a kind of strength, and he had committed himself to it beyond any decent thought of retreat. If he had ever paused to cross-examine his own motives, he would still have answered—stubbornly, almost piously—that a life without some nobility in it was no life at all. Only of late, and only in odd flashes, had he suspected there might be a cost hidden in that answer—some day, perhaps, when his powers reached their crest and began their inevitable fall, he would be left with the question whether less exacting ideals might have bought him more ease. But to anticipate that day was useless.

For the present, his removal into the old house—its brickwork still tattooed here and there with the faint, stamped scars of long-ago insurance marks—had only interrupted *Romilly Bishop* at the fifteenth chapter.

He moved about his new rooms with a tall, lean exactitude, his ascetic face set in a kind of quiet fastidiousness that might, in another sex and another century, have been called spinsterish. Twenty years of lodgings and garrets had trained him to do most things for himself, and he had learned that method saves both time and irritation. He had arranged with the long-nosed Barrett’s wife—a stout Welshwoman with a high, almost piping voice, her Merionethshire accent surviving London as if it were a stubborn seed—to come each morning across the square to prepare his breakfast, and on Saturday to “turn the place out.” For the rest he found, not without surprise, that a little housework made a tolerable counterweight to the strain of writing: the mind, after all, does not float well in a room that smells of stale dust and old paint.

The kitchen, with the narrow strip of adjoining space where a modern bath had been fitted, looked down upon the alley at the side of the house. There, at one end, was a large closet with a door, and in the upper part of that door a square sliding hatch. Oleron stood before it longer than he meant to. The shape of it pricked some half-remembered image—then the use came to him, and his mouth softened into a faint smile that carried, inexplicably, a little tenderness. It had been a powder-closet, once; through that hatch an elaborately dressed head had been thrust to receive, with practiced resignation, the click and puff of the powder-pistol.

He would put it to a different purpose now. It would, he supposed, serve as his larder.

He opened it and rummaged. The back was shelved, the upper shelf running deep into the wall like a recess made for secrets. Pushing his hand into the shadowed reach, he drew out two mushroom-shaped wooden wig-stands, old, smooth, and oddly patient-looking under the grime. He could not imagine how they had come to be there. Perhaps the painters had found them and stuffed them away. But cupboard-space was scarce in his five rooms, and he had already been forced into little feats of ingenuity to house linen, boxes, and those accumulations of papers that were seldom used yet somehow could not be destroyed. He set the wig-stands aside with a brief shrug, as one notes a curiosity and postpones its meaning.

It was early spring when he took up the tenancy, and he was anxious to have *Romilly* ready for publication in the coming autumn. Even so, he did not intend to whip it into premature completion. If it demanded longer, then longer it must have. He knew—more sharply than he liked to admit—its importance in his development; it had to be allowed its own length and time. In the rooms he had just left he had made excellent progress; *Romilly* had begun, as people say, to speak and act of herself, the characters moving with their own weight. He had no doubt she would continue, once the fuss of removal quieted.

The fuss was nearly done. He told himself it was time to pull his mind into harness again. On a March morning he went out and returned with two great bunches of yellow daffodils—their cut stems bleeding cold sap, their trumpets smelling of green water and roadside earth. He set one bunch on the mantelpiece between the Sheffield candlesticks, and the other on the table directly before him, so that the sharp, clean scent rose into his face as he sat. Then he drew out the half-completed manuscript of *Romilly Bishop*.

But before he began he went to a small rosewood cabinet, took out his cheque-book and pass-book, and totted up his figures. His monk-like face grew thoughtful as the sum diminished under his pencil. Installation had cost more than he meant, and he had rather less than fifty pounds left, with no immediate prospect of more.

“Hm. I’d forgotten rugs and chintz curtains and so forth mounted up so,” he said aloud, as if speaking made the fact less humiliating. “But it would have been a pity to spoil the place for the want of ten pounds or so…. Well. *Romilly* simply *must* be out for the autumn. So here goes—”

He drew his papers toward him.

But he did not work. Or rather, his hand made the motions of work while his mind was tugged sideways by the world outside and, more subtly, by the house itself. The square had its own noises—frequent, various, newly intimate. Hawkers came first, the rattle of their carts and the practiced cries that cut through the air like knife-strokes. At midday, children returning from school poured into the square in a rush of voices and boots; they swung on his gate until the hinges complained, and their laughter ricocheted off the opposite brickwork. When they went back for afternoon school, an itinerant musician stationed himself under Oleron’s window and began to strum a mandolin.

The sound was not unpleasant—thin, metallic, but at least it was human. Oleron pushed up the sash and threw the man a penny. The coin rang faintly as it hit; the mandolin answered with a livelier phrase, and then settled back into its repetitive, commercial sweetness. Oleron returned to his table.

No. It was no good.

He came to himself at long intervals to find he had been staring about the room, not at his own furniture but at ghosts of other arrangements: a settee in buttercup satin, perhaps, or petunia; a glimmering crystal chandelier suspended from the centre moulding of the lofty ceiling; a tambour-frame in the light; a picquet-table near the window where someone’s rings might tap against a glass. He grew tired without having produced anything but a kind of mental dust.

Better, he decided, to be frankly idle than to wear himself out on futile effort. He would take a walk. Yet, chancing to sit for a moment, he fell asleep in his chair.

“This won’t do,” he yawned when he woke at half-past four, the late light slanting changedly across the floor. “I must do better than this tomorrow—”

He felt so luxuriously lazy that for a few minutes he even considered breaking an appointment he had for the evening, as if he could buy rest by a small act of disloyalty.

The next morning he sat down to work without allowing himself even the delay of answering his three letters—two tradesmen’s accounts and a note from Miss Bengough forwarded from his old address. It was a bright day of white and blue, a gay, noisy wind sweeping the square, and a subtle shift in the colour of growing things—buds tightening, grass darkening, the first hint of sap rising. Over and over, once or twice a minute, the room brightened suddenly and then subdued again as shining clouds rolled north-eastward. The soft, fitful illumination winked in the polished table-top and even in the worn old boards of the floor. The morning sounds had begun again—the cartwheels, the distant knocks, the small clink of milk bottles, the too-bright cries.

Oleron made a pattern of dots on the paper before him, then broke off to move the jar of daffodils precisely opposite the centre of a creamy panel, adjusting it as carefully as one might adjust a garnish to make the plate look balanced. He wrote a sentence that ran cleanly for a couple of lines; then it broke down into notes and jottings like a recipe abandoned halfway when the cook loses the thread of heat and timing. For a time he managed to persuade himself that the memoranda were work. Then he rose and began to pace.

As he moved, an idea struck him with the odd brightness of a practical solution. The place, he thought, might be improved by more positive colour. It was pale—mild and sweet as a kind old face—yet a little devitalised, even wan. It would bear a warmer note: richer flowers, perhaps; and some gay stuff for cushions on the window-seats, something that would not look frightened of light.

“Of course I really can’t afford it,” he muttered, and fetched a two-foot rule to measure the width of the recesses, as if numbers would discipline the desire.

He stooped to measure. Then his whole attitude changed. He paused, peering; his fingers went to the painted wood with a new delicacy. Presently he straightened, rubbing his hands together with gentle glee.

“Oho, oho!” he said. “These look to me very much like window-boxes, nailed up. We must look into this! Yes—those are boxes, or I’m… oho, this is an adventure!”

On that wall of his sitting-room were two windows; the third sat in another corner, and beyond the open bedroom door on the same wall was yet another. The seats of all had been painted, repainted, and painted again until the wood seemed sealed under a skin of history. His investigating finger had barely found the old nailheads beneath the layers. Under the ledge, an old keyhole had been puttied over.

He took out his penknife and worked carefully for five minutes, prising, scoring, lifting paint in neat curls. Then he went into the kitchen for a hammer and chisel. Driving the chisel cautiously under the seat, he started the lid slightly. He used the penknife again, cutting along the hinged edge, outward along the ends. Then he fetched a wedge and a wooden mallet.

“Now for our little mystery—” he said.

The first blows fell.

In that sweet, pale apartment the sound seemed brutal—almost indecent. The panelling rang and rattled and vibrated like the body of some large instrument struck in the wrong place. The house answered from within itself. From cellarage to garrets a flock of echoes woke and ran, overlapping, multiplying, as if every hollow space had been waiting to be made vocal. The noise got on his nerves in a way that was not simply annoyance; it was as if the vibration made his teeth and thoughts buzz together.

All at once he stopped, fetched a duster, and muffled the mallet, ashamed—absurdly—of the violence of his own hands.

When the edge was raised enough, he slid his fingers under and lifted. The paint flaked and starred; rusty nails squeaked and grunted like reluctant mouths; the lid came up and laid open the box beneath.

Oleron looked in.

It was empty, except for a couple of inches of scurf and mould and old cobwebs—matter that had once been soft and now looked like the desiccated remains of softness. No letters, no heirloom, no hoard.

“No treasure there,” he said, amused with himself for having half expected one. “*Romilly* will still have to be out by the autumn. Let’s have a look at the others.”

He turned to the second window. The raising of the remaining seats occupied him until well into the afternoon, his hands cramped with careful leverage, his ears still prickling with the remembered resonance of the house. The bedroom seat, like the first, was empty. But from the second seat in his sitting-room he drew out something yielding and folded, furred over with an inch of dust. It came away reluctantly, as if it had been pressed there for safety and did not like the light.

He carried it into the kitchen and swept it over a bucket; a small cloud of dry powder rose, smelling of old plaster, cold wood, and a faint, stale sweetness like cloth stored too long with dried flowers. Then he took a duster and began to uncover it.

It was some sort of large bag made of an ancient, frieze-like material. When he unfolded it, it occupied most of the little kitchen floor, its breadth forcing him to step back against the sink. In shape it was an irregular—very irregular—triangle, with a couple of wide flaps and the remains of straps and buckles. The patch that had been uppermost in the folding was faded to a yellowish brown, as if sun had worried it for years; the rest was in shades of crimson that shifted according to how long each portion had been exposed, like meat darkening where air has reached it.

“Now whatever can that have been?” Oleron mused, standing over it as if over an unknown cut of something once familiar. “I give it up. Whatever it is, it’s settled my work for today, I’m afraid—”

He folded it up carelessly and thrust it into a corner of the kitchen, where it slumped like a thing too tired to hold its shape. Then, taking pans and brushes and an old knife, he returned to the sitting-room and began to scrape and wash and line with paper his newly discovered receptacles. The work steadied him. The wet rag made a small, controlled sound on the wood; the paper crackled as it smoothed down; the house’s earlier ringing seemed to withdraw, listening with sullen patience.

When he had finished, he stowed spare boots and books and papers into the boxes and closed the lids again. He was amused by his little adventure—yet not quite at ease. The day had been eaten up by the house, and he was anxious, more anxious than he admitted, for the hour to come when he should sit down and *Romilly* would speak again as she had spoken before, without the square’s cries, without the house’s answering echoes, without these concealed spaces that seemed made to keep what could not be easily named.
