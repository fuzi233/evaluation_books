The adrenaline of our escape from the Place of Death had barely subsided when the grim reality of our surroundings gave way to a new, more insidious horror. While we stood transfixed by the grisly tableau of the dead, Gagool had already resumed her frantic activity. With a agility that belied her age, she scaled the massive central table, descending upon the corpse of Twala beneath the dripping stalactite. Her intent remained obscure—perhaps a macabre attempt to "pickle" the remains, or some darker ritualistic necessity—but her movements were swift and deliberate. After leaning forward to press her lips against his frozen mouth in a grotesque parody of affection, she shuffled backward, pausing intermittently to mutter unintelligible phrases directed at the shadowed figures surrounding us, treating them with the casual familiarity of an old neighbor. Once this bizarre ceremony concluded, she settled directly beneath the looming presence of the White Death, commencing a series of fervent, silent supplications. The sight of such a vile entity prostrating itself before the ultimate adversary of humanity sent a fresh chill through our veins, compelling us to abandon our stunned observation and move with renewed urgency.

"Now, Gagool," I whispered, my voice barely audible in the oppressive silence, "guide us to the chamber."

The witch descended the table instantly. "Are my lords not afraid?" she sneered, lifting her face to meet my gaze with a leer that promised malice.

"Lead on," I commanded, steeling myself against the dread coiling in my gut.

"Good, my lords," she replied, turning to circle behind the great effigy of death. "Here lies the chamber. Light the lamp, and enter." She deposited a gourd filled with oil onto the cavern floor and leaned casually against the cave wall. I struck a match from our dwindling supply, igniting a rush-wick, only to find that the path ahead was blocked by sheer, unbroken rock. Gagool erupted in a burst of manic laughter. "The way is there, my lords. *Ha! ha! ha!*!"

"Do not mock us," I snapped, my tone sharpening with frustration.

"I jest not, my lords. Behold!" She gestured emphatically at the solid wall.

As she spoke, raising the newly lit lamp, a miracle unfolded before our eyes. A colossal slab of stone, roughly ten feet high and five feet thick—weighing an estimated twenty to thirty tons—began to rise silently from the floor, sliding upward into a concealed cavity within the ceiling. The mechanism was deceptively simple, likely employing a system of counter-weights akin to a modern sash window. Though we witnessed neither the trigger nor the lever that initiated this movement, the physics were clear: a minimal pressure applied to a hidden point shifted the balance, effortlessly lifting the monolith. Slowly, inexorably, the barrier ascended until it vanished completely, revealing a yawning darkness where the door had stood.

Our hearts hammered against our ribs as the entrance to Solomon's Treasure Chamber finally swung open. Doubt gnawed at me; was this a cruel hoax, or did Da Silvestra's warnings hold truth? Vast hoards awaited us, capable of making us the wealthiest men on Earth, or was this the final trap? We would soon know.

"Enter, white men from the Stars," Gagoo intoned, stepping into the threshold. "But first, listen to your servant, Gagool the aged. The luminous stones you seek were excavated from the pit guarded by the Silent Ones and stored here, though by whom and when I cannot recall, for it predates my memory. Yet, this sanctum has been breached only once since the original depositors fled in terror, abandoning their riches. The legend of the treasure spread through the ages, yet the location and the secret of the door remained lost to all. Until a white man arrived from beyond the mountains—perhaps one who truly hailed 'from the Stars'—and was welcomed by the reigning king. It was he who sits yonder," she indicated the fifth figure at the table of the dead, "who, accompanied by a local woman, journeyed to this place. By chance, the woman discovered the secret of the door—a mystery that would elude a thousand years of searching. Together, they entered, finding the stones and filling the skin of a small goat, brought by the woman for provisions, with the precious gems. As the white man departed, clutching one final, large stone in his hand..."

She trailed off, letting the narrative hang in the stale air.

"Well," I breathed, my curiosity overriding my caution, "what befell Da Silvestra?"

The mention of the name startled the hag. "How dost thou know the name of the deceased?" she demanded sharply, before continuing without awaiting a reply. "No mortal can recount the end. Fear seized the white man; he cast aside the goat-skin laden with stones and fled, retaining only the single stone. The king claimed it—that very stone which thou, Macumazahn, removed from Twala's brow."

"Has no one entered since?" I pressed, peering into the abyss.

"No, my lords. Only the secret of the door has been preserved. Every king has opened it, yet none has dared to cross the threshold. There is a prophecy: those who enter shall perish within a lunar cycle, just as the white man died in the cave upon the mountain where ye found him, Macumazahn. Therefore, the kings remain outside. *Ha! ha!* My words are true."

Our gazes locked, and a wave of nausea washed over me. How could this crone possess such knowledge?

"Enter, my lords," she urged, her voice dropping to a sinister purr. "If I speak truth, the goat-skin lies upon the floor; if death awaits those who enter, ye shall discover that later. *Ha! ha! ha!*!" She shuffled through the doorway, carrying the light, though I hesitated, paralyzed by the weight of her warning.

"Oh, confound it all!" Good declared, breaking the spell. "Here we go. I shall not be cowed by that ancient devil." Followed closely by Foulata—who visibly trembled, her courage failing her—he plunged into the tunnel. We followed suit, driven by a mixture of greed and desperation.

A short distance down the narrow, rock-hewn passage, Gagool halted, holding the lamp aloft. "Behold, my lords," she announced, illuminating the gloom. "Those who cached the treasure fled in haste, attempting to seal the passage but lacking the time." She indicated large square blocks of stone, stacked two courses high, forming a makeshift barricade. Alongside them lay additional blocks, a pile of mortar, and two trowels. These tools, identical in shape and manufacture to those used by laborers today, confirmed that the sealing effort was recent, perhaps even contemporary.

Foulata, overcome by panic and exhaustion, collapsed against the unfinished wall, claiming she could proceed no further. We lowered her beside the basket of provisions and retreated briefly to allow her to compose herself.

Pressing onward for approximately fifteen paces, we emerged abruptly before an elaborately painted wooden door, standing ajar. Whoever had preceded us had either forgotten to close it or lacked the time.

"Hee! hee! white men," Gagool cackled as the lamplight caught the object. "Did I not foretell that the white man who ventured here would flee in terror, dropping the woman's burden? Behold it! Gaze within; ye shall find a water-gourd nestled among the stones."

Good stooped, lifting the sack. It was impossibly heavy, jingling with the metallic clatter of countless hard objects.

"By Jove! I believe it is full of diamonds," he whispered, his voice thick with awe. The mere concept of a small goatskin stuffed with diamonds was enough to send shivers down anyone's spine.

"Move along," Sir Henry urged impatiently. "Old lady, give me the lamp." Taking the light from her grasp, he thrust it high above his head and stepped through the doorway. We pressed in behind him, momentarily forgetting the bag of potential fortune, and found ourselves inside King Solomon's treasure chamber.

Initially, the dim illumination revealed only a room carved directly from the living rock, seemingly no larger than ten feet square. Then, as our eyes adjusted, a breathtaking array of elephant tusks came into view, stacked one atop another until they nearly touched the arched ceiling. Hundreds, perhaps five hundred, of the finest ivory stretched back into the darkness, a testament to unimaginable wealth. Alone, this stockpile would secure a lifetime of prosperity for any man. I recalled the biblical description of Solomon's throne of ivory, wondering if this very cache had supplied the raw materials for such splendor.

Opposite us stood roughly twenty wooden crates, resembling oversized Martini-Henry ammunition boxes, painted a vivid, blood-red.

"There are the diamonds!" I shouted, signaling for the light.

Sir Henry complied, bringing the flame close to the topmost crate. Its lid, rotted by time even in this arid environment, had been shattered inward, likely by Da Silvestra's desperate entry. Pushing my hand through the jagged hole, I withdrew it filled not with stones, but with gold coins of a shape and design unknown to us, stamped with what appeared to be Hebrew characters.
