To truly grasp the architecture of Colonel Trench's confinement, one must first confront the absolute sterility of his universe. For three days, the weight of their shared sentence pressed upon them like a physical mass, sealing their lips in a pact of silent endurance. Yet, the dawn broke not with liberation, but with betrayal; Trench woke convulsed by a fever that seized him with terrifying speed. By afternoon, the man who had once been the anchor of Henry Feversham's sanity was reduced to a trembling child, his mind clouded, his strength evaporating. The thousand nights spent within the House of Stone had finally completed their slow, insidious work. It was no accident that this collapse occurred precisely as the iron key turned in the lock of his prison cell. The sheer, overwhelming surge of anticipated freedom—the crushing gravity of survival—had shattered his exhausted frame.

"In a few days he will be well," Feversham insisted, his voice a shield against the encroaching dread. "It is nothing."

"It is *Umm Sabbah*," corrected Ibrahim, shaking his head with the grim certainty of a man who had seen too many souls extinguished in that infected hellscape. He spoke of the typhus that swept through the gaol, claiming victims on the seventh day.

Feversham refused to accept the verdict, clinging to denial with a passionate, stubborn obstinacy. Yet, beneath his refusal, a new anxiety gnawed at him: *Will the men with the camels wait?* Day after day, he ventured to the Nile, only to see Abou Fatma standing motionless in his blue robe, a sentinel of hope. The man made his sign—a silent promise—and Feversham, paralyzed by illness, offered none in return. Meanwhile, alongside Ibrahim, he tended to Trench. The boy arrived daily with provisions, venturing out to procure tamarinds, dates, and bitter roots from which Ibrahim brewed cooling draughts. Together, they dragged Trench from shade to shade as the sun scorched the zareeba. Assistance trickled in for the starving family of Idris, and the crushing forty-pound chains were finally removed. Vegetable marrow soaked in brine filled Trench's stomach; butter packed his mouth; his body was anointed and swaddled tightly in coarse camel-cloths.

On the seventh day, Ibrahim delivered the final blow. "This is the last," he whispered. "Tonight he will die."

"No," Feversham retorted, his voice rising in defiance. "That is impossible. 'In his own parish,' he said, 'beneath the trees he knew.' Not here, no." He spoke with the fervor of a man fighting a god. The image of the man in the blue robe outside the walls faded; the calculus of escape vanished. Now, only the terror remained—that the third feather would never reach Ethne, that she would never voluntarily reclaim the fourth. Even the grand horizon of "the afterwards" was erased from his mind. All that existed was Trench, and the few, halting words exchanged in the corner of the zareeba on their first night under the vast, indifferent sky. "No," Feversham repeated, his resolve steel-hardening. "He must not die here." Through that agonizing day and night, he waged a long, hard battle beside the dying man, watching the tug-of-war between life and death. At moments, the three years of starvation seemed destined to claim victory; at others, Trench's wiry constitution fought back with surprising ferocity.

For that night, they won. The struggle prolonged itself, defying the natural order. The dangerous seventh day passed. Hope flickered in Ibrahim's eyes. On the thirteenth day, Trench slept without the delirious rambling of madness. When he awoke, his mind was clear. He found himself alone, swathed in camel-cloths so thick he could not stir, lying in the cooling shadows of the House of Stone. The heat of the day had broken, leaving the sand black and still. He had no desire to move, drifting in a haze of wonder at the duration of his sickness. Then, the sounds returned: the shouts of the gaolers, the cries of prisoners near the river. The gate swung open, and the flock surged inside. Feversham was among them, walking straight to Trench's corner.

"Thank God!" he cried, collapsing beside his friend in exhaustion. "I would not have left you, but I was compelled. We have been unloading boats all day."

"How long have I lain ill?" Trench asked, his voice raspy.

"Thirteen days."

"It will be a month before I can travel," Trench said, his eyes darting toward the exit. "You must go, Feversham. Leave me here while you still can. Perhaps when you reach Assouan, you can arrange something for me. I cannot move now. Will you go tomorrow?"

"No," Feversham answered firmly. "I should not go without you in any case. As it stands, it is too late."

"Too late?" Trench repeated, the words tasting strange on his tongue. He absorbed their meaning slowly, reluctant to be disturbed even by the sound of them; he wished simply to lie idle in the cool of the sunset. But gradually, the implication forced its way into his consciousness.

"Too late? Then the man in the blue gown has gone?"

"Yes," Feversham confirmed. "He spoke to me yesterday by the river. The camel drivers would wait no longer. Fearful of detection, they intended to return regardless of whether we accompanied them."

"You should have gone with them," Trench murmured. For himself, he cared little whether he rotted in the prison for a lifetime, provided he could lie quietly where he was. Without a hint of despair, he added, "So our one chance is lost."

"No," Feversham replied, his tone steady. "Deferred." He reached into his robes and produced a folded piece of paper, a pen, and a lump of wood-soot mixed with water. "The man in the blue gown dropped these by my side as I lay on the ground. I hid them beneath my *jibbeh*. Last night, under the moonlight, I wrote to a Greek merchant who runs a café at Wadi Halfa. I handed him the letter this afternoon. He has departed. He will deliver it and collect payment. In six months, or at the very latest a year, he will be back in Omdurman."

"Very likely," Trench said skeptically. "He will demand another letter for more money, promising to return in six months or a year. I know these people."

"You do not know Abou Fatma," Feversham countered. "He served Gordon before Khartum fell; he has been mine since. He came with me to Obak, waiting while I descended to Berber. He risked his very life to reach Omdurman. Within six months, he will surely be back."

Trench ceased his argument. His gaze wandered the enclosure until it settled on a fresh mound of turned earth in a corner. "What are they digging?" he asked.

"A well," Feversham answered.

"A well?" Trench fretted. "And so close to the Nile! Why? What is the purpose?"

"I don't know," Feversham admitted, though a cold dread coiled in his gut. He suspected the truth: the well was being dug to trap them forever. He held back his revelation, unwilling to burden his companion with the crushing weight of certainty before he was strong enough to bear it. But within days, his suspicion crystallized into fact. It was publicly announced that a high wall would encircle the House of Stone. Too many prisoners had escaped in their fetters along the riverbank; henceforth, they were to remain imprisoned from year's beginning to year's end. The prisoners themselves constructed the barrier from sun-dried mud-bricks. Feversham took his share of the labor, and Trench, as soon as he could stand, joined him.

"Here goes our last hope," Trench muttered. Though Feversham did not openly agree, his own heart began to surrender. Brick upon brick, mortar binding them together, they raised the wall. Each day it grew a foot higher. With their own hands, they sealed their fate. Twelve feet high, smooth and formidable, the wall stood complete. There was no ledge, no projection upon its surface to offer a foothold; it could not be breached in a single night. Trench and Feversham stared at it in despair. The vibrant palm trees of Khartum were now invisible, hidden behind the gray expanse. Their world shrank to a square of bright blue by day, dark blue by night, jeweled with distant points of silver and gold. Trench covered his face with his hands.

"I dare not look at it," he whispered, his voice breaking. "We have been building our own coffin, Feversham. That is the truth." Then, casting up his arms, he screamed into the silence: "Will they never ascend the Nile, the gunboats and the soldiers? Have they forgotten us in England? Good God! Have they forgotten us?"

"Hush!" Feversham commanded softly. "We shall find a way of escape, never fear. We must wait six months. Well, we have both waited years already. Six months—what are they?"

Though he spoke stoutly for his comrade's sake, Feversham's own heart sank within his chest.
