“It will hardly be denied that even in this frail and corrupted world, we sometimes meet persons who, in their very mien and aspect, as well as in the whole habit of life, manifest such a signature and stamp of virtue, as to make our judgment of them a matter of intuition rather than the result of continued examination.”—ALEXANDER KNOX: quoted in Southey’s Life of Wesley.

Mirah said she had slept well that night. When she came down wearing Mab’s black dress, her dark hair curling softly as it dried from its long bath, she looked like someone slowly emerging from sorrow into quiet comfort. The pale cheeks and shadows beneath her eyes still spoke of recent grief, but now there was a new light in her presence—gentle, steady, as though hope had begun to settle within her.

It was Mab who brought her breakfast and led her downstairs, proud of the tiny felt slippers she’d rushed out to buy—there were no shoes small enough for Mirah’s feet. The borrowed dress ended just above her ankles, revealing plain, humble footwear that clung close to her feet like tender buds wrapped in their own casing. To Mab, they were treasures.

“Oh, if you please, mamma?” she exclaimed, clasping her hands and bending toward Mirah’s feet as they entered the parlor. “Look at the slippers—how beautifully they fit! I declare, she looks just like Queen Budoor—‘two delicate feet, the work of the protecting and all-recompensing Creator, support her; and I wonder how they can sustain what is above them.’”

Mirah glanced down at her feet with innocent curiosity, then smiled up at Mrs. Meyrick, who stood silently, her heart stirred. *One could hardly imagine this creature harboring an evil thought,* she reflected. *But wise people would warn me to be cautious.* Still, she returned Mirah’s smile warmly. “I fear those little feet have carried more than their share lately,” she said gently. “But today, they may rest—and you’ll be my companion.”

“And she’ll tell you so many things, and I won’t hear a word,” Mab grumbled, already missing the unfolding story like a child left behind at the start of a fairy tale. She had duties to attend to—pupils to teach, lessons to prepare.

Kate had already gone sketching by the river, and Amy was out on errands. It was precisely as Mrs. Meyrick had wished—to be alone with this quiet stranger whose story must be sorrowful, yet one that needed to be told.

The small front parlor glowed that morning like a sanctuary. Sunlight spilled across the river and drifted through the open window, filling the room with warmth and softness. On the walls hung silent witnesses in black-and-white prints—the Virgin ascending among cherubs, Melancholia with her vast, brooding sky, prophets and sibyls, the School of Athens, the Last Supper, mystic gatherings where time dissolved into unity, solemn portraits by Holbein and Rembrandt, the Tragic Muse, children of past centuries lost in thought or play, Italian poets—each image speaking without sound.

Mrs. Meyrick, neat and composed after years of hardship, sat sorting colored wools for embroidery. Hafiz purred contentedly on the windowsill. The mantel clock ticked steadily, unhurried. Outside, the occasional rumble of wheels seemed distant, almost forgotten, beneath the calm that settled over the room.

Mrs. Meyrick believed this quiet might be the best invitation to speech. So she stayed silent, letting the peace speak for her.

Mirah sat opposite, hands folded in her lap, ankles crossed, eyes at first wandering slowly around the room—taking in every detail—then resting quietly upon Mrs. Meyrick, as though seeing not just a woman, but a vessel of kindness and safety. At last, she began to speak, her voice low and tender.

“I remember my mother’s face better than anything else. Yet I was not seven when I was taken from her. Now I am nineteen.”

“I can understand that,” Mrs. Meyrick murmured. “Some earliest memories last the longest.”

“Oh, yes—it was the earliest thing. I think my life truly began when I woke and saw her face. It was so near to me, her arms around me, and she sang to me. One hymn she sang again and again—so often that I learned it by heart. It was the first song I ever knew. They were always Hebrew hymns, and though I didn’t understand the words, they felt full of love and joy—just ours. When I lay in my little bed, everything white above me, she would lean over me, between me and the ceiling, and sing in a sweet, low voice. I can dream myself back into that time, even when I’m awake. Sometimes in my dreams, my hand—so small—is raised to her face, and she kisses it. Then I begin to tremble, thinking we are both dead. But I wake, and my hand lies here—still, and I don’t know who I am for a moment. But if I could see her again, I would know her instantly.”

“You must expect some change after twelve years,” Mrs. Meyrick said gently. “See my grey hair—ten years ago it was bright brown. Time moves over us like restless birds, leaving footprints forward and backward. Especially when they’re heavy-hearted birds—they tread hard.”

“Ah, I know her heart has been heavy, longing for me. But to feel her joy if we could meet again—if I could show her I love her, give her comfort after all her mourning—that would be worth every trial. If that could happen, I would gladly bear all I’ve suffered. I did despair once. The world seemed cruel and unjust. No one helped me. I couldn’t bear their looks or words. I thought my mother was dead, and death was the only way back to her. But then—yesterday, when I stood by the water, wishing it would close over me, thinking death was the kindest mercy—something changed. Goodness came alive to me. I felt trust in life again. And strangely, I began to believe she might still live. And now—here, with you, this morning—peace has flooded into me. I want nothing. I can wait. Because I hope. I believe. And I am so grateful—oh, so deeply grateful. You haven’t judged me. You haven’t despised me.”

Mirah spoke with quiet fervor, sitting perfectly still, like a painting come to life.

“Many others would feel as we do, my dear,” Mrs. Meyrick whispered, mist rising in her eyes as she stared at her embroidery.

“But I never met them. They never came to me.”

“How did you come to be taken from your mother?”

Mirah paused, then sighed. “Ah, it takes me a long while to reach that. It’s terrible to speak of—but I must. My father—he took me away. I thought we were going on a short journey. I was pleased. There was a box with all my little things inside. But we boarded a ship, and sailed farther and farther from land. Then I became ill—my first real misery, and it seemed endless. But finally, we landed. I knew nothing. I believed what my father said. He comforted me, promised I’d return to my mother. But we were in America, and it was years before we came back to Europe. At first, I kept asking when we’d go home. I tried to learn writing quickly so I could write to her. But one day, when he found me writing a letter, he took me on his knee and told me my mother and brother were dead. That was why we couldn’t return.”

She remembered her brother vaguely—he had once carried her, but he wasn’t always at home. She believed her father. She saw them buried under the earth, their eyes forever closed. She never doubted it. For a long time, she wept every night. But then, she began to dream of her mother often. In those dreams, she felt her presence—alive, near, though unseen. That comforted her. She wasn’t afraid in the dark anymore. Often during the day, she would shut her eyes, bury her face, and try to see her, hear her singing. Eventually, she could do it without closing her eyes.

Mirah paused, her face softened by memory, gazing out toward the river as if she saw her mother there.

“Still, your father was kind to you, I hope?” Mrs. Meyrick asked, needing to draw her back.

“No—he petted me, taught me carefully. He was an actor—I later learned that ‘Coburg’ was a theatre. But he did more than act. He’d been a teacher, knew many languages. His acting wasn’t strong, but he managed the stage, wrote and translated plays. An Italian singer lived with us for years—Signora. She and my father taught me. I had a tutor too, who made me memorize and recite. I worked hard, even as a child. I was nine when I first stepped on stage. I learned easily and wasn’t afraid. But from that moment on, I hated our life. My father had money, and we lived in disorderly finery. Always men and women came and went—loud laughter, arguments, strutting, snapping fingers, jeering faces—many petted me, but I disliked them all. I remembered my mother. Even then, when I understood little, I pulled away from all that noise, retreating into thoughts unlike theirs. I read constantly—plays, poetry, Shakespeare, Schiller—learning about good and evil. My father thought I might become a great singer. My voice was considered extraordinary for a child. He gave me the best teachers. But it hurt that he boasted of me, making me sing at any moment—as if I were a musical box.”

Once, at nine, she played a girl abandoned without knowing it, singing while playing with flowers. She performed effortlessly, but the applause, the chaos of the theatre, repelled her. She didn’t enjoy the praise—it felt cold, unloving. She missed the warmth of her early life, the trust she’d been born into.

She built a world within herself—different from everything around her. She chose beauty from the plays, shaped her own inner life from it. But it was like a knife cutting her—a constant clash between two lives: women gentle and noble on stage, saying kind words as if they meant them, then stepping off to behave crudely. My father noticed my withdrawal. Once, after rehearsal, Signora said, “She’ll never be an artist. She has no idea of being anyone but herself. That’s fine now, but soon you’ll see—she’ll have no more expression than a singing bird.” My father grew angry, and they quarreled. I sat alone and wept—because her words revealed a future I feared. I didn’t want to be an artist, but that was what he expected. Afterward, Signora left. A governess came to teach me other subjects, because my father feared I’d sing too much. But I still acted occasionally.

Rebellion grew stronger. I wanted to escape—but I didn’t know where to go, nor did I dare. I feared the world. And I dreaded leaving my father. I feared doing wrong. I thought if I turned wicked, I’d lose the world of happy thoughts where my mother lived. That belief held me through all those years. Oh, how long they were!”

Mirah fell into silence again.

“Did you have no teaching about duty?” Mrs. Meyrick asked, hesitating to say “religion”—uncertain how Jewish faith might have evolved.

“No—only that I should do what my father wished. He didn’t follow our religion in New York. I think he didn’t want me to know much about it. But because my mother used to take me to the synagogue, and I remembered sitting on her knee, looking through the railing, hearing the chanting and songs—I longed to go. Once, when I was very young, I slipped away and tried to find it. I got lost for hours until a peddler found me and took me home. My father, frantic, was furious. I was so terrified I didn’t dare go out again for a long time. But after Signora left, we moved into rooms where our landlady was a Jewess who practiced her faith. I asked her to take me to the synagogue. I read her prayer books and Bible. When I had money, I bought my own books. These texts felt like a deeper connection to my mother—I knew she had read these same words, spoken them aloud. That way, I came to understand a little of our religion, our history. I pieced together what I read in plays and stories about Jews and Jewish women—because I was sure my mother followed her faith. I stopped asking my father about her. It’s dreadful to say, but I began to doubt him. I realized he didn’t always tell the truth, made promises he didn’t keep. That made me suspect my mother and brother were still alive. As I grew older, I saw clearly that my mother had been deceived—she expected us back soon. My father’s words on my knee, telling me they were dead—now it seemed like a performance, meant to calm me. The cruelty of that lie sank deep. I came to hate falsehood because of it.”

She had written to her mother secretly—knowing the street, Colman Street, where they’d lived. She knew their name was Cohen, though my father called us Lapidoth, claiming it was a name from his Polish ancestors. She sent the letter in secret—but no reply came. I thought there was no hope.

Our life in America didn’t last long. Suddenly, my father announced we were packing for Hamburg. I was glad. I hoped for different people. I spoke German well—knew many plays by heart. My father spoke it better than English. I was thirteen then, and I felt old—knowing so much, yet so little. I often wished I’d drowned on the boat away from my mother. But I resolved to obey and endure. What else could I do?

One day, on our voyage, a new thought struck me. I wasn’t very ill, and I spent much time on deck. My father performed and joked to entertain passengers. I heard remarks about him. One man said, “Oh, he’s one of those clever Jews—a rascal, I shouldn’t wonder. There’s no race like them for cunning in men and beauty in women. I wonder what market he means for that daughter.”

When I heard that, it dawned on me: my suffering came from being a Jewess. The world would always look down on me because of my name. I would be judged by that. But it comforted me to believe my pain was part of a greater affliction—my share in the ancient song of mourning that has echoed through ages. For if many of our people were wicked and revelled in sin—was that not part of the burden borne by the just among them? Those who were despised for the sins of others?

“But you have not rejected me,” Mirah said softly, suddenly aware of her gratitude instead of sorrow.

“And we will try to save you from being judged unfairly, my poor child,” Mrs. Meyrick replied, having long since put down her work. She sat with folded hands, listening intently—her face as eager as Mab’s might have been. “Go on. Tell me all.”

After that, we lived in various towns—Hamburg, Vienna, mostly. I began studying singing again. My father earned money through theatres. I think he brought a fortune from America—I never knew why we left. For a time, he was excited about my voice. He made me rehearse constantly, preparing me for opera. But gradually, my voice failed to strengthen. My master in Vienna said, “Don’t strain it further. It won’t serve the public. It’s gold, but a thread of gold dust.” My father was crushed. We weren’t as well off.

I hadn’t fully told you about my feelings toward him. I knew he loved me, wanted to indulge me. That made me afraid of hurting him. But he never understood what would bring me joy. He took everything lightly. I stopped asking him about things I cared for, because he’d brush them aside with jokes. He even mocked our people. Once, after imitating their prayers just to make others laugh, I couldn’t hold back. I’d always carried anger about my mother. Alone, I said, “Father, you shouldn’t mock our people before Christians who already scorn them. Would it be right if I mocked you, so they could ridicule you?” He shrugged, laughed, pinched my chin. “You couldn’t do it, my dear.”

That dismissiveness created a wall between us. Whatever I felt most, I hid from him. Some things—when ridiculed—I couldn’t bear. The world seemed like hell. Is life only a farce, a vaudeville? Why then are there tragedies and grand operas, where men choose to suffer, do difficult things? To call all of it a joke feels silly. I saw that his desire for me to sing great music was only about profit—what would fetch the highest price. That limited my gratitude for his affection. The deepest feeling I had for him was pity. Yes, I pitied him. He aged, changed. He wasn’t lively anymore. I thought he grew worse—less kind to others than to me. Occasionally, his cheer vanished suddenly. He’d sit silently at home, gloomy. Or come in, throw himself down, and cry—as I have done in sorrow. If I touched his knee and asked, “What’s wrong, father?” he wouldn’t answer. Instead, he’d pull me close, wrap his arm around me, and weep. No real confidence passed between us. But oh, I was sorry for him. At those moments, I knew he felt his life was bitter. I pressed my cheek against his head and prayed. Those moments bound me to him most tightly. I often thought of how much my mother once loved him—else she would not have married him.

But then came the terrible time. We had been in Pesth and returned to Vienna. Despite my master Leo’s warning, my father secured me an engagement—not at the opera, but at a suburban theatre. He had nothing to do with the theatre then—I didn’t understand what he did, but I think he spent much time at a gambling house. Though he was careful never to take me there.

I was miserable. The plays I performed were hateful. Men approached me, wanting to talk. Women and men watched me with sneering smiles. It was like a fiery furnace. Perhaps I exaggerate—you don’t know that world. But the glare, the faces, the need to perform what I despised, then see people staring behind the scenes—it was worse than when I was a child. I endured it. I did it. I had decided to obey my father, to work—for I saw no better choice. But I felt my voice weakening. I knew my acting was only good when it wasn’t acting at all—when the role allowed me to be myself, and some inner feeling carried me. That happened rarely.
