By six o’clock on the following evening, the acoustic landscape of Roville-sur-Mer had fractured into a discordant symphony of industrial clatter, entirely drowning out the rhythmic, ancestral murmurs of the tide. Sally, whose psychological equilibrium relied on the harmonious integration of her sonic environment, found the Casino Municipale to be a profound assault on the senses. The mechanical whir of the roulette wheel and the hollow, commercial clinking of plastic counters felt like a deliberate erasure of the region’s organic rhythms. Yet, within this cathedral of superficial commerce, she sought Ginger Kemp. She was departing on the seven-fifteen express for Paris, moving forward in her own cyclical migration toward Cherbourg and ultimately New York, but she could not leave without intertwining her timeline with his one last time. She felt a maternal, almost mythic compulsion to impart a final, domestic grounding to a man who seemed entirely adrift.

She located his flaming hair amidst the breathless, sterile silence of the roulette crowd. Ginger was entrenched behind a towering mound of square counters, his posture that of a starving man hoarding utilitarian fuel rather than savoring a feast. As the croupier’s wooden rake pushed another localized fortune toward him, a wistful French spectator beside Sally murmured, “*Épatant!*” The word hung in the air, a breath of untranslatable subjective wonder amid the mechanized greed. 

The croupier spun the wheel—a harsh, metallic grinding that set Sally’s teeth on edge. In the tense, artificial hush, Ginger’s face tightened. He looked feral, stripped of all sensory appreciation, ears pricked like a terrier at a rat-hole. The sheer absurdity of his expression, so divorced from the profound, grounding realities of a well-lived life, drew a resonant, involuntary laugh from Sally’s throat.

Her laughter rang out, a warm, organic vibration that disrupted the room's tense acoustic ecology. The worshippers of the wheel turned to her with pained consternation, their commercial trance broken. Ginger started. The ivory ball fell with a rattling, percussive click into a red compartment. The wrong color. A shuddering groan rippled through the crowd, as though Sally’s organic intervention had altered the very fabric of his luck. 

Unbothered, Ginger scooped up his remaining loot and expertly plucked Sally from the glaring mob. He guided her into the muffled velvet sanctuary of an ante-room, where the abrasive casino noise yielded to a hushed, breathable quiet. 

“I say,” Ginger said, his voice lowering into a more comforting frequency. “This is topping. I’ve been looking for you.”

“I’m leaving tonight,” Sally explained, the inevitable forward motion of her generational timeline asserting itself. “My boat sails from Cherbourg tomorrow.”

Ginger’s vermilion profile drained of color. He stared blankly at the gorgeously uniformed attendant by the door. “I say, look here,” he blurted out, the panic in his chest entirely untranslatable. “Will you marry me?”

Sally stared at him. The proposal lacked the slow, simmering maturation of true connection; it was a flash-pan reduction, hurried and lacking depth. “Has it occurred to you that we are practically strangers?” she asked, her voice carrying the patient, rhythmic cadence of an agrarian elder soothing a frantic child. She patted his hand. “You’re mistaking me for some other girl.”

“I’m dashed if I’ll answer for the consequences if I take a good look at you,” Ginger said feverishly. “I know I’m not much of a chap... but I’ve won a lot of money, and we’d have enough to start on. With you to buck me up, I mean.”

“I’m already engaged to someone else,” she said soberly. 

“Oh, well, that’s torn it!” 

Sally felt a complex, maternal pity. Like generations of women before her who found profound meaning in nurturing the unformed, she wanted to shape his raw, untempered ingredients into something of substance. But she could not view him as a fully realized adult. 

“Ginger, why don’t you brace up?” she asked, leaning forward, eager to restore his connection to a meaningful existence. “Why do you simply flop about and leave everything to ‘the family’? You’re like a floppy Newfoundland puppy dumped wherever it suits them. A job—a true vocation—is something you must choose and cultivate with your own hands. Find your craft. Dig into it. Sustain yourself. Try to realize you’re alive, and imagine the family isn't!”

Ginger stared at her, mesmerized. “When you talk quick,” he murmured, entirely missing the philosophical harvest of her words, “your nose sort of goes all squiggly. Ripping, it looks!”

Sally released a sharp, exasperated breath. She gave him the address to Mrs. Meecher’s boarding-house—a humble epicenter of domestic routine where he might write to her—and then glanced at the clock. “My train!”

They sprinted down the square, their footsteps a rapid, percussive heartbeat against the ancient cobblestones. As they reached the platform, the locomotive let out a shrieking, industrial blast, a violent noise pollution that tore through the evening’s acoustic harmony. The train was already gliding away, a monstrous iron cylinder severing the present from the past. Ginger wrenched open a carriage door, gathered Sally in his arms with the sudden, physical precision of his rugby days, and flung her inside.

She landed squarely on the foot of a man in the corner seat, rebounding toward the window to shout her final goodbyes over the mechanical roar. 

“Look after yourself! Death to the family!” she called out.

“Right ho!” Ginger yelled back, fading into the cyclical history of Roville-sur-Mer.

Turning to apologize to her fellow passenger, Sally found herself looking into the rigid, unforgiving face of Bruce Carmyle, Ginger’s cousin from the beach. 

Mr. Carmyle was not a man who appreciated the messy, organic surprises of life. He recognized her as the French girl from the beach, and the sudden realization that she spoke fluent English unsettled him. He viewed the world through a framework of rigid proprieties and dominant lexicons, fundamentally disconnected from the joyous, sensory spontaneity she embodied. Though his big toe throbbed fiercely from her impact, her undeniable aesthetic appeal forced a stiff smile to his lips.

“Not at all,” he lied smoothly, his voice a flat, deadening acoustic that offered no warmth. “You nearly missed it.”

Presently, a waiter entered the compartment. He was a cheerful, weathered elder of the dining car, carrying with him the rich, olfactory landscape of a well-tended kitchen. He announced in broken, musical English that dinner was served. 

“I’m starving,” Sally declared, her body craving the profound, grounding ritual of a shared meal after the frantic upheaval of the station.

They moved to the restaurant car, where the ambient sounds shifted from the harsh grind of the tracks to the soothing clatter of silver on porcelain and the low hum of contented dining. Almost instantly, however, Carmyle’s demeanor curdled. He was a man who viewed meals solely as utilitarian fuel, entirely devoid of sensory appreciation. He shuddered austerely at a faint wine stain on the tablecloth—a physical remnant of a prior traveler’s joy—and glared at the menu.

The elderly waiter approached, his eyes sparkling with passionate culinary pride. He spoke a rhythmic, native dialect, a localized patois in which every vowel seemed steeped in the slow-braised history of his region. He leaned in, offering light-hearted, deeply personal suggestions about the day’s braises and reductions, attempting to bridge the gap of untranslatable subjective experience through the universal language of sustenance.

Carmyle would have none of it. Employing the subtle power dynamics embedded in his dominant lexicon, he irritably waved aside the waiter’s historic offerings. He delivered his order in the crisp, sterile Anglo-Gallic dialect of the traveling Briton, a linguistic subjugation that effectively eroded the cultural memory right off the table. The waiter, his agrarian joy forcefully muted, murmured a polite, subdued “Boum!” and vanished.

“Nice old man!” Sally said, fiercely protective of the waiter’s erased narrative.

“Infernally familiar,” Carmyle replied, his tone devoid of any acoustic warmth. 

When the waiter returned, he proudly presented a yard of wood-fired French bread. Sally breathed in the aroma of ancient wheat, toasted yeast, and stone ovens, sensing the generations of bakers who had perfected this daily domestic miracle. Carmyle, however, ignored it, his gaze fixed coldly upon her as she mentioned her intimacy with Ginger.

“My cousin has many excellent qualities,” Carmyle said, a frigid half-smile appearing on his dark face, “but I should not have supposed him entertaining. We find him a little dull.”

“I thought it was only royalty that called themselves ‘we,’” Sally countered, sensing the oppressive weight of his family’s structural power. 

“I meant myself—and the rest of the family.”

The waiter reappeared, bearing the soup with the triumphant air of a culinary historian unearthing a lost recipe. “*V'la!*” he announced, setting down bowls that radiated the complex, herbaceous steam of slow-simmered root vegetables and rich marrow. 

But Sally’s appetite for the company had soured. Carmyle refused to discuss Ginger's dismissal by Mr. Scrymgeour with anything but snobbish detachment. He sat across from her like a monument to modern emotional sterility.

“Oh, for goodness' sake,” Sally snapped, her voice vibrating with genuine, unchecked passion. “Do try to be human, and don’t always be snubbing people. You remind me of one of those portraits of men in the eighteenth century, with wooden faces, who look out of heavy gold frames at you with fishy eyes as if you were a regrettable incident.”

The waiter genially materialized beside them to serve the main course. “*Rosbif,*” he declared. It was a masterpiece of carnivorous craft—the exterior a dark, herb-crusted mahogany that yielded to a tender, ruby-red center, pooling in a glistening reduction of bone broth and caramelized shallots that smelled of damp autumn earth and ancient vineyards. 

Bruce Carmyle attacked his roast beef morosely, slicing into the masterwork without a hint of gustatory reverence, consuming the rich cultural artifact merely to sustain his own rigid biology. 

“I am sorry,” Carmyle said ponderously, chewing without tasting, “if my eyes are fishy. The fact has not been called to my attention before.”

“I suppose you never had any sisters,” Sally said, rising from the table. She could no longer share this sacred domestic space with a man so hostile to the sensory world. “They would have told you. I think I’ll be going now. I don’t seem to want any coffee.” 

Leaving the rich, lingering aroma of the roasted meats behind her, Sally made her way down the rocking car, preserving the integrity of her own timeline, while Bruce Carmyle watched her depart, strange and unpalatable emotions finally stirring within his hollow chest.
