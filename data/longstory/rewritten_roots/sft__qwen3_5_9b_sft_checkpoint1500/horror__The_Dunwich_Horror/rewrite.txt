A traveler venturing north-central Massachusetts who mistakes the turn at the Aylesbury Pike, just past Dean's Corners, finds himself stranded in a landscape that defies nature's usual order. Here, the earth climbs steeply, forcing the stone walls—fringed with thorny briars—to squeeze tighter against the rutted, dust-choked path until the road itself seems hemmed in. The forests that break the terrain grow with a oppressive density, their trees swollen and unnatural, while the undergrowth of weeds and brambles thrives with a luxuriance alien to civilized lands. Yet amidst this wild vigor, the cultivated fields are shockingly sparse and barren, looking like scars on the skin of the land. The scattered dwellings, few and far between, share a disturbing uniformity: they are aged, squalid, and crumbling, standing as monuments to a forgotten decline. One hesitates, an instinctive dread seizing the throat, to approach the gnarled, solitary figures occasionally spotted on rotting doorsteps or wandering the rocky slopes. These people move with a silence so profound and a furtiveness so unsettling that they feel less like inhabitants and more like guardians of some forbidden, malignant secret, things best left undisturbed.

When the road finally ascends enough to reveal the mountains looming above the deep, suffocating woods, the chill of unease settles deeper into the bone. The summits are grotesquely symmetrical, lacking the rugged irregularity of true peaks, appearing instead as artificial, rounded domes. And atop these strange hills stand the stone pillars, tall and circular, rising like the teeth of some prehistoric beast against the sky. Deep gorges and ravines of impossible depth slice through the valley, crossed only by crude wooden bridges that look perpetually ready to collapse into the abyss below. When the road dips again, it plunges into stretches of marshland that strike the blood cold, especially at twilight. There, unseen whippoorwills chatter with a frantic, insect-like urgency, and fireflies bloom in an abnormal, sickly profusion, dancing to the raucous, grating rhythms of bullfrogs whose pipes sound like the grinding of stones.

The river, the Miskatonic, snakes through this nightmare, its thin, shimmering ribbon winding dangerously close to the feet of the domed hills, suggesting a serpentine malice rather than a life-giving flow. As the hills press closer, their dark, precipitous wooded sides loom like the flanks of sleeping giants, offering no escape route other than the treacherous path ahead. Crossing a covered bridge reveals a tiny, huddled village trapped between the streaming water and the sheer, vertical drop of Round Mountain. It is a cluster of rotting gambrel roofs, architectural ghosts from a century or two prior, starkly contrasting with the thriving towns nearby. A closer look confirms the despair: most houses are empty shells, decaying into ruin, and the broken steeple of the local church now shelters the sole, slovenly store of the hamlet. One cannot help but fear the shadows within the bridge's tunnel, yet there is no alternative. Emerging onto the village street, the air is thick with a faint, malign odor—the cloying scent of massed mold and centuries of slow decay. Relief comes swiftly when the road swings away from the place, circling the base of the hills and crossing the flat, open country beyond until the original Aylesbury Pike is reached again. Many travelers never know they have passed through Dunwich, for outsiders visit rarely, and since a season of unspeakable horror, every signpost pointing toward the town has been torn down.

Judged by any standard aesthetic, the scenery is breathtakingly beautiful, yet it draws no artists and no summer tourists. Two hundred years ago, when superstition ran high and tales of witch-blood and satanic worship were taken seriously, locals gave ample reasons for shunning the area. But in our sensible age, following the "Dunwich Horror" of 1928—which was quietly suppressed by those concerned with the town's and the world's welfare—people simply avoid it, unable to articulate why. Perhaps, though strangers remain ignorant, the truth lies in the natives' repulsive decadence. Isolated generations, cut off from outside influence, have walked a long, backward path of regression, forming a distinct and degenerate race marked by the physical and mental stigmata of severe inbreeding. Their collective intelligence is woefully low, and their history reeks of overt viciousness, hidden murders, incest, and acts of violence so perverse they defy naming. Even the old gentry, the remnants of two or three armigerous families who arrived from Salem in 1692, have barely maintained a foothold above the sordid populace. Many branches have sunk so deep into the sludge that only their names remain, serving as keys to an origin they utterly disgrace. Some of the Whateleys and Bishops still send their eldest sons to Harvard or Miskatonic University, only for those young men to rarely return to the moldering gambrel roofs where they and their ancestors were born.

No one, not even those privy to the facts of the recent tragedy, can precisely define what is wrong with Dunwich. However, old legends persist, whispering of unhallowed rites and Indian conclaves held amidst the hills, where forbidden shapes of shadow were summoned forth and wild, orgiastic prayers answered by loud cracklings and rumblings from beneath the earth. In 1747, Reverend Abijah Hoadley, newly installed at the Congregational Church, delivered a memorable sermon on the proximity of Satan and his demons. He declared:

"It must be allowed that these blasphemies of an infernal train of daemons are matters of too common knowledge to be denied; the cursed voices of Azazel and Buzrael, of Beelzebub and Belial, being heard from under ground by above a score of credible witnesses now living. I myself did not more than a fortnight ago catch a very plain discourse of evil powers in the hill behind my house; wherein there were a rattling and rolling, groaning, screeching, and hissing, such as no things of this earth could raise up, and which must needs have come from those caves that only black magic can discover, and only the devil unlock."

Reverend Hoadley vanished shortly after preaching these words, though the text, printed in Springfield, survives to this day. Reports of noises in the hills continued annually, baffling geologists and physiographers alike. Other traditions speak of foul odors wafting from the hill-crowning circles of stone pillars and of rushing, airy presences heard faintly from specific points at the bottom of the great ravines. There is also the Devil's Hop Yard, a bleak, blasted hillside where no tree, shrub, or blade of grass dares to take root. Furthermore, the natives hold a mortal terror of the numerous whippoorwills, which become vocal on warm nights. Folklore claims these birds are psychopomps, waiting to snatch the souls of the dying. They time their eerie cries in unison with the sufferer's gasping breath; if they catch the fleeing soul as it leaves the body, they instantly flutter away in demoniac laughter, but if they fail, they slowly subside into a disappointed silence.

Of course, these tales are obsolete and ridiculous, relics of a superstitious past. Yet Dunwich is ridiculously old, far older than any community within thirty miles. To the south of the village, one can still spy the cellar walls and chimney of the ancient Bishop House, constructed before 1700, while the ruins of the mill at the falls, built in 1806, constitute the most modern architecture visible. Industry never flourished here, and the nineteenth-century factory movement proved fleeting. The oldest structures of all are the great rings of rough-hewn stone columns crowning the hills, generally attributed to the indigenous peoples rather than the settlers. Within these circles and around the sizable table-like rock on Sentinel Hill, deposits of skulls and bones support the popular belief that these spots were once burial grounds for the Pocumtucks, even though many ethnologists, dismissing the absurd improbability of such a theory, insist the remains are Caucasian.

The cold wind off the hills bit at the exposed earth, carrying with it a silence that felt less like absence and more like a held breath. In the township of Dunwich, far from the clustered warmth of the village and isolated by four miles of jagged terrain and a lonely stretch of road from any neighbor, stood a farmhouse that clung to the hillside like a leech. It was here, at the break of dawn on the second of February, 1913, that Wilbur Whateley entered the world. The date marked Candlemas, a festival observed in Dunwich with a superstitious reverence that bordered on the occult, but the true omens lay in the unnatural stillness broken by the persistent, frantic barking of every dog within hearing distance. Throughout the preceding night, the hills themselves had groaned with a subterranean hum that vibrated in the teeth of the locals.

Born to Lavinia Whateley, a woman whose pale, albinism-stricken skin seemed to absorb the scant light rather than reflect it, the child was a study in grotesque contrasts. Lavinia, thirty-five and deformed, lived with her aged father, Old Whateley, a man whose reputation for black magic had turned the local folklore into a tapestry of terrors. While Lavinia possessed no legal husband, she wore the child's existence with a strange, feverish pride, refusing to disown him despite the scandal. To her, the dark, goatish infant was not a shame but a prophecy made flesh, a vessel for powers that would reshape the future. Her mind, uneducated by schools but filled with the disjointed, rotting scraps of ancient lore passed down through generations, wandered constantly between the reality of her filth-ridden home and grandiose, terrifying visions.

The night of Wilbur's birth was marked by a scream that tore through the mountain air, louder and more terrible than the howling of the wind or the chorus of baying hounds. Yet, no human hand—no doctor, no midwife—attended the delivery. For weeks, the world outside knew nothing of the boy. It was only a week later, when Old Whateley dragged his sleigh through the deep snow into Dunwich Village, that the secret spilled out. The old man, usually a figure of quiet, if eccentric, dread, appeared changed. A new layer of furtiveness had settled over his clouded eyes, transforming him from an object of superstition into a subject of genuine, primal fear. Standing before the loungers at Osborn's General Store, he spoke of the child with a mixture of shame and defiance. "I dun't keer what folks think," he slurred, his voice raspy. "Ef Lavinny's boy looked like his pa, he wouldn't look like nothin' ye expeck... I calc'late her man is as good a husban' as ye kin find this side of Aylesbury." He paused, staring into the middle distance, his voice dropping to a whisper that carried across the frozen square. "_Some day yew folks'll hear a child o' Lavinny's a-callin' its father's name on the top o' Sentinel Hill!_"

For the first month, Wilbur's only companions were his grandfather and Mamie Bishop, the common-law wife of Earl Sawyer. Mamie visited with frank curiosity, her stories later painting a picture of a creature that defied description, but it was Zechariah Whateley, representing the untainted line of the family, who brought the Alderney cows to the farm. This purchase signaled the start of a bizarre era for the Whateleys, a cycle of cattle buying that would drag on until the final horror of 1928. Yet, the barn remained unnervingly sparse. Those brave enough to sneak onto the steep hillside above the farmhouse to count the grazing herd could never find more than a dozen animals. They were thin, bloodless things, afflicted by mysterious sores and incision-like wounds that hinted at a blight rising from the tainted soil and the decaying timber of the shed. Even the old man and his daughter bore traces of these maladies, faint scars around their necks suggesting they, too, were victims of some unseen contagion.

As spring approached, Lavinia resumed her solitary wanderings into the storm-laden hills, carrying the swarthy child in her misshapen arms. The villagers, having seen the infant once, lost their interest, failing to register the terrifying speed at which Wilbur grew. Within three months, he possessed the musculature and stature of a toddler nearing two, moving with a deliberate, predatory restraint. By seven months, he walked unaided, his steps faltering briefly before becoming steady.

Then came Halloween, and the fire. At midnight, atop Sentinel Hill where the ancient stone table stood surrounded by a mound of bleached bones, a great blaze erupted. Silas Bishop, a man of the undecayed lineage, claimed to have seen the boy running up the hill ahead of his mother an hour prior. In the dim glow of his lantern, the figures had moved with a silent, fluid grace, seemingly naked save for a fringed belt and dark trousers—a stark, shocking contrast to the modesty expected of children. Later, it became clear that Wilbur could never be seen alive without complete, tightly buttoned clothing; any threat to his attire ignited a surge of pure, animalistic rage within him.

By January, the town gossiped of "Lavinny's black brat" speaking. At eleven months, the boy articulated words with a fluency that belied his infancy, devoid of the usual lisps. But it was not just what he said; it was *how* he said it. An elusive, alien quality resonated in his intonation, a sound that seemed to vibrate from the very core of his being, unmoored from the dialect of Dunwich. His face, though sharing the jawlessness of his kin, featured a firm, prematurely adult nose and large, dark eyes that burned with a preternatural intelligence. Yet, beneath this semblance of brilliance lay a profound ugliness. His lips were thick and goatish, his skin yellow and pocked, his hair coarse and crinkled, and his ears strangely elongated. He was hated with a visceral intensity, his presence causing dogs to cower and flee. And whispers circulated, spiced with madness, about the boy echoing the dreadful name _Yog-Sothoth_ in circles of stone, shaking the foundations of the hills before he had ever drawn breath.

Old Whateley moved with a terrifying, grinding persistence, buying cattle not to expand his herd, but perhaps to feed something else, and cutting timber with the same relentless rhythm. He turned his hand to repairing the vast, peaked-roofed ruin of his home, where the rear wall vanished into the jagged rock of the hillside. For years, the three remaining ground-floor rooms had sufficed for him and his daughter, Lavinia, but now he sought to reclaim the dead weight of the upper stories. It required a prodigious, unnatural reserve of strength to haul lumber and mortar across the decaying floors, yet his movements, though occasionally interrupted by moments of babbling madness, possessed a cold, mathematical precision. The restoration had truly begun the moment Wilbur drew breath, evidenced by the sudden, frantic organization of a toolshed—clapboards nailed tight, locks forged strong. Now, applying the same meticulous care to the forgotten attic, he transformed it into a fortress.

His obsession manifested most clearly in the windows of the reclaimed space, which he boarded over with iron-clad determination until not a sliver of light could enter, despite the local gossip claiming such labor was pointless folly. Downstairs, however, he prepared a sanctuary for his grandson, a chamber visible to the curious eyes of callers but barred to entry by heavy doors. Inside, Whateley erected towering shelves of rough-hewn wood, lining them with the rotting, ancient volumes and fragments of books that had cluttered his mind and walls for decades. With a trembling, reverent hand, he sorted these forbidden tomes, arranging them in an order that suggested a terrible cataloging system.

"I made some use of 'em," the old man would murmur, attempting to glue together a tattered page of black-letter script using paste boiled on a scorching stove, "but the boy... the boy will know how to wield them. He ought to keep them close, safe as he can, for they hold all his knowledge."

By September of 1914, when Wilbur was just a year and seven months old, his development had crossed the boundary from natural to monstrous. He stood as tall as a four-year-old, his intellect flowing with a fluency that unnerved the adults around him. While he roamed freely across the fields, shadowing his mother, he spent hours inside, poring over the cryptic charts and diagrams in his grandfather's library. In those long, silent afternoons, Old Whateley would lecture the child, his voice dropping to a hush that seemed to dampen the air itself. As the house renovation neared completion, observers noted a peculiar alteration: one of the upper windows had been replaced by a solid oak door, reinforced with cleats. A narrow, rickety wooden ramp wound up from the earth to this inaccessible portal, perched high against the eastern gable where the hill pressed close. Simultaneously, the toolhouse, previously secured with iron and glass-sealed since Wilbur's birth, fell into abandonment. When Earl Sawyer ventured inside after selling cattle, he recoiled from the air—a stench so profound and alien it felt less like decay and more like the presence of some non-earthly entity, a气味 reminiscent of the pagan circles whispered about on the hills. Yet, Dunwich was accustomed to the foul, the homes of its folk rarely smelling of cleanliness.

For months, nothing happened visibly, save for a slow, creeping crescendo of sounds emanating from the hill. By May Eve of 1915, the ground shook beneath the feet of the Aylesbury townsfolk. Then, on Halloween, the earth below Sentinel Hill groaned, a subterranean rumble perfectly timed with flashes of unnatural fire bursting from the summit—"the witch Whateleys' work," the locals whispered. Wilbur grew at a pace that defied biology; entering his fourth year, he resembled a boy of ten. Yet, the boy who once chattered endlessly became a creature of silence. A deep, settling taciturnity consumed him, replacing his curiosity with a brooding malice. The local children spoke of the devil's seedling taking root in his goatish features. He began to mutter words that sounded like no tongue on earth, chanting in jagged, bizarre rhythms that froze the blood and filled listeners with a primal, unexplainable dread. Even the dogs, loyal guardians of the valley, turned away from him with snarls of hatred, forcing Wilbur to carry a pistol if he wished to walk the roads without being torn apart.

Inside the house, Lavinia often sat alone on the ground floor, listening to the chaotic symphony of steps and wild cries echoing through the sealed-off second story. She never revealed the nature of the activities upstairs, though once, her face drained of color, she fled when a joking fishmonger attempted to pry open the locked stairwell door. To the idlers in Dunwich Village, the fishmonger claimed he heard horses stampeding on that floor. The villagers, piecing together the clues—the sealed ramp, the missing livestock, the old tales of sacrifices to heathen gods—shivered with a mixture of awe and terror. Soon, the entire Whateley property became a zone of aversion for every dog in the county, hated as fiercely as the boy himself.

Then, in 1917, the Great War arrived. As chairman of the local draft board, Squire Sawyer Whateley found himself paralyzed by a lack of eligible men; the youths of Dunwich seemed physically and mentally degraded. Alarmed by reports of wholesale regional decadence, federal officers and medical experts descended upon the town, conducting an investigation that would later be etched into the annals of New England journalism. It was the scandalous nature of this inquiry that put the spotlight squarely on the Whateleys. Reporters from the *Boston Globe* and the *Arkham Advertiser* swarmed the farm, printing lurid Sunday specials about Wilbur's terrifying precocity, Old Whateley's dealings with black magic, the shelves of arcane texts, the sealed second story, and the eerie, singing hills. At the time, Wilbur was four and a half, looking like a fifteen-year-old. His lips and cheeks were fuzzed with coarse, dark hair, and his voice cracked with a harsh, adult timbre.

Earl Sawyer stood before the throng of cameras and journalists, gesturing toward the house where a faint, sickening odor now drifted down from the sealed upper chambers. "It smells," he declared, his voice grim, "just like I found in that toolshed when we first fixed the house, and like the faint, wrong smells I catch near the stone circles on the mountain." The papers printed their sensational accounts, and the townsfolk of Dunwich read them with a cynical grin, dismissing the obvious errors as mere hysteria. Still, they marveled at the reporters' fixation on one detail: that Old Whateley invariably paid for his cattle in gold coins of extreme antiquity, minted centuries ago. The Whateleys received the press with a cold, distasteful politeness, neither inviting further scrutiny nor daring to offer a violent resistance that might draw unwanted attention from the authorities.

For ten long years, the Whateley name sank silently into the muck of Dunwich's collective psyche, indistinguishable from the town's own decaying morals. The villagers, hardened by a lifetime of witnessing the family's grotesque rituals—their May Eve and All-Hallows orgies—had learned to look away. Yet, beneath the surface of this morbid acceptance, the house remained a festering wound. Twice annually, fires blazed atop Sentinel Hill, triggering earth-shaking rumblings that grew more violent with passing time, while within the lonely farmhouse, the air thickened with portents. Callers claimed to hear the hollow echoes of the sealed upper stories even when every soul below was accounted for, whispering theories about the speed at which livestock met their ends. Rumors swirled of complaints to the Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals, but these never materialized; Dunwichites possessed a stubborn, insular pride that refused to invite the scrutiny of the wider world.

By 1923, when Wilbur Whateley was merely ten years old, his physical presence already betrayed a terrifying maturity. With a beard, a deep voice, and a stature that defied childhood, he appeared as an ancient sage. During this period, a second siege of construction descended upon the old house, confined entirely to the sealed upper floors. Observers pieced together the chaos from discarded timber, concluding that Wilbur and his grandfather had obliterated every partition and stripped the attic floor, carving a single, vast, cavernous void between the ground floor and the peaked roof. They had demolished the massive central chimney, replacing it with a flimsy tin stovepipe that clattered ominously in the wind.

In the spring following this transformation, Old Whateley became obsessed with the proliferation of whippoorwills emerging from Cold Spring Glen to chorus beneath his window. To the old man, their arrival signaled a profound, inevitable shift. At Osborn's saloon, he confided in the local loafers, his eyes clouded with a feverish certainty.

"They whistle jest in tune with my breathin' naow," he rasped, leaning forward as if sharing a sacred secret. "An' I guess they're gittin' ready to ketch my soul. They know it's a-goin' aout, an' dun't calc'late to miss it. Yew'll know, boys, arter I'm gone, whether they git me er not. Ef they dew, they'll keep up a-singin' an' laffin' till break o' day. Ef they dun't, they'll kinder quiet daown like. I expeck them an' the souls they hunts fer hev some pretty tough tussles sometimes."

Then came Lammas Night, 1924. Dr. Houghton of Aylesbury was dragged into the dark, summoned urgently by Wilbur, who had lashed his solitary horse through the gloom and dialed Osborn's from the village. Inside, Old Whateley lay in a cataleptic trance, his breathing stertorous and ragged, marking an end imminent in the cosmic scale. Beside the bed stood the shapeless albino daughter and her oddly bearded grandson. Above them, from the yawning abyss of the newly created void, emanated a disquieting, rhythmic lapping sound, like water washing over unseen shores on a desolate beach. But it was the cacophony without that truly unsettled the doctor: a seemingly infinite legion of whippoorwills crying an endless, repetitive dirge, perfectly synchronized with the dying man's wheezing gasps. It was uncanny, unnatural—a suffocating excess that mirrored the hellish nature of the region itself.

Around one o'clock, Old Whateley broke through his stupor, choking out fragmented words for his grandson.

"More space, Willy, more space soon," he gasped, his vision likely failing. "Yew grows--an' *that* grows faster. It'll be ready to sarve ye soon, boy. Open up the gates to Yog-Sothoth with the long chant that ye'll find on page 751 *of the complete edition*, an' *then* put a match to the prison. Fire from airth can't burn it nohaow!"

His mind was clearly unhinged, fractured by visions only he could see. After a pause, during which the flock of birds outside shifted their cries to match the altered tempo of the room, he added a final, chilling warning.

"Feed it reg'lar, Willy, an' mind the quantity; but dun't let it grow too fast fer the place, fer ef it busts quarters or gits aout afore ye opens to Yog-Sothoth, it's all over an' no use. Only them from beyont kin make it multiply an' work.... Only them, the old uns as wants to come back...."

Speech dissolved back into ragged gasps, and Lavinia screamed, her terror amplified by the frantic flight of the birds tracking the man's suffering. For over an hour, this agonizing cycle continued until the final, throaty rattle silenced the old man. As Dr. Houghton drew shrunken lids over the glazing gray eyes, the tumult of birds faded imperceptibly into a silence so absolute it felt heavy with dread. Lavinia sobbed, broken, but Wilbur only chuckled, a low, resonant sound that vibrated through the room as the strange hill noises rumbled faintly in the distance.

"They didn't git him," Wilbur muttered, his heavy bass voice devoid of remorse.

By then, Wilbur Whateley had evolved into a scholar of truly terrible erudition, known by correspondence to librarians across the globe who guarded rare and forbidden tomes. Yet, around Dunwich, he was both reviled and feared, particularly due to the mysterious disappearances of youths that suspicion inevitably linked to his door. Despite this, inquiries were stifled by a paralyzing fear or by the simple, brutal reality of the Whateley gold, which flowed steadily and generously for cattle purchases, just as it had in Old Man Whateley's day.

Physically, Wilbur was a monstrous anomaly. Having reached the normal adult limit, his height seemed poised to exceed it further. By 1925, when a correspondent from Miskatonic University visited and departed pale and bewildered, Wilbur stood fully six and three-quarters feet tall. Throughout these years, he had treated his half-deformed albino mother with a contempt that bordered on hatred, eventually forbidding her from joining him on the hills for May Eve and Hallowmass. By 1926, Lavinia, trembling with a primal fear, confessed to Mamie Bishop that there was more about her son than she could comprehend.

"They's more abaout him as I knows than I kin tell ye, Mamie," she whispered, tears streaming down her pale face. "An' naowadays they's more nor what I know myself. I vaow afur Gawd, I dun't know what he wants nor what he's a-tryin' to dew."

That Hallowe'en, the hill noises roared louder than ever, and fires burned on Sentinel Hill as tradition dictated. But the villagers turned their gaze from the flames to the farmhouse, where vast flocks of unnaturally belated whippoorwills gathered in a rhythmic, screaming assembly. After midnight, their shrill notes erupted into a pandemonium of laughter and shrieks that filled the countryside, a sound so alien it seemed to tear at the fabric of the night. Not until dawn did they finally fall silent, vanishing in a hurry southward, a full month overdue for their migration. No one could say what this meant, though the implication hung heavy in the air: none of the countryfolk had perished, yet poor Lavinia Whateley, the twisted albino, was never seen again.

In the summer of 1927, Wilbur repaired two sheds in the farmyard and began transferring his books and effects to them. Shortly thereafter, Earl Sawyer noted to the regulars at Osborn's that more carpentry was underway at the Whateley home. Wilbur was sealing off every door and window on the ground floor, simultaneously removing internal partitions in a manner eerily similar to the work done four years prior. He resided in one of the sheds, appearing unusually agitated and tremulous. The townspeople generally suspected he knew something about his mother's fate, and few dared approach his neighborhood. His height had swollen past seven feet, showing no sign of stopping, as if the earth itself were stretching to accommodate his impossible growth.

The winter following the first strange journey outside Dunwich brought a trial of equal strangeness to Wilbur Whateley. Every letter sent to the Widener Library at Harvard, the Bibliothèque Nationale in Paris, the British Museum, the University of Buenos Aires, and the Library of Miskatotic at Arkham had returned empty-handed; the volume he desperately sought refused to leave its sanctuary. Thus, shabby, filthy, beard-bristling, and speaking with a jarring accent, Wilbur traveled personally to Arkham to consult the forbidden copy held within the university's locked vaults. Standing nearly eight feet tall and dragging a cheap, new valise from Osborn's general store, this dark, goatish gargoyle arrived one day seeking the dreaded tome kept under guard—the hideous *Necronomicon* of the mad Arab Alhazred, as rendered in Olaus Wormius's Latin translation and printed in Spain during the Seventeenth Century. Having never witnessed a city, his mind knew no path other than to reach the university grounds, where he carelessly stepped past the great white-fanged hound that barked with unnatural fury and tugged frantically at its heavy chains.

In his possession lay the priceless, though flawed, English edition of Dr. Dee's work, bequeathed to him by his grandfather. Upon gaining access to the Latin original, Wilbur immediately began comparing the texts, hunting for a specific passage located on the 751st page of his own defective volume. He could not help but mention this mission to the librarian—Erudite Henry Armitage, A.M., Miskatonic, Ph.D., Princeton—who had once visited the farm and now questioned him politely. Wilbur admitted he sought a formula or incantation containing the frightful name *Yog-Sothoth*. To him, the text offered discrepancies, duplications, and ambiguities that made locating the passage a labyrinthine struggle. As he copied down the chosen formula, Dr. Armitage glanced involuntarily over his shoulder at the open pages. The left-hand page, in the Latin version, brimmed with monstrous threats against the peace and sanity of the world.

*"Nor is it to be thought,"* the text read, as Armitage mentally translated the chilling words, *"that man is either the oldest or the last of earth's masters, nor does the common bulk of life and substance walk alone. The Old Ones were, the Old Ones are, and the Old Ones shall be. Not in the spaces we know, but BETWEEN them. They walk serene and primal, undimensioned and to us unseen. Yog-Sothoth knows the gate. Yog-Sothoth IS the gate. Yog-Sothoth is the key and guardian of the gate. Past, present, future—all are ONE in Yog-Sothoth. He knows where the Old Ones broke through of old, and where THEY shall break through again. He knows where THEY have trod earth's fields, and where THEY still tread them, and why no one can behold THEM as THEY tread. By THEIR smell can men sometimes know THEM near, but of THEIR semblance can no man know, saving ONLY in the features of those THEY have begotten on mankind; and of those are there many sorts, differing in likeness from man's truest eidolon to that shape WITHOUT sight or substance which is THEY. They walk unseen and foul in lonely places where the WORDS have been spoken and the RITES howled through at their SEASONS. Man rules NOW where THEY ruled once; THEY shall soon rule WHERE man rules now. After summer is winter, and after winter summer. They wait patient and potent, for HERE shall THEY reign AGAIN."*

Dr. Armitage, connecting the text with what he had heard of Dunwich's brooding presences and of Wilbur Whateley's dim, hideous aura stretching from a dubious birth to a cloud of probable matricide, felt a wave of terror as tangible as a draft from a tomb's cold dampness. The bent, goatish giant before him seemed the spawn of another planet or dimension; something only partially human, linked to black gulfs of essence and entity that stretched like titanic fantasies beyond all spheres of force, matter, space, and time.

Soon, Wilbur raised his head and began to speak in a strange, resonant fashion that hinted at sound-producing organs unlike any found in ordinary mankind.

"Mr. Armitage," he said, "I calc'late I've got to take that book home. They's things in it I've got to try under sarten conditions that I can't git here, an' it 'ud be a mortal sin to let a red-tape rule hold me up. Let me take it along, sir, an' I'll swar they wun't nobody know the difference. I dun't need to tell ye I'll take good keer of it. It wa'n't me that put this Dee copy in the shape it is...."

He paused, seeing firm denial hardening the librarian's face, and his own goatish features twisted into cunning. Armitage, half-ready to suggest copying just the necessary portions, suddenly recoiled at the potential consequences. There was too much terrible responsibility in handing such a being the key to such blasphemous outer spheres. Whateley sensed the refusal and attempted to answer lightly.

"Well, all right, if ye feel that way 'bout it. Maybe Harvard won't be so fussy as yew be." With that, he rose and strode out of the building, stooping low at every doorway as if avoiding invisible eyes.

From behind the glass, Armitage heard the savage yelping of the great watchdog and studied Whateley's gorilla-like lope across the visible patch of campus. He thought of the wild tales he'd heard, recalling the old Sunday stories in the *Advertiser*, the lore gathered from Dunwich rustics and villagers during his single visit. Unseen entities—not of earth, or at least not of tri-dimensional earth—rushed fetid and horrible through New England's glens and brooked obscenely atop the mountain peaks. Of this, he had long been certain. Now, however, he sensed the close presence of some terrible fragment of the intruding horror and glimpsed a hellish advance within the black dominion of the ancient, once-passive nightmare. With a shudder of disgust, he locked away the *Necronomicon*, yet the room still reeked with an unholy, unidentifiable stench. *"As a foulness shall ye know them,"* he whispered, quoting the text. Yes—the odor was identical to that which had sickened him at the Whateley farmhouse less than three years prior. Thinking of Wilbur, goatish and ominous, he laughed mockingly at the village rumors regarding his parentage.

"Inbreeding?" Armitage muttered, half-aloud to himself. "Great God, what simpletons! Show them Arthur Machen's *Great God Pan* and they'll think it a common Dunwich scandal! But what thing—what cursed, shapeless influence on or off this three-dimensional earth—was Wilbur Whateley's father? Born on Candlemas, nine months after May Eve of 1912, when the talk of queer earth noises reached clear to Arkham... what walked on those mountains that May Night? What Roodmas horror fastened itself on the world in half-human flesh and blood?"

During the ensuing weeks, Dr. Armitage devoted himself to collecting every scrap of data concerning Wilbur Whateley and the formless presences surrounding Dunwich. He contacted Dr. Houghton of Aylesbury, who had attended Old Whateley in his final illness, finding much to ponder in the grandfather's last words as recounted by the physician. A visit to Dunwich Village yielded little new information, but a close scrutiny of the *Necronomicon*, specifically in the sections Wilbur had sought so avidly, revealed terrifying new clues to the nature, methods, and desires of the strange evil vaguely threatening the planet. Conversations with various students of archaic lore in Boston and letters to others elsewhere fed a growing amazement that slowly transmuted from varied degrees of alarm into a state of truly acute spiritual fear. As summer drew on, he felt a dim, pressing certainty that something must be done about the lurking terrors of the upper Miskatonic valley and about the monstrous being known to the human world as Wilbur Whateley.

It was the season between Lammas and the autumn equinox of 1928 when the horror of Dunwich truly began to coalesce, and Dr. Armitage stood as a witness to its terrifying prologue. Even before the night broke, whispers had reached him of Wilbur Whateley's grotesque pilgrimage to Cambridge, of his feverish, desperate attempts to scour the Widener Library for copies or excerpts from the *Necronomicon*. These endeavors had failed, thwarted by the fierce, urgent warnings Armitage himself had hurled at every librarian guarding the forbidden tome. Yet, Wilbur remained a study in jarring anxiety; he was a man vibrating with a need for the book, simultaneously terrified by the thought of returning home after such an absence, as if the silence of his journey might bring a doom far worse than the knowledge he sought.

In early August, the anticipated catastrophe arrived. In the small, suffocating hours of the third day, Dr. Armitage was ripped from his slumber by the wild, primal screams of the college's savage watchdog. The snarls were deep, terrible, and half-mad, growing in volume until they shattered the quiet of the campus, punctuated by pauses that held a hideous, ominous significance. Then, a second voice cut through the darkness—a scream drawn from a throat that could belong to no creature of Earth, nor entirely to it. It was a sound that would haunt the dreams of Arkham for years to come, a vocalization of pure, non-earthly agony.

Driven by a panic that superseded reason, Armitage threw on clothes and burst onto the street, racing toward the college buildings. Others were already there, but the echoing shrill of a burglar alarm still tore at the air, coming from the library. An open window gaped wide against the moonlight, a black void. The intruder had made its entry complete; the chaotic noise within—the barking, the screaming, now dissolving into a low, guttural growling and moaning—could only proceed from inside. A cold instinct warned Armitage that what transpired there was not fit for unshielded eyes. With an authoritative sweep of his arm, he pushed back the gathering crowd and unlocked the vestibule door. There, he found Professor Warren Rice and Dr. Francis Morgan, men to whom he had previously voiced his deepest fears and conjectures. He motioned for them to enter with him.

Inside, the sounds had largely subsided, save for a watchful, droning whine from the dog. But as Armitage stepped forward, a sudden, jarring realization struck him: the shrubbery outside had erupted into a chorus of whip-poor-wills, piping with a damnable, rhythmic precision, as if mimicking the final breaths of a dying man. The air in the building was thick with a stench so foul and specific that Armitage knew it intimately. Together, the three men rushed across the hall to the genealogical reading-room, the source of the low, wet whining. For a heartbeat, no one dared touch the switch to illuminate the scene. Finally, summoning a courage that felt like physical pain, Armitage flipped the lever.

One of the three men shrieked, the voice lost somewhere between a sob and a gurgling choke. Whatever had sprawled before them, amidst overturned chairs and scattered debris, had caused him to lose consciousness instantly, though he did not fall. Lying half-bent on its side in a fetid pool of greenish-yellow ichor and tar-like slime was a thing almost nine feet tall. The dog's teeth had stripped away most of its clothing and much of its skin. It was not dead, yet it twitched silently and spasmodically, its chest heaving in a monstrous, jerky unison with the frenzied piping of the birds outside. Shreds of leather and fabric littered the floor, and near the broken window lay an empty canvas sack where the intruder had presumably discarded his garments. Beside the central desk sat a revolver, a dented weapon with a single cartridge that had never been discharged, explaining the lack of gunfire.

But the image of the creature itself crowded out all else, rendering language inadequate. To claim it was indescribable is a cliché, though truthfully, no mind grounded in the common forms and contours of our three-dimensional reality could visualize it. It bore undeniable traces of humanity: hands and a head that were distinctly manlike, and a face that was goatish, chinless, and stamped with the madness of the Whateleys. Yet, below the waist, biology dissolved into a fantasy so terrible it defied categorization. While the upper torso retained a semblance of form—though the chest, where the dog's paws clung vigilantly, was covered in the leathery, reticulated hide of a crocodile—the lower body was a nightmare. Thick, coarse black fur blanketed the skin, from which a dozen long, greenish-gray tentacles with red, sucking mouths protruded limply. Their arrangement followed a symmetry alien to Earth, a cosmic geometry unknown to our solar system. Deep within pinkish, ciliated orbits on each hip sat rudimentary eyes, blind and vacant. Instead of a tail hung a trunk-like feeler marked with purple rings, an undeveloped appendage that hinted at a secondary mouth or throat. The limbs, rough and veined, resembled the hind legs of prehistoric saurians, ending in pads that were neither hooves nor claws. As the beast breathed, its tail and tentacles shifted color in a slow, pulsating rhythm, draining of life and regaining it in hues of sickly gray and yellow, devoid of genuine blood, leaving only the curdling ichor that stained the floorboards.

As the presence of the three men seemed to jar the dying entity, it began to murmur, lips moving without lifting the head. Dr. Armitage noted later that no words of English escaped the thing. At first, the syllables were unintelligible, a chaos of clicks and gutturals, but toward the end, disjointed fragments emerged, clearly drawn from the *Necronomicon*, the blasphemous grimoire that had driven the boy to ruin. Armitage recalled them faintly: *"N'gai, n'gha'ghaa, bugg-shoggog, y'hah; Yog-Sothoth, Yog-Sothoth..."* The fragments trailed off into nothingness as the whip-poor-wills shrieked in a crescendo of unholy anticipation.

Then, a halt came to the gasping. The dog lifted his head, letting out a long, lugubrious howl. A transformation swept over the yellow, goatish face of the prostrate thing; the great black eyes fell, sinking deep with an appalling finality. Outside, the shrilling of the birds ceased abruptly, replaced by the sound of panicked flapping and whirring. Against the moonlit sky, vast clouds of feathered creatures rose and raced from sight, frantic at the prey they had sought. Suddenly, the dog bolted, leaping nervously out the window by which it had entered. A cry rose from the crowd, and Dr. Armitage shouted to those outside that no one must approach until the police or a medical examiner arrived. He was grateful the windows were too high for prying eyes and drew the heavy curtains down over each pane.

By this time, two policemen had arrived, and Dr. Morgan met them in the vestibule, urgently pleading for them to delay entering the stench-filled room until the examiner could cover the remains. Meanwhile, frightful changes were consuming the figure on the floor. Before the eyes of Armitage and Professor Rice, the process of shrinkage and disintegration unfolded with terrifying speed. Aside from the remnants of the face and hands, the truly human elements of Wilbur Whateley were vanishing, reduced to insignificance. When the medical examiner finally arrived, there was little more than a sticky, whitish mass clinging to the painted boards, the monstrous odor nearly gone. It appeared Whateley possessed no skull or stable bony skeleton; he had taken, in death, something very much like his unknown father.

Yet this was merely the prelude to the true Dunwich horror. Formalities were swept aside by bewildered officials, who diligently suppressed the abnormal details from the press and the public eye. Men were dispatched to Dunwich and Aylesbury to assess the damage and locate any heirs of the late Wilbur Whateley. They found the countryside in a state of feverish agitation, driven not only by the subterranean rumblings beneath the domed hills but also by a putrid, unwonted stench and the surging, lapping sounds emanating from the great, hollow shell of Whateley's boarded-up farmhouse. Earl Sawyer, the man who had tended Wilbur's livestock during his master's absences, had succumbed to a woefully acute nervous breakdown. The officials crafted elaborate excuses to avoid entering the noisome, sealed dwelling, contenting themselves with a single, hurried survey of the deceased's living quarters and the newly mended sheds. Their ponderous report was filed at the courthouse in Aylesbury, sparking a labyrinth of litigation concerning heirship among the innumerable, decaying branches of the Whateley clan in the upper Miskatonic valley.

Scattered across the old bureau, which had served as the desk for the departed, lay an almost interminable manuscript in strange, alien characters. Judged to be a diary due to its erratic spacing and shifting ink densities, it presented a baffling puzzle to those who discovered it. After a week of intense debate, it was forwarded to Miskatonic University alongside the deceased's collection of bizarre books for study and potential translation. Yet even the university's finest linguists soon conceded that unraveling its secrets would be no easy task. Not a trace of the ancient gold with which Wilbur and Old Whateley invariably settled their debts had ever been found.

Then, in the dark hours of September ninth, the horror broke loose. The hill noises had grown deafening throughout the evening, causing the local dogs to bark frantically all night long. By dawn on the tenth, an eerie, peculiar stench hung heavy in the air. Around seven o'clock, Luther Brown, the hired hand at George Corey's farm situated between Cold Spring Glen and the village, rushed frenziedly back from his morning milking at Ten-Acre Meadow. He was nearly convulsed with terror as he stumbled into the kitchen; outside, the entire herd pawed the ground and lowed pitifully, following the boy back in a shared panic. Gasping for breath, Luther attempted to stammer out his tale to Mrs. Corey.

"Up there in the rude beyond the glen, Mis' Corey—something's been there!" he cried, his voice trembling. "It smells like thunder, and all the bushes and little trees have been pushed back from the ridge as if a house had slid along atop them. But that ain't the worst, nuther. There's *prints* in the mud, Mis' Corey—great round prints as big as barrel heads, sunk deep as if an elephant had walked there. Only they're a sight larger than four feet could make. I peeked at a couple before I ran, and I seen that every single one was covered with lines spreading outward from the center, like giant palm-leaf fans pounded twice or thrice deeper than any ordinary tread. And the smell... it was awful, just like around Wizard Whateley's old house."

Here, he faltered, shivering anew with the primal fright that had sent him fleeing. Mrs. Corey, unable to draw further words from him, began calling the neighbors, thereby initiating the overture of panic that would herald the major terrors. When she reached Sally Sawyer, the housekeeper at Seth Bishop's—the nearest residence to Whateley's—it became her turn to listen rather than speak. Sally's boy, Chauncey, who slept fitfully, had bolted from his bed, raced up the hill toward Whateley's, and dashed back in absolute terror after a single glimpse of the place and the pasture where Mr. Bishop's cattle had grazed overnight.

"Yes, Mis' Corey," came Sally's tremulous voice over the party line. "Chauncey just come back a-postingin', and he couldn't hardly talk for bein' scared! He says Old Whateley's house is all blown apart, the timbers scattered around like dynamite had gone off inside. Only the bottom floor isn't through; it's covered with a kind of tarlike stuff that smells awful and drips down the edges onto the ground where the side timbers blew away. And there's awful kinds of marks in the yard, two of 'em—great round marks bigger round than a hogshead, all sticky with that same gunk on the blown-up house. Chauncey says they lead off into the meadows, where a great swath wider'n a barn lies matted down, and all the stone walls tumbled every which way wherever it goes."

"And he says, says he, Mis' Corey," Sally continued, her voice rising in urgency, "how he sat to look for Seth's cows, frightened as he was, and found 'em in the upper pasture nigh the Devil's Hop Yard in an awful shape. Half on 'em are clean gone, and nigh half of them that's left is sucked most dry of blood, with sores on 'em like they'd been on Whateley's cattle ever since Lavinny's black brat was born. Seth has gone out now to look at 'em, though I'll vow he won't care to get very nigh Wizard Whateley's! Chauncey didn't look careful to see where the big matted-down swath led after it left the pasture, but he says he thinks it pointed toward the glen road to the village."

"I tell ye, Mis' Corey, there's something abroad that shouldn't be abroad," Sally whispered, her eyes wide with dread. "And I, for one, think that black Wilbur Whateley, who came to the bad end he deserved, is at the bottom of this breeding. He wasn't all human himself, I always says to everybody; and I think he and Ol' Whateley must've raised somethin' in that there nailed-up house that ain't even so human as he was. There's always been unseen things around Dunwich—livin' things—that ain't human and ain't good for human folks."

"The ground was talkin' last night," Sally pressed on, recounting Chauncey's harrowing account. "Towards mornin', Chauncey he heard the whippoorwills so loud in Cold Spring Glen he couldn't sleep none. Then he thought he heard another faintlike sound over towards Wizard Whateley's—a kind of rippin' or tearin' of wood, like some big box or crate was bein' opened far off. What with this and that, he didn't git to sleep at all till sunup. No sooner was he up this mornin' than he had to go over to Whateley's and see what was the matter. He see enough, I tell ye, Mis' Corey! This don't mean no good, and I think as all the men-folks ought to git up a party an' do somethin'. I know somethin' awful's about, and feel my time is nigh, though only Gawd knows jest what it is."

"Did your Luther take account of where them big tracks led to?" Mrs. Corey asked urgently. "No? Wal, Mis' Corey, if they was on the glen road this side o' the glen, and ain't got to your house yet, I calculate they must go into the glen itself. They would do that. I always says Cold Spring Glen ain't no healthy nor decent place. The whippoorwills and fireflies there never did act like they was creatures o' Gawd, and there's them as says ye can hear strange things a-rushin' and a-talkin' in the air down there ef ye stand in the right place, betweeen the rock falls and Bear's Den."

***

By noon, fully three-quarters of the men and boys of Dunwich were trooping over the roads and meadows between the newly made Whateley ruins and Cold Spring Glen. They examined in horror the vast, monstrous prints, the maimed Bishop cattle, the strange, noisome wreck of the farmhouse, and the bruised, matted vegetation of the fields and roadside. Whatever had burst loose upon the world had assuredly gone down into the great, sinister ravine. For all the trees on the banks were bent and broken, and a great avenue had been gouged into the precipice-hanging underbrush. It was as though a house, launched by an avalanche, had slid down through the tangled growths of the almost vertical slope. From below, no sound came, save for a distant, undefinable fetor; and it was not to be wondered at that the men preferred to stay on the edge and argue rather than descend to beard the unknown Cyclopean horror in its lair. Three dogs accompanying the party had barked furiously at first, but seemed cowed and reluctant when drawn near the glen. Someone telephoned the news to the *Aylesbury Transcript*; but the editor, accustomed to wild tales from Dunwich, did no more than concoct a humorous paragraph about it—an item soon afterward reproduced by the Associated Press.

That night, everyone went home, and every house and barn was barricaded as stoutly as possible. Needless to say, no cattle were allowed to remain in open pasturage. About two in the morning, a frightful stench and the savage barking of the dogs

While the chaos unfolded in the streets, a far quieter, yet spiritually suffocating, unraveling took place behind the locked doors of a book-lined chamber in Arkham. Here sat the manuscript of Wilbur Whateley, a document delivered to Miskatonic University that had already driven the university's foremost linguists to a state of profound bewilderment. To the untrained eye, the script bore a faint resemblance to the shadow-heavy Arabic of ancient Mesopotamia, yet to the experts, it was a void—a symbol system utterly alien to every known tongue. After weeks of futile scrutiny, the consensus hardened: the text was not a foreign language, but an artificial cipher, a labyrinthine grid of keys that defied standard cryptographic solutions regardless of the assumed mother tongue.

Ancient tomes seized from Whateley's quarters offered no illumination, save for a heavy volume bound with iron clasps. This book contained a script distinct from the manuscript, evoking the ghostly shapes of Sanskrit rather than any Semitic root. Ultimately, this chaotic trove was entrusted to Dr. Armitage. A septuagenarian scholar with a mind sharpened by decades of studying mystical formularies and the arcane traditions of the medieval and Saracen worlds, Armitage accepted the burden. He harbored a suspicion that these symbols belonged to a forgotten, esoteric cult, perhaps inheriting rites from the wizards of old Arabia. Yet, he reasoned that the origin mattered less than the function; if the sheer volume of text suggested otherwise, the writer likely employed his native voice, reserving the gibberish for specific incantations. Thus, Armitage approached the manuscript with the working hypothesis that beneath the code lay ordinary English.

He understood, from the collective failures of his colleagues, that this was no simple puzzle. For weeks, Armitage fortified himself with the dense lore of cryptography, immersing himself in the arcana of Trithemius, Porta, Vigenère, and the myriad treatises of the eighteenth century. He interwove his study of ancient texts with relentless attacks on the manuscript, eventually realizing he faced a cryptogram of terrifying subtlety—one constructed from multiple shifting letter lists arranged like a multiplication table, keyed by arbitrary words known only to the initiates. The older authorities offered glimmers of hope, leading Armitage to conclude that the code predated modern history, handed down through a lineage of mystical experimenters. Time and again, he neared a breakthrough, only to be knocked back by unforeseen obstacles. But as September drew near, the fog began to lift. Certain characters, when viewed in isolation, resolved into definitive shapes, revealing that the underlying text was, indeed, English.

On the evening of September second, the final wall crumbled. Dr. Armitage read the first continuous passage of Wilbur Whateley's annals. It was, as everyone had guessed, a diary, written in a jagged style that betrayed a mixture of occult erudition and profound illiteracy. The first long entry, dated November 26, 1916, sent a chill through the old man's spine. Written by a boy who appeared to be twelve or thirteen, yet possessed the mind of a toddler, it read like a prophecy of doom: *"Today learned the Aklo for the Sabaoth... which did not like, it being answerable from the hill and not from the air."* The child spoke of an upstairs presence that was "ahead of me," lacking "earth brain," and of shooting Elam Hutchins's dog, Jack. He mentioned the "inner city" at the magnetic poles and a plan to clear the Earth when the "outside" helped, noting that the entities from above required human blood to manifest physically. The handwriting, erratic and childish, hinted at a transfiguration, a shedding of humanity in favor of something vast and ancient.

By morning, Dr. Armitage was drenched in a cold sweat, his mind a furnace of wakeful terror. He had not slept; instead, he sat under the harsh glare of an electric lamp, his hands shaking as they turned page after page, deciphering the nightmare with frantic speed. He had called his wife, assuring her he would not return home, and when she brought breakfast, he could barely swallow a mouthful. Throughout the day, he devoured the text, halting repeatedly as he struggled to reapply the complex keys. Lunch and dinner arrived, eaten with mechanical indifference. Toward midnight, exhaustion dragged him into a chair, but dreams soon tore him free—nightmares so horrific, so vivid, they mirrored the truths of the diary itself.

On the morning of the fourth, Professors Rice and Morgan demanded an audience, leaving the study pale and trembling. That same evening, Armitage slept fitfully. By Wednesday, he returned to the desk, his pen flying across paper as he transcribed notes from the newly decoded sections. He collapsed briefly in an armchair around noon, but rose before dawn, driven by an insatiable hunger for the truth. Just then, his physician, Dr. Hartwell, burst in, demanding he stop. Armitage, with eyes burning with feverish intensity, refused, insisting the work was vital and promising an explanation later.

That twilight hour marked the climax of his ordeal. As he finished the final pages, he sank back, utterly drained. When his wife entered with dinner, she found him in a semi-comatose stupor. However, as her gaze drifted toward his scattered notes, he jolted awake, issuing a sharp cry to keep her away. Weakly rising, he gathered the scribbled sheets, sealing them tightly into a large envelope and tucking it into his inner coat pocket. He managed to stumble home, where Dr. Hartwell was summoned immediately. Lying in bed, the old doctor muttered over and over, "But what, in God's name, can we do?"

His sleep the following day was plagued by delirium. Armitage spoke in fragments, urging a long conference with Rice and Morgan, his words jumping from the need to destroy a boarded-up farmhouse to visions of an extermination of all life by an elder race from another dimension. He shouted that the world was in peril, that the Elder Things sought to strip the planet bare and drag it into some other phase of existence, abandoning it as they had done eons ago. He screamed for the *Necronomicon*, hoping for a counter-formula, and begged them to stop the Whateleys from letting them in. "I know how to make the powder..." he gasped, speaking of chemicals that hadn't been fed since the second of August. Despite his age, Armitage's robust physique held firm against the fever, and by Friday, his mind, though sober, was gnawed by a terrible, crushing responsibility.

Saturday afternoon, he summoned Rice and Morgan to the library. For the rest of the day and into the night, the three men tormented their brains with wild speculation and desperate debate. Strange, forbidden books were pulled from the stacks, diagrams sketched with feverish haste, and formulas copied in bewildering abundance. Skepticism vanished completely; all three had seen the corpse of Wilbur Whateley lying on the floor of that same building, and no sane man could dismiss the diary as mere madness. Opinions clashed regarding whether to notify the State Police, but the negative vote prevailed. There were matters too terrible to be explained to the common lawmen.

Late that night, the conference broke without a concrete plan, but Sunday passed in a haze of chemical mixing and formula comparison. The more Armitage reflected on the hellish journal, the more he doubted that material agents alone could stamp out the entity Whateley had unleashed—the earth-threatening horror that, unknowingly, was ready to burst forth within hours. Monday and Tuesday saw endless research and experimentation, mapping out a tentative line of action. By Tuesday, Armitage believed he could travel to Dunwich within the week. Then, on Wednesday, the shock struck. Buried in a corner of the *Arkham Advertiser* was a facetious blurb from the Associated Press claiming that the bootleg whisky of Dunwich had spawned a record-breaking monster. Stunned, Armitage telephoned Rice and Morgan. Through the long, dark hours, they debated, and the following day became a whirlwind of preparation. Armitage knew he was meddling with terrible powers, yet he saw that there was no other way to annul the deeper, malign interference that others had wrought before him.

Friday morning, Professor Armitage, Dr. Henry Arthur Rice, and Mr. Morgan departed by automobile for Dunwich, reaching the desolate village around mid-afternoon. Though the air was unseasonably mild, a suffocating, silent dread hung over the landscape—a palpable foreboding clinging to the bizarre, dome-shaped hills and the abyssal, shadow-choked ravines of the blighted area. Occasionally, against the pale sky, the gaunt circles of standing stones atop the mountains pierced the gloom. The atmosphere within Osborn's store was thick with a hushed, superstitious terror; word had already spread of the total annihilation of the Elmer Frye household. For hours, the trio circled the town, interrogating the terrified locals and witnessing firsthand the horrors of the scene: the charred, sticky ruins of the Frye homestead, the blasphemous, impossible tracks etched into the earth, the mutilated livestock of Seth Bishop, and vast swathes of vegetation torn and twisted as if by some titanic, unseen force. To Armitage, the path winding up and down Sentinel Hill radiated a catastrophic significance, drawing his gaze repeatedly to the sinister, altar-like stone crowning the summit.

Eventually, learning that a contingent of State Police from Aylesbury had arrived earlier to investigate the initial reports of the tragedy, the scholars resolved to locate them and exchange information. However, locating the officers proved more difficult than anticipated. Despite the testimony of five men who had ridden together, their vehicle sat abandoned and empty near the Frye ruins. The villagers, all of whom had spoken with the police, initially appeared as bewildered as their guests. Then, Old Sam Hutchins, a local elder, suddenly went pale, nudged Fred Farr, and pointed frantically toward the damp, yawning hollow of Cold Spring Glen.

"Gawd," he gasped, his voice trembling, "I tol' 'em not ter go down into the glen! An' I nevah thought nobody'd go down there with them tracks... an' that smell... an' the whippoorwills screechin' down thar in the middlin' o' the day..."

A chill, primal shudder seized both the townsfolk and the outsiders. Every ear seemed to stretch, straining for a sound that wasn't yet there. Armitage, now fully confronted with the reality of the monstrosity, trembled with the crushing weight of his perceived duty. Nightfall approached rapidly—the hour when the mountainous blasphemy would resume its eldritch wanderings. *Negotium perambulans in tenebris...* The old librarian silently recited the Latin formulas he had committed to memory, clutching the sheet of paper bearing the alternatives he had failed to commit. He verified that his electric flashlight functioned, while Rice, standing nearby, retrieved a metal insecticide sprayer from a valise. Meanwhile, Morgan, disregarding Rice's warnings that physical weapons were futile, unslung a heavy big-game rifle.

Having studied Wilbur Whateley's diaries, Armitage understood precisely the nature of the manifestation awaiting them, yet he carefully withheld any specific details from the locals, hoping to contain the horror without exposing the world to its full, terrible truth. As the shadows lengthened, the villagers began to scatter, retreating indoors despite the evident futility of locks and bolts against a force capable of bending timber and crushing homes. They dismissed the scholars' plan to station guards near the glen, shaking their heads with a grim resignation that suggested they doubted the watchers would survive the night.

That night, the earth groaned beneath the hills, and the whippoorwills sang a dirge of warning. Periodically, a wind sweeping from Cold Spring Glen carried a trace of an indescribable, ancient rot—an odor familiar to the three watchers from their previous encounter with the dying entity buried beneath the rubble fifteen and a half years prior. Yet, the expected terror remained absent. Whatever dwelt in the glen was biding its time, and Armitage advised his colleagues that attempting an assault in the dark would be suicide.

Daybreak arrived wan and gray, accompanied by a persistent drizzle. Heavy clouds piled ominously over the northern horizon, promising a tempest. Stood beneath the shelter of a solitary, undestroyed outbuilding of the Frye estate, the men from Arkham debated whether to wait for the storm to pass or take the offensive immediately. The rain intensified, turning to deluges, while distant thunder rolled and sheet lightning fractured the sky. Finally, a forked bolt of lightning descended directly toward the accursed glen, illuminating the darkness with a sickly white flash. The sky sank into a bruised, oppressive blackness, and the watchers prayed for a brief, sharp storm followed by clearing skies.

Less than an hour later, amidst a squall that rendered visibility nearly nil, a confused cacophony of voices erupted down the road. Soon after, a ragged company of more than a dozen men burst into view—running, shouting, and whimpering in hysterical panic. One man at the forefront began to sob, his words coalescing into a coherent nightmare.

"Oh, my Gawd, my Gawd!" the voice choked out, raw with fear. "It's a-goin' agin! _An' this time by day_! It's aout—it's aout an' a-movin' this very minute, an' only the Lord knows when it'll be on us all!"

Another man took up the tale, recounting how Zeb Whateley, hearing the phone ring, had connected with Mrs. Corey, whose husband lived near the junction. She reported that her hired boy, Luther, had been driving cows home from the storm following the great lightning strike. Luther claimed to have seen all the trees bending at the mouth of the glen—on the opposite side—and to have smelled that same awful, tarry stench he had encountered the Monday before. According to Mrs. Corey, Luther heard a swishing, lapping sound, deeper and more resonant than any tree or bush could produce. Suddenly, the trees along the road began to push sideways, accompanied by a dreadful stomping and splashing in the mud. Crucially, Luther admitted he saw nothing solid, only the bending foliage and underbrush.

Further ahead, near where Bishop's Brook crossed under the railroad track, Luther heard a terrifying creaking and straining, as if wood itself were cracking and splitting apart. Yet, he saw no creature, only the bending trees. When the swishing sound drifted further, heading toward the road leading to Wizard Whateley's farm and Sentinel Hill, Luther summoned the courage to approach the spot where he first heard the noise. The ground was a churn of mud and water, the rain erasing tracks as fast as they formed—but starting from the glen's mouth, where the trees had bowed, there remained faint impressions of footprints massive as barrels, identical to those seen the week prior.

She said she'd just seen the trees bending beside the track, making a mushy sound, like an elephant puffing and trudging, heading straight for the house. "And then she let out a terrible yell, saying the shed down the road had just caved in, as if the storm had blown it over, though the wind wasn't strong enough to do that! Everyone was listening, gasping, when she yelled again. She said the front-yard picket fence had just crumpled up, though there was no sign of what could have done it. Rain lashed the muddy road below Sentinel Hill, turning the earth into a slick, sucking mire that slowed every step. Three men from Arkham—Dr. Armitage, a frail elder with a beard like spun snow; Professor Rice, a sturdy man of iron-gray hair; and Dr. Morgan, thin and sharp-eyed—climbed upward alone. They had spent hours teaching the terrified locals how to operate the great brass telescope, leaving the glass in the hands of the villagers who stood shivering in the rain. As the trio ascended, their progress agonizingly slow, the men on the road watched them with wide, fearful eyes. Above them, high on the slope, the massive swath of black shrubbery trembled. Its creator moved within it with a slowness that felt deliberate, a snail-like deliberation that belied the speed of its approach. It was clear now: the pursuers were closing in.

At the critical juncture, Curtis Whateley, representing the undecayed line of the family, gripped the telescope. He directed the crowd, pointing out that the Arkham men had deviated sharply from the path to seize a lower ridge overlooking the swath. His prediction proved correct; shortly after the invisible abomination tore past their position, the three men scrambled onto the minor elevation, placing themselves directly between the horror and the fleeing villagers.

Wesley Corey, now holding the lens, screamed that Armitage was adjusting the spray mechanism Rice clutched. The air grew heavy with anticipation; everyone knew the powder was designed to momentarily manifest the unseen nightmare. A few men squeezed their eyes shut, but Curtis lunged forward, snatching the telescope back. He strained his vision until his eyes watered, desperate to see the truth. Through the lenses, he saw Rice poised perfectly, ready to unleash the potent dust with devastating effect. To those without the glass, it appeared as nothing more than a fleeting flash of gray cloud near the summit—a disturbance the size of a house. But Curtis saw the reality.

With a piercing shriek that cut through the rain, Curtis dropped the instrument. It crashed into the ankle-deep mud, splattering with dirt. He reeled backward, his knees giving way as if struck by a physical blow. Two strong hands caught him before he hit the ground, but he offered no resistance, collapsing into a heap of pure, animalistic terror.

"Oh, oh, great Gawd... *that*... *that*...." he gasped, the words barely audible over the drumming rain.

A cacophony of confused shouting erupted from the crowd. Only Henry Wheeler, trembling with a mixture of pity and dread, managed to scoop up the fallen glass and brush away the filth. Curtis lay broken, his mind shattered, unable to speak coherently save for fragmented, hysterical bursts.

"Bigger 'n a barn!" Curtis wheezed, his voice cracking. His breath hitched, ragged and wet. "*An' Gawd in Heaven—that haff face on top!*..."

That final image broke the dam. Curtis collapsed completely, his body convulsing as the vision burned itself into his soul. Fred Farr and Will Hutchins dragged him to the side of the road, laying him gently on the damp grass. Henry Wheeler, shaking uncontrollably, lifted the telescope again to peer at the mountain. Through the lenses, he saw only three tiny, frantic figures scrambling toward the summit, their movements blurring against the steep incline. Nothing else. Yet, a strange, unnatural silence fell over the deep valley behind them, followed by a sound that chilled the blood: the piping of countless whippoorwills. Their shrill chorus, usually a sign of summer, now sounded twisted, threaded with a note of tense, malignant expectancy.

Earl Sawyer seized the glass, scanning the peak. "Three figures," he reported, his voice tight. "They're standin' on the topmost ridge, right next to the altar-stone. One o' 'em... he seems ta be raisin' his hands above his head at rhythmic intervals." As Sawyer spoke, the crowd seemed to hear it too—a faint, half-musical hum drifting from the distance, as if a loud chant accompanied the wild gestures. The silhouette on that remote peak must have been a spectacle of infinite grotesquery, but no one present possessed the capacity for aesthetic appreciation. "I guess he's sayin' the spell," Wheeler whispered, snatching the telescope back from Earl. The whippoorwills were piping wildly, their rhythm singularly irregular, clashing with the visible frenzy of the ritual.

Suddenly, the sunshine seemed to dim, not due to clouds, but as if the light itself had been poisoned. A peculiar, suffocating gloom descended, marked by every observer. Beneath the hills, a deep rumble brewed, mixing strangely with a discordant thrumming that seemed to emanate from the sky itself. Lightning flashed overhead, illuminating the scene with a jagged, unnatural brilliance, though the heavens offered no promise of a storm. The chanting of the men from Arkham grew louder, unmistakable now. Wheeler saw through the glass that they were raising their arms in a synchronized, rhythmic incantation. Far away, from some lonely farmhouse, the frantic barking of dogs pierced the air.

The quality of the daylight shifted again, growing heavier, pressing down on the rumbling hills. A purplish darkness, born of a spectral deepening in the sky, enveloped the landscape. Lightning flashed again, brighter and more violent, and the crowd, staring in helpless wonder, fancied they saw a certain mistiness surrounding the altar-stone on the distant height. No one had touched the glass at that precise moment. The whippoorwills continued their erratic, pulsating song, while the men of Dunwich braced themselves, muscles taut, against some imponderable menace that seemed to saturate the atmosphere.

Then, without warning, the air filled with those deep, cracked, raucous vocalizations that would haunt the stricken group forever. They did not emerge from any human throat; the organs of man could produce no such acoustic perversions. If one had to assign a source, it would have been the pit itself, yet the origin was unmistakably the altar-stone on the peak. It was almost wrong to call them *sounds*, for so much of their ghastly, infra-bass timbre vibrated through the bones, striking dim seats of consciousness and terror far subtler than the ear. Still, they took the form of half-articulate *words*. They were loud—so loud as to echo the rumblings and thunder above—and yet they appeared to come from nowhere. Because the imagination conjured a source in the realm of non-visible beings, the huddled crowd at the mountain's base pressed closer together, wincing as if expecting a physical blow.

"*Ygnaiih ... ygnaiih ... thflthkh'ngha ... Yog-Sothoth....*" The hideous croaking rang out, vibrating through the earth. "*Y'bthnk ... h'ehye ... n'grkdl'lh....*"

The speech impulse seemed to falter, as if some frightful psychic struggle were raging within the speaker. Henry Wheeler strained his eye at the telescope, seeing only the three grotesquely silhouetted figures on the peak, flailing their arms in strange, frantic gestures as the incantation neared its climax. From what black wells of Acherontic fear, from what unplumbed gulfs of extra-cosmic consciousness, were those half-articulate thunder-croakings drawn? Presently, they gathered renewed force and coherence, swelling into stark, utter, ultimate frenzy.

"*Eh-ya-ya-ya-yahaah ... e'yaya-yayaaaa ... ngh'aaaa ... ngh'aaaa_ ... h'yuh ... h'yuh ... HELP! HELP! ... _ff--ff--ff_--FATHER! FATHER! YOG-SOTHOTH!..."*

And then, silence. The pallid group in the road, still reeling from the *indisputably English* syllables that had poured thickly and thunderously down from the frantic vacancy beside the altar-stone, were never to hear such words again. Instead, they jumped violently at the terrific report that rent the hills—a deafening, cataclysmic peal whose source, whether inner earth or sky, no hearer could ever place. A single lightning bolt shot from the purple zenith to strike the altar-stone. Immediately, a great tidal wave of viewless force and indescribable stench swept down from the hill, washing over the countryside.

Trees, grass, and underbrush were whipped into a fury, tearing apart in the wind. The frightened crowd at the mountain's base, already weakened by the lethal fetor that threatened to asphyxiate them, were almost hurled off their feet.
