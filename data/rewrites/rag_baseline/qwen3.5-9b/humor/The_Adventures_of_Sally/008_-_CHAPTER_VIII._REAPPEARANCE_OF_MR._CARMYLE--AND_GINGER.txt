The train rattled forward, a metallic serpent shedding the static of Detroit for the kinetic pulse of the journey north. Sally sat with a posture that felt suddenly too upright, her spirit buoyed by a recovery she attributed to the collective adoration of the city. They had loved *The Primrose Way*; the critics, usually a fractious pack, had banded together to hail Gerald as the rising tide and Elsa Doland as the sun. Even Fillmore, the manager, had been granted a fleeting mention, though the critic's typewriter had betrayed him, spelling his sister's name as "Wunch" in the byline of those "also included."

"One of the greatest character actresses," Fillmore muttered later, his voice tight with a bitterness that evaporated as quickly as it formed. Life, he decided, was too bright to dwell on the single dark pixel of a misspelled name. The receipts were high. Ike Schumann, the titan who owned half of New York's theaters, had stamped the play with his approval. On the train, Fillmore radiated a contentment that bordered on arrogance.

"Yes, do," Sally said, breaking a silence that stretched between them like a taut wire.

"Eh?"

"I said 'Yes, do.' Buy the coat. You owe it to your position."

"Astrakhan?"

"Don't be a chump," Fillmore snapped, though a blush betrayed him. He had toyed with the idea; a man must keep warm.

"With big cuffs," Sally pressed.

Fillmore's expression hardened. "What I was thinking of was what Ike said."

"Ike?"

"Ike Schumann. He's on the train. I met him."

"He wears a fur coat," Sally murmured, the image taking root.

Fillmore's annoyance spiked. "Leave off about the coat!"

"'And it isn't only the coat,'" Sally recited, her voice weaving a tapestry of imagined grandeur. "'Tucked away inside that mass of fur... is one to whom we point with pride.'"

Fillmore checked his watch, the ticking sound sharp in the confined space. "I've got to go and see Ike."

"We are in hourly consultation with Ike."

"He wants to move the show to Chicago."

"Oh no," Sally cried, the dread immediate. To open in Chicago meant separation from Gerald, a prolonging of the distance she had only just begun to bridge. But logic, cold and efficient, intervened. If they built a reputation in Chicago, they would conquer New York.

"All wrong," Sally declared.

"Not a bit like it. Compress the lips. Forefinger against the temple. You've a lot to learn, Fill."

"Stop it!"

"If you knew the pain it gives me to josh my only brother..." She launched into a monologue, constructing a scene where she was the loyal confidante and Fillmore the dutiful son, while Ike Schumann waited in the shadows, his heart broken by the thought of a brother who might forget him amidst the luxury of fur and cigars. She performed the role with theatrical fervor, unaware that Fillmore was already moving down the aisle, his rigid back a silent rejection of her script.

As the car emptied, a voice cut through the ambient noise. "How do you do, Miss Nicholas?"

Bruce Carmyle slid into the seat across from her. He carried himself with a deferential restraint that felt like a mask, a well-oiled mechanism designed to smooth over the rough edges of social interaction.

Sally stiffened. Finding a man in America whom she had presumed to be in Europe was a statistical anomaly, a glitch in the narrative of her life. It felt as though the clock had reversed, reopening a chapter she had carefully burned.

"Mr. Carmyle!"

Her composure returned, brittle but intact. "We're always meeting on trains, aren't we? I never expected to see you in America."

"I came over."

She hesitated. The memory of her rudeness at their last meeting surfaced, a ghost that demanded penance. She settled for a tame "Yes."

"Yes," Carmyle replied, his tone oracular, attempting to convince himself—and perhaps her—that his presence was purely educational. "My doctor thinks I'm run down. Everybody ought to visit America. It's part of one's education."

"And what are your impressions?" Sally rallied, deflecting the intimacy.

"I've been visiting Chicago. A wonderful city."

"I've never seen it. I've come from Detroit."

"You heard I was in Detroit?" Her eyes widened.

"I called at your New York address and made inquiries."

"But how did you know where I lived?"

"My cousin—Lancelot—told me."

Silence descended, thick and suffocating. Sally felt the prickle of a detective story protagonist realizing they are being shadowed. This stranger found her significant. It was a compliment, yet it tasted of danger. She seized on the mention of Ginger as a lifeline, steering the conversation away from the dangerous proximity of their gazes.

"How is Mr. Kemp?"

Carmyle's face darkened, the shadow deepening into obsidian. "We have had no news of him."

"No news? You speak as though he had disappeared."

"He has disappeared!"

"Good heavens! When?"

"Shortly after I saw you last."

"Disappeared!"

Carmyle frowned, the lines around his eyes etching deeper. There was a hardness to him, an instinctive repulsion that Sally recognized instantly. "The Family have washed their hands of him. He insulted his uncle, Donald. Grossly. He defied him. He won some money at the tables at Roville, and it turned his head. My uncle insists he is mad. I agree. Nothing has been heard of Lancelot since that dinner."

Before Sally could press further, the imposing figure of Fillmore loomed in the aisle. He scanned the room, his eyes landing on the stranger occupying his sister's seat.

"Oh, hullo, Fill," Sally said, introducing them with practiced ease. "This is Mr. Carmyle. We met abroad. My brother Fillmore, Mr. Carmyle."

Fillmore nodded approvingly. Carmyle's air of specific importance suited him. "Strange you meeting again like this," he said affably.

The porter hovered nearby, ready to clear the path. "You two had better go into the smoking room," Sally suggested. "I'm going to bed."

She needed solitude. Carmyle's tale of a ruined Ginger had ignited a fire in her chest. She felt a surge of pride, a creator's delight. She had stirred him up; she had unleashed the ravening creature who now insulted uncles. It was a feat. By the rules of poetry, she deserved rest.

Yet, as the train lurched into the small hours, testing new rhythms of motion, sleep eluded her. The Spectre of Doubt visited her bedside, gaunt and questioning. *Had she wrought so well?*

Daylight offered no comfort. Breakfast failed to manufacture peace. At Grand Central, Sally stepped onto the platform in a state of remorseful concern. She declined Carmyle's offer of a ride, preferring the crisp morning air to wash away the stain of her interference.

She walked toward the boarding-house, wondering what demon had possessed her to meddle in lives that weren't hers. Here was an estimable young man, destined to rely on his relatives, and she had deliberately egged him on to wreck his prospects. She recalled the mad wireless message she had sent from the boat, the spark that had set the fuse.

*Miserable Ginger.* She pictured him: money gone, wandering foot-sore through London, forced to call on Uncle Donald only to be thrown down the front steps by haughty footmen. Sleeping on the Embankment. Gazing into the dark waters of the Thames with the stare of hopelessness. Climbing to the parapet.

"Ugh!"

She arrived at the door of the boarding-house. Mrs. Meecher regarded her with welcoming eyes, little knowing that to all practical intents and purposes, she had slain in his prime a red-headed young man of amiable manners and, when not ill-advised by meddling females, of excellent behavior.
