Margaret Hale stood by the window, her fingers trembling as she pressed them against the cool glass. The weight of falsehoods and unspoken truths bore down on her, but she refused to let the tears fall. Not yet.

Mr. Thornton’s absence these past days had been a silent torment. She had seen the betrayal in his eyes—the moment he realized she had lied. And worse, the assumption behind that look: that she loved another. The thought clawed at her. If only he knew the truth—but the truth would ruin lives.

At Marlborough Mills, Thornton paced his office like a caged beast. His mother’s words echoed in his skull: *Miss Hale was seen walking with a man at Outwood Station. What do you make of that?* He knew what he made of it—the same thing any man would. Yet his heart revolted against the conclusion. Margaret, who had faced down a mob for him, now seemed a stranger.

It wasn’t the falsehood itself that gutted him, but what it implied. She would lie to protect someone else. Someone who mattered more.

Mrs. Thornton folded her hands, watching her son’s restless movements with a frown. "John, sit. This agitation does you no credit."

"I’m not agitated," he snapped, though his knuckles whitened around the chair back.

"Betsy’s tale troubles you. Admit it."

"Betsy is a gossip."

"And the surgeon’s report? Leonards died of drink, not a blow. Yet Miss Hale was there that night—with a man."

Thornton’s jaw tightened. "I’ve no interest in speculation."

"You’ve every interest," his mother retorted. "Or does your pride blind you? She played you for a fool, John. Led you on while entangled elsewhere."

He turned sharply, his voice low and dangerous. "You will not speak of her that way."

Mrs. Thornton sighed. "Then speak to her yourself. If she’s in some ‘strait,’ as you claim, she needs guidance—not coddling."

Margaret’s pen hesitated over her letter to Edith. *How much to confess?* The ink blotted as the door creaked open.

Mrs. Thornton stood in the doorway, her gaze sharp as flint. "Miss Hale."

Margaret rose, smoothing her skirts. "Mrs. Thornton. To what do I owe the pleasure?"

The older woman’s smile was thin. "Pleasure? No. Duty. Your mother asked me to watch over you. And what I’ve heard—"

"From Betsy, no doubt," Margaret interjected, her cheeks flushing.

"From *reliable* sources. Walking out at dusk with a gentleman? Compromising yourself so soon after your mother’s death?" Mrs. Thornton stepped closer. "You’ve disappointed my son. But you’ve shamed yourself more."

Margaret’s composure cracked. "You know nothing of my circumstances!"

"I know enough. John pleaded your case—said you might be in distress. But I see no distress. Only recklessness."

The words struck like a lash. Margaret lifted her chin. "Your son is kinder than you."

"And you, Miss Hale, are not worthy of his kindness."

Higgins loitered by the gates, his cap twisted in his hands. When Thornton finally stormed past, he blocked his path. "A word, sir."

Thornton barely glanced at him. "Not now."

"It’s about work."

That gave him pause. "Work? You’ve nerve, Higgins. After the strikes—after the speeches against me?"

Higgins met his glare. "A man’s got to eat. I’m a fair hand. Ask Hamper."

Thornton scoffed. "Hamper would sooner hire a scab."

"Then it’s your loss." Higgins turned away, but Thornton’s voice halted him.

"Wait." A grudging respect flickered in Thornton’s eyes. "Report to the carding room tomorrow. But cross me again, and you’re out."

Higgins nodded. It wasn’t forgiveness. But it was a start.

Thornton stared into the fire, the letter from Margaret’s London solicitor crumpled in his fist. *Legal matters concerning her brother Frederick…*

The pieces aligned with brutal clarity. The stranger at the station. The lies. The fear in Margaret’s eyes.

He exhaled slowly. She hadn’t been protecting a lover.

She’d been protecting a fugitive.
