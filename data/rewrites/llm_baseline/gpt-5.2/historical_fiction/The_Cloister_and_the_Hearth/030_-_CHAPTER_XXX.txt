Gerard had no sooner crossed the threshold of that house than he felt the place speak to him in two tongues at once: the low, continuous Latin of rule and ritual, and the bright, vernacular chatter of tools and timbers—pegs tapped home, a plane singing on oak, a wheel murmuring where water took its turn. The cloister had its own music, not merely in the choir, but in the whole ordered soundscape of living: footsteps softened by worn stone, the dry rustle of a friar’s sleeve as he passed, the far-off cough of a kitchen fire settling into embers. After the road’s coarse din—shouting, tramping, the sudden clang of danger—this was a meadow of sound to rest the mind in.

It was here he made acquaintance with an old monk who was never without some contrivance in hand. The prior’s garden, they told him, bore witness: a great dial that could catch the sun and tell the hours as soberly as any bell; a wheel for drawing water, obedient as a beast; a machine to winnow grain, so that the chaff fled and the good kernels stayed. The man had fashioned psalteries too—several—and two dulcimers; and now he was at work on regalles, a little organ for the choir, its wooden pipes laid out like clean bones waiting for breath.

Gerard, who could play a humble psaltery a little, came forward with the pleased modesty of one who has a craft and meets another craftsman. But the monk touched the instrument as if he were not plucking strings so much as loosening prayer from them; and in a few moments Gerard’s pride in his small skill was turned, not to shame, but to that wholesome wonder which makes youth eager to learn. In return, Gerard’s penmanship—clear, patient, and beautiful—shone beside the monk’s, for the old man illuminated finely, yet his hand in letters could not match Gerard’s steady elegance. Comparing their acquirements with the earnestness and simplicity of an age when “accomplishments” were not ornaments but true bends of nature, the two were soon like brothers: Youth leaning toward Age, and Age warming to Youth as to a late-found son.

So the monk pressed Gerard hard to stay the night.

Gerard consulted Denys. Denys assented with a rueful shrug, as one who had grown used to choosing between hardships and mercies and taking either with the same stiff neck. Then, in the monk’s cell—where the smell was of clean wood, beeswax, and the faint sour sweetness of leather stops—Gerard told his old new friend whither he was bound, and of their late adventures, softening down the bolster, as was his wont.

“Alack!” said the good old man. “I have been a great traveller in my day, but none molested me.”

He shook his head and began to give counsel with that affectionate authority monasteries breed.

“Avoid inns,” said he. “They are ever haunted by rogues and roysterers; and though thy body should escape, thy soul may take harm by what it hears and sees. Shape each day’s journey so thou mayst lie at some peaceful monastery.”

Then he broke off, and his keen eyes—sharp as a needle—ran Gerard through.

“How long since thou wast shriven?”

Gerard coloured. “Better than a fortnight,” he said, but feebly.

“And thou an exorcist!” cried the monk. “No wonder perils have overtaken thee. Come—thou must be assoiled out of hand.”

“Yes, father,” said Gerard, with all his heart; and he began to sink to his knees, hands joined.

But the monk stopped him, half fretful, half troubled.

“Not to me! not to me! not to me!” he cried. “I am as full of the world as thou, or any that lives in’t. My whole soul is in these wooden pipes and sorry leathern stops, which shall perish—with them whose minds are fixed on such like vanities.”

“Dear father,” said Gerard, honest and eager, “they are for the use of the Church, and surely that sanctifies the pains and labour spent on them?”

“That is just what the devil has been whispering in mine ear this while,” said the monk; and he put one hand behind his back and wagged a finger at Gerard, half threateningly, half playfully, as if he scolded temptation itself. “He was even so kind as to mind me Solomon built the Lord a house with rare hangings, and it was counted gracious and no sin. Oh, he can quote Scripture rarely.”

Then suddenly, with a flash of defiance not to Gerard but to Vacancy—to the air where an invisible adversary might be supposed—he cried:

“But I am not so simple a monk as you think, my lad! This one toy finished—vigils, fasts, and prayers for me; prayers standing, prayers lying on the chapel floor, and prayers in a right good tub of cold water.”

He nudged Gerard and winked, conspirator against hell.

“Nothing he hates and dreads like seeing us monks at our orisons up to our chins in cold water. For corpus domat aqua. So now go confess thy little trumpery sins—pardonable in youth and secularity—and leave me to mine, sweet to me as honey, and to be expiated in proportion.”

Gerard bowed his head; yet he could not help saying, with the instinct that seeks the best, “Where shall I find a confessor more holy and clement?”

“In each of these cells,” replied the monk simply—for they were now in the corridor. “There. Go to Brother Anselm—yonder.”

Gerard went; but the cell-doors were close, one to another, and it seems he mistook. For just as he was about to tap, he heard his old friend behind him in an agitated whisper:

“Nay! nay! nay!”

He turned—and there stood the monk at his own cell-door, in a strange state of anxiety, going up and down, beating the air double-handed like a bottom sawyer. For a moment Gerard truly believed the cell he was approaching held some dangerous wild beast; if not that darker personage whose presence the old man had so distinctly proclaimed.

He looked back, inquiring, and went on to the next door.

At once his old friend nodded rapidly, relief breaking over his face, and shot back into his den like a hare to cover. He took his hour-glass, turned it, and went again to work on his regalles; and often, pausing to look at the running sand, he murmured to himself, “Well-a-day, the sands how swift they run when the man is bent over earthly toys.”

Father Anselm proved venerable indeed: ample head, a face in which dignity sat as naturally as kindness, and love did not soften authority but crowned it. Gerard, confessing to him, answered gentle though searching questions; and even in the midst of shame and contrition his artist’s hunger rose up like a child tugging at his sleeve.

Here is a head, he thought—oh dear! oh dear! I wonder whether you will let me draw it when I have done confessing.

And so his own head got a little confused, and he forgot a crime or two. Yet he did not lower the bolstering this time; nor was he so uncandid as to detract from the pagan character of the bolstered. When all was said, the penance was brief and plain.

“You shall enter the convent church,” said Anselm, “and prostrating thyself, kiss the lowest step of the altar three times; then kneel on the floor and say three paternosters and a credo. This done, come back to me on the instant.”

Gerard obeyed. The church had that cool breath that stone keeps even when winter claws at the world outside; it smelled faintly of old incense and damp mortar, and the silence there was not empty but held, as in a bowl, by the distant tick of the place—some small sound of life in the building’s body. Gerard kissed the lowest step, and the cold bit his lips. He said the prayers, his voice low, and felt his own words come back to him in his native cadence even when the Latin was fixed; for the heart has its dialect, and it will not always be forced to march in another’s measure. Then he returned at once.

He found Father Anselm spreading plaster.

“After the soul, the body,” said the monk. “Know that I am the chirurgeon here, for want of a better. This is going on thy leg—to cool it, not to burn it; the saints forbid.”

As the plaster was laid—smooth, careful, with the practised touch of one who has seen pain often and learned to be swift—Anselm, who had naturally been interested by the Düsseldorf branch of Gerard’s confession, leaned rather to Denys upon the matter of bleeding.

“We Dominicans seldom let blood nowadays,” said he. “The lay leeches say ’tis from timidity and want of skill; but in sooth, we have long found that simples will cure most of the ills that can be cured at all. Besides, they never kill in capable hands; and other remedies slay like thunderbolts. As for the blood, the Vulgate saith expressly it is the life of a man. And in medicine or law, as in divinity, to be wiser than the All-wise is to be a fool.

“Moreover, simples are mighty. The little four-footed creature that kills the poisonous snake—if bitten herself—finds an herb powerful enough to quell that poison, though stronger and swifter of operation than any mortal malady; and we, taught by her wisdom and our own traditions, still search and try the virtues of those plants the good God hath strewed this earth with: some to feed men’s bodies, some to heal them.”

At the word feed, Gerard’s mind, which never quite let go of the earth even in holy talk, ran for a moment to the monastery’s stores—the smell of clean grain, the bins of beans, the dried herbs that were not only physic but seasoning; and he heard, somewhere beyond the wall, the steady hush and clatter of the house’s daily bread being made ready: water poured, a knife on board, a pot-lid set down with care. Such domestic sounds were more than comfort. They were memory made audible, and he felt—without quite knowing why—that where a people keeps its food and its prayers, it keeps its language too; and where those die, even saints’ names grow strange upon the tongue.

“Only in desperate ills,” Anselm went on, “we mix heavenly with earthly virtue. We steep the hair or the bones of some dead saint in the medicine, and thus work marvellous cures.”

“Think you, father, it is along of the reliques?” asked Gerard; “for Peter a Floris, a learned leech and no pagan, denies it stoutly.”

“What knows Peter a Floris? And what know I?” said Anselm simply. “I take not on me to say we can command the saints, and, will they nill they, draw corporal virtue from their blest remains. But I see that the patient drinking thus in faith is often bettered as by a charm. Doubtless faith in the recipient is for much in all cures. So ’twas ever. A sick woman, that all the Jewish leeches failed to cure, did but touch Christ’s garment and was healed in a moment. Had she not touched that sacred piece of cloth, she had never been healed. Had she without faith not touched it only, but worn it to her grave, I trow she had been none the better.

“But we do ill to search these things too curiously. All we see around us calls for faith. Have then a little patience; we shall soon know all. Meantime I—thy confessor for the nonce—do strictly forbid thee, on thy soul’s health, to hearken learned lay folk on things religious. Arrogance is their bane; with it they shut heaven’s open door in their own faces. Mind, I say—learned laics. Unlearned ones have often been my masters in humility, and may be thine.

“Thy wound is cared for. In three days ’twill be but a scar. And now God speed thee, and the saints make thee as good and as happy as thou art thoughtful and gracious.”

Gerard hoped there was no need to part yet; he was to dine in the refectory, and his heart was hungry for company as well as meat. But Father Anselm told him—with a shade of regret just perceptible and no more—that he did not leave his cell this week, being himself in penitence. Then, as if to make amends for the rigour of that solitude, he took Gerard’s head delicately in both hands, kissed him on the brow, and almost before the cell-door had closed, was back to his pious offices, swallowed up in the silence he had chosen.

Gerard went away chilled to the heart by the isolation of the monastic life, and saddened too.

“Alas!” he thought, “here is a kind face I must never look to see again on earth; a kind voice gone from mine ear and my heart for ever. There is nothing but meeting and parting in this sorrowful world. Well-a-day! well-a-day!”

A young monk came for him and led him to the refectory. As they went, Gerard heard the place change its note: the corridor’s hush gave way to the mild clink of trenchers, the soft scrape of benches, the controlled murmur that is not talk but many men breathing near one another, and above it all the single voice that read aloud, steady as a metronome. The sound was as ordered as the fare.

There he found several monks seated at a table, and Denys standing like a poker, being examined as to the towns he should pass through. The friars then clubbed their knowledge and marked out the route, noting all the religious houses on or near that road; and this they gave Gerard, as if handing him not merely directions but a chain of safe hearths strung across the winter world.

Then supper: plain, wholesome, made not for display but for strength. Yet even simplicity had its savor here—bread with a crust that crackled properly, pottage whose steam carried the honest smell of leeks and herbs, and that faint, blessed tang of ale which tells you grain has been coaxed, not abused. Gerard, who had lived too long upon chance morsels and the harshness of the road, felt the food go into him like a benediction. The monks ate with that quiet economy of movement that keeps a refectory’s peace: spoons rising and falling like a small tide, a cup set down with care rather than thumped. It struck him, oddly, how much a man’s soul may be guarded by such manners; for noise, he had begun to learn, is not only nuisance but temptation, and a mind battered by din grows raw and easily led.

After, the old monk carried Gerard to his cell, and they had an eager chat. The friar’s hands kept straying to his regalles as he spoke, as if his thoughts and fingers were one creature; but his eyes were on Gerard, quick and affectionate. Incidentally he revealed the cause of his pantomime in the corridor.

“Ye had well-nigh fallen into Brother Jerome’s clutches. Yon was his cell.”

“Is Father Jerome an ill man, then?” asked Gerard.

“An ill man!” cried the friar, and crossed himself. “A saint, an anchorite, the very pillar of this house! He had sent ye barefoot to Loretto. Nay—I forgot, y’are bound for Italy: the spiteful old saint upon earth had sent ye to Canterbury or Compostella.”

Gerard stared, half amused, half dismayed.

“But Jerome was born old and with a cowl,” the monk went on. “Anselm and I were boys once, and wicked beyond anything you can imagine.”

Gerard wore a somewhat incredulous look.

“This keeps us humble more or less,” said the monk, “and makes us reasonably lenient to youth and hot blood.”

Then, at Gerard’s earnest request, the old man took up the psalterion and drew from it one more heavenly strain—so clear, so tender, it seemed to cleanse the very air of the day’s dust, and to lay a soft hand upon Gerard’s troubled spirit. Outside, winter might grind its teeth; inside, the strings spoke a language older than Gerard’s own and yet intimately understood, as if sound itself could translate what words cannot.

So to bed: the sore heart soothed, the mind quieted, the body given its little mercy.

That day, marked only by contrast—coming like oil upon waves after so many passions and perils—must stand here as the representative of many such days that now succeeded. For on their weary way the travellers learned what most learn on the longer road of life: stirring events are not evenly dealt out, but come by fits and starts, and in clusters, as if each drew the next by hidden links. Between those knots of fate lie long stretches where nothing “happens” save the plain business of staying alive: walking, eating, seeking shelter, mending what can be mended, and carrying one’s own language in one’s mouth as a man carries fire from hearth to hearth.

So it was with them. Though there were no more vivid adventures for a long while, one day’s march succeeded another; one monastery after another fed and lodged them gratis with a welcome always charitable, sometimes genial. They met no enemy but winter and rough weather—antagonists not always contemptible. The road had its own hard music now: boots sucking at mud, wind worrying the bare branches into a thin complaining, rain drumming on hood and cloak; yet, again and again, the bell of a house would answer that wilderness, and the door would open on warmth, broth, bread, and the gentler acoustics of human order.

And so the pair—Gerard bronzed in the face and travel-stained from head to foot, Denys with his shoes in tatters, stiff and footsore both—drew near the Burgundian frontier.
