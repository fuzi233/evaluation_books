“But I shall say no more of this at this time; for this is to be felt and not to be talked of; and they who never touched it with their fingers may secretly perhaps laugh at it in their hearts and be never the wiser.”—JEREMY TAYLOR.

There was once a legend that the Roman Emperor, seeking vengeance for Joseph’s betrayal by his brothers, ordered ten learned Israelites put to death. And through history, men like him have flourished—those whose piety lies not in mercy, but in punishing what they see as injustice, yet blind to the deeper truth of gratitude. For how can one ever convince a heart ruled by pride that love has its own reason? Or make a soul untouched by affection believe that there is meaning in devotion? We learn this from language itself: *love* comes before *lovable*. The feeling precedes the justification.

When Daniel Deronda arrived at the banking house on Schuster Strasse in Mainz, he carried a letter of introduction and a quiet hope. He asked for Joseph Kalonymos, and was promptly shown into a private room where an elderly man sat sorting letters at a table. It was the same white-bearded figure Deronda had glimpsed the year before in the synagogue at Frankfurt. The man wore his old felt hat still, and beside him stood a packed portmanteau, wrapped coat and shawl draped over it—like a traveler preparing to leave.

At Deronda’s entrance, Kalonymos rose slowly, but did not step forward or offer his hand. His eyes—small, sharp, glittering like dark stones beneath a pale yellow skin—fixed upon the young man. In German, he said, “Good. Now you come to me.”

“Yes,” replied Deronda, “I come to thank you—for your kindness, for your care, for helping me find my way back to what was mine.” His voice was calm, fluent, shaped by years of listening to many tongues, yet rooted in English clarity.

Kalonymos then extended his hand, and his tone softened. “So you are no longer angry at being more than just an Englishman?”

Deronda smiled. “On the contrary. I am grateful. You helped me uncover a truth I had been kept from—my blood, my name, my grandfather’s legacy. And you preserved the chest he left behind, entrusted to you.”

“Sit down,” Kalonymos said, motioning to a chair, his voice low and urgent. He set aside his hat, revealing a head thick with white hair, and began to stroke his beard as he studied Deronda’s face. The moment stirred something deep within the young man—an almost electric connection to a past he had never known, yet now felt as if it were part of his breath. Here was a man who had walked beside his grandfather, who had loved him fiercely, who had guarded his memory. To stand before such a witness, to feel the weight of ancestral bonds rekindled, was like stepping into a sacred space where the dead still spoke.

It was a sensation beyond logic—something only those with open hearts could truly feel. Others might dismiss it as sentimentality, but Deronda knew it was real. And in that awareness, his expression softened, brightened—something Kalonymos seemed to notice, and approve.

Then, speaking in Hebrew, Kalonymos quoted a line from one of the ancient liturgical hymns:
“As thy goodness has been great to the former generations, even so may it be to the latter.”

He paused, then continued. “Young man, I am glad I have not yet departed. That you have come now, when I am still here, allows me to see the likeness of my dear friend—his youth reborn in you. No longer estranged from your people. No longer turning away in anger from one who would claim you as a Jew. You come not with resentment, but with thanks. You come willingly to say, ‘I am the grandson of Daniel Charisi.’ Is this true?”

“It is,” Deronda answered firmly.

“And let me say,” he added gently, “that I would never have treated a Jew poorly simply because he was a Jew. But I understand why I hesitated to speak of my mother—to strangers, I mean. I could not tell them I knew nothing of her.”

“A sin,” Kalonymos said sharply, raising a hand and closing his eyes as if in pain. “A sin against our people—just as when our children were raised for Rome. But it is broken. I broke it. When Daniel Charisi—may God protect him—was young, and I was barely taller than his shoulder, we made a vow. We swore to be friends always. He said, ‘Let us bind ourselves with duty, as if we were sons of the same mother.’ That was his nature—always binding himself with purpose. He believed, ‘Let us bind love with duty; for duty is the love of law, and law is the nature of the Eternal.’ So we bound ourselves. Though life took us apart, the bond held. When he died, others sought to steal from him—but they could not steal me. I rescued what he had saved for his heir. And now I return it to him.”

Kalonymos stepped out briefly, returning with a clerk who bore a heavy chest. They placed it on the floor, removed a leather cover, and left. The chest was not large, but sturdy—adorned with gilt iron fittings, its wood engraved with Arabic script. It looked both ancient and precious.

“So,” Kalonymos said, resuming his seat. “And here is the key.” He pulled it from a small leather pouch. “Keep it carefully. I trust you are careful, and wise.”

He gave Deronda a look—sharp, cautious, the kind elders give to youth when entrusting something sacred. Deronda smiled. “I will guard this more closely than any other possession. It is not just an object—it is a symbol of hope, of effort, of love passed down. And I will never forget that you were part of that effort.”

“Have you time to hear more of your grandfather?” Kalonymos asked. “Or must you go?”

“I would stay,” Deronda said, “but I have duties calling me home. Friends in England need me. I’ve been away too long. Yet knowing more about you—and your family—would be reason enough to return.”

“Good,” Kalonymos said. “But you will not find me easily. I am beyond seventy, and I wander. I carry my shroud with me. But my sons and their children live here in comfort and unity. Times have changed since Karl the Great brought our ancestors from Italy to teach knowledge to the Germans. We fought for every inch of progress. Our youth suffered, but we won—wealth, safety, influence. Germany’s learning thrives on Jewish minds—even if not always on Jewish hearts.”

“Were you left entirely ignorant of your people’s life?” Kalonymos asked.

“No,” Deronda replied. “Even before I suspected my roots, I studied their history—more deeply than anything else. It turns out I was preparing myself to understand my grandfather without even knowing it.”

Kalonymos nodded, pleased. “Ah. Then you might have become like him—if your upbringing had not blocked the way. You resemble him in face—but not quite. He had strength in his features—a will like iron. He already had a deep furrow in his brow when young. I see none of that in you. Still, you are his grandson. Daniel Charisi once said, ‘Better a wrong will than a wavering one; better a steadfast enemy than an uncertain friend; better a false belief than no belief at all.’ What he despised most was indifference.”

“And yet,” Deronda ventured, “he was not narrow-minded?”

“Narrow? Never,” Kalonymos said, shaking his head with gentle amusement. “From childhood, he drank in knowledge as a plant drinks water. He studied medicine, philosophy, the mysteries of life and health. He traveled far, spent much, to see and know. He believed humanity’s strength lay in balance—between separation and connection. He warned against our people losing themselves among the Gentiles. ‘It’s no better,’ he said, ‘than grain blending into sameness.’ He gathered wisdom from all sides—like the Arab scholars of the golden age. We studied together, but he went further. Though we were close, he was different from me—like the inside and outside of a bowl. I held firm to two things: faith and travel. I accepted his words as I accepted the shape of trees—true, but not to be questioned. We both remained faithful Jews—thankful not to be Gentiles. I have lived as I do now—wandering, observing, loving life, unbothered by hardship. But Charisi thought constantly of our future—of our people’s destiny. That was his passion. Not mine. So while I found freedom in movement, he found purpose in vision.”

Kalonymos paused. “When I am in the East, I lie on deck and watch the stars. Their sight fills me. I know them by their rise, and I do not hunger for more. But Charisi—no sight satisfied him. He filled each glimpse with memory and prophecy. Yet we loved each other. As he said, we bound our love with duty. We pledged to help and defend one another to the end. And I have kept that pledge.”

He rose, and Deronda followed.

“And in keeping it,” Deronda said, “you have done justice to me. I would have been robbed—not just of a chest, but of my heritage, of my identity. I thank you with all my heart.”

“Be worthy of him,” Kalonymos said. “What is your vocation?”

The question caught Deronda off guard. He hesitated. He could not honestly call his law studies a calling—though he had begun them. He chose his words carefully. “I cannot say I have one yet.”

“Get one,” Kalonymos insisted. “The Jew must be diligent. Will you call yourself a Jew? Will you profess your fathers’ faith?”

He placed a hand on Deronda’s shoulder, his gaze piercing.

“I will call myself a Jew,” Deronda said, his voice steady though his face grew slightly pale under the scrutiny. “But I will not claim to believe exactly as they did. Our ancestors changed their beliefs—they learned from others, adapted. Yet I believe in my grandfather’s idea: separateness with communication. My first duty is to my people. If there is work to be done—restoring, improving their life—I will make that my purpose.”

In that moment, as he spoke, something settled within him. The need to answer revealed the truth he had not yet named. He had found his path.

“Ah,” Kalonymos said softly, “you argue and you look ahead—you are indeed Daniel Charisi’s grandson.” He blessed him in Hebrew.

They parted soon after. And not long after Deronda reached London, Kalonymos was once again aboard ship, gazing at the stars above the sea, content with what he saw, needing no more.
