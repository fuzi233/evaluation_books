The table of contents lay open, a map of a journey that refused to begin. It listed eighty-nine chapters, a procession of titles promising blood, love, and the grand movements of history. *Harry to the Rescue.* *Fortunatus Nimium.* *Yankee Doodle comes to Town.* These were the promises of the epic, the scaffolding built to support a hero who would never quite stand.

Instead, the narrative started in the silence between the lines.

One of the Virginians visited Home. Not a triumphant return, but a visitation where the house itself seemed to judge the visitor. Harry had to pay for his supper, a transaction that cost him more than silver shillings; it cost him dignity, a small coin of self-respect exchanged for the privilege of eating. The Esmonds in Virginia were not a dynasty rising like a storm cloud, but a family jar, cracked and leaking, holding together only through the sheer friction of daily survival.

Harry found a new relative, a stranger in blood who offered no comfort, only a new set of expectations. The Virginians began to see the world, but their vision was narrow, focused on the immediate horizon of debt and duty rather than the distant glory of empire. Preparations for war were made not with fanfare, but with the quiet, grinding anxiety of men who knew the cost of a bullet. George suffered from a common disease, a vulnerability that stripped away any pretense of invincibility.

Hospitalities were extended, yet the warmth was thin. A hot afternoon stretched endlessly, a pause in the machinery of fate where nothing happened except the slow decay of patience. The two Georges prepared for blood, sharpening edges that would soon turn inward. News from the camp arrived late, filtered through layers of rumor until it sounded like fiction. The quest was profitless, a hunt for something that could not be caught.

Harry flew to England, seeking a stage larger than the one he left behind, only to find himself trapped in the same cycles of vanity. Sundays at Castlewood were marked by the ticking of clocks and the settling of dust. Gumbo showed skill with the old English weapon, a proficiency that highlighted the absurdity of training for battles that might never come. An old story was told, a ghost haunting the present moment.

Love and luck arrived together, indistinguishable from one another, leading to a descent that was too easy, too smooth. The company fell silent, eyes fixed forward, waiting for a signal that never came. Harry smoked his pipe at home, watching the smoke curl upward, a solitary figure in a crowded room. Letters to Virginia carried news that changed nothing. The bear and the leader faced each other across a divide of misunderstanding.

Chains and slavery were discussed as abstract concepts, far removed from the hands that forged them. Visitors in trouble sought shelter, adding weight to the household scales. An apparition flickered in the corner, dismissed as fatigue. Friends in need were counted, then lost. Morality was found in the finest moments, fleeting sparks in a dark room. All eyes were held fast, listening to a story that ended before it began.

Harry lived to fight another day, a promise that became a curse. Soldiers returned empty-handed. Courting was a performance of roles played badly. A tragedy was acted, and two more were begun, overlapping in a chaotic mess of grief. Harry went westward, chasing a horizon that receded. Cupid played a considerable part, pulling strings that snapped under tension. Favors were granted and withheld. My Lady climbed the ladder, reaching for a rung that did not exist.

Christmas at Castlewood in 1759 was celebrated with food that tasted of ash. News from Canada drifted across the ocean, irrelevant to the local concerns. The course of true love ran counter to reason. Mr. Warrington jumped into a landau, and everyone else followed suit, moving in a synchronized dance of folly. Pyramus and Thisbe reenacted their deaths in a garden that refused to bloom. Comedy and tragedy merged into a single, confusing noise.

Pocahontas was remembered as a legend, not a person. Distress at home was the only constant. Miles moidores were spent on things that vanished instantly. Harry submitted to the common lot, accepting his place in the queue. A port was found, but it was not the harbor he sought. He was finally at home, surrounded by people who were strangers. The last notes of God Save the King faded into Yankee Doodle, a march for a nation that hadn't yet decided what it wanted to be.

They fought and ran away simultaneously. Under the vine and fig tree, the sun beat down on a world that cared nothing for their struggles. The Virginians closed the book, not because the story was finished, but because the next page promised only more of the same.
