The library was a room of dust and silence, where the air hung still and heavy, smelling of old paper and the slow decay of time. I sat at a desk carved from wood that had absorbed generations of secrets, tasked with arranging the dead into a coherent lie called history. It was a mechanical labor, the quiet shuffling of falsehoods to create a semblance of order. In this sterile atmosphere, a volume arrived, printed in Amsterdam by a man named Pierre Rouge. Its title, *Memoirs of M. d'Artagnan*, suggested a flight from the Bastille, a refuge for those whose honesty carried the price of imprisonment. Now, both the writer and his jailers were equally forgotten ghosts.

I opened the book. Within its pages lay the usual statues of kings and queens, cardinals and courtiers—interchangeable faces painted in the varnish of officialdom. They held no interest for me. But in the margins of this grand narrative, the author described a mundane waiting room, an antechamber belonging to M. de Tréville. Here, amidst the bureaucratic hum of the Musketeers, d'Artagnan encountered three young men in uniform.

Athos. Porthos. Aramis.

The names were artificial constructs, mythological remnants ending in *-os* and *-is*. They were clearly false, pseudonyms adopted by men who had likely traded their given identities for uniforms born of financial ruin or social disgrace. They had erased themselves to become functions of the state. I felt a sudden, inexplicable kinship with this erasure.

I began to search for them. I spent weeks requesting catalogs, folios, and registers, compiling lists of nothing. The effort was immense and entirely pointless. I was on the verge of abandoning the inquiry, ready to accept that these men were merely the inventions of a mediocre soldier, when a prompt from an acquaintance named Paulin Paris led me to request another manuscript.

Harry Warrington possessed none of the rhetorical power of Aeneas, yet his narrative struck a chord in the solitary heart of Madam Bernstein. His voice was artless, cordial, and devoid of the dangerous eloquence that had doomed Dido, but it was undeniably touching. More than once, the Baroness found herself yielding to a softness she rarely permitted. Life had been a desert for her, a long stretch of loneliness punctuated by few sweet resting places. Until this friendly voice entered her ears, causing her heart to beat with strange pangs of love and sympathy. She doted on the lad, intoxicated by this new sensation of compassion. Save for a fleeting moment in her earliest youth, she had felt no tender sentiment for any human being. Such a woman watches her own sensations keenly, and surely she must have smiled inwardly at the sight of this boy, noting how her pulses rose above their ordinary rhythm. She longed for him. Her cheeks flushed with happiness when he approached; her eyes welcomed him with fond pleasure.

*"Wait,"* whispered Conscience, the dark scoffer mocking within her, *"wait, Beatrix Esmond. You know you will weary of this inclination, as you have of all. You know that when the passing fancy subsides, the boy may perish, and you will have no tear for him; or he may talk, and you will tire of his stories. Your lot in life is to be lonely—lonely."*

Well? Suppose life is a desert? There are halting-places and shades, and refreshing waters; let us profit by them for today. We know we must march when tomorrow comes, and tramp on our destiny onward. She smiled inwardly, recognizing in his simple tales about his mother traits of family resemblance. Madam Esmond was very jealous? Yes, Harry admitted. Was he fond of Colonel Washington? He liked him, but only as a friend, Harry declared. A hundred times he had heard his mother vow she felt no other emotion toward him. He was ashamed to admit that he himself had once been absurdly jealous of the Colonel.

"Well, you will see that my half-sister will never forgive him," said Madam Beatrix. "And you need not be surprised, sir, at women taking a fancy to men younger than themselves; for don't I dote upon you? And don't all these Castlewood people fret with jealousy?"

However great their jealousy of Madame de Bernstein's new favorite, the family of Castlewood allowed no feeling of ill-will to appear in their language or behavior toward their young guest and kinsman. After a couple of days in the ancestral house, Mr. Harry Warrington had become Cousin Harry to the young and middle-aged. Especially in Madame Bernstein's presence, the Countess of Castlewood was most gracious to her kinsman. She seized many amiable private opportunities to inform the Baroness how charming the young Huron was, vaunting the elegance of his manners and appearance, and wondering how, in his distant province, the child should ever have learned to be so polite.

These notes of admiration or interrogation, the Baroness took with equal complacency. Speaking parenthetically, the present chronicler cannot help putting in a little respectful remark regarding the conduct of ladies toward one another, and the things they say, which they forbear to say, and which they whisper behind each other's backs. With what smiles and curtseys they stab each other! With what compliments they hate each other! With what determination of long-suffering they refuse to be offended! With what innocent dexterity they can drop the drop of poison into the cup of conversation, handing round the goblet, smiling, to the whole family to drink, and make the dear, domestic circle miserable! I burst out of my parenthesis. I fancy my Baroness and Countess smiling at each other a hundred years ago, giving each other the hand or the cheek, calling each other *My dear, My dear creature, My dear Countess, My dear Baroness, My dear sister*—even when they were most ready to fight.

"You wonder, my dear Maria, that the boy should be so polite?" cried Madame de Bernstein. "His mother was bred up by two very perfect gentlefolk. Colonel Esmond had a certain grave courteousness, and a grand manner, which I do not see among the gentlemen nowadays."

"Eh, my dear, we all of us praise our own time! My grandmamma used to declare there was nothing like Whitehall and Charles the Second."

"My mother saw King James the Second's court for a short while, and though not a court-educated person, as you know—her father was a country clergyman—yet was exquisitely well-bred. The Colonel, her second husband, was a person of great travel and experience, as well as of learning, and had frequented the finest company of Europe. They could not go into their retreat and leave their good manners behind them, and our boy has had them as his natural inheritance."

"Nay, excuse me, my dear, for thinking you too partial about your mother. She could not have been that perfection which your filial fondness imagines. She left off liking her daughter—you have owned that she did—and I cannot fancy a complete woman who has a cold heart. No, no, my dear sister-in-law! Manners are very requisite, no doubt, and, for a country parson's daughter, your mamma was very well—I have seen many of the cloth who are very well. Mr. Sampson, our chaplain, is very well. Dr. Young is very well. Mr. Dodd is very well; but they have not the true air—as how should they? I protest, I beg pardon! I forgot my lord bishop, your ladyship's first choice. But, as I said before, to be a complete woman, one must have, what you have, what I may say and bless Heaven for, I think I have—a good heart. Without the affections, all the world is vanity, my love! I protest I only live, exist, eat, drink, rest, for my sweet, sweet children—for my wicked Willy, for my self-willed Fanny, dear naughty loves!"

(She rapturously kissed a bracelet on each arm which contained the miniature representations of those two young persons.)

"Yes, Mimi! Yes, Fanchon! You know I do, you dear, dear little things! And if they were to die, or you were to die, your poor mistress would die too!"

Mimi and Fanchon, two quivering Italian greyhounds, jumped into their lady's arms and kissed her hands, but respected her cheeks, which were covered with rouge.

"No, my dear! For nothing do I bless Heaven so much (though it puts me to excruciating torture very often) as for having endowed me with sensibility and a feeling heart!"

"You are full of feeling, dear Anna," said the Baroness. "You are celebrated for your sensibility. You must give a little of it to our American nephew—cousin—I scarce know his relationship."
