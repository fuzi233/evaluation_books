A few months slid by in Mr. Arthur Pendennis’s life with that smooth, unromantic sameness which makes a man feel very busy indeed, and makes his biographer feel very sleepy. We left him harnessed—like the rest of us—to his daily bread. He had become a professional writer: which is a grand name for a person who must sit down at such and such an hour, and produce wit, judgment, and genius by the column, much as a cook must send up a dinner at seven, whether the muse is hungry or the stomach inspired.

The public has a charitable notion that Pegasus, because he has wings, ought never to be put between shafts; that he should soar for pure delight, and never be made to tug at a cab over the stones. But when Pegasus has no other property than his own ribs, and a landlord who likes to be paid in coin rather than in odes, he is apt to find the collar clasped round his throat. He trots; he pants; he gets a cut of the whip; and—between ourselves—he very often deserves it. There is no more reason why a man of letters should be exempt from work, indigestion, or old age, than a butcher, a curate, or a clerk. Genius, like cookery, is a fine thing; but it is not a substitute for paying taxes.

So the Pall Mall Gazette being established, and Mr. Pendennis being found to have a light hand with a lash, he reviewed industriously each week whatever volumes tumbled into his portion of the basket; and he did it with flippancy, certainly, but with a kind of honest appetite for the truth, as a good trencherman has for a sirloin. He would carve up a solemn history—grey as yesterday’s porridge—after two mornings at the British Museum, while the author, poor old scholar, had been stewing it for five-and-twenty years like a family stock-pot. He would taste a poet’s sonnets, sniff, and declare them heavy, as though he were a judge and the bard a trembling suitor. Actors hated him too; perhaps he peppered them too freely. Yet in those days there were not many great men to be wounded. The few who received a whipping probably got something wholesome with it, as people do with bitter medicine. And if Mr. Pen ever showed a disposition to think himself the cleverest cook in the kitchen, Warrington stood at his elbow like a grim head-chef, ready to taste, grimace, and send the sauce back.

By these labours—reviews, occasional leading articles when his conscience would let him season the politics, and sundry magazine dishes—Arthur earned four pounds four shillings a week, which is not a romantic sum, but it will buy coals, candles, and chops. There were other little profits besides; and it is believed (though he never chose to confess it, any more than a person likes to name the shop where he buys his dinner) that he wrote those very brilliant London letters which appeared in the Chatteris Champion. By one way and another he made nearly four hundred a year; and on the second Christmas after his arrival he carried a hundred pounds down to his mother, as a dividend upon the money he owed Laura.

Mrs. Pendennis read every line of his printed work as though it were Scripture newly revealed; she believed him the profoundest thinker in the kingdom; she regarded the hundred pounds as a seraphic action; she trembled lest his brain should be over-boiled by labour; she rejoiced to hear of the fine people who invited him, and of the great men whose hands he shook. Whoever has seen a mother in the country listening to a son’s London triumphs knows how the smallest item becomes a feast. A brief held, a ball attended, a lord met at dinner—these are roasted, carved, and served up again and again at the family table, until the very village has had a slice.

Pen came down for a very brief space that winter and made the lonely house at Fairoaks shine. Helen had him all to herself; Laura was away visiting old Lady Rockminster; the people at Clavering Park were absent. Doctor Portman and the few old friends called, and treated Mr. Pen with marked respect—as if the ink upon his fingers had suddenly turned to blue blood. Between mother and son there was nothing but tenderness and confidence. It was the happiest fortnight of the widow’s life—perhaps of both of theirs. Then the clock struck, the coach rolled away, and London swallowed him again; and the gentle woman was left with her quiet rooms and the echoes. She sent Arthur’s money to Laura. Why Laura chose to be absent when he came, whether he was piqued or relieved by it, is one of those domestic riddles which women answer with a look, and men never translate at all.

Meanwhile Pen’s own London was growing large. His uncle’s introductions and his own pliant good-humour brought him into literary circles and into fashionable ones; and, curiously, the latter helped him with the former. A young man who is supposed to be writing for amusement is received by publishers and editors with a respect they seldom show to a hungry genius. Bacon, Bungay and Co. were proud of him; Wenham asked him to dinner; Wagg looked on him with favour. They reported how they met him in Mayfair; and Mayfair, seeing him in Mayfair, invited him because somebody else had invited him. So the young man wandered through the whole pantry and drawing-room of London—from Paternoster Row to Pimlico—and was as much at home at a nobleman’s table as in the back room of an office where proofs lay damp, ink smelt sharp, and the air buzzed with the scraping of pens.

He might breakfast at Mr. Plover’s in the company of a Peer, a Bishop, a parliamentary orator, two blue ladies, a popular preacher, the author of the last novel, and the newest imported lion; and then slip away to the newspaper office where Finucane the sub-editor had the latest whispers from the Row. Shandon would come in presently, nod, and begin writing his leading article at the far end of the table, flanked—like a fortress—by his pint of sherry, which the boy brought as silently and inevitably as a saucepan to a fire. In the front room Bludyer’s roaring voice would be heard as he bullied the timid publisher, impounded books upon the counter, and presently sold them at his stall; then, having dined and drunk upon the author’s value, he would call for paper and ink and “smash” the very man who had paid for his chops. Toward evening Pen would stroll to his club and take up Warrington for a walk—a constitutional, as they call it, meaning a march to blow the lungs open and invite appetite. Then dinner; then perhaps the Opera, the Eagle, a Mayfair ball, a quiet cigar with a book, or a night of talk with Warrington, or some new song bawled out at the Back Kitchen. He ran everywhere, tasted everything, listened to every sound—coach-wheels on the stones, fiddles in the gallery, laughter in taverns, the thin scratch of quills. Very likely he did not know how much he enjoyed it until later years, when the same music would sound like a barrel-organ, and pleasure itself would have the flavour of warmed-over soup.

And those times, too, have passed away. It is but a few years since—and yet most of the men are gone. Bludyer will bully no more. Shandon sleeps. Honest Doolan has been buried. The town keeps making its noise; but the voices change.

London that season was in full bloom. The fashionable papers were thick with dinners, routs, and balls; the Sovereign held levees and drawing-rooms; club bow-windows were packed with respectable red-faced gentlemen reading news as solemnly as if it were a sermon; along the Serpentine rolled endless carriages; over Rotten Row clattered squadrons of horsemen. Everybody was in town; and of course Major Arthur Pendennis, who counted himself somebody, was not absent.

On a certain morning the worthy Major sat by his fire with his head tied up in a smart bandanna, his thin frame wrapped in a brilliant Turkish dressing-gown, and his feet simmering in a bath, as though he were a pair of delicate cutlets being gently brought to tenderness. He could not face the day without his two hours’ toilet, his early tea, and his Morning Post. Nobody in the world except Morgan—not even the Major himself, perhaps—knew how feeble and ancient the old gentleman was growing, and what a thousand small comforts, like secret seasonings, were required to make him presentable.

We laugh at old ladies for their paints and perfumes and ringlets, as if time were a scandalous enemy only to women; but men are vain too, and an old buck’s toilet is as elaborate as any Belgravian Venus’s. Where does old Blushington get that constant rose tint? What conjuration makes old Blondel’s silver pass for gold? Have you ever seen Lord Hotspur dismount when he thinks nobody is looking? From behind in Rotten Row he is a young thunderbolt; on foot he totters like an umbrella in a gust. Did you ever imagine Dick Lacy—Dick for sixty years—in a natural state, without stays? These are as instructive objects as any elderly beauty; and if a parson wished, he might set such a gentleman in an armchair in the aisle and preach a whole homily upon him, to wholesome use for once. But we stray, while Major Pendennis’s feet cool in their bath and Morgan lifts them out, dries them as carefully as if they were porcelain, and proceeds to set his master upon his legs with waistband and wig, starched cravat, and boots and gloves spotless enough to eat off—though heaven forbid anybody should.

It was during these toilet hours that Morgan and his employer held their confidential conversations; for they met little at other times. The Major detested his own lodging-room as if it were a prison, and Morgan, once letters were delivered and the last curl adjusted, had leisure enough to employ as he pleased.

He employed it among the noblemen’s servants—his acquaintance—so that Morgan Pendennis (for by such compound titles gentlemen’s gentlemen recognise one another) was a frequent guest at tables where the wine was good, the joints well carved, and the talk better than either. He belonged to two influential clubs in Mayfair and Pimlico; and thus, while the town’s great people tried to keep their lives private behind glitter and etiquette, their lives were being discussed downstairs with the exactness of a kitchen inventory. Nothing is secret. Leave a manuscript open on a table, and the maid will read it; quarrel with your friend, and it will be analysed over tea in the lower regions with impartial eloquence; and if another maid drops in, her presence is no restraint—she will contribute her judgment, and carry it home next day. So it is in palaces as in parlours. A duke is no hero to his valet-de-chambre; and his Grace’s Man, at his club among other Men of equal dignity, will speak of his master’s economies, debts, intrigues, and matrimonial schemes with a candour that would shock the master upstairs. If old Pendennis was thought to know everything, and to be deliciously scandalous and discreet, it is only fair to say Morgan foraged much of that knowledge on the kitchen floor and brought it home like game.

Now there had been a drawing-room the previous day; and the Major, sipping his tea, read in the paper the presentation of Lady Clavering by Lady Rockminster, and of Miss Amory by her mother Lady Clavering; and farther down he studied the description of their dresses—those strange names and textures which future antiquaries will puzzle over as they do over dead dialects. The sight of the names carried him back to the country.

“How long have the Claverings been in London?” he asked. “Pray, Morgan, have you seen any of their people?”

“Sir Francis have sent away his foring man, sir,” Morgan replied; “and have took a friend of mine as own man, sir. Indeed he applied on my reckmendation. You may recklect Towler, sir,—tall red-aired man—but dyes his air. Was groom of the chambers in Lord Levant’s family till his Lordship broke hup. It’s a fall for Towler, sir; but pore men can’t be particklar,” said Morgan, and the very sound of his pity was like the clink of plates—melancholy, but practical.

“Devilish hard on Towler, by gad!” said the Major, amused. “And not pleasant for Lord Levant—he, he!”

“Always knew it was coming, sir,” Morgan continued, stropping the razor with a brisk rasping music that seemed part of the household’s acoustic order. “I spoke to you of it Michaelmas was four years: when her Ladyship put the diamonds in pawn. It was Towler, sir, took ’em in two cabs to Dobree’s—and a good deal of the plate went the same way. Don’t you remember seeing of it at Blackwall, with the Levant arms and coronick, and Lord Levant settn oppsit to it at the Marquis of Steyne’s dinner? Beg your pardon; did I cut you, sir?”

He had the Major’s chin in hand, and talked on as the razor went—soft, precise, and dangerous. “They’ve took a house in Grosvenor Place, and are coming out strong, sir. Her Ladyship’s going to give three parties, besides a dinner a week, sir. Her fortune won’t stand it—can’t stand it.”

“Gad, she had a devilish good cook when I was at Fairoaks,” the Major said, with the morality of a man who can feel for a soufflé more readily than for a widow.

“Marobblan was his name, sir; Marobblan’s gone away, sir,” Morgan answered; and the Major, this time with heartfelt sympathy, declared he was “devilish sorry to lose him.”

“There’s been a tremenjuous row about that Mosseer Marobblan,” Morgan went on. “At a ball at Baymouth, sir, bless his impadence, he challenged Mr. Harthur to fight a jewel, sir, which Mr. Arthur was very near knocking him down, and pitchin’ him outawinder, and serve him right; but Chevalier Strong, sir, came up and stopped the shindy—I beg pardon, the holtercation, sir—them French cooks has as much pride and hinsolence as if they was real gentlemen.”

“I heard something of that quarrel,” said the Major. “But Mirobolant was not turned off for that?”

“No, sir—that affair, sir, which Mr. Harthur forgave it him and beayved most handsome, was hushed hup: it was about Miss Hamory, sir, that he ad is dismissial. Those French fellers, they fancy everybody is in love with ’em; and he climbed up the large grape vine to her winder, sir, and was a trying to get in, when he was caught, sir; and Mr. Strong came out, and they got the garden-engine and played on him, and there was no end of a row, sir.”

“Confound his impudence! You don’t mean to say Miss Amory encouraged him?” cried the Major, startled by a particular look—one of those tiny inflections in a servant’s face which carry whole paragraphs of meaning without the risk of plain English.

Morgan became at once as blank and polished as a silver dish. “Know nothing about it, sir. Servants don’t know them kind of things the least. Most probbly there was nothing in it—so many lies is told about families—Marobblan went away, bag and baggage, saucepans, and pianna, and all—the feller ad a pianna, and wrote potry in French, and he took a lodging at Clavering, and he hankered about the primises, and it was said that Madam Fribsy, the milliner, brought letters to Miss Hamory, though I don’t believe a word about it; nor that he tried to pison hisself with charcoal, which it was all a humbug betwigst him and Madam Fribsy; and he was nearly shot by the keeper in the park.”

Thus the Major’s morning, which began with tea and a newspaper, was seasoned with scandal, like pepper shaken over a dish that was already rich enough.

And that very day, by chance, the Major took his post in the great window of Bays’s Club in St. James’s Street, at the hour when you may see half-a-score of respectable old bucks likewise ranged—heads forward, bodies comfortably withdrawn—watching the world pass as they have watched it for years. Bays’s is old-fashioned now, and its members are mostly beyond middle age; but in the Prince Regent’s time these same windows held the same sort of gentlemen, only then they were thought the finest dandies in the empire, whereas time has since altered the cut of their coats and the opinion of the public.

Major Pendennis, peering out, spied his nephew Arthur walking down the street with his friend Mr. Popjoy.

“Look!” said Popjoy to Pen as they passed, in that airy tone with which youth insults age because it has not yet been introduced to it. “Did you ever pass Bays’s at four o’clock without seeing that collection of old fogies? It’s a regular museum. They ought to be cast in wax, and set up at Madame Tussaud’s—”

“—In a chamber of old horrors by themselves,” Pen said, laughing.

“—In the chamber of horrors! Gad, doosid good!” Pop cried. “They are old rogues, most of ’em, and no mistake. There’s old Blondel; there’s my Uncle Colchicum, the most confounded old sinner in Europe; there’s—hullo! there’s somebody rapping the window and nodding at us.”

“It’s my uncle, the Major,” said Pen. “Is he an old sinner too?”

“Notorious old rogue,” Pop said, wagging his head. (“Notowious old wogue,” he pronounced it, making wickedness sound almost affectionate.) “He’s beckoning you in; he wants to speak to you.”

“Come in too,” Pen said.

“—Can’t,” replied Popjoy. “Cut uncle Col. two years ago, about Mademoiselle Frangipane—Ta, ta.” And with that the young sinner sauntered away to Blacquiere’s, where reprobates of his own age made a fresher noise; while Pen turned toward the window full of the elder criminals, and obeyed the Major’s summons.
