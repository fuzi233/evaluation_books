The Reverend Septimus Crisparkle—Septimus because six earlier little brothers, lighted like rushlights, had gone out one by one—had already made his morning covenant with Cloisterham: not in a study, but at the weir, where the day’s first skin of ice took a crisp crack under his cheerful plunge. The river answered him in its own tongue: a quick sucking hush round his shoulders, reeds whispering at the edges, and the far Cathedral bell laying a slow, solemn line over the water as if sound itself were a kind of blessing that could be heard and felt.

Back in Minor Canon Corner, with his hair still holding the scent of cold stones and running water, he stood before the pier-glass and boxed at his own reflection with the calm exactness of an artist. His gloves—great, padded, ridiculous-looking things—might have belonged to a prizefighter; his face belonged to a child who had never been able to keep a hard thought against anybody for long. He feinted, he slipped, he struck straight from the shoulder; the leather thudded softly, and each controlled exhalation was a little gust that made the room’s quiet audible: the tick of the clock, the faint creak of oaken beam, a rook’s croak outside as if even the birds kept their voices low out of respect for the Cathedral’s shadow.

Mrs. Crisparkle—his mother, not his wife—came in at that very moment, neat as a china shepherdess, bright-eyed, trim and compact, her ribbons as carefully chosen as a cook chooses herbs. Septimus stopped as if obeying a bell, took her entering face between the boxing-gloves, and kissed it.

“I say, every morning of my life, that you’ll do it at last, Sept,” she observed, watching with affectionate alarm.

“Do what, Ma dear?”

“Break the pier-glass, or burst a blood-vessel.”

“Neither, please God, Ma dear. Here’s wind, Ma. Look at this!”

He gave a final round that was severe in form but merry in spirit, and ended by getting the old lady’s cap into Chancery—so the professors of the Noble Art called that light entanglement—without stirring the smallest lavender or cherry riband. Then, with the quick humility of a man who likes to seem caught doing nothing remarkable, he slipped his gloves into a drawer and assumed a contemplative gaze out of window just as a servant entered.

Breakfast in Minor Canon Corner had its own ritual sounds and smells: the urn beginning to sing, a lid’s soft click, china whispering against cloth, a warm breath of tea rising with the faintly hay-like fragrance of Souchong, and beneath it the comfortable, persuasive odours of ham and toast and eggs. The Cathedral did not fall silent for domesticity; rather, domesticity and Cathedral made a duet—bells, rooks, rare footsteps echoing down the old stones, and now and then an organ-roll like distant weather. Cloisterham’s noises were not the clattering assault of a modern street, but an acoustic ecology that soothed by being measured: each sound knew its place, and kept it.

When the two were alone again, she stood to say the Lord’s Prayer aloud. Septimus stood with bent head to hear it, though he was within five years of forty—much as he had stood to hear the same words from the same lips when he was within five months of four. It was one of those circular moments that make a life feel stitched, not torn: the same cadence, the same voice, the same small room holding the same act as if time were a patient housekeeper.

They sat down opposite each other. On the table lay a letter she had just read and set upon the breakfast-cloth as if it were a dish not yet served.

“And what, Ma dear,” asked the Minor Canon, doing honest work upon his breakfast, “does the letter say?”

She passed it over.

Mrs. Crisparkle’s pride was that her bright eyes could read writing without spectacles; her son’s pride was to make her pride bloom. He had therefore invented the fiction that he could not read manuscript unless he put on a pair of grave and prodigious spectacles, which pinched his nose, clouded his breakfast, and made his eyes water—eyes which, unassisted, were as sharp as a microscope married to a telescope.

“It’s from Mr. Honeythunder, of course,” she said, folding her arms with a little preparatory stiffness, as one might brace for a strong draught.

“Of course,” returned Septimus, and began, stumbling in a way that belonged not to letters but to lenses. “‘Haven of Philanthropy, Chief Offices, London, Wednesday. Dear Madam. I write in the—;’ In the what’s this? What does he write in?”

“In the chair,” said the old lady, quick as a knife.

Septimus lifted off the spectacles to see her face, half protesting, half appealing. “Why, what should he write in?”

“Bless me, bless me, Sept, you don’t see the context! Give it back to me, my dear.”

Glad to be rid of the watery torture, he returned it, murmuring dutifully that his sight for reading got worse and worse daily.

She read, very perspicuously: “‘I write from the chair, to which I shall probably be confined for some hours.’”

Septimus glanced at the row of chairs against the wall with a look that suggested chairs could be prisons if one insisted upon it.

“‘We have,’” she continued, with a touch more emphasis, “‘a meeting of our Convened Chief Composite Committee of Central and District Philanthropists, at our Head Haven as above; and it is their unanimous pleasure that I take the chair.’”

Septimus breathed more freely. “O! if he comes to that, let him,” he muttered.

“‘Not to lose a day’s post, I take the opportunity of a long report being read, denouncing a public miscreant—’”

“It is a most extraordinary thing,” broke in the gentle Minor Canon, laying down knife and fork to rub his ear in vexation, “that these philanthropists are always denouncing somebody. And it is another most extraordinary thing that they are always so violently flush of miscreants!”

“‘Denouncing a public miscreant,’” his mother resumed, “‘to get our little affair of business off my mind. I have spoken with my two wards, Neville and Helena Landless, on the subject of their defective education, and they give in to the plan proposed; as I should have taken good care they did, whether they liked it or not.’”

“And it is another most extraordinary thing,” Septimus added in the same tone, “that these philanthropists are so given to seizing their fellow-creatures by the scruff of the neck, and—one may say—bumping them into the paths of peace. I beg your pardon, Ma dear, for interrupting.”

“‘Therefore, dear Madam, you will please prepare your son, the Rev. Mr. Septimus, to expect Neville as an inmate to be read with, on Monday next. On the same day Helena will accompany him to Cloisterham, to take up her quarters at the Nuns’ House, the establishment recommended by yourself and son jointly. Please likewise to prepare for her reception and tuition there. The terms in both cases are understood to be exactly as stated to me in writing by yourself, when I opened a correspondence with you on this subject, after the honour of being introduced to you at your sister’s house in town here. With compliments to the Rev. Mr. Septimus, I am, Dear Madam, Your affectionate brother (In Philanthropy), Luke Honeythunder.’”

Septimus rubbed his ear again, as if the very name made a noise in it. “Well, Ma, we must try it. There can be no doubt we have room for an inmate, and that I have time to bestow upon him, and inclination too. I must confess to feeling rather glad he is not Mr. Honeythunder himself. Though that seems wretchedly prejudiced—does it not?—for I never saw him. Is he a large man, Ma?”

“I should call him a large man, my dear,” she replied after hesitation, “but that his voice is so much larger.”

“Than himself?”

“Than anybody.”

“Hah!” said Septimus; and finished his breakfast as if the flavour of the Superior Family Souchong, and also of the ham and toast and eggs, had taken a little fright and withdrawn.

The slender history of how Mr. Honeythunder had come to press his wards upon Cloisterham was soon told in Minor Canon Corner. Mrs. Crisparkle’s sister—another Dresden piece, so neatly matched that the two ought never to have been parted—was the childless wife of a City clergyman. During the last annual rematching of those ornaments (during Mrs. Crisparkle’s last visit), Mr. Honeythunder, in his public capacity of Professor of Philanthropy, had made their acquaintance after a charitable occasion where devoted orphans of tender years had been glutted with plum buns and plump bumptiousness. From that sugared, noisy scene had come this quiet domestic consequence.

Septimus thought the matter over as the morning went on, the house breathing its old calm—ivy against brick, lattices keeping out glare, panelled rooms smelling faintly of beeswax and seasoned oak, and stone-walled gardens where annual fruit still ripened on monkish trees as if the trees remembered a language their keepers had forgotten.

“I am sure you will agree with me, Ma,” he said at last, “that the first thing to be done is to put these young people as much at their ease as possible. There is nothing disinterested in the notion, because we cannot be at our ease with them unless they are at their ease with us. Now, Jasper’s nephew is down here at present; and like takes to like, and youth takes to youth. He is a cordial young fellow, and we will have him to meet the brother and sister at dinner. That’s three. We can’t think of asking him, without asking Jasper. That’s four. Add Miss Twinkleton and the fairy bride that is to be, and that’s six. Add our two selves, and that’s eight. Would eight at a friendly dinner at all put you out, Ma?”

“Nine would, Sept,” returned the old lady, visibly nervous.

“My dear Ma, I particularise eight.”

“The exact size of the table and the room, my dear,” she said, as if numbers were not arithmetic but architecture.

So it was settled. When Septimus called with his mother on Miss Twinkleton to arrange for Miss Helena Landless’s reception at the Nuns’ House, the two other invitations having reference to that establishment were proffered and accepted. Miss Twinkleton did indeed cast a glance at her globes—as if regretting they were not formed to be taken out into society—but reconciled herself to leaving them behind. Messages were dispatched to the philanthropist, instructions given for the departure and arrival in good time for dinner; and, in Minor Canon Corner, stock for soup began to make the very air fragrant, as if the house itself were practicing hospitality in advance.

In those days there was no railway to Cloisterham, and Mr. Sapsea said there never would be. Mr. Sapsea said more: he said there never should be. (And yet, marvellous to consider, later times would bring express trains that did not think Cloisterham worth stopping at, but yelled and whirled through it on larger errands—leaving only dust, and a kind of insult, as testimony against its insignificance.) For now, Cloisterham’s traffic, already unsettled by some remote fragment of a Main Line to somewhere else, came sneaking in by a back stable-way for many years labelled at the corner, Beware of the Dog.

To that ignominious avenue Septimus repaired, awaiting the daily omnibus between Cloisterham and external mankind: a short, squat vehicle with a disproportionate heap of luggage on the roof, like a little Elephant with infinitely too much Castle.

As it lumbered up, he could hardly see anything else of it for a large outside passenger on the box-seat, elbows squared, hands on knees, compressing the driver into a most uncomfortably small compass. The man glowered about him with a strongly-marked face, and his very presence seemed to raise the volume of the street, as if he carried his own thunder and compelled the air to be louder.

“Is this Cloisterham?” demanded the passenger, in a tremendous voice.

“It is,” replied the driver, rubbing himself as if he ached, after throwing the reins to the ostler. “And I never was so glad to see it.”

“Tell your master to make his box-seat wider, then,” returned the passenger. “Your master is morally bound—and ought to be legally, under ruinous penalties—to provide for the comfort of his fellow-man.”

The driver began a superficial perquisition into his own skeleton with both palms, which did not improve his temper.

“Have I sat upon you?” asked the passenger.

“You have,” said the driver, as if he did not like it at all.

“Take that card, my friend.”

“I think I won’t deprive you on it,” returned the driver, casting his eyes over the card without taking it. “What’s the good of it to me?”

“Be a Member of that Society,” said the passenger.

“What shall I get by it?” asked the driver.

“Brotherhood,” returned the passenger, in the same ferocious key.

“Thankee,” said the driver, very deliberately, climbing down. “My mother was contented with myself, and so am I. I don’t want no brothers.”

“But you must have them,” replied the passenger, descending too. “Whether you like it or not. I am your brother.”

“I say!” expostulated the driver, growing chafed. “Not too fur! The worm will, when—”

Here Septimus interposed, aside, in a friendly voice that was itself a kind of soothing sound in the little street. “Joe, Joe, Joe! Don’t forget yourself, Joe, my good fellow!” And then, when Joe peaceably touched his hat, Septimus addressed the passenger with: “Mr. Honeythunder?”

“That is my name, sir.”

“My name is Crisparkle.”

“Reverend Mr. Septimus? Glad to see you, sir. Neville and Helena are inside. Having a little succumbed of late, under the pressure of my public labours, I thought I would take a mouthful of fresh air, and come down with them, and return at night. So you are the Reverend Mr. Septimus, are you?”

He surveyed Septimus with disappointment, and twisted a double eye-glass by its ribbon as if he were roasting it, but not otherwise using it.

“Hah! I expected to see you older, sir.”

“I hope you will,” replied Septimus, good-humouredly.

“Eh?” demanded Mr. Honeythunder.

“Only a poor little joke. Not worth repeating.”

“Joke? Ay; I never see a joke,” Mr. Honeythunder retorted, frowning. “A joke is wasted upon me, sir. Where are they? Helena and Neville, come here! Mr. Crisparkle has come down to meet you.”

They appeared: an unusually handsome, lithe young fellow, and an unusually handsome, lithe girl—much alike; both very dark and very rich in colour. She had almost the gipsy type; there was something untamed about them both, a hunter-and-huntress air, yet also the air of being the objects of the chase rather than the followers. Slender, supple, quick of eye and limb; half shy, half defiant; fierce of look; and on each, face and form alike, an indefinable pause that came and went—like the pause before a crouch or a bound.

Septimus invited Mr. Honeythunder to dinner with a troubled mind, for he could already feel, as one feels a draught before a door opens, the discomfiture it would bring upon his dear old china shepherdess at home. He gave his arm to Helena Landless. As they walked through the ancient streets, brother and sister took great delight in what he pointed out—Cathedral, monastery ruin, old stones speaking in their worn dialect of chisels and prayers—and they wondered much, as if they had been beautiful barbaric captives brought from some wild tropical dominion where the birds cried in another key and the herbs had other names.

Mr. Honeythunder walked in the middle of the road, shouldering the natives out of his way, and loudly developing a scheme for making a raid on all the unemployed persons in the United Kingdom, laying them every one by the heels in jail, and forcing them, on pain of prompt extermination, to become philanthropists. His voice rolled through Cloisterham’s mild soundscape like a cartload of iron bars; it bruised the quiet, and seemed to make even the rooks listen with offence.

Mrs. Crisparkle had need of her own share of philanthropy when she beheld this very large and very loud excrescence on the little party. Always something in the nature of a boil upon the face of society, Mr. Honeythunder expanded into an inflammatory wen in Minor Canon Corner.

Though it was not literally true—as public unbelievers facetiously charged—that he called aloud to his fellow-creatures, “Curse your souls and bodies, come here and be blessed!” still his philanthropy was of that gunpowderous sort whose difference from animosity was hard to determine. You were to abolish military force, but first bring all commanding officers who had done their duty to trial by court-martial for that offence, and shoot them. You were to abolish war, but make converts by making war upon them and charging them with loving war as the apple of their eye. You were to have no capital punishment, but first sweep off the face of the earth all legislators, jurists, and judges who differed. You were to have universal concord, and get it by eliminating all who wouldn’t—or conscientiously couldn’t—be concordant. You were to love your brother as yourself, but only after an indefinite interval of maligning him (very much as if you hated him) and calling him all manner of names.

Above all things, you were to do nothing in private, or on your own account. You were to go to the offices of the Haven of Philanthropy and put your name down as a Member and a Professing Philanthropist; then pay your subscription, receive your card, your riband and medal, and evermore live upon a platform—evermore say what Mr. Honeythunder said, what the Treasurer said, what the sub-Treasurer said, what the Committee said, what the sub-Committee said, what the Secretary said, what the Vice-Secretary said—until your own voice became merely the echo of a dominant lexicon. In that lexicon, everybody outside the Haven was defined by resolutions unanimously carried, under hand and seal, to the effect: “That this assembled Body of Professing Philanthropists views, with indignant scorn and contempt, not unmixed with utter detestation and loathing abhorrence—” in short, the baseness of all those who did not belong to it; and pledged itself to make as many obnoxious statements as possible about them, without being at all particular as to facts. It was language as cudgel, language as badge; and one could hear in it the slow erosion of whatever small, local, home-spoken tenderness people might otherwise have used—those unrecorded dialect-words that fit a hearth the way a well-worn spoon fits a hand.

Dinner, meant to be a gentle net cast to catch two shy young creatures and draw them into ease, became a most doleful breakdown. The soup, for all its honest fragrance—long-simmered bones giving up their comfort, onions melting to sweetness, herbs releasing their hidden green—could not repair the table’s ruined harmony. Mr. Honeythunder deranged the symmetry at once: he planted himself where waiting was impeded, blocked the thoroughfare, and drove Mr. Tope (assisting the parlour-maid) to the verge of distraction by passing plates and dishes on over his own head as if service were a moral obstacle course.

Nobody could talk to anybody, because Mr. Honeythunder held forth to everybody at once, as if the company had no individual existence but were a Meeting. He impounded Septimus as an official personage to be addressed—a human peg on which to hang his oratorical hat—and fell into the exasperating habit, common among such speakers, of impersonating him as a wicked and weak opponent. He would cry, “And will you, sir, now stultify yourself by telling me—” when the innocent Minor Canon had not opened his lips, nor meant to. Or he would thunder:

“Now see, sir, to what a position you are reduced. I will leave you no escape. After exhausting all the resources of fraud and falsehood, during years upon years; after exhibiting a combination of dastardly meanness with ensanguined daring, such as the world has not often witnessed; you have now the hypocrisy to bend the knee before the most degraded of mankind, and to sue and whine and howl for mercy!”

At which, Septimus would look partly indignant, partly perplexed—like a man suddenly accused of crimes in a language he had never learned—while his worthy mother sat bridling, with tears in her bright eyes. The remainder of the party lapsed into a sort of gelatinous state, in which there was no flavour or solidity and very little resistance. Even the room’s kind sounds—the clink of cutlery, the pour of wine—seemed to shrink, as if the house itself were trying to keep small under the booming oppression.

Yet when at last Mr. Honeythunder’s departure began to impend, the gush of philanthropy that burst forth must have been highly gratifying to his feelings. His coffee was produced by the special activity of Mr. Tope a full hour before he wanted it. Septimus sat with his watch in his hand for about the same period, lest the man should overstay his time. The four young people were unanimous in believing the Cathedral clock struck three-quarters when it struck but one. Miss Twinkleton estimated the distance to the omnibus at five-and-twenty minutes’ walk when it was really five. The affectionate kindness of the whole circle hustled Mr. Honeythunder into his greatcoat and shoved him out into the moonlight as if he were a fugitive traitor with whom they sympathised and a troop of horse were at the back door. Septimus and his new charge, escorting him to the omnibus, were so fervent in their apprehensions of his catching cold that they shut him up in it instantly and left him there with still half-an-hour to spare—Cloisterham’s quiet closing round the vehicle again the moment his tremendous voice was corked inside, like a kitchen settling back into its own proper murmurs after a pot has boiled over.
