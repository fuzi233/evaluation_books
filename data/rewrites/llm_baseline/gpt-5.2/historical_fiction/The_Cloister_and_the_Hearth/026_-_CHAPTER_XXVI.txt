The next afternoon brought a thin, grey light, and with it a Gerard altered. The swimming in his head was gone; in its place came a fretful heat, as if his very thoughts had been salted and left too long by the fire. He lay picking at the linen, inventing miseries out of shadows, until in one of those restless fancies he fixed upon a single desire.

“Denys,” he whispered, swallowing with pain, “get me a lemon. I would suck it. My mouth is ashes.”

Denys—who had marched through camps hard as iron, and yet, by some quiet miracle of companionship, had turned tender as an old grandsire over this sick youth—started up at once.

“A lemon? Thou shalt have lemons in the twinkling of a quart-pot,” he said, as if he were calling for salt in a kitchen, not a fruit in a northern town. “Set thy mind at ease.”

He was out, boot-heels fading down the stair.

Lemons, however, were no common thing there: not like onions that hang in every market, or cabbages you could smell before you saw. Those sharp golden suns came by long road and longer sea, and changed hands like relics. Denys was gone so long that Gerard’s thirst grew spiteful; he listened to every footfall as if it were water being poured, and each time it passed his door he clenched the sheet.

At last the latch gave softly.

Gerard lifted his head, ready to snap at Denys for delay—and saw, not Denys at all, but an old man who entered as solemnly as if he came with the Host.

He wore a long sober gown, yet trimmed richly with fur at the edges; cherry-coloured hose and pointed shoes; a sword hung at his side in a morocco sheath. About his neck rose a ruff starched into cruel furrows, stiffened by hidden wood so that it held his head like a yoke. Above a majestic white beard sat a four-cornered cap bordered in fur. Behind him followed a boy with a basket carried in both hands; and the basket was a little portable apothecary—phials that caught what light there was, rolls of lint, instruments of iron that glimmered like fish-bones.

The old gentleman drew near, and, leaning to the bed with a practiced softness of step and voice, said under his breath, “How is’t with thee, my son?”

Gerard, schooled to gratitude even when peevish, answered, “My wound pains me little now, sir; but my throat is parched, and my head lies heavy.”

“A wound! They told me not of that. Let me see.” The doctor’s fingers went at once, deft and curious. “Ay, ay—a good clean bite. The mastiff had sound teeth that took this out, I warrant me.” And in his tone there was almost a fond respect, as if the brute’s jaw were the true artist and he but the interpreter.

“This must be cauterized forthwith,” he continued briskly, “or we shall have thee starting back from water and turning somersaults in bed under our hands. ’Tis the year for raving curs, and one hath done thy business; but we will baffle him yet. Urchin—go heat thine iron.”

“But, sir,” Gerard put in, with that weary politeness sick men use when they are being hurried, “’twas no dog, but a bear.”

“A bear!” The physician drew himself up, offended, as if a patient had contradicted a Gospel. “Young man, think what thou sayest. ’Tis ill jesting with the man of art who brings his grey hairs and long study to heal thee. A bear, quotha! Hadst thou dissected as many bears as I, or the tithe, and drawn their teeth to keep thine hand in, thou wouldst know no bear’s jaw ever made this foolish trifling wound. I tell thee ’twas a dog—nay, since thou puttest me to it, I deny ’twas even a dog of magnitude; it was one of these little furious curs that run devious, biting each manly leg and laying its wearer low—but for me and my learned brethren, who stay the mischief with knife and cautery.”

“Alas, sir,” said Gerard, trying not to lose patience, “when said I ’twas a bear’s jaw? I said, ‘a bear’: it was his paw.”

“And why didst not tell me that at once?”

“Because you kept telling me instead,” Gerard murmured—then bit his tongue for having spoken at all.

“Never conceal aught from thy leech, young man,” the doctor went on, as if he had not heard him. He was a torrent in a narrow channel: swift, loud, and heedless of stones. “This is ill business. All horny excrescences of animals—to wit, claws of tigers, panthers, badgers, cats, bears, and the like, and horn of deer, and nails of humans, especially children—are imbued with direst poison. Thou hadst better have been bitten by a cur than scratched by bear. Yet we will do what may be done. A good biting cataplasm for thy leg; meantime keep we the body cool. Put out thy tongue—good—fever. Pulse—good—fever. I ordain flebotomy, and on the instant.”

“Flebotomy?” Gerard repeated, and the word tasted sour in his mouth, like wine gone to vinegar. “That is bloodletting. Humph. Well—if it be sure to cure, for I will not lie idle here.”

The physician assured him, with the confidence of a man who has never doubted himself long enough to learn fear, that it was infallible—especially in this case.

“Hans,” he said to the boy, “go fetch the things needful, and I will entertain the patient meantime with reasons.”

And now the old man began to lecture as if Gerard’s sickbed were a chair of medicine, and the room a disputation hall. He spoke of blood as of stew set too close to the coals: it grew hot, distempered, more or less poisonous; draw off a portion of the corrupted broth, and Nature would be forced to make a fresher pot. Bleeding cooled and purified, and so was a specific in all diseases, “for all diseases are febrile, whatever empirics may say.”

“But think not,” he added, warming to his own voice, “that it suffices to bleed; any paltry barber can open a vein—though not all can close it again. The art is to know what vein to empty for what disease. T’other day they brought me one tormented with earache. I let him blood in the right thigh, and away flew his earache. By-the-by, he hath died since then. Another came with toothache. I bled him behind the ear and relieved him in a jiffy. He is also since dead, as it happens. I bled our bailiff between thumb and forefinger for rheumatism. Presently he comes with a headache and drumming in the ears and holds out his hand over the basin; but I smiled at his folly and bled him in the left ankle sore against his will, and made his head as light as a nut.”

Gerard lay and listened as one listens to church bells when fevered: the sound is beautiful and tormenting, each peal striking not only the air but the bone. Beyond the window the town had its own music—the scrape of carts, the holler of children, a hammer on metal—yet inside the chamber the doctor’s voice ruled everything, booming and clacking, drowning even the faint swallow of Gerard’s parched throat.

Then, like a cook who cannot name a sauce without reciting all the kitchens of Christendom, the physician diverged into schools and authorities. Arabia and Greece marched through the room in his phrases; Avicenna and Rhazes stood and bowed; Hippocrates entered with bandages; Aristotle named the aorta as if he had kneaded it out of dough. The doctor’s words were thick with learned titles—rich, heavy, difficult to digest—like meat overspiced to hide its age.

“Surely, sir,” Gerard ventured at last, in humble pain, “the Almighty gave us all that is in our bodies, and not Aristotle, nor any Grecian man.”

“Child!” snapped the physician. “Of course He gave us the thing; but Aristotle did more—he gave us the name. Comprehension is your only true possession. Are you answered?”

“I am put to silence, sir.”

“And that is better still; for garrulous patients are ill to cure—especially in fever.” His voice swelled again into the litany of anatomists, nerves, vessels, pia mater, milk-veins—until the room seemed packed with invisible parts being discovered and christened by men long dead.

Hans returned with a basin and other gear; another boy—or perhaps the same, moving with quick practiced hands—had a little chafer, and blew it so that coals glowed red as pomegranate seeds. The air took on that faint choking smell of heated iron and smouldering cloth: a smell that makes the skin remember pain before it arrives.

It was in this posture—Gerard’s ankle half-bared, the basin ready, the coals brightening—that Denys came back at last with lemons, their skins dimpled, pale-gold, and fragrant as a southern memory. He paused in the doorway, startled by the tableau.

“What sport is toward?” he asked, brows lifted.

Gerard coloured and said, trying to make it small, “The learned doctor is going to flebotomize me and cauterize me; that is all.”

“Ay, indeed,” said Denys, looking from basin to chafer. “And yon imp, what bloweth he hot coals for?”

“What should it be for,” the doctor answered Gerard, not Denys, “but to cauterize the vein when opened and the poisonous blood let free? ’Tis the only safe way. Avicenna indeed recommends a ligature, but how it is to be done he saith not, nor knew he himself, nor any of the spawn of Ishmael. For me, I have no faith in tricksy expedients; and take this for a safe principle: whatever an Arab or Arabist says is right, must be wrong.”

“Oh, I see now,” said Denys, and his voice sharpened like a knife on a stone. “And art thou so simple as to let him put hot iron to thy living flesh? Didst ever keep thy little finger but ten moments in a candle? And this will be as many minutes. Art not content to burn in purgatory after thy death? Must thou buy a foretaste on’t here?”

“I never thought of that,” Gerard said gravely, because he had indeed been lulled by syllables. “The good doctor spake not of burning, but of cautery. To be sure ’tis all one—but cautery sounds not so fearful as burning.”

“Imbecile!” Denys broke out. “That is their art: to confound a plain man with dark words, till his hissing flesh tells him their meaning. Now listen to what I have seen.” And he launched into the bitter witness of battlefields: how leeches bleed men already bleeding; how weakness follows fever, and the poor wounded needs every drop; how veterans die under lancet and fire, while those far from chirurgeons live by chance and frost and ignorance. His voice rose and filled the chamber; each sentence struck the air like the crack of a whip.

The physician, deaf to anything but opposition, roared back, calling Denys a brute whose trade was killing not curing, and cursed the meddling of unlearned men. Denys answered with mock courtesy, naming himself a man of blood indeed, but claiming the doctor spilt more lives with a bodkin than soldiers with swords. The doctor thundered that a sickroom was no place for jesting; Denys retorted it was no place for bawling.

Gerard’s head throbbed with the noise; each shout was a hammer on his temples. He had lived among chants and choir-like calm; he knew what sound could do to a soul. Here was a kind of noise that did not merely offend the ear—it undid the mind, took hold of the nerves and shook them, as a rough hand shakes a bell to make it clang too loud.

When the physician began boasting of Montpellier—of dririmancy, scatomancy, anatomy—and how authorities “winked,” and how twice a year a living condemned criminal was sent for science to “deal with as science asks,” Denys bellowed blasphemies in disgust. Gerard actually squeaked, clapped fingers to his ears, then—suddenly stung past endurance—dragged them away and shouted, with a fury that surprised even him:

“You great roaring, blaspheming bull of Basan, hold your noisy tongue!”

Denys fell contrite at once, but the doctor flowed on, grandly, describing inoculations and watched diseases—words that made suffering into a lesson, as if men were pigs to be fattened and opened for knowledge.

The wrangling swelled again, and with it the threat of steel: Denys, pale now, folded his arms and said coldly that if the physician pricked Gerard, his sword-hilt would knock against the man’s ribs.

Gerard, exhausted by their clamor, lifted a hand.

“Now,” he said, very quietly, “as all this is about me, give me leave to say a word.”

The stillness that fell was immediate; even the coals seemed to hush, though they continued to glow. Gerard spoke with a gentleness that shamed the room.

“I doubt not you both mean me well; but you assassinate me between you. Calmness and quiet are everything to me; but you are like two dogs growling over a bone. And in sooth, bone I should be, did this uproar last long.

“First, venerable sir, I thank you for coming to see me, whether from humanity or in the way of honest gain; all trades must live.

“Your learning seems great, to me at least, and your age vouches experience.

“You say you have bled many, and of these many, many have not died thereafter, but lived and done well. I must needs believe you.”

The physician bowed; Denys made a sound between scorn and impatience.

“Others, you say, you have bled—and they are dead. I must needs believe you.

“Denys knows few things compared with you, but he knows them well. He is not given to conjecture; this I myself have noted. He says he has seen the fevered and blooded for the most part die; the fevered and not blooded live. I must needs believe him.

“Here, then, all is doubt.

“But thus much is certain: if I be bled, I must pay you a fee, and be burnt and excruciated with a hot iron, who am no felon.

“Pay a certain price in money and anguish for a doubtful remedy—that will I never.

“Next to money and ease, peace and quiet are certain goods, above all in a sick-room; but ’twould seem men cannot argue medicine without heat and raised voices. Therefore, sir, I will essay a little sleep; and Denys will go forth and gaze on the females of the place; and I will keep you no longer from those who can afford to lay out blood and money in flebotomy and cautery.”

The physician’s face, which had held itself in dignified patience, reddened and tightened. He drew himself up as if to leave with lofty silence—the only dignity left him—turned, and strode to the door with Hans and the basket at his heels.

At the threshold his anger choked him; he swelled, burst, and whirled back, mouth open. Hans and the basket had to dodge like ducklings before a cart.

“Ah! You reject my skill, you scorn my art. My revenge shall be to leave you to yourself. Lost idiot, take your last look at me—and at the sun. Your blood be on your head!”

He stamped away—then at the door turned again, rage dragging him as a hook drags a fish.

“In twelve hours at furthest,” he cried, “you will be in the secondary stage of fever. Your head will split. Your carotids will thump. Aha! And let but a pin fall, you will jump to the ceiling. Then send for me—and I’ll not come!”

Again he went—again he returned, fury gathering like wind that cannot find an outlet.

“Next will come—CRAMPS of the STOMACH. Aha!

“Then—BILIOUS VOMIT. Aha!

“Then—COLD SWEAT, and DEADLY STUPOR.

“Then—CONFUSION OF ALL THE SENSES.

“Then—BLOODY VOMIT.

“And after that nothing can save you—not even I; and if I could I would not—and so farewell!”

Even Denys, who had faced cannon, changed colour at so precise a curse. Gerard’s eyes, however, lit with a kind of desperate rage—not at the prophecy, but at the noise, the pounding, the assault on his already battered head. He seized his hard bolster as a man seizes a shield.

This motion brought the physician back yet again, as if the sight of resistance was wine to his temper.

“And after that—MADNESS!

“And after that—BLACK VOMIT!

“And then—CONVULSIONS!

“And then—THAT CESSATION OF ALL VITAL FUNCTIONS THE VULGAR CALL ‘DEATH,’ for which thank your own Satanic folly and insolence. Farewell!”

He turned, but could not let the door go without one last barb.

“And think not to be buried in any Christian church-yard; for the bailiff is my good friend, and I shall tell him how and why you died: felo de se! felo de se! Farewell!”

That did it. Gerard sprang to his feet on the bed with a sudden strength, unnatural and fierce, as if the soul itself lent muscle to the body for one instant. His face was white with pain and fury, his hearing raw as flayed skin. The physician, seeing him so moved, came back at him with fist shaking to launch some final thunder.

Gerard hurled the bolster straight into his face.

The old man went down like a felled ox. His falling head struck the boy; the boy’s skull cracked against his master’s mass; and both went crashing into the basket. Phials shattered with bright, sharp noises, and odors burst out—the sour bite of spirits, the bitter sweetness of tinctures, the rank medicinal stink that clings to a leech’s hands. The doctor and boy landed wedged absurdly, like grotesque specimens arranged by size: the smaller flung aside, squatting and howling.

Then the physician’s face changed from anger to horror. He uttered a screech more unearthly than all his threats, and kicked and struggled with astonishing agility for so old a man.

He was sitting on the hot coals.

They had singed the cloth; now they bit into flesh. He writhed but could not free himself from the basket’s grip. The basket rolled sideways with him, and there came a great hiss as spilled compounds met the heat. The sickroom filled with steam and stench—burning wool, scorched skin, sharp alcohol, and the sickly-sweet rot of herbs kept too long.

Gerard’s rage broke at once into humane alarm. He leapt down, ignoring the pull of his wound, and with Denys’s help wrenched off the tight basket—not without struggle, for it clung like a crab. The doctor lay on his face, groaning, his handsomely singed palms and backside slaked a moment too late by his own villainous mixtures. Those same mixtures, spilled and splattered, had painted his sober robe in stains of green and violet and rusty red, gaudier than any jester’s and far less neat.

Gerard and Denys raised him up between them. The physician gasped, half-choked with pain and humiliation, his stiff ruff askew, his beard damp with tears and spilt physic. Gerard, still trembling with the aftershock of noise and fury, tried to soothe him.

“Courage, man,” he said, with a faint, bitter edge the room could not help but hear. “’Tis but cautery—balm of Gilead. Why, you recommended it but now to my comrade here.”
