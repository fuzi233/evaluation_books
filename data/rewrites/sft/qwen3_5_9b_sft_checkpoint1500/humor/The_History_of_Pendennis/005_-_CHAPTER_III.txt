Arthur Pendennis was about sixteen when his reign officially commenced. To the uninitiated eye, he possessed a "dumpy" figure, though his mother insisted on styling it "neat." His hair, a healthy brown that shimmered like gold in the sun, framed a round, rosy, and freckled countenance radiating good humor. Nature eventually granted him the whiskers he had so ardently coveted, painting them in a distinctly reddish hue. While not conventionally beautiful, his frank expression and merry laughter, delivered from honest blue eyes, secured him the status of the county's pride in Mrs. Pendennis's estimation. Between the ages of sixteen and eighteen, he grew from five feet six to five feet eight inches, pausing abruptly at this altitude. His mother, however, remained baffled by the mathematics of his growth, noting with concern that he had outstripped his father by a full three inches. One might reasonably ask if any man possesses the audacity to tower so significantly above his progenitor.

School proved a dismal failure for young Arthur. The discipline of the establishment clashed violently with his spirit, and he harbored a profound preference for domestic comfort. Although the question of his return was vigorously debated, and his uncle advocated for a return to the classroom, the verdict was sealed by medical authority. The Doctor argued that a thorough knowledge of Greek plays was essential for future success, but Pen adroitly exploited his mother's sensitivities. He painted a vivid picture of Greyfriars School as a den of dangerous wildlings, causing the timid soul to immediately capitulate to his plea to remain at home.

Consequently, Pen's uncle offered to leverage his influence with His Royal Highness, the Commander-in-Chief, who was notably kind to the boy. The proposal was to secure a commission in the Foot Guards. Pen's heart leapt with joy; he had recently attended the band playing at St. James's Park on a Sunday outing. There, amidst the crimson and gold of the regiments, he had witnessed Tom Ricketts, a former tormentor from the fourth form who usually wore jackets so comically tight they served as targets for older boys. Now, Tom stood aglow in scarlet, topped with an immense bearskin cap, staggering beneath the weight of the colors. Having once cut Tom over the back with a hockey-stick last term, Pen now saw him rallying around the national flag, surrounded by bayonets and warriors with chin-tufts and Waterloo medals. Tom recognized him and bestowed a patronizing nod. Standing in the center of the square, talking familiarly to giants, Pen realized what he would give to wear such epaulettes and join such a glorious service.

However, Helen Pendennis, when the prospect of the army was broached, adopted a visage of pure terror. She declared that while she respected differing opinions, a Christian had no right to make the army a profession. She reminded Pen that Mr. Pendennis would never have allowed his son to become a soldier and expressed that she would be deeply unhappy if he pursued such a path. Pen, possessing a generosity that bordered on self-abnegation and a determination never to deliberately distress his mother, instantly surrendered his vision. He presented her with a gift of sorts: the forfeiture of his red coat, his epaulettes, and his fervent ardor for military glory.

Her reaction was to deem him the noblest creature in the world. Meanwhile, Major Pendennis, upon acknowledging and refusing the commission via letter, replied with curt anger, dismissing his nephew as merely "spooney." Yet, peace returned when the holidays arrived. During Christmas, when the Major descended to Fairoaks, Pen showcased his talents in hunting. He rode a spirited mare with uncommon pluck and grace, clearing fences with cool judgment rather than reckless bravado. He wrote enthusiastic letters to his schoolmates detailing his top-boots and exploits across the country, secretly beginning to envision a scarlet coat. His mother, though she believed it would become him remarkably well, spent hours in anguish during his absences, expecting to receive news of him arriving home on a shutter.

Despite these diversions, Pen did not neglect his intellectual pursuits entirely. He possessed a natural taste for literature that extended far beyond his curriculum, refusing to drink from the waters of knowledge only when forced. At home, he devoured everything from *Inchbald's Theatre* to *White's Farriery*. At Clavering, he unearthed an old cargo of French novels, reading them with intense devotion. He frequently perched atop the highest step of Doctor Portman's library, a folio balanced on his knees, whether tackling Hakluyt's Travels, Hobbes's *Leviathan*, Augustine's works, or Chaucer's poetry. His friendship with the Vicar was strong, and it was from the Reverend that Pen inherited his lifelong appreciation for port wine. As for Mrs. Portman, who was not jealous despite her husband's admitted affection for Mrs. Pendennis, her sole sorrow was that her daughter, Minny, was too old for Pen. At that juncture, Miss Myra Portman was only two years younger than Pen's mother and weighed as much as both mothers combined.

One might consider these details insipid, but reflect upon your own youth. How was it? I cherish the image of a well-nurtured boy: brave, gentle, warm-hearted, loving, and gazing upon the world with kind, honest eyes. Life wears brighter colors then, and the enjoyment is profound, though often forgotten until the time has passed.

To prevent the idleness predicted by the Doctor of the Cistercians, Mr. Smirke, the Vicar's curate, was hired at a liberal salary to accompany Pen daily. Smirke was a man of impeccable manners, sporting a curl on his fair forehead and tying his cravat with a melancholy grace. He was a competent scholar and mathematician, teaching Pen as much as the boy wished to learn—which was surprisingly little. Pen quickly measured his tutor, finding him absurdly inept at horseback riding. When Smirke mounted Pen's mare, Rebecca (named for Pen's favorite heroine), he turned his toes out with ridiculous flair, leaving a gaping space between his knees and the saddle. Any lad with a sense of humor could not possibly respect such an equestrian. Pen nearly terrified Smirke to death by forcing him onto Rebecca and leading him over a common where the county fox-hounds, led by the staunch sportsman Mr. Hardhead, happened to be hunting. Smirke, astride Rebecca, astounded the hounds and disgusted the huntsman, laming one of the dogs by persisting in riding among the pack and earning a lecture from the huntsman so energetic it rivaled any oration he had heard since leaving the bargemen on the banks of the Isis.

Smirke confided his Latin and English poems to his pupil, presenting Mrs. Pendennis with a volume of his English verse, printed at Clapham. Together, they rattled through ancient poets at a brisk, galloping pace, a stark contrast to the slow, methodical grubbing of the Cistercian scholars who dissected every word. Pen, unwilling to halt, compelled his tutor to construe whenever he stumbled, thereby galloping through the *Iliad*, the *Odyssey*, the tragedians, and the wicked Aristophanes, whom Pen declared the greatest poet of all. Though he covered considerable ground, he forgot it almost entirely in later life, retaining only a vague recollection akin to a Member of Parliament keeping two or three quotations or a reviewer hinting at a smattering of Greek for decency's sake. Despite this, the superstition of a gentleman's education was faithfully transmitted from generation to generation.

Beyond the ancients, Pen approached English literature with great gusto. Smirke sighed and shook his head regarding Byron and Moore, but Pen was a sworn devotee. He memorized them, taking little Laura into the window to recite lines like "Zuleika, I am not thy brother" in tones so tragic that the solemn little maid widened her eyes in astonishment. She sat sewing at her mother's knee, listening to Pen read aloud through the night, failing to comprehend a single word. He read Shakespeare to his mother (who claimed to like it but clearly did not), Byron, Pope, and his favorite *Lalla Rookh*, which pleased her indifferently. However, Bishop Heber and Mrs. Hemans, above all, caused her to melt into her pocket-handkerchief as Pen recited them in his kind, boyish voice. The *Christian Year*, which appeared around this time, became a shared secret between mother and son, whispered to with awe. The faint, solemn church-music echoed in his memory, filling him with hope and devoid of doubt as bells rang on Sunday mornings.

During this era, Pen debuted in the Poets' Corner of the *County Chronicle* with verses of which he was perfectly satisfied. These included "To a Tear," "On the Anniversary of the Battle of Waterloo," "To Madame Caradori," and "On Saint Bartholomew's Day"—a vehement denunciation of Popery warning Protestants against Catholic emancipation. These masterpieces, signed 'N.E.P.', are presumably preserved by Mrs. Pendennis today alongside his first socks, haircut, and bottles. He rode Rebecca over Dumpling Downs or into the county town of Chatteris, spouting his own poetry with a Byronic afflatus.
