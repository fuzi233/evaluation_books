The Carnival's tumult had left Romola with but one immediate burden—the care of her aging cousin Brigida, whose resignation to time’s passage wavered like a candle in the wind. Yet those revelries had ushered in a Lenten season that thrummed with tension, a period where her mind and hands were ceaselessly occupied.

Bernardo del Nero had been elected Gonfaloniere, a victory wrung from the political fray by the Medicean party. The triumph should have been a relief, but instead, it gnawed at Romola like a silent warning. Each dawn, as pale light crept into her chamber, she carried the weight of foreboding into Florence’s streets, where hunger and murmurs of discontent festered. Only in the echoing vault of the Duomo did the dread loosen its grip—there, Savonarola’s voice, defiant against the looming threat of excommunication, filled the air with a battle cry that seemed to transcend the city’s walls.

He preached of a Church rotting from within, of a coming reckoning, of renewal clawing its way through corruption. At times, he spoke as if he would be the voice to shake Christendom awake; at others, as though his path led only to martyrdom. To Romola, whose spirit burned with the same fervor, his words were a clarion call, a reason to endure the weariness of tending the sick, the hungry, the disillusioned. It was not mere pity that drove her now, but the belief in something greater—a fight for a future where mercy might someday bear fruit.

Yet the Frate’s sermons carried discordant notes. Once, he had preached unity, forgiveness; now, his tone bristled with defiance. Visions and prophecies, once dismissed as distractions, now spilled from his lips as proof of divine favor. The promise of miracles, once scorned, became his shield. And threading through his righteous fury was a thread of something darker—personal grievance, the sting of betrayal sharpening his threats against Florence’s enemies.

Romola flinched at the change, but to another listener, those thundering denunciations were the only truth that mattered. Baldassarre, drawn like a shadow to the Duomo, drank in every word. Justice, it seemed, had a voice—and he waited, watching Romola as she in turn watched for him, each glance a silent omen.

Then came Camilla Rucellai’s summons.

Romola had no love for the woman, whose shrill prophecies and ecstatic visions grated like broken glass. Worse still, rumors swirled that Camilla had turned her “revelations” against Bernardo. Romola nearly refused—what use were more whispers of doom? But habit, that stern taskmaster, overruled her reluctance. Duty demanded she go.

She found Camilla in a chamber mimicking a nun’s cell, her fingers clutching, her voice a fevered whisper. There had been a vision, she insisted—Romola’s own angel had revealed that she held secrets about Bernardo, secrets that could save Florence. The words tumbled higher, wilder, until they became a command: Romola must denounce her godfather, must stand with God’s chosen against the enemy.

Revulsion surged. Romola tore free, but Camilla’s grip was relentless. More visions spilled forth—Christ Himself, she claimed, had ordered Bernardo’s death, hurled from the Palazzo’s windows. Even Savonarola, she shrilled, had not dared deny it.

“Let me go!” Romola’s voice was a whip-crack. “God grant you are mad—else you are vile!”

She wrenched away, fleeing until the Badia’s quiet swallowed the street’s clamor. Kneeling before Filippino Lippi’s tranquil Virgin, she sought stillness, but her mind churned. Was this the fruit of Savonarola’s cause? Visions twisted into warrants for murder? She knew he would never endorse such madness—yet why did he not silence these fanatics?

The answer struck like a blow: because his own authority rested on the same ground. To condemn Camilla was to undermine himself.

The realization left her hollow. The Frate’s teachings had once seemed a beacon, but now they felt shackled by the very divisions they decried. Her heart recoiled, clinging instead to Bernardo—to his steady wisdom, his untheoretical goodness. Yet even there, dread lurked. If whispers of Medici plots were true, then his loyalty was a crime.

Caught between two betrayals, Romola knelt, adrift. Faith had led her here, but now it offered no harbor. Only action remained—the small, immediate kindnesses she could still perform.

She rose, resolved to return to her work—and found Baldassarre standing before her, his gaze a silent storm.
