For a fleeting moment, my only desire was to remain in the company of the Little Ones, to let the days dissolve in their quiet presence. But soon, a restless current stirred within me. A vague, insistent thought took root: *I ought to be doing something.* I was not meant merely to be the fattener of boors, a passive observer in a world that demanded discovery. This was a marvellous realm, and it was my duty to uncover its laws. If I wished to repay the children's boundless goodness, I had to learn more of them than their simple words could convey—and to do that, I needed freedom. Surely, no suppression of growth could be essential to their loveliness, their truth, or their purity! In no conceivable world could such a discord exist between a constitution and its natural outcome; life and law could not be so at variance that perfection required thwarting development. Yet, the growth of the Little Ones *was* arrested. Something interfered. What was it?

Lona appeared to be the eldest, though she seemed no older than fifteen, yet she had long overseen a multitude who, in semblance and behavior, were mere children regarding her as their mother. Were they growing at all? I doubted it. They possessed scarcely an idea of time, and knew nothing of their own age. Lona herself believed she had lived forever. Full of wisdom yet empty of knowledge, she was simultaneously their Love and their Law. But what I perceived as her ignorance might simply be my own lack of insight. Her singular anxiety was clear: she feared her Little Ones would grow and change into "bad giants." Their "good giant" was bound to do his best for them, yet without deeper knowledge of their nature or history, he could do nothing and thus felt compelled to leave. They remained as they were; they had not become dependent on me. They were still my protectors, not mine. My presence, I realized with a sinking heart, only brought them closer to danger from their idiotic neighbors. I longed to teach them mathematics, to help them write down the dainty melodies they murmured and forgot. But knowledge, I mused, could make bad people worse; it must make good people better. I was convinced they would learn math, but might they not also be taught to record their songs?

The conclusion was inevitable: I must rise and continue my travels, hoping to find an elucidation of the fortunes and destiny of these bewitching creatures.

Yet, my design did not pass swiftly into action until what now occurred.

Preparing them for my temporary absence, I spoke to them one day while working, confessing that I would long ago have fled the bad giants if not for my love of the Little Ones. As if by one accord, they rushed forward, crowding upon me. They scrambled over one another, climbed the tree, and dropped onto my head until I was nearly smothered. With three tiny ones in my arms, one clinging to each shoulder, another standing straight up on my head, and four or five holding me fast by the legs, while a multitude climbed and descended upon this chaotic mass, I was helpless, overwhelmed like a boulder by lava. Absorbed in the merry struggle, none of them saw my tyrant approaching until he was almost upon me. With a single cry of "Take care, good giant!" they scattered like mice, dropped like hedgehogs, and fled up the tree like squirrels. The very same moment, the bad giant struck, his club landing with a sharp blow around the stem, knocking me to the ground. The children later told me they had sent him "such a many bumps of big apples and stones" that he was frightened and ran blundering home.

When I regained consciousness, it was night. Above me hung a few pale stars waiting for the moon. I thought I was alone. My head throbbed, and a terrible thirst gripped me. Turning wearily to my side, the moment my ear touched the earth, I heard the gushing and gurgling of water. The soft noises made me groan with longing, and instantly I was surrounded by a multitude of silent children. Delicious little fruits visited my lips, arriving again and again until my thirst was quenched.

Then, I became aware of sounds I had never heard there before: the air was filled with little sobs.

I tried to sit up, and a pile of small bodies instantly heaped itself against my back. Struggling to my feet amidst much pushing and pulling, I found the Little Ones were wonderfully strong for their size.

"You must go away, good giant," they said. "When the bad giants see you hurt, they will all trample on you."

"I think I must," I answered.

"Go and grow strong, and come again," they urged.

"I will," I replied, and sat down.

"Indeed you must go at once!" whispered Lona, who had been supporting me and now knelt beside me.

"I listened at his door," said one of the bigger boys. "I heard the bad giant say to his wife that he had found you idle, talking to a lot of moles and squirrels, and when he beat you, they tried to kill him. He said you were a wizard, and they must knock you, or they would have no peace."

"I will go at once," I said, "and return as soon as I have found out what is needed to make you bigger and stronger."

"We don't want to be bigger," they answered, their faces grave. "We WON'T grow bad giants! We are strong now; you don't know how much strong!"

It was useless to hold out a prospect that held no attraction for them. I said nothing more, rose, and moved slowly up the slope of the valley. Instantly, they formed a long procession; some led the way, some walked beside me helping me, and the rest followed. They kept feeding me as we went.

"You are broken," they said, "and much red juice has run out of you: put some in."

When we reached the edge of the valley, the moon was just lifting her forehead over the rim of the horizon.

"She has come to take care of you, and show you the way," said Lona.

I questioned those around me as we walked and learned of a great place with a giant-girl for queen. When I asked if it was a city, they said they did not know. Neither could they tell how far off it was, in what direction, or the giant-girl's name. All they knew was that she hated the Little Ones and would like to kill them, though she could not find them. I asked how they knew this; Lona answered that she had always known it. If the giant-girl came to look for them, they must hide hard. When I told them I would go to ask her why she hated them, they cried out, "No, no! she will kill you, good giant; she will kill you! She is an awful bad-giant witch!"

I asked them where I was to go then. They told me that beyond the baby-forest, away where the moon came from, lay a smooth green country, pleasant to the feet, without rocks or trees. But when I asked how I was to set out for it, they replied, "The moon will tell you, we think."

They were taking me up the second branch of the river bed; when they saw the moon reach her height, they stopped to return.

"We have never gone so far from our trees before," they said. "Now mind you watch how you go, that you may see inside your eyes how to come back to us."

"And beware of the giant-woman that lives in the desert," said one of the bigger girls as they turned, "I suppose you have heard of her!"

"No," I answered.

"Then take care not to go near her. She is called the Cat-woman. She is awfully ugly—and SCRATCHES."

As soon as the bigger ones stopped, the smaller had begun to run back. The others now looked at me gravely for a moment, then walked slowly away. Last to leave me, Lona held up the baby to be kissed, gazed into my eyes, whispered, "The Cat-woman will not hurt YOU," and departed without another word. I stood a while, gazing after them through the moonlight, then turned and, with a heavy heart, began my solitary journey. Soon the laughter of the Little Ones overtook me, like innumerable sheep-bells rippling the air and echoing in the rocks. I turned again, gazing after them: they went gamboling along with never a care in their sweet souls. But Lona walked apart with her baby.

Pondering as I went, I recalled many traits of my little friends. Once, when I suggested they leave their country to go with me to find another, they answered, "But that would be to NOT ourselves!" So strong was their love of place that their country seemed essential to their very being. Without ambition or fear, discomfort or greed, they had no motive to desire change; they knew of nothing amiss. Except for their babies, they had never had a chance to help anyone but myself. How were they to grow? But again, why should they grow? In seeking to improve their conditions, might I not do them harm? To enlarge their minds after the notions of my world—might it not be to distort and weaken them? Their fear of growth as a possible start for gianthood might be instinctive!

The part of the philanthropist is indeed a dangerous one; and the man who would do his neighbor good must first study how not to do him evil, and must begin by pulling the beam out of his own eye.
