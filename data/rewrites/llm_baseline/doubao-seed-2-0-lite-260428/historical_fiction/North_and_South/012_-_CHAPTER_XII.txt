“Well—I suppose we must.”

Mr. Thornton had struggled a little to coax his mother into the proper frame of mind for the polite call he’d been urging. She rarely paid social visits, and when she did, she carried out the duty with the heavy, stately formality of someone performing an unavoidable ritual. Her son had long given her a carriage, but she refused to let him keep horses stabled for it year-round: they were hired only for those rare, solemn occasions when she went out to pay morning or evening calls. Only a fortnight prior she’d kept hired horses for three full days, clearing every last social acquaintance off her visit list, so that they might now take their turn putting themselves to the trouble and expense of calling on her.

But Crampton was far too distant to walk, and she had pressed her son repeatedly to confirm that his wish for her to call on the new Hales was strong enough to justify the cost of a cab ride. She would have been no small amount relieved if he’d backed down, for as she said, “I see no use in striking up intimacies with every last teacher and schoolmaster in Milton. Next you’ll be asking me to call on Fanny’s dancing master’s wife, mark my words.”

“And so I would, mother, if the Masons were as friendless and adrift in a strange town as the Hales are now,” John replied evenly.

“Oh, don’t be so hasty. I’ll go tomorrow, never you mind. I only wanted to be quite clear on just how much this matters to you.”

“If you’re going tomorrow, I’ll send word to have the horses brought round,” he said.

“Nonsense, John. Anyone would think you were rolling in gold.”

“Not quite yet, but the horses are non-negotiable. Last time you took a common cab home, you came in with a throbbing headache from all the jolting over Milton’s cobblestones.”

“I never once complained about it, I’m sure.”

“No, my mother is not one for complaining,” he said, a quiet note of pride in his voice. “Which is all the more reason I owe it to you to look after your comfort. As for Fanny, a little bit of hardship would do her a world of good.”

“She is not made of the same tough stuff as you are, John. She could never bear it,” Mrs. Thornton said, and fell silent. Her last words had touched on a subject that mortified her: she held an unconscious, quiet contempt for weak character, and Fanny was soft exactly where her mother and brother were unyielding. Mrs. Thornton was no woman for long, winding arguments with herself; her sharp judgment and firm will served her far better than any overlong deliberation, and she knew instinctively that nothing would ever toughen Fanny up to endure hardship patiently, or face difficulty bravely. Though the admission stung, it only filled her with a pitying, tender softness towards her daughter, the same careful tenderness most mothers reserve for a sickly, delicate child.

A careless stranger might have thought she loved Fanny far more than she loved John, for all the pet names—dear, love, dearest—were saved for her daughter alone. But that stranger would have been deeply mistaken. The unflinching honesty with which mother and son spoke hard, unpalatable truths to each other spoke of a rock-solid trust in the strength of each other’s core, far more secure a bond than the over-anxious softness Mrs. Thornton lavished on Fanny. She was quietly ashamed of how little her daughter possessed the very grit she valued highest in the world, and that shame betrayed the fact that her affection for Fanny had no firm, unshakable resting place, the way her pride in John did. Day and night she gave quiet thanks for him, and walked prouder than any other woman in Milton on his account.

“Fanny, dear,” she said, turning to her daughter. “We’ll have the horses to the carriage today to call on these Hales. Why don’t you pop in to see Nurse while I pay my visit? It’s on the same route, and she’s always so glad to see you. You can stay with her while I’m with Mrs. Hale.”

“Oh mamma, it’s such a terribly long way, and I feel so tired already.”

“Tired of what?” Mrs. Thornton asked, her brow tightening slightly.

“I don’t know—the weather, I think. It’s so relaxing, oppressive, like. Couldn’t you send for Nurse to come here instead? The carriage can fetch her, and she can spend the rest of the day with me, I know she’d love that.”

Mrs. Thornton laid her work down on the table, considering. “It would be a long walk back for her at night, after dark,” she said at last.

“Oh, I’ll send her home in a cab, of course. I’d never make her walk.”

Just then Mr. Thornton stepped in, on his way out to the mill. “Mother, I don’t need to tell you, if there’s any small comfort you can offer Mrs. Hale as an invalid, you’ll be sure to offer it, I know.”

“I will, if I can think of what it might be. But I’ve never been ill a day in my life, so I’m no expert on the little fancies sick people like.”

“Well, Fanny’s the one who’s rarely without some small ailment,” John said, nudging his sister lightly. “You’ll be full of little ideas for Mrs. Hale, won’t you, Fan?”

“I am not always ailing,” Fanny snapped, pouting. “And I’m not going with mamma today. I’ve got a headache, and I shan’t stir out of the house.”

Mr. Thornton’s face fell. His mother’s eyes stayed fixed on her stitching, her needle moving fast across the cloth.

“Fanny, I wish you to come,” he said, his tone firm and authoritative. “It will do you far more good than harm. You will oblige me by going, no more discussion needed.”

He left the room abruptly before she could protest. If he’d stayed a minute longer, Fanny would have burst into tears at the sharpness of his command, even soft as it was with those words “you will oblige me.” Left behind, she grumbled under her breath.

“John always speaks as if I’m making up illnesses for no reason. Who even are these Hales, that he makes such a fuss over?”

“Fanny, do not speak of your brother that way. He would not ask us to go if he did not have good cause. Go and get your things on at once.”

The small squabble between her son and daughter did nothing to soften Mrs. Thornton’s feelings towards these strangers, the Hales. A small, jealous part of her echoed Fanny’s question, over and over, like a persistent refrain: Who are they, that he is so determined we should heap all this attention on them? The thought lingered long after Fanny had forgotten all about it, distracted by the pleasant thrill of checking her new bonnet’s reflection in the glass.

Mrs. Thornton was shy of new company. She had only gained enough leisure to move in society in the last few years, and she took no enjoyment in casual calls. She loved hosting dinner parties, and criticising other people’s dinners, but walking into a stranger’s home to strike up an acquaintance felt awkward and unnatural, and she looked sterner and more forbidding than usual as she stepped across the threshold of the Hales’ small drawing room.

Margaret was bent over a piece of fine cambric, embroidering a little garment for Edith’s coming baby, work Mrs. Thornton immediately judged as flimsy and useless. She approved far more of Mrs. Hale’s steady double knitting, that was sensible work that served a purpose. The whole room was cluttered with tiny knick-knacks that must take hours to dust, and time for a household on a tight income was as precious as money, she thought to herself, as she went through all the expected polite small talk with Mrs. Hale in her stately, formal way.

Mrs. Hale was putting far more effort into her conversation than she usually bothered with, captivated by the wide band of ancient hand-made point lace around Mrs. Thornton’s collar. “That lace,” she later told Dixon, “is old English point, the kind no one has made for seventy whole years, the kind you can’t buy in any shop. It must be an heirloom—shows she has proper old ancestors, after all.” So the wearer of the heirloom lace earned far more than the lazy, half-hearted politeness Mrs. Hale usually extended to new visitors, and before long the two women were deep in the endless, familiar subject of the failings of household servants.

“I suppose you are not musical,” Fanny said to Margaret, “I don’t see a piano anywhere.”

“I love listening to good music, but I don’t play very well myself. Papa and mamma never cared much for it, so we sold our old piano before we left home to move here.”

“I can scarcely imagine existing without one. It feels like an absolute necessity of life to me.”

“Fifteen shillings a week, and three pence saved out of it to feed a whole family,” Margaret thought to herself, and a faint, unspoken coldness crept into her manner. “I believe you have fine concerts here in Milton, though.”

“Oh yes! Delicious, they are. Only problem is they’re far too crowded— the directors let absolutely anyone buy a ticket. But you’re guaranteed to hear all the newest music in London there. The day after every concert I go straight to Johnson’s music shop and place a huge order for all the new pieces.”

“Do you like new music just for the sake of its newness, then?”

“Oh, you know if it’s being performed here, it’s all the rage in London, or the singers would never bother to bring it north. You’ve lived in London, of course, haven’t you?”

“Yes,” Margaret said. “I lived there for several years.”

“Oh! London and the Alhambra are the two places in the whole world I long to see!”

“London and the Alhambra?”

“Yes! Ever since I read *Tales of the Alhambra*. You’ve read that, haven’t you?”

“I don’t think I have. But surely London is a very easy trip from here.”

“Yes, but somehow,” Fanny whispered, lowering her voice, “mamma has never been to London herself, and she can’t understand why I long to go. She’s absurdly proud of Milton, dirty, smoky old thing as I think it is. I believe she likes it more precisely because of the smoke and the grime.”

“If Milton has been your mother’s home for all these years, I can perfectly understand her loving it,” Margaret said, in her clear, bell-like voice.

“What were you saying about me, Miss Hale? May I ask?”

Margaret was caught off guard, and fumbled for a response, but Fanny cut in before she could speak. “Oh mamma, we were just saying we can’t think of why you love Milton so much!”

“Thank you,” Mrs. Thornton said, dry as dust. “I hardly think my natural affection for the town I was born and raised in, that has been my home for decades now, requires any special explaining away.”

Margaret was vexed. It did sound, after Fanny’s clumsy phrasing, as if they had been gossiping impertinently about Mrs. Thornton’s private feelings, and she resented the sharp, offended edge to the older woman’s tone.

After a short pause, Mrs. Thornton went on. “Do you know anything of Milton, Miss Hale? Have you seen any of our factories? Our grand, magnificent warehouses?”

“No,” Margaret said. “I have not seen anything of that sort yet.” She felt that to pretend she cared a whit for such sights would be dishonest, so she added, “I dare say papa would have taken me long ago if I’d had any interest. But I really do not take much pleasure in touring manufactories.”

“They are very curious places,” Mrs. Hale put in gently, “but there is always so much noise and dirt. I remember once I went to see candles being made wearing a brand new lilac silk gown, and it was utterly ruined beyond repair.”

“Very likely,” Mrs. Thornton said, short and displeased. “I merely thought, as new residents of a town that has risen to national fame on the back of its industry and progress, that you might care to visit the sites where that work is done—places that are unique across the whole country, I’ve been told. If Miss Hale changes her mind and condescends to be curious about Milton’s manufactures, I shall be very happy to arrange access to print works, or reed-making workshops, or the simpler spinning operations in my son’s mill. Every single new improvement to textile machinery is on show there, in its most perfect form.”

“I am so glad you do not like mills and manufactories and all that horrid sort of thing,” Fanny whispered to Margaret, as she rose to follow her mother, who was taking her dignified leave of Mrs. Hale in a rustle of silk.

“If I were you, I think I should very much like to learn all about them,” Margaret replied, quiet and steady.

As they drove back through the smoky streets, Fanny’s mother turned to her. “We will be polite to these Hales, but do not go rushing into one of your silly hasty friendships with the daughter. I can see she will do you no good at all. The mother looks very ill, and seems a quiet, nice enough sort of person.”

“I don’t want to be friends with Miss Hale at all, mamma,” Fanny pouted. “I was only talking to her and trying to amuse her because I thought it was my duty.”

“Well, no matter,” Mrs. Thornton said. “At least John will be fully satisfied now.”
