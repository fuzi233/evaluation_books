Mrs. Adair was not a woman to ask for explanations outright. Behind her calm, unreadable expression lay a mind that preferred to uncover secrets through quiet observation rather than direct inquiry. She would rather peer through a keyhole than walk through an open door, and knowledge gained by subtlety was always more satisfying to her than anything freely given.

So when she noticed the small white feather Ethne treasured, or puzzled over Captain Willoughby’s surprising news about Harry Feversham, she said nothing. Instead, she filled the lunch table with light conversation, though she might as well have spoken to the walls.

"You haven’t heard a word I’ve said, have you?" she finally exclaimed.

Ethne laughed, unapologetic, and retreated to her room afterward, craving solitude. At her window, she revisited Willoughby’s story, each detail sending a thrill through her. The regret that he hadn’t brought this news a year earlier—when she was still free—was fleeting, swallowed by the sheer joy of it. Her pride, once shattered by Harry Feversham’s actions, was now restored, and by the very man who had broken it. The thought warmed her, sent her pulse racing as if youth itself had returned.

She unlocked a drawer in her dressing-case and withdrew the one keepsake she had saved from Harry—his portrait. She traced the lines of his face, not with longing, but with quiet gratitude. He had needed no faith from her to inspire him, no encouragement to keep him steadfast. She placed the portrait back, tucking the white feather beside it before locking them away together.

Outside, the afternoon light softened into gold, the shadows of the trees stretching long across the lawn. But her heart ached for Donegal—for the heather-clad hills and the voice of the wind over granite. There, joy like this would have been complete. Here, she had only Dermod’s old collie for company, and to him she whispered her thoughts, her voice sometimes breaking unnoticed.

She pieced together what Willoughby had left unsaid—Harry’s years hiding in Suakin, the fear of being recognized by old friends, the terror in Berber, the desperate search for water in the desert. Each moment painted itself vividly in her mind, and with each, she wondered: *Where was I then? What was I doing?*

The evening crept in, the creek turning silver under the fading light, the rooks settling noisily into the trees. By dinner, her spirits had lifted so visibly that even Mrs. Adair couldn’t ignore it. The meal passed with an ease that had been absent for months. Durrance, too, seemed lighter, his usual tension gone.

"You’ll play for us tonight?" he asked as they rose from the table.

"Yes," she answered. "With all my heart."

The violin had stayed locked away for too long—a barometer of her emotions. When the world was kind, it sang; when it was cruel, it stayed silent. Tonight, it would speak freely.

Mrs. Adair lingered as Ethne left. "You’ve seen the change in her?"

Durrance’s smile was bright. "How could I miss it? She’s always been kind, but tonight—there’s something more."

Mrs. Adair said nothing, her face unreadable, and slipped out to the terrace while Ethne prepared to play.

Durrance found Ethne alone in the drawing room, the moonlight spilling through the open windows. She tightened the strings of her violin, then extinguished the lamp, leaving only the silver glow of the night.

"What shall I play?"

"The *Musoline Overture*," Durrance said. "The first piece you played for me at Ramelton. I want to hear it again—to compare."

She lifted the violin to her chin, and as the music filled the room, she forgot Durrance entirely. The melody carried her away—across the creek, over the sea, to some distant shore where a man might stir in his sleep beneath the stars and hear, if only for a moment, a message of friendship woven into the notes.

The chords swelled, proud and bright, echoing the joy she had reclaimed today. For a fleeting instant, she imagined the music as a bridge between worlds, a place where she and Harry Feversham might meet, if only in passing. She lingered on the final notes, half-expecting an answer.

And then, from the shadows behind her, Durrance spoke.

"Ethne, do you know where I last heard that overture?"

She blinked, startled back to the present. "At Ramelton, you said."

"No. Not played by you. Not really played at all. Just a fragment of it, badly strummed on a zither by a Greek in a café at Wadi Halfa."

Her breath caught. "This overture?"

"Yes. The Greek was Harry Feversham."

Silence. The answer had come, not across oceans, but from the man beside her. She didn’t question why Durrance had kept this secret until now, or what had brought Harry to that café. It didn’t matter. The music had been the bridge, and he had spoken through it.

"When?" she asked softly.

"February this year." Durrance leaned forward. "Let me tell you."

And so, from the shadows, he began.
