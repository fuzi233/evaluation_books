The letters I have previously detailed serve as sufficient justification for my decision to bypass the narrative of Lady Verinder's passing, offering only a stark, final announcement of the fact that concluded my fifth chapter.

Since that tragic event, a full month had passed before I encountered Rachel Verinder again. Our reunion became the catalyst for several days spent under the same roof. During this brief sojourn, a matter concerning her betrothal to Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite arose—one significant enough to warrant specific mention in these records. Once this final, painful family circumstance is fully revealed, my chronicle of events as an unwilling eyewitness shall reach its conclusion.

Following the interment of my aunt in the secluded cemetery within her estate grounds, I was invited to the funeral by the wider family. However, given my own religious convictions, I found it impossible to compose myself so quickly from the shock of her death as to attend. Furthermore, knowledge reached me that the rector of Frizinghall was to deliver the service. Recalling that this clergyman, long ago dismissed from his parish, had once sat at Lady Verinder's whist table, I harbor doubts regarding whether I would have felt morally justified in witnessing the ceremony, even if physical ability permitted travel.

Upon Lady Verinder's demise, custody of her ward fell to her brother-in-law, Mr. Ablewhite the elder, as stipulated by the will until marriage or majority. Consequently, Mr. Godfrey communicated this new familial bond to his father. Within ten days of the aunt's death, the secret of the engagement ceased to be a secret within the household. The paramount concern for Mr. Ablewhite Senior—a man of similar "castaway" status as his son—became how to render himself and his authority as palatable as possible to the wealthy young lady destined for his son's hand.

At first, Rachel presented difficulties regarding her residence. The home in Montagu Square was tainted by her mother's fatal accident; the ancestral seat in Yorkshire was shadowed by the scandal surrounding the missing Moonstone; and her guardian's own dwelling at Frizinghall offered neither refuge nor privacy following recent bereavement, dampening the spirits of his garrulous daughters. Rachel, however, politely deferred her visit to a more propitious season. Eventually, Old Mr. Ablewhite proposed a solution: a furnished cottage in Brighton. He, his infirm wife, and Rachel were to occupy it, with the expectation that he would join them later in the season. Their social circle would be restricted to a handful of old acquaintances, while his son, Mr. Godfrey, would commute daily from London, always available.

I recount this restless shifting of residences, this jarring combination of physical mobility and spiritual stagnation, solely to reach a pivotal result. The event that, by divine providence, reunited Rachel Verinder and myself was simply the leasing of that Brighton cottage.

My Aunt Ablewhite is a tall, silent woman of fair complexion, possessing one singular trait: from infancy, she has never acted independently. She has traversed life accepting aid from others and adopting their opinions without question. Spiritually, she is a hopeless case, devoid of any internal material upon which to work. Whether presented with the wisdom of the Grand Lama of Tibet or my own humble advice, she receives it with equal placidity. She secured the Brighton lodging by lingering in a London hotel, reclining on a sofa, and summoning her son. She identified the necessary staff by dining in bed one morning, granting her maid a holiday contingent upon the girl "begun enjoying herself by fetching Miss Clack." I found her leisurely fanning herself in her dressing-gown at eleven o'clock. "Drusilla, dear, I require servants," she declared. "You are so astute; please procure them for me." Surveying the untidiness of the chamber, noting the church bells ringing for weekday services, I felt compelled to offer gentle admonition. "Oh, aunt!" I sighed. "Is *this* fitting for a Christian Englishwoman? Must the transition from time to eternity commence in *this* fashion?" She replied, "I'll don my gown, Drusilla, if you will kindly assist me." What further words could I utter? I have achieved wonders with murderers, yet I have made zero progress with Aunt Ablewhite. "Where is the list?" I inquired. "Of the servants you need?" She shook her head, lacking even the energy to maintain one. "Rachel possesses it, dear," she stated, indicating the adjacent room. Entering, I beheld Rachel Verinder for the first time since our separation in Montagu Square.

She appeared pitifully diminutive and gaunt within her deep mourning. Were I to judge by such transient attributes as countenance, I might note that her complexion suffers severely without the relief of a border of white near the skin. Yet, what significance hold our looks? They are merely hindrances and pitfalls on the path to higher things! To my profound surprise, Rachel rose as I entered and approached me with an outstretched hand.

"I am delighted to see you," she said. "Drusilla, I have often spoken foolishly and rudely to you on previous occasions. I implore your forgiveness. I trust you will grant it."

My expression, I suspect, betrayed my astonishment. She flushed momentarily before explaining.

"In my mother's lifetime," she continued, "those who were her friends were not invariably my friends. Now that I have lost her, my heart seeks solace among the people she cherished. She loved you. Pray, attempt friendship with me, Drusilla, if you are capable."

To any mind properly constituted, this motive was simply shocking. Here, in Christian England, a young woman in mourning possessed so little discernment for true comfort that she anticipated finding it amongst her mother's acquaintances! Here was a relative of mine, awakening to her deficiencies toward others not through conviction or duty, but through sentiment and impulse! Most lamentable indeed—but suggestive of hope, for one experienced in the "good work." I resolved to ascertain the extent of the transformation wrought by her mother's loss. As a prudent test, I probed her regarding her engagement to Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite.

Initially receiving my advances with cordiality, she subsequently requested I sit beside her on the sofa. We discoursed on family affairs and future plans—save for that singular future plan destined to conclude in matrimony. Despite my efforts to steer the conversation toward this topic, she resolutely declined the implication. Any direct inquiry would have been premature at this nascent stage of reconciliation. Moreover, I had gleaned everything I required. She was no longer the reckless, defiant spirit I had witnessed during my ordeal in Montagu Square. This alone sufficed to encourage me to assume charge of her future conversion, commencing with earnest warnings against hastily forming marital bonds, and proceeding to loftier subjects. Observing her now with renewed interest, recalling the precipitous nature of her initial acceptance of Mr. Godfrey's proposals, I felt a solemn duty to intervene with fervor, assured of achieving uncommon results. Speed, I believed, was crucial. I returned immediately to the matter of the desired servants.

"Where is the list, dear?"

Rachel produced it.

"Cook, kitchen-maid, housemaid, and footman," I read aloud. "My dear Rachel, these servants are required only temporarily—the duration of your guardian's tenure of the house. Securing individuals of character and capacity to accept a fleeting engagement in London presents considerable difficulty. Has the house in Brighton been located?"

"Yes. Godfrey secured it; the prospective employees requested he engage them, but deemed them unsuitable for us and returned without settling anything."

"And you possess no experience in such matters, Rachel?"

"Not whatsoever."

"And will Aunt Ablewhite not exert herself?"

"No, poor soul. Do not blame her, Drusilla. I consider her the only truly contented woman I have ever encountered."

"There exist degrees of happiness, darling. We must discuss this subject sometime. Meanwhile, I shall address the servant issue. Your aunt will draft a letter to the proprietors of the house..."

"She will sign it if I compose it for her, rendering the action identical."

"Precisely. I shall retrieve the letter and depart for Brighton tomorrow."

"How exceedingly kind! We shall join you forthwith. You will remain, I hope, as *my* guest. Brighton is vibrant; you are certain to revel in it."

Thus was the invitation extended, and the glorious prospect of intervention unveiled before me.

It was mid-week when this occurred. By Saturday afternoon, the residence was prepared. Within that brief span, I scrutinized not only the characters but the religious convictions of all unemployed servants applying to me, successfully selecting those whose conscience approved. I also solicited two serious friends residing in the town—individuals to whom I could confidently entrust the pious objective drawing me to Brighton. One, a clerical acquaintance, generously assisted in securing seating for our small party in the church where he served. The other, a spinster akin to myself, placed the resources of her library, comprised entirely of precious publications, wholly at my disposal. I borrowed half a dozen works, meticulously curated for Rachel. Upon distributing them judiciously among the rooms she would occupy, I deemed my preparations complete. Sound doctrine in the servants attending her; sound doctrine in the preacher addressing her; sound doctrine in the volumes resting on her table—thus was the triple welcome prepared by my zeal for the orphaned girl! A celestial composure filled my mind on that Saturday afternoon as I sat by the window awaiting the arrival of my kinsfolk. The giddy throng passed and repassed before my gaze. Alas! How many of them perceived my exquisite sense of duty performed?
