“Wooed and married and a’.”

“Edith!” Margaret called softly, her voice barely above a whisper. But as she had half expected, Edith was already asleep—curled up like a contented cat on the crimson damask sofa in the back drawing-room of 96 Harley Street. She looked ethereal in her white muslin dress, tied with delicate blue ribbons, her silken curls spilling over the armrests. If Titania had ever been dressed in such finery and fallen into a peaceful slumber in a London parlor, she might have passed for this sleeping beauty. Margaret paused, struck anew by her cousin’s grace and charm. They had grown up together, side by side, and though everyone else had long admired Edith’s loveliness, Margaret had never truly noticed it—until now. With Edith’s marriage just days away, every feature, every quiet gesture seemed to glow with a deeper significance.

They had been talking—whispering, really—about wedding dresses and ceremonies, about Captain Lennox and his tales of life in Corfu, where his regiment was stationed. They’d discussed the challenge of keeping a piano in tune (a concern Edith treated as one of the greatest perils of married life), and what gowns she would need for their immediate trip to Scotland after the wedding. But the conversation had grown sleepy, the voices hushed, until finally Edith had drifted off, wrapped in soft muslin and dreams.

Margaret had been about to share some thoughts of her own—visions of her future life at Helstone parsonage, where her parents lived, and where she had spent all her happiest holidays. Though she had lived with her aunt Shaw for the past ten years, Helstone remained her true home, the place where she felt most herself. Now, however, with Edith leaving soon, that sense of belonging was tinged with melancholy. The thought of returning there alone stirred both joy and sorrow—joy at the prospect of simplicity, peace, and family; sorrow at the thought of parting from her gentle aunt and dear cousin.

From the next room came fragments of conversation. Mrs. Shaw was speaking with the usual circle of neighbors—women she called friends not out of deep affection, but because they dined together often enough to form a routine. Their husbands still lingered in the dining room, enjoying Mr. Grey’s wit, the man who always sat at the bottom of the table and whose jokes were as predictable as they were popular.

Mrs. Shaw had invited them for a farewell dinner in honor of Edith’s upcoming marriage—a gesture Edith had hesitated over. Captain Lennox was expected to arrive late that evening, and she had wished to wait until he was there. But Edith, though indulged and spoiled, lacked strong will. When her mother insisted—especially when she ordered those seasonal delicacies said to soothe grief—Edith gave in. She merely leaned back in her chair, played with her food, and stared ahead with a look of distant seriousness, while others laughed at Mr. Grey’s latest anecdote.

Then came the request: “Edith, my dear, would you play something for us?”

But Edith did not stir.

And so, without warning, Mrs. Shaw called again—this time louder, more urgently: “Edith! Edith!”

She paused, breathless, as if the effort had exhausted her.

Margaret stepped forward. “She’s asleep, Aunt Shaw. Is there anything I can do?”

A chorus of sympathy followed. “Poor child!” they murmured. Even Tiny, the lapdog curled in Mrs. Shaw’s arms, began to bark, as if disturbed by the sudden wave of pity.

“Hush, Tiny,” Mrs. Shaw said gently. “You naughty little thing—you’ll wake your mistress.” Then, turning to Margaret: “Would you mind asking Newton to bring down the shawls? Perhaps you could go, dear?”

Margaret climbed the stairs to the old nursery at the top of the house—the same room where she had first arrived nine years before, a wild child from the country, thrust into London life. Newton was busy arranging laces for the wedding. As she waited, Margaret looked around. This was the first room in the house she had known, dim and quiet, presided over by a strict nurse who demanded clean hands and perfect behavior. She remembered her first tea here—alone, high above her father and aunt, who dined far below, seemingly miles away in the depths of the house. At home, her mother’s dressing room had been her nursery; meals had been shared with her parents, life simple and close-knit.

Now, as an eighteen-year-old girl, she recalled the night she had wept beneath the bedclothes, afraid to cry too loudly, afraid to disturb Miss Edith. And how the nurse had scolded her, how her father had come upstairs quietly, how her aunt had comforted her—softly, gently—until she had stopped sobbing, pretending to sleep so as not to sadden her father.

Now, the room stood empty, stripped of its former liveliness. Yet Margaret loved it. She looked around with a quiet sadness, knowing she would leave it forever in three days.

“Ah, Newton,” she said, “I think we’ll all miss this old room.”

Newton grumbled under her breath. “Indeed, miss, I shan’t. My eyes aren’t what they used to be, and the light’s poor. I can only mend laces by the window—and even then, it’s cold enough to freeze a man’s bones.”

“Well,” Margaret replied, “you’ll have better light and warmth in Naples. Keep your mending till then. Thank you—I’ll take the shawls down.”

She descended the stairs, wrapped in the rich scent of Indian silk—spicy, warm, exotic. Her aunt asked her to stand as a model, since Edith was still asleep. No one questioned it. Margaret, tall and slender in her black mourning dress, stood beneath the chandelier, silent and still, while her aunt draped the shawls over her shoulders. The folds fell like water, shimmering with gold and crimson, far too grand for Edith’s delicate frame.

Occasionally, she caught sight of herself in the mirror—her familiar face, calm and composed, framed by the luxurious fabric. She smiled faintly, enjoying the moment—not as a queen, but as a girl delighted by a fleeting splendor.

Then the door opened.

Mr. Henry Lennox was announced.

The ladies started slightly, as if caught in a moment of girlish excess. Mrs. Shaw greeted him warmly, but Margaret remained still, expecting to be needed again as a shawl holder. Instead, she met his gaze with a quiet amusement—knowing he would see the absurdity of the situation.

Her aunt was instantly absorbed in questions about the bridegroom, the bridesmaid, the Lennox family—all the details of the coming union. Margaret, no longer required, turned her attention to the other guests.

Just then, Edith appeared from the back drawing-room—blinking, yawning, her curls slightly tousled. She looked like a fairy awakened from a dream. Even in sleep, she had sensed the arrival of someone important.

She rushed into the room, eager to question her brother-in-law-to-be about Janet, the sister-in-law from Scotland, whom she adored with such intensity that Margaret couldn’t help but feel a flicker of envy—though she wouldn’t admit it.

As Edith engaged Henry Lennox in conversation, Margaret slipped into the background. She saw him glance toward a vacant chair near her, and knew he would claim it as soon as he could. She hadn’t been sure he’d come tonight—her aunt’s account of his plans had been vague. His arrival was a surprise, and a welcome one.

He liked the same things she did—books, music, honest talk. He didn’t pretend to care about fashion or ceremony.

When he finally sat beside her, she smiled—openly, without hesitation.

“Well,” he said, “I suppose you’re all buried in ladies’ business. Very different from my work—real law, real paperwork. Playing with shawls is nothing compared to drafting settlements.”

“Oh, I knew you’d find this amusing,” she replied. “But really, Indian shawls are beautiful. Perfect, even.”

“Perfect in price, too,” he said, laughing. “No flaws there.”

The gentlemen trickled in, the noise rising.

“This is your last dinner party, isn’t it?” someone asked. “No more before Thursday?”

“No,” Mrs. Shaw replied. “After tonight, I’ll finally breathe easy. All the arrangements are done. I’ll have time to think—something I haven’t had in weeks. And I’m sure Edith will, too.”

“I’m not so sure about her,” Henry said. “But I can imagine you will. You’ve been swept along by other people’s whirlwinds lately.”

“Yes,” Margaret said, a touch of weariness in her voice. “I wonder if every marriage must be preceded by such chaos. Or if sometimes—just sometimes—there could be calm instead.”

“Cinderella’s godmother planning the trousseau, writing invitations, organizing the breakfast,” Henry said, laughing. “But are all these necessary?”

“Of course,” he added, growing serious. “There are forms to go through—not for ourselves, but to satisfy the world. Without them, life feels incomplete. But how would you plan a wedding?”

“I’ve never thought much about it,” she said. “But I’d want a fine summer morning. A walk to church through trees. Few bridesmaids. No breakfast. I suppose I’m rejecting everything that’s caused me trouble lately.”

“No,” he said, “you’re not. The idea of stately simplicity suits you well.”

She winced slightly. She didn’t like being analyzed—especially when he praised her character in a way that felt like flattery disguised as insight.

She cut him short. “It’s natural to think of Helstone church, walking through the woods, rather than driving up to a London church in the middle of a paved street.”

“Tell me about Helstone,” he said. “You’ve never described it to me. I’d like to know what you’ll be living in, when 96 Harley Street feels dull and shut up.”

“Oh, it’s just a hamlet,” she said. “Not even a village. Just a church and a few cottages on the green—roses climbing over the walls.”

“And flowering all year round, especially at Christmas,” he added, teasing.

“No,” she said, annoyed. “I’m not making a picture. I’m describing reality. You shouldn’t say that.”

“I’m sorry,” he said. “But it *does* sound like a scene from a story.”

“And so it is,” she replied, warming. “All the places I’ve seen seem hard and plain after the New Forest. Helstone feels like a poem—like Tennyson’s poetry. But I won’t try to describe it further. You’d only laugh.”

“I wouldn’t,” he said. “But you’re determined not to tell me.”

“You’ve made me angry,” she said. “So I won’t tell you about Helstone—or my home.”

“But you could,” he said gently. “Tell me what you do there. Here, you read, study, walk. What will you do in Helstone? Ride? Drive? Walk?”

“Walk,” she said firmly. “We don’t have a horse—even for Papa. He walks to the farthest end of his parish. The paths are so beautiful—it would be a shame to drive.”

“What about gardening? That’s proper for young ladies in the country.”

“I don’t know,” she said. “I’m afraid it’s too hard.”

“Archery parties? Picnics? Hunt balls?”

“Oh no,” she laughed. “Papa’s income is small. Even if we were near such things, I doubt I’d go.”

“I see,” he said. “You won only tell me what you won’t do.”

“I’ll tell you when you visit,” she said. “Before vacation ends, I hope you’ll come. Then you’ll see for yourself how beautiful Helstone is.”

She rose. “Now I must go. Edith is playing, and I know just enough music to turn the pages.”

Edith played brilliantly. Halfway through the piece, the door creaked open. Captain Lennox stood there, uncertain whether to enter.

Edith dropped her music and rushed out, leaving Margaret standing, blushing, trying to explain to the astonished guests what had startled her cousin.

Captain Lennox had arrived earlier than expected—or perhaps it was simply very late. Watches were consulted, expressions shocked, and the guests took their leave.

Then Edith returned, glowing, leading in her tall, handsome fiancé. His brother shook hands, Mrs. Shaw welcomed him with her usual kindness—soft, gentle, yet tinged with a quiet sorrow, born of years spent feeling trapped in an unloving marriage.

Now, with the General gone, she had wealth and comfort, yet still found reasons to worry—most recently, her health. A nervous cough, a doctor’s prescription, a winter in Italy. She preferred to believe she was obeying necessity, not desire. It allowed her to complain softly, to mourn her fate, while secretly delighting in the journey.

She began to speak of her plans to Captain Lennox, who nodded politely, his eyes fixed on Edith, who was busily re-arranging the tea-table, ordering up cakes and sweets despite his protests that he had eaten recently.

Henry Lennox leaned against the chimney-piece, watching the scene with quiet amusement. He was plain in a family of good looks, but his face was sharp, intelligent, alive. Margaret sometimes wondered what he was thinking—what thoughts passed behind those observant eyes.

He watched Edith, who insisted on doing everything herself, proud to show her lover how capable she was. She discovered the urn’s water was cold and sent for the kitchen kettle. When it arrived, she tried to carry it in—but it was too heavy. She stumbled in, pouting, a smudge on her gown, her hand marked by the handle. She showed it to Captain Lennox like a child with a bruise—and, naturally, the remedy was the same: Margaret lit the spirit-lamp.

It worked perfectly. Not romantic, not like a gypsy camp—yet practical, efficient.

After that evening, the house became a hive of activity—preparing, packing, planning—until the wedding day arrived.
