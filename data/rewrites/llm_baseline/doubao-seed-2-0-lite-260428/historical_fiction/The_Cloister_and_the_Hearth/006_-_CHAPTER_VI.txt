“Peer into your own heart and write!” thundered Herr Cant, and every scribbling poet and prattling self-styled philosopher across the Low Countries took up the cry like cuckoo birds echoing one another’s call. They bade folk sink their buckets in the deepest eddies of the Rhine, the murkiest bends of the Thames, and dredge up the unvarnished truth at the very bottom. Lower a pail into a well of self-deception, they swore, and what you haul back can be nothing less than immortal truth, can it? But the plain fact of the matter is: no child of Adam can read his own heart at all until he has spent years poring over the hearts of other men, learning their secrets, their lies, their quiet buried joys and griefs. Even then, with all that borrowed light and hard-won wisdom, he can only spell out his own soul letter by letter, deciphering it slow, never reading it smooth and unhesitating.

Halfway down the sun-dappled road to Sevenbergen, Gerard did exactly what that popular maxim bade him: he peered deep into his own breast, and asked why he was tramping all that way to see Margaret Brandt. His heart replied at once, no stutter, no hesitation. “We go out of simple curiosity, to learn why she cast us off so suddenly, to show her our hearts never broke. We are perfectly content with our new honours, our promised benefice, and we want no part of her, nor any other of that fickle, inconstant sex.”

He found Peter Brandt’s cottage soon enough, its thatch glowing gold in the late afternoon light. There in the doorway sat Margaret, plying her needle through a length of fine linen, while a broad, stalwart figure leaned on a war-worn yew longbow, chatting away at her. A sharp, unaccountable pang twisted in Gerard’s chest at the sight of the stranger. Then he breathed easy: the man was well past fifty, an old veteran who had shot at the village butts a hundred times, and Gerard had seen him split a willow wand at a hundred paces more than once.

A minute later, the young scribe stood before them. Margaret looked up, dropped her needlework with a soft thud, and let out a faint, breathless cry. Her face flushed bright red then drained white in the space of a heartbeat. She stamped that flutter of emotion down fast, though, turning far chillier and more indifferent than she would have needed to be, had she not already betrayed how much his sudden appearance had shaken her.

“Is that you, Master Gerard? What in the world brings you tramping all this way out here, I wonder?”
“I was passing by the lane, saw you sitting in the door, so I thought I would stop and bid you good day, and ask after your father.”
“My father is well. He will be home before sundown.”
“Then I may as well wait here until he comes back.”
“Please yourself. Martin, step down to the village and tell my father an old friend of his is waiting for him.”
“And not a friend of yours?”
“My father’s friends are my friends.”
“That is a doubtful claim, Margaret. A friend does not promise to wait, then vanish the very moment a man turns his back. Cruel girl, you have no idea how I tore through every street in Rotterdam searching for you, how nothing on earth felt the least bit pleasant, once I found you gone.”
“Empty words, all of them. If you had truly wanted my company, or my father’s, you would have come back for us. I laid a bed out for you at my cousin’s house, sir. He would have fussed over you, fed you the best he had, and who knows? I might have fussed over you too, that day. I was in just the humour to forgive you any foolish slight. You will never catch me in that soft a temper again, not you, nor any other young man, I swear it.”
“Margaret, I came back the very second the Countess let me leave! But you were all gone, the whole house empty.”
“No, that is not true. If you had come back that day, you would have found Hans Cloterman sitting at our table—we left him behind to wait for you, to bring you after us.”
“I saw no one at that table but a drunken lout, who had just tumbled face down on the floorboards.”
“By our table? What sort of clothes was he wearing?”
“I did not stop to look close. Dull, sad-coloured garb, nothing remarkable.”

Slowly the flush crept back into Margaret’s cheeks, warm and soft. She fixed him with a mock-severe, incredulous stare, and fired off a dozen sharp, shrewd little questions, one after another. Gerard answered every last one of them honestly, no evasions, no falsehoods, and little by little the clouds of misunderstanding cleared completely away. At last they both saw how that stupid, harmless mix-up had torn them apart for weeks.

A great rush of tenderness flooded them both, sharper and sweeter for the wrongs they had each done the other, all unknowing. And then came something far more dangerous than soft looks: they confessed, freely, that neither of them had known a single happy day since they parted, that they would have gone on half-miserable forever, if they had not stumbled into this meeting.

Then Gerard’s eye fell on a hand-copied Vulgate, lying open on the rough wooden table. He pounced on it like a hawk after a sparrow—illuminated manuscripts were his dearest delight. But before he could glance at the text, two small white hands slammed flat down on the page, a bright red face leaning over them.
“Nay, Margaret, take your hands away. Let me see what passage you are reading now, and I will read that very same verse at home tonight. So our two souls may meet, side by side, on that sacred page. You will not move your hands? Then I shall have to kiss them off of there.”
He pressed his lips to her knuckles, over and over, until she grew so flustered and shamefaced she could not hold them down any longer, and pulled them away. There the sacred book lay open, the line glowing ink-black on vellum: *“An apple of gold in a network of silver.”*
“There!” she cried, half laughing, half exasperated. “I have been hunting that exact proverb for weeks, only found it a moment before you knocked on the door. And you caught me at it!” Even as she complained, she pointed to the line with the tip of her white finger.
“Aye,” said he, grinning. “Though it is all hidden under that great plain linen cap you are wearing now.”
“It is a handsome cap, so some folk tell me.”
“Maybe. But what it hides beneath it is far more beautiful than any cap could ever be.”
“That is not true. My hair is plain and ugly.”
“Well, it was not ugly that day in Rotterdam.”
“Aye,” she sighed soft, “everything was beautiful that day.”

Just then old Peter trundled through the gate, and welcomed Gerard like a long-lost son, insisting he stay for supper. Margaret slipped away quietly, and Gerard sat for an hour chatting with the old weaver about old books, Latin verse, and the new printing presses that were just starting to creep across the border from Mainz. Then Margaret came back, her pale fair hair twisted up and caught in that fine silver wire net the proverb spoke of, glinting in the candlelight, her glance half arch, half coy. She glided soft around the room, laying out rye bread, spiced butter, a bowl of steaming hare stew, mugs of home-brewed ale, and the whole room seemed to glow with her quiet gaiety and bright happiness.

Later, when the air turned cool, and the dusk smelled of clover and new-mown hay, Gerard coaxed her outside. She pretended to protest, but came all the same. He coaxed her further, down the lane leading toward Tergou. She said no, no, she could not go so far, but walked beside him anyway. Up and down that quiet stretch of road they wandered, hand in hand, with no one to see them. When it came time for Gerard to go home, they pledged to one another that they would never let stupid misunderstandings, or other people’s meddling, ever drive a wedge between them again. They sealed that promise with a long, sweet, lingering kiss, and Gerard tramped all the way back to Tergou walking on wings, his feet barely touching the dirt road.

From that day on, Gerard spent almost every evening he could steal out at Sevenbergen. Their love deepened, week by week, until the hours they spent together were the only hours they truly lived. All the rest of the time, apart, was just empty waiting, a chore they endured. At the start of that great, consuming affection, everything ran smooth. There were obstacles on the horizon, of course, but to the eyes of youth, hope, and foolish young love, they looked tiny, distant, easy to step around. None of the many people whose lives this attachment would thwart gave so much as a wisp of warning smoke to hint at the volcanic, fiery fury they would soon unleash. The course of their true love ran placid, smooth as a mill pond, drawing both their young hearts deep into its current, until there was no turning back.
