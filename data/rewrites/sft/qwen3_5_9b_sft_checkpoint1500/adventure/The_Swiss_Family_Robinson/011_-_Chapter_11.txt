"Now for the finalizing of this unpleasant business!" I cried cheerfully as the dawn broke, waking us all. Once the routine chores were complete, we set to work constructing a rough scaffold. Upon this platform, we stacked the tubs filled with blubber, applying heavy pressure so that the purest oil overflowed into waiting vessels below.

Subsequently, the remaining blubber was boiled in a large cauldron over a distant fire. Through careful skimming and straining via coarse cloth, we secured a generous supply of fine train oil. This precious resource was stored safely in casks and bags crafted from intestines within our makeshift cellar—the children's term for our crudest storage room.

The labor was far from agreeable, and the stench was overwhelming, particularly for my poor wife. Yet, she endured it with her customary good temper, though she urgently proposed that we establish a new colony headquarters elsewhere. She argued that any animals left behind would be safe from apes and thieves, and that the new location would be far more convenient for boiling blubber and processing oil.

Her suggestion received enthusiastic approval, especially from the boys, who delighted in any new venture. However, upon my reminder of the rotting carcass currently lying there, they wisely conceded that it would be better to let wind, storms, birds, and insects perform their natural work. They agreed to wait until the whale was reduced to a skeleton before returning to the island.

An idea for a mechanical rower kept circling in my mind, and I resolved to build one. I selected an iron bar that spanned the center of our boat, projecting a foot on either side. I fitted this bar with ribbed machinery in the middle and attached oblique paddles, resembling spokes, at each end. A jack mechanism was rigged to engage the central gears, ensuring that when wound up, the bar would rotate rapidly, driving the paddles to propel the boat.

Though the invention lacked refinement, when Fritz and I wound it up for a test run across the bay, we surged forward with surprising speed. The cheering and applause from the family echoed off the shores, thrilled by what they deemed a brilliant triumph. Everyone clamored to join the cruise, but as twilight approached, I refused to consent. Instead, I promised an excursion the following day to Cape Disappointment and Prospect Hill.

The proposal pleased everyone. The evening was devoted to preparing attire, weapons, and provisions, after which we retired early. Leaving the house in order, we departed for the expedition, armed with spades and mattocks. My goal was to collect young coconut trees and various shrubs so that, on our return, we could transport them to Whale Island and commence our plantation.

Steering toward the opposite side of the bay, the sea was calm, and the machine operated smoothly. Passing Safety Bay and Shark Island, we admired the panoramic coastal scenery. Upon landing near Prospect Hill, we moored the craft and ventured into the woods toward our farm. Along the way, we gathered fresh coconuts and seedlings.

Before reaching the cottage, the crowing of roosters pierced the air. The sound evoked a poignant surge of emotion, recalling countless rides and walks at home where such familiar greetings awaited us. Here, however, save for the oblivious animals, absolute solitude reigned. My dear family, whose presence would have been welcomed with joy in so many homes, advanced unnoticed toward this lonely dwelling.

Our prolonged absence had created chaos. The original inhabitants had forgotten us, and their offspring—lambs, kids, and chickens, who had never seen a human face—regarded us as a terrifying army of foes. The boys struggled to milk the goats until, using lassos, they captured them one by one. Binding their legs and offering salt to lick, they soon secured a supply of excellent milk, pouring the excess into calabash flasks for later consumption.

The poultry were coaxed with handfuls of grain and rice, allowing my wife to gather as many as needed. By this time, hunger had overtaken us. We spread out our cold provisions, the centerpiece being a piece of whale tongue cooked specifically for this feast at the boys' request.

Yet, disaster struck when the tongue was tasted. One by one, with grim expressions, they declared it "horrid stuff," begging for pickled herrings to counteract the flavor of train oil, and willingly offering their share to Fangs. Fortunately, ample supplies of other foods remained, and the fresh, delicious coconuts and goat's milk restored everyone's spirits.

While the mother packed away the leftovers, Fritz and I harvested sugar-cane shoots for planting. Returning to the shore, we embarked once more. Before heading back to Whale Island, I felt compelled to circle Cape Disappointment to survey the coast beyond. However, the promontory lived up to its name; a long sandbar, concealed reefs, and jagged rocks extended far into the sea.

Spotting breakers ahead, Fritz signaled us to turn back immediately. With a light breeze aiding us, we steered toward Whale Island. Upon landing, I immediately planted the sugar-cane shoots. The boys assisted briefly before growing weary and scattering to hunt for shells and coral, leaving their mother and me to finish the task.

Soon, Jack returned, shouting loudly: "Father! Mother! Do come and look! There is an enormous skeleton lying here—the skeleton of some terrible beast, a mammoth, I should think!"

"Why, Jack!" I laughed. "Have you forgotten our old acquaintance, the whale? What else could it be?"

"Oh no, father! It isn't the whale. This thing lacks fish bones; they are real, solid, huge beast bones. I don't know what happened to the whale—it probably floated out to sea. This mammoth is vastly larger. Come and see!"

Just as I moved to follow, a voice called from another direction: "Father! Father! A great enormous turtle! Hurry, please! It is waddling back to the sea as fast as it can, and we can't stop it!"

This urgent plea outweighed Jack's excitement. Snatching up an oar, I raced to assist. Indeed, a massive turtle scrambled desperately toward the water, mere paces away despite Ernest's valiant grip on one of its hind legs.

Springing down the bank, I used the oar as a lever. With some difficulty, we managed to flip the creature onto its back. It was a huge specimen, nearly eight feet long. Now helpless, we abandoned it sprawling and returned to inspect Jack's "mammoth" skeleton. As expected, it was merely the whale's remains. I convinced Jack by pointing out the marks of our feet on the ground and the broken jaws where we had hacked out the whalebone.

"What led you to suspect a mammoth, my boy?" I asked.

"Ernest put the idea in my head," Jack admitted. "He said it looked like an antediluvian monster, so I investigated closely. I didn't realize it was the whale when I couldn't see fish bones. I suppose Ernest was joking."

"Whales are often mistaken for fish by those unfamiliar with the animal kingdom," I explained gently. "Yet they belong to the mammalian class, alongside humans, monkeys, bats, dogs, cats, hoofed animals, and others, including the sloth. Their name derives from the Latin word 'mamma,' meaning breast, referring to the mammary glands that secrete milk to nourish their young."

"The whale's bones differ from terrestrial animals primarily by being hollow and filled with air, rendering the carcass buoyant," I continued. "Birds possess similar hollow bones for the same reason. In all this, we witness the wisdom and benevolence of the Great Creator."

"What a marvelous structure it is!" Fritz exclaimed. "Such a ponderous mass of bone! Might we not utilize any of them?"

"Not a thing strikes me at the moment," I replied. "Let us leave them to bleach for a while. Perhaps later, by sawing them up, we can fashion chairs or a desk for the museum. But now, it is time to return. Round up the boat to where the turtle awaits his fate; we must decide his destiny."

It was quickly decided that the turtle must swim. I fastened an empty water-cask to a long line, securing one end to the bow of the boat and looping the other carefully around the turtle's neck and fore-paws. Lifting the creature allowed it to regain its footing; instantly, it lunged for the water. Plunging in, the floating cask towed behind it, preventing it from sinking.

Boarding swiftly, the worthy fellow attempted to dive in vain but instead swam diligently forward, towing us comfortably. I stood ready to sever the line at the slightest sign of danger, steering the turtle—and us—with a boat-hook to keep him on course for Safety Bay, correcting his turns whenever necessary.

The boys were enchanted, comparing me to Neptune in his chariot drawn by dolphins, attended by Amphitrite and Tritons. We landed safely near Rockburg, and shortly thereafter, the turtle was condemned and dispatched. Its shell, measuring eight feet long and three broad, was cleaned and prepared to serve as a trough for the water supply at the cave. The meat was carefully salted and stored for future meals.

Originally, I had planned to cultivate a patch of land before the next rainy season, sowing various grains. However, unforeseen circumstances hindered progress, and our animals, unaccustomed to the yoke, were unavailable for plowing. Thus, I abandoned the plan temporarily and turned my attention, with Ernest's help, to completing the loom. Although the craftsmanship was somewhat clumsy, I managed to render it functional.
