When the legendary Trojan wanderer told Queen Dido of his perils and escapes, her heart was captivated by his eloquence and the sorrowful grace with which he recounted his trials. So moved was she that a tale more poignant than any before unfolded—one that would leave her mourning the moment she first listened to that persuasive voice. Harry Warrington lacked Aeneas’s rhetorical power, and his aunt, Madame Bernstein, was far from the sentimental nature of Dido. Yet as the young man spoke—his words simple but sincere, his tone warm and unassuming—she found herself stirred in ways she had long believed impossible. There were few moments of joy in her solitary life, few places where tenderness could take root. But now, with this boy’s presence, something unfamiliar stirred within her: a softness, a longing, an almost painful warmth.

She grew fond of him—not merely for his charm, but for the gentle affection he inspired. It was a feeling she had not known since childhood, and it both startled and delighted her. She watched him closely, her pulse quickening when he entered a room, her cheeks warming at his nearness. Her eyes followed him with quiet admiration. “Ah,” she thought, “if only I had had a son like this—I would have loved him beyond all measure.”

But a voice within her whispered caution. “Wait, Beatrix Esmond. You know better. These feelings will pass. The boy may fade from your memory; he may speak too much, or too little. And you—you will be alone again. That is your fate.”

Yet what if life were indeed a desert? Then even brief oases mattered. Let one enjoy the shade, the water, the fleeting comfort—today. Tomorrow would bring its own burdens, its own march forward. For now, she allowed herself to feel.

As Harry spoke of his mother, Madame Bernstein noticed familiar traits—her manner, her speech, the way she looked upon things. He spoke of his mother’s pride, her jealousy—something she admitted with a touch of shame. “Yes,” he said, “she was jealous. Of Colonel Washington?” “She liked him,” Harry insisted, “but only as a friend. She swore a hundred times she felt nothing else.” And then, with a blush, he confessed, “I once… I was absurdly jealous myself.”

“Then you’ll see,” Madame Bernstein replied softly, “that my half-sister will never forgive him.”

“And don’t be surprised,” she added, “if women are drawn to men younger than themselves. Do I not dote on you? And aren’t these Castlewood folk positively consumed with envy?”

Though there might be murmurs behind closed doors, no ill will showed in the household’s behavior toward their young guest. Within days, Harry Warrington had become “Cousin Harry” to all—youth and adults alike. Especially in Madame Bernstein’s presence, the Countess of Castlewood treated him with particular kindness. In private, she often praised him to the Baroness, marveling at his poise and elegance. “How could such a child from so distant a land learn to carry himself so well?” she would ask.

The Baroness took these compliments with quiet pleasure. (And here, let me pause—though I am but a humble chronicler, I cannot help admiring the subtle art of ladies in conversation. How they praise while they wound! How they smile while they stab! With what delicate precision they drop poison into the cup, hand it around with a grin, and watch the family drink it down—unaware, perhaps, that the sweetness hides a bitter sting.)

“I wonder,” Madame de Bernstein once remarked, “how the boy came to be so refined.”

“His mother,” the Countess replied, “was raised by two truly noble people. Colonel Esmond had a dignified courtesy, a manner rare today.”

“Ah, but we all praise our own time!” countered the Countess. “My grandmother used to say there was nothing like Charles the Second’s court.”

“My mother saw King James the Second’s court briefly,” Madame Bernstein said. “She was not born to it—her father was a country clergyman—but she was exquisitely bred. Colonel Esmond, her second husband, traveled widely, knew the best society in Europe. They did not leave their manners behind in the wilderness. Our boy inherited them naturally.”

“Nay,” the Countess replied gently, “I do not think you’re being fair to your mother. No woman can be perfect who turns cold toward her daughter—as you’ve admitted she did. A complete woman must have a heart. Manners matter, yes, but without affection, all is vanity. I live only for my children—for Willy, though he’s stubborn, and Fanny, though she’s willful. My sweet, dear ones!”

She kissed the bracelets on each arm—miniatures of her children—and sighed. “If they were to die,” she whispered, “I would die too.”

Two Italian greyhounds, Mimi and Fanchon, leapt into her arms, nuzzling her hands but avoiding her rouged cheeks.

“You have a good heart,” said Madame Bernstein. “That is what matters most.”

“Indeed,” the Countess agreed. “And you must share some of it with our American nephew—our cousin—though I’m not sure how he fits into the family.”

“I am but a guest here,” Madame Bernstein replied. “Castlewood belongs to Lord Castlewood. What can I do for the young Virginian that hasn’t already been done? He is charming. Are we jealous of him for it? And though we see how deeply I care for him, do your nephews and nieces protest? Poor children—they may feel slighted. In just hours, this boy has won more favor than they’ve earned in years. But do they resent him? Willy took him riding. This morning, Maria played the harpsichord while Fanny taught him the minuet. Such a lovely scene—it brought tears to my eyes.”

Madame Bernstein, however, had overheard what truly happened during that moment. Lady Castlewood entered the room, boots still dusty from the kennels, her son William beside her.

“Bravi! Bravi!” she exclaimed, clapping. “Charming!” She smiled warmly at Harry, then shot a glance at Fanny that the girl understood perfectly. Maria at the harpsichord played with renewed vigor, nodding her curls over the keys.

“Infernal young Choctaw!” William muttered. “Is he teaching Fanny the war-dance? Is Fan going to show him her tricks now?”

That was what his mother’s look meant.

Fanny made Harry a low curtsey. He blushed, and they stopped dancing, flustered.

“Don’t stop!” the Countess said, sitting at the instrument. “Let me play for you.”

“I don’t know how to dance,” Harry admitted, hanging his head.

“And Fanny was teaching you? Go on, dearest Fanny!”

“Go on!” William growled.

“I’d rather not,” Harry said, regaining composure. “I’d rather not embarrass myself.”

“When you know how,” he added, “I’ll ask my cousin to dance with me.”

“That will be soon, dear Cousin Warrington,” the Countess said, smiling.

“What game is she playing now?” William wondered, unable to fathom his mother’s schemes.

As the Countess called Fanny to her side and left the room, her tone changed completely.

“You booby!” she snapped. “You double idiot! What are you doing with that Huron? You don’t want to marry a creature like that and end up a squaw in a wigwam!”

“Don’t, mamma!” Fanny gasped, as her mother pinched her arm until it turned blue.

“I’m sure he’s very well,” Fanny whimpered. “You said so yourself.”

“Very well? Yes! But heir to a swamp, a negro, a log cabin, and a barrel of tobacco! My Lady Frances Esmond, remember your rank, your name, your mother! At three days’ acquaintance, you’re dancing—with this brat from Virginia?”

“He’s our cousin,” Fanny pleaded.

“A creature from nowhere isn’t your cousin! How do we know he is? He might be a valet who stole his master’s coat and ran off in his carriage.”

“But Madame de Bernstein says he is,” Fanny insisted. “And he looks exactly like the Esmonds.”

“Madame de Bernstein has her favorites. She likes him today—why should she tomorrow? Before company, be civil. But in private, I forbid you to see him or encourage him.”

“I don’t care if you forbid me!” Fanny cried, rising in rebellion.

“Very well,” her mother said coldly. “Then I speak to your father. If I can’t reason with you, your brother will.”

At that point, the conversation ended—or at least, Madame Bernstein’s informant could hear no more. Later, she would tell Harry only part of what she knew. For now, he saw only kindness. Lady Castlewood was polite. The young ladies welcomed him. Lord Castlewood, though reserved, treated him no differently than others. William invited him to ride, race, drink, and play cards. When Harry mentioned leaving, they all urged him to stay.

He did not know that his presence stirred hidden currents—jealousies, ambitions, secrets. He believed he was welcomed because he was kin. He did not suspect that the very people whose hands he shook, whose cups he drank from, might one day turn against him.
