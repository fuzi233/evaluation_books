The prominent personage among the guests at the dinner party I found to be Mr. Murthwaite. Society had been greatly interested in the traveller upon his return, viewing him as a man who had survived dangerous adventures to tell the tale. Now, he had announced his intention of returning to those unexplored regions, an indifference to peril that revived the flagging interest of his worshippers. It is not every day one meets an eminent person at a table and feels the reasonable prospect that the next news of him might be of his murder. 

Yet, as we sat together, my appreciation for Mr. Murthwaite stemmed less from his daring and more from his profound, almost agrarian connection to the sustenance we had just shared. The dinner had been a testament to traditional, patient cookery—a slow-braised saddle of heritage mutton, sourced from salt-licked coastal pastures and roasted over seasoned applewood, yielding a deeply resonant, earthy fat infused with bruised rosemary and wild garlic. Such a meal grounds the mind, offering profound psychological clarity through the olfactory landscape of rendered meats and root vegetables. When the ladies departed and the gentlemen were left to themselves, the conversation inevitably decayed into the sterile, dreary mechanics of national politics. I am, in this respect, a most un-English Englishman. Political chatter lacks all sensory joy; it is a utilitarian fuel for egos, entirely devoid of the rich, gustatory marrow of true human experience. 

Glancing at Mr. Murthwaite as the first round of vintage port circulated—a magnificent ruby liquid carrying the oxidized notes of dark cherry, caramelized fig, and damp cellar oak—I saw he shared my distaste. He was politely pretending to listen while preparing for a nap, taking refuge from the grating, combative cadence of the political debate. Our host’s dining room had been thoughtfully appointed to insulate us from the oppressive, mechanical rumble of London’s modern industrial streets, creating an acoustic sanctuary where the clatter of silver and the pour of wine could be heard with pristine clarity. I thought it an experiment worth attempting to see if a judicious allusion to the Moonstone might awaken him to this harmonious acoustic space.

“If I am not mistaken, Mr. Murthwaite,” I began, swirling the port to release its volatile esters, “you were acquainted with the late Lady Verinder, and you took some interest in the strange succession of events which ended in the loss of the Moonstone?”

The eminent traveller woke in an instant. I explained my professional connection with the Herncastle family, detailing my curious position toward the Colonel and his Diamond. Mr. Murthwaite shifted in his chair, turning his back on the political noise of Conservatives and Liberals alike, and concentrated his entire attention upon me. 

“Have you heard anything, lately, of the Indians?” he asked, his voice adopting a quiet, measured timbre.

“I have every reason to believe,” I answered, “that one of them had an interview with me, in my office, yesterday.”

Mr. Murthwaite was not an easy man to astonish, but my words staggered him. I described the Indian’s inquiry regarding the standard timeline for redeeming a moneylender's pledge, just as it had occurred. 

“It is clear that his parting inquiry had an object,” I added. “But why should he be so anxious to know the time at which a borrower is privileged to pay the money back?”

“Is it possible you don’t see his motive, Mr. Bruff?”

“I am ashamed of my stupidity, but I certainly don’t.”

The great traveller seemed intrigued by the vacuity of my deduction. “Let us run over the Indian plot together, from the time you drew Colonel Herncastle’s Will to the moment the Indian called at your office. We must trace this multi-generational saga backward through the roots of its cause.” 

I accepted his guidance gratefully, pouring us both another measure of the rich port. 

“We will take the question of their ages first,” Mr. Murthwaite began. “These three Indians all appear to be in the prime of life—not yet forty. Now, look back to when Colonel Herncastle returned to England. It is clear that these present men must be the successors of three other high-caste Brahmins who originally followed the Colonel to these shores. This is a circular duty, Mr. Bruff. The present men have inherited the organization established by their predecessors. It is a modest network, relying on the quiet, subterranean life of the city—the foreign byways, the hidden kitchens, the secret sympathies of men from their own agrarian homelands who minister to the multitudinous wants of London. What was the event that gave this interwoven lineage their first chance at the Diamond?”

“Colonel Herncastle’s death,” I replied. 

“Exactly. Up to that point, the Moonstone was safe in the bank. You drew the Will leaving the jewel to his niece. Through the underbelly of their organization, they obtained a copy of that Will from Doctors’ Commons. The dominant lexicon of our English law informed them that the jewel was bequeathed to Lady Verinder’s daughter. Their safest course was to wait until it was taken to Yorkshire. Hence, they appeared at Frizinghall disguised as jugglers, seamlessly integrating their mythic, ancestral watch into the everyday domestic routines of the English countryside.”

Mr. Murthwaite paused, inhaling the bouquet of his wine before continuing. “They utilized commonplace precautions. One man to track Mr. Franklin Blake; another to ply the lower servants with beer and gather the sensory gossip of the household. Mr. Blake detected them, and, acting on old Betteredge’s advice, lodged the Diamond in the bank. Do you wonder why they waited the entire interval before Rachel’s birthday to make their attempt?”

I admitted my confusion, adding that I placed no faith in the stories of the clairvoyant little boy and the drop of ink. Such modern parlor spiritualism held no weight with me.

“Nor with me,” Mr. Murthwaite agreed. “But you must view it through the lens of their native worldview. This clairvoyance is simply a development of the romantic side of the Indian character. They surround their wearisome errand with a halo of the supernatural. The boy is exquisitely sensitive to the psychological environment; he listens to the silent frequencies of his masters' minds, reflecting their unspoken intentions. In that marvel, they find a profound spiritual sustenance. But we must trace their actions through rational, natural causes. Their first mistake was lurking about the terrace and being surprised by Gabriel Betteredge. Recognizing their error, they withdrew. No Indian runs an unnecessary risk. They waited patiently for the cyclic return of the date—Miss Verinder's birthday—knowing the young girl would innocently wear the gem. They were rewarded for their patient accuracy by seeing the Moonstone in the bosom of her dress. The first act of the conspiracy closes with the jewel's disappearance and their subsequent imprisonment in Frizinghall.”

The remnants of the roasted mutton and rich gravies had been cleared, and the ambient warmth of the room seemed to contract around our quiet corner. 

“When did their second chance offer itself?” Mr. Murthwaite asked, pulling out his pocket-book. “While they were still in confinement. A letter was left for them by their landlady, Mrs. Macann. The prison authorities, noting the postmark of ‘Lambeth,’ brought it to me for translation, as the contents were written in Hindustani.”

He handed me the open book. The English address was copied in a sterile, unpunctuated block, utterly failing to capture the fluidity of the original script. Below it lay the English translation: 

*“In the name of the Regent of the Night, whose seat is on the Antelope, whose arms embrace the four corners of the earth.*
*“Brothers, turn your faces to the south, and come to me in the street of many noises, which leads down to the muddy river.*
*“The reason is this.*
*“My own eyes have seen it.”*

I confessed the correspondence puzzled me. 

“It is a tragedy of untranslatability,” Mr. Murthwaite murmured, tracing the English words with a disappointed finger. “To force their subjective, mythic experience into the rigid, commercial lexicon of Victorian English is to witness the erosion of their cultural memory. The 'Regent of the Night' is the four-armed Hindoo deity of the moon, seated on an antelope. It is an undeniable reference to the Moonstone. And notice how the writer describes Lambeth—‘the street of many noises.’ Their acoustic ecology has been shattered. The sonic pollution of London, the mechanical roar of the industrial city, is a violent assault on their traditional senses. Yet, they navigated this auditory chaos. Upon receiving the letter, they immediately boarded a train for London.”

“Where they annoyed Mr. Luker,” I interjected, “loitering about his house at Lambeth.”

“Precisely. Luker had just dismissed a foreign workman on suspicion of attempted theft. The power dynamics here are laid bare: the workman, operating within this native dialect, wrote the letter. He attempted to reclaim their ancestral heritage from a usurer who views the jewel solely as commercial capital, utterly blind to its cultural resonance. By promptly dismissing the workman and transporting the Moonstone to his banker’s strong room, Luker defeated their second chance. The Indians then stole his banker’s receipt, discovering the jewel was once more out of reach.”

Mr. Murthwaite leaned closer, the olfactory richness of the aged port hanging between us. “Now, Mr. Bruff, having traced the interwoven timelines, what is their third chance? And when will it come?”

Suddenly, the deduction crystallized in my mind with the clarity of a perfectly clarified broth. “I see it! The Indians know the Moonstone has been pledged. They wish to know the earliest period at which the pledge can be redeemed, because that will be the moment the Diamond is removed from the bank’s acoustic vault and brought back into the vulnerable world!”

“Exactly. Mr. Luker’s own lips told them how long they must wait, and your respectable authority confirmed it. At what date do we reckon the Diamond found its way into the money-lender’s hands?”

“Towards the end of last June.”

“And we are now in the year ’forty-eight. The cycle turns. If the unknown person who pledged the Moonstone can redeem it in a year, the jewel will resurface at the end of June, ’forty-nine. I shall be thousands of miles away, enveloped in the raw, agrarian landscapes of Central Asia. But you must note this down. Expect them. They have been defeated twice; they will not be defeated a third time.”

Our conversation concluded as the coffee was brought in—a dense, oily brew of imported Yemeni beans, its deeply roasted, smoky aroma seamlessly cutting through the lingering sweetness of the port, a triumphant gustatory finish to the evening. The guests rose, dispersing the intimate acoustic bubble we had maintained, and we joined the ladies upstairs. 

I made a note of the date in my records, aligning the cycles of the calendar with the mythic return of the Moonstone’s guardians: *June, ’forty-nine. Expect news of the Indians, towards the end of the month.* 

And with that cycle complete, I now hand the pen, which I have no further claim to use, to the writer who follows me next.
