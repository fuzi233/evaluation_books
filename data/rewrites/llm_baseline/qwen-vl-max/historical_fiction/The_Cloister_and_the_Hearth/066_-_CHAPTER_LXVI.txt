Two nights after the events that had stirred their household, Pietro Vanucci and Andrea sat in quiet anticipation of Gerard’s return for supper. The hour had long passed nine, yet no sign of him. Pietro grew restless, his patience wearing thin. At last, unable to endure the silence any longer, he sent Andrea to check Gerard’s chamber—perhaps he had slipped in unnoticed.

Andrea shrugged, as if the task were futile, but obeyed. He returned moments later without Gerard, yet clutching a folded slip of paper. Though illiterate by modern standards—his mind sharp, but his letters untaught—he had learned to recognize certain names by sight. “That is for you,” he declared proudly, handing it over. “I know the name.”

Pietro snatched the note and read aloud, his voice edged with irony, though his hands trembled beneath his cloak.

“'Dear Pietro, dear Andrea, life is too great a burden.'”

“So 'tis,” Pietro muttered, though his tone belied his words. “But that’s no reason to be absent at supper-time. Supper is not a burden.”

“'Wear my habits!'”

“Said the poplar to the juniper bush,” he quipped, but his smile did not reach his eyes.

“'And thou, Andrea, mine amethyst ring; and me in both your hearts a month or two.'”

“And why, Andrea?” Pietro asked, half-jesting.

“'For my body, ere this ye read, it will lie in Tiber. Trouble not to look for it. 'Tis not worth the pains. Oh unhappy day that it was born—oh happy night that rids me of it.'”

“'Adieu! adieu!'”

“'The broken-hearted Gerard.'”

“Here is a sorry jest from the peevish rogue,” Pietro said, forcing lightness into his voice. But his pale cheeks and chattering teeth spoke otherwise.

Andrea, however, saw no joke. He burst into wails that echoed through the house. “O, miserable day! O, calamity of calamities! Gerard, my friend, my sweet patron! Help! help! He is killing himself! Oh, good people, help me save him!”

He ran from room to room, then flung open the door and rushed into the street, bareheaded, shouting to every passerby. Soon a small crowd gathered, drawn by his cries. But they hesitated. Go down to the river? No. It was not their concern. Which part? Who knew? It was dangerous after sunset—ghosts wandered the banks, they said, and misfortune followed those who ventured near.

Yet one man stood apart—a lackey who had lingered opposite the house. He stepped forward. “I will go,” he said quietly. A few young men, stirred by the urgency, joined him.

They hurried toward the Tiber, Andrea sobbing as he recounted the letter and Gerard’s recent melancholy. The lackey listened closely. He was no ordinary servant—his master was Princess Claelia, and he watched all things with a keen eye.

They walked in silence once they neared the riverbank. The spring night was still, the moon bright and clear above. The air carried the damp scent of water, and their footsteps sounded loud and ominous on the cobbles. Then, rounding a corner, they met a man.

He stopped abruptly, uncertain. He wore no hat; his hair clung to his face, glistening with moisture. His clothes were soaked, heavy with water.

“Here he is!” cried one of the youths, unfamiliar with Gerard’s features.

The man turned and fled.

They gave chase—Andrea leading, the lackey close behind. Andrea gained ground, but in an instant the fugitive darted into a narrow alley. Andrea shot past, unable to stop. The others followed, only to find themselves lost in a maze of dark streets, where every turn led deeper into confusion. The man vanished—known every inch of the way, he could now only be tracked by sound.

They returned to the spot where he’d stood and found their companions standing there, bewildered.

Pietro had told them plainly: the man was not Gerard—neither in height nor face.

“But are you sure?” they pressed. “He came from the river. Why did he run?”

Vanucci shook his head. “Friends, I believe this has nothing to do with our search. What shall we do, Andrea?”

Then the lackey spoke. “Let us follow the trail to the water’s edge. See—his steps drip all the way.”

They agreed. With little difficulty, they traced the wet path. But soon it took a strange turn. After fifty yards, the drops veered away from the river and led them to a large, shadowed gate. A monastery.

They stood before it, silent and uneasy. The building loomed like a tomb, its walls thick with secrets. Then Andrea shouted again. “Here are the drops!” he called. “And this road leads back to the river!”

They resumed their pursuit. The trail grew wetter, clearer. It wound back toward the Tiber. And there, on the bank, a bucketful of water seemed to have been emptied from the stream.

At first, they thought they’d found proof. But reflection cooled their hopes. Yes, someone had been in the river—and escaped. But it was not Gerard.

One suggested a fisherman nearby who owned nets and drags. They found him, paid him well, and begged him to cast nets below the site and drag the river above and below. Gold would be his reward if he found a body.

Then they searched the riverbanks, calling out into the calm, whispering waters. The river flowed on, indifferent, holding its mysteries tight.

Suddenly, Andrea broke from the group, racing back to the house. He returned within half an hour, breathless and broken.

“No,” he groaned, wringing his hands. “He is gone.”

“What is the hour?” asked the lackey.

“Four hours past midnight.”

“My pretty lad,” the lackey said solemnly, “say a mass for thy friend’s soul. For he is not among the living.”

Dawn broke slowly. Exhausted, Andrea and Pietro trudged home, their hearts heavy with grief. Days passed, silent as the Tiber, offering no word of Gerard’s fate.
