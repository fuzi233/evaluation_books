Gerard stirred awake, his eyes blinking in the bright daylight, to find Denys seated beside him, face etched with quiet concern. “You’re still sleeping!” Denys murmured. “It’s high noon already.”

“A blessed sleep,” Gerard replied, stretching slowly. “It feels as though Heaven itself laid a veil over that dreadful night. To think we sit here now—alive, unharmed. It seems like a dream, a terrible dream I can scarcely believe was real.”

“Aye, lad,” Denys said gently, “that’s the wise way to see such things once they’re past. They fade into shadows, dreams. Eat something, and you’ll forget it all. Besides, I promised to bring you into town by noon—and take you to the alderman.”

Gerard nodded, accepting a piece of coarse rye bread soaked in red wine. He ate slowly, savoring the simple fare that broke his long fast. Then, with Denys at his side, he walked back over the site of their violent encounter. The ground bore the scars of struggle—the broken weapons, the bloodstained earth—yet no enemy remained. Still, Gerard shuddered at the memory, his nerves taut as bowstrings. As they made their way toward the town, he kept glancing through the hedges, expecting ambushes from every shadow, until at last they reached the quiet streets of the little settlement.

Denys led him to an inn called “The White Hart.” “No danger here,” he assured Gerard. “I’ve known the landlord for years. He’s a burgess, and likely to be bailiff. This is where I meant to go yesterday—but we lost time, and darkness fell upon us. And then—”

“And you saw a woman at the door,” Gerard finished, “and decided to be wiser than a Jeanneton. She told us there was nothing to fear.”

“Indeed,” Denys said, smiling faintly. “But what saved our lives if not that woman? And she risked her own to do it.”

“That is true,” Gerard said, his voice softening. “Though women mean little to me, I would give every coin in my purse to thank this poor girl, to repay her kindness. Do you not feel the same?”

“Parbleu,” Denys muttered, but there was warmth in his tone.

“But where might we find her?” Gerard asked.

“Perhaps the alderman will know,” Denys suggested. “We must speak with him first.”

The alderman greeted them with a face that held more mystery than welcome—a look both wary and unreadable. After a moment’s silence, a grim smile touched his lips, and he began questioning Gerard, recording each answer with careful precision.

When he had finished, he declared, “You must remain in town until the thieves are tried. You are required to testify—under penalty of fine or imprisonment.”

The two men exchanged stunned glances.

“However,” the alderman added, “it won’t be long. The culprits were caught red-handed.”

He paused, then continued, “And in any case, you could not leave this place this week.”

Denys blinked, taken aback. Gerard smiled—though not with humor, but with a quiet amusement at what he took to be the old man’s naivety. A provincial town in Burgundy, he thought, could hardly detain one bound for Rome and a beloved Margaret.

Then, turning to the matter closest to his heart, Gerard said, “Your worship, we cannot find our benefactress in the town.”

“Your benefactress?” the alderman repeated, puzzled.

“The good girl,” Gerard explained earnestly, “who came to you in the night and saved our lives at great peril to herself. Oh, sir, our hearts burn with gratitude—we wish only to thank her, to bless her. Where is she?”
