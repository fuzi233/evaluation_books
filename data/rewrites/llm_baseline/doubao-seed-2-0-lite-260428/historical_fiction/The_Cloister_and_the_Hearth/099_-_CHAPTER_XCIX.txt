Jerome remained as dour and unyielding in his general temperament as he had always been, yet a soft spot had grown in his stern heart for Gerard. All the months he spent in England, he had found he missed the quiet, frail young monk far more than he had ever expected he would, and in the years since, he had often wondered what had become of him. The stories he heard of Gerard in Gouda – above all, the account of his standing firm against the Pope and the Minorites to denounce what Jerome himself called “the infernal heresy of the Immaculate Conception” – had raised the young brother high in his esteem. But when a tearful young novice came to him to reveal that Gerard was wasting away not from bodily ailment, but from grief for a woman, all his old sharp contempt flared back.
He was determined to stamp out this unseemly scandal. He visited Clement (Gerard’s monastic name) twice each day in his cell, bringing all his old authority, all his fiery, persuasive eloquence, to rouse the young man from his unspiritual despondency, to stop him robbing the Church of such rare piety and such a gifted preacher at so critical a time. Gerard heard every word, nodded, agreed his reasoning was sound, admired Jerome’s iron strength, confessed his own weakness – and still visibly faded, day by day, toward the land of the faithful departed.
One day Jerome told him he had learned the full story of Gerard’s life, and heard it with great pride. “But now,” he said, “you spoil it all, Clement. This is no noble end, it is the triumph of earthly passion. Better to have yielded to your love while she lived and repented after, than to resist it all her days, only to succumb to it now, body and soul.”
“Dear Jerome,” Gerard replied, his tone so soft and sweet it stripped the rebuke from Jerome’s remonstrance, “you do me an injustice here. There is no raging passion left in me, only a deep, quiet affection I will not blush for here, for I will never blush for it in heaven. Bethink you, Jerome: the poor hound that dies of grief on his master’s grave – no one calls that sinful passion, do they? Neither am I guilty of it. Passion would have saved my life and lost my soul. She was my good angel. She sustained me in my duties and my works of charity, her face gave me courage in the pulpit, her lips soothed me when the people I served turned ungrateful. She wove herself into every good part of my life, and after leaning on her so long, I could not bear to walk the rest of the road alone. And dear Jerome, believe me, I am no rebel against Heaven. It is God’s own will to release me. When they threw the first spadeful of earth onto her poor coffin, something snapped right here in my bosom that no man can mend. I heard it, I felt it. Ever since that hour, no food I put in my mouth has any savour. Blindfolded, I could not tell you bread from meat by the taste.”
“Holy saints!” Jerome gasped.
“And from that very same hour, my heavy dark despair left me, and I smile again. I know why. The Lord who holds life and death in his hands gave me my great summons that minute: some last thin cord of mortal life snapped within me. He is very pitiful. I would have lived a long, miserable empty life, but He said, ‘No; enough is done, enough is suffered, poor feeble loving servant. Thy shortcomings are forgiven, thy sorrows draw to an end. Come thou to thy rest.’ I come, Lord! I come!”
Jerome groaned. “The Church has ever had her holy but feeble servants. I would give ten years of my own life to save yours. But I see it may not be. Die in peace.”
And so, a few days later, Gerard lay dying in a frame of mind so holy and bright that more than one aged, long-proven saintly monk lingered by his cell to garner his dying words. That evening, he had seen his brother Giles, and begged him not to let poor old Jack starve, to see that little Gerard’s trustees did their duty, to kiss his elderly parents for him, and to send his old comrade Denys safely to his family in Burgundy: “Poor thing, he will feel so strange here without his old companion.”
After that, he spoke alone with Jerome. What passed between them was never distinctly known, but it must have been a profound, remarkable meeting, for Jerome stepped out of the cell with his arms crossed over his breast, his proud high head bowed low, sighing softly as he walked.
The two monks who kept watch over him through the night till matins reported that he broke out every little while in pious ejaculations, praises, and quiet thanksgivings. Only once, they said, he wandered a little, and cried that he saw Margaret walking in green meadows among other spirits clad all in white, all of them smiling and beckoning him to come. Both the monks swore (though it may well have been fancy) that just before dawn, three soft, slow taps sounded on the cell wall, one after another. The dying man heard them, and whispered, “I come, love, I come.”
This much is certain: Gerard spoke those words, and made ready for his departure. He sent for all the monks who were keeping their vigils at that hour, and they came, hovering like gentle spirits round him with words of holy comfort. Some knelt in silence, their faces pressed to the cold stone floor to pray for him; others tenderly supported his head. When one of them began to speak of his life of self-denial and charity, Gerard stopped him, and addressed them all clearly:
“My dear brethren, take note that he who dies here so happy does not hold to these new-fangled doctrines of man’s own merit. Oh, what a miserable hour this would be for me if I did! Nay, I hold, with the Apostles, and their pupils the ancient Church fathers, that we are justified not by our own wisdom, or piety, or the works we have done in holiness of heart, but by faith.”(1)
Silence fell, and the monks glanced at one another significantly.
“Please you sweep the floor,” the dying Christian said, his voice suddenly restored to all its old clarity and force. They obeyed at once, awed and curious. “Make me a great cross with wood ashes.” They strewed the ashes across the stone floor in the shape of a wide, great cross. “Now lay me down on it, for so will I die.” They lifted him gently from his bed, and laid him soft upon the ash cross.
“Shall we spread out thine arms, dear brother?” one asked.
“Now God forbid! Am I worthy of that?”
He lay silent, his eyes raised in ecstasy. Presently he spoke half to them, half to himself: “Oh, it feels buoyant. It lifts me, floating up to the sky, whence my own merits would have sunk me like lead.”
Day broke, and its light fell on his face cast upward in silent rapture, his hands folded tight together, just as Margaret’s had been. Around the very same hour she had breathed her last, he spoke his final word in this world: “Jesu.” And even with that word on his lips – he fell asleep.
They laid him out for his last resting place. Under his linen robe, they found his horsehair shirt. “Ah!” the young monks cried, “behold a saint!” But under the rough hair cloth, they found a long, thick tress of auburn hair. They recoiled, horrified, a babel of voices breaking out, some condemning him, some excusing him.
Right in the midst of the uproar, Jerome walked in, heard the dispute, and turned to the ardent young monk Basil, who was shouting scandal the loudest. “Basil,” he said, “is the woman who owned this hair alive or dead?”
“How may I know, father?”
“Then for aught you know, it may be the relic of a saint?”
“Certes it may be,” Basil replied sceptically.
“Then you have broken our rule,” Jerome told him, “which saith, ‘Put no ill construction on an act done by a brother which may be construed innocently.’ Who are you to judge such a man as this was? Go to your cell, and stir not out for a week by way of penance.”
He carried the lock of hair away himself. When the coffin was to be closed, he cleared all the other monks out of the cell, and laid the tress soft on the dead man’s bosom. “There, Clement,” he whispered to the still face. He set himself a private penance for the act, and nailed the coffin shut with his own hands.
The next day Gerard was buried in Gouda churchyard. The monks followed him in procession from the convent, and Jerome, carrying out the unspoken wishes of the deceased, read the full burial service. The grave was dug extra deep, and at its bottom rested Margaret’s lead coffin. Gerard’s own little coffin, light as a feather he was so wasted away, was lowered down and placed gently by its side.
After the service, Jerome spoke a few words to the crowd of parishioners who had gathered to take one last look at their best friend. When he spoke of the departed’s gentle virtues, loud wailing and weeping burst forth, and tears fell on the coffin like rain.
The monks returned to the convent, and Jerome gathered them all in the refectory to speak to them. “We have this day laid a saint in the earth,” he said. “The convent will keep his trentals, but we will feast, not fast; for our good brother is freed from the burden of the flesh. His labours are over, and he has entered into his joyful rest. I alone shall fast, and do penance; for to my shame I say it, I was unjust to him, and knew not his true worth till it was too late. And you, young monks, do not be curious to inquire whether the lock he bore on his bosom was a token of pure earthly affection or the relic of a saint. Remember only the heart that beat beneath it. Most of all, fix your eyes upon his life and conversation, and follow them an ye may: for he was a holy man.”
Thus after all life’s fitful fever, these two true lovers were at peace. The grave, kinder to them than the Church ever was, united them for ever; and now a man of another age and nation, touched deeply by their fate, has laboured to raise their tombstone, to rescue them from long and unmerited oblivion. He asks for your sympathy, but not your pity. Put this story to a wholesome use. So much fiction peddles false views of life and death: here, curbed by history, it gives you true ones. Let the cruel barriers that kept these two apart prepare you to accept that on this earth, there will nearly always be some obstacle or other to your perfect happiness. To their early death, apply your Reason and your Faith, by way of exercise and preparation. For if you cannot bear to be told that these two died young – who had they lived a hundred years would still be dead – how shall you bear to see the gentle, the loving, and the true glide from your own bosom to the grave, and fly from your house to heaven? Yet this is in store for you. In every age, the Master of life and death, who is kinder as well as wiser than we are, has transplanted to heaven, young, earth’s sweetest flowers. I ask your sympathy, then, for their rare constancy and pure affection, and their cruel separation by a vile heresy(2) in the bosom of the Church; but not your pity for their early but happy end.
(1) He was citing from Clement of Rome – *οὐ δι ἑαυτῶν δικαιούμεθα οὐδὲ διὰ τῆς ὑμετέρας σοφίας, ἢ εὐσεβείας ἢ ἔργων ὧν κατειργασάμεθα ἐν ὁσιότητι καρδίας, ἀλλὰ διὰ τῆς πίστεως*. – *Epist. ad Corinth.* i. 32.
(2) Celibacy of the clergy, an invention truly fiendish.
