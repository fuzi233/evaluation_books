Silence enveloped the evening streets of Manhattan. Newland Archer strolled along Fifth Avenue, the gas lamps casting a warm glow on the deserted sidewalks. As he walked, the sound of horse hooves echoed through the air, and the occasional carriage passed by, its occupants lost in conversation. The windows of the Reggie Chiverses' mansion, where a dinner for the Duke was underway, shone brightly, drawing Archer's attention. Further down the street, he spotted Mr. du Lac ascending the steps of the Dagonets' brownstone, and Mr. Skipworth hurrying towards the Lanning residence.

Archer's thoughts turned to Beaufort, who had recently begun to frequent a small house beyond Lexington Avenue, adorned with beribboned window curtains and flower-boxes. The canary-colored brougham of Miss Fanny Ring often waited outside, hinting at a clandestine connection. The world beyond Mrs. Archer's narrow circle was a mystery to her, and she preferred to keep it that way. The artists, musicians, and writers who inhabited this quarter lived in their own realm, separate from the social hierarchy.

Medora Manson's failed literary salon had been an attempt to bridge the gap, but it had ultimately faltered due to the reluctance of the literary community to participate. The Blenkers, with their intense mother and blowsy daughters, had tried to recreate the salon's spirit, hosting Edwin Booth, Patti, and other notable figures from the arts. However, Mrs. Archer and her circle viewed these individuals with a mix of fascination and trepidation, sensing that there was something unknown and unsettling about them.

Literature and art were respected in the Archer household, but the notion of gentility had changed over time. The celebrated authors of the past, like Washington Irving and Fitz-Greene Halleck, had been gentlemen; the modern writers and artists, however, seemed to inhabit a different world, one that was harder to navigate. When discussing the changing social landscape, Mrs. Archer would often reminisce about the simplicity of her youth, when everyone between the Battery and Canal Street knew each other, and only those one knew had carriages. Now, the lines were blurred, and Mrs. Archer preferred not to try to understand.
