Tito returned swiftly from Siena, only to embark immediately upon another journey, leaving behind the heavy silence of the city until August seventeenth. Two weeks had elapsed since the arrest, and the prisoners remained in their confinement, their fate suspended in a limbo of uncertainty. To Romola, the world outside the prison walls had ceased to exist; her entire reality narrowed to the trembling balance of probability regarding those inside. At times, hope flared brightly. The delay itself became an instrument of survival; the authorities, terrified of the odium attached to condemning five distinguished citizens, hesitated to strike the blow. A sentence of death and confiscation would ignite a rancorous hatred from the families of the accused, falling like a curse upon anyone deemed responsible. Conversely, lenity appeared dangerous, with enemies of the Republic whispering that mercy would be the signal for their assault. Yet, the scales tipped neither way decisively.

The power to judge rested with the Eight, the body governing criminal justice. Their sentence required a majority of six—a threshold known as *set fave*, or "six beans," a phrase that echoed the fragile political pulse of Florence. However, the Eight recoiled from the weight of this responsibility, petitioning the Signoria—the Gonfaloniere and the eight Priors—to share the burden. The Signoria, in turn, shrugged off the heat of the matter, proposing an appeal to the Great Council. According to a law championed by Savonarola three years prior, a citizen condemned by the fatal six votes possessed the right of appeal.

Yet, the friends of the accused, driven by a desperate need for time, fiercely resisted the formal appeal. Strict legality demanded the sentence be pronounced before the appeal could be lodged, creating a procedural deadlock. They argued that the sentence itself was premature. This resistance held sway, leading to a convoluted middle path: the case was referred to a massive assembly convening on the seventeenth, comprising all higher magistrates, the Senate of Eighty, and a select circle of citizens.

On this day, Romola, her heart hammered by the fear that her godfather's destiny would be sealed before the session ended, secured leave to visit the prisoners. It was permitted only in the presence of witnesses. Returning home along the Via de' Bardi with her cousin Brigida, she remained in a state of suspended animation, unaware of the council's verdict. Brigida departed quickly to wait for news at a friend's house within one of the magistracies, promising to return with authentic tidings.

Romola collapsed onto the first seat of the bright salon, too agitated, too sick at heart, to care for propriety or the discordant beauty surrounding her. With her back to the door, her head resting in her hands, she waited. Time stretched thin; Brigida's absence felt like an eternity. Finally, the door swung open. Romola braced herself, expecting her cousin, but the figure stepping inside brought a chill that froze the blood.

It was Tito.

Since their parting on that fateful night, Romola had clung to the illusion of his innocence, bolstered by evidence that the Medici still trusted him and sent him on errands into Romagna. The obscurity surrounding the conspirators allowed her to believe he had escaped implicating himself. But now, standing before her, the illusion shattered. Her horror of his cruelty toward Baldassarre cast a shadow over every other act he had performed. It was as if she saw fresh blood on his hands, a stain that could never be washed clean.

Hearing his step on the stone floor, a shudder racked her frame. She could not turn, could not rise. He moved silently, pausing before settling on the bench opposite her. He offered no greeting, no apology. His voice, when it finally came, was cool and detached.

"This meeting corresponds with our parting, Romola. But I understand it is a moment of terrible suspense. I have come, if you will listen, to bring you the relief of hope."

Romola started, shifting her posture but fixing him with a doubtful, searching gaze.

"It will not be unwelcome to you to hear—even though it is I who tell it—that the council is prorogued till the twenty-first. The Eight have been frightened at last into passing a sentence of condemnation, but the demand has now been made on behalf of the condemned for the Appeal to the Great Council."

The doubt vanished from Romola's face, replaced by eager anticipation. "And when is it to be made?"

"It has not yet been granted; but it *may* be granted. The Special Council meets again on the twenty-first to deliberate whether the Appeal shall be allowed. In the meantime, there is an interval of three days. Chances may occur in favor of the prisoners; interest may be used on their behalf."

Romola sprang from her seat. Color flooded her cheeks, her hands trembling violently. For a fleeting second, her revulsion toward Tito faded, swallowed by the urgent necessity of saving lives.

"Possibly," Tito observed, rising as well, "your own intention anticipated what I was going to say. You are thinking of the Frate."

"I am," Romola admitted, surprised. "Has he done anything? Is there something to tell me?"

"Only this. It was Messer Francesco Valori's bitterness and violence that chiefly determined the course of affairs today. Half the men who voted against the prisoners were cowed into it. Yet, there are many friends of Fra Girolamo within this Special Council and without—Piero Guicciardini, for instance, a member of the Signoria who made the stoutest resistance; and Giovan Battista Ridolfi, who, as a *Piagnone*, will not lightly forgive the death of his brother Niccolò."

"But how can the Appeal be denied?" Romola cried, indignant. "When it is the law! Was it not one of the chief glories of our popular government to enact this law?"

"They call this an exceptional case. There are ingenious arguments, certainly, but there is much more loud bluster regarding the danger to the Republic. However, no opposition could prevent the assembly from being adjourned. A certain powerful influence, rightly applied during these next three days, might determine the wavering courage of those desiring the Appeal and check the headlong enmity of Francesco Valori. It has come to my knowledge that the Frate has already intervened, sending a message to Valori in favor of Lorenzo Tornabuoni. I know you sometimes have access to the Frate; it would surely be worthwhile to exercise that privilege now."

"It is true," Romola murmured, her expression abstract, distant. "I cannot believe the Frate would approve of denying the Appeal."

"Tis said by many in the courtyard of the Palazzo, before I left, that it would be an everlasting discredit to Fra Girolamo to allow a government composed almost entirely of his party to deny the Appeal without protest, especially after he boasted in his books and sermons that he was the author of the law." Tito leaned closer, his tone dropping to a confidential murmur. "But between ourselves, with all respect for your Frate's ability, my Romola, he has fallen into the habit of preaching a form of human sacrifice called 'killing tyrants.' Some of his followers are beginning to suspect that this rhetoric is inconsistent with the leniency required in the present case."

*I know,* Romola thought, a pang of sorrow cutting through her. *He is driven into those excesses of speech.*

"He is driven into them," she whispered aloud, her voice thick with emotion. "It used to be different. I *will* ask for an interview. I cannot rest without it. I trust in the greatness of his heart."

Her eyes were fixed on the floor, hazy and unfocused, lacking the sharp consciousness that the words issuing from her mouth were spoken to her husband.

"Better lose no time, then," Tito suggested, his manner suavely smooth as he twirled his cap in his hand, preparing to depart. "And now, Romola, you will perhaps be able to see, in spite of prejudice, that my wishes align with yours in this matter. You will not regard the misfortune of my safety as an offense."

An electric shock coursed through Romola. The full, terrifying consciousness of her husband's presence returned to her. She stared at him, silent and stunned.

"At least," he added, his tone hardening imperceptibly, "you will endeavor to base our intercourse on reasoning other than the fact that an evil deed is possible, therefore *I* have done it. Am I alone to be cast beyond the pale of your extensive charity?"

The feelings that had been suppressed for two weeks surged back, gathering the force of a tidal wave. She spoke with a decision that showed her indifference to consequences.

"It is too late, Tito. Deceit, once begotten, cannot be killed. And now I know everything. I know who that old man was: he was your father, to whom you owe everything—to whom you owe more than if you had been his own child. By comparison, it is a small thing that you broke my trust and my father's. As long as you deny the truth about that old man, a horror rises between us. The law that should make us one can never be obeyed. I, too, am a human being. I have a soul of my own that abhors your actions. Our union is a pretense—as if a perpetual lie could constitute a sacred marriage."
