Sir Francis Clavering had spoken of the lady who had fetched him home with her fortune as though she were a sort of rich parcel misdirected by Providence; and—making all allowances for a baronet’s gratitude when it has to dine at the same table with it—he was not entirely astray. Lady Clavering was not what the world calls instructed. Her education had been a short, sharp one, like a cook’s lesson given to a child who keeps tasting the sugar: a couple of years in Europe, in a London suburb which she steadfastly christened “Ackney,” as if the place had offended her and she would not grant it its proper consonants to its dying day. At fifteen she was shipped off to her father in Calcutta; and it was during that voyage—aboard the Ramchunder East Indiaman, Captain Bragg—that she made the acquaintance of Mr. Amory, third mate, and (as the sequel proved) first-rate nuisance.

We need not unpack all the early seasoning of Lady Clavering’s history. Enough that Captain Bragg, who had undertaken to deliver Miss Snell safely to her father—an attorney, a speculator, a man who owned pieces of ships as other men own slices of cheese—found reason, before they reached the Cape, to clap the rebellious rascal of a mate into irons. The voyage itself was stormy enough to make even a curry go cold; the Ramchunder, her cargo, and the passengers were well shaken, wetted, and terrified. Bragg left Mr. Amory behind at the Cape like a bad smell one can’t carry into polite company, and presented his ward at Calcutta to her father, who no doubt weighed her with his eye as he would have weighed a bag of rupees.

A few months later—because a man like Amory is never properly thrown away—he turned up in Calcutta again, having worked his passage out before the mast. He married the rich attorney’s daughter in spite of the old gentleman, then tried, one after another, to set himself up: indigo-planter and failed; agent and failed; editor of the Sunderbund Pilot and failed. Through each enterprise he quarrelled incessantly with his father-in-law and his wife, as if ill-temper were a kind of capital that might replace cash. At last there came a crash so final it compelled him to bolt the place altogether and go to New South Wales.

It was in the course of those unlucky proceedings that he likely made the acquaintance of Sir Jasper Rogers, the respected Calcutta judge we have already met; and since truth has a way of insisting on being served, even when no one has ordered it, it must be said that Mr. Amory’s ruin was clinched by an improper use of his father-in-law’s name—his father-in-law being perfectly able to write for himself, and having no need of a son-in-law to act as pen, ink, and conscience. Europe, which does not read Calcutta law-reports with the appetite it bestows on French menus, knew little of these particulars; Bengal knew them all, and repeated them with the relish people give to stories not happening to themselves.

Mrs. Amory and her father decided that India was not a comfortable kitchen for her to stew in. She returned to Europe with her little daughter Betsy—Blanche—then four years old. With them came Betsy’s nurse, the same Mrs. Bonner who now, in a higher domestic mythology, figures as Lady Clavering’s confidential maid. Captain Bragg took a house for them near his own in Pocklington Street; and the summer that greeted them was bitter as over-brewed tea. Rain drummed daily on the windows—an incessant, joyless percussion like a bad military band practising next door—so that the very sound of London seemed to Mrs. Amory a scolding.

Bragg, pompous and disagreeable, strutted about as though he had personally invented propriety and must keep it varnished. He was perhaps ashamed of his Indian passenger, perhaps merely anxious to be rid of her. She, poor soul, imagined all London was gossiping over her husband’s disgrace: that the King, the Queen, and the Court of Directors were all whispering over their cutlets about Mrs. Amory. She had an allowance from her father and no reason to be drenched in English rain beneath Captain Bragg’s surveillance. So she resolved—like a sensible woman driven from a noisy street—to go where the air was kinder.

Off she went abroad, glad to slip away from that odious bully. Continental towns received her willingly enough. Boarding-houses adored her; she paid royally, like a lady who believes money is chiefly meant to prevent arguments. She called Hackney “Ackney,” certainly—an act of pronunciation like putting cloves in a pudding whether the recipe allows it or not—and though she spoke English with a little foreign twang, it was curious and not unpleasant, like a spice you can’t name but keep tasting. She dressed amazingly. She loved eating and drinking with the devotion other people reserve for religion, and in every boarding-house she turned the common kitchen into a small Calcutta: curries ground and simmered, pillaws steamed, rice washed and aired, onions fried to a brown so fragrant it would have converted a fasting hermit. You could tell when Mrs. Amory was in the building: the corridors carried cumin and ghee the way churches carry incense; even the staircases seemed to breathe.

Her singularities, far from ruining her, gave her society a zest. She was jovial, good-natured, generous—ready for any party of pleasure, by whomsoever proposed. At picnics she brought three times more champagne and fowl and ham than anyone else, and distributed it with a laugh that made the corks sound cheerful instead of merely loud. She took endless boxes at the play, tickets for masked balls, and flung them about as if gaiety were a household staple. She paid boarding-house people months beforehand; she helped shabby moustachioed bucks and anxious dowagers whose remittances lagged, feeding them from her purse as she fed herself from her saucepans. So she tramped through Europe—Brussels, Paris, Milan, Naples, Rome—following fancy like a spoon following gravy.

At Rome news came to her that Amory was dead; and at Rome, too, Captain Clavering happened to be staying—unable to pay his hotel bill, as indeed was his friend the Chevalier Strong. The widow, who professed no particular grief for a husband she had lost in all but geography long before, married the descendant of the ancient house of Clavering with the same good-humoured practicality with which she would order a supper for unexpected guests. Thus she arrived at the present moment: mistress of Clavering Park, posed while Mr. Pinckney, the celebrated painter, portrayed her with her little boy beside her—Master Francis, the heir and the appetite.

Missy—Blanche—had followed her mamma through most of those peregrinations, and learned a great deal of life, as children do when the world is their schoolroom and boarding-house tables their desks. There had been a governess for a while; then, after the second marriage, the select pension of Madame de Caramel in the Champs Elysees—a place where young ladies were taught accomplishments with the same careful tyranny with which cooks are taught to whip eggs. When the Claverings came to England, Blanche came too.

It was only within a few years—after the death of her grandfather, and after the birth of her little brother—that she began to understand the change in her place at table. Miss Amory, nobody’s daughter, was suddenly a very small personage in a great house compared with Master Francis Clavering, heir to an ancient baronetcy and a noble estate. But for little Frank she would have been an heiress, in spite of her father. She knew, and did not much care about money—she had never known stint. She was a romantic little Muse, full of verse and attitudes. Yet she could not reasonably be grateful to the people who had helped to alter her condition; nor did she fully understand what that condition meant until she had made further progress and acquired more exact knowledge of the world’s dictionary—where certain words are written in larger type than others.

This much, however, was clear to her. Her stepfather was dull and weak; mamma dropped her H’s and was not refined; and little Frank was a spoiled, quarrelsome urchin, always having his way, always treading on Blanche’s feet, always upsetting his dinner on her dresses—as if gravy, like the estate, were meant to flow toward him—and, worst of all in Blanche’s eyes, keeping her out of her inheritance. None of them, she felt, could comprehend her. So her solitary heart pined for other attachments, and she cast about for someone upon whom she might bestow the precious boon of her unoccupied affection.

The consequence was that, from want of sympathy or from surplus of temper, she made herself so disagreeable at home, frightened her mother, and bored her stepfather so much, that they were almost as anxious as she could be that she should settle for herself. Hence Sir Francis Clavering’s wish, expressed to his friend in the last chapter, that Mrs. Strong should die—so that he might take Blanche to himself as a second Mrs. Strong. But as Mrs. Strong persisted in living, any other person was welcome to win Blanche; and a smart young fellow, good-looking and well educated like Arthur Pendennis, might have proposed for her if he had the mind—and would have been received with open arms by Lady Clavering as a son-in-law—had he possessed the courage to come forward.

Mr. Pen, however, was afflicted with that particular vanity which calls itself diffidence. Ashamed of his late failures, his idle and nameless condition, and the poverty he had brought upon his mother by his folly, he nursed doubt and distrust as though they were virtues. How could he ever hope for such a prize as brilliant Blanche Amory—she in a fine park and mansion waited on by a score of grand domestics—while at Fairoaks a maid-servant brought in a meagre meal and his mother pinched and managed and made two ends meet only by sewing them together? Obstacles looked insurmountable which would have melted before a bold advance. He preferred despairing, or dallying with his wishes—or perhaps not quite shaping them—to making a gallant attempt. Many a young man fails through that species of vanity called shyness, who might, for the asking, have his will. We do not even say that Pen had yet ascertained his will, or was doing more than thinking about falling in love.

Miss Amory was charming and lively. She fascinated and cajoled him by a thousand arts—or natural graces, or flatteries. Yet besides shyness and vanity, there were other doubts. Pen’s mother had divined the girl and did not trust her. Mrs. Pendennis saw Blanche light-minded and frivolous; detected wants in her which offended the pure and pious lady—a want of reverence for parents and for things more sacred; worldliness and selfishness lurking beneath pretty words and tender expressions.

Laura and Pen battled these points at first with the widow—Laura still enthusiastic about her new friend, and Pen not far gone enough to hide his feelings. He laughed at his mother’s objections. “Psha, mother! you are jealous about Laura—all women are jealous.”

But when, in the course of a month or two, Helen watched with that anxious attention with which brooding women watch a son’s affection—there is, in the confession of it, a kind of sexual jealousy in the mother, a secret pang—when she saw the intimacy progressing; saw the two young people perpetually finding pretexts to meet; saw Miss Blanche at Fairoaks or Mr. Pen at the Park every day; then the poor widow’s heart began to fail her. Her darling project seemed to vanish. Giving way to her weakness, she told Pen one day what her hopes had been: that she felt herself breaking and not long for this world; and that she prayed before she went, she might see her two children one.

Late events—Pen’s career, his life, his former passion for the actress—had shaken the tender lady’s spirit. She felt he had escaped her; that he was in the maternal nest no more. She clung with a sickening fondness to Laura—Laura left to her by Francis in Heaven. Pen kissed and soothed her with a grand, patronising kindness. He had long suspected his mother’s wish. Did Laura know anything of it? Not she, Mrs. Pendennis said; not for worlds would she have breathed a word to Laura.

“Well, well,” Pen replied, laughing in that way young men do when they are frightened but would rather sound witty than honest. “There is time enough. You won’t die. And as for the Muse—she is too grand a lady to think about poor little me. And as for Laura—who knows she would have me? She would do anything you told her, to be sure. But am I worthy of her?”

“O, Pen, you might be,” the widow answered; and though Mr. Pen never truly doubted his worthiness, a pleasant self-complacency came over him as he thought of the proposal and imaged Laura as he had known her for years: fair and open, kindly and pious, cheerful, tender, and true.

At the end of this talk Laura came in from the garden. Her cheeks were a little flushed; her look frank and smiling; in her hand a basket of roses. She took the finest and brought it to Mrs. Pendennis, refreshed the room with its colour and odour, and hung over her mother with fondness as she gave it.

“And I might have this prize for the asking!” Pen thought, with a thrill of triumph, looking at the kindly girl. “Why, she is as beautiful and as generous as her roses.” The image of the two women remained with him ever after; he never recalled it without tears rising in his eyes.

Before many weeks had passed, however, Laura was obliged to concede that Helen’s judgment of Blanche was right: the Muse was selfish, unkind, inconstant. Of course Blanche confided to her bosom friend the little griefs and domestic annoyances: how the family could not comprehend her; how she moved among them isolated; how poor mamma’s education had been neglected and Blanche was forced to blush for her blunders; how Sir Francis was weak and deplorably unintellectual and only happy when smoking odious cigars; how since the birth of her little brother she had seen her mother’s precious affection estranged from the once darling daughter; how she was alone, alone, alone in the world.

These griefs, however real to a young lady of exquisite sensibility, did not persuade Laura that Blanche’s conduct in small incidents was proper. Little Frank might be provoking; might have deprived Blanche of mamma’s caresses; but this was no reason for Blanche to box the child’s ears because he upset a glass of water over her drawing; nor to call him opprobrious names in English and French. Nor was the preference accorded to little Frank any reason for Blanche to assume imperial airs toward the boy’s governess, and send that young lady on errands to fetch a book or a pocket-handkerchief, as if a governess were a bell-pull.

When a domestic did an errand for Laura, she always thanked them with that warmth which costs nothing and buys comfort; whereas Laura could not help seeing that Blanche gave commands as naturally as she breathed, disturbing anybody’s ease to administer to her own. It was Laura’s first great experiment in friendship; and it pained her generous heart to strip away, one by one, the charms she had dressed her new friend in, and to find the fascinating fairy a mortal—and not a very amiable mortal. What generous person has not been deceived so? And who has not, in turn, deceived somebody else?

After the scene with little Frank—after the French and English compliments, and the box on the ear—Laura, who had plenty of humour, could not help remembering some touching verses Blanche had read from Mes Larmes. They began: “My pretty baby brother, may angels guard thy rest,” and proceeded to compliment the infant upon the station he was about to occupy, contrast it with Blanche’s own lonely condition, and vow that he would never find affection so constant as a sister’s heart.

“It may be,” the forlorn one had sighed, “it may be, you will slight it, my pretty baby sweet,
You will spurn me from your bosom, I’ll cling around your feet!
O let me, let me, love you! the world will prove to you
As false as ’tis to others, but I am ever true.”

And here was the Muse boxing the darling’s ears instead of kneeling at his feet; giving Laura her lesson in cynical philosophy—not quite her first, indeed: she had seen something of the same contrast between poetry and practice at home, in Mr. Pen. But then Pen was different. Pen was a man. It seemed somehow natural that he should be self-willed and have his own way; and under his selfishness there was a kind and generous heart. Hard, thought Laura, that such a diamond should be exchanged for such a false stone.

In a word, Laura began to tire of Blanche. She had assayed her and found her not true. Her former admiration—so frankly expressed—gave way to a feeling we will not call contempt, but which lived next door to it; and Laura adopted toward Blanche a grave, tranquil superiority which was not at all to the Muse’s liking. Nobody enjoys being found out; nobody who has posed as a queen likes being asked to step down and mind the scullery. Blanche’s knowledge that she was slipping in Laura’s esteem did not improve her temper; it made her peevish, dissatisfied with herself, and therefore less agreeable to others.

So there arose, one fatal day, a battle-royal between dearest Blanche and dearest Laura, in which their friendship was all but slain outright.

Blanche had been unusually capricious and wicked. She had been insolent to her mother; savage with little Frank; odiously impertinent to the boy’s governess; intolerably cruel to Pincott, her attendant—menial, confidante, companion, and always slave, according to the weather in Miss Blanche’s mind. The Muse, timid as a cat with claws only for weaker creatures, did not venture to attack Laura outright; so she raked everyone else.

Pincott, driven from the room in tears by her mistress’s cruelty and sent sobbing out with a parting sarcasm like a thrown crust, was too much for Laura. Laura broke out into loud indignation. How could one so young forget the deference owed to elders as well as to inferiors? How could one who talked so much of sensibility torture other people’s feelings wantonly? Laura told Blanche her conduct was wicked—absolutely wicked—and that she ought to ask pardon of Heaven on her knees for it.

Having delivered herself of a hot, voluble speech whose vehemence astonished the speaker almost as much as the auditor, Laura fled for her bonnet and shawl and went home across the park in a great flurry, to Mrs. Pendennis’s surprise, who had not expected her until night.

Alone with Helen, Laura recounted the scene and gave up her friend henceforth. “O Mamma,” she cried, “you were right. Blanche, who seems so soft and kind, is selfish and cruel. She who is always speaking of her affections can have no heart. No honest girl would afflict a mother so, or torture a dependant; and—and I give her up from this day, and I will have no other friend but you.”

The two ladies performed the osculatory ceremony in which they habitually indulged. Mrs. Pendennis derived a secret comfort from the quarrel. It seemed to say, “That girl can never be a wife for Pen. She is light-minded and heartless, unworthy of our noble hero. He will find out her unworthiness, and be saved.”

But Laura did not tell her mother—perhaps did not tell herself—what the real spark had been.

Blanche, in a wicked mood and bent upon mischief as other people are bent upon cookery, had begun her tricks early. Laura had come to pass a long day; and as they sat together in Blanche’s room, Blanche artfully steered the talk toward Mr. Pendennis.

“I am afraid he is sadly fickle,” Miss Blanche observed. “Mrs. Pybus, and many more Clavering people, have told us all about the actress.”

“I was quite a child when it happened,” Laura answered, blushing, “and I don’t know anything about it.”

“He used her very ill,” Blanche said, wagging her little head. “He was false to her.”

“I am sure he was not!” Laura cried. “He acted most generously by her. He wanted to give up everything to marry her. It was she that was false to him. He nearly broke his heart about it. He—”

“I thought you didn’t know anything about the story, dearest,” Blanche interposed sweetly, letting the word dearest do the work of a pin.

“Mamma has said so,” Laura faltered.

“Well, he is very clever,” Blanche continued, as though nothing had happened. “What a sweet poet he is! Have you ever read his poems?”

“Only the ‘Fisherman and the Diver,’ which he translated for us, and his Prize Poem, which didn’t get the prize; and indeed I thought it very pompous and prosy,” Laura said, laughing in spite of herself.

“Has he never written you any poems, then, love?” asked Miss Amory.

“No, my dear,” said Miss Bell.

Blanche ran up, kissed her, called her “my dearest Laura” at least three times, looked archly in her face, nodded, and said, “Promise to tell no-o-body, and I will show you something.”

She tripped to a little mother-of-pearl inlaid desk, opened it with a silver key, and drew out two or three papers crumpled and stained with green—like leaves pressed too roughly in a book. She put them in Laura’s hands.

Laura read them. They were love-verses—something about Undine, a Naiad, a river. She looked long, but the lines were not distinct before her eyes; the room seemed to swim as if the river in the poem had overflowed.

“And you have answered them, Blanche?” she asked at last, putting the papers back.

“O no! not for worlds, dearest,” Blanche replied; and when Laura had finished, Blanche popped the verses back into the pretty desk as one hides a sweetmeat from a hungry child.

Then Blanche went to the piano and sang two or three Rossini songs. Her flexible little voice executed the flourishes to perfection, trilling and running like a bright stream over stones. Laura sat by, vaguely listening as the notes filled the room and made a kind of gaiety in the air; but inside her the sound went oddly, as if it had found some hollow place. What was she thinking? She hardly knew. She sat silent while the songs rolled by.

After the concert they were summoned to luncheon; they went, of course, with arms around each other’s waists. And it could not have been jealousy or anger that had made Laura silent; for as they passed along the corridor, descended the steps, and paused at the door leading into the hall, Laura stopped, looked Blanche kindly and frankly in the face, and kissed her with sisterly warmth.

Then something occurred—Master Frank’s manner of eating, perhaps, or mamma’s blunders, or Sir Francis smelling of cigars—that vexed Blanche. She gave way to that chain of naughtiness: insolence, savagery, cruelty, tyranny; and it ended, as we have seen, with Pincott driven off in tears, Laura’s indignant outburst, and the friendship lying wounded on the floor like a dropped fan nobody thinks to pick up until it is broken.
