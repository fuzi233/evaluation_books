The moment I crossed the threshold, cold met me as if I had walked into the belly of an ice-house—air so clean it bit, so dry it seemed to scour the palate like crushed snow. The door fell to behind us with a hush that swallowed the last familiar sound, and in that sudden stillness the sexton leaned toward his wife and said something under his breath.

She turned.

What had happened to her in the few minutes since I last saw her was beyond any easy telling. It was as if the great splendour that used to blaze from her eyes had overflowed its bounds and filled her whole face; not a feverish flush, but a lucent quiet, like light held in milk. Her hands, too—those hands that had seemed carved for gentle labour—shone pale as pearl, every little “helmet” of nail catching the candle’s frail flame and returning it with a moonstone gleam. The sight struck me with the same pressure as a scent too strong in a close room: beautiful, overpowering, almost suffocating. I was glad when she turned that radiance away.

The candlelight reached no farther than a few cautious steps. At first the place was only blackness and cold—an immense, stored cold, like a larder cut in rock where winter is kept for summer’s need. Then the flame fell on something that glimmered just above the floor.

A bed?

Who could sleep in such mortal chill? If anything here did sleep, no wonder it did not wake of itself.

Beyond that first shine there came another, fainter, as though the darkness were sprinkled with frost. And then—uncertain at first, then undeniable—gleams on every side. We took a few paces and the candle disclosed the thing beneath the shimmer: a human form under a sheet, straight and still. I could not tell whether man or woman; the light seemed to slide away from the face as we passed, as if unwilling to touch it.

We were walking, I saw, along an aisle of couches. On almost every one, with its head toward the passage, lay something asleep—or dead—covered with a sheet white as sifted flour. My mind, that had been loud with questions, became quiet with dread. Aisle after aisle we went, couches without count. I could see only a handful at once, but they were on all sides, vanishing as if the chamber had no walls—only distance.

Was this where my choice of bed waited? Must I lie down among the unwaking, with no one to rouse me? Was this the sexton’s library, and were these his books—human volumes closed and shelved on stone?

Truly it was no half-way house: this was a store-room of the unanswering.

“One of the cellars I am placed to watch,” remarked Mr. Raven, and his voice was low, as if even breath were noise enough to offend. “Much wine is set here to ripen!—But it is dark for a stranger!” he added.

“The moon is rising; she will soon be here,” said his wife.

Her clear voice, low and sweet, sounded like an old song hummed while hands are busy—something sorrowful, yet long since folded and put away, as one lays dried herbs between paper to keep their scent without letting them dominate a room.

Even as she spoke, the moon looked in through an opening in the wall, and a thousand little gleams answered her. The sheets took her light the way salt takes moonlight by a coast—coldly, instantly. Yet even with that added brightness I could not descry beginning or end. Couches stretched away and away, as if the whole disparted world had been given each a narrow bed, each a private silence.

At first I thought their sleep was simply death. But as the moon climbed, and the pallid faces emerged from shadow into her bluish glare, I knew it was something deeper still—something I did not know, and had no name for.

The moon rose higher and shone through other openings. Still I could never see enough at once to understand the chamber. Now it seemed a cathedral nave with its long aisles of devotion; now a huge barn repurposed as a dwelling of tombs. The moon herself looked colder than any moon I had known, and where she shone straight upon the sleepers, she cast an icy glaze upon the sheets and the features—though it might have been that the faces made the moon so cold.

Of such as I could see, all were alike in the brotherhood of this sleep; all were unlike in the histories written upon them. Here lay a man who had died—though this was not death, and I had no other word—at the height of strength: his dark beard flowed from his frozen countenance like a stream released from a glacier’s lip; his forehead was smooth as polished marble; a shadow of pain lingered around his mouth, but it was only a shadow, thinned as a remembered bitterness in a dish long since sweetened.

On the next couch lay a girl, passing lovely to behold. The sadness of parting had not yet been wholly absorbed into peace, but absolute submission possessed her placid features. There was no sign of wasting disease, no mark of “killing care or grief of heart.” If pain had visited, it had been charmed asleep as utterly as a bruise that no longer aches when the body is healed past reminding.

Many were beautiful, lying very still—some mere children; but I saw no infant. The most beautiful of all was a lady whose white hair—and that alone—suggested she was old when first she fell asleep. On her stately countenance rested not submission, but a noble acquiescence, an assurance firm as the foundations of the universe that all was as it should be.

On some faces lingered the nearly erased scars of strife, the faint marrings of hopeless loss, the last shadows of sorrows that must once have seemed unendurable. It was as if dawn had begun its work but had not yet melted every rim of frost. Those faces were few, and each seemed to plead, “Pardon me: I died only yesterday!” or, “Pardon me: I died but a century ago!” That some had lain thus for ages I knew—not merely by their unutterable repose, but by something for which I had neither word nor symbol, as untranslatable as the taste of a childhood broth remembered years later in a foreign land.

At last we came to three empty couches. Immediately beyond them lay the form of a beautiful woman a little past her prime. One arm was outside the sheet, and her hand lay palm upward. In the centre of it was a dark spot.

Next to her lay the stalwart figure of a man in middle age. His arm too was outside the sheet, the strong hand almost closed as if still gripping the hilt of a sword. I thought he must be a king who had fallen fighting for truth.

“Will you hold the candle nearer, wife?” whispered the sexton, bending to examine the woman’s hand.

The candle moved, and its little flame made the dark spot look darker.

“It heals well,” he murmured to himself. “The nail found in her nothing to hurt!”

At last I ventured to speak, though my voice felt out of place here, like a spoon clinking in a sanctuary of soundless snow.

“Are they not dead?” I asked softly.

“I cannot answer you,” he replied, still subdued. “I almost forget what they mean by DEAD in the old world. If I said a person was dead, my wife would understand one thing, and you would imagine another.—This is but one of my treasure vaults,” he went on, “and all my guests are not laid in vaults: out there on the moor they lie thick as the leaves of a forest after the first blast of your winter—thick, let me say rather, as if the great white rose of heaven had shed its petals over it. All night the moon reads their faces, and smiles.”

“But why leave them in the moonlight?” I asked. “Would it not corrupt them?”

“Our moon,” he answered, “is not like yours—the old cinder of a burnt-out world; her beams embalm the dead, not corrupt them. You observe that here the sexton lays his dead on the earth; he buries very few under it! In your world he lays huge stones on them, as if to keep them down; I watch for the hour to ring the resurrection-bell, and wake those that are still asleep. Your sexton looks at the clock to know when to ring the dead-alive to church; I hearken for the cock on the spire to crow; ‘AWAKE, THOU THAT SLEEPEST, AND ARISE FROM THE DEAD!’”

The words, in that enormous hush, sounded less like preaching than like an old refrain—one that had been carried through generations, mouthed even when its meaning was lost, and yet still held power over those who remembered the dialect of its first singing. I began to suspect the self-styled sexton was in truth an insane parson; the whole thing was too mad.

But how was I to get away from it? I was helpless. In this world of sleepers the raven and his wife were the only living I had yet seen. Whither should I turn for help? I felt myself lost in a space larger than imagination; and a thought came, swift and sickening, that if here two things could occupy the same place—if realities could interpenetrate without mingling—why not twenty, why not ten thousand? I dared not follow that thought to its end.

“You seem in your dead to see differences beyond my perception!” I ventured.

“None of those you see,” he answered, “are in truth quite dead yet, and some have but just begun to come alive and die. Others had begun to die, that is to come alive, long before they came to us; and when such are indeed dead, that instant they will wake and leave us. Almost every night some rise and go. But I will not say more, for I find my words only mislead you!”

He stopped beside the three empty couches.

“This is the couch that has been waiting for you,” he said, and pointed to one.

“Why just this?” I asked, and I began to tremble—more with the cold than fear, I told myself, though the lie tasted thin.

“For reasons which one day you will be glad to know.”

“Why not know them now?”

“That also you will know when you wake.”

“But these are all dead, and I am alive!” I objected, shuddering.

“Not much,” rejoined the sexton with a smile, “—not nearly enough! Blessed be the true life that the pauses between its throbs are not death!”

“The place is too cold to let one sleep!” I said.

“Do these find it so?” he returned. “They sleep well—or will soon. Of cold they feel not a breath: it heals their wounds.—Do not be a coward, Mr. Vane. Turn your back on fear, and your face to whatever may come. Give yourself up to the night, and you will rest indeed. Harm will not come to you, but a good you cannot foreknow.”

We stood by the couch: the sexton and I at its side; his wife at its foot with the candle. Her eyes were full of light still, but her face had gone back to a still whiteness; it no longer poured radiance.

“Would they have me make of a charnel-house my bed-chamber?” I cried aloud, and my voice broke the air like a dropped pan in a quiet kitchen. “I will not. I will lie abroad on the heath; it cannot be colder there!”

“I have just told you that the dead are there also, ‘Thick as autumnal leaves that strow the brooks in Vallombrosa,’” said the librarian.

“I will NOT,” I cried again.

In the compassing dark the two of them gleamed out like spectres that waited on the dead. Neither answered me. Each stood still and sad, and looked at the other. The silence around us did not merely lack sound; it held sound at bay, as if this place were an acoustic sanctuary where even fear ought to tread softly.

“Be of good comfort; we watch the flock of the great shepherd,” said the sexton to his wife.

Then he turned to me.

“Didst thou not find the air of the place pure and sweet when thou enteredst it?” he asked.

“Yes; but oh, so cold!” I answered.

“Then know,” he returned, and his voice grew stern, “that thou who callest thyself alive, hast brought into this chamber the odours of death, and its air will not be wholesome for the sleepers until thou art gone from it!”

They went farther into the great chamber, the candle dwindling as they withdrew, and I was left alone in the moonlight with the sleepers.

I turned to escape.

What a long way it was back through the dead—or through those not dead, those not yet awake. At first I was too angry to be afraid; my indignation burned like pepper on a raw tongue. But as I went on, and the anger cooled, the still shapes grew terrible. The moon lay on the sheets like frost. Every face was a mute record in a language I could not read, yet somehow I understood the power of it—the way a dominant word can crush a lesser one into silence, and with it a whole history of taste and tone and home.

At last, with loud offence to the gracious quiet, I ran. I fled wildly along the narrow ways, and bursting out, flung to the door behind me.

It closed with an awful silence.

I stood in pitch-darkness. Feeling about me, I found another door and opened it. A dim lamp-light met me, as familiar as the smell of old leather.

I stood in my library, with the handle of the masked door in my hand.

Had I come to myself out of a vision—or lost myself by going back to one? Which was real: what I now saw, or what I had just ceased to see? Could both be real, interpenetrating yet unmingling—like two airs in one room, each with its own smell, each insisting it was the only true atmosphere?

I threw myself on a couch and fell asleep.

In the library there was one small window to the east. At this time of year the first rays of the sun struck through it upon a mirror, whence they were reflected straight to the masked door. When I woke, there they shone—thin gold, clean and insistent—and thither they drew my eyes.

With the feeling that behind it must lie the boundless chamber I had left by that door, I sprang up and opened it.

The light, like an eager hound loosed, shot before me into the closet and pounced upon the gilded edges of a large book.

“What idiot,” I cried, “has put that book in the shelf the wrong way?”

But the gilded edges caught the sun and flung it on a nest of drawers in a dark corner. One of them was half open.

“More meddling!” I cried, and went to close it.

It was stuffed with old papers, so full it would not shut. I took out the topmost. The moment I saw the hand, my own breath changed; it was my father’s writing, and of some length. The words on which my eyes first fell made me suddenly hungry to know the whole, as if I had caught a first whiff of something long absent from the house—a spice my mother used, a name spoken in the old way, a taste that could not be translated into any other tongue.

I carried it into the library, sat down in one of the western windows, and read what follows.
