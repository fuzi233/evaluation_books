To Mr. Thornton, Margaret's transgression was not merely a breach of truth; it was a betrayal of trust that pointed directly to another lover. The memory of that glance, exchanged with a man whose identity he refused to name, burned in his mind with a ferocity that no amount of rationalization could extinguish. He saw the scene replay in his imagination: the dusky twilight, the desolate distance from home, the intimacy that bordered on endearment. While his better judgment whispered that such a meeting might be innocent, the shadow of her deliberate lie crushed that hope. That lie, so unlike her usual candor, proved to him that she possessed a consciousness of guilt regarding some secret attachment. It was a damning evidence that she, despite his fierce love for her, was spiritually bound to another. He chafed under the comparison: she, soft and bewitching with a man who truly held her heart, versus him, rough, stern, and unyielding. He mocked himself for valuing her practical heroism during the riot above the reality of her tender devotion elsewhere.

Consequently, Mr. Thornton found himself consumed by an irritability unknown to him in years. Every interaction felt like a chore, every question demanding a curt, abrupt answer. Though he strove to mask this turmoil with a facade of quiet deliberation, his internal storm manifested in restless pacing throughout the evenings, a habit that grated heavily on his mother's nerves.

"I pray you, sit down," Mrs. Thornton urged, cutting short his endless strides. "We must speak of Betsy."

Mr. Thornton obeyed instantly, perching on a chair by the wall. "Speak."

"Betsy informs me that she must depart," Mrs. Thornton said, her voice tinged with frustration. "Her lover's passing has shattered her spirits; she claims she cannot give her heart to the work anymore."

"And?"

"She adds, however, that she knows the ways of the house intimately. Furthermore, she mentions something concerning your friend, Miss Hale."

"My friend Mr. Hale is not a friend of mine," Mr. Thornton snapped, his composure cracking slightly. "Miss Hale is simply a neighbor."

"If she had been your friend, her words would surely annoy you," Mrs. Thornton pressed gently. "Pray, listen."

Betsy recounted that on the night Leonard died, Miss Hale was seen at the station with a young man whom she believed had pushed or struck Leonard.

"That is false," Mr. Thornton interrupted, his voice rising. "Leonard did not die by violence."

"How do you know?"

"The surgeon of the Infirmary examined the body. He attributed the death to a chronic internal disease aggravated by excessive drinking. The fall, yes, but no blow."

"But Betsy insists there was a push," Mrs. Thornton argued. "And she further states that Woolmer, a grocer's assistant, swore he saw Miss Hale walking with a young man at that precise hour."

"What is this to us?" Mr. Thornton stood abruptly, turning his back to the room, his face a mask of grim stone. "Miss Hale is free to please herself."

"It matters little to you now, John," Mrs. Thornton pleaded, seizing upon the opportunity. "Yet, I made a solemn promise to Mrs. Hale. I vowed I would not let her daughter go astray without advising and remonstrating with her. I intend to fulfill that vow."

"There is no harm in what she did," Mr. Thornton retorted, his shoulders stiff. "Do not compare her to Fanny. Miss Hale possesses weighty reasons for her actions, reasons that justify her conduct in ways I cannot fathom. I believe she guards herself."

"A fine compliment, John!" Mrs. Thornton exclaimed, stepping closer. "You admit he is her lover, do you not?"

"Yes, mother," he said, his voice hollow. "Whatever his name, he is her lover. But..." He turned back, writhing as if in physical pain, leaning his forehead against his hand. "...she may need help. Womanly counsel. There are difficulties, perhaps temptations, that I do not fully grasp. I fear there are. Go to her, Mother. Gain her confidence. Tell her what is best. Some dread tortures her soul."

"For God's sake, John!" Mrs. Thornton cried, shocked by the intensity of his plea. "What do you mean? What accusation have you leveled against her?"

"Not an accusation," he insisted, his eyes blazing. "I *could* not speak against her. But her character... it requires a shield. I have cause to believe she is in straits arising from an attachment that, in itself, is innocent. But the circumstances surrounding it are dire. Never let anyone imply anything worse than that she needs gentle counsel. You promised to be that counselor!"

"No!" Mrs. Thornton declared firmly, her resolve hardening. "I promised counsel and advice, as I would give to my own daughter. I shall speak to her as I would to Fanny if she had gone gallivanting in the dusk. I shall judge her based on facts, not on some mysterious 'strait' you refuse to define. I shall fulfill my promise to Mrs. Hale."

"She will never bear it!" he protested passionately.

"She will have to bear it if I speak in her dead mother's name."

"Well!" He broke away, pacing frantically. "Leave me! I cannot endure this thought. It is better you speak to her than that she remain unheard. Oh, that look of love! And that cursed lie! It revealed a terrible shame she sought to hide from the light in which I believed she lived! Oh, Margaret! Could you not have loved me? I am uncouth, I am hard, but I would never have led you into falsehood."

As Mr. Thornton retreated to his chamber, Mrs. Thornton sat alone, her thoughts turning bitter. The more she reflected on his desperate plea for mercy, the more she felt a savage pleasure in judging Margaret harshly. She delighted in the prospect of stripping away the "glamour" that Margaret wielded over others, proving herself untainted by the beauty that had ensnared so many. With a snort of scorn, she dismissed the image of Margaret's jet-black hair and luminous eyes, resolving to spend the night crafting a speech of stern, just reproach.

"Is Miss Hale within?"

Mrs. Thornton knew the answer before Martha could respond. Margaret was already seated in the little hall, pen in hand, writing letters to Edith detailing her mother's final days. It was a softening exercise, and she brushed aside unbidden tears as the announcement of the visitor reached her ears.

Mrs. Thornton entered, and though Margaret received her with gentle, ladylike decorum, the visitor felt daunted. The atmosphere shifted; it became impossible to launch into the prepared tirade. Margaret's voice was low and rich, her manner gracious, masking a deep gratitude for the courtesy of the call. She found topics of conversation, praising Martha and discussing a Greek melody Edith had mentioned. Mrs. Thornton felt disarmed, her sharp tongue finding no purchase among rose-leaves. Yet, beneath the surface, a suspicion gnawed at her: that this sweetness was a calculated strategy to appease Mr. Thornton, perhaps signaling that the other attachment had failed and that Miss Hale intended to reclaim her rejected lover. Driven by maternal instinct and a fear of losing a man she respected, Mrs. Thornton forced herself to summon her resolve.

Standing up, she cleared her throat. "Miss Hale, I have a duty to perform. Your mother requested that I not allow you to act wrongly—or inadvertently—without remonstrating. I am here to offer advice, whether you accept it or not."

Margaret rose, flushing crimson, her eyes wide. She assumed Mrs. Thornton had come to discuss the lie she had told Mr. Thornton, expecting an explanation for the danger she had placed herself in. Though her heart sank at the thought that Mr. Thornton had not chosen to confront her personally, she bowed her head in humble submission, ready to bear any blame.

"At first, I could scarcely believe the account given by my servant regarding your walk at the Outwood Station," Mrs. Thornton continued, her voice gaining strength. "But my son confirms it. It was indiscreet, to say the least. Many a young woman has lost her reputation for similar lapses."

Margaret's eyes flamed with sudden anger. This was a new, insulting angle. If the subject were the lie, she would own it immediately. But to have her *character* attacked by a stranger? By Mrs. Thornton? It was intolerable. She said nothing. Mrs. Thornton, sensing the battle-spirit igniting in Margaret's gaze, felt her own combative nature stir.

"For your mother's sake, I have felt it right to warn you against such improprieties," Mrs. Thornton pressed, undeterred by the silence. "They must degrade you in the estimation of the world, even if they do not lead to immediate harm."

"For my mother's sake," Margaret replied, her voice trembling with tears, "I will bear much. But I cannot bear everything. She never intended for me to be exposed to insult, I am certain."

"Insult, Miss Hale?"
