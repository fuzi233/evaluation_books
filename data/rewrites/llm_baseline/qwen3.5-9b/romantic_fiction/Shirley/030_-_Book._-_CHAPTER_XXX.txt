Everyone in Briarfield whispered that it was high time for Mr. Moore to return home. The entire town marveled at his strange absence, with Whinbury and Nunnely each contributing their own share of astonishment. Yet, the reason for his delay was well-known, debated, and exaggerated until there were at least forty plausible theories circulating. It wasn't business—the gossips agreed unanimously. He had finished the business that drove him away long ago. He had tracked down his four ringleaders, secured their convictions, overseen their sentencing, and watched them sail away toward transportation. This was common knowledge in Briarfield; the *Stilbro' Courier* had printed every detail, embellished with extra flourishes.

Despite the newspapers' reports, no one applauded his perseverance or celebrated his success. The local mill-owners were merely relieved, hoping that the terror of the law would finally paralyze the sinister courage of the dissenters. However, disaffection still muttered in the shadows. In the dim light of alehouses, men swore ominous oaths over drugged beer and toasted strange causes with fiery British gin. One persistent rumor claimed that Moore simply dared not return to Yorkshire, fearing his life was forfeit if he did.

"I'll tell him that," Mr. Yorke declared when his foreman mentioned the rumor. "And if that doesn't bring him home full gallop, nothing will."

Whether fear or some other motive finally recalled him, Moore announced his arrival date to Joe Scott, instructing him to send the hackney carriage to the George Inn for his stay. Upon learning this, Mr. Yorke made it his business to meet him.

It was market day. Moore arrived just in time to join the customary market dinner. As a somewhat unfamiliar figure and a man of recent notoriety, the gathered manufacturers treated him with a certain degree of distinction. Some, who would have dared barely to acknowledge him publicly for fear of attracting the hatred and vengeance stored up against him, privately hailed him as a champion. Once the wine began to circulate, their respect might have ignited into enthusiasm had not Moore's unshaken nonchalance kept it in a damp, low, smoldering state.

Mr. Yorke, the permanent president of these gatherings, watched his young friend's behavior with great satisfaction. Nothing irritated or disgusted him more than seeing a man intoxicated by flattery or elated by popularity. Conversely, nothing soothed and charmed him quite like witnessing a public figure utterly incapable of enjoying their own fame—indeed, *incapable*, as he insisted. Disdain would only have angered him; it was indifference that appeased his rough spirit.

Robert leaned back in his chair, quiet and almost surly, while the clothiers and blanket-makers boasted of his prowess and recounted his deeds. These stories were often punctuated by coarse insults directed at the working class. To Mr. Yorke, this was a delightful sight. His heart beat faster with the pleasing conviction that these crude praises shamed Moore deeply, causing him to half-despise himself and his own achievements. It is easy to smile at abuse or reproach, but truly painful is the panegyric of those we secretly scorn. Moore had often faced hostile crowds from a hustings with a brilliant countenance and gallant bearing, but here he drooped his head under the half-baked praise of tradesmen, shrinking chagrined before their congratulations.

Yorke couldn't resist asking how Moore liked his new supporters and whether he didn't think they honored his cause. "But it is a pity, lad," he added, "that you didn't hang these four samples of the unwashed. If you'd managed that feat, the gentry here would have ripped the horses from the coach, hitched a dozen asses to them, and dragged you into Stilbro' like a conquering general."

Soon, Moore abandoned the wine, broke away from the group, and took the road. Within five minutes, Mr. Yorke followed. They rode out of Stilbro' together.

It was early to return home, yet late in the day. The last rays of the sun had already faded from the cloud edges, and the October night was casting its shadow over the moorlands.

Mr. Yorke, moderately exhilarated by his drink and pleased to see young Moore back in Yorkshire with whom to share the long ride, took the conversation largely into his own hands. He touched briefly and scoffingly on the trials and the conviction, then moved quickly to neighborhood gossip before attacking Moore on his personal affairs.

"Bob, I believe you are worsted, and you deserve it. Everything seemed smooth. Fortune had fallen in love with you. She had decreed you the first prize in her wheel—twenty thousand pounds. She only needed you to hold out your hand and take it. And what did you do? You called for a horse and rode hunting to Warwickshire. Your sweetheart—Fortune, I mean—was perfectly indulgent. She said, 'I'll excuse him; he's young.' She waited, like 'Patience on a monument,' until the chase ended and the quarry was run down. She expected you to return then, to be a good lad. You might still have had her first prize.

"It capped everything, beyond expression, to find that instead of thundering home in a breakneck gallop to lay your assize laurels at her feet, you coolly took the coach up to London. What you did there, Satan knows; I believe you simply sat and sulked. Your face was never lily-fair, but it is olive-green now. You aren't as bonny as you were, man."

"And who is to have this prize you talk so much about?"

"Only a baronet; that's all. I have no doubt in my mind you've lost her. She will be Lady Nunnely before Christmas."

"Hem! Quite probable."

"But she needn't have been. Fool of a lad! I swear you might have had her."

"By what token, Mr. Yorke?"

"By every token—by the light of her eyes, the red of her cheeks. They turned red when your name was mentioned, though usually they are pale."

"My chance is quite over, I suppose?"

"It ought to be. But try; it's worth trying. I call this Sir Philip milk and water. And besides, he writes verses, they say—mere tags and rhymes. *You* are above that, Bob, at all events."

"Would you advise me to propose, late as it is, Mr. Yorke—at the eleventh hour?"

"You can but make the experiment, Robert. If she has a fancy for you—and, on my conscience, I believe she has or had—she will forgive much. But, my lad, you are laughing. Is it at me? You had better grin at your own perverseness. I see, however, you laugh at the wrong side of your mouth. You have as sour a look at this moment as anyone could wish to see."

"I have quarreled with myself, Yorke. I have kicked against the pricks, struggled in a strait waistcoat, dislocated my wrists wrenching them in handcuffs, and battered my hard head against a harder wall."

"Ha! I'm glad to hear that. Sharp exercise, you say! I hope it has done you good—taken some of the self-conceit out of you?"

"Self-conceit? What is it? Self-respect, self-tolerance—even what are they? Do you sell the articles? Do you know anyone who does? Give an indication. They would find in me a liberal chapman. I would part with my last guinea this minute to buy."

"Is it so with you, Robert? I find that spicy. I like a man to speak his mind. What has gone wrong?"

"The machinery of all my nature; the whole engine of this human mill; the boiler, which I take to be the heart, is fit to burst."

"That should be put in print; it's striking. It's almost blank verse. You'll be jingling into poetry just now. If the inspiration comes, give way, Robert. Never heed me; I'll bear it this time."

"A hideous, abhorrent, base blunder! You may commit in a moment what you will rue for years—what life cannot cancel."

"Lad, go on. I call it pie, nuts, sugar-candy. I like the taste uncommonly. Go on. It will do you good to talk. The moor is before us now, and there is no life for many miles around."

"I *will* talk. I am not ashamed to tell. There is a sort of wild cat in my breast, and I choose that you shall hear how it can yell."

"To me it is music. What grand voices you and Louis have! When Louis sings—tones off like a soft, deep bell—I've felt myself tremble again. The night is still. It listens. It is just leaning down to you, like a black priest to a blacker penitent. Confess, lad. Smooth nothing down. Be candid as a convicted, justified, sanctified Methodist at an experience meeting. Make yourself as wicked as Beelzebub. It will ease your mind."

"As mean as Mammon, you would say. Yorke, if I got off horseback and laid myself down across the road, would you have the goodness to gallop over me, backwards and forwards, about twenty times?"
