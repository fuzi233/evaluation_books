Little Miles entered this world mere days after the birth of the gracious Prince who now commands the regiment. Great illuminations and thunderous cannonades celebrated the arrival of the Royal George, drawing multitudes to gaze upon him behind the gilt railings of the Palace, tended by noble nurses. In stark contrast, few guards stood watch over the cradle of our own small prince. No courtiers, no faithful retainers offered their salutations, save for our trusty Gumbo and kind Molly, who loved and admired the heir of my poverty with a loyalty that warmed my heart. Why was our boy not named George, like the paragon above and like his father? Instead, I chose the name of a little scapegrace from my own family—a name borne by many generations of Warringtons. Yet, my poor little Miles's love and kindness touched me at a time when affection from my own blood was scarce. Thus, Theo and I agreed that our child should bear the name of that single, bright friend of my paternal race.

We penned letters to inform our royal parents of the auspicious event and boldly announced the birth in the *Daily Advertiser*, specifying our humble abode at Church Street, Lambeth. "My dear," Aunt Bernstein replied in her letter, "how could you point out to all the world that you live in such a trou as that in which you have buried yourself? I kiss the little mamma, and send a remembrance for the child." This remembrance was a fine silk coverlet edged with lace fit for a prince. It was hardly practical; the cost of the lace alone would have served us far better. Yet, Theo and Molly were delighted, and my eldest son's cradle was draped in finery equal to any nobleman's.

Good Dr. Heberden visited my wife several times to ensure all went well, recommending a local surgeon who took charge of her recovery. Luckily, my dear patient required little care beyond what our landlady and her attendant could provide. Soon, our humble precinct was once again adorned with the gilded wheels of Lady Castlewood's chariot. She arrived bearing a pot of jelly, which she believed Theo might enjoy, no doubt having been served at one of her ladyship's banquets the day prior. She regaled us with tales of court ceremonies and the splendor surrounding the birth of the august heir.

Our good Mr. Johnson happened to drop by on one of those days when the Countess's carriage blazed up to our gate. He was struck by her magnificence and offered her bows that were more respectful than graceful. She addressed me as "cousin" with affable warmth and helped transfer her gift of jelly from her silver dish to our crockery pan with benign grace. The Doctor tasted the sweetmeat and pronounced it excellent. "The great, sir," he remarked, "are fortunate in every way. They can engage the most skilful practitioners of the culinary art, as they can assemble the most amiable wits round their table. If, as you suggest, this sweetmeat has already graced his lordship's table, it has done so in good company. It has quivered before celebrated beauties, been tasted by ruby lips, and shared the attention of distinguished company alongside fruits, tarts, and creams, which I make no doubt were equally delicious." As he spoke, the good Doctor consumed a considerable portion of Lady Castlewood's benefaction. Though regarding the epithet "delicious," I must confess that my poor wife, after tasting the jelly, set it aside with distaste, and Molly, throwing up her head, declared it was mouldy.

My boy, however, enjoyed the privilege of having an earl's daughter for his godmother. This honor was performed by his cousin, our poor Lady Maria, whose kindness and attention to both mother and infant were beyond praise. Having lost her own solitary chance for maternal happiness, she yearned over our child in a manner deeply touching to behold. Captain Miles is a mighty fine gentleman, and his uniforms of the Prince's Hussars are as splendid as any that ever bedizened a soldier of fashion; yet he possesses a heart too good, and a nature too truly gentlemanly, to begrudge remembering that his own infant limbs were once dressed in garments prepared for the poor player's child. Sampson christened him in the very chapel in Southwark where our marriage ceremony had taken place. Never were the words of the Prayer-book read more beautifully or impressively than by the celebrant, until the end, when his voice failed him, and he and the small congregation were fain to wipe their eyes. "Mr. Garrick himself, sir," said Hagan, "could not have read those words so nobly. I am sure little innocent never entered the world accompanied by wishes and benedictions more tender and sincere."

Now, I must explain how the Captain came by the name of Miles. A couple of days before the christening, when it was still intended that our firstborn should bear his father's name, a light patter of horse's hoofs galloped up to our gate. Who should pull at the bell but young Miles, our cousin? I fear he had disobeyed his parents on that undutiful journey.

"You know," he said breathlessly, "cousin Harry gave me my little horse; and I can't help liking you, because you are so like Harry, and because they're always saying things of you at home, and it's a shame. I have brought my whistle and coral that my godmamma Lady Suckling gave me for your little boy. And if you're so poor, cousin George, here's my gold moidore. It's worth ever so much, and it's no use to me, because I mayn't spend it, you know."

We lifted the boy up to Theo in her room (he mounted the stairs in his little tramping boots, of which he was very proud). Theo kissed him and thanked him, and that moidore has remained in her purse from that day to this.

My mother, writing through her ambassador as usual, informed me of her royal surprise and displeasure upon learning that my son had been christened Miles—a name unknown, at least in the Esmond family. I did not care to tell the reason at the time. But when, in after years, I recounted to Madam Esmond how my boy came by his name, I saw a tear roll down her wrinkled cheek. I heard afterwards that she had asked Gumbo many questions about the boy who gave his name to our Miles—our Miles Gloriosus of Pall Mall, Valenciennes, Almack's, and Brighton.
