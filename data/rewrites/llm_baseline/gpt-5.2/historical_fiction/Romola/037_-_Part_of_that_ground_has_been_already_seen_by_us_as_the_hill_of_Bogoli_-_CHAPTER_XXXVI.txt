It was more than three weeks before every last volume, stand, bust, and casket in the Bardi library had been swaddled in straw, corded into cases, and borne away as if the very air of that room were being emptied. Romola did not close her eyes to it. Each evening, when the men’s boots and gruff calls had ceased on the stair, she took her hand-lamp and went among the littered straw and the fresh-splintered wood, pausing where a pedestal stood naked, where a familiar object lay on its side like a body flung down. At first she went with a bitter hunger for proof—proof that there was reason enough why love had fallen dead in her, why the world had turned barren. Later she went because that pain, kept warm by sight and touch, was the only strong life left to her affections. It was as if grief, by being walked through and handled, could be made to answer like a living thing.

On the twenty-third of December she knew the last packages were to go. She ran to the loggia at the top of the house, not from hope but from the need of one more pang: she could not bear that the final moment should pass without her eyes, as if by not seeing she could be accused of consenting.

The day was clouded and already edging toward dusk. The Arno ran dark, shivering under its own skin; the hills looked bruised with shadow; and Florence, with her ring of stone towers, had the hush of a city seen from above when light is too weak to pick out movement—tomb-like, as if the living had withdrawn and left only architecture to remember them. Santa Croce, where her father lay, was a deeper blot within the general dimness. Across the bridge, slow as deliberate cruelty, crawled the white load—white canvas over timber and straw—moving with the steadiness of Fate that does not hasten because haste would be mercy. It went inch by inch over the arch, and then slid into the narrow street and was swallowed. Romola felt less that she witnessed it than that her father, imprisoned beneath stones, was compelled to be conscious of it without the solace of her hand reaching his. In her imagination he lay there listening, helpless, while his hope was carried away to be buried where no name would call it back.

She remained after it had vanished, unheeding the chill that crept under her garments. The gloom soothed her, covering her like mourning cloth and keeping out the bright, discordant life of others. Then, abruptly, the great bell in the palace-tower burst out—not the iron hammer of alarm, but a restless, triumphant peal, as if someone shook victory into the air. Before its vibration had died, other bells answered from other towers, one after another, until the whole city seemed a single instrument. The sound swelled and spread; it filled the space between walls and across the river so completely that the air appeared made of ringing. And with the growing clang came little flames—signal-lights kindling between the palace turrets and along the girdling towers—quivering as if the sound itself had struck fire.

That sudden noise and leaping light wounded Romola like sharp blows. In her bitterness they were not civic rejoicing but demons’ triumph—Florence exulting at the very hour her life was being stripped bare by her husband’s treachery. Little more than three weeks ago she had been intoxicated by these same bells, hearing in the city’s gladness a prophecy of her own. Now the general joy was cruelty. It was Florence flinging out loud exultation to stun sorrow and loneliness, and she stood apart from it as from a crowd that has no ear for the one person weeping.

She told herself she would never join hands with gladness again, only with those whom gladness, by its hard nature, forgets. Rejoicing seemed mockery—men shouting paeans with heaviness in their souls and then searching their neighbours’ faces to discover whether joy truly existed. She had lost faith in the happiness she once thirsted for. It looked to her now like a soft-handed smiling thing with a narrow selfish heart—always fed, always eager to be amused, always turning away from endurance as from a bad smell.

She ran down from the loggia, pressing her hands against her ears as if she could push the sound back out of her skull. She was hurrying across the antechamber when she was startled into stillness by meeting Tito, who was coming to seek her.

His step was elastic; a radiance of satisfaction—not quite his usual easy brightness, but something more confident—shone about him. The bells had set their glitter on his face as firelight does.

“What! the noise was a little too much for you?” he said lightly, for her hands clapped tighter over her ears at the sight of him, as if the city’s clang and his presence were one assault. He took her gently by the wrist and drew her arm within his, leading her into the saloon with its painted nymphs and fauns that danced perpetually above human misery. Then he went on, speaking with the airy ease that made words seem like ornaments rather than pledges.

“Florence is gone quite mad at getting its Great Council, which is to put an end to all the evils under the sun; especially to the vice of merriment. You may well look stunned, my Romola—and you are cold. You must not stay so late under that windy loggia without wrappings. I was coming to tell you that I am suddenly called to Rome about some learned business for Bernardo Rucellai. I am going away immediately, for I am to join my party at San Gaggio to-night, that we may start early in the morning. I need give you no trouble; I have had my packages made already. It will not be very long before I am back again.”

His hand, as he spoke, held hers with an accustomed right, like one who expects no resistance, only submission under a smooth voice. He knew he had nothing to look for from her except quiet endurance. He could not even dare to kiss her brow that evening; but he pressed her hand to his lips and left her.

As the door closed, Tito felt what he had been obliged to feel for days: Romola was more unforgiving than he had imagined. Her love was not that sweet clinging instinct stronger than all judgments—the charm, as he began to see now, that makes a wife’s anger melt into need. Yet this petrified coldness was, for him, more convenient than passionate opposition. Her pride, her capacity of seeing where resistance would be useless, saved him trouble.

But the moment he was gone, the cold immobility that came over her like inevitable frost in his presence broke. She was far indeed from quiet endurance. The days since the scene that had severed her from Tito had been filled with active planning: she had been measuring out hours like portions of bread, storing up resolve as if it were provisions for a journey.

She called old Maso.

“Maso,” she said, her voice suddenly firm with decision, “we take our journey to-morrow morning. We shall be able now to overtake that first convoy of cloth, while they are waiting at San Piero. See about the two mules to-night, and be ready to set off with them at break of day, and wait for me at Trespiano.”

She meant to take Maso as far as Bologna, and then send him back with letters—one to her godfather, one to Tito—telling them she was gone and never meant to return. She had arranged everything so that secrecy might be perfect: her broken love, her broken life were not to be made a spectacle for curious eyes. Bernardo del Nero was absent at his villa, slipping away from political suspicions into his chosen refuge—land and labour—and she had paid him without seeking an interview. He did not even know the library had been sold; he would naturally conjecture that Tito had found some sudden good fortune to raise the sum. Maso, with his wintry withered age, cared for one thing—to do what she told him—and had been admitted into her confidence only so far as to understand that the journey must be hidden.

Romola did not mean to go to bed that night. When she had fastened her door, she took her taper to the carved and painted chest that held her wedding-clothes. The white silk and gold lay there, the long white veil, the circlet of pearls. A sob rose against her will; the garments looked like a shroud for dead happiness. In a tiny gold loop of the circlet a sugar-plum had lodged—a pink hailstone left from the shower of sweets. Tito had detected it first and had said it should always remain there.

And now, as she stared, memory rose in a wave so sudden that it seemed to carry her bodily backward into the time of perfect trust. She felt again the presence of the husband whose love had made the world fresh and wonderful to her, as to a child sitting still among sunny flowers—heard the gentle tones, saw the soft eyes before any lie had looked out of them, breathed again that large freedom of the soul that comes from believing the nearest being to us is also the greater. In those brief moments the tears always came: the tenderness in her, so long trained to give itself, felt akin to the bereaved mother’s pang when she imagines the tiny fingers still warm on her breast and then finds them marble to her lips as she bends above the silent bed.

But besides the white and gold there was something else in the chest: a dark coarse bundle rolled and corded. Romola turned her eyes from the bridal brightness to the roughness, and as her hands touched the serge the tears were checked. The harshness recalled her to the present as cold water recalls a fevered patient. She unfastened the thick white cord and spread the bundle on the table. It was the grey serge dress of a sister of the third order of Saint Francis—living in the world, yet set apart for pious works—one of those whom Florentines called a Pinzochera.

Romola was going to wear it as disguise. She determined to put it on at once, so that if she must snatch any sleep before dawn she would wake ready to depart. She put off her black garment. As she thrust her soft white arms into the harsh sleeves and felt the hard rope-girdle bite her fingers while she tied it, she welcomed the rude sensations. They suited the contempt she had conceived for pleasure—that dexterous contrivance for selfish ease, that shrinking from endurance when others were bending under burdens too heavy for them—which now, in her mind, had Tito’s face. Then she gathered her long hair, drew it tight back from her face, bound it in a hard knot, and took a square of black silk, tying it as a kerchief across her head and under her chin; over that she drew the cowl.

She lifted the taper to the mirror. The disguise would be complete to any one who had not lived close to her. To herself she looked strangely like Dino—only let the cheeks waste, let the eyes sink a little more, and the resemblance would be almost a sorrowful mockery. Was she becoming like him in anything else? Only in this: she understood now how men could be driven to rush away forever from earthly delight, how they could choose images of sorrow rather than of joy, as if the world’s beauty were an insult.

She did not linger at her own reflection. She began to gather and pack the relics of her father and mother—those too large for her small travelling-wallet—into the chest with the wedding-clothes, to be committed to her godfather when she was safely gone. First the portraits; then, one by one, the small objects that had sacred memory clinging to them, each going either into the wallet or into the chest with a sense of laying away pieces of herself.

She paused. There was still something to strip away, something that belonged to the past she meant to turn her back on forever. She put thumb and forefinger to her betrothal ring—but for a moment they rested there without drawing it off.

Her mind had been running like a strong current toward the act of quitting Tito, breaking an outward tie that no longer matched the inward bond. Yet the mere motion toward removing the ring brought an arresting sense, as if she were violently rending her life in two. Outward symbols have a stubborn power; they knit our active life into an external identity that does not yield easily to the wavering changes of consciousness. The ring, that small circle of gold, seemed suddenly to hold the weight of a whole history.

If the beloved Tito who had put it on her finger were not in any valid sense the same Tito she had ceased to love, why return him this sign? Why not keep it as memorial of what had been? The ring, by asserting their identity in palpable metal, shook her in a way she could not explain. Half-truths are like that: they haunt us as dull pulses never fully born into sound.

But another voice in her rose and drowned those muffled murmurs, fierce with wounded pride.

“It cannot be! I cannot be subject to him. He is false. I shrink from him. I despise him!”

She snatched the ring from her finger and laid it on the table beside the pen she meant to use. Again she clung to the one law she recognized—the law of her affections. Tenderness and keen fellow-feeling for those near and loved had been the religion of her life: it had made her patient where she was naturally impetuous; it could have made her heroic. Now that strength was gone—or rather it had become the strength of repulsion. She recoiled from Tito in proportion to the energy of the love he had disappointed, and to her lifelong devotion to her father which he had violated past forgiveness. It seemed as if every motive had slipped away except indignation and scorn, which now served her as oars to drive herself out of the harbour.

She was not following precedent, nor obeying adopted maxims. The grand severity of the stoic philosophy her father had made familiar was ready on her lips; its lofty spirit had stirred echoes in her. But she had never used it as rule. She had endured and forborne because she loved; and maxims that told her to feel less, to hold her heart loose lest Nature’s onward course should jar it, had been powerless against her tenderness—just as they had been powerless against her father’s yearning for fame. She had appropriated no theories. She had simply lived strong in affection; and life without that energy came to her as a new problem.

She meant to solve it in what seemed a simple way: to cut herself loose from the man she loved no longer. Her mind had never bowed to obligation except where personal love and reverence were concerned; other human relations had never pressed upon her with equal reality. Now, she felt, she had only one thing to obey—the instinct to sever herself.

Yet that firm resolution did not save her from shifting anguish. When the preparations were nearly complete, she lingered. She put off writing the irrevocable words that would part her from her little world. The past weeks rushed in again with cruel speed and seized even her limbs: she sat to write, and her hand fell. Tears came—not the sob of remembered happiness at the pearls and pink hailstone, but bitter tears for the delusion that had blighted her youth. And with them came tingling shame at the ignominious words she had flung at Tito—“Have you robbed some one else who is not dead?” To have had such speech wrung from her, to have spoken so to her husband, felt like a stain on her whole life. Hard words between those who have loved remain hideous in memory, like seeing greatness and beauty sunk into vice and rags.

The comparison of past and present pressed on her until it turned into bodily wretchedness. She felt benumbed to everything except inward throbbing and needed some hard contact as if to keep herself from dissolving. She drew her hands tight along the harsh knotted cord at her waist. She rose and seized the rough lid of the chest: was there anything more to go in? No. She shut it, pressing her palm on the carving as if it were a rough plank to hold her up, and locked it.

Then she remembered the last details of her equipment as a Pinzochera. The large leather purse—the scarsella—must hang from her rope-girdle, with small coin in it. Her florins and small jewels, gifts from her godfather and cousin Brigida, were safely fastened within her mantle. On the other side must hang the rosary.

As she hung the rosary at her waist, it did not occur to her that more than garments might be required for her disguise—that her air, her carriage, her very eyes were as unlike as possible those of a sister trained to lowered lids and lips that moved in silent iteration. Her inexperience kept her from picturing distant details; it also fed her proud courage by shutting out foreboding of insult or danger. She knew that unhappy wives sometimes fled to friends or to a cloister; both were impossible to her. She had devised a lot of her own: to go to Venice, to the most learned woman in the world, Cassandra Fedele, and ask how an instructed woman might support herself in lonely life.

Practical difficulties did not daunt her; nor did the darkness at the end. Her life, she believed, could never be happy again, but it must not—could not—be ignoble. And with a pathetic mixture of childish romance and woman’s trial, her father’s philosophy, which had little to do with this decisive deed, still coloured her imagination of the future. So far as she could conceive her solitary loveless life, she saw it animated by proud stoic heroism and by an indistinct yet strong purpose of labour—labour that might make her wise enough to write something that would rescue her father’s name from oblivion. She was, after all, only a young girl, poor Romola, who had found herself at the end of her joys.

There were still things to do. There was a small key in a casket on the table—but now she saw her taper was dying and she had forgotten to provide another light. In a few moments the room was in total darkness. Feeling her way to the nearest chair, she sat down to wait for morning.

The thought of that key had summoned memories which, through the past week, had returned with the sharpened vividness remembered words gain when we learn a new meaning for them. Since the shock of revelation that had seemed to divide her forever from Tito, her last interview with Dino had never been absent from her mind for many hours. It solicited her the more because, while the images pressed with almost the force of sensation, they stirred thoughts within her that resisted.

Inwardly she heard again and again the dying prophetic voice: “The man whose face was a blank loosed thy hand and departed; and as he went, I could see his face, and it was the face of the great Tempter… And thou, Romola, didst wring thy hands and seek for water, and there was none… and the plain was bare and stony again, and thou wast alone in the midst of it. And then it seemed that the night fell, and I saw no more.”

She saw again the wasted face, the straining gaze at the crucifix, the awe that had compelled her to kneel, the last broken words and then the unbroken silence. The scene had been like a sudden opening into a world separate from all her lifelong knowledge. It came back now not as tale but as presence—sound remembered so vividly she could almost hear the thin breath stop.

Yet her mind roused itself to resist impressions that, from being mere phantoms, seemed to grow solid even in daylight. As a strong body struggles against fumes more violently when they begin to stifle, a strong soul struggles against phantasies more urgently when they threaten to govern in place of thought.

What had Dino’s vision to do with her real sorrows? The fitting of certain words was chance; the rest was vague—nay, those words themselves were vague, determined by nothing but Dino’s memories and beliefs. He believed pagan learning fatal; he believed celibacy holier than marriage; he remembered their home and the objects in the library; out of such threads, his vision had been woven. What warrant could she have for believing and acting on it? None. True as the foreboding had proved, she could still see with unshaken conviction that to have renounced Tito in obedience to a warning like that would have been meagre-hearted folly. Her trust had been delusive, but she would choose again to have acted on it rather than become a creature led by phantoms and broken whispers, in a world where there was the large music of reasonable speech and the warm grasp of living hands.

And yet those memories persisted, linking themselves with her present lot, offering her a narrow glimpse into lives she had never understood—the lives of men and women led by inward voices and images. She heard herself think, in the darkness, as if speaking to another self:

“If they were only a little stronger in me, I should lose the sense of what that vision really was, and take it for prophetic light. I might in time get to be a seer of visions myself, like the Suora Maddalena, and Camilla Rucellai, and the rest.”

She shuddered. All her instruction, all the main influences of her life had fortified her scorn of that sickly superstition—men and women with eyes too weak for daylight, sitting in dark swamps and trying to read destiny by the chance flame of wandering vapours.

Yet she was conscious of something deeper than mere coincidence of words. Something in her was moved by the idea of Dino’s experience and by the fellowship it implied—a supreme fellowship with suffering. If there were much more of such experience in the world, she felt she would like to understand it; would like to know what thoughts could make men sink in ecstasy before painted agonies of martyrdom. There seemed to be something there beyond madness. Around her, all springs felt dried; she wondered what other waters existed where men drank and found strength in the desert.

And then, in the darkness, the memory returned of those moments in the Duomo when she had sobbed with a mysterious mingling of rapture and pain while Fra Girolamo offered himself as willing sacrifice for the people—moments that came back like a brief taste of some far-off fountain. But again she shrank from the allure of impressions that would draw her into the sphere of visions and narrow fears—those fears that had compelled Dino to outrage natural affection.

She sat waiting for the morning, in her coarse serge, with the rope at her waist and the rosary hanging at her side, while outside the city’s bells died slowly into distance and the winter night settled over Florence like ash over a fire.
