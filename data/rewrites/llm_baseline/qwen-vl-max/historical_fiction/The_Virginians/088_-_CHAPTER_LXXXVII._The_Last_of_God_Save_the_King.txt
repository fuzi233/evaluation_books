What strange twist of fate has it been that I should always find myself standing apart, outnumbered, and out of step? Were a law proposed to surrender this realm to the Pretender in Rome or even to the Grand Turk—were we to bow our heads to a foreign faith—I am certain I would be the one voting against it. Yet in my own home, in Virginia, I found myself no less isolated. The Patriots called me a Tory, though I confessed myself one; the Tories, in turn, branded me a dangerous Republican. The world seemed turned upside down.

No sooner had I settled into Virginia than I longed for the banks of the Waveney, for the quiet life I had left behind. But events were too turbulent to allow such a retreat. My mother, alone and vulnerable, could not be abandoned to face war and ruin. Nor would she leave her homeland at such a time. And so, as any man of spirit must, I stayed. At His Excellency’s table, over glasses of rich claret, many like-minded souls vowed allegiance to the Crown. “We are all Cavaliers,” they declared, “and we will stand by the standard!” Governor Dunmore spoke with fire and pride, and his words stirred us all.

Of all those I came to know in Virginia, none stood more firmly in her convictions than Madam Esmond. When news reached Williamsburg of the Boston Port Bill, a wave of outrage swept through the colony. The House of Burgesses passed a formal protest, declaring a day of fasting and prayer, beseeching Heaven to avert civil war. Yet the ball for the Governor’s lady had already been announced and accepted. The gentlemen decided to proceed with the event, then don sackcloth and ashes afterward.

“*A ball!*” exclaimed Madam Esmond. “I shall not attend a celebration hosted by rebels who will soon publicly insult His Majesty! I would rather die!” She wrote to the stewards, explaining that given the perilous state of the nation, she could not, in good conscience, participate.

Imagine her surprise when, on her next outing in her chair, she was greeted by a hundred people—white and black alike—shouting, “Huzzah, Madam! Heaven bless your ladyship!” They believed her refusal to go to the ball was an act of patriotism. To make sure there was no misunderstanding, she leaned out of the carriage and shouted, “God save the King!” The crowd echoed her cry. In those days, “God save the King” rang from every tongue.

On the night of the ball, my poor Harry, now a Burgess and one of the hosts, donned his red coat—once worn by Wolfe—and went off with Madam Fanny. My Lady Warrington and I, being strangers and Englishmen, were permitted to attend, though Madam Esmond refused to join. I had the honor of dancing a country dance with the lady of Mount Vernon, whom I found charming and lively. But when my wife recounted the evening to my mother, the old lady received her praise with a grim silence. “Could not Sir George have danced with my Lady Dunmore or her daughters?” she muttered. “Why must he choose Mrs. Washington? She once surveyed roads for a guinea a day!”

Indeed, the pride of these provincial upstarts knew no bounds. And what was worse, Lord Dunmore’s favor toward Sir George had clearly inflated his head. I cannot speak for Mr. Washington’s pride—but I do know that my mother never warmed to him or anything belonging to him. She disapproved of his attending the ball, and even more so three days later, when he joined the service held on the appointed day of fast and humiliation.

That day, she invited Mr. Belman and several colonial officials to dinner. The clergyman excused himself. Madam Esmond tossed her head. “Let him do as he pleases,” she said. She made a show of a grand dinner, lighting her house while the rest of Williamsburg remained dark and somber. She begged Mr. Hardy, one of His Excellency’s aides, to sing “God save the King.” The people outside listened, thinking it part of some religious observance. Then she called for “Britons, strike home!” The young gentleman, fresh from Europe, began to sing—when suddenly a great yell rose from the street, and a stone crashed into the punch bowl before me, scattering its contents across the room.

My mother rushed to the window, undaunted. I can still see her—small, stiff, arms outstretched in her frilled sleeves—singing in full voice: “Britons, strike home! strike home!” The crowd roared back: “Silence! For shame! Go back!” But she would not yield. “Throw more stones if you dare!” she challenged.

Gentlemen from the Raley Tavern stepped forward, calming the mob. “You mustn’t insult a lady,” someone said—a voice I thought I recognized. “Huzza, Colonel! Hurrah, Captain! God bless your honour!” the crowd cried, and the disturbance ended.

My mother insisted the evening was over and urged Mr. Hardy to sing another song. But he gave a weak smile and said, “I really don’t care to sing to such accompaniments.” The music ceased. Yet that night, scoundrels returned, terrified my wife nearly out of her wits, and shattered every window in our front façade. “Britons, strike home!”—it was perhaps too much. She should have stuck to “God save the King.”

Meanwhile, militia drilled, bullets were cast, ammunition stockpiled, and schemes devised to thwart royal orders. But everywhere, “God save the King” was still the cry. When I objected to the patriots’ plans, warning them they were courting separation and bringing down the wrath of the greatest power on earth, their answer was always the same: “We mean no separation. We are loyal Britons. We glory in our name.” So they claimed.

The powder barrels were stacked in cellars, the fuse laid—but Mr. Fawkes, in his duty, only petitioned the King and Parliament, meaning no harm. True, when I spoke of British strength, I assumed it would be used wisely. I never imagined we’d try to crush three million fellow Britons with a few battalions, half a dozen generals from Bond Street, and thousands of German mercenaries. Why must we send Hessians and Indian warriors to fight our own people? Was our great quarrel to be fought with foreign auxiliaries and savage allies?

Ah, now that we’ve lost, it is easy to point to maps and say how things should have been. Long Island could have destroyed Washington’s army. He should never have escaped Valley Forge alive. The South was ours after Camden—if only the Commander-in-Chief at New York hadn’t interfered, paralyzing the only capable British general, sending him into the trap at Yorktown, where he could only surrender.

Oh, for one more week, one more day, one more hour of darkness or light! Now, reviewing our American campaigns—from their ill-fated start to their dishonorable end—we can see how a single march, a single advance, might have brought the enemy to our mercy. But it was ordained by Heaven, and for the good of both empires, that the Western Republic should break away. And the gallant soldiers who fought for her—above all, her indomitable leader—earned glory not just by facing seasoned troops well-supplied and battle-hardened, but by enduring starvation, cold, betrayal, and disunity within their own ranks. Only the unquenchable flame of patriotism kept them going.

What constancy! What magnanimity! What perseverance against fortune! On the battlefield, Washington was no greater than hundreds who fought beside or against him—many mocked him as “Fabius.” But as commander of a nation in arms, leading divided factions, calm amid conspiracy, serene before open foes and hidden enemies, inspiring order into ragged, hungry troops, stung by ingratitude yet never showing anger, forgiving constantly, invincible in defeat, generous in victory—never more sublime than when he laid down his sword and retired to peace—here was a man worthy of admiration and reverence. A life without stain, a fame without flaw. *Quando invenies parem?*

In the larger work I have begun on this great war, I hope I have done justice to his character. [And I trust that in expressing my opinions about him, I have shown fairness and generosity—even toward those who view me with hostility. For my brother Hal, visiting Mount Vernon, showed General Washington some early pages of my history. Though the General read little and cared nothing for literature, he remarked: “If you’ll have my opinion, my dear General, I think Sir George’s projected work, from the specimen I’ve seen, is certain to offend both parties.”—G.E.W.] This came not from bias, but from respect for his virtues.

With the young Mr. Washington of my youth, I never shared much sympathy. My brother, more open-hearted than I, was his close friend in early days—when they were equals—and later, when the General, as I confess, became the superior of all mankind.

I have spoken of the contradictions in our natures—the traits that placed us on opposing sides in the coming conflict, which raged for five years until the mother country acknowledged defeat. Harry should have been the Tory, and I the Whig. Theoretically, my views were far more liberal than his. After his marriage, he became what our Indian nabobs call a Bahadoor—ceremonious, proud, demanding respect.

When Lord Dunmore suggested freeing the slaves to rally them to the King’s cause, Hal wanted to hang the Governor and his Black Guards. “If you’re fighting for freedom,” I said, “then surely the negroes may fight too.” Harry roared, shaking his fist: “Infernal villains! If I meet one, they’ll die by this hand!” My mother agreed—this idea of slave rebellion was the most abominable, parricidal notion ever born in her land. She was more consistent than Harry. She demanded obedience from both black and white. Harry, however, admitted freedom only for the latter.

To prove her point, both Madam Esmond and Harry pointed to Gumbo, a servant in our household. Having earned his freedom from me as a reward for loyalty during hard times, Gumbo returned to Virginia—only to be unwelcome among my mother’s servants. He was free; they were not. He strutted like a king among them, boasting of his friends in England (“at home,” as he called it), his adventures abroad. He drew the others around him, like a monkey who’d seen the world.

Sady, Hal’s boy, who had come to America of his own will, was not free. Jealousy flared between him and Gumbo, leading to frequent boxing matches—learned at Marybone Gardens and Hockley-in-the-Hole. Sady was not alone in his resentment. Nearly all the servants hated Gumbo for his airs. Even Molly, his wife, was jealous—though she was faithful to him. The blacks despised her for marrying one of their kind. She received no respect, could not command, and constantly complained to my mother about theft, laziness, lies among the other servants. Finally, she accused Gumbo of jealousy toward Dinah—or Diana—whom I hope was as innocent as the moonlight visitor of Endymion.

Madam Esmond, in matters of morality, was a Draconess. Accused, one was guilty. She charged Gumbo, who responded angrily. Forgetting he was a free man, she ordered him whipped. Molly flew at her, her fury over her husband’s supposed infidelity replaced by rage at the indignity. Rebellion broke out in Castlewood.

I sided with Gumbo. Hal and Fanny supported my mother. This disagreement, though brief, brought a rare closeness between Madam and her younger children. But it was clear the symbol of insurrection had to leave. We decided Gumbo and Molly would return to Europe.

My wife and I wished to go with them—God knows, our son was sickly, weakened by fever in the swampy land. But my wife was expecting our second child, Henry, and I had promised to remain. We agreed to send them. When I offered Theo to accompany them, she refused—her place was with her husband. Her father and Hetty would care for the children. I barely saw a tear in her eyes as she prepared them for departure.

Do you remember, madam, the silence in the sewing rooms as tiny shirts were folded for the journey? The secret visits to the children’s beds while they slept? The heartbreak of parting, as our barge carried servants and children to the ship, and you stood on shore?

Had the Prince of Wales sailed on that voyage, he could not have been better provided. Where, sirrah, is the Tompion watch your grandmother gave you? And how did you survive the boxes of cakes the good lady packed into your cabin?

The ship that carried my poor Theo’s children returned with the Reverend Mr. Hagan and my Lady Maria, who humbly resigned her title and was known simply as Mrs. Hagan in the colony—soon to be no colony at all.

When I was in favor with Lord Dunmore, he granted a living in Westmoreland County to our kinsman, who arrived in time to christen our son Henry and preach sermons on the dire state of affairs—sermons Madam Esmond pronounced “prodigious fine.”

I believe Lady Maria won her heart by insisting on leaving the room after her. “My father was an earl,” she said, “but you know your ladyship is a marquis’s daughter. I could never claim precedence over you.” So fond did Madam become of her niece that she allowed Hagan to read plays—even mine—and admitted, grudgingly, that my tragedy *Pocahontas* had merit, especially when delivered with passion by our parson.

Hal and Fanny rarely visited Castlewood or Richmond while the chaplain and his wife were with us. Fanny was curt and rude to Maria, giggling strangely in her presence, reminding her repeatedly of her age—much to my mother’s confusion. “Is there any quarrel between my niece and my daughter-in-law?” she would ask.

I kept silent, moved by the quiet endurance of the elder woman. Fanny loved to torment her in front of her husband—poor fellow, blissfully ignorant of his wife’s past. The other bore it with stoicism, wincing only slightly.

I often reproached Madam Harry: “Are you a Red Indian, that you torture your victims so?”
“Have I not suffered enough?” she replied, her eyes hard.
“Nay,” I said, “you were raised in our wigwam. I remember only kindness.”
“Kindness!” she cried. “No slave was ever treated as I was. The deepest wounds are those never aimed. Those who hate us are not the ones we’ve wronged.”

I thought of little Fanny in our childhood—silent, smiling, eager to run errands for us. And I grieved for my poor brother, who had taken this sly creature into his heart.
