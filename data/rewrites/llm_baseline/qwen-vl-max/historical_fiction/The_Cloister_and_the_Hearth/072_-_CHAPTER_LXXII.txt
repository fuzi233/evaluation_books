The Cloister

The Dominican Order, once the most influential religious force across Europe, was now in decline. Its rivals, the Franciscans, had gained strength and influence throughout Christendom—even in England, where the Black Friars had once held sway. In such times of shifting power, the order’s leaders grew more vigilant, seeking out gifted and devout men to send forth as missionaries and preachers, hoping to reclaim their former glory.

Among these rising figures was Clement, whose rare talents quickly drew notice—especially his command of languages. He spoke Latin with precision, Italian with grace, French with fluency, and both high and low Dutch with ease. His intellect and fervor made him a promising candidate for a mission to England, where he would serve as a spiritual ambassador to Rome.

But Jerome, who held the ear of the superior, stood in opposition.

“Clement,” he said, “still carries the milk of the world in his veins—the passions, the weaknesses of youth. Let not his new zeal or humility blind us to ancient wisdom. Try him first. Temper him. For fear one day we lean on a reed, mistaking it for a staff.”

“The counsel is sound,” replied the prior. “Take him under your wing, Jerome.”

And so Jerome, adhering to tradition, set about testing Clement’s mettle.

One afternoon, he led him into a field where young men gathered for games—a place Clement once frequented. Soon enough, Pietro Vanucci and Andrea passed by, casting a casual glance at the two friars. They did not recognize their old friend, now shaven-headed and cloaked in black.

Clement flinched slightly, then bowed his head and murmured a *Pater Noster*.

“Would you not speak with them, brother?” Jerome asked, watching closely.

“No, brother. Yet it was good to see them. They remind me of sins I can never fully repent.”

Jerome nodded, satisfied. Later, he submitted a cautious but favorable report.

Then came further trials: visits to deathbeds, where suffering clung like smoke; descent into dungeons thick with darkness, stench, and despair. Men lay there like beasts, wrapped in rags, their spirits broken. The physical toll was great, but Clement’s soul rose to meet them—offering comfort where it could still be given. He shone in those moments.

“His spirit is willing,” Jerome reported, “but his flesh is weak.”

“Good,” said Anselm. “For even if the body falters, the will remains strong.”

Yet greater tests awaited.

Let me tell it as others saw it.

One morning, Rome’s main street teemed with people, the avenues choked with heads. An execution was underway—not for a petty crime, nor against a common man. The governor of Rome had been found slain in his bed at dawn. His hand, likely raised in defense, lay severed at the wrist; his throat was cut, his temples bruised by a blunt weapon. The murder had been traced to his servant, and justice would be swift and severe.

The criminal was brought to the governor’s house and bound to its wall for half an hour—a foretaste of vengeance. Then, his left hand was chopped off, mirroring his victim’s fate. A freshly killed bird was opened and tied around the bleeding stump—why, no one knew, though some whispered it was a superstition of the physicians. Mutilated and bandaged, the man was dragged to the scaffold.

There, a young friar knelt beside him, praying earnestly—holding the crucifix close to his eyes, speaking words of mercy and salvation.

Suddenly, the executioner shoved the friar aside and struck the prisoner down with a heavy mallet, then fell upon him, slicing his throat from ear to ear.

A cry rose from the crowd.

The young friar collapsed, unconscious.

A massive monk stepped forward and carried him away like a child.

Brother Clement returned to the convent, heavy-hearted and dispirited. Before the prior, he confessed with tears.

“Courage, son Clement,” the prior said. “A Dominican is not forged in a day. You shall have another trial. And I forbid you to face it fasting.” Clement bowed in obedience.

He did not wait long. A notorious robber, a monster of cruelty, was sentenced to hang. He had murdered men not for gain, but for sport. Clement spent his final night in prison with him, walked with him to the gallows, and prayed beside him with such passion that even the hardened criminal wept and embraced him. Clement returned the embrace, though his body trembled with revulsion, holding the crucifix before the dying man’s eyes.

The rope tightened. The man died. But then, the hangman raised his hatchet and quartered the corpse on the spot.

And here, something strange happened: the people who had watched the killing with cold indifference now wailed at each blow upon the lifeless body.

Clement shuddered—but stood firm, like a rock that vibrates but does not fall.

Then, suddenly, Jerome’s voice pierced the silence.

“Brother Clement! Mount the cart. Preach to the people. Quickly! Strike while the iron is hot, while souls are ripe for salvation!”

Clement’s color rose and fell. He breathed hard. But he obeyed. With unsteady steps, he climbed onto the cart and delivered his first sermon to the first crowd he had ever faced. Oh, that sea of faces! His throat burned, his heart pounded, his voice shook.

But slowly, the moment took hold. The sight of eager, upturned faces, the fire of his own conviction—these kindled within him. He recounted the robber’s story, fresh from their time together in prison, and showed how sin climbs step by step, from folly to ruin. He thundered against bloodshed, yet moved the crowd to pity. His tremors vanished. He felt taut, like a lute strung tight, filled with an unexpected strength. He commanded the audience, sensing their pulse, shaping their emotions as a musician plays a psaltery.

Sobs and groans rose from the crowd—proof of his power.

Jerome stared, as if witnessing a spark ignite a rocket.

After a time, Clement noticed the look of astonishment on Jerome’s face—and seeing no approval, broke off abruptly and approached him.

“It was my first attempt,” he said humbly. “Your command came like a thunderbolt. Was I… did I…? Oh, correct me, guide me, Brother Jerome.”

“Humph,” Jerome muttered, uncertain. After long thought, he added grudgingly, “Give the glory to God, Brother Clement. My opinion is—you were born to preach.”

He reported this to headquarters, half-reluctantly. For though Jerome was stern and difficult, he was honest.

Another trial soon followed.

Julio Antonelli was accused of sacrilege. Three witnesses swore they saw him leave the church where candlesticks had been stolen—at the exact time. Others proved an alibi just as strongly. Neither side could be shaken. So, the law allowed a trial by water—either hot or cold.

In the hot test, the accused plunged his bare arm into boiling water fourteen inches deep and retrieved a pebble. In the cold test, he was lowered into eight feet of water.

The clergy, believing him innocent, recommended the hot trial—less fearsome for those they favored. But Antonelli lacked courage and chose the cold ordeal.

This gave Jerome another chance to test Clement.

Antonelli received communion, then was stripped naked on the banks of the Tiber, bound hand and foot to prevent thrashing that might sink him.

Gently, he was lowered into the river, floating for a moment, only his hair above water.

A roar erupted from both sides of the bank—guilty!

But then the ropes, newly wet, slipped, and he sank.

Another roar—innocent!

They left him at the bottom for over thirty seconds, then pulled him up gasping, choking, screaming for mercy.

After prayers, he was declared innocent.

Throughout, Clement prayed on the shore.

When it ended, he thanked God aloud—his voice strong, though slightly trembling.

Later, he turned to Jerome.

“Should he not be compensated?”

“For what?”

“For the pain, the terror, the suffocation. Poor soul—he lives, but has tasted death’s bitterness. Yet he did no wrong.”

“He is rewarded enough,” Jerome replied. “He is cleared of guilt.”

“But being innocent of that fault, he still drank from Death’s cup—though not to the dregs. His accusers, less innocent than he, suffer nothing.”

Jerome answered sharply:

“This world is not where men are truly punished. Woe to those who sin yet escape pain. Blessed are those who endure suffering—such pain is counted for them in heaven, yes, a hundredfold.”

Clement bowed his head.

“May your words take root in my heart, Brother Jerome.”

But the harshest trial Clement endured was unplanned.

It began when Jerome, in a rare moment of leniency, took him to visit Fra Colonna. On the table lay a freshly copied manuscript—*The Dream of Polifilo*. The author, proud and eager, pointed to a passage he called a masterpiece.

“For ages,” he declared, “fools have praised mortal women—earthly creatures, plainly marked by their origin. Sirens at windows, where Roman ladies have mastered the art of showing only one beauty—an ear, an eyelash—through a lattice, hiding all else. Magpies at doors, Capre n’i giardini, Angeli in Strada, Sante in chiesa, Diavoli in casa. Then I came—and gathered all amorous phrases from minstrels, even those Petrarch wasted on Laura, the most deceitful of all—and offered them not to mere women, but to the Nine Muses alone—worthy of divine love.”

“By which noble trick,” Jerome interjected, flipping through the pages, “you, a friar of St. Dominic, have written an obscene book.” And he slammed the manuscript down.

“Obscene? You insolent monk!” The author sprang around the table, snatched the book, locked it away, and, trembling with shame, said, “My Gerard—pshaw! Brother What’s-his-name didn’t find it obscene. *Puris omnia pura*.”

“Those who read it—may Heaven spare them—will find it what I do.”

Colonna swallowed the insult as best he could. Had he not been in his own lodgings, a nobleman and scholar, a brawl might have followed.

Instead, he shifted the topic to a beautiful chrysolite lent by Cardinal Colonna. As Clement handled it, Colonna extolled its moral virtues—just as scholars of the age revered jewels.

But Jerome refused to follow. He challenged him.

“You believe a dead stone can grant courage, chastity, immunity from poison, temperance?”

“The wisdom of centuries proves it,” Colonna replied. “Take the amethyst—look at this one. Clear, rich, flawless. This Gerard—Brother Thingemy—comes from the north, where men drink like fish, yet he is always sober. Why? Because he wears an amethyst. Where is yours, Brother? Show it to this unbeliever!”

“And that stone made him temperate?” Jerome asked, mocking.

“Certainly. The name itself means ‘not to tipple.’ Names are signs. A stone is not called *amethustos* for two thousand years without reason.”

He went on, detailing the virtues of ruby, sapphire, emerald, opal—citing grave historians.

“These are mere tales of old wives,” Jerome scoffed. “Such credulity!”

Now, credulity is a charge often hurled at believers—and it wounds deeply.

Colonna winced. Then, misreading his silence, Jerome urged him to abandon pagan lore and study the saints.

“Blot out these heathen superstitions, brother. Christianity has already erased them.”

He continued, carelessly repeating popular but loose theological claims.

Then, the wounded pride of *Polifilo* burst forth.

Colonna exploded, pouring forth a torrent of learning—some of which I can only summarize.

“The heathens blotted out? They control four-fifths of the world. What have we Christians invented without their help? Painting? Sculpture? These are heathen arts. We are dwarfs beside them. Who can carve forms as godlike as the Athenians, Licas of Libya, Dinocrates of Macedon, Scopas, Timotheus, Leochares, Briaxis, Chares, Lysippus, and the three masters of Rhodes who carved Laocoon from a single block? Who dares turn mountains into statues, as at Bagistan or Athos? Who builds a colossus in the sea, so ships sail between his legs?

“Architecture? We are children. Can we match the Parthenon, with its clusters of Doric columns? Or the Greek and Roman theatres? Or the Egyptian temples—whose portals stand a mile long, guarded by sphinxes as large as Venetian palaces? Their porphyry, polished like crystal, still gleams today, mocking our clumsy work.

“Roads? We’ve lost the art—despite having the greatest models right before us. Tombs? No Christian nation has built a monument that doesn’t make a scholar laugh. Think of the Mausoleum, the Pyramids, the tombs of the Indus and Ganges—mountains outside, mines of gems inside.

“You haven’t seen the East, Jerome, or you wouldn’t despise the heathens.”

Jerome insisted these were merely material things. True greatness lies in the soul.

“Then,” Colonna retorted, “what have we discovered in the mind? Geometry? Logic? We are pupils of Euclid and Aristotle. Writing? We didn’t invent it any more than Cadmus did. Poetry? Homer, Virgil, Horace—we’ve never matched them. Tragedy or comedy? Our mysteries and moralities—frozen personifications, dog-Latin—are pitiful compared to Greek drama, performed in marble amphitheatres before entire cities, by poets like Sophocles.

“What have we invented? Monotheism? The wise Greeks and Romans held it. Even their poets believed in one supreme power: *Zeus estin ouranos, Zeus te gy Zeus toi panta*—‘Jupiter is the sky, and Jupiter is all things.’ Lucan echoes: ‘Jupiter est quod cunque vides quo cunque moveris.’

“Their masses worshipped many gods. Ours? We haven’t invented ‘invocation of saints.’ Our saints mirror their *Daemones* and *Divi*. The heathens prayed to deified mortals to intercede with higher gods. The ignorant among them worshipped them outright. So do our common folk today—following heathen tradition.

“In scripture, there is no polytheism. We haven’t invented a new form of it. The pagans worshipped deified mortals; each had a favorite, to whom they prayed ten times for once to the Omnipotent. We do the same—praying to canonized saints ten times for once to God. Is that invention?

“Invention belongs to the East. Among the ancient vulgar, only sailors were monotheists—they worshiped Venus, calling her ‘Stella maris,’ ‘Regina caelorum.’ Among us, only sailors are monotheists—they worship Mary, ‘Star of the Sea,’ ‘Queen of Heaven.’ Is that a new religion? An old coat with a new button.

“We make images and adore them—absurd, for adoration is due only from creature to creator. Here, man is the creator. So the statues should worship him—if they had brains enough to justify a rat worshipping them. But even this abuse, though childish, is ancient. Pagans made images, knelt before them, adorned them, burned incense, lit candles, carried them in procession, made pilgrimages—just as we do today.”

Jerome interrupted impatiently.

“The most revered images in Christendom were not made by human hands—they fell from heaven.”

“Ay,” Colonna shot back, “such as the tutelary statues of Italian cities. I’ve examined nineteen. If they came from the sky, our worst sculptors must be angels. But I’m at ease—no ugly statue or bad painting ever fell from heaven to steal bread from skilled artisans.

“All this is pagan. The Trojans, with Oriental imaginations, claimed their Palladium—a wooden statue—fell from the sky. The Greeks brought it home from Troy, and soon statues rained down on every city. One such statue troubled St. Paul at Ephesus—it was Diana’s statue, said to have fallen from Jupiter. *Credat qui credere possit*.”

“What about the image of our blessed Lady, which hung in the air over Rome barely a century ago, taken by the Pope and placed in St. Peter’s?”

“I have no doubt,” Colonna said. “That’s the story of Numa’s shield, revived by theologians with a taste for fiction—but no talent. Numa’s sacred shield, the *ancile*, hung in the air over Rome until he took it down and placed it in Jupiter’s temple. Be just—accept both stories or neither.

‘Qui Bavium non odit amet tua carmina, Mavi.’

“And indeed, all our miracle statues—those that speak, nod, sweat, bleed—owe their existence to the heathens. The Athenian statues sweated before Chaeronea. Roman statues bled during Tully’s consulship—Victory at Capua, Mars at Rome, Apollo at the gates. The Palladium spoke in Vesta’s Temple. Juno at Veii nodded to go to Rome. Antony’s statue bled before Actium. Others cured diseases—like Pelichus, mocked by Lucian. The wise heathens believed in sweating marble, weeping wood, bleeding brass—as I do.

“Our holy marks—knees worn by saints, fingers pressed into stone—have heathen origins. Hercules’s footprints were shown on a rock in Scythia. Castor and Pollux left hoofprints on a rock at Regillum. A temple was built there, and the marks remained in Tully’s day. Near Venice, a stone split by St. George’s sword—except he never did it. It was a Roman who split a whetstone with a razor.

‘Qui Bavium non odit amet tua carmina, Mavi.’

“Kissing images? The Pope’s toe? That’s Eastern paganism. Egyptians learned it from Assyrians, Greeks from Egyptians, Romans from Greeks, we from Romans—whose Pontifex Maximus had his toe kissed under the Empire. Druids kissed their High Priest’s toe a thousand years before Christ. Muslims, who claim to hate paganism, kiss the Black Stone of Mecca—a pagan practice.

“The priests of Baal kissed their idols.

“Tully tells of a beautiful Hercules statue at Agrigentum, whose chin was worn smooth by kisses. The lower part of the statue we call Peter is really Jupiter. The toe is worn—but not all by Christian lips. The heathens laid theirs there first. We followed them like monkeys after their masters.

“So let us destroy the poor heathens!

“Our infant baptism is Persian—font and signing of the brow. Throwing earth on coffins and saying ‘dust to dust’ is Egyptian.

“Our incense is Oriental, Roman, pagan. Early Church Fathers feared it, died for refusing to handle it. Holy water? Pagan, and all its uses. See—this is a pagan aspersorium. Could you tell it from ours? It stood in the same place in their temples, used in worship and purification. They called it *Aqua lustralis*. Their people, like ours, believed drops could wash away sin. Their wise men, like ours, smiled or sighed at such belief.

What says Ovid of this folly, which outlived him?

You see—the heathens were not all fools. Nor are we. Not all.”
