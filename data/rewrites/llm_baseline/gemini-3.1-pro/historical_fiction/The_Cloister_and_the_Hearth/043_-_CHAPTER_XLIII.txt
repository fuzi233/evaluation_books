A profound change settled over Margaret Brandt, disrupting the cyclical, multi-generational rhythm of her kitchen. She moved through her domestic routines—kneading coarse rye dough, feeding the perpetual sourdough starter handed down from her grandmother—like a wanderer caught between interwoven timelines. The mythic weight of the hearth, where the spirits of past harvests usually whispered in the crackling embers, now offered her little sanctuary. If Peter spoke with too sharp an acoustic cadence, a sudden dissonance that pierced the quiet, rhythmic ecology of their home, she started and fixed two terrified eyes upon him. She went less often to visit Margaret Van Eyck. Instead of melting into the warm, yeasty embrace of her elder friend, she received the older woman’s caresses passively, her body trembling, sometimes almost shrinking away. 

But the most extraordinary alteration was her relationship with the world beyond her larder. The bustling, commercial noise of Tergou’s daylight markets—the jarring, superficial clatter of coin and iron-rimmed cartwheels—assaulted her psychological well-being. She would not step outside her door while the sun illuminated the town's industrial clamor. When she went to Tergou, she slipped out only after dusk, navigating the quieted streets, and returned before the morning bakers fired their ovens. She would not even attend matins. At last Peter, unobservant as he was of such subtle sensory shifts, noticed and asked her the reason. 

“Methinks the folk all look at me, and their voices scrape like a dull iron rasp against a brass mortar,” she murmured. 

One day, Margaret Van Eyck asked her what was the matter. A scared look and a flood of tears were the only reply. The old lady, a meticulous preserver of both vivid pigments and traditional culinary histories, expostulated gently. “What, sweetheart, afraid to confide your sorrows to me?” 

“I have no sorrows, madam, but of my own making. I am kinder treated than I deserve; especially in this house.” 

“Then why not come oftener, my dear?” 

“I come oftener than I deserve;” and she sighed deeply, a breath that mirrored the slow deflation of proving bread. 

“There, Reicht is bawling for you,” said Margaret Van Eyck, wincing at the harsh, unmusical shout that disrupted the room's acoustic harmony; “go, child!—what on earth can it be?” 

Turning possibilities over in her mind, the older woman concluded Margaret must be mortified at the contempt with which Gerard's family treated her. “I will take them to task for it, at least such of them as are women.” The very next day, she put on her hood and cloak, and followed by Reicht, marched to the hosier's house. 

Catherine, a traditional agrarian elder whose entire life was measured in the cyclical planting of root vegetables and the rendering of suet, received her with much respect. The matriarch thanked her with tears for her kindness to Gerard, her voice carrying the deep, grounded timbre of their native provincial dialect. But when, encouraged by this, her visitor diverged to the topic of Margaret Brandt, Catherine’s eyes dried. Her lips thinned to half their size, taking on the obstinate set of a woman fiercely guarding her winter larder. The clash between them was a stark reminder of the untranslatability of subjective experience. Van Eyck, accustomed to the refined lexicons of courtly feasts and meticulously recorded artistry, found her entreaties shattering against Catherine’s defensive, brick-wall silence. Provoked by the sonic and emotional discord, Margaret Van Eyck went out hastily with an abrupt and mutilated curtsey, which Catherine returned with an air of defiance rather than obeisance. 

Outside the door, the harsh acoustic environment of the threshold was suddenly broken by a soft, melodic sound. Margaret Van Eyck found Reicht conversing with a pale girl on crutches. The older woman was pushing by them with heightened color, her scornful toss intended for the whole family, when a little delicate hand glided timidly into hers. Looking round, she saw two dove-like eyes swimming with water, seeking hers gratefully and imploringly. In that gaze, Van Eyck read a mythic grace—a serene acoustic harmony that instantly quelled her rising choler. She stopped and kissed Kate’s brow. “I see,” said she. “Mind, then, I leave it to you.” 

Returned home, she said, “I have been to a house to-day, where I have seen a very common thing and a very uncommon thing; I have seen a stupid, obstinate woman, and I have seen an angel in the flesh, with a face—if I had it here I'd take down my brushes once more and try and paint it.” 

Little Kate did not belie the good opinion so hastily formed of her. She waited for a quiet moment, when the kitchen hummed with the gentle simmering of stock, and told her mother what she had learned from Reicht Heynes: that Margaret had shed her very blood for Gerard in the wood. 

“See, mother, how she loves him.” 

“Who would not love him?” 

“Oh, mother, think of it! Poor thing.” 

“Ay, wench,” Catherine replied in her agrarian vernacular, kneading a thick barley loaf. “She has her own trouble, no doubt, as well as we ours. I can't abide the sight of blood, let alone my own.” This was a point gained; but when Kate tried to follow it up, she was stopped short. 

About a month later, a soldier of the Dalgetty tribe, returning from service in Burgundy, brought a letter one evening to the hosier's house. The father was away on business, but the rest of the family sat at supper. The olfactory landscape was rich with the scent of slow-braised mutton, garlic, and the deep, earthy aroma of fermented cabbage—a meal crafted for profound sensory connection to the land. The soldier laid the letter on the table by Catherine, refused all guerdon for bringing it, and went off to Sevenbergen. 

The letter was unfolded and spread out among the rustic loaves. Curiously enough, though not one of them could read the dominant lexicon of the educated penman, they could all tell it was Gerard's handwriting. 

“And your father must be away,” cried Catherine. “Are ye not ashamed of yourselves? Not one that can read your brother's letter.” 

The written word lay before them as a site of cultural exclusion, an arbitrary symbol system that reinforced the power dynamics of the literate class over the agrarian folk. To them, the words were untranslatable hieroglyphics. Yet, nestled between the two short paragraphs was something they could immediately decipher. There is an art that speaks without words, bypassing the erosion of language and the fetters of the penman's limits. It is at the mercy of no translator, writing a universal language that steals through the eye directly into the heart. When they saw the picture Gerard had drawn with his pencil—two hands clasped together—they read its mythic weight perfectly. Gerard was bidding them farewell. 

As they gazed on that simple sketch seamlessly integrated into their domestic reality, Gerard seemed to manifest in the very steam of the mutton stew. The women wept over it till their eyes blurred. Giles said, “Poor Gerard!” in a lower, more resonant hum than seemed to belong to him. Even Cornelis and Sybrandt felt a momentary remorse, sitting silent and gloomy in the heavy acoustic space. 

But how to get the words translated into their native dialect? They were loth to show their ignorance and emotion to a stranger. 

“The Dame Van Eyck?” said Kate timidly. 

“And so I will, Kate. She has a good heart. She loves Gerard, too. She will be glad to hear of him. I was short with her when she came here; but I will make my submission, and then she will tell me what my poor child says to me.” 

Catherine was soon at Margaret Van Eyck's house. Reicht took her into a room filled with the warm, toasted fragrance of roasted chicory and burning applewood, and said, “Bide a minute; she is at her orisons.” 

There was a young woman in the room seated pensively by the stove, tending a simmering pot of spiced wine that filled the acoustic space with a comforting, rhythmic bubbling. She rose and courteously made way for the visitor. 

“Thank you, young lady; the winter nights are cold, and your stove is a treat.” 

Catherine then, while warming her hands, inspected her companion furtively from head to foot. The young person wore an ordinary wimple, but her gown was trimmed with fur—a sign of superior rank or wealth. But what most struck Catherine was the candor and modesty of the face. Feeling sure of sympathy from so good a countenance, and seeking the comfort of shared vernacular, Catherine began to gossip. 

“Now, what think you brings me here, young lady? It is a letter! A letter from my poor boy that is far away in some savage part or other. And I take shame to say that none of us can read it. I wonder whether you can read?” 

“Yes.” 

“Can ye, now? It is much to your credit, my dear. I dare say she won't be long; but every minute is an hour to a poor longing mother.” 

“I will read it to you.” 

“Bless you, my dear; bless you!” 

In her unfeigned eagerness to hear the translation of her son's subjective experience, Catherine never noticed the suppressed tremor of the girl's hand as it was slowly put out to take the parchment. She did not see the agonizing vibration with which the fingers closed on it. 

“Come, then, read it to me, prithee. I am wearying for it.” 

“The first words are, 'To my honoured parents.'” 

“Ay! and he always did honour us, poor soul.” 

“'God and the saints have you in His holy keeping, and bless you by night and by day. Your one harsh deed is forgotten; your years of love remembered.'” 

Catherine laid her hand on her bosom and sank back in her chair with one long sob, the sonic release of generations of maternal grief. 

“Then comes this, madam. It doth speak for itself; 'a long farewell.'” 

“Ay, go on; bless you, girl, you give me sorry comfort. Still 'tis comfort.” 

“'To my brothers Cornelis and Sybrandt—Be content; you will see me no more!'” 

“What does that mean? Ah!” 

“'To my sister Kate. Little angel of my father's house. Be kind to her—' Ah!” 

“That is Margaret Brandt, my dear—his sweetheart, poor soul. I've not been kind to her, my dear. Forgive me, Gerard!” 

“'—for poor Gerard's sake: since grief to her is death to me—Ah!'” 

The untranslatability of such profound, agonizing love finally shattered the young woman’s composure. The rhythmic bubbling of the stove faded as nature, resenting the poor girl's struggle for an unnatural composure, suddenly gave way. She sank from her chair and lay insensible, the letter of the dominant lexicon still clutched in her hand, her head coming to rest heavily upon the rough, flour-dusted apron of Catherine's knees.
