A profound remorse for his past follies seized Harry Warrington the moment he realized he was a prisoner in that dismal lock-up house, mingled with a sharp wrath at the indignity of arrest. Yet, beneath the present misery, he clung to the certainty that his confinement was merely momentary. He possessed twenty friends who would surely release him; the question remained only to whom he should appeal. There was Mr. Draper, the man of business, who had been so obsequious; his kind uncle, the Baronet, who offered his home as Harry's sanctuary; his cousin Castlewood, who had drained him of so much gold; his noble associates at the Chocolate-House; and his good Aunt Bernstein. Any of these would undoubtedly provide aid, though some might administer a stern scolding for his imprudence. The paramount concern was discretion. Mr. Warrington was anxious that as few members of the public as possible should witness a gentleman of his prodigious importance subjected to such a vulgar process as arrest.

As if the public does not eventually learn everything it cares to know. As if the dinner I shall have today, or the hole in the stocking I wear while writing this, can remain hidden from some prying enemy, even if my boots are on and my door locked! I mention the stocking merely as an example. The world can pry out whatever it wishes to know. But there is a consolation men rarely accept in their own cases: the world does not care. Consider the sheer volume of scandal it has endured; how weary and blasé it must be of such intelligence. You are taken to prison, imagining yourself indelibly disgraced? You are bankrupt under odd circumstances? You strike a queer bargain and are found out, convinced the world will punish you? Psha! Your shame is merely vanity. Go and talk to the world as if nothing had happened. Tumble down, brush the mud from your clothes, appear with a smiling countenance, and nobody cares. Do you suppose Society will produce its pocket-handkerchief and be inconsolable when you die? Why should it care whether your worship graces or disgraces itself? Whatever happens, it talks, meets, jokes, yawns, and dines pretty much as before. Therefore, do not be so conceited as to fancy your private affairs are of such importance, *mi fili*. Yet, Mr. Harry Warrington chafed and fumed as though the entire world was tingling with the touch of the hand that had rested on his sublime shoulder.

"A pretty sensation my arrest must have created at the club!" Harry thought. "I suppose Mr. Selwyn will be cutting all sorts of jokes about my misfortune, plague take him! Everyone round the table will have heard of it. March will tremble about the bet I owe him, and faith, 'twill be difficult to pay him when I lose. They will all be setting up a whoop of congratulation at the Savage, calling me 'the Savage' because I was taken prisoner. How shall I ever appear in the world again? Whom shall I ask to come to my help?" No, he decided with his mingled acuteness and simplicity. He would not write to his relations or his noble friends at White's initially. He required Sampson's counsel. The chaplain had often been in similar predicaments and would know how to advise him.

Accordingly, as soon as the light of dawn appeared—after an almost intolerable delay, for it seemed to Harry as if the sun had forgotten to visit Cursitor Street that morning—and as soon as the inmates of the house of bondage were stirring, Mr. Warrington despatched a messenger to his friend in Long Acre, acquainting the chaplain with the calamity and beseeching his advice and consolation.

Mr. Warrington did not know that sending such a message to the parson was akin to saying, "I am fallen amongst the lions. Come down, my dear friend, into the pit with me." Harry likely thought Sampson's difficulties were over, or was so engrossed in his own perplexities that he bestowed little thought on his neighbor's. Having sent the missive, the captive's mind was somewhat more at ease, and he condescended to call for breakfast. The attendant serving him asked if he would order dinner or dine at Mrs. Bailiff's table with other gentlemen. No. Mr. Warrington would not order dinner. He informed the chamberlain that he intended to quit the place before dinner-time. The man departed, thinking this was not the first young gentleman who had announced such an intention within two hours. "Well, if your honour stays, there is good beef and carrot at two o'clock," the sceptic said, closing the door on Mr. Harry and his solitary meditations.

Harry's messenger returned with a note from Mr. Sampson stating he would arrive as soon as possible. But ten o'clock came, then eleven, then noon, and no Sampson. About twelve, however, Gumbo arrived with a portmanteau of his master's clothes, flinging himself, roaring with grief, at Harry's feet. With a thousand vows of fidelity, he expressed readiness to die, to sell himself into slavery again, to do anything to rescue his beloved Master Harry from this calamitous position. Harry was touched by the lad's affection and told him to rise from the ground where he was groveling. "All you have to do, sir, is give me my clothes to dress, and hold your tongue about this business. Mind you, not a word to anybody!" Mr. Warrington commanded severely.

"Oh no, sir, never to nobody!" Gumbo replied solemnly, proceeding to dress his master carefully. Harry needed a change and a toilette after his sudden capture and dismal night. Gumbo flung a dash of powder into Harry's hair and arrayed him so elegantly that Mr. Warrington looked as fine and splendid as if he were stepping into his chair to go to St. James's.

Indeed, all that love and servility could do, Gumbo faithfully did for his master, whom he held in extreme regard. But there were things beyond Gumbo's power. He could not undo what was already done, nor help lying and excusing himself when pressed on disagreeable points. The language of slaves is lies—I mean both black and white. The creature slinks away and hides with subterfuges, as a hunted animal runs to his covert at the sight of the tyrant. Strange relics of feudality, consequences of our ever-so-old social life! Our domestics (are they not men, too, and brethren?) are all hypocrites before us. They never speak naturally or the whole truth. We should be indignant; we should say, confound their impudence, and turn them out. But *quo me rapis*, O my unbridled hobby?

The truth was, an oath to say not a word about his master's arrest was impossible to keep for Gumbo. With a heart full of grief but a tongue that never ceased wagging, bragging, joking, and lying, he had already announced the woeful circumstance to a prodigious number of acquaintances, chiefly gentlemen of the shoulder-knot and worsted lace. We have seen how he carried the news to Colonel Lambert's and Lord Wrotham's servants; he had proclaimed it at the footman's club frequented by the nobility; he had subsequently partaken of ale in Sir Miles Warrington's butler's room, repeating and embellishing the story. Then he had gone to Madame Bernstein's people, informing that domestic circle of his grief and his master's capture, noting there was no call for his personal services that evening. Finally, he had stepped up to Lord Castlewood's, informing the gentry there of the incident. So, when laying his hand on his heart and with gushing tears, Gumbo replied to his master's injunction, "Oh no, master! nebber to nobody!" we are in a condition to judge the credibility of his statement.

The black had long completed his master's toilet; the dreary breakfast was over. Slow as the hours went to the prisoner, they passed one after another, but no Sampson came. At length, some time after noon, there arrived not Sampson, but a billet from him, sealed with a moist wafer and with the ink almost wet. The unlucky divine's letter ran as follows:

"Oh, sir, dear sir, I have done all that a man can at the command and in the behalf of his patron! You did not know, sir, to what you were subjecting me, did you? Else, if I was to go to prison, why did I not share yours, and why am I in a lock-up house three doors off?

"Yes. Such is the fact. As I was hastening to you, knowing full well the danger to which I was subject:—but what danger will I not affront at the call of such a benefactor as Mr. Warrington hath been to me?—I was seized by two villains who had a writ against me, and who have lodged me at Naboth's, hard by, and so close to your honour, that we could almost hear each other across the garden walls of the respective houses where we are confined.

"I had much and of importance to say, which I do not care to write down on paper regarding your affairs. May they mend! May my cursed fortunes, too, better themselves, is the prayer of—

"Your honour's afflicted Chaplain-in-Ordinary, J. S."

And now, as Mr. Sampson refused to speak, it will be our duty to acquaint the reader with those matters whereof the poor chaplain did not care to discourse on paper.

Gumbo's loquacity had not reached so far as Long Acre, and Mr. Sampson was ignorant of the extent of his patron's calamity until he received Harry's letter and messenger from Chancery Lane. The divine was still ardent with gratitude for the service Mr. Warrington had just conferred on him, and eager to find some means to succour his distressed patron. He knew what a large sum Lord Castlewood had won from his cousin, had dined in company with his lordship the day before, and now ran to Lord Castlewood's house, hoping to arouse him to some pity for Mr. Warrington. Sampson made a very eloquent and touching speech to Lord Castlewood about his kinsman's misfortune, speaking with real kindness and sympathy, which, however, failed to touch the nobleman to whom he addressed himself.

"My lord peevishly and curtly put a stop to the chaplain's passionate pleading. 'Did I not tell you, two days since, when you came for money, that I was as poor as a beggar, Sampson,' said his lordship, 'and has anybody left me a fortune since? The little sum I won from my cousin was swallowed up by others. I not only can't help Mr. Warrington, but, as I pledge you my word, not being in the least aware of his calamity, I had positively written to him this morning to ask him to help me.' And a letter to this effect did actually reach Mr. Warrington from his lodgings, whither it had been despatched by the penny post.

'I must get him money, my lord. I know he had scarcely anything left in his pocket after relieving me. Were I to pawn my cassock and bands, he must have money,' cried the chaplain.

'Amen. Go and pawn your bands, your cassock, anything you please. Your enthusiasm does you credit,' said my lord; and resumed the reading of his paper, whilst, in the deepest despondency, poor Sampson left him.

My Lady Maria meanwhile had heard that the chaplain was with her brother and conjectured the subject of their talk. She seized upon the parson as he issued from his fruitless interview with my lord. She drew him into the dining-room; the strongest marks of grief and sympathy were in her countenance. 'Tell me, what is this has happened to Mr. Warrington?' she asked.

'Your ladyship, then, knows?' asked the chaplain.

'Have I not been in mortal anxiety ever since his servant brought the dreadful news last night?' asked my lady. 'We had it as we came from the opera—from my Lady Yarmouth's box—my lord, my Lady Castlewood, and I.'

'His lordship, then, did know?' continued Sampson.

'Benson told the news when we came from the playhouse to our tea,' repeats Lady Maria.

The chaplain lost all patience and temper at such duplicity. 'This is too bad,' he said, with an oath; and he told Lady Maria of the conversation he had just had with Lord Castlewood, of the latter's refusal to succour his cousin after winning great sums from him, and with much eloquence and feeling, of Mr. Warrington's most generous behaviour to himself.

Then my Lady Maria broke out with a series of remarks regarding her own family, which were by no means complimentary to her own kith and kin. Although not accustomed to tell the truth commonly, yet, when certain families fall out, it is wonderful what a number of truths they will tell about one another. With tears, imprecations, and language stronger than I care to repeat, Lady Maria burst into a furious and impassioned tirade, touching upon the history of almost all her noble family. She complimented the men and ladies alike; she shrieked out interrogatories to Heaven, inquiring why it had made such [she named her brothers, sisters, uncles, aunts, parents]; and, emboldened with wrath, she dashed at her brother's library door, so shrill in her outcries, so furious in her demeanour, that the alarmed chaplain, fearing the scene which might ensue, made for the street.

My lord, looking up from his book, regarded the furious woman with some surprise and selected a good strong oath to fling at her to check her onset.

But, when roused, we have seen how courageous Maria could be. Afraid as she was ordinarily of her brother, she was not in a mood to be frightened now by any language of abuse or sarcasm at his command.
