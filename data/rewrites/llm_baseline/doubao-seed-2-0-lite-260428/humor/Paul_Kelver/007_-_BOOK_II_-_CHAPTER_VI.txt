Of the Shadow That Slipped Between the Man in Grey and the Lady With the Love-Lit Eyes

“There’s not a single thing missing, far as I’ve searched,” my mom announced, patting her apron like she was double-checking no silverware had vanished down the sofa cushions. “Mark my words, that’s the only explanation: she got spooked out of her wits and legged it.”

Dad froze mid-pour, decanter in one hand, unopened bottle of sherry in the other, blinking like he’d just been caught red-handed sneaking an extra pre-dinner glass. “Spooked? What on earth was there to scare her?”

“The whole idea!” Mom huffed. “She’s never waited on anyone in her life. Last night I found her blubbering over a tray, convinced she’d dump the entire soup tureen on a guest’s best waistcoat.”

“Uhh… what do we do?” Dad glanced at the mantel clock, face going pale. “They’ll be through the front door in less than an hour.”

“We’ll have no dinner at all,” Mom said, “unless I yank an apron on and haul every course up the stairs myself.”

“Where’s her home again? Ilford, right?”

“Yep.”

“Easy,” Dad said, brightening up. “We just play the whole thing off as a silly joke! No one’ll mind.”

Mom sat straight down on the nearest chair and started crying. The previous week had already been absolute hell. This was the first proper fancy dinner we’d ever hosted, the kind that started with anchovies and ended with ices from the confectioner’s down the road, and we’d spent every waking minute prepping. We’d scrubbed the house top to bottom, bought all those tiny useless frilly knickknacks that no normal household ever needs that Ostentation demands the second you invite a business contact over for a meal. Our kitchen range had straight up gone on strike like a union rep from the Boiler Workers’ Guild, refusing to bake so much as a scone until we called three plumbers to beg it to behave. The dusty old cookbooks we’d dug out from the back of the shelf had been argued with, cussed at, and half the ice recipes we tested had turned into runny cold custard disasters, but we’d finally nailed it. Victory was ours. Until the only maid we’d trained for a whole week had up and vanished, leaving a massive uncrossable chasm between the perfectly cooked dinner in the kitchen and the empty dining table. The bridge had bolted. No wonder mom was sobbing.

“Can’t we grab someone else last minute?” Dad asked.

“Impossible,” Mom sniffled. “I rehearsed every step of the serving routine with her for seven whole days. She could even carry three plates without dropping one.”

“Have the whole dinner in the kitchen!” my aunt suggested, folding napkins into shapes she insisted were “fancy sailboats” that actually looked more like crumpled handkerchiefs. “Call it a picnic! Sounds fun.”

It did sound like the only workable plan, until a soft knock tapped at the front door.

“Already?!” Dad yelped, diving for his suit coat like the tax man had just caught him fudging receipts.

“It’s Barbara,” Mom sighed, already shifting into full pessimist mode. “She promised to come help me do my dress hair, but now obviously I don’t need her.”

But before anyone could move, Barbara was already in the room, I’d let her in myself — back in those days, every shadow in the house would sprint out the window the second Barbara stepped through the door. She kissed all three of us in one quick, fluid movement, and instantly the whole stupid mess felt funny instead of catastrophic.

“No dinner?” Dad cackled. “We’ll just all stand around looking confused and pretend the party was yesterday. Their faces are gonna be priceless.”

“There’s a perfectly good dinner,” Mom corrected, wiping her eyes, “but no one to ferry it up from the kitchen.”

Barbara stood quiet for half a second, her sweet face serious, before a big grin broke out. “I’ll get you someone,” she said.

“Sweetie, you don’t even know anyone in this whole neighborhood!” Mom started to protest, but Barbara had already snatched the front door key off its nail and vanished.

The second she was gone, the gloom settled back over us. “If only there was one single hotel within ten miles of this godforsaken place,” Dad grumbled.

“You’ll just have to host them yourself, Luke,” Mom said, already heading for the kitchen to grab an apron. “I’ll wait tables, that’s all.”

“Don’t be ridiculous!” Dad snapped, getting huffy. “Can’t the cook bring the courses in herself?”

“Nobody can cook a whole seven-course meal and serve it at the same time!” Mom said, frustrated. “Besides, she’s still got flour all over her face and her slippers are full of holes.”

“What about Fan?” Dad whispered, like the gardener’s daughter might overhear him through the wall.

Mom just gave him a look that said *are you kidding me*, and that settled that.

“Paul?” Dad tried.

“Absolutely not,” Mom crossed her arms. “I’m not dressing my son up as a footman just to impress a bunch of stuffy businessmen.”

“Fine, well, you’d better go get your fancy frock on then,” Dad said.

“I won’t need more than two minutes to slip an apron on,” Mom replied.

“I was really looking forward to seeing you in that new silk dress you saved up three months for,” Dad said. With almost anyone else you’d have called that calculated flattery, but with Dad? It was just a happy, unplanned accident.

Mom confessed, a little sheepish, that she’d been looking forward to wearing it too. The last of the tension melted right out of the room.

“I wanted everything to go perfect for you, Luke,” Mom said, soft. “I knew you were hoping this dinner would land that big new contract.”

“I was only thinking about you, Maggie dear,” Dad said. “You’re my favorite business, anyway.”

“I know,” Mom said, and her voice wobbled. “This is so stupidly unfair.”

We all went quiet when we heard the front door key turn in the lock.

“She came back alone,” Mom sighed. “I knew it was hopeless.”

The door swung open.

“Please ma’am,” said the brand new parlour maid, grinning, “will I do?”

There Barbara stood, framed in the doorway, wearing the frilliest little apron you ever saw, a dainty lace cap perched perfectly on her golden hair, so bright and bubbly she blew every last leftover worry right out of the room. No one could ever say no to Barbara, even if you tried.

“You’re footman,” she ordered, pointing straight at me, and to my shock Mom didn’t even protest, she just laughed. “Wait right there till I come back down.” She turned back to Mom, all professional now. “Alright, ma’am, your guests will be here in ten minutes. Ready?”

When Mom stepped out in her new evening dress, I was genuinely a little scandalized, I’d never seen any real-life lady showing her bare shoulders before. I must have made a funny face, because Mom flushed and grabbed a shawl to cover the gleaming white silk, muttering something about being cold. Barbara yelled at her for that, saying it was a crime to hide such pretty clothes, and Dad did this dramatic dramatic little knee-drop like he was a knight in a fairy tale, lifting Mom’s hand up to kiss it with ridiculous grave reverence. Barbara made me kneel too, insisted we were doing a royal audience and the Queen was receiving us. So I knelt, glancing up shyly at Mom’s smiling face, and that was the exact second I figured out the whole weird philosophy of fancy clothes.

My memory lingers on that soft, silly scene, because the days that followed got so sad, so fast. There would be nights Mom’s eyes would flash angry at Dad, her voice sharp and cruel, and the second he left the room her lips would start trembling and she’d be crying. Dad would sit with his face turned away, jaw clamped tight, or storm out of the room slamming the door so hard the pictures rattled, and hours later I’d find him sitting alone in the dark, head in his hands. I’d lie awake listening to them yell through the thin walls, their voices rising sharp then dropping to cold, cruel whispers, and it felt like there was some invisible grinning shadow that had crept into our house, that kept pushing itself between them so they could never look each other in the loving eyes ever again, only stare straight into the face of that evil thing. I swore I could sometimes see its outline in the dim air, feel a cold chill when it brushed past me. It’d whisper mean lies in Mom’s ear one minute, then scuttle over to Dad and talk out of his voice, meaner than he ever meant to be. To this day, every love story I read I spot that shadow peeking over my shoulder, snickering. Every boy and girl whispering in the twilight, I see it skulking behind them, tiptoeing slow after them as they walk hand in hand down the quiet lane. Will any of us ever outrun it? Or does every couple have to walk through that dark valley of the shadow of dead love? Is it just Love’s stupid test, sorting out the weak from the ones who’ll find their way back to each other on the other side?

Of the actual dinner up till dessert I can barely tell you anything coherent, because I was on footman duty running back and forth so fast I barely had time to peek through the dining room door crack. All I knew for sure was the big guest of honor was old Mr. Teidelmann — or was it Tiedelmann? I never could remember — a snuff-dusted, mumbly old grump whose name was plastered on every blank brick wall down by Limehouse in giant letters, with a & Co. at the end. He was sat at Mom’s right hand, and he was so deaf she had to yell every other sentence right in his ear. “Eh? What? Say that again!” he’d squawk every thirty seconds, holding his hand up to his ear. Old Hasluck, across the table, was yelling so loud his face went purple, gushing about some new disinfectant that Teidelmann apparently owned.

“My wife swears by the stuff!” Hasluck boomed across the table.

“Our drains!” Mrs. Hasluck piped up, a very no-nonsense sort of lady. “You’d never guess we had drains in the house now, it smells like roses everywhere.”

“What’re they talking about?” Teidelmann yelped, turning to Mom. “What’d he say his wife does?”

“Your disinfectant,” Mom explained, shouting a little. “Mrs. Hasluck swears by it!”

“Who?”

“Mrs. Hasluck!”

“Does she? Nice to hear,” Teidelmann grunted, clearly bored out of his mind.

“Nothing better for a sick room!” Hasluck kept yelling. “You could almost call it a perfume!”

“Makes you kind of hope you get ill soon,” my aunt muttered, speaking to absolutely no one.

“Reminds me of cocoanuts!” Hasluck continued. Teidelmann didn’t seem to hear, but Hasluck was determined he get his compliment. “I SAY IT REMINDS ME OF COCOANUTS,” he screamed.

“Oh, does it?” Teidelmann said.

“Doesn’t it remind you of cocoanuts?”

“Can’t say it does,” Teidelmann said. “Fact is, I don’t even know that much about the stuff. Never used it once.”

“Take my advice!” Hasluck bellowed. “Go buy a bottle!”

“Buy a what?”

“A BOTTLE,” Hasluck roared, straining so hard his eyes watered.

“What’s he saying now?” Teidelmann yelped, turning back to Mom.

“He says you ought to buy a bottle of your own disinfectant,” Mom explained.

“Of what?”

“Your own disinfectant.”

“Silly fool,” Teidelmann said, under his breath — but loud enough for everyone in the room, including me out in the hallway, to hear. The whole table went dead quiet for a full ten seconds before Hasluck burst out cackling.

“Fair enough!” Hasluck laughed. “He’s completely right, that’s exactly what I am. I’m so obsessed with talking shop I assume everyone else is too.”

He wasn’t even that much of a fool, though, because by the time I snuck back down the hall he was yelling about art, and Teidelmann was suddenly fully interested. Apparently Teidelmann was one of the best art judges in Europe, and he’d just bought six paintings from a young painter who’d be world famous soon for two thousand quid, and they’d be worth twenty grand someday. He said he’d never sell them, though. His widow would get the cash. A soft, musical little laugh drifted from the far corner of the table.

“It’s Anderson’s great big disappointment,” a soft, lazy, purring voice said, the laugh turned into words. “He’s spent thousands buying paintings of ragged little kids and badly dressed Madonnas, and I just can’t make myself care about any of them. Total waste of money, as far as I’m concerned.”

“But you’d pay thousands for a diamond to hang around your neck,” Dad pointed out.

“It makes my neck look nicer,” she purred back.

“Which is an even bigger waste of money,” Dad said, quiet, and she laughed that tinkly little laugh again.

“Who is she?” I whispered to Barbara. We were sat on the top of the stairs, splitting a shared jelly spoon to spoon.

“Second Mrs. Teidelmann,” Barbara whispered back. “Married him for his money. I don’t like her much, but she’s drop dead gorgeous.”

“Pretty as you?” I asked, disbelieving. We were sat with our backs to the light, so I couldn’t see her face.

“Oh, me!” Barbara scoffed. “I’m just a kid. Nobody looks at me like that, not unless they’re other little children like you.”

“Everybody thinks you’re beautiful,” I insisted.

“Who?” she asked, quick.

“Dr. Hal,” I said.

Her voice softened, a little happy. “Next time you see him, ask him what he honestly thinks of me, like you’re just curious for no reason. And don’t forget a single word he says this time. I’m curious.” It sounded like a silly task, but I never said no to anything Barbara wanted.

Turns out Barbara was far and away the star of the whole dinner anyway. Everyone was gushing over her: Mrs. Florret called her a perfect neat-handed Phillis, the Reverend Cottle’s wife — a broad, loud lady with eleven kids, eight of them boys — laughed and said “I’d never let a girl that pretty wait on my table, my sons would trip over each other trying to hand her the salt.” Dad accidentally called her “my dear” mid-plate-pass, and Hasluck snuck a silly little pinch to her elbow when no one was looking, and Barbara yelped “augh!” so loud Mom decided they might as well just tell everyone the whole story, rather than look weird. Everyone loved it even more, and when Barbara took off her apron but kept her cute little lace cap and came to sit down with the rest of the guests for dessert, every single person in the room was crowding her to say how clever and kind she was.

“That’s way better than being beautiful,” Mrs. Cottle said, to round off all the compliments.

“Kind hearts are more than coronets,” Dr. Florret said, perfectly on cue like he always did, and it never sounded annoying coming from him, just like the Amen at the end of a church service.

My aunt, of course, had one last observation to make, breaking the quiet lull. “When I was a girl,” she announced, “I used to stand in front of the mirror and tell myself ‘Fanny, you’ve got to be amiable.’ And I was! For ten whole years. No one can say I wasn’t.”

“Didn’t it pay off?” Hasluck asked, grinning.

“Got absolutely zero attention,” my aunt said, defiant.

Most of the rest of the dinner everyone argued about Dr. “Fighting Hal” Washburn’s big rant: he insisted all the respectable middle class folks were nothing but marionettes, dancing to the rules of etiquette, not their own actual feelings. He told a story about a patient of his whose wife was doing laundry in the front room when her husband died, and didn’t even stop wringing out her stocking to yell into the back room “Jim, you still there? Nope? Guess he’s dead.” No fake sobbing, no fainting on the doorstep. Dr. Florret said he’d much rather hang out with the polite, well-behaved dolls than the rough unfiltered East End folks. Dad chimed in with a story about a masked ball he went to in Paris when he was a student, where everyone acted exactly the same, and he bailed before midnight ‘cause he hated all the masks so much.

“That’s a shame,” the new Mrs. Teidelmann purred at him. “I’d have loved to see what they all looked like underneath.”

“I probably would’ve been disappointed,” Dad shrugged. “I’m with Dr. Florret. Sometimes the mask is the better part.”
