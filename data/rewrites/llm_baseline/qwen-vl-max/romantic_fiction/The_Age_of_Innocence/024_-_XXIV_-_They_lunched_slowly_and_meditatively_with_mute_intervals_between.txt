XXIV. They lunched slowly, each bite a quiet ritual between them, punctuated by silences that stretched like shadows across the table. Once the first words had fallen, they found themselves with so much to say—and yet, at times, silence became its own kind of conversation, rich and unspoken. Archer avoided speaking of himself, not out of calculation, but because he could not bear to lose even a single sentence of her story. Leaning forward, chin cradled in her hands, she told him of the year and a half since their last meeting.

She had grown weary of what people called “society.” New York had welcomed her back with warmth—almost too much warmth. She would never forget how kindly it embraced her return. But after the initial thrill wore off, she realized she was too different, too aware, to care about the things others did. So she had decided to try Washington—where, she hoped, she might meet more kinds of people, more kinds of ideas. And perhaps, she said, she would settle there. It would be a home for poor Medora, who had exhausted the patience of every relative just when she needed protection most—especially from the dangers of marriage.

“But Dr. Carver—aren’t you afraid of him? I hear he’s been staying with you at the Blenkers’.”

She smiled gently. “Oh, the danger is past. Dr. Carver is clever—he wants a wealthy wife to fund his ambitions. Medora is simply good publicity as a convert.”

“To what?”

“To all sorts of new, strange social ideas. But honestly, I find them more interesting than the blind obedience to tradition—someone else’s tradition—that I see among our friends. It feels foolish to have discovered America only to turn it into a copy of another country.”

She looked across the table, her eyes soft with irony. “Do you suppose Christopher Columbus sailed all that way just to go to the opera with the Selfridge Merrys?”

Archer flushed. “And Beaufort—do you talk like this to him?” he asked sharply.

“I haven’t seen him in a long time. But I used to. He understands.”

Ah,” he said, “that’s always been my point—you don’t like us. You like Beaufort because he’s not like us.”

He glanced around the sparse room, then out to the bare beach and the row of white houses lining the shore. “We’re terribly dull. We lack character, colour, variety. I wonder…” he broke off, voice low, “…why you don’t just go back?”

Her eyes darkened, and he braced himself for anger. But she remained still, as if weighing his words. He feared she might agree. Then, quietly, she said: “I believe it’s because of you.”

It was said without emotion, without flattery—so plainly that it startled him. He turned red to the temples, but dared not move. Her words felt like a rare bird perched on the edge of silence—any motion might scare it away, but if left undisturbed, it might draw others.

“At least,” she continued, “you were the one who made me see that beneath the dullness, there are things so delicate, so fine, that even those I loved most in my old life seem cheap now. I can’t explain it properly”—she frowned, pressing her brows together—“but it’s as though I’ve only just learned how deeply the coarse and base can mix with the most exquisite pleasures.”

“Exquisite pleasures,” he thought—“it’s something to have known them!” But the look in her eyes held him mute.

“I want,” she went on, “to be honest—with you, and with myself. For a long time, I’ve hoped for this moment—to tell you how much you’ve changed me, what you’ve made of me…”

Archer sat frozen, brows furrowed. Then he laughed, a short, brittle sound. “And what do you think you’ve made of me?”

She paled slightly. “Of you?”

“Yes. Because I am far more your creation than you ever were mine. I’m the man who married one woman because another told him to.”

Her pallor deepened into a fleeting flush. “I thought… you promised… not to say such things today.”

“Oh,” he said, “how typical of a woman! None of you can ever finish a difficult thing properly.”

She lowered her voice. “Is it really a bad thing—for May?”

He stood by the window, fingers drumming against the raised sash, every nerve tingling with the tenderness in her tone when she spoke her cousin’s name.

“That’s exactly what we must always remember, isn’t it?” she pressed. “By your own account.”

“My own account?” he echoed, still staring at the sea.

“If not,” she continued, “if it’s not worth giving up, if it’s not worth missing things—just so others may avoid pain and disappointment—then everything I came back for, everything that made my old life feel empty and hollow because no one there valued these things… all of it becomes a lie or a dream.”

He turned, still rooted to his place. “Then,” he finished for her, “there’s no reason why you shouldn’t go back.”

Her eyes clung to him, desperate. “Oh, is there no reason at all?”

“None,” he said, “if you’ve staked everything on my marriage. My marriage,” he added fiercely, “won’t be anything worth keeping you here.”

She didn’t answer. He went on: “What’s the point? You showed me a real life, and at the same time asked me to live a lie. That’s beyond human endurance.”

“Oh, don’t say that,” she burst out, tears rising. “When I’m enduring it!”

Her arms fell limply along the table, her face turned toward him, open and exposed—like a soul laid bare. Archer stood stunned, overwhelmed by what it revealed. “You too… oh, all this time, you too?”

For an answer, she let the tears fall, slow and steady down her cheeks.

Half the room separated them. Neither moved.

Archer felt strangely detached from her presence—until one hand, resting on the table, caught his gaze, just as it had once before in that small house on Twenty-third Street. Now, his mind spun around it, as if it were the edge of a whirlpool. Still, he did not step closer.

He had known love that lived in touch and touch alone. But this—this was deeper, closer than bone. His only fear was to act in a way that might erase the echo of her words. His only thought: he would never again be truly alone.

But then, the weight of it crashed over him—the waste, the ruin. They were close, safe, enclosed. Yet bound by fate, as distant as continents.

“What’s the use,” he said suddenly, “when you’ll go back?”

A silent cry beneath his words: *How can I keep you?*

She sat still, eyes lowered. “Oh—I shan’t go yet.”

“Not yet? Then later? Some time you already know will come?”

At that, she lifted her clear, calm eyes. “I promise you—not as long as you hold out. Not as long as we can look at each other like this.”

He sank into his chair.

Her meaning was plain: *If you take a step, if you act, you’ll drive me back—back to all the horrors you know, all the temptations you suspect.*

He understood perfectly. And the knowledge anchored him to his seat, in a kind of hushed reverence.

“What a life for you,” he groaned.

“Oh—as long as it’s part of yours.”

“And mine, part of yours?”

She nodded.

“And that’s all—everything—between us?”

“Well,” she said softly, “isn’t it?”

At that, he rose, forgetting everything but the sweetness of her face.

She stood too—not to flee, not to rush to him, but quietly, as if the hardest part was done, and she only waited.

As he approached, her hands reached out—not to stop him, but to guide him. They fell into his, her arms extended but not stiff, holding him at a distance just enough to let her face speak.

They stood that way—perhaps minutes, perhaps longer. Long enough for her silence to carry all she meant, long enough for him to feel that only one truth mattered: he must not destroy this moment. He must leave their future in her hands, trusting only that she would hold on.

“Don’t—don’t be unhappy,” she whispered, voice breaking, as she drew her hands away.

“You won’t go back,” he said, as if it were the only possibility he could not endure.

“I won’t go back,” she replied.

Then she turned, opened the door, and led him into the public dining room.

The schoolteachers were gathering their things, preparing to scatter toward the wharf. Across the beach, the white steamboat rested at the pier. Over the sunlit water, Boston rose in a haze, golden and distant.
