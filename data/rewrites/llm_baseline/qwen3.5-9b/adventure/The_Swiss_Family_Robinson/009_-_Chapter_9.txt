The tempest finally subsided. The sun pierced through the fractured clouds with brilliant rays, the relentless rain ceased, and spring had arrived. No liberated prisoner could have felt a greater surge of joy than we did as we emerged from our winter shelter. Our eyes drank in the refreshing verdancy surrounding us, our ears were filled with the cheerful songs of thousands of birds, and our lungs expanded with the pure, balmy air of the new season.

Our plantations were flourishing with vigorous life; the seeds we had sown were pushing through the moist earth, signaling that all of nature was being revitalized.

However, our immediate concern was our nest. Torn apart by the fierce winds and filled with debris, it looked thoroughly dilapidated. We labored tirelessly, and within a few days, it was restored to habitability. My wife earnestly requested that I begin teaching her the art of flax processing. Consequently, I constructed a drying oven as soon as possible and prepared it for her use. After overcoming several challenges, I also fashioned a beetle reel and a spinning wheel. Soon, she and Franz were hard at work, with the little boy diligently reeling off the thread his mother spun.

I was deeply anxious to visit Tentholm, fearing that our precious stores might have suffered greatly during the storm. Fritz and I ventured there. While the damage to Falconhurst was significant, the scene awaiting us at Tentholm was catastrophic. The tent had been blown flat onto the ground, its canvas shredded into rags, provisions soaked beyond salvage, and two casks of gunpowder completely destroyed. We immediately spread whatever items we could save out in the sun to dry.

Miraculously, the pinnace remained safe, but our faithful tub-boat had been dashed into pieces. The irreparable nature of this loss compelled me to resolve on constructing safer, more stable quarters before the onset of the next rainy season. Fritz suggested hollowing out a cave in the rock. Although the difficulties of such an undertaking seemed nearly insurmountable, I determined to attempt it. Perhaps we could not carve out a cavern large enough to serve as a main room, but we might at least excavate a cellar to store our most valuable and perishable goods.

Several days later, we departed Falconhurst with a cart loaded with spades, hammers, chisels, pickaxes, and crowbars, commencing our project. On the smooth face of the perpendicular cliff, I outlined the dimensions of the proposed entrance in chalk. With our minds focused entirely on success, we began to batter away at the stone.

Six days of ceaseless, arduous toil yielded little progress; I doubted whether the resulting hole would even provide satisfactory shelter for Master Knips. Yet, we did not despair. Presently, we were rewarded by encountering softer, more yielding rock strata. Our work accelerated, and our spirits lifted considerably.

On the tenth day, as we delivered heavy blows to the stone, Jack, working diligently with a hammer and crowbar, shouted triumphantly: "Gone, Father! Fritz, my bar has gone through the mountain!"

"Run around and retrieve it," laughed Fritz. "Perhaps it has dropped into Europe—you must not lose a good crowbar."

"But seriously, it *has* gone through! It penetrated right through the rock; I heard it crash down inside. Oh, do come and see!" he cried excitedly.

We rushed to his side. I thrust the handle of my hammer into the hole he indicated; it encountered no resistance whatsoever. I could rotate it freely in any direction. Fritz handed me a long pole, and I tested the depth. I felt nothing but emptiness. A thin wall, it seemed, was all that stood between us and a vast cavern.

With shouts of joy, the boys hammered vigorously at the remaining rock. Piece by piece, fragments fell away, and soon the opening was wide enough for entry. I stepped near the aperture, intending to conduct a further examination, when a sudden rush of poisonous air rendered me dizzy. Shouting to my sons to step back, I leaned against the solid rock.

Regaining my composure, I explained the grave danger of entering any cavern where air had stagnated for a prolonged period. "Unless air is constantly renewed, it becomes vitiated," I warned, "and fatal to anyone who breathes it. The safest method to restore it is to subject it to fire; throwing a few handfuls of blazing hay into this hole may, if the space is small enough, sufficiently purify the internal air to permit safe entry."

We attempted the experiment. However, the flame was extinguished the instant it entered. Even bundles of blazing grass produced no change.

*I observed that we required a more effective remedy. I dispatched the boys to fetch a chest of signal rockets we had salvaged from the wreck. We launched dozens of these fiery serpents, which whizzed inward and vanished at what appeared to be a great distance. Some soared like radiant meteors, circling the chamber, illuminating the massive circumference and revealing, as if by a magician's wand, a sparkling, glittering roof. They resembled avenging dragons driving a foul, malignant fiend out of a beautiful palace.*

After waiting briefly following these experiments, I once again threw in lighted hay. This time, it burned clearly; the air was purified.

Fritz and I widened the entrance, while Jack, mounting his buffalo, thundered away toward Falconhurst to deliver the astonishing news to his mother.

The impact of Jack's enthusiastic report on those at home was profound. Almost immediately, the timbers of the bridge resonated under the swift, heavy tread of his horse. He was swiftly followed by the rest of our party in the cart.

Everyone was in a state of high excitement. Jack had stowed every candle he could find into the cart, and now, lighting them, we shouldered our arms and entered. I led the way, probing the ground ahead with a long pole to ensure we did not accidentally fall into any hidden chasms.

In silence, we marched—my wife, the boys, and even the dogs appearing awestruck by the grandeur and beauty of the scene. We had entered a grotto of diamonds—a vast cave of glittering crystal. The candles cast a golden light upon the walls, bright as the stars of heaven, while great crystal pillars rose from the floor like mighty trees, their branches mingling high above us and drooping in hundreds of stalactites that sparkled with every color of the rainbow.

The floor of this magnificent palace consisted of hard, dry sand, so dry that I instantly realized we could safely reside there without fear of dampness.

Judging by the brilliance of the crystals surrounding us, I suspected their composition. I tasted a fragment. Without a doubt, this was a cavern of rock-salt—an unlimited supply of the finest, purest salt imaginable!

Yet, one detail marred my total delight: large crystals were scattered here and there, having detached from the roof and fallen to the ground. If this were to recur, it would place us in constant peril. Examining the masses, I discovered they had all been recently separated, leading me to conclude that the concussive force of the air generated by the rockets had caused their fall. To verify that no other pieces were tottering above us, we discharged our guns from the entrance and observed the result.

Nothing else fell. Our magnificent abode was secure. We returned to Falconhurst with minds brimming with wonder at our new discovery and eager plans for utilizing it to the fullest extent.

Now, conversation centered exclusively on the new house: its arrangement, its furnishings. The safety and comfort of Falconhurst, which had previously seemed paramount, now dwindled in our estimation to insignificance. We resolved to maintain it merely as a summer residence, while transforming our cave into a winter home and an impregnable castle.

Our attention became wholly absorbed by this new dwelling. We hewed a row of windows into the rock, fitting the casements we had retrieved from the officers' cabins. We also installed the door from Falconhurst into the newly created aperture. Regarding the opening in the trunk of the tree, I decided to conceal it with bark, rendering it less likely to attract the attention of wild beasts or savages should they approach during our absence.

Inside, we divided the cave into four distinct sections: at the front, a large compartment accessible via the door, subdivided into sitting, dining, and sleeping areas; to the right, a division housing our kitchen and workshop; to the left, our stables; and behind all these, in the dark recess of the cave, lay our storehouse and powder magazine.

Having endured one rainy season, we understood its discomforts intimately and devised numerous practical arrangements for our new home. Determined never to be "smoke-dried" again, we constructed a properly built fireplace and chimney. Our stable arrangements were improved, and ample space was reserved in the workshop to accommodate even the most extensive operations.

Our frequent visits to Tentholm revealed several unforeseen advantages. Numerous splendid turtles frequently came ashore to deposit their eggs in the sand, providing us with sumptuous meals. When multiple turtles appeared simultaneously, we blocked their retreat to the sea, turned them onto their backs, and fastened them to stakes driven close to the water's edge using cords threaded through holes in their shells. Thus, we maintained a continuous supply of fresh turtles; remarkably, these animals thrived in captivity, appearing in as good condition after several weeks as they would have been if freshly caught. Lobsters, crabs, and mussels also abounded on the shore.
