"And I cannot touch her face,
And I cannot touch her hair,
And I kneel to empty shadows—
Just memories of her grace;
And her voice sings in the winds,
And in the sobs of dawn,
And among the flowers at night,
And from the brooks at sunrise,
And from the sea at sunset,
And I answer with vain callings..."

It was the joy of the sunset that brought us to speech. I had wandered far from my home, walking alone, pausing often to watch the battlements of evening rise like ancient fortresses against the sky, and to feel the quiet hush of dusk settle over the world.

The last time I stopped, I was lost in a deep reverence for the coming night—so much so that I let out a soft laugh, standing there in solitude beneath the dimming light. And then, from the trees lining the road to my right, came an answer: *"And thou also!"*—a voice full of understanding, as if some hidden spirit had mirrored my own joy. I laughed again, half-doubting it was real, wondering if it were only a dream or a phantom tuned to my mood.

But then she spoke—clearly, directly—calling me by name. When I stepped to the side of the road to see her, I recognized her instantly: Lady Mirdath the Beautiful, famed across Kent for her grace and elegance, and neighbor to me, since her guardian’s lands adjoined mine. Yet until that moment, we had never met. I had been away often, absorbed in study and training, and though rumors spoke of her, I had known little more than her name—and even that, only in passing.

Now, I stood with my hat in hand, bowing as best I could, while peering through the twilight at this radiant figure. Rumor had not done justice to her beauty. She stood before me, laughing lightly, claiming kinship as cousin—a truth now revealed, once I remembered our shared bloodline. She called me by my boyhood name, and welcomed me to call her simply Mirdath, no title, no formality—just as she was.

She invited me through a gap in the hedge, a secret path she used when dressed as a village maid for country revels. I passed through, and found myself beside her. She seemed tall, but I towered over her—by a head and more. She asked me to walk with her to the Hall, to meet her guardian, Sir Alfred Jarles, and to apologize for my long neglect in visiting. Her eyes sparkled with mischief as she teased me gently for my absence.

But then she grew still. She raised a finger, signaling silence—something moved in the woods. I heard it too: rustling leaves, then the sharp crack of a twig snapping in the quiet.

Three men burst from the trees, rushing toward us. I shouted a warning, stepping between them and Mirdath, shielding her with one arm while gripping my oak staff with the other. They made no sound, just charged forward. I saw the glint of knives. Without hesitation, I struck—swift and sure. My staff drove into the first man’s chest; he fell like a stone. I swung hard at the second’s head, cracking his skull. The third I met with a fist—no need for a second blow. He crumpled to the ground.

Before the fight had truly begun, it was over. I smiled faintly, proud and unshaken, knowing the look of astonishment on Mirdath’s face as she watched me in the fading light.

Then—three great boar-hounds burst from the woods, summoned by a silver whistle she blew. She struggled to hold them back, and I turned to drive them off the fallen men, lest they tear them apart. Soon after, shouts echoed, lanterns flared, and footmen arrived with cudgels and torches. They hesitated—unsure whether to attack or defend me. But when they saw the men down and recognized my name, they held their distance. Still, Mirdath showed no fear—only a deeper warmth, a new closeness.

The servants asked what should be done with the thieves. I left it to them, giving them silver. Justice was swift—I heard their cries long after we left.

At the Hall, Mirdath led me to her guardian, Sir Alfred Jarles—an old man of noble bearing whom I knew slightly. She praised me warmly, teasingly, and he thanked me with honor and grace. From that moment, I was welcomed as a friend.

That evening, I dined with them, then walked again with Mirdath through the gardens. She was more open than any woman I’d ever known—gentle, familiar, as though we had always belonged together. I felt the same—like we shared a silent language, a hidden history. We discovered common dreams, shared fancies, and found delight in each other’s thoughts.

One thing especially captivated her: my strength. After the fight, she asked plainly if I were truly strong. I laughed, proud and young. Then she took my arm, testing it—her fingers tightening, her breath catching as she felt the power beneath my skin. She released me suddenly, gasping in surprise. From then on, she walked beside me quietly, thoughtful, never straying far.

And I, in turn, marveled at her beauty—especially under candlelight, where every feature shone with quiet fire.

In the days that followed, we found deeper joys. She loved the mystery of twilight, the magic of night, the tender glow of dawn. One evening, as we strolled through the park, she murmured—half to herself—that it was an *elves-night*. She stopped herself, as if afraid I wouldn’t understand. But I did. I replied calmly, “The Towers of Sleep will rise tonight. I feel it in my bones—the night for the Giant’s Tomb, or the Tree with the Painted Head…” I paused. She gripped my arm—her hand trembling. “Say on,” she whispered, breathless. “Say on.”

I told her I meant the Moon Garden—an old dream of mine. At that, she gasped, pulled me to a halt, and questioned me fiercely. I answered just as earnestly. For the first time, I sensed something profound: she knew these places too. She confessed she believed she was the only one who dreamed of them. Now, to find someone else who had journeyed through those same dreamlands—this was wonder beyond words.

She admitted she had felt drawn to me that night, seeing me pause on the road. Though she’d learned of our kinship before—having seen me ride past often—she had grown annoyed that I paid no heed to *Lady Mirdath the Beautiful*. But I had been lost in thought, unaware of her presence. If only I had met her sooner…

Still, I was stirred—not by pride, but by awe. Our dreams overlapped, yet not entirely. Some things I knew she didn’t; others she knew I couldn’t grasp. Yet again and again, one would speak of a place or feeling, and the other would finish the tale—filling in the gaps with perfect understanding. In that way, hour by hour, we aged together in friendship, growing closer with every word.

Time slipped away unnoticed—until sudden shouts and barking dogs broke the silence. Lanterns flickered in the dark. I realized we had wandered so long, so deeply in conversation, that her guardian, uneasy after the attack, had sent men to search. We laughed—she with a soft, strange amusement—then turned homeward.

The dogs found us first, leaping around me, friendly now. The men returned to tell Sir Jarles all was well.

That was how we met. That was how our bond began—and how my love for Mirdath the Beautiful was born.

From then on, every evening I walked the quiet road from my estate to hers. Always, I entered through the hedge-gap. Often, I found Mirdath wandering in the woods—always with her boar-hounds, as I had begged for her safety. She seemed to enjoy pleasing me, yet often defied me, testing how far she could go before I turned away.

Once, approaching the gap, I saw two country maids emerge from the woods. I barely glanced at them—until one curtseyed with such unnatural grace. I paused. The taller one… could it be her?

I stepped forward, curious. When I asked her name, she simpered and bowed again. I wasn’t sure—but I followed.

They moved quickly, as if wary of me. They reached the village green, where a torchlit dance was underway, music drifting on the air, ale flowing freely. The two joined the dance, dancing only with each other, avoiding the flames.

Now I was certain: it was Mirdath, disguised. I waited until they danced near me, then stepped forward boldly. “A dance?” I asked.

She smiled coyly. “Promised.” And immediately, she took the hand of a large, clumsy farmer and twirled him about. Poor girl—she nearly tripped under his heavy steps. I watched, amused and irritated.

When the dance ended, I approached. “Mirdath,” I whispered, naming her.

She turned away, stamped her foot, and returned to the farmer. He escorted her partway. His companion followed. But as they left the torchlight, the rough lads grabbed the women’s waists, uncaring of who they touched.

Mirdath could endure no more. She screamed in terror, striking the man who held her—hard enough to make him recoil, swearing. But he came back, grabbing her again, forcing a kiss. She fought wildly, beating his face with her hands. Then—she cried out my name.

I lunged forward, seized the man, and struck him once—hard enough to leave a memory, but not harm. I threw him aside. The second fled, hearing my name. Word of my strength had spread.

I caught Mirdath by the shoulders, shook her sharply in anger. Then I sent her maid onward—she had no orders to stay. We walked in silence to the gap. She walked close, almost as if she enjoyed being near me.

I led her through, to the Hall. She bid me goodnight at a side door, holding the key. Her voice was soft, quiet—almost reluctant to leave.

But the next day, she mocked me playfully. So, when dusk came, I confronted her alone. “Why do you always defy me?” I asked. “I ache to be near you. Instead, you deny me.”

Her tone softened. She understood. She brought out her harp and played old songs—melodies from our childhood. That night, she walked me to the gap, her dogs trailing behind. I followed her silently, watching until she was safely inside. One dog ran back to me, nosing me affectionately. I sent it away—she never knew I was there.

And as she walked home, singing softly, I wondered: did she love me? Or was it only kindness?

The next evening, I arrived early. There stood Mirdath—talking to a man in fine court clothes. He blocked the gap, staring at me with insolent eyes. I reached out, lifted him aside.

Mirdath turned on me—bitter, cutting. She called me crude, uncouth, brutal—before this stranger. I was stunned. A true lover would never shame her true cousin so.

There was truth in her words—but the man could have shown more respect. And she had no right to humiliate me, her friend, before a stranger. I bowed low to her, then offered a small apology to the man. I had been better served showing more courtesy.

I turned and walked away.

Twenty miles I traveled before reaching home. That night, I was consumed—deadly in love, heartbroken. For weeks, I walked elsewhere. But at the end of the week, I returned to the old path—hoping only to glimpse her.

And I did.

She stood near the edge of the wood, arm linked with the courtier. I saw it clearly: lovers. She had no brothers, no suitors—only him.

When she saw me, she pulled away, flushed, bowed stiffly. I bowed in return—still young, still respectful—and walked on. My heart was dead.

As I passed, I saw her lover return to her, arm around her again. They watched me go—stiff, desperate. I did not look back.

For a month, I stayed away. Love burned in me, pride wounded. No justice had been done. But slowly, pain shaped me. Love and sorrow carved a gentler man from the one I had been.

At the end of that time, I began walking past the gap again—though I never saw her. Once, one of her dogs came out of the woods, nosed at me, then vanished. I waited. But she did not come.

Two weeks passed—lonely, aching. I decided: I would go through the gap, reach the gardens, and perhaps see her.

That evening, I went. Through the gap. Into the gardens. And there—lanterns, torches, laughter. A feast. People danced in colorful dress. I feared it was her wedding. But no—she had turned twenty-one. This was her coming-of-age celebration.

The scene was bright and lively—yet I felt only loneliness. The lawn was lit with lamps, arbors woven from leaves, tables laden with food and silver. The dance never ceased.

Then—Mirdath stepped from the crowd, lovely in her gown, yet pale beneath the lights. She sat. Instantly, a dozen young noblemen gathered around her, jesting, vying for her attention. She was radiant—but distant. Her gaze drifted beyond them. I understood: her lover was not there. Perhaps recalled to court.

I watched the men—burning with jealousy. I could have torn her from them, dragged her into the woods as we once did. But it would mean nothing. For I saw plainly: her heart belonged to the small man of the Court.

And so I stood in the shadows, a ghost among the light, watching the woman I loved—knowing she was lost to me.
