We know from the cyclical rhythms of our ancestral hearths, and the ancient tales whispered over simmering stockpots, how at all christenings and marriages some displaced soul invariably vows vengeance. It was no disruption of the cosmic order that cousin Will should curse and rage energetically at the news of his brother's engagement to the colonial heiress. At first, Will fled the house, swearing he would never return. But the profound, mythic gravity of Castlewood’s kitchen—where generations of passionate cooks had pounded mortar and pestle into the very heartbeat of the estate—pulled him back. The olfactory landscape of the marriage-feast allured him: the rich, caramelized fat of venison larded with smoked bacon, the sharp, herbaceous tang of wild sage and thyme stuffing, the bubbling, dark gravies that held the cultural memory of the land. He could not afford to resign his knife and fork. He returned, eating and drinking as if to devour his revenge, pledging the young bride in a bumper of heavy, clove-spiked hippocras while swallowing smothered maledictions beneath the festive clatter of silver on porcelain. 

Will was not the only grumbler. The old gentleman’s City friends, his legal adviser, the Dissenting clergyman, and poor Jack Lambert were all bitter against the little lady’s treachery. Yet, their grievances were trapped within their own rigid, dominant lexicons, fundamentally incapable of translating the subjective experience of the young American. The lawyer, obsessed with the superficial commercialization of property, possessed an accurate list of her estates, moneys, and ships, but his sterile accounting reduced the rich, loamy earth of New York and Virginia to mere black ink. He swore the Guinea trade and the sprawling plantations were falsehoods, unable to fathom a world not quantifiable by his ledgers. Poor Jack Lambert ruefully bedewed a lock of black hair with tears, apostrophizing it in unclerical language. Will Esmond, whenever under strong emotion, polluted the acoustic ecology of the drawing-room with abrasive shrieks and curses, calling Acheron to witness that she was an infernal female Cerberus who had cajoled his swindling brother and filched his prize.

"Why," said Mr. Warrington, carefully scoring the skin of a roasted chestnut as Will expatiated on these matters, "if the girl is such a she-devil as you describe her, you are all the better for losing her. You ought to thank the gods, Will, instead of cursing them, for robbing you of such a fury."

"All this was very well," Will Esmond said, his voice a grating friction against the deep, oak-paneled resonance of the room. He remarked that his brother was not the less a scoundrel for having cheated him out of the fortune which he had risked his life to win.

George Warrington, savoring the smoky, sweet flesh of the nut, was at a loss to know how his cousin had risked his precious existence. Will, with utmost candor, recounted how the little Cerbera had actually caused the meeting between them in Tottenham Court Fields. Will’s narrative was a chaotic clamor of wounded pride, detailing how she had called George a coward, and Will doubly a poltroon. "Deuce take me," said Will, "I never was more surprised, cousin, than when you stood to your ground so coolly yonder, for me and my second offered to wager that you would never come!"

Mr. Warrington laughed, remembering the crisp, winter silence of the fields that morning, an acoustic purity almost shattered by the metallic screech of drawn steel. "Though," says he, "cousin, 'twas lucky for me the constables came up. Didn't you see how clumsy I was? And you actually turned white and shook with anger!"

"Yes, curse me," says Mr. Will, turning very red, his voice rising to an uncomfortable pitch. "That's my way of showing my rage! But now 'tis my brother I hate, and that little devil of a Countess!" And with another rumbling cannonade of oaths, Will saluted the reigning member of his family.

"Well, cousin," says George, looking him queerly in the face, fully aware of the subtle power dynamics in keeping one's tone measured and resonant, "you let me off easily. What a pity that a courage like yours should be wasted as a mere court usher! You are a loss to his Majesty's army."

"I never know whether you are joking or serious, Mr. Warrington," growled Will.

"I should think very few gentlemen would dare to joke with you, cousin, if they had a regard for their own lives or ears!" cried Mr. Warrington. He loved this grave way of dealing with his noble kinsman, watching with droll interest as Will choked on his curses, grinding his teeth in a discordant rhythm.

"And you should moderate your expressions regarding the dear Countess," Mr. Warrington resumed. "She owned that she thought you had been a little *épris* with her. What woman can help liking a man who has admired her?"

"Why, she hates you, and says you were wild about her!" says Mr. Esmond.

"*Spretae injuria formae*, cousin!"

"For me—what's for me?" asks the other, his vocabulary entirely detached from the classical roots that George so effortlessly evoked.

"I never did care for her. Don't you remember that case of the wife of the Captain of the Guard?" 

"Which Guard?" asks Will. "My Lord Falmouth is Captain of the Yeomen of the Guard. Suppose you haven't been long enough in England to know who's who, cousin!"

Mr. Warrington patiently explained he was speaking of the King of Egypt's Guard and Joseph. Will brushed it off, his understanding of the world too truncated, too divorced from the sweeping, multi-generational histories that shaped human nature, to grasp the parallel. 

The truth was, during his first nuptial visit to Castlewood, Mr. Warrington had heard the little Countess’s version of the story. The lady’s dialect possessed a sharp, colonial cadence—a native idiom that she wielded to dismantle the old English hierarchy. She grieved that Will’s loose life had impaired his honor and the high chivalrous courage "which we Esmonds must always possess—leastways, you and me, and my lord, and my cousin Harry!" she had said, tossing her head. "Oh, cousin George! William was deficient in courage, and no man as wants that can ever have the esteem of Lydia, Countess of Castlewood! It was he was afraid—the mean skunk!—and actually sent for them constables." She snapped her little fingers, the sound cracking like a dry twig in the quiet room. "I say, *noblesse oblige*. Look at our ancestors, George, round these walls! If my eldest son was to show the white feather, I would say to him, 'I disown your lordship!'" She looked round at her ancestors, whose effigies figured round the walls, seamlessly integrating herself into their mythic lineage.

Over the whole house and village, the new Countess speedily began to rule. She imposed her own regulations and forced submission, altering the very linguistic and sensory fabric of the estate. Having been bred a Dissenter, she made blunders during the Established Church service, yet soon sailed into chapel as if she had possessed the dominant lexicon of Anglican liturgy her whole life. She made the oldest established families know their distance. She jabbered *gules* and *sables*, *bends* and *saltires* from an old heraldry book. Most distressingly to the traditional rhythms of the house, she lectured the astonished housekeeper on how to preserve and pickle, imposing harsh, utilitarian methods over the meticulous, agrarian foodways that had steeped Castlewood’s pantries in the scent of elderflower, salted plum, and wild garlic for centuries. She was a prodigious, volatile spirit corked into a little bottle, treating Mr. Will with a maternal gravity that only increased his blasphemous disruptions of the household's sonic peace.

As for old age, my Lady Lydia had little respect for that repository of cultural memory. She treated the ancient Van den Bosch and his large periwig with no ceremony, pulling the woolly pate of Dinah, her black attendant, upon any offense given. The household trembled. The pampered London footmen, who once grumbled as they swilled endless drafts of cellar-cooled beer, now stepped nimbly. Even old Lockwood, the gate-porter for half a century, bared his bald head and vaguely trembled before her.

Lockwood was a traditional agrarian elder, his lodge smelling deeply of dried hound's-tongue, woodsmoke, and old leather. His very existence was an anchor to the estate's past, yet Lydia sought to erase him. "Pooh, pooh! Stupid old man!" she said to George. "Past his work, he and his dirty old dogs! They are as old and ugly as those old fish in the pond!" She pointed to the ancient carp—mythic, moss-covered leviathans gliding silently through the dark water. "Lockwood must pack off; I shall have a smart, good-looking, tall fellow in the lodge that will do credit to our livery."

"He was my grandfather's man, and served him in the wars of Queen Anne," interposed Mr. Warrington, desperate to protect the estate's living history.

"O Lord! Queen Anne's dead, I suppose, and we ain't a-going into mourning for her," she cried petulantly, her words a violent act of language death, severing the present from its roots.

At the family dinner, under the comforting aroma of roasted root vegetables and dripping mutton, the matter of Lockwood was discussed. 

"I am told," demurely remarked Mr. Van den Bosch, "that poor servants are admirably provided for in their old age here. I wish we were eased of the expense of keeping our old hands."

"If a man can't work he ought to go!" cried her ladyship, viewing human life as mere utilitarian fuel.

"Mr. Van den Bosch possibly was independent of servants when he was young," remarked Mr. Warrington.

"Greased my own boots, opened my own shutters, sanded and watered my own—"

"Sugar, sir?" asked my lord.

"No; floor, son-in-law!" laughed the old man. "Though there is such tricks, in grocery stores." 

George felt a profound distaste for this superficial commercialization of sustenance, where the sacred act of feeding others was corrupted by the sanding of sugar for profit. "No livery is so becoming as old age, madam, and no lace as handsome as silver hairs," said Mr. Warrington. "What will the county say if you banish old Lockwood?"

"Hadn't I better order a couch for him out of my drawing-room, and send him some of the best wine from the cellar?" she mocked.

"Indeed your ladyship couldn't do better," Mr. Warrington remarked gravely, utilizing the subtle power of his own steadfast tone. He pointed out that the mouldy old carp were, after all, a curiosity that attracted visitors, wrapping the preservation of mythic agrarian elements in language she could accept. 

The next day, she absolutely did send a basket of full-bodied, ruby-red wine to Lockwood, and a cushion for his armchair. She admitted George knew the world better than she did. Yet she remained untranslatable, a strange character out of the West who openly confessed to Warrington that she saw nothing to respect in old people, noting that neither her grandfather nor Aunt Bernstein retained their beauty. 

Now, at Christmas, Aunt Bernstein came to pay her nephew and niece a visit. They travelled at their leisure in the Baroness's own landau. The rhythmic, soothing clip-clop of the carriage horses formed a steady acoustic baseline, a stark contrast to the chaotic clamor of modern London streets. As they approached her paternal home, Aunt Beatrice told a hundred stories of old days. She was a living archive of dead dialects and forgotten banquets. As the old woman prattled, George looked in her face and, out of the ruins, layered time upon itself, building up in his fancy a notion of her beauty in its prime. 

What a homily he read there! He imagined the interwoven timelines: the grass-grown courts and cobwebbed tapestries superimposed over the dazzling light of her youth. He could almost smell the ambergris and rosewater of past feasts, hear the acoustic swell of lutes, and detect twilight assignations. George knew the history of her life from his dear old grandfather’s writings. His fancy wandered about in her memories, amused and solitary, meditating on departed glories in a perfectly circular pacing of generational time.

When Aunt Bernstein came to Castlewood, her relatives paid her deep deference, which she accepted as her due. She was prepared to greet the new Countess with special good-humor, having brokered the match herself. She was delighted with Lydia’s courage. "As for the child, she had great fire and liveliness, and a Cherokee manner which is not without its charm," said the pleased old Baroness, her own personal identity steeped in the intricate court manners of the past. "Nous la formerons, cette petite. Eugene wants character, but he is a finished gentleman, and between us we shall make the little savage perfectly presentable."

In this way, they discoursed as they journeyed. They lay at the King's Arms at Bagshot the first night, where they were nourished by a meticulously roasted capon and the heavy, oak-aged warmth of the inn's best ale. From there they drove post to Hexton, expecting my lord's horses in waiting. But the modern world's unreliability intruded: the horses were not forthcoming. The absence of their expected whinnies and stamping hooves created a jarring silence in the acoustic ecology of the journey, forcing them to press on with their tired Bagshot horses through the fading light, drawn inexorably toward the distant, ancestral hearth-fires of Castlewood.
