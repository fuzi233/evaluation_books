"To tell you the truth," said the young stranger to Nello, as they moved slightly apart from the tangled mass of carts and mules, "I am not sorry to be handed over by that patron of mine to one who speaks with a less barbarous accent and conducts a less enigmatic trade. Is it common among you Florentines for a wandering dealer in shattered glass and rags to boast of a shop where he sells lutes and swords?"

"Common? By no means. Our Bratti is no ordinary man. He possesses a theory and lives strictly by it—a feat I cannot claim for any philosopher I have the honor of shaving," replied Nello. His talkativeness was like an overfull bottle; it could never dispense merely a small draught. "Bratti intends to extract the maximum possible pleasure—that is to say, the hardest possible bargaining—from this life, concluding it with a deal for the smoothest passage through purgatory by handing his winnings over to the Holy Church once the game ends. He has drawn up his will to this precise effect on the cheapest terms a notary could be found. Yet I have often told him, 'Bratti, your bargain is a limp one, and you stand on the lame side of it. Does it not sadden you to gaze upon the paintings of Paradise? You will never be able there to haggle over rags and rusty nails; the saints and angels require neither pins nor tinder. Save perhaps with Saint Bartholomew, who carries his skin about in an inconvenient manner, I see no chance of your striking a deal for second-hand clothing.' But God forgive me," Nello added, shifting his tone and crossing himself, "such light chatter ill becomes a morning when Lorenzo lies dead and the Muses tear out their hair—a thought always painful to a barber. And you yourself, Messere, are likely under a cloud; when a man of your speech and bearing settles for such poor lodging, it argues some misfortune has befallen him."

"What Lorenzo is the one whose death you speak of?" asked the stranger, appearing to dwell with too anxious an interest on this point to have noticed the indirect inquiry that followed.

"What Lorenzo? There is but one Lorenzo, I imagine, whose passing could throw the Mercato into an uproar, make the lantern of the Duomo leap in desperation, and force the lions of the Republic to devour one another immediately. I mean Lorenzo de' Medici, the Pericles of our Athens—if I may draw such a comparison in the hearing of a Greek."

"Why not?" said the other, laughing. "For I doubt whether Athens, even in the days of Pericles, could have produced so learned a barber."

"Yes, yes; I thought I could not be mistaken," said the rapid Nello. "Otherwise, I have shaved the venerable Demetrio Calcondila to little purpose. But pardon me, I am lost in wonder: your Italian is better than his, though he has lived in Italy for forty years—even better than that of the accomplished Marullo, who may be said to have wed the Italic Muse in more senses than one, having married our learned and lovely Alessandra Scala."

"It will lighten your wonder to know that I come of Greek stock planted in Italian soil far longer than the mulberry trees which have taken so kindly to it. I was born at Bari, and my—I mean, I was raised by an Italian. In fact, I am a Greek, very much as your peaches are Persian. The Greek dye was subdued in me, I suppose, until I was dipped anew by long residence and much travel in the land of gods and heroes. And to confess something of my private affairs to you, this same Greek dye, along with a few ancient gems I carry about me, is the only fortune shipwreck has left me. But—when the towers fall, you know it is an ill business for the small nest-builders—the death of your Pericles makes me wish I had turned my steps toward Rome, as I should have done but for a fallacious Minerva in the shape of an Augustinian monk. 'At Rome,' he said, 'you will be lost in a crowd of hungry scholars; but at Florence, every corner is penetrated by the sunshine of Lorenzo's patronage: Florence is the best market in Italy for such commodities as yours.'"

"_Gnaffè_, and so it will remain, I hope," said Nello. "Lorenzo was not the sole patron and judge of learning in our city—heaven forbid! Because he was a large melon, every other Florentine is not a pumpkin, I suppose. Have we not Bernardo Rucellai, and Alamanno Rinuccini, and plenty more? And if you wish to be informed on such matters, I, Nello, am your man. It seems a thousand years until I can serve a *bel erudito* like yourself. And first, in the matter of your hair. That beard, my fine young man, must be parted with, were it as dear to you as the nymph of your dreams. Here at Florence, we dislike to see a man with his nose projecting over a cascade of hair. But remember, you will have crossed the Rubicon once you have been shaven: if you repent and allow your beard to grow after it has acquired stoutness through a struggle with the razor, your mouth will by-and-by show no longer what Messer Angelo calls the divine prerogative of lips, but will appear like a dark cavern fringed with horrent brambles."

"That is a terrible prophecy," said the Greek, "especially if your Florentine maidens are many of them as pretty as the little Tessa I stole a kiss from this morning."

"Tessa? She is a rough-handed peasant girl: you will rise into the favor of ladies who bring no scent of the mule-stables with them. But to that end, you must not have the air of a *sgherro*, or a man of evil repute: you must look like a courtier, and a scholar of the more polished sort, such as our Pietro Crinito—like one who sins among well-bred, well-fed people, and not one who sucks down vile *vino di sotto* in a chance tavern."

"With all my heart," said the stranger. "If the Florentine Graces demand it, I am willing to give up this small matter of my beard, but—"

"Yes, yes," interrupted Nello. "I know what you would say. It is the *bella zazzera*—the hyacinthine locks, you do not choose to part with; and there is no need. Just a little pruning—ecco!—and you will look not unlike the illustrious Prince Pico di Mirandola in his prime. And here we are in good time in the Piazza San Giovanni, and at the door of my shop. But you are pausing, I see: naturally, you want to look at our wonder of the world, our Duomo, our Santa Maria del Fiore. Well, well, a mere glance; but I beseech you to leave a closer survey until you have been shaved: I am quivering with the inspiration of my art even to the very edge of my razor. Ah, then, come round this way."

The mercurial barber seized the arm of the stranger and led him to a point on the south side of the piazza from which he could see at once the huge dark shell of the cupola, the slender soaring grace of Giotto's campanile, and the quaint octagon of San Giovanni in front of them, displaying its unique gates of storied bronze, which still bore the somewhat dimmed glory of their original gilding. The inlaid marbles were then fresher in their pink, white, and purple than they are now, when the winters of four centuries have turned their white to the rich ochre of well-mellowed meerschaum; the façade of the cathedral did not stand ignominious in faded stucco, but bore upon it the magnificent promise of the half-completed marble inlaying and statued niches, which Giotto had devised a hundred and fifty years before; and as the campanile, in all its harmonious variety of color and form, led the eyes upward, high into the clear air of this April morning, it seemed a prophetic symbol, telling that human life must somehow and sometime shape itself into accord with that pure aspiring beauty.

But this was not the impression it appeared to produce on the Greek. His eyes were irresistibly led upward, yet as he stood with his arms folded and his curls falling backward, there was a slight touch of scorn on his lip, and when his eyes fell again they glanced around with a scanning coolness that was rather piquing to Nello's Florentine spirit.

"Well, my fine young man," he said, with some impatience, "you seem to make as little of our Cathedral as if you were the Angel Gabriel come straight from Paradise.
