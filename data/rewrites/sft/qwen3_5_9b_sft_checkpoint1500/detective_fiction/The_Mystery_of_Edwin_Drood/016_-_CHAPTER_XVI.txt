When John Jasper emerged from his paralyzing fit, the room was filled with the gentle, bustling care of Mr. and Mrs. Tope, summoned by his visitor. Sitting rigid in a chair, hands resting heavily upon his knees like stone pillars, Mr. Grewgious watched Jasper's return with a wooden, unblinking stare.

"You've rallied nicely, sir!" cried Mrs. Tope, her voice thick with tears. "You were utterly drained, no wonder!"

"A man," Mr. Grewgious recited, his tone flat and pedagogical, "cannot endure broken rest, cruel torment of mind, and physical exhaustion without being thoroughly worn out."

"I fear I have frightened you?" Jasper murmured, accepting help to an easy chair.

"Not at all, I thank you," Mr. Grewgious replied, the phrase automatic, devoid of warmth.

"You are too considerate."

"Not at all, I thank you," the lawyer echoed again, a hollow echo.

"Take some wine, sir," urged Mrs. Tope, "and the jelly I prepared, which you refused at noon despite my warnings, and since you hadn't breakfasted... and a wing of the roast fowl, which I'm sure has been reheated twenty times if it's been once. It'll be served in five minutes, and this good gentleman will likely stay to ensure you eat."

The "good gentleman" responded with a sharp snort that conveyed nothing, perhaps everything, leaving Mrs. Tope mystified until the clatter of silverware distracted her.

"Do you join me?" Jasper asked as the table was laid.

"I cannot swallow a morsel, I thank you," Grewgious answered.

Jasper devoured the meal and drink with frantic voracity. His haste betrayed a total indifference to flavor; he consumed food solely to fortify his failing spirit against further collapse, a desperate anchor against the abyss. Meanwhile, Mr. Grewgious remained erect, his face a mask of imperturbable politeness, radiating a refusal to engage in discourse. He seemed to declare internally: *I possess no thoughts, observations, or ideas worthy of articulation.*

"When I have finished," Jasper said, pushing away his empty plate, "I find... crumbs of comfort in what you have revealed."

"Do you?" Grewgious returned, his silence speaking volumes.

"After the shock of news regarding my beloved boy—so unexpected, so destructive of every future I had constructed for him; and after time to reflect..." Jasper paused, searching for a glimmer in the gloom. "Is there, perhaps, hope? That realizing the heavy burden of explanation required by this new position, he fled to avoid the awkwardness?"

"Grewgious," Jasper whispered, following the thread eagerly, "if he vanished of his own volition, is the cruelty to her mitigated? Yes. And what of me? If he foresaw my inquiries, if he anticipated the inferences I would draw, then the cruelty to me vanishes! John Jasper, Music Master, disappears!"

Grewgious, after a moment's consideration, could not deny the logic.

"I have harbored terrible distrusts," Jasper confessed, his voice trembling with a fragile, rising hope. "Yet your disclosure, however shocking, kindles a belief. It is reasonable. I begin to think he may have absconded of his own accord and remains alive."

At that precise moment, Mr. Crisparkle entered. Jasper repeated his theory to him. The Minor Canon, whose mind was already prepared to view Edwin's disappearance in a new light due to the boy's sudden exposure to social embarrassment, listened intently. Jasper detailed his prior apprehensions regarding Neville Landless, citing the fury of their first meeting and his own diary entries warning of madness.

"This fairness troubles me," Crisparkle admitted later, reflecting privately. He had suppressed two instances of Neville's violent temper and the flame of jealous passion burning in the youth's breast. While convinced of Neville's innocence, the accumulation of circumstantial evidence weighed heavily. Yet, before him sat a model of integrity. Addressing Mr. Grewgious, Crisparkle testified to Jasper's justice, declaring absolute faith in Edwin's clearance from suspicion, regardless of Neville's fiery nature and romantic delusions concerning Jasper's niece.

Jasper, though pale, clung to the hope. If Edwin vanished by his own wild will, the tragedy ended.

But peace was fleeting. Crisparkle, leaving the conference uneased and troubled for the "prisoner" in his own home, took a memorable night walk. He wandered to Cloisterham Weir, a place he visited often, yet tonight his mind was so preoccupied that he arrived unaware, startled only by the roar of falling water nearby.

"How did I come here?" he wondered. "Why?"

Standing by the weir, he heard the water, but his mind conjured images of airy tongues syllabling names. It was starlight, cold and clear. The tide had been running strongly downward on Christmas Eve, washing away any bodies that might have fallen below. Yet, a strange, haunted air clung to the spot. Reason failed him; no sense detected anything unusual. Still, his hawk-like eyes strained forward. He approached the timbers, peering into the shadows. Nothing moved. Resolute, he vowed to return at dawn.

Sleep eluded him. At sunrise, the frost bit at his cheeks, revealing the world in crisp, minute detail. Surveying the weir, his gaze locked onto a single speck—a corner where something glistened, stationary amidst the moving water.

"It must be there," he thought. Stripping off his coat, he plunged into the icy stream, swimming frantically to the spot. Clinging to the timbers, he retrieved a gold watch, engraved on the back with *E. D.*

Returning to shore, he dove again, emerging with only a shirt-pin stuck in the mud. With these findings, he took Neville Landless to the Mayor. Jasper was summoned. The watch and pin were identified. A frenzy of evil report rose against Neville; he was detained.

Suspicion mounted rapidly. Neville was vindictive, violent, and prone to murder. He had threatened lives repeatedly. He had been brought from London by a philanthropist who believed him to be a danger to the smallest number. He had armed himself on the night of the disappearance and departed early. Traces of blood marred him, and a search warrant revealed his room stripped bare, papers destroyed, possessions rearranged—the hallmarks of a guilty conscience fleeing evidence.

The watch found at the weir, the jeweler insisted, had been wound at two-twenty PM on that afternoon and had never been rewound thereafter. This implied Edwin carried it when he left Jasper's house at midnight and discarded it shortly after. Who else? Neville had been seen wandering the streets in a half-distracted, miserable state. Furthermore, the reconciliation meeting between Edwin and Neville, orchestrated by Crisparkle, suggested Neville's presence was necessary. Yet Edwin had vanished before the meeting concluded.

Interrogations confirmed Edwin's fiancée had explicitly planned to wait for her guardian, Mr. Grewgious, and yet he had disappeared before the gentleman arrived. Every angle pointed to Neville. The suspicion grew so potent that authorities felt compelled to detain him. Search parties combed every inch of the countryside. Nothing was found. No body. No grave.

Eventually, the law demanded release. Neville was set at large. But the verdict of the court, though silent, spoke loudly. The town shunned him. The Dean of Cloisterham, a man of prudent judgment, met with Crisparkle.

"Human justice may err, but it acts by its lights," the Dean stated gently. "Sanctuary is a relic of old times. This young man must depart."

"Neville must leave my house, sir?" Crisparkle asked, despair creeping into his voice.

"The authorities require it," the Dean nodded smoothly. "To deprive him of the advantages of your counsel is a necessity."

"It is lamentable," Crisparkle faltered.

"As you find it," the Dean agreed. "Perfectly. There is no alternative."

"And if it be a necessity—"

"Just so." The Dean glanced around, lowering his voice. "I would not state it generally, Mr. Crisparkle, that he is innocent. Enough of suspicion attaches to him."

Crisparkle bowed, defeated. "It does not become us to be partisans. We clergy keep our hearts warm and our heads cool."

"I hope you do not object to my stating publicly that he will reappear whenever new circumstances arise?" Crisparkle asked, seeking a lifeline.

"Not at all," the Dean smiled, though his eyes remained cool. "Though I hardly think I would state it *emphatically*. Emphasize? No, no. In point of fact, we need do nothing emphatically."

Thus, Minor Canon Row knew Neville Landless no more. The youth vanished, carrying a blight upon his name, forced to wander wherever he could, exiled by the weight of suspicion.

It was not until then that John Jasper silently resumed his place in the choir. Haggard, red-eyed, the sanguine hope that had briefly illuminated his soul had extinguished. All his darkest misgivings returned with renewed force. A day or two later, while unrobing, Jasper produced his Diary.
