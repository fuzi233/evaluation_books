The heavy, metallic silence of the mill in the pre-dawn hours held the residual violence of the night's shattered frames—a mythic severing of iron and wood that felt less like an act of men and more like the inevitable clash of two warring eras. Robert Moore and his overlooker, Joe Scott, had spent the dark hours in the recesses of the counting-houses, breathing in the cold, oily dust of dormant machinery. 

When Moore awoke, he instinctively sought to push back the oppressive, industrial quiet by summoning the sonic landscape of his ancestors. A French song, rich with the melodic cadences of old Antwerp, slipped from his lips as he made his toilet, the vowels echoing against the sterile, whitewashed walls like a fragile ghost of a dying language.

"Ye're not custen dahn, then, maister?" cried Joe, his thick Yorkshire dialect cutting through the delicate acoustic fabric of the French melody.

"Not a stiver, mon garçon—which means, my lad. Get up, and we'll take a turn through the mill before the hands come in. We'll have the machinery yet, Joseph. You never heard of Bruce, perhaps?"

"And th' arrand? Yes, but I hev. I've read th' history o' Scotland, and happen knaw as mich on't as ye; and I understand ye to mean to say ye'll persevere."

"I do."

"Is there mony o' your mak' i' your country?" Joe inquired, folding away the temporary bed.

"In my country! Which is my country?"

"Why, France—isn't it?"

"Not it, indeed! The circumstance of the French having seized Antwerp, where I was born, does not make me a Frenchman." 

The untranslatability of his own bloodline was a constant, low-level ache. "Holland, then?" Joe pressed.

"I am not a Dutchman. You confound Antwerp with Amsterdam."

"Flanders?"

"I scorn the insinuation, Joe! Am I all body and no legs, like a Flamand? My mother was an Anversoise, though of French lineage, which is the reason I speak French."

"But your father war Yorkshire, which maks ye a bit Yorkshire too; and onybody may see ye're akin to us, ye're so keen o' making brass."

"Joe, you're an impudent dog," Moore said, leaning into the subtle power dynamics of their shared lexicons. "But I've always been accustomed to a boorish sort of insolence. The *classe ouvrière* in Belgium bear themselves brutally towards their employers; and by *brutally*, Joe, I mean *brutalement*—a word whose exact social weight and sensory texture, when properly translated, merely becomes *roughly* in your English."

"We allus speak our minds i' this country," Joe replied, firmly rooted in his native tongue. "I reckon 'at us manufacturing lads i' th' north is a deal more intelligent nor th' farming folk i' th' south. Trade sharpens wer wits. When I look after machinery, I've getten into that way that when I see an effect, I look straight out for a cause. There's many a one amang them greasy chaps 'at smells o' oil that has a long head, and can tell what a fooil of a law is, a deal better nor grand folk fro' London, or soft uns like Christopher Sykes, or yond' Irish Peter, Helstone's curate."

"You're a sublime fellow, but a conceited noodle with it all, Joe," Moore countered, rejecting the simplistic taxonomy of class. "Because you have found some scantling of chemistry at the bottom of a dyeing vat, you need not suppose the whole form of government is wrong, or that the virtues have taken refuge in cottages and wholly abandoned slated houses. Human nature is human nature everywhere, whether under tile or thatch. I have seen villains who were rich, and villains who were poor. The clock is going to strike six. Ring the mill bell."

The bell shattered the morning. It was an unnatural, rhythmic thud of commerce that violently punctured the quiet, ancient frequency of the sodden earth. February's pale dawn leaked over the horizon—a colourless, silver-gleaming ring separating the sodden, rain-drenched hills from a sky choked with vapour. The little children came running in from the damp, their footfalls small and hurried, seeking the warmth of the work-rooms despite the mechanical roar that awaited them.

Moore watched them pass, counting them. Those who arrived late were met with strict, metallic fines—a penny down for the first offense, twopence for the next. The rules were rigid, but devoid of the physical savagery practiced by coarser masters; neither Moore nor Joe Scott ever struck a child in the mill, preferring the sterile governance of the ledger over the brutal crack of a whip. At eight o'clock, the looms ceased their deafening clatter, granting the hollow a brief, restorative silence. The children opened their tin cans of coffee and baskets of bread, finding a momentary communion in their rations.

Leaving the mill-yard, Moore walked the short, high-banked lane to his cottage. The whitewashed walls and green porch stood as a solitary outpost of domesticity against the encroaching industrial sprawl. The garden borders held the emerald shoots of snowdrops, pushing through the black, rain-bleached earth. Rather than immediately entering, Moore fetched a spade and began to turn the soil. He dug for a quarter of an hour, the rhythmic, agrarian labor serving as a tactile tether, a necessary grounding of his senses after the unnatural vibrations of the factory floor. 

His communion with the earth was interrupted by the opening of a window. A female voice, carrying the rich, rounded vowels of his native tongue, drifted out:

*"Eh, bien! Tu ne déjeûnes pas ce matin?"*

"Is breakfast ready, Hortense?" Moore replied, shifting effortlessly into English, though the translation always felt like a minor betrayal.

"Certainly; it has been ready half an hour."

"Then I am ready too. I have a canine hunger." It was not merely a need for utilitarian fuel, but a profound yearning for the sensory anchor of her table. 

Moore dropped the spade and entered the narrow parlor. Here, Hortense Moore presided over a meticulous sanctuary of culinary memory. The small room was saturated with the dark, oily scent of roasted coffee beans and the warm, spiced vapor of stewed pears. Hortense, tall and stout, with dark hair wound tightly in curl-papers, stood armored in her traditional stuff petticoat and striped cotton camisole. She refused to adopt the sterile fashions of England, wearing her Belgian morning costume as the sacred vestments of a food historian fiercely guarding her heritage.

To her, the preparation of sustenance was an art of profound generational consequence. She cut her brother a generous Belgian *tartine*, the bread thick and yielding beneath a heavy spread of cultured butter. The pears, slow-simmered until their cell walls collapsed into a translucent, ruby-fleshed sweetness, swam in a syrup deeply infused with the memories of Antwerp.

"Quelle idée! to destroy them," Hortense began, pouring out her horror at the night's frame-smashing, seamlessly weaving the mythic violence of the moors into their domestic ritual. "Quelle action honteuse! C'était absolument comme les domestiques anglais—rien d'insupportable comme cette Sara, par exemple!"

"She looks clean and industrious," Moore remarked, savoring the profound complexity of the spiced fruit.

"Looks! I do not say she is dirty, but she is of an absolute insolence!" Hortense’s dark eyes flashed, wounded by the erosion of her culinary lexicon. "She disputed with me yesterday about the cooking of the beef. I employ the slow, ancestral methods of our *bouilli*, and she claimed I boiled it to rags! She called the rich, bone-deep nourishment of our *bouillon* 'greasy warm water.' And as to the *choucroute*—that barrel in the cellar, carefully lacto-fermented and delightfully prepared by my own hands—she termed it a tub of 'hog-wash'!" 

The dominant English lexicon was a blunt instrument, erasing the delicate, sour-tang history of the cabbage in a single, ignorant stroke. "I am harassed with the girl," Hortense sighed. "You are in the same position with your workmen, pauvre cher frère!"

"I am afraid you are not very happy in England, Hortense."

"It is my duty to be happy where you are, brother, but all the world here appears to me ill-bred. My habits are considered ridiculous. Of what an excellent family are the Gérards! We have a right to claim respect. Last Sunday, if you recollect, it was wet; accordingly, I went to church in my neat black sabots. As I paced up the aisle, composed and tranquil, four ladies and as many gentlemen laughed."

"Well, well! don't put on the sabots again. I told you they were not quite the thing for this country."

"But, brother, they are *sabots noirs, très propres*. At Mons and Leuze, respectable people wear nothing else in winter. The wood striking the stone—it is a proper, sensible sound! Let any one try to wade the mud of the Flemish *chaussées* in a pair of Paris *brodequins*!"

"Do at Rome as the Romans do," Moore said softly. "Ask Caroline Helstone about your dress."

"Caroline? I consult *her*? It is she who should consult me. She is a child."

"She is eighteen. Is she coming this morning to take her French lesson?"

"She will come at ten. She appreciates me better than any one else here, seeing that I have the education and manner of a person well born. But yet, possessed of penetration, I perceive clear defects in her."

"Amuse me with an account of her faults," Moore said, finishing his coffee.

"There is about her an occasional something—a reserve—and glimpses of an unsettled hurry in her nature which put me out," Hortense explained, deeply troubled by anything that disturbed her meticulously curated psychological environment. "I sometimes make her read French poetry by way of practice in pronunciation. She went through much of Corneille and Racine in a steady, sober spirit, but with a degree of languor partaking of apathy. Abstract, classical perfection bores her. The other day, I gave her a volume of short fugitive pieces. She turned the leaves impatiently, curling her lip in scorn. I chid her. 'Ma cousine,' she said, 'tout cela m'ennuie à la mort.' She asked if there were not two lines of true poetry in all French literature!"

Hortense adjusted her camisole, her voice tightening at the linguistic rebellion. "But then she found a short thing by Chénier, 'La Jeune Captive.' She stood before me, folded her hands, and repeated it. Brother, if you had heard the manner in which she went through it, the visceral, almost acoustic grief she pulled from the syllables, and the incoherent comments she uttered afterward... You would have thought Chénier was more moving than all Racine! This disproportionate preference argues an ill-regulated mind. I must give her a perfect control of her feelings."

"Be sure you do, Hortense. Here she comes. That was her shadow passed the window."

"Ah! truly. Half an hour before her time." 

The cottage door opened, admitting a slender figure wrapped in a heavy winter mantle. Caroline Helstone stepped into the sensory warmth of the parlor, bringing with her the lingering dampness of the moors.

"I came in haste to see how you were, Hortense, and how Robert was too," Caroline breathed, the anxiety in her voice bridging the timeline of the night's industrial violence with the morning's quiet domesticity. "I was sure you would be grieved by what happened last night. My uncle told me at breakfast."

"Ah! it is unspeakable. Your uncle sympathizes with us?"

"My uncle is very angry. Was he not with you to Stilbro' Moor, Robert?"

"Yes," Moore replied, the dark reality of the night encroaching once more. "We set out in very martial style, Caroline; but the prisoners we went to rescue met us half-way."

"Of course nobody was hurt?"

"Why, no; only Joe Scott's wrists were a little galled with being pinioned too tightly behind his back."

"You were not there? You were not with the wagons when they were attacked?"

"No. One seldom has the fortune to be present at occurrences at which one would particularly wish to assist." Moore turned his gaze toward the window.

"Where are you going this morning? I saw Murgatroyd saddling your horse in the yard."

"To Whinbury," Moore said, stepping away from the fragrant sanctuary of the breakfast table and back toward the harsh, unyielding noise of the world. "It is market day."
