Need I paint, dear readers, the joy of returning home, the warmth of reunion, and the quiet bliss that filled our house when all the children were once more gathered beneath their mother’s loving roof? It shone in her eyes, it trembled in her voice, and it found its truest expression on her knees in silent gratitude. Our home was spacious enough to hold us all, yet Aunt Hetty refused to stay. She confessed plainly that surrendering her role as mother to the elder children—those she had cared for nearly three years—was a wound too deep to bear so soon. She could not endure being near them while taking second place. So she and her father withdrew to a modest cottage at Bury St. Edmunds, not far from us, where they lived quietly, indulging her eldest nephew and niece with affectionate secrecy.

It was the year after our return that Mr. B., the Jamaican planter, passed away, leaving her half his fortune. Then, for the first time, I learned how deeply he had loved her during his time in the West Indies—how, though she had turned him down, he had honored her memory by bequeathing her wealth. Heaven only knows what part of Aunt Hetty’s inheritance Monsieur Miles has already consumed—the cost of his commission, his grand uniforms, his debts at the gaming tables, his little ventures in the Minories. Do you think, sirrah, I do not know the price of Pall Mall taverns, of petits soupers, or even of moderate play at the Cocoa-Tree? A gentleman cannot enjoy such pleasures on five hundred pounds a year—no matter how generously I allow him.

Aunt Hetty insists she will remain an old maid. “I vowed,” she declared, “never to marry until I found a man as good as my dear father. And I never did, Sir George. Not half as good. You may put that in your pipe and smoke it.”

And yet, when the General died—calm, aged, and ready to depart—it was my wife who wept most bitterly. “I weep,” she said softly, “because I feel I did not love him enough.” Hetty, meanwhile, remained outwardly composed, speaking of him as if he still walked among us. She recalled his jests, his gentle kindness to the children—then brightened like a young girl again—and sat contentedly gazing up at the stone in church that bore his name and virtues, never lying about him, for once.

I had long suspected that my brother Hal, whom Hetty had once adored in her youth, still held a place in her heart. When he came to visit us ten years ago, I told him of this early infatuation, hoping—though I confess it—that he might offer to replace Mrs. Fanny, who had passed on, and whose memory my wife, ever prone to self-reproach, felt she had mourned insufficiently. Hal arrived steeped in grief over his loss, swearing there was no woman like her in the world. Our beloved General, still living then, welcomed him back into the family fold as he had done in happier times. The women played the same melodies he’d heard as a boy at Oakhurst. Every heart softened with nostalgia, and Harry poured out his sorrow and praise for his late wife to Hetty, often speaking fondly of his dear Aunt Lambert, whose virtues delighted the faithful husband, whose thoughts, I believe, never strayed from her memory for more than three waking minutes a day.

General Hal journeyed to Paris as an American officer in his blue and yellow uniform—fashionable here thanks to Mr. Fox and others—and was well received at Versailles, though presented by Monsieur le Marquis de Lafayette to the Most Christian King and Queen, who did not favor the Marquis. I suspect a certain Marquise took a fancy to the Virginian General and would have married him willingly had he not resisted and fled back to England, to Warrington, and especially to Bury, where people listened patiently to his stories of his departed wife.

As for us, who had known the poor paragon, we were courteous but less fervent in our admiration, often puzzled when our children asked about Uncle Hal’s angelic wife.

The two Generals, myself, Captain Miles, and Parson Blake—knocked unconscious at Monmouth the year after I returned from America—used to reenact the battles of the Revolution over wine. The parson would cry, “By Jupiter, General!” (he substituted for Jupiter when he laid aside his military garb), “you are the Tory, and Sir George is the Whig! He always finds fault with our leaders, while you defend them endlessly. And last Sunday, when I prayed for the King, I heard you following me—loudly!”

“And so I do, Blake, with all my heart,” replied Hal. “I can’t forget I wore his coat.”

“Ah, if Wolfe had lived twenty years longer!” lamented Lambert.

“Ah, sir,” cried Hal, “you should hear the General talk about him!”

“What General?” I asked, just to tease him.

“My General,” said Hal, rising and filling a bumper. “His Excellency, General George Washington!”

“With all my heart,” I answered—but the parson looked displeased, as if the toast or the claret offended him.

Hal never tired of speaking of his General. It was on one such evening, warm with camaraderie, that he revealed how he had once fallen out with the very man he revered above all others. Their disagreement concerned the Marquis de Lafayette, the young French nobleman who had made such a mark in history—and who had so abruptly vanished from it.

Lafayette’s rank in the French service and his gallantry during the war should have secured Colonel Warrington’s promotion in the Continental army. Instead, a mere youth like M. de Lafayette arrived and was immediately elevated to Major-General by Congress. Hal, speaking freely as an old soldier, had expressed scorn for some of Congress’s appointments—appointments born of intrigue and ambition among officers hungry for rank. Mr. Warrington, perhaps emulating his now-famous friend from Mount Vernon, carried himself as a gentleman: he accepted pay, yes, but spent it on comforts and clothing for his men. Rank, he said, mattered not at all; he would serve equally well as colonel or general. He also voiced disdain for certain officers, particularly regarding the sudden elevation of the young Frenchman—this Marquis, as they called him—who, incidentally, was immensely favored by the Commander-in-Chief himself.

Few in the Continental army could speak French. Thus, Hal had a clearer view of the young Major-General than most, including the Commander-in-Chief. General Washington, good-naturedly, chided Hal for jealousy toward the beardless commander of Auvergne. He appreciated the youthful nobleman’s devotion and recognized political necessity in treating the Marquis kindly.

Yet behind the scenes, Washington was urgently advocating for Colonel Warrington’s promotion before Congress. At a time when winter gripped Valley Forge, where Washington commanded barely five or six thousand men—without fire, blankets, food, or ammunition—while facing Sir William Howe’s fully equipped and thrice-larger force—his troubles were immense. As if these hardships weren’t enough, he faced the cowardly distrust of Congress and rebellion within his own ranks. During the terrible winter of ’77, when a single blow from the British could have ended the war, despair reigned in the American camp—except in one unyielding heart.

My brother had a meeting with the Chief, which he later described to me, and which he could never recount without emotion. Washington had won no great triumphs like those of Arnold or Burgoyne—whose folly had brought victory to Gates and the northern army. Aside from minor skirmishes that proved his courage and vigilance, Washington had suffered defeat after defeat against a superior enemy. Congress distrusted him. Many officers hated him. Those disappointed in ambition, caught in corruption, or exposed for incompetence—all conspired against him. Gates became their leader. Gates, they believed, was the only genius fit to lead the war. And with a vanity he later admitted, he accepted the homage paid him.

To grasp the depth of Washington’s burdens, consider the “Conway Cabal.” An Irishman—a Chevalier of St. Louis and officer in the French army—arrived in America early in 1777 seeking employment. He was swiftly promoted to brigadier, but demanded immediate advancement to major-general.

Mr. Conway had allies in Congress who promised him swift promotion. Washington protested, arguing it was unjust to elevate the youngest brigadier to the highest rank. While the matter lingered, he received a letter from Conway to Gates, praising him: “Heaven must have been determined to save America, or a weak general and bad councillors would have ruined it.” Washington forwarded the letter to Conway without comment. Conway offered his resignation, which Congress refused, instead appointing him Inspector-General with the rank of Major-General.

“And it was at this time,” said Harry, passionate with regret and awe, “when the glorious Chief was burdened with troubles enough to drive ten thousand men mad—that I had to interfere with my jealousies about the Frenchman! I didn’t say much—just some nonsense to Greene and Cadwalader about getting frogs against the Frenchman. But I should have known the Chief’s trials, and that he had a better head than mine. I should have held my tongue.”

For a time, Washington said nothing, but Hal noticed a chill between them. Mrs. Washington, visiting camp, sensed something was wrong. Women, he said, have ways of uncovering secrets. “I might not have sought the cause,” Hal added, “for I am as proud as he is. And when the Chief is angry, it’s no pleasure to approach him.”

Indeed, my brother was subdued by his old friend, obeying him like a boy before a schoolmaster.

“At last,” Hal continued, “Mrs. Washington discovered the truth. ‘Speak to me after dinner, Colonel Hal,’ she said. ‘Come to the parade-ground before the dining-house, and I’ll tell you all.’ I left a dozen generals and brigadiers drinking around the General’s table and found her waiting. She told me it was my remark about the box of Marquises that had offended the General. ‘I wouldn’t have minded another,’ he said, ‘but I never thought Harry Warrington would oppose me.’”

That night, Hal waited to speak. He found Washington alone at his desk.

“Can your Excellency give me five minutes?” he asked, heart pounding.

“Yes, sir,” Washington replied, pointing to the chair. “Please sit.”

“It wasn’t always ‘Sir’ and ‘Colonel Warrington’ between us,” Hal said.

“The times are altered,” Washington answered calmly.

“Et nos mutamur in illis,” Hal quoted. “Times and men change.”

“You had business with me?” Washington asked.

“Am I speaking to the Commander-in-Chief or to my old friend?” Hal questioned.

The General looked at him gravely. “Well—to both, sir. Pray sit, Harry.”

“If to General Washington,” Hal began, “I tell his Excellency that I, and many officers, are not pleased to see a boy of twenty made major-general over us because he’s a Marquis and can’t speak English. If I speak to my old friend, I must say he has shown me little trust or friendship lately. I have no desire to sit at your table while others make impertinent remarks about your turning your back on me.”

“Which charge shall I take first, Harry?” Washington asked, turning his chair and crossing his legs.

“You’re jealous of the Marquis?” he guessed.

“Jealous, sir!” Hal exclaimed. “An aide-de-camp of Mr. Wolfe is not jealous of a Jack-a-dandy who was being whipped at school five years ago!”

“You yourself declined higher rank,” the Chief said, turning slightly red.

“But I never agreed to have a macaroni Marquis command me!” Hal shouted. “I will not carry the young gentleman’s orders. Since Congress and your Excellency choose to appoint generals from the nursery, I humbly request leave to resign and retire to my plantation.”

“Do, Harry,” Washington said gently, surprising him. “That is true friendship. Now that your old friend is in trouble, it’s the best time to leave.”

“Sir!” Hal gasped.

“Do as so many others are doing, Mr. Warrington,” Washington said. “Et tu, Brute, as the play says. Well, well, Harry—I did not think it of you. But at least, you are in fashion.”

“You asked which charge I should take first?” Hal reminded him.

“Oh, the Marquis’s appointment? I recommended it to Congress, no doubt. You and others disapprove.”

“I have spoken for myself, sir,” Hal said.

“If you take me in that tone, Colonel Warrington, I have nothing to answer!” the Chief rose sharply. “I presume I may recommend officers for promotion without your prior approval.”

“Being on that tone, sir,” Hal replied, “let me respectfully submit my resignation, based on the fact that Congress, at your recommendation, offers its highest commands to boys of twenty who barely know our language.” He stood and bowed.

“Great heavens, Harry!” Washington cried—beaten on the matter of the Marquis, unable to reply. “Can’t you believe that in this critical hour, there are reasons why special honor must be shown to the first distinguished Frenchman to join us?”

“No doubt, sir,” Hal said. “If you acknowledge that Monsieur de Lafayette’s merits have nothing to do with it.”

“I acknowledge or deny nothing!” the General snapped, stamping his foot. “Am I here to be catechised by you? Wait—listen, Harry. I speak to you as a man of the world—nay, as an old friend. This appointment humiliates you and others, you say? Be it so! Must we not bear humiliation, along with other burdens, for our country? It may be unjust that the Marquis commands you, just as it would be unfair if your Prince Ferdinand or Prince of Wales commanded veterans at home. But if appointing this young nobleman brings us twenty million allies, will you sulk because we honor him? It’s easy to sneer at him—though, believe me, the Marquis has more merit than you allow. It would be more generous, more polite, for Harry Warrington to welcome this stranger for the sake of our nation’s benefit—not mock his quirks, but aid him with your experience. That is what I expect of you—what I would do. But you choose to join my enemies, and when I am in distress, you say you will leave me. That is why I’ve been hurt. That is why I’ve been cold. I trusted you as a brother. Tell me, was I right? I relied on you, and you come and tell me you will resign. Be it so. Having entered this struggle by God’s will, I will see it through to the end. You are not the first, Mr. Warrington, to leave me on the way.”

He spoke with such tenderness, his face so full of sorrow, that Hal was seized with remorse and pity. He blurted out incoherent words about old times, vowing he would never leave him if he said the word.

“You never loved him, George,” Hal said, turning to me, “but I did beyond any mortal man. Though I’m not clever like you, I think my instinct was right. He has a greatness unmatched by any other.”

“I don’t say no, brother,” I replied.

“Greatness, pooh!” growled the parson, swirling his wine.

“We walked into Mrs. Washington’s tea-room arm-in-arm,” Hal continued. “She looked up kindly and saw we were reconciled. ‘Is it all over, Colonel Harry?’ she whispered. ‘I know he’s applied often about your promotion—’”

“I never will take it,” Hal said.

“And that,” he concluded, “is how I came to do penance with Lafayette the next winter.” (Hal imitated the Frenchman perfectly.) “‘I will go weez heem,’ I said. ‘I know the way to Quebec. When we’re not fighting Sir Guy, I can hear His Excellency the Major-General recite his lesson.’ There was no fight—we couldn’t raise an army for Canada. We returned to headquarters. And what disturbed the Frenchman most? The idea that people would laugh at him because his command had achieved nothing. And they did—almost to his face. How could one help it? If our Chief had a weakness, it was this Marquis.”

After their quarrel, they became closer than ever—if a man may be said to be friends with a sovereign prince, for Hal could not help seeing Washington as one. One night, after the others had retired, they talked of old days—the joyful hunts, the adventures before and after Braddock’s defeat, and that near-duel between us when we were boys. Washington laughed, saying he had never seen anyone look more deadly intent on killing than I did. “And to do Sir George justice,” he added, “I think he has hated me ever since.” Then he paused. “Ah! An open enemy I can face easily. It is the secret foe who causes doubt and anguish. Today, I have sat with men at my table who must pretend civility, shake hands with me, knowing they stab my reputation and seek to pull me down. You recently complained of being humiliated by a junior commanding you. What is your humiliation compared to mine? I must play the farce of welcome to traitors, endure Congress’s neglect, and watch men who insult me promoted in my own army. If I followed my own feelings, would I continue in command? You know my temper—whether as a private man I could endure such slights. But for the sacred cause, we must endure hardship, danger, calumny, and wrong. May God give us strength to do our duty!” Then he showed me the papers concerning Conway—whom Congress promoted despite the conspiracy—and how John Cadwalader sent the best ball he ever fired down the black throat of that fellow.

“And it was here,” Hal said, concluding, “as I watched the Chief speaking in the quiet of the camp, remembering how lonely he was, how vast his responsibility, how spies and traitors fed from his plate, and an enemy lay before him ready to crush him—then I thought: ‘Surely, this is the greatest man in the world. And what a wretch I am to fret over petty jealousies while he walks serenely under his immense burdens.’”

“We spoke of Wolfe just now,” I said. “Here, indeed, is a greater than Wolfe. To endure is greater than to dare. To wear down hostile fortune. To be daunted by no difficulty. To keep heart when all have lost it. To pass through intrigue untouched. To renounce ambition when victory is won—can any say this is not greatness? Or show another Englishman who has achieved so much?”

“I wonder, Sir George,” grumbled Parson Blake, “you didn’t take Washington’s side and wear the blue and buff yourself.”

“You and I thought scarlet suited our complexions, Joe Blake,” Sir George replied. “And my wife thinks there wouldn’t have been room for two such great men on one side.”

“Well, at any rate,” Lady Warrington interjected, “you were better than that odious, swearing, crazy General Lee, who was second in command! And I’m certain Mr. Washington never wrote poetry or tragedies like you can! What did the General say about George’s tragedies, Harry?”

Harry burst into laughter—Captain Miles joining in.

“Well!” he said, “it’s true Hagan read one at my house to the General, Mrs. Washington, and others—and they all fell sound asleep!”

“He never liked my husband,” Theo declared, tossing her head. “And it’s all the more magnanimous of Sir George to speak so well of him.”
