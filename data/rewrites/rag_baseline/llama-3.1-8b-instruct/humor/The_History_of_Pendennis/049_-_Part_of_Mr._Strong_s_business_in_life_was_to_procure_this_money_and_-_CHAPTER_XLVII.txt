As the social calendar drew to a close, Monseigneur's entourage began to disperse, leaving him to ponder the implications of his recent nocturnal exploits at Gaunt House. Weary from the exertions of that fateful evening, Major Pendennis retreated to Buxton in search of relief, his body crying out for the soothing balm of its renowned mineral waters. The season, once teeming with high-society gatherings and diplomatic intrigue, had reached its zenith, only to slowly unwind like a worn tapestry, thread by thread.

Parliament's recess had brought an eerie quiet to the city, as politicians and socialites alike bid farewell to the capital, seeking solace in the countryside, the Continent, or more exotic locales. Sir Francis Clavering and his family had departed, leaving behind a vacuum of sorts, and the affairs that had been brewing remained in limbo, awaiting the return of key players like Captain Shandon, who had decamped to the spa town of Boulogne-sur-Mer with his family.

In this lull, the city's usual rhythms were disrupted, its vibrant pulse slowed to a gentle throb. The bow-windows of the gentlemen's clubs stood empty, the pavement of Pall Mall eerily deserted. Red-coated footmen, once a ubiquitous sight outside the Palace gates, had vanished, replaced by the quieter hum of tradespeople enjoying their own leisure activities. Tailors, once busily attending to the sartorial needs of the aristocracy, had donned mustachios and set off on the Grand Tour, their destinations ranging from the Rhine to the spas of Ems and Baden. Bootmakers, meanwhile, had retreated to the gaming tables, where they mingled with their creditors, their faces flushed with embarrassment and excitement.

As the city slumbered, only a handful of stalwarts remained, including Clarence Bulbul, a Treasury clerk bound to his duties. During his afternoon rides through Rotten Row, he couldn't help but feel a sense of isolation, likening the desolate thoroughfare to the vast expanse of the Arabian desert and himself to a lone Bedouin traversing its dunes. Warrington, ever the practical one, had packed his carpet-bag with Cavendish tobacco and made his way to his brother's country estate in Norfolk, seeking refuge from the city's din.
