Calvin C. Bunner tipped his hat with a brisk nod, the unlit cigar still clamped between his teeth. “Calvin C. Bunner, at your service,” he amended, his American cadence cutting through the English air. He wasn’t accustomed to such abruptness from the locals, and Trent’s directness had thrown him momentarily off-balance. “You’d be Mr. Trent, then,” he continued, shifting his gaze to Inspector Murch. “Captain.”

Murch acknowledged the greeting with a curt nod.

“I was on my way upstairs when I heard voices,” Bunner explained, his dark eyes flickering between the two men. “Caught a word or two about a pistol—this one, I reckon—and thought I’d see what was what.” He chuckled, easy and unbothered. “No eavesdropping intended.”

Bunner was a lean, compact man with a clean-shaven face that bordered on delicate, saved from softness only by the sharp intelligence in his large, watchful eyes. His dark hair was neatly parted, and his lips—usually occupied with a cigar—rested slightly parted, as if perpetually on the verge of speaking. Without the cigar, he looked restless; with it, he transformed into the picture of cool, calculating efficiency.

A Connecticut native, he’d clawed his way up from a broker’s office to become Manderson’s private secretary—not just for his methodical precision, but for his uncanny instinct for the market’s pulse. Manderson valued men who could think fast and keep secrets.

Trent and Bunner sized each other up in silence, each approving of what he saw.

“I was just learning,” Trent remarked, “that finding this pistol doesn’t mean much. Apparently, it’s as common in America as umbrellas in London.”

Bunner took the weapon, handling it with familiarity. “Right you are. We call it a *Little Arthur* back home. Lightweight, popular—half the country’s probably carrying one just like it.” He shrugged, then produced his own pistol from under his coat. “Personally, I prefer something with more heft.” He offered it to Trent. “Care to feel the difference?”

Trent declined with a shake of his head.

“Marlowe bought this *Little Arthur* for Manderson before we sailed,” Bunner continued. “The old man insisted a man ought to carry a pistol in this day and age. Didn’t ask my opinion, of course.” He inspected the weapon critically. “Decent piece, though. Marlowe was hopeless at first, but I’ve drilled him into something passable. Still, he never took to carrying it. Me? I’ve had one on me for years. When you work for a man like Manderson, you learn to expect trouble.” His expression darkened. “And now they got him when I wasn’t there.”

He straightened abruptly. “Well, gentlemen, I’ve got cables to send—enough to choke a horse. Mr. Trent, if you’re heading to the *Three Tuns*, I can drop you off. The car’s just out back.”

Trent accepted, and together they made their way to the garage, a shaded retreat from the midday sun. Bunner took his time, lighting a cigar and settling onto the car’s footboard before fixing Trent with a keen stare.

“Listen, Trent,” he began, exhaling smoke. “I’ve got some information that might help you. I know your reputation—you’re sharp. That inspector, though? Strikes me as slow. I’ll answer his questions, but I’m not handing him theories on a platter. You, though—that’s different.”

Trent smiled faintly. “Murch isn’t as dull as he seems. He’s one of the best in Europe—just methodical.”

Bunner waved a dismissive hand. “This isn’t some routine case. Manderson knew something was coming for him. I’d bet my last dollar on it.”

Trent leaned forward. “Go on.”

“The old man wasn’t himself these past few weeks. Normally, he was ice—coolest head in any crisis. But lately? He’d snap over nothing. A letter with bad news could set him off like a powder keg. And for the first time ever, he let business slide. I told him to see a doctor. He told me to go to hell.”

“You think it was fear?” Trent asked.

Bunner’s lips thinned. “Not fear—not exactly. More like he was waiting for the axe to fall. The strange part? He stopped taking precautions. Sat by open windows at night, like he was daring someone to take a shot.”

“Any idea who?”

Bunner barked a laugh. “You ever been to the States? Manderson had enemies by the thousands—men who’d wait years for revenge. The question isn’t *who* wanted him dead. It’s why he stopped trying to stay alive.”

Trent stood, exhaling a slow stream of smoke. “You’ve given me a lot to think about, Mr. Bunner. Let’s continue this another time.” He checked his watch. “For now, I’ve kept my friend waiting long enough.”

Bunner rose, tapping his own watch. “Two o’clock here—ten a.m. in New York. Wall Street’s hell on earth right now. Let’s hope neither of us ever gets closer to the real thing.”

With that, they left the garage, each lost in his own thoughts.
