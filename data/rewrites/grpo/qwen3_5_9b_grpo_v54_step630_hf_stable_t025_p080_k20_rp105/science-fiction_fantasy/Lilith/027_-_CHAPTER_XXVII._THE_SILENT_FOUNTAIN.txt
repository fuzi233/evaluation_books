The spotted leopardess tore up the brow of the hill, a blur of motion against the night, leaving me only a fleeting glimpse of her retreat toward the palace gates. By the time I reached the entrance-hall, the princess was hastily draping a robe around her shoulders, the fabric catching the wind that had already stilled the flow of blood from her wounds, leaving them dark and crusted.

When our eyes met, a flash of irritation crossed her features before she forced a brittle smile. "A small accident," she said, her voice tight. "Hearing the cat-woman was back in the city, I went to drive her away. But she brought one of her horrid creatures. It sprang upon me, claws finding my neck before I could strike!"

She shivered, and despite knowing the truth—that her injuries were real, yet her story a fabrication born of fear—I felt a pang of guilt. I had let her fight alone. My expression must have betrayed my pity.

"Child of folly!" she snapped, though the anger lacked heat. "Do not cry. Wait here; I must enter the black hall briefly. Fetch me something for these scratches."

I followed her, though a primal instinct warned me to stay back. The moment she crossed the threshold, the air filled with a low, buzzing hum, as if a thousand voices whispered in unison. Light began to shift across the vast assembly, traveling like a ripple from spot to spot. Groups of figures would flare into brilliance—illuminating the skull-headed dancers in the forest-hall where the princess had once watched—and then sink back into shadow, only to reveal the chaos of the Evil Wood where she had urged the fight. Yet, through this kaleidoscope of memory and presence, I saw her now: standing motionless, head bowed, while the light settled finally on the hollow in the heath. There, she walked in vain circles, trying to wrap the elusive vapour around her.

Then, the old librarian stepped forward. He regarded her for a heartbeat, and suddenly, she collapsed. Her limbs fled her body, and her form dissolved into nothingness.

A wild shriek tore through the echoing space. As her eidolon fell, the princess herself, who had stood like a statue before me, crumpled to the ground, lifeless. I turned and fled immediately. Not again would I seek to restore her. Trembling beside the cage, I realized with a jolt of horror that within the black ellipsoid, I had been inside the princess's own mind. I saw the tail of the leopardess quiver once more in the periphery of that fractured vision.

Before I could fully compose myself, her voice cut through the silence beside me. "Come now," she commanded, her tone shifting instantly from despair to command. "I will show you what I need."

She led me into the court. The moon hung near the zenith, its silver radiance outshining the absent gold of the sun. She guided me through the trees to the tallest one, the central sentinel. Its branches drew together at the apex, forming a cluster that resembled a fir-cone from below. Standing beneath it, she gazed upward, speaking to the darkness.

"On the summit of that tree grows a tiny blossom that would heal my scratches instantly," she murmured. "I could be a dove and fetch it, but there is a little snake in the leaves. Its bite would be worse to a dove than a tiger's bite to me. How I hate that cat-woman!"

She turned to me, her face softening into one of her sweetest smiles. "Can you climb?"

The smile vanished as quickly as it came, replaced by a look of profound suffering. I should have left her to her pain, but the way she pressed her hand to her wounded neck struck a chord deep within me.

I studied the tree. From base to branch, the stem was lined with projections, like the remnants of fallen leaves on a palm.

"I can climb that tree," I answered.

"Not with bare feet!" she insisted.

In my haste to pursue the leopardess, I had forgotten my sandals in my room.

"It is no matter," I said. "I have long gone barefoot."

I looked up again, my eyes tracing the rugged bole until they lost themselves in the canopy. The moonlight dappled the trunk like silvery foam, and a faint breeze rustled the tops with a sound like water falling softly into water. I stepped forward to begin my ascent, but she stopped me.

"I cannot let you attempt it with your feet bare!" she argued. "A fall from that height would kill you!"

"So would a bite from the snake!" I retorted, admitting I did not truly believe a snake existed.

"It would not hurt *you*!" she replied sharply. "Wait a moment."

She tore from her garment the two wide borders that met in the front. Kneeling on one knee, she bound my left foot, then my right, securing them with thick, embroidered strips.

"You have left the ends hanging, princess," I noted.

"I have nothing to cut them with," she said, tying the knots tight. "But they are not long enough to get entangled."

I turned to the tree and began to climb.

In Bulika, the cold after sundown was mild compared to other regions, especially near the sexton's cottage. Yet, as I ascended, the chill intensified. A little way up, I felt the cold; higher still, it bit deeper; and among the upper branches, it became absolute. I shivered violently, feeling as though my hands and feet had vanished entirely.

There was hardly any wind, and the branches remained rigid, yet as I neared the summit, a peculiar unsteadiness seized me. Every limb I touched seemed poised to give way. When my head rose above the foliage into the open moonlight, searching for the blossom, I was suddenly drenched from head to foot. The next instant, as if plunged into a violent storm, I was flung about wildly. I felt myself sinking, tossed up and down, rolled over and over, checked and spun again, descending lower and lower. Gasping, gurgling, choking, I crashed onto a solid bottom.

"I told you so!" a voice croaked in my ear.
