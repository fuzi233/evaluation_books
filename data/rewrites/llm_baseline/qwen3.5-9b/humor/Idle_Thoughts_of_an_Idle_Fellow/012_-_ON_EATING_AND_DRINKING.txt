I have always possessed a profound affection for eating and drinking, though in my youth, the passion leaned heavily toward the former. Back then, I boasted both a voracious appetite and a robust digestion. I recall one particular evening when a gentleman of dull eyes and a complexion the color of a bruised plum arrived to dine at our home. For approximately five minutes, he stared at me devouring my food, appearing utterly transfixed. Finally, turning to my father, he asked, "Does your boy ever suffer from dyspepsia?"

My father replied, "Never; I've never heard him complain of such a thing."

"Do you yourself suffer from dyspepsia, Collywobbles?" the guest inquired. (They called me Collywobbles, though it wasn't my actual name.)

"No, Pa," I answered promptly. Immediately following this declaration, I added, somewhat innocently, "But what *is* dyspepsia, Pa?"

The livid-complexioned stranger regarded me with a mixture of astonishment and envy. Then, in a tone dripping with infinite pity, he slowly intoned, "You will know... some day."

My late, beloved mother often remarked that she delighted in watching me eat. It remains a source of enduring satisfaction to me that I must have provided her with considerable gratification in that regard. Generally speaking, a growing, healthy lad who exercises regularly and avoids excessive studying can easily satisfy even the most demanding expectations regarding his consumption.

It is genuinely amusing to observe boys eating when you aren't paying for the privilege. Their concept of a "square meal" involves a staggering array of provisions: a pound and a half of roast beef accompanied by five or six substantial potatoes (preferably soapy ones, as they offer greater substance), generous portions of greens, four thick slices of Yorkshire pudding, followed by a pair of currant dumplings, several green apples, a penworth of nuts, half a dozen jumbles, and a bottle of gingerbeer. Once finished, they proceed to play at horses. One cannot help but wonder how they must disdain us men, who are required to sit quietly for hours after consuming merely a spoonful of clear soup and the wing of a chicken!

However, boys do not possess all the advantages. A boy never truly enjoys the luxury of being satisfied. He never feels full. He can never stretch out his legs, place his hands behind his head, close his eyes, and sink into the ethereal bliss that envelops the well-dined man. A dinner makes absolutely no difference to a boy. To a man, however, it acts like a good fairy's potion; afterwards, the world seems brighter and better. A man who has dined satisfactorily develops a yearning love for all his fellow creatures. He strokes the cat gently, cooing "Poor pussy" with tender emotion. He sympathizes with the musicians of the German band outside, wondering if they are cold, and momentarily ceases to hate his wife's relatives. A good dinner reveals the softer side of a man. Under its genial influence, the gloomy and morose become jovial and chatty. Individuals who spend the rest of the day looking as though they subsist solely on vinegar and Epsom salts break into wreathed smiles after dinner, exhibiting a sudden urge to pat small children on the head and speak vaguely to them about sixpences. Serious men thaw into mild cheerfulness, and snobbish young men with heavy mustaches forget to be objectionable.

I always feel sentimental after dinner. It is the only time I can properly appreciate love stories. When the hero clasps "her" in a final, wild embrace and stifles a sob, I feel as devastated as if I had played a disastrous hand at whist and turned up only a deuce. When the heroine meets her tragic end, I weep. If I were to read the same tale early in the morning, I would simply sneer at it. Digestion—or rather, indigestion—has a marvelous effect upon the heart.

If I wish to compose something truly pathetic, I consume a large plate of hot buttered muffins about an hour prior to sitting down to work. By the time I begin, an unutterable melancholy descends upon me. I envision heartbroken lovers parting forever at lonely wayside stiles while the sad twilight deepens around them, broken only by the tinkling of a distant sheep-bell. I imagine old men gazing at withered flowers until their sight dims with tears; dainty maidens waiting at open casements as "he cometh not," watching the golden tresses of their loves turn white and thin. The babies they once dandled have become grown adults burdened by their own pains, and the playmates they laughed with lie silent beneath the waving grass. They wait and watch until the dark shadows of the unknown night gather round them, and the world's childish troubles fade from their aching eyes. I see pale corpses tossed on white-foamed waves, deathbeds stained with bitter tears, and graves in trackless deserts. I hear the wild wailing of women, the low moaning of children, and the dry sobbing of strong men. It is all the muffins. I could not conjure a single melancholy fancy upon a mutton chop and a glass of champagne. A full stomach is a great aid to poetry; indeed, no sentiment of any kind can stand upon an empty one.

We lack the time or inclination to indulge in fanciful troubles until we have dispelled our real misfortunes. We do not sigh over dead dicky-birds while the bailiff is in the house, nor do we worry about whether our mistress's smiles are cold, hot, or lukewarm when we don't know where to find our next shilling.

Foolish people—that is to say, those who entertain opinions differing from mine—will tell you that mental distress is far more agonizing than bodily pain. This is romantic and touching theory, so comforting to the love-sick youth who looks down patronizingly on a starving man and thinks, "Ah, how happy you are compared with me!" So soothing to fat gentlemen who cackle about the superiority of poverty over riches. But it is all nonsense—all cant. An aching head soon makes one forget an aching heart. A broken finger will banish all memories of an empty chair. And when a man feels truly hungry, he feels nothing else.

We sleek, well-fed folk can hardly conceive what hunger is like. We know the sensation of having no appetite and ignoring dainties placed before us, but we do not understand the agony of sickening for food—to die for bread while others waste it, to gaze with famished eyes at coarse fare steaming behind dingy windows, longing for a penworth of pea pudding but lacking the penny to buy it. To feel that a crust would be delicious and a bone a banquet. Hunger is a luxury to us, a piquant, flavor-enhancing sauce. It is worthwhile to get hungry and thirsty merely to discover the depth of gratification obtainable from eating and drinking.

If you wish to thoroughly enjoy your dinner, take a thirty-mile country walk after breakfast and refrain from eating until you return. Your eyes will glisten at the sight of the white tablecloth and steaming dishes! You will sigh with contentment as you set down the empty beer tankard and pick up your knife and fork. Later, as you push back your chair, light a cigar, and beam upon everyone, you will feel incredibly comfortable. Just ensure, when adopting this plan, that the promised dinner actually exists, or the disappointment will be trying.

I recall one occasion involving a friend, dear old Joe. Alas, how we lose one another in life's mist! It must have been eight years since I last saw Joseph Taboys. How pleasant it would be to meet his jovial face again, clasp his strong hand, and hear his cheery laugh! He also owes me fourteen shillings.

Well, we were on holiday together. One morning we broke fast early and embarked on a tremendous long walk. We had ordered a duck for dinner the previous night. We instructed the hostess, "Get a big one, for we shall come home awfully hungry." As we departed, the landlady appeared in high spirits. "I have secured you gentlemen a duck," she announced cheerfully. "If you manage to finish that, you'll do well." She held up a bird roughly the size of a door-mat. We chuckled at the spectacle and declared we would attempt it, speaking with the self-conscious pride of men aware of their own endurance.

Then we set off. Naturally, we lost our way. I invariably do so in the countryside, and it renders me frantic, for it is useless to ask locals for directions. One might as well inquire of a lodging-house servant how to make beds as expect a country bumpkin to know the road to the next village. You must shout the question three times before the sound penetrates his skull. At the third shout, he slowly raises his head and stares blankly. You yell it a fourth time, and he repeats it. He ponders while you count hundreds, after which, speaking at a rate of three words per minute, he suggests you "couldn't do better than..."
