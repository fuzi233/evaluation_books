If I had to pick one word to sum up Doctor Florret, headmaster of our respectable middle-class academy, it would be “correct”. The man was the exact, factory-issue specification of what a headmaster should be: large but not alarmingly large, stout but nowhere near fat, built to look like a walking advertisement for steady, comfortable respectability. His hands were white and perfectly shaped, his single diamond ring glinted just the right amount—no tasteless sparkles that would make the local vicar side-eye him, no tiny dud that looked like he fished it out of a Christmas cracker. He spoke of even the driest topics (rain, school dinner menus, the state of the cloakroom boots) in a tone that lent them the grandeur of a royal coronation speech. Clean-shaven, radiating benignity tempered by exactly the right amount of sensible discretion, he was less a person than a walking manual of “How To Be A Respectable Victorian Gentleman”.

Everyone and everything around him matched the brief: his wife was his feminine carbon copy, the sort of woman who could pour tea and scold a misbehaving boy with equal, perfectly calibrated politeness. Stare at the pair of them standing side by side and you’d half believe the old story about the rib was true, and some divine factory had cranked her out directly from one of his own to be his perfect helpmeet. His furniture was solid, heavy, built to last 200 years if it didn’t break your toe first, and so unobtrusive you’d never notice it till you tripped over it. His pictures hung on the walls without ever daring to draw attention to themselves, and you could set your pocket watch by the things he said: not the clock time, mind you, but the exact minute it was Proper To Say Whatever he was about to say. If you were ever stuck wondering what the “right” thing to do in any situation was, you only had to hang about till Doctor Florret did it, and instantly you’d realise every other possible choice on God’s green earth would have been absurdly wrong.

At the cost of four more or less wasted years, I learned his school’s teaching methods were also 100% correct—correct, that is, for every English schoolmaster who’d ever drawn breath since the days of Edward VI., not correct for overwrought 12-year-olds who’d much rather be doing literally anything else.

Heaven knows I tried. Boyhood ambition, the big messy kind that doesn’t know what it wants to be when it grows up, burned right through me. If I passed a ragged orator on a street corner yelling about Utopia at a crowd of passersby, I instantly saw myself as Demosthenes swaying mobs, the Prime Minister of England so beloved even the opposition papers were forced to print grudging praise of me. If I heard a distant military band, I was leading an army home from glorious war, perched on a horse I spent three whole maths lessons debating (snow white? Too easy to get mud on. Coal black? Too grim for a victory parade. I settled on dappled grey and was still arguing with myself about it when the bell rang). When I saw a sailor staggering out of a pub swinging his tankard, I was Columbus discovering a whole new continent, defeating the French, dying heroically at the peak of victory and getting buried in Westminster Abbey, then waking up half a second later to walk straight into a lamppost or step on some grumpy old man’s toes. If the other boys chattered about the Lord Mayor’s Show? I was a millionaire merchant prince, lending nobles millions from my counting house and marrying their daughters. If I heard a snippet of conversation about a new novel? I was a world-famous author, adored by everyone, no grumpy reviewers in sight. The only fly in all this grand glory? My handwriting was atrocious and spelling was a total mystery to me, writing pages full of blots and crossed-out words till I nearly gave up on being a famous author entirely.

No matter which grand path to fame I daydreamed about, though, I understood even in my foggy boy brain that education was the key I needed. So I pored over my textbooks till my eyes ached, sitting at my wobbly one-legged desk in my cold little bedroom, jumping every time my hair got too close to my single candle and started fizzling. On winter nights I’d wear my overcoat buttoned to the chin, wrap a blanket round my legs, and stuff my fingers in my ears to drown out the noise from the street below, droning “amo, amas, amat” over and over till my own voice sounded like a buzzing fly in my ear, and my head felt so small I’d pat it to make sure it was still attached to my shoulders.

Was I stupider than the average boy? Or is a ten to fourteen year old’s brain literally not built to handle the absurd workload our education system dumps on it?

“Latin and Greek,” Doctor Florret would purr in that smooth, correct voice of his, echoing the solemn croak of the entire Victorian establishment, “are excellent mental gymnastics.” My dear silly Doctor Florret, can’t you pull one single useful subject from the thousands that exist that will actually strengthen a kid’s brain instead of making it want to hide under the desk? You, fully grown, with a brain trained for decades to parse your own native language—go dig out a page of Browning, and parse every single line, break every word down into its parts. Would you call that fun for a tear-stained, ink-smeared little Tommy? It’s just as sensible as handing him 100lb dumbbells and telling him to build up his skinny little arms.

Half our time at school was wasted on total nonsense that none of us would ever use, all of us sons of clerks, shopkeepers, minor civil servants and small professional men who were going to spend our whole lives in the regular workaday world. I hated those old dead poets with every fibre of my being. Homer? I wished those fishermen who met him had asked him that stupid riddle about the thing they caught and didn’t catch a few years earlier, so he’d have dropped dead before writing a single line of the Iliad. Horace? Why couldn’t that shipwreck he wrote about have actually drowned him, like it would have done for any normal person not labelled a “classic”?

Until one blessed, glorious day, I found a magic talisman. Listen up, all you little overburdened schoolboys: don’t waste your pocket money on marbles or sweets, save up. Somewhere near Paternoster Row there’s a very clever bookseller who will sell you a little thing called a “translation key” for a few pence, that flings open the gates of all that stuffy old Greek and Latin nonsense and turns it into a story you actually understand. With that key, Aristophanes’ Frogs were my silly, goofy friends, and I wandered through Odysseus’ adventures like they were my own. Doctor Florret was delighted with my sudden “progress”, and for once it was real progress—I finally got to understand the story first, instead of wasting three hours picking apart each word for no reason.

I can’t take credit for the discovery, though. It was Dan—big Dan, strong Dan, soft-smiling Dan, the laziest, sharpest lad who ever hated useless work—who showed me the trick. Dan turned up in the Lower Fourth a term after me, and within a week he was handling Latin verse like he’d made a deal with the devil. We snuck off to a quiet corner of Regent’s Park once, he checked to make sure no one was within earshot, and spilled the beans.

“Don’t tell the rest of the lads,” he told me, “they’ll blab it, and then no one will get away with it.”
“But is that allowed?” I asked him, all nervous and proper.
“Look here, young ‘un,” he said, “what’s your dad paying all these school fees for? Are you here to play a silly game against the masters, or are you here to actually learn something? If it’s just a game, fine, we play by their rules. If it’s to learn? You’ve been in this class four months, I’ve been here a week, and I already know more Ovid than you do.” Which was 100% true.

So I thanked him, we shared our keys, and all the Latin I still remember to this day I owe to Dan. Even more than that, I owe him the common sense that balanced out all my silly, daydreamy nonsense—he was my best friend, and being the mate of big, wise Dan, who could read a bloke’s character in two seconds, is still one of my proudest achievements. He said he liked me because I was “jolly green”, a “rummy little mug”, and he’d never explain further, just ruffle my hair with his great big hand. I was a soft, shy little thing back then, pink and white, hair that would never lie flat no matter how much water I dumped on it, growing so fast I ached all over. Every morning I was up at six, caught the 7:15 train from Poplar, walked two miles from Chalk Farm to school. But I never minded that walk, because Dan was always waiting for me at Chalk Farm station, and he’d walk back home with me in the afternoon too. When my back ached so bad it felt like someone had cut a chunk out of it, he’d sling his big arm round my shoulders, and leaning against him was as comfy as sinking into an armchair. I swear, walking next to that big solid fellow, looking up at his face, I got a tiny little inkling of how a woman can fall in love. It was lovely to feel weak, for once, when you had someone that strong to lean on.

Technically, Dan should have been in the Upper Fourth. He was six inches taller than every other boy in the Lower Third, years older, and the Doctor would yell at him in front of the whole class twenty times a week for wasting his potential. Old Waterhouse, the form master who secretly adored him, was genuinely upset that he wouldn’t move up. All the rest of us were a bit miffed that our hero was stuck down in our lowly class. The only person perfectly happy about it was Dan. The Lower Third suited him just fine. The Upper Fourth had nothing he wanted, and no one was going to make him go there.

He deliberately picked the very last seat in the back row, nearest the door, and fought to keep it no matter what. It meant he could be first out the door at playtime and last in when the bell rang, and he was so far back most teachers forgot he was even there. He was almost always bottom of the class, and he preferred it that way. Most of the tedious questions about participles, square roots, or meridians never even made it to him, snatched up by the swots waving their hands in the front row. The odd one that drifted back he’d just ignore, like it was an annoying fly buzzing near his ear.

Only once did he lose that perfect seat. Caught off guard, he accidentally blurted out the correct answer to a geography question, and before he knew it he was dragged up to the exposed front bench, fuming so bad if any of us had dared to whisper congratulations he’d have punched all our heads in. Old Waterhouse was over the moon, rubbing his hands together like he’d won the pools. “Come on, Brian!” he said, “you’re not half the fool you pretend to be, are you?”
“Never said I was,” Dan muttered, staring longingly back at his empty little nook by the door. By the end of the day, he’d slithered his way back there again.
Later, we passed Old Waterhouse on the stairs. “Haven’t you any sense of shame, my boy?” he asked sadly, laying a kind hand on Dan’s shoulder.
Dan gave him that bright, unapologetic smile of his. “Plenty, sir. It just isn’t yours, that’s all.”

He was the best fighter in the whole school, bar maybe three or four Upper Sixth boys old enough to wear top hats and carry umbrellas, who wouldn’t fight out of sheer dignity. But he almost never fought, and when he did he did it like he was doing you a huge favour out of pure boredom. One winter afternoon, we were about to walk through Regent’s Park when a big errand boy with an empty basket and two little sidekicks blocked our path.
“Can’t come in here,” the boy said.
“Why not?” Dan asked.
“‘Cos if you do, I’m gonna kick you,” he explained simply.
Dan just turned around, ready to walk round to the next gate. The errand boy, emboldened, followed us. “Now I’m gonna give you your coward’s blow,” he said, stepping right up to us. “You gonna take it quiet?”
Dan stopped short. “I’ll tell you afterwards.”
The boy slapped him gently on the cheek. No one’s honour could survive that, according to every schoolboy rule we had.
“Is that all?” Dan asked.
“That’s all—for now,” the boy said.
“Goodbye,” Dan said, and we walked off.
“Glad he didn’t insist on a scrap,” Dan said cheerfully as we went. “I’m going to a party tonight, don’t wanna mess my new shirt.”
But a week later, off Lisson Grove, he picked a fight with a street tough half again his size, who’d knocked his hat off into the mud. “I wouldn’t have said a word about him knocking it off,” Dan explained afterwards, tenderly brushing the muck off his poor battered hat with his coat sleeve, “if he hadn’t kicked it.”

Another sweltering summer afternoon, a group of us were trekking out to Hadley Woods, when Dan pulled a massive, juicy pear out of his pocket. “Got it from the greengrocer’s opposite Barnet Church,” he said, and offered us a slice.
“You told me you had no money left!” one of the boys, Dudley, said, outraged.
“Don’t have any money left.”
“Then how’d you buy the pear?”
“Didn’t buy it.”
“You stole it? You’re a thief!”
Dan blinked at him. “Stealing’s stealing. You robbed that orchard at Mill Hill last Wednesday, ate all the apples and gave yourself stomach ache. You’re a thief too.”
“That’s not stealing!” Dudley protested.
“Then what is it?” Dan asked, and no one could explain the difference to him. We all took a bite of the pear, all of us pretending to be deeply shocked while we chewed. None of us argued back very hard.

As the end of year exams rolled around, I was a nervous wreck. My mum and dad never said a word, but I knew they were waiting anxiously for the results—Dad to see what I’d learned, Mum to see how hard I’d tried. I’d worked my socks off, but I knew prizes never went to the kid who knows the most stuff, they go to the kid who’s best at convincing the examiner they know all the stuff. Same rule applies to every prize after school, if you ask me.
“Are you going for any of the prizes, Dan?” I asked him, tramping over Primrose Hill one bright June morning. I knew the question was daft, but I wanted him to ask me about my own plans.
“They’re not giving away anything I particularly want,” he drawled. Looked at from that angle, school prizes really aren’t worth all the stress and sweat, are they?
“Are you sweating your guts out for something, young ‘un?” he asked, just like I knew he would.
“I’m going to go for the History prize,” I admitted. “Wish I was better at all the stupid dates.”
“It’s two thirds dates, guaranteed,” Dan said, crushing my hopes a little. “Old Florret thinks you can’t eat a potato unless you know exactly what year Walter Raleigh was born.”
“I prayed really hard I’d win the History prize,” I told him. I never felt shy telling Dan anything, he never laughed at me.
He frowned. “You shouldn’t have done that. That’s not fair to the other lads. That’d mean you’re not winning the prize on your own merits, you’re just getting it through favouritism.”
“But they can pray too!” I protested.
“If all of you pray for it,” Dan said, “then it’s not gonna go to the bloke who knows the most history. It’s gonna go to the bloke who prayed the hardest. That’s definitely not what old Florret intended, is it?”
And no argument I ever came up with, then or years later, could talk him round. He judged everything entirely by his own common sense. For him, Achilles wasn’t a hero, he was a coward. “He should’ve told the Trojans they could only hurt him in the heel, and let ‘em take a shot at it,” he argued. “All those old heroes with magic swords and magic armour? Cheats, the lot of ‘em. Fighting when you know you can’t lose? Where’s the pluck in that? Total cads, all of them.”

I didn’t win any prize that year. Oddly enough, Dan did—for arithmetic, the only subject in the whole school that ever interested him. He said he liked it because you knew all the numbers were going to end up right, no nonsense.
My dad shut himself in the study with me for half an hour, quizzing me on everything. “It’s very curious, Paul,” he said, “you seem to know a great deal.”
“They asked me all the stuff I didn’t know!” I blurted out, and buried my face in my arm. Dad crossed the room, sat down next to me, and used that silly old childhood nickname he hadn’t called me in years. “Spud,” he said, “I think maybe you’re going to turn out like me. One of the unlucky ones.”
“Are you unlucky?” I asked, surprised.
“Always,” he said, rumpling his hair. “I try hard. I do the right thing, and it always turns out wrong. Without fail.”
“But I thought Mr Hasluck was bringing us all good luck!” I said. “We’re doing well, aren’t we?”
“I’ve thought that so many times before,” he said, “and it always ends in a collapse.”
I threw my arms round his neck, because I always felt like Dad was just another bigger, older boy, not that much older than me. “You’ll get all the luck back soon!” I cried.
