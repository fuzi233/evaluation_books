Long winter evenings passed in our cozy study, filled with stories told by the boys and Jenny as we drew, wove, and plaited beneath the soft glow of candlelight. It was a season of quiet joy, so peaceful that when spring finally returned—when the rain ceased and the sun warmed the earth once more—we could hardly believe we had been confined within our rocky home for so long.

Now life burst into motion again. The fields, garden, and orchard called to the boys, while their mother and sister busied themselves with poultry and household tasks. Our scattered settlements—Falconhurst, Woodlands, Prospect Hill, Shark Island, Whale Island—each needed attention. Jack and Franz were assigned the task of tending to the island battery.

They spent the entire day repairing the flagstaff, hoisting the flag, and preparing the two cannons. As evening approached, the rest of us strolled along the beach, enjoying the cool sea breeze. We watched as the boys finished their work: they loaded the guns, launched a small tub in the cajack, and floated it out as a target. Then they fired—crash!—and the barrel shattered. With shouts of triumph, they cleaned the guns and pulled them back into place.

No sooner had they done than three sharp gunshots echoed across the water from the west.

We froze. Was it real? A ship? Or had the boys fired again? No—it was clearly not them. Jack and Franz leapt into their canoe and paddled toward us at full speed, having heard the sound too.

A storm of emotions swept through us—fear, hope, doubt, joy—all clashing together. Could this be a European vessel near our shores? Would we soon reconnect with civilization? Or was it a pirate ship, ready to attack? What kind of people would we meet—friends, enemies, or desperate souls in need?

Before anyone could speak, the canoe reached shore. “Did you hear them?” Jack gasped. “What do we do?”

“Father!” cried Fritz, his eyes shining. “It must be an English ship. We’ll find her. We’ll see England again!” He seized his brother’s hands, overwhelmed with emotion.

Until that moment, I hadn’t realized how deeply the presence of their sister, now grown into a young woman, had stirred their longing for home.

All eyes turned to me. What should we do?

“We can do nothing tonight,” I said. “Night is falling. Let’s prepare what we can—and pray.”

Back at Rockburg, excitement buzzed like bees in a hive. No one could sleep. Jack and I took turns watching from the veranda, listening for more signals or signs of danger. But around midnight, the wind began to rise. By morning, a fierce storm raged across the bay. Waves crashed violently against the cliffs. Even if another ship had been nearby, we wouldn’t have heard a shot over the roar of the gale.

For two days and nights, the hurricane pounded the coast. On the third day, the sun broke through. The wind calmed, and the sea subsided. Still anxious, I agreed with the boys’ wish to go to Shark Island and fire the guns again. Perhaps the ship had run aground—or perhaps it had fled into open waters. We couldn’t know.

So I went with Jack and Franz to the fort. One… two… we fired. We waited.

Minutes passed. Then—a distant reply rolled across the water.

There was no doubt now. The strangers were still there. We waved the flag to signal safety, then hurried back.

The family was in a frenzy. I tried to calm them, though I could not answer their questions. I couldn’t hide the truth: the ship might not bring good news.

Fritz and I decided to scout the area. We armed ourselves with guns, pistols, cutlasses, and took a spyglass. In the cajack, we paddled out of the bay, past the high cliffs, heading toward where the shots had come from.

For nearly an hour, we saw only jagged rocks and churning waves. Then we reached Cape Pug-Nose and began rounding the point.

In an instant, all fear vanished. Joy flooded our hearts. There, tucked in a sheltered cove beyond the cape, lay a brig-of-war—her sails furled, anchor dropped, the British flag flying proudly.

Through the glass, I saw figures on deck and tents set up on the shore, smoke curling from fires among the trees. But a sudden unease gripped me. Why was an English ship here, far from known routes? Could it be mutineers hiding out, disguised and ready to raid?

Then Fritz exclaimed, “Father! I see the captain—he’s speaking to an officer. His face is clear. He’s English! I’m sure of it!”

He handed me the glass. I looked. The ship was neat, orderly. Decks gleamed. Brass and steel shone. Discipline reigned. My fears melted away.

We studied the scene carefully, then decided not to reveal ourselves yet. We didn’t want to appear as ragged castaways, but as masters of our land, ready to greet visitors on equal terms.

We returned to Rockburg, where the family waited anxiously. We told them everything. They approved our caution—even Jenny, whose heart had soared at the thought of seeing Englishmen again, agreed to wait until tomorrow.

That day, we prepared. Our little yacht was scrubbed clean, her brass polished, her decks cleared of clutter. The English flag flew proudly from her peak.

My wife sorted through our clothes. Neat uniforms were laid out for the boys and me. Though we had lived simply—wearing wide-brimmed hats, linen shirts, leather belts, and sturdy boots—we knew such attire might shock the sailors. So we dressed properly for the occasion.

At dawn, we gathered in the breakfast room. The meal was eaten quietly, our minds too full for words. Afterward, Fritz and Jack brought baskets of fresh fruit—bananas, oranges, pineapples—from our gardens. These we carried aboard the yacht as gifts.

We weighed anchor. Sails unfurled. The little boat danced over the water, towing the canoe behind. We rounded the Reef, avoided its dangerous rocks, and headed toward the cove.

When we rounded the cape, the strangers were stunned. Here, in this wild, inhospitable land, came a sleek pleasure yacht, manned by a family of settlers, sailing with dignity and grace.

Fritz and I rowed to the brig. The captain greeted us warmly, leading us into his cabin.

“Tell me,” he said, “how did you come to live here? This coast is supposed to be deserted—or ruled by savages.”

I told him our story—the wreck, our survival, our life on the island, and how we had rescued Miss Montrose.

Then he rose, grasping Fritz’s hand. “Let me thank you, on behalf of Colonel Montrose. He has not given up hope of finding his daughter. Three weeks ago, survivors from the *Dorcas* told me of four boats leaving the wreck—only one reached land. I came searching. And now—I’ve found her.”

Fritz humbly accepted the praise. Then the captain asked to meet my wife and Jenny.

“And,” he added, “if it’s not against your rules, I’d like to send a boat for the rest of your party.”

An officer was dispatched. Soon, my wife, Jenny, and the other boys arrived aboard.

The crew welcomed us warmly. Officers vied to honor us. They were gracious hosts. At luncheon, the captain mentioned that he had sailed from Sydney with Mr. Wolston—an invalid gentleman—his wife, and two daughters. The voyage had not helped Mr. Wolston’s health. The storm had worsened his condition, and he longed to rest on land.

We were eager to meet them. That afternoon, we visited their camp under the trees. Mr. Wolston sat by a tent, enjoying the breeze. When we arrived, he and his family were delighted.

We stayed late into the evening. It was too dark to return to Rockburg, so the captain kindly offered tents. The boys slept ashore.

That night, I had a long talk with my wife. Should we return to Europe? It would be foolish to leave just because a chance arose.

We both hesitated—afraid to voice our true feelings. But slowly, we realized neither truly wished to leave. Our hearts belonged to New Switzerland.

She told me she wanted to stay, as long as I and at least two sons chose to remain. If others left, she would accept it—but only if they promised to send skilled emigrants to help build a colony. She suggested the island keep its name, even if future settlers came from England.

I agreed wholeheartedly. We would discuss it with Captain Littlestone, and ask that the island fall under British protection.

Then came the question: who would stay, and who would go?

We decided to let the boys choose.

After breakfast, it was proposed that the captain bring his ship into Safety Bay, so we could host a visit at Rockburg. We invited Mr. Wolston and his family, hoping the change would improve his health.

No sooner was the plan made than Fritz and Jack raced off in the canoe to prepare. The brig and our yacht followed.

When our guests rounded the Rocky Cape, their amazement knew no bounds. Before them lay Safety Bay—our beautiful estate, Rockburg.

And then—eleven cannon boomed from Shark Island. The Union Jack fluttered proudly in the wind.

Faces lit up with wonder and joy. Poor Wolston seemed to revive at the sight of peace and comfort.

He was carried ashore gently and settled into my room. A camp bed was added for Mrs. Wolston, so she could care for him.

At the harbor, excitement ran wild. Guests explored every corner of our home. Dinner was barely noticed.

But later, when a tour of Falconhurst was planned, everyone finally sat down. Yet even at supper, the young ones wandered through lawns and gardens, making the place seem thronged with visitors.

By evening, calm returned. Mr. Wolston joined us, stronger and more hopeful. He declared his desire to stay, at least for a time. His wife and eldest daughter—both fragile in health—would remain. The younger daughter would go to her brother at the Cape.

If he chose to settle permanently, he would send his son to join us.

I welcomed the idea with great joy. “New Switzerland will be our home,” I said.

“Hurrah for New Switzerland!” the company shouted, raising glasses in a joyful toast. “Prosperity to New Switzerland! Long may she flourish!”

Ernest surprised us all. “Long life and happiness to those who make New Switzerland their home!” he said, ringing his glass with mine, my wife’s, and Mr. Wolston’s.

Jenny smiled. “Won’t someone cheer for England and Colonel Montrose?” she asked playfully. “I miss my father, but I’ll feel torn if all the cheers are for staying!”

Fritz laughed. “Three cheers for England! Success and happiness to those returning to Europe!” The hall rang with cheers.

Jenny looked at him gratefully—knowing he understood her longing.

I spoke. “Since Fritz chooses England, he shall carry this dear daughter back to her father—whom I’ve loved as my own. Ernest stays with me. His mother and I rejoice. He shall have every scientific post we can offer.”

Then I asked Jack. “You’re a fine comic actor. You must go to Europe to shine on the stage.”

“No,” Jack replied. “I’m staying. When Fritz leaves, I’ll be the best rider and sharpest shot in New Switzerland. That’s enough for me. I’m afraid of European schools—I’d get caught and forced into one!”

Franz stepped forward. “I want a good school. Among students, there’s competition, progress. I can rise. Fritz may return someday. But one of us should go home—and stay. I’m youngest. I can adapt better. But Father will decide.”

“You may go,” I said. “God bless all our plans. The world belongs to the Lord. Wherever you live faithfully, there is your home.”

Then I turned to Captain Littlestone. “Will you help them fulfill their choices?”

He paused. Then spoke with conviction:

“My orders were to search for survivors. Two wrecks found. Three passengers wish to stay. Three wish to go. It fits perfectly. I gladly take charge of those entrusted to me. Everything has been ordered by Providence. If England gains a thriving colony, it will be the perfect ending to this chain of blessings. Three cheers for New Switzerland!”

Emotion swelled in every heart. Many felt they stood at the edge of a new life. For me, a heavy burden lifted. I thanked God for resolving a worry that had haunted me for years.

From that moment, all thoughts turned to preparations. The departure was brief, but we worked tirelessly. Every comfort was packed—clothes, books, tools. A rich cargo of pearls, corals, furs, spices—enough to give our children a strong start in England.

I gave them private papers, money, and jewels belonging to our ship’s captain—asking them to deliver them to his heirs if possible. I also gave Captain Littlestone a short account of the wreck, a list of the crew.

Our gratitude overflowed. We supplied the *Unicorn* with fresh food, fish, vegetables, and fruit—anything we could spare.

In a long talk with my sons, I warned them of life’s dangers—temptations of wealth, pride, ambition. I urged them to hold fast to faith, duty, and love.

And so, with hearts full of hope and farewell, we watched as the ship set sail—carrying part of our family back to the world they once knew, while we remained, rooted in the soil we had built into a new home.
