When Romola reached the threshold of San Marco, a friar awaited her. Monna Brigida withdrew into the adjacent church, and Romola was ushered to the door of the chapter-house in the outer cloister, the boundary beyond which no woman was permitted to pass.

As the heavy door swung open, the dim light from outside merged with the glow of two tapers set behind a narrow bed. There lay Fra Luca, his face wasted to an emaciated skeleton, the golden tonsure framing a skull-like visage. His deep-sunken hazel eyes were fixed upon a small crucifix held before him. He was propped up in a semi-sitting position. As Romola threw aside her veil, she became aware of another monk standing beside the bed, his black cowl pulled low, who retreated toward the door as she entered. In the background, a crucified figure loomed pale and high on the frescoed wall, its lower half surrounded by ghostly, sorrowful faces.

Then, her eyes met Fra Luca's as he lifted his gaze from the cross. A sharp pang of recognition struck her, binding this haggard monastic form to the memory of her fair young brother.

"Dino!" she cried, her voice a low, pained exclamation. Yet she did not lean toward him; she remained rigid, holding her distance at two yards. An insurmountable repulsion gripped her at the sight of him in this garb. To her, it seemed the mark of a cowardly abandonment that had left her father desolate—a groveling superstition that could rebrand such betrayal as piety. Her father, a man of proud sincerity and simple living, had raised her to ignore the Church's claim to govern the minds of rational beings. In her view, the Church belonged to the chaotic masses from which they had always separated. Consequently, she felt nothing but incurious, indignant contempt for her brother's path. Still, the loving nature of her soul clung to the image of the past, and despite her rigid stance, a yearning search flickered in her eyes for something too faint to grasp.

But the monk's face offered no such warmth. He regarded the little sister, returned to him in her full womanly beauty, with the distant gaze of a spirit revisiting a lost world.

"My sister!" he managed, his voice feeble and broken yet distinct. "It is well you have not delayed further. I have a message for you, and my time is short."

Romola stepped closer. She expected a confession of affectionate penitence directed at her father. Her heart began to soften; nothing could erase the long years of desertion, but a sinner looking back with the weight of irremediable wrong might evoke pity. At the end, surely, there would be understanding and forgiveness. She imagined Dino pouring out natural filial feelings, asking about his father's blindness, the rapidity of its onset, the long dark days, and the life in the home where he had been nourished. She anticipated a final message of tenderness and regret.

"Romola," Fra Luca began, "I have had a vision concerning you. Three times in the last two months, and each time it has grown clearer. I came from Fiesole believing it a divine mandate to deliver this. And I find a promise of mercy in this, that my breath is preserved to—"

His speech was cut short by the difficult, rattling breath that constantly interrupted him.

Romola felt her heart chill. It was a vision, then—a message born of those delusions her father had bitterly alluded to. Indignation surged in her throat.

"Dino, I thought you had words for my father. You abandoned him when his sight failed; you made his life a desert. Did you never care? Never repent? What is this religion of yours that places visions above natural duties?"

The deep-sunken eyes turned slowly toward her, resting upon her in silence for several moments, as if weighing the cost of an answer.

"No," he said finally, in a low, passionless tone, sounding less like a dying man and more like a spirit speaking through mortal organs. "No. I have never repented for fleeing the stifling, poisonous breath of sin that choked me, threatening to overwhelm my senses like besotting wine. My father could not hear the voice calling me night and day; he knew nothing of the demon-tempters trying to drag me back. He lived amidst human sin and misery without believing in them, like one busy picking shining stones in a mine while a world died of plague above him. I spoke, but he listened with scorn. I told him the studies he wished me to pursue were either childish trifles or must be fueled by worldly ambition and fleshly lusts—the very substances of the poetry and history he demanded I read."

"Has my father not led a pure and noble life?" Romola burst out, unable to remain silent against this implied accusation. "He sought no worldly honors; he was truthful; he denied himself all luxuries; he lived like an ancient sage. He never wished you to live for worldly ambitions or lusts. He wanted you to live as he did, according to the purest maxims of philosophy, in which he raised you."

Romola spoke partly from rote, as ardent young creatures often do, but with intense conviction. A pink flush colored her cheeks, and she quivered from head to foot. Her brother was slow to answer, regarding her passionate face with strange, unfeeling eyes.

"What were the maxims of philosophy to me? They told me to be strong when I felt weak; to roll myself among thorns and court smarting wounds as deliverance from temptation. For Divine love had sought me, penetrating me and creating a great need within me, like a seed demanding room to grow. I was raised in carelessness of the true faith; I had not studied our doctrines, yet they seemed to possess me like a rising flood. I felt there existed a life of perfect love and purity for the soul, free from uneasy hunger for pleasure, tormenting questions, or fear of suffering. Before knowing the history of the saints, I had a foreshadowing of their ecstasy. The same truth permeated even pagan philosophy: that it is a bliss within man's reach to die to mortal needs and live in the life of God as the Unseen Perfection. But to attain this, I must forsake the world. I must have no affection, no hope wedded to that which passes away. I must live with my fellow-beings only as human souls related to the eternal unseen life. This need urged me continually. It came over me in visions when my mind grew weary of the vain words recording the passions of dead men; it seized me after I had been tempted into sin and turned away with loathing from the scent of the emptied cup. In these visions, I saw the meaning of the Crucifix."

He paused, breathing hard for a minute or two. Romola did not speak. It was futile for her mind to attempt contact with this unearthly brother, as useless as trying to grasp a shadow. When he spoke again, his heaving chest was quieter.

"I felt whom I must follow. But I saw that even among the servants of the Cross who claimed to have renounced the world, my soul would be stifled by the fumes of hypocrisy, lust, and pride. God had not chosen me, as He chose Saint Dominic and Saint Francis, to wrestle with evil in the Church and the world. He called upon me to flee. I took the sacred vows and I fled—to lands where danger, scorn, and want bore me continually, like angels, to repose on the bosom of God. I lived as a hermit, ministering to pilgrims, but my task was short. The veil dividing me from my everlasting rest has worn very thin. I returned to Florence that—"

"Dino, you *did* want to know if my father was alive," Romola interrupted, the picture of that suffering life stirring in her again, sparking a desire for union and forgiveness.

"—That before I died, I might urge our brethren to study Eastern tongues, as I failed to do, and go forth to greater ends. I find them already bent on the work. And since I came, Romola, I have felt I was sent partly to you—not to renew bonds of earthly affection, but to deliver the heavenly warning conveyed in a vision. For I have had that vision thrice. Through all the years since the Divine voice first called me, while I was still in the world, I have been taught and guided by visions. For in the painful linking of our waking thoughts, we can never be sure we have not mingled our own errors with the light we prayed for; but in visions and dreams, we are passive, and our souls become instruments in the Divine hand. Therefore, listen, and speak no more—for the time is short."

Romola's mind recoiled from listening to this vision. Her indignation had subsided, only because she felt the chasm between herself and her brother widening. As Fra Luca spoke, the figure of another monk entered, standing on the opposite side of the bed with his cowl drawn low.

"Kneel, my daughter, for the Angel of Death is present and waits while the message of heaven is delivered. Bend your pride before it is bent for you by a yoke of iron," a strong, rich voice declared, startlingly contrasting with Fra Luca's frailty.

The tone was not imperious but one of quiet self-possession and assured right, blended with benignity. Romola, vibrating at the sound, turned to the figure on the other side. His face was barely discernible under the shadow of the cowl, but her eyes fell immediately on his hands, folded across his breast and resting on the edge of his black mantle. They possessed a marked physiognomy that enforced the voice's authority: very beautiful, almost of transparent delicacy. Romola's disposition to rebel against command, doubly active in the presence of monks she had been taught to despise, would have latched onto any repulsive detail as a point of support. But the face was hidden, and the hands seemed to hold an appeal against all hardness. The next moment, the right hand took the crucifix to relieve Fra Luca's fatigued grasp, while the left touched his lips with a wet sponge lying nearby. As he bent, the cowl was pushed back, revealing the features of the monk to the full light of the tapers. They were striking features, lending themselves to popular description: a high-arched nose, a prominent under-lip, a coronet of thick dark hair above the brow—all suggesting energy and passion. There were blue-grey eyes, shining mildly beneath auburn lashes, seeming, like the hands, to radiate acute sensitiveness. Romola was certain these were the features of Fra Girolamo Savonarola, the prior of San Marco, whom she had chiefly considered more offensive than other monks because he was more noisy. Her rebellion rose against the first impression, which had almost forced her to kneel.

"Kneel, my daughter," the penetrating voice repeated. "The pride of the body is a barrier against the gifts that purify the soul."

He looked at her with mild fixedness, and again she felt that subtle, mysterious influence of personality by which rare men move their fellows. Slowly, Romola fell to her knees. In the very act, a tremor seized her; in the renunciation of her proud erectness, her mental attitude seemed transformed, and she found herself in a new state of passiveness.

Her brother began to speak again.

"Romola, in the deep night, as I lay awake, I saw my father's room—the library—with all the books and the marbles and the leggio where I used to stand and read. And I saw you—you were revealed to me as I see you now, with fair long hair, sitting before my father's chair. And at the leggio stood a man whose face I could not see. I looked, and looked, and it was a blank to me, even as a painting effaced. I saw him move and take you, Romola, by the hand; and then I saw you take my father by the hand; and you all three went down the stone steps into the streets, the man whose face was a blank leading the way. You stood at the altar in Santa Croce, and the priest who married you had the face of death. The graves opened, and the dead in their shrouds rose and followed you like a bridal train. You passed on through the streets and gates into the valley, and it seemed to me that he who led you hurried you more than you could bear, and the dead were weary of following you, turning back to their graves. Finally, you came to a stony place where there was no water, no trees, no herbage. Instead of water, I saw written parchment unrolling everywhere; instead of trees and herbage, I saw men of bronze and marble springing up and crowding round you. My father was faint for want of water and fell to the ground. The man whose face was a blank loosed your hand and departed. As he went, I could see his face; it was the face of the Great Tempter. You, Romola, wrung your hands and sought for water, but there was none. The bronze and marble figures seemed to mock you and hold out cups of water. When you grasped them and put them to my father's lips, they turned to parchment. The bronze and marble figures seemed to turn into demons and snatch my father's body from you, and the parchments shriveled up, and blood ran everywhere instead of them, and fire upon the blood, till they all vanished, and the plain was bare and stony again, and you were alone in the midst of it. Then it seemed that the night fell, and I saw no more... Thrice I have had that vision, Romola. I believe it is a revelation meant for you: to warn you against marriage as a temptation of the enemy; it calls upon you to dedicate yourself—"

His pauses had grown longer and more frequent, until he was compelled to cease by a severe fit of gasping, his eyes turned on the crucifix as on a light vanishing. Presently, he found strength to speak again, but in a feebler, scarcely audible tone.
