The dinner was served just as Arthur burst in, looking like a man who’d been chased by a bear and lost the race. Lady Rockminster, ever the queen of punctuality, began to scold him for his tardiness—until Laura, peering at her cousin with the sharp eye of a hawk trained on emotional distress, noticed that Arthur’s face was paler than a ghost’s handkerchief and his eyes were wide enough to swallow a small village.

“Arthur!” she exclaimed, cutting off the old lady mid-rant. “Are you ill? What has happened?”

Arthur gulped down a whole glass of sherry like it was a potion to cure existential dread. “I’ve heard something extraordinary,” he said, glancing nervously at the servants. “But I’ll tell you later.”

He spent the rest of the meal twitching under the table like a squirrel trying to hide from a cat. Lady Rockminster, meanwhile, was losing patience faster than a teapot loses steam.

“Stop stamping your feet!” she snapped. “You’ve trodden on Fido, and knocked over his saucer. Look at Mr. Warrington—he keeps his boots still, as if he were made of marble!”

At dessert—the eternal torture of dinners that never end—Lady Rockminster sighed dramatically. “This has been the most tedious dinner in history. I suppose something’s happened, and you’re all huddled together plotting secrets. Well, I’m going to bed. No tea for me tonight. Good night, Mr. Warrington. Do come again—when there’s no business to discuss.”

With that, she swept out of the room like a storm cloud retreating after a thunderclap. George and the others followed, but Warrington paused, about to bid farewell to Laura, when Arthur stopped him with a hand like a vice.

“Wait, George,” Arthur said. “You must hear this too. It’s urgent. I need your advice.”

Laura’s heart skipped a beat. Her cheeks flared up like sunrise over a volcano. “It’s about Blanche?” she whispered, barely audible.

“Yes,” Arthur replied, “and it’s the most unbelievable story.” He leaned forward, lowering his voice. “When I left you to go to my uncle’s lodgings, I found Morgan—the servant who’s been with him for years—at the door. He told me my uncle had left the house earlier that day, gone to an hotel. I came in, asked for him—gone out to dinner. Then Morgan said he had something *very* important to tell me. So I went into the house—now *his* house, apparently—and he dropped the bombshell: Blanche’s father is not dead. He’s alive. And the marriage between Clavering and the Begum? Not a marriage at all.”

Warrington blinked. “So Blanche inherits everything?”

“Possibly,” Arthur said. “But let’s think about who her father is. Amory—Clavering knows it, my uncle knows it—is an escaped convict. He wasn’t drowned in New South Wales, as Blanche thought. He was a prisoner, not a ship captain. Lady Clavering told me they weren’t happy. She said she’d tell me everything one day. And once, she wept and said it was hard to admit she was glad her husband was dead. Twice in her life, she said, she’d chosen badly.”

A long silence fell. The candle flames flickered like nervous witnesses.

“And now,” Arthur continued, “this convict has returned. But he can’t come forward. If he does, he’ll be caught and sent back to the hulks. But he’s been holding this secret over Clavering’s head—extorting money from him for years. And now, Morgan—who knows everything—has decided to sell the secret to *us*. He even asked me for an annuity to keep quiet. Said I was trafficking with the Begum’s misfortune, and would extort a seat in Parliament from Clavering. As if I’d stoop to such treachery!”

He looked furious enough to start a war. “My uncle—my own flesh and blood—was involved in this! Trading on a woman’s misery for political gain! Can you imagine? Our mother’s son—me—dealing in such dishonour?”

Laura clutched his hand. “Oh, Arthur,” she said softly. “I can’t believe it.”

Warrington cleared his throat. His voice trembled. “No,” he said. “Arthur Pendennis cannot marry a convict’s daughter and sit in Parliament as a member for the hulks. You must break it off. No explanations. Just say family reasons make it impossible. Better that those poor women think you’re faithless than know the truth. And I’ll get Clavering to sign a letter acknowledging your decision. It’s better than being tied to a lie.”

He didn’t dare look at Laura. He knew what he was saying was the end of his own hopes. The wave of loss crashed over him. He buried his face in his hands.

Arthur stood up suddenly, eyes locked on Warrington. Then he turned away.

Laura rose, walked to Arthur, took his hand, and kissed it. “She thinks so too,” George murmured. “God bless her.”

“Her father’s shame isn’t Blanche’s fault,” Laura said, pale but firm. “If you were married, would you leave her because she did nothing wrong? Are you not pledged to her? Would you abandon her in misfortune? And if she’s unhappy, wouldn’t you comfort her? Our mother would have done it—if she were here.”

She wrapped her arms around him. “Our mother is an angel with God,” Arthur sobbed. “And you are the dearest, best woman—pure heart. Teach me my duty. Pray for me. God bless you—God bless you, my sister!”

“Amen,” Warrington groaned, head in hands. “She’s right. She can’t do any wrong. That girl… she looks like an angel.”

And indeed, she did. He would remember that smile for years—her radiant face, her curls falling back, blushing and laughing, still gazing fondly at Arthur. For a moment, she rested her hand on the table, tapping it gently. “And now,” she said, turning to the men, “what shall we do?”

George blinked. “What now?”

“We’ll have some tea,” said Miss Laura, smiling.

But before the unromantic conclusion could unfold, a servant arrived with news: Major Pendennis had returned to the hotel and was waiting to see his nephew.

Laura exchanged a glance with Arthur—one that said, *Behave. Be gentle. Be firm. Do your duty.* Then she retreated to her room.

Warrington, who usually avoided tea like the plague, now wished it were still ten minutes away. Why couldn’t the old man have come later? Or sooner? It didn’t matter. The hour struck. The moment came. Farewell. Hand shaken. Door closed. Friend gone. Joy over. You are alone.

“In which window does her light shine?” he wondered, walking down the street. He strode into the smoking-room of a nearby club, lit a cigar, and joined the chaos—men shouting about politics, opera girls, horse races, and the tyranny of the committee. He sat among them, bearing the sacred secret, listening to their jokes and wild stories. How strange, he thought, to stand in the smoke and noise, knowing every man here carries a secret of his own—sitting quietly apart, far from the game.

Arthur, meanwhile, marched through the hotel corridors, anger rising like a tide. He couldn’t believe his uncle had used him as a puppet, compromising his honour, dragging him into this mess.

When he reached the room, the Major was coughing, grumbling about the fire, complaining that Frosch couldn’t bring his dressing gown or arrange his papers properly—unlike that “d——d impudent scoundrel” Morgan.

“The confounded rascal!” the old man muttered. “Drunk last night, challenged me to a duel—begad, I nearly stabbed him. And he’s made ten thousand pounds! Deserves to be hanged. But curse him, I wish he’d lasted out my time. He knew my ways. When I rang the bell, he brought exactly what I wanted. Unlike that stupid German lout.”

Arthur clenched his jaw. “How have you been, Uncle?”

“Been a good deal with Lady Rockminster?” the Major asked. “Can’t do better. She’s of the old school—vieille ecole, bonne ecole, hey? Dammy, they don’t make gentlemen and ladies anymore. In fifty years, you won’t know one man from another. But they’ll last my time. I ain’t long for this world. Getting very old, Pen. My boy. And I was thinking today, packing up my little library—there’s a Bible among the books. Belonged to my poor mother. I’d like you to keep it.”

Arthur felt a pang. The old man’s kindness softened his anger. He knew what he was about to say would shatter the Major’s dreams.

“I went to your lodgings at Bury Street,” Arthur began slowly. “I spoke with Morgan.”

“Indeed?” The Major’s face flushed. “The cat’s out of the bag now, begad!”

“He told me something that shocked and pained me deeply,” Arthur said.

The Major tried to seem casual. “Ah—about—about—what-d’-you-call-’em, hey?”

“About Miss Amory’s father. About Lady Clavering’s first husband. Who he is. What he is.”

“Hem—a dev’lish awkward affair,” the old man said, rubbing his nose. “I—I’ve known about it for some time.”

Arthur sighed. “I wish I had known earlier—or not at all.”

“You’re right,” the Major said, relieved. “There’s no reason for those two poor women to know. I’d never tell them—though that villain Morgan might.”

“He wants to trade on his secret,” Arthur said grimly. “Already asked me for money to stay silent. I wish I’d known this sooner. It’s not pleasant to think I’m engaged to a convict’s daughter.”

“Exactly why I kept it from you, my dear boy,” the Major said. “But Miss Amory isn’t a convict’s daughter. She’s Lady Clavering’s daughter—with fifty or sixty thousand pounds. Her father-in-law, a Baronet, approves the match and gives up his seat. What could be simpler?”

“Is that true?” Arthur asked.

“Begad, yes. Of course it’s true. Amory’s dead. I tell you he’s dead. The first sign of life he shows, he’s dead. We’ve got him at a deadlock, like the fellow in *The Critic*—dev’lish amusing play. Monstrous witty man, Sheridan. By Gad, when I was at the Cape…”

The old man rambled on, clearly trying to avoid the real issue. But Arthur interrupted.

“If you’d told me this sooner,” he said, “you’d have spared us both pain. I’d never have entered into this engagement—knowing what I now know.”

“No, begad, we’ve fixed you,” the Major said. “A man with a seat in Parliament and a pretty girl with two thousand a year? That’s no bad thing.”

“Great heavens, sir!” Arthur cried. “Are you blind? Can’t you see?”

“See what?”

“That rather than trade on this secret,” Arthur said, “I’d join my father-in-law at the hulks! That rather than take a seat in Parliament as a bribe from Clavering, I’d throw the spoons off the table! You’ve given me a felon’s daughter for a wife—doomed me to poverty and shame! You’ve cursed my career! We’ve been playing a guilty game, and we’ve been overreached. I’ve been degrading myself—selling my soul for money and a seat. And now I’ve lost both.”

“What in Heaven’s name do you mean?” the old man shrieked.

“I mean,” Arthur said, “that there’s a line of baseness I will not cross. I’ve sinned—but I will sin no more. I will let Clavering off that bargain. I’ll take no more than Blanche’s original fortune. I’ll try to make her happy. You brought this on me, sir. But you didn’t know better. I forgive you.”

“Arthur—in God’s name—in your father’s name, who was the proudest man alive—don’t fling this chance away! I beg you! On my knees, I implore you! This is your making! You’ll be a Baronet! Three thousand a year! Don’t do this!”

And with that, the old man actually sank to his knees, clutching Arthur’s hand, tears streaming down his wrinkled face.

“Ah, sir,” Arthur said, groaning. “You’ve caused me enough pain. Spare me this. I’ll marry her. For God’s sake, rise!”

“You mean to take her as a beggar?” the Major gasped, rising and coughing violently.

“I see her as a person who’s suffered a great calamity. And I promised her. She can’t help her misfortune. I won’t withdraw my word now. I won’t take Clavering’s seat unless he offers it freely. I won’t take a penny more than her original fortune.”

“Ring the bell,” the old man said, voice breaking. “I’ve said my say. I’m a dev’lish old fellow. And—it doesn’t matter. Shakespeare was right. Cardinal Wolsey—‘And had I but served my God as I’ve served you.’ Yes. On my knees, by Jove, to my own nephew—I mightn’t have been—Good night, sir. You needn’t call again.”

Arthur took his hand. It was cold and limp. The Major looked older, broken. The next day, he stayed in bed, refusing to see anyone.

And so ended the chapter where the truth came out—and the dream died.
