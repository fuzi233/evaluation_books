Captain Shandon, with all the flourish of a man announcing a revolution, unveiled his grand prospectus for *The Pall Mall Gazette*. It was a masterpiece of dramatic outrage—a call to arms for the gentlemen of England, who, according to the Captain, were under siege from every direction: radical upstarts, scheming industrialists, and the unwashed masses who had the audacity to question their betters.

*"The monarchy is sneered at!"* Shandon declared, slapping the paper for emphasis. *"The Church is undermined by dissenters and atheists! Our glorious institutions—those very pillars that made England the envy of the world—are left defenseless against the barbarians who respect neither history nor law!"*

Bungay, the publisher, nodded along like a man who had heard this tune before but was happy to tap his foot to it again. Warrington, meanwhile, burst into laughter.

"You see how stirred our friend Bungay is," Shandon remarked, grinning. *"That’s* the true test. I’ve trotted out the Duke of Wellington and Waterloo a hundred times, and they never fail to rouse the patriotic spirit."

The Captain then lamented how the aristocracy had been *too* secure, *too* indifferent, leaving their interests in the hands of hired pens and political lackeys. But no more! The time had come for gentlemen to take up their own defense—no more letting Grub Street scribblers dictate the narrative.

*"That’s a dig at Bacon’s crew,"* Shandon said, winking at Bungay, who thumped his stick on the floor in approval. *"Oh, give it to 'em, Captain! Nobody slashes like you!"*

The prospectus promised a journal of unimpeachable loyalty—to Crown, Church, and Country—written *by* gentlemen *for* gentlemen. No radical rags or dissenting drivel here! *The Pall Mall Gazette* would be the voice of the elite, the informed, the *right sort*.

Warrington, amused, ruffled the hair of Shandon’s young daughter, Mary, who promptly retreated to the safer company of Arthur Pendennis, playing idly with his watch chain. Pen, flattered by her preference, sat quietly while Shandon continued his grand oration.

*"We cannot, for obvious reasons, reveal our contributors,"* Shandon said, pausing dramatically. Warrington snorted. *"Ah, you laugh! But mark this—we have allies in Parliament, in every embassy in Europe! Our sources are unparalleled! While other papers grovel for scraps, we dine at the table of power!"*

Bungay, who had dozed off twice during this performance, woke long enough to declare it *"all right"* before shuffling out, tossing a penny to little Mary as he left.

Once the business was settled, Shandon launched into sparkling anecdotes about high society—stories of lords, ladies, and scandalous suppers, all delivered with the air of a man who moved in such circles daily. His wife listened, enraptured, believing every word. *Hadn’t he once been invited to dine with an earl?* (The invitation card was still tucked reverently in her sewing box.)

Pen, fascinated and faintly melancholy, watched the scene—the shabby prison room, the hopeful wife, the clever, broken man spinning tales of grandeur between sips of gin. When the party finally dispersed, Shandon bolted for the tavern, the five-pound note from Bungay already burning a hole in his pocket.

Left alone, Mrs. Shandon brewed a pot of tea—that universal solace of disappointed women. Little Mary nibbled a sweet from Jack Finucane, Shandon’s ever-loyal shadow, who dropped by with treats and blind admiration.

*"He won’t keep that five pounds long,"* Bungay predicted as they walked away. He was right. By nightfall, Shandon had settled old tavern debts, lost half-crowns at cards, and returned home as penniless as he had begun.

Later, over brandy, Pen mused on the tragedy of wasted genius.

*"It’s a shame,"* he said, *"a man of his talent, rotting in debtors' prison."*

Warrington scoffed. *"Talent doesn’t excuse vice. If a man drinks away his earnings and ignores his debts, why should he escape consequences? The world owes him nothing."*

Pen argued for compassion. Warrington retorted with brutal practicality. The debate ended as most did—with Warrington ordering another drink and Pen sighing over the romance of doomed brilliance.

That night, as Pen lay awake, thoughts of Shandon’s charm, his wasted potential, and his long-suffering wife swirled in his mind. Meanwhile, Warrington’s snores rumbled next door—a man untroubled by sentiment, or at least very good at pretending to be.
