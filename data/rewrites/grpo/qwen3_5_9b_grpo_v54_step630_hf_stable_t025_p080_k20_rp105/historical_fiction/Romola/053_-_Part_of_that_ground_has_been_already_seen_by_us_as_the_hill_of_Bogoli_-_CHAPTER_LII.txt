To Romola, the chaotic whirlwind of the Carnival seemed to leave no lasting mark upon her life save a singular, heavy duty: the tender, exhausting care of her cousin Brigida, whose spirit wavered between resignation and the frailty of age. Yet, beneath this domestic burden, a Lenten season had begun, one that demanded of her a feverish intensity of mind and a relentless physical exertion.

Bernardo del Nero had been elected Gonfaloniere. Through immense effort, the Medicean faction had secured a victory that now cast a long shadow over Romola's soul. She felt a gnawing presentiment that this triumph was merely the prologue to a secret scheme, poised to either flourish into success or curdle into betrayal within the next two months of her godfather's authority. Each morning, as the pale dawn pierced her curtains, that dread returned, cold and insistent. It accompanied her as she navigated the crowded streets toward the early sermon in the Duomo, a chilling companion until she stepped inside the great cathedral. There, amidst the clash of voices and the fervor of the crowd, the fear dissolved, much as the terror of death vanishes in the heat of battle.

Within the Duomo, Romola felt herself swept into a struggle of passion that transcended the walls of Florence itself. Savonarola was preaching—the final series of Lenten sermons he would ever be permitted to deliver before the threat of exclamation loomed. He knew his end was near, yet he preached with a defiant confidence, holding up the rot of the Church in a mirror of unflinching truth. He spoke of renovation, of a coming revolt against corruption that would shake the very foundations of Christendom. His vision oscillated wildly: at times, he saw himself as the glorious voice of a new age, resurrecting the dead body of the Church; at others, he saw only the path to martyrdom, viewing this life as a mere vigil before the true dawn.

Such a crisis possessed a terrifying gravity for all but the dull-minded, even for Machiavelli, who viewed the Friar through a lens of cynical pragmatism. But for Romola, whose own ardour mirrored Savonarola's, the stakes felt personal. The crisis blended with her daily labours, demanding not just perseverance but a new, fierce courage. Famine still stalked Florence, and the long endurance of distress had hardened the hearts of the people; disease festered in the crowded quarters, and the Plague was whispered to be at the gates. As Romola walked, weary and heavy-hearted, among the sick, the hungry, and the murmuring masses, she found solace not in pity alone, but in the belief that she served a heroic struggle for sublime ends. Her pity was but the dew that prepares the soil for a future harvest, however unseen.

Yet, the mighty music that stirred her in the Duomo was not without dissonance. Since the early days of glowing hope, when the Frate had preached peace and the coming of the French deliverer, the atmosphere had shifted. Political intrigue proved too stubborn for oblivion; the dream of French salvation had crumbled as the King turned his back on his mission; and hostility, both petty and grand, now assailed the Prophet with renewed ferocity.

Consequently, the spirit of contention seeped into his sermons. Urged by popular demand, Savonarola began to rely increasingly on visions and private revelations, adopting a tone of defiant challenge against his objectors. Having once denounced the desire for miracles, he now claimed that Divine power would soon attest his truth through a sign. In the rapid, excited transitions of his speech, as the vision of triumph receded behind the reality of evil, his threats against vicious tyrants and corrupt priests gathered momentum, fueled by personal exasperation as much as indignant zeal.

In the career of a great orator yielding to the moment's inspiration, the hidden conflict of selfish and unselfish emotion is laid bare in letters of fire. While these tones of exasperation jarred Romola, they resonated with a thrilling bass note for another member of the congregation: Baldassarre. He had discovered the Frate was preaching again and attended whenever possible, drinking in the threats of a voice that seemed to champion justice. He watched Romola closely, waiting for the right moment to seek an interview with her, noting how she too sought the Frate's gaze. Twice she had caught sight of his dark, fixed stare in the nave. She wished to avoid it, yet she looked for it, like a superstitious peasant awaiting a portent. Any revelation concerning this old man was now a secondary fear; her anxiety was consumed by the pressing present.

The stirring Lent passed, and April, the final month of her godfather's supreme authority, drew to a close. Nothing had occurred to fulfill her presentiment. Rumors from Rome spoke of menacing activity by Piero de' Medici, but soon the feared Bernardo would depart from power.

It was on the twenty-seventh, as Romola walked out on her afternoon errands of mercy, that a messenger from Camilla Rucellai intercepted her. Camilla, one of Florence's chief feminine seers, demanded Romola's presence immediately on matters of the highest moment. Romola shrank with unconquerable repulsion from the shrill volubility of such "illuminated" women, and her recent knowledge that Camilla had announced revelations hostile to Bernardo del Nero made her instinctively recoil. She was tempted to refuse flatly, to close her ears to knowledge that would only weigh down her spirit. But habit had trained her to reject impulsive cowardice; reproving herself for letting presentiments dictate her actions, she complied and went straight to Camilla.

She found the nervous, grey-haired woman in a chamber arranged like a convent cell. Thin fingers clutched at Romola as she sat, and the eager, loud whisper caused a physical shrinking that made it difficult for her to remain seated.

Camilla had a vision to communicate—a revelation from Romola's Angel stating that Romola knew certain secrets concerning her godfather, Bernardo del Nero, which, if disclosed, could save the Republic. Camilla's voice rose in pitch as she narrated, ending with an exhortation to obey the Angel's command and separate herself from the enemy of God.

Romola's impetuosity was that of a massive nature; usually calm and self-controlled, she possessed a constitutional disgust for the shallow excitability of women like Camilla, whose faculties seemed wrought entirely into fantasy. Before the exhortation concluded, Romola sprang up, attempting to wrench her arm from Camilla's tightening grasp. It was useless. The prophetess held on like a crab, only incited to greater fervor by the resistance, and launched into a shrill declaration of corroborating visions. Christ himself had appeared, ordering her to send commands to citizens in office to throw Bernardo del Nero from the window of the Palazzo Vecchio. Even Fra Girolamo knew of it and had dared not deny its divine authority this time.

"And since then," Camilla cried in her excited treble, straining upward with wild eyes toward Romola's face, "the Blessed Infant has come to me and laid a wafer of sweetness on my tongue in token of his pleasure that I had done his will."

"Let me go!" Romola said, her voice deep with anger. "God grant you are mad! Else you are detestably wicked!"

The violence of her effort finally broke Camilla's hold. Romola wrenched free and rushed out of the room, not pausing until she had hurried far down the street, finding herself close to the church of the Badia. She slipped behind the curtain under the old stone arch, entering a sanctuary shut in from the noise and hurry of the street, where every object suggested the thought of eternal peace amidst turmoil.

She sank down on the step of the altar before Filippino Lippi's serene Virgin appearing to Saint Bernard, waiting for the inward tumult to subside. The thought that pressed most acutely was that Camilla could allege Savonarola's countenance of her wicked folly. Romola did not for a moment believe he had sanctioned the throwing of Bernardo from the window as a Divine suggestion; she felt certain there was falsehood or mistake in that allegation. Savonarola had become more severe in his views on resisting malcontents, yet strict law and order were fundamental to his political teaching. Still, since he knew the fatal effects of such visions, and since he distrusted spirit-seeing women, why did he not publicly denounce these pretended revelations that brought darkness instead of light?

The answer came with painful clarity: he was fettered inwardly by the consciousness that such revelations were not distinctly separable from his own visions; he was fettered outwardly by the foreseen consequence of raising a cry against himself, even among his own party, as one who would suppress all Divine inspiration of which he himself was not the sole vehicle.

Kneeling with her face buried on the altar-step, Romola endured one of those sickening moments when the enthusiasm that had made life worthy seemed inevitably bound up with vain dreams and wilful eye-shutting. Her mind rushed back with new attraction toward the strong worldly sense, the dignified prudence, and the untheoretic virtues of her godfather. He was to be treated as a sort of Agag because he believed a restricted form of government was better than the Great Council, and because he would not pretend to forget old ties to the banished family.

With this thought rose the presentiment of a plot to restore the Medici, and then again she felt the popular party was half-justified in its fierce suspicion. She felt that keeping the Government of Florence pure was a sacred cause; the Frate was right there, and had carried her understanding irrevocably with him. But at this moment, the assent of her understanding went alone; it was given unwillingly. Her heart recoiled from a right allied to so much narrowness, a right entailing a hard, systematic judgment of men that measured them by superficial symbols rather than the manhood within. Her affection and respect clung with new tenacity to her godfather, and with him to those memories of her father, which stood in opposition to the division of men into sheep and goats by the easy mark of some political or religious symbol.

After all has been said about the widening influence of ideas, it remains true that they would hardly be such strong agents unless taken in a solvent of feeling. The great world-struggle of developing thought is continually foreshadowed in the struggle of the affections, seeking a justification for love and hope.

If Romola's intellect had been less capable of discerning the complexities in human things, all the early loving associations of her life would have forbidden her to accept implicitly the denunciatory exclusiveness of Savonarola. She had simply felt that his mind suggested deeper and more efficacious truth than any other, and the large breathing-room she found in his grand view of human duties had made her patient toward that part of his teaching she could not absorb, so long as its practical effect collided with no strong force in her. But now a sudden insurrection of feeling had brought about that collision. Her indignation, once roused by Camilla's visions, could not pause there; it ran like an illuminating fire over all the kindred facts in Savonarola's teaching, and for the moment she felt what was true in the scornful sarcasms flung against him more keenly than she felt what was false.

But it was an illumination that made all life look ghastly to her. Where were the beings to whom she could cling, with whom she could work and endure, believing she was working for the right? On the side from which moral energy came lay a fanaticism from which she was shrinking with newly-startled repulsion; on the side to which she was drawn by affection and memory, there was the presentiment of secret plotting, which her judgment told her would not be unfairly called crime. And surmounting every other thought was the dread inspired by Tito's hints, lest that presentiment should be converted into knowledge, tearing her by irreconcilable claims.

Calmness would not come even on the altar-steps; it would not come from looking at the serene picture where the saint, writing in rocky solitude, was visited by faces with celestial peace. Romola was in the hard press of human difficulties, and that solitude was too far off. She rose from her knees to hasten to her sick people in the courtyard, hoping to revive that sense of worth in life which, at this moment, was unfed by any wider faith. But when she turned round, she found herself face to face with a man standing only two yards away. The man was Baldassarre.
