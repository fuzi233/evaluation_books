Old Epigram on Drake

Five years have slipped past. It is nine o’clock on a still, glinting November morning in Bideford, and the parish church bells are ringing two full hours past their usual scheduled service time. They do not toll with the sober, measured pace the town knows so well: every five minutes they burst out into giddy, leaping peals, tumbling headlong in unashamed ecstasy that no bell-ringer can rein in.

Bideford’s streets blaze like a cut flower bed, thronged with seamen, town burghers, and their wives and daughters all decked out in their finest holiday finery. Garlands of autumn leaves and late wild roses are strung across the road, every upper window draped with rich woven tapestries that catch the sun. The ships bobbing in the river pool fly every flag and pennant they own, firing volleys of cannon shot of every size to roar their own joy out across the water. Every stable within miles is crammed full of horses, and Sir Richard Grenville’s manor house is busier than a popular roadside tavern: servants run back and forth with tankards of ale and platters of spiced beef, grooms unsaddle steaming mounts, and the whole place hums with unruly celebration.

The little churchyard is packed tight with women, and through them streams all the gentle blood of North Devon: tall, stately lords and fair, sharp-witted ladies, the kind of ruling class that earned their place leading the people not just by birth, but by personal courage, physical grace, and sharp, learned minds. First comes the Countess of Bath, escorted cap-in-hand by Sir Richard Grenville (her husband the Earl Bourchier is in London attending the queen). Close behind are the Bassets of Umberleigh, the Carys of wild, beautiful Clovelly, a whole host of Fortescues from Wear, Buckland, and half a dozen other family estates, the Coles of Slade, the Stukelys of Affton, the St Legers of Annery, the Coffins of Portledge, and even the Coplestones who rode thirty miles all the way from Eggesford. Last, but so far from least that the crowd automatically parts to make space for them, comes Sir John Chichester of Ralegh, followed in the old patriarchal fashion by his eight daughters and three of his five famous sons. One of his boys is off fighting in Ireland to avenge his murdered brother, and will one day rule there wisely as Lord Deputy and Baron of Belfast. At the church lychgate he meets his cousin from Arlington, who trails behind him four daughters and nineteen sons: the very last of that brood is still passing the town hall, while the eldest has already reached the gate. They laugh and joke as they step aside to let the elder, though shorter, Chichester line go first, and the whole crowd files into the church.

The churchwardens and sidesmen, who have never had to seat so many grand, status-conscious guests in their lives, bustle about red-faced and flustered, nearly frantic as they try to sort out who takes precedence over whom. They end up begging Sir Richard Grenville to step in and tell them which families belong to the elder branch, which carry eight quarterings on their coat of arms, and which only four, before they accidentally spark a generations-long feud between half the fine ladies of the shire. All the elderly men are already settled safely in the corporation pews, and the young lords are not bothering themselves with the seating disputes: they only care about grabbing a spot where they can get a good look at the ladies.

At last a hush falls. Every head turns toward the church doors, and distant music drifts up: flutes, hautboys, drums and trumpets, braying and thundering merrily until they stop right on the doorstep. The churchwardens hurry down with their wands of office, and a soft murmur ripples through the congregation: many a woman wipes happy, unbidden tears from her cheeks, even a few men mutter quiet blessings, as the wonder of the day steps over the threshold. The rector does not open the regular morning service: he launches straight into the ancient, beloved thanksgiving liturgy for a victory at sea.

The whole town has gone wild with that old “pious mirth and goodly joy” only remembered in fragments now in modern Psalm translations, and for good reason. All eyes fix on five weather-beaten seamen, their coats pinned with knots and silk ribbons stitched for them by loving hands. Most of all, they stare at the gigantic figure walking at their head: he is barely more than a boy, no beard yet on his jaw, but built like Hercules, standing a full head and shoulders above every other man in the congregation, his loose golden hair spilling down over his shoulders. As the five men walk instinctively up to the altar rail and drop to their knees, every eye turns to a quiet pew at the side, where Mrs. Leigh of Burrough has hidden her face in her hands, her hood shaking with soft, overcome sobs of joy. This is Amyas Leigh of Burrough, alongside John Staveley, Michael Heard, Jonas Marshall of Bideford, and Thomas Braund of Clovelly: the very first English mariners to circumnavigate the globe with Francis Drake, home at last to give God thanks for bringing them safe across the world.

The story of how Amyas came to sail with Drake stretches back years, nearly to the point our first tale left off. For more than a year after Mr. Oxenham sailed away, young Amyas kept his promise to stay home, save for the occasional bursts of fierce, unruly energy common to all strong-willed teenage boys. His Latin studies still made barely any progress, but his real-world education thrived: still barely out of childhood, he was already becoming an excellent archer, a fearless rider, and a sharp hand at the old traditional school of buckler swordplay. Then his father traveled down to Exeter for the assizes, and caught the gaol fever that ran rampant in overcrowded, unhygienic prisons of the era. He sickened right there in the courtroom, and died within a week.

Mrs. Leigh was left to raise her fierce, untamed lion-cub of a son alone, guided only by her own faith and quiet strength. She had loved her husband deeply and reverently. He had been a often peevish, melancholy, disappointed man: his father’s foolish mismanagement had left the family estate deep in debt, and his own youthful ambition had drawn him to Elizabeth’s court, where he wasted years and his whole fortune chasing after empty honors. He was a man with every possible gift save the ability to use them: a fine scholar, a polished courtier, and a brave soldier, but past forty he found himself with no steady career, no clear purpose in life. A stubborn shyness, fierce pride, and delicate sense of honor always kept him from playing the cruel, political game required to get ahead at court, even as he still ached for the prizes it offered. Eventually he met Miss Foljambe, a quiet young lady from Derbyshire then serving in the queen’s household, who was just as tired as he was of the corruption and vanity of court life. The two fell in love, won the queen’s grumbling permission to marry (she was never pleased to see her ladies leave her service), and retreated to the quiet manor of Burrough to live in peace. Her husband knew full well what a treasure he had found in her.

Mrs. Leigh had been, since her girlhood, one of those noble, unassuming English Protestant women of that heroic age: devout, free of superstition, and never cruel or judgmental. A faint, gentle sadness hung over her all her life, forged in the fires of Mary Tudor’s persecution. She could clearly remember, twenty-five years prior, the burning of blind martyr Joan Waste at Derby, and of Joyce Lewis, a gentlewoman of good birth just like herself. She still sometimes woke at night hearing her own mother’s cries to God in her head, begging him either to spare her the agony of being burned alive, or to give her the strength to bear it, as other Protestant martyrs had before her. Her mother had been a lady in Catherine of Aragon’s bedchamber, the closest friend and devoted follower of Anne Askew. She had sat in the crowds at Smithfield, blood running cold in her veins, to see Anne carried to the stake in a chair, her limbs broken and crippled by the rack, just a month after she had been the paragon of beauty and wit in Henry VIII’s court. Thunder had rolled overhead that day, heavy rain drops hissing on the crackling firewood, as if the heavens themselves wept at the injustice.

She had hidden for months among the caves and moors of the Peak District during Mary’s reign, a marked heretic, and only survived because her husband had boldly declared to every constable, justice, and even bishop who came near his home that he would run through the body any man who dared serve an arrest warrant on his wife, no matter their rank. The shadow of that suffering never fully left her, and it touched Amyas too as he grew up in her quiet household. She lived fully for her husband, her children, and the poor of the nearby town of Northam, and was beloved by all the great Devon families around her: the Grenvilles, Fortescues, Chichesters, all of whom admired her husband’s sharp wit and respected his learning. She followed quiet, gentle spiritual austerities that her husband often teased her about gently, but no thought of trying to earn God’s favor or appease any supposed wrath ever crossed her mind. She was raised on the freest, most hopeful doctrines of Luther’s school, and the little ancient text *Alt-Deutsch Theologie* – which Luther claimed he owed more to than any book save the Bible and St Augustine – was her constant daily comfort and guide.

Now just past forty, a widow still beautiful of face and figure, she carried a divine calm about her that rippled through every word and gesture: a sweetness forged by storm as much as sunshine, a gift the world had never given her and could never take away. No wonder Sir Richard and Lady Grenville loved her, no wonder her children adored her. When Amyas had grieved his fill of his father’s death, he realized that it was no longer his mother’s turn to care for him: it was his turn to protect her. The very day after his father’s funeral, he walked straight from the grammar school up to Sir Richard Grenville’s house, and asked to see his godfather.

“You must be my father now, sir,” he said, steady and unflinching.

Sir Richard looked at the boy’s broad, strong, earnest face, and swore a great, holy old oath “by oak, ash, and thorn” that he would be a father to him, and a brother to his mother, for Christ’s sake. Lady Grenville took Amyas by the hand and walked home with him to Burrough, where the two women fell into each other’s arms and wept together: one grieveing for the husband she had lost, the other, with a quiet prophetic instinct, grieving for the loss she knew awaited her, for she knew her husband’s fiery, fearless spirit would never die peacefully in his bed, but fall sword in hand fighting for duty and honor, as he prayed he would every night. They swore an everlasting sisterhood that they kept for the rest of their lives. Amyas still rode, shot, boxed, and wandered the quayside at Sir Richard’s side, for Mrs. Leigh was far too wise to change a single part of the rough, robust training her husband had planned for his younger son.

Her elder son Frank had long since chosen the very different path of quiet learning that she had once hoped both her boys would follow. He had won renown at Bideford grammar school, then Exeter College, Oxford, where he became a close friend of Sir Philip Sidney and a whole circle of other brilliant, promising young men of rank. In the summer of 1572, on his way to Heidelberg University, he stopped over in Paris with letters of recommendation to Sir Francis Walsingham at the English embassy – a twist of luck that saved his life, just as it saved Sidney’s, during the St Bartholomew’s Day Massacre. He stayed at Heidelberg for two years, earning more respect from everyone who knew him, turning down Sidney’s repeated invitations to travel to Italy because he would not be a financial burden on his parents. Instead, he took a position as tutor to two young German princes, traveling with them to Italy to complete their princely education. He talked about the art of writing sonnets with Tasso, debated history with Sarpi, listened to the radical, unproven theories of Galileo with equal parts awe and disbelief, took his pupils to Venice to have their portraits painted by Paolo Veronese, wandered the palaces built by Palladio, watched Tintoretto work on his huge, dramatic canvases, and even secured a private audience with the ancient, white-bearded Titian in his studio, who told the young Englishman stories of Raphael, Michelangelo, the sack of Rome, and the time King Francis I of France had knelt down to pick up his paintbrush for him. He even traveled to Rome, much to the shock of his more strictly Protestant acquaintances back home, met the sitting Pope, heard Palestrina conduct his own music beneath the dome of St Peter’s, until the memory of the blood in the Paris streets he had witnessed during the massacre woke him from his fascination with the beauty of Catholic art and ritual.

A few months before his father died, he dropped his pupils back home in Germany, and was dismissed with generous rich gifts. Mrs. Leigh’s heart swelled at the thought her wanderer was coming home at last – but instead, a letter arrived from Frank, written unaware of his father’s death, describing his travels through the Alps and the valleys of the Waldenses, and saying that he had made friends with the great Hungarian scholar Stephanus Parmenius, commonly called Budaeus, who had convinced him to sail down the Danube to Buda to complete his studies of Hungarian scholarship. For nearly two years he wrote letters home addressed to his father, never getting a reply, until he grew afraid something terrible had happened and hurried back to England, to find his mother a widow, and his brother Amyas already gone to the South Seas with Captain Francis Drake of Plymouth.

Even then, he was not allowed to settle down at home. Sir Richard, who could not tolerate idleness as anything but a sin, got him a position in Queen Elizabeth’s household within six months, secured through the influence of Carew, Sidney, and his uncle the Earl of Leicester. Where Amyas was huge and rough-hewn, Frank was delicately, strikingly beautiful, and he soon became a favorite of the queen’s court, basking in the favor of Gloriana, the attention of fair court ladies, and the fast friendship of Philip Sidney, who was then one of the most celebrated men in Europe, patron of all the poets and scholars of the age.

Mrs. Leigh, who had long since learned to live for her children rather than for herself, submitted without a single murmur, and only smiled at her stern friend Sir Richard. “You took my rough mastiff puppy away to sea,” she said, “and now you must needs take my gentle greyhound too.”

“Would you have your greyhound grow into a true, sturdy Cotswold hound that can pull down a ten-point stag?” Grenville replied, “or one of those silly smooth poppets Florence ladies lead around on leashes, with little bells around their necks and flannel petticoats wrapped around their loins, good for nothing but looking pretty?”

Mrs. Leigh submitted, and a few months later was rewarded with a personal letter from Queen Elizabeth herself, thanking her for “the loan of that most delicate and flawless crystal, the soul of your excellent son.” She praised her far above the famed ancient Roman matron Cornelia, who had only shown off her sons as her private jewels: “you, madam, who have two sons as precious as any that Roman lady ever bore, have lent them both, far beyond her courage, to your country and your queen. I hold myself deeply indebted to you for that gift, and with God’s grace I will repay it as befits us both.” Mrs. Leigh bedewed the letter with tears of joy, and tucked it away in her cedarwood box of precious household treasures, right next to all Frank’s diplomas and letters of recommendation from princes, scholars, and nobles across Europe. She would pore over the Latin texts for hours even though she barely understood a word, just to find the line that called him *Noster excellentissimus Franciscus Leighius Anglus* – “our most excellent Englishman Francis Leigh” – that filled her mother’s heart to bursting.

But why did Amyas sail off to the South Seas in the first place? He set out for two very ordinary reasons that had sent many a bolder man to far worse places long before him: first, because of a petty feud with an old schoolmaster, and second, because of a beautiful young girl. To tell that story, we turn back to that fateful afternoon in Bideford grammar school.

Vindex Brimblecombe, formerly a poor servitor student at Exeter College Oxford – known colloquially around town as Sir Vindex, per the custom of the era – was headmaster of the grammar school. At heart he was a godly, good-natured pedant, but like most schoolmasters of the flogging era, his soul had been hardened by years of unrestricted power to inflict pain on boys weaker than himself. That kind of power may well have been short, sharp, and wholesome for the boys, but for the man holding the rod it erodes all but the noblest of spirits of every trace of chivalry, tenderness for the weak, and even self-control. After Amyas’s father died, Vindex decided it was his Christian duty to take extra special care of the orphaned boy, and the only way he knew how to do that was to flog him once a day, rather than the usual two times a week. The consequence of that new routine would soon land back on the pedagogue himself.

Amyas had never once, for a single moment, let go of his dream of going to sea. When he could not wander the quay staring up at the tall masts of the merchant ships, or sit on the pebble ridge at Northam staring out across the wide blue ocean that seemed to call him out into endless, uncharted space, he passed the time in grammar class drawing imaginary ships and islands on his slate instead of paying attention to his Latin grammar.
