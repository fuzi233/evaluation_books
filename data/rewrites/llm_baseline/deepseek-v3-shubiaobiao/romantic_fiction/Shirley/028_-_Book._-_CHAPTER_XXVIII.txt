The morning after her evening with Sir Philip, Shirley descended in rare spirits, her mood bright as the dawn that had yet to break through the lingering mist.

"Who will walk with me?" she asked at breakfast, her voice light. "Isabella? Gertrude?"

The invitation, so seldom extended to her cousins, gave them pause. But at their mother’s approving nod, they fetched their bonnets, and the three set out.

Shirley was not one for the company of many women—Mrs. Pryor and Caroline Helstone being her sole confidantes. Yet today, even the reserved Misses Sympson found themselves charmed by her. Though she spoke of nothing extraordinary, her words carried an uncommon spark, her laughter like the chime of silver bells.

What had lifted her so? The day itself offered no cheer—a dull, damp autumn morning, the sky heavy with unshed rain. But in Shirley’s heart, it seemed, the sun of Italy still blazed, its warmth glowing in her eyes.

A delay with John, her foreman, kept her behind as they neared home. Twenty minutes passed before she rejoined them, and then only to excuse herself from luncheon, retreating upstairs.

"Is Shirley not coming down?" Isabella asked. "She said she was hungry."

An hour later, she had not reappeared. One of her cousins sought her out and found her seated at the foot of her bed, pale, her hand pressed to her brow—her vivacity vanished.

"You’re unwell?"

"A little," Shirley admitted.

The change was startling. No explanation came but those three words. Yet the shadow that had fallen over her did not lift like a passing cloud. At dinner, she spoke as usual; through the evening, she played her part. When pressed about her health, she dismissed it—a mere faintness, nothing more. But the difference lingered.

Days passed. Weeks. The quietude in her manner deepened, her voice softer, her movements measured. The change was subtle, but undeniable—a veil drawn over her usual fire. To remark on it was to invite her scorn.

"Are you ill?"

"I am *not*."

"Does something trouble you?"

She laughed, sharp as flint. "What spirits? I have none to trouble."

"Then why this change?"

"Must I account for every shift in mood?"

But the effort to feign cheer exhausted her. Alone, she chastised herself. *Fool. Coward.* If she must tremble, let it be in solitude.

She took to long rides, heedless of weather. Tartar kept pace beside her, tireless. Gossips noted her path no longer curved at Rushedge but stretched onward—to the town, to the solicitor’s office. Whispers flared. Was it business? A marriage settlement?

In the schoolroom, Henry Sympson bent over his books, his tutor waiting.

"Henry, the afternoon wanes."

"Is it?" The boy’s voice was odd.

"Have you prepared your lesson?"

"No."

Moore looked up. "What troubles you?"

"Sir, I am heartsick."

Setting the book aside, Moore drew him near. The boy wept openly.

"This grief—does it pain your body?"

"No. It is Shirley."

Moore stilled. "You think her altered?"

"Everyone sees it. Even you."

"She claims she is well."

"That is the worst of it. She could be dying and still smile."

"What have you learned?"

Henry swallowed. "She has made her will."

A silence fell.

"She told you this?"

"Cheerfully. As if it were nothing. She said I was the only one besides her solicitor, Mr. Helstone, and Mr. Yorke who knew. She wished me to understand its terms."

"Go on."

Henry’s voice broke. "She said—she said if she died without a will, all would come to me, but she did not intend that. She left my sisters money, though she loves them not half so much as me. Caroline Helstone, too. This house, its books, she left to me. The rest—twelve thousand pounds—she willed not to me, but to a man she called good. Gentle, brave, strong. A man who visits the widowed and keeps himself unspotted from the world."

Moore’s breath caught, but his face betrayed nothing.

"And then?"

"She asked if I approved. I could not speak. She called me generous, said she was proud. ‘If anything happens,’ she told me, ‘you will know I loved you, Harry. No sister could have loved you more.’" The boy shuddered. "Sir, if she goes before me, I will follow soon. Lay me beside her."

Moore exhaled slowly. "You are both wrong. Shadows seem eternal to youth. What else did she say?"

"We settled family matters. That if I inherit, I take the name Keeldar. Henry Shirley Keeldar."

Moore almost smiled. "Eaglets, both of you. But she speaks of death—why?"

"She wastes away. Her hands grow thin."

"Does she complain?"

"Never. She laughs at concern."

"Where is she now?"

"Not out. It rains."

"Lately, weather does not stop her."

Henry hesitated. "Last week, in the storm, she walked to Nunnely. When I asked if she feared illness, she said, ‘It would be too much luck. The best thing would be to take a fever and pass on like other Christians.’"

Moore’s jaw tightened. "Reckless indeed. Find her. If you can speak privately, ask her to come to me."

Henry rose.

"And, Harry—make it casual. As you once summoned her to lessons."

The boy left. Alone, Moore steadied himself.

A door opened. Shirley entered, her sewing in hand—no warrior today, but a quiet figure from the hearth.

"Did you want me?" she asked, eyes down.

"I asked to speak with you."

She waited.

"Your health troubles Henry. It troubles us all."

"I am well."

"Yet changed."

"That is my concern."

"Does it not concern those who care for you?"

She met his gaze at last. "Very well. I am nervous. Now, let us speak of rain instead."

"Nervous—but why?"

"Must we dissect it?"

"If it pains you, yes."

She set her work aside. "I would rather tell you than the others. They would fuss, and I detest fuss."

"I can bear the truth."

Her fingers twisted in her lap. "Then here it is."
