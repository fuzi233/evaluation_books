Midsummer could coax the North to its feet on a pale edge of dawn—the dew still clinging like sifted salt to grass beside a dusty path, the young shoots in the coppice lifting their sharper green through the older oak and fir—without any need to remember whose feast it was.

But a Florentine, and most of all a Florentine of the fifteenth century, woke as if the very light had been named. The eastern sun struck the Arno with a particular gaiety, as though the river itself had been rinsed and set out like a bright platter; and the bells did not merely ring: they spoke, in a language every street knew, saying, San Giovanni. Their peal went through plaster and stone, through the sleeping crumbs of last night’s bread on a board, through the thin films of wine left in cups—an acoustic tide that made the city’s nerves lift and settle in time, until even the busiest mind moved to the festival’s cadence.

San Giovanni had worn Florence’s devotion for centuries—since Lombard queens commanded honour, since chroniclers like Villani could reach back to Constantino and Pope Sylvester, to that older, shrewder compromise with the past when the idol Mars was not shattered but set respectfully on a tower by the Arno, lest the city suffer “mutation” if the old tutelar force were affronted. Yet the fifteenth century had lost that cautious politeness toward the Man-destroyer. The spear and shield no longer kept watch beside the water. Florence now trusted in craft and coffers; and if arms were needed, arms could be hired with florins—and on the florins, always, San Giovanni looked out, stamped into gold as if sanctity were a guarantee of weight and purity.

Much had happened between the old patron and the new: Guelf and Ghibelline had made their arguments in blood, Blacks and Whites had split families, famine and flood and pestilence had emptied bowls and beds alike. And still the city had grown fat with glory—taking towns that once overawed it, pressing down especially on Pisa, whose marble had risen too proudly and whose masts had been too much admired on coasts where Florence meant to be known. In courts across Europe, even as far as Africa, the Florentine name circulated like coin: pure gold, dyes that held their colour like stubborn fruit skins, textures finished with such cunning that other cities’ cloth seemed raw beside them, scholarship and poetry and the kind of wit that could keep a bank’s books honest while it bent princes to its terms. A Pope, with a taste for pointed sayings, had called Florentines “the fifth element.” And, the people told themselves, for so high a destiny the thanks were due, in no small part, to San Giovanni, whose image rode every florin as it passed from palm to palm.

So his day must be kept with a devotion that did not separate the sacred from the street. The vigil came first—old Florentine fashion: dancing and jesting, and the likelihood of stones thrown and shutters broken; but above all, sights that only a city with a clever Cecca in its service could manage. Saints, with almond-shaped glory and their ring of winged cherubs, seemed to float down from painted domes into the narrow lanes, moving slowly through the press as if heaven had folded itself to street width. More wonderful still, there were saints made gigantic, attended by angels, not seated in fresco stillness but advancing in a mysterious glide like colossal figures escaped from high tribunes.

The clouds were woven cloth; the saints and cherubs were unglorified men propped by firm bars; the giants were steady-headed fellows on stilts, broadened like Greek tragedians by masks and stuffed shoulders. Yet only a miserably unimaginative Florentine thought of the carpentry first—and not merely unimaginative, but edging on impiety; for if sacred things were imaged, was not some virtue of the sacred carried in the image? And when a troupe of merry black demons followed—armed with claws and thongs and other instruments of sport, ready for impromptu farces of bastinado and clothes-tearing—why, that was simply the demons’ manner of keeping vigil. Tuscany slipped from devout to burlesque as water slips around an angle; the saints had had their passage, had made their pause before the gates of San Giovanni, and now the city made room for laughter that tasted of pepper after honey.

On the morrow—the great day thus opened—Florence must see her dependent places render their symbols: standards and offerings from conquered towns, protected districts, villages held since memory’s beginning. All were to be carried in solemn pomp to the old octagonal church—once cathedral, now baptistery—where every Florentine brow had felt the chrism traced into the sign of the Cross. The whole city, from white-haired men to striplings, matrons to lisping children, must be dressed in its best to honour the day and watch the great sight. And later, when the sun sloped and the streets cooled, there would be the Corso: unsaddled horses clothed in rich trappings, running right across the city from the Porta al Prato through the Mercato Vecchio to the Porta Santa Croce, where velvet and brocade banners—_palii_ with silk linings and gold fringe—waited on a triumphal car, fit trophies for the city that half-clothed the well-dressed world.

Afterward: more dancing; and the chroniclers of the century’s opening could swear that through the whole day there were weddings, and gatherings grand as any court’s, with so much piping and song, so many balls and feasts and ornaments, that earth might have been mistaken for Paradise.

In 1492 it was harder to make that mistake and keep a straight face. Lorenzo—magnificent, subtle—was dead; in his place sat Piero, arrogant and incautious, a change that did not bode well unless, as some wryly said, a wise horse might prefer a bad rider because he is easier to throw. Already grief for Lorenzo began to thin into murmurs: talk of a broader basis of government, of arresting corruption, of giving every man his share in jealousy and ambition—the old ideal liberty of quarrelsome days when Florence raised great buildings, reared her own soldiers, drove out would-be tyrants at sword-point, and boasted of keeping faith even to her own loss. Lorenzo was dead; Pope Innocent lay dying; the Neapolitan succession threatened trouble; Milan intrigued; Italy might soon be set by the ears. Difficult times—so much the more reason, many thought, to keep the festivals. When a city’s breath grows tight, it listens harder for the bells that tell it its own name.

Midsummer morning, that year, was bright enough to sting the eyes. Early—betimes—offerings for the grand procession assembled in the Piazza della Signoria: that famous space watched by the massive turreted Palazzo Vecchio and the spacious Loggia of Orcagna, scene of state ceremony. The sky spread a fair blue tent; beneath it the bells swung so vigorously that any evil spirit with sense enough to be formidable must have fled long ago. Faces swarmed at windows and terrace edges; sombre stone houses were brightened with hanging draperies; the palace tower, the older square tower of the Bargello, and the spire of the Badia nearby stood like stern cooks tasting the air, keeping watch over the simmer of the crowd below. On the broad polygonal flags of the piazza, banners unfurled; horses shifted under rich trappings; and the gigantic _ceri_—tapers fitly called towers—rose like strange, aggrandised descendants of those torches that once lit the Church in catacombs. In Florence’s midsummer glare, procession must move early; narrow streets offered shade, but the wide spaces flung back heat like a stone oven.

And where the pomp was to pause—the Piazza del Duomo—Florence had contrived another tent than the sky. From the octagonal baptistery in the centre to the cathedral façade and the surrounding walls, blue drapery was stretched forty feet high or more, embroidered with yellow lilies and familiar coats of arms; beneath it, sheaves of banners drooped at careful angles, a rainbow-lit shelter for the waiting spectators who leaned from windows, lined the pavement’s edge, and strained their necks toward the coming show.

One of those spectators was Tito Melema.

Bright among brightness, he sat at the window of the room above Nello’s shop. His right elbow rested on the red drapery at the sill; his head leaned back into his right hand, which pressed his curls against his ear as if he were listening not only to voices in the room but to the city’s festival-speech below. The months since his first appearance in Florence—weather-stained tunic and hose then—had changed him less than one might expect, except for that added radiance good fortune gives, like the barely perceptible finishing of a flower after it has drunk morning sun. His face held a bland liveliness, far from excitability and far from gloom: the expression of a companion who is welcome with men and women alike, never noisy from uneasy vanity, never contracted by resentment.

Close behind him, snug in the narrow angle between chair and frame, stood Nello in holiday suit, slender and alert. At Tito’s left was the younger Cennini—Pietro, the erudite corrector of proof-sheets, not Domenico the practical—his mind as neat as a compositor’s case. Tito’s gaze moved between the pageant below and the knot of talkers around him, several of whom had come in after seeing the procession begin in the Signoria.

Piero di Cosimo was among them, raising laughter with grimaces and anathemas against the bells. No ear-stuffing was barricade enough, he declared; the more he stopped his ears the more he felt the vibration in his skull, as if the sound were kneading his very brain. He swore he would bury himself in the most solitary spot of the Valdarno on a _festa_, if he were not condemned, as a painter, to lie in wait for secrets of colour—those sudden recipes of banner-light and human grouping that could be caught only when the city boiled over.

Tito turned from the whimsical painter, smiling, and looked down at the little drama playing along the chequered border of spectators under the blue canopy. It was then that he saw, near the marble steps before the Duomo—almost opposite Nello’s shop—a face upturned toward him, fixing on him a gaze too intent to be the common glance of a stranger.

A tonsured head rose above the black mantle and white tunic of a Dominican—common enough in Florence. But the look was not common. It carried a faint suggestion—certainly not unpleasant—and for that very reason it startled Tito. What pleasant association had he ever had with monks? None. Yet the flash of recognition, or something like it, struck and was gone almost before he could grasp it.

“Nello!” Tito said hastily, then, as the figure turned away, added with a note of disappointment, “Ah—he has turned round. That tall, thin friar going up the steps. I wanted you to tell me if you knew aught of him.”

“One of the Frati Predicatori,” Nello answered carelessly. “You don’t expect me to know the private history of the crows.”

“I seem to remember something about his face,” Tito said, frowning a little as if trying to recover a taste that had vanished from the tongue. “It is an uncommon face.”

“What? you thought it might be our Fra Girolamo? Too tall; and he never shows himself in that chance way.”

“Besides,” put in Francesco Cei, the popular poet, “that loud-barking ‘hound of the Lord’ is not in Florence just now. He has taken Piero de’ Medici’s hint—to carry his railing prophecies on a journey for a while.”

“The Frate neither rails nor prophesies against any man,” said a middle-aged personage at the other corner of the window, with the tone of one who had heard too much careless phrasing. “He only prophesies against vice. If you think that an attack on your poems, Francesco, it is not the Frate’s fault.”

“Ah—he’s gone into the Duomo now,” Tito murmured; his eyes had followed the Dominican eagerly. “No, I was not under that mistake, Nello. Your Fra Girolamo has a high nose and a large under-lip. I saw him once—he is not handsome; but this man…”

“Truce to your descriptions!” Cennini broke in, leaning forward with the impatience of scholarship confronted by spectacle. “Hark—see! Here come the horsemen and the banners. That standard—carried on the horse with white trappings—that with the red eagle holding the green dragon between his talons, and the red lily over the eagle—is the Gonfalon of the Guelf party; and those cavaliers close round it are the chief officers of the Guelfs. One of our proudest banners, grumble as we may. It means triumph of the Guelfs—which means triumph of Florentine will—which means triumph of the _popolani_.”

“Nay, go on, Cennini,” said the middle-aged man, dryly. “Which means triumph of the fat _popolani_ over the lean, which again means triumph of the fattest _popolano_ over those who are less fat.”

“Cronaca, you are becoming sententious,” said the printer, chuckling. “Fra Girolamo’s preaching will spoil you and make you take life by the wrong handle. Trust me, your cornices will lose half their beauty if you begin to mingle bitterness with them; that is the _maniera Tedesca_ you used to declaim against when you came from Rome. The next palace you build we shall see you trying to put the Frate’s doctrine into stone.”

“That is a goodly show of cavaliers,” Tito said—he had learned, already, the surest way to please Florentines was to admire what they admired, and to ask for the names. “But are there not strangers among them? I see foreign costumes.”

“Assuredly,” Cennini said. “There are the Orators from France, Milan, and Venice. Behind them English and German nobles; it is customary that all foreign visitors of distinction pay their tribute to San Giovanni in the train of that gonfalon. For my part, I think our Florentine cavaliers sit their horses as well as any of those cut-and-thrust northerners, whose wits lie in their heels and saddles; and yon Venetian—he would feel himself more at ease on the back of a dolphin. We ought to know something of horsemanship: we excel all Italy in the sports of the Giostra, and in the money we spend on them. But you will see a finer show of our chief men by-and-by, Melema; my brother himself will be among the officers of the Zecca.”

“The banners are the better sight,” Piero di Cosimo broke in, for once forgetting his quarrel with noise as the winding stream of colour poured forward. “The Florentine men are so-so; they make but a sorry show at this distance with their patch of sallow flesh-tint above black garments. But those banners—with velvet and satin and minever and brocade, with their endless play of delicate light and shadow! _Va!_ Your human talk and doings are a tame jest; the only passionate life is in form and colour.”

“Ay, Piero, if Satanasso could paint, thou wouldst sell thy soul to learn his secrets,” Nello retorted. “But there is little likelihood of it, seeing the blessed angels themselves are such poor hands at chiaroscuro—if one may judge from their _capo-d’opera_, the Madonna Nunziata.”

“There go the banners of Pisa and Arezzo,” Cennini said. “Ay, Messer Pisano, it is no use for you to look sullen; you may as well carry your banner to our San Giovanni with a good grace. ‘Pisans false, Florentines blind’—the second half will hold no longer. There come the ensigns of our subject towns and signories, Melema; they will all be suspended in San Giovanni until this day next year, when they will give place to new ones.”

“They are a fair sight,” Tito said. “San Giovanni will surely be as well satisfied with that produce of Italian looms as Minerva with her peplos—especially as he contents himself with so little drapery. But my eyes are less delighted with those whirling towers, which would soon make me fall from the window in sympathetic vertigo.”

The “towers” were indeed a Florentine glory: the gigantic _ceri_, tapers so tall that the name was half-hyperbole and half devotion to wax as if wax could purchase grace. Yet hyperbole must be engineered to stand upright. These _ceri_, some so large they had to be carried on wheels, were hollow—wood and pasteboard under their waxen show, gilded and carved and painted in successive rings: warriors on horseback, foot-soldiers with lance and shield, dancing maidens, animals, trees, fruits—everything, the old chronicler said, that could delight eye and heart. Their hollowness had a further virtue: men could stand within them and whirl them continually, making the painted circles spin into phantasms. Numerous together, they were calculated to breed dizziness on a scale truly magnificent—like looking too long into boiling sugar as it turns from clear to gold, the eye drawn and drawn until it aches.

“_Pestilenza!_” Piero di Cosimo muttered, backing from the window as the rotating colours made his stomach revolt. “Those whirling circles one above another are worse than the jangling of all the bells. Let me know when the last taper has passed.”

“Nay, you will surely like to be called when the _contadini_ come carrying their torches,” Nello said, teasing. “You would not miss the country-folk of the Mugello and the Casentino, of whom your favourite Leonardo would make a hundred grotesque sketches.”

“No,” Piero said stubbornly. “I will see nothing till the car of the Zecca comes. I have seen clowns enough holding tapers aslant—with and without cowls—to last me for my life.”

“Here it comes, then, Piero—the car of the Zecca,” Nello called after an interval in which towers and tapers, descending in size, had made their slow transit under the blue canopy.

“_Fediddio!_” Francesco Cei exclaimed as the next spectacle rolled into view. “That is a well-tanned San Giovanni—some sturdy Romagnole beggar-man, I’ll warrant. Our Signoria plays host to all the Jewish and Christian scum that every other city shuts its gates against, and lets them fatten on us like Saint Anthony’s swine.”

The car of the Zecca—the Mint—had once been simply an immense wooden tower adorned like the other tributary _ceri_, mounted on a splendid car and drawn by two mouse-coloured oxen whose mild heads looked out from rich trappings bearing the arms of the Zecca. But the latter half of the century had begun to be ashamed of the painted spirals and rings that had delighted the earlier half; the “tower-puppets” had become a proverb—any ill-painted figure that looked, as figures sometimes will even in great ages, as if it had been boned for a pie, was called a _fantoccio da cero_. Improved taste, with Cecca to serve it, had devised for the magnificent Zecca a triumphal car like a pyramidal catafalque, with ingenious wheels warranted to turn all corners easily.

Round the base stood living figures of saints and angels arrayed with a sculptor’s care, stiffly graceful as if carved. And on the summit, thirty feet above the flags, well bound to an iron rod and holding an iron cross firmly fixed, stood the living representative of Saint John the Baptist: arms and legs bare, a garment of tiger-skins about his body, a golden nimbus fastened upon his head—Precursor as cloister and church had long imagined him, not yet revealed to painters as the brown, sturdy boy who might play among the Holy Family. Where could the patron saint be more fitly set than on the symbol of the Mint? Was not coining money the surest token that a city had won independence? By San Giovanni’s blessing, this “beautiful sheepfold” of his had shown that token earliest among Italian cities.

Yet the annual honour of representing the saint was not one of the high prizes of public life. It was paid with something like ten shillings, a cake weighing fourteen pounds, two bottles of wine, and a handsome supply of light eatables. The money came from the Zecca; the payment in kind arrived by peculiar “privilege” in a basket suspended on a pole from an upper window of a private house. Thereupon the austere saint’s eidolon did not disdain to refresh himself: he ate a reasonable share of sweets, drank wine, flung the remnants to the crowd, and then—embracing the mighty cake securely with his right arm—kept that comical, bountiful posture through the rest of his passage as the tall car jerked and vibrated on its slow way around the piazza toward the northern gate of the Baptistery.

“There go the Masters of the Zecca, and there is my brother—you see him, Melema?” Cennini cried, pride stirring as pleasantly as a well-fed fire. “Behind come the members of the Corporation of Calimara, the dealers in foreign cloth which we have given our Florentine finish—men of ripe years, matriculated before you were born. And then comes the famous Art of Money-changers.”
