Philip Trent had never set out to be a detective.

A painter by trade and an artist by blood, he had drifted into the world of crime-solving as one might stumble upon a peculiar and fascinating street—by accident, and with no intention of staying. Yet here he was, once again, standing on the immaculate gravel drive of White Gables, his sharp eyes already cataloging the details of the place where a man had been murdered.

The house itself was a study in quiet opulence—red brick, twin gables, a sprawl of well-tended lawn that sloped toward the cliffs. It was the sort of home that spoke of money, discipline, and an unshakable belief in order. The kind of place where violence should have been unthinkable. And yet, just beyond the hedge, a gardener’s toolshed stood as a grim marker. That was where the body had been found.

Trent lit a cigarette and exhaled slowly, watching the smoke curl into the still air. He had arrived too late to see the scene undisturbed, but not too late to feel its weight. Inspector Murch was already here, of course—reliable, methodical Murch, who moved through cases like a steady tide. They had crossed paths before, in that odd dance of rivalry and respect that defined their professions.

He found Murch in the library, hunched over a spread of papers. The inspector turned at the sound of Trent’s footsteps, his broad face breaking into a wry smile.

“Knew you’d turn up,” Murch said. “Couldn’t resist, could you?”

“Professional curiosity,” Trent admitted, stepping inside. “And a certain fondness for puzzles. What have you got so far?”

Murch sighed, rubbing his jaw. “Not suicide, despite appearances. No weapon. Bruises on the wrists. And—” he paused, “—Manderson left his false teeth behind.”

Trent raised an eyebrow. “An oversight for a man dressing for his own death.”

“Exactly.”

They fell into step easily, falling back on the unspoken rules of their peculiar partnership. Trent would poke, prod, and theorize; Murch would weigh each idea against the cold facts. It was a game they both understood.

The house yielded little at first glance. Manderson’s bedroom was austere, almost monastic—no personal effects, no clutter. His wife’s adjoining room was a stark contrast, lush and feminine, but she had abandoned it, too shaken to stay. The secretaries—one English, one American—were polite, watchful. The staff moved quietly, their faces carefully blank.

Trent lingered in the library, sketching the room in his notebook. The details mattered—the position of the desk, the open French window, the way the light fell. Somewhere in this stillness, there was a lie waiting to be uncovered.

“So,” Murch said, watching him. “Who’s your pick for it?”

Trent grinned. “Everyone. Until proven otherwise.”

Murch snorted. “Practical as ever.”

But Trent’s mind was already turning over the pieces. Manderson had been rich, powerful, and recently distant from his wife. He had left his teeth behind. He had been shot at close range, yet no gun was found. And someone—someone in this house, perhaps—had wanted him dead badly enough to risk it all.

Outside, the sunlight was golden, the gardens peaceful. A perfect backdrop for a perfect crime.

Trent intended to ruin it.
