"Then proudly, proudly up she rose,

Whate'er ye say, think what ye may,
Ye's get na word from me!" — *A Scotch Ballad.*

To Mr. Thornton, Margaret's deception was not merely a breach of truth; it was a specific betrayal that pointed toward another lover. He could not shake the memory of the intimate glance exchanged between her and another man, nor the posture of confident familiarity—or perhaps even endearment—that had marked their interaction. This image haunted him relentlessly, a phantom picture that followed him everywhere. Compounding this sting was the setting: the dusky twilight, the distance from home, and the relative solitude of the location. While his higher nature initially rationalized these factors as accidental or innocent, the reality gnawed at him. Had he any grounds to deny her the right to love? Her rejection of his affections had been explicit. Yet, granting her that freedom, she could easily have been led astray into lingering walks and delayed hours. But the lie itself was the poison. It revealed a fatal consciousness of wrongdoing, a need to conceal something unlike the open honesty she usually displayed.

Thornton granted her the credit of knowing she had erred, yet it offered no comfort. The misery stemmed from his passionate love; he viewed her, despite her flaws, as superior to any other woman. Yet, he believed her heart belonged entirely to another—a dark, slight, elegant, and handsome figure—while he remained rough, stern, and heavily built. The very falsehood that stained her character served as proof of her blind devotion to this rival. He festered in an agony of jealous rage, recalling her tender glances and fond detainment. He mocked his own earlier gratitude for the way she had mechanically shielded him from the mob's fury; now he saw how soft and bewitching she appeared when with a man she truly loved. He replayed her sharp words in his mind: *"There was not a man in all that crowd for whom she would not have done as much, far more readily than for him."* He had shared the mob's desire to prevent bloodshed, but this hidden lover claimed her loyalty exclusively, sharing in her looks, her words, her physical touch, and her lies.

Thornton realized he had never been so irritable. Every query elicited a curt, bark-like response, a behavior that wounded his pride. He prided himself on self-control, and he resolved to master it. Consequently, his demeanor shifted to a cold deliberation, though his thoughts remained harsher than usual. He spent his evenings pacing restlessly around the house, a habit that would have exasperated anyone else, including his mother, who struggled to maintain patience with her beloved son.

"Can you stop? Can you sit for a moment?" Mrs. Thornton asked, interrupting his endless stride. "I have something to say to you if you'd abandon that perpetual walking."

He seated himself instantly on a chair against the wall.

"I wish to speak of Betsy. She insists on leaving us. Her lover's death has shattered her spirits, rendering her unable to focus on her work."

"Very well. Presumably, other cooks can be found."

"That is so typical of a man. It isn't just the cooking; she knows every corner of our household. Moreover, she mentioned something concerning your friend, Miss Hale."

"Miss Hale is no friend of mine. Mr. Hale is my friend."

"I am glad to hear you say that, for if she were your friend, Betsy's account would surely annoy you."

"Speak," he commanded, maintaining the extreme quietude he had adopted recently.

"Betsy claims that the night her lover—whose name she forgets, though she always refers to him simply as 'he'—"

"Leonards," he supplied.

"The night Leonards was last seen at the station, performing his duties, Miss Hale was present. She was walking with a young man whom Bessy believes struck or pushed Leonards, causing his death."

"Leonards was not killed by a blow or a push."

"How can you be certain?"

"Because I questioned the infirmary surgeon directly. He stated there was a chronic internal disease resulting from Leonards' excessive drinking. The rapid deterioration during his intoxicated state confirmed that the final fatal episode was caused by alcoholism or a fall precipitated by it."

"A fall! What fall?"

"Caused by the blow or push Betsy describes."

"So, there was a blow or push?"

"I believe so."

"And who delivered it?"

"As no inquest was held due to the doctor's assessment, I cannot disclose the identity."

"But Miss Hale was there?"

Silence.

"And with a young man?"

Still silence. Finally, he snapped, "I tell you, mother, there was no inquest. No judicial inquiry, I mean."

"Betsy asserts that Woolmer, a man she knows working in a grocery store in Crampton, can testify that Miss Hale was at the station at that hour, pacing back and forth with a young man."

"I see no relevance to us. Miss Hale is free to conduct herself as she pleases."

"I am relieved to hear you say that," Mrs. Thornton replied eagerly. "It matters little to us, especially to you given recent events. However, I made a solemn promise to Mrs. Hale that I would not permit her daughter to stray without offering advice and remonstrance. I intend to convey my views on such conduct."

"I perceive no harm in her actions that evening," Mr. Thornton said, rising and moving closer to the fireplace, turning his face away from the room.

"You would not have approved of Fanny being seen out after dark in such a secluded place with a young man. Let alone choosing a time when her mother lay unburied for such a stroll. Would you have relished having your sister noticed by a grocer's assistant for such behavior?"

"First, as I was once a draper's assistant myself, the opinion of a grocer's clerk holds no special weight regarding the morality of an act. Second, there is a profound distinction between Miss Hale and Fanny. I can imagine Miss Hale possesses weighty reasons that compel her to overlook apparent improprieties. I never knew Fanny to have such motives. Others must guard her. I believe Miss Hale guards herself."

"A charming characterization of your sister! Truly, John, one would think Miss Hale's actions had rendered you clear-eyed. She seduced you into an offer through a bold display of feigned affection, likely playing you off against this very young man, I have no doubt. Her entire conduct is now transparent to me. You believe he is her lover, do you? You agree to that?"

He turned abruptly, his face pale and grim. "Yes, mother. I do believe he is her lover." Upon speaking, he twisted in pain, leaning his forehead against his hand. Before she could respond, he spun back around with renewed sharpness:

"Mother. He is her lover, whoever he may be; yet she requires help and feminine counsel. There are difficulties, temptations unknown to me. I fear they exist. I do not wish to know their specifics, but since you have always been a good, tender mother to me, go to her. Gain her confidence and advise her on the proper course. I know something is wrong; some dread that must be a terrible torment to her."

"For God's sake, John!" Mrs. Thornton exclaimed, genuinely shocked. "What do you mean? What do you know?"

He offered no reply.

"John! Unless you speak, I shall assume the worst. You have no right to accuse her thus."

"Not against her, mother! I _could_ not speak against her."

"Well! You have no right to imply accusations without elaboration. Such half-truths destroy a woman's reputation."

"Her reputation! Mother, you do not dare—" He faced her, his eyes blazing. Then, composing himself into determined dignity, he declared, "I will say no more than this: it is the simple truth, and I trust you believe me. I have compelling reason to believe Miss Hale is in distress connected to an attachment which, based on my knowledge of her character, is perfectly innocent and righteous. My reasons remain confidential. But never let me hear anyone suggest anything more serious than that she requires the counsel of a kind and gentle woman. You promised Mrs. Hale to be that woman!"

"No!" Mrs. Thornton retorted. "I am happy to admit I did not promise kindness and gentleness, for I felt at the time I lacked the capacity to extend them to someone of Miss Hale's character and disposition. I promised counsel and advice, exactly as I would give to my own daughter. I shall speak to her as I would to Fanny if she were wandering with a young man at dusk. I shall base my words solely on the circumstances I know, uninfluenced by the 'strong reasons' you refuse to share. Thus, I shall fulfill my promise and discharge my duty."

"She will never bear it," he cried passionately.

"She will have to bear it if I speak in her deceased mother's name."

"Well!" he broke away, frustrated. "Do not lecture me further. I cannot endure thinking of it. It is preferable that you speak to her than that she remains unspeaken to. Oh, that look of love! And that cursed lie, hinting at a terrible shame she sought to hide from the light in which I believed she lived perpetually! Oh, Margaret, Margaret! Mother, how you have tormented me!
