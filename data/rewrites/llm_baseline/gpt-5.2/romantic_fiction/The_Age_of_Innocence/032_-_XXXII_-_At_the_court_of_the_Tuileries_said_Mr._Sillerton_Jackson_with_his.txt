“At the court of the Tuileries,” said Mr. Sillerton Jackson, and the phrase came out of him the way a well-worn spice comes out of a tin—faintly sweet, faintly stale, and always ready—“such things were pretty openly tolerated.”

They were in the van der Luydens’ black-walnut dining-room on Madison Avenue, the air held in a costly stillness by heavy curtains, thick carpets, and the discipline of servants trained to move without a syllable. The long table shone with massive plate and a row of Skuytercliff carnations that looked as if they had been cut with a jeweller’s care—stems trimmed to equal length, blooms opened to precisely the same degree, their clove-like scent pressed down beneath the odor of hot wax and old wood.

Mr. and Mrs. van der Luyden had come in from Skuytercliff for a few days, drawn back to town as if by a rope thrown across the river of scandal. Beaufort’s failure had shaken New York’s social house like a sudden draft down the chimney: doors banged, ashes rose, and every one ran to see who would pretend not to notice the smell of smoke. It had been represented to the van der Luydens—as to a pair of ancestral trees whose shade was thought indispensable—that their presence was more necessary than ever. As Mrs. Archer had explained, with the pious irritation of a woman who knew the weight of such obligations, they “owed it to society” to go to the Opera, and even to open their own doors.

“It will never do, my dear Louisa,” Mrs. Archer had urged, in the tone with which an old farmer’s wife might speak of blight creeping into a treasured orchard, “to let people like Mrs. Lemuel Struthers think they can step into Regina’s shoes. It is just at such times that new people push in and get a footing. It was owing to the epidemic of chicken-pox in New York the winter Mrs. Struthers first appeared that the married men slipped away to her house while their wives were in the nursery. You and dear Henry, Louisa, must stand in the breach as you always have.”

And so—reluctantly, heroically—the van der Luydens had returned, unmuffled the house, and sent out invitations for two dinners and an evening reception, as if reopening shutters to show the street that the old hearth still burned.

This evening’s party was small: Sillerton Jackson; Mrs. Archer; Newland Archer and May. They were to go afterward to the Opera, where Faust was being sung for the first time that winter. Nothing under the van der Luyden roof was done without ceremony; and though there were only four guests besides the hosts, dinner began at seven punctually so that the proper procession of courses could be served without haste before the men settled with their cigars. Even the meal’s quiet sounds—spoons laid down, the soft slide of plates, the muffled pouring of wine—were part of the house’s acoustic order, a carefully kept silence that protected the mind from the rougher noises outside, from the street’s iron wheels and hawkers, from the city’s growing roar.

Archer had not seen May since the night before. He had left early for the office, and in the afternoon a senior partner had taken him unexpectedly; he reached home so late that May had already driven to the van der Luydens’ and sent back the carriage. Now, across the carnations and the bright moat of plate, she struck him as pale and languid; yet her eyes shone with a kind of excited light, and her talk had an exaggerated animation that made him think of a hand that keeps beating egg whites past the right moment—airier, brighter, and suddenly fragile.

It was their hostess who brought up the subject that had tempted Sillerton Jackson to his favorite reminiscence. The Beaufort failure—more precisely, the Beaufort attitude since the failure—was still rich game for drawing-room moralists; and after it had been hunted, worried, and condemned, Mrs. van der Luyden turned her scrupulous gaze on May.

“Is it possible, dear, that what I hear is true? I was told your grandmother Mingott’s carriage was seen standing at Mrs. Beaufort’s door.”

It was noticeable that she no longer called the offending lady by her Christian name, as if even the syllables of “Regina” had become contaminated.

May’s color rose; and Mrs. Archer interposed quickly: “If it was, I’m convinced it was there without Mrs. Mingott’s knowledge.”

“Ah—you think—?” Mrs. van der Luyden paused, sighed, and glanced at her husband. In the pause, the dining-room’s hush made the faintest domestic noises loom: a servant’s careful breath, the distant clink of a dish in the pantry, the soft tick of time in a house built to resist change.

“I’m afraid,” Mr. van der Luyden said, “that Madame Olenska’s kind heart may have led her into the imprudence of calling on Mrs. Beaufort.”

“Or her taste for peculiar people,” Mrs. Archer added dryly; and though her eyes rested with an innocent steadiness on her son’s, the words had the bite of something sharp slipped into a sweet sauce.

“I’m sorry to think it of Madame Olenska,” Mrs. van der Luyden said; and Mrs. Archer murmured, with a sigh that had in it both reproach and proprietary hurt: “Ah, my dear—and after you’d had her twice at Skuytercliff!”

It was then that Jackson, feeling the gaze of the company turn toward him as toward a dish expected at its proper moment, placed his familiar offering on the table.

“At the Tuileries,” he repeated, smiling as if he were uncorking a bottle of old talk, “the standard was excessively lax in some respects; and if you’d asked where Morny’s money came from—or who paid the debts of some of the Court beauties—”

“I hope, dear Sillerton,” Mrs. Archer said, “you are not suggesting that we should adopt such standards?”

“I never suggest,” Jackson returned imperturbably. “But Madame Olenska’s foreign bringing-up may make her less particular—”

“Ah,” the two elder ladies sighed together, as if the single sound covered a whole vocabulary of disapproval that politeness forbade them to articulate. There are judgments in New York that are never spoken in full; they are conveyed by a pause, a look, an “ah”—a dominant lexicon in which a woman’s fate can be decided by what is not said.

“Still, to have kept her grandmother’s carriage at a defaulter’s door!” Mr. van der Luyden protested; and Archer guessed that he was remembering—and resenting—the hampers of carnations he had sent to the little house in Twenty-third Street, as if flowers given in good faith had been made to decorate a betrayal.

“Of course I’ve always said that she looks at things quite differently,” Mrs. Archer summed up.

A flush rose to May’s forehead. She looked straight at Archer and said, too quickly: “I’m sure Ellen meant it kindly.”

“Imprudent people are often kind,” Mrs. Archer returned, as if kindness, when it strayed beyond prescribed boundaries, ceased to be a virtue and became merely a weakness. Mrs. van der Luyden murmured: “If only she had consulted some one—”

“Ah, that she never did!” Mrs. Archer said, with the satisfaction of an accusation that fits too well.

Then Mr. van der Luyden glanced at his wife; she bent her head slightly toward Mrs. Archer; and the glimmering trains of the three ladies swept out while the men remained, as if some rite had been completed and the sexes separated into their appointed chambers.

On Opera nights Mr. van der Luyden supplied short cigars; but they were so good that they made his guests deplore his inexorable punctuality. The first draw was full and mellow, and the smoke lay on the tongue like a dark sweetness. Archer, however, tasted little. He felt the evening’s discourse like a layer of dust over everything—over the carnations’ perfume, over the food’s careful richness, over the smooth ritual of a world that could condemn a woman with a few phrases while still insisting on the correct order of courses.

After the first act he detached himself from the party and made his way to the back of the club box. From there he watched, over various Chivers, Mingott, and Rushworth shoulders, the same familiar scene he had seen two years before, on the night of his first meeting with Ellen Olenska. The house itself seemed to echo that earlier night: the rustle of silk, the muffled coughs held back by good breeding, the orchestra’s disciplined swell, and, above it, the city’s far-off hum blocked at the doors as if the Opera were a sanctuary against modern noise.

He had half-expected Ellen to appear again in old Mrs. Mingott’s box, but it remained empty. He sat motionless, his eyes fixed on the vacancy as on an unanswered letter, until suddenly Madame Nilsson’s pure soprano broke out into “M’ama, non m’ama...”

He turned to the stage where, in the familiar setting of giant roses and pen-wiper pansies, the same large blonde victim was succumbing to the same small brown seducer. The painted flowers looked too large, too perfect—like the society bouquets that smelled of nothing once you leaned close. His eyes wandered again, to the point of the horseshoe where May sat between two older ladies, just as, on that former evening, she had sat between Mrs. Lovell Mingott and her newly arrived “foreign” cousin.

As on that evening May was all in white; and Archer, who had not noticed what she wore, recognized the blue-white satin and old lace of her wedding dress. It was the old New York custom for brides to appear in the costly garment during the first year or two of marriage: his mother kept hers in tissue paper, cherishing a hope that Janey might someday wear it—though poor Janey was nearing the age when pearl-grey poplin and no bridesmaids would be thought more “appropriate.”

It struck Archer that since their return from Europe May had seldom worn her bridal satin; and the surprise of seeing her in it made him compare her with the girl he had watched two years earlier, his heart full of anticipations as fixed and glossy as the satin itself. Her outline was slightly heavier, as her goddesslike build had foretold; but the athletic erectness of her carriage and the girlish transparency of her expression remained unchanged. But for the slight languor he had lately noticed, she would have been the exact image of that girl playing with her lilies-of-the-valley bouquet on her betrothal evening.

The sight appealed to his pity with a sharpness that was almost physical. Such innocence moved him like a child’s trusting clasp. And then he remembered the passionate generosity that could flare under that incurious calm: May’s understanding glance when he had urged that their engagement be announced at the Beaufort ball; the voice in the Mission garden saying: “I couldn’t have my happiness made out of a wrong—a wrong to some one else.” An uncontrollable longing seized him to tell her the truth, to throw himself on her generosity, and ask for the freedom he had once refused—freedom not only from marriage, but from the whole vocabulary in which his life had been spelled out for him.

Newland Archer was quiet and self-controlled. Conformity to the discipline of a small society had become almost a second nature. It was deeply distasteful to him to do anything melodramatic and conspicuous—anything Mr. van der Luyden would have deprecated, and the club box condemned as bad form. Yet suddenly he was unconscious of the club box, of Mr. van der Luyden, of all that had enclosed him so long in the warm shelter of habit. He walked along the semicircular passage at the back of the house and opened the door of Mrs. van der Luyden’s box as if it were a gate into the unknown.

“M’ama!” thrilled out Marguerite; and the occupants of the box looked up in surprise. He had already broken one of the rules of his world, which forbade entering a box during a solo. Slipping between Mr. van der Luyden and Sillerton Jackson, he leaned over his wife.

“I’ve got a beastly headache; don’t tell any one, but come home, won’t you?” he whispered.

May gave him a glance of comprehension. He saw her whisper to his mother, who nodded sympathetically; then May murmured an excuse to Mrs. van der Luyden and rose from her seat just as Marguerite fell into Faust’s arms.

As Archer helped her with her Opera cloak, he noticed the exchange of a significant smile between the older ladies—a smile as smooth and finished as icing laid over a cake to hide whatever cracks might be underneath.

In the carriage May laid her hand shyly on his.

“I’m so sorry you don’t feel well. I’m afraid they’ve been overworking you again at the office.”

“No—it’s not that: do you mind if I open the window?” he returned, confused, letting down the pane. Cold air rushed in, full of street-sounds—hooves, wheels, the distant shout of a driver—noisy, alive, uncontained. He stared out, keeping his eyes fixed on the passing houses as if the moving darkness could give him words. Beside him he felt May’s presence like a silent, watchful interrogation, gentle but steady.

At their door she caught her skirt in the carriage step and fell against him.

“Did you hurt yourself?” he asked, steadying her with his arm.

“No; but my poor dress—see how I’ve torn it!” she exclaimed.

She bent to gather up a mud-stained breadth, the bridal satin taking on the city’s grime as if the outside world had at last reached it. They went up the steps into the hall. The servants had not expected them so early; only a glimmer of gas burned on the upper landing. Archer mounted the stairs, turned up the light, and put matches to the brackets on each side of the library mantelpiece.

The curtains were drawn, and the warm, friendly aspect of the room smote him like that of a familiar face met during an unavowable errand. The fire’s smell—coal and a faint dusty sweetness from the logs’ char—rose to meet him; the upholstered chairs held the remembered shapes of evenings; even the clock’s tick had the intimate confidence of a household sound. Against that domestic comfort, his purpose felt like a knife drawn at the table.

May was very pale. He asked if he should get her some brandy.

“Oh, no,” she exclaimed, flushing for an instant as she took off her cloak. “But hadn’t you better go to bed at once?” she added as he opened a silver box on the table and took out a cigarette.

He threw down the cigarette and walked to his usual place by the fire.

“No; my head is not as bad as that.” He paused, feeling the words bunch and stick in his throat like dough refusing to rise. “And there’s something I want to say; something important—that I must tell you at once.”

She had dropped into an armchair and raised her head. “Yes, dear?” she replied—so gently that he wondered at the lack of surprise with which she received his preamble.

“May—” he began, standing a few feet from her chair and looking over at her as if the small space between them were an unbridgeable abyss.

His voice echoed uncannily through the homelike hush, and he repeated: “There is something I’ve got to tell you… about myself…”

She sat silent, without a movement or even a tremor of the lashes. She was still extremely pale, but her face had a curious tranquillity, as if drawn from some secret inner storehouse where she kept her strength, as women in old houses keep preserves—quietly, in the dark, against the time when they are needed.

He checked the conventional phrases of self-accusal crowding to his lips. He was determined to put the case baldly, without recrimination or excuse.

“Madame Olenska—” he said.

At the name May raised her hand as if to silence him. As she did so, the gaslight struck on the gold of her wedding ring.

“Oh, why should we talk about Ellen tonight?” she asked, with a slight pout of impatience.

“Because I ought to have spoken before.”

Her face remained calm. “Is it really worth while, dear? I know I’ve been unfair to her at times—perhaps we all have. You’ve understood her, no doubt, better than we did: you’ve always been kind to her. But what does it matter, now it’s all over?”

He looked at her blankly. The sense of unreality in which he felt himself imprisoned—had it spread to her too, turning everything into a dream where nothing had weight?

“All over—what do you mean?” he asked, in an indistinct stammer.

May still looked at him with transparent eyes. “Why—since she’s going back to Europe so soon; since Granny approves and understands, and has arranged to make her independent of her husband—” She broke off.

Archer grasped the corner of the mantelpiece in one convulsed hand, as if holding to it could steady the room’s spinning. He made a vain effort to extend the same control to his reeling thoughts.

“I supposed,” he heard May’s even voice go on, “that you had been kept at the office this evening about the business arrangements. It was settled this morning, I believe.”

She lowered her eyes under his unseeing stare, and another fugitive flush passed over her face. He understood that his own eyes must be unbearable. Turning away, he rested his elbows on the mantel-shelf and covered his face. Something drummed and clanged furiously in his ears; he could not tell if it was the blood in his veins or the tick of the clock, loud now in the closed room as if the house were counting out the last measures of some old tune.

May sat without moving or speaking while the clock slowly measured out five minutes. A lump of coal fell forward in the grate; she rose to push it back, and the small, domestic sound of the poker against iron struck him like an answer in a language he could not translate—plain, practical, and final.

He turned and faced her.

“It’s impossible,” he exclaimed.

“Impossible—?” Her voice did not rise; she only waited, as if he were the one who had come late to what had already been decided.

“How do you know—what you’ve just told me?”

“I saw Ellen yesterday—I told you I’d seen her at Granny’s.”

“It wasn’t then that she told you?”

“No; I had a note from her this afternoon.—Do you want to see it?”

He could not find his voice. May went out of the room and came back almost immediately.

“I thought you knew,” she said simply.

She laid a sheet of paper on the table; Archer put out his hand and took it up.

The letter contained only a few lines:

“May dear, I have at last made Granny understand that my visit to her could be no more than a visit; and she has been as kind and generous as ever. She sees now that if I return to Europe I must live by myself, or rather with poor Aunt Medora, who is coming with me. I am hurrying back to Washington to pack up, and we sail next week. You must be very good to Granny when I’m gone—as good as you’ve always been to me. Ellen.

“If any of my friends wish to urge me to change my mind, please tell them it would be utterly useless.”

Archer read the letter two or three times, as if repetition could alter its meaning, as if the same words might yield a different taste on the tongue—salt becoming sugar by sheer will. Then he flung it down and burst out laughing.

The sound startled him. It recalled Janey’s midnight fright when she had caught him rocking with incomprehensible mirth over May’s telegram announcing that the date of their marriage had been advanced. This laugh too had no lightness in it; it was the noise of something breaking inside a man who had long prided himself on not making a noise.

“Why did she write this?” he asked, checking himself with a supreme effort.

May met the question with unshaken candour. “I suppose because we talked things over yesterday—”

“What things?”

“I told her I was afraid I hadn’t been fair to her—hadn’t always understood how hard it must have been for her here, alone among so many people who were relations and yet strangers; who felt the right to criticise, and yet didn’t always know the circumstances.”

She paused, and in the pause Archer heard again the house’s sounds: the clock, the fire, the distant, well-trained quiet of servants. Outside, somewhere beyond the drawn curtains, the city kept grinding and rattling, but here the old order held its breath.

“I knew you’d been the one friend she could always count on,” May went on, “and I wanted her to know that you and I were the same—in all our feelings.”

She hesitated, as if waiting for him to speak, and then added slowly: “She understood my wishing to tell her this. I think she understands everything.”

She came up to Archer and took one of his cold hands, pressing it quickly against her cheek. The touch was innocent, intimate, and strangely irrevocable—like a seal set on a document in a language he had been raised to obey.

“My head aches too; good-night, dear,” she said.

She turned to the door, her torn and muddy wedding dress dragging after her across the room, satin and lace whispering over the carpet with a soft, persistent sound, as if the past itself were being drawn away—still beautiful, still heavy, and stained now where it had touched the street.
