—Shakespeare

Sir Hugo Mallinger was slower to depart for Genoa than Mr. Gascoigne had been, and Deronda, for many reasons, would not leave before seeing him. There was not only Grandcourt’s death to discuss, but the recent upheaval in Deronda’s own life—his mother’s sudden appearance and disappearance, a specter from the past who had left as swiftly as she had come. In letters, he had avoided the finer details, knowing some truths were best shared face to face.

It was the fifth evening when Deronda, as arranged by telegram, waited at the station for Sir Hugo’s arrival between eight and nine. The thought of seeing that familiar face—one of his earliest memories—brought an involuntary softness to his expression, despite the weight of recent sorrow. There was, too, a quiet amusement in imagining Sir Hugo’s satisfaction at finally inheriting the estates outright, free to leave them to his daughters—or, as inheritance laws often insisted, to some future grandson.

Human nature is a curious thing. We ought to rejoice in another’s joy, even when it doesn’t align with our own ideals of justice. To begrudge happiness simply because it doesn’t fit our personal philosophy would make the world a bitter place indeed. Deronda, though he could not approve of the deception that had shaped his upbringing, could not summon lasting anger toward Sir Hugo. The baronet had acted in ignorance, believing he was securing the boy’s future as an English gentleman—never suspecting the grief his kindness had unwittingly caused.

When Sir Hugo stepped from the railway carriage, the affection of a lifetime swept aside any lingering reproach.

"Well, Dan," said Sir Hugo, gripping Deronda’s hand with unusual gravity. No other words were needed; the years between them spoke plainly enough.

After arranging for his luggage, Sir Hugo suggested a stroll beneath the fading evening light. "I took my time coming," he admitted. "Wanted to gather a few facts first—saw your letter to Lady Mallinger before I left. But tell me, how is the widow?"

"Calmer," Deronda replied. "She’s escaped the illness one might have feared after the shock. Her uncle and mother arrived two days ago—she’s well looked after."

"Any chance of an heir?"

Deronda hesitated. "Mr. Gascoigne seemed to think not. He spoke as though the widow would retain the estates only for her lifetime."

Sir Hugo glanced sideways. "I doubt her heart is much broken."

"The suddenness of it was a terrible blow," Deronda answered, sidestepping the implication.

"Did Grandcourt ever hint at the terms of his will?"

"Do *you* know them, sir?"

"Yes," said Sir Hugo briskly. "If no legitimate heir appears, everything goes to a boy he had by Mrs. Glasher—you’ve heard nothing of her, I suppose? She was as near a wife as he ever had for years. There are three older girls, but the boy takes the name: Henleigh Mallinger Grandcourt. Not that ‘Mallinger’ does him any good, thank God. The lad will want for nothing, with fourteen years of minority ahead—no need for my fifty thousand to patch up Diplow for him. Meanwhile, our lovely widow must content herself with two thousand a year and Gadsmere. A fine exile, if she chooses it—though I doubt she will. The boy’s mother has been living there. Disgusting business. I’d think no better of him for drowning if it didn’t conveniently solve my own affairs."

"He was wrong to marry her, not to leave the estate to his son," Deronda said coolly.

"Oh, I’ve no quarrel with the boy inheriting. But having raised the girl to his rank, he ought to have settled a proper income on her—four or five thousand a year, the London house. She had nothing; her family couldn’t insist. Trusting a man’s will after marriage is folly. Even the wise grow whimsical when the pen’s in their hand. And if a man’s a tyrant, his will is the last place he’ll show mercy. Clearly, Grandcourt meant his death to snuff her out if she bore no heir."

"And had she done so, the roles would reverse—the illegitimate children would be cast aside?" Deronda’s voice sharpened.

"Exactly. Gadsmere and the paltry two thousand. Strange, isn’t it? Worse still, he’s made me an executor. As his uncle, I can’t refuse—but I’ll bear it easier if I can be of use to the widow. Lush seems to think she knew of the other family, the will’s intent. Hints they didn’t get on. But *you* likely know her mind better, eh?"

There was no jest in the question, only quiet curiosity. Deronda answered plainly. "She was unhappy. They were ill-matched. But as for the will—knowing her, I believe she’ll be content."

"Then she’s unlike any woman I’ve met," Sir Hugo remarked dryly. "But she must be remarkable, given how fate’s tangled you two. When your telegram came, Lady Mallinger said at once, ‘How strange that it should be Daniel sending it!’ I’ve known such twists myself. Once, at a foreign hotel, I helped a lady abandoned by her husband—turned out to be an old flame who’d married a fool of an Austrian. But my being there was pure chance—no knight-errantry, any more than your coming to Genoa was about the Grandcourts."

A silence fell. Both men sensed the deeper conversation waiting beneath the surface. At last, as the port faded behind them, Sir Hugo turned, his tone softening.

"And the real reason for your coming, Dan—has it brought you pain? Nothing that alters your position, I hope? Whatever concerns you will always matter to me."

Deronda met his gaze. "I owe you complete honesty, sir. The past has hurt me. Meeting my mother, seeing her suffering, has been hard. Yet I’m grateful for the truth. As for my position—my gratitude to you is unchanged. But knowing I was born a Jew may change the course of my life in ways I can’t yet explain."

Sir Hugo studied him, then walked on. After a moment, he said lightly, "I’ve long expected something extraordinary from you, Dan—but for God’s sake, no eccentricities! A man can hold any opinion, so long as he doesn’t dress it up as madness. The world has no patience for melodrama these days. Not that I suspect *you* of it—but you’ve a habit of championing the downtrodden. A dangerous taste in company. Still, I won’t press you. When you’re ready, you’ll speak. And if you ever need funds, there’s sixteen thousand put aside for you, beyond your usual income. Now—I suppose you’re eager for England?"

"First to Mainz," Deronda said. "There’s a chest of my grandfather’s, and perhaps a friend to see. I’ve an odd urgency about it—as though leaving it longer risks losing it. And I would have gone sooner, but I couldn’t abandon Mrs. Grandcourt with only servants to rely on."

Sir Hugo’s laugh held an edge. "I hope you’re not valuing a dead Jew above a living Christian."

Deronda flushed but held his tongue. They turned into the *Italia* as the night settled around them.
