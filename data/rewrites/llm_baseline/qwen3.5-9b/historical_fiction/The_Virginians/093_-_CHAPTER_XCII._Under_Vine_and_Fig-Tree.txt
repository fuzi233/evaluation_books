Need I describe, young folks, the sheer delight of returning home, and the radiant joy of a mother welcoming her entire brood beneath her protective wings? It was written plainly across her face and acknowledged with humble gratitude on her knees. While our house was spacious enough to accommodate everyone, Aunt Hetty refused to remain within its walls. She stated quite frankly that relinquishing her role as the primary caregiver to her eldest children—who had been under her care for nearly three years—inflicted a pain too severe to bear. She simply could not stand the thought of sharing her maternal duties and settling for a secondary position among them. Consequently, she and her father departed for a residence in Bury St. Edmunds, not far from us. There, in private, she indulged her eldest nephew and niece with undivided affection.

It was the year following our return that Mr. B, the Jamaican planter, passed away. In his will, he left Aunt Hetty half of his fortune. Only then did I learn, for the first time, of the worthy gentleman's deep infatuation with her during their time in Jamaica. Though she had initially refused his advances, he demonstrated his unwavering constancy by providing for her after his death. Heaven knows how much of Aunt Hetty's inheritance Monsieur Miles has already consumed! Consider the cost of his military commission and outfit, his ostentatious uniforms, his gambling debts, and his various small ventures in the Minories. Do you truly suppose, sirrah, that I am unaware of human nature? What is the price of taverns like Pall Mall, *petits soupers*, and even moderate gaming at the Cocoa-Tree? Can a gentleman sustain all such pleasures on the five hundred pounds per annum that I currently allow him? Aunt Hetty has firmly resolved to remain a spinster. "I made a vow never to marry until I could find a man as good as my dear father," she declared. "And I never did, Sir George. Not half as good, my dearest Theo. Let Sir George put that in his pipe and smoke it."

Yet, when the good General finally died—calm, full of years, and glad to depart—I believe it was my wife who shed the most tears. "I weep because I fear I did not love him enough," confessed this tender soul. In contrast, Hetty barely concealed her composure, at least outwardly and in front of others. However, she speaks of him constantly as if he were still alive, recalling his witty remarks and his gentle, kind manner toward his children. When she does so, she brightens, looking momentarily like a girl again. She sits cheerfully gazing at the memorial slab in church that records his name and virtues, and for once, she tells no lies.

I had occasionally fancied that my brother Hal, for whom Hetty harbored a youthful passion, always retained a hold on her heart. Ten years prior, when he visited us, I confided in him about this childish romance of Hetty's. I admitted, with some reservation, that I hoped he might ask her to replace Mrs. Fanny, who had recently passed away. Regarding Fanny, my wife (ever prone to viewing herself as a miserable sinner) frequently reproached herself, claiming she did not regret Fanny's loss sufficiently. Upon arriving, Hal was plunged in grief over Fanny's death, vowing that no such woman existed anywhere else in the world. Our dear old General, still living at the time, welcomed him into his home and provided lodging, just as he had in those happier early days. The women played the very same tunes Hal had heard as a boy at Oakhurst. Hearts were softened by these nostalgic memories. Harry never grew weary of pouring out his sorrows and recounting his wife's virtues to Hetty, and then turning to speak fondly of his beloved Aunt Lambert, whom he cherished above all else. One can imagine how welcome such praise was to the faithful old husband, whose thoughts of his wife seemed never to be absent for more than three waking minutes throughout the day.

General Hal later traveled to Paris as an American General Officer, dressed in the fashionable blue and yellow livery that Mr. Fox and other gentlemen had popularized here. He was highly regarded at Versailles, despite being presented by Monsieur le Marquis de Lafayette to the Most Christian King and Queen—a couple who held little affection for the Marquis. I believe a Marquise developed a crush on the Virginian General and would have married him immediately had he not resisted, fleeing instead back to England, Warrington, and Bury, particularly the latter, where locals listened to his tales of his late wife with endless patience and sympathy. As for us, having known the poor paragon personally, we were merely civil, lacking the same enthusiasm, and often puzzled by our children's questions regarding Uncle Hal's angelic wife.

The two Generals, myself, Captain Miles, and Parson Blake—who was knocked down at Monmouth the year after I left America to change his coat and accept my parish living—used to reenact the battles of the Revolution over a bottle of wine. The parson would often exclaim, "By Jupiter, General!" (having compromised the deity's name when laying aside his military attire), noting, "You are the Tory, and Sir George is the Whig! He is forever finding fault with our leaders, while you stand up for them endlessly. Last Sunday, when I prayed for the King, I heard you following me quite loudly."

"And so I do, Blake, with all my heart," replied Hal. "I cannot forget that I wore his coat."

"Ah, if Wolfe had lived twenty more years!" lamented Lambert.

"Ah, sir," cried Hal, "you should hear the General talk about him!"

"What General?" I asked, intending to vex him.

"My General," Hal responded, standing up and raising a glass. "His Excellency, General George Washington!"

"With all my heart!" I shouted, though the parson looked as though he disliked either the toast or the claret.

Hal never tired of speaking of his General. It was on an evening similar to this, amidst friendly conversation, that he recounted how he had actually fallen into disgrace with the very General he loved so dearly. Their conflict seemingly revolved around the aforementioned Monsieur le Marquis de Lafayette, who had played such a significant historical role and had so mysteriously vanished from the public eye. Hal's previous rank in our own service and his acknowledged gallantry during the war should have secured his promotion within the Continental Army. Yet, a young upstart like M. de Lafayette arrived and was promptly complimented by Congress with the rank of Major-General. With the boldness of an old soldier, Hal had expressed considerable contempt for certain appointments made by Congress, accusing unscrupulous officers of engaging in miserable intrigues and cabals driven solely by greed for promotion. Mr. Warrington, perhaps imitating the example of his now illustrious friend at Mount Vernon, chose to treat the war *en gentilhomme*. Of course, he accepted his pay, spending it on comforts and clothing for his men, but he declared rank to be of no earthly consequence, stating he would serve equally well as a colonel as in any higher grade. Undoubtedly, he added scathing remarks concerning certain General Officers of the Continental Army, their origins, and the reasons for their advancement. He was particularly incensed by the sudden promotion of the young French lad—the Marquis, as they affectionately called him—in the Republican army. By the way, he was a prodigious favorite of the Chief himself. There were not three officers in the entire Continental force (after the unfortunate capture and disgrace of the reckless Lee) who could speak the Marquis's language. Thus, Hal could judge the young Major-General more closely and familiarly than other gentlemen, including the Commander-in-Chief. Mr. Washington, however, good-naturedly chided friend Hal for his jealousy of the beardless commander of Auvergne. Washington himself was not displeased by the filial regard and profound veneration the enthusiastic young nobleman always displayed toward him. Moreover, Washington possessed excellent political reasons for treating the Marquis with friendship and favor.

As events later revealed, the Commander-in-Chief was most urgently pressing Colonel Warrington's promotion upon Congress. Compounding his difficulties before the enemy, he was entrenched at Valley Forge during this harsh winter, commanding five or six thousand men at most, almost entirely lacking firewood, blankets, food, or ammunition. Facing Sir William Howe's army—which was fully equipped and three times larger—he encountered not only military peril but also the cowardly distrust of Congress and insubordination and conspiracy among his own officers. During the terrible winter of 1777, when a single blow from the sluggish leader of the British forces could have ended the war, and doubt, confusion, and despair reigned in the opposing camp (except in one indomitable breast), my brother had an interview with the Chief. He has since described this event to me, and Hal could never speak of it without succumbing to the deepest emotion. Mr. Washington had won no such triumph as that achieved by the daredevil courage of Arnold and the elegant incompetence of Burgoyne, which benefited Gates and the northern army. Save for one or two minor engagements proving his daring bravery and ceaseless vigilance, General Washington faced defeat after defeat from an enemy superior in every aspect. Congress mistrusted him. Many officers in his own camp hated him. Those who had been disappointed in their ambitions, those caught in embezzlement, and those whose selfishness or incapacity his honest eyes had exposed—all were, more or less, in league against him.
