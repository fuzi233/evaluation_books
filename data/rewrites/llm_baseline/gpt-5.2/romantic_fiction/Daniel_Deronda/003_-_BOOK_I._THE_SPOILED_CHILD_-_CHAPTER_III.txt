Pity that Offendene had not clung to Miss Harleth from the beginning, as flavors cling to a childhood tongue—some first sweetness of milk, some crust of home-baked bread, some herb-smell rising whenever the wind turns a hedge. A life, if it is to have any steadiness, should be planted deep in one patch of native earth: not merely so the eyes know the shape of the hills, but so the ears learn the right hush of evening and the particular morning racket of rooks; so the very air—smoke from damp logs, turned soil, bruised apple—becomes a private language the heart understands without translating. It is a cruel thing to ask a child to be “of the world,” as if the world were not first given to us in the narrow compass of a kitchen-sound and a garden-scent, in a dialect spoken by familiar mouths.

But that anchoring had been missing in Gwendolen’s life. Only a year before the summons home from Leubronn, Offendene had been chosen as her mamma’s house—chosen for convenience, because it lay near Pennicote Rectory. Mrs. Davilow, Gwendolen, and the four half-sisters, with governess and maid following, had first come down the avenue on a late October afternoon when the rooks were loud overhead and the elm leaves—yellow as old saffron—went spinning in the draught of their wheels.

The season flattered the old red-brick house, oblong and self-conscious in its stone trimming—stone at every line as if it needed insisting upon, even at the double row of narrow windows and the square portico. The stone had gathered greenish lichen; the brick had dulled to a powdery grey, so that rigidity softened into something like age’s indulgence. Old plantation trees circled the immediate grounds, and the house looked out by its three cut avenues—east, west, and south—across flat pastures. One wished it stood on a knoll, so its windows might command thatched villages, church towers, and scattered homesteads; but from behind its shelter it had at least, on one side, a glimpse of the chalk downs, those steadfast pale curves that took light and shadow like slow-breathing creatures.

It was barely large enough to deserve “mansion,” and only moderately rented, with no manor attached and furniture so sombre and upholstery so faded that tenants were scarce. Yet it had the one advantage that mattered to Mrs. Davilow’s pride: it could not be mistaken for the refuge of retired tradespeople. That negative distinction was worth discomfort to those who lived on the borderland of rank, where one’s name and one’s rooms are forever being measured by the world. To step into a house that had been thought good enough for dowager countesses gave Mrs. Davilow a visible lift—made her seem, for moments, less worn.

How it had suddenly become possible, after the death of Captain Davilow, was a mystery Gwendolen did not care to solve. She cared for the fact: that the roving life—foreign watering-places, Paris apartments, new hired furniture that never smelled like “theirs,” introductions that kept her feeling unimportant—was ended. Even the two showy years at school, where she had always been placed foremost on days of display, had only sharpened her conviction that she could not be meant for ordinary circumstances. Now, with an establishment of her mamma’s own, she felt sheltered from the dread of social meanness. On questions of birth she was easy; her maternal grandfather had been a West Indian—which to her mind was a sort of locked cupboard that one did not open; and her father’s family was “so high” they took no notice of her mamma, who nevertheless kept with pride a miniature of some Lady Molly to prove connection.

She might have asked more about her father, but for an old incident. When she was twelve, Mrs. Davilow had taken out—rarely, and with ceremony—memorials of her first husband, and, showing his miniature, spoke with a fervor that demanded a particular filial response: dear papa had died when his little daughter was in long clothes. Gwendolen, thinking immediately of the unloved stepfather who had occupied her short-frock years, blurted—

“Why did you marry again, mamma? It would have been nicer if you had not.”

Mrs. Davilow colored, a convulsive movement passed over her face, and she shut the memorials away as if shutting a wound.

“You have no feeling, child!” she said, with a violence quite unlike her.

Gwendolen, fond of her mamma and newly ashamed, had never since dared to question her father’s story.

That was not the only time she had pricked herself on the hidden tenderness of others. It was always arranged, when possible, that she should have a small bed in her mamma’s room; Mrs. Davilow’s motherly devotion clung most to the eldest girl, born in happier days. One night, under an attack of pain, she found the medicine meant to stand by her bed had been forgotten, and she begged Gwendolen to rise and fetch it. The young lady, warm as a child in her little nest, objected to stepping into the cold. She lay stubbornly still, and refused with a grumble. Mrs. Davilow went without, and never reproached her; but next day Gwendolen, with that quick inward sting which can come even to the spoiled, knew what her mamma must have felt. She made amends by caresses that cost little.

Having been the household’s pet and pride—waited on by mother, sisters, governess, maids, as if she were a princess in exile—she found it hard to imagine that other pleasures could outweigh her own. When they did, she felt a surprised resentment which, in her cruder days, had flared into sudden acts that seemed to contradict her general nature. She was not thoughtlessly cruel; she would rescue drowning insects and watch them recover. Yet there was the disagreeable memory—like a sharp note in silence—of having strangled her sister’s canary in a final exasperation at its shrill, piercing singing that had again and again broken into her own. She had bought her sister a white mouse by way of reparation; and though she excused herself inwardly by calling it “sensitiveness,” a mark of superiority, the thought of that infelonious murder always made her wince. Her nature was not remorseless—only she preferred penances that did not humiliate. Now that she was twenty and more, her force had hardened into self-control; the show of fire and will was stronger than ever, but calculation lay beneath it like a hidden hand on the reins.

On the day of arrival at Offendene—new even to Mrs. Davilow, for the place had been taken by her brother-in-law, Mr. Gascoigne—the carriage stopped under the porch before the open door. They all stood there, with a glimpse into a stone hall and staircase hung with sombre pictures; the one cheer was the bright wood-fire that snapped and settled with domestic confidence, as if the house had kept a little heart beating while it waited for tenants. No one spoke. Mrs. Davilow, the four sisters, and the governess all looked at Gwendolen, as if her opinion would flavor everything that followed—sweeten it or sour it.

Alice at sixteen and Isabel at ten were simply girlish at first glance, their black dresses already shabby. Miss Merry, elderly, wore neutrality like a shawl. Mrs. Davilow’s worn beauty looked the more touching for the complete appeal in her eyes. Gwendolen glanced round—the house, the landscape, the entrance hall—with rapid judgment, like a young racehorse among patient hacks.

“Well, dear, what do you think of the place?” Mrs. Davilow asked at last, gently, as if asking permission to breathe.

“I think it is charming,” Gwendolen said quickly. “A romantic place; anything delightful may happen in it; it would be a good background for anything. No one need be ashamed of living here.”

“There is certainly nothing common about it.”

“Oh, it would do for fallen royalty, or any sort of grand poverty. We ought properly to have been living in splendor and have come down to this. That would have been romantic indeed.” Then her tone sharpened. “But I thought my uncle and aunt Gascoigne would be here to meet us—and my cousin Anna.”

“We are early,” said Mrs. Davilow, and as they entered the hall she spoke to the housekeeper who came forward briskly. “You expect Mr. and Mrs. Gascoigne?”

“Yes, madam. They were here yesterday to give particular orders about the fires and the dinner. As to fires, I’ve had them in all the rooms for the last week, and everything is well aired. I could wish some of the furniture paid better for all the cleaning it’s had, but I think you’ll see the brasses have been done justice to. I think when Mr. and Mrs. Gascoigne come, they’ll tell you nothing has been neglected. They’ll be here at five, for certain.”

That promise of their arrival soothed Gwendolen at once; she was not prepared to have them treated as negligible. She tripped partway up the matted stone stairs to survey the landing, then came down, and with the girls behind her peeped into the rooms opening from the hall: the dining-room, all dark oak and worn red satin damask, with a copy of snarling, worrying dogs above the sideboard, and a Christ breaking bread above the mantel—bread one could almost imagine smelling faintly in the varnished air; the library with its brown-leather scent, dry and old as pressed leaves; and lastly the drawing-room, reached through a little antechamber crowded with venerable knick-knacks.

“Mamma, mamma, pray come here!” Gwendolen called, for Mrs. Davilow had lagged behind with the housekeeper. “Here is an organ. I will be Saint Cecilia: someone shall paint me as Saint Cecilia. Jocosa—let down my hair. See, mamma?”

She had flung off hat and gloves, seated herself at the organ, and lifted her face in an admirable pose, eyes upward. Miss Merry—whom Gwendolen called Jocosa—obediently drew out the comb that held the coil, and shook the mass free till it fell in a smooth light-brown stream far below its owner’s slim waist.

Mrs. Davilow smiled. “A charming picture, my dear!”—not indifferent to the display of her pet even under a servant’s eye.

Gwendolen rose and laughed, delighted, as if the house had already offered her its proper stage. “What a queer, quaint, picturesque room! I like these old embroidered chairs, and the garlands on the wainscot, and the pictures that may be anything. That one with the ribs—nothing but ribs and darkness—I should think that is Spanish, mamma.”

“Oh, Gwendolen!” cried little Isabel, astonished, her thin voice lifting like a sudden squeak of a door. She had pushed open a hinged panel of the wainscot at the far end of the room.

They all went to look, Gwendolen first. The panel disclosed a painting: an upturned dead face, pallid in gloom, and an obscure figure fleeing with outstretched arms.

“How horrible!” said Mrs. Davilow, with plain disgust.

Gwendolen did not cry out; she shuddered, the chill striking through her quick vanity like a draught through silk. Isabel, plain and inconvenient—one of those children who remember everything and speak it at the worst moment—said with alarming certainty, “You will never stay in this room by yourself, Gwendolen.”

“How dare you open things which were meant to be shut up, you perverse little creature?” Gwendolen snapped, anger rising in reaction against her own fear. She snatched the panel from Isabel and closed it hastily. “There is a lock—where is the key? Let the key be found, or else let one be made, and let nobody open it again; or rather, let the key be brought to me.”

With her cheeks flushed, she turned away as if shaking off a cold touch. “Let us go up to our own room, mamma.”

The housekeeper searched, found the key in a drawer of the cabinet near the panel, and presently handed it to Bugle, the lady’s-maid, with a significant nod. “Give it to her Royal Highness,” Mrs. Startin said.

“I don’t know what you mean, Mrs. Startin,” Bugle returned stiffly. She had been busy upstairs and resented irony from a new servant.

“I mean the young lady that’s to command us all—and well worthy for looks and figure,” Mrs. Startin added, mollified. “She’ll know what key it is.”

Upstairs, in the black-and-yellow bedroom, the best bed stood like a catafalque under its heavy hangings, while by its side a pretty little white couch waited for Gwendolen.

“If you have laid out what we want, go and see to the others, Bugle,” Gwendolen said as she entered with her mamma. “I will help mamma.”

But her first movement was to the tall mirror between the windows, which caught the whole room and her own figure in one obedient frame. Mrs. Davilow sat down and looked too, as mothers will, measuring their own hopes and losses in their child’s reflection.

“That is a becoming glass, Gwendolen; or is it the black and gold that sets you off?” she said.

Gwendolen stood obliquely, three-quarter face turned, brushing back her loosened hair with her left hand, intent as an artist on her own effect. “I should make a tolerable St. Cecilia with some white roses on my head—only how about my nose, mamma? I think saints’ noses never in the least turn up. I wish you had given me your perfectly straight nose; it would have done for any sort of character—a nose of all work. Mine is only a happy nose; it would not do so well for tragedy.”

“Oh, my dear, any nose will do to be miserable with in this world,” Mrs. Davilow said, and sighed heavily as she set her black bonnet down, weariness settling on her like dust.

“Now, mamma,” Gwendolen protested, vexed, turning from the mirror as if it had shown her something disagreeable. “Don’t begin to be dull here. It spoils all my pleasure, and everything may be so happy now. What have you to be gloomy about now?”

“Nothing, dear,” Mrs. Davilow said, rousing herself and beginning to unfasten her dress. “It is always enough for me to see you happy.”

“But you should be happy yourself,” Gwendolen insisted, discontent still in her voice though her hands grew caressing as she helped. “Can nobody be happy after they are quite young? You have made me feel sometimes as if nothing were of any use. With the girls so troublesome, and Jocosa so dreadfully wooden and ugly, and everything make-shift about us—and you looking so dull—what was the use of my being anything? But now you might be happy.”

“So I shall, dear,” said Mrs. Davilow, patting the cheek near hers.

“Yes, but really. Not with a sort of make-believe.” Gwendolen’s persistence was resolute. “See what a hand and arm!—much more beautiful than mine. Anyone can see you were altogether more beautiful.”

“No, no, dear; I was always heavier. Never half so charming as you are.”

“Well, but what is the use of my being charming, if it is to end in my being dull and not minding anything? Is that what marriage always comes to?”

“No, child, certainly not. Marriage is the only happy state for a woman, as I trust you will prove.”

“I will not put up with it if it is not a happy state.” Gwendolen’s chin lifted. “I am determined to be happy—at least not to go on muddling away my life as other people do, being and doing nothing remarkable. I have made up my mind not to let other people interfere with me as they have done. Here is some warm water ready for you, mamma.” She began to undress herself, then waited for her mamma to wind up her hair.

Silence fell while Mrs. Davilow coiled the long strands—quiet, methodical work, the kind that almost convinces the heart the world can be managed if only one keeps one’s hands busy. At last Mrs. Davilow said softly, “I am sure I have never crossed you, Gwendolen.”

“You often want me to do what I don’t like.”

“You mean, to give Alice lessons?”

“Yes. And I have done it because you asked me. But I don’t see why I should, else. It bores me to death, she is so slow. She has no ear for music, or language, or anything else. It would be much better for her to be ignorant, mamma: it is her rôle, she would do it well.”

“That is a hard thing to say of your poor sister, Gwendolen, who is so good to you, and waits on you hand and foot.”

“I don’t see why it is hard to call things by their right names, and put them in their proper places. The hardship is for me to have to waste my time on her. Now let me fasten up your hair, mamma.”

“We must make haste; your uncle and aunt will be here soon.” Mrs. Davilow’s voice took on its pleading note. “For heaven’s sake, don’t be scornful to them, my dear child! or to your cousin Anna, whom you will always be going out with. Do promise me, Gwendolen. You know, you can’t expect Anna to be equal to you.”

“I don’t want her to be equal,” said Gwendolen, tossing her head with a smile, and the matter ended as such matters did—with her will unshaken and her mamma soothed by the appearance of affection.

When Mr. and Mrs. Gascoigne and their daughter arrived, Gwendolen was not scornful. She was all grace—prettily attentive, as if kindness were a natural perfume she could pour out at pleasure. These were relatives who had not seen her since she was sixteen and unfinished; she was resolved that they should admire her.

Mrs. Gascoigne had the family likeness to her sister, but darker, slighter; her face unworn by grief, her movements brisker, her expression more alert and critical—the expression of a rector’s wife who feels bound to a beneficent authority. Their closest resemblance was a non-resistant disposition inclined to imitation and obedience; but in different lives such pliancy hardens into different shapes. The younger sister had been indiscreet, or at least unfortunate, in marriage; the elder believed herself enviable, and her pliancy had ended in opinions surprisingly definite. On church government, on the character of Archbishop Laud, her convictions seemed to remain equally decided through every alteration, as if arrived at less by inquiry than by receptive loyalty to her husband.

And there was much to encourage trust in Mr. Gascoigne. He had agreeable virtues, striking advantages, and even the failings imputed to him leaned toward success. One advantage was his fine person—perhaps more impressive at fifty-seven than earlier. There were no distinctively clerical lines in his face, no professional starchiness or affected ease. In his Inverness cape he could have passed for any gentleman: handsome dark features, a nose that began to be aquiline and then, as if thinking better of itself, became straight; iron-grey hair. He owed that freedom from clerical make-up—skin-deep and gesture-deep—to the fact that he had once been Captain Gaskin, taking orders and a diphthong only shortly before his engagement to Miss Armyn. If anyone questioned the adequacy of his preparation, friends might ask who made a better figure in the parish, who preached better, who carried more weight? He had a native gift for administration, tolerant of opinion and conduct because he felt himself able to overrule both, and free from the irritations of conscious weakness. He smiled at foibles he did not share—floriculture, antiquarianism, so dear to fellow clergy—preferring campaign histories, or divining from his knowledge of Nesselrode how policy would have shifted if our cabinet had chosen differently. His thinking had settled into what he would have called sound English—ecclesiastical rather than theological—national religion seen in daylight and in relation to other things. No clerical magistrate had greater weight at sessions, or less mischievous impracticableness about worldly affairs. The worst charge against him was worldliness: it could not be proved he forsook the unfortunate, yet his friendships were undeniably useful to a father of six sons and two daughters; and sour observers—Wessex had them, not long ago, in a bitterness that now may seem incredible—remarked that the color of his opinions changed with that principle. But cheerful, successful worldliness has a false look of selfishness beside the acrid, unsuccessful kind whose whole history is contained in the words: “Sold, but not paid for.”

Gwendolen wondered she had not remembered how fine a man her uncle was; at sixteen she had been a duller judge. Now it thrilled her to have, at last, the near presence of a dignified male relative—family life no longer entirely, insipidly feminine. She did not intend he should control her; but she saw at once how agreeable it would be for him to be proud of introducing her as his niece. And every sign said he was likely to feel that pride.

“You have outgrown Anna, my dear,” he said, putting his arm tenderly round his daughter and drawing her forward. Anna’s shy face was a tiny copy of his own. “She is not so old as you by a year, but her growing days are certainly over. I hope you will be excellent companions.”

His glance compared them; if he saw Anna’s inferiority, he likely saw too that her timid air and miniature figure would appeal to a different taste than Gwendolen’s vivid, commanding beauty. Gwendolen knew it, and kissed her cousin with real cordiality as well as practiced grace.

“A companion is just what I want,” she said. “I am so glad we are come to live here. And mamma will be much happier now she is near you, aunt.”
