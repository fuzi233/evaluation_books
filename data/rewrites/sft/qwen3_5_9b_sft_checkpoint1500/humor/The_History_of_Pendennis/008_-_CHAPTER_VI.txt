For a stretch of time following the previous tumult, Cicero and Euripides ceased to occupy Mr. Pen's weary mind, leaving the honest Mr. Smirke to glide through his lessons with an unusually effortless grace. Yet, amidst this academic peace, Rebecca suffered the most grievous torment. While Pen occasionally announced his intention to travel to Chatteris for fencing lessons—a journey undertaken openly when three clear hours lay ahead, accompanied by his mother's knowledge—the young rascal frequently bolted for the city. He would vanish into Prior's Lane, driven by a frenzy that matched Richard III's despair at Bosworth when his horse was slain beneath him. When Rebecca grew lame, Pen's distress was so profound he delved deep into the medical manuals of the stable keeper at Chatteris, seeking cures for his own imaginary ailments and those of others.

Under the guise of reading a Greek drama with Smirke, this young reprobate would occasionally slip away, timing his departure perfectly to catch the "Competitor" coach. He would linger in Chatteris for two hours before returning via the "Rival" line at ten o'clock. On one occasion, his secret nearly unraveled thanks to the Curate's simplicity. When Mrs. Pendennis asked if they had read much the night prior, Smirke hovered on the brink of the truth, admitting he hadn't seen Mr. Pen at all. Just as the lie was about to surface, Pen's boot-heeled ground heavily onto Smirke's toe beneath the table, a silent, violent warning that saved the curate from betrayal.

Naturally, the pair had discussed the tender subject. It is excellent sport, for anyone not personally entangled, to overhear two lovers speaking. Under solemn vows of secrecy, the Curate confessed Pen's condition, trembling slightly as he asked if the object of Pen's affections was "unworthy"—perhaps an "unlawful attachment." If so, the poor fellow felt compelled to break his vow and inform Pen's mother, fearing a quarrel and the loss of his chance to see what he loved most in the world.

"Unlawful, unworthy!" Pen exploded, bouncing out of his chair. "She is as pure as she is beautiful! I would give my heart to no other woman. I keep this matter secret in my family for reasons of a weighty nature which I am not at liberty to disclose. But any man who breathes a word against her purity insults both her honor and mine—and, by Jove, I shall not stand it!"

Smirke, offering a faint, nervous laugh, merely said, "Well, well, don't call me out, Arthur, for you know I cannot fight." With this compromise, the wretched curate remained entirely at the mercy of his pupil, causing Greek literature and mathematics to suffer accordingly.

Had the Reverend Gentleman possessed discernment and inspected the 'Poet's Corner' of the County Chronicle arriving in the Wednesday bag, he might have spotted verses titled 'Mrs. Haller,' 'Passion and Genius,' and 'Lines to Miss Fotheringay.' These appeared weekly, glowing with gloomy, thrilling passion. However, as these poems were no longer signed 'NEP' by their artful composer, but rather subscribed 'EROS,' neither the tutor nor Helen—the good soul who diligently cut her son's verses from the paper—suspected that Nep was the very same flaming Eros singing the praises of the new actress.

Eventually, Mrs. Pendennis asked, "Who is the lady whom your rival is always singing about in the County Chronicle? He writes somewhat like you, dear Pen, but yours are far superior. Have you seen Miss Fotheringay?"

Pen admitted he had. That night, he had gone to watch "The Stranger," where she played Mrs. Haller. He noted she was preparing for a benefit performance as Ophelia. "Suppose we go?" he suggested. "Shakespeare, you know, Mother. We can get horses from the Clavering Arms." Little Laura sprang up with delight, her heart racing at the prospect of a play.

Pen invoked "Shakespeare" because the deceased Father Pendennis, true to his character, had professed an uncommon reverence for the Bard of Avon, claiming his works contained more poetry than all Johnson's poets combined. Though Mr. Pendennis himself rarely read these volumes, he had often instructed Pen to peruse them, promising future joys when the boy reached a proper age to accompany him and his wife to the theater.

Tears welled in the kind mother's eyes as she recalled these speeches. She kissed her son fondly and agreed to go. Laura jumped for joy. Was Pen happy? Was he ashamed? Holding his mother, he longed to confess everything but kept his counsel. He intended to observe her reaction; the play itself would serve as the test, much like Hamlet testing Gertrude.

Helen, in a mood of high spirits, invited Mr. Smirke to join the party. The ecclesiastic, raised by a fond parent at Clapham who forbade dramatic entertainments, had never seen a play. But Shakespeare! To ride in Mrs. Pendennis's carriage and sit beside her for an entire night! He could not resist the allure of such pleasure. He delivered a feeble speech, weaving themes of temptation and gratitude, before accepting her generous offer. As he spoke, however, he bestowed upon her a lingering look that rendered her exceedingly uncomfortable. She had seen that gaze many times lately, persistently following her. He became positively odious in the widow's eyes with every passing day.

We shall not dwell extensively on Pen's courtship of Miss Fotheringay, for the reader has already sampled her conversation, much of which requires no repetition. Pen sat with her hour after hour, pouring forth his honest, boyish soul. He recounted everything he knew, hoped, felt, read, or imagined. He never tires of talking and longing. Her role in these tête-à-têtes was not to converse, but to feign understanding (a difficult feat, given the quantity of nonsense the youth blurted out) and to maintain an appearance of exquisite handsomeness and sympathy. In reality, while Pen delivered twenty-minute tirades, intoxicated by his own eloquence, the lovely Emily—who could comprehend less than a tenth of his ramblings—used the time to arrange her thoughts. She planned how to dress the cold mutton, how to turn the black satin, or how to fashion a bonnet from her scarf à la Miss Thackthwaite. Pen spouted Byron and Moore; passion and poetry. Her business was simply to lift her eyes, fix them on his face, and cry, "Oh, 'tis beautiful! Ah, how exquisite! Repeat those lines again." And off the boy would go, while she returned to her simple domestic concerns regarding the hashed mutton.

Indeed, Pen's passion was not long a secret from Miss Fotheringay or her father. Upon his second visit, his admiration was evident to both. As he departed, the old gentleman winked at his daughter over a glass of grog, remarking, "Faith, Milly darling, I think ye've hooked that chap."

"Pooh, 'tis only a boy, papa dear," Milly replied lightly. "Sure he's but a child." Pen would have been delighted to hear this phrase; he was currently galloping home wild with pleasure, shouting her name as he rode.

"Ye've hooked 'um any how," the Captain insisted. "Let me tell ye he's not a bad fish. I asked Tom at the George and Flint, the grocer, about his mother. Fine fortune, drives in a chariot, possesses Fairoaks Park, the finest grounds. Only son, property all his own at twenty-one. Ye might go further and not fare so well, Miss Fotheringay."

"Them boys are mostly talk," Milly said seriously. "Ye know at Dublin how ye went on about young Poldoody. I have a whole desk full of verses he wrote me from Trinity College, but he went abroad, and his mother married him to an Englishwoman."

"Lord Poldoody was a young nobleman; in their cases, it's natural. And ye weren't in the position ye're in now, Milly dear. But ye mustn't encourage this young chap too much, for, bedad, Jack Costigan won't have any trifling with his daughter."

"No more will his daughter, papa, you may be sure of that," Milly assured him. "A little sip more of the punch—sure, 'tis beautiful. Ye needn't be afraid about the young chap—I think I'm old enough to take care of myself, Captain Costigan."

Thus, Pen visited day after day, rushing in and galloping away, growing more wild with every encounter. Sometimes the Captain attended, but trusting his daughter implicitly, he preferred to leave the young couple to themselves, cocking his hat over his eye and strutting off on some errand when Pen entered. How delightful those interviews were! The Captain's drawing-room was a low, wainscoted space with a large window overlooking the Dean's garden. There, Pen sat and talked endlessly. Emily sat at her work, looking beautiful and calm, bathed in sunlight streaming through the great windows, illuminating her superb features. Mid-conversation, the great bell would boom, causing Pen to pause, smiling and silent until the vast music faded away—or the rooks in the cathedral elms erupted into noise at sunset—or the sound of the organ and choristers drifted over the quiet air, gently hushing Pen's chatter.
