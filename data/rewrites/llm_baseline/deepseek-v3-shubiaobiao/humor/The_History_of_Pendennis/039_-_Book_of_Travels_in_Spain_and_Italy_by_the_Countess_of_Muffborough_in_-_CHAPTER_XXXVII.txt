Let us glide swiftly over a few uneventful months in the life of Mr. Arthur Pendennis, for while the daily grind of a literary man may be of thrilling interest to himself, it is seldom so to the reader. We last left our hero firmly entrenched in the trenches of Grub Street, a humble scribe for the *Pall Mall Gazette*, where he toiled with all the dignity of a man who has exchanged lofty dreams for hard cash.

A professional writer’s life, much like that of a clerk, a curate, or a subaltern, is a monotonous parade of deadlines, ink-stained fingers, and the occasional sting of criticism—both given and received. Pen, though no stranger to indolence, found himself laboring under the whip of necessity, churning out reviews with the brisk efficiency of a hackney cab horse. His critiques were flippant, occasionally unjust, but seldom malicious—unless, of course, the unfortunate subject was an actor, in which case no mercy was shown.

Yet let us not weep too profusely for poor Pegasus, condemned to trot in harness. The beast of poetry must earn his oats like any other, and Pen was not one to demand special exemption from the vulgar demands of rent and taxes. He wrote because he must, and if his reviews were sometimes dashed off with more haste than reverence, well, so be it. The literary world of his day was not so overstuffed with genius that his hasty judgments did much harm.

By diligent scribbling—reviews, magazine articles, even the occasional anonymous letter to the *Chatteris Champion*—Pen managed to scrape together a modest income. So modest, in fact, that when he proudly presented his mother with a hundred pounds toward his debt to Laura, the good widow nearly fainted with maternal pride. To her, this act was nothing short of saintly restitution, and she devoured every word he wrote with the fervent conviction that her son was the wittiest, wisest man in London.

Meanwhile, Pen was making his way in society, thanks to his uncle’s introductions and his own agreeable manners. He was that rare creature: a gentleman who wrote for amusement rather than bread, and thus was welcomed in circles where mere drudges of the pen would not dare to show their ink-stained cuffs. He dined with dukes, debated with critics, and lounged in club windows—yet still found time to haggle with publishers in the back rooms of newspapers, where ink and sherry flowed in equal measure.

His days were a whirl of contradictions: breakfasting with bishops, lunching with booksellers, evenings divided between the opera and the tavern. He was young enough to enjoy it all, and wise enough (thanks to Warrington’s sardonic eye) not to take himself too seriously.

But while Pen was thus engaged, London society marched on without him. The Season was in full bloom, and the *Morning Post* dutifully chronicled the triumphs of Lady Clavering and her daughter Blanche, newly arrived in town and setting tongues wagging with their lavish entertainments.

Meanwhile, Major Pendennis, that polished relic of a bygone era, held court in his dressing gown, sipping tea and absorbing the latest scandals from his valet, Morgan—who, like all great domestic spies, knew everything worth knowing about everybody who was anybody.

“The Claverings are making a splash,” Morgan observed, as he scraped the Major’s chin with a razor. “Three parties a week, dinners besides. Her ladyship’s fortune won’t hold out—mark my words.”

“She had a damned good cook at Fairoaks,” the Major mused, with more interest in the memory of sauces than the state of the Clavering finances.

“Gone now, sir. That Frenchman, Mirobolant—there was a row about him.”

“A row?”

“Tried to climb up to Miss Amory’s window, sir. Got the garden hose turned on him. Then there was talk of poison, and a duel, and all sorts of nonsense.”

The Major’s eyebrows shot up. “Surely Blanche didn’t encourage the fellow?”

Morgan’s face remained a perfect blank. “Servants never know, sir. But where there’s smoke…”

Later that day, as the Major lounged in the window of Bays’s Club—that venerable gathering place of aged rakes—he spotted his nephew strolling down St. James’s Street with young Popjoy, who was busy declaring all the old members “a chamber of horrors.”

Pen laughed, but the Major rapped the glass, beckoning him inside.

“Your uncle wants you,” Popjoy said. “Notorious old rogue, that one.”

Pen grinned. “Aren’t they all?”

And with that, he stepped into the club, leaving the younger reprobate to his own devices—while the Major prepared to impart some choice wisdom, no doubt tinged with a lifetime of cheerful cynicism.

Thus did Mr. Arthur Pendennis navigate the treacherous waters of London life—now a critic, now a clubman, now a son, but always, inevitably, himself.
