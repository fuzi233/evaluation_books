Old habits proved a powerful shield. The automatic chatter of the ballroom filled their mouths, and forced smiles plastered themselves onto their faces, convincing every soul in Lennon House that no rift had formed between them that night. Harry Feversham watched Ethne laugh and speak with a carefree abandon that baffled him, constantly surprised by her ability to maintain the mask of gaiety just as well as he did. When she swept past him, the light rhythm of her steps nearly convinced him that her heart was truly in the dance; it seemed she could even command the color returning to her cheeks. Together, they wore the brave faces she had demanded. They even danced together.

Yet, beneath the surface, Ethne bore a crushing load of pain and humiliation that threatened to collapse her at any moment. Feversham, meanwhile, felt the sting of the four feathers burning against his chest. It amazed him that the entire company remained ignorant of his disgrace. Every time he approached a partner, he braced himself for the moment she would turn on him, spitting out the contemptuous title that hung around his neck. Yet, strangely, he felt no fear. If she had called him that, he would not have cared. He had already lost Ethne.

He watched her intently, scanning the crowd of guests in a desperate search for someone worthy of comparison. There were women—pretty, graceful, undeniably beautiful—but Ethne stood apart. Her beauty possessed a specific, commanding quality: a broad forehead, perfectly arched eyebrows, large steady grey eyes, and full red lips capable of softening into tenderness or hardening into resolve. Her carriage radiated a royal grace that marked her instantly. Feversham watched her with a despairing wonder, marveling at the chance he had once had to own her.

Only once did her endurance crack, and then only for a fleeting second. While dancing with Feversham, her gaze drifted to the windows where the dawn was breaking—a pale, cold light seeping through the blinds.

"Look!" she whispered.

Feversham suddenly felt her entire weight press against his arms. Her complexion drained, turning grey and exhausted. Her eyes squeezed shut tight before forcing themselves open again. He feared she would faint.

"The morning has finally arrived!" she exclaimed, her voice trembling. Then, in a tone as weary as her face, she added, "I wonder if it is right that one should suffer so much pain."

"Hush!" Feversham whispered urgently. "Courage! Just a few more minutes—only a very few!"

He stepped aside and stood facing her until her strength returned. "Thank you," she said gratefully, and the spinning wheel of the dance caught them once more.

It was strange that he should be urging her to find courage while she thanked him for support, yet neither noticed the ironic reversal of their positions. Ethne was too drained by the strain of those final hours. Feversham, having witnessed the depth of her suffering in the pallor of her face and the agony in her eyes, realized fully how deeply he had wounded her. He no longer spoke of losing her; he ceased to think of his own loss entirely. Instead, he clung to her words: *"I wonder if it is right that one should suffer so much pain."*

Those words echoed in his mind, resonating with the unique timbre of her voice. He was certain he would hear them echoing above the noise of the world when he died, serving as his eternal condemnation. Because, he knew, it simply was not right.

The ball ended soon after. Carriages departed, and the guests retired to the smoking room or their bedrooms. Feversham, however, lingered in the hall beside Ethne. She understood his purpose.

"There is no need," she said, turning her back to him to light a candle. "I have told my father. I told him everything."

Feversham bowed his head in silent agreement. "Still, I must wait and see him," he stated firmly.

Ethne did not object, though she glanced at him sharply, her brow furrowed in perplexity. Waiting for her father under such circumstances required a courage she respected, though it also stirred a flicker of apprehension within her. Soon, the study door opened, and the heavy footsteps of Dermod Lennon echoed on the floor.

Dermod walked straight up to Harry Feversham. Looking at him now, he appeared merely as a very old man. He stopped, staring into Feversham's face with a muddled, bewildered expression. Twice he opened his mouth to speak, but no sound emerged. Eventually, he turned to the table, lighting his own candle and Feversham's. He turned back, moving quickly enough that Ethne stepped forward instinctively to intervene, but he did nothing more than stare at Feversham again, holding the gaze for a long, uncomfortable time.

Finally, he picked up his candle. "Well—" he started, then stopped. He snuffed the wick with his scissors and tried again. "Well—" and halted once more. His candle offered no inspiration. He stared into the flame itself for an equal duration, unable to formulate anything meaningful, yet acutely aware that something had to be said.

In the end, he spoke lamely: "If you want any whiskey, stamp twice on the floor with your foot. The servants understand."

With that, he turned and walked heavily up the stairs. The old man's forbearance was perhaps the least of Harry Feversham's punishments.

***

By broad daylight, Ethne was finally alone in her room. She drew up the blinds and threw the windows wide open. The cool, fresh air of the morning struck her like a draft of spring water. Outside, she saw a world untouched by color, a reflection of her future days. The tall trees loomed black; the winding paths appeared as a singular, dead white; the lawns were dull and grey, covered in a network of frost-like dew. Yet, despite its quiet appearance, the world was noisy. Blackbirds called from the branches and the grass, and below, the river Lennon flowed in music between its banks.

Ethne drew back from the window. She had much to do before sleep claimed her. With her natural thoroughness, she intended to cut all ties with Harry Feversham immediately. She wished that from the moment she woke next, she would never encounter a single thing that recalled him. Driven by stubborn persistence, she began the work.

But midway through, she changed her mind. As she collected the gifts he had given her, her resolve softened. Each item carried its own history, and as she looked upon them, the days preceding this miserable night slowly returned, darkening her happiness one by one. She decided to keep one small, worthless thing that had belonged to Harry—a penknife he had once lent her, which she had forgotten to return.

Almost instantly, she dropped it and hurried away. She was, after all, an Irish girl; though she did not strictly believe in superstitions, she preferred safety. She selected his photograph instead, locking it safely away in a drawer.

She gathered the remaining presents, packing them carefully into a box, sealing it, addressing it, and carrying it downstairs so the servants could dispatch it in the morning. Returning to her room, she took his letters, piled them on the hearth, and set them alight.

They burned for some time. She sat upright in her armchair, watching the flame creep from sheet to sheet, discoloring the paper, blackening the writing like a stream of ink, until only flakes of ash remained—white flakes resembling white feathers.

Just as the last sparks were dying out, she heard a cautious step on the gravel beneath her window. It was broad daylight, yet her candle still burned on the table beside her. Instinctively, she reached out and snuffed it out. She sat very still and rigid, listening.

For a while, silence prevailed, broken only by the blackbirds in the trees and the thrumming music of the river. Then, the footsteps returned, cautiously retreating. Despite her efforts to formally dispose of the letters and gifts, she was suddenly overwhelmed—not by pain or humiliation, but by an overpowering sense of loneliness. She felt as though she were seated high atop an empty world of ruins.

She rose quickly from her chair, and her eyes fell upon a violin case. With a sigh of relief, she opened it. Shortly thereafter, one or two of the guests sleeping in the house woke to hear the music of a violin drifting down the corridors, played very lovingly and softly. Ethne was unaware that the instrument she held was the Guarnerius violin sent to her by Durrance. She only knew that she had found a companion to share her solitude.
