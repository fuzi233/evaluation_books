Bartolommeo Scala, Secretary of the Florentine Republic—the man to whom Tito Melema had fastened his hopes as one ties a boat to a stout ring in a shifting current—lived near the Porta Pinti in a handsome palace whose very threshold preached its owner’s story. Over the entrance were the arms: an azure ladder laid across a golden field, and above it the motto, Gradatim. Step by step. It was a boast made visible, and he meant it to be read without anyone’s indulgence.

He was vain and ceremonious; but there was a blunt honesty in him too, for he truly believed himself deserving and felt no need to counterfeit humility. The top rung of his ladder had long been reached. For twenty years he had held the secretaryship; he had declaimed on the ringhiera of the Palazzo, while Marzocco the lion wore his gold crown for princely occasions and the crowd shouted “Viva Messer Bartolommeo!” He had gone ambassador to Rome and come back jeweled with titles—titular Senator, Apostolic Secretary, Knight of the Golden Spur; and eight years earlier he had worn, for his turn, the gonfalonier’s dignity, last summit of a Florentine citizen’s appetite. Meanwhile, like so many men whom the world feeds, he had grown richer and heavier, and, like so many whom the world flatters, more tender of his own importance; and like successful mortality in general, he had also grown more gouty.

That afternoon in May, when he granted Tito the interview Tito had so carefully sought, the secretary sat under the loggia he had built at the back of his palace, where the garden opened in a breadth of lawn and flowers. The sun warmed the borders beyond the shade; bees made their small diligent music above rosemary and thyme, and from somewhere deeper among the citrus there came the wet trickle of water into stone—sound that soothed even those whose tempers were otherwise inflamed. Scala’s too stately silk lucco had been cast aside; he wore a looser mantle over his tunic, and his swollen foot lay propped and cushioned with the helpless dignity of a man obliged to rule from a chair.

On one side of him sat his beautiful daughter Alessandra, with her husband Marullo—the Greek soldier-poet—whose foreign consonants, even in Italian, carried a different weight, like a song learned in another valley. On the other were two friends, not oppressively illustrious and therefore easier to please. Yet even in this agreeable shade, with the garden scents rising and the soft city-noise muffled by walls, Messer Bartolommeo’s content was soured.

For on the table before him lay certain papers—letters—whose very ink seemed to have dried with a sting in it. They were chiefly a correspondence between himself and Politian; and, in that age when scholars could turn their quarrels into entertainment as readily as cooks turn yesterday’s bread into a new dish, Scala had again brought them out to be tasted, savored, and judged.

The quarrel was pretty enough in its origin: one of those learned squabbles that begin like play with a pin and end like war with a spear. Politian, having once sought Alessandra’s hand and been refused, kept a sharp tooth ready for the too-prosperous secretary who had declined to call the greatest scholar of the time son-in-law. Scala, for his part, was an able public servant, and—worse in the eyes of offended genius—a lucky one. But the world, which seldom gives a man one weakness, had bestowed on him another that made him vulnerable: he had an itch for authorship, and he wrote badly.

He was one of those excellent men who, sitting in their slippers with an aching foot, will “pen poetical trifles” to amuse their own hours, and then, under pretense of privacy, send them out to friends in letters—the fifteenth-century’s way of printing without confessing to it. Scala had friends ready to praise: Ficino and Landino, amiable wanderers in the Medicean park, called his Latin prose elegant and masculine. The terrible Joseph Scaliger, who would in another century name him totally ignorant of Latinity, was comfortably unborn. Yet the fatal coquetry of writing is rarely satisfied with praise from those already disposed to be kind. That supercilious Politian—so acute, so disliked, so necessary in a circle that wished to think itself refined—must be made to know that the solid secretary, even amid affairs, could throw off verses that showed what he might do if he chose.

So Scala had sent Politian a Latin epigram, and—ineffable moment—there was in it a false gender. The culex, the gnat—too well known along the Arno, where summer evenings are sweet until that needle-hum begins at the ear—had been treated as of the inferior, feminine kind. To a man whose mind pricked at errors as a gnat pricks at skin, it was an invitation.

Politian replied with the freedom of a friend, and pleasantly, in the same form: a Latin epigram correcting the mistake. Scala answered with a bad joke in Latin verse, referring to Politian’s unsuccessful suit for Alessandra—salt thrown into the wound and calling itself wit. Politian found Scala’s verses very pretty, very facetious; the more pity, he said, that they were incorrect. And since Scala had alleged imitation of a Greek epigram, Politian—instructive, generous—enclosed a Greek epigram of his own on the same insect. There was a lively conceit about Venus, Cupid, and the culex, much relished at the time, founded partly on the zoological fancy that the gnat, like Venus, was born from the waters.

Scala’s reply had been full of perfumed contempt. His verses, he said, had never been intended for a scholar with such delicate olfactories as Politian, nearest of living men to the perfection of the ancients; a taste so fastidious that sturgeon itself must seem insipid to him. Scala defended his own lines nevertheless—they were hasty, uncorrected, meant as a cooling distraction in summer heat for himself and friends satisfied with mediocrity, he not being one of those who courted publicity through the booksellers. As for Greek, he had barely enough to make out the sense of Politian’s gracious epigram, to say nothing of tasting its elegances; but—Politian had written it. What more could be required?

Then, in a postscript—like a cook who cannot resist putting his finger into another’s sauce and declaring what spice is wrong—Scala ventured criticism. The comparison of the gnat to Venus, on account of watery origin, was ticklish, he feared. Venus might be offended; and unless the poet meant Thales’s doctrine, that cold damp origin was doubtful for a creature so fond of warmth. A fish were a better comparison; or, for flight, an eagle; or, for darkness, a bat or owl—so on, so on, with the heavy assurance of a man who will not admit he has missed the very flavor of the dish he is judging.

Here Politian had found his true feast. He wrote that he had not been aware, when Scala’s verses were placed before him, that there was any question of sturgeon: rather of frogs and gudgeons. He made short work of Scala’s defense of his Latin, and mangled him terribly for the stupid cavils against the Greek epigram. For the damp origin of the gnat there was Virgil’s authority—alumnus of the waters. And the fish, eagle, bat, owl? Nihil ad rem. An eagle might fly higher; it did not follow the gnat could not fly at all. He was ashamed, he said, to dwell on such trivialities and swell a gnat into an elephant; yet he added, with that elegant sting of his, that he was not to be caught by false blandishments in the presence of those who slandered him in his absence—capping his point with a Greek quotation like powder packed into a bullet.

It did not end there. The logic could scarcely get worse, but the secretary grew more pompously self-assertive, and the poet’s temper more venomous. Politian posed as one willing to hold up a mirror to an inflated man, that he might cease defending bad Latin against authorities sanctified by centuries—unless, indeed, he designed to sink in literature as he rose in honors, so that men of letters might at least feel themselves his equals. Scala retorted that nowhere would Politian find a more devout admirer of antiquity than in Scala’s writings. Scala blushed for the age. Some, indeed, wanted their own works exalted beside the divine monuments of the ancients; he could not oblige them. And as to honors—if envious men found them offensive—let them look at his life, from the day he came in penury to Florence.

Politian’s reply was savage enough to make even paper smell rank. He was not surprised Scala found the age distasteful, since Scala was so distasteful to the age. It was perfectly accurate, he said, that he had called Scala a branny monster: formed from the off-scourings of monsters, born amid the refuse of a mill, eminently worthy the long-eared office of turning the paternal millstones—in pistrini sordibus natus et quidem pistrino dignissimus.

These letters, the secretary had brought out today not merely for old relish. Here was a new Greek scholar—Tito—whose palate for language could be tried. And on nothing did Scala more crave an opinion from someone who might claim superior knowledge than on Politian’s Greek epigram about the culex.

So, after introductions and polite talk of Tito’s travels—overseas, under other skies, where the sea has its own constant breath and the evening air tastes of resin and salt—they fell to examining Tito’s gems. The stones went from hand to hand like small preserved fragments of an older world: the light caught their veins and inclusions; they were discussed with the same minute affection a careful cook gives to rare spices brought in from afar, pinched and smelled before they are trusted to the pot. From the gems it was an easy slide—like a knife slipping from fruit to meat—into mention of the lamented Lorenzo’s eagerness in collecting such relics, and from that into classical tastes and their present condition in Florence.

Politian, inevitably, had to be named: a man of eminent ability, no doubt, but arrogant—setting himself up as a Hercules appointed to destroy the literary monstrosities of the age; writing letters to elders without signing them, as if the words were revelations that could have only one source. And after all, were not his criticisms often questionable, his tastes perverse? He was fond of mocking men who thought they wrote like Cicero because they ended every sentence with esse videtur; yet, boasting freedom from servile imitation, did he not run after strange words and affected phrases? Even in the much-praised Miscellanea—was every point tenable?

Tito, who had lately been looking into the Miscellanea, had much to say that pleased the secretary—he would have found it easy to please, from disposition alone, and not from calculation, though calculation sat quietly in him like yeast waiting to work. He spoke with the right assurance, seasoning his praise and censure so that Scala could taste himself justified. Thus he showed himself, in Scala’s eyes, quite worthy to sit in judgment on the notable correspondence concerning the culex.

The Greek epigram was produced—as carefully as a choice morsel set before a guest whose approval is desired—and Tito read it with a composed face. The garden’s sounds went on: the water’s trickle; a distant vendor’s call softened by distance; a blackbird fluting from the greenery; and, at intervals, the thin whine of the very creature they were disputing about, threading through the afternoon like a mocking accompaniment. Scala’s gouty foot throbbed in time with his irritation, and he leaned forward, eager.

Tito was ready. He scarified the epigram to Scala’s content—finding fault where fault was wished for, touching the places that could be made tender, and doing it with the deftness of a man who knows how to carve at table so the best slices fall where he intends. Yes: the comparison was strained. Yes: there were affectations. Yes: the transmarini—the Greeks themselves—might well make light of such elegance. Tito’s Greek, which had seemed to promise impartial authority, became the very knife Scala needed to cut Politian down again in his imagination.

“O wise young judge!” Scala’s look said plainly, and his voice soon followed. Here, at last, was a person whose ear could catch the fine errors, who could appreciate satire even in the vulgar tongue. And Scala—who, excellent man, never seeking publicity through booksellers, was never without some “hasty uncorrected trifles” ready like sherbet for a visitor on a hot day, or in cold weather a cordial—produced a few little matters: sonnets turning on well-known foibles of Politian’s. They were not to go farther, of course; merely to amuse the company. They were passed round, relished with that furtive pleasure which always accompanies forbidden sweetness.

Enough: Tito took his leave under urgent invitation to come again. His gems were interesting—especially the agate with a lusus naturae, a most wonderful semblance of Cupid riding on a lion; and the “Jew’s stone,” with a lion-headed serpent enchased in it. The secretary agreed to buy both—the latter as reinforcement of his preventives against the gout, which gave him twinges severe enough to make plain how intolerable life would be without rings of rare virtue and an amulet worn close under the right breast. Remedies, like words, were half their power in the belief that named them; and Scala loved names, mottoes, titles, talismans—things that could be displayed or touched as proofs.

But Tito was assured, as he was escorted through the palace and out toward the Porta Pinti, that he himself was more interesting than his stones. He had come by recommendation of Bardo de’ Bardi—an old acquaintance of Scala’s, a worthy scholar, though, like many of the secretary’s friends, perhaps inclined to overvalue himself. Yet Tito must return not on Bardo’s account, but on his own manifest accomplishments.

The interview could hardly have ended more auspiciously. As Tito walked out near the gate, where the street’s noise rose a little harsher—iron on stone, wheels on paving, voices crossing like competing birds—he let himself enjoy, in the shelter of his own thoughts, a quiet laugh over the affair of the culex. Fortune, he felt, could scarcely mean to turn her back on him again just now, since she had taken him by the hand so decidedly within that cool loggia, among herbs and water-sound and the sweet, sharp pleasure of a learned spite.
