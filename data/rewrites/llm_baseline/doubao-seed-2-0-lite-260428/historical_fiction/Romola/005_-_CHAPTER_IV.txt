The almond-scented lather curled thick in Nello’s barber bowl, and the air of his little shop hummed with the distant clatter of Florence’s streets—all still reeling, that day, from the shock of the great man’s sudden death, the whole city talking in hushed, orphaned tones as if the very pivot of Italy had been wrenched from its place. So the barber’s grin was extra bright when two men stepped over his threshold, the first a familiar, grave, silver-bearded figure: “Good day, Messer Domenico!” he called, nodding quiet greeting to the man trailing at his heels. “You could not have turned up more opportunely than melted cheese on hot macaroni. I see you are in haste—off with your doublet collar, no delay on the shave, ecco! Everyone in the city carries grave matter on his mind this morning, heaven itself half at a loss what to do next, *oimè*! But the sun still drifts steady towards dinner time, and see how fortune serves you: this young stranger was just asking for an honourable, trustworthy trader to advance him a small sum on a fine ring of value, and I could not count every goldsmith and moneylender in Florence from sunrise to sundown and name a better man than Menico Cennini. Even better, he carries the very ware your printing house hungers for: fluent Greek, sharp young eyes to pore over manuscripts, a double treasure you printers would chase across the Apennines for.”

Domenico Cennini, son of the master craftsman Bernardo who had cast Florence’s very first set of printing types twenty years prior, was forced to hold his lathered, passive silence while Nello poured out his stream of chatter, turning only a slow, sidelong gaze on the young stranger lingering by the workbench.
“This fine lad has unlimited Greek, Latin, and Italian at his service,” Nello pressed on, ever fond of amplifying a man’s virtues by a generous half. “He is as much a prodigy of juvenile learning as Francesco Filelfo, or our own incomparable Poliziano. A second Guarino, too, for he has suffered the cruel misfortune of a shipwreck, and doubtless lost whole chests of precious manuscripts that would have corrected even your most precise printed editions, Domenico. The luck of it is, he saved a few rare gems from the waves. Your name, Messer—you said it was—?”

“Tito Melema,” the stranger said, slipping the carved ring from his finger just as Nello whipped the shaving cloth from Cennini’s shoulders, his razor as quick as his tongue.

The second man who had entered at Cennini’s side—tall, near fifty years old, his beard trimmed short, an old faded felt hat pulled low over his eyes, his mantle frayed thin at the cuffs—had not once taken his gaze off Tito. Now he spoke abruptly, no preamble at all:
“Young man, I am painting a canvas of Sinon deceiving old King Priam at the gates of Troy. I would pay you well to sit for me; your face is exactly what I need for my Sinon.”

Tito started as if he had been struck, his face draining pale as if he had just been caught in a hidden wrong, but Nello gave him no time to fumble for a reply.
“Piero! Thou art the most ridiculous jumble of humours and fancies ever stuffed into a single human skin,” the barber squawked. “What trick wilt thou work on this fine young scholar’s face to turn it into a traitor’s? Ask him to tilt his eyes upward and thou couldst paint a Saint Sebastian that would draw whole troops of devout women to worship at it; or if thou art in a classical mood, twist myrtle in his curls and make him a young Bacchus, or better yet Apollo the sun god—his face glows as warm and bright as a summer dawn, it made me his loyal friend in the space of one short credo.”

“Ay, Nello,” the painter replied, his speech coming in sharp, abrupt pauses, “if thy tongue would stop its everlasting chirping long enough for thy wits to catch up, thou wouldst see thou hast just given the very reason his face is perfect for my Sinon. The ideal traitor wears a face no vice can leave a mark on: lips that lie with a soft dimpled smile, eyes so bright and agate-deep no amount of infamy can ever dull them, cheeks that walk straight from a murder and never look haggard. I do not say this young man is a traitor. I say he has the face that would make him a far more perfect traitor if he had the heart of one—which is no more than to say he has a beautiful face, fed full with rich young blood, that will keep its colour on a full stomach without needing much help from virtue. He may have the heart of a hero under it all, for all I know. Ask Domenico there if even the finest lapidary can judge a gem’s true worth by sight alone. Now I am going to stuff tow in my ears, for thy chatter and the church bells clashing outside are more than my wits can bear: say no more, sit thee down and trim my beard.”

Piero—called Piero di Cosimo, after his old master Cosimo Rosselli—pulled two wads of rough tow from his pocket, stuffed them tight in his ears, and dropped heavily into the empty shaving chair. Nello shrugged, pulling a face full of playful meaning at Tito, as plain as saying *A whimsical old fool, is he not? Everyone writes his odd speeches off as mere jokes*.

Tito, who had stood transfixed, his long dark eyes fixed on the strange painter who had read some unnameable, unspoken thing in his face, snapped back to his self-possession at the shift in the room. He turned his full attention back to Cennini, who was now turning the small ring over in his fingers.
“This is a curious, valuable ring, young man,” the printer-goldsmith said. “This intaglio of the fish beneath the crested serpent, carved deep into the black stratum of this onyx—or nicolo, more correctly—stands out beautifully against the pale blue upper layer of the stone. This ring surely has a long history?” he added, lifting his gaze to study Tito keenly.

“It does indeed,” Tito met his scrutiny openly, smiling easy. “It was dug up in Sicily, and the scholars who study old gems and sigils say both the stone and the carving hold a charm to make the wearer fortunate, especially at sea, and restore to him all he has lost. But,” he chuckled, turning his palm up, “though I have worn it every day since I left Greece, it has not made me altogether lucky at sea, as you see—unless escaping drowning counts as sufficient proof of its virtue. It remains to be seen if my lost chests will ever be recovered, but to lose no chance of that, Messer, I beg you will hold this ring only a short while as pledge for a small sum far beneath its true value. I will redeem it as soon as I can sell a few other small gems I sewed into the lining of my doublet, or sooner still if I can earn coin doing scholarly work, if I am lucky enough to find such employment.”

“That can be arranged easily, young man, if you will come with me,” Cennini said, holding the ring back out to Tito. “My brother Pietro is a far better judge of scholarship than I am, and he can surely set you to work transcribing and correcting Greek manuscripts that will test your capabilities. Take your ring back for now, I will bring you the florins you need once we reach the shop, come along with me.”

“Yes, yes!” Nello chimed in, bustling to hold the shop door open for them. “Go with Messer Domenico, you could not ask for better company! He was born under the constellation that grants a man skill, riches, and perfect integrity, whatever that constellation might be—no matter, since no baby gets to choose their own horoscope anyway, and if they did all the mothers would crowd to give birth on the same lucky day. Besides, our incomparable phoenix Pico della Mirandola already proved all horoscopes are nothing but nonsensical dreams, which is a far less troublesome opinion. *Addio*, bel giovane! Do not forget to come back to see me.”

“Never fear that,” Tito laughed, throwing Nello a bright, smiling wave as he stepped over the threshold. “You have done me a great service already: that is the surest possible guarantee you will see me again.”

“Say what thou wilt, Piero,” Nello muttered to the painter, once Tito had vanished down the street, Piero still half deaf with tow stuffed in his ears. “I will never look on a face as sweet and open as that and not take it as a sign of a lovable, honest nature. Next thou wilt be telling me that Leonardo, whom thou art forever raving about, should have painted Judas as beautiful as Saint John! But thou art as deaf as the top of Mount Morello with that cursed tow in thy ears. Well, well. I will get the rest of this young man’s story out of him soon enough, before I bring him to the house of old Bardo Bardi.”
