Solitude has a way of erasing one from the memories of those who live beyond its walls. No matter how vivid the moments shared, how frequent the letters or visits, there comes a silence—unexplained, unbroken—as if the world beyond has simply forgotten.

For seven weeks after that fateful evening at the theater, I heard nothing. No letters, no messages, no tokens of remembrance. The days stretched before me, blank and hollow, each one a silent rebuke. I told myself it was the way of things—destiny’s design—and yet, the heart does not so easily accept neglect.

I filled the emptiness as best I could: with lacework, with German verbs, with the driest tomes from the library shelves. But it was all husk, no sustenance. The post-hour became my torment, a daily ritual of hope and disappointment. I ached like a caged creature, straining for a morsel of connection.

Then, one evening, as I reread the five treasured letters—their magic fading with each repetition—Ginevra Fanshawe burst in, a whirl of silk and petulance.

*"My uncle de Bassompierre is here,"* she announced, flinging herself onto my bed. *"And I detest him."*

I knew the name, though not the man. A foreign title, inherited wealth, a daughter—Miss de Bassompierre—who had, according to Ginevra, *"airs and affectations."*

*"She played the invalid after that theater mishap,"* Ginevra sneered. *"As if she were crushed to jelly. And now Dr. Bretton dotes on her."*

Her bitterness was plain. The Brettons had been frequenting the de Bassompierre household, an intimacy rekindled. Ginevra, once the center of attention, had been displaced.

The next morning, a letter arrived—not from the one I longed for, but from Mrs. Bretton. Warm, lively, full of news. Graham was thriving, sought after, though she teased him mercilessly. She spoke of a sky-blue turban placed mischievously upon his sleeping head, of his laughter, his unshaken good humor. And then, the invitation:

The snow fell thick that afternoon, but the carriage came regardless. At the château, warmth and light greeted me—Mrs. Bretton’s embrace, the fire’s glow, the vivid hues of the salon after months of austerity.

Then, upstairs, a vision—a slight, white-clad figure before the mirror. Dark lashes, a slow smile.

*"You are changed,"* she said, *"but still yourself."*

I did not recognize her—not until she spoke of Bretton, of Mr. Home.

And then I knew.

The child I had once comforted was now a young woman, poised and knowing, her gaze holding memories I had let slip away.

The past had returned, not as a specter, but as a living thread—woven back into the tapestry of my life.
