To truly grasp the architecture of this confession, one must first understand the absolute isolation of Ethne's universe at that precise moment. She sat rigid, a statue carved from moonlight and denial, her violin resting upon her knees like a sacred relic. She refused to turn toward Durrance, refusing to acknowledge the physical reality of the man sitting opposite her. Instead, she locked her gaze across the garden, fixing her will upon the band of silver light cutting through the tree line—a visual anchor designed to convince her that Harry Feversham himself was speaking directly to her spirit, bypassing the mortal vessel of Durrance entirely. In this trance-like state, the presence of Durrance dissolved; he ceased to be a man and became merely a conduit, a hollow tube through which the voice of her lost love might flow. She listened with a ferocity born of desperation, holding her breath, waiting for the voice to stretch its syllables, to drag the message out in agonizing slow motion so that she might hoard every fragment of it, gathering the truth jealously into the fortress of her heart.

"It was on the night before I started eastward into the desert—for the last time," Durrance began, his tone heavy with a profound, lingering regret. Yet, strangely, this weight of finality failed to touch Ethne. It slid off her like water off oil.

"Yes," she replied, her voice steady despite the tremor in her soul. "That was in February. The middle of the month, wasn't it? Do you remember the day? If you can tell me the exact date, I wish to know it."

"The fifteenth," Durrance confirmed.

Ethne repeated the date meditatively, letting the numbers settle in the quiet air. "I was at Glenalla all February," she stated, though the question hung suspended. "What was I doing on the fifteenth? It does not matter."

A peculiar, electric shock of realization had coursed through her earlier that morning while Willoughby recounted his tale. She had felt a dissonance, a strange instinctual void, realizing she had known nothing of these specific incidents at the very moment they occurred. Now, that surprise returned with double force. It was absurd, almost grotesque, that she should have to wait for this August night, for this garden of moonlight and closed flowers, to learn of the meeting between Feversham and Durrance on February 15th, to receive the message that had been delivered months ago. Remorse, sharp and stinging, pierced her defenses. "It was my own fault," she whispered to the darkness. "If I had kept my faith in him, I should have known at once. I am well punished."

But a deeper, more insidious logic protected her. It never occurred to her that the message could convey anything but the finest possible news. How could it possibly be otherwise? It would simply carry the good tidings she had already heard, enlarging and completing them until this day was rounded to perfection. This certainty was her shield.

"Well?" she urged, her voice tight. "Go on!"

"I had been busy all that day in my office, finishing up my work," Durrance continued, his narrative painting a vivid, cinematic tableau. "I turned the key in the door at ten o'clock, thinking with relief that for six weeks I should not open it again. I strolled northward out of Wadi Halfa along the Nile bank into the little town of Tewfikieh. As I entered the main street, I saw a small crowd—Arabs, negroes, a Greek or two, and some Egyptian soldiers—standing outside a café, illuminated by a glare of light from within. As I drew nearer, I heard the sound of a violin and a zither, both played with a vile, grating incompetence, jingling out a waltz that grated on the nerves.

I stood at the back of the crowd, peering over the shoulders of the men in front of me into the room. It was a place of four bare, whitewashed walls; a bar stood in one corner, a couple of wooden benches ranged against the sides, and a single unshaded paraffin lamp swung and glared from the ceiling, casting harsh shadows. A troupe of itinerant musicians played to that motley assembly of negroes, Arabs, and Egyptians, hoping for a night's lodging and the price of a meal. There were four of them, and so far as I could see, all four were Greeks. Two were evidently man and wife. They were both old, both slatternly and almost in rags; the man a thin, sallow-faced fellow with grey hair and a black moustache; the woman fat, coarse of face, unwieldy of body.

Of the other two, one appeared to be their daughter, a girl of seventeen. She was not particularly beautiful, yet she was dressed and turned out with a scrupulous care that, in those sordid and mean surroundings, lent her an artificial grace. The care with which she was attired assured me beyond doubt that she was their daughter. To tell the truth, I was rather touched by the thought that the father and mother would go in rags so that she at all costs might remain trim. A clean ribbon bound back her hair; an untorn frock of some white stuff clothed her tidily; even her shoes were neat. The fourth was a young man, seated in the window with his back towards me, bending over his zither. But I could see that he wore a beard.

When I came up, the old man was playing the violin, though 'playing' is hardly the word. The noise he made was more like the squeaking of a pencil on a slate; it set one's teeth on edge. The violin itself seemed to squeal with pain. While he fiddled and the young man hammered at his zither, the old woman and girl slowly revolved in a waltz. It may sound comic to hear about, but if you could have seen! It fairly plucked at one's heart. I do not think I have ever in my life witnessed anything quite so sad. The little crowd outside, the negroes especially, laughed at the troupe, passing from one to the other any sort of low jest at their expense. Inside, the four white people—the old woman, clumsy and heavy-footed, shining with heat, lumbering round slowly, panting with her exertions; the girl, lissom and young; the two men with their discordant, torturing music; and just above you the great planets and stars of an African sky, and just about you the great silent and spacious dignity of the moonlit desert. Imagine it! The very ineptness of the entertainment actually hurt one."

Durrance paused, allowing Ethne to picture the scene. She saw Harry Feversham bending over his zither, and immediately a question seized her: *What was he doing with that troupe?* It was intelligible enough that he would not care to return to England. It was certain that he would not come back to her unless she sent for him. She knew from Captain Willoughby that he expected no message from her. He had not left with Willoughby the name of any place where a letter could reach him. But what was he doing at Wadi Halfa, masquerading with this itinerant troupe? He had money; so much Willoughby had told her.

"You spoke to him?" she asked suddenly, breaking the spell of the description.

"To whom? Oh, to Harry?" Durrance returned. "Yes, afterwards, when I found out it was he who was playing the zither."

"Yes, how did you find out?" Ethne pressed.

"The waltz came to an end," Durrance explained. "The old woman sank exhausted upon the bench against the whitewashed wall; the young man raised his head from his zither; the old man scraped a new chord upon his violin, and the girl stood forward to sing. Her voice had youth and freshness, but no other quality of music. Her singing was as inept as the rest of the entertainment. Yet the old man smiled, the mother beat time with her heavy foot, and nodded at her husband with pride in their daughter's accomplishment. And again in the throng the ill-conditioned talk, the untranslatable jests of the Arabs and the negroes went their round. It was horrible, don't you think?"

"Yes," answered Ethne, but slowly, in an absent voice. Just as she had felt no sympathy for Durrance when he began to speak, so she had none to spare for these three outcasts of fortune. She was too absorbed in the mystery of Harry Feversham's presence at Wadi Halfa. She was listening too closely for the message which he sent to her. Through the open window, the moon threw a broad panel of silver light upon the floor of the room close to her feet. She sat gazing into it as she listened, as though it was itself a window through which, if she looked but hard enough, she might see, very small and far away, that lighted café blazing upon the street of the little town of Tewfikieh on the frontier of the Soudan.

"Well?" she asked. "And after the song was ended?"

"The young man with his back towards me," Durrance resumed, "began to fumble out a solo upon the zither. He struck so many false notes, no tune was to be apprehended at the first.
