Shirley proved she had meant what she said—meant it with the frank obstinacy of a nature used to having its affections answered—by coming often to the rectory, and, oftener still, by contriving that Caroline should come to Fieldhead. Had Shirley waited, she might have waited long; for Miss Helstone did not readily believe herself desired. She carried, like a pinched shawl about her shoulders, a shy persuasion that she could not be wanted—could not enliven any circle—least of all the bright circle that seemed to move about Shirley Keeldar as naturally as summer air about a lark.

Yet no one, however rich, however young, however dazzling in talk and motion, is independent of genial companionship. Shirley had, within a month, been called on, visited, invited, and claimed by the neighbourhood. She knew all the Misses Sykes, all the Misses Pearson, and those two faultless Misses Wynne of Walden Hall; she could thread their drawing-rooms as easily as she threaded the shrubbery walks at Fieldhead. But it appeared she found none of them, in her own phrase, “genial.” She would say, with a careless candour that was part pride, part mischief, to Mrs. Pryor, “If I had the bliss of being Shirley Keeldar, Esq., lord of the manor of Briarfield, there is not a single fair one in this parish, nor in the two adjoining, whom I should feel disposed to request to become Mrs. Keeldar, lady of the manor.”

Mrs. Pryor, who received most of her pupil’s outbursts with a quietness that neither chilled nor indulged, answered, as she sat with her work laid straight upon her knee, “My dear, do not allow that habit of speaking of yourself as a gentleman to be confirmed. It is a strange one. Those who do not know you, hearing you, would think you affected masculine manners.”

Shirley never laughed at Mrs. Pryor; and if she had, she would have shown weakness. Quiet worth, even when it is formal, has a claim to reverence; the very little prim ways of that lady—harmless as the orderly folding of a napkin—were respectable in Shirley’s eyes. She took the remonstrance in silence, stood near the window, and looked out upon the great cedar on the lawn. A bird was on one of the lower boughs; its throat swelled and fell with a clean, content sound that seemed to rinse the room of its pauses.

Presently Shirley began to answer it—at first a low chirrup, then clearer; then, forgetting herself, she whistled. The whistle struck into a tune, and she carried it with a sweet precision, as if she had a musician’s palate for notes—knowing which should be crisp, which lingering, which bright as a drop of water on stone.

“My dear!” expostulated Mrs. Pryor.

“Was I whistling?” said Shirley, stopping short. “I forgot. I beg your pardon, ma’am. I had resolved to take care not to whistle before you.”

“But, Miss Keeldar, where did you learn to whistle? You must have got the habit since you came down into Yorkshire. I never knew you guilty of it before.”

“Oh! I learned to whistle a long while ago.”

“Who taught you?”

“No one. I took it up by listening, and I had laid it down again. But lately—yesterday evening—as I was coming up our lane, I heard a gentleman whistling that very tune in the field on the other side of the hedge, and that reminded me.”

“What gentleman was it?”

“We have only one gentleman in this region, ma’am, and that is Mr. Moore—at least he is the only gentleman who is not gray-haired. My two venerable favourites, Mr. Helstone and Mr. Yorke, it is true, are fine old beaus, infinitely better than any of the stupid young ones.”

Mrs. Pryor was silent; her silence had always an edge—as if it were cut from something firm.

“You do not like Mr. Helstone, ma’am?” Shirley pursued, turning from the window.

“My dear, Mr. Helstone’s office secures him from criticism.”

“You generally contrive to leave the room when he is announced.”

“Do you walk out this morning, my dear?”

“Yes. I shall go to the rectory, and seek and find Caroline Helstone, and make her take some exercise. She shall have a breezy walk over Nunnely Common.”

“If you go in that direction, my dear, have the goodness to remind Miss Helstone to wrap up well; there is a fresh wind, and she appears to me to require care.”

“You shall be minutely obeyed, Mrs. Pryor. Meantime, will you not accompany us yourself?”

“No, my love; I should be a restraint upon you. I am stout, and cannot walk so quickly as you would wish to do.”

So Shirley went alone, and she brought Caroline back—brought her, at first, like some timid creature coaxed from its shelter by a hand that offers no snare. Once they were fairly out on the quiet road, with the open sweep of Nunnely Common spreading before them—green turf flowing away into ridges of heath—Shirley made conversation as naturally as she drew breath. The first diffidence once overcome, Caroline felt relief in speaking; there was a comfort in Shirley’s companionship that did not exact perpetual effort, but warmed and encouraged, as a well-tended hearth warms without scorching.

A first exchange of slight remarks sufficed to give each an idea of the other. Shirley said she liked the green sweep of the common turf, and, better still, the heath on its ridges, because it reminded her of moors—moors she had seen in travelling near the Scottish border.

“I remember,” she said, “a district we crossed one long afternoon, on a sultry but sunless summer day. We journeyed from noon till sunset over what seemed a boundless waste of deep heath; and we saw nothing but wild sheep, and heard nothing but the cries of wild birds.”

“I know how the heath would look on such a day,” said Caroline, the quiet accuracy of her mind finding its picture at once: “purple-black—a deeper shade of the sky-tint; and that would be livid.”

“Yes—quite livid; with brassy edges to the clouds, and here and there a white gleam, more ghastly than the lurid tinge; and as you looked you momentarily expected it would kindle into blinding lightning.”

“Did it thunder?”

“It muttered distant peals; but the storm did not break till evening, after we had reached our inn—an isolated house at the foot of a range of mountains.”

“Did you watch the clouds come down over the mountains?”

“I did. I stood at the window an hour watching them. The hills seemed rolled in a sullen mist; and when the rain fell in whitening sheets, suddenly they were blotted from the prospect—they were washed from the world.”

“I have seen such storms in hilly parts of Yorkshire,” Caroline answered. “At their riotous climax—while the sky was all cataract, the earth all flood—I have remembered the Deluge.”

“It is singularly reviving,” said Shirley, “after such hurricanes, to feel calm return; and from the opening clouds to receive a consolatory gleam—softly testifying that the sun is not quenched.”

“Miss Keeldar,” Caroline said then, stopping on the brow where the wind met them, clean and salted with earth and leaf, “just stand still now, and look down at Nunnely dale and wood.”

They halted. Beneath them lay the valley robed in May—meads pearled with daisies, and others golden with king-cups; the young verdure smiling clear in sunlight, as if it had a taste of its own sweetness. On Nunnwood—the one remaining remnant of an antique British forest, where once the lowlands had been all chase and the highlands breast-deep in heather—slept the shadow of a cloud. Beyond, hills were dappled; the horizon was shaded and tinted like mother-of-pearl—silvery blues, soft purples, evanescent greens and rose-shades melting into fleeces of white cloud, pure as azury snow. The whole scene had its own hush and its own music: not the shriek and stamp of wheels, but the honest wind, the far-off call of birds, the faint stir of leaves; a sound-world that soothed by being true.

“Our England is a bonny island,” said Shirley, “and Yorkshire is one of her bonniest nooks.”

“You are a Yorkshire girl too?”

“I am—Yorkshire in blood and birth. Five generations of my race sleep under the aisles of Briarfield Church. I drew my first breath in the old black hall behind us.”

Caroline, with a sudden pleasure that was like tasting something homely and remembered, held out her hand. “We are compatriots,” she said.

“Yes,” Shirley agreed, gravely nodding, and taking that hand as if the clasp had sealed more than a light morning’s intimacy.

“And that,” Caroline asked, pointing, “that is Nunnwood?”

“It is.”

“Were you ever there?”

“Many a time.”

“In the heart of it?”

“Yes.”

“What is it like?”

“It is like an encampment of forest sons of Anak. The trees are huge and old. When you stand at their roots, the summits seem in another region. The trunks remain still and firm as pillars, while the boughs sway to every breeze. In the deepest calm their leaves are never quite hushed; and in high wind a flood rushes, a sea thunders above you.”

“Was it not one of Robin Hood’s haunts?”

“Yes; and there are mementos of him still. To penetrate into Nunnwood is to go far back into the dim days of old. Can you see a break in the forest, about the centre?”

“Yes—distinctly.”

“That break is a dell—a deep hollow cup, lined with turf as green and short as the sod of this common. The very oldest of the trees—gnarled, mighty oaks—crowd about the brink; and in the bottom lie the ruins of a nunnery.”

“We will go,” said Caroline, and her voice, though soft, had a longing in it like hunger—“you and I alone, early some fine summer morning, and spend a long day there. We can take pencils and sketch-books, and any interesting reading book we like; and of course we shall take something to eat. I have two little baskets, in which Mrs. Gill—my housekeeper—might pack our provisions; and we could each carry our own. It would not tire you too much to walk so far?”

“Oh no,” Caroline answered quickly—pleased, not only by the plan, but by the domestic certainty of it: the baskets, the packing, the thought of food made ready with care. “Especially if we rested the whole day in the wood. And I know all the pleasantest spots. I know where we could get nuts in nutting time; I know where wild strawberries abound; I know certain lonely, quite untrodden glades carpeted with strange mosses—some yellow as if gilded, some a sober gray, some gem-green. I know groups of trees that ravish the eye with their picture-like effects—rude oak, delicate birch, glossy beech clustered in contrast; ash trees stately as Saul, standing isolated; and superannuated wood-giants clad in bright shrouds of ivy. Miss Keeldar, I could guide you.”

“You would be dull with me alone?”

“I should not. I think we should suit; and what third person is there whose presence would not spoil our pleasure?”

“Indeed, I know of none about our own ages—no lady at least; and as to gentlemen—”

“An excursion becomes quite a different thing when there are gentlemen of the party,” Caroline interrupted, with a quick, almost frightened truthfulness.

“I agree with you—quite a different thing to what we were proposing.”

“We were going simply to see the old trees, the old ruins; to pass a day in old times, surrounded by olden silence, and above all by quietude.”

“You are right; and the presence of gentlemen dispels the last charm, I think. If they are of the wrong sort—like your Malones, your young Sykes, and Wynnes—irritation takes the place of serenity. If they are of the right sort, there is still a change; I can hardly tell what change—one easy to feel, difficult to describe.”

“We forget Nature, imprimis.”

“And then Nature forgets us,” said Shirley, “covers her vast calm brow with a dim veil, conceals her face, and withdraws the peaceful joy with which—if we had been content to worship her only—she would have filled our hearts.”

“What does she give us instead?”

“More elation and more anxiety; an excitement that steals the hours away fast, and a trouble that ruffles their course.”

“Our power of being happy lies a good deal in ourselves, I believe,” Caroline replied, with the sober sense she often used as a defence. “I have gone to Nunnwood with a large party—all the curates and some other gentry of these parts, with sundry ladies—and I found the affair insufferably tedious and absurd. And I have gone quite alone, or accompanied only by Fanny—who sat in the woodman’s hut and sewed, or talked to the goodwife, while I roamed about and made sketches or read—and I have enjoyed much happiness of a quiet kind all day. But that was when I was young—two years ago.”

“Did you ever go with your cousin, Robert Moore?”

“Yes; once.”

“What sort of a companion is he on these occasions?”

“A cousin, you know, is different to a stranger.”

“I am aware of that; but cousins, if they are stupid, are still more insupportable than strangers, because you cannot so easily keep them at a distance. But your cousin is not stupid?”

“No; but—”

“Well?”

“If the company of fools irritates, as you say,” Caroline answered, and her colour changed as if some nearer heat than the sun touched her, “the society of clever men leaves its own peculiar pain also. Where the goodness or talent of your friend is beyond and above all doubt, your own worthiness to be his associate often becomes a matter of question.”

“Oh! there I cannot follow you. That crotchet is not one I should choose to entertain for an instant,” said Shirley, with the proud generosity of her temperament. “I consider myself not unworthy to be the associate of the best of them—of gentlemen, I mean—though that is saying a great deal. Where they are good, they are very good, I believe. Your uncle, by-the-bye, is not a bad specimen of the elderly gentleman. I am always glad to see his brown, keen, sensible old face, either in my own house or any other. Are you fond of him? Is he kind to you? Now, speak the truth.”

“He has brought me up from childhood,” said Caroline. “I doubt not, precisely as he would have brought up his own daughter if he had had one; and that is kindness. But I am not fond of him. I would rather be out of his presence than in it.”

“Strange—when he has the art of making himself so agreeable.”

“Yes, in company; but he is stern and silent at home. As he puts away his cane and shovel-hat in the rectory hall, so he locks his liveliness in his bookcase and study-desk: the knitted brow and brief word for the fireside; the smile, the jest, the witty sally for society.”

“Is he tyrannical?”

“Not in the least. He is neither tyrannical nor hypocritical. He is simply a man who is rather liberal than good-natured, rather brilliant than genial, rather scrupulously equitable than truly just—if you can understand such superfine distinctions.”

“Oh yes,” said Shirley. “Good-nature implies indulgence, which he has not; geniality, warmth of heart, which he does not own; and genuine justice is the offspring of sympathy and considerateness, of which my bronzed old friend is quite innocent.”

“I often wonder,” Caroline went on, with that inward question she could never quite put away, “whether most men resemble my uncle in their domestic relations; whether it is necessary to be new and unfamiliar to them in order to seem agreeable or estimable in their eyes; and whether it is impossible to their natures to retain a constant interest and affection for those they see every day.”

“I don’t know,” Shirley replied. “I can’t clear up your doubts. I ponder over similar ones myself sometimes. But, to tell you a secret—if I were convinced they are necessarily and universally different from us—fickle, soon petrifying, unsympathizing—I would never marry. I should not like to find out that what I loved did not love me; that it was weary of me; and that whatever effort I might make to please would hereafter be worse than useless, since it was inevitably in its nature to change and become indifferent. That discovery once made, what should I long for? To go away—to remove from a presence where my society gave no pleasure.”

“But you could not if you were married.”

“No, I could not. There it is. I could never be my own mistress more. A terrible thought! It suffocates me! Nothing irks me like the idea of being a burden and a bore—an inevitable burden, a ceaseless bore. Now, when I feel my company superfluous, I can comfortably fold my independence round me like a mantle, and drop my pride like a veil, and withdraw to solitude. If married, that could not be.”

“I wonder we don’t all make up our minds to remain single,” said Caroline. “We should, if we listened to the wisdom of experience. My uncle always speaks of marriage as a burden; and I believe whenever he hears of a man being married he invariably regards him as a fool—or, at any rate, as doing a foolish thing.”

“But, Caroline, men are not all like your uncle. Surely not. I hope not.”

Shirley paused a moment, musing; the wind moved the heath with a low, continuous whisper, like a remembered language spoken under breath—intimate, yet half lost.

“I suppose we each find an exception in the one we love, till we are married,” Caroline suggested.

“I suppose so. And this exception we believe to be of sterling materials. We fancy it like ourselves; we imagine a sense of harmony. We think his voice gives the softest, truest promise of a heart that will never harden against us; we read in his eyes that faithful feeling—affection. I don’t think we should trust to what they call passion at all, Caroline. I believe it is a mere fire of dry sticks, blazing up and vanishing. But we watch him, and see him kind to animals, to little children, to poor people. He is kind to us likewise—good, considerate. He does not flatter women; but he is patient with them, and he seems to be easy in their presence, and to find their company genial. He likes them not only for vain and selfish reasons, but as we like him—because we like him. Then we observe he is just; that he always speaks the truth; that he is conscientious. We feel joy and peace when he comes into a room; we feel sadness and trouble when he leaves it. We know this man has been a kind son, that he is a kind brother. Will any one dare to tell me he will not be a kind husband?”

“My uncle would affirm it unhesitatingly. ‘He will be sick of you in a month,’ he would say.”

“Mrs. Pryor would seriously intimate the same.”

“Mrs. Yorke and Miss Mann would darkly suggest ditto.”

“If they are true oracles, it is good never to fall in love.”

“Very good—if you can avoid it.”

“I choose to doubt their truth.”

“I am afraid that proves you are already caught.”

“Not I. But if I were—do you know what soothsayers I would consult?”

“Let me hear.”

“Neither man nor woman—elderly nor young: the little Irish beggar that comes barefoot to my door; the mouse that steals out of the cranny in the wainscot; the bird that in frost and snow pecks at my window for a crumb; the dog that licks my hand and sits beside my knee.”

“Did you ever see any one who was kind to such things?” Caroline asked.

“Did you ever see any one whom such things seemed instinctively to follow—like—rely on?”

“We have a black cat and an old dog at the rectory,” Caroline answered, and now her voice softened, as if the simple household creatures drew her nearer to confession. “I know somebody to whose knee that black cat loves to climb—against whose shoulder and cheek it likes to purr. The old dog always comes out of his kennel and wags his tail, and whines affectionately when somebody passes.”

“And what does that somebody do?”

“He quietly strokes the cat, and lets her sit while he conveniently can; and when he must disturb her by rising, he puts her softly down, and never flings her from him roughly. He always whistles to the dog and gives him a caress.”

“Does he? It is not Robert?”

“But it is Robert.”

“Handsome fellow!” said Shirley, with sudden enthusiasm; her eyes sparkled as if the very name had struck fire on flint. “Is he not handsome? Has he not fine eyes and well-cut features, and a clear, princely forehead?”

“He has,” said Caroline—low.

“He has all that, Caroline. Bless him! he is both graceful and good.”

“I was sure you would see that he was. When I first looked at your face I knew you would.”

“I was well inclined to him before I saw him. I liked him when I did see him. I admire him now. There is charm in beauty for itself; when it is blent with goodness, there is a powerful charm.”

“When mind is added, Shirley?”

“Who can resist it?”

“Remember my uncle; Mesdames Pryor, Yorke, and Mann.”

“Remember the croaking of the frogs of Egypt. He is a noble being. I tell you, when they are good, they are the lords of the creation—they are the sons of God. Moulded in their Maker’s image, the minutest spark of His spirit lifts them almost above mortality. Indisputably, a great, good, handsome man is the first of created things.”

“Above us?”

“I would scorn to contend for empire with him—I would scorn it. Shall my left hand dispute for precedence with my right? Shall my heart quarrel with my pulse? Shall my veins be jealous of the blood which fills them?”

“Men and women—husbands and wives—quarrel horribly, Shirley.”

“Poor things! Poor, fallen, degenerate things! God made them for another lot—for other feelings.”

“But are we men’s equals, or are we not?”

“Nothing ever charms me more than when I meet my superior—one who makes me sincerely feel that he is my superior.”

“Did you ever meet him?”

“I should be glad to see him any day. The higher above me, so much the better. It degrades to stoop; it is glorious to look up. What frets me is, that when I try to esteem, I am baffled; when religiously inclined, there are but false gods to adore. I disdain to be a pagan.”

They had reached the rectory gates; the familiar sound of the place met Caroline’s ear—the faint clink from within, the subdued stir of a house lived in quietly, the outer world kept at a managed distance.

“Miss Keeldar, will you come in?” Caroline asked.

“Not to-day,” Shirley answered. “But to-morrow I shall fetch you to spend the evening with me. Caroline Helstone—if you really are what at present to me you seem—you and I will suit. I have never in my whole life been able to talk to a young lady as I have talked to you this morning. Kiss me—and good-bye.”

* * *

Mrs. Pryor, too, appeared disposed to cultivate Caroline’s acquaintance. She who went nowhere else called, early, at the rectory. She came in the afternoon, when the rector happened to be out. The day was close; the air lay thick as if it had been breathed over many times and not renewed. Mrs. Pryor’s face was flushed, and she seemed fluttered—more by entering a strange house than by any fatigue. Her habits were so secluded, so retired, that a new threshold affected her like a sudden change of climate.

When Caroline went into the dining-room she found her seated on the sofa, trembling, fanning herself with her handkerchief, and contending with a nervous discomposure that threatened hysteria.

Caroline marvelled at so unusual a want of self-command in a lady of Mrs. Pryor’s years; and, too, at the lack of real strength in one who appeared almost robust. Mrs. Pryor hastened to allege the fatigue of her walk—the heat of the sun—this, that, and the other, as reasons for her indisposition; and still, with more hurry than coherence, she repeated these causes, while Caroline, gently and quietly, sought to relieve her—unfastening the shawl, removing the bonnet, making the close room seem, by these small orderly offices, more breathable.

Attentions of this sort Mrs. Pryor would not have accepted from every one. Usually she recoiled from touch or close approach with a mixture of embarrassment and coldness that repelled the very kindness offered. To Caroline’s little light hand, however, she yielded tractably, and seemed soothed by the contact. In a few minutes she ceased to tremble, grew quiet, and resumed her habitual composure, as if the disturbance had been some sudden, passing storm of the nerves.

Then she began to talk of ordinary topics. In mixed company Mrs. Pryor rarely spoke, or, if obliged to speak, spoke under restraint and therefore not well. In dialogue she was different: a good converser—formal in phrase, but choosing her words with care; just in sentiment; various and accurate in information. Caroline found it pleasant to listen—more pleasant than she had expected; it was as though, behind Mrs. Pryor’s reserve, there existed a mind that could season common things with intelligence, as a careful hand seasons plain food so that it nourishes and satisfies.

On the wall opposite the sofa hung three pictures: in the centre, over the mantelpiece, the portrait of a lady; on either side, male portraits.

“That is a beautiful face,” said Mrs. Pryor at length, breaking a brief pause that had followed half an hour’s animated conversation. “The features may be termed perfect; no statuary’s chisel could improve them. It is a portrait from the life, I presume?”

“It is a portrait of Mrs. Helstone,” Caroline answered.

“Of Mrs. Matthewson Helstone—of your uncle’s wife?”

“It is; and is said to be a good likeness. Before her marriage she was accounted the beauty of the district.”

“I should say she merited the distinction. What accuracy in all the lineaments! It is, however, a passive face. The original could not have been what is generally termed ‘a woman of spirit.’”

“I believe she was a remarkably still, silent person.”

“One would scarcely have expected, my dear, that your uncle’s choice should have fallen on a partner of that description. Is he not fond of being amused by lively chat?”

“In company he is. But he always says he could never do with a talking wife. He must have quiet at home. You go out to gossip, he affirms; you come home to read and reflect.”

“Mrs. Matthewson lived but a few years after her marriage, I think I have heard?”

“About five years.”

“Well, my dear,” said Mrs. Pryor, rising to go, “I trust it is understood that you will frequently come to Fieldhead. I hope you will. You must feel lonely here, having no female relative in the house; you must necessarily pass much of your time in solitude.”

“I am inured to it,” Caroline replied. “I have grown up by myself. May I arrange your shawl for you?”

Mrs. Pryor submitted to be assisted; and, when Caroline had set the folds straight, and placed the clasp with quiet neatness, the older lady added, in her grave tone, “Should you chance to require help in your studies, you may command me.”
