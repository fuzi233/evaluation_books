At five o'clock, the shop stood silent and shuttered until the Christian servant, a figure of quiet efficiency, unlocked the door and ushered Deronda into the rear chamber. There, the atmosphere transformed. Though the house was old and the room likely gloomy by day, the evening light, filtered through a magnificent brass lamp with seven oil-flames, bathed the space in a warm, Venetian glow. The smoke-stained ceiling and dark walls served only to heighten the brilliance of the scene below.

The tableau was one of striking, almost theatrical domesticity. The grandmother, draped in yellowish-brown silk and adorned with a heavy gold chain that replaced her necklace, sat with a face painted in shades of yellow and framed by dark brows and a roll of gray hair; she possessed a beauty suited entirely to the picturesque. Beside her, young Mrs. Cohen wore red and black, her neck encircled by a thick string of artificial pearls. Between them lay the sleeping infant, swaddled under a scarlet counterpane. Adelaide Rebekah shimmered in braided amber, while Jacob Alexander stood in black velveteen with scarlet stockings. As the four pairs of dark eyes glistened with welcome, Deronda felt a sudden flush of shame for the haughty disdain he had harbored during the daylight hours. The warmth of their greeting was absolute; both mother and grandmother seemed to derive a heightened dignity from the intimacy of the private hearth.

Deronda allowed his gaze to drift over the surroundings with parenthetic wonder. The oaken bureau and high side-tables appeared to be artifacts of necessity rather than taste, yet they held a certain solidity. On the side-table rested a large dish of blue and yellow ware, flanked by two tarnished silver vessels, before which lay a massive volume bound in darkened vellum with a deeply ribbed back. An open door at the far end revealed a second room, also aglow with light.

While absorbing these details, Deronda was drawn into the immediate business at hand by Jacob's earnest solicitude regarding a knife. The boy had procured a specific tool—a hook-blade with a white handle—and presented it for inspection.

"Is that the sort of thing you want, Jacob?" Deronda asked.

Jacob subjected the item to a rigorous scrutiny, opening the hook and blades, then producing the cork-screw knife for comparison. "Why do you like a hook better than a cork-screw?" Deronda inquired.

"'Caush I can get hold of things with a hook," Jacob explained, his logic impeccable. "A cork-screw won't go into anything but corks. But it's better for you, you can draw corks."

"You agree to change, then?" Deronda asked, noting the grandmother's delighted attention.

"What else have you got in your pockets?" Jacob demanded with deliberative seriousness.

"Hush, hush, Jacob, love," the grandmother chided gently. Mindful of propriety, Deronda replied, "I think I must not tell you that. Our business was with the knives."

Jacob scanned Deronda's face for a moment, seemingly satisfied with his assessment, and declared gravely, "I'll shwop," handing over the cork-screw knife. Deronda pocketed it with equal gravity.

Instantly, the small son of Shem darted into the next room, his voice rising in rapid chatter, only to return moments later. Seeing his father enter, the boy seized a little velveteen hat from a chair and donned it, rushing forward to greet the visitor. Cohen, however, kept his own hat on, ignoring the child's gesture, and stood still as the two youngsters climbed to clasp his knees. He laid his hands upon each in turn, uttering a Hebrew benediction. Then, the wife, having lifted the sleeping baby from the cradle, brought the infant forward to hold beneath her husband's outstretched hands for blessing. In that suspended moment, Deronda perceived that this pawnbroker, proud of his vocation, possessed a soul that was not entirely prosaic.

"Well, sir, you found your welcome in my family, I think," Cohen said, removing his hat and resuming his usual demeanor. "And you've been punctual. Nothing like a little stress here," he added, tapping his side pocket as he sat. "It's good for us all in our turn. I've felt it when I've had to make up payments. I began to fit every sort of box. It's bracing to the mind."

"That is the ring I spoke of," Deronda said, lifting it from his finger. "I believe it cost a hundred pounds. It will be a sufficient pledge to you for fifty, I think. I shall probably redeem it in a month or so."

Cohen's glistening eyes narrowed slightly as he met the ingenuous gaze of this crude young gentleman, who apparently assumed that redemption was a source of satisfaction to a pawnbroker. He took the ring, examined it, and returned it with an air of indifference. "Good, good. We'll talk of it after our meal. Perhaps you'll join us, if you've no objection. Me and my wife'll feel honored, and so will mother; won't you, mother?"

The invitation was doubly echoed, and Deronda gladly accepted. The family gathered around the table. No dish was visible save one covered by a napkin; Mrs. Cohen had placed a china bowl near her husband for hand-washing. But just as Cohen prepared to sit, he paused, donning his hat again, and called out loudly, "Mordecai!"

Could this be part of the religious ceremony? Deronda wondered, unsure what ancient rites might be expected. He heard a "Yes" from the next room and turned toward the open door. To his astonishment, he saw the enigmatic Jew he had encountered that morning in the bookshop. Their eyes met, and Mordecai looked as surprised as Deronda; neither offered a sign of recognition. Yet, as Mordecai seated himself at the far end of the table, he merely bowed his head to the guest in a cold, distant manner, as if the disappointment of the morning remained a disagreeable association with this new acquaintance.

Cohen now washed his hands, pronouncing Hebrew words throughout the ritual. He removed the napkin to reveal two long, flat loaves sprinkled with seed—the memorial of the manna that fed the wandering forefathers. Breaking off small pieces, he distributed them to each member of the family, including Adelaide Rebekah, who stood on a chair, her entire length displayed in her amber garment, her little Jewish nose comically lengthened by the compression of her lips in an effort to maintain a suitable posture. Cohen uttered another blessing, uncovered the male heads, and the meal commenced without any peculiarity that particularly interested Deronda.

Preoccupied with a desire to steer the conversation toward a leading question, and thinking constantly of Mordecai, Deronda ate with little consciousness of the dishes. Between them, Mordecai and himself had exchanged fascinated, half-furtive glances. Mordecai wore no handsome Sabbath garment; instead of the threadbare rusty black coat of the morning, he donned one of light drab that looked as if it had once been a handsome loose paletot, now shrunk and worn by washing. This change of clothing accentuated his dark-haired, eager face, which might have belonged to the prophet Ezekiel—certainly not modish in the eyes of contemporaries. It was noticeable that the thin tails of the fried fish were reserved for Mordecai, a share assigned to a poor relation, a survival of prehistoric practice not yet generally admitted to be superstitious.

Mr. Cohen maintained the conversation with lively zest, introducing subjects always in taste—the Queen and the Royal Family, the Emperor and Empress of the French—into which both grandmother and wife entered with enthusiasm. Mrs. Cohen the younger displayed an accurate memory of distinguished birthdays, while the elder assisted her son in recounting the events of the Emperor and Empress's visit to the city ten years prior.

"I dare say you know all about it better than we do, sir," Cohen repeated, serving as a preface to full information, as the trio continued the narrative.

"Our baby is named *Eugenie Esther*," said young Mrs. Cohen, vivaciously.

"It's wonderful how the Emperor's like a cousin of mine in the face," the grandmother remarked. "It struck me like lightning when I caught sight of him. I couldn't have thought it."

"Mother and me went to see the Emperor and Empress at the Crystal Palace," Mr. Cohen declared. "I had a fine piece of work to take care of, mother; she might have been squeezed flat—though she was pretty near as lusty then as she is now. I said if I had a hundred mothers I'd never take one of 'em to see the Emperor and Empress at the Crystal Palace again; and you may think a man can't afford it when he's got but one mother—not if he'd ever so big an insurance on her." He stroked his mother's shoulder affectionately and chuckled at his own humor.

"Your mother has been a widow a long while, perhaps," Deronda seized his opportunity. "That has made your care for her the more needful."

"Ay, ay, it's a good many *yore-zeit* since I had to manage for her and myself," Cohen replied quickly. "I went early to it. It's that makes you a sharp knife."

"What does—what makes a sharp knife, father?" asked Jacob, his cheek very much swollen with sweet-cake.

The father winked at his guest and said, "Having your nose put on the grindstone."

Jacob slipped from his chair with the piece of sweet-cake in his hand and approached Mordecai, who had been totally silent hitherto. "What does that mean—putting my nose to the grindstone?"

"It means that you are to bear being hurt without making a noise," Mordecai answered, turning his eyes benignantly on the small face close to his. Jacob put the corner of the cake into Mordecai's mouth as an invitation to bite, saying meanwhile, "I shan't though," and keeping his eyes on the cake to observe how much of it went in this act of generosity. Mordecai took a bite and smiled, evidently meaning to please the lad, and the little incident made them both look more lovable. Deronda, however, felt with some vexation that he had taken little by his question.

"I fancy that is the right quarter for learning," Deronda said, carrying on the subject to secure an excuse for addressing Mordecai. He turned to the silent man and asked, "You have been a great student, I imagine?"

"I have studied," was the quiet answer. "And you?—You know German by the book you were buying."

"Yes, I have studied in Germany. Are you generally engaged in bookselling?" Deronda asked.

"No; I only go to Mr. Ram's shop every day to keep it while he goes to meals," Mordecai replied. Now looking at Deronda with what seemed a revival of his original interest, it appeared as if the face held some attractive indication for him which now neutralized the former disappointment. After a slight pause, he said, "Perhaps you know Hebrew?"

"I am sorry to say, not at all."

Mordecai's countenance fell; he cast down his eyelids, looking at his hands which lay crossed before him, and said no more. Deronda had now noticed more decisively than in their former interview a difficulty in breathing, which he thought must be a sign of consumption.

"I've had something else to do than to get book-learning," Mr. Cohen interjected. "I've had to make myself knowing about useful things. I know stones well"—here he pointed to Deronda's ring. "I'm not afraid of taking that ring of yours at my own valuation. But now," he added, with a certain drop in his voice to a lower, more familiar nasal, "what do you want for it?"

"Fifty or sixty pounds," Deronda answered, rather too carelessly.

Cohen paused a little, thrust his hands into his pockets, fixed on Deronda a pair of glistening eyes that suggested a miraculous guinea-pig, and said, "Couldn't do you that. Happy to oblige, but couldn't go that lengths. Forty pound—say forty—I'll let you have forty on it."

Deronda was aware that Mordecai had looked up again at the words implying a monetary affair, and was now examining him again, while he said, "Very well, I shall redeem it in a month or so."

"Good. I'll make you out the ticket by-and-by," Cohen said indifferently. Then he held up his finger as a sign that conversation must be deferred. He, Mordecai, and Jacob put on their hats, and Cohen opened a thanksgiving, which was carried on by responses, till Mordecai delivered himself alone at some length, in a solemn chanting tone, with his chin slightly uplifted and his thin hands clasped easily before him. Not only in his accent and tone, but in his freedom from the self-consciousness which has reference to others' approbation, there could hardly have been a stronger contrast to the Jew at the other end of the table. It was an unaccountable conjunction—the presence among these common, prosperous, shopkeeping types, of a man who, in an emaciated threadbare condition, imposed a certain awe on Deronda, and an embarrassment at not meeting his expectations.

No sooner had Mordecai finished his devotional strain than, rising with a slight bend of his head to the stranger, he walked back into his room and shut the door behind him.
