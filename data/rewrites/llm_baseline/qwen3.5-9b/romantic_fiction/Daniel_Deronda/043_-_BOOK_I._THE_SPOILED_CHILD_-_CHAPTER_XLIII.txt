For a few moments, an unusual silence settled over Mordecai's consciousness. He looked up at Deronda, not with confusion or surprise, but with a gaze heavy with peaceful contentment. Deronda rose and pulled his chair closer, ensuring no need existed to raise his voice. To Mordecai, this gesture felt like the gentle easing of a pillow beneath a weary head. Speaking in a low, intimate murmur—as if articulating thoughts meant only for himself—he began.

"In the teachings of the Cabbala, souls are reborn repeatedly into new vessels until they achieve perfection and purification. When a soul is freed from a worn-out body, it joins the fellow-soul that requires it, so that together they may be perfected and fulfill their earthly mission. Only then do they depart the mortal realm, leaving space for fresh souls drawn from the eternal reservoir. It is the lingering imperfection of those already born into mortality that delays the arrival of new souls and postpones the Messianic era. Thus, the mind gives shape to what is hidden, casting a shadow of the known, speaking truth even in parable. When my wandering soul is finally liberated from this tired body, it will unite with yours, and our shared work will be completed."

Mordecai's pause hung in the air, an appeal Deronda could not ignore. Struggling to find words that were truly honest, he spoke anyway, though for Mordecai's ear, every syllable carried an unspoken depth. "Whatever I can do, in clear conscience, to make your life meaningful, I shall do."

"I know," Mordecai replied, his tone radiating a quiet certainty that rendered further reassurance unnecessary. "I heard you. You see everything; you stand beside me on the mountain of vision, witnessing the paths of fulfillment that others refuse to acknowledge."

After a brief silence, Mordecai continued, his voice meditative. "You will carry on where my life was severed. I feel myself transported back to that fateful day when my life broke. The bright morning sun shone on the quay at Trieste; the garments of men from every nation glittered like jewels; boats pushed off into the water. The Greek vessel bound for Beirut was scheduled to depart within the hour. I was traveling with a merchant as his clerk and companion. I told myself I would behold the lands and peoples of the East, and speak with a fuller vision. I breathed then as you do now, without effort; I possessed the light step and enduring strength of youth. I could fast, I could sleep on hard ground. I had married poverty, and I loved my bride—for to me, poverty was freedom. My heart exulted as if it belonged to Moses ben Maimon, strengthened by the vigor of sixty years, knowing the vast work ahead.

"It was the first time I had traveled south; the soul within me felt its ancient sun. Standing on the quay, where the very ground seemed to emit light and the shadows wore an azure glory as if spirits were made visible, I felt immersed in a glorious life, so overwhelming that my own small, counted existence melted away, unnoticed. A great sob rose within me, like the rush of waters too powerful to resist—a bliss too strong to bear. So I waited for my companion, and I did not see him until he said, 'Ezra, I have been to the post; here is your letter.'"

"Ezra!" Deronda exclaimed, unable to hold back his shock.

"Ezra," Mordecai repeated, affirmative and absorbed in memory. "I was expecting a letter, for I wrote constantly to my mother. That sound of my name was like the touch of a wand, recalling me to the body from which I had been released, allowing me to mingle once more with the ocean of human existence, free from the pressure of individual isolation. I opened the letter; and the name came again as a cry that would have disturbed me even in the bosom of heaven, making me yearn to return to that sorrow. 'Ezra, my son!'"

Mordecai paused again, his imagination arrested by the grip of that distant moment. Deronda's mind hung suspended, breathless, as a strange possibility suddenly crystallized. Mordecai's eyes were cast downward in abstracted contemplation. Moments later, he resumed.

"She was a mother worthy of the saying, 'Her children arise and call her blessed.' In her, I understood the meaning of the Master who, perceiving his mother's footsteps, rose and declared, 'The Majesty of the Eternal draws near!' That letter was her cry from the depths of anguish and desolation—the scream of a mother robbed of her little ones. I was her eldest. Death had taken four babies one after another. Then came, late, my little sister, who was, more than all the rest, the desire of my mother's eyes. The letter was a piercing cry to me: 'Ezra, my son, I am robbed of her. He has taken her away and left disgrace behind. They will never come again.'"

Here, Mordecai lifted his eyes suddenly, laid his hand on Deronda's arm, and said, "Mine was the lot of Israel. Because of the father's sin, my soul must go into exile. Because of the father's sin, the work was broken, and the day of fulfillment delayed. She who bore me was desolate, disgraced, destitute. I turned back. On the instant I turned, the spirit of my mother and the spirit of her fathers, who held worthy Jewish hearts, moved within me and drew me forward. God, in whom the universe dwells, became within me the strength of obedience. I turned and traveled with hardship—to save the scant money she would need. I left the sunshine and journeyed into freezing cold. In the final stages, I spent a night exposed to snow and ice. That was the beginning of this slow death."

Mordecai let his eyes wander again and withdrew his hand. Deronda resolutely suppressed the questions rising in his throat. While Mordecai remained in this state of emotion, no other confession could be sought than what came spontaneously; indeed, Deronda felt a kindred emotion that made him dread his own speech as too momentous.

"But I worked. We were destitute—everything had been seized. And she was ill: the clutch of anguish was too strong for her, working alongside some lurking disease. At times she could not stand against the pounding of her heart, and the images in her brain became chambers of terror where she beheld my sister being raised in evil. In the dead of night, I heard her crying for her child. Then I rose, and we stretched forth our arms together and prayed. We poured our souls out in desire that Mirah might be delivered from evil."

"Mirah?" Deronda repeated, wishing to ensure his ears had not betrayed him to a forecasting imagination. "Did you say Mirah?"

"That was my little sister's name."

"After we had prayed for her, my mother would rest awhile. It lasted hardly four years, and in the minute before she died, we were praying the same prayer—I aloud, she silently. Her soul went out upon its wings."

"Have you never since heard of your sister?" Deronda asked, as quietly as he could manage.

"Never. Never have I heard whether she was delivered according to our prayer. I know not, I know not. Who shall say where the pathways lie? The poisonous will of the wicked is strong. It poisoned my life—it is slowly stifling this breath. Death delivered my mother, and I felt it a blessedness to be alone in the winters of suffering. But what are the winters now?—they are far off." Here Mordecai rested his hand on Deronda's arm again, looking at him with that joy of the hectic patient that pierces the observer to sadness. "There is nothing to wail in the withering of my body. The work will be done the better. Once I said the work of this beginning was mine, I am born to do it. Well, I shall do it. I shall live in you. I shall live in you."

His grasp had become convulsive in its force. Deronda, agitated as he had never been before—the certainty that this was Mirah's brother suffusing his own strange connection to Mordecai with a new solemnity and tenderness—felt his strong young heart beating faster and his lips growing pale. He shrank from speech. He feared that, in Mordecai's present state of exaltation (an already alarming strain on his feeble frame), to utter a word of revelation about Mirah would be disastrous. He feared to offer an answer below that high pitch of expectation, which resembled a flash from a dying fire, causing watchers to fear seeing it extinguish all the faster. His dominant impulse was to act as he had once before: he laid his firm, gentle hand on the hand that gripped him.

Mordecai's hand, as if possessing a soul of its own—for he was not distinctly willing to release it—relaxed its grasp and turned upward under Deronda's. As the two palms met and pressed together, Mordecai regained some sense of his surroundings and said, "Let us go now. I cannot talk any longer."

Indeed, they parted at Cohen's door without exchanging another word—only with another pressing of hands. Deronda felt a weight upon him that was half joy, half anxiety.
