It is a perilous and poorly compensated endeavor to attempt to pre-order the chaotic river of existence according to the strict blueprints of one's own safety, a folly Machiavelli attributed to Cesare Borgia. The condottiere, in his grim foresight, calculated every contingency following his father's demise, fortifying against every conceivable misfortune except one singular, catastrophic oversight: he never conceived that the shadow cast by his father's death would inevitably swallow his own life in its wake.

Grandcourt's significance within this realm was of a grandly passive magnitude, rooted solely in the inheritance of acres. The tides of political upheaval and social reform washed over him only through the slender wire of his rental income; his biography could remain untouched by the complexities of Schleswig-Holstein, Bismarck's iron policies, the rise of trade unions, household suffrage, or the latest commercial panics. He skimmed the broadsheets on these tumultuous subjects, his opinions lacking any genuine breadth, categorizing all Germans, all merchants, and all voters prone to using inferior soap under the sweeping, dismissive label of "brutes." Yet, he took no tangible action on these agitated matters beyond glancing from beneath his lashes at anyone who dared mention them, cultivating a silence so profound it shook the foundations of timid intellects.

Within his own narrow sphere, however, Grandcourt displayed the qualities of triumphant diplomacy in its wildest continental incarnation. Not a movement of Gwendolen in relation to Deronda escaped his scrutiny. He would vehemently deny possessing jealousy, for such an admission would imply a doubt in his absolute power to obstruct what he had resolved against. That his wife should harbor inclinations toward another man's society caused him no pain; what he demanded was that she remain as acutely aware of her confinement as one is of a locked handcuff, rendering her inclination helpless to contradict his resolve. Though his entry into matrimony may have been marked by vacillating whims, his interpretation of the marital bond was unyielding. He harbored no repentance; indeed, the union had injected a new aim into his life, fresh objects upon which to exert his will. He had not regretted his choice. His taste was fastidious, and Gwendolen satisfied it. He would not tolerate a wife who had not ascended in rank through him, nor one who failed to command admiration through her bearing and beauty, nor one whose nails lacked proper shape, nor one whose ear lobes were too large and red. Nor would he accept a woman who, despite physical perfection, remained a ninny, incapable of delivering spirited replies. These demands may not seem excessive to refined contemporaries whose capacity for love hangs suspended due to a lack of indispensable details, yet few could match his satisfaction in securing a wife inclined to fly out if she dared, and urged into marrying him by sentiments other than passionate attachment. Still, for those who prefer command to love, the necessity of altering one's mindset precisely at the threshold of matrimony remains obscure.

Grandcourt did not believe he had selected the wrong wife; having assumed the role of husband, he refused to be duped or viewed in a light that invited pity. This was his state of mind—not jealousy, yet his behavior mirrored it as closely as yellow resembles yellow, a hue known to stem from vastly different origins.

He had returned to town earlier than usual, driven by the need for legal counsel regarding his will, the transfer of mortgages, and the transaction with his uncle concerning the succession to Diplow. The lure of ready money, skillfully dangled without importunity, had finally secured his agreement. Another acceptable justification for his presence in the city was the presentation of himself alongside the beautiful bride he had chosen, defying all external expectations. True, Grandcourt moved with the conviction that he cared not a languid curse for anyone's admiration; yet this state of non-care, like desire itself, required an object—a world of admiring or envious spectators. For if one delights in staring stonily at smiling persons, those persons must smile, a rudimentary truth often forgotten by those who lament mankind's general contemptibility, since any other aspect of the race would fail to satisfy the voracity of their disdain. In town for the first time with his wife, Grandcourt expanded his abstention from curses through splendid receptions, conspicuous rides, and public appearances designed to showcase her. He desired that she be sought after; he relished seeing "fellows" eager to converse with her and escort her within his line of sight. There was even a certain lofty coquetry on her part that he found acceptable. What he detested, however, were her interactions with Deronda.

Following the musical gathering at Lady Mallinger's, where Grandcourt had watched the dialogue on the settee with the intensity of a hawk, it was characteristic of him to invite Deronda alongside the Mallingers, tenaciously avoiding any implication that Deronda's presence mattered to him in the slightest. He refrained from commenting directly on Gwendolen's behavior that evening, fearing his disgust might be too potent to satisfy his own pride. A few days later, however, he remarked, disregarding propriety, "Nothing makes a woman more of a gawky than looking out after people and showing tempers in public. A woman ought to have good manners. Else it's intolerable to appear with her."

Gwendolen made the expected connection, alarmed by the prospect of being deemed a gawky. Like him, she possessed a melancholy distaste for things, preferring that her aversion extend to admirers as well. Yet, the looming threat of rebuke only intensified the tension surrounding any potential encounter with Deronda. The novelty and excitement of her urban existence resembled the hurried, constant flux of foreign travel; regardless of her internal despondency, there was a program to fulfill, yielding gratification to her multifaceted self. As always occurs with deep interest, the relatively rare opportunities to exchange words with Deronda created a diffusive effect in her consciousness, magnifying their communications and inflating the significance she imagined they held in his mind. How could Deronda prevent this? He certainly did not avoid her; rather, he endeavored to convince her through every delicate, indirect means that her confidence in him had not been indiscreet, as it had not diminished his respect. Furthermore, he enjoyed her proximity—how could it be otherwise? She was more than a problem; she was a lovely woman, for whose mind and fate he harbored a care that, however futile, solicited him as a responsibility. Indeed, this burden grew heavier when he contemplated his own future, envisioning it lying far distant from this splendid, sad-hearted creature. Because he had once been impelled to arrest her attention momentarily—as if seizing her arm to warn her from stepping into danger—she had turned to him with a beseeching, persistent need.

One instance where Grandcourt stimulated a feeling in Gwendolen that he wished to suppress without appearing concerned involved Mirah. Gwendolen's inclination to pursue singing lessons lingered as a form of obedience to Deronda's advice, but day followed day with that palpable lack of leisure characteristic of lives devoid of work to mark intervals. The constant liability of Grandcourt's presence and surveillance flattened every effort to the level of the boredom his manner expressed; his negative mind was as diffusive as fog, clinging to all objects and spoiling all contact.

Yet, one morning during breakfast, Gwendolen, in a recurring fit of determination to revive her old spirit, said, dallying prettily over her prawns without consuming them, "I think of making myself accomplished while we are in town, and having singing lessons."

"Why?" Grandcourt asked, languidly.

"Why?" Gwendolen echoed, playing at sauciness. "Because I can't eat *pâté de foie gras* to make me sleepy, and I can't smoke, and I can't go to the club to make me like to come away again—I want a variety of *ennui*. What would be the most convenient time, when you are busy with your lawyers and people, for me to have lessons from that little Jewess, whose singing is getting all the rage?"

"Whenever you like," Grandcourt replied, pushing his plate aside and leaning back in his chair, regarding her with his most lizard-like expression while playing with the ears of the tiny spaniel on his lap—a dog Gwendolen had come to dislike for fawning upon him. Then, in the same languid tone, he added, "I don't see why a lady should sing. Amateurs make fools of themselves. A lady can't risk herself in that way in company. And one doesn't want to hear squalling in private."

"I like frankness: that seems to me a husband's great charm," Gwendolen said, executing a little upward movement of her chin as she turned her eyes away from his. Lifting a prawn before her, she regarded the boiled ingenuousness of its eyes as preferable to the reptile's. "But," she added, having devoured her mortification, "I suppose you don't object to Miss Lapidoth's singing at our party on the fourth? I thought of engaging her. Lady Brackenshaw had her, you know: and the Raymonds, who are very particular about their music. And Mr. Deronda, who is a musician himself and a first-rate judge, says there is no singing in such good taste as hers for a drawing-room. I think his opinion is an authority."

She intended to sling a small stone at her husband in that manner.

"It's very indecent of Deronda to go about praising that girl," Grandcourt said in a tone of indifference.
