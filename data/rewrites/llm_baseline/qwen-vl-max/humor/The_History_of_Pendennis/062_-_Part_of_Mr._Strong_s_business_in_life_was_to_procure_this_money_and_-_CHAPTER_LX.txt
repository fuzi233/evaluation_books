CHAPTER LX. Explanations (Rewritten for Humor)

Ah, time—how it creeps along like a bad butler who’s forgotten where the wine is kept! Nearly a year has passed since we last saw our dear Arthur Pendennis, and what a transformation he’s undergone! Gone are the days of mourning—he’s traded in his black coat for a dashing blue one, as if to say, “I’ve moved on from grief, though I still haven’t quite mastered the art of not being ridiculous.” His wig? Shunned like a bad idea at a dinner party. His hair, thinner than a poorly written novel, now flutters in the breeze like a flag of surrender to aging. And then there’s the uniform—Cornet of the Clavering troop of the ——shire Yeomanry Cavalry! Not exactly the Household Cavalry, but hey, it’s something. He was presented to the Sovereign by none other than the Marquis of Steyne, a man so noble he could probably make a sandwich out of silk and call it diplomacy.

This grand gesture was insisted upon by Arthur’s uncle, Major Pendennis, whose enthusiasm for gentlemanly rituals knew no bounds. The Major believed that every young man should be properly introduced into society—preferably before he starts quoting poetry to waitresses. He also took great pleasure in announcing, with the solemnity of a weather forecast, that Arthur’s fortune had turned out “not by any means as well as he could have hoped,” amounting to a mere fifteen hundred pounds a year. Which, in the world of high society, is about as impressive as showing up to a ball wearing a hat made of cardboard and calling it “art.”

But don’t let that fool you. In the literary world, Arthur’s status has soared. Publishers now treat him like a long-lost prince, and even mothers—those formidable creatures who once wouldn’t give him the time of day—are suddenly civil. Why? Because while the dazzling Myra gets to marry an Earl (a man who probably thinks “politics” means “where the good wine is served”), poor little Beatrice Hawxby, whose shoulder is slightly higher than the other (a fact she wears like a badge of honor), needs someone reliable. And who better than Mr. Pendennis? A man with literary credentials and a face that says, “I may be awkward, but I’m trying.”

Indeed, during the first winter after inheriting his mother’s estate, Mrs. Hawxby—who clearly thought she’d found her next Jane Austen—had her daughter learn billiards from Arthur and insisted on being driven only by him in the pony carriage. She declared, with the dramatic flair of a woman who’s read too many novels, that Arthur had behaved “most infamously” in trifling with Beatrice’s feelings. But the truth? The Major, ever vigilant, had swooped in like a hawk protecting its nest, whisking Arthur away from Mrs. Hawxby’s clutches—though not from her tongue, which continued to wag like a dog’s tail at a squirrel convention.

The Major had wanted Arthur to spend Christmas at Clavering Hall, but Arthur declined. Clavering was too close to Fairoaks, the old family home now haunted by memories of lost love and tragic misunderstandings. So instead, we skip ahead to the Epsom race-ground, where the Claverings reappear—looking rather like a family that’s been through a hurricane and decided to keep living in the wreckage.

Poor Lady Clavering, once a woman whose smile could light up a room and whose grammar could trip up a professor, has had her appetite and cheerfulness severely tested. Her dinners now come with side orders of family drama, and even the finest French cook can’t make a meal palatable when your husband is gambling away the house and your daughter is flirting with the wrong kind of men.

“I’d rather have a turnip, Strong,” she sighs, staring at a pineapple like it’s plotting against her, “than all these Muscatel grapes from Clavering—if only I could eat it in peace. Oh, how much happier I was when I was a widow!” And truly, she was. Now she’s trapped in a marriage where the only thing more predictable than her husband’s debts is his ability to charm his way out of them—until he doesn’t.

The Clavering family, bless them, made a disastrous entrance into society. Their London season was met with polite indifference, followed by outright rejection. Sir Francis Clavering? Too low, too dull, too disreputable. People whispered he had “a taint”—like a bad smell you can’t quite place. Who was the Begum? Where did she come from? Why did she speak English with such strange pronunciation? And why did her daughter insist on behaving like a French aristocrat who’d just escaped from a costume party?

Sir Francis, it turns out, was not only a gambler but also a card cheat who showed the white feather—meaning he folded when the stakes got high. Hely Clinker, a fellow officer, confirmed this with the gravity of a man delivering bad news at a funeral. Lady Rockminster, who had once taken Lady Clavering under her wing, dropped her like a hot potato. The elite refused to bring their daughters to her parties. Young men attended, yes—but with the freedom of wolves at a sheep farm. Poor Lady Clavering was forced to invite “the canal”—a term for those who weren’t quite top-tier but were still better than nothing—to her parlour, because “the tip-tops” wouldn’t come.

And yet, she bore no ill will. She wasn’t proud. She didn’t think herself better than anyone. She simply followed the rules laid down by her social godmother: know whom they know, ask whom they ask. The “canal,” in fact, was far more enjoyable than “society,” which is always full of people pretending to be happy while secretly hating each other.

One young man, however, remained loyal—Harry Foker, Esq. But loyalty, as we know, often comes with consequences. His passion for Blanche Clavering became public knowledge, reached his mother’s ears, and eventually landed on the desk of Foker senior—a man whose bald head seemed to reflect the emptiness of his heart. A violent scene ensued, ending in Harry’s exile to France for a year. He was told to return and marry his cousin—or retire into private life with three hundred pounds a year and never see his father or brewery again.

So off Harry went, carrying with him the sorrow of the exiled, which, as we’re told, passes freely through customs. Even Paris looked bleak to him, and Italy’s sky seemed perpetually overcast. One might say he brought the clouds with him.

Meanwhile, Sir Francis Clavering faced his own disaster. That same year, Lord Harrowhill’s horse—named Podasokus, because apparently classical names were all the rage—won the Derby. Most experts had backed Borax, who finished nowhere near the finish line. Sir Francis, relying on “valuable information” from shady turf characters, had bet heavily against Podasokus and lost—seven thousand pounds, to be exact.

Lady Clavering, already burdened with paying off her husband’s debts, now faced another crushing blow. She had paid his money-lenders, furnished both houses, and endured countless promises of reform—only to be called upon again to bail him out. It was a cruel irony, especially since she had done everything to save him, only to find him still incapable of managing his own finances.

Enter Major Pendennis, the wise old owl of the family. He had become the Claverings’ unofficial advisor, having inspected every room of their house—including that dreaded closet where families hide their skeletons. As for Sir Francis’s finances? Well, they were so tangled in lies that even the most skilled accountant would have given up and gone to bed early.

But the Major knew Lady Clavering’s story—her past, her struggles, her first marriage, which ended in scandal. He had been in India when it happened and could tell her exactly where to find the Calcutta newspaper that reported Amory’s trial. He had kept this secret, choosing friendship over gossip, and earned her gratitude.

“Of course I have interested motives,” he confessed, “and mine, I won’t deny, is to marry my nephew to your daughter.”

Lady Clavering, surprised but willing, agreed. The Major then laid out his plan: Arthur had five hundred a year, but with Blanche’s fortune, he could rise in the world. “Your son-in-law could be a name that his sons shall be proud of,” he said, with the confidence of a man who believes in destiny and a good marriage contract.

“And what use is that seat in Parliament?” he asked, referring to Sir Francis, who rarely spoke in the House. “Arthur was famous as an orator at Oxbridge—once put his foot in the stirrup and mount him, I’ve no doubt he won’t be the last of the field.”

He urged Lady Clavering to consider a match that would benefit both families. After all, marriage isn’t about sentiment—it’s about convenience. “A girl who runs away with Jack to Gretna Green,” he said, “often runs away with Tom to Switzerland afterwards. The point is to agree to be useful to one another.”

As for Miss Amory (Blanche), she was content with Arthur as long as no one better came along. How many young ladies are like that? How many love matches survive? How many sentimental firms go bankrupt? The Major, ever practical, believed that the best marriages were built on mutual advantage—not romance.

Arthur, meanwhile, had begun to absorb his uncle’s wisdom. He spent much of the spring at Lady Clavering’s house, accepted by Blanche without being engaged, familiar without being sentimental. They met and parted with perfect good humor—no grand declarations, no tears, just quiet companionship.

“And I,” Arthur mused, “am the same fellow who eight years ago had a Grand Passion, and last year was raging in a fever about Briseis!”

Yes, time changes us. We don’t become different people—we just reveal the parts we’ve always had. The selfishness we now feel was once the fire of ambition. The weariness we complain about is the aftermath of joy. The arrogance of success is merely the continuation of struggle.

And so, as we look back on our own lives—whether we’ve sailed into port or drowned at sea—we must remember: we are still the same person. The child who dreamed of greatness, the lover who fell out of love, the sinner who repented—all of it is part of the same journey. Whether you’re riding triumphantly into fame or clinging to a spar in the ocean, you’re still thinking of home. Still remembering childhood. Still wondering how it all turned out.

And if you’re reading this, dear reader, perhaps you’re doing the same.
