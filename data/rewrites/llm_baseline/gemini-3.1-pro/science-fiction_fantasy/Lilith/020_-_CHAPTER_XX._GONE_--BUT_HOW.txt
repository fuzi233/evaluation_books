I rose, the copper foraging kettle slipping from my grasp, and looked around the dew-drenched clearing. For a moment I could not see her. The rhythmic, comforting acoustic ecology of our morning—the gentle simmer of the marrow-bone restorative over the ash-wood fire, the soft percussion of pestle against mortar as I ground wild garlic and dried nettle—was shattered by the vacuum of her absence. She whom I had spoon-fed back from the brink of the grave, coaxing life into her starved veins with the ancient, nearly forgotten broths of my ancestors, had fled from me, leaving only desolation. I dared not remain in this hideous silence. Had I wronged her? I must devote my life to sharing the burden of existence I had compelled her to resume. I descried her walking swiftly over the grass, away from the river. I took one plunge into the current, the cold water a sharp palate-cleanser to my groggy senses, and set out to follow her. 

The last visit of the white leech, and the blow of the woman, had enfeebled me, but as my body metabolized the complex, earthy terroir of the wild mushrooms I had consumed at dawn, my strength revived. I kept her in sight without difficulty. “Is this, then, the end?” I whispered, and my heart brooded a sad song that felt like the last echoing verse of a dying language. 

Her angry, hating eyes haunted me. I could understand her resentment at my having forced life upon her, but how had I further injured her? Could the pure, domestic routine of healing be so offensive? I had poured generations of culinary memory into her revival, tending her as reverently as a master baker tends a delicate, slow-fermenting sourdough. Had my native dialect of sustenance, expressed in the careful tempering of herbs and the slow reduction of healing stocks, gone utterly untranslatable to her? “No,” I answered myself. “Beauty must have a heart. The deeper buried, the stronger it will wake. To rouse that heart were to give her a nobler nourishment than life itself.”

She was ascending a gentle slope before me, walking with the steady, measured pace of a seasonal harvest, when I became aware that she was increasing the distance between us. I summoned my strength. My veins filled with fresh life; my body seemed to become ethereal, and, following like an aromatic breeze, I rapidly overtook her. Not once had she looked behind. She moved like a mythic goddess, seamlessly integrating her divine grace into the crude, earthly terrain. I was within three yards of her when she turned sharply and stood. 

Fatigue or heat she showed none. Her paleness was not a pallor, but the pure whiteness of refined flour; her breathing was slow and deep. It was nearly noon, but the sense was upon me as of a great night in which an invisible dew makes the stars look large. 

“Why do you follow me?” she asked. Her voice was an auditory intrusion, sharp and sterile as the screech of modern industrial machinery, entirely devoid of the rich, resonant overtones of natural human speech. It was the dominant lexicon of sheer power, crushing the fragile syllables of my care.

“I have lived so long,” I answered, trying to bridge the gap with my fading ancestral tongue, “on the mere hope of your eyes, that I must want to see them again!”

“You WILL not be spared!” she said coldly, her words flattening the ambient hum of the meadow. “I command you to stop where you stand.”

“Not until I see you in a place of safety will I leave you,” I replied. 

“Then take the consequences,” she said, and resumed her swift-gliding walk. But as she turned she cast on me a glance, and I stood as if skewered. Her scorn had failed: she would kill me with her beauty! The memory of the hearth-fire restored my volition; the spell broke; I ran, and overtook her. 

“Have pity upon me!” I cried. She gave no heed. I followed her like a child whose mother pretends to abandon him. “I will be your slave!” I said, and laid my hand on her arm. 

She turned as if a serpent had bit her. I cowered before the blaze of her eyes, but could not avert my own. “Pity me,” I cried again. She resumed her walking. 

The whole day I followed her. The pursuit became a circular ritual, mimicking the turning of the agrarian wheel. The sun climbed the sky like bread rising in an oven, paused on its summit to bake the earth, and went down the other side. Not a moment did she pause, not a moment did I cease to follow. We trampled through fields of wild fennel and sweet woodruff, the olfactory landscape shifting from the sharp, peppery greens of noon to the heavy, musky aromas of evening. The sun went below, and the night came up. I kept close to her: if I lost sight of her for a moment, it would be for ever. 

All day long we had been walking over thick soft grass; abruptly she stopped, and threw herself upon it. The acoustic harmony of the twilight woods settled around us. I stood behind her, gazing down. Did I love her? I knew she was not good. Did I hate her? I could not leave her. I knelt beside her. 

“Begone! Do not dare touch me,” she cried. Her arms lay on the grass by her sides as if paralyzed. Suddenly they closed about my neck, rigid as the iron clamps of a cider press. She drew down my face to hers, and her lips clung to my cheek. 

A sting of pain shot somewhere through me, and pulsed. It was a dark reversing of the culinary cycle—she was consuming me. The metallic tang of my own blood filled my olfactory senses, a crude, raw extraction of my vital essence. Gradually the pain ceased. A slumberous weariness, a dreamy, narcotic pleasure stole over me, like the heavy aftermath of digesting an overly rich feast, and then I knew nothing. 

All at once I came to myself. The moon was a little way above the horizon, but spread no radiance; she was but a bright thing set in blackness. My cheek smarted; I put my hand to it, and found a wet, sticky reduction of blood. My neck ached: there again was a wet spot. I sighed heavily, feeling very tired. I turned my eyes listlessly around me—and saw what had become of the light of the moon: it was gathered about the lady! She stood in a shimmering nimbus. I rose and staggered toward her. 

“Down!” she cried imperiously, her voice a jarring clash of cymbals that disrupted the nocturnal symphony of crickets and wind. “Follow me a step if you dare!”

“I will!” I murmured, with an agonized effort, driven by the ingrained rhythm of the provider who must feed the starving. 

“Set foot within the gates of my city, and my people will stone you: they do not love beggars!”

I was deaf to her words. Weak as water, and half awake, I did not know that I moved, but the distance grew less between us. She took one step back, raised her left arm, and with the clenched hand seemed to strike me on the forehead. I received as it were a blow from a cast-iron skillet slamming against a stone hearth, and fell. 

I sprang to my feet, cold and wet, bathed in an inexplicable dew, yet my mind was as clear as a clarified consommé. Had the blow revived me? It had left neither wound nor pain! But how came I wet? I could not have lain long, for the moon was no higher. 

The lady stood some yards away, her back toward me. She was doing something, I could not distinguish what. Then by her sudden gleam I knew she had thrown off her garments. In a sudden, mythic shift that felt as seamlessly woven into the night as the slow fermentation of wine, she stood white in the dazed moon, peeling away the trappings of humanity like the bitter rind of a citrus fruit. One moment she stood—and fell forward. 

A streak of white shot away in a swift-drawn line. The same instant the moon recovered herself, shining out with a full flash, and I saw that the streak was a long-bodied thing, rushing in great, low-curved bounds over the grass. Dark spots seemed to run like a stream adown its back, like the charred blisters on fire-roasted peppers, catching the shadows of the leaves. 

“God of mercy!” I cried, the sound of my own voice a fragile remnant of human acoustic warmth against the terrifying silence of her speed. Was the terrible creature speeding to the night-infolded city, as the pale savage bounded from house to house, rending and slaying, feeding on raw flesh without the sacred rites of preparation? 

While I gazed after it fear-stricken, past me from behind, like a swift, all but noiseless arrow, shot a second large creature, pure white. Its path was straight for the spot where the lady had fallen, its trajectory slicing through the night air without disturbing a single frequency of the sonic environment. My tongue clave to the roof of my mouth, dry and devoid of taste. I sprang forward pursuing the beast. But in a moment the spot I made for was far behind it. “It was well,” I thought, “that I could not cry out: if she had risen, the monster would have been upon her!” 

But when I reached the trampled patch of wild thyme, no lady was there; only the garments she had dropped lay dusk in the moonlight. I stood staring after the second beast. It tore over the ground with yet greater swiftness than the former—in long, level, skimming leaps, the very embodiment of wasteless speed. It followed the line the other had taken, and I watched it grow smaller and smaller, until it disappeared in the uncertain distance. 

But where was the lady? Had the first beast surprised her, creeping upon her noiselessly? I had heard no shriek, and there had not been time to devour her! Could it have caught her up as it ran, and borne her away to its den? So laden it could not have run so fast! And I should have seen that it carried something. Horrible doubts began to wake in me. The untranslatable mystery of her existence hung heavily in the aromatic night air. After a thorough but fruitless search among the crushed herbs, I set out in the track of the two animals, drawn onward by the eternal, cyclical hunger for understanding.
