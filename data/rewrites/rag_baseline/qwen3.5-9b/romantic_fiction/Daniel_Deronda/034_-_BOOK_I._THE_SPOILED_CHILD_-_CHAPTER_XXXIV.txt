When Deronda arrived at five o'clock, the shop's iron-bound door yielded to the touch of a Christian servant, ushering him into a space that defied the grim expectations of a pawnbroker's rear chamber. The house, old and sprawling, concealed a sanctuary behind the storefront; by daylight, the room might have been a cavern of gloom, but now it breathed with the warm, amber luminescence of a fine brass lamp. Seven oil-flames danced above a table draped in snow-white linen, casting the smoky ceiling and shadowed walls into a chiaroscuro that rendered the gathered figures with the luminous intensity of a Venetian painting.

Here, the domestic tableau was arranged with theatrical precision. The grandmother, a woman of formidable presence, wore a gown of yellowish-brown, accented by a heavy gold chain that replaced a missing necklace. Under the lamp's glow, her face, marked by age and framed by a roll of gray hair, possessed a picturesque severity. Beside her, young Mrs. Cohen cut a striking figure in red and black, her throat encircled by a garland of large, artificial pearls. Between them, the household slept or waited: a baby nestled beneath a scarlet counterpane in the cradle, Adelaide Rebekah radiant in braided amber silk, and Jacob Alexander stooping in black velveteen with scarlet stockings.

As the four pairs of dark eyes glistened with a sudden, collective welcome, Deronda felt a flush of shame rise in his cheeks. By the harsh light of day, these creatures had inspired in him a supercilious distaste, a judgment born of prejudice and distance. Yet here, in the intimate theater of the private hearth, that disdain evaporated. The greeting was cordial, the atmosphere thick with a dignity that seemed to bloom specifically from the act of hosting. Deronda glanced around, marveling at the clutter of the old furniture. The oaken bureau and the high side-table appeared less like expressions of refined taste and more like artifacts of economic necessity, yet they were set with a deliberate care. A large dish of blue-and-yellow ware dominated the side-table, flanked by tarnished silver vessels, before which rested a massive volume bound in darkened vellum with a deeply ribbed spine. An open doorway at the far end revealed a second room, also aglow with light.

While absorbing these details through parenthetic glances, Deronda was drawn into the immediate transaction by Jacob's earnest solicitude regarding a knife. The boy had procured a specific tool, complete with a hook and a white handle, presenting it with the gravity of a merchant closing a deal. "Is that the sort of thing you want, Jacob?" Deronda asked, noting the item's utility. Jacob subjected the blade to a rigorous inspection, opening the hooks and comparing it to a cork-screw he produced from his pocket. "Why do you like a hook better than a cork-screw?" Deronda queried. "'Caush I can get hold of things with a hook," Jacob replied, his logic sound and practical. "A cork-screw won't go into anything but corks. But it's better for you, you can draw corks."

"You agree to change, then?" Deronda asked, catching the grandmother's delighted attention. "What else have you got in your pockets?" Jacob pressed, his demeanor shifting from childlike curiosity to deliberative seriousness. "Hush, hush, Jacob, love," the grandmother chided gently. Mindful of propriety, Deronda demurred, "I think I must not tell you that. Our business was with the knives." Jacob scanned Deronda's face for a moment, seemingly weighing the risk, before nodding gravely. "I'll shwop," he declared, handing over the cork-screw knife. Deronda pocketed it with corresponding solemnity.

Immediately, the small son of Shem darted into the adjacent room, his voice a rapid chatter that soon drew him back. Seeing his father enter, the boy snatched a little velveteen hat from a chair and donned it, rushing forward to greet the visitor. Cohen, however, retained his own hat, standing still as the two children climbed onto his legs, clasping his knees. He laid his hands upon each in turn, uttering a Hebrew benediction. His wife, having lifted the sleeping infant from the cradle, brought the child forward, holding it beneath her husband's outstretched palms to receive the blessing even in slumber. For a fleeting moment, Deronda perceived that this pawnbroker, proud of his trade, was not entirely prosaic; there was a sacred rhythm to his actions that transcended mere commerce.

"Well, sir, you found your welcome in my family, I think," Cohen said, removing his hat and assuming his former, bustling persona. "And you've been punctual. Nothing like a little stress here," he added, tapping his side pocket as he settled into a chair. "It's good for us all in our turn. I've felt it when I've had to make up payments. I began to fit every sort of box. It's bracing to the mind."

"Now then!" Cohen exclaimed, leaning forward. "Let us see, let us see." Deronda produced the ring he had spoken of earlier. "That is the ring I spoke of," he said, sliding it from his finger. "I believe it cost a hundred pounds. It will be a sufficient pledge to you for fifty, I think. I shall probably redeem it in a month or so."

Cohen's glistening eyes narrowed slightly as they met the ingenuous gaze of this young gentleman, who seemed to operate under the naive assumption that redemption was a source of satisfaction for a lender. Cohen took the ring, examined it with casual indifference, and returned it. "Good, good. We'll talk of it after our meal. Perhaps you'll join us, if you've no objection. Me and my wife'll feel honored, and so will mother; won't you, mother?" The invitation was doubled, and Deronda gladly accepted.

All turned and stood around the table. No dish was visible save one covered by a napkin; Mrs. Cohen had placed a china bowl near her husband for the ritual washing of hands. But before proceeding, Cohen paused, donning his hat again, and called out in a loud voice, "Mordecai!"

Deronda wondered if this was part of some religious ceremony, unsure what ancient rites might be expected of the household. From the next room came a distinct "Yes," prompting Deronda to look toward the open door. To his astonishment, he saw the figure of the enigmatic Jew he had encountered that morning in the bookshop. Their eyes met, and Mordecai appeared as surprised as Deronda; neither made any sign of recognition. Yet, as Mordecai moved to seat himself at the far end of the table, he bowed his head to the guest in a cold, distant manner, as if the disappointment of the morning remained a bitter stain on this new acquaintance.

Cohen now washed his hands, pronouncing Hebrew words throughout the motion. He removed the napkin to reveal two long, flat loaves sprinkled with seeds—the memorial of the manna that sustained the wandering forefathers. Breaking off small pieces, he distributed them to each member of the family, including Adelaide Rebekah, who stood on a chair, her entire length displayed in her amber garment, her little Jewish nose comically elongated by the compression of her lips in an effort to maintain a dignified posture. Cohen recited another Hebrew blessing, and then, uncovering the male heads, the family seated themselves. The meal commenced without any peculiarity that initially arrested Deronda's attention.

Preoccupied with a desire to steer the conversation toward a leading question, and haunted by the lingering image of Mordecai—who shared with him a series of fascinated, half-furtive glances—Deronda ate with little consciousness of the food. Mordecai wore no handsome Sabbath garment; instead of the threadbare, rusty black coat of the morning, he donned a light drab coat that appeared to have once been a handsome loose paletot, now shrunk tight with repeated washings. This change of attire accentuated his dark-haired, eager face, which bore a resemblance to the prophet Ezekiel, certainly not modish in contemporary eyes.

It was noticeable that the thin tails of the fried fish were reserved for Mordecai, a share typically assigned to a poor relation—a survival of prehistoric practice, perhaps, no longer widely admitted as superstition. Mr. Cohen maintained the conversation with lively vigor, introducing subjects in good taste: the Queen and the Royal Family, the Emperor and Empress of the French. Both the grandmother and wife entered these topics with zest. Mrs. Cohen the younger displayed an accurate memory of distinguished birthdays, while the elder assisted her son in recounting the visit of the Imperial couple to the city ten years prior.

"I dare say you know all about it better than we do, sir," Cohen prefaced, eager to impart full information. "Our baby is named Eugenie Esther," young Mrs. Cohen announced vivaciously. "It's wonderful how the Emperor's like a cousin of mine in the face," the grandmother remarked, her eyes widening. "It struck me like lightning when I caught sight of him. I couldn't have thought it."

"Mother and me went to see the Emperor and Empress at the Crystal Palace," Mr. Cohen recounted. "I had a fine piece of work to take care of, mother; she might have been squeezed flat—though she was pretty near as lusty then as she is now. I said if I had a hundred mothers I'd never take one of 'em to see the Emperor and Empress at the Crystal Palace
