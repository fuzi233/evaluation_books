Where have you gone, loves of my youth? Tubby with the golden hair; Langley with the dented nose; Shamus, stout-hearted but weak-limbed, easy to knock down but impossible to make beg for mercy; Neal the skinny; and Dicky, "dicky Dick" the portly; Ballett with the weepy eye; Beau Bunnie, master of many ties, who always fought in black kid gloves; and all the rest of you, whose names I've forgotten but whose warmth I cherish—where have you vanished to? Where do your ghostly forms haunt now? If someone had told me back then that there would come a day when I'd never see your merry faces again, never hear your shrill greetings, never feel the grip of your ink-stained fingers, never fight or quarrel with you, never hate you, never love you—I wonder if I could have endured that thought?

Once, I think, not long ago, I spotted you, Tubby—the one with whom I mapped the North Pole and investigated the Nile's sources. Haven't you forgotten, Tubby, our secret campsite by the lonely waters of the Regent's Park canal? There, amidst our frugal feast of toasted elephant's tongue (which the uninitiated mistake for jumbles), the distant roars of hungry lions or tigers would mingle with the mournful, drawn-out grumbling of polar bears, growing louder and more impatient until precisely half-past four. Then we'd snatch our rifles and sneak through the jungle—until halted by the spiked fencing around the Zoo. I'm convinced it was you, despite your side-whiskers and the grey thinning of your once-golden mane. You were rushing down Throgmorton Street, shackled to a small black bag. I should have stopped you, but I had no time; I needed to catch a train at Liverpool Street and get shaved on the way. Did you recognize me? You seemed to stare at me a bit too hard, I thought.

Gallant, kind-hearted Shamus—you who once fought for half an hour to save a frog from being skinned—they say you're now an Income Tax assessor. A man reportedly possessing a power of disbelief rare even among Inland Revenue circles; little faith, lacking the charity that thinks no evil. May Providence guide you to districts other than mine.

Time, Nature's busy handyman, bustles between the many rooms, tidying everything, covering burnt volcanoes with sweet earth, turning ancient debris to use, smoothing the ground above the dead, healing the beech bark scarred by lovers.

At first, I wasn't popular with my schoolmates, and this was the first time trouble truly visited me. Later, adults convince themselves that whatever else we miss in life, we've at least secured some measure of popularity, for without it entirely, few of us would dare face existence. But where children suffer more acutely than men is in discovering the harsh truth without the protective layers of self-deception. My exclusion was painfully obvious, and true to my nature, I brooded over it in silence.

"Can you run?" one day asked a very important personage whose name I've forgotten. He headed the Lower Fourth—a tall youth with a beak-like nose and the demeanor of one born to rule. Son of a draper in the Edgware Road, his father failed, forcing him to settle for a junior clerkship in the Civil Service. But to us youngsters, he always seemed an embryonic Duke of Wellington, and under different circumstances, perhaps he could have become one.

"Yes," I answered. In fact, running was my sole achievement, and rumor must have reached his ears.

"Run round the playground twice at your fastest," he commanded. "Show me."

I clenched my fists and charged off. How grateful I felt for being addressed publicly, the outcast of the class, I showed only through my efforts to please him. When I pulled up before him, panting heavily, I saw satisfaction in his eyes.

"Why don't the fellows like you?" he asked bluntly.

If only I could have stepped past my shyness and voiced my true thoughts! "Oh, Lord of the Lower Fourth! Upon whom success—the only success in life worth having—has descended like gifts from the gods! You to whom all Lower Fourth hearts turn! Tell me the secret of this popularity. How may I acquire it? No price is too high for me to pay. Vain little egoist that I am, it is the sum of my desires, and will remain so until the long years teach me wisdom. Its absence makes all my days bitter. Why does silence fall upon their chattering groups when I approach? Why do they drive me from their games? What shuts me out, repels them from me? I creep into corners, shedding scalding tears of shame. I watch with envy all you blessed ones. What is your secret? Is it Tommy's swagger? Then I'll swagger too, with anxious heart, mixed fear and hope. But why—why, seeing they admire it in Tommy, do they wait for me with imitations of cock-a-doodle-do, strutting beside me like pouter pigeons? Is it Dicky's playfulness? Dicky, who steals their balls, snatches caps from heads, leaps onto backs when least expected?

"Why should Dicky's reward be laughter, and mine a bloody nose and a widening circle of dislike? I'm no heavier than Dicky—if anything, a pound or two lighter. Is it Billy's friendliness? I'd fling my arms around their necks too; but they angrily wrench themselves free. Is indifference the best plan? I walk apart with steps I try so hard to make careless; yet none follows, no friendly arm slips through mine. Should one win by kind deeds? Ah, if only I could! How I'd toil for them. I could do their sums—I'm good at sums—write their punishments, gladly take their blame, would they but return my service with a little love—and more importantly—a little admiration."

But all I could muster, sulkily, was: "They do like me, some of them." I dared not admit the truth aloud.

"Don't lie," he answered. "You know they don't—not one of them." And I hung my head.

"I'll tell you what I'll do," he continued in his lordly tone. "I'll give you a chance. We're starting hare-and-hounds next Saturday; you can be a hare. Don't tell anyone. Just appear on Saturday, and I'll handle it. Mind, you'll have to run like the devil."

He walked away without waiting for my reply, leaving me to meet Joy running toward me with outstretched hands. The great moment comes to all of us: to the politician when the Party whip slips from conference to congratulate him on his admirable speech; to the young dramatist reading the manager's note inviting him to call at eleven; to the subaltern beckoned to his chief's stirrup—the moment when sunlight breaks through morning mists, and the world stretches before us, our path clear.

Obeying orders, I gave no hint in school of the great fortune that had come my way; but hurrying home, I burst into the passage before the front door could close behind me.

"I'm to be a hare because I run so fast. Anyone can be a hound, but there are only two hares, and they both want me. Can I have a jersey? We start next Saturday. He saw me run. I ran twice round the playground. He said I was splendid! Of course, it's a great honor to be a hare. We start from Hampstead Heath. And may I have a pair of shoes?"

That very day, my mother and I purchased the jersey and shoes, fearing that unless we hurried, the last blue-and-white-striped jersey in London might sell out, leaving the market bare of running footwear. That evening, before bed, I dressed in full costume to admire myself in the mirror; and from then until the end of the week—to my mother's terror—I practiced leaping over chairs, and my stair-descent became perilous and circuitous. But as I explained, the Lower Fourth's reputation was at stake, and banisters and legs counted for nothing compared to fame and honor. My father nodded supportively with manly arguments; my mother added another line to her prayers.

Saturday arrived. Most hunt members were local boys, so arrangements were made to meet at the turnpike gate outside the Spaniards at half-past two. I brought my lunch and ate it in Regent's Park, then took the bus to the Heath. One by one, others arrived. Beyond mere glances, none paid me any notice. Wearing ordinary clothes over my jersey, I knew they thought I'd come merely to watch them start, and I clutched to myself the dream of the surprise awaiting them, of which I'd be the hero.

Then he arrived, one of the last, our leader and chief. I sidled up behind him, waiting while he organized and constructed.

"But we've only got one hare," cried one of them. "We ought to have two, you know, in case one gets blown."

"We've got two," answered the Duke. "Think I don't know what I'm about? Young Kelver's going to be the other one."

Silence fell upon the gather.

"Oh, I say, we don't want him," finally broke in a voice. "He's a muff."

"He can run," explained the Duke.

"Let him run home," came another voice, greeted with laughter.
