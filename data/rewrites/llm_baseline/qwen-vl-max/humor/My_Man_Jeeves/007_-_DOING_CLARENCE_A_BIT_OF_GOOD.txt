Have you ever paused to consider—really stopped and thought about—the sheer audacity, the nerve, or if you prefer it, the downright cheek with which women, as a species, can sometimes carry off a situation? I have. By Jove, I have! And not just casually, either. It’s been thrust upon me in a way that I suspect very few fellows have experienced. The climax came with the whole Yeardsley “Venus” affair.

To understand how things stood between Mrs. Yeardsley and myself, I’ll need to back up a bit.

When I first met her, she was Elizabeth Shoolbred—old Worcestershire stock, plenty of money, and lovely as a spring morning. Her brother Bill had been at Oxford with me.

I liked Elizabeth Shoolbred. I really did. There was even a brief period—about a week—when we were engaged. But just as I was beginning to take life seriously, studying furniture catalogues and getting solemn when the restaurant band played “The Wedding Glide,” she called it off. A month later, she married Clarence Yeardsley—an artist.

With golf, billiards, a little racing, and good company at the club, I managed to move on. I came to see the whole thing as a closed chapter. I didn’t expect to run into her again—she and Clarence had settled in the country and never visited London. By the time her letter arrived, I’d pretty much put it behind me. In fact, I was quite relieved it had ended the way it did.

Then one morning, out of nowhere, came this note:

“MY DEAR OLD REGGIE,—It feels like ages since we last saw each other. How are you? We’ve settled into a wonderful old house with a lovely garden, right in the middle of beautiful countryside. Could you come down for a few days? Clarence and I would be so happy to see you. Bill is here and eager to meet you again—he mentioned you only this morning. _Do_ come. Wire your train, and I’ll send the car to pick you up.

—Yours sincerely, ELIZABETH YEARDSLEY.

P.S.—We can offer fresh milk and eggs. Think of that!

P.P.S.—Bill says our billiard table is one of the best he’s ever played on.

P.P.S.S.—We’re half a mile from a golf course. Bill insists it’s better than St. Andrews.

P.P.S.S.S.—You _must_ come!”

Now, imagine waking up with a slight hangover and finding such a letter from a woman who could have ruined your life! It threw me a bit, I must admit.

Still, that mention of golf sealed the deal. I knew Bill knew his stuff, and if he said the course was top-notch, it had to be something special. So I went.

Old Bill met me at the station with the car. We hadn’t seen each other in months, and I was glad to see him—and he seemed glad to see me too.

“Thank goodness you’re here,” he said as we drove off. “I was nearly at my wits’ end.”

“What’s up?” I asked.

“If I had any artistic sense—if just talking about pictures didn’t make me want to crawl under the bed—I might be able to cope. As it is, it’s unbearable.”

“Pictures?”

“Nothing else comes up around here. Clarence is an artist. His father was one too. And you know what Elizabeth is like when she gets her way?”

I remembered then—though it hadn’t occurred to me before—that most of our time together had been spent wandering through art galleries. When I let her lead, I followed her through gallery after gallery, though pictures mean nothing to me—just as they do to Bill. I hadn’t realized she’d still be doing it after marrying an artist. I figured by now, she’d be sick of them. But according to Bill, she wasn’t.

“They talk about paintings at every meal,” he said. “It makes a fellow feel like a stranger in his own home. How long are you staying?”

“A few days.”

“Take my advice—let me send you a wire from London. I’m going tomorrow. I promised to play against the Scottish team. The plan was to come back after the match. But don’t try to get me back with a rope.”

I tried to stay positive.

“But Bill, your sister says there’s a great golf course nearby.”

He turned sharply, almost swerving into a ditch.

“You don’t mean she actually said that?”

“She said you said it was better than St. Andrews.”

“So I did. Was that all she said?”

“Well, wasn’t that enough?”

“She didn’t happen to mention I added, ‘I don’t think’?”

“No, she left that part out.”

“It’s the worst course in Britain.”

I felt a bit stunned. I don’t know if it’s a habit or not, but I simply can’t go without my daily dose of golf when I’m not in town.

I tried again.

“We’ll have to make up for it with billiards. I’m glad the table’s good.”

“It depends what you mean by good. It’s half-size, and there’s a seven-inch gap near the baulk where Clarence’s cue slipped. Elizabeth patched it with pink silk. Looks smart, but it doesn’t help the game.”

“But she said you said——”

“Must have been teasing you.”

We pulled up at the gates of a large house set well back from the road. It looked dark and imposing in the twilight, and I couldn’t help feeling like one of those characters in stories who’s lured to a lonely house for suspicious reasons and hears a cry just as he arrives. Elizabeth knew me well enough to know that a decent golf course would draw me in. She’d used that knowledge deliberately. But why? That was the question. Then it hit me—she had someone here she wanted me to meet. I’d heard stories about young married women doing that sort of thing. Certainly, she’d said no one else was staying, but a woman who could claim St. Andrews was inferior wouldn’t hesitate over small details.

“Bill,” I said, “there aren’t any girls here, or anything like that, are there?”

“Wish there were,” he replied. “No such luck.”

As we reached the front door, it opened and a woman stepped out.

“Did you bring him, Bill?” she asked. At the moment, it struck me as oddly dramatic—like something Lady Macbeth might say.

“Do you mean me?” I asked.

She came into the light. It was Elizabeth—looking exactly as she had years ago.

“Is that you, Reggie? I’m so glad you made it. I was afraid you’d forgotten. You know what you’re like. Come in and have some tea.”

Have you ever been rejected by a woman who later married, and then met her husband? If so, you’ll understand how I felt when Clarence appeared. You know the feeling. First, you wonder what he’s like. Then you meet him and think, “There must be a mistake. She couldn’t possibly prefer *this* to me!” That’s what I thought when I saw Clarence.

He was a thin, nervous-looking man in his mid-thirties. His hair was graying at the temples and sparse on top. He wore pince-nez and had a drooping mustache. I’m no model myself, but next to Clarence, I felt like a king.

And Elizabeth—well, she’s the kind of tall, elegant woman who looks like royalty. Honestly, I believe women do it just for fun.

“How do you do, Mr. Pepper? Hark! Can you hear a cat meowing?” Clarence said—all in one breath.

“Eh?” I replied.

“A meowing cat. I’m certain I hear one. Listen!”

Just then, the door opened and an older gentleman with white hair entered. He looked like an earlier version of Clarence. I correctly guessed he was Mr. Yeardsley, senior. Elizabeth introduced us.

“Father,” Clarence said, “did you hear a cat outside? I’m sure I heard one.”

“No,” the elder man shook his head. “No cat.”

“I can’t stand cats,” Clarence muttered. “A meowing cat sets my nerves on edge.”

“A meowing cat is unbearable,” Elizabeth agreed.

“I detest meowing cats,” the father added.

That was that. They seemed satisfied and returned to discussing art.

They talked about paintings steadily until dinner time. I just sat quietly. Then the topic of art thefts came up. Someone mentioned the “Monna Lisa,” and I recalled seeing a news item on the train about a valuable painting stolen from a house the night before. It was my first chance to contribute meaningfully. The paper was in my coat pocket. I fetched it.

“Here it is,” I said. “A Romney belonging to Sir Bellamy Palmer——”

They all shouted “What!” at once, like a choir.

Elizabeth snatched the paper.

“Let me see! Yes—‘Late last night, burglars broke into Sir Bellamy Palmer’s residence at Dryden Park, Midford, Hants——’”

“That’s near here,” I said. “I passed through Midford——”

“Dryden Park is just two miles from this house,” Elizabeth said. I noticed her eyes sparkled.

“Only two miles!” she exclaimed. “It could have been us! It could have been the ‘Venus’!”

Old Mr. Yeardsley jumped up.

“The ‘Venus’!” he cried.

They were all thrilled. My little contribution had made quite an impression.

Why I hadn’t noticed it before, I don’t know, but it wasn’t until after dinner that Elizabeth showed me the Yeardsley “Venus.” When she led me to it and turned on the light, I couldn’t believe I’d sat through dinner without seeing it. But at meals, my attention is usually on the food. Anyway, it wasn’t until she pointed it out that I realized it was there.

After dinner, she and I were alone in the drawing room. Old Mr. Yeardsley was writing letters, while Bill and Clarence played billiards on the half-size table with its pink silk patch. Everything was cheerful, as it should be.

Elizabeth, who had been deep in thought, leaned toward me.

“Reggie.”

The moment she said it, I knew something was coming. You know that premonition you sometimes get?

“What?” I asked nervously.

“Reggie,” she said, “I want to ask a huge favor.”

“Yes?”

She added a log to the fire and continued, her back to me.

“Do you remember once saying you’d do anything in the world for me?”

“Oh, yes,” I said.

“There’s something you can do now that will make me eternally grateful.”

“Yes?”

“Do you know,” she said suddenly, “that only a few months ago, Clarence loved cats?”

“Well, he still seems—er—interested in them.”

“Now they drive him mad. Everything does.”

“Some people swear by those remedies you see advertised everywhere——”

“No, that won’t help. He doesn’t need medicine. He needs to get rid of something.”

“I don’t follow. Get rid of something?”

“The ‘Venus,’” Elizabeth said.

She turned and caught my startled look.

“You saw the ‘Venus,’” she said.

“Not that I recall.”

“Come into the dining room.”

We walked in, and she turned on the lights.

“There,” she said.

On the wall near the door—probably why I hadn’t seen it before—was a large oil painting. It was classical, I suppose. You know the type. All I can say is, it’s funny I hadn’t noticed it.

“Is that the ‘Venus’?” I asked.

She nodded.

“How would you like to stare at that every time you sit down to eat?”

“I don’t know. I’d probably manage.”

She sighed impatiently.

“But you’re not an artist,” she said. “Clarence is.”

Then I began to understand. It was clearly about the Artist’s Temperament. I could believe anything about that. It explains everything. Like the Unwritten Law in America—you use it when you don’t want to go to jail. If you’re acting strangely, you just say it’s your Artistic Temperament, and they apologize and leave. So I waited to hear how it had affected Clarence.

And it had hit him hard.

It turned out old Mr. Yeardsley was an amateur painter, and this “Venus” was his masterpiece. He’d given it to Clarence as a wedding gift and hung it himself. Fine so far. But then—here’s the twist—Clarence, being a professional artist, saw flaws. He couldn’t bear looking at it. The drawing, the expression, the colors—it made him ill. Yet, being devoted to his father, he couldn’t bring himself to hide it. The strain of seeing it three times a day had begun to wear him down.

“Now you see,” she said.

“In a way,” I said. “But isn’t this making a mountain out of a molehill?”

“Oh, can’t you understand? Look!” Her voice dropped, as if in church. She turned on another light. It fell on the painting beside the “Venus.” “There!” she said. “Clarence painted that!”

She looked at me expectantly, as if waiting for me to faint or cheer. I studied Clarence’s work. It looked very similar to the other.

Clearly, some art commentary was expected. I gave it a shot.

“Er—‘Venus’?” I ventured.

Sherlock Holmes would have made the same mistake, based on the evidence.

“No,” she snapped. “‘Jocund Spring.’” She turned off the light. “I see you still don’t understand. You’ve never had any taste for art. When we used to visit galleries, you’d rather have been at your club.”

That was so true, I couldn’t reply. She came closer and placed her hand on my arm.

“I’m sorry, Reggie. I didn’t mean to snap. But I need you to understand—Clarence is suffering. Imagine a great musician forced to listen to the same vulgar tune every day. Wouldn’t his nerves break? It’s just like that with Clarence. Now you see?”

“Yes, but——”

“But what? Don’t I explain clearly enough?”

“Yes. But what’s my role? What do you want me to do?”

“I want you to steal the ‘Venus.’”

I stared.

“You want me to——?”

“Steal it, Reggie!” Her eyes sparkled. “Don’t you see? It’s fate. When I invited you, I already had the idea. I knew I could count on you. And then—miraculously—the Romney theft happens just two miles away. It covers everything. The old man won’t suspect a thing. It’s the greatest compliment he’ll ever receive. One night, thieves steal a Romney; the next, they take his ‘Venus.’ It will be the proudest moment of his life. Do it tonight, Reggie. I’ll give you a sharp knife. Just cut the canvas from the frame, and it’s done.”

“But wait,” I said. “I’d love to help, but wouldn’t it be better—shouldn’t we ask Bill?”

“I asked Bill yesterday. He refused.”

“But if I’m caught?”

“You won’t be. Just take the picture, open a window, leave it open, and return to your room.”

It sounded simple.

“And what about the picture itself—once I have it?”

“Burn it. I’ll make sure your room has a good fire.”

“But——”

She looked at me. She always had those remarkable eyes.

“Reggie,” she said. Nothing more. Just “Reggie.”

She looked at me.

“Well, after all, if you see what I mean—the days that are gone, don’t you know. Auld Lang Syne, and all that.”

“All right,” I said. “I’ll do it.”

If you’re not familiar with crime, you might not grasp how uneasy I felt when I sat in my room, waiting to act. On paper, it seemed easy. But in reality, I couldn’t shake the feeling something was wrong. Time crawled. The plan was for midnight, but at quarter to, I couldn’t wait. I lit the lantern from Bill’s bike, grabbed the knife, and crept downstairs.

The first thing I did was open the window. I almost considered breaking it for effect, but decided against it—too noisy. I placed the lantern on the table and reached for it when—something happened. I couldn’t tell what at first. An explosion? An earthquake? Something solid slammed into my chin. Sparks flew inside my head. The next thing I knew, something cold and wet splashed my face, and I heard a voice—Bill’s—that said, “Feeling better now?”

I sat up. The lights were on. I was on the floor, with Bill kneeling beside me, holding a soda siphon.

“What happened?” I asked.

“I’m terribly sorry, old chap,” he said. “I didn’t realize it was you. I saw a lantern, an open window, and a man with a knife. I didn’t stop to ask. I just swung at his jaw. What on earth were you doing? Were you sleepwalking?”

“It was Elizabeth,” I said. “You know all about it. She told you.”

“You don’t mean——”

“The picture. You refused, so she asked me.”

“Reggie,” he said, “I’ll never believe in repentance again. It’s a fool’s game. If I hadn’t repented—thought it was unfair to Elizabeth not to do something for her—and come down to do it myself, you wouldn’t have blocked that sleeping potion with your chin. I’m sorry.”

“Me too,” I said, shaking my head to make sure it was still attached.

“Are you feeling better?”

“Better than before. But not by much.”

“Want more soda water? No? Well, let’s finish this and go to bed. And quickly. You made a noise like a ton of bricks falling. Some servant might have heard. Toss you who cuts.”

“Heads.”

“Tails,” he said, revealing the coin. “Up you get. I’ll hold the light. Don’t stab yourself with that blade.”

It was easier than Elizabeth said. Four quick cuts, and the canvas slid out like an oyster. I rolled it up. Bill had moved the lantern and was gathering whisky, soda, and glasses.

“We’ve got a long night ahead,” he said. “You can’t burn a painting that size in one go. You’d set the chimney ablaze. Let’s do it properly. Clarence won’t mind. We’ve done him a kindness. Tomorrow will be the happiest day of his year. On we go.”

We went upstairs and sat smoking, talking, and sipping drinks, cutting bits of the painting and feeding them to the fire until it was gone. With the warmth, the glow, and the quiet joy of doing a good deed secretly, I can’t remember having a more pleasant evening since school days.

Just as we finished the last piece, Bill sat up suddenly and gripped my arm.

“I heard something,” he whispered.

I listened. By Jove, I heard it too. My room was above the dining room, and the sound came through clearly—stealthy footsteps. Then a chair toppled.

“There’s someone in the dining room,” I whispered.

There’s a type of man who enjoys chasing trouble. Bill is one. If I’d been alone, I’d have convinced myself I’d imagined it. I’m peaceful by nature. But Bill? A burglar invasion was pure excitement. He leapt up.

“Come on,” he said. “Bring the poker.”

I took the tongs too. I felt like it. Bill grabbed the knife. We crept downstairs.

“We’ll burst in and charge,” Bill said.

“What if they shoot?” I asked.

“Burglars never shoot,” Bill said.

Comforting, provided the burglars believed it.

Bill grasped the handle, turned it, and charged in. Then we froze, staring.
