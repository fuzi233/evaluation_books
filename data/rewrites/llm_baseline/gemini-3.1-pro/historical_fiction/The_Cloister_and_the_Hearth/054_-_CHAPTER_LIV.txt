When Margaret recovered, the first sensation to pierce the dark was deeply olfactory—the agrarian, grounding musk of Catherine’s woollen sleeves, redolent of toasted barley, aged milk, and the earthy dampness of root cellars. The honest half of the family she had invaded like a foe stood around her, their voices a symphony of rough, Low Dutch dialects. Their words were a percussive, homely cadence, especially Giles, whose booming resonance disrupted the delicate acoustic ecology of the parlor, roaring at her that she was not to take on so. Gerard was alive, he insisted, or he could not have penned a lexicon so vast, nor crafted script so meticulously small.

“Ay, good Master Giles,” sighed Margaret feebly, finding a fragile anchor in the rhythmic sounds of the hearth. “He was alive. But how know I what hath since befallen him? Oh, why left he Holland to go among strangers fierce as lions? And why did I not drive him from me sooner than part him from his own flesh and blood? Forgive me, you that are his mother!”

She gently removed Catherine's arm, her body seeking the solid floor, but a brief, tactile struggle against the elder woman’s steadfast, flour-dusted embrace ended with Margaret resting against Catherine's bosom. The transfer of the parchment from Margaret to Eli was accompanied by the dry, rustling crackle of the heavily inked vellum. “I will trust it from my hand now,” she said faintly. “In sooth, I am little fit to read any more—and loth to leave my comfort.” 

“Read thou, Richart,” said Eli, his voice carrying the deep, unhurried timbre of the old ways. “Thine eyes be younger than mine.”

Richart took the letter. “Well,” he murmured, marveling at the dense preservation of memory etched upon the page. “A writeth with a needle's point. Why is he not in my counting-house at Amsterdam instead of vagabonding it out yonder!”

Richart cleared his throat, adjusting to the rhythm of his brother's distant voice:

*“When I came to myself I was seated in the litter, and my good merchant holding of my hand. I babbled I know not what in our native tongue, the untranslatable syllables of my distress lost on him, and then shuddered awhile in silence. He put a horn of wine to my lips.”*

Catherine’s eyes shone with maternal warmth. “Bless him! bless him!”
“Whisht!” commanded Eli, protecting the sonic clarity of the reading.

*“And I told him what had befallen. My leg was sprained sore, my points broken. ‘Sir,’ I said, ‘I shall be but a burden, and can make you no harmony now; my poor psaltery it is broken.’ I did grieve over my broken music, that pure acoustic companion which had shielded my mind from the chaotic noise of so many weary leagues. But he tended me like a kind father.*

*“January 19.—Strange! whene'er I fall in with men like fiends, God sendeth some good soul, lest I turn away from human kind. And lo! if good Master Fugger hath not bought me a new psaltery, restoring the acoustic sanctuary of my travels.”*

“Eli, my man,” Catherine interrupted, the agrarian matriarch recognizing a debt of profound sustenance, “an yon merchant comes our way, let us buy a hundred ells of cloth of him, and not higgle.”
“That will I, take your oath on't!”

While Richart prepared to read on, Kate caught her mother's eye. With a faint blush, she drew a piece of work from under her apron, her needle making a soft, rhythmic *shhh-shhh* through the linen. Catherine, matching the domestic cadence, drew her own work from her pocket. Both sweet, surreptitious creatures were sewing babies' caps. Horror! They were shaped like little monks' cowls—a circularity of generations, the mythic weight of the cloister seamlessly integrated into the intimate, everyday utility of swaddling clothes.

*“January 20.—Lombardy plains burst on me. Oh, Margaret! A land flowing with milk and honey. The olfactory landscape is intoxicating; even in winter, it looks warmer than beloved Holland. Here be vines, cedars, and silver-leaved olives. The draught oxen wear white linen on their necks. We crossed a river in a chained ferry-boat, the windlass creaking a steady, rhythmic measure. An old woman, dreading the evil eye, held out her crucifix to a young wench—a timeless, mythic warding performed amidst the mundane crossing.*

*“January 25.—Safe at Venice. Dost mind how Peter would oft fill our ears with tales of this sea-enthroned city, the forum of St. Mark? Lying in pain, I turn mine eyes inward, binding my sheaves of wisdom. This then have I, Sir Footsore, learned as a traveling historian of hearth and habit: each nation’s soul is inextricably bound to its sustenance and its native dialect. The Germans in the north are frank; their general blot is drunkenness. They say commonly, ‘Kanstu niecht sauffen und fressen so kanstu kienem hern wol dienen.’ An idiom where the power dynamics of servitude and the consumption of food are fiercely untranslatable.* 

*“In England, the worshipful sugar their wine and drink for courtesy. In their cups, the Germans use little discourse, crying ‘Seyte frolich!’ Their drunken sport, ‘Kurlemurlehuff,’ is an elaborate acoustic ritual of clinking glass, beard, and table, intermixed with precisely ordered whistlings and finger-snappings. Their topers sleep with pebbles in their mouths—a tactile mitigation to vent the heat of their fleshy wine-pots. Yet their women are most temperate, far before our Holland lasses, who, being maids, put the question to the men, and being wived, do lord it over them.”*

“'Tis sooth!” Catherine nodded vigorously. “'Twas Beck Hulse. This comes of a woman wedding a boy.”

*“In the south where wine is, the gentry drink themselves bare; but in the north, with their heavy, malt-thickened beers, a noble shall sooner burst his body than melt his lands. The French are fairer spoken, but their sweet words cost them nought. They call it payer en blanche.”*

Denys slapped his knee. “Les coquins! ha! ha!”

*“Such straws of speech show how blows the wind of their lexicon. Tie but your napkin round a bench, and no Frenchman will sit there. Natheless, they say things in sport which are shocking: ‘Allez au diable!’ Custom belike, an erosion of meaning through overuse. They are moderate in drinking, mixing water with their wine, and are enchanting company over their cups. The lower half of their windows is wood, the upper glass, but fixed; the German windows are all glass, movable, shining far and near. In France, they thrust the law’s finger into every pie, and often hang their female malefactors instead of drowning them decently. In Germany, the snails be red. I lie not.*

*“The French buy minstrelsy, but make their own mirth. The Germans foster paid fools with ear-caps to simulate madness—a superficial commercialization of calamity that asks pity, not laughter.* 

*“The Italians judge a man by his speech and gesture. They are singular in their temperance of food, yet foolish in their extortionate laws. Delicate in eating, they abhor putting their hand in the plate. They tell of a cardinal at Rome who armeth his guest's left hand with a little bifurcal dagger to hold the meat, while his knife cutteth it. A sterile, metallic intrusion, methinks, trying to be wiser than Him who made the hand so tactile and prehensile.”*

“I am of your mind, my lad,” Eli murmured, a traditionalist who understood the sacred, tactile connection to daily bread.

*“Their bread is lovely white, fermented with meticulous care to a delicate crumb. Yet their meats they wholly spoil with a harsh sprinkling of dry cheese over them; O, perversity of the palate! Their salt is black; without a lie. In commerce, these Venetians are masters, yet twenty thousand Jezebels pay a tax to the State. The commercial din here pollutes the mind's quiet; ointment is cried at every corner. They even bend nature to their commerce, forcing roses to bloom unseasonably in January by digging round the discouraged roots and bruising them with cloves—an artificial scent masking an unnatural harvest.* 

*“And for artful fountains, Augsburg harnesses the foul knave Smoke to good Sir Spit, turning stout Master Roast. Yet for pure utility, London and Lubeck bring water tame in pipes to every burgher's door. But I pray God for little Holland, that there an assassin may be yclept an assassin in our honest mother tongue, till domesday; and let others gloze foul faults with silken lexicons who can! Finally, a true-hearted Frenchman is near perfect; such a man is my Denys, spite of his foul mouth.”*

Denys bristled, inspecting the letter gravely. “I read not the letter so. 'Tis kittle work perusing these black scratches. My foul mouth! Eh, mesdames?” But the women merely smiled slyly over their rhythmic sewing, and Denys, feigning patient innocence, bade Richart continue.

*“January 26.—I was carried to St. Mark his church. Outside are four famous brass horses. Inside is a treasure greater than Toledo: a jewelled pitcher from a Persian king, a ducal cap blazing with chrysolite. But what are these commercial hoardings compared to the sacred relics? I stood and handled St. Mark's gospel, written with his own hand. Dear Margaret, his sacred body was brought here in 810, but the very place it lay was forgotten. Then holy priests fasted, and lo! the Evangelist's body brake at midnight through the marble and stood before them. They laid him beneath the altar, preserving the miraculous crevice. To see such mythic power carved into the very architecture of their daily worship! I saw the Virgin's stone chair, and her picture that brings the rain.*

*“Outside, on a great porphyry slab in the piazza, were three ghastly heads rotting. These were traitors, their friends having sold their blood for two thousand ducats. But what I most pondered was a stone effigy of an enormous act enacted last year. Four brethren conspired to poison the other two to halve their land. At a mutual banquet, twain drugged the wine, and the other twain envenomed a marchpane. A tragic inversion of the culinary arts! That marchpane, pounded from sweet almonds, carried the bitter, hidden alkaloid tang of death, while the wine masked their mortal agony within its terroir. The same afternoon lay four 'brave men' grovelling around one table, weaponizing the sacred act of sustenance, cursing one another to a miserable conclusion.* 

*“After this, they took me to the quay. I espied among the masts one garlanded with amaranth flowers. I let my guide know the custom of our Dutch skippers to hoist flowers when courting a maid. But oh, Margaret! The sight of that ship made my heart leap with the sudden, overwhelming acoustic and visual assurance of a countryman. On the stern of that Dutch boy was written in our honest, uncorrupted alphabet:*

*RICHART ELIASSOEN, AMSTERDAM.”*
