They were midway through their pot of pottage at Tergou—barley gone soft as old linen, onions melted sweet, a heel of smoked bacon lending the whole dish its quiet salt—when the latch flew up, and Luke Peterson blundered in as if the very wind had chased him. Dust lay on him like flour on a baker, and his breath came in ragged spurts that filled the low room with a harsh, panting music; outside, the evening crows and the far creak of a cart-wheel kept their steadier measures.

“Good people,” he burst out, “Mistress Catherine is wanted instantly at Rotterdam.”

Catherine’s spoon halted. “My name is Catherine, young man. Kate, it will be Margaret.”

“Ay, dame,” said he, swallowing air and words together. “She said to me, ‘Good Luke, hie thee to Tergou, and ask for Eli the hosier, and pray his wife Catherine to come to me, for God His love.’ I didn’t wait for daylight.”

“Holy saints!” Catherine cried. The sound of her voice struck the wooden walls and came back sharper. “He has come home, Kate. Nay—she would sure have said so. What on earth can it be?” And as steam from the pot rose, so did her fears—each one swelling upon the last, until the very smell of their dinner turned thin and far away.

“Mayhap the young man can tell us,” Kate ventured, timid as one tasting a strange broth.

“That I can,” said Luke. “Why—her babe is a-dying. And she was so wrapped up in it!”

Catherine sprang to her feet as if scalded. “What is his trouble?”

“Nay, I know not. But it has been peaking and pining worse and worse this while.”

Across the table, Cornelis and Sybrandt exchanged a glance—quick as a knife-flash, and satisfied. Catherine did not see it; she was already turned to her husband, her whole face a command.

“Now, Eli,” she cried, fierce as a housewife defending her hearth, “if you say a word against it, you and I shall quarrel, after all these years.”

“Who gainsays thee, foolish woman?” Eli answered, without heat. “Quarrel with your own shadow, while I go borrow Peter’s mule for ye.”

“Bless thee, my good man! Bless thee!” She pressed her hands together once, as if prayer were a thing to be kneaded into deeds. “Didst never yet fail me at a pinch. Now eat your dinners who can, while I go and make ready.”

She took Luke back with her in the cart; the wheels sang over the hard ruts, and the hedgerows whispered in the mild night wind. All the way she questioned and cross-questioned him—now coaxing like a woman seasoning a reluctant stew with honey, now sharp as vinegar—until she had turned his mind inside out, what there was of it.

At Rotterdam Margaret met her at the door, pale and shaken, and flung her arms about Catherine’s neck; her hair smelt of sour sweat and milk, the smell of a mother who has sat too long above a failing child, listening for breath.

Catherine held her at arm’s length and scanned her. “Come,” she said briskly, catching Margaret’s pulse with her eye as another woman would catch it with her fingers. “He is alive, thank God.”

Inside, the room was close; the fire snapped now and then, and the kettle’s low simmer made a comfort that did not reach the bed. The child lay there like a little candle guttering in its own grease. Catherine bent, looked at the faint mouth, the dull skin, the slack hands; then she looked at the poor hollow-eyed mother, and back again, measuring with the certainty of long kitchens and many cradles.

“Lucky you sent for me,” said she. “The child is poisoned.”

“Poisoned!” Margaret gasped. “By whom?”

“By you.”

Margaret staggered as if struck. “By me? Nay, indeed, mother. How can I help fretting?”

“Don’t tell me, Margaret.” Catherine’s voice had the hard ring of a wooden spoon on an iron pot. “A nursing mother has no business to fret. She must turn her mind away from her grief to the comfort that lies in her lap. Know you not that the child pines if the mother vexes herself? This comes of your reading and writing. Those idle crafts befit a man; but they keep all useful knowledge out of a woman. The child must be weaned.”

Margaret’s face flamed. “Oh, you cruel woman! I am sorry I sent for you. Would you rob me of the only bit of comfort I have in the world? A-nursing my Gerard, I forget I am the most unhappy creature beneath the sun.”

“That you do not,” Catherine snapped, “or he would not be the way he is.”

“Mother!” Margaret pleaded, broken between anger and fear.

“’Tis hard,” Catherine said at last, and the edge of her tone dulled a little, like steel warmed by the hearth. “But bethink thee; would it not be harder to look down and see his lovely wee face a-looking up at you out of a little coffin?”

“Oh, Jesu!”

“And how could you face your other troubles with your heart aye full, and your lap empty?”

Margaret’s hands clutched the bedclothes as if they were the last plank on the sea. “Oh, mother, I consent to anything. Only save my boy.”

“That is a good lass. Trust to me! I do stand by, and see clearer than thou.”

Yet there was another consent to be gained: the babe’s. And he, when Catherine tried the first shift, turned away, wailing thinly, as if the world itself had changed its taste.

“There,” said Margaret, trying for a smile through her misery, “he loves me too well.”

Catherine had not lived through harvests and hunger to be beaten by a sucking child. On the road she had marked a healthy young woman sitting outside her own door with an infant, her breast full and her face calm. Catherine went to her at once, told her the case plain as a recipe, and asked would she nurse the pining child for the nonce, till matters were ready to wean him.

The young woman consented with a smile, set her own child into the cradle, and came in. She dropped a curtsey. Catherine placed little Gerard in her hands. The woman’s touch was sure and warm; she examined him, pitied him softly, and then, with a little crooning sound that rose and fell like the doves under the eaves, she set him to her breast as naturally as if he had been born of her. The room changed in an instant: the sharp, desperate crying gave way to the thick, greedy pull of a child drinking life; and that simple, domestic sound—milk drawn, breath steadied—was like rain on a roof after drought.

Margaret, paralyzed by the sight, cast one wild, reproachful look at Catherine and burst into tears. The visitor looked up, startled.

“What is to do?” said she. “Wife, ye told me not the mother was unwilling.”

“She is not,” Catherine returned. “She is only a fool. Never heed her; and you, Margaret, I am ashamed of you.”

“You are a cruel, hard-hearted woman,” Margaret sobbed, the words tasting bitter on her tongue.

“Them as take in hand to guide the weak need be hardish. And you will excuse me; but you are not my flesh and blood; and your boy is.”

She let that lie a moment, heavy as a stone in a bowl. Then she added, “Come now, she is robbing her own to save yours, and you can think of nothing better than bursting out a-blubbering in the woman’s face. Out fie, for shame!”

“Nay, wife,” said the nurse gently, her voice like warm milk itself. “Thank Heaven, I have enough for my own and for hers to boot. And prithee wyte not on her! Maybe the troubles o’ life ha’ soured her own milk.”

“And her heart into the bargain,” Catherine said, remorseless as ever.

Margaret looked full at her; then her eyes dropped. “I know I ought to be very grateful to you,” she sobbed to the nurse. But she turned her head away and leaned over the chair, unable to endure the intolerable spectacle of another nursing her Gerard—and Gerard, with the innocent tyranny of hunger, drawing no distinction between the new breast and the old, only between plenty and pain.

“You are very welcome, my poor woman,” said the nurse. “And so are you, Mistress Catherine, which are my townswoman, and know it not.”

“What, are ye from Tergou?” Catherine said, quickening. “All the better. But I cannot call your face to mind.”

“Oh, you know not me,” the woman replied. “My husband and me, we are very humble folk by you. But true Eli and his wife are known of all the town; and respected. So, I am at your call, dame; and at yours, wife; and yours, my pretty poppet; night or day.”

When she had gone, the door closing on her quiet step, Catherine nodded. “There’s a woman of the right old sort.”

“I HATE her. I HATE her. I HATE her,” Margaret cried, with a fervour that surprised even herself; and the words seemed to scrape out of her like a pot scoured raw.

Catherine only laughed, and the laugh was the clack of a knife on a board—practical, almost cheerful. “That is right; better say it, as set sly and think it. It is very natural after all. Come, here is your bundle o’ comfort. Take and hate that, if ye can;” and she put the child into Margaret’s lap.

“No, no,” Margaret said, turning half away; she could not for her life turn the other half. “He is not my child now; he is hers. I know not why she left him here, for my part. It was very good of her not to take him to her house, cradle and all; oh! oh! oh! oh! oh! oh! oh!”

“Ah! well,” said Catherine, softer now, “one comfort, he is not dead.”

But Margaret’s grief, finding an old channel, ran into a bitter jest. “This gives me light: some other woman has got him away from me; like father, like son; oh! oh! oh! oh! oh!”

Catherine was sorry for her, and let her cry in peace. After that, when she needed Joan’s aid, she would take Gerard out “for a little fresh air.” The street’s small sounds—children’s feet on the stones, a fishwife’s call, the faint tapping of a cooper’s hammer—seemed meant to soothe; yet to Margaret they were only the world going on while her own life stood arrested. She never objected, nor challenged the excuse; but when Catherine returned with the child, Margaret was always in tears, as if each coming-back were a reminder that some going had not been undone.

This connivance did not last. Margaret grew as eager to wean little Gerard as she had once been frantic to keep him. It was done—by methods Catherine knew as well as she knew the right moment to lift a loaf from the oven—and the child’s health returned, colour creeping back like sunrise into his cheeks. Another trouble fell on him directly: teething. But here Catherine’s experience was a storehouse; she knew what cooled and what inflamed, what to rub and what to withhold, how to keep the little gums from swelling into fever. In the midst of her grief and her gnawing dread for the father, Margaret found sudden, piercing moments of bliss watching the tiny teeth press and show.

“Teeth, mother?” she would cry, laughing through tears. “I call them not teeth, but pearls of pearls.”

And each pearl that peeped and sparkled on his red gums was, to her, the greatest feat Nature had ever achieved. Catherine caught the same fancy. Had any man dared tell them standing corn was equally admirable, Margaret would have looked at him like a reproachful gazelle, and Catherine would have turned him out of doors. So each pearl’s arrival was announced with a shriek of triumph by whichever of them discovered it first, and the whole house would ring with that bright, foolish sound—life insisting on its own small ceremonies.

During these days Catherine gossiped with Joan, and learned she was wife to Jorian Ketel of Tergou, once servant to Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, but fallen out of favour and returned to Rotterdam, his native place. His friends had gotten him the place of sexton to the parish; and with that and carpentering besides, he did pretty well. Catherine, in turn, told Joan whose child it was she had nursed, and all about Margaret and Gerard, and how his silence had plunged them into deep anxiety.

“Ay,” said Joan, rocking her own child and listening to the room’s noises as if they were part of the tale, “the world is full of trouble.”

One day she said to Catherine, low, “It’s my belief my man knows more about your Gerard than anybody in these parts; but he has got to be closer than ever of late. Drop in some day just afore sunset, and set him talking. And for our Lady’s sake say not I set you on. The only hiding he ever gave me was for babbling his business; and I do not want another. Gramercy! I married a man for the comfort of the thing, not to be hided.”

So Catherine dropped in before sunset, when the day’s work slackens and tongues, like fires, are easiest stirred. Jorian sat by the fireside; the flames licked the pot-hook and made a faint singing that filled the pauses. He was ready enough to tell her, with a rough pride, how he had once befriended her son, and perhaps saved his life. But this was no news to Catherine; she had gratitude enough for that already, salted and stored away. The moment she began to cross-question him—why Gerard neither came nor wrote, what held him silent—Jorian threw a grim look at his wife. Joan met it with an air of stolid candour and innocent unconsciousness; but Catherine heard the change as one hears a bird cease singing when a hawk passes.

His answers grew short and sullen. “What should he know more than another?” and the like.

After a pause he added, grudgingly, “Think you the burgomaster takes such as me into his secrets?”

“Oh, then the burgomaster knows something?” Catherine’s voice sharpened at once.

“Likely. Who else should?”

“I’ll ask him,” she said.

“I would.”

“And tell him you say he knows.”

“That is right, dame. Go make him mine enemy. That is what a poor fellow always gets if he says a word to you women.”

From that moment Jorian shrank in and became impenetrable as a hedgehog—and almost as prickly. His very silence had a sound to it, a bristling hush that made the room feel smaller. To Catherine and Margaret it was torment: Ghysbrecht had for some cause been Gerard’s mortal enemy—had stopped his marriage, imprisoned him, hunted him. And here was the late servant, who, when off his guard, had hinted that this enemy held the clue to Gerard’s silence. They sifted Jorian’s every word and look as carefully as flour through a cloth; all remained dark.

At last Catherine said to Margaret, “Go thyself to him. You are young, you are fair. You will maybe get more out of him than I could.”

The guess was reasonable. Margaret went with her child in her arms and tapped timidly at Jorian’s door just before sunset, when the street noises thin and each step seems louder.

“Come in,” said a sturdy voice.

She entered; Jorian sat by the fire. At sight of her he rose, snorted, and burst out of the house as if the devil had crossed his threshold.

“Is that for me, wife?” Margaret asked, cheeks burning.

“You must excuse him,” Joan replied, rather coldly, and her coldness was like skim-milk after cream. “He lays it to your door that he is a poor man instead of a rich one. It is something about a piece of parchment. There was one amissing, and he got nought from the burgomaster all along of that one.”

Margaret’s arms tightened round her boy. “Alas! Gerard took it.”

“Likely. But my man says you should not have let him: you were pledged to him to keep them all safe. And sooth to say, I blame not my Jorian for being wroth. ’Tis hard for a poor man to be so near fortune and lose it by those he has befriended. However, I tell him another story. Says I, ‘Folk that are out o’ trouble like you and me didn’t ought to be too hard on folk that are in trouble; and she has plenty.’ Going already? What is all your hurry, mistress?”

“Oh, it is not for me to drive the goodman out of his own house.”

“Well, let me kiss the bairn afore ye go. He is not in fault anyway, poor innocent.”

Margaret bent her head, received that kiss like a blessing she did not deserve, and went away with her heart bruised. On that rebuff she made a resolution, and she told it to no one—not even to Catherine.

After six weeks’ stay the good Catherine returned to Tergou. The house there, with its familiar smells of wool and soap and smoked meat, took her back into its old rhythm as if she had never left; and yet something had shifted, as if a beam had been moved and the whole roof now bore weight differently.

Soon after came the child’s birthday. Margaret did no work that day. She put on her Sunday clothes, clean linen that smelt of sun and lye, and took her boy in her arms. She went to the church and prayed long and fervently for Gerard’s safe return. The church had its own sound-world: the hush that holds even footsteps to gentleness, the small coughs swallowed, the murmur of distant beads, the faint wheeze of the bell-rope. Her words, formed in her own tongue, seemed to cling to the stone and the timber; she felt, with a kind of despair, how no prayer—however honest—can be translated into certainty.

That same day and hour, far away, Father Clement celebrated a mass and prayed for Margaret’s departed soul in the minster church at Basle.
