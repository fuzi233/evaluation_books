The kitchen was a sanctuary, a refuge from the desolate emptiness that had settled over our household like a shroud. The scent of baking bread wafted through the air, a comforting reminder of life's simple pleasures. Yet, amidst the warmth and light, a sense of unease hung heavy, like the weight of an unspoken truth. For Catherine, my adopted mother, had lost her closest confidant – the aged Martin – and her grief was palpable.

As she attempted to comfort me, her words felt hollow, like the echoes of a distant storm. She spoke of the release that death brought, of the tree being ripe for harvest, but her eyes betrayed a deep sorrow. Her usual stoicism, a shield she wore like armor, faltered, and I saw the cracks in her facade. The realization that there were now only two mouths to feed, and one care the less, only added to her anguish.

Margaret, my dear friend and fellow mourner, refused to be consoled. Her heart, once full of love and compassion, now felt empty and desolate. She gazed longingly at the vacant chair where Martin used to sit, her eyes welling up with tears. Her words, a poignant mix of sadness and longing, cut through the silence: "Oh, empty chair, my bosom aches to see thee... Poor old man, how could I love him by halves, I that did use to sit and look at him and think, 'But for me thou wouldst die of hunger.'"

Catherine, moved by Margaret's words, finally held her peace, and her eyes met mine with a deep understanding. Later, in a moment of introspection, she confessed that she, too, would mourn Martin's passing just as deeply as Margaret. This small act of empathy brought Margaret some solace, and for a brief moment, the pain seemed to lift.

However, Catherine's words soon took a turn that left me reeling. She spoke of Gerard, my absent brother, and the possibility that he might be dead or worse – a traitor. Her words cut me to the quick, and I felt a pang of fear for his safety. She continued, her voice tinged with a mix of concern and pragmatism, "My poor girl, you were not born to live alone. I have got to look on you as my own daughter. Waste not thine youth upon my son Gerard... I'd take him and Comfort together."
