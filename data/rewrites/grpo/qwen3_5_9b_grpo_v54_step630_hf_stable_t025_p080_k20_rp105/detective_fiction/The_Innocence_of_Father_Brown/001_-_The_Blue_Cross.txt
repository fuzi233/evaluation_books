The morning sun cast a silver ribbon across the water, meeting the green glitter of the sea just as the boat docked at Harwich. From the vessel poured a swarm of holidaymakers, indistinguishable from the throng, yet among them walked the man we must follow. He was unremarkable, save for a jarring contrast between the festive gaiety of his attire and the stern gravity of his face. He wore a pale grey jacket, a white waistcoat, and a silver straw hat trimmed with a grey-blue ribbon. His lean, dark face ended in a curt black beard that evoked a Spanish count or an Elizabethan ruff. He smoked a cigarette with the air of a man who had all the time in the world, showing no sign that the grey jacket concealed a loaded revolver, the waistcoat hid a police card, or the hat sheltered one of the most formidable intellects in Europe. This was Aristide Valentin, the head of the Paris police and the world's most celebrated investigator, arriving from Brussels to London to execute the arrest of the century.

His target was Flambeau, the colossus of crime. The police forces of three nations had finally tracked the fugitive from Ghent to Brussels, and then to the Hook of Holland. It was widely believed Flambeau intended to exploit the confusion of the Eucharistic Congress currently convening in London, perhaps disguising himself as a minor clerk or secretary. Yet, Valentin knew that with Flambeau, certainty was a luxury no one possessed.

Years had passed since the criminal ceased his reign of terror following the death of Roland, leaving a profound quietude over the earth. But in his prime, Flambeau was a figure as monumental and international as the Kaiser himself. Every morning, newspapers announced his latest escape, achieved only by committing another extraordinary crime. A Gascon of gigantic stature and daring, he was rumored to have overturned judges to "clear their minds" and run down the Rue de Rivoli with policemen under each arm. While his physical feats were often bloodless and undignified, his true crimes were ingenious robberies of staggering complexity. He had once run a dairy company in London with no cows, serving customers by simply moving milk cans from their doors to his own. He had maintained a secret correspondence with a young lady by photographing his messages onto microscope slides. He had repainted street numbers in the dead of night to lure travelers into traps and invented portable pillar-boxes to intercept postal orders. Furthermore, despite his massive size, he was a startling acrobat, capable of leaping like a grasshopper and climbing trees like a monkey. Valentin knew that finding Flambeau would be only the beginning of their adventure.

The problem remained: how to find him? Valentin's strategy was still evolving. There was one trait Flambeau could not disguise: his immense height. If Valentin's sharp eye had spotted a tall apple-woman, a grenadier, or a duchess, he would have arrested them instantly. However, throughout his journey, he had seen no one who could possibly be a disguised Flambeau. He had checked the passengers on the boat and the few people he had picked up at Harwich. There was a short railway official, three market gardeners, a very short widow, and finally, a diminutive Roman Catholic priest traveling from a small Essex village.

Valentin had initially scoffed at the priest, laughing at the absurdity of the man. He was the epitome of Eastern flatness, with a face as round and dull as a Norfolk dumpling and eyes as empty as the North Sea. He carried several brown paper parcels he seemed incapable of managing, a large shabby umbrella that constantly slipped from his grasp, and a return ticket he could not decipher. With a moon-calf simplicity, he explained to everyone in the carriage that he must be careful because he possessed something made of real silver "with blue stones" in his parcels. This quaint blend of Essex dullness and saintly simplicity amused the French detective endlessly until the priest miraculously arrived at Tottenham with all his parcels, only to return for his umbrella. Even then, Valentin, with good nature, warned him not to advertise his silver. Yet, amidst this amusement, Valentin kept his eyes peeled for someone else: anyone, rich or poor, male or female, standing well over six feet tall. Flambeau was four inches taller than that.

Arriving at Liverpool Street, Valentin felt secure that he had not missed the criminal. He visited Scotland Yard to organize assistance, then lit another cigarette and strolled through the streets of London. Pausing in a quiet, typical square beyond Victoria, he observed the tall, flat houses that looked simultaneously prosperous and uninhabited. In the center lay a shrubbery as deserted as a green Pacific islet. One side of the square rose higher like a dais, broken by a restaurant that seemed to have wandered from Soho. It was an unreasonably attractive object, featuring dwarf plants in pots and long, striped blinds of lemon yellow and white. It stood high above the street, accessed by a flight of steps that ran up to the front door like a fire-escape. Valentin stood before the yellow-white blinds, smoking and contemplating them.

To Valentin, the most incredible thing about miracles was that they happened. Clouds sometimes formed the shape of a human eye; trees occasionally stood in the exact shape of a question mark. Nelson died in the instant of victory; a man named Williams accidentally murdered a man named Williamson. Life contained an element of elfin coincidence that the prosaic often missed. Wisdom, as Poe had noted, must reckon on the unforeseen.

Aristide Valentin was unfathomably French. His intelligence was not that of a "thinking machine"—a phrase of modern fatalism—but of a thinking man. He was a plain man who achieved wonders through plodding logic and clear, commonplace French thought. The French electrify the world not by paradox, but by carrying out truisms to their extreme limits. Because Valentin understood reason, he understood its boundaries. Here, he had no strong first principles. Flambeau had been missed at Harwich; if he was in London, he could be anything from a tall tramp on Wimbledon Common to a toastmaster at the Hotel Metropole. In this state of uncertainty, Valentin had a unique method.

When the reasonable path was blocked, he followed the unreasonable. Instead of checking banks or police stations, he systematically went to the wrong places, knocking on empty houses and turning down dead ends. He defended this madness logically: if one had a clue, the direct route was best; but with no clue, the erratic path offered the chance that an oddity catching the pursuer's eye was the same one that had caught the fugitive's. Something about the flight of steps to the restaurant roused his romantic fancy, and he resolved to strike at random. He ascended the steps and ordered a cup of black coffee.

It was mid-morning, and he had not eaten. Adding a poached egg to his order, he musedly shook white sugar into his coffee, thinking of Flambeau. He recalled how the criminal had escaped via nail scissors, a burning house, unpaid postage, and a telescope aimed at a comet. Valentin believed his detective brain was equal to the criminal's, yet he acknowledged the disadvantage. "The criminal is the creative artist; the detective only the critic," he muttered with a sour smile, lifting his cup slowly and setting it down quickly. He had put salt in it.

Looking at the vessel from which the powder came, he noted it was unmistakably a sugar-basin. He wondered why they kept salt in it. Checking for orthodox vessels, he found two salt-cellars quite full. He tasted the contents; it was sugar. Refreshed by this discovery, he looked around the restaurant for other signs of this singular artistic taste—perhaps sugar in the salt-cellars and salt in the sugar-basin. Aside from a dark splash on a white-papered wall, the place was neat and ordinary. He rang the bell for the waiter.

When the fuzzy-haired, blear-eyed official hurried up, Valentin asked him to taste the sugar and judge its quality. The waiter yawned suddenly and woke up.

"Do you play this delicate joke on your customers every morning?" Valentin inquired. "Does changing the salt and sugar never pall on you as a jest?"

The waiter stammered, assuring him it was a curious mistake. He picked up the sugar-basin and the salt-cellar, his face growing bewildered. Finally, he excused himself and returned with the proprietor, who also examined the containers with confusion. Suddenly, the waiter became inarticulate with a rush of words.

"I zink," he stuttered eagerly, "I zink it is those two clergy-men."

"What two clergymen?" Valentin asked.

"The two clergymen," the waiter said, "that threw soup at the wall."

"Threw soup at the wall?" Valentin repeated, sensing an Italian metaphor.

"Yes, yes," the attendant pointed excitedly at the dark splash on the wall. "They threw it over there."

Valentin turned to the proprietor, who confirmed the story. Two quiet, respectable clergymen had arrived early. One paid and left; the other lingered. Just before stepping out, he deliberately picked up his half-empty soup cup and threw the contents slap onto the wall. The waiter had rushed out in time to find the wall splashed and the shop empty. It was cheeky, but the men had gone round the corner into Carstairs Street.

Valentin was on his feet, hat settled and stick in hand. In the universal darkness of his mind, he could only follow the first odd finger that pointed. Paying his bill, he clattered through the glass doors and swung into the adjacent street.

Fortunate that his eye remained cool, he noticed a shop-front flash by and went back to inspect it. It was a popular greengrocer's, displaying goods openly with ticketed prices. In the two most prominent compartments were heaps of oranges and nuts. On the nut heap lay a cardboard scrap reading, "Best tangerine oranges, two a penny." On the oranges was the equally clear description, "Finest Brazil nuts, 4d. a lb." Valentin felt he had seen this subtle humor recently. He drew the attention of the red-faced fruiterer to the inaccuracy. The shopman said nothing but sharply corrected the cards. Leaning on his cane, Valentin asked a question in experimental psychology.

"Why," he pursued gaily, "why are two tickets wrongly placed in a greengrocer's shop like a shovel hat that has come to London for a holiday? Or, to make myself clear, what is the mystical association connecting the idea of nuts marked as oranges with the idea of two clergymen, one tall and the other short?"

The shopman's eyes bulged like a snail's; he seemed ready to attack. Stammering angrily, he declared he would knock the heads of any parsons who upset his apples again.

"Indeed?" Valentin asked sympathetically. "Did they upset your apples?"

"One of 'em did," the heated shopman admitted. "Rolled 'em all over the street. I'd 'ave caught the fool but for havin' to pick 'em up."

"Which way did these parsons go?" Valentin asked.

"Up that second road on the left-hand side, and then across the square," the shopman replied promptly.

"Thanks," Valentin said, vanishing like a fairy. On the other side of the square, he found a policeman. "This is urgent, constable; have you seen two clergymen in shovel hats?"

The policeman chuckled heavily. "I 'ave, sir; and if you arst me, one of 'em was drunk. He stood in the middle of the road that bewildered that—"

"Which way did they go?" Valentin snapped.

"They took one of them yellow buses over there," the man answered, pointing to the vehicle heading to Hampstead.

Valentin produced his official card and demanded two men to accompany him. Crossing the road with contagious energy, he joined an inspector and a plainclothesman on the opposite pavement.

"Well, sir," the inspector began with smiling importance.

Valentin pointed suddenly with his cane. "I'll tell you on the top of that omnibus," he said, darting across the traffic. When they sank panting on the top seats, the inspector remarked they could go four times faster in a taxi.

"Quite true," Valentin replied placidly, "if we only had an idea of where we were going."

"Well, where are you going?" the inspector stared.

Valentin smoked frowningly for a few seconds, then removed his cigarette. "If you know what a man's doing, get in front of him; but if you want to guess what he's doing, keep behind him. Stray when he strays; stop when he stops; travel as slowly as he. Then you may see what he saw and may act as he acted. All we can do is to keep our eyes skinned for a queer thing."

"What sort of queer thing?" the inspector asked.

"Any sort of queer thing," Valentin answered, falling into obstinate silence.

The yellow omnibus crawled up the northern roads for what seemed like hours. Valentin refused to explain further, and his assistants grew silent doubts, perhaps mixed with a desire for lunch. The long roads of North London suburbs stretched out like an infernal telescope. London died away in draggled taverns and dreary scrubs, only to be reborn in blazing high streets and blatant hotels. It was like passing through thirteen separate vulgar cities. As winter twilight threatened, the Parisian detective sat silent and watchful. By the time they left Camden Town, the policemen were nearly asleep until Valentin leapt erect, struck a hand on each shoulder, and shouted for the driver to stop.

They tumbled down the steps, disoriented, only to find Valentin triumphantly pointing at a window on the left. It was a large window in a gilt and palatial public-house, labeled "Restaurant." Like the rest of the façade, it was frosted glass, but in the middle was a big, black smash, like a star in the ice.

"Our cue at last," Valentin cried, waving his stick. "The place with the broken window."

"What window? What cue?" his assistant asked. "Why, what proof is there that this has anything to do with them?"
