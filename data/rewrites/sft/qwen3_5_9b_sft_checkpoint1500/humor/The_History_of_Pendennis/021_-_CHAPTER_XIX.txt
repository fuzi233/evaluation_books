Our friend Pen felt a distinct relief when his mentor finally departed, leaving the young gentleman on the second day after their arrival at Oxford. If Pen was relieved, the Major was undoubtedly ecstatic to have discharged his duty and secured a respite from it. For over three months, that martyred Major had devoted himself entirely to his nephew—a sacrifice so profound one wonders if any other man, save perhaps a woman accustomed to it, would endure. While a man might lay down his head or risk his honor, asking him to surrender his ease or his heart's desire is a trial few survive. Yet, the Major deserved praise for his conduct; he had earned his holiday. Foker and Pen saw him off in the coach, with the younger gentleman giving specific instructions to the driver to ensure the Major's comfort. The elder Pendennis delighted in watching his nephew accompanied by a young fellow who would introduce him to the University's finest circles. With the Major rushing off to London and subsequently to Cheltenham to descend upon local estates for hunting and company, the stage was set.

A quarter of the serial space has been dedicated to this single episode of Pen's career, a testament to the fact that his adventures cannot be chronicled with equal minutess. Unless a descendant of the chronicler takes up the pen at his death, the full scope of Pen's life remains untold. We shall not traverse his academic journey with such granularity. Alas, the lives of such boys do not lend themselves to total narration. As long as one's "honor" appears clear, the mind feels relatively secure. However, let us admit the candor that women are generally pure and unselfish, whereas men are rarely so. My list of "spotless" acquaintances is short; I struggle to find ten saints among them.

During Pen's first term, he attended classical and mathematical lectures with reasonable diligence. However, discovering quickly that he lacked the genius for exact sciences—and annoyed by being thoroughly beaten in the lecture-room by several vulgar youths wearing shoes so thick and coarse they required straps—he abandoned mathematics. He announced to his indulgent parent that he would henceforth devote himself exclusively to Greek and Roman literature. Mrs. Pendennis was wholly satisfied with this shift, urging only that he not ruin his health through over-study. She had heard harrowing tales of students consumed by brain-fever and perishing prematurely. Pen, though unaware of any latent disease, promised not to sit up reading late, adhering to this pledge with a tenacity that belied his usual remissness.

Soon, however, Pen realized he learned little of value in the classics. His fellow students were dull, and his tutor, Mr. Buck, possessed no more scholarly insight than a fifth-form boy at Grey Friars. Mr. Buck might have some humdrum notions about the meter of Aeschylus, but his understanding of poetry was as shallow as Mrs. Binge's. Pen grew weary of watching dull students and a mediocre tutor stumble through lines of plays he could read in a fraction of the time they spent. Private reading, he concluded, was the only truly profitable study. He informed his mother of this plan, intending to read significantly more privately and less publicly. The excellent woman, knowing no more of Homer than algebra, accepted his arrangements with contentment, confident he would merit his eventual position.

Pen did not return home until after Christmas, a slight disappointment to his mother and to Laura, who missed him for building snow fortifications. Instead, he was invited to Logwood, the residence of Lady Agnes Foker, where private theatricals and a gay Christmas party of "very fine folks" awaited. These were precisely the company the Major would want his nephew to avoid neglecting, though Pen stayed at home for the final three weeks of the vacation. Laura noted the quantity of fine new clothes he brought, and his mother admired his improved, manly appearance.

At Easter, he returned again, bringing even smarter attire: wonderful shooting jackets with remarkable buttons, and in the evenings, gorgeous velvet waistcoats paired with richly embroidered cravats. Peering into his room, his mother discovered a beautiful dressing-case with silver mountings, filled with lovely rings and jewelry. Most notably, he had acquired a new French watch and gold chain, replacing the big old chronometer inherited from John Pendennis—the watch by which the late doctor had felt countless pulses. Just prior to college, Helen had given him this timepiece, unwinding it from her trinket-box with a solemn speech honoring her husband's virtues and the proper use of time. Now, Pen declared the portly chronometer "out of date," comparing it disrespectfully to a warming-pan, and relegated it to a drawer alongside soiled primrose gloves and outdated cravats. He also swapped his old mare, Rebecca, for a more powerful steed, paying a heavy sum for the exchange, much to Laura's tears.

Furthermore, Pen returned with a large box of cigars bearing exotic names like *Colorados*, *Afrancesados*, and *Telescopios*. He began consuming them not only around the stables and greenhouses, where they proved beneficial for Helen's plants, but also in his study. Initially disapproving, his mother eventually relented. Pen claimed he was working on a prize poem and could not compose without tobacco, citing Lord Byron. When Helen entered the room one afternoon and found him engrossed in a recent novel—cultivating light literature for both his nation and others—she did not disturb him. Instead, retrieving a light-box and cigar-case from his adjoining bedroom, she gently placed a cigar in his mouth and lit the match. Pen laughed, kissing her hand as it hovered protectively over the back of the sofa. "Dear old mother," he remarked, "if I were to tell you to burn the house down, I think you would do it." Indeed, the foolish woman likely would have burned it down for him.

Beyond English "light literature," Pen imported boxes of French fiction. Helen, dipping into these volumes, read passages that opened her eyes with wonder, though Pen insisted it was his duty to keep up with contemporary authors like Paul de Kock, just as much as Swift or Molière. While Mrs. Pendennis sighed in perplexity and acquiesced, Miss Laura was strictly warned off the books by both her anxious mother and the rigorous moralist, Arthur Pendennis himself, who deemed such reading inappropriate for a young lady.

During this long vacation, Pen drained the bin of claret his father had stored, famously remarking that there was not a headache in a hogshead. Upon exhaustion, he wrote to "Messrs. Binney and Latham" of Mark Lane, following the advice of the late Doctor Portman, who had recommended a reputable London house over Oxbridge tradesmen for their deleterious and expensive liquor. Hinting that the university account should accompany the domestic bill, the poor widow was frightened by the amount. Pen laughed at her old-fashioned views, insisting the bill was moderate and that everyone drank claret and champagne now. The widow paid, dimly sensing her household expenses were swelling beyond her narrow income, though she consoled herself that these were only occasional expenditures. In the brief moments he was home, surely they ought to make him happy?

Arthur's allowances were remarkably liberal, far exceeding those of many wealthier peers. Years ago, the thrifty John Pendennis had established an "Education Fund," adding sums annually. Even after his death, the widow continued to augment it, so that by the time Pen entered Oxford, the fund was substantial. The Major's philosophy was clear: let the boy be liberally allowanced, enter the world as a gentleman, and take his place among men of rank. It would be his own duty to maintain it. To stint a boy or put him below his fellows' standard was bad policy; he would soon face the world and fight for himself. Thus, young Pen, the only son of an estate-gentleman with a good allowance and a gentlemanlike bearing, appeared far more consequential than he truly was.

To the authorities, tradesmen, and undergraduates, Pen was viewed as a "young buck" of the aristocracy. His manner was frank, brave, and perhaps slightly impertinent, fitting for a high-spirited youth. Generous and free-handed, he seemed plentiful in funds. He loved joviality, possessed a good singing voice, and while boat-racing had not yet reached its later fever pitch, riding and tandem-driving were his fashions. He rode well to hounds, appeared in pink, and managed to run up significant bills at Nile's livery-stable and other quarters. He was fond of books, particularly rare editions with beautiful bindings, filling his shelves with tall copies featuring gilding and marbling. His taste in art was keen, favoring Strangers and Rembrandt etchings over the tawdry racing prints that delighted his predecessor, Mr. Spicer. He was also partial to rings, jewelry, and fine raiment, dressing with such care for dining at friends' rooms as if courting a mistress. Rumors circulated that he wore rings over his kid gloves, a folly he stoutly denied, though youth perpetuates many such absurdities with admirable gravity.

In his second year, coinciding with Miss Fotheringay's triumph in London, Pen hung a print of her in his bedroom and confessed to his circle how "awfully, wildly, madly, passionately" he had loved her. Recalling this fatal period, his brow darkened, eyes rolled, and chest heaved with emotion as he described his woes.
