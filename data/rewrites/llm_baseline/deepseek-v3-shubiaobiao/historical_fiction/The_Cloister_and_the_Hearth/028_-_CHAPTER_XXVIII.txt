Good and evil have a curious way of balancing themselves, often with unexpected symmetry. The steel bow strapped to the arbalestrier’s back, rising above his head, had dragged him under. Yet that same bow, positioned just so, could not escape Gerard’s grasp—one hand seized it, the other slipped between the bowstring and the frame, securing a firm hold. The next instant, Denys was wrenched upward with such force that half his body burst from the water.

“Don’t grab me! For pity’s sake, don’t grab me!” Gerard cried, terrified of the fatal mistake.

*“Pas si bête,”* Denys sputtered, water choking his words.

Relief washed over Gerard. Here was a man who understood. “On your back!” he commanded, seizing the crossbow and pulling forward to guide the motion. “Hand on my shoulder—slap the water with the other. No, downward, like this. Do nothing more than I say.”

Twisting his fingers into Denys’s long hair, Gerard clenched the ends between his teeth, using the strength of his neck to keep the soldier’s head above the surface. Then he struck out fiercely against the current. For a heartbeat, he hesitated—which bank to aim for? The western shore lay slightly closer. A simple choice, yet one with fateful weight. Had he chosen the other, they might have swum straight into the arms of the law.

The river fought them, dragging them sideways as they struggled forward. When at last their feet touched gravel, they staggered onto solid ground, breathless and dripping. For a moment, they simply stared at one another, as if to confirm they were both whole. Then, without a word, they flung their arms around each other’s necks, hearts too full for speech.

To save a life—to save a friend—what joy could surpass it? No poet’s flight could reach such heights. For Gerard, the exiled wanderer, this moment was sweeter than any relic or shrine.

Calmer now, they clasped hands and walked along the bank like sweethearts, careless of direction. Behind them, the boat’s survivors clung to the overturned hull, the turnip-faced man still wailing as though nothing had changed.

“Shall we wait for them to right the boat?” Denys asked.

Gerard shook his head. “Our fare is paid. We owe them nothing. Let’s move on—swiftly.”

Denys agreed. “We can walk all the way to Cologne from here.”

“I’m not going to Cologne.”

Denys stopped short. “Where, then?”

“Burgundy.”

“Burgundy?” Denys’s voice cracked. “You can’t mean it.”

“I do. What does it matter how I reach Rome?”

“But—the churches, the relics! You were eager to see them.”

“Foolish Denys,” Gerard said softly. “That was when we were to see them together. The world is full of churches, full of bones. But a friend like you? There’s only one. I won’t drag you farther from home, nor will I lose you now. Since I fished you from the Rhine, I find I love you too well to part.”

Denys let out a joyous whoop. *“Courage! Va pour la Bourgogne!”* He clapped Gerard’s shoulder. *“Oh, soyez tranquille! Cette fois, il est bien décidément mort, ce coquin-là.”*

And so they turned their backs on the Rhine.

But across the water, the law had not forgotten them. A voice rang out: *“HALT!”*

Gerard turned. One of the men from the boat brandished an official badge and a parchment. His stomach twisted—a law-abiding man at heart, he had always obeyed authority.

Denys, however, was French. And a soldier. He assessed the situation: the river between them, the boat still adrift, no ferry in sight.

“Quick,” he muttered. “To that hill—out of sight, then double time.”

They walked on, deliberately unhurried.

Denys turned, snapped his fingers insolently, and kept walking.

Another finger-snap for his grace.

Denys dismissed his majesty with the same contempt.

Gerard winced at the theatrics. “Now we must *run* to Burgundy,” he said grimly. And run they did, Gerard outpacing Denys with a stamina that left the soldier gasping.

“Where in hell’s name is your fever?” Denys panted.

“Drowned in the Rhine, I suppose,” Gerard shot back.

At a village, Denys bought bread and a bottle of Rhenish wine. “No inns,” he warned. “We’ll sleep rough—else we’ll be taken in our beds.”

As night fell, they found a barn, its floor heaped with fresh straw. Denys buried Gerard in it, leaving only a breathing hole. “Water may kill fevered men,” he joked, “but I’ll make warm water of you yet.”

Gerard laughed. “A few drops from the Rhine won’t chill me. I’ve heat enough to boil a cloud.”

Sleep took him instantly. Denys lay awake longer, listening to the distant drum of hoofbeats and the eerie howls of pursuit. He held his breath until the sounds faded, then slept like the dead.

Morning came. Gerard sat up, straw steaming around him. “Lazy loon,” he teased Denys, who only grinned.

They set out, Denys scouting ahead from a treetop. “Clear to the next village,” he reported.

As they neared the settlement, Denys stopped. “How do you feel?”

“As if Rome were just past those houses.”

“And your body?”

“Neither hot nor cold. Only this cursed leg—itches like the devil.”

Denys sighed in mock solemnity. “Unhappy youth. Your fever is gone, and your wound is healing. Which means I must now tell you: *the hue and cry is out for us, and they’re on fast horses.*”

Gerard’s face fell. *“Oh.”*
