The banns of marriage were to be read three times, as was the custom—though in those days, the readings could be hurried if the priest was willing. The young couple, hearts pounding with hope, persuaded the new cure to declare their intent three times in a single day. By Monday’s end, the words had been spoken at both matins and vespers, and to their relief, no one from Tergou had been present to hear.

But joy turned to terror the next morning when a stranger stood in the church and forbade the union. The man claimed they were underage, their parents unaware. Outside, Margaret and Gerard clung to each other, trembling, despairing—until the very man who had ruined them approached with a sly offer.

“A crown,” he said, “and I’ll undo my work. Tell the priest I was mistaken, and all will be well.”

Gerard, desperate, promised him a golden angel instead. The bargain struck, the man retracted his objection before the cure, who then agreed to marry them the following morning.

Yet the would-be extortionist, drunk on his prize, boasted of the scheme at an archery green in Tergou—where Sybrandt, Gerard’s own brother, listened with keen interest.

Sybrandt rushed home, finding his father absent but his elder brother Cornelis at hand. The two, idle and covetous, saw Gerard’s love as a threat to their future inheritance. Without a word to their mother—whose loyalties were uncertain—they went straight to the burgomaster.

Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, though disdainful of their greed, saw his opportunity. “Leave this to me,” he said, dismissing them with cold authority.

Meanwhile, Gerard, unaware of the storm brewing, sat at his mother’s knee, her fingers stroking his hair as she softened toward his love. Her guilt over destroying Margaret’s portrait had turned her heart. For once, she was his ally.

But the next dawn brought disaster.

In the quiet church at Sevenbergen, Gerard stood radiant, Margaret blushing at his side, with only Peter and Martin as witnesses. The cure began the rite—only for a harsh voice to cut through the sacred words.

“Forbear!”

Constables seized Gerard in the name of the law. Martin’s knife flashed, but the priest stayed his hand. “What madness is this?”

“He weds against his father’s will,” declared the officer.

Margaret’s cry of despair echoed as Gerard was torn from her arms. They dragged him away, feigning a journey to Rotterdam—only to double back, smuggling him into the haunted tower of Tergou’s Stadthouse.

The cell was small, the window barred, the only furniture a massive oak chest. The air was thick with the dread of slow death—starvation, poison, or worse. Gerard knelt, praying for deliverance, then seized the rusted iron bar of the window, wrenching it free just as Ghysbrecht entered.

The burgomaster’s eyes flickered to the loosened bar but said nothing. He left bread and water, then delivered his ultimatum: *Renounce Margaret, or rot here.*

Gerard’s defiance was instant. “Death first.”

But hope came from below. Martin, ever watchful, had seen Gerard’s face at the slit of a window. A plan was set in motion—a bow, an arrow, a message tied in silk.

With trembling hands, Gerard obeyed. The cord came, then a thicker rope. He fastened it, tested the chest, and—heart hammering—climbed out into the free air, descending hand over hand toward Margaret’s outstretched arms.

Yet freedom was not yet won. A lantern’s light swept the courtyard. Guards.

They pressed into the shadows, but the light found them. Martin nocked an arrow—then froze, face blanched with terror.

“The haunted tower,” he gasped. “Hell walks tonight.”

A figure, its head aflame, scaled the wall like a spider.

Gerard gripped his knife. If mortal foes did not take him, the devil might.
