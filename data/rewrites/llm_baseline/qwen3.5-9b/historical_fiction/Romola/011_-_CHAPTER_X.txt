Three weeks had passed since Tito delivered his florins to Cennini. When he set out toward the Via de' Bardi, he carried himself with the visible ease of a mind entirely at peace. How could it be otherwise? His nature was too buoyant, too unburdened by fear, for hidden dangers or distant troubles to weigh upon him. Stepping from the blinding noonday sun into the cool shadow of a narrow alley, he removed his black *berretta*—a simple cap with an upturned flap—that framed his brown curls. He tossed his head back to invite the breeze, revealing a forehead that was neither marked by deceit nor stamped with naive innocence; it was simply a finely formed, smooth, square surface of youth. His slow, wandering glance at the upper windows of the houses possessed neither cunning nor guile, merely the innocent, wide-open gaze of a child's eye, clear and unclouded.

Was Tito's face a cipher, readable only by those who knew the specific key of the observer's heart? The dominant expression in his entire demeanor was a profound negativity—not of malice, but of emptiness. It was a truthful declaration of freedom from anxiety, restless vanity, or any uneasy claim upon his spirit. Consequently, the admiration that trailed him through the throngs of holidaymakers was offered willingly and without reservation.

By this time, the excitement of the Feast of St. John had seeped even into the smallest side streets. The massive crowds that had once lined the procession route now streamed outward in every direction, chasing new distractions. These interludes of a festival are precisely when the latent energy of a multitude becomes petulant, ready to sacrifice a single individual for the collective amusement. As Tito approached the neighborhood of San Martino, the crowd thickened. Near the inn known as the *Bertucce*, or Baboons, a cluster of people had gathered around some unseen attraction.

Tito prepared to turn aside when, cutting through the noise of laughter, his ears caught a distressed, childish cry: "Loose me! Holy Virgin, help me!" This sound compelled him to push his way into the knot of onlookers. He realized instantly that the voice belonged to a young peasant girl (*contadina*) whose white hood had slipped during her struggle to escape the grip of a man dressed in the motley garb of a *cerretano*, or conjurer. The magician was attempting to soothe and cajole her, earning the amused sympathy of the spectators. Through a chorus of mocking words calling her a simpleton—the Florentine tongue being rich in such epithets—they argued with the girl against her stubborn resistance.

At first, the girl's face was turned away; Tito saw only her light-brown hair, plaited and pinned with a long silver needle. But the struggle soon forced her face toward him. There, through the mist of swelling tears, he recognized the baby features of Tessa. Her blue eyes were filled with moisture, her lower lip trembling. Yet, Tessa also saw *him*. A sudden hope, akin to that which lights a child's face when held captive by a stranger and spotting a familiar hand reaching out, beamed across her tear-stained visage.

In an instant, Tito broke through the barrier of curious bystanders. Grasping Tessa's waist, he demanded, "Loose this child! What right have you to hold her against her will?"

The conjurer—a man with a face where the angles of the eyes, brows, nose, mouth, and jaw all tilted upward—displayed a row of small, regular teeth in an impish yet harmless grin. He released Tessa's hands, stretching his own backward, shrugging his shoulders in a half-apologetic, half-protesting gesture.

"I mean the ragazza no evil in the world, Messere," the conjurer replied. "Ask this respectable company. I was merely demonstrating a few tricks, in which this little damsel might have assisted due to her kitten-like face, promising her a handful of confetti as a reward. But what then? Messer surely has better confetti at hand, and she knows it."

A wave of laughter rippled through the crowd, likely provoked by the look of relief and confidence with which Tessa clung to Tito's arm as he drew her close and placed her hand within his. To Tessa, the laughter was as terrifying as the roar of wild beasts from which she was fleeing; she attached no meaning to it. Tito, however, foreseeing the awkwardness of having interrupted an entertainment, hastened to move away from observers who, deprived of their amusement, would inevitably seek to restore the balance with jests.

"See, see, little one! Here is your hood," the conjurer called out, flinging the piece of white drapery over Tessa's head. "*Orsù*, bear me no malice; come back to me when Messere can spare you."

"Ah! Maestro Vaiano, she'll return presently, as the toad said to the harrow," shouted a spectator, noting how Tessa shrank back at the conjurer's dismissal.

Vexed by this delay in his journey to the Via de' Bardi, Tito pushed vigorously toward the corner of a side street. Before they had fully entered, he addressed her in a kind but hurried tone, "Now, little one, where were you going? Did you come to the Festa alone?"

"No!" Tessa answered, her face regaining its look of fright and distress. "I have lost my mother in the crowd—my mother and my father-in-law. They will be angry; he will beat me. It happened in the crowd at San Pulinari; someone pushed me, and I couldn't stop myself, so I drifted away from them. Oh, I don't know where they've gone! Please, don't leave me!"

Her eyes swelled anew with tears, ending in a sob.

Tito hurried onward; the Church of the Badia was nearby. They could enter via the cloister at the rear, where he might speak with her—or perhaps leave her there. But it was past the hour when the church was open. Pausing under the shelter of the cloister, he asked, "Do you have no cousin or friend in Florence whose house you could reach? Or are you afraid to walk alone after being frightened by the conjurer? I am in a rush to get to Oltrarno, but if I could take you somewhere near—"

"Oh, I *am* frightened," Tessa whispered. "He was the devil—I know he was. I don't know where to go. I have nobody. My mother meant to have her dinner somewhere, and I don't know where. Holy Madonna! I shall be beaten."

The corners of her pouting mouth drooped pitifully, and the poor little bosom, adorned with prayer beads above her green serge gown, heaved violently. A loud sob was inevitable, and the great tears fell as if trying to make up for lost time. Here was a dilemma! To leave her would be brutal, and Tito's nature was all gentleness. Wishing he hadn't been summoned to the Via de' Bardi, he watched her lift her holiday apron to catch the rushing tears. Laying his own hand upon the fabric, he gently wiped one of her cheeks and kissed the baby-like roundness.

"My poor little Tessa! Leave off crying. Let us see what can be done. Where is your home—where do you live?"

There was no immediate answer, only subsiding sobs and slower-falling tears.

"Come! I'll take you a little way, if you'll tell me where you want to go."

The apron fell, and Tessa's face transformed, becoming as contented as a cherub emerging from a cloud. The conjurer, the anger, and the threat of beating seemed worlds away.

"I think I'll go home, if you'll take me," she said in a half-whisper, looking up at Tito with wide blue eyes and a sweetness surpassing a smile—a childlike calm.

"Come, then, little one," Tito replied in a caressing tone, tucking her arm inside his again. "Which way is it?"

"Beyond Peretola—where the large pear-tree is."

"Peretola? Which gate, *pazzarella*? Remember, I am a stranger."

"Out at the Por del Prato," Tessa said, gripping his arm tightly as they moved forward.

Unfamiliar with every twist and turn, and realizing that the busiest streets offered the greatest chance of encountering Monna Ghita to conclude his quest, Tito steered them straight for Porta Rossa and then toward Ognissanti. He presented his usual bright, propitiatory face to the mixed crowd, who threw their jests at him and his heavy-shod maiden with generous abandon. Alongside the decent holiday-goers were frolicking apprentices, envious of his good fortune; bold-eyed women wearing the yellow veil of penance; beggars thrusting forward their caps in mockery of his haste; gamblers, sharpers, and loafers of the worst sort; and boys trained to chant insults at brutal street games. For the streets of Florence were not always a moral spectacle in those days, and Tessa's terror of being lost in the crush was entirely justified.
