The doctor's pretty housemaid stood waiting for me, holding the street door open. As I turned to face her, the morning light flooded the hall, illuminating the face of Mr. Candy's assistant. It was undeniable, as Betteredge had claimed, that Ezra Jennings' appearance was a disadvantage. His gypsy complexion, fleshless cheeks, gaunt bones, dreamy eyes, and extraordinary, parti-colored hair created a confusing contradiction between old age and youth that struck a stranger unfavorably. Yet, despite my instinctive wariness, an inscrutable appeal to my sympathies held me rooted to the spot. While my knowledge of the world dictated that I should acknowledge Mr. Candy's sad change and depart, my interest in this strange man gave him the opportunity to speak to me privately, a conversation he had clearly been waiting for.

"Are you walking my way, Mr. Jennings?" I asked, noting he held his hat. "I am going to call on my aunt, Mrs. Ablewhite."

"I have a patient to see," Ezra replied, "but I am walking your way."

We left the house together. I noticed that the cheerful servant girl, who had greeted me with smiles, received a modest message from Ezra regarding his return time. Her pursed lips and eyes, which deliberately avoided his face, revealed she was no favorite. Outside, Betteredge confirmed he was unpopular everywhere. "What a life!" I thought as we descended the steps.

Ezra seemed determined to let me resume the topic of Mr. Candy's illness. His silence was significant; it was my turn. I readily accepted the responsibility.

"Judging by the change I see in him," I began, "Mr. Candy's illness must have been far more serious than I had supposed?"

"It is almost a miracle," Ezra said, "that he lived through it."

"Is his memory never any better than I found it today? He has been trying to speak to me..."

"Of something which happened before he was taken ill?" Ezra asked, sensing my hesitation.

"Yes."

"His memory of events at that past time is hopelessly enfeebled," Ezra continued. "It is almost to be deplored that even the wreck of it remains. He remembers dimly plans he formed, things he had to say or do, but he is incapable of recalling what those plans were or what he had to say. He is painfully conscious of his deficiency and anxious to hide it. If he could have recovered in a complete state of oblivion, he would have been happier. Perhaps we should all be happier if we could completely forget!"

"There are some events surely in all men's lives," I replied, "the memory of which they would be unwilling entirely to lose?"

"That is, I hope, to be said of most men, Mr. Blake. I am afraid it cannot truly be said of *all*." He paused, then asked, "Have you any reason to suppose that the lost remembrance Mr. Candy tried to recover was a remembrance important to *you* that he should recall?"

He had touched the very point I wished to discuss. My interest in this strange man had compelled me to give him the chance to speak, reserving my own questions until I was satisfied of his discretion. The little he had said convinced me he was a gentleman possessing an unsought self-possession, a sure sign of good breeding. Whatever his motives, I felt justified in answering without reserve.

"I believe I have a strong interest in tracing the lost remembrance Mr. Candy was unable to recall," I said. "May I ask whether you can suggest any method by which I might assist his memory?"

Ezra looked at me, a sudden flash of interest lighting his dreamy brown eyes.

"Mr. Candy's memory is beyond the reach of assistance," he said. "I have tried to help it often enough since his recovery to speak positively on that point."

This disappointed me. "I confess you led me to hope for a less discouraging answer," I admitted.

"It may not be a final answer, Mr. Blake," Ezra smiled. "It may be possible to trace Mr. Candy's lost recollection without appealing to Mr. Candy himself."

"Indeed? Is it an indiscretion to ask how?"

"By no means. My difficulty lies in explaining myself. May I trust to your patience if I refer once more to Mr. Candy's illness, speaking this time without sparing you certain professional details?"

"Pray go on! You have interested me already."

My eagerness amused him. We had left the town behind. Ezra stopped to pick wild flowers from the roadside hedge. "How beautiful they are!" he said simply, showing me his nosegay. "And how few people in England seem to admire them as they deserve!"

"You have not always been in England?" I asked.

"No. I was born and partly brought up in one of our colonies. My father was English, but my mother..." He stopped, realizing we were straying. "The truth is, I have associations with these modest flowers. To Mr. Candy, let us return."

Connecting his reluctant words about himself with his melancholy view that human happiness lay in forgetting the past, I felt satisfied that the story written in his face was true. He had suffered as few men suffer, and there was a mixture of foreign race in his English blood.

"You have heard of the original cause of Mr. Candy's illness?" he resumed. "The night of Lady Verinder's dinner-party was a night of heavy rain. My employer drove home wet to the skin and found an urgent message from a patient. He went immediately to visit the sick person without changing his clothes. I was detained elsewhere that night. When I returned the next morning, Mr. Candy's groom was waiting in alarm. By then, the mischief was done; the illness had set in."

"The illness has only been described to me as a fever," I said.

"I can add nothing to make the description more accurate," Ezra answered. "From first to last, the fever assumed no specific form. I consulted two of Mr. Candy's medical friends. They agreed it was serious but strongly dissented from my view of the treatment. We differed entirely on the patient's pulse. The two doctors argued from its rapidity that a lowering treatment was necessary. On my side, I pointed to its alarming feebleness as indicating an exhausted system requiring stimulants. They wanted gruel and lemonade; I wanted champagne, brandy, ammonia, and quinine. For the first few days, I had to yield to my elders. The patient sank steadily. I made a second attempt to appeal to the evidence of the pulse. Its rapidity was unchecked, and its feebleness had increased. The doctors took offense. 'Mr. Jennings, either we manage this case, or you manage it. Which is it to be?' I said, 'Give me five minutes.' When the time expired, I said, 'You positively refuse to try the stimulant treatment?' They refused. 'I mean to try it at once.' 'Try it, Mr. Jennings, and we withdraw.' I fetched a bottle of champagne and administered half a tumbler-full with my own hand. The two physicians took up their hats in silence and left."

"You had assumed a serious responsibility," I said. "In your place, I am afraid I should have shrunk from it."

"In my place, Mr. Blake, you would have remembered that Mr. Candy had taken you into his employment under circumstances that made you his debtor for life. You would have seen him sinking hour by hour and risked anything rather than let the one man on earth who had befriended you die before your eyes. Don't suppose I had no sense of the terrible position! There were moments when I felt all the misery of my friendlessness. If I had been a happy man, I believe I should have sunk under the task. But I had no happy time to look back at, no past peace of mind to contrast with my present anxiety. I held firm. I took an interval in the middle of the day for repose. For the rest of the twenty-four hours, as long as his life was in danger, I never left his bedside. Towards sunset, the delirium came on. It lasted through the night and intermitted at that terrible time in the early morning—from two to five—when Death gathers in his harvest most abundantly. It was then that Death and I fought over the man who lay on the bed. I never hesitated. When wine failed, I tried brandy. When stimulants lost their influence, I doubled the dose. After an interval of suspense—the like of which I hope to God I shall never feel again—there came a day when the rapidity of the pulse slightly diminished, and there came also a change in the beat to steadiness and strength. *Then*, I knew I had saved him; and then I own I broke down. I laid the poor fellow's wasted hand back on the bed and burst out crying. An hysterical relief, Mr. Blake—nothing more! Physiology says, and truly, that some men are born with female constitutions—and I am one of them!"

He made that bitterly professional apology for his tears, speaking quietly and unaffectedly. His tone showed he was morbidly anxious not to set himself up as an object of interest.

"You may well ask why I have wearied you with these details," he went on. "It is the only way I can see of properly introducing what I have to say next. Now you know my position, you will understand the sore need I had of lightening the burden on my mind. I have occupied my leisure for some years writing a book on the brain and nervous system, addressed to members of my profession. My work will probably never be finished or published, but it has been the friend of many lonely hours. I told you he was delirious? And I mentioned the time?"

"Yes."

"Well, I had reached a section of my book touching on delirium. I doubt whether we can justifiably infer that the loss of the faculty of speaking connectedly implies the loss of the faculty of thinking connectedly. Mr. Candy's illness gave me an opportunity to test this. I understand shorthand and took down the patient's 'wanderings' exactly as they fell from his lips. Do you see, Mr. Blake, what I am coming to?"

I waited with breathless interest.

"At odd and ends of time," Ezra continued, "I reproduced my shorthand notes in ordinary writing, leaving large spaces between the broken phrases and single words as they had fallen disconnectedly. I treated the result on the principle of putting together a child's puzzle. It was all confusion to begin with, but it may be brought into order if you find the right way. Acting on this plan, I filled in each blank space with what the words on either side suggested as the speaker's meaning, altering over and over until my additions followed naturally. The result was that I occupied many vacant and anxious hours and arrived at a confirmation of my theory. In plainer words, after putting the broken sentences together, I found the superior faculty of thinking going on, more or less connectedly, in my patient's mind, while the inferior faculty of expression was in a state of almost complete incapacity."

"One word!" I interposed eagerly. "Did my name occur in any of his wanderings?"

"You shall hear, Mr. Blake. Among my written proofs of the assertion I have just advanced—or, rather, among the written experiments tending to put my assertion to the proof—there *is* one in which your name occurs. For nearly the whole of one night, Mr. Candy's mind was occupied with *something* between himself and you. I have the broken words on one sheet of paper and the links of my own discovery connecting them on another. The product is an intelligible statement—first, of something actually done in the past; secondly, of something Mr. Candy contemplated doing in the future if his illness had not stopped him. The question is whether this represents the lost recollection he vainly attempted to find this morning?"

"Not a doubt of it!" I answered. "Let us go back directly and look at the papers!"

"Quite impossible, Mr. Blake."

"Why?"

"Put yourself in my position for a moment," Ezra said. "Would *you* disclose to another person what had dropped unconsciously from the lips of your suffering patient and your helpless friend, without first knowing that there was a necessity to justify you in opening your lips?"

I felt he was unanswerable, yet I tried to argue. "My conduct in such a delicate matter would depend greatly on whether the disclosure was of a nature to compromise my friend or not."

"I have disposed of all necessity for considering that side of the question long since," Ezra said. "Wherever my notes included anything Mr. Candy might have wished to keep secret, those notes have been destroyed. My manuscript experiments include nothing now which he would have hesitated to communicate to others if he had recovered his memory. In your case, I have every reason to suppose my notes contain something he actually wished to say to you."

"And yet, you hesitate?"

"And yet, I hesitate. Remember the circumstances under which I obtained the information! Harmless as it is, I cannot prevail upon myself to give it up to you unless you first satisfy me that there is a reason for doing so. He was so miserably ill, Mr. Blake! and so helplessly dependent upon me! Is it too much to ask if I request you only to hint to me what your interest is in the lost recollection—or what you believe that lost recollection to be?"

To answer him with the frankness his manner claimed would have been to commit myself to openly acknowledging that I was suspected of the theft of the Diamond. Strongly as Ezra had intensified my interest, he had not overcome my unconquerable reluctance to disclose the degrading position in which I stood. I took refuge once more in the explanatory phrases I had prepared for strangers.

This time, Ezra listened patiently, even anxiously, until I had done.

"I am sorry to have raised your expectations, Mr. Blake, only to disappoint them," he said. "Throughout the whole period of Mr. Candy's illness, not one word about the Diamond escaped his lips. The matter with which I heard him connect your name has, I can assure you, no discoverable relation whatever with the loss or recovery of Miss Verinder's jewel."
