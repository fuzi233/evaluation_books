By six o’clock that evening, the people of Florence breathed a collective sigh of relief. The grand entrance of the new Charlemagne—King Charles VIII of France—had at last come to an end. Earlier, when the thunder of drums, the blare of trumpets, and the steady tramp of horses along the Pisan road began to blend with the frantic ringing of church bells, it had been a moment of awe for those perched atop turreted roofs. From there, they watched the long, imposing procession unfold against the backdrop of green hills and valleys. Though the sky was overcast and no sunlight illuminated the banners, spears, plumes, or silken surcoats, the absence of dust allowed every detail to be seen clearly. The picked troops advanced in disciplined order: tall, wiry Scottish archers; broad-shouldered Swiss halberdiers; agile Gascons ready to shift formation or scale walls; and cavalrymen who looked like knights from legend, each mounted on a powerful steed and armed with a spear that seemed capable of piercing any foe. To the citizens, this display of martial strength was reassuring—it meant these men would harm only the enemies of God.

With that certainty, the spectacle became more than mere pageantry. It was a triumph of power and piety alike. Nobles and knights rode in full regalia, their swords encrusted with jewels, their scarves embroidered with sacred symbols or gallant emblems, their chains and aigrettes gleaming under the dim light. Horses were draped in rich brocade, and above the king himself floated a magnificent canopy carried by chosen youths. As one old chronicler noted, though his language lagged behind the splendour he witnessed—“_fù gran magnificenza_.”

Yet for the Signoria, gathered on their platform near the city gates, the occasion had been less glorious. They had waited anxiously, knowing they must march out at precisely the right moment, led by their orator, to greet the royal guest. But when rain began to fall, it disrupted the ceremony. Messer Luca Corsini, tasked with delivering a formal Latin welcome, found his words obscured by the downpour. The sudden discomfort among men and horses caused the speech to falter, and the dignitaries, unprepared for such chaos, were forced into improvisation. In desperation, Soderini called out, “Someone step forward and say a few words in French!”

No high-ranking official stepped forward, fearing another failure. Then Gaddi, hesitating, pushed Tito Melema forward. “You, Melema—you can speak.”

Tito moved without hesitation. With the natural grace and deep deference that came as easily to him as breathing, he delivered a brief, polished greeting in French on behalf of the Signoria. He then stepped aside, allowing the king to pass. His composure—so absent earlier that morning during the crisis—now served him well. It was a rare quality: the kind of presence of mind that appears only when danger is not immediate, but real.

When praised later, Tito dismissed the moment as trivial, even jokingly attributing the success to Gaddi. That humility won him favor. People often judge others by how well they serve their own pride—and Tito knew how to make them feel generous.

Still, other aspects of the day had gone poorly. The Florentine procession of clergy and laymen had been blocked, forcing them to take a detour through narrow back streets just to reach the Cathedral. And while the young king sat beneath his golden canopy, lance resting on his thigh, he did not look like the noble Charlemagne of legend. Instead, he resembled a hastily carved puppet—his leg thin and deformed, his mouth too wide, his nose and head exaggerated in outline. Yet none of this mattered much to the crowd. The leg rested on cloth of gold and pearls; the face was merely a small flaw amid the blaze of rubies, velvet, and embroidery. The canopy itself was a masterpiece of artistry, and when the people tore it apart—as custom demanded—they roared _Francia, Francia!_ with fervor. The king kissed the altar, and despite the missteps, he and his retinue were lodged in the Palace of the Via Larga. The nobles dispersed among the great houses of Florence, and the army took up positions in the Prato and other open spaces.

The day’s work was done.

But Florence remained alive under the November stars. Where once darkness had settled, broken only by faint lamplight or the glow from open doors, now every window was lit. The streets shimmered with illumination, so bright that walking at night felt as safe and easy as daylight. Again, the chronicler wrote—“_fù gran magnificenza_.”

At about eight o’clock, Tito Melema walked home along the Via dei Benci, passing from the Piazza di Santa Croce toward the Borgo Santa Croce. He had spent the entire day managing his duties while wrestling with private fears. Now, finally free from the noise and obligation of supper parties, he walked in silence, trying to gather his thoughts. He hoped, if he could fully confront his situation, that he might rid himself of the childish dread that still clung to him.

He regretted not recognizing Baldassarre earlier—under that shock of surprise. Had he done so, things might have turned out differently. Still, he felt a pang of guilt. He wished his father could live prosperously, happily. But now there was no turning back. No conflict remained. Only survival. He must protect himself.

As he approached the corner of the Borgo, a sound struck his ear—not music of revelry, but the rhythmic clang of metal on metal. The music of the anvil.

Tito quickened his pace. The sound belonged to Niccolò Caparra, the renowned blacksmith whose workshop was famed throughout Florence for its exquisite ironwork. Why was the giant working so late? Tito wondered. But it was good fortune. He could attend to his business tonight instead of waiting until tomorrow.

Though preoccupied, he paused before the shop, drawn by its grandeur. The wide doorway stood at the truncated angle of a large block of buildings, topped by a loggia with fluted tiles and stone columns bearing roughly carved capitals. Against the red glow within, framed by the architectural lines, stood Niccolò—a towering figure, arms rising and falling in perfect rhythm, hammering the steel. His face, firm-jawed and strong-browed, appeared and disappeared in shadow. Two smaller figures worked beside him—one at the anvil, the other pumping the bellows—highlighting Niccolò’s massive presence.

Tito stood in the doorway, silent. There was no point speaking until the smith noticed him.

It wasn’t until Niccolò finished sharpening the head of an axe and set it aside that he turned, hammer resting on his hip, and gave a gruff but friendly nod. “What is it, Messer Tito? Business?”

“Assuredly,” Tito replied. “Else I wouldn’t dare interrupt you when you’re working beyond hours. I take it as a sign your work is urgent.”

“I’ve been at the same task all day—making axes and spearheads,” Niccolò said. “And every fool who passed by stuck his head in and asked, ‘Niccolò, will you come see the King of France?’ I told them all, ‘No. I don’t want to see their faces—I want to see their backs.’”

“Are you forging arms for the citizens?” Tito asked. “So they may have better weapons than rusty scythes and kitchen spits if trouble breaks out?”

“We shall see,” Niccolò answered. “Arms are good. Florence may need them. The Frate says we’ll reclaim Pisa. I believe him. But how will it happen if we don’t have proper weapons? The Frate sees far ahead—that I grant. But he doesn’t think birds can be caught by blinking at them, as some of our people pretend. He sees reason, not nonsense.”

“You’re a bit of a Medicean yourself, Messer Tito Melema,” Niccolò added. “Ebbene! So was I, once—before the cask began to sour. What do you want?”

“Only to know the price of the fine coat of mail I saw here the other day,” Tito said. “I wish to buy it for someone who needs protection beneath his doublet.”

“Let him come and buy it himself,” Niccolò replied bluntly. “I’m careful whom I sell to. I like to know my customer.”

“I understand your scruples,” Tito said. “But this is defensive armour. It cannot hurt anyone.”

“True,” Niccolò said. “But it may make the man who wears it feel safer—safer to hurt someone else. It’s not my own work—but it’s fine craftsmanship by Maso of Brescia. I wouldn’t want it covering the heart of a villain. I must know who will wear it.”

“Well, then,” Tito said, “I want it myself.” He knew persuasion would fail. “To be honest, I am planning a journey—and you know what journeys mean these days. You don’t suspect me of treason against the Republic?”

“No,” Niccolò said, straightforward as ever. “I know no harm in you. But do you have the money? You’ve passed my shop often enough to see my sign—the burning account-books. I trust no one. Twenty florins. It’s second-hand, but still worth that. You’re unlikely to carry so much coin tonight. Let it wait till tomorrow.”

“I happen to have it,” Tito said. He had won at cards the day before and hadn’t emptied his purse. “I’ll take it now.”

Niccolò reached down and lifted the finely forged coat of mail. It folded into little more than two handfuls. After Tito counted the florins into his palm, Niccolò handed it over.

“There,” he said. “It will guard you from sword, poniard, or arrow. But for my part, I’d never wear such a thing. It’s like carrying fear with you.”

Tito smiled, though the words struck him deeply. “Ah, Niccolò,” he said, “we scholars are all cowards. A pen does not strengthen the arm as your hammer does.”

“Addio,” Niccolò said.

Tito folded the armour under his mantle and hurried across the Ponte Rubaconte, the weight of it pressing against his chest—not just metal, but a garment of fear.
