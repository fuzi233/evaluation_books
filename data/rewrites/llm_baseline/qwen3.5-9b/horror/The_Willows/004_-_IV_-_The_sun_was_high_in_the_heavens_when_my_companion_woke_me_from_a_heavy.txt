The sun hung high and indifferent in the heavens when my companion roused me from a heavy, dreamless sleep. He announced that the porridge was ready and that we had just enough time to bathe before the river claimed us. Immediately, the savory scent of frizzling bacon drifted through the flap of the tent, cutting through the damp air.

"The river is still rising," he said, his voice carrying a strange edge. "Several islands in the mid-stream have vanished entirely. Our own island has shrunk considerably."

"Is there any wood left?" I asked, my voice thick with sleep.

"The wood and the island will finish each other off in a dead heat tomorrow," he laughed, though the sound lacked its usual warmth. "But there's enough to last us until then."

I plunged into the water at the point of the island, which had indeed shifted dramatically in size and shape during the night. I was swept downstream in moments toward the landing place opposite the tent. The water was ice-cold, and the rushing banks blurred past like scenery flying by from an express train. Bathing under such violent conditions was exhilarating; the terror of the previous night seemed to evaporate from my brain, washed away by the shock of the current. Above, the sun blazed with relentless intensity; not a cloud marred the azure sky. Yet, despite the heat, the wind had not diminished—not one jot.

Suddenly, the implication of the Swede's earlier words struck me with force. "Enough to last till tomorrow"—he meant we would remain on the island for another night. It was a startling reversal. The night before, he had been adamant about leaving immediately. How had his resolve shifted so completely?

Breakfast was marked by great crumbling of the banks, accompanied by heavy splashes and clouds of spray driven into our frying pan by the gale. While my fellow traveler talked incessantly about the impossibility of the Vienna-Pesth steamers finding a channel in such a flood, his mental state fascinated and unsettled me far more than the raging river. He had changed since the evening prior. His manner was different—a trifle excited, a trifle shy, laced with a suspicion that colored his voice and gestures. I struggled to articulate it now, in the calm of hindsight, but at the time, I was certain of one thing: he was frightened.

He ate almost nothing and, for the first time, omitted his pipe. Instead, he spread the map open beside him, studying its markings with intense focus.

"We'd better get off sharp in an hour," I said eventually, probing for an opening that might lead to a partial confession. His response puzzled me deeply. "Rather! If they'll let us."

"Who'll let us? The elements?" I asked quickly, masking my anxiety with affected indifference.

"The powers of this awful place, whoever they are," he replied, refusing to lift his gaze from the map. "The gods are here, if they are anywhere at all in the world."

"The elements are always the true immortals," I retorted, forcing a laugh that felt brittle. Yet I knew my face betrayed my true feelings when he looked up gravely, speaking across the smoke.

"We shall be fortunate if we get away without further disaster."

These were precisely the words I had dreaded. I steel-ed myself for the inevitable confrontation. It was like consenting to a tooth extraction; the pain was unavoidable, and the rest was mere pretense.

"Further disaster! What has happened?"

"For one thing—the steering paddle is gone," he said quietly.

"The steering paddle!" I repeated, my excitement spiking. Without a rudder, the Danube in flood was suicide. "But what—"

"And there is a tear in the bottom of the canoe," he added, a genuine tremor shaking his voice.

I stared at him, unable to form coherent speech, merely echoing the horror in his face. There, under the scorching sun on the burning sand, a freezing atmosphere descended around us. He nodded gravely and led the way toward the tent, a few yards away from the fire. The canoe lay there as I had seen her the night before, ribs exposed, with the single paddle resting on the sand beside her.

"There is only one," he said, stooping to retrieve it. "And here is the rent in the base-board."

It was on the tip of my tongue to correct him, noting that I had clearly seen *two* paddles just hours ago, but a second impulse silenced me. I stepped closer to inspect the damage.

There was a long, cleanly made tear in the bottom of the canoe, as if a sharp rock or snag had gnawed through her length. Investigation confirmed the hole went all the way through. Had we launched without noticing, the initial swelling of the wet wood might have sealed it temporarily, but once in mid-stream, the water would have poured in instantly. The canoe, barely two inches above the surface, would have filled and sunk with terrifying speed.

"There," he murmured, more to himself than to me, running his fingers along the slit. "An attempt to prepare a victim for sacrifice. Two victims, rather."

I began to whistle—a reflexive habit when utterly nonplussed—and deliberately chose to ignore his words. I resolved to dismiss them as foolishness.

"It wasn't there last night," he stated, straightening up and avoiding my gaze.

"We must have scratched her during landing," I interjected, stopping my whistle. "The stones are very sharp."

I halted abruptly. He turned then, meeting my eye squarely. We both knew the lie was transparent. There were no stones.

"And then there is this to explain," he added softly, handing me the paddle and pointing to the blade.

A new, chilling emotion spread over me as I examined it. The blade was scraped down uniformly, smoothed with meticulous care, as if sanded by invisible hands until it was so thin that the first vigorous stroke would snap it off at the elbow.

"One of us walked in his sleep and did this," I said weakly. "Or... perhaps filed by the constant stream of sand particles blown against it by the wind."

"Ah," the Swede said, turning away with a slight, mocking laugh. "You can explain everything."

"The same wind that caught the steering paddle and flung it so near the bank that it fell into the crumbling earth," I called after him, desperate to construct a rational narrative.

"I see," he shouted back, turning his head briefly before vanishing into the willow bushes.

Left alone with these perplexing evidences of deliberate malice, my first thought was, "One of us must have done this, and it certainly wasn't me." But my second thought dismissed the possibility entirely. Could my companion, the trusted friend of a dozen expeditions, knowingly sabotage us? Absurd. Was this impervious, practical man suddenly insane? Equally ridiculous.

Yet, the disturbing reality remained: a profound alteration in his mind. He was nervous, timid, suspicious, aware of unseen movements, waiting for a climax he expected imminently. This intuition grew within me, unbidden and undeniable.

I conducted a hurried inspection of the tent and surroundings. The measurements of the night remained unchanged, save for deep, basin-shaped hollows in the sand, ranging from the size of a teacup to a large bowl. The wind, undoubtedly, created these miniature craters, just as it had tossed the paddle. The rent in the canoe remained the sole inexplicable anomaly. Clinging to the theory that a sharp point had caught it during landing, I relied on that diminishing fragment of my intelligence I called "reason." An explanation was an absolute necessity, a psychological requirement for facing the horrors of existence.

I melted the pitch, and shortly the Swede joined me. Under ideal conditions, the canoe could not travel safely until the following day. I casually drew his attention to the hollows in the sand.

"Yes," he replied. "They are all over the island. But *you* can explain them, no doubt!"

"Wind, of course," I answered without hesitation. "Have you never watched those little whirlwinds in the street that twist debris into circles? This sand is loose enough to yield."

He offered no reply. We worked in silence for a while. I watched him surreptitiously, sensing he was watching me in return. He appeared constantly attentive to sounds I could not hear, or perhaps to something he awaited. He turned frequently, staring into the bushes, up at the sky, and out across the water visible through the willow gaps. Occasionally, he pressed his hand to his ear, holding it there for minutes at a stretch. He said nothing, and I asked no questions. Meanwhile, as he mended the torn canoe with the skill of a red Indian, I was relieved by his absorption in the task, fearing he might speak of the changing aspect of the willows. If he had noticed *that*, my imagination would no longer suffice to explain the situation.

After a long pause, he began to speak.

"Queer thing," he said in a hurried tone, eager to get it over. "Queer thing. About that otter last night."

I braced myself for something entirely different, catching him off guard. I looked up sharply.

"Shows how lonely this place is. Otters are awfully shy—"

"I don't mean that," he interrupted. "I mean—do you think... did you think it really was an otter?"

"What else, in the name of Heaven, what else?"

"You know, I saw it before you did, and at first it seemed—so *much* bigger than an otter."
