In the cool, blue twilight of two steep streets in Camden Town, a confectioner's shop at the corner glowed with a chaotic, multi-colored brilliance. It was less like a cigar butt and more like a dying firework, its light fractured by mirrors and dancing upon piles of gilt-wrapped sweets and gaily decorated cakes. Against this fiery glass, the noses of many "gutter-snipes" were pressed, drawn by chocolates wrapped in metallic hues of red, gold, and green—colors deemed superior to chocolate itself. Dominating the display was a massive white wedding-cake, a spectacle that seemed simultaneously remote and satisfying, evoking the impossible allure of a edible North Pole.

While such rainbow provocations naturally drew the local youth aged ten or twelve, this corner held a specific magnetism for older boys as well. Standing before the window was a young man of twenty-four, tall, burly, and red-haired. Though his face was resolute, his manner was listless. Under his arm, he carried a flat, grey portfolio containing black-and-white sketches he had sold to various publishers with mixed success. Since his uncle—an Admiral—had disinherited him following a lecture against socialism, his financial prospects had been limited, though he was far from despising the confectioner's wares.

His name was John Turnbull Angus.

Finally entering the shop, Angus walked through to the back room, a sort of pastry-cook restaurant, raising his hat only slightly to the young lady serving there. She was a dark, elegant, and alert girl dressed in black, with a high complexion and very quick, dark eyes. After a customary interval, she followed him into the inner room to take his order.

His request was precise and usual. "One halfpenny bun and a small cup of black coffee," he stated. Before the girl could turn away, he added, "Also, I want you to marry me."

The young lady stiffened instantly. "Those are jokes I don't allow."

Angus lifted his grey eyes, which held an unexpected gravity. "Really and truly," he said, "it's as serious—as serious as the halfpenny bun. It is expensive, like the bun; one pays for it. It is indigestible, like the bun. It hurts."

The dark-eyed girl studied him with almost tragic exactitude. By the end of her scrutiny, a shadow of a smile had appeared on her lips, and she sat down.

"Don't you think," observed Angus, absently, "that it's rather cruel to eat these halfpenny buns? They might grow up into penny buns. I shall give up these brutal sports when we are married."

The girl rose and walked to the window, lost in strong but not unsympathetic cogitation. When she swung back with an air of resolution, she was bewildered to see Angus carefully arranging various objects from the shop window on the table: a pyramid of highly colored sweets, several plates of sandwiches, and two decanters containing the mysterious port and sherry peculiar to pastry cooks. In the center of this neat arrangement, he had placed the enormous, sugared wedding-cake that had been the window's ornament.

"What on earth are you doing?" she asked.

"Duty, my dear Laura," he began.

"Oh, for the Lord's sake, stop a minute," she cried, "and don't talk to me in that way. I mean, what is all that?"

"A ceremonial meal, Miss Hope."

"And what is that?" she asked impatiently, pointing to the mountain of sugar.

"The wedding-cake, Mrs. Angus," he said.

Laura marched to the article, removed it with a clatter, and put it back in the window. Returning, she rested her elegant elbows on the table, regarding Angus not unfavorably but with considerable exasperation.

"You don't give me any time to think," she said.

"I'm not such a fool," he answered, "that's my Christian humility."

She grew considerably graver behind the smile. "Mr. Angus," she said steadily, "before there is a minute more of this nonsense, I must tell you something about myself as shortly as I can."

"Delighted," replied Angus gravely. "You might tell me something about myself, too, while you are about it."

"Oh, do hold your tongue and listen," she said. "It's nothing I'm ashamed of, and it isn't even anything I'm specially sorry about. But what would you say if there were something that is no business of mine and yet is my nightmare?"

"In that case," said Angus seriously, "I should suggest that you bring back the cake."

"Well, you must listen to the story first," persisted Laura. "To begin with, my father owned the inn called the 'Red Fish' at Ludbury, and I used to serve people in the bar."

"I have often wondered," Angus said, "why there was a kind of Christian air about this one confectioner's shop."

"Ludbury is a sleepy, grassy little hole in the Eastern Counties," Laura continued. "The only people who ever came to the 'Red Fish' were occasional commercial travelers, and for the rest, the most awful people you can imagine—you've never seen them. I mean little, loungy men who had just enough to live on and nothing to do but lean about in bar-rooms betting on horses, dressed in bad clothes that were just too good for them. Even these wretched young rotters were not very common at our house; but there were two of them that were a lot too common—in every sort of way. They both lived on money of their own and were wearisomely idle and over-dressed. Yet, I was a bit sorry for them, because I half-believe they slunk into our little empty bar because each of them had a slight deformity—the sort of thing some yokels laugh at. It wasn't exactly a deformity either; it was more an oddity.

"One of them was a surprisingly small man, something like a dwarf, or at least like a jockey. He was not at all jockeyish to look at; he had a round black head and a well-trimmed black beard, bright eyes like a bird's. He jingled money in his pockets and jangled a great gold watch chain, never turning up except dressed just too much like a gentleman to be one. He was no fool, though a futile idler; he was curiously clever at all kinds of things that couldn't be the slightest use—a sort of impromptu conjuring. Making fifteen matches set fire to each other like a regular firework, or cutting a banana into a dancing doll. His name was Isidore Smythe, and I can see him still, with his little dark face, just coming up to the counter, making a jumping kangaroo out of five cigars.

"The other fellow was more silent and more ordinary; but somehow, he alarmed me much more than poor little Smythe. He was very tall and slight, with light hair. His nose had a high bridge, and he might almost have been handsome in a spectral sort of way; but he had one of the most appalling squints I have ever seen. When he looked straight at you, you didn't know where you were yourself, let alone what he was looking at. I fancy this sort of disfigurement embittered the poor chap a little. While Smythe was ready to show off his monkey tricks anywhere, James Welkin—that was the squinting man's name—never did anything except soak in our bar parlour and go for great walks by himself in the flat, grey country all round. All the same, I think Smythe was a little sensitive about being so small, though he carried it off more smartly. And so it was that I was really puzzled, as well as startled, and very sorry, when they both offered to marry me in the same week.

"Well, I did what I've since thought was perhaps a silly thing. But after all, these freaks were my friends in a way; and I had a horror of their thinking I refused them for the real reason, which was that they were so impossibly ugly. So I made up some gas of another sort, about never meaning to marry anyone who hadn't carved his way in the world. I said it was a point of principle with me not to live on money that was just inherited like theirs. Two days after I had talked in this well-meaning sort of way, the whole trouble began. The first thing I heard was that both of them had gone off to seek their fortunes, as if they were in some silly fairy tale.

"Well, I've never seen either of them from that day to this. But I've had two letters from the little man called Smythe, and really they were rather exciting."

"Ever heard of the other man?" Angus asked.

"No, he never wrote," Laura said, after an instant's hesitation. "Smythe's first letter was simply to say that he had started out walking with Welkin to London; but Welkin was such a good walker that the little man dropped out of it, and took a rest by the roadside. He happened to be picked up by some travelling show, and, partly because he was nearly a dwarf, and partly because he was really a clever little wretch, he got on quite well in the show business, and was soon sent up to the Aquarium to do some tricks that I forget. That was his first letter. His second was much more of a startler, and I only got it last week."
