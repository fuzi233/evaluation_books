Colonel Lambert, no doubt, had conferred with his wife, and together they resolved to remove their daughter Hetty from the vicinity of the young man who had stirred such tender feelings in her heart. In matters of affection, absence often proves a powerful remedy—especially for men. But women, I suspect, are not so easily cured by mere distance. Some will journey far and remain apart for years, yet still carry their secret sorrows like hidden burdens. You may scold them, punish them, even wound them deeply—but still they cling to their devotion. Indeed, if I may speak from long observation, there is perhaps no surer way to secure a woman’s loyalty than through measured neglect, tempered now and then with a gentle kindness. Too much love, too freely given, breeds indifference. A woman spoiled by constant attention may grow bored with her husband, seeking solace in the company of the most ordinary man. Yet, witness Chloe—abused at breakfast, cursed before the servants at dinner—how she creeps back to her husband's side at tea, softening into affection, patting his head, playing his favorite tune. When the butler or maid enters with the bed-candles, she looks up proudly, as if to say: “See, John, how good my dear Henry is!” Gentlemen, take note: this is the art of mastery—not through indulgence, but through restraint.

I recall what Daniel O’Connell once said: “Hereditary bondsmen, know ye not that to be free, you must strike the blow?” So it is in marriage, as in politics. Rise up, then, you wronged and oppressed husbands! Women will appreciate these words, for they understand irony and humor. And I expect some young scribbler from Grub Street—whose only claim to fame is writing for three-penny papers—will cry, “He advocates beating women! He has no soul!” But I have no more soul than he has ears. Dear ladies, let me assure you: I am merely jesting. I do not endorse violence toward any woman. On the contrary, I hold your sex in higher regard than our own—for your constancy, your tenderness, your enduring fidelity.

So what purpose does it serve for Hetty’s parents to take her away? If her heart remains fixed on Harry, whether he is near or far, then distance alone will not heal her. Why not let her see him one last time before Ball and Dobbin depart? Let her say, “Good-bye, Harry—I was unkind last night, but you were kind to me.” She will show no great emotion. Ashamed of her secret, she will hide it behind a smile. Harry, preoccupied with his own thoughts, will not perceive her sorrow. He will part from her calmly, believing she is glad to return to her music, her chickens, her flower garden. He will not ride beside her carriage. He has another engagement. By the time he returns, the Lamberts are already gone from Tunbridge Wells. Their windows stand open; a card hangs in one, announcing the rooms are available again.

A fleeting pang may cross Harry’s mind when he sees the empty chambers where friendly faces once laughed and talked. But he dines at the White Horse at four o’clock, eats heartily, and demands his wine with gusto. Meanwhile, poor little Hester chokes on her tea when the family arrives at Westerham. The next morning, her cheeks are pale, dark circles shadow her eyes. It was the storm, she says—the night was hot, sleepless. She will feel better tomorrow. And tomorrow comes. They return home. There is the gate where he fell. There is the bed where he lay, the chair where he sat. How long it seems since yesterday! What a gulf between past and present! Who is that child tending chickens, watering roses? Is that girl truly Hester Lambert? She seems older than Theo, who has always been so composed, so wise beyond her years. But in just a few days, Hester has lived an eternity. So have others. No poppy nor mandragora can restore them to the peace they knew before.

Maria Esmond watched the Lamberts leave, feeling a strange relief. When Harry enters his aunt’s card room, flushed from Barbeau’s wine, she watches him closely. He laughs, answers lightly when asked which girl is his sweetheart. “I love them both like sisters,” he says cheerfully. “The Lamberts are excellent people. Why isn’t Lambert a general? He’s served with distinction. The Duke is fond of him.” Madame Bernstein suggests Harry should speak to Lady Yarmouth on his behalf. “Elle ravvole de fous, cher bedid anche!” she mimics the Countess’s German accent. The Baroness beams with pride at her nephew’s success. “You charm all the old ladies, don’t you, Maria?” she sneers at her niece, who flinches beneath the barb. “You were quite right not to notice her cheating at cards—you play like a grand seigneur,” continues Madame de Bernstein. “Did she cheat?” Harry asks, surprised. “I saw no unfair play,” he insists. “Neither did I,” replies the Baroness, “but I’m certain she did. Bah! Every woman cheats when she can. Even I, and Maria, when opportunity allows. But when playing with Walmoden, losing moderately is acceptable. Many men do it too. Cultivate her—she’s taken a fancy to your eyes. Why shouldn’t you become Governor of Virginia? Go pay your respects to the Duke and the King at Kensington. Lady Yarmouth will be your greatest ally.”

“Why don’t you introduce me, Aunt?” Harry asks. The old lady’s rouged cheek reddens further. “I am not in favor at Kensington,” she admits. “Once I might have been. But kings remember those they wish to forget. We all have something we’d rather erase. Perhaps our young gentleman here would like to wipe a few debts from his ledger. Would you not, Harry?”

Harry turns red. So does Maria. His aunt lets out a laugh—sharp, mischievous, unsettling. What secrets lie behind those crimson cheeks? What sins haunt their memories that they long to forget? I fear Madame Bernstein is right: most of us carry hidden reckonings we'd gladly escape.

Had Maria known the reason for Harry’s unease, she would have been even more distressed. For several days, he had been missing a pocket-book. He remembered having it the night he drank heavily at the White Horse, when Gumbo carried him to bed. He searched in the morning—no trace. He asked his servants, the innkeeper—nothing. He could not admit the loss. To reveal it would mean exposing letters he had written to her—letters full of confessions, satire, jealousy, declarations of love. Letters that spoke of her faults, her opinions, her private thoughts. Letters that contained family secrets, criticisms of friends, bitter complaints about other women, passionate protests of eternal devotion. The usual mix, dear madam, that every lover writes to their beloved—especially in youth. Would you want such letters read aloud? Recall what you wrote about the Miss Browns, or Mrs. Thompson’s character? Or your remarks about Edward, whom you later abandoned for Jones? Would you want Mr. Jones to see those words? Of course not. Then do not accuse Lady Maria Esmond without cause. Her letters may have been foolish—love letters often are—but that does not make them wicked. Such things are natural. Young hearts write wildly. Old ones write with regret. Still, no one wants their innermost thoughts exposed.

And so, I resolve never again to write or speak plainly about anyone. I shall say of every woman that she is virtuous and beautiful; of every man, that he is noble and clever; of every book, that it is delightful; of Snobmore, that he is gentlemanly; of Screwby’s dinners, that they are sumptuous; of Jawkins’s conversation, that it is sparkling; of Xantippe, that she is sweet-tempered; of Jezebel, that her color is natural; of Bluebeard, that he was kind to his wives—and likely they died of bronchitis. As for Messalina? Spotless, of course! Cheops? A perfect king! Oh, woe to the critic who dares question nobility!

When this tale ends, I shall discard the jaundiced tone of my earlier works. My books shall wear rose-colored covers, adorned with cherubs. All characters shall be angels. But until then, we live among mortals—flawed, human beings. Take Madame Bernstein: she sleeps after overeating and drinking too much. That is her failing. Mr. Harry Warrington plays billiards with Count Caramboli—his flaw, perhaps, is idleness. This is what Chaplain Sampson murmurs to Lady Maria, speaking softly so as not to wake the sleeping Baroness.

“A gentleman of Mr. Warrington’s means can afford leisure,” says Maria. “But surely you enjoy cards and billiards yourself, Mr. Sampson?”
“I do not claim my practice is good,” he sighs, “only my doctrine is sound. This young man should have purpose. He should appear at court, serve his country, settle down, and marry a woman of suitable rank.”

Sampson gazes steadily at her. “Indeed, my cousin wastes his time,” Maria murmurs, blushing.
“Perhaps he should visit his father’s relatives,” suggests the chaplain.
“Suffolk yokels drinking beer and hunting foxes! What benefit would come of that?”
“They are of ancient lineage,” replies Sampson. “Sir Miles Warrington has a daughter—her age, and lovely.”
“I know nothing of Sir Miles or his daughters!” Maria exclaims, flustered.

The Baroness stirs—no, she sleeps soundly. “I fear for your cousin, madam,” says Sampson. “His youth, his temptations. Folly, extravagance, intrigue—many will try to lead him astray. His lordship, my patron, sent me to watch over him. I know the ways of young men. I confess—perhaps I once walked the same path.” He adds this with solemnity, though no blush appears.

“Between ourselves,” he continues, “I fear Mr. Warrington is troubled.”
“What? Again?” shrieks Maria.
“Hush! Your ladyship’s invalid!” whispers Sampson, pointing to the sleeping Baroness.
“Do you think he has feelings for anyone—anyone in the Lambert family? For example, Miss Lambert?”
“There is nothing between them,” says Maria.
“Are you certain?”
“Women have keen eyes in such matters,” she replies coolly. “I thought she followed him.”
“Then I am mistaken again,” says the chaplain. “Mr. Warrington called her a pert, stuck-up little hussy and told her to go back to her doll.”
“Oh,” sighs Maria, relieved.
“Then there must be someone else,” says Sampson. “Has he confided anything to you?”
“To me? Where? How?” she cries.
“Six days ago, after dining at the White Horse, he lost a pocket-book containing letters.”
“Letters?” gasps Maria.
“And likely more money than he wishes to admit,” adds Sampson gravely. “He is greatly disturbed. We’ve made quiet inquiries—”
“Gracious powers! Is your ladyship ill?”

Maria gives three piercing screams and collapses from her chair.
“I will see the Prince! I have a right to see him! What is this? Where am I?” cries Madame Bernstein, waking abruptly. She shakes violently, her face flushed. She stares wildly, then staggers forward with her cane. “What—what’s happened?”
“Have you killed her, sir?”
“Only a sudden faintness,” says Sampson, alarmed. “Shall I loosen her laces? Send for a doctor?”
“What passed between you, sir?” demands the Baroness fiercely.
“I swear I did nothing. I only mentioned the lost pocket-book—then she fainted.”

Madame Bernstein splashes water on Maria’s face. A weak moan follows—she is regaining consciousness. The Baroness glares at Sampson as he departs for the doctor. Maria awakens, trembling, confused.
“What happened?” she whispers.
“You know best,” says the Baroness coldly. “What has occurred before in our family?”
“Oh, yes—the letters have been lost—ach lieber Himmel!” Maria begins speaking in her mother’s tongue.
“The seal is broken, the letters lost—it is the old story of the Esmonds,” cries the elder woman bitterly.
“Seal broken? Letters lost? What do you mean, Aunt?”
“I mean my mother was the only honest woman ever to enter this family!” she stamps her foot. “She was a parson’s daughter—no connections. Had she been born to wealth, she too would have fallen. Good heavens—must we all be...?”
“To be what, madam?”
“To be what Lady Queensberry said last night. To be what we are! You know the word!”
“What has come over our race? Your father’s mother was honest. Why did I leave her? Why couldn’t you stay so?”
“Madam!” cries Maria. “I swear before Heaven, I am—”
“Bah! Don’t ‘madam’ me! Don’t call heaven to witness—there’s no one here! And if you swore until your teeth fell out, I wouldn’t believe you!”
“Oh! You told him!” gasps Maria.
“I saw something foolish between you two,” says the Baroness. “I told him you were as old as his mother. Yes, I did. I won’t let Henry Esmond’s son waste himself on a worn-out rock like you. Not a penny of mine will go to any of you if he suffers.”
“You told him!” Maria cries, rising in defiance.
“Well then! I don’t care a penny for your petty money! I have Mr. Harry Warrington’s word—and his letters. I know he’ll die before breaking it.”
“He will die if he keeps it!” (Maria shrugs.)
“But you don’t care—your heart is stone.”
“Than my father’s sister!” cries Maria.
“Ah! Why didn I marry an honest man?” the Baroness laments. “Henry Esmond was noble. Perhaps he could have made me so. But no—we all carry the stain. You won’t sacrifice this boy, Maria?”
“Madame ma tante, do you take me for a fool?”
“Set him free! I’ll give you five thousand pounds—in my will, Maria. I swear it!”
“When you were young, you cast off Colonel Esmond for an earl, and the earl for a duke?”
“Yes.”
“Eh! Bon sang ne peut mentir! I have no money, no friends. My father was a spendthrift, my brother a beggar. I have Mr. Warrington’s word. I know he’ll keep it. And that is what I tell you!”
“Suppose my letters are published tomorrow? Apres? I know they contain things I’d rather not reveal. Things not only about me. Comment! Do you think there are no stories but mine in this family? It’s not my letters I fear—so long as I have his. Yes, his word and his letters. I trust both.”
“I’ll send to my merchant—give you the money now,” pleads the old lady.
“No,” says Maria. “I’ll have my Harry—and ten times five thousand pounds!”
“Not until your mother dies—she’s just your age!”
“We can wait, Aunt. At my age, I’m not so eager as young girls for a husband.”
“But waiting for your sister’s death—is that fair?”
“Offer me ten thousand pounds, Madam Tusher, and then we’ll see!”
“I have no such sum,” says the old lady.
“Then let me earn what I can for myself!”
“Ah, if he heard you!”
“Apres? I have his word. I know he’ll keep it. I can wait, madam,” she says, and strides from the room—just as the chaplain returns.

Now it was Madame Bernstein who needed cordials. She was profoundly shaken by the revelation that had erupted so suddenly.
