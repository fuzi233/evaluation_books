Fanny, trembling with a chill that went deeper than the damp air of the passage, saw the two ladies approaching. The elder, whose countenance wore a mask of inscrutable alarm, fixed her gaze upon the poor girl with a look that promised no solace. Instantly, Fanny knew the truth: Pen's mother was there. A terrible resemblance haunted the widow's haggard eyes, mirroring the fever-dream visage of Arthur as he tossed in his bed. Beside her stood Laura, a statue carved from ice and malice; her face held no flicker of mercy, only a cold, stony hardness. Hard-heartedness and gloom clung to both women like a heavy shroud. Fanny cast a desperate glance toward the Major behind them. Old Pendennis, usually so steady, lowered his lids, peering up from beneath them with a stealthy, worried look at the little nurse who had done so much for his son.

"I—I wrote to you yesterday, if you please, ma'am," Fanny stammered, her voice cracking as every limb shook with fear. "I'm pale as Laura," she whispered to herself, noting the menacing shadow on the younger woman's face looming over the older one.

"Did you?" Mrs. Pendennis replied, her tone sharp and cutting. "I suppose I may now relieve you from nursing my son. I am his mother, you understand."

"Yes, ma'am. I—" Fanny faltered, then suddenly cried out, "Wait a minute! I must prepare you for his..."

The widow, whose features had been etched into something hopelessly cruel and ruthless, recoiled with a startled gasp and a stifled cry.

"He's been so since yesterday," Fanny murmured, her teeth chattering violently.

From within the open door of Pen's room, a horrid shriek of laughter erupted. After several frantic shouts, the poor wretch began to sing a college drinking-song, then hurrahed and shouted as if surrounded by a riotous wine party, thumping his fist against the wainscot. He was utterly delirious.

"He does not know me, ma'am!" Fanny sobbed.

"Indeed. Perhaps he will know his mother," the widow said, pushing past the weeping girl with hurried steps. "Let me pass, and go in to him." Laura sailed by without a word, and the Major followed them into the dark passage leading to the sick chamber. Left alone, Fanny sank onto a bench, crying and praying as best she could. She would have died for him, yet they hated her. These fine ladies offered not a word of thanks or kindness. She sat there in the gloom, unsure of the passing time, until Doctor Goodenough arrived for his second visit.

He found the little maid at the door. "What, nurse? How's your patient?" the good-natured doctor asked gently. "Has he had any rest?"

"Go and ask them. They're inside," Fanny replied, pointing toward the door where the widowed mother and Laura sat.

"Who? His mother?" the doctor queried, his brow furrowing slightly. Fanny nodded silently, unable to speak.

"You must go to bed yourself, my poor little maid," the doctor advised, his voice softening. "You will fall ill if you don't."

"Oh, mayn't I come and see him? Mayn't I come and see him! I—I love him so," the little girl pleaded, falling to her knees and clutching the doctor's hand with an agony that melted his heart and blurred his spectacles.

"Pooh, pooh! Nonsense! Nurse, has he taken his draught? Of course you must come and see him. So must I."

"They'll let me sit here, won't they, sir? I'll never make no noise. I only ask to stop here," Fanny insisted. The doctor, chuckling at her earnest simplicity, called her a "stupid little thing," tapped her pale cheek with his finger, and bustled into the farthest room, leaving her to wait.

Inside, Mrs. Pendennis sat pale and solemn in a great chair by the bedside. Her watch lay on the table beside Pen's medicines, her bonnet and cloak draped in the window. Her Bible rested in her lap, a constant companion. Upon entering, her first act had been to retrieve Fanny's shawl and bonnet from the drawers and discard them onto the study table, effectively closing the door on the Major and Laura to claim possession of her son.

Her greatest terror—that Arthur would not recognize her—was partially assuaged. Pen knew his mother intimately, smiling and nodding familiarly. As soon as she entered, his mind, wandering in fever, snapped back to the comfort of home at Fairoaks. He talked and chattered and laughed wildly, unaware of the poison his laughter shot into Laura's heart. It was true, then: guilt, an intrigue with a servant-maid, a betrayal of love, and now, a dying raving unrepentant. The Major occasionally hummed a word of consolation, but Laura barely heard him.

It was a dismal sitting for all parties. When Doctor Goodenough finally appeared, he walked in like an angel, bringing a light that banished the shadows. For the sick man, yes, but also for the friends surrounding him, his presence was a balm. How we watch for him! How the thrill of his carriage wheels makes our hearts leap! We hang on his words, seeking a smile to lighten our darkness. Who has not seen the mother pry into his face, searching the eyes of the silent infant battling fever? Ah, how she looks! What thanks if there is hope; what grief if he casts his eyes down, fearing to utter the word. Whether it be the father or the son, the physician stands as Fate, the dispenser of life and death, bearing the crushing weight of responsibility. To let a patient live when he should die, or vice versa, is a cruelty that haunts the conscience. Yet, amidst this gravity, there arose a strange contrast.

After a hasty introduction to the new arrivals, the doctor examined the patient. High fever demanded immediate action; antiphlogistic remedies were necessary. He consoled the mother with comforting assurances of youth and strong constitution, doing his utmost to calm her alarms before stepping aside into the vacant room (formerly Warrington's) for a private consultation with the Major.

"The case is critical," the doctor noted seriously. "If not stopped, the fever will carry him off. He must be bled forthwith. You must inform her of this necessity." He paused, his expression shifting to one of mild amusement. "Why was that other young lady brought with her? She is out of place in a sick-room."

"And there was another woman still, be hanged to it!" the Major interjected, his voice rising. "The—the little person who opened the door! My sister-in-law brought out the poor little devil's bonnet and shawl and flung them upon the study table. Did Goodenough know anything about the—the little person?" The Major leaned forward, his eyes twinkling despite the grim situation. "I just caught a glimpse of her as we passed in, and begad, she was uncommonly nice-looking."

The Doctor looked queer, then smiled—a grave, satirical smile that somehow made the gloom darker, mocking the seriousness of life and death.

"I have it," the doctor said eventually, re-entering the study. He wrote two notes hastily at the table, sealing one. Taking up Fanny's discarded shawl and bonnet along with the notes, he stepped back into the passage. "Quick, nurse," he commanded, addressing the little figure waiting outside. "You must carry this to the surgeon and bid him come instantly. Then go to my house in Hanover Square, ask for my servant Harbottle, tell him to compound this prescription immediately, and wait until I return. It may take a little time."

Poor Fanny trudged away with her two notes, finding the apothecary in the Strand, who came straightaway with his lancet, and then hastened to the doctor's house. While the prescription was being compounded by Harbottle, who took a long time, the doctor returned home before it was finished. During the remainder of Arthur's illness, Fanny never again appeared in the chambers as his nurse. But for those two days, a little figure might be seen lurking about Pen's staircase, a sad, sad face interrogating the apothecary, his boy, the laundress, and the kind physician as they passed.

On the third day, the doctor's carriage stopped at Shepherd's Inn. The good, honest, benevolent man entered the porter's lodge and tended to a little patient there. It was on this day, when he could finally tell Fanny Bolton that the crisis was over and hope remained for Arthur Pendennis, that the tide turned.

J. Costigan, Esquire, late of Her Majesty's service, observed the carriage and critiqued its horses. "Green liveries, bedad!" the General remarked, admiring the high-stepping bees. "There's no end to the pride and arrogance of doctors nowadays—not but that is a good one, a scientific character, and a right good fellow, bedad; and he's brought the poor little girl well through her fever." Mr. Costigan was so pleased with the doctor's behavior that whenever he met Dr. Goodenough thereafter, he saluted him with the courtesy due a Lord Lieutenant.

The widow's gratitude knew no bounds—or perhaps scarcely any.
