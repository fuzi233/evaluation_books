No tongue I have can compass the night that followed our sealing in that stone belly of the mountain. Weariness, like a blunt mercy, did press our eyelids down at whiles; yet for me sleep came in thin scraps, shying off as a wild thing shies from fire.

It was not only the thought of what waited us—thirst first, then the slow failing of the heart, then that last indignity of the body in darkness. It was the silence. Not the common quiet a man meets in a veld at midnight, where the grass whispers to itself and some far jackal threads his thin cry through the stars; not even the hush of a snowfield, where one’s own breath seems loud. This was silence made solid—so complete it had weight, so perfect it seemed to press on the ears like a hand.

We lay under a mountain’s white cap, thousands upon thousands of feet of rock and frozen air above us; and though the dawn might be kindling up there on the snow-wreaths, though a breeze might be combing the ridges into streaming banners, not the faintest syllable of it reached our living grave. A long passage and yards of stone shut us off even from the great chamber where the dead kings sat—dead folk who make no sound. We knew it, for we lay near poor Foulata, and death had already taken her voice.

In that oppressive stillness my mind, like a tongue worrying a sore tooth, kept returning to the same bitter jest: that about us, stacked in chests, lay wealth enough to load a nation’s coffers and buy ships and guns and whole cities’ worth of noise—yet we would have given it all, every glittering stone, for one rude mouthful of water from a gourd, for one crust, for one chance—only a chance—of crawling back to daylight. Soon, I thought, we should have been glad to exchange it for a swallow; later still, even for the favour of dying quickly. There is no jewel that can be chewed, no diamond that melts on the tongue.

At last Sir Henry’s voice came, low but startling in that stillness, as if speech were a blasphemy there.

“Good,” he said, “how many matches have you?”

“Eight, Curtis.”

“Strike one. Let us see the time.”

Good fumbled, the sulphur rasped, and the sudden flare near blinded us after so long a blackness. It was five o’clock by my watch. Somewhere far above our heads, in a world of air and colour, morning must be lifting her lids.

“We had better eat,” I said, for I have ever held that a man must keep his strength like a miser keeps coin, spending it slowly. “If we can swallow, we can think.”

“What’s the use of eating?” Good answered, his voice harsh and raw, as if his throat were lined with hot sand. “The sooner it’s done, the better.”

“While there is life there is hope,” said Sir Henry; and he spoke as he always did, quietly, as though hope were a thing you might lay a hand upon and hold steady.

So we chewed a little biltong—dry as old hide, yet how precious, how full of remembered smoke and salt when one’s mouth is parched—and we sipped our water, each swallow small, measured, as a careful cook measures spice when there is little left in the jar. Then time thickened again, slow and tasteless.

Presently Sir Henry bade us creep down the passage toward the stone door and hail, on the foolish chance that some sound might leak through. Good, with a sailor’s lungs, went, groping with one hand, and set up such yells as might have raised a mutiny on a deck. In that dead air they seemed to be devoured the moment they were born; for all the answer they got, he might as well have been a mosquito.

He came back thirstier for the effort and drank, and we ceased that folly, because even shouting spends water—if not from the skin, then from the throat.

Then came the worst part: the waiting. Our backs against chests of stones that could not buy us one draught, our hands empty, our mouths growing furred, our thoughts circling like trapped birds. In that helplessness my courage broke clean. I laid my head against Sir Henry’s shoulder—broad as a shield, warm with life—and I wept like a child, the tears burning as they ran. I heard Good on the other side gulping and cursing himself under his breath for it.

Sir Henry—God bless the man—forgot his own misery to nurse ours. He spoke softly, telling of men buried in collapsed workings, of sailors shut in holds, of miners who had gnawed leather and lived, of miracles that come like a loaf slid under a door. When those stories failed, he tried another kind of comfort: that death comes to all, that we were but taking the road a little sooner, that exhaustion is merciful (which it is not, for thirst has sharp teeth). And then, in that diffident way of his, as though asking pardon for speaking of such things, he said we should commend ourselves to a higher Power. I did so with all the force I had left, for when a man’s mouth is dry and the dark is thick, prayer seems less like a pious habit and more like a handhold.

Thus the hours wore, though in that blackness “day” and “night” were only names. When I struck another match it showed seven o’clock. We ate and drank again—each morsel chewed until it was almost memory, each drop made to last on the tongue—and as I swallowed, a small, sensible thought rose up in me like a spark in ash.

“How is it,” I said, “that the air stays fresh here? It is heavy, but it is not foul.”

Good started as if stung.

“Great heavens!” he cried. “Of course. If there were no air moving, we’d have been poisoned when first we came. It can’t come through that door—air-tight as a drum. It must come from somewhere. Come—let’s feel for it.”

You could have fed us on that mere notion of a draught. It was as if, after gnawing on leather, a man suddenly smells broth.

Down we went on hands and knees, crawling among tusks and chests and carved stone, pressing our cheeks and palms to cracks, searching for the faintest coolness. Once my hand touched something cold and smooth and I snatched it back with a shudder; it was Foulata’s face. For an hour we hunted, bruising heads on ivory and rock, until Sir Henry and I, sore and dulled, were ready to collapse. But Good kept at it with a stubborn kind of cheer.

“Better than sitting,” he muttered.

Presently he called us, his voice tight.

“Here! Come here.”

We scrambled to him.

“Quatermain—put your hand where mine is. Feel anything?”

I held my breath, laid my fingers down, and felt—yes—there was the faintest tickle of air, as though the mountain itself were drawing breath.

“I think—yes—I do,” I whispered.

“Now listen.”

He rose and stamped. The sound came back wrong—hollow, as if the stone were a lid.

With hands that shook, I struck a match—three left now, and each one a small life in itself. The flame showed we were in the far corner, tucked into an angle where sound and sight had cheated us before. There, cut clean in the rock floor, ran a seam; and set level with the stone was a ring—stone also, not iron—waiting like a handle on a buried pot.

We said nothing. Our hearts did all the speaking.

Good drew his knife—the back of it had a little hooked tool for picking stones from a horse’s hoof—and he scraped around the ring until he could lever it up. It moved obediently, as stone will after centuries if it has not rusted. He tugged. Nothing.

I tried, straining till my wrists hurt. Nothing.

Sir Henry tried, and though his shoulders bunched like a bull’s, the slab did not give.

Good scraped again around the seam where the air came, then wiped his brow with a hand that shook less now, because hope was a steadier thing than fear.

“Now, Curtis,” he said, “you’re as strong as two. Put your back into it. Stop.”

He took off his black silk handkerchief—neat to the last, even in a tomb—and ran it through the ring to make a hold.

“Quatermain, get round him. When I say, pull as if the devil’s behind you. Now.”

Sir Henry heaved. Good and I hauled with him, my arms locked about Sir Henry’s middle, my feet scraping for purchase on rock dust.

“Heave!” gasped Sir Henry. “It’s giving—”

I heard, horribly, the crack and creak of muscle in his back as if a rope were fraying. Then came a grating, a sudden rush of cold air up at our faces, and we all went over backwards as the flag-stone came free and toppled.

“Light a match,” Sir Henry said when we had scrambled clear, panting. “Careful.”

I did. And there, blessed as bread, was the first step of a stone stair dropping down into blackness.

“What now?” Good asked.

“Follow it,” said Sir Henry. “And trust Providence.”

“Stop,” he added, practical even in ecstasy. “Quatermain—fetch what biltong and water are left. We may need them.”

I crept back among the chests to our place. The jewels lay open-mouthed, winking faintly when my matchlight reached them, like cold fish-eyes. For twenty-eight hours those stones had meant nothing but mockery. Yet habit is a hard master, and I thought, If we do get out, it will be a strange thing to have come this far and carry nothing but hunger.

So I plunged my fist into the nearest chest and filled every pocket I had, not daintily but by handfuls, as one might cram meal into a sack before a drought. Then, remembering the third chest, I topped up with a few of the biggest—heavy, blunt lumps that sat in the palm like ice. By an afterthought I stuffed Foulata’s basket—now almost empty save for a gourd and a little biltong—with diamonds until it felt like carrying a pot of stones.

“I say!” I called. “Won’t you take some? I’ve filled my pockets and the basket.”

“Oh, come on, Quatermain—and hang the diamonds!” Sir Henry answered. “I hope I never see another.”

Good said nothing. He stood near where Foulata lay, and though I could not see his face, I knew he was taking leave of what was left of her. In that silence, grief had its own soundless weight.

If you, sitting by a cheerful hearth with supper on the table, could see the heaps we abandoned, you might think us mad. Yet I tell you truly: after a day and more of thirst and darkness, a man would not burden himself with jewels while crawling into unknown tunnels, save that old habit in me that hates leaving value behind when there is the least chance of carrying it away.

Sir Henry stood on the first step.

“Steady. I go first.”

“Mind your footing,” I said. “There may be a pit under us.”

“More like another chamber,” he answered, and began descending, counting under his breath.

“Fifteen,” he said, and stopped. “Bottom. Thank God. A passage, I think. Follow.”

Good went next, and I last with the basket. At the bottom I struck one of the two matches remaining. By that thin light we saw we stood in a narrow tunnel running right and left at right angles to the stair. The flame bit my fingers and died before more could be made out.

Then came the cruel choice: which way? One turn might lead to air; the other to nothing.

Good’s mind—sharp as a cook’s nose over a simmering pot—caught a detail.

“When you lit it,” he said, “the flame bent to the left. There’s a draught. Go against it. Air draws in, not out.”

We took his counsel. Feeling along the wall, testing the ground with each step, we left that accursed treasure chamber behind. If any living man ever enters it again—and I doubt it—he will find the open chests, the empty lamp, and the white bones of poor Foulata; and perhaps, if he listens, he will hear only that same thick silence we escaped.

For a quarter-hour we groped, then the passage turned sharp, or met another. We took it, then another, and another, until it seemed the mountain was nothing but corridors, like a great ant-hill of stone. We went on for hours, stumbling, turning, losing all sense of direction. We thought they must be old mine-workings, where men had chased the glittering ore like hunger through rock, driving galleries and adits wherever the vein tempted them. The very idea made my mouth ache: men digging for stones while their tongues dried in dust.

At length we halted, worn out, hope sickening within us. We ate the last strip of biltong—chewed it slowly, as a hungry man chews a memory of fat and smoke—and drank the last sup of water. Our throats felt like lime-kilns, baked and cracking. It seemed we had fled death in the treasure chamber only to meet him again in these blind tunnels.

As we stood, despair settling down like dust, I thought I heard something. Not imagined, not the beating of blood in the ear—something faint, far, yet real. I held up my hand and the others listened.

A murmur—soft, continuous.

No words can tell the blessedness of that sound after so long in absolute stillness, for sound itself felt like life returning.

“By heaven,” said Good, and his voice shook, “it’s running water.”

“Come on,” said Sir Henry.

We went toward it, the murmur guiding us as a scent guides a dog. I set the heavy basket down, my shoulders begging for mercy—then picked it up again. A foolish thought, yet stubborn: if we must die, let it be with riches at least. Hunger makes a man strange.

The sound grew clearer: a swirl, a rush. Yet how could there be running water in the belly of rock? Nearer—now it was loud in the quiet. Good, leading, said hoarsely, “I can smell it.”

“Gently,” Sir Henry warned. “We must be close.”

Splash! A cry—Good had vanished.

“Good!” we shouted, our hearts jumping into our throats. “Where are you?”

“All right,” came his voice, choky and strained. “I’ve got a rock. Strike a light—show me where you are.”

I lit our last match. The flame trembled, and in its sickly circle we saw dark water rushing at our feet. How wide the stream was we could not tell, but out in it Good’s shape clung to a projection.

“Stand clear!” he called. “I must swim for it.”

We heard him plunge, then the slap and drag of a fierce struggle. The current ran like a mill-race; you could hear its haste, feel its cold breath. A moment later his hand caught Sir Henry’s outstretched arm and we hauled him up, dripping and gasping.

“My word,” he choked, “that was touch and go. If I hadn’t caught that rock—and if I couldn’t swim—I’d have been done. No bottom I could feel. It runs like fury.”

We dared not creep along the bank in darkness for fear of stepping into it again. But thirst has its own courage. We knelt and drank our fill, that sweet cold water tasting like salvation, and washed our faces as best we could, mud and dust streaking away. The sound of it—splash, swallow, breath—was music after the tomb-quiet, and in that moment I understood how a man’s mind is fed by sound as much as by food. Silence had starved us.

Then, with Good unpleasantly wet in front, we turned back from that subterranean river—our African Styx, if you like—and retraced our steps until we found another tunnel branching right.

“We may as well take it,” Sir Henry said wearily. “All roads are alike here. We can only go on till we drop.”

On we stumbled for a long, long while, Sir Henry now leading, our feet dragging. Again I thought of abandoning the basket; again I did not. The diamonds knocked dully together with each step, a small, domestic sound almost—like dried beans shaken in a gourd—strangely homely in that place of death.

Suddenly Sir Henry stopped so sharp we bumped into him.

“Look,” he whispered. “Is my brain going—or is that light?”

We stared. Far ahead, no larger than a cottage window-pane, was a faint glimmer. So faint that in any ordinary place you might have missed it; but our eyes, trained by days of blackness, seized it greedily.

We pushed on. The patch grew. A minute more and a breath of real air, living air, came fanning our cheeks—air that tasted of earth and leaf-mould instead of stone.

The tunnel narrowed. Sir Henry went to his knees. Smaller still, until it was no bigger than a fox’s earth. And now, mind you, it was earth—soft, crumbly—no longer rock. A squeeze, a scrape, a struggle—Sir Henry wriggled out, then Good, then I, dragging Foulata’s heavy basket after me.

Above us—stars. Cool, vast, indifferent, and yet never did heaven look so friendly. The air filled our lungs like a draught of clear broth after famine.

Then something gave beneath us and we were rolling—over and over—through grass and bushes and soft wet soil, the slope taking us like a hand. The basket caught in something and jerked me to a stop. I sat up and shouted. An answering cry came from below where Sir Henry’s career had been checked by level ground. I scrambled down and found him shaken but unhurt. We found Good a little way off, jammed in a forked root, bruised and dazed, but soon swearing feebly as life returned.

We sat together there on the grass, and the revulsion of it was so great that I think we cried with joy, not ashamed. We had come out of that dungeon that had been so near to our grave. Some merciful Power must have guided our steps to that jackal hole—what else could it be?—at the tunnel’s end.

And yonder, on the mountain’s shoulder, the dawn we had never thought to see again was blushing rosy red, like fire under ash. Grey light stole down the slopes. We saw then that we were near the bottom of the vast pit before the cave entrance; and on its rim, dim against the paling sky, sat the three Colossi, solemn as judges.

Those countless passages we had wandered in the night must, in old times, have had their use in the working of the diamond mine. As for the river running in the mountain’s guts, Heaven only knows its source or end. I have no desire to learn.

As the light strengthened we looked at one another, and such scarecrows I hope never to see again: gaunt cheeks, hollow eyes, faces plastered with dust and mud, lips cracked, bruises darkening, blood dried in lines—fear written plain. Yet even then, absurdly, Good’s eye-glass still sat in his eye as if nothing on earth—not darkness, not drowning, not tumbling down a hill—could part him from it.

Fearing our limbs would stiffen, we rose and began, with slow painful steps, to climb the pit’s sloping side, dragging ourselves by roots and grasses through the blue clay. For more than an hour we toiled. And now the basket was no burden to be abandoned; it was a pledge of having endured, a proof in the hand that the mountain had not swallowed us for nothing. Nothing but death would have parted me from it.

At last we gained the great road on the side opposite the Colossi. A hundred yards away a fire burned before some huts. Shapes moved around it. Smoke drifted up with the familiar smell of wood and ash—how human a smell, how full of the memory of cooking pots and evening talk. We staggered toward it, supporting one another and stopping every few paces.

One of the figures rose, saw us, and fell flat, crying out in terror, as though ghosts had stepped from the earth.

“Infadoos! Infadoos!” we called. “It is we—thy friends!”

He got up, ran to us, stared wildly, shaking like a leaf.

“Oh, my lords, my lords,” he cried, and his voice broke, “it is indeed you—come back from the dead—come back from the dead!”

And the old warrior flung himself down and clasped Sir Henry’s knees, weeping aloud for joy.
