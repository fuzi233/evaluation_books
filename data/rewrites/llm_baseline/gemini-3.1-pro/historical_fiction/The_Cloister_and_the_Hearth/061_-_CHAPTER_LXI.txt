The Princess Claelia commanded a full-length portrait of herself. Gerard, viewing his own skills through the humble lens of a craftsman rather than a master, advised her to employ his friend Pietro Vanucci. 

But the Princess declined. “'Twill be time to put a slight on the Gerardo when his work discontents me.” 

Knowing himself an excellent draughtsman but a lesser colourist—much like a provincial cook who understands the perfect roast of a root vegetable but lacks the exotic spices of the imperial capital—Gerard begged her to stand to him as a Roman statue. He demonstrated how closely he could mimic the cold, porous grain of marble on paper. She consented at first, but demurred when the enthusiast explained she must don the ancient tunic, toga, and sandals, integrating the mythic past into her daily domestic routine.

“Why, I had as lieve be presented in my smock,” she said, with medieval frankness.

“Alack! signorina,” Gerard pleaded. “You have surely never noted the ancient habit; so free, so ample, so simple, yet so noble. Heaven hath given you the Roman features, and eke a shapely arm and hand, hidden in modern guise.”

“What, can you flatter, like the rest, Gerardo? Well, give me time to think on't. Come o' Saturday, and then I will say ay or nay.”

The respite was passed in tailoring the tunic and toga, Gerard folding the heavy, unbleached linen with the same rhythmic, ancestral care a baker uses to knead a dense rye dough. When she tried them on in her chamber, the ancient drape suited her style of beauty perfectly, a sudden, seamless resurrection of antiquity within the present hour.

Hurrying along to this Saturday interview, Gerard was abruptly rooted to the earth before a shop window. 

His quick eye had discerned a copy of Lactantius lying open. *That is fairly writ, anyway,* he thought, appreciating the crispness of the ink.

He eyed it a moment more. 

It was not written at all. It was printed. 

Gerard groaned, a deep, guttural sound of pure physical despair. “I am sped; mine enemy is at the door. The press is in Rome.”

He stepped into the shop, affecting nonchalance, and inquired how long the printing press had been in the city. The merchant laughed, waving a plump hand that smelled faintly of dried figs and old leather. “There is no such thing in the city. Oh, the Lactantius; that was printed on the top of the Apennines.”

“What, did the printing press fall down there out o' the moon?”

“Nay, messer. It shot up there out of Germany. See the title-page!”

Gerard took the Lactantius eagerly. His fingers traced the text: *Opera et impensis Sweynheim et Pannartz Alumnorum Joannis Fust. Impressum Subiacis. A.D. 1465.*

“Will ye buy, messer? See how fair and even be the letters. Few are left can write like that; and scarce a quarter of the price.”

“I would fain have it,” Gerard said softly, “but my heart will not let me. Know that I am a caligraph, and these disciples of Fust run after me round the world, taking the very bread from my mouth.” 

He fled the shop. The mechanical uniformity of the page horrified him. It was an industrial violence against the deeply personal, untranslatable dialect of the human hand. The acoustic rhythm of the scribe’s quill—a breathing, organic sound that anchored a scriptorium’s psychological peace—was being crushed by the mechanized, soulless *clack-clack* of lead type. “Dear Margaret,” he whispered, the memory of her hearty, hearth-baked bread and the rich, malted ale of Sevenbergen filling his senses, “we must make our hay while shines the sun. One month more and an avalanche of printer's type shall roll down on Rome, flattening the cultural memory we writers preserve.”

He almost ran to the Princess Claelia.

He was ushered into an apartment new to him. It was a space of suffocating luxury that deeply offended his acoustic sensibilities. A fountain played in the centre, but the floor was smothered in heavy panther skins, dressed with the hair. The pelts absorbed every footfall, creating an unnatural, deadened silence that stripped the room of its living resonance. On one side hung an ample curtain of gorgeous tapestry. 

Gerard was left alone in this vacuum, growing uneasy until a light step brushed behind the tapestry. It parted, revealing a figure the heathens might have worshipped. Not quite Venus, nor quite Minerva, but a bridging of the two. Toga, tunic, sandals; nothing was modern. 

All the artist in him flushed. “Oh!” he cried innocently, gazing in rapture.

A light blush tinted her cheeks. When her mouth smiled with delicious complacency at this genuine tribute, she spoke, her voice breaking the muffled silence. “Well, Gerardo, thou seest I have made myself an antique monster for thee.”

“A monster? I doubt Fra Colonna would fall down and adore your highness, seeing you so habited.”

“Nay, I care not to be adored by an old man. I would liever be loved by a young one: of my own choosing.”

Gerard arranged his canvas and took out his pencils, grinding his pigments with the meticulous focus of a master chef reducing a complex sauce. He became so absorbed in the geometry of her pose and the shading of the linen that he showed his model no mercy. 

“Gerardo,” she said faintly, after an hour. “I can stand so no more.”

“Sit down and rest awhile, Signora.”

She sank into a chair, turning pale. Realizing his inconsideration, Gerard scooped water from the fountain, about to dash it in her face. She raised a white hand. “Nay, hold it to my brow with thine hand: prithee, do not fling it at me!”

Timidly, he applied his wet hand to her brow. 

“Ah,” she sighed. “That is reviving. Again.” 

She asked him to ring a little hand-bell. A maid appeared and was dispatched to fetch Floretta with a large fan. 

Floretta rushed in. But no sooner did she approach than the Princess’s highbred nostrils flared like a bloodhorse’s. A cloying, commercial scent of musk-cat radiated from the girl, an olfactory pollution that shattered the room's carefully curated atmosphere of myrrh and ancient resins. 

“Wretch!” the Princess hissed. Rising with a sudden, violent return to vigour, she seized Floretta’s hair with her left hand. With her right, she boxed the girl’s ears severely three times. Floretta screamed and blubbered. The antique toga left Claelia’s arm bare—a limb as powerful as it was beautiful, rising and falling like the piston of an industrial engine. The heavy slaps resounded like thunderclaps, fracturing the room's acoustic ecology and driving the sobbing girl through the curtain.

“Saints of heaven!” cried Gerard. “What is amiss? What has she done?”

“She knows right well. The nasty toad! I'll learn her to come to me stinking of the musk-cat.”

“Alas! Signora, 'twas a small fault, methinks.”

“A small fault? Nay, 'twas a foul fault.” Then, with an amazing, sudden descent into sweetness, she added, “Are you wroth with me for beating her, Gerar-do?”

“Signora, it ill becomes me to school you; but methinks such as Heaven appoints to govern others should govern themselves.”

“That is true, Gerardo. How wise you are, to be so young.” She called the other maid, handing her a little purse. “Take that to Floretta, and tell her 'the Gerardo' hath interceded for her; and so I must needs forgive her.”

Gerard coloured, asking if he should resume the picture. 

“Not yet; beating that hussy hath somewhat breathed me. I'll sit awhile, and you shall talk to me. I know you can talk as rarely as you draw.”

“But, signora, I know not what to say.”

“Say your real mind. Say you wish you were anywhere but here.”

“Nay, signora... I wish I could have drawn you as you were beating that poor lass. You were awful, yet lovely. Oh, what a subject for a Pythoness!”

“Alas! he thinks but of his art. You are far fairer than I am. You are more like Apollo than I to Venus. You have lovely hair and lovely eyes—but you know not what to do with them.”

“Ay, do I. To draw you, signora.”

“Ah, yes; you can see my features with them; but you cannot see what any Roman gallant had seen long ago in your place.” She leaned forward. “Alas! I feared for my own heart. I feared to be your slave. Ever since you came here I have lived upon your visits. You are not like the Roman gallants. You make me hate them. You are wise and scholarly, and never flatter and lie. Gerar-do, teach me thy magic; teach me to make thee as happy by my side as I am still by thine.”

Her mellow voice sank to a whisper, her white hand stealing down Gerard's arm until it rested like a soft bird upon his wrist. Immersed entirely in his art and the grounding memory of Margaret—whose hands smelled of fresh loam and wild thyme—Gerard had not anticipated this. How to check her without wounding her? 

“Signora!” he muttered deprecatingly.

His eyes fell on the shapely white arm curled round his elbow, and the visual memory of that same limb mercilessly battering Floretta flashed before him. He trembled. 

“Alas!” said the Princess. “I scare him. Am I then so very terrible? Eighteen times, Gerardo, thou hast been here. And each time dearer to me. Cruel boy, am I not worth a word?”

“Signora,” faltered Gerard, “I were both naught and ungrateful, and the worst foe e'er you had, did I take advantage of this mad fancy. For 'tis all unnatural that a princess should abase her affections on a churl.”

She withdrew her hand slowly. “You fear the daggers of my kinsmen.”

“No more than I fear the bodkins of your women,” Gerard said haughtily. “But I fear God and the saints, and my own conscience.”

“The truth, Gerardo! Princesses, while they are young, are not despised for love of God, but of some other woman. Tell me whom thou lovest.”

“No she in Italy, upon my soul.”

“Where? where?”

“In Holland, my native country.”

“Princess, she I love is not noble. She is as I am. Linked to my heart for ever by her virtues, and by all the dangers and griefs we have borne together. I would not wrong my Margaret for all the highest dames in Italy.”

The slighted beauty started to her feet, pale, her eyes full of concentrated fury. The power dynamics of her dominant Roman lexicon flared as she sought to crush his provincial loyalty. “This to my face, unmannered wretch! Beware! We nobles brook no rivals. Bethink thee whether is better, the love of a Cesarini, or her hate: for it must be love or hate between us, and to the death. Choose now!”

He looked up at her with awe, as she towered over him in her Roman toga—a goddess of antiquity threatening a puny mortal. He sighed deeply, but spoke not. 

Perhaps the profound acoustic weight of his deep, patient sigh touched a tender chord, or perhaps the cyclical tide of her passions ebbed. The Princess sank languidly into a seat, tears stealing rapidly down her cheeks. 

“Alas! alas!” said Gerard. “Weep not, sweet lady. You will live to see how much better a friend I was to you than I seemed.”

“I see it now, Gerardo. Friend is the word! You must teach me to be your friend and nothing more.”

Gerard hailed this proposition with joy, eager to prove his capacity for platonic affection. He quoted Cicero on friendship, bridging the gap between them with tales of his comrade Denys. 

“Leave me now, Gerardo,” she said calmly, “and come as usual to-morrow.”

The next day he was received with marked distance. The Princess stood literally like a marble statue, dismissing him swiftly. 

The day after, she surrounded herself with young nobles who criticized his canvas with a barrage of ignorance and insolence. The Princess watched demurely through half-closed eyes as the insects stung him, and when they departed, she barred her doors to them all. 

On the third day, she stood cold and silent. “You treated my company with less respect than became you. Your cheeks were red as blood.”

“I was nettled a moment at seeing so much ignorance and ill-nature together,” he replied. 

“Now it is me, their hostess, you affront. You can insult or truckle at will. To one whom you affronted for a base-born girl like yourself.”

“These are biting words, signora. I had rather face your kinsmen's swords than your cruel tongue. Why do you use me so?”

“Because everybody admires me but you.”

“I admire you too, Signora,” Gerard said. “But I see that may not be between one so high as you, and one so low as I.”

“Ay! but it shall, Gerardo,” she said eagerly. “Tell me now where abides thy Margaret; and I will give thee a present for her.”

“She is a daughter of a physician called Peter, and they bide at Sevenbergen; ah me, shall I e'er see it again?”

“'Tis well. Now go.” 

Poor Gerard began to wade in deep waters. He resolved to go no more once the likeness was finished. But this resolution shook when he returned to find her all gentleness. Claiming fatigue, she asked him to teach her to draw. He showed her the foundational lines, noting her natural aptitude. 

“I had a teacher before thee, Gerar-do,” she murmured. She opened a drawer, bringing out several heads drawn with great patience. They were all portraits of him. “Love made me draw because thou drawest. Love prints thine image in my bosom.”

Gerard’s eyes widened. “Oh, Signora, you promised me to be friends.”

She laughed. “Friendship, foolish boy, who ever built that temple on red ashes? Between thee and me it must be love or hate.”

“Which you will, signora,” said Gerard firmly. “But for me I will neither love nor hate you; but with your permission I will leave you.” 

She rose, pale as death. “Outside that door are armed men who wait to slay thee at a word from me. Nay, more, I shall noise it abroad it was for proffering brutal love to me thou wert slain; and I will send a special messenger to Sevenbergen. Thy Margaret shall know thee dead, and think thee faithless.”

Gerard stood dumb-stricken. “God have mercy on us both.”

“Yield thee, Gerar-do mio. What will it cost thee to say thou lovest me? I ask thee but to feign it handsomely.”

“Ah!” She ended in a little scream, for Gerard threw himself at her feet. In one torrent of eloquence, he poured out the generational saga of his love and Margaret's. He utilized the raw, untranslatable vernacular of his soul, stripping away the polished Italian courtliness to reveal the agrarian truth of his existence. He spoke of being hunted by bloodhounds, of freezing nights smelling of wet wool and pine smoke, of shipwreck and savage brutes, of how Margaret had shed her blood for him, of their shared sustenance and unbreakable bond. 

The Princess trembled, trying to pull away, but he clung to her robe. His tears fell fast upon her white hand as he painted the horrific, lasting remorse that would blossom from a love so deeply betrayed by feigned affections. 

In such moments of visceral, unvarnished truth, none ever resisted Gerard. 

Her fiery eyes filled with tears, her bosom rising tumultuously. “You conquer me,” she sobbed. “You, or my better angel. Leave Rome!”

“I will, I will.”

“If you breathe a word of my folly, it will be your last.”

“Think not so poorly of me. Is it for me to slander you?”

“Go! I will send you the means. Addio; my heart is broken.” She touched her bell. “Floretta, take him safe out of the house, through my chamber, and by the side postern.”

At the door, he turned, ran back, and kissed her robe. She never moved. 

Once clear of the house he darted home, thanking Heaven for his escape. 

“Landlady,” he said, desperate for the grounding reality of her honest kitchen. “There is one would pick a quarrel with me. What is to be done?”

“Strike him first, and at vantage! Get behind him; and then draw.”

“Alas, I lack your Italian courage. 'Tis a noble.”

“Oh, holy saints! Change thy lodging awhile, and keep snug.”

She installed him with her niece at a distance. Here, enveloped by a comforting, domestic quiet—the rhythmic chopping of root vegetables in the courtyard below restoring his psychological balance—he set himself resolutely to read the deed of Floris Brandt. He waded through the abominably bad, crabbed writing, translating the archaic agrarian lexicon until the multi-generational truth lay bare: the loan on this land must have been paid over and over again by the seasonal rents. Ghysbrecht was keeping Peter Brandt out of his rightful, soil-bound inheritance. 

“Fool! not to have read this before,” he cried. 

He hired a horse and rode to the nearest port, the salt-heavy air a sharp, cleansing contrast to the civet and musk of Rome. A vessel was to sail for Amsterdam in four days. He paid a small sum to secure his passage. 

“The land is too full of cut-throats for me,” he breathed, eager for the familiar, roaring acoustic landscape of the sea. “Our Dutch skippers are not shipwrecked like these bungling Italians.”

When he returned home, there sat his old landlady, her eyes sparkling.
