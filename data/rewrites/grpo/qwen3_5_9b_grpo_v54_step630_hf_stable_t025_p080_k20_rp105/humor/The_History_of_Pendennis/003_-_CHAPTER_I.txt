It was a brilliant morning in the height of the London season, the kind of day that makes the city sparkle like a jeweled box. Major Arthur Pendennis, a man whose very presence seemed to command the air, strode into his favorite club in Pall Mall. To the uninitiated, he was simply a retired officer; to the rest of London, he was the institution itself. As the club's premier connoisseur of vintage wines and a man of such vigorous, inquiring spirit that he effectively ran the place, the stewards and waiters bowed to him with the deference usually reserved for Dukes or Field Marshals.

At ten-thirty, punctual as a clockwork mechanism, Pendennis arrived. He was a study in perfection: boots polished to a mirror shine, a morning cravat crisp enough to cut glass, and a buff waistcoat bearing the royal crown on its buttons. His linen was so immaculate that Beau Brummel, had he not fled the country in disgrace, would have hired the Major's laundryman immediately. From his whiskers to his cane, every item was a flawless specimen of military fashion *en retraite*. To a stranger, he looked barely thirty; a closer look revealed the artificiality of his rich brown hair and the faint crow's-feet around his handsome, mottled face, but his Wellington-pattern nose and long, white-gloved hands remained undeniably aristocratic.

He claimed his usual corner table, a fortress no one dared breach. In the past, a few reckless youths had tried to evict him, but the sheer, quiet dignity with which the Major settled in, surveying the room with his gold-rimmed spectacles, made it clear that no one could breakfast under his gaze. His table was a shrine to social importance. Letters were laid out in anticipation of his arrival, a mountain of correspondence that baffled the younger set. If there was any doubt about etiquette, marital status, or the age of a Duke, Pendennis was the oracle. Even Marchionesses drove up to the club specifically to leave notes for him or fetch him out. He was affable, charming, and treated every man he met as a peer.

While the waiters brought his toast and the morning paper, Pendennis surveyed his mail. He carried his spectacles with such casual flair that they looked like accessories rather than tools of vision. He sorted through the pile: solemn dinner cards promising heavy conversation, intimate notes from women, and a formal invitation from the Marquis of Steyne to a French-speaking party at Richmond. There was also a request from the Bishop of Ealing and Mrs. Trail. He read them all with graceful satisfaction, enjoying the envy of Glowry, the Scotch surgeon sitting opposite. Glowry, who received no such invitations, watched with a mixture of hatred and admiration as the Major dismissed the letters, checking his pocket-book to decide which engagements to accept and which to decline.

He tossed aside the invitation from Cutler, the East India Director, in favor of Lord Steyne's French party. He accepted the Bishop, reasoning that bishops always made for slow, dignified dinners. With his schedule arranged, he ate his breakfast, scanned the gazette for his name among the guests of the season, and chatted cheerfully with his acquaintances.

Amidst the glittering correspondence of high society, however, lay one solitary letter. It sat apart, marked with a country postmark and a humble seal, unlike the grand notes from the grandees who had already driven away. It was written in a delicate female hand, stamped "Immediate" with a dash of anxiety. For reasons of his own, the Major had neglected this humble rural petitioner, allowing her to wait in the social ante-chamber while the nobility were served.

Finally, it was her turn. The Major broke the seal, which bore the engraving of "Fairoaks."

"My dear Major Pendennis," the letter began, "I beg and implore you to come to me *immediately*." Pendennis paused, expecting a summons to Steyne's dinner. "I am in the very greatest grief and perplexity. My dearest boy... is grieving me *dreadfully*. He has formed—an infatuation—for an actress who has been performing here. She is at least twelve years older than Arthur—who will not be eighteen till next February—and the wretched boy insists upon marrying her."

"Hay! What's making Pendennis swear now?" Glowry muttered to himself, witnessing the Major's jaw drop in open-mouthed shock.

"Do, my dear friend," the letter continued, "come to me instantly... and entreat, command, the wretched child to give up this most deplorable resolution." The letter concluded with the signature of his "unhappy affectionate sister, Helen Pendennis."

"Fairoaks, Tuesday," the Major grumbled, finishing the letter. "A d—d pretty business at Fairoaks, Tuesday; now let us see what the boy has to say." He picked up the second letter, written in a frantic, floundering boy's hand and sealed with a massive Pendennis signet, splattered with extra wax to show the writer's trembling agitation.

The epistle read:

*In informing you of my engagement with Miss Costigan, daughter of J. Chesterfield Costigan, Esq., of Costiganstown, but, perhaps, better known to you under her professional name of Miss Fotheringay, of the Theatres Royal Drury Lane and Crow Street, and of the Norwich and Welsh Circuit, I am aware that I make an announcement which cannot, according to the present prejudices of society at least, be welcome to my family...*

*My dearest mother... is deeply moved and grieved... by the intelligence which I have this night conveyed to her. I beseech you, my dear Sir, to come down and reason with her and console her. Although obliged by poverty to earn an honourable maintenance by the exercise of her splendid talents, Miss Costigan's family is as ancient and noble as our own. When our ancestor, Ralph Pendennis, landed with Richard II. in Ireland, my Emily's forefathers were kings of that country...*

*It is in vain I have attempted to argue with my dear mother... my poor mother has prejudices which it is impossible for my logic to overcome... Although Miss Costigan is some years older than myself, that circumstance does not operate as a barrier to my affection... A love like mine, Sir, I feel, is contracted once and for ever. As I never had dreamed of love until I saw her—I feel now that I shall die without ever knowing another passion. It is the fate of my life...*

*I press for a speedy marriage with my Emily—for why, in truth, should it be delayed? A delay implies a doubt, which I cast from me as unworthy. It is impossible that my sentiments can change towards Emily... I entreat you, my dear Uncle, to come down and reconcile my dear mother to our union, and I address you as a man of the world, *qui mores hominum multorum vidit et urbes*, who will not feel any of the weak scruples and fears which agitate a lady who has scarcely ever left her village.*

*Pray, come down to us immediately. I am quite confident that—apart from considerations of fortune—you will admire and approve of my Emily.*

When the Major finished reading, his countenance twisted into an expression of pure rage and horror. Glowry, the surgeon, reached for his lancet in his card-case, fearing his friend was having a stroke. The news was staggering: the head of the Pendennis house, a man of his standing, going to marry an actress ten years his senior—a headstrong boy plunging blindly into matrimony.

"The mother has spoiled the young rascal," the Major groaned inwardly. "With her cursed sentimentality and romantic rubbish. My nephew marry a tragedy queen! Gracious mercy, people will laugh at me so that I shall not dare show my head!" He felt a sharp pang of regret; he would have to cancel Lord Steyne's dinner at Richmond, lose his rest, and spend the night in a foul mail-coach instead of enjoying the select society of England.

He realized he couldn't just cancel one dinner; he would have to cancel them all for some time. Who knew how long this domestic crisis would drag on? Quitting his breakfast table, he retreated to the adjoining writing-room. There, with a rueful sigh, he penned refusals to the Marquis, the Earl, the Bishop, and every other host who had invited him. He then ordered his servant to book seats in the mail-coach for that very evening, naturally charging the cost to the account of the widow and the young scapegrace he was forced to guard.
