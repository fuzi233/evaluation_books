The signing of the Will was concluded with unsettling haste. Samuel, the footman, was summoned as a witness, and before I could gather my thoughts, the pen was thrust into my aunt’s trembling hand. The solemnity of the moment demanded reflection, but Mr. Bruff’s impatient demeanor stifled any words I might have offered. In mere minutes, it was done—Samuel dismissed, the document sealed, and my aunt left paler than before.

Mr. Bruff studied me with the weary skepticism of a man accustomed to dismissing zealots. I, however, was immovable. My satchel of devotional tracts weighed heavy in my lap, a silent testament to my resolve. Recognizing futility, he muttered something unbecoming and withdrew, leaving me alone with my aunt.

She reclined upon the sofa, her voice faint with exhaustion. “You mustn’t think yourself slighted, Drusilla. Your legacy shall be given with my own hand.”

Here was my opportunity! With practiced ease, I retrieved the first of my treasured volumes—*The Serpent at Home*—a work of singular moral clarity. Its chapters, such as *Satan in the Hairbrush* and *Satan Behind the Looking-Glass*, laid bare the devil’s cunning in domestic trivialities.

“Dear aunt,” I urged, “this book will provide all the comfort you require.” I pressed it into her hands, opened to a particularly stirring passage: *Satan Among the Sofa Cushions*.

She glanced at it, then away, her discomfort plain. “Drusilla, I fear I must wait until I am stronger. The doctor insists—”

Ah, the doctor! That agent of secular oppression! How often had his kind interposed between a soul and salvation under the guise of *rest* and *quiet*? Yet I would not be deterred.

“Of course,” I conceded with false meekness. “But perhaps tomorrow, when your mind is clearer?” I slipped the book discreetly between the sofa cushions, where her fingers might later brush against its divine wisdom.

A sudden inspiration struck me as I noted the flowers lining her window—her one worldly indulgence. “Might I take a bloom?” I inquired, drifting toward them. Instead, I left another volume nestled among the geraniums.

Thus began my campaign. In the library, I replaced frivolous novels with uplifting tracts. In the breakfast room, I tucked a pamphlet beside the canary’s seed. The drawing-room piano received two more beneath its sheet music, and a third hid within her embroidery frame. Behind a curtain in the back parlor, I wedged another into her fan.

Emboldened, I ascended to her bedchamber. There, upon her nightstand, I placed a volume beside the matches—should she wake in darkness, she would find illumination of a higher kind. Another nestled near her chocolate drops. Only one book remained in my satchel, and only one room unexplored: the bath. A dressing gown hung nearby, its pocket gaping. With reverent hands, I deposited my final offering.

My mission complete, I slipped away, my heart light as a child’s. That night, I slept the untroubled sleep of the righteous.

The next morning, Samuel arrived at my lodgings with a parcel—my aunt’s reply. His manner was skittish, his face flushed. “Her ladyship’s regards,” he stammered, depositing the bundle as though it burned him.

Inside lay all twelve of my books, returned. A note accompanied them—my aunt dared not defy her physician.

Undaunted, I devised a new stratagem: if books were forbidden, letters would serve. Selecting the most potent passages, I transcribed them into personal missives. Some I posted; others I would deliver by hand.

By afternoon, I stood once more at her doorstep. Samuel informed me she rested in the drawing room, still unwell. The house was quiet—Rachel and her frivolous companions, including the lamentably wayward Mr. Ablewhite, were likely at some concert.

I set to work. In the library, I left one letter prominently upon the mantel. Another I dropped conspicuously in the breakfast room. As I moved toward the stairs, a knock echoed through the hall—soft, yet deliberate.

Samuel’s voice carried up to me. “Upstairs, if you please, sir.”

Footsteps followed. A man’s. The doctor? No—the cadence was too restless. A murmur reached me, a voice I almost recognized. Then, clear as a struck bell:

I knew that voice. It belonged to Godfrey Ablewhite.
