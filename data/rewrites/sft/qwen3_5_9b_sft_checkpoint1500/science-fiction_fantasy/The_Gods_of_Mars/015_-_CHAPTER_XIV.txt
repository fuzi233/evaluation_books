"My son!" The words tore from my lips, a shockwave that nearly knocked me back. I rose slowly, turning to face the handsome youth standing before me. Now, looking at him with the scrutiny of a lifetime, I understood why his countenance had captivated me from afar. There was the incomparable beauty of his mother carved sharply into his features, yet it was tempered by a strong, virile masculinity. His grey eyes, burning with an intensity I knew well, reflected my own spirit.

The boy stood frozen, his gaze a mixture of desperate hope and trembling uncertainty.

"Tell me of your mother," I commanded, my voice thick with emotion. "Speak to me of the years stolen from me by a ruthless fate, the years I have spent without her companionship."

A cry of pure joy erupted from him. He lunged forward, wrapping his arms around my neck. For a fleeting moment, holding my son close, the dam broke; tears welled in my eyes, choking me raw. I did not care for the shame of it, nor did I regret the weakness. A long life has taught me that a man may crumble under the weight of love for a child or a wife, and yet stand like iron in the sterner paths of duty.

"Your stature, your manner, the terrible ferocity of your swordsmanship," the boy whispered, pulling back to look into my face. "These are as my mother has described a thousand times. Yet, even with such proof, I could scarcely believe the truth of what seemed so improbable, however deeply I wished it to be so. Do you know what finally convinced me?"

"What, my boy?"

"Your first words," he said softly. "None but the man who loved her as she tells me you did would think first of her."

"For many years, my son, I have scarce recalled a single moment when the vision of your mother's face has not haunted me. Tell me of her."

"The elders who have known her longest say she has not changed, growing only more beautiful—a miracle if such a thing is possible," Carthoris recounted. "Only when she believes I am unseen does her face fall into a deep, wistful sadness. She thinks constantly of you, Father. All Helium mourns with her and for her. Her grandfather's people adore her, and they worship your memory as the saviour of Barsoom. Each year, on the anniversary of the day you raced across the dying world to unlock the secrets of that terrible portal, a great festival is held. But mixed with the thanks are tears of profound regret—the sorrow that the author of their happiness is not there to share the joy of living."

"And by what name has your mother called you?" I asked.

"The people of Helium requested I bear your name, but my mother refused. She said you and she had chosen a name together, and your wish must honor all others. So, she calls me Carthoris, a blend of yours and hers."

Xodar, who had been steering the ship, suddenly called out.

"She is dropping badly by the head, John Carter!" he shouted over the hum of the engines. "So long as we climbed at a steep angle, it wasn't visible. But now that I'm trying to hold a horizontal course, it's disastrous. The wound in her bow has ripped open one of the forward ray tanks."

I checked the damage instantly. It was far worse than anticipated. The forced angle required to maintain a level flight severely hampered our speed, and with the repulsive rays leaking from the bow tanks, we wouldn't stay airborne for much longer than an hour. If we didn't correct, we would float stern-up, helpless.

We had slowed slightly earlier, lulled by a false sense of security. Now, I snatched the helm and pulled the throttle wide open, driving the noble little engine to its limit. Again, we raced north at terrifying velocity. Meanwhile, Carthoris and Xodar worked frantically with tools against the gaping rent in the bow, in a futile struggle to stem the tide of escaping energy.

It was still dark as we crossed the northern boundary of the ice cap. Below us lay a quintessential Martian landscape: rolling ochre seabeds of long-dead oceans, low hills, and scattered grim, silent cities of the dead past. Great piles of mighty architecture stood tenant-less, preserved only by the ancient memories of a powerful race and the presence of the great white apes of Barsoom.

Maintaining a horizontal posture became increasingly impossible. The bow sagged lower and lower until I was forced to kill the engine to avoid a swift, fatal dive into the ground.

Just as dawn broke, sweeping away the night's darkness, our craft gave a final, spasmodic lurch. It turned partially onto its side, the deck tilting at a sickening angle, before swinging in a slow, spiraling circle. The bow dropped further below the stern with every passing second.

Clinging to handrails and stanchions, we braced ourselves. Seeing the inevitable end approach, we snapped the buckles of our harnesses to the rings embedded in the hull sides. In a heartbeat, the deck reared up to a ninety-degree angle, and we hung suspended by our leather straps, feet dangling a thousand yards above the barren earth.

Swinging dangerously close to the control levers, I reached out and engaged the repulsion rays. The boat responded, gently beginning to descend toward the surface.

Half an hour later, we touched down. Directly north rose a lofty range of hills. Deciding they offered better concealment from the pursuers we feared might be hot on our trail, we resolved to make for them.

An hour later, we navigated the time-worn gullies of the hills, amidst the beautiful flowering plants that thrive in Barsoom's arid wastes. Beneath a cluster of massive milk-bearing shrubs—which serve as sustenance for the wild hordes of green men—we lay down to sleep. For me, it was the first rest I had taken in many hours. This marked the beginning of my fifth day on Barsoom, since I was suddenly transported from my cottage on the Hudson to Dor, the valley that is both beautiful and hideous. In all this time, I had slept twice, though once within the storage vaults of the Therns.

Mid-afternoon, I was awakened by a hand seizing mine, covered in kisses. Startled, I opened my eyes to behold the beautiful face of Thuvia.

"My Prince! My Prince!" she cried, her voice filled with an ecstatic happiness. "'Tis you whom I had mourned as dead! My ancestors have been good to me; I have not lived in vain."

Her cries roused Xodar and Carthoris. The boy stared at the woman in stunned surprise, but she seemed oblivious to anyone other than me. She would have flung her arms around my neck and smothered me with affection had I not gently, firmly, disengaged myself.

"Come, Thuvia," I said soothingly. "You are overwrought by the dangers and hardships you've endured. You forget yourself, as you forget that I am the husband of the Princess of Helium."

"I forget nothing, my Prince," she replied, her eyes shining. "You have spoken no word of love to me, nor do I expect you ever shall; but nothing can prevent me from loving you. I would not take the place of Dejah Thoris. My greatest ambition is to serve you, my Prince, forever as your slave. No greater boon could I ask, no greater honor could I crave."

As I have often admitted, I am no lady's man, and I must confess I have rarely felt so ill-at-ease. While I am familiar with the Martian custom allowing female slaves to Martian men—who possess a high, chivalrous honor sufficient to protect every woman in their households—I had never voluntarily chosen women as my personal attendants.

"And I ever return to Helium, Thuvia," I said. "You shall go with me, but as an honored equal, not as a slave. There you shall find plenty of handsome young nobles who would face Issus herself to win a smile from you, and we shall marry you to one of the best of them in short order. Forget this foolish, gratitude-born infatuation, which your innocence mistook for love. I like your friendship better, Thuvia."

"You are my master," she answered simply, a note of sadness coloring her voice. "It shall be as you say."

"How came you here, Thuvia?" I asked. "And where is Tars Tarkas?"

"The great Thark, I fear, is dead," she replied, her voice heavy with grief. "He was a mighty fighter, but a multitude of green warriors from a horde other than his overwhelmed him. The last I saw, they were bearing him, wounded and bleeding, to the deserted city from which they sallied to attack us."

"You are not sure he is dead, then?" I pressed. "And where is this city?"

"It lies just beyond this range of hills. The vessel in which you so nobly surrendered a seat so we might escape defied our small skills in navigation, causing us to drift aimlessly for two days. We abandoned the craft and attempted to reach the nearest waterway on foot. Yesterday we crossed these hills and encountered the dead city beyond. We had passed within its streets, walking toward the center, when a body of green warriors appeared at an intersecting avenue."
