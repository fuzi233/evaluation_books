Denys waited until the next day to speak privately with Catherine and Kate, carefully omitting any mention of Cornelis and Sybrandt. “It’s not my place to darken their names,” he said. “They come from good stock. But Gerard sees them as no friends in this matter—and I’m Gerard’s comrade. A soldier keeps his secrets from the enemy—save for lies.”

Catherine sighed but held her tongue. His tale of Gerard’s trials wrung their hearts, and tears fell freely at the parting of the two friends—a moment Denys himself could not recount without a tremor in his voice. Yet grief soon gave way to hope, for surely Gerard would return to them soon.

Still, Denys insisted, whether Gerard came back tomorrow or next year, his last wishes must be honored. “If he rides home to find no Margaret, where would I stand then?”

Catherine, her heart now softened toward the girl, was as eager as Denys to find her before Gerard’s return. But she disagreed with his plan to scour the countryside. “She must have told someone where she was bound. They’re not fugitives—they owe no man a penny in Sevenbergen. And, Denys, you can’t hunt all Holland for her.”

“Can’t I?” he muttered. “We’ll see.” After a pause, he proposed dividing their efforts: she would stay, ears open for any whisper, while he combed every town if need be. “Three don’t flee as fast or as far as one,” he reasoned.

“That’s sense,” Catherine conceded. Yet she urged him first to seek the demoiselle Van Eyck. “She and Margaret were close as sisters. If anyone knows where the lass has gone, ’tis she—if she’ll tell.”

Denys would have gone that instant, but Catherine tidied herself swiftly, and together they sought the painter’s house.

Margaret Van Eyck received them with grace, seated amidst the rich furnishings of her chamber. But at Catherine’s news, the tapestry slipped from her fingers.

“Gone? Without a word to me? The thankless child!”

This reaction startled them. “You didn’t know? When did you last see her?”

“Ten days past, perhaps. I had taken up my brushes again—after years—to paint her portrait. But I did not.” She hesitated, then confessed, “When I learned her condition, I set them down. I could not make her the Virgin’s likeness—not at my age.”

Catherine frowned. “But dame, surely you see how this would wound her? She loved you dearly.”

“Perhaps. Yet I could not help it.” The old woman sighed. “I am not some man who can paint saints and sinners from the same model. My art is half craft, half devotion.”

Then Reicht Heynes, the Frisian maid, spoke bluntly from the doorway. “You drove her off, mistress—with your cold looks and sharp words. And not just you. Gerard’s folk have slighted her sorely. Who would stay where she’s unwelcome?”

Catherine flushed. “I meant to visit—”

“Aye, ‘meant,’” Reicht scoffed. “Words lift the heart, but deeds dash it down again. She sat waiting, hoping, while you stayed away.”

The Van Eyck, stung, dismissed her on the spot. Reicht merely shrugged. “Turn me out if you like. But I’ll not weep till you’re dead and buried.”

Then Denys, with sudden gravity, turned to Reicht. “Since you’re no longer a servant, be a queen instead.”

The women stared.

“Marry me,” he said. “I’ve twenty gold pieces, a strong arm, and admiration for a lass who speaks her mind.”

Reicht eyed him sidelong. “I’ll look a man over well before I leap.”

“Then look your fill,” said Denys, squaring himself proudly.

At this, the Van Eyck cried out, seized by artistic frenzy. “Stay! You are beautiful—in your folly. I must capture you!” She snatched up her pencils, commanding Reicht to hold her place before him.

Catherine gaped, convinced the woman had gone mad. Reicht, cross at neglecting her boiling pot, muttered until her mistress snapped, “Go, then! Bury yourself in your work—you’ve no soul beyond it.”

Reicht departed with a final jab. “When you’re tired of life, mistress, die. But take your time. And soldier, if you’re still of the same mind then, speak up. If not—well, ’tis all one to Reicht Heynes.”

Her words struck home. Catherine and Denys left, both certain now that Margaret had fled heartbroken.

That afternoon, Denys set out on his quest—a needle in Holland’s haystack. He would comb every town, asking after an old physician, his fair daughter, and a bowman. He would question burgomasters, watch the wells where women gathered.

For months he wandered, a pilgrim of friendship, sober and steadfast where once he might have been reckless. He flirted lightly, wasted no time, and never again declared marriage “no habit of his.”

But Margaret Brandt remained as elusive as a shadow.
