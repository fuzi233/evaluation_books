A Blow and Its Consequences.

"But work grew scarce, while bread grew dear,
And wages lessened, too;
For Irish hordes were bidders here,
Our half-paid work to do."
—Corn Law Rhymes.

Margaret was ushered into the drawing-room, where the atmosphere had settled into a grim, stagnant normalcy. The windows stood ajar against the stifling heat, the Venetian blinds casting a gray, distorted light from the pavement below. This unnatural illumination, mingled with the greenish gloom filtering through the upper panes, rendered even Margaret's complexion ghostly and wan in the mirrors. She sat in silence, waiting. No one approached. From afar, the wind occasionally seemed to carry the indistinct hum of a multitude closer, only to die away into a profound, suffocating stillness.

Finally, Fanny entered.

"Mamma will be with you shortly, Miss Hale," Fanny said, her voice trembling slightly. "She wanted me to apologize for the delay. You likely know that my brother has hired hands from Ireland, and it has enraged the Milton workers to the point of madness. They claim he has no right to seek labor elsewhere, and in their fury, they've threatened these poor, starving newcomers so severely that we dare not let them out. You'll find them huddled in the top room of the mill to keep them safe from the brutes who refuse to work for him or let them live. Mother is arranging their food, and John is trying to calm them, as some of the women are already crying to return. Ah! Here comes Mamma!"

Mrs. Thornton appeared, her face a mask of black, stern severity. To Margaret, the sight confirmed that she had arrived at a perilous moment. Yet, she had come solely at her mother's request to inquire about necessities for the progression of the mistress's illness. Mrs. Thornton's brow furrowed deeply, her lips tightening as Margaret gently described her mother's restlessness and Dr. Donaldson's recommendation for a water-bed. Mrs. Thornton offered no immediate response. Then, abruptly, she sprang up, her composure shattering.

"They're at the gates! Call John, Fanny! Call him from the mill immediately! They're battering at the gates! They'll break them down!"

Simultaneously, the gathering tramp she had ignored while listening to Margaret's words crashed against the wooden barrier outside. An angry din of voices roared behind the fence, shaking it as if invisible maddened giants used their bodies as battering rams. They pulled back only to surge forward again with renewed, united impetus, causing the strong gates to vibrate violently, like reeds before a gale.

Women clustered around the windows, transfixed by the terrifying spectacle. Mrs. Thornton, the servants, and Margaret stood frozen. Fanny had scrambled upstairs in a panic, returning moments later in hysterical sobs, collapsing onto the sofa. Mrs. Thornton scanned the room for her son, who remained in the mill. He emerged, looked up at the pale, terrified cluster of faces, and offered a smile of grim courage before locking the factory door. He then signaled a servant to unlock it, allowing Fanny—who had bolted the door in her flight—to pass.

As Mrs. Thornton stepped forward to enter the factory, the sound of her son's familiar, commanding voice seemed to strike the infuriated crowd like a blow of blood. Until now, they had been voiceless, gasping for breath as they struggled to breach the gates. Hearing him speak, however, they erupted into a fierce, unearthly groan that turned Mrs. Thornton's face ashen with fear.

John Thornton entered, flushed but his eyes gleaming with a proud, defiant light. To Margaret, who had always feared she lacked courage, this moment transformed everything. She felt not fear, but an intense, painful sympathy for the crisis unfolding.

"I'm sorry, Miss Hale," John said, stepping forward frankly. "You have visited us at this unfortunate hour, and I fear you are now involved in whatever risk we must bear. Mother! Had you not better retire to the back rooms? I suspect they may have broken through from Pinner's Lane into the stable yard. If not, you will be safer there. Go, Jane!"

Jane obeyed, followed closely by the others.

"I stay here!" Mrs. Thornton declared firmly. "Where you stand, I remain." Indeed, retreat offered no safety; the crowd had surrounded the outbuildings at the rear, unleashing a dreadful, threatening roar from behind. The servants fled to the garrets, their cries echoing in the panic. John smiled with scorn at their flight. He glanced at Margaret, who stood solitary by the window nearest the factory. Her eyes glinted, her cheeks flushed with a deep, fiery color. Sensing his gaze, she turned to him with a question that had been forming in her mind:

"Where are the poor imported workpeople? Are they in the factory?"

"Yes," he replied. "I left them cowering in a small room at the top of the back stairs, ordering them to flee down there if the mill doors were attacked. But it is not them they seek; it is me."

"When can the soldiers arrive?" his mother asked, her voice low but unsteady.

John consulted his watch with the same steady composure he displayed in all matters. He calculated briefly.

"If Williams departed the moment I told him and avoids the throng, it will be twenty minutes yet."

"Twenty minutes!" Mrs. Thornton gasped, terror finally cracking her voice.

"Close the windows instantly, Mother! These gates cannot withstand another shock. Close that window, Miss Hale."

Margaret shut her window and moved to support Mrs. Thornton's trembling hand. For several minutes, the unseen street fell silent. Mrs. Thornton stared wildly at her son's countenance, searching for an interpretation of the sudden quiet. His face was carved from rigid lines of contemptuous defiance; neither hope nor fear could be read there.

Fanny sat up. "Are they gone?" she whispered.

"Gone!" John replied. "Listen!"

They heard it: the single, straining breath, the slow creak of yielding wood, the wrench of iron, and the mighty crash of the ponderous gates falling. Fanny staggered, took a step toward her mother, and collapsed into a fainting fit. With a strength born of sheer will, Mrs. Thornton lifted her and carried her away.

"Thank God!" John murmured as he watched her exit. "Had you not better go upstairs, Miss Hale?"

Margaret's lips formed a "No," but he could not hear her. Beneath the walls, the tramp of innumerable footsteps thundered, accompanied by a fierce, low growl of angry voices that held a ferocious, satisfied murmur.

"Never mind!" John said, attempting to reassure her. "I am sorry you were trapped in this alarm, but it cannot last long. A few more minutes, and the soldiers will be here."

"Oh, God!" Margaret cried suddenly. "There is Boucher! I know that face, though he is livid with rage. Look! Look!"

"Who is Boucher?" John asked coolly, moving to the window to identify the man.

As soon as they spotted John, the crowd let out a yell that was not human—it was the demoniac hunger of a wild beast denied its prey. John drew back momentarily, dismayed by the intensity of hatred radiating from them.

"Let them yell!" he shouted. "In five minutes more... I only hope my poor Irishmen are not terrified out of their wits by such fiendlike noises. Hold your courage for five minutes, Miss Hale."

"Don't be afraid for me," she said hastily. "But what will happen in five minutes? Can you do nothing to soothese poor creatures? It is awful to witness."

"The soldiers will be here directly, and that will bring them to reason."

"To reason!" Margaret repeated quickly. "What sort of reason?"

"The only reason that deals with men who turn themselves into wild beasts. By heaven! They are rushing toward the mill door!"

"Mr. Thornton," Margaret said, her whole body shaking with passion, "go down this instant, if you are not a coward. Go down and face them like a man. Save these poor strangers whom you have brought here. Speak to your workmen as if they were human beings. Speak kindly. Do not let the soldiers come in and butcher these poor, mad creatures. I see one there who is insane. If you possess any nobility, go out and speak to them, man to man!"

He turned to look at her as she spoke. A dark cloud passed over his face, and he set his teeth tight.

"I will go. Perhaps I may ask you to accompany me downstairs and bar the door behind me; my mother and sister require that protection."

"Oh! Mr. Thornton! I do not know—I may be wrong—only—"

But he was already gone. Downstairs in the hall, he unbolted the front door. All she could do was follow quickly, fasten it behind him, and climb the stairs again with a sick, dizzy heart. Once more, she positioned herself by the farthest window. Below, on the steps, he stood visible to the thousand angry eyes of the crowd, yet she could see or hear nothing but the savage satisfaction of their rolling, angry murmur.
