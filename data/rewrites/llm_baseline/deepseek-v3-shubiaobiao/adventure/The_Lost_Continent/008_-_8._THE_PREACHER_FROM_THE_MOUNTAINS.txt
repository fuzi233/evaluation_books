The damp chill of the temple clung to my skin as I stirred from a heavy slumber, my muscles stiff from the hard stone beneath me. Nais sat watchful nearby, her eyes reflecting the dim glow of the campfires outside. The night had turned wet, a steady drizzle hissing against the temple walls, and through the cracks in the stone, I could see the ragged figures huddled around their fires, steam rising from their soaked hides.

Nais frowned. "A poor night for stealth. The cold keeps them restless. We must wait until sleep takes them deeper."

So we waited, side by side in that forgotten place, speaking little, yet the silence between us was comfortable. The hours stretched, but the camp did not quiet. Instead, a disturbance grew—a harsh, blaring note from a horn or shell, cutting through the rain. The sound swelled nearer, and the people, roused from their uneasy rest, grumbled and cursed. Then, as the noise settled, a voice rose above the murmurs, fervent and commanding.

Nais tensed. "They gather to listen. If all who are awake are drawn there, you might slip away unseen."

But fate, it seemed, had other plans. A group of rebels splashed toward the temple, calling for Nais. She had a gift for words, they said, and they needed her to counter this preacher who threatened to sway the crowd.

I braced for violence, but Nais was quick. "Come with me," she whispered. "Stay in shadow. If they question you, say nothing."

"And if they recognize me?"

Her lips twisted wryly. "They will assume you are my lover. Be grateful they do not expect a shaved chin."

The rabble paid me little heed, too eager to drag Nais to their gathering. We moved through the press of bodies, the rain spitting against the fires, the scent of wet ash thick in the air. The crowd thickened as we neared the center, where a figure stood upon the rusted frame of a war machine—an old man, bare but for a loincloth, his white hair wild about his shoulders. In his hand, he held a staff crowned with a blazing light, the Symbol of the Sun God, and his voice rang with the cadence of prophecy.

"Who are your chiefs?" he demanded, and the crowd roared back, "We have none! We bow to no rulers!"

The preacher’s eyes burned. "Then you choose suffering over salvation. The Gods set the Priests to guide you, and in their wisdom, Atlantis thrived. But pride blinded you. You forgot the hands that fed you, and so the Gods sent Phorenice—your scourge, your punishment."

A young rebel, brash and armed, leapt up beside him. "We reject all masters!" he shouted. "Even if you strike me down with your magic, I will not yield!"

The old man—Zaemon, I realized with a jolt—merely gazed at him. No gesture, no incantation, yet the youth’s defiance withered. His mouth worked soundlessly before he stumbled back, fear in his eyes. The crowd murmured, uneasy.

Zaemon’s voice dropped, low and terrible. "You beg for signs like children. The Gods do not perform for your amusement. Humble yourselves, or face annihilation."

The throng seethed, torn between defiance and dread. And then Zaemon’s gaze found me.

A ripple passed through the rebels as they sensed the shift. Nais’s fingers tightened on mine—a silent farewell. I tensed, ready to sell my life dearly. But Zaemon did not denounce me. Instead, he stepped down, the Symbol blazing before him, and the crowd shrank back, clearing a path.

He halted before me, and the world narrowed to the space between us.

"Deucalion," he said, and the name rolled like thunder. The rebels gasped, their hatred flaring, yet none dared strike. The Symbol’s light bathed us, sealing us in a circle of eerie radiance.

In the sacred tongue, he spoke: "Will you serve yourself, or Atlantis?"

"My life is the Gods’ to command."

"Then hear your duty. Phorenice seeks a husband strong enough to prolong her reign. You will return to her side—not as her enemy, but as her doom. Make her see the futility of defiance. Turn her from destruction."

A cold weight settled in my chest. Marriage to Phorenice meant power, yes—but it also meant severing the fragile bond with Nais. Yet I had sworn obedience.

"If it is the will of the Priests," I said.

Zaemon nodded, satisfied. "Then come. The night wanes."

He turned, and the crowd parted before the Symbol’s glow. I cast one last glance at Nais—her eyes fierce, her lips parted as if to speak—but no words came. The rebels’ fury simmered, yet none barred our way.

As we strode into the darkness, the rain slicking our skin, the mountain loomed ahead, its peak ringed with eternal fire. The path was set. By dawn, I would stand before Phorenice again—not as a conqueror, but as her destined end.
