In the dimly lit nursery, a tiny monarch sat enthroned, her kingdom comprised of a plush armchair and a patchwork quilt. The wind outside whispered secrets through the half-open window, its mournful sighs mingling with the soft hum of the grandfather clock in the hallway. Lady Catherine, the infant queen, regarded her reflection in the polished surface of a silver locket, her gaze lingering on the miniature portrait within. Her eyes sparkled with an inner light, as if the very essence of vanity had taken up residence within her. She was a creature of whimsy, a being both delicate and resilient, with a heart that swelled with every praise and deflated with each criticism.

Her uncle, a man of wit and wisdom, sat nearby, observing the scene with a mixture of amusement and dismay. He had tried, on one fateful occasion, to temper her exuberance with a dose of reality. "No, I am not pleased with you," he had said, recalling the events of that very morning: the rousing of the household at five o'clock, the upset water-jug, the cat-in-the-bath incident, and the unfortunate trampling of her father's hat. But his words had fallen on deaf ears, and she had responded with a cacophony of protests, her small form quivering with indignation.

Since then, her uncle had adopted a more diplomatic approach, lavishing her with effusive praise, regardless of her actions. "Oh, you are a clever girl, indeed!" he would exclaim, his words dripping with insincerity. And she would nod her head, her face aglow with pleasure, as she repeated his sentiments to the rest of the household. It was a game they played, a delicate dance of approval and expectation, with vanity as the ultimate prize.

As the afternoon wore on, the nursery grew quieter, the only sound the soft rustle of leaves outside and the occasional creak of the old wooden floorboards. Lady Catherine's uncle sat lost in thought, his mind wandering to the countless others who suffered from the same affliction: children, adults, even animals. He remembered a great Newfoundland dog he had seen in Regent's Circus, admiring its reflection in a shop window with an air of smug satisfaction that rivaled the most self-important vestry member. And the cow at the farm-house, adorned with a garland of flowers, prancing about like a schoolgirl on her first day of term.
