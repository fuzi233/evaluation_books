The morning of October first broke with a deceptive stillness. The sky was a patchwork of soft gray clouds drifting from east to west, as if the heavens themselves were resting after the feverish summer of war and storm. The *Vengeance* slid silently over the bar, her anchor dropping off Appledore with her flag hanging heavy at half-mast. On board lay the cold weight of Salvation Yeo.

A small boat pulled away from the ship, heading for the western end of the strand. Cary and Brimblecombe helped the newly blinded Amyas Leigh to his feet, guiding him slowly up the hill toward his home. Behind them, a crowd had already formed—a sea of faces filled with cheers, blessings, and the raw, weeping pity of women who knew exactly what had happened to their hero.

"Spare me, my good friends," Amyas murmured, his voice steady despite the darkness that now filled his world. "I have landed here only to go quietly home, without becoming a spectacle in the town. Think not of me, good folks, nor speak of me. Come behind me decently, as Christian men, and follow to the grave the body of a better man than I."

As he spoke, a second boat approached, carrying the coffin of Salvation Yeo, draped in the red cross of England. The people obeyed his wish. A runner was sent ahead to Burrough to summon Mrs. Leigh. When the coffin was lifted from the boat, Amyas and his friends took their places behind it as chief mourners, the ship's crew following in orderly ranks. The crowd swelled behind them, growing with every step until, before they reached Northam town, the funeral procession numbered nearly five hundred souls.

The sexton had been summoned the previous day to dig the grave. By the time they entered the churchyard, the parson waited at the gate. Mrs. Leigh remained inside her home, unable to face the throng. Though her heart ached for her son, she found a strange comfort in knowing he honored his ancient master. She sat in the bay-window, Ayacanora beside her, opening her Prayer-book to the Burial-service as the bell tolled its final notes.

"Ayacanora," she whispered, "they are burying old Master Yeo, who loved you and sought you across the wide world, saving you from the crocodile's teeth. Are you not sorry for him, child, that you look so bright today?"

Ayacanora blushed, lowering her head. Her mind was occupied with nothing but Amyas.

When the service concluded and the parson stepped back, the crowd lingered, crowding around the fresh earth. Amyas stood motionless at the head of the grave. It had been dug at the west end of the church, near the base of the tall, gray, windswept tower that watched over the land and sea. Perhaps the old man wished to see the ships crossing the bar or hear the winter wind roar through the belfry above. It was a simple fancy, yet Amyas felt a kinship with the spot; perhaps Yeo would like it too.

The people continued to stare, first at the grave, then at the blind giant standing over it, sensing by some instinct that something more was due. And so it was. Amyas straightened to his full height, waving his hand majestically as if ready to address a throne. All eyes fixed upon him.

Twice he tried to speak, but his words choked in his throat. Finally, he found his voice.

"Good people all, and seamen, among whom I was bred and to whom I return home blind this day, to dwell with you till death—Here lies the flower and pattern of all bold mariners; the truest of friends and the most terrible of foes; unchangeable in purpose, crafty in council, and swift in execution. In triumph, sober; in failure, possessing an endurance beyond mortal man. He was the first Briton to humble the pride of the Spaniard at Rio de la Hacha and Nombre, and the first to sail those South Seas which, by God's grace, shall one day be as free to English keels as the bay outside. Having been purged from his youthful sins by strange afflictions and torments unspeakable, suffering at the hands of the Popish enemy, he learned there to fear God and naught else. Having acquitted himself worthily as a righteous scourge of the Spaniard and a faithful soldier of the Lord Jesus Christ, he is now exalted to his reward, as Elijah was of old, in a chariot of fire unto heaven. Letting fall, I trust and pray, upon you who remain, the mantle of his valor and his godliness, that these shores may never lack brave and pious mariners who count their lives worthless in the cause of their Country, their Bible, and their Queen. Amen."

Feeling for his companions' hands, Amyas walked slowly from the churchyard, across the village street, and up the lane to Burrough Gate. The crowd parted in solemn silence, treating him as an awe-inspiring figure, shut up alone with all his strength and fame within the dark prison of his blindness.

He knew precisely when they reached the gates, unlocking them with his own hands and stepping boldly onto the gravel path. Cary and Brimblecombe followed, trembling; they expected a violent outburst of emotion from him or his mother, their own hearts fluttering like a girl's. He went to the door as if he could see it, felt for the latch, stepped inside, and called quietly, "Mother!"

In an instant, his mother was in his arms.

For a while, neither spoke. She wept silently, her eyes dry, while he stood firm and cheerful, his great arms wrapped around her.

"Mother," he said finally, "I am come home, you see, because I needs must. Will you take me in and look after this useless carcase? I shall not be so very troublesome, mother, shall I?" He looked down, smiling, and kissed her brow.

She said nothing, but passed her arm gently around his waist and led him inside. "Take care of your head, dear child; the doors are low." They entered together.

"Will! Jack!" Amyas called, turning back. But the two good fellows had already walked briskly away.

"I'm glad we are gone," Cary said later. "I should have made a baby of myself in another minute, watching that angel of a woman. How her face worked and how she kept it in!"

"Ah, well!" Jack replied. "There goes a brave servant of the Queen's cut off before his work was a quarter done. Heigho! I must home now to see my old father, and then—"

"And then home with me," Cary insisted. "You and I never part again! We have pulled in the same boat too long, Jack. You must not go spending your prize-money in riotous living. I must see after you, old Jack ashore, or we shall have you treating half the town in taverns for a week to come."

"Oh, Mr. Cary!" Jack protested, scandalized.

"Come home with me, and we'll poison the parson; my father shall give you the rectory."

"Oh, Mr. Cary!"

Thus, the two departed for Clovelly that very day.

Amyas sat alone. His mother had stepped out briefly to speak to the seamen who had brought his luggage, setting them down to eat and drink. Amyas remained in the old bay-window where he had sat as a tiny boy, reading "King Arthur," "Fox's Martyrs," and "The Cruelties of the Spaniards." He reached out, feeling for the books; they lay side by side just as they had twenty years prior. The window was open, and a cool air brought in the familiar scents of four-season roses, rosemary, and autumn gilliflowers.

On the table sat a dish of apples. He knew them by their scent—the very same old apples he used to gather as a boy. He reached out, taking them, feeling over their skins, playing with them as if the intervening twenty years had never existed. As his fingers traced their curves, his entire past life rose before him like a dream flashing across the mind of a drowning man. He saw every place he had ever visited, heard every word spoken to him—until he arrived at that fairy island on the Meta. He heard the roar of the cataract once more, saw the green tops of palm trees sleeping in the sunlight far above the spray, and stepped amid the smooth trunks across the flower-fringed boulders, leaping down to the gravel beach beside the pool.

Then, rising from the fern-grown rocks, appeared the beautiful vision of Ayacanora. *Where was she?* He had not thought of her until now. How he had wronged her! Let it be; he had been punished, and the account was squared. Perhaps she cared for him no longer. Who would care for a great blind ox like him, who must be fed and tended like a baby for the rest of his lazy life? *Tut!* How long his mother was away!

He began playing again with his apples, thinking of nothing but them and his climbs with Frank in the orchard years ago.

Suddenly, one slipped through his fingers and fell to the floor. He stooped to feel for it, but could not find it. *Vexatious!* He turned hastily to search another direction and struck his head sharply against the table.

Was it the pain? The slight disappointment? Or was it the sudden, humiliating realization of his blindness brought home in such a ludicrous, commonplace way? Or perhaps it was the revulsion of overstrained nerves triggered by the shock? Had he become a child once more? He knew not. But he stamped on the floor in pettishness, then checked himself and burst into a violent flood of tears.

A quick rustle passed him. The apple was replaced in his hand, and Ayacanora's voice sobbed out:

"There! There it is! Do not weep! Oh, do not weep! I cannot bear it! I will get you all you want! Only let me fetch and carry for you, tend you, feed you, lead you, like your slave, your dog! Say that I may be your slave!"

Falling to her knees at his feet, she seized both his hands and covered them with kisses.

"Yes!" she cried. "I will be your slave! I must be! You cannot help it! You cannot escape from me now! You cannot go to sea! You cannot turn your back upon wretched me. I have you safe now! Safe!" She clutched his hands triumphantly. "Ah! And what a wretch I am, to rejoice in that! To taunt him with his blindness! Oh, forgive me! I am but a poor wild girl—a wild Indian savage, you know—but—but—" and she burst into tears.

A great spasm shook the body and soul of Amyas Leigh. He sat silent for a minute, then said solemnly:

"And is this still possible? Then God have mercy upon me, a sinner!"

Ayacanora looked up at his face, inquiring. Before she could speak again, he bent down, lifting her as a lion lifts a lamb, pressing her to his bosom and covering her face with kisses.

The door opened. There was the rustle of a gown. Ayacanora sprang from him with a little cry, standing half-trembling, half-defiant, as if to declare, *He is mine now; no one dare part him from me!*

"Who is it?" asked Amyas.

"Your mother."

"You see that I am bringing forth fruits meet for repentance, mother," he said with a smile.

He heard her approach. Then a kiss and a sob passed between the women, and he felt Ayacanora sink once more upon his bosom.

"Amyas, my son," said the silver voice of Mrs. Leigh, low and dreamy, like the far-off chimes of angels' bells from the highest heaven, "fear not to take her to your heart again; for it is your mother who has laid her there."

"It is true, after all," Amyas thought to himself. "What God has joined together, man cannot put asunder."

***

From that hour, Ayacanora's power of song returned to her. Day by day, year after year, her voice rose within that happy home, soaring on skylark's wings into the highest heaven, bearing with it the peaceful thoughts of the blind giant back to the Paradises of the West, in the wake of the heroes who from that time forth sailed out to colonize another and a vaster England, to the heaven-prospered cry of *Westward-Ho!*
