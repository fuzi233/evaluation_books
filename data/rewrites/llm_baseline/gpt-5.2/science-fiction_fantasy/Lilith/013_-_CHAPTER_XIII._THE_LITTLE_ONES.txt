I had scarcely set my tool again to the bark—scrape, lift, scrape, lift, with the sap’s faint sourness rising like a bruised herb—when I heard, close at hand, a scatter of tiny voices. They were not loud; they were quick, threaded through leaves, and their laughter chimed like a flock of sheep-bells shaken in play. The sound made the wood feel inhabited in a different way: not by weight and thud, but by breath, by the small consonants of feet on moss and the clean punctuation of whistles.

They came out from among the little trees that grew like brushwood between the great trunks—creeping, peering, then suddenly, as if a curtain had been pulled aside, pouring forth until there were scores and scores of them around my knees and up the roots, bright-eyed and brown-limbed, with hair like seed-down and bracken.

I signed to them—clumsy signals with my hands—that the giants had only just gone, that they were near. They answered with a ripple of mirth.

“The wind is quite clean,” said one, and another echoed it, and the phrase ran through them like a tune. “They are too blind to see us!”

A little fellow cocked his head at the rope about my ankles, where it bit and rubbed.

“Do you like that rope?” he asked.

“I want them to think I cannot take it off,” I whispered back, not knowing why I too lowered my voice, as if the air itself could betray us.

“They can scarcely see their own feet!” he cried, and danced where he stood, a tiny heel-drumming on a root. “Walk with short steps and they will think the rope is all right.”

As he capered, one of the bigger girls—still small to me, but among them tall, with a steadier face—dropped to her knees. Her fingers went to the knot. I smiled at the futility of those pretty hands against the giants’ clumsy tying; but she touched the cord as if it were dough she had kneaded a hundred times, felt where it crossed, found the strain, and in a breath the thing lay loose. She had unmade it the way one unthreads a string from a stitched seam—no tugging, no waste.

They made me sit down as if I were another child and not a captive. Then they fed me.

It was not the giants’ coarse fare, flung like fodder, but little fruits—plums no bigger than the end of my thumb, berries with skins tight as lacquer, and something like a quince in miniature whose scent rose before it touched my tongue, all honeyed and floral, with a faint sting like ginger at the back of the throat. They placed them with care, climbing the tree’s roots, reaching up to my mouth, their fingertips cool and quick, leaving on my lips the dust of bloom and crushed leaf. The first bite burst with cold juice, and the world, which had seemed built for bruising, suddenly remembered sweetness.

Afterward the smaller ones began to play with me—tugging at my sleeves, clambering onto my shoulders, sliding down my back, hiding behind my elbows, darting in and out like wrens. Their bodies were light, but their merriment had weight enough to topple any intention of work. When the first group tired, others took their place, and the hours went by in a round of their inventions, each game rising out of the last, as if play were a craft passed down in a lineage older than my own habits of seriousness.

Toward sunset the acoustic world changed. The high tinkling voices drew back; the wood seemed to hold its breath. From far off came the heavy approach: not one sound but a cadence—thud, drag, thud—each step a dull blow to earth that made the roots under me tremble.

The little people sprang away as if the ground had grown hot. I made haste to wind the rope again about my ankles and knot it as I had seen the giants do, though my fingers were slow and sore.

“We must have a care,” said the girl who had freed me. Her voice was grave now, the way a careful cook’s voice changes when a child reaches toward a pot. “A crush of one of their horrid stumpy feet might kill a very little one.”

“Can they not perceive you at all then?” I murmured.

“They might see something move,” she said. “And if the children were in a heap on the top of you, as they were a moment ago, it would be terrible; for they hate every live thing but themselves—Not that they are much alive either!”

She lifted her face and whistled like a bird—so exact an imitation that the note seemed not made by her throat but borrowed from the branches. The instant the whistle ended, not one of them was to be seen or heard. The girl herself vanished as if the wood had swallowed her whole. Only the quiet remained—quiet with a listening edge.

My master, as doubtless he counted himself, came and loomed. He bent, untied my ankles, and dragged me to the door of his hut. There he threw me down, tied my feet again, gave me a kick—his foot like a blunt log—and left me.

I might then have escaped. The rope was nothing to me now; the knot was no longer a mystery. But I had—at last—friends. Their small hands had put fruit to my mouth; their small voices had made the brutal forest sound like a home. I could not think of leaving them.

“To-morrow,” I told myself, with a delight that was almost hunger, “I shall see them again!”

And indeed, from the moment silence settled in the huts until sleep took me, I heard them whispering all about me—soft sibilants in the grass, tiny breathy calls like insects speaking. I knew, without seeing, that I was watched; and the watch was loving.

After that I think they hardly ever left me quite alone.

The giants I did not come to know at all; I suspect there was scarcely anything in them to know. They never grew friendly, but they were too stupid to invent cruelties beyond what their clumsiness did by accident and habit. More than once I avoided a bad kick by catching a descending foot and giving its owner a fall; when he sprawled, amazed at his own gravity betrayed, he never on that occasion renewed his attempt. Their world was a world of impacts.

The Little Ones’ world was a world of attentions.

While I scraped at bark and laboured in the monotony the giants required, the small folk kept coming and going—amusing and delighting me, taking the misery, and much of the weariness, out of my toil. Every day I grew more loath to leave them. Very soon I loved them more than I can tell.

They did not know much in the giants’ sense—no counting, no measuring, no hoarding of facts like stones in a pocket. Yet they were wise, and seemed capable of learning anything; and their wisdom was woven into their care for one another as surely as a good broth is made from bones and patience.

I had no bed save the bare ground, but almost as often as I woke, it was in a nest of children—one or other of them in my arms, though which I seldom could tell until the light came, for they ordered the succession among themselves. When one crept into my bosom, unconsciously I clasped him there; the rest lay close around me, the smaller nearer, as if warmth were a thing to be distributed with justice. Cold could not find me through that living quilt.

The first thing they did in the morning, and the last before sunset, was to bring the good giant plenty to eat. They fed him as they fed me: not with submission, but with the household rhythm of those who keep someone alive because life matters. Their offerings had the scent of crushed green, of fruit opened by fingernail, of the wood’s own pantry.

One morning I woke and found myself alone.

For a moment the loneliness startled me—an empty plate after one has grown used to company. Then I heard subdued sounds of approach: not the giants’ thud, but the careful, gathered hush of many little steps constrained by love. Presently the girl I had first spoken with appeared from the wood, tallest and gravest of the community, regarded by all as their mother. Behind her came the multitude in jubilation manifest—but silent, lest they should rouse the sleeping giant at whose door I lay.

She carried a boy-baby in her arms.

Hitherto a girl-baby, apparently about a year old, had been the youngest. Three of the bigger girls were her nurses, but they shared their treasure with all the rest. Among the Little Ones, dolls were unknown; the bigger had the smaller, and the smaller the still less, to tend and play with. Care was their play; play was their schooling.

“Lona,” I heard someone breathe—a name passed between them like a word for bread.

Lona came to me and laid the infant in my arms. He opened his eyes, looked at me as if tasting me with sight, closed them again, and fell asleep with his cheek against my wrist.

“He loves you already,” Lona said, her face alight with a pride that was not possessive, only glad.

“Where did you find him?” I asked.

“In the wood, of course,” she answered, eyes beaming as if she had brought in a harvest. “Where we always find them. Isn’t he a beauty? We’ve been out all night looking for him. Sometimes it is not easy to find!”

“How do you know when there is one to find?”

“I cannot tell,” she replied. “Every one makes haste to tell the other, but we never find out who told first. Sometimes I think one must have said it asleep, and another heard it half-awake. When there is a baby in the wood, no one can stop to ask questions; and when we have found it, then it is too late.”

“Do more boy or girl babies come to the wood?” I persisted.

“They don’t come to the wood; we go to the wood and find them.”

“Are there more boys or girls of you now?” I tried again, changing the question as if that might unlock it.

She hesitated, brows drawing together—their sign of a place where language snagged. “I do not know.”

“You can count them, surely.”

“We never do that,” she said, as if I had suggested weighing sunlight. “We shouldn’t like to be counted.”

“Why?”

“It wouldn’t be smooth,” she replied simply. “We would rather not know.”

The answer made me think of the way names can bruise what they claim to describe. In my world we count and label until the living thing becomes a number that can be traded; here even the notion felt like grit between teeth.

“Where do the babies come from first?” I asked, and heard my own voice take on the greedy tone of a historian prying into an archive.

“From the wood—always. There is no other place they can come from.” She knew where they came from last, and thought nothing else was to be known about their advent.

“How often do you find one?”

She smiled at me over the baby’s head. “Such a happy thing takes all the glad we’ve got, and we forget the last time. You too are glad to have him—are you not, good giant?”

“Yes, indeed, I am,” I answered, and it was true: the weight of that sleeping warmth in my arms made my heart ache like hunger.

“But how do you feed him?”

“I will show you,” she said, and went away—returning almost at once with two or three ripe little plums.

She put one to the baby’s lips. “He would open his mouth if he were awake,” she said, and took him back into her arms as naturally as breathing. She squeezed the fruit until a drop rose to the surface—dark gold, fragrant—and touched it again to his mouth. Without waking, he began at once to suck it, small mouth working with a seriousness that made every onlooker quiet. Lona went on, slowly squeezing, careful as if she were pressing oil from olives, until nothing but skin and stone were left.

“There!” she cried in a tone of gentle triumph. “A big-apple world it would be with nothing for the babies! We wouldn’t stop in it—would we, darling? We would leave it to the bad giants!”

“But what if you let the stone into the baby’s mouth when you were feeding him?” I said, thinking of choking hazards and all the crude accidents of bodies.

“No mother would do that,” she answered, almost offended—not by me, but by the imagined carelessness. “I shouldn’t be fit to have a baby!”

As she spoke I saw what she might become: a woman strong in tenderness, a keeper of lives and flavours. Yet the question rose in me like smoke: what became of them when they grew up? Where did they go? And again, behind that: where did they come from first?

“Will you tell me where you lived before?” I said.

“Here,” she replied.

“Have you never lived anywhere else?”

“Never. We all came from the wood. Some think we dropped out of the trees.”

“How is it there are so many of you quite little?” I asked, and my words felt blunt beside their smooth not-knowing.

“I don’t understand,” she said. “Some are less and some are bigger. I am very big.”

“Baby will grow bigger, won’t he?”

“Of course he will!”

“And will you grow bigger?”

“I don’t think so. I hope not. I am the biggest.” Her gaze flicked away, as if the thought made a shadow on the air. “It frightens me sometimes.”

“Why should it frighten you?”

She did not answer.

“How old are you?” I tried, reaching for time as one reaches for a common tongue.

“I do not know what you mean,” she said. “We are all just that.”

“How big will the baby grow?”

“I cannot tell.” Then, with trouble in her voice, she added, “Some begin to grow after we think they have stopped. That is a frightful thing. We don’t talk about it!”

“What makes it frightful?”

She was silent a moment, then said low, “We fear they may be beginning to grow giants.”

“Why should you fear that?”

“Because it is so terrible. I don’t want to talk about it!” She pressed the baby to her bosom with such anxious protectiveness that I dared not press her further. In that embrace was a whole unspoken genealogy of dread.

Before long I began to perceive in two or three of the smaller children traces of greed and selfishness—little grabs, little refusals, a berry held back while others watched. I saw too that the bigger girls cast on these children not infrequent glances of anxiety, like elders noticing blight in a row of beans.

None of them put a hand to my work; they would do nothing for the giants. But they never relaxed their loving ministrations to me. They would sing to me, one after another, for hours; sometimes the song was no more than a chain of sounds that fitted their mouths like a dialect I could not enter—bright syllables, soft clicks, breathy vowels that seemed made to travel through leaves. Sometimes they climbed the tree to reach my mouth and popped fruit into it with their dainty fingers, leaving smears of juice that smelled of summer and earth. Always they kept constant watch for the approach of a giant, listening to the ground more than the air.

Sometimes they would sit and tell me stories—childish stories that often seemed to mean hardly anything, and yet the cadence of them carried a kind of memory, a taste I could not name. It troubled me: I could sense significance the way one smells bread before seeing the oven, but the words would not translate themselves into my mind. Their experience was untranslatable, and the gap in me had the ache of a language dying.

Now and then they would call a general assembly to amuse me. On one such occasion a moody little fellow sang a strange crooning song, with a refrain so pathetic that, although unintelligible to me, it made the tears run down my face.

The sight of water startled them.

Those who saw my tears stared as if some impossible fruit had appeared on my cheeks. Then first I bethought myself that I had not once, in that world, looked on water, falling or lying or running. Plenty there had been in some long-vanished age—that was plain enough from the old channels and the worn stones—but the Little Ones had never seen any before they saw my tears.

And yet it seemed they had some dim, instinctive perception of their origin; for a very small child went up to the singer, shook his clenched pud in his face, and cried in the blunt baby-speech of their kind something like, “’Ou skeeze ze juice out of ze good giant’s seeberries! Bad giant!”

The accusation, ridiculous and tender, made my throat hurt more than the song had done.

One day, as Lona sat with the baby in her arms at the foot of my tree, the question that had been circling in me returned.

“How is it,” I said, “that I never see any children among the giants?”

She stared, searching my words for a handle. Then she answered as if it were self-evident: “They are giants; there are no little ones.”

“Have they never any children?”

“No; there are never any in the wood for them. They do not love them. If they saw ours, they would stamp them.”

“Is there always the same number of the giants then?” I asked. “I thought—before I had time to know better—that they were your fathers and mothers.”

She burst into the merriest laughter; it rang like struck glasses. “No, good giant; we are their firsters.”

But as she said it, the merriment died out of her, and her face went white with fear, as if she had spoken a word that did not like being spoken.

I stopped working. I stared at her, bewildered. “How can that be?”

“I do not say; I do not understand,” she replied quickly, as if she wished to fold the thought up and hide it away. “But we were here and they not. They go from us. I am sorry, but we cannot help it. They could have helped it.”

“How long have you been here?” I asked, hoping time might throw side-light on the matter.

“Always, I think,” she said. “I think somebody made us always.”

I turned back to my scraping. The bark came away in thin curls like peelings; the sound was dry, oddly domestic. She saw I did not understand.

“The giants were not made always,” she went on. “If a Little One doesn’t care, he grows greedy, and then lazy, and then big, and then stupid, and then bad. The dull creatures don’t know that they come from us. Very few of them believe we are anywhere. They say, NONSENSE!—Look at little Blunty: he is eating one of their apples! He will be the next! Oh! oh! he will soon be big and bad and ugly, and not know it!”

She pointed. A child stood a little way off by himself, eating an apple nearly as big as his head. The fruit was pale green, thick-skinned, with a harsh shine like wax. He bit into it greedily, the crunch loud in the hush, as if he wanted the sound of his eating to be heard. I had often thought he did not look so good as the rest; now, with the apple juice shining around his mouth, he looked—yes—disgusting, as if the fruit were changing him from the inside out.

“I will take the horrid thing from him!” I cried, and made to rise.

“It is no use,” Lona said sadly. “We have done all we can, and it is too late. We were afraid he was growing, for he would not believe anything told him; but when he refused to share his berries, and said he had gathered them for himself, then we knew it! He is a glutton, and there is no hope of him. It makes me sick to see him eat!”

“Could not some of the boys watch him,” I said, “and not let him touch the poisonous things?”

“He may have them if he will: it is all one—to eat the apples, and to be a boy that would eat them if he could.” Her voice held the weary authority of an elder who has seen the same mistake ripen over seasons. “No; he must go to the giants! He belongs to them. You can see how much bigger he is than when first you came! He is bigger since yesterday.”

“He is as like that hideous green lump in his hand as boy could look,” I muttered.

“It suits what he is making himself,” she returned.

“His head and it might change places!”

“Perhaps they do!” she said, and though she smiled, her eyes did not.

“Does he want to be a giant?” I asked.

“He hates the giants,” Lona answered, “but he is making himself one all the same: he likes their apples!” She hugged the baby closer, as if appetite were an infection. “Oh baby, baby, he was just such a darling as you when we found him!”

“He will be very miserable when he finds himself a giant.”

“Oh, no; he will like it well enough. That is the worst of it.”

“Will he hate the Little Ones?”

“He will be like the rest; he will not remember us—most likely will not believe there are Little Ones. He will not care; he will eat his apples.”

“Do tell me how it will come about,” I pleaded. “I understand your world so little! I come from a world where everything is different.”

“I do not know about world. What is it? What more but a word in your beautiful big mouth?—That makes it something!” She said it with wonder, and I felt the power of a dominant tongue—how naming pretends to mastery, how a word can be used like a fence. Yet in her mouth “world” was just a strange seed that might or might not take root.

“Never mind the word,” I said. “Tell me what next will happen to Blunty.”

“He will wake one morning and find himself a giant—not like you, good giant, but like any other bad giant.” Lona spoke as if reciting a known recipe: if you mix greed and laziness and let it sit, this is what it becomes. “You will hardly know him, but I will tell you which. He will think he has been a giant always, and will not know you, or any of us.”

Then, as if repeating something she had heard from an older voice, she added, “The giants have lost themselves, Peony says, and that is why they never smile. I wonder whether they are not glad because they are bad, or bad because they are not glad. But they can’t be glad when they have no babies!” She looked down at the infant’s sleeping face. “I wonder what bad means, good giant!”

“I wish I knew no more about it than you,” I said. “But I try to be good, and mean to keep on trying.”

“So do I,” she answered softly, “and that is how I know you are good.”

A long pause followed. Around us the wood’s small sounds returned—the tick of a beetle, the far creak of a bough. Somewhere a giant snored like a distant saw.

“Then you do not know where the babies come from into the wood?” I asked, making one attempt more, though I felt the question itself was a kind of violence, a prying at a mystery that resisted my language.

“There is nothing to know there,” she said, almost impatient now. “They are in the wood; they grow there.”

“Then how is it you never find one before it is quite grown?” I persisted.

She knitted her brows. “They’re not there till they’re finished,” she said at last.

“It is a pity the little sillies can’t speak till they’ve forgotten everything they had to tell!” I remarked, half in jest, half in grief for all the lost stories that never make it into words.

“Little Tolma,” Lona said, “the last before this baby, looked as if she had something to tell, when I found her under a beech-tree, sucking her thumb, but she hadn’t. She only looked up at me—oh, so sweetly! She will never go bad and grow big! When they begin to grow big they care for nothing but bigness; and when they cannot grow any bigger, they try to grow fatter.” Disgust curled her lip. “The bad giants are very proud of being fat.”

“So they are in my world,” I said under my breath; “only they do not say fat there, they say rich.”

“In one of their houses,” Lona continued, “sits the biggest and fattest of them—so proud that nobody can see him; and the giants go to his house at certain times, and call out to him, and tell him how fat he is, and beg him to make them strong to eat more and grow fat like him.”

As she spoke, I thought of temples built around emptiness, of languages bent to praise what cannot even answer. The wood around us, for all its strangeness, suddenly felt cleaner than that unseen throne.

In time the rumour reached my ears that Blunty had vanished. I saw a few grave faces among the bigger ones, but he did not seem much missed; the community closed around the loss as a hearth closes after a spark has flown.

The next morning Lona came to me and whispered, “Look! look there—by that quince-tree: that is the giant that was Blunty! Would you have known him?”

I looked where she pointed. A giant stood by the quince-tree, staring with a dull, fogged expression, an apple in his hand. His cheeks were slack; his eyes seemed set too far apart, as if thought had retreated inward and left only appetite at the door.

“Never,” I answered. “But now you tell me, I could fancy it might be Blunty staring through a fog. He does look stupid!”

“He is for ever eating those apples now,” she said.

“That is what comes of Little Ones that won’t be little,” I murmured.

“They call it growing-up in my world,” I said to myself, and the phrase tasted bitter, like peel left too long on the tongue. If only she would teach me to grow the other way, and become a Little One—shall I ever be able to laugh like them? I had had the chance, and had flung it from me.

Blunty and I were alike.

He did not know his loss, and I had to be taught mine.
