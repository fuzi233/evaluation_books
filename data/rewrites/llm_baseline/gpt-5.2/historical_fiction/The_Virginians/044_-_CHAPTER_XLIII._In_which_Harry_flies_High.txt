So Harry Warrington of Virginia took rooms in Bond Street, and for a while it seemed London had been laid out like a table expressly for his appetite. He lived where coaches rolled day and night like a ceaseless surf; where the street-cries stitched the hours together—oysters! hot pies! broom!—and where, beneath the winter soot, kitchens sent up their honest signals: beef fat browning, coffee scorching, wet wool steaming in passages. In such a town a young man might fancy fortune itself had a smell—made of warm wine, truffles, wax candles, and new-guinea gold.

The world called him Fortunate Youth, and—being young, comely, well-born, and, above all, visibly provided—society found him eminently tolerable. He was liked because he could like in return: because he could sing when the bottle made men tender; because he could ride when the morning made men rash; because he had that modest blush with ladies which is often taken for virtue, and is at least a kind of courtesy; because with tradesmen, chairmen, and serving folk he had a way of paying largely and speaking as if their annoyance were an affront to him. He carried his rank like a sword worn easy in its belt: not always drawn, but always there.

The winter of ’56-’57 offered him its round of pleasures: Italian operas, which he praised with the politeness of a man who would rather hear a hunting-horn; the theatres where Garrick and Pritchard made whole houses breathe together; masquerades with their rustle of silk and their false laughter; and private assemblies that began and ended in cards, where the air grew stale with melted tallow and hot hands, and where Harry, who preferred higher and fairer play, found himself missing the downright, roaring honesty of White’s.

One day his kinsman, Lord Castlewood, carried him to Court and presented him at St. James’s. The King had come in from Kensington, and looked as if he had brought the damp with him. His Majesty received the young Virginian with a glance as short as a snapped thread.

“Oh—heard of you from Lady Yarmouth,” he said; then, turning to Castlewood and speaking in German, added a sentence which ended like a slap. And with that the Defender of the Faith showed Harry the royal back.

Castlewood withdrew, yellow with dismay, as if a chill had struck him through his lace.

“What did he say?” Harry asked, not much troubled, only curious.

“His Majesty thinks they play too high at White’s, and is displeased,” Castlewood whispered.

“If he does not want us, we had better not come again,” Harry said, simply. “I never somehow considered that German fellow a real King of England.”

“Hush! for Heaven’s sake, hold your confounded colonial tongue!” Castlewood hissed. “Don’t you see the walls have ears?”

“And what then?” Harry said, looking about him. “Why, hang me, it is a curiosity. They were shaking hands with me and bowing and sweetening their voices a moment ago—and now they step away as if I carried the plague.”

A hand like a ham, brown and hard as a block on a butcher’s stall, came out of the crowd and closed on Harry’s.

“Shake hands, nephew,” said a broad-faced gentleman in a great old-fashioned wig and scarlet-laced waistcoat. “I heard what you said. I have ears like the wall, look you. If other folk show you the cold shoulder, I’ll give you my hand. There’s something of my brother about your eyes. Though in your island you grow more wiry and thin, I reckon. I am thine uncle, child. Sir Miles Warrington. My lord knows me well enough.”

Castlewood, made suddenly smaller by fear, bowed as if a pistol were at his head. “Yes—my dear Harry—this is your paternal uncle, Sir Miles.”

“Might as well have come to see us in Norfolk as dangle about playing the fool at Tunbridge, Mr. Warrington—or Mr. Esmond—which do you call yourself?” the Baronet went on, in that downright, farm-yard voice that seemed to bring the smell of stable and roast meat into the gilded room. “The old lady calls herself Madam Esmond, don’t she?”

“My mother is not ashamed of her father’s name, nor am I,” Harry answered, the pride rising in him like heat.

“Well said, lad!” Sir Miles cried, clapping him with approval. “Come home and eat a bit of mutton with Lady Warrington at three, in Hill Street—if you can do without your White’s kickshaws. You needn’t look frightened, my Lord Castlewood! I shall tell no tales out of school.”

“I—I am sure Sir Miles Warrington will act as a gentleman!” Castlewood stammered.

“Belike he will,” growled the Baronet, wheeling on his heel. “And thou wilt come, young man, at three. Mind, good roast mutton waits for nobody. Thou hast a great look of thy father. Lord bless us, how we used to beat each other! He was smaller and younger, but often had the best of it. Take it he was henpecked when he married, and Madam Esmond took the spirit out of him when she got him in her island. Virginia’s an island. Ain’t it an island?”

Harry laughed. “No.”

“Well, island or not, thou must come and tell all about it to my lady. She’ll know whether ’tis an island or not.”

When Sir Miles had tramped away, Castlewood drew nearer with an anxious, confidential air, as if the very draughts in the corridor might carry tales.

“My dear Mr. Warrington,” he said, “in this great city every man has enemies; there is detraction and scandal beyond belief. I never spoke to you about Sir Miles precisely because I did know him, and because we have had differences. Should he permit himself remarks to my disparagement, you will receive them cum grano, and remember it is from an enemy they come.”

They went out into St. James’s Street, where the coach-wheels made their perpetual thunder, and the cries and hoofbeats rose and fell like a living thing. By the time Harry reached White’s, the sound of his cold reception had flown ahead of him as swiftly as a rumour always does when it can feed on envy. The King had turned his back upon him. The King was jealous of his favour with the favourite. Harry was au mieux with Lady Yarmouth. Men who would have crossed the room to avoid him yesterday now pressed near to congratulate him, as if royal sulkiness were a badge.

And when Harry, obedient to roast mutton and blood, appeared at three in Hill Street, Sir Miles rallied him at table in his plain, hearty fashion, as though a King’s back were no more than a weathercock.

Lady Warrington at first received him with a majesty so stiff it seemed starched; she had the air of a woman who had taken up salvation as one takes up housekeeping—resolutely, with rules and a cupboard of admonitions. Later, when the first scrutiny had passed, she confessed (with a chill candour) that the town’s account of him had been so bad she had scarcely known how to greet him.

The Misses Warrington—prim young women with clean collars and careful voices—curtsied and said, “How d’ye do, cousin?” and “No, thank you, cousin,” as if even gratitude must be rationed. The little boy, the heir, dined too, guarded by his governor. After dinner Sir Miles gave the child a small glass of port, and the warm wine loosened the innocent tongue as surely as any sermon.

Master Miles stared long at Harry, as if he sought wickedness the way a child seeks a bruise beneath a stocking.

“Are you wicked, cousin Harry?” he asked at last. “You don’t look very wicked!”

“My dear Master Miles!” the tutor, Mr. Walker, protested, reddening to the ears.

“But you said he was wicked!” the boy cried. “You did. And mamma said so too—that Lady Yarmouth was a wicked woman.”

“We are all miserable sinners, Miley,” Sir Miles said, half laughing. “Haven’t you heard the clergyman say so every Sunday?”

“Yes,” the child insisted, “but not so very wicked as cousin Harry. Is it true you gamble, cousin, and drink all night with wicked men, and go with wicked women? You know you said so, Mr. Walker—and mamma said so.”

“And you are a little pitcher,” Sir Miles roared, pleased and vexed at once. “Take him to his sisters, Mr. Walker, and Topsham shall give thee a ride in the park on thy little horse.”

The promise of the pony dried the tears that had been swelling; for Master Miles began to bawl that he would rather stay with his wicked cousin than go to his sisters.

“They’ve made you a sad reputation among ’em, nephew,” Sir Miles said when the child was gone. “My wife, since my poor eldest died, has took to—hum!—Tottenham Court Road and Mr. Whitfield’s preaching; and we’ve had one Ward about the house, a friend of Mr. Walker’s, who has told bad stories about you and your brother at home.”

“As for me, sir, let him say what he pleases,” Harry cried, warmed with port and quick blood; “but I’ll break any man’s bones who dares say a word against my brother! That fellow wasn’t fit to buckle my dear George’s shoe; and if I find him repeating in England what he dared to say in our house in Virginia, I promise him a second caning.”

“You stand up for your friends, nephew Harry,” Sir Miles said, looking at him with a rough liking. “Fill thy glass. Thou art not as bad as thou hast been painted. I always told my lady so. I drink Madam Esmond Warrington’s health—of Virginia—and will have a full bumper for that toast.”

Harry emptied his glass, filled again, and drank Lady Warrington and Master Miles.

“Thou wouldst be heir to four thousand acres in Norfolk, did he die, though,” Sir Miles added, with the bluntness of a man who counts fields as other men count cards.

“God forbid,” Harry said quickly, “and be praised that I have acres enough in Virginia of my own.”

After dinner he drank coffee with Lady Warrington—the bitter fragrance rising from the cup like an old friend calling him back to mornings at home—and talked easily with the young ladies. One of them had a look that made him think of Fanny Mountain, and his manner softened toward her, as if memory were a seasoning that changed the whole dish. When he took his leave, they agreed their wicked cousin was not nearly so wicked as rumour had made him; Lady Warrington, indeed, conceived strong hopes of plucking him from the pit by a steady diet of improving pages.

That evening she sent him a good book—Law’s Call—with a pretty note praying it might profit him. And then, having discharged conscience, she and her daughters went to a rout at the house of a minister’s lady, where piety and vanity took hands and danced without blushing.

Harry, before going to White’s, had driven to Mr. Sparks in Tavistock Street and purchased more trinkets for his cousins—“from their aunt in Virginia,” he said. He was full of kindness in those days; prosperity made him blaze like a well-fed fire. The girls in Hill Street were delighted and wrote a collective note to Madam Esmond Warrington across the seas—a letter that would astonish her when it reached Virginia in spring, when she and Mountain and Fanny were visiting the grim, deserted Castlewood again, and the snows were gone and a thousand peach-trees stood flushed with bloom like a young bride’s cheek.

“Poor boy,” the mother would think then. “This is some present he gave his cousins in my name, in the time of his prosperity—nay, of his extravagance and folly. How quickly his wealth has passed away! But he ever had a kind heart for the poor Mountain; and we must not forget him in his need. It behoves us to be more than ever careful of our own expenses, my good people!”

And so, one may fancy, they warmed themselves by one log, and ate from one dish, and worked by one candle, while the house’s sounds—scrape of ladle in pot, creak of spinning-wheel, the quiet crackle of stingy fire—made their own small music against the wind. Yet pinching a household does not always save it: the servants, pressed more and more, lied and stole more and more; what was hoarded in one corner went missing in another, as if thrift and loss were sisters who could not be separated.

Meanwhile, in Bond Street, one afternoon, Harry sat in his dressing-gown—satin against his wrists, fine linen at his throat—sipping chocolate that had been whipped to a froth and scented faintly of vanilla. The cup was warm in his hand, the room handsome, the fire bright; but care lay on him like a heavy cloak. Outside, the city’s roar kept its endless sermon: wheels, cries, iron-shod hoofs, the occasional hammering that made a man’s teeth clench without knowing why. London’s noise could turn rich rooms into cages.

A few weeks before, when luck had sat by him at the table like a flattering companion, he had royally bidden Parson Sampson draw up a list of his debts which he, Harry Warrington, would pay. Sampson had gone to work, and brought him—not all (no man ever sets down all), but enough to make a decent show of conscience.

The chaplain waited humbly at breakfast until Harry chose to appear. At last the young man came in pale and languid, curl-papers still in his hair, his appetite gone. Sampson, fingering his schedule in his pocket, ventured to ask whether his patron had had a bad night. Indeed he had: Harry had been brought home from White’s by two chairmen at five in the morning; one of the chair windows would not shut, and rain and snow had blown in, and he had caught a confounded cold and an even worse humour. Sampson tried his small jests, talked of muffins and eggs, tea and news, plays and players; but the smiles he sought were as hard to raise as a soufflé in a draught.

At last, however, Harry broke into a laugh—sudden, loud, and anything but merry—when Sampson drew out his paper and said in a piteous tone, “Here is that schedule of debts which your honour asked for—two hundred and forty-three pounds—every shilling I owe in the world, thank Heaven!—that is—ahem!—every shilling of which the payment will in the least inconvenience me—and I need not tell my dearest patron I shall consider him my saviour and benefactor!”

Harry took the paper, eyed Sampson with a look that made the poor divine’s stomach sink, and laughed again with a bitter edge. An oath or two followed—hot, disconnected words, like sparks that fly from a pokered coal.

“Confound it,” Harry said, “why didn’t you bring it on Monday?”

“Confound me, why did I not bring it on Monday?” Sampson echoed, timidly, as if agreeing might mend it. “It is my luck—my usual luck. Have the cards been against you, Mr. Warrington?”

“Yes,” Harry said, with a scowl. “A plague on them. Monday night and last night both went against me. Don’t be frightened, chaplain; there’s money enough in the locker yet. But I must go into the City and get some.”

“What—sell out, sir?” Sampson asked, his voice pretending alarm and betraying relief.

“Sell out, sir? Yes. I borrowed a hundred off Mackreth in counters last night, and must pay him at dinner-time. I will do your business nevertheless, and never fear, my good Mr. Sampson. Come to breakfast to-morrow, and we will see and deliver your reverence from the Philistines.”

When the chaplain had gone, Harry’s shoulders dropped, and his head sank toward his chest. He sat over the fire, beating the coals about with the poker as if he could stir luck into them, muttering disjointed wicked words that showed his agitation without easing it. The chocolate on the table cooled and thickened, its sweetness turning cloying; the room smelled of scorched sugar and damp wool from his cloak, and he felt as if all London’s noise were inside his skull.

In this mood he was interrupted by a visitor who, on any other day—even on this day with conscience gnawing—would have been welcome: Colonel Lambert. The Colonel came in his military dress, a cloak over it, smelling of cold air and the road, as if he had brought the country’s silence (and the memory of it) into Bond Street’s din. He had been to the Captain-General’s levee and came thence to see his young friend.

Harry fancied Lambert’s greeting colder than usual, but his own affairs were too loud within him for him to attend closely. How were the ladies of Oakhurst? and Miss Hetty, who had been ailing when he passed in autumn? Better? He was glad. They were in London now, staying with their friend Lord Wrotham? Harry said he was delighted—though his face, truthfully, did not brighten as the words required.

“And so you live at White’s and with the great folks,” Lambert said, watching him, “and you fare sumptuously every day, and you pay your court at St. James’s, and make one at my Lady Yarmouth’s routs, and at all the card-parties in the Court end of the town?”

“My dear Colonel,” Harry answered, a little stiffly, “I do what other folks do.”

“Other folks are richer folks than some folks, my dear lad.”

“Sir!” Harry cried, rising on pride as on sudden wind. “I would thank you to believe I owe nothing for which I cannot pay!”

“I should never have spoken of your affairs,” Lambert said, not taking up the heat, “but that you yourself confided them to me. I hear all sorts of stories about the Fortunate Youth. Only at his Royal Highness’s even to-day they were saying how rich you were already, and I did not undeceive them—”

“Colonel Lambert, I cannot help the world gossiping about me!” Harry broke in, impatience sharpening.

“—And what prodigious sums you had won,” Lambert continued steadily. “Eighteen hundred one night—two thousand another—six or eight thousand in all. Oh, there were gentlemen from White’s at the levee too, I can assure you, and the army can fling a main as well as you civilians.”

“I wish they would meddle with their own affairs,” Harry said, scowling.

“And I, too, you look as if you were going to say,” Lambert replied. “Well, my boy, it is my affair, and you must let Theo’s father and Hetty’s father—and Harry Warrington’s father’s old friend—say how it is my affair.”

He drew from his pocket a packet, the generous skirts and lappets of his coat accommodating it with ease, and laid it down as if laying down a charge.

“Look you, Harry. These trinkets you sent with the kindest heart in the world to people who love you, and would cut off their little hands to spare you needless pain—could never be bought by a young fellow with two or three hundred a year. Why, a nobleman might buy these things, or a rich City banker, and send them to his—to his daughters, let us say.”

“Sir,” Harry said, burning red, “as you say, I meant only kindness.”

“But you must not give them to my girls,” Lambert said. “Hester and Theodosia Lambert must not be dressed up with the winnings off the gaming-table, saving your presence. It goes to my heart to bring them back. Mrs. Lambert will keep her present, which is of small value, and sends you her love and a God bless you—and so say I, Harry Warrington, with all my heart.”

His voice thickened; his face grew very red; he passed a hand across his eyes, and then held that same hand out to Harry—an honest hand, offered as it had been offered in old days, without bargain.

But rebellion was strong in Harry; shame and pride, like two cooks quarrelling over the same pot, spoiled what might have been wholesome. He rose and did not take the hand.

“Give me leave to tell Colonel Lambert,” he said, each word rustling like stiff silk, “that I have had somewhat too much advice from him. You are forever volunteering it, sir, and when I don’t ask it. You make it your business to inquire about my gains at play and the company I keep. What right have you to control my amusements or my companions? I strive to show my sense of your former kindness by little presents to your family, and you fling—you bring them back.”

“I can’t do otherwise, Mr. Warrington,” Lambert said, looking very sad.

“Such a slight may mean nothing here, sir,” Harry cried, the Virginian in him leaping up, “but in our country it means war, sir! God forbid I should talk of drawing a sword against the father of ladies who have been as mother and sister to me; but you have wounded my heart, Colonel Lambert—you have, I won’t say insulted, but humiliated me—and this is a treatment I will bear from no man alive. My servants will attend you to the door, sir!”

And with that, in his brocade dressing-gown and all the state a young man can borrow from cloth, Harry Warrington turned away and walked into his bedroom, shutting out with the door not only an old friend, but for a moment the very possibility that any language—English or Virginian, courtly or homely—could translate what he felt without wounding him anew.
