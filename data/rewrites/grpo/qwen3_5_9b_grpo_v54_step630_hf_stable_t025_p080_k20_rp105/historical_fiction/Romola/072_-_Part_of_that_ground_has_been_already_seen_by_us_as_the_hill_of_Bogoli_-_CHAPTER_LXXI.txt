The spring had nearly faded when Romola returned home with Tessa and the children, her heart heavy with a fresh, crushing weight. The publication of Fra Girolamo's trial—the confessions extracted by the sixteen Florentine citizens commissioned to interrogate him—had ignited a storm of public suspicion and rage. The Signoria, alarmed by the uproar, ordered the document recalled, yet copies had slipped into the hands of eager readers, and a second, unauthorized edition circulated swiftly through the city.

Romola, having begun to despair of ever seeing the friar again, devoured the printed evidence. She sought a clearer light than the contradictory assertions swirling through the mouths of partisans and enemies alike. To the devout followers of Savonarola, his lack of constancy under torture and his retraction of prophetic claims caused a consternation too deep to be easily healed. They clung to the hope that any words contradicting their faith were not spoken by the prophet but forged by Ser Ceccone, the notary of evil repute who had compiled the digest. Yet, glaring flaws undermined the document's credibility. Was it not true that half the panel of sixteen examiners were bitter enemies of the prophet? Was not Dolfo Spini, a notorious figure, among the newly elected Eight, seemingly placed there to load the dice against a man whose ruin was already decided by the ruling party? It appeared to be a slow, formalized murder within the Old Palace. The Signoria had resolved to strike a bargain with the Pope and the Duke of Milan by extinguishing a man who was a nuisance to vicious citizens, greedy foreign tyrants, and a corrupt clergy alike. The Frate was doomed beforehand; the only debate remaining was whether the Republic should deliver him alive to the Pope in exchange for taxing ecclesiastical property, or if the Pope would grant the Republic the dignity of hanging and burning its own prophet on its own piazza.

Under such circumstances, who could give full credit to this so-called confession? If the Frate had denied his prophetic gift, it had been wrenched from him by the agony of torture—an agony that, in his sensitive frame, must quickly induce raving. What if the wicked examiners claimed he suffered only three times on a single day, and confessed only when free from bodily coercion? Could that be believed? He had been tortured far more; he had been broken in proportion to the distress his confessions caused in the hearts of those who loved him.

Other friends of Savonarola, less ardent in their partisanship, doubted the confession's genuineness only insofar as it had been colored by the notary's transpositions. They argued indignantly that nothing warranted a death sentence or even grave punishment. To any impartial observer, it was clear that if this examination were the sole evidence, the Frate died not for a crime, but because he had become inconvenient to the Pope, to the rapacious Italian states seeking to dismember Tuscany, and to unworthy citizens chasing private ambition against the common weal. No shadow of political crime had been proved against him. No stain marred his private conduct. Even his fellow monks, including a former secretary who possessed more culture than his companions and a tendency to critique Fra Girolamo's rule as Prior, testified to an unimpeachable purity and consistency that had commanded their veneration, even after the shock of his retraction. The Pope himself could raise no charge of heresy except for disobedience to a mandate and disregard of excommunication. While difficult to justify by argument, this breach of discipline sparked a moral insurgence in the minds of grave men, blurring the line between the Church and its men and softening the scandal of disobedience.

Men of ordinary morality and public spirit felt that the triumph of the Frate's enemies was truly the triumph of gross license. Shrewd Florentines like Soderini and Piero Guicciardini likely wore an angry smile at a severity that dispensed with all law to hang and burn a man whose zeal had warped the strictness of his veracity. They might have noted that if the Frate had mixed deeper fraud with a zeal less inconvenient to high personages, that fraud would have served as excellent oil for ecclesiastical and political wheels.

Yet, even these shrewd men were forced to admit that, however clumsily the Florentine government pretended to a judicial warrant for what was actually a predetermined act of policy, the Pope's measures against Savonarola were necessary acts of self-defense. To fail to rid himself of a man stirring the Powers of Europe to summon a General Council and depose him would have been adding ineptitude to iniquity. There was no denying that toward Alexander the Sixth, Savonarola was a rebel, and a dangerous one. Florence had heard him say, and understood, that he would not obey the devil. It was inevitably a life-and-death struggle between the Frate and the Pope; but it was less inevitable that Florence should make itself the Pope's executioner.

Romola's ears were filled with the suggestions of a faith still ardent under its wounds and the judgments of worldly discernment operating on a moderate standard of human nature. She could find satisfaction in neither. Her long meditations over the printed document were haunted by painful observations, registered consciously or unconsciously through years of discipleship, whispering a presentiment that Savonarola's retraction was not merely a spasmodic effort to escape torture. On the other hand, her soul cried out for an explanation that would allow her to believe the main striving of his life had remained pure and grand. The recent memory of selfish discontent, which had swept over her like a blighting wind alongside her loss of trust in this incarnation of the highest motives, had produced a reaction familiar to many: a faith springing from the very depths of despair. It was impossible, she told herself, that the negative, disbelieving thoughts which had made her soul arid could be founded in the truth of things. Impossible that it had not been a living spirit, not a hollow pretence, which once breathed in the Frate's words and kindled a new life in her. Whatever falsehood existed in him, it had been a fall, not a purpose; a gradual entanglement in which he struggled, not a contrivance encouraged by success.

Reading the printed confessions, she saw many sentences bearing the stamp of bungling fabrication: the emphasis and repetition of self-accusation used only by low hypocrites. Yet, the fact that these sentences stood in striking opposition to Savonarola's character and the general tone of the document strengthened her impression that the rest of the text represented what had truly fallen from his lips. Hardly a word was dishonorable to him except regarding his prophetic annunciations. He was unvarying in stating the ends he pursued for Florence, the Church, and the world. Apart from the mixture of falsity in his claim to special inspiration, there was no admission of using unworthy means. Even here, without expurgating the notary's malign phrases, Fra Girolamo shone as a man who sought his own glory indeed, but sought it by laboring for the highest end—the moral welfare of men—not through vague exhortations, but by striving to turn beliefs into energies working in the details of life.

"Everything that I have done," read one memorable passage, perhaps bearing erasures and interpolations, "I have done with the design of being for ever famous in the present and in future ages; and that I might win credit in Florence; and that nothing of great import should be done without my sanction. And when I had thus established my position in Florence, I had it in my mind to do great things in Italy and beyond Italy, by means of those chief personages with whom I had contracted friendship and consulted on high matters, such as this of the General Council. And in proportion as my first efforts succeeded, I should have adopted further measures. Above all, when the General Council had once been brought about, I intended to rouse the princes of Christendom, and especially those beyond the borders of Italy, to subdue the infidels. It was not much in my thoughts to get myself made a Cardinal or Pope, for when I should have achieved the work I had in view, I should, without being Pope, have been the first man in the world in the authority I should have possessed, and the reverence that would have been paid me. If I had been made Pope, I would not have refused the office: but it seemed to me that to be the head of that work was a greater thing than to be Pope, because a man without virtue may be Pope; but *such a work as I contemplated demanded a man of excellent virtues*."

That blending of ambition with belief in the supremacy of goodness held no new tone for Romola, who had heard it ring through the Duomo. It was the habit of Savonarola's mind to conceive great things and feel himself the man to accomplish them. Iniquity should be brought low; the cause of justice, purity, and love should triumph, and it should triumph by his voice, his work, and his blood. In moments of ecstatic contemplation, doubtless, the sense of self melted into the sense of the Unspeakable, containing the elements of genuine self-abasement; but in the presence of his fellow-men for whom he acted, pre-eminence seemed a necessary condition of his life.

Perhaps this confession, even describing a doubleness that was conscious and deliberate, implied no more than the wavering of belief concerning one's own impressions and motives to which most human beings are liable under a marked change of external conditions. In a life so tumultuously mixed as the Frate's, what possibility was opened for a change of self-judgment? Instead of eyes that venerated and knees that knelt, instead of a great work nearing accomplishment and prosperity stamping the agent as a chosen instrument, there came the hooting and spitting of the crowd, the hard faces of enemies acting as judges, and then the horrible torture, accompanied by the irrepressible cry, "It is true, what you would have me say: let me go: do not torture me again: yes, yes, I am guilty. O God! Thy stroke has reached me!"

As Romola thought of the anguish following the confession—whether conscience retracted or confirmed the self-taxing words in the subsequent solitude of the prison—that anguish pressed upon her own heart, urging slow, bitter tears. Every vulgar, self-ignorant person in Florence glibly pronounced on the man's demerits, while *he* knew a depth of sorrow known only to the soul that has loved and sought the most perfect thing, and beholds itself fallen.

She had not yet seen—what she would see later—the evidence of the Frate's mental state after laying his mouth in the dust. As days passed, reports of new, unpublished examinations eliciting no change in his confessions ceased. Savonarola was left alone in his prison, allowed pen and ink for a while so that, if he wished, he might use his poor, bruised, and strained right arm to write. He wrote; but what he wrote was no vindication of innocence, no protest against the proceedings used toward him. It was a continued colloquy with that divine purity with which he sought complete reunion; an outpouring of self-abasement; one long cry for inward renovation. There were no lingering echoes of the old vehement self-assertion, "Look at my work, for it is good, and those who set their faces against it are the children of the devil!" The Voice of Sadness told him, "God placed thee in the midst of the people even as if thou hadst been one of the excellent. In this way thou hast taught others, and hast failed to learn thyself. Thou hast cured others: and thou thyself hast been still diseased. Thy heart was lifted up at the beauty of thy own deeds, and through this thou hast lost thy wisdom and art become, and shalt be to all eternity, nothing... After so many benefits with which God has honoured thee, thou art fallen into the depths of the sea; and after so many gifts bestowed on thee, thou, by thy pride and vainglory, hast scandalised all the world." And when Hope spoke and argued that divine love had not forsaken him, it said nothing now of a great work to be done, but only, "Thou art not forsaken, else why is thy heart bowed in penitence? That too is a gift."

There was no jot of worthy evidence that from the time of his imprisonment to the supreme moment, Savonarola thought or spoke of himself as a martyr. The idea of martyrdom had been to him a passion dividing the dream of the future with the triumph of beholding his work achieved. And now, in place of both, had come a resignation which he called by no glorifying name.

*But therefore he may the more fitly be called a martyr by his fellow-men to all time.* For power rose against him not because of his sins, but because of his greatness—not because he sought to deceive the world, but because he sought to make it noble. And through that greatness he endured a double agony: not only the reviling, the torture, and the death-throe, but the agony of sinking from the vision of glorious achievement into that deep shadow where he could only say, "I count as nothing: darkness encompasses me: yet the light I saw was the true light."
