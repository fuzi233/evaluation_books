The Princess Claelia commissioned a full-length portrait of herself. Gerard, eager to assist, suggested his friend Pietro Vanucci for the task.

She refused with a smile. “I’ll slight Gerardo only when his work displeases me.” Knowing his skill lay more in draftsmanship than color, Gerard then proposed an unconventional approach—drawing her as a Roman statue, her form rendered in meticulous marble-like strokes upon paper.

She hesitated when he explained the required attire: the tunic, toga, and sandals of antiquity.

“I might as well stand in my smock,” she retorted with medieval bluntness.

Gerard, undeterred, praised the classical garb’s elegance. “Signora, you possess the very features of Rome—the noble bearing, the shapely arm. Modern dress does you injustice.”

She arched a brow. “Flattery, Gerardo? Very well. Return on Saturday; I shall decide then.”

The intervening days were spent in secret fittings—testing whether the ancient garments flattered her enough to justify their antiquity.

On his way to the appointment, Gerard was halted by a sight in a shop window: a printed copy of *Lactantius*. His heart sank. *The press has reached Rome.*

Entering, he feigned casual inquiry. “How long has printing flourished here?”

The shopkeeper laughed. “Not in Rome, messer. This was printed in the Apennines.”

Gerard examined the title page—*Opera et impensis Sweynheim et Pannartz Alumnorum Joannis Fust. Impressum Subiacis. A.D. 1465.*

“Will you buy?” the man urged. “No scribe matches its even letters, and at a fraction the cost.”

Gerard shook his head. “I am a calligrapher. These Fustian disciples hound me, stealing bread from my lips. Yet I bear them no ill will.” He left, troubled. *Margaret, we must act swiftly.*

At the princess’s palazzo, he was led to a luxurious chamber—panther skins underfoot, a fountain murmuring at its center. A heavy tapestry curtain divided the space.

Then—silence.

A rustle. The curtain parted.

Before him stood a vision between Venus and Minerva: Claelia, draped in the toga, her arm bared, her pose regal. Gerard caught his breath.

She blushed under his gaze. “Well? Am I monstrous enough for your art?”

“Fra Colonna would worship you,” he breathed.

She laughed, then struck a pose. For an hour, he worked in absorbed silence until she swayed, fatigued. “Enough, Gerardo. I am no marble yet.”

He apologized, offering water. She demurred at his haste. “Gently. Press it to my brow.”

As he obeyed, she sighed. “Again.”

A maid was summoned. But when Floretta approached, the princess’s nostrils flared. “Wretch!” In a flash, she seized the girl, boxed her ears, then drove her out with slaps.

Gerard gaped. “Was that necessary?”

“She reeks of musk,” Claelia snapped, then softened. “Are you angry with me?”

He spoke carefully. “Those who command should master themselves.”

She conceded with a purse for Floretta. “Tell her Gerardo pleaded for her.”

Then, seating herself, she bade him talk.

Gerard, uneasy, stammered until she leaned close, her voice trembling. “Gerar-do, teach me your magic. Make me as happy near you as I am beside you.”

Her hand slid down his arm, light yet possessive.

He stiffened. “Signora, this is madness. I am no fit object for such affection.”

She withdrew, eyes flashing. “You fear my kin’s daggers?”

“I fear God and my conscience. My heart is pledged—to Margaret, in Holland.”

The name struck like a blow. Claelia paled. “A common girl? You scorn me for *her*?”

“Not scorn—honor. I cannot feign what I do not feel.”

Her fury erupted. “Then know this: beyond that door, armed men wait to cut you down at my word. And I will send tidings to your Margaret—of your faithlessness, then your death.”

Gerard dropped to his knees. “God have mercy!” He poured out his tale—his and Margaret’s trials, their sacrifices, their love. Tears wet her hand as he clasped it. “Would you part us? What remorse would follow such cruelty?”

Her defiance crumbled. “Go,” she whispered. “Leave Rome. If we meet again, I *will* kill you.”

A gesture summoned Floretta. “See him out—by the postern.”

As he turned, Claelia stood motionless, head bowed, weeping.

At his lodgings, he wasted no time. “A noble seeks my life,” he told his landlady.

“Change your quarters,” she advised.

He did, then pored over Floris Brandt’s deed—realizing Ghysbrecht had cheated Peter Brandt of his dues. *Fool, not to have read this sooner!*

A ship to Amsterdam sailed in four days. He booked passage.

“Italy’s air is too thick with knives,” he muttered. “And Dutch skippers seldom drown.”

His landlady met him upon return, eyes alight with news.

But Gerard’s thoughts were already on the sea, and home.
