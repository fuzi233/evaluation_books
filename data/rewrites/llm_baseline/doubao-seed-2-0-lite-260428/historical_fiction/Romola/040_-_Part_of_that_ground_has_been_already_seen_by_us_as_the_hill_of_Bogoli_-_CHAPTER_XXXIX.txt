The moment Tito stepped across the pavilion threshold, his sharp eyes scanned the assembled guests and confirmed his quiet guess: this was no casual, leisurely feast, but a political gathering, engineered to bind party loyalties over good wine and richer food. The Florentines of 1492 held a firm, unshakable belief that a well-filled stomach softened reserve and sharpened conviction far better than any stuffy council speech, that the warm ease of after-supper banter would clarify a man’s own political loyalties with a clarity no hungry, uninvited person could ever achieve. Though Florence was by and large a sober, frugal republic, the Rucellai household was one of the rare few Florentine families that kept a lavish, open table, welcoming guests to a spread fine enough to rival the banquets of Roman nobility. No heavy philosophical theses were going to be forced on anyone that night, of that Tito was certain. The venerable marble bust of Plato over the mantel could look down unmolested, as could the modest frescoed figures of the cardinal virtues lining the walls.

That same bust of Plato had presided over far more rarefied, transcendental gatherings for decades, carried over from Lorenzo de’ Medici’s villa after his death, when the meetings of the famous Platonic Academy moved to these very gardens. Every thirteenth of November, the reputed anniversary of Plato’s death, it had gazed down from under a woven crown of laurel at a hand-picked circle of humanists and scholars: Pico della Mirandola, once a wild, curly-haired young prodigy who shocked all Rome with his unorthodox religious theses, later humbled by the realization that the vast universe was far more astonishing than his own cleverness; gentle, tireless Marsilio Ficino, raised from childhood to be a custodian of Platonism, fed on the master’s works so exclusively his mind grew soft and saturated as overripe fruit; Angelo Poliziano, the age’s greatest living literary genius, a poet whose lines still thrum with warm, living blood centuries later; even old Leon Battista Alberti, that universal man of robust, unparalleled intellect, equal parts architect, scientist, inventor, and poet, decades older than the other three. And beneath the bust’s gaze sat dozens more unnamed, uncelebrated diligent workers whose quiet labours form an unrecognized part of our modern inheritance, no less valuable than the ploughing and sowing of generations of anonymous farmers whose fields we walk today.

Bernardo Rucellai, their host, was a man who would have held a distinguished place in that old Academy even before he became its patron. He was barely forty-four, still in the prime of life, with a haughty, carefully dignified bearing, famed for his immaculate, perfect Latin that no visiting German scholar could ever coax out of him in casual speech. He greeted Tito with more open favour than usual, and seated him between Lorenzo Tornabuoni and Giannozzo Pucci, two polished, accomplished young loyalists of the old Medicean party.

The opening chatter was light as the rose-scented water in the brass hand-washing bowls passed around the table, rings glinting on pale white fingers under the glow of the wax tapers, the fresh, soft scent of newly imported French white damask hanging in the air. The joke of the hour was a familiar Florentine one: someone asked what Dante’s archetypal old 13th century Florentine, leather belt and bone clasps and all, would make of waking to see silver forks laid out on the table before him. Every guest agreed with laughter that their medieval ancestors would be endlessly bewildered by the small luxuries their descendants took for granted.

As the appetizers arrived—delicate slivers of liver cooked to such perfection they dissolved the moment they touched the tongue, whetting appetites for the heavier main courses—guests paused to admire the enameled silver inlays set at the centre of each brass serving dish, chatting about the famous confetti platter, a masterpiece by Antonio Pollajuolo that a wealthy Pope had lured away from his native Florence to work on grander commissions in Rome.

“Ah, I remember Pollajuolo flying into a rage once over all this precious metalwork,” said Niccolò Ridolfi, a middle-aged man with the lazy, unassuming ease that comes of a lifetime of unassailable noble rank. “He threw down his chisel and swore to stick to painting, saying, ‘The artist who casts his work in gold and silver is melting his own brains down into the pot with the rest.’”

“He may well have spoken a true prophecy,” said Giannozzo Pucci, swirling his wine gently. “If this war with Pisa drags on, and the revolt spreads to our other subject towns, we won’t just be losing our silver dishes. I half expect Pollajuolo’s silver saints around the altar of San Giovanni to be melted down into coin one day, to feed the war chest.”

“The Frate is already prepping the people for that,” put in Tornabuoni. “He’s telling them God cares far more about full bellies than silver crucifixes, that the church’s true adornment is holiness and brotherly love, not precious gems and metal.”

“A very convenient doctrine for war finance, as every mercenary captain worth his salt has known for a hundred years,” Rucellai said dryly. “But politics come after the sweetmeats, Lorenzo, when we’ve drunk enough wine to wash that heavy stuff down. It’s far too solid to digest alongside roast and boiled meat.”

Ridolfi agreed, launching into a story about the late, irreverent poet Luigi Pulci, who’d once joked that all opinion corrupted the saliva, hence why people started using pepper, and only perfect scepticism left the palate untainted. He recalled Lorenzo de’ Medici teasing Pulci that their friend Matteo Franco, the self-proclaimed sceptic, used more hot sauce than anyone at the table, and Pulci zinged right back that it was because Franco’s only real unshakable belief was in the immortality of his own verses, a man stubborn enough to debate a preaching friar for hours about the exact layout of the paving stones in the bottomless pit. They all agreed that Pulci, for all his sharp, cutting wit that sliced through every pretension, was as gentle as a butterfly, and as temperate as a child no matter how rhapsodic he grew about fine food and drink.

The supper dragged on slow and sumptuous: roasted and boiled meats gave way to tender capon, game birds, and the centerpiece dish of the night, a peacock prepared according to the ancient Roman gourmet Apicius’s recipe, its full iridescent feathers left intact so it looked as if it was peacefully roosting alive on the platter. The official carver, a trusted senior servant, carefully turned the great bird on its back to slice the tender breast for each guest, no small feat of skill. Hardly any diner dared turn down the expensive novelty. Tito quoted a line from Horace as he scattered his tiny slice across his plate, Rucellai delivered a learned disquisition on the exorbitant price of peacock eggs in ancient Rome without taking a single bite, and Ridolfi held a mouthful on his fork to tell a joke about a thrifty Sienese host who dressed up a wild goose with clipped beak and webbed feet, boiled in its own feathers, to pass it off as a peahen for a grand party. Barely any of the peacock was eaten, but every man there felt the quiet, proud satisfaction of knowing he was part of that tiny, wealthy elite that could afford such absurd, unnecessary luxuries—all the more meaningful at a time when Fra Girolamo was railing from the pulpit against rich men hoarding finery while the poor starved.

Outside the warm, glowing pavilion, in the cold dark of the gardens, Baldassarre wandered in ever-tightening circuits among the cypress trees. He paused beneath a window, pressing his face close to the glass, to stare in at the scene of light and laughter. He could see Tito, gesturing easily, laughing without a care, his voice rising and falling through the merry confusion of overlapping speeches. Baldassarre’s mind was strung tight as a bowstring, steeling himself for the moment he could burst in and confront his betrayer, a savage, cruel satisfaction curling in his chest at the sight of Tito’s unknowing, happy ease. But none of the men inside, clustered under the branching wax tapers, their goblets glinting, had any idea the pale, feral face watched them from the dark beyond the light. The glow of candle and hearth makes as impenetrable a curtain as any pitch black midnight.

Back inside, the chatter grew less trivial, sharper, more political. Every last man at the table had the firebrand Friar Girolamo’s words and schemes at the front of his mind, and before the stewed fish course was cleared and the favourite sweets were brought out, his name slipped to the surface of the conversation, and politics took over the table, Rucellai’s earlier gentle ban forgotten. As long as the servants lingered, it was harmless gossip: how the first votes for the new Great Council had gone in the Palazzo della Signoria, how overbearing Francesco Valori was, acting as if his austere piety gave him the right to dictate every decision, and how obvious it was that Soderini’s pro-Council speeches were exactly aligned with the sermons the Frate was preaching in the Duomo.

“I say the Frate is the one running the show, not Soderini,” said Ridolfi, leaning back in his chair. “Soderini is nothing but his mouthpiece.”

Rucellai shook his head. “No, Niccolò. The Frate is sharp, sure, and knows how to use events to his own ends, but Soderini has decades of political experience, has studied the Venetian Council for years. He did not need a monk to tell him how to structure the Great Council. Soderini loads the cannon, I will grant you that, but Fra Girolamo brings the powder and lights the fuse. The people are his, and the people are starting to run all of us.”

Once all the servants had cleared out, leaving only bottles of wine passing between the guests, Tornabuoni leaned forward, and revealed he had met with the Frate privately, in his own cell—a favour that even Francesco Valori rarely got. He told the table he had gone in as a sceptic, and left convinced that the only safe path for the old Medicean faction was to throw their weight behind the popular party and the Frate. If the Frate’s government fell, the vicious Arrabbiati faction, the bitter enemies of both Mediceans and the Frate’s Piagnoni followers, would seize power, and they would tear every last Medici supporter apart, half the city exiled in a week.

All the men around the table murmured their full agreement. Tornabuoni laid out their quiet, long-game plan: pretend to be loyal followers of the new popular government, bide their time, until the natural restlessness of Florentine politics swung power back to a single family, the Medici, not the fantasy of a perfectly equal, selfless republic the Frate was preaching. Ridolfi agreed, blunt and plainspoken, saying he was far too old to pretend he had ever changed his loyalties, they could all just stay quiet, lie low like sleeping old hounds, no need to bark when it would only get them in trouble. The new Great Council would be formally voted into being tomorrow, he said, but it would go the way every other government ever had, either people snarl at each other, or they grovel before whoever is strongest. Bernardo Rucellai, ever the dignified, intellectual sceptic, agreed that allying with the popular party was the most prudent course, even if he did not fully endorse any of the current factions, all of which were full of crude, untested ideas.

As the wine flowed freer, the great table broke off into small clusters of twos and threes, chatting privately. Tornabuoni and Pucci pulled their chairs close to Tito, their backs to the rest of the room, and laid out their secret offer. They told Tito he was the perfect man for their covert missions, far better than any of their other known loyalists, who were too obvious, their prejudices too easy to spot. They wanted him to travel to Rome as their secret agent, to negotiate with Piero de’ Medici and the French nobles, using his reputation as a wandering scholar as perfect, unassailable cover. With his charm and his wit, they told him, he could rise farther than he ever dreamed, even make his way to a cardinal’s hat one day if he chose to take holy orders.

Tito laughed, flattered even if he saw straight through their exaggerated flattery. He said he would be happy to settle for the generous income of a couple of abbeys without needing to shave his head, for the moment. They went on to tell him he should win the trust of the followers of San Marco, the Frate’s circle, once he returned from Rome, to gather intelligence on their plans. Then they added that they needed a second, completely unknown agent to infiltrate the Arrabbiati, the violent secretive rival faction, to spy on their plots, since they were far more dangerous than the Frate’s open, idealistic followers.

Tito did not hesitate for a second. He said no other agent was needed. He could easily win over Dolfo Spini, the hot-headed leader of the Arrabbiati, become his closest confidant, and know all their plans before even Spini himself knew what he intended to do. A thrill of exaltation shot through him, sudden and bright. The game before him was far bigger than he had ever dreamed. He was not just going to be a favoured hanger-on any longer—he could play all three factions against each other, hold quiet, unacknowledged power in his own hands. His status as a foreigner, his complete indifference to all their ideological loyalties and old family feuds, that had once seemed like a weakness, was now his greatest strength. All the small lies and compromises he had made since he first arrived in Florence had slowly stripped away every last moral scruple that might have held him back, and now the triple deceit he was about to embark on looked like nothing more than a brilliant, easy game, with every prize Florence had to offer already within his grasp.
