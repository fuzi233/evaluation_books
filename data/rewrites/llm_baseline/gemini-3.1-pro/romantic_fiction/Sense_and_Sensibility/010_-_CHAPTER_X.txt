The heavy copper preserving pan, a heat-blackened heirloom that had anchored four generations of Dashwood women to the cyclical rhythms of the harvest, sat upon the hearth. Within it, early damson plums simmered with bruised cardamom and star anise, filling the cottage with an olfactory landscape so dense and mythic it seemed to fold past and present into a single, fragrant continuum. It was into this rich domestic alchemy that Marianne’s preserver—whom Margaret, with more elegance than precision, styled Willoughby—stepped early the next morning to make his personal enquiries. 

He was received by Mrs. Dashwood with more than politeness; he was welcomed with the warmth of a fellow initiate into the sensory world. Sir John’s account, combined with her own profound gratitude, smoothed the way, and every exchange during the visit assured Willoughby of the family’s deep, mutual affection and their meticulous reverence for the sustenance of daily life. Of their personal charms, he required no second taste to be convinced. Miss Dashwood possessed a delicate complexion and a remarkably pretty figure, but Marianne was still handsomer. Her form was striking, and her face held the kind of radiant, earth-rooted loveliness that made the common cant of praise feel violently inadequate. Her skin was a rich, toasted hazelnut brown, yet from its transparency, her complexion possessed the brilliant clarity of wildflower honey; her features were beautifully composed, and in her dark eyes danced a life, an eagerness, that engaged the observer’s every sense.

From Willoughby, the full expression of those eyes was at first withheld, shuttered by the embarrassment of his recent rescue. But as the morning’s natural acoustic ecology settled—the rhythmic bubbling of the fruit, the gentle, percussive ticking of the mantel clock—her spirits collected. She saw that to his perfect good-breeding he united a frank vivacity. When he produced a small, damp bundle of wild chanterelles he had foraged along the woodland edge, speaking passionately of their earthy, apricot scent and the ancient mycelial networks beneath the soil, Marianne’s hesitation vanished. Here was a man who did not view the world as mere utilitarian fuel, but as a symphony of flavor and sound. When he further declared that he was passionately fond of music and dancing—the rhythmic, untranslatable expressions of the body’s native dialect—she gave him such a look of approbation as secured the largest share of his discourse for the rest of his stay. 

It was only necessary to introduce a favorite flavor or melody to engage her completely. She possessed neither shyness nor reserve when such vital points were raised. They speedily discovered a mutual resonance in their acoustic and gustatory worlds, an immediate harmony in all matters of judgment. Encouraged by this shared lexicon, she proceeded to question him on the subject of books. Her favorite authors were brought forward, their syllables savored and dwelt upon with such rapturous delight that any young man of five and twenty must have been insensible indeed not to become an immediate convert to the excellence of their prose. Their tastes were strikingly alike. They consumed the same passages with a shared hunger; if any difference arose, it lasted only until the force of her arguments and the luminous spirit in her eyes could be displayed. He acquiesced to her palate, caught all her enthusiasm, and long before the aroma of the finished preserves signaled the visit’s end, they conversed in the intimate, idiosyncratic dialect of long-established acquaintances.

“Well, Marianne,” said Elinor, as she methodically began to knead a heavy mound of rye dough, the rhythmic *thump* and *shush* of her palms grounding the room once more after he had left, “for *one* morning I think you have done pretty well. You have already ascertained Mr. Willoughby’s palate in almost every matter of importance. You know what he thinks of Cowper and Scott; you are certain he digests their beauties as he ought, and you have received every assurance that he admires Pope no more than is proper. But how is your shared vocabulary to be long supported under such extraordinary, ravenous dispatch? You will soon have exhausted every favorite vintage. Another meeting will suffice to explain his sentiments on picturesque beauty and the nuances of second marriages, and then you will be left with a dead language between you, with nothing farther to ask.”

“Elinor,” cried Marianne, “is this fair? Is this just? Are my inner landscapes so scanty? But I see what you mean. I have been too much at my ease, too happy, too resonant. I have erred against the sterile, commonplace notions of decorum; I have offered my true dialect where I ought to have been reserved, spiritless, dull, and deceitful—had I spoken only of the weather and the roads in the mechanized drone of polite society, and had I offered a syllable only once in ten minutes, this reproach would have been spared.”

“My love,” said her mother, carefully ladling the deep-purple plums into glass jars, sealing the morning’s history away, “you must not be offended with Elinor—she was only in jest. I should scold her myself if she were capable of wishing to silence the beautiful acoustic harmony of your conversation with our new friend.”

Marianne was softened in a moment. Willoughby, on his side, gave every proof of his hunger for their society. He came to them every day. The circular pacing of the week folded into a shared rhythm. To enquire after Marianne was at first his excuse, but as the daily offerings of freshly baked bread and rich, steeped teas gave greater kindness to his reception, the excuse became delightfully obsolete upon Marianne’s perfect recovery. She was confined for some days to the house, but never had any confinement felt less like stagnation. Willoughby was a young man of quick imagination, lively spirits, and a profound sensory appreciation. He was exactly formed to engage Marianne’s heart, for he joined a captivating person to a natural ardor of mind that resonated perfectly with her own. 

His society gradually became her most exquisite daily nourishment. They read, they tasted the nuances of new recipes, they sang together. His musical talents were considerable, his baritone warming the cottage’s sonic environment, and he read aloud with a rich, evocative timbre that Edward had unfortunately lacked. In Mrs. Dashwood’s estimation, he was faultless; and Elinor saw nothing to censure in him save a propensity—one in which he strongly resembled and peculiarly delighted her sister—of speaking his unedited thoughts with too much volume, careless of the delicate social ecologies around them. In hastily giving his opinion of others, and in slighting the quiet, established forms of worldly propriety for the immediate gratification of his own desires, he displayed a linguistic dominance and a lack of caution that Elinor could not approve.

Marianne now perceived that the desperation which had seized her at sixteen and a half, of ever finding a man who spoke her exact soul-language, had been rash. Willoughby was all that her fancy had delineated in its brightest periods. Her mother, too, in whose mind not one utilitarian thought of his wealth had intruded upon the poetry of their union, was led before the end of a week to expect their marriage, secretly marveling at having garnered two such harmoniously aligned sons-in-law as Edward and Willoughby.

Yet, Colonel Brandon’s silent, deep-rooted partiality for Marianne—a steady, agrarian devotion early noticed by his friends—now first became perceptible to Elinor just as the others ceased to hear it. Their attention was entirely consumed by the louder, more immediate symphony of his fortunate rival. Elinor was obliged to believe that the sentiments Mrs. Jennings had coarsely broadcast were actually excited by her sister. She observed that however a general resemblance of disposition might forward Willoughby’s affection, an equally striking opposition of character was no hindrance to the deep, resonant regard of Colonel Brandon. 

She saw it with concern, for what could a quiet man of five and thirty hope, whose soul hummed at the slow, deliberate frequency of the earth, when opposed to a very lively one of five and twenty? Since she could not wish him successful, she heartily wished him indifferent. She liked him—in spite of his gravity, she beheld in him a sanctuary from the superficial commercialization of modern society. His manners were serious but mild; his reserve seemed the result of an oppressive silencing of his spirits by past injuries rather than any natural gloominess. Sir John had dropped hints of historical disappointments, and she regarded the Colonel with respect and compassion. Perhaps she pitied and esteemed him the more because he was discarded by Willoughby and Marianne, who, prejudiced against him for lacking the spicy vivacity of youth, seemed resolved to undervalue the rich, slow-cooked depth of his character.

“Brandon is just the kind of man,” said Willoughby one day, as they were casually stripping the leaves from a bundle of fresh thyme, “whom everybody speaks well of, and nobody cares about; whose presence is an expected background noise, but whom nobody remembers to truly listen to.”

“That is exactly what I think of him,” cried Marianne.

“Do not boast of it, however,” said Elinor, “for it is a failure of your own acoustics. He is highly esteemed by all the family at the park, and I never see him myself without taking pains to tune into his conversation.”

“That he is patronized by *you*,” replied Willoughby, tossing a stripped stem aside, “is certainly in his favor; but as for the esteem of the others, it is a reproach in itself. Who would submit to the indignity of being approved by such women as Lady Middleton and Mrs. Jennings, whose minds are cluttered with nothing but the shrill, superficial commerce of society?”

“But perhaps the abuse of such people as yourself and Marianne will make amends for their regard. If their praise is censure, your censure may be praise, for they are not more undiscerning than you are deafened by your own prejudices.”

“In defense of your *protégé*, you can even be saucy.”

“My *protégé*, as you call him, is a sensible man; and sense will always have a profound flavor for me. Yes, Marianne, even in a man between thirty and forty. He has seen a great deal of the world; he has been abroad, he has read extensively, and he has a mind that understands the deep, generational roots of culture. I have found him capable of giving me the most evocative histories of untranslatable local dialects and forgotten agricultural practices; he answers my inquiries with a readiness of good-breeding and a genuinely amiable heart.”

“That is to say,” cried Marianne contemptuously, “he has told you, in the most sterile prose imaginable, that in the East Indies the climate is hot, and the mosquitoes are troublesome.”

“He *would* have told me so, I doubt not, had I made any such superficial inquiries. But they happened to be points on which I was already informed.”

“Perhaps,” said Willoughby, his voice dripping with playful mockery, “his observations may have extended to the grand, commercial spectacles of nabobs, gold mohrs, and palanquins.”

“I may venture to say that *his* observations have stretched much further into the true sustenance of those cultures than *your* candour allows you to admit. But why should you dislike him?”

“I do not dislike him. I consider him, on the contrary, a very respectable man, who has everybody’s good word, and nobody’s true notice; who has more money than he can spend, more time than he knows how to employ, and two new coats every year.”

“Add to which,” cried Marianne, “that he has neither genius, taste, nor spirit. That his understanding possesses no brilliant spice, his feelings no roaring fire, and his voice lacks the slightest melodic expression.”

“You decide on his imperfections so much in the mass,” replied Elinor, wiping flour from her hands, “and so much on the strength of your own narrow lexicon, that the commendation *I* am able to give him must seem comparatively cold and insipid to your palates. I can only pronounce him to be a sensible man, deeply rooted to the earth, well-informed, possessing a gentle address and an amiable heart.”

“Miss Dashwood,” cried Willoughby, “you are now using me unkindly. You are endeavoring to disarm me by reason, and to convince me against my will. But it will not do. You shall find me as stubborn as you can be artful. I have three unanswerable reasons for disliking Colonel Brandon: he threatened me with rain when I wanted the skies clear for my own amusement, ignoring that the soil desperately thirsted for it; he found fault with the mechanized hanging of my curricle; and I cannot persuade him to part with his heritage brown mare. If it will be any satisfaction to you, however, to be told that I believe his character to be in other respects irreproachable, I am ready to confess it. And in return for an acknowledgment which must give me some pain, you cannot deny me the privilege of refusing to speak his language, and disliking him as much as ever.”
