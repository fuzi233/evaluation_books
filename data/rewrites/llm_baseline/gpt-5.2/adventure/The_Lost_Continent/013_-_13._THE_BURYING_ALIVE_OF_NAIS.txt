Phorenice’s wishes did not linger in Atlantis like incense that might be allowed to fade. They struck, and the city moved.

Her edict went out for the burying alive of Nais, and though the words named me as builder of the throne of stone, everyone understood what that meant in practice: I would stand as patron and symbol, while a whole hive of hands did the lifting, chiselling, hauling, and dying.

Heralds cried the proclamation in every ward, their voices bouncing off pyramid-flanks and colonnades, their brass trumpets cutting through the morning’s softer sounds—the cluck of courtyard fowl, the splash of women rinsing grain, the low patient groan of donkeys under panniers. At once the guilds poured toward the square: masons with chalk-dusted beards, stonecutters with mallets slung like butchers’ cleavers, engineers smelling of hot metal and oil, architects with tablets and plumb-lines, sculptors with their fine blades wrapped in cloth. They came as if summoned to a feast—only this feast would be of granite and blood.

Any block they sealed for use was seized without coin or apology. The chosen stones were monstrous, and to bring them through the streets the guilds tore down whatever stood in the way—shops where spices had hung in fragrant braids, a bakery whose oven still breathed yesterday’s barley-sweet warmth, a row of poor dwellings where the cooking stones were scarcely cooled. The city’s domestic life—pots, hearths, pounding-stones, jars of preserved fruit—was shoved aside so the Empress’s stone could pass.

All this while the rebels’ engines still battered at the walls. Yet in Atlantis the mania for building did not pause for war. The guilds kept to their craft as if stone could drown out danger, as if architecture were a prayer louder than fear. They could not lift the impossible monoliths the early Gods had planted for the Sun’s sacred circle, but they had rams and cranes and wheeled trucks, and Phorenice’s new devices besides—metal that changed with heat and cold, machinery that made the city’s old acoustic ecology into something harsher. Where once you heard wind in palms and the measured chanting of temple choirs, now the square rang all night with hammer-blows, whip-cracks, the squeal of rollers, the groan of strained ropes, the sharp calls of foremen. The noise lay on the mind like grit, wearing it down.

The throne rose in the open square before the royal pyramid. Seven tiers formed its base, each knee-high, each front cut with three steps, like a ritual staircase for a god. At the topmost layer, the builders gouged a cavity—an awful pantry, a sealed larder—not for grain or cured meat, but for a living woman. Above it the huge seat-stone hung poised, held aloft by the heated rams that would, by slow contraction, settle it into place.

By torchlight, relay after relay worked through the night. Men sweated till their skins shone black in the flame-glow; some fainted and were dragged aside; some slipped and broke; some were crushed. Others stepped into their places at once. The whips did not cease. Phorenice had spoken; the city obeyed.

At last, as dawn cooled the stones and the square began to fill with people come to stare, the chippings were cleared away, the scaffolding pulled down, and along with it the bodies of half a score who had died from accident or exhaustion. The throne stood finished in its lower courses: carvings crisp as knife-work, the royal serpent coiling around the chair, its hooded head poised above like a shadow that would never lift. The upper block still hung on the rams, ready for the final act that everyone pretended was justice and everyone knew was spectacle.

In the cool of the next morning, the chamberlains summoned me from the royal pyramid. I stepped out into a world already arranged for pageantry. Each great man announced himself with banner-bearers and trumpet-calls. The middle classes glittered in their bright garments. Even the poor—dusty, ribbed with hunger—had wrapped scraps of colour around their heads, as if a strip of dyed cloth could turn dread into holiday.

My own trumpets sounded. The crowd shouted welcome. Chamberlains in gorgeous robes walked backward before me, and I crossed to the scarlet awning prepared in my honour and sat upon the cushions beneath it.

Then Phorenice came, fresh from sleep and made magnificent by it. They bore her in an open litter of gold and ivory, carried by those European savages she favoured—massive, scarred men whose brutishness threw her refinement into sharper brilliance. The crowd drew a single deep breath when they first saw her, the way a kitchen draws breath when a new spice is unstoppered. There was not a man in that throng who did not envy me, nor a soul who could imagine I was not drunk with gratitude at being chosen.

I had to summon all the iron I owned to keep my face still. This was ceremony, and in Atlantis ceremony mattered—older than any private grief. I meant Phorenice to see nothing to cavil at. My plan—my necessity—could not afford a trembling hand or a twitching cheek. When the time came, I told myself, I would place my love into that hollow as calmly as a priest lays a stranger into a tomb, and she would give no sign that betrayed what lay between us. With her, too, it was always, unbreakably, before all Atlantis.

If there was mercy anywhere in that morning, it was that no one treated the rite as a joke. The whole proceeding moved with a strict solemnity. Phorenice, for all her shifting moods, could be rigid in old ritual when it pleased her, and she had commanded pomp and precision in the ancient manner.

They carried her to the scarlet awning. I handed her down. She sat, beckoned me to her side, and intertwined her fingers with mine as rulers and consorts have always done, binding the public image together like cords on a ceremonial roast.

A body of soldiery marched up, opened out, and showed Nais among them.

They had clamped a metal collar around her neck with chains descending from it, held tight by two guards so she could not fling herself on a spear and steal a quick death. Yet she still wore her clothing. That small decency mattered. In Atlantis it was common for soldiers to strip the condemned and divide the garments like kitchen-scraps after a banquet. Someone had paid these guards to spare her that. I thought of Ylga—veiled somewhere, perhaps—and felt gratitude like a warm taste in the mouth amid bitterness.

The soldiers halted at the edge of the pavilion’s shade. Nais was ordered to prostrate herself, and she did so with wisdom, sparing herself the roughness of forced handling. Her face was pale, but there was neither fear nor defiance in it; her eyes were calm, as if she listened to something within herself that no one else could hear. She remembered what was due to Atlantis, and love and pride twisted in me like a knife.

Outwardly, I held myself like carved stone. Phorenice watched my face, and I let her see nothing but what I would have her see.

“Nais,” said the Empress, her voice carrying as cleanly as a bell across the square, “you have eaten from my platter when you were young and drank from my cup. I gave you what was yours. You should have learned gratitude—and knowledge—that the arm of the Empress is long and her hand heavy. Yet you have tried to take what I have set apart for myself. You were offered pardon on terms. You rejected it. Foolish. But to-day I am inclined to clemency. Presently, seated on that carved throne of granite, I shall take my Lord Deucalion to husband. Give me a plain word that you are sorry, girl, and name a man you would choose. I will remember the brightness of this occasion. You shall be pardoned and wed before we rise from these cushions.”

“I will not wed,” Nais said quietly.

Phorenice’s lips tightened, not quite into cruelty, but into the neatness of a blade being honed. “Think for the last time of what is the other choice. You will be taken warm and quick and beautiful as you stand there now, and laid in the hollow beneath the throne-stone. Deucalion—who is to be my husband—will lay you in that bed as a symbol that so shall perish all my enemies. Then he will release the rams and lower the upper stone into place, and the world shall see your face no more. Look at the bright sky, Nais. Fill your chest with the sweet warm air. Then think what this death will mean. Believe me, I do not wish to make you an example unless you force me.”

“I will not wed,” said the prisoner, in the same unbroken tone.

The Empress released my hand and lay back against the cushions. “If she presumes on old familiarity, or thinks that I jest, show her now, Deucalion, that I do not.”

I rose as though rising for a duty no more intimate than inspecting a granary. “The Empress is far from jesting,” I said. “I will do this thing because it is her wish, and because it is her command that a symbol of it remain for ever as an example. Lead your prisoner to the place.”

The soldiers wheeled. The guards tightened on the chains, ready to drag. But Nais walked willingly, colour unchanged, and I followed them after making obeisance to Phorenice.

Before ten thousand eyes, we showed no emotion. Not only for Atlantis’ sake, but because pride lived in both of us. We were not as Phorenice, who could flaunt endearments like jewels.

At the upper tier I ordered the collar removed. Then I clapped my arms around Nais with a roughness meant for witnesses, holding her as a captor holds his prey. The touch nearly unmanned me. I had never embraced a woman except with the arm of brutality; now I held the one I loved with tenderness so great it was like pain. Nais made no sign—no tremor, no glance that would betray us. She lay half-strangled in my grasp as any captive might, and the crowd below drank in the theatre.

We moved beneath the overhanging mass of the seat-stone. There, where Phorenice’s view did not reach, but where eyes on roofs and pyramid-sides could still see, I dragged Nais across the level and put her down into the hollow with deliberate brutality. She did not resist me even once. That, too, was courage.

To prevent the condemned from struggling up and being crushed—thus snatching an easier end—brazen clamps lay ready, fitted to grooves above the cavity. With one hand I held her down; with the other I fastened bar after bar. Each click of metal into stone sounded louder than any hymn. Sweat ran off me and spattered the thirsty granite. I could not stop that.

I locked the last two bars and lifted my hand away from her throat.

My fingers had left pale furrows in her skin like marks pressed into dough. I could not help looking at her face. She was stronger than I. She did not give me any last look. Her eyes stayed fixed on the cruel stone overhead, as if she studied it the way a housewife studies a ceiling beam for cracks before the rains. I left her then, because to linger would have broken me.

I stepped out from beneath the poised block and signalled the engineers. They drew away the fires that heated the rams. As the metal cooled and contracted, the vast throne-stone began to creep downward toward its bed.

Slowly. Too slowly. Every inch felt like a year.

I stood with a face as blank as an officer overseeing work done under his authority. I watched the descent without flinching until the stone settled with a final deep sound that rolled through the square and through the bones, a noise that seemed to swallow all other living sound—the crying of infants, the gossip of women, the very wing-beat of pigeons. The rams were removed. Then, by guild custom, the master architect came with plumb-line and level to test what calculation already assured.

It was useless formality, a performance for the middle-class to seem mysterious and impressive to the poor. Yet even that had to be endured. A man dragged around to verify the perfect level of the tomb where his love lies buried living may be forgiven bitterness.

I had gone up those steps full of grief, though outwardly unmoved. Coming down, I felt hollowed out, as if half my insides had been left behind in that cavity, and the space filled with an aching void. Still my face remained passive. Always, before private matters, stood the command of the High Council before the Ark of the Mysteries.

I returned to Phorenice and spoke the prescribed words that certified her wish had been carried out.

“Then now,” she said, “I will give myself to you as wife. We are not as others, you and I, Deucalion. There is law and form for common people; it is useless for our purposes. We will have neither priest nor scribe to join us. I am law in Atlantis, and you soon will be part of me. We will not be demeaned by profaner hands. We will make the ceremony for ourselves, with sufficient witnesses. Afterwards the record shall be cut deep in the granite throne and filled with gold, so it endures and remains bright always.”

“The Empress can do no wrong,” I said, and took her hand and helped her rise.

We walked from the awning into the harsh sunlight. She leaned on me, flushing, radiant. The people shouted, “A Goddess; our Goddess Phorenice.” For me they had no cheer. The grim set of my face frightened them, as if they sensed that beneath it something human had been sealed away like Nais.

We climbed the steps to the throne. I sat Phorenice in the royal seat beneath the serpent’s hooded head, and she drew me down beside her.

She raised a jewelled hand. Silence fell over the throng as if someone had closed a door on all sound.

“Hear me, O my people,” Phorenice proclaimed, “and hear me, O High Gods from whom I am come. I take this man Deucalion to be my husband, to share with me the prosperity of Atlantis and join me in guarding our great possession. May all our enemies perish as she is now perishing above whom we sit.” She put her arms around my neck and kissed me hotly on the mouth.

In turn I spoke the answering form, my voice steady by force alone: “Hear me, O most High Gods, whose servant I am, and hear me also, O ye people. I take this Empress, Phorenice, to wife, to help with her the prosperity of Atlantis, and join with her in guarding the welfare of that great possession. May all the enemies of this country perish as they have perished in the past.”

Then I kissed her—my first kiss given to any woman—while the taste of it turned to ash in my mouth because another mouth, the one I wanted, lay below stone.

But the rite demanded more.

“A woman is one, and man is one,” Phorenice said, taking up the ancient words, “but in marriage they merge, so that wife and husband are no more separate, but one conjointly. In token of this we will make the symbolic joining, that all may see and remember.” She drew her dagger and pricked the brawn of my forearm until a bead of blood rose. She pressed her red lips to it and took it into herself.

“Ah,” she breathed, eyes bright. “Now you are part of me indeed, Deucalion, and I feel you have strengthened me already. Let me make you my return.”

She pulled down the neck of her robe. I pricked the rounded whiteness of her shoulder. For a heartbeat, remembering who lay beneath us, I wanted to drive the blade through to her heart. Instead I bent and took the offered drop of blood.

My tongue went dry; my throat tightened; my face burned. I thought I would choke on the act.

Phorenice mistook it for passion. “It thrills you?” she cried. “It burns like living fire? I have just felt it. By my face, Deucalion, if I had known the pleasure of being made a wife, I should not have waited so long. With another man I should have had no thrill. They say this feeling comes to a woman but once, and I would not change it for the glory of all conquests.” She leaned close so her red curls brushed my cheek, her breath hot against my mouth. “Tasted you ever any sweet so delicious as this knowledge that we are one now, past all dissolving?”

My endurance ended there. I had lied with my face all morning; my tongue would not forge lover’s phrases over a living tomb. Yet I felt, too, a kind of pity: her passion had grown wild, and I had done nothing to check it. Her frankness was the habit of rulers who display what common folk would hide.

“Words are poor things,” I said. “I am a man unused to women, and have but a small stock of phrases except the driest. Remember, Phorenice, a week ago I did not know what love was, and now that I have learned it—somewhat suddenly—the language has yet to come. My inwards speak; indeed they are full of speech; but I cannot translate into bald cold words what they say.”

Perhaps the High Gods took pity on my bound tongue and misery, for an interruption tore through the square.

A man ran in shouting, blood dripping from a wound. The cry leapt from mouth to mouth, multiplying until the whole crowd surged in panic, hands groping for weapons.

“The rebels are in!”
“They have burrowed a path into the city!”
“They have killed the cave-tigers and taken a gate!”
“They are putting the place to storm!”
“They will leave none of us alive!”

With rebels merely biting at the walls, it had been easy for the city to pretend contempt and keep pageants turning like millstones. But rebels inside the city changed everything—sound, breath, thought. Phorenice’s lover-glow snapped into the hard glare of a fighter as the messenger shrieked his tidings.

I stood and drew in air as if I had been underwater. “Here,” I said, “is work I can understand. I will go and sweep this rabble back to their burrows, Phorenice.”

“But not alone. I come too. It is my city still. Nay, sir—we are too newly wed to be parted yet.”

“Have your will,” I said. Under my breath, as we descended the steps, I spoke a farewell to Nais that no one could hear.

Armour-bearers met us with weapons. We stepped into litters, and slaves took us off at speed. The wounded man who had brought the news collapsed into a faint, giving no more detail, but the growing din of fighting guided us. Soon we met knots of people fleeing from the breach, screaming, loaded with household goods—bundles of bedding, jars of oil, baskets of grain, even a cooking-pot still swinging from its handle, sloshing thin stew onto the stones. Panic trampled domestic life into the dust.

It was useless to stop them. They could not fight; if they stayed, they would die and clog the streets for our soldiers. So we drove the litters through them with little regard for their coward convenience.

When we reached the place of irruption, the rebels’ advantage proved small under a soldier’s eye. They had tunnelled from behind a covering mound beneath the walls and opened cleverly through the floor of a middle-class house. There they had gathered their men under shelter, hoping the Empress’s marriage would dull vigilance. But they had been discovered too soon; troops were barracked nearby. Their extermination would be only a matter of time even without us.

Phorenice saw it as quickly as I did. She laughed, sank back into the litter cushions, and handed her weapons back to an armour-bearer who came panting up. “We grow nervous with our married life, my Deucalion,” she said. “We fear this new-found happiness will be taken from us too suddenly.”

I would not surrender the breathing-space so easily. “Let me crave a wedding gift of you,” I said.

“It is yours before you name it.”

“Then give me troops,” I said, “and set me wide a city gate a mile away from here.”

“You can gather five hundred as you go,” she answered, already calculating, “taking two hundred of those here. If you want more, they must be fetched from other barracks along the walls. But where is your plan?”
