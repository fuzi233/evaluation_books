Our Virginians, with that quick wisdom which often visits youth before it deserts them, had taken to talking loudly of their Jacobite leanings, and to professing, with a prodigious warmth, a tenderness for the exiled family over the water. It cost them little—fine sentiments are cheap in a drawing-room—and it pleased Madam Esmond mightily; for the banished Prince had been pleased to recognise her father as Marquis of Esmond, and she could never be angry long with any unfortunate race that showed itself so ready to bow to the merits of her blood.

As for the little scandal—whispered once, and never spoken aloud in her presence—touching her sister Madame de Bernstein and the Old Chevalier, Madam flung the recollection away as one might toss a fish-bone from the lips, with a face of scorn, and with perfect truth for her seasoning declared that the first two monarchs of the House of Hanover were quite as culpable as any Stuart where domestic morality was concerned. Yet king de facto was king, she would say, and no amount of sighing for a de jure sovereign could alter the fact that the other had been crowned in church and had beaten his rival in the field. Her judgment was clear, brisk, and practical. Her sons, she held, owed it to themselves and to the sovereign to appear at court; and if his Majesty should be minded to confer a lucrative post or a blue ribbon or a red, why, she for her part would not be surprised in the least. She made no doubt the King knew the Virginian Esmonds as well as he knew any of his nobility. They were commanded, then, to present themselves; and had she known that George borrowed Harry’s laced coat on the day he went to make his bow at Kensington, Madam would have been very angry indeed—as angry as a woman can be who likes a good effect and a handsome embroidery, but cannot endure that her household should contrive it without her.

A hundred years ago the King’s drawing-room was open almost every day to the gentry and nobility; and loyalty—especially since war had begun—might gratify itself twenty times in a month with the august sight of the sovereign. The enemy’s ships were abroad; it was a wise prudence, and a gracious care for the inestimable loss the islands would suffer were the royal person snapped up at sea, which kept his Majesty from those Hanoverian visits dear to his heart, and compelled him, somewhat unwillingly, to remain among his loving Britons. Yet there was at Kensington a comfort ready to hand: a Hanoverian lady whose voice brought back to him the very cadence of home. From the lips of the Countess of Walmoden—whom imperial beneficence had decorated with a high British title—the Defender of the Faith could hear the accents he had sucked with his mother’s milk.

To this sovereign Mr. Warrington begged his uncle, an assiduous courtier, to present him; and as Mr. Lambert likewise must go to court and thank the King for his promotion, the two gentlemen made the journey to Kensington together, hiring a hackney-coach for the purpose, since my Lord Wrotham’s carriage was wanted by its rightful owner, newly returned to his town house.

The coach rolled through wet streets, and the rain had a steady, industrious sound upon the leather curtain—one of those honest rains that hush London for a moment, so that you may hear the iron shoes of horses, the rattle of wheels, and, now and then, a hawker’s cry thinned by weather. George, who had tramped forests where the sound of a distant twig might mean death, found himself listening—as men will—to the city’s different wilderness: the water’s patter, the sentry’s call, the faint murmur of a crowd kept in order by walls and etiquette instead of trees.

They alighted at the palace gate. The sentries knew and saluted the good General, and from thence the two proceeded on foot, modestly enough, under the portico and into the gallery that leads to the great black marble staircase, richly decorated and painted by Mr. Kent. Passing through several rooms hung with tapestry and adorned with pictures and busts, they came at last to the King’s great drawing-room, where that famous Venus by Titian hangs, and, among other masterpieces, the St. Francis by Rubens. Here, with the rest of the visitors, they waited while his Majesty was still in his private apartments with those personages the newspapers of that day called, in their clipped and reverent fashion, his M-j-ty’s M-n-st-rs.

George Warrington, who had never been in a palace before, had leisure to feast his eyes. The place itself was like a kind of grand banquet set out for sight: colour laid upon colour, gold upon marble, and framed histories staring down from the walls. He saw fine pictures for the first time, and lingered—hungrily, as a man lingers over a savour he did not know the world contained—before Vandyck’s King Charles and his family; and before Tintoret’s Esther, where all the figures wear the magnificent Venetian habit as if cloth itself were a language and splendour an accent. He was so taken that he scarce heard General Lambert whispering names into his ear, as a guide muttering over dishes to a guest who is staring at the table rather than eating.

“Yonder,” said Mr. Lambert, “are two of my Lords of the Admiralty, Mr. Gilbert Elliot and Admiral Boscawen—your Boscawen whose fleet fired the first gun in your waters two years ago. That stout gentleman all belated with gold is Mr. Fox—was Minister and is now content to be Paymaster with a great salary.”

“He carries the auri fames on his person,” George said, glancing at the glitter. “Why, his waistcoat is a perfect Potosi.”

“Aliena appetens—how goes the text?” Lambert continued dryly. “He loves to get money and to spend it. Yon is my Lord Chief Justice Willes talking to my Lord of Salisbury, Doctor Headley—who, if he serve his God as he serves his King, will be translated to very high promotion in Heaven. He belongs to your grandfather’s time, and was loved by Dick Steele and hated by the Dean. With them is my Lord of London, the learned Doctor Sherlock. My lords of the lawn sleeves have lost half their honours now. I remember when I was a boy in my mother’s hand, she made me go down on my knees to the Bishop of Rochester—him who went over the water, and became Minister to somebody who shall be nameless—Perkin’s Bishop.”

Lambert’s voice, trained in camp and council, carried a blunt music; and around them the room had its own sound—silks brushing, a low hum of talk held down to a respectful simmer, now and again a laugh quickly swallowed, as if laughter were a sauce too strong for so solemn a dish. George noticed how each man modulated himself to the place: accents softened or sharpened, words chosen as carefully as a cook chooses salt. A title here, a “my lord” there: the dominant lexicon in which people paid—coin by coin—with deference.

“That handsome fair man is Admiral Smith,” Lambert went on. “He was president of poor Byng’s court-martial and strove in vain to get him off his penalty; Tom of Ten Thousand they call him in the fleet. The French Ambassador had him broke, when he was a lieutenant, for making a French man-of-war lower topsails to him, and the King made Tom a captain the next day. That tall, haughty-looking man is my Lord George Sackville—who, now I am a Major-General myself, will treat me somewhat better than a footman. I wish my stout old Blakeney were here; he is the soldier’s darling, and as kind and brave as yonder poker of a nobleman is brave and—”

Lambert stopped short, for the poker of a nobleman had approached as if to be acknowledged. The General turned and made his bow with perfect composure.

“I am your lordship’s very humble servant. This is a young gentleman who is just from America, and was in Braddock’s sad business two years ago.”

“Oh, indeed!” said the nobleman, as though America were a distant cupboard and Braddock’s defeat an ill-kept secret in it. “I have the honour of speaking to Mr.—?”

“To Major-General Lambert,” said Lambert, with a civility sharpened to a point, “at your lordship’s service—and who was in his Majesty’s some time before you entered it. That, Mr. Warrington, is the first commoner in England, Mr. Speaker Onslow. Where is your uncle? I shall have to present you myself if Sir Miles delays much longer.”

And as he spoke the worthy General turned his whole attention to George, making no account at all of his colleague, who stalked away with a look as if Lambert’s easy audacity had given him a fright. A hundred years ago a nobleman was a nobleman, and expected admiration as naturally as he expected his dinner.

Sir Miles’s red waistcoat appeared presently, advancing through the crowd like a flag of hospitality. Many cordial greetings passed between him, his nephew, and General Lambert; for Sir Miles Warrington was the most affectionate of men. So the General had quitted my Lord Wrotham’s house? It was time, to be sure, since his lordship wished to occupy it again. Very good; but consider what a loss for the neighbours!

“We miss you, we positively miss you, my dear General,” cried Sir Miles. “My daughters were in love with those lovely young ladies—upon my word, they were; and my Lady Warrington and my girls were debating over and over how they should find an opportunity of making the acquaintance of your charming family. We feel as if we were old friends already; indeed we do, General, if you will permit me the liberty of saying so; and we love you—if I may be allowed to speak frankly—on account of your friendship and kindness to our dear nephews: though we were a little jealous, I own a little jealous of them, because they went so often to see you. Often and often have I said to my Lady Warrington, ‘My dear, why don’t we make acquaintance with the General? Why don’t we ask him and his ladies to come over in a family way and dine with some other plain country gentlefolks?’ Carry my most sincere respects to Mrs. Lambert, I pray, sir; and thank her for her goodness to these young gentlemen. My own flesh and blood, sir; my dear, dear brother’s boys!”

He passed his hand across his eyes, choking almost with generous emotion, while George, standing by in borrowed lace, held in his laughter with admirable gravity.

At that moment the door of the King’s apartments opened. The pages entered, preceding his Majesty. He was followed by his burly son, the Duke—a very corpulent Prince with a coat and face of blazing scarlet—then various gentlemen and officers of state. Among them George recognised at once Mr. Secretary Pitt, by his tall stature, eagle eye and beak, and the grave, majestic carriage which made space around him as flame makes air. Even across a century one may feel the room alter for such a presence. Men who are not frightened at kings will hush for a commoner who can command words the way an admiral commands a sea: not with noise, but with an understanding of currents.

Perhaps, too, as we see him issuing with dark looks from the royal closet, angry scenes have been passing between him and his master—Pitt pouring out long speeches, noble phrases on the commonest topics; and the little shrewd old gentleman, who loved plain sense and disliked fine brose and fine poetry alike, enduring it as a man endures an over-spiced dish because the cook is important.

The sublime Minister passed solemnly through the crowd. The company ranged itself along the wall. His Majesty walked round the circle, his royal son lagging a little behind and engaging select individuals in conversation on his own account.

The monarch was a little, keen, fresh-coloured old man with very protruding eyes, dressed in plain, old-fashioned snuff-coloured clothes and brown stockings, his only ornament the blue ribbon of the Garter. He spoke in a German accent, but with ease and shrewd simplicity, addressing those he chose to notice, passing others with a bow. He knew Mr. Lambert well—Lambert had served under his Majesty at Dettingen and under the royal son in Scotland—and he congratulated him good-humouredly on his promotion.

“It is not always,” his Majesty was pleased to say, “that we can do as we like; but I was glad when, for once, I could give myself that pleasure in your case, General; for my army contains no better officer as you.”

The veteran blushed and bowed, taking the praise as a soldier takes a dram on a cold day: with gratitude that warms but does not intoxicate.

Meanwhile the Best of Monarchs was looking at Sir Miles Warrington—whom he knew perfectly well, as the eager recipient of all favours from all Ministers—and at the young gentleman by his side.

“Who is this?” the Defender of the Faith condescended to ask, pointing toward George Warrington, who stood before his sovereign with respectful gravity in Harry’s best embroidered suit.

With the deepest reverence Sir Miles informed his King that the young gentleman was his nephew, Mr. George Warrington of Virginia, who begged leave to pay his humble duty.

“This, then, is the other brother?” the Venerated Prince observed. “He came in time, else the other brother would have spent all the money. My Lord Bishop of Salisbury, why do you come out in this bitter weather? You had much better stay at home!”

And with this domestic admonition, as if he were scolding an old uncle at a fireside instead of a prelate in a palace, the revered wielder of Britannia’s sceptre passed on to other lords and gentlemen. Sir Miles was deeply affected by the royal condescension. He clapped his nephew’s hands as though the King’s few words were a benediction that must be pressed into the skin.

“God bless you, my boy!” he cried. “I told you you would see the greatest monarch and the finest gentleman in the world. Is he not so, my Lord Bishop?”

“That, that he is!” cried his lordship, clasping his ruffled hands and turning his fine eyes upward—“the best of princes and of men.”

“That is Master Louis, my Lady Yarmouth’s favourite nephew,” Lambert said, pointing to a young gentleman with a crowd about him; and presently the Duke of Cumberland came up to their little group. His Royal Highness held out his hand to his old companion-in-arms.

“Congratulate you on your promotion, Lambert,” he said good-naturedly.

Sir Miles’s eyes were ready to burst with rapture.

“I owe it, sir, to your Royal Highness’s good offices,” said the grateful General.

“Not at all; not at all: ought to have had it a long time before. Always been a good officer; perhaps there’ll be some employment for you soon.” Then, looking at George with the quick curiosity of a man who loves the scent of action more than the perfume of rooms: “This is the gentleman whom James Wolfe introduced to me?”

“His brother, sir.”

“Oh, the real Fortunate Youth! You were with poor Ned Braddock in America—a prisoner, and lucky enough to escape. Come and see me, sir, in Pall Mall. Bring him to my levee, Lambert.”

And the broad back of the Royal Prince was turned, leaving his words behind like the last roll of a drum fading down a street.

“It is raining!” cried Sir Miles, as if he had only just noticed the wet world beyond court walls. “You came on foot, General Lambert? You and George must come home in my coach. You must and shall come home with me, I say. By George, you must! I’ll have no denial.”

He drove them back to Hill Street, where he presented the General to my Lady Warrington and his darlings Flora and Dora, and insisted upon their taking a collation, as they must be hungry after their ride.

“What, there is only cold mutton?” he cried, when the dish was set. The meat lay in sober slices, its fat turned pale as ivory with the chill, the gravy long since congealed to a trembling jelly at the edge. “Well, an old soldier can eat cold mutton. And a good glass of my Lady Warrington’s own cordial, prepared with her own hands, will keep the cold wind out. Delicious cordial! Capital mutton! Our own, my dear General—our own from the country, six years old if a day. We keep a plain table; but all the Warringtons since the Conqueror have been remarkable for their love of mutton; and our meal may look a little scanty—and is, for we are plain people, and I am obliged to keep my rascals of servants on board-wages. Can’t give them seven-year-old mutton, you know.”

The cordial came in little glasses, amber-bright, with a smell that tried to be all spices at once—clove shouting over cinnamon, lemon peel turned sharp, and a medicinal sweetness beneath. The mutton itself was honest enough, though firm as if it had been cut from an ancestor. Sir Miles ate with the air of a man serving up not meat but lineage.

In George’s presence and hearing, Sir Miles described to his wife and daughters George’s reception at court in such flattering terms that George hardly knew himself, or the scene, or how to look his uncle in the face, or how to contradict him before the ladies in the midst of so astonishing a narrative. The very words Sir Miles chose gilded and magnified: a bow became a conversation, a glance became favour, a sentence became a prophecy. Lambert sat by for a while with open eyes. He, too, had been at Kensington; he had heard the King’s German-accented civility and seen Cumberland’s broad shoulders; yet none of the wonders Sir Miles now ladled out had been served to him there.

“We are proud of you, dear George. We love you, my dear nephew—we all love you, we are all proud of you—” cried my Lady.

“Yes; but I like Harry best,” said a little voice from below, with the cruel honesty of childhood, as if truth had no need of ceremony.

“—not because you are wealthy!” my Lady hurried on, her cheeks stiffening at the interruption. “Screwby, take Master Miles to his governor. Go, dear child. Not because you are blest with great estates and an ancient name, but because, George, you have put to good use the talents with which Heaven has adorned you; because you have fought and bled in your country’s cause, in your monarch’s cause, and as such are indeed worthy of the favour of the best of sovereigns.”

General Lambert, who had eaten his cold mutton without complaint, listened to this moral sauce poured over it, and held his tongue.

“My dear General,” Sir Miles bawled, “you have kindly condescended to look in on a country family and partake of our unpretending meal. I hope we may see you some day when our hospitality is a little less homely. Yes, by George, General, you must and shall name a day when you and Mrs. Lambert, and your dear girls, will dine with us. I’ll take no refusal now—by George I won’t.”

“You will accompany us, I trust, to my drawing-room?” said my Lady, rising.

Mr. Lambert pleaded to be excused; but the ladies on no account would let dear George go away. No, positively he should not. They wanted to make acquaintance with their cousin; they must hear about that dreadful battle and his escape from the Indians. Tom Claypool came in and caught some of the story, the words and pauses of it—how a man’s throat tightens when he names a comrade dead, how the room grows quieter without anybody commanding it. Flora listened with her handkerchief to her eyes, though she peeped between the folds with the alertness of a flirt who will not miss a detail; and little Miles, before Screwby could carry him off, said plainly—

“Why do you take your handkerchief, Flora? You’re not crying a bit.”

Being a man of great humour, Martin Lambert, when at last he went home, could not help entertaining his wife with an account of the new family with which he had made acquaintance. A certain cant word, humbug, had lately come into vogue, and—will it be believed?—the General used it to designate the household of this virtuous country gentleman. He described the eager hospitalities of the father, the pompous flatteries of the mother, and the daughters’ looks of admiration; the toughness and security of the mutton; and the abominable taste and odour of the cordial—spices turned sour, sweetness turned cloying, as if good fruit had been bullied into liquor rather than persuaded. Mrs. Lambert, hearing, could not but set Lady Warrington’s present warmth beside her former behaviour to poor Harry, and found the contrast hard to swallow.

“Is this Miss Warrington really handsome?” asked Mrs. Lambert.

“Yes,” said the General, “she is very handsome indeed—and the most astounding flirt I have ever set eyes on.”

“The hypocrite! I have no patience with such people!” cried the lady.

To which the General—strange to say—only replied with the monosyllable, “Bo!”

“Why do you say ‘Bo!’ Martin?” asks the lady.

“I say ‘Bo!’ to a goose, my dear,” answers the General.

And his wife vows she does not know what he means, or of what he is thinking; and the General says, with a quiet that ended the matter as a closed door ends a draught—

“Of course not.”
