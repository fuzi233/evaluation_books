Tito's arrival at San Marco had been anticipated. Fra Niccolò, the secretary of the friar known as the Prophet, ushered him up the spiraling stone stairs and into the long, echoing corridors lined with cells. Here, amidst the silence of the monastery, the delicate frescoes painted by Fra Angelico caught the eye. Like rainbows shimmering on melting clouds, these paintings seemed to reflect an ethereal world where the Madonna sat crowned in radiance and the Divine Infant gazed forth with eternal promise.

It was a quiet hour; most cells stood empty, their narrow windows casting light onto bare walls, hard straw pallets, and wooden crucifixes. Even in the innermost chamber, accessible only through an antechamber where the Prior typically worked, the scene remained stark. A single beam of light illuminated the back of a figure clad in the long white tunic and scapular of the Dominicans. He knelt with his head bowed before a cross. To the casual observer, he appeared to be any ordinary Brother Girolamo—a man concerned only with avoiding sin during choir practice or suppressing petty jealousies while illuminating manuscripts, striving merely to climb the narrow ladder of prayer toward Paradise.

Yet, beneath that simple white garment beat a heart pulsing with a consciousness unknown to the average monk. Perhaps no man outside of those who have traversed a tumultuous inner journey could fully grasp it. Within this soul, irredeemable errors and noble aspirations were tangled together; self-serving expedients were woven into the fabric of a monumental mission that the entire being refused to abandon. It was a state where conscience found no perfect rest, regardless of the path chosen.

Savonarola was not merely in the posture of prayer. Latin words of supplication escaped his lips—"Create in me a clean heart"—yet his spirit was not truly praying. He had collapsed to his knees seeking a divine calm that would validate his resolve against the storm of conflicting thoughts and passions raging within him. However, the visions and impulses driving him were too imperative to be silenced. While his hands pressed against his face and his voice murmured the ancient plea, his mind was consumed by the traps his enemies had set. He argued internally, constructing defenses against their taunts and accusations.

His struggle extended beyond his adversaries. That morning, he had received fresh evidence that his own followers and friends were as eager to push for the "Trial by Fire" as his detractors. They desired, silently and openly, that he finally accept the challenge to evoke the miracle that would dispel doubt and crush malice. Had he not proclaimed that God would reveal Himself at the appointed time? To the straightforward minds of the Florentines, anxious to settle political disputes, no moment seemed more fitting than this. If Brother Domenico emerged from the flames unscathed, it would indeed be a miracle, and the fervent faith of the *Piagnoni* viewed it as a promising omen.

But Savonarola was painfully aware that his explanations had failed to quell their secret longing to see him step forward. Yet, satisfying them was impossible. With bitter anguish, he realized he could no longer resist the pressure to allow the trial to proceed regarding Brother Domenico.

He had not lied when he claimed belief in a future supernatural confirmation of his work. His nature allowed him easily to believe in distant, undefined miracles supported by logical reasoning, and even more readily to trust in internal miracles like his own prophetic inspirations. Conversely, it was insurmountably difficult for him to believe in a specific, tangible miracle—one requiring detailed visualization and demanding extraordinary action. For a man of his constitution, the prospect of walking through fire was terrifying. His acute physical sensitivity made the concept of pain and destruction vivid and overwhelming. Just as a man might theoretically accept that hot iron burns, yet practically dare to touch it with a wet finger based on immediate sensation, Savonarola found that when the Trial by Fire was presented as an immediate demand, his imagination recoiled. He could not believe anyone could pass through flames unharmed, nor could he believe that even if he resolved to offer himself, he would not shrink at the final moment.

The common people, however, lacked such nuance. To the general mind, finding moral answers easy and judging conduct by clear-cut choices was a mark of vigor. Nothing seemed clearer than that a man who promised a miracle yet hesitated when tested had spoken falsehoods. How could Brother Domenico and Brother Mariano, along with hundreds of others, possess such courage unless their faith was superior? Savonarola could not reconcile his actions with his friends' expectations, nor could he fully explain them to himself. Human emotions rush to cloak themselves in convenient phrases, but articulating the truth of one's inner turmoil requires more than mere sincerity.

In these very moments, as Savonarola knelt and prayed aloud, he stopped hearing his own words. They were drowned by the clamor of argumentative voices shaping reasons for an external audience. *"To appeal to Heaven via a reckless acceptance of a trap set by ignoble foes is tempting God... Let the Pope's legate come, let the ambassadors of all powers arrive, promising that a General Council and church reform depend on this miracle—I will enter the flames, trusting God will not withhold His seal until then. Until that time, I reserve myself for higher duties..."*

But no! When Savonarola focused his mind on the Piazza, imagining a human body stepping into the fire, his belief shattered. It was not a scene his imagination could view calmly; he felt it with shuddering tremors running through his sensitive fingertips. The miracle could not happen. Indeed, the trial itself should not occur; he was justified in using every means to prevent it. Fuel might be gathered, crowds assembled, formalities completed—all of this might become inevitable, forcing him to act lest he bring dishonor upon himself and, consequently, upon the cause of God.

However, it was not truly intended that the Franciscan should enter the fire. While Savonarola hesitated, there would be ways to stop Brother Domenico. At the absolute worst, if Domenico were forced to proceed, he could carry the consecrated Host, offering a mystery that might suspend the fire's effects. More likely, resistance would halt the attempt entirely, serving as a final barrier to the ordeal. These strategies could not be voiced; he must appear to wait frankly for the trial and trust in its outcome.

This dissonance between his inner reality and outer appearance betrayed the Christian simplicity he had preached throughout his youth and maturity. In the heat of the day, with burning cheeks and shouting crowds ringing in his ears, how blessed is one who remembers the yearnings of the cool, silent morning and knows they have not betrayed them?

*"O God, it is for the sake of the people—they are blind, their faith rests on me. If I don the sackcloth and throw myself among the ashes, who will raise the banner and lead the battle? Was I not led by a path I did not know to the work lying ahead?"*

The conflict was endless. Amidst his prayers, his troubled mind washed its wounds in thoughts of the immense task awaiting him, a task no other man could fulfill if he abandoned it. It was rare for a man to be inspired with the vision and daring required to become a sacred rebel. Soon, the words of prayer faded completely. Savonarola continued to kneel, though his mind drifted to the consequences rippling across Europe, losing the immediacy of his difficulties in the glow of that vast vision.

Then, a knock at the door announced the expected visitor. Savonarola drew his mantle around him, adhering to his custom for receiving guests. Possessing a nature accustomed to exerting influence through speech, he met Tito with a gaze as composed and strong as if he had risen from a moment of resolution rather than internal strife.

Tito did not kneel. Instead, he offered a greeting of profound deference, which Savonarola accepted quietly, omitting sacerdotal formulas. After inviting him to sit, Savonarola asked immediately, "Is your business of sufficient weight to bypass intermediaries?"

"Assuredly, Father," Tito replied. "Otherwise, I would not presume. I will not waste your time with preliminaries. From a remark by Messer Domenico Mazzinghi, I understood you might wish to utilize the next special courier bound for France, carrying dispatches from the Ten. I beg your pardon for my officiousness, but since Messer Domenico is currently at his villa, I wished to inform you that a courier bearing urgent letters departs for Lyons at daybreak tomorrow."

The muscles of Fra Girolamo's face remained perfectly controlled, a hallmark of powerful personalities who habitually caution their speech and share intentions only when necessary. Yet, under strong mental stimuli, his eyes were prone to dilating and brightening—a reaction no willpower could suppress. He stared steadily at Tito, pausing to consider whether this news served any of his hidden purposes. Tito, whose gaze was rarely observant yet missed nothing, had expected exactly this dilation and flash of intensity. Seeing it, Tito immediately began adjusting his gold fibula, seemingly implying patient expectation.

In truth, Savonarola had hoped to receive this intelligence from Domenico Mazzinghi himself, an ardent disciple he had already tasked with writing a private letter to the Florentine ambassador in France. This letter was to pave the way for a missive from Savonarola to the King of France, drafted and ready in his desk beside him. It called upon the monarch to aid in convening a General Council to reform the Church's abuses, beginning with the deposition of Pope Alexander VI, whom Savonarola deemed a corrupt usurper elected through simony and governed by vice.
