To map the contours of a mind stripped bare by the sterility of a fastidious egoism is a difficult task. In such a psyche, all direct desires have dulled, shrinking from active motives into a wavering anticipation of what others might desire. It is a landscape composed entirely of shifting moods, where a fitful, rank impulse sprouts here and there amidst a sea of general, overgrown weeds. Such a condition often befalls a life lived too freely, unshaped by the heavy, molding pressure of obligation. As the old maxim suggests, "As you like" is a treacherous signpost when the terrain is so vast and empty.

Powerful figures announce their intentions with minimal effort, affecting significant shifts in fortune for the price of a few casual words. When Lord Grandcourt learned that Gwendolen Harleth had fled Leubronn, he casually remarked that the fashionable resort was a "beastly hole," inferior even to Baden. To Mr. Lush, this offhand comment was a definitive signal that his patron intended to return immediately to Diplow. Yet, the execution of this plan lagged far behind the speed of the intention. For the following day, Grandcourt loitered, issuing no distinct orders for departure. Perhaps he sensed Lush waiting, perhaps he simply relished the delay. He lingered over his grooming, descending finally with a faded, yet perfectly distinguished countenance that made the fresh complexions of lesser men appear as signs of raw vulgarity. He drifted through the terraces, the gambling rooms, and the reading-room, cultivating an air of profound indifference toward everyone and everything around him.

Only when he encountered Lady Mallinger did he exert any effort. He raised his hat, paused, and feigned attention to her recommendation of the local waters, replying, "Yes; I heard somebody say how providential it was that there always happened to be springs at gambling places."

"Oh, that was a joke," said innocent Lady Mallinger, misled by Grandcourt's languid seriousness, "an imitation of the old saying about towns and rivers, you know."

"Ah, perhaps," said Grandcourt, his expression unchanged. Lady Mallinger deemed this conversation worthy of sharing with Sir Hugo, who chuckled softly. "Oh, my dear, he is not a fool," the baronet assured her. "Do not suppose he cannot see a joke. He can play his cards as well as most of us."

"He has never seemed to me a very sensible man," Lady Mallinger murmured, offering an excuse for her own unease. She harbored a secret aversion to meeting Grandcourt; to her, he was little more than a looming monument to her failure as a wife—a failure defined by her inability to bear Sir Hugo a son. Her constant reflection was that her husband might fairly regret his choice, and if he were not a saint, he might treat her with some roughness, for gentlemen naturally dislike being disappointed.

Deronda, too, received a nod of recognition from Grandcourt, a gesture for which he felt no gratitude, though he returned it with perfect civility. No amount of reasoning regarding the foundations of custom could erase the early-rooted conviction that his birth had been attended with injury for which his father was to blame. Seeing that but for this injury, Grandcourt's prospects might have been his own, Deronda was proudly resolute not to behave in any way that could be interpreted as irritation. He saw a very easy path into mean, unreasoning rancor and triumph in another's frustration, and being determined not to descend into that ugly pit, he turned his back on it, clinging to the kinder affections within him as a possession. Pride, indeed, served him well—the pride of not acknowledging a disadvantage for oneself that vulgar minds are prone to exaggerate, such as the shabby trappings of poverty. He would not allow a man like Grandcourt to suppose himself envied by him. But there is no guarding against interpretation. Grandcourt believed that Deronda, the poor devil whom he was certain was his cousin by the father's side, inwardly winced under their shared position. Therefore, the presence of that less fortunate person was more agreeable to Grandcourt than it would otherwise have been. An imaginary envy, the notion that others feel their comparative deficiency, is the ordinary escort of egoism; and his pet dogs were not the only creatures he delighted in making jealous. Consequently, he was civil enough to exchange several words with Deronda on the terrace about the hunting around Diplow, even suggesting, "You had better come over for a run or two when the season begins."

Mr. Lush, not displeased by the delay, amused himself well, partly by gossiping with Sir Hugo and answering his questions about Grandcourt's affairs as far as they might affect his willingness to sell his interest in Diplow. Regarding Grandcourt's personal entanglements, the baronet knew enough already for Lush to feel released from silence on a sunny autumn day, when there was nothing more agreeable in the lounging promenades than to speak freely of a tyrannous patron behind his back. Sir Hugo willingly inclined his ear to a little good-humored scandal, which he affectionately called *traits de mœurs*; but he was strict in keeping such communications from hearers who might take them too seriously. Whatever knowledge he held of his nephew's secrets, he had never spoken of it to Deronda, who considered Grandcourt a pale-blooded mortal but was far from wishing to learn how the red corpuscles had been washed out of him. It was Lush's policy and inclination to gratify everybody when he had no reason to the contrary, and the baronet always treated him well, as one of those easy-handled personages who, frequenting the company of gentlemen without being exactly gentlemen themselves, can be the more serviceable, like the second-best articles of our wardrobe, which we use with a comfortable freedom from anxiety.

"Well, you will let me know the turn of events," said Sir Hugo, "if this marriage seems likely to come off after all, or if anything else happens to make the want of money pressing. My plan would be much better for him than burdening Ryelands."

"That's true," said Lush, "but it must not be urged upon him—simply placed in his way so that the scent may tickle him. Grandcourt is not a man to be always led by what makes for his own interest; especially if you let him see that it makes for your interest too. I'm attached to him, of course. I've given up everything else for the sake of keeping by him, and it has lasted a good fifteen years now. He would not easily get anyone else to fill my place. He's a peculiar character, is Henleigh Grandcourt, and it has been growing on him of late years. However, I'm of a constant disposition, and I've been a sort of guardian to him since he was twenty; an uncommonly fascinating fellow he was then, to be sure—and could be now, if he liked. I'm attached to him; and it would be a good deal worse for him if he missed me at his elbow."

Sir Hugo did not think it needful to express his sympathy or even assent, and perhaps Lush himself did not expect this sketch of his motives to be taken as exact. But how can a man avoid himself as a subject in conversation? And he must make some sort of decent toilet in words, as in cloth and linen. Lush's listener was not severe: a member of Parliament could allow for the necessities of verbal toilet; and the dialogue went on without any change of mutual estimate.

However, Lush's easy prospect of indefinite procrastination was cut short the next morning by Grandcourt saluting him with the question, "Are you making all the arrangements for our starting by the Paris train?"

"I didn't know you meant to start," said Lush, not exactly taken by surprise.

"You might have known," said Grandcourt, looking at the burned length of his cigar and speaking in that lowered tone which was usual with him when he meant to express disgust and be peremptory. "Just see to everything, will you? And mind no brute gets into the same carriage with us. And leave my P.P.C. at the Mallingers'."

In consequence, they were in Paris the next day; but here Lush was gratified by the proposal or command that he should go straight on to Diplow and see that everything was right, while Grandcourt and the valet remained behind. It was not until several days later that Lush received the telegram ordering the carriage to Wanchester station.

He had used the interim actively, not only in carrying out Grandcourt's orders about the stud and household, but in learning all he could of Gwendolen and how things were going on at Offendene. What was the probable effect that the news of the family misfortunes would have on Grandcourt's fitful obstinacy, he felt to be quite incalculable. So far as the girl's poverty might be an argument that she would accept an offer from him now in spite of any previous coyness, it might remove that bitter objection to risk a repulse which Lush divined to be one of Grandcourt's deterring motives; on the other hand, the certainty of acceptance was just "the sort of thing" to make him lapse hither and thither with no more apparent will than a moth. Lush had had his patron under close observation for many years and knew him perhaps better than he knew any other subject; but to know Grandcourt was to doubt what he would do in any particular case.
