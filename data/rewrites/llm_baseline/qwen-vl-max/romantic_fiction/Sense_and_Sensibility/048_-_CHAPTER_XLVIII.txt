Elinor now understood the true weight of certainty—how different it was from mere expectation, however firmly one might believe in an outcome. She had always allowed herself a quiet hope, even as she told herself it was impossible, that Edward would never marry Lucy. Perhaps he would change his mind. Perhaps friends would intervene. Maybe some better opportunity for her to secure a future would arise—something that would spare them all this pain. But now, the truth was undeniable: Edward was married. And with that knowledge came a sharp pang of regret for the secret wish she’d nurtured, a flattery of her own heart that only made the blow more painful.

She was startled at first by the news that they had married so soon—before Edward could be ordained, before he could take possession of the living. But then she saw how natural it must have seemed to Lucy, who had long been driven by caution and haste, determined to claim him before time slipped away. They had wed in London, and were already on their way to her uncle’s house. How Edward must have felt, riding within four miles of Barton, seeing the familiar face of her mother’s servant, hearing Lucy’s message—she could not imagine. Soon enough, they would settle at Delaford. Delaford—the place where so much of her past lingered, where memories intertwined with longing. She longed to know it, yet dreaded being near it. In her mind’s eye, she saw them already in the parsonage: Lucy, brisk and clever, managing every detail with careful frugality, pretending to be carefree while secretly saving every penny; charming Colonel Brandon, winning over Mrs. Jennings, courting favor wherever she could. And Edward—what did she see in him? Nothing clear. No joy, no sorrow—only a blankness that hurt her more than any emotion might have. She turned away, unable to bear the thought of him.

She hoped someone in London would write soon, to confirm the news and tell more. But day after day passed without word. Though she knew no one was necessarily at fault, she found herself blaming each absent friend—careless, indifferent, slow to act. “When do you write to Colonel Brandon, ma'am?” she asked one evening, her voice tinged with impatience.

“I wrote last week, my dear,” Mrs. Dashwood replied, “and I expect to see him rather than hear from him again. I urged him strongly to come here. He may walk in today—or tomorrow—or any day.”

That was something—a reason to wait, to anticipate. Colonel Brandon _must_ know what had happened. She had scarcely formed the thought when a rider appeared on horseback, drawing her gaze to the window. He stopped at the gate. A gentleman—tall, dark-haired—dismounted. It wasn’t Colonel Brandon. Not in stature or manner. Could it be—Edward? Her breath caught. She looked again. Yes—it was him.

She rose quickly, stepped back, and sat down. “He’s come from Mr. Pratt’s just to see us,” she whispered to herself. “I will be calm. I will be composed.”

But her family had seen too. She watched her mother and Marianne flush, exchange glances, whisper urgently. She longed to speak—to assure them that no coldness, no distance, should show in their welcome—but she couldn’t. She had no words. All she could do was sit still and let silence carry the moment.

They waited. The gravel path echoed with his footsteps. Then he stood in the hall, and next moment, he was before them. His face was pale, agitated—fearful of how he would be received, aware he deserved little kindness.

Mrs. Dashwood, guided by her love for Elinor and her desire to follow her lead, met him with a smile that strained to look warm. She offered her hand and said, “Welcome, Edward. We are so glad to see you.”

He blushed, stammered, and muttered something unintelligible. Elinor’s lips had moved with hers, but once the gesture was done, she wished she had touched him too. Too late. She sat back, forced a light tone, and began talking about the weather.

Marianne had withdrawn into the shadows, hiding her turmoil. Margaret, sensing only part of the truth, chose dignity—she sat far from him, silent, rigid.

When Elinor ran out of things to say about the season, an awkward silence settled over the room. Mrs. Dashwood broke it with a hesitant question: “How is Mrs. Ferrars?”

He answered quickly, “Very well, thank you.”

Another pause. Elinor gathered her courage. “Is Mrs. Ferrars at Longstaple?”

“At Longstaple?” he repeated, surprised. “No—my mother is in town.”

“I meant,” Elinor said, picking up her embroidery, “to ask about Mrs. _Edward_ Ferrars.”

She dared not lift her eyes. But both her mother and Marianne turned sharply toward him. He flushed, hesitated, looked uncertain. After a long silence, he said, “Perhaps you mean—my brother. You mean Mrs.—Mrs. _Robert_ Ferrars.”

“Mrs. Robert Ferrars!” exclaimed both women, stunned.

Even Elinor, though she remained silent, fixed her gaze on him—her eyes wide with astonishment.

He rose, walked to the window, picked up a pair of scissors, and began cutting the leather sheath to pieces as he spoke, his voice rushed and unsteady. “You may not know… you may not have heard… my brother is recently married—to Miss Lucy Steele. The youngest.”

The room fell into silence, broken only by the sound of the scissors tearing through leather. Everyone stared—except Elinor, who sat hunched over her work, trembling, overwhelmed.

“Yes,” he continued, “they were married last week. They’re now at Dawlish.”

Elinor could stay no longer. She rose, hurried from the room, and once the door closed behind her, she sank to her knees, overcome by tears—tears of relief, of joy, of release.

Edward, who had avoided looking at her until now, noticed her sudden departure. He saw her distress—or perhaps heard her sobs. From that moment, he fell into deep silence, lost in thought. No words, no questions, no affectionate greeting from Mrs. Dashwood could reach him. At last, without speaking, he left the room and walked toward the village, leaving the others bewildered, puzzled by a transformation so sudden and profound—one they could not begin to understand.
