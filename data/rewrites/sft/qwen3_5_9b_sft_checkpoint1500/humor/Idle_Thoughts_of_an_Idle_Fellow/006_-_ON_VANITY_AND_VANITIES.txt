Everything is vanity, and everyone is vain. Women are dreadfully vain. Men are worse—if that is possible—and children, particularly children, take the cake. Right now, one of them is pounding on my knees. She demands to know my verdict on her new shoes. Frankly, I think nothing of them. They lack symmetry, they lack curve, and they possess an indescribable lumpiness (which leads me to suspect they've been swapped between feet). But I cannot say this. What she craves is not criticism, but flattery, and I must pour over them with a degrading torrent of effusion. Nothing less will satisfy this self-important cherub. I once attempted the "conscientious friend" approach with her, but it failed miserably. She asked for my judgment on her general behavior, specifically asking, "What do you think of me? Are you pleased with me?" Seizing this golden opportunity for a lesson, I told her bluntly, "No, I am not pleased with you." I reminded her of that same morning's events: waking the house at five, overturning a water jug, chasing it down the stairs at seven, attempting to bathe the cat at eight, and sitting on her father's hat at nine-thirty-five.

Did she respond with gratitude? Did she ponder my words and resolve to lead a better life?

Not a bit of it. She howled.

"That done," she became abusive. "I'm naughty—I'm naughty! Bad unkie! Bad man! I'll tell Mummy!"

And she did.

Since that incident, whenever my opinion is solicited, I keep my real thoughts locked inside me, offering instead unbounded admiration for this young person's actions, regardless of their actual merit. She nods approvingly and trots off to broadcast my "opinion" to the household. She seems to treat it as a testimonial for mercenary ends; shortly after, I hear distant shouts of, "Uncle says I'm a good girl—I get two biscuits!"

Off she goes, staring rapturously at her toes and murmuring "pretty"—a towering monument of conceit and vanity, alongside other wickednesses.

They are all alike. I recall sitting in a suburban London garden one sunny afternoon when a shrill treble voice called from a top-floor window to someone unseen below, "Gamma, my good boy, my very good boy, Gamma! I ate Bob's biscuit!"

Even animals are vain. I recently spotted a massive Newfoundland dog sitting before a mirror at the entrance to Regent's Circus, examining himself with a smug satisfaction rarely seen outside a vestry meeting.

I was once at a farmhouse during some high holiday—a May Day or Quarter Day, perhaps—when they placed a garland of flowers around the head of one cow. That absurd quadruped went about all day as perky as a schoolgirl in a new frock. When they removed the wreath, she became instantly sulky and refused to stand still for milking until it was replaced. This is not a tall tale; it is plain, sober truth.

As for cats, they rival humans in vanity. I have known a cat walk right out of a room if a visitor makes a derogatory remark about felines, yet a neatly turned compliment will set them purring for hours.

I do like cats. They are unconsciously amusing. There is a comic dignity about them, a distinct "How dare you!" air. Dogs, conversely, are "Hail, fellow, well met" to everyone. When I meet an old acquaintance, I slap his head, hurl opprobrious epithets, and roll him onto his back. He lies there gaping, utterly unmindful.

Try doing that with a cat! She would never speak to you again. To win a cat's approval, you must tread carefully. Begin with, "Poor pussy," adding "did hums" in a tone of soothing sympathy. You know not what you mean, nor does she, but the sentiment implies a proper spirit. If you are polite and presentable, she will prick up her ears and rub her nose against you. At this stage, you may venture to chuck her under the chin and tickle her head; the intelligent creature will then dig her claws into your legs, and all is friendship and affection, as described in the beautiful lines:

"I love little pussy, her coat is so warm,
And if I don't tease her she'll do me no harm;
So I'll stroke her, and pat her, and feed her with food,
And pussy will love me because I am good."

The final two lines offer a true insight into Pussy's notion of human goodness. In her estimation, goodness consists solely of stroking, patting, and feeding. I fear this narrow-minded view of virtue is not unique to cats. We all tend to adopt a similar standard when judging others. A good man is one who is good to *us*; a bad man is one who fails to do what we want. The truth is, each of us harbors an inborn conviction that the entire world exists merely as a necessary appendage to ourselves. Our fellow men and women were created to admire us and serve our needs. You and I, dear reader, are each the center of the universe in our own eyes. As I understand it, Providence brought you into existence so that you might read and pay for what I write; while, in your opinion, I am an article dispatched into the world to produce something for you to consume. The stars—the myriad worlds rushing silently through eternity—were placed in the heavens merely to make the night sky interesting for us. The moon, with its dark mysteries, is arranged for us to flirt beneath.

I fear we are mostly like Mrs. Poyser's bantam cock, who fancied the sun rose every morning solely to hear him crow. "'Tis vanity that makes the world go round." I do not believe a man has ever existed without vanity. If one did, he would be an extremely uncomfortable companion. He would, of course, be a very good man, and we should respect him greatly. He would be admirable—a man to be placed under glass, mounted on a pedestal, copied like a school exercise, and revered. Yet, he would not be a man to be loved, not a human brother whose hand we care to grip. Angels may be excellent in their way, but poor mortals like us would likely find them slow company. Even merely good people can be depressing. It is in our faults and failings, not our virtues, that we touch one another and find sympathy. We differ widely in our nobler qualities, but we unite in our follies. Some are pious, some generous, a few honest, and a handful truthful. But in vanity and kindred weaknesses, we can all join hands. Vanity is one of those touches of nature that make the whole world kin. From the Indian hunter proud of his belt of scalps to the European general swelling beneath his row of stars; from the Chinese gleeful at the length of his pigtail to the "professional beauty" enduring tortures to shrink her waist to a peg-top; from draggle-tailed little Polly Stiggins strutting through Seven Dials with a tattered parasol to the princess sweeping a drawing-room with a four-yard train; from 'Arry, winning laughter with vulgar chaff, to the statesman whose ears are tickled by high-sounding cheers; from the dark-skinned African bartering ivory for glass beads to the Christian maiden selling her white body for tiny stones and an empty title—all march, fight, bleed, and die beneath its tawdry flag.

Yes, vanity is truly the motive power that drives humanity, and flattery is the grease that keeps the wheels turning. If you wish to win affection and respect, you must flatter. Flatter the high and the low, the rich and the poor, the silly and the wise. Praise a man's virtues and another's vices. Compliment everyone on everything, and especially on what they lack. Admire beauty in the handsome, wit in the foolish, and breeding in the boor. Your discernment and intelligence will be extolled to the skies.

Everyone can be swayed by flattery. The "belted earl"—whatever that may entail—is get-overable by flattery, just as every other human being, from a duchess to a cat-food seller, from a plow boy to a poet. And the poet is far easier to butter than the plowboy, for butter sinks better into wheaten bread than into oaten cakes.

As for love, flattery is its lifeblood. Fill a person with love for themselves, and what runs over becomes your share, says a certain witty Frenchman whose name escapes me. (Confounded it! I never can remember names when I need them.) Tell a girl she is an angel, only more angelic; a goddess, only more graceful; fairer than Venus, more enchanting than Parthenope; more adorable than any woman that ever lived, does live, or could live, and you will make a favorable impression on her trusting little heart. Sweet innocent! She will believe every word. It is so easy to deceive a woman thus.

My dear little souls, they claim to hate flattery. And when you say, "Ah, darling, it isn't flattery in your case; it is plain, sober truth; you really are, without exaggeration, the most beautiful, the most good, the most charming, the most divine, the most perfect human creature that ever trod this earth," they will smile a quiet, approving smile, lean against your manly shoulder, and murmur that you are a dear good fellow after all
