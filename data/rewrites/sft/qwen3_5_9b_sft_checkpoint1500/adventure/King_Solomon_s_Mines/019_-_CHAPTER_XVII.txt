While Quatermain, Good, and Sir Henry recovered from the initial terror of the Place of Death, examining the gruesome remains of the "Silent Ones" around them, the ancient sorceress Gagool had already moved with terrifying agility. With a scramble that belied her age, she climbed onto the massive slab where Twala lay frozen, moving to inspect the deceased warrior as if checking the ripeness of fruit. After a grotesque, affectionate kiss to the man's ice-cold lips, she shuffled backward, muttering unintelligible phrases to the shadowy figures draped in cloth. Once this bizarre ritual concluded, she sat directly beneath the "White Death" and began chanting prayers that sounded like supplications to the ultimate enemy of humanity. The sheer uncanniness of watching such a wicked creature beg the devil compelled us to move quickly.

"Now, Gagool," I whispered, keeping my voice low enough to avoid echoing, "guide us to the chamber."

The witch descended instantly from the table. "Are my lords not afraid?" she sneered, lifting her chin. "Lead on."

"Guide us," I replied.

"Good, my lords," she hobbled, circling to the rear of the great stone figure. "Here lies the chamber. Light the lamp, and step inside." She placed a gourd containing oil upon the floor and leaned against the cavern wall. I struck a match from our dwindling supply and ignited a rush-wick, scanning the space ahead, but there was merely solid rock blocking our path. Gagool grinned, revealing yellow teeth. "The way is here, my lords. *Ha! ha! ha!*"

"Do not joke with us," I warned sternly.

"I jest not," she insisted, pointing at the rock. "Behold!"

As she raised the lamp, a miracle occurred. A massive block of stone, roughly ten feet high and five feet thick, began to rise from the floor, sliding silently upward into a cavity within the ceiling. It was a feat of engineering, weighing perhaps thirty tons, operated by a system of counterweights akin to a modern sash window. Though we did not witness the mechanism itself, I deduced it relied on a simple lever concealed beneath a specific pressure point; a slight touch shifted the balance, lifting the monolith. Slowly, gently, the stone ascended until it vanished completely, revealing a dark, gaping maw in the rock.

Excitement surged through us, shaking my frame. Was this a hoax, or did the legendary hoards lie within? Would we become the wealthiest men on earth? We would know shortly.

"Enter, white men from the Stars," Gagool called, stepping into the darkness. "First, hear the tale of Gagool the Old. Those brilliant stones you seek were excavated from the pit guarded by the Silent Ones and stored here centuries ago. Few have entered this place since the original guardians fled, leaving the treasure behind. Yet, a legend persisted among the people: a white man arrived from over the mountains, welcomed by the King. A local woman accompanied him, and by chance, she learned the secret of the door—a secret that would elude a thousand years of searching. Together, they entered, filling a small goat-skin with the stolen stones. On his departure, the white man clutched one final, large gem in his hand."

She paused, watching us intently.

"Well," I breathed, "what became of Da Silvestra?"

The old hag recoiled at the name. "How knowest thou the dead man's name?" she demanded sharply, then continued. "None can say. Terrified, the white man dropped the goat-skin and fled, clutching only the single stone. The King seized it—that very stone, Macumazahn, which you removed from Twala's brow."

"Has anyone else entered since?" I asked, peering down the tunnel.

"No, my lords. Only the secret of the door has been preserved. Every King opens it, though none dares enter. There is a prophecy: those who enter shall die within a moon, just as the white man died in the cave upon the mountain. Therefore, the Kings keep away. *Ha! ha!* My words are true."

Her gaze locked with mine, sending a chill through me. How could this crone know so much?

"Enter, my lords," she urged. "If I speak truth, the goat-skin lies upon the floor. And if the prophecy holds, you will learn soon enough. *Ha! ha! ha!* She hobbled through the doorway, carrying the light, though I hesitated once more.

"Oh, confound it all!" shouted Good. "I'm not scared of that old devil!" Followed closely by the trembling Foulata, he plunged into the passage. We quickly followed.

A few yards down the narrow rock-hewn corridor, Gagool waited. "Observe, my lords," she illuminated, "those who hid the treasure fled in haste, lacking time to seal the entrance forever." She indicated large square blocks of stone laid across the passage, intended to wall it up. Alongside them lay more blocks, a heap of mortar, and two trowels that appeared indistinguishable from modern tools.

"Foulata feels faint," she announced, and the girl collapsed, unable to proceed. We lowered her onto the unfinished wall beside her basket of provisions and left her to rest.

Pressing onward for fifteen paces, we encountered an elaborately painted wooden door, standing wide open. Someone had forgotten—or lacked the time—to close it. Lying across the threshold was a goatskin bag, seemingly stuffed with pebbles.

"Hee! hee!" Gagool snickered as the lamplight hit it. "Did I not say the white man fled in haste? Behold the woman's bag! Look within."

Good stooped, lifting the heavy, jingling sack. "By Jove! It appears to be full of diamonds!" he whispered in awe.

"Go on," Sir Henry commanded impatiently, taking the lamp from Gagool and holding it aloft. Stepping through, we followed, momentarily forgetting the diamond-filled skin, and found ourselves in the treasure chamber.

Initially, the dim light revealed only a square room hewn from living rock, roughly ten feet across. Soon, however, hundreds of splendid elephant tusks stacked from floor to arch came into view. Alone, this stockpile of ivory would secure a lifetime of wealth for any man. Perhaps this was the raw material for Solomon's famous throne of ivory. Opposite the tusks stood eighteen wooden crates, painted red and resembling ammunition boxes, though slightly larger.

"There are the diamonds!" I cried. "Bring the light."

Sir Henry approached the nearest crate. Its lid, rotted by time, had been smashed inward. Reaching through the hole, I pulled out a handful not of diamonds, but of gold coins of a shape unknown to us, stamped with Hebrew characters.

"A couple of thousand pieces in each box," I calculated. "Eighteen boxes... surely this was payment for the laborers and merchants."

Good shook his head. "I see no diamonds here, unless the old Portuguese hid them all in his bag."

"Gagool," I said, turning to her, "if no one has entered since that white man, how do you know where the stones are?"

"The Watcher by Night," she mocked, "does not dwell in the stars alone, does he? Some live long, and some have eyes that pierce stone. *Ha! ha! ha!*"

"Look in that corner, Curtis," I instructed.

"Hullo!" Curtis exclaimed. "A recess, shaped like a bow window. Three stone chests stand against the wall. Two are sealed, but the third..."

Curtis held the lamp over the open chest. For a moment, the silvery sheen blinded us, but as our vision adjusted, we saw the chest was three-quarters full of uncut diamonds, mostly of considerable size. I knelt, picking up a few. They possessed the distinct, soapy luster of real diamonds. I gasped as I dropped them.

"We are the richest men in the world," I declared. "Monte Cristo was a fool compared to us."

"We shall flood the markets," Good agreed.

"But first," Sir Henry reminded us, "we must transport them."

Standing amidst the glittering gems, we stared at one another, our faces pale, acting like conspirators rather than the fortunate heirs of the ages. Suddenly, the old witch cackled behind us. "Take them, run them through your fingers, eat them, drink them! *Hee! hee!*"

The absurdity of eating diamonds broke the tension. We burst into raucous laughter, an example the others followed. For a moment, we forgot the danger, reveling in the thought that Solomon's overseer, whoever he was, had laid these treasures specifically for us. Millions of pounds' worth of diamonds, plus gold and ivory, waited only to be claimed.

Then the laughter ceased abruptly.

"Open the other chests, white men," Gagool croaked, creeping away like a snake toward the chamber door. "Take your fill! *Ha! ha!*"

We worked frantically, breaking the seals on the remaining chests. The second was brimming with stones; the third, though only partially full, contained only the finest picks, many as large as pigeon eggs, though some exhibited a slight yellow hue, "off-color" as the miners called it. What we failed to notice was the look of pure malice fixed on us by Gagool as she slipped out of the chamber and down the passage.
