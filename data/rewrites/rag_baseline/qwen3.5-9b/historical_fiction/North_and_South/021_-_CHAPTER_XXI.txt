The rain lashed against the leaded glass of the library windows, a rhythmic drumming that felt, to Margaret Hale, like the frantic, indifferent heartbeat of the city itself. She stood in the doorway of the drawing-room, her white silk dress, usually a symbol of her gentle femininity, feeling suddenly stark against the gloom. Outside, the streets were clean, the night fine, yet inside, the air was thick with the weight of impending doom.

"You look pale, Margaret," her father said, his voice trembling slightly as he steadied himself against the doorframe. "And you've thrown off your gown?"

"I couldn't stay in it, Papa," she replied, her voice barely audible over the storm. "It feels like a costume for a party I'm no longer invited to."

Earlier that evening, walking home from the ball, they had discussed Mr. Thornton. Margaret recalled his cool demeanor, the way he had spoken with detached precision while the workers' anger simmered beneath the surface. "He seemed very anxious tonight," she had told her father then. Now, looking at him, she realized her father was right; his anxiety was a physical ache, a tension that pulled at his shoulders. He was a man of immense strength of character, forged in the fires of practical necessity, yet he carried the burden of being perceived as a 'hard man,' a title that felt heavier than any stone.

"He is proud," her father had argued earlier, defending Thornton's stoicism. "Too proud to show his feelings. A man of unusual intellect, despite his lack of advantage."

"And yet," Margaret had countered softly, "he stands upon his rights as no human being ought to, considering what we and all our petty rights are in the sight of the Almighty."

Now, as the doctor's carriage rattled away, leaving them alone with the silence of the house, the reality crashed down. The servant Dixon burst into the room, her face a mask of terror. "Oh, Master! Oh, Miss Margaret! Thank God you are come! Dr. Donaldson is here. The charwoman is gone home. She's better now; but, oh sir! I thought she'd have died an hour ago!"

Mr. Hale caught Margaret's arm, his knees buckling under the shock. He looked at her, expecting the same calm composure she had shown throughout the day, but found instead an expression of supreme sorrow mixed with a terrifying clarity. She knew more than he did, yet she listened with that hopeless, awed apprehension that only comes when one realizes the finality of loss.

"Oh! I should not have left her—wicked daughter that I am!" Margaret moaned, supporting her father's stumbling steps as they ascended the stairs.

Dr. Donaldson met them on the landing, his face grim. "She is better now," he whispered urgently. "The opiate has taken effect. The spasms were very bad... but she'll rally this time."

"This time!" The word hung in the air, a cruel echo. In that instant, Mr. Hale aged decades. His sight dimmed, his senses wavered, his walk tottered as if he were seventy years old. Dr. Donaldson took his arm, leading him into the bedroom where his wife lay. She slept, but the look on her face was unmistakable; Death had signed her for his own, and it was clear he would return soon to take possession.

Mr. Hale stared at her for a long time without a word. Then, he began to shake all over. Turning away from the doctor's anxious care, he groped blindly for the door, unable to see it despite the flaring candles. He staggered into the drawing-room and collapsed onto a chair.

"Speak to him, Miss Hale. We must rouse him."

"Papa!" Margaret cried, her voice wild with pain. "Papa! Speak to me!"

The speculation returned to his eyes, clouding his reason. "Margaret, did you know of this? Oh, it was cruel of you!"

"No, sir, it was not cruel!" Dr. Donaldson interjected with quick decision. "Miss Hale acted under my directions. There may have been a mistake, but it was not cruel. Your wife will be a different creature tomorrow, I trust. She has had spasms, as I anticipated... she will have a good long sleep; and tomorrow that look which has alarmed you so much will have passed away."

"But not the disease?"

Dr. Donaldson glanced at Margaret. Her bent head, her face raised with no appeal for a temporary reprieve, showed that quick observer of human nature that she understood the gravity of the situation.

"Not the disease. We cannot touch the disease, with all our poor vaunted skill. We can only delay its progress—alleviate the pain it causes. Be a man, sir—a Christian. Have faith in the immortality of the soul, which no pain, no mortal disease, can assail or touch!"

But all the reply he got was in the choked words, "You have never been married, Dr. Donaldson; you don't know what it is," and in the deep, manly sobs that went through the stillness of the night like heavy pulses of agony.

Margaret knelt beside him, caressing him with tearful touches. Time lost its meaning. Mr. Hale, realizing the necessity of the present moment, asked, "What must we do? Tell us both. Margaret is my staff—my right hand."

Dr. Donaldson gave his clear, sensible directions. No fear for tonight, nay, even peace for tomorrow and for many days yet. But no enduring hope of recovery. He advised Mr. Hale to go to bed, leaving only one to watch the slumber. He promised to return early in the morning. With a warm, kindly shake of the hand, he left them.

They spoke but few words; they were too exhausted by their terror to do more than decide upon the immediate course of action. Mr. Hale was resolved to sit up through the night, and all Margaret could do was to prevail upon him to rest on the drawing-room sofa. Dixon stoutly and bluntly refused to go to bed; as for Margaret, it was simply impossible that she should leave her mother, let all the doctors in the world speak of "husbanding resources," and "one watcher only being required."

So, Dixon sat, staring, winking, drooping, picking herself up again with a jerk, and finally giving up the battle, fairly snoring. Margaret had taken off her gown and tossed it aside with a sort of impatient disgust, putting on her dressing-gown instead. She felt as if she could never sleep again; her whole senses were acutely vital, endowed with double keenness for the purpose of watching. Every sight and sound—even every thought—touched some nerve to the very quick.

For more than two hours, she heard her father's restless movements in the next room. He came perpetually to the door of her mother's chamber, pausing there to listen, until she went and opened it to tell him how all went on, in reply to the questions his baked lips could hardly form. At last, he too fell asleep, and all the house was still.

Margaret sat behind the curtain, thinking. Far away in time, far away in space, seemed all the interests of past days. Not more than thirty-six hours ago, she had cared for Bessy Higgins and her father, and her heart had been wrung for Boucher; now, that was all like a dreaming memory of some former life. Everything that had passed outdoors seemed dissevered from her mother, and therefore unreal. Even Harley Street appeared more distinct; there she remembered, as if it were yesterday, how she had pleased herself with tracing out her mother's features in her Aunt Shaw's face—and how letters had come, making her dwell on the thoughts of home with all the longing of love. Helstone itself was in the dim past. The dull gray days of the preceding winter and spring, so uneventful and monotonous, seemed more associated with what she cared for now above all price. She would fain have caught at the skirts of that departing time, praying it to return, and give her back what she had too little valued while it was yet in her possession.

What a vain show Life seemed! How unsubstantial, and flickering, and flitting! It was as if from some aerial belfry, high up above the stir and jar of the earth, there was a bell continually tolling, "All are shadows!—all are passing!—all is past!"

When the morning dawned, cool and gray, like many a happier morning before, Margaret looked one by one at the sleepers. It seemed as if the terrible night were unreal as a dream; it, too, was a shadow. It, too, was past.

Mrs. Hale herself was not aware when she awoke how ill she had been the night before. She was rather surprised at Dr. Donaldson's early visit, and perplexed by the anxious faces of husband and child. She consented to remain in bed that day, saying she certainly was tired; but the next, she insisted on getting up; and Dr. Donaldson gave his consent to her returning into the drawing-room. She was restless and uncomfortable in every position, and before night she became very feverish. Mr. Hale was utterly listless, and incapable of deciding on anything.
