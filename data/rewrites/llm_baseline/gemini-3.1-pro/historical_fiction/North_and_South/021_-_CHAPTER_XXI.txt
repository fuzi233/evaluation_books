Margaret and her father walked home through the labyrinth of Milton’s streets. The night air was cool, though it carried none of the restorative aromas of their Southern past—no damp loam, no crushed wild garlic, nor the slow, sweet woodsmoke of ancestral hearths. Instead, the olfactory landscape was coated in the sterile, burnt-sugar taint of the mills. Yet Margaret, her pretty white silk kilted up to her knee like Leezie Lindsay’s gown in the old ballads, felt a surge of physical vitality, ready to dance along with the sharp bite of the evening. 

“I rather think Thornton is not quite easy in his mind about this strike,” Mr. Hale said, his voice a low, melodic baritone that momentarily humanized the oppressive acoustic ecology of the industrial streets. “He seemed very anxious to-night.”

“I should wonder if he were not,” Margaret replied. “But he spoke with his usual coolness to the others, when they suggested different things, just before we came away.”

“So he did after dinner as well. It would take a good deal to stir him from his cool manner of speaking; but his face strikes me as anxious.”

“I should be, if I were he. He must know of the growing anger and hardly smothered hatred of his workpeople, who all looked upon him as what the Bible calls a ‘hard man’—not so much unjust as unfeeling. He stands upon his ‘rights’ as no human being ought to stand, using the dominant lexicon of commerce to justify himself, while entirely erasing the ancient, communal dialect of sustenance and mutual care. I am glad you think he looks anxious. When I remember Boucher’s half-mad words and ways, the raw, untranslatable vernacular of his hunger, I cannot bear to think how coolly Mr. Thornton spoke.”

“In the first place,” Mr. Hale countered, “I am not so convinced as you are about that man Boucher’s utter distress. But from what you said, it was evident the man was of a passionate, demonstrative nature, and gave strong expression to all he felt.”

“Oh, papa!”

“Well! I only want you to do justice to Mr. Thornton, who is, I suspect, of an exactly opposite nature—a man who is far too proud to show his feelings. Just the character I should have thought beforehand you would have admired, Margaret.”

“So I do—so I should; but I don’t feel quite so sure as you do of the existence of those feelings. He is a man of great strength of character, but his worldview is so terribly mechanized.”

“He has led a practical life from a very early age,” Mr. Hale said gently. “To be sure, he needs some of the knowledge of the past, which gives the truest basis for conjecture as to the future; but he knows this need. You are quite prejudiced against Mr. Thornton, Margaret.”

“He is my first olive, papa: let me make a face while I swallow it. I know he is good of his kind, and by and by I shall like the kind. But I was so terribly disappointed by the women to-night. When Miss Thornton took me away to the drawing-room, I found their company utterly bereft of sensory appreciation. They possessed no reverence for the sustenance they had just consumed. The clear consommé, the heavily spiced imported meats—they ate without tasting the soil, without acknowledging the meticulous culinary techniques or the agrarian hands that harvested the ingredients. They viewed the feast solely as utilitarian fuel and a display of commercial triumph.”

“What do you mean, child?” asked Mr. Hale.

“They spoke a sterile language of status,” Margaret explained, her frustration mounting at the erosion of true cultural memory. “They took nouns that were signs of things which gave evidence of wealth—housekeepers, under-gardeners, extent of glass, valuable lace, diamonds—and each one formed her speech to bring them all in. It reminded me of a parlor game. There was no talk of the changing seasons, no sharing of preserving techniques or ancestral recipes that bind a family to its past. I felt like a great hypocrite, sitting there in my white silk gown with my idle hands, when I remembered all the good, thorough, domestic work they had done to-day to prepare. They took me for a fine lady, entirely divorced from the physical realities of the hearth.”

“Even I was mistaken enough to think you looked like a lady, my dear,” said Mr. Hale, quietly smiling.

But those smiles evaporated into white, trembling dread the moment Dixon opened their front door. The acoustic environment of the house had shifted entirely; the comforting, rhythmic domestic sounds were gone, replaced by a vacuum of hushed terror. 

“Oh, master!—Oh, Miss Margaret! Thank God you are come!” Dixon gasped, her usually steadfast, traditional Southern composure shattered. “Dr. Donaldson is here. The servant next door went for him. She’s better now; but, oh sir! I thought she’d have died an hour ago.”

Mr. Hale caught Margaret’s arm. His face contracted not with the agony of terror, but with an unprepared, visceral shock. Margaret, however, listened with a hopeless expression of awed apprehension. She knew more than he did; the mythic shadow of mortality had been weaving itself into their daily routines for weeks, an unseen guest lingering by their dining table. 

“Oh! I should not have left her—wicked daughter that I am!” Margaret moaned, supporting her father’s tottering steps upstairs. 

Dr. Donaldson met them on the landing, carrying with him the sharp, bitter scent of medicinal tinctures—a smell of macerated poppy and distilled earthy roots that harshly overwhelmed the house’s usual olfactory warmth. “She is better now,” he whispered into the fraught silence. “The opiate has taken effect. The spasms were very bad: no wonder they frightened your maid; but she’ll rally this time.”

*This time!* Half an hour ago Mr. Hale was a hale, middle-aged man. Now his sight was dim, his senses wavering, stepping into the bedroom as if he were seventy. 

There lay Mrs. Hale. The ancient, mythic presence of Death had visibly signed her for his own, resting upon her brow as naturally as the evening shadows in the room. Mr. Hale stared at his wife, his entire frame beginning to shake. Turning blindly from the doctor, he groped for the door he could no longer see. He staggered into the drawing-room, collapsing into a chair Dr. Donaldson wheeled forward.

“Speak to him, Miss Hale. We must rouse him.”

“Papa!” Margaret cried, her voice wild with an untranslatable pain. “Papa! Speak to me!”

He made a great effort. “Margaret, did you know of this? Oh, it was cruel of you!”

“No, sir, it was not cruel!” replied Dr. Donaldson with quick, clinical decision. “Miss Hale acted under my directions. She has taken the opiate I brought; she will have a good long sleep, and to-morrow that look which has alarmed you so much will have passed away.”

“But not the disease?” 

Dr. Donaldson glanced at Margaret’s bent head. He saw no appeal for a false reprieve. “Not the disease. We cannot touch the disease, with all our poor vaunted skill. We can only delay its progress—alleviate the pain. Be a man, sir—a Christian.”

The only reply was Mr. Hale’s choked words, “You have never been married, Dr. Donaldson; you don’t know what it is.” Then came his deep, manly sobs, breaking through the stillness of the night like heavy pulses of agony. It was a primal, acoustic expression of grief, a profound subjective experience that no dominant lexicon could ever capture or console. Margaret knelt by him, caressing him with tear-stained hands. 

Time became a circular, fluid entity. Dr. Donaldson gave his sensible directions, prescribing peace for the immediate morrow but no enduring hope, before leaving them to the quiet of the night. Mr. Hale was prevailed upon to rest on the drawing-room sofa. Dixon, after stoutly refusing to leave, finally succumbed to exhaustion, her snores providing a rhythmic, organic baseline that contrasted with the oppressive silence. 

Margaret discarded her silk gown with impatient disgust, wrapping herself in her dressing-gown. Sleep was impossible; her senses were acutely vital. She sat behind the curtain, a meticulous historian of her own fading life. Far away in time and space seemed the events of thirty-six hours ago. Her concern for Bessy Higgins, her heart wrung for Boucher’s starvation—it all felt like a dreaming memory. 

In the quiet, Helstone returned to her, not as a distant geography, but as an immediate sensory haunting. The dull gray days of the preceding winter, the smell of roasted root vegetables, the seasonal rhythms of the foraging harvest, the meticulous, slow-simmered broths her mother once presided over—these agrarian memories felt more fiercely real than the soot-stained windows of Milton. She mourned the erosion of that cultural memory, the slow death of the native dialect of her childhood. What a vain show Life seemed in this industrial North! It was as if from some aerial belfry, high above the harsh, mechanized noise pollution of the earth, a bell tolled: *All are shadows!—all are passing!—all is past!*

When morning dawned, cool and gray, the terrible night felt as unreal as a dream. Mrs. Hale awoke unaware of how close she had brushed against the veil. She consented to remain in bed that day, but by the next, the heavy opiate’s reaction left her restless, feverish, and deeply uncomfortable. 

On the third day, Dr. Donaldson analyzed her distress. “It is the reaction after the powerful opiates. I think if we could get a water-bed it might be a good thing to prevent the physical toll on her skin. Mrs. Thornton has one, I know. It would do you no harm to have a brisk walk to Marlborough Street, Miss Hale, and ask if she can spare it.”

“Certainly,” Margaret agreed. “I could go while mamma is asleep.”

Mrs. Hale seemed brighter that afternoon after a small, carefully prepared broth, her hand lying in her husband’s. Margaret left them and stepped out into the crushing August heat. 

The sun beat straight down onto the Milton cobbles, baking the dust until the air tasted of hot iron and stale ash. Margaret walked the first mile absorbed in her impending loss, threading through the irregular stream of human beings. But gradually, her finely tuned acoustic awareness registered a profound disruption in the city’s ecology.

Usually, the oppressive, relentless violence of the power-looms dictated the psychological weather of Milton—a mechanical noise pollution that commodified the very air. Today, that rhythmic industrial heartbeat was missing. In its place was a low, distant roar, as of myriads of fierce indignant voices. The inhabitants of the squalid dwellings were gathered in the narrow ways, buzzing with a restless, oppressive irritation. 

From every lane opening onto Marlborough Street came the sound of a gathering tempest. It was the chaotic symphony of the local dialect, an untranslatable wave of acoustic fury rising from the eroded, displaced agrarian souls of the workers. Their voices—fierce, dilated with fear, relentless with threat—surged together. As Margaret reached the small side-entrance by the folding doors in the great dead wall of Marlborough mill-yard, she heard the first slow-surging wave of the dark crowd tumble over and retreat. 

The circumstances pressed upon her senses, but her heart, occupied solely by the deep, physical ache of her mother's fading life, could not process the mob’s significance. The porter opened the door cautiously, his face pale, widening the entrance just enough to admit her before hastily bolting it shut.

“Th’ folk are all coming up here, I reckon?” he breathed heavily.

“I don’t know,” Margaret said. “Something unusual seemed going on.”

She went across the yard. The acoustic vacuum within the walls was terrifying. There was no near sound—no steam engine at work with beat and pant, no click of machinery, no mingling of sharp commercial voices. There was only, from far away, the ominous, deep-clamoring roar of the tempest, asserting its ancient, untranslatable power against the silent mill.
