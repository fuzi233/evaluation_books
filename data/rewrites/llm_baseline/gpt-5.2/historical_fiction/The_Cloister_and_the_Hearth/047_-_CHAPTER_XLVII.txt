Denys chose his hour next day, when the house was quiet and the kettles no longer sang, to tell mother and daughter all that still lay unsaid. He did it with that rough, soldierly delicacy that will spare a foe’s name even while it shields a friend’s honour.

“It is not for me to blacken them,” said he, speaking of Cornelis and Sybrandt. “They come of good stock. But Gerard counts them no friends of his in this matter; and I am Gerard’s comrade. We soldiers have a rule—tell the enemy nothing—save lies.”

Catherine’s hands, that had been busy with a cloth and a trencher, went still. She drew breath, but answered him with silence. When he came to the parting of the friends—how one road cleft into two, and how a brave heart turned away alone—his voice thickened in spite of him; and they wept, all three, as if grief had been boiling there like broth too long left untended, rising sudden and salt to the brim.

Yet, as happens at hearths, sorrow did not end the day. It turned, by degrees, into a hungry hope: Gerard might return—soon, even any day. That prospect warmed them like a pan set near the embers; and Denys, who shared their confidence, would not let it soften him into neglect.

“All this joy,” said he, “is no reason to forget his last words. If he comes back next week and no Margaret to be found—what figure do I cut? What figure do you?”

Catherine, who had never once blessed the truant Margaret in her heart before, felt now a strange tenderness toward her, as if the girl were a loaf mislaid—no use to scold it into appearing; it must be sought, and when found, handled gently lest it crumble.

“I am as anxious as you to find her,” said she. “And to be kind to her before Gerard comes—Heaven knows I am. But I cannot agree to your roving from here. She must have told somebody whither she went. They owe not a stiver in Sevenbergen. And dear heart, Denys, you cannot hunt all Holland for her.”

“Can I not?” said Denys grimly. “That we shall see.”

Then, after a pause in which one could hear, outside, the steady, wholesome sound of a cart far off on the road and the nearer cluck of a hen settling—those homely noises that keep a mind from flying wholly to misery—he set his plan.

“We must divide,” said he. “You stay here, with eyes and ears wide open. I will ransack every town in Holland for her, if need be. But she will not be many leagues from here. They be three. Three fly not so fast, nor far, as one.”

“That is sense,” said Catherine; and because she was a woman that trusted in counsel tried nearest first, she insisted he go before all else to the demoiselle Van Eyck.

“She and our Margaret were bosom friends,” said Catherine. “If any know where the girl is gone, it is she—if she will but tell.”

Denys would have set off that instant; so Catherine, with that swift, household magic by which a woman can turn herself from apron to visiting-gown in the time a man takes to find his cap, made herself one shade neater and went with him.

The old lady received them graciously, seated in a rich room where the hangings drank the light, and the air held a faint, dear odour of dried pigments and old wood—like a chest where summer herbs have lain among linens. Catherine spoke straight.

At the word “gone,” the tapestry slid from Margaret Van Eyck’s hands as if it had been cut.

“Gone? Gone from Sevenbergen and not told me?” cried she. “The thankless girl!”

This heat surprised them.

“What,” asked Catherine, “you know not? When was she here last?”

“Maybe ten days agone,” said the old lady. “I had ta’en out my brushes—after so many years—to paint her portrait. I did not do it, though; for reasons.”

Catherine clasped her hands, half in prayer, half in domestic impatience at a mischance that would not be mended by wringing.

“’Tis a most strange thing,” said she, “she should go away bag and baggage like this, without with your leave or by your leave—why, or wherefore. Was ever aught so untoward? Just when all our hearts are warm to her; and here is Gerard’s mate come from the ends of the earth with comfort for her from Gerard, and cannot find her—and Gerard himself expected. What to do I know not. But sure she is not parted like this without a reason. Can you not give us a clue, my good demoiselle? Prithee now.”

“I have it not to give,” said the elder lady, peevish now, as if ashamed of her own surprise.

“Then I can,” said a voice from the doorway.

Reicht Heynes stood there, colour high, as if she had come in from a hot kitchen where the steam blinds and the pot-lids chatter.

“So you have been hearkening all the time, eh?” said her mistress.

“What are my ears for, mistress?”

“True. Then throw us light.”

“There is no darkness that I see,” said Reicht. “And the clue—why, an ye call’t a two-ply twine, and the ends on’t in this room e’en now, ye’ll not be far out. Oh, mistress, I wonder at you sitting there pretending.”

“Marry, come up!” cried the old lady, her cheek flushing till it matched the servant’s. “So ’twas I drove the foolish girl away.”

“You did your share, mistress,” said Reicht, steady as a chopping-knife. “What greeting gave you her last time? Think you she could miss it—she, and friendless? And you said, ‘I have altered my mind about painting of you,’ says you, a-turning up your nose at her.”

“I did not turn up my nose,” snapped the mistress. “It is not shaped like yours for looking heavenward.”

“Oh, all our noses can follow our hearts’ bent,” said Reicht. “Poor soul. She came into the kitchen to me. ‘I am not to be painted now,’ said she, and the tears in her eyes. She said no more. But I knew well what she meant. I had seen ye.”

Margaret Van Eyck drew a long breath, and her pride, like a pot taken suddenly from the fire, boiled over into explanation.

“Well,” said she, “I confess so much, and I make you judge, madam. These young girls can do nothing of their own heads, but are apt to mimic aught their sweethearts do. Your Gerard is handy at many things, and among the rest at the illuminator’s craft. Margaret is his pupil—patient as a nun. What marvel? Having a woman’s eye for colour, and eke a lover to ape.

“’Tis a trick I despise at heart: by it, the great art of colour—which should be royal and free—becomes slave to the petty crafts of writing and printing; is fettered and made little to match the littleness of books, and go to church in a rich fool’s pocket.

“Nathless affection rules us all. When the poor wench brought me her thorn-leaves, and lilies, and ivy, and dewberries, and ladybirds, and butterfly grubs—all the scum of Nature stuck fast in gold-leaf like wasps in a honey-pot—and withal her day-book, showing she had pored an hundred, or an hundred and fifty, or two hundred hours over each singular page—certes I was wroth that an immortal soul, and many hours, and much manual skill, should be flung away on Nature’s trash and barren letters.

“But having bowels, I restrained, and looked kindly to better it. I said, ‘Sith Heaven for our sins hath doomed us to spend time and soul and colour on great letters and little beetles, omitting saints and heroes—why not present the scum naturally? The grapes I see abroad hang i’ the air, not stick in a wall; and these insects—Nature’s slime in general—pass not their noxious lives wedged in metal prisons like flies in honey and glue, but hover at large, infesting air.’

“‘Ah, my dear friend,’ says she, ‘I see whither you drive; but this ground is gold; whereon we may not shade.’

“‘Who said so?’ quoth I.

“‘All teachers of this craft,’ says she—‘Gerard himself!’

“‘That for Gerard himself,’ quoth I, ‘and all the gang; gi’e me a brush!’”

Here Denys, who had been swallowing laughter and admiration together like a man trying to eat too-hot pottage without wincing, contrived at last to wedge in a word.

“Did she now?”

Margaret Van Eyck stared at him, then smiled, as one who finds a spice she did not expect in the dish.

Then she told them how, step by step, she had been drawn to take up again the art she had laid aside when her brothers died; and how she promised—God forgive her—once more to paint the Madonna, with Margaret for model. In passing, she let slip, with an old painter’s plainness, how girls are turned to saints by brush and by language.

“‘Thy hair is adorable,’ said I.

“‘Why, ’tis red,’ quo’ she.

“‘Ay,’ quoth I, ‘but what a red! how brown! how glossy! Most hair is not worth a straw to us painters; thine is the artist’s hue. Thy violet eyes—which smack of earth, being now languid for lack of one Gerard, now full of fire in hopes of him—these will I lift to heaven in fixed and holy meditation. And thy nose, which already somewhat aspires that way (though not so piously as Reicht’s), will I debase a trifle, and somewhat enfeeble thy chin.’”

“Enfeeble her chin?” cried Catherine, scandal and curiosity kneaded together in her voice. “Alack, what means that?”

“’Tis a resolute chin,” said the old lady. “Not a jot too resolute for this wicked world; but when ye come to a Madonna—no thank you.”

“A resolute chin,” echoed Denys, with soft absurd tenderness, as if he could see Margaret’s face before him.

“The darling!” he added.

“And now comes the rub,” said Margaret Van Eyck, her own voice lowering. “When you told me she was—the way she is—it gave me a shock. I dropped my brushes. Was I going to turn a girl that could not keep her lover at a distance into the Virgin Mary at my time of life?

“I love the poor ninny still. But I adore our blessed Lady. Most painters are men; men are fine fellows—they can do aught. Their saints and virgins are neither more nor less than their lemans, saving your presence. And half their craft is lost on me, which find beneath angels’ white wings the very trollops I have seen flaunting it in the streets, bejewelled like Paynim idols.

“I am not a fine fellow, but only a woman; and my painting is but one half craft, and t’other half devotion. So you may read me. ’Twas foolish, maybe, but I could not help it; yet am I sorry.”

She ended despondently, though she had begun defiant. Catherine, who measured holiness by kindness as much as by doctrine, answered gently:

“You must think it would go to the poor girl’s heart—she so fond of you.”

The old lady sighed and said no more. Then Reicht, who had been biting her lip as if holding back sharper words than any mustard, turned on Catherine.

“Why, dame,” said she, “think you ’twas for that alone Margaret and Peter hath left Sevenbergen? Nay.”

“For what else, then?”

“What else? Because Gerard’s people slight her so cruel. Who would bide among hard-hearted folk that ha’ driven her lad to Italy, and now he is gone, relent not, but face it out, and ne’er come anigh her that is left?”

“Reicht,” began Catherine, stung, “I was going—”

“Oh ay, going, and going, and going,” cried the Frisian girl. “Ye should ha’ said less or else done more. With your words ye uplift her heart and let it down wi’ your deeds. ‘They have never been,’ said the poor thing to me, with such a sigh.

“Ay, here is one can feel for her; for I too am far from my friends, and when first I came to Holland I used to take a hearty cry all to myself. But ten times liever would I be Reicht Heynes with nought but leagues atwixt me and my kith, than be as she is in the midst of them that ought to warm to her, and yet to fare as lonesome as I.”

“Alack,” pleaded Catherine, tears rising in spite of her, “I did go but yestreen—and had gone before, but one plaguy thing or t’other did still come and hinder me.”

“Mistress,” said Reicht, “did aught hinder ye to eat your dinner any one o’ those days? I trow not. And had your heart been as good towards your own flesh and blood as ’twas towards your flesher’s meat, nought had kept you from her that sat lonely, watching the road for comfort—with your child’s child a-beating ’neath her bosom.”

At this, the old Van Eyck—nettled, and yet too proud to seem wounded—sprang from ambush.

“Since you disrespect my guests,” she cried, “seek another place!”

“With all my heart,” said Reicht stoutly.

“Nay, mistress,” put in Catherine, softening as one does when the pot threatens to boil over. “True folk will speak out. Her tongue is a stinger; but better she said it than thought it, so it will not rankle. And part with her for me—that shall ye not. Beshrew the wench, she wots she is a good servant and takes advantage. We poor wretches that keep house must still pay ’em tax for value.”

And then, half laughing through wet eyes, Catherine fell into that domestic confession that comes so readily among women when the air is charged.

“I had a good servant once, when I was young. Eh dear, how she did grind me down into the dust. In the end, by Heaven’s mercy, she married the baker, and I was my own woman again. ‘So,’ said I, ‘no more good servants shall come hither, a-hectoring o’ me.’ I get a fool and learn her; and whenever she knows her right hand from her left, she sauceth me: then out I bundle her neck and crop and take another dunce. ’Tis wearisome, teaching a string of fools by ones; but there—I am mistress.”

Here she forgot she defended Reicht, and turned, spite pricking through fondness, on the girl.

“And you be mistress here, I trow.”

“No more than that stool,” said the Van Eyck loftily. “She is neither mistress nor servant; but gone. She is dismissed the house, and there’s an end. What, did ye not hear me turn the saucy baggage off?”

“Ay, ay,” said Reicht, vast in her indifference. “We all heard ye.”

Then Denys, who had stood hitherto like a man at table watching a quarrel he cannot season to his liking, spoke, and his grave tone made them all wheel toward him as if on axles. The room, a moment before full of voices, fell quiet enough that the small sounds outside—the distant lowing of cattle, the faint tapping of a shutter—came in clear, and with them the sense of how much a house’s peace hangs upon what is said within its walls.

“Mistress Reicht,” said he, with a dignity so ceremonious it threatened to become comical, “you are turned off. If on a slight acquaintance I might advise, I’d say—since you are a servant no more, be a mistress, a queen.”

“Easier said than done,” replied Reicht bluntly.

“Not a jot,” said Denys. “You see here one who is a man, though but half an arbalestrier owing to that devilish Englishman’s arrow—in whose carcass I have, however, left a like token, which is a comfort. I have twenty gold pieces”—he showed them—“and a stout arm. In another week or so I shall have twain. Marriage is not a habit of mine; but I capitulate to so many virtues. You are beautiful, good-hearted, outspoken, and above all, you take the part of my she-comrade. Be then an arbalestriesse!”

“And what the dickens is that?” asked Reicht.

“I mean,” said Denys, “be the wife, mistress, and queen of Denys of Burgundy here present.”

A dead silence fell, like a cloth thrown suddenly over a table. It did not last; and when it broke it broke into a burst of female indignation so instant and unanimous it might have been rehearsed.

“Well, did you ever?” cried Catherine.

“Never in all my born days,” cried Margaret Van Eyck.

“Before our very faces,” said Catherine.

“Of all the absurdity and insolence of this ridiculous sex—” began the old lady.

Then Denys observed drily that the only female to whom he had addressed himself was mute; and the others, on whose eloquence there was no immediate demand, were fluent. At that, the voices stopped; eyes swung pivot-like upon Reicht.

She gave Denys a sly look from under her lashes, and said, “I mean to take a good look at any man ere I leap into his arms.”

Denys drew himself up as if on parade.

“Then look your fill,” said he, “and leap away.”

This, of all things, produced a new result. Margaret Van Eyck thrust out a long white finger straight at Denys’s face, as if taking aim.

“Stand!” she cried, in a shaken voice. “In the name of all the saints—stand!”

Then, as if seized by some fit between worship and folly, she exclaimed, “You are beautiful, so. You are inspired—with folly. What matters that? you are inspired. I must take off your head.”

And in a moment she had pencil in hand. The room, that had been a court of reproach, became a studio; the air seemed to change taste, as if chalk-dust and gum-water had been stirred. Her voice snapped like a cook’s command when the bread must be saved at the last moment.

“Come out, hussy!” she screamed to Reicht. “More in front of him, and keep the fool inspired and beautiful. Oh, why had I not this maniac for my good centurion? They went and brought me a brute with a low forehead and a shapeless beard.”

Catherine stood and stared, half scandalized, half amused, and secretly concluded her venerable hostess had been a disguised lunatic all this time and was now throwing off the mask. Reicht, for her part, was plainly unhappy and cross; and she made no scruple to say why.

“I left my caldron in a perilous state,” said she. “Duties so grave as mine leave no time to waste, a-playing the statee and the fool both at one time.”

Her mistress, without looking up from her furious sketching, reminded her that it was possible to be rude and rebellious to one’s poor, old, affectionate, desolate mistress without being utterly heartless and savage—and a trampler on arts. At that, Reicht stopped, pouted, and looked like a little basilisk at the “inspired” model who was the cause of her woe. Denys retorted with unshaken admiration, standing as still as a soldier ordered, while the pencil ran like a mouse over the paper.

At last the artist’s wrist cramped from long disuse; and only then did she let the moment die, though she had made a rough but noble sketch.

“I can work no more at present,” said she sorrowfully.

“Then, now, mistress,” said Reicht, “I may go and mind my pot?”

“Ay, ay, go to your pot!” cried the old lady. “And get into it, do; you will find your soul in it—so then you will all be together.”

“Well, but, Reicht,” said Catherine, laughing despite herself, “she turned you off.”

“Boo, boo, boo!” said Reicht contemptuously. “When she wants to get rid o’ me, let her turn herself off and die. I am sure she is old enough for’t. But take your time, mistress; if you are in no hurry, no more am I. When that day doth come, ’twill take a man to dry my eyes; and if you should be in the same mind then, soldier, you can say so; and if you are not, why, ’twill be all one to Reicht Heynes.”

And with that she went her way.

But her words did not fall to the ground. Catherine and Denys, walking back together, could not help tasting in them a bitter truth—like smoke in good milk: that blunt, solitary girl had likely read Margaret Brandt aright; and that she had left Sevenbergen not out of caprice, nor only for a painter’s rebuke, but broken-hearted—driven off by the coldness of those who should have spoken to her in the language of kin.

They bade the Van Eyck farewell; and that same afternoon Denys set out upon what Catherine, with a sigh, called his wild-goose chase.

His plan—like many great things—was simple. He would go to a hundred towns and villages and ask in each after an old physician with a fair daughter and an old long-bow soldier. He would inquire of burgomasters about all new-comers. He would go to fountains and watch the women and girls as they came with their pitchers, listening as much as looking—for the turn of a word, the burr of a dialect, the borrowed cadence of a stranger among natives can betray a whole history where eyes see only faces. And away he went.

He was months and months on the tramp, and could not find her.

Yet this chivalrous feat of friendship was in some degree its own reward. He that lives habitually by the drum and the sudden cry does not, between dangers, hunger chiefly for tranquil innocence; he thirsts for keen moments, for the world’s sharp savour. Denys was no exception. His soldier’s life had been half Sparta, half Capua; and he was too good a soldier and too good a libertine to have ever mixed either habit with the other.

But now, for the first time, he found them mixed: at peace, and yet on duty; for he chose to name this roaming a duty, and so kept himself in hand. So all these months he was a demi-Spartan—sober, prudent, vigilant, indomitable; and, though constantly disappointed, happy as men are who have a purpose that holds their heart tight.

He flirted gigantically upon the road; but wasted no time about it. Nor in these wanderings did he tell a single female that marriage was not one of his habits.

And so we leave him on the tramp—Pilgrim of Friendship—as his poor comrade was of Love.
