The Avenger's Secret.

For the first time since his escape, Baldassarre stood within the Piazza del Duomo. A fierce longing burned within him to hear the monk preach again, yet he recoiled from returning to the very ground where he had appeared half-naked, with matted hair and a rope around his throat, labeled a madman. The sting of those words—"surely some madman"—spoken by Tito, remained fresh and venomous. It was not merely their cruelty that wounded him, but the sudden, bitter realization that he might indeed be powerless to prove them false. Alongside his burning desire for vengeance, a cold, calculating fear had taken root: the suspicion that his ability to exact that revenge was precarious. It felt as though Tito had been guided by a diabolical whisper, revealing Baldassarre's deepest shame to the traitor. He was not mad, for he retained the terrible, lucid awareness of his shattered faculties; he measured his own feebleness with a grim precision. Yet, as the first surge of vindictive rage coursed through him, a primal caution followed, akin to a wounded beast or an insect pausing in a tremor of distrust. This wariness, this refusal to take any step that might betray his condition, was precisely why he had spurned Piero di Cosimo's friendly overtures.

He had practiced similar caution at the hospital, answering the brothers' inquiries only with the lie that he had been captured by the French on his journey from Genoa. However, his age and the peculiar cadence of his speech, suggesting a background far removed from the ordinary mendicants sheltered there, prompted the monks to extend extraordinary charity. They gifted him a coarse woolen tunic against the chill, a pair of peasant shoes, and a handful of *danari*, the smallest Florentine coin, to aid his travels. Early the next morning, he began the road toward Arezzo. But at the first small town, he halted and spent two of his coins at a barber's shop. He demanded to be shaved and have his tangled hair cut short, restoring it to its former style. The barber produced a small hand-mirror of polished steel. It had been years since Baldassarre had gazed into glass, and as his eyes met the reflection, a jarring thought pierced his mind. "Is he so altered that Tito truly does not know me?" The thought arrested his impulsive currents with a painful shock; his hand trembled like a leaf as he reached to retrieve the mirror from the barber's grip. He needed to see himself before the transformation was complete.

The barber, noting his trembling, held the glass steady.

"No," Baldassarre whispered, the word heavy with resignation. "I am not so changed." He recognized the deepening lines etched into his brow, the same rough, leathery skin marked by the ravages of time, appearing now like a withered rind. The shaggy white beard offered no disguise to eyes that had studied him closely for sixteen years, eyes that should have searched for him with the fervor of a lover seeking a lost kin among the dead. There was a difference in his gaze, yes—but it was a difference that should have made recognition all the more startling. A known voice is always more chilling when heard as a cry. Doubt was a luxury he could not afford; he felt certain Tito knew him. Pushing the mirror away, he forced the stagnant waters of his spirit to rush anew, reigniting the engines of hatred and vengeance.

Turning back toward Florence, he avoided the city until dusk. Seeking solitude, he diverted from the highroad and sat by a tranquil pool shaded by alders still clinging to yellow leaves. It was a calm November afternoon, and the still surface of the water beckoned as a potential mirror. He sat on the bank, leaning forward to scrutinize his reflection with an intensity he had lacked courage to muster before the barber.

Was there a madness lingering in his eyes? Something akin to the confusion he often felt within?

Now, under the scrutiny of his own gaze, his expression was one of intense purpose. Yet, what of the long, lonely hours when the fog returned? When he sat in a dark void, haunted by whispers that mocked his efforts to catch them, and visions that drifted away the moment he reached for them? In those moments, his countenance must bear the mark of continual frustration and bewilderment. Even worse, when the clouds briefly parted, offering a glimpse of hope, only to roll back together and leave him helpless as before—then his face must have shown a blank, terrifying confusion, as if struck blind.

"Can I prove anything?" he asked the silence, his heart heavy. "Can I even begin to speak with the confidence that my thoughts will not unravel?" Would anyone believe that a mind once filled with rare knowledge, busy with profound contemplation, and eloquent in speech, had been reduced to this? That vast store of understanding had slipped away, like water poured from an urn into the boundless sea? Or did it remain, trapped behind some internal barrier, waiting for a crack to burst free?

He clung desperately to the latter hope. Since the day he first limped from his straw cot, feeling a new, suffocating darkness beneath the sun, his mind had undergone a tumultuous shift. Some changes were gradual, others fleeting. As his physical strength returned, so did his self-mastery and the energy of his will. He had reclaimed the memories of his life intimately woven with emotion, yet the gnawing sensation of lost knowledge plagued him constantly. But occasionally, when strongly agitated, he seemed to momentarily inhabit his former self entirely, as an old man might briefly recall the vigor of his youth. He saw Greek texts clearly again, felt his mind move freely among familiar concepts. It was but a flash, and the encroaching darkness made it seem more horrific, but could it not return and stay long enough for him to execute his revenge? To devise a torment so exquisite that a mere arm could never inflict it?

Rising from his hunched posture, Baldassarre folded his arms and focused every ounce of his remaining mental fortitude onto the plan he must immediately adopt. He needed time for knowledge and opportunity, and during that wait, he required a means of survival without begging. His greatest dread was being perceived as a foolish, helpless senile old man. No one must know that half his memory was gone; the lost strength might return, and even a brief resurgence could suffice.

He knew how to acquire the information regarding Tito. The name "Bratti Ferravecchi" had become etched into his mind through constant repetition. A man in Genoa, having seen Tito's ring, had informed Baldassarre that the jewel had been purchased in Florence from a young Greek, well-dressed and dark-complexioned, at the shop of a rag-and-bone dealer named Bratti Ferravecchi, located in the street also known as Ferravecchi. This revelation sent a violent shockwave through Baldassarre. Until then, he had clung tenaciously to his faith in Tito, refusing to believe he had been willfully abandoned. Initially, he had reasoned, "My piece of parchment has not reached him; hence I toil still at Antioch. But he searches; he knows where I was lost; he will trace me." Later, at Corinth, he had convinced his captors to secure insurance against any inquiries regarding his fate at Antioch, thinking joyfully, "Here, at last, he must find me." But before a year had passed, illness had seized him, leaving him so shattered that he was deemed worthless save for the ransom that never materialized. Sitting helpless in the morning light, supporting his head with blank eyes and lips moving uncertainly, he had looked so much like a hopeless imbecile that his owners gladly released him to a Genoese merchant who showed compassion for an Italian soul.

Months later, aboard a galley traversing the Aegean and the coast of Asia Minor, Baldassarre regained his physical strength, yet upon landing in Genoa, he was consumed by a weariness so profound he wished he had died at Corinth. Only one possibility staved off suicide: that Tito lived, perhaps in imprisonment or destitution. If Tito lived, there remained a faint, deferred hope that Baldassarre might find his son again, clasp his hands, and meet his face-to-face with the one being who remembered him as he had been before his mind broke. It was in this fragile state of feeling that he encountered the stranger wearing Tito's onyx ring. Though Baldassarre could not describe it beforehand, the sight of it awakened dormant fibers, and he recognized it instantly. The fact that Tito, nearly a year after their separation, lived in apparent prosperity in Florence, selling a gem he ought not to have parted with until the last extremity, was a reality Baldassarre shrank from comprehending. He preferred to be stunned by it rather than to analyze it distinctly, focusing instead on the joy of seeing eyes that remembered him. "At least I am not alone in the world," he murmured. "And I shall never be alone, for my revenge walks beside me."

With the resolve of an avenger, he bent down to examine his reflection in the pool again, viewing himself not with the tenderness due a forsaken elder, but with the hard curiosity of an instrument serving a higher master. Human passion, whether low or high, reaches a point where it ceases to be purely egoistic, becoming a fire within the being to which everything else is merely fuel.
