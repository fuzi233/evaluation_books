When my mother learned of my acceptance of a modest position at home, her displeasure was thinly veiled. She had, perhaps, withdrawn her support in hopes of driving me back to Virginia—back to dependence beneath her roof. Yet we never spoke openly of the breach between us. She severed my allowances; I did not protest but found means to live without them. Only through Harry’s private letters did I ever hear that she might have regretted her severity. In later years, when we met again, her manner softened—gentleness toward my wife, a quiet deference—yet no words of apology ever passed her lips, nor did I demand them.

Pride is a curious thing. Was hers greater than mine? I could have spoken, could have bridged the silence with a word, yet I chose to let the wound fester. When I err, I confess it—not for virtue’s sake, but to soothe my own vanity. To beg forgiveness is to humble the self, and I would sooner swallow bitterness than admit fault. There are souls who forgive as easily as they breathe; I merely learned to endure.

Thus, we remained estranged, exchanging only the barest civilities—announcements of births, formalities—until the political storms of the colonies reached even my quiet life in London. Though I had sided with the Crown in the Stamp Act disputes, a rash letter I penned, criticizing the Crown’s heavy hand, cost me my post. My uncle, ever a creature of convention, regarded me with horrified pity, as though I were a wayward sinner beyond redemption. Yet Theo and our child remained dear to him, and he could not wholly sever ties, though he shook his head over my ruin.

Fate, however, is a fickle tide. The death of the Duke of Cumberland—an event of no consequence to me—proved the turning point. My uncle, ever eager to attend royal obsequies, caught a fatal chill at the funeral. Before I could grasp the news, a lawyer stood at my door, addressing me as *Sir George Warrington*.

The title came with lands, tenants, and obligations. We buried my uncle beside his little son in the family vault, where generations of Warringtons lay. The dowager and her daughter retreated to Bath, leaving me master of an estate I had never expected to inherit.

Prosperity, I soon learned, is not the same as contentment. Tenants bowed, the great coach rolled, yet I missed the simplicity of our Lambeth cottage. In London, we revisited old haunts—Theo embracing former landladies, I smiling at memories of leaner, livelier days. Friends from those times—Hagan, Sampson—came to us, though Dr. Johnson, alas, found our quiet house dull.

And I? I grew restless. The hunt, the endless talk of foxes and horses, the dreary sermons—it all wearied me. My wife, ever patient, masked her worry, but I saw it. Even in Eden, Adam must have sighed, *Is this all?*

Here, three pages of my journal are lost—torn out, perhaps in some late-night fit of melancholy. No matter. What remains is the truth: no man confesses everything, not even to the woman who shares his life. Theo knew my moods, my silences, yet she never pressed. She forgave my sons their boyish sins, shielded them from my temper, and played the diplomat when my pride clashed with the world’s expectations.

Money freed me from want but not from unease. The county deemed me aloof; my refusal to bribe or bully my way into Parliament marked me as a man of "dangerous opinions." Meanwhile, Harry, across the ocean, thrived—his letters full of cheer, his plantation flourishing, his loyalty to the colonial cause unshaken. *He* should have been the squire, not I. He would have charmed the neighborhood, ridden to hounds, toasted with the gentry. I merely endured it.

And my mother? Her letters, once icy, now carried grudging news of Harry—his reliance on his wife and mother-in-law, his frugality (negroes without shoes at a governor’s ball!), her thinly veiled scorn for his choices. Yet she wrote, and Theo, ever the peacemaker, wove threads of reconciliation where I would have left the fabric torn.

So passed the years—crowned with comfort, yet shadowed by a quiet disquiet. For what is a harbor, if not also a cage?
