Detective Inspector William Gryce felt the familiar, heavy weight of age settling upon his shoulders. He had reached that particular epoch in a man's life when the natural spirits begin to flag and enthusiasm requires a relentless, artificial spur. Lately, the streets of New York had offered little in the way of special excitement, leaving him feeling dull, superannuated, and distinctly bored. In fact, he was seriously contemplating resigning his commission, trading the chaos of the force for the quiet solitude of the little farm he had purchased in Westchester. Such a retirement, however, failed to lift his gloom; action was a biological necessity for Gryce, and the exercise of his mental faculties provided a vitality that any material advantage could never hope to match.

Yet, destiny had other plans. Just as his secret dissatisfaction peaked, a telephone message crackled through Headquarters, jolting the old man back to a vigor reminiscent of his younger days. It was a dispatch from Carter's famous drugstore, reporting that a young boy had been sent inside by a mysterious woman. She claimed a bizarre crime had been committed in the mansion located on the corner of ---- Street. The boy, trembling and visibly shaken, admitted he did not know the lady, though it was obvious she had paid him handsomely. He was too frightened to speak freely until he realized the police were listening, at which point he agreed to wait at the drugstore for instructions.

*"Strange"* crime. The adjective hung in the air, striking Gryce with a quiet, persistent insistence that made him forget his advancing years in a desperate desire to understand its meaning. Around him, the uniformed officers exchanged hushed remarks about the house, a landmark of the previous century pressed tight between modern clubs and restaurants. It was a spot notorious among the locals for its history and traditions, regardless of whether anyone actually knew them. Now, a crime had occurred within those walls.

Gryce caught the eye of the inspector in charge, receiving immediate authorization to investigate. Without hesitation, he moved out, heading first to the drugstore where the boy was waiting. He took the lad with him, marching straight to the indicated address. En route, Gryce prodded the poor waif for details, but the boy's narrative remained static, offering no new data beyond the initial report. He insisted that a "lady"—a term he used carefully, avoiding "woman"—approached him while he gazed at toys in a shop window. She handed him a coin, dragged him down the street, and eventually produced another piece of money at the drugstore counter. Her promise was clear: if he delivered a specific message to the telephone operator regarding a strange crime in the old house, she would pay him. Frightened by the gravity of her words, the boy had scrambled away, only to be shaken back to his senses by the woman's grip before fleeing to deliver the news.

There was a certain candor in the boy's tone, and Gryce was inclined to believe him. However, when asked to describe the lady, the boy's observational skills proved woefully inadequate, typical of his class. All he could muster was that she was a "stunner," wearing shiny clothes and jewels. Recognizing these limitations just as he stood before the ominous building, Gryce ceased questioning the child and focused entirely on the architecture ahead.

To the naked eye, nothing suggested crime or disturbance. A closed door, a pristine stoop, and windows draped in heavy curtains hinted at inner tranquility and domestic respectability. The calm brick façade, trimmed in brownstone, stood in stark, pleasing contrast to the chaotic, sign-laden buildings jutting out on either side.

"Some mistake," Gryce murmured to himself as the perfect silence of the establishment struck him anew. Before he could conclude he was the victim of a hoax, a figure emerged from beneath the stoop—an officer whose countenance betrayed sufficient perplexity to prompt Gryce's urgent inquiry.

"Anything wrong? Bloodshed? Everything appears quiet here."

The officer saluted respectfully. "I cannot gain entry. I've rung every bell. If I hadn't seen what looked like a stir in an upper window, I'd assume the house is empty. Should I attempt to breach the rear yard via the lower windows of Knapp & Co., next door?"

"Yes," Gryce commanded, pointing toward the boy. "Take this lad with you. Secure him in one of their offices, then find a way into this house from the backyard. It should be straightforward."

The officer nodded, seized the boy by the arm, and vanished instantly into the neighboring store. Gryce remained in the area square, soon besieged by a throng of curious passersby eager to add their gossip to the growing trouble. Their clamor was cut short by the sudden opening of the front door, allowing Gryce to slip inside before the crowd could swarm.

Within, the atmosphere was one of absolute stillness. He descended into a basement hall leading to a kitchen that appeared cleaner and more orderly than usual for a household engaged in significant activity, yet he saw no evidence of tragedy or a disruption of daily routine. Satisfied that the basement held no clues, Gryce ascended the stairs alongside the officer.

As they emerged onto the parlor floor, the officer whispered a crucial detail: "Mr. Raffner, from the firm next door, tells me the occupant is a peculiar sort—a 'bookworm,' they say. He has lived here for six months, yet scarcely a soul other than himself and a strange, uncommunicative old servant has ever been seen upon the premises."

"I know," Gryce muttered softly. Everyone knew. This house, formerly the seat of one of New York's most aristocratic families, was currently inhabited by Mr. Adams. Adams was renowned for his formidable personal charm, his immense wealth, and above all, his uncongenial temperament, which strictly precluded social interaction. It was precisely this knowledge that lent zest to the investigation. Entering the home of such a man was an event in itself; entering it on an errand of life and death was something else entirely. Under the inspiration of such rare opportunities, life was reawakened in the old veins of a sagacious, albeit octogenarian, detective.

They traversed a wide, old-fashioned hall sparsely furnished in an ancient style. Two doors stood open, inviting exploration. They entered the nearest, nearly opposite the staircase, and found themselves in a room barren as a physician's outer office. There was nothing of note worthy of attention, and they would have departed as unceremoniously as they arrived had it not been for a glimpse of richness peeking through the half-drawn folds of a portière at the far end of the room.

Advancing through the indicated doorway, they paused. A single look about them rendered them speechless, appalled. Nothing in their vast experiences—few things in life prepare one for the thrilling, solemn nature of what lay before them—could have prepared them for the scene they were now summoned to contemplate.

Should I attempt its description?

It was a room of small, circular proportions, hung with strange tapestries relieved here and there by priceless curios. Although it was still daylight, the space was illuminated not by the sun, but by a jet of rose-colored light concentrated with unnatural intensity. This light did not fall upon the endless rows of books lining the lower walls, nor did it touch the great painting which, in another era, might have captured the eye. Instead, the crimson glow bathed the upturned face of a man lying upon a bearskin rug.

In his heart, a dagger pierced deep. Upon his breast lay a cross, its golden lines sharply outlined against the long, dark, swathing garment covering his body. The illumination gave him the appearance of a saint prepared in some holy sanctuary for burial, save for two fatal discrepancies: the dagger spoke of a violent end, and his face bore an anguish for which Mr. Gryce, despite a lifetime of solving mysteries, found no name. It was an expression that answered to neither fear, pain, nor surprise, nor any emotion typically visible on the countenances of victims struck down by assassins.
