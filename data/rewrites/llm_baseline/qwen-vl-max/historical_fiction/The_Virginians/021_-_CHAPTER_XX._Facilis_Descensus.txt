While the venerable Bishop of Cambray, in his tale recently mentioned, lamented Calypso’s sorrow at Ulysses’ departure, I wonder if he ever noted the grief of Calypso’s maid as she bade farewell to Odysseus’s own servant. Surely, in the kitchen shadows, the two servants wept together while their masters and mistresses clung in passionate embrace in the drawing-room. They stood side by side in the fore-cabin, hearts aching, as their superiors tore themselves apart in the grand saloon. When the bell tolled for the final time, and Ulysses’s mate roared, “Now! Any one for shore!” both Calypso and her maid stepped across the same plank—palms trembling, eyes brimming with tears. Each waved a handkerchief—though of vastly different worth and weave—as they stood on the quay, watching the ship sail away. The crowd ashore and the crew on deck shouted “Hip, hip, hooray!” or whatever Greek equivalent might have echoed through the harbor. But the truth remains: if Calypso could not be consoled, neither could her maid. They shared the same grief, walked the same path of parting, and shed tears equally salt and deep—whether in marble halls or in the servants’ quarters.

Not only did Harry Warrington leave Castlewood undone by love, but Gumbo too departed from those halls, captivated by the same tender passion. His wit, charm, cheerful nature, and talents in dance, music, and cookery had won him the affection of every woman in the household. While some men might have resented him, the ladies adored him. In England at that time, there was no widespread prejudice against Black servants such as later emerged among white society. Though not equal in status, they were tolerated, even welcomed with a kind of whimsical sympathy—and especially so by women, whose kindness often knew no bounds. When Ledyard and Parke, in Blackmansland, faced persecution from men, did not the Black women show them compassion? Women have always shown tenderness toward our kind. What (mental) negroes do they not cherish? What (moral) hunchbacks do they not adore? What lepers, fools, dullards, or misshapen creatures (I speak metaphorically) do they not cradle and delight in? Gumbo was treated with more warmth than many who were no better than himself—only the male servants in the hall rejoiced at the Virginian boy’s departure. I should like to see him say goodbye. I would love to see Molly, the housemaid, slipping into the terrace gardens at dawn to gather a wistful bouquet. I’d like to watch Betty, the kitchen girl, snipping a thick lock of her chestnut curls, intending to trade it for a woolly token from young Gumbo’s head. Of course, he claimed to be *regum progenies*, a descendant of Ashante kings. In Caffraria, Connaught, and other lands where hereditary bondage once flourished, surely countless such royal bloodlines must have existed—judging by their descendants today.

On the morning of Madame de Bernstein’s departure, all the servants of Castlewood gathered at doors and passages—some hoping for one last glimpse of her ladyship’s attendants and the enchanting Gumbo, others to bid farewell to her maid, all eager to catch the Baroness and her nephew for parting gifts. It was customary in those days for departing guests to bestow generous tips upon the household staff. Both the Baroness and her nephew gave liberal rewards—to the liveried men, the gentlemen in black and ruffles, and the swarm of female servants. Castlewood had been the Baroness’s childhood home; and as for Harry, who had lived there free of charge, winning horses and money—or promises of money—from his cousin and the unfortunate chaplain—he naturally felt generous. “My mother,” he thought, “would expect me to be kind to all who served the Esmond family.” So he scattered gold pieces freely, as though he were as rich as Gumbo proclaimed him. No one came near him without receiving a token of his goodwill. From the major-domo to the shoeblack, Mr. Harry offered a peace offering to each. He gave something to the stern housekeeper in her still-room, the frail old porter in his lodge—every soul touched by his generosity. When a man falls in love with one woman in a family, how quickly he grows fond of everyone connected to her. He flatters the maids, is courteous to the butler, takes interest in the footman, runs errands for the daughters, gives advice and lends money to the younger son at college, pats dogs he would otherwise kick, smiles at old stories told by papa, drinks sweet port wine he’d otherwise curse, endures the grumpy maiden aunt, taps his fingers to little Fanny’s piano piece, and laughs when naughty Bobby spills coffee on his shirt.

Harry Warrington, in his own way and according to the customs of the age, had, for a brief time (which suggests his love had only just been declared and accepted), offered these innocent tokens of affection to the Castlewood household. Cousin Will should have reclaimed his money and welcome—or at least as much of Harry’s as the lad could spare. Yet the young man, though smitten, remained sharp, clever, and fond of fair play, and knew a good horse when he saw one. Having won all of Will’s money—plus numerous valuable autographs from Mr. Esmond—Harry was delighted to claim Will’s brown horse—the very animal that nearly threw him into the water on the night of his arrival. He had seen the horse perform many times, and even amid his romantic fervor, he took pride in owning such a sound, swift, well-bred hunter. After gazing long enough at the stars above his beloved’s window and extinguishing her candle, he retired to his room, no doubt dreaming of Maria and his new horse, imagining how sweet it would be to ride across the island with her arms wrapped around his waist. He fell asleep musing on these joys, blessing Maria in his heart, knowing he would enjoy her company for at least one more week.

Early the next morning, Chaplain Sampson sent over his small black mare, delivered by his groom, footman, and gardener—all of whom wept and kissed the horse’s white nose before handing it over to Gumbo. Both Gumbo and Harry were moved by the man’s emotion—Gumbo showing his feelings through tears, Harry by producing two guineas, which astonished and comforted the chaplain’s boy. Then Gumbo and the former groom led the mare to the stable, instructed to bring it back with Mr. William’s horse after breakfast, at the appointed hour for the Baroness’s carriages.

So respectful was he to his aunt—or so grateful for her leaving—that the master of the house even appeared at breakfast to bid farewell to his guests. The ladies and the chaplain were present; the only absent family member was Will, who left a note for his cousin, written in poor spelling: he had to go to Salisbury Races that morning but had left the horse Harry had won the previous night, which Tom, his groom, would deliver to Mr. Warrington’s servant. Will’s absence did not stop the others from sharing a peaceful cup of tea. Soon, the carriages rolled into the courtyard, servants loaded the Baroness’s extensive luggage, and the moment of departure arrived.

A large open landau carried the stout Baroness and her niece, with two male servants riding atop, pistols and blunderbusses ready in case of highwaymen. Another carriage held the ladies’ maids and a guard over the trunks—numerous and bulky, though nothing compared to the vast baggage trains of modern ladies. Mr. Warrington’s modest valises were placed in this second carriage under the maid’s care, and Gumbo planned to ride beside the window for most of the journey.

Lord Castlewood, with his stepmother and Lady Fanny, escorted their kinswoman to the carriage steps, embracing her with heartfelt farewells. Lady Maria followed in a riding dress, which Harry Wawah considered the most becoming attire imaginable. A host of servants stood nearby, begging Heaven bless her ladyship. The Baroness’s departure was known in the village, and dozens of villagers waited beneath the trees outside the gates, cheering and waving hats as the heavy carriages rolled away.

Gumbo returned with Mr. Warrington’s horses as Lord Castlewood walked arm-in-arm with his young guest through the courtyard. “I hear you’re taking some of our horses out of Castlewood?” the lord said.

Harry blushed. “A gentleman cannot refuse a fair game at cards,” he replied. “I never wanted to play, nor would have bet money had my cousin William not forced me. As for the chaplain—I hated to win from him, but he was just as eager as Will.”

“I understand, I understand. There’s no blame on you, my boy. At Rome, you do as Rome does. And I’m glad you’ve given Will a lesson. He’s mad for gambling—would stake his coat off his back—and I and the family have paid his debts far too often. May I ask how much you’ve won?”

“Eighteen pieces the first few days, plus his note for a hundred and twenty, and the brown horse—sixty. That’s nearly two hundred. But, you know, cousin, it was all fair play. I didn’t want to gamble at all. Will isn’t a match for me, my lord—that’s the truth. He really isn’t.”

“He’s a match for most people,” said Lord Castlewood. “His brown horse, you said?”

“Yes. Prince William, out of Constitution. You don’t suppose I’d set sixty against his bay, my lord?”

“Oh, I didn’t notice. I saw Will riding out this morning—perhaps I didn’t pay attention to his mount. And you won the black mare from the parson?”

“For fourteen. He’ll suit Gumbo well. Why hasn’t that fellow come back with the horses?” Harry’s thoughts were already with lovely Maria. He longed to be riding beside her.

“When you reach Tunbridge, cousin Harry, keep an eye out for sharper players than the chaplain and Will. There’s all sorts of odd characters at the Wells.”

“A Virginian learns to take care of himself, my lord,” Harry replied with a knowing nod.

“So it seems! I entrust my sister to you, Harry. Though she’s not a child in years, she’s as innocent as one. See that she comes to no harm?”

“I’ll guard her with my life, my lord!” Harry declared.

“You’re a brave fellow. By the way, unless you’re deeply attached to Castlewood, I wouldn’t rush to return to this lonely, crumbling old house. I myself plan to go elsewhere and won’t be back until partridge-shooting season. Go and look after the women—my sister and the Baroness.”

“I will indeed,” said Harry, his heart swelling with joy at the thought.

“And I’ll write when you should bring my sister home. Here come the horses. Have you said goodbye to the Countess and Lady Fanny? They’re waving from the music-room balcony.”

Harry hurried upstairs to bid the ladies farewell, making it brief—eager to rejoin his beloved. He descended to mount his newly acquired steed, which Gumbo, astride the parson’s black mare, held by the reins.

There stood Gumbo on the black mare, indeed, holding another horse—but it was not brown. It was a bay, with broken knees—an old, worn-out beast.

“What is this?” Harry exclaimed.

“Your honour’s new horse,” said the groom, touching his cap.

“This brute?” Harry protested, using one of the expressions common in England and Virginia at the time. “Bring me Prince William, Mr. William’s horse—the brown one!”

“Mr. William rode Prince William to Salisbury Races this morning. His last words were, ‘Sam, saddle my bay horse, Cato, for Mr. Warrington. He’s Mr. Warrington’s horse now. I sold him to him last night.’ And I know your honour is generous—you’ll consider the groom.”

Lord Castlewood couldn’t help laughing at Sam’s words, while Harry let loose several remarks too vulgar for polite print.

“Mr. William said he’d never part with Prince William for less than a hundred and twenty,” the groom added, looking at Harry.

Lord Castlewood laughed harder. “Will has been too much for you, Harry Warrington.”

“Too much for me, my lord? Like a man rolling loaded dice and throwing sixes—too much for me. I don’t call this betting. I call it cheating.”

“Mr. Warrington! Spare me foul language about my brother, please. I assure you, I’ll see justice done. Farewell. Ride quickly, or your coaches will reach Farnham before you.” With that, the lord waved goodbye and entered the house, while Harry and Gumbo rode out of the courtyard. The young Virginian was too eager to reunite with the carriages and his charmer to notice the unspoken devotion Gumbo cast toward a young girl in the porter’s lodge.

After the youth left, the chaplain and Lord Castlewood sat down to finish their breakfast in peace. The two ladies did not return.

“That was one of Will’s confounded tricks,” said the lord. “If our cousin breaks Will’s head, I wouldn’t be surprised.”

“He’s used to it, my lord. Still,” added the chaplain with a grin, “last night, when we played, the horse’s color wasn’t mentioned. I couldn’t escape—I had only one. And the black boy rode off on him. The young Virginian plays like a man, I must give him credit.”

“He wins because he doesn’t fear losing. I suspect he’s quite wealthy. His mother’s legal agents are my lawyers, and they report the estate is practically a principality, growing richer every year.”

“If it were a kingdom, I know whom Mr. Warrington would make queen,” said the chaplain, sycophantically.

“Who can judge taste, parson?” asked Lord Castlewood with a sneer. “All men are alike. The first woman I loved was forty—and as jealous as if she were fifteen. It runs in the family. Colonel Esmond (the one in scarlet and breastplate yonder) married my grandmother, who was almost old enough to be his mother. If this lad chooses to take an elderly princess to Virginia, we mustn’t stand in his way.”

“‘Twere a consummation devoutly to be wished!” cried the chaplain. “Shall I go to Tunbridge Wells myself, my lord, to be on hand and ready to perform my sacred duty for the young couple?”

“You shall have a pair of new nags, parson, if you do,” said the lord. And with that, we leave them enjoying a quiet pipe after breakfast.

Harry was so eager to join the carriages that he nearly forgot to remove his hat and acknowledge the cheers of the Castlewood villagers. They all liked the lad—his honest face and warm manner earned him respect wherever he went. Legends still lingered in Castlewood about his grandparents, particularly how his grandfather, Colonel Esmond, might have become Lord Castlewood but refused the title. Old Lockwood at the gate often recounted the colonel’s bravery in Queen Anne’s wars. His feats were exaggerated, and the behavior of the current family contrasted sharply with that of the old lord and lady—who may not have been popular in their day, but were seen as better folk than those now in power. Lord Castlewood was a harsh landlord, perhaps disliked more because he was known to be poor and in debt than because of his severity. As for Mr. Will, no one liked him. He’d had fights and quarrels throughout the village, suffered and dealt broken heads, had unpaid bills in neighboring towns, been brought before magistrates for seducing village girls, and been ambushed and beaten by angry husbands, fathers, and lovers. A century ago, his character might have been painted in full detail by a social observer; today, the Comic Muse merely hints at someone behind Molly Seagrim’s curtain, then passes on primly, fanning herself in horror. The village knew the young Virginian had beaten Mr. Will at riding, jumping, shooting, and finally at cards—everything was known—and they respected Harry all the more for it. Above all, they admired him for the legend of immense wealth Gumbo had created for his master. This fame had spread across the county and now preceded him on the boxes of Madame Bernstein’s carriages, where valets, descending at inns to rest the horses, told wild tales of the young Virginian’s rank and splendor. He was a prince in his own land. He had gold mines, diamond mines, furs, tobacco—what else, who knew? No wonder the honest Britons cheered him and respected him for his success, as noble-hearted people always do. I’m surprised city corporations didn’t send him formal addresses and golden boxes with the freedom of the city—he was so rich. Ah, what a proud thing it is to be a Briton, to know that nowhere else is prosperity so honored, and achievement so warmly celebrated!

Thus, leaving the villagers shouting and tossing hats in the air, Harry spurred his sorry beast and galloped forward, with Gumbo behind, until he caught up with the cloud of dust surrounding his charmer’s carriage. Breaking through the haze, he found himself at the window. Lady Maria occupied the back seat alone; by staying slightly behind the wheels, he could gaze upon her divine eyes and smile. She raised a finger to her lips. Madame Bernstein was already sleeping. Harry didn’t wish to disturb the old lady. To see his cousin was bliss enough. The landscape might have been beautiful, but what did he care? All the skies and trees of summer meant nothing compared to her face; the hedgerow birds sang no sweeter song than her soft syllables.

The Baroness’s fat horses were accustomed to short rides, gentle paces, and frequent feeding; so despite Harry’s poor mount, he managed easily to keep pace with his elderly kinswoman. At two o’clock, they stopped for two hours to dine. Mr. Warrington paid the landlord generously. What price could be too high for the pleasure of being near his beloved Maria, and having the chance to talk with her, scarcely interrupted by the soft breathing of Madame de Bernstein, who, after a satisfying meal, slipped into a pleasant half-hour nap? In low, tender voices, Maria and her young man repeated their sweet nonsense—words no one in Harry’s condition ever tires of hearing.

They were heading to a crowded spa town, where beauty and fashion would abound. Timid Maria feared that among the young beauties, Harry would find someone far more worthy of his attention than her plain face and figure. “By all the gods,” Harry vowed, “Venus herself could not lure me from your side.” Yet it was he who truly had reason to worry. When fashionable young men saw his exquisite Maria, they would surround her carriage, make her forget the rough, humble American lad who knew nothing of elegance or wit—whose only treasure was a faithful heart.

Maria smiled, looked heavenward, and declared that Harry misunderstood women. It was men, she insisted, who were famously faithless, who toyed with fragile hearts. A minor argument ensued. A clatter broke out among the dessert knives and forks. A glass tumbled and shattered. An “Oh!” escaped Maria’s lips. The disturbance came from Harry’s broad coat sleeve, stretched across the table to grasp Lady Maria’s hand, knocking over the wineglass. Was there anything more natural—or necessary—than for Harry, upon hearing his sex accused of fickleness, to seize his fair accuser’s hand and swear eternal loyalty on those lovely fingers?

What a role hands once played in courtship! How delicately they were squeezed at that stage of life! How eagerly thrust into conversation! What absurd vows and declarations passed through their touch! What purpose could there be in pulling and pressing a thumb and four fingers? I imagine Alexis laughing, reading this page beside Araminta. To talk about thumbs indeed!... Maria glanced around to see if Madame Bernstein had awakened from the crash—yet the old lady slept peacefully, so her niece deemed it safe to yield to Harry’s gentle pressure.

The horses were put back to work. Paradise ended—at least until the next opportunity. When the landlord entered with the bill, Harry stood at a distance from his cousin, staring out the window at the gathering cavalcade below. Madame Bernstein stirred awake, smiling and unaware. With profound care and reverent politeness, Mr. Warrington helped his aunt into her carriage. How demure and simple Lady Maria looked as she followed! The carriages rolled away, amid deep bows from the landlord and waiters, countryfolk gathered around the blazing inn sign, shopkeepers peering from their modest doors, boys and market-goers under the colonnade of the old town hall, and loungers along the gabled street. “It’s the famous Baroness Bernstein. That’s her, the old lady in the capuchin. It’s the rich young American just come from Virginia—worth millions and millions. Well, sure, he might have a better horse.” The procession vanished, and the little town sank back into its usual quiet. The landlord returned to his friends at the club to recount how the great ones were spending the night at The Bush in Farnham.
