The morning light struggled through the heavy clouds of a January day, cold and dull, as Marianne sat half-dressed on a window seat, her face turned toward the weak glow filtering through the glass. Tears fell steadily onto the page before her, blurring her writing, yet she pressed on, driven by an urgency that left no room for hesitation. Elinor, awakened by the sound of her sister’s quiet sobs, found her there—hunched over the letter, trembling, her breath shallow and uneven. For a moment, Elinor watched in silence, her heart clenched with helpless sorrow. Then, softly, she asked, “Marianne, may I ask—?”

“No,” Marianne whispered, her voice flat and brittle, “ask nothing. You will soon know.”

That brief calm shattered instantly. A fresh wave of grief surged through her, and she buried her face in her hands, shoulders shaking. It took minutes to gather herself enough to write again. Each pause was marked by a sob, each line stained with tears. Elinor stood nearby, offering only quiet presence—no words, no comfort that might be mistaken for judgment. She knew this letter was likely the last Marianne would ever send to Willoughby.

When Marianne finally rose, still unsteady, she wandered the house like a ghost—avoiding eyes, seeking solitude, restless and broken. At breakfast, she refused food entirely. Elinor, aware of how much attention Mrs. Jennings might give to her sister’s distress, quietly drew the older woman into conversation, keeping her occupied with talk of wool and knitting patterns. The meal dragged on, and just as they settled around the work table, a servant delivered a letter to Marianne.

She snatched it from his hand, her face draining of color as if struck. Without a word, she fled the room.

Elinor saw it all—and in that instant, she knew. The letter must be from Willoughby. Her chest tightened. Her breath caught. She sat frozen, trembling, afraid to move, afraid to breathe. But Mrs. Jennings, oblivious, laughed. “Ah! What a lovely surprise! I do hope he’s written something sweet. Poor dear Miss Marianne—she’s been quite the love-struck bird since she came to town!”

Elinor forced a smile. “You’re mistaken, Ma’am. There is no engagement. I assure you, I’d be more surprised than anyone to hear otherwise.”

“Nonsense!” Mrs. Jennings clucked, measuring yarn. “Don’t pretend to be so coy. Everyone knows it! They were head over heels from the first moment. I saw them together every day in Devonshire—always walking, always talking. And don’t tell me your sister didn’t come to London to buy wedding clothes? Come now, it’s common knowledge. Even Charlotte says so.”

“I beg you not to spread such falsehoods,” Elinor said, her voice low but firm. “It’s cruel to imagine what you do not know.”

Mrs. Jennings chuckled, unconvinced. But Elinor had no strength left for argument. She hurried upstairs, heart pounding, desperate to see the letter.

In the bedroom, she found Marianne sprawled across the bed, gasping, clutching a single letter in one hand, others scattered beside her. Elinor approached slowly, silently. She sat beside her sister, took her hand, kissed her cheek—then broke down, weeping as fiercely as Marianne had done. In that shared sorrow, words were unnecessary. After a while, Marianne handed over the letters. Then, covering her face, she let out a cry so raw it tore through the silence.

Elinor waited until the storm passed, then read the letter:

*I have just received your letter and thank you for it. I regret any offense I may have given you last night, though I cannot understand where it might have arisen. I assure you, it was never my intention to displease. I value my acquaintance with your family deeply and shall always recall our time in Devonshire with gratitude. Should any misunderstanding arise, I hope it will not sever our connection. My respect for your household is sincere—but if I’ve led you to believe more than I felt, I take full responsibility for my carelessness. I can only say that my affections have long been pledged elsewhere. I expect this engagement to be fulfilled within weeks. It is with great regret that I return the letters and lock of hair you so kindly gave me.*

Elinor read it twice. Then three times. The cruelty of it—the coldness, the pretense of politeness masking utter betrayal—left her reeling. This was not merely a rejection. It was a declaration of indifference, a dismissal of everything Marianne had believed. No remorse. No explanation. Only a polite lie wrapped in formality.

She looked up at her sister, whose face was contorted with pain. Yet even in her agony, Marianne seemed to sense the depth of Elinor’s own anguish.

“Poor Elinor,” she whispered. “How unhappy I make you.”

“If only there were something I could do,” Elinor murmured.

“You *can* do nothing,” Marianne choked out. “Leave me. Hate me. Forget me. But don’t stand there and watch me suffer. How can you speak of strength when you feel nothing but joy? You are happy—happy because Edward loves you.”

“Happy?” Elinor repeated, her voice breaking. “Do you think I am? I see you in this state, and I am miserable.”

“But I am worse,” Marianne cried. “No one can understand. Nothing can ease this.”

“You must not say that,” Elinor insisted. “Think of Mama. Think of how her heart breaks for you. For her sake, you must endure.”

“I cannot,” Marianne whispered. “I cannot bear it. I am not strong enough.”

“Then consider what might have been,” Elinor said gently. “Had you been engaged for months, had you planned a future—wouldn’t the blow have been worse? At least now, you know the truth before it’s too late.”

“Engagement?” Marianne snapped. “There was no engagement.”

“No engagement?” Elinor echoed.

“No,” Marianne said firmly. “He never promised. Never said he loved me—only implied it. I thought he did. But he never truly professed it.”

“And yet you wrote to him?”

“Yes,” Marianne admitted. “But how could I not? Everything between us… it felt real. I trusted him.”

Elinor remained silent, then picked up the other letters. She read them quickly—the first, written upon arriving in London; the second, after missing him at the Middleton ball; the third, demanding answers after their final meeting. Each was filled with affection, expectation, longing. They spoke of trust, of intimacy, of a bond that felt irrevocable.

And yet Willoughby had responded with this—this icy refusal.

Elinor sighed. “I would not have believed such a letter could come from him.”

“It wasn’t him,” Marianne said, her voice rising. “It couldn’t be. He was kind once. He loved me. I saw it. That look he gave me when I gave him the lock of hair—do you remember? His voice—his eyes—he was torn. He said he might not see me for weeks. He was distressed. He cared.”

For a moment, she was lost in memory. Then she returned, shaken. “He has been used. Not by himself. By someone else. Someone who lied about me. Who poisoned his mind. I would rather suspect every person in the world than believe Willoughby capable of such cruelty.”

Elinor hesitated. “Whoever it was, let them not triumph. Let your integrity be your strength.”

“No,” Marianne said, her voice cracking. “I have no pride left. I do not care who sees me broken. Let them enjoy it. I cannot pretend to be strong. I am wretched—and I will not hide it.”

They sat in silence. Elinor paced, lost in thought. Marianne stared at the letter, reading each sentence again, trembling.

“It is too much,” she breathed. “Oh, Willoughby—could this be yours? To call the lock of my hair ‘so obligingly bestowed’—as if it were a favor, not a gift from my heart! Where was your heart when you wrote that? Barbarous. Insolent. Unforgivable.”

“No,” Elinor said softly. “Nothing excuses him.”

“And this woman—this unknown woman—who is she? How long has this been planned? Did he ever speak of her? Of anyone? No—never. He only spoke of me.”

Another pause. Then: “I must go home. I must go to Mama. Can we leave tomorrow?”

“Tomorrow?” Elinor asked, startled.

“Yes. Why stay? I came here for him. Now—now no one cares for me. No one sees me.”

“We cannot leave tomorrow,” Elinor said gently. “We owe Mrs. Jennings kindness. We cannot vanish so abruptly.”

“Then in two days,” Marianne insisted. “I cannot stay longer. I cannot face the pity of the Middletons, the Palmers—especially Lady Middleton. Oh, what would *he* say to see me pitied by her?”

Elinor helped her back onto the bed. Marianne tried to rest, but her body would not still. She shifted, writhed, gasped. Her breathing grew erratic. Elinor held her, whispering reassurances, pressing lavender drops to her lips until the spasms eased.

At last, she lay still—motionless, pale, exhausted. Elinor stayed by her side, watching, waiting, holding the weight of her sister’s sorrow in her arms.
