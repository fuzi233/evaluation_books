As the sun cast its warm rays upon the estate, Mrs. Ferrars stood firm, her resolve unwavering like the ancient trees that surrounded her home. It was a stance born of years of practice, honed to perfection by the delicate balance between maternal love and societal expectations. And so, when Edward finally gained permission to enter her presence, she regarded him with a measured gaze, pronouncing the words that would change the course of their lives: "My son."

For a long while, Mrs. Ferrars' household had been in a state of flux. The loss of her sons, first Edward and then Robert, had left her with a sense of emptiness that time and routine could hardly fill. But with Edward's return, her world was once more filled with the familiar rhythms of motherhood. Yet, despite this renewal, a thread of uncertainty still lingered within her, a reminder that even the most ardent mother's love cannot guarantee a son's security. It was this awareness that governed her response to Edward's revelation of his engagement to Miss Dashwood.

With calculated caution, Edward shared his news, aware that its disclosure might yet prove a catalyst for a fate he had narrowly escaped once before. His mother listened with an air of tranquil acceptance, her expression a testament to the lessons she had learned from past experience. She attempted to dissuade him from marrying Miss Dashwood, citing the advantages of uniting with Miss Morton, whose noble birth and substantial dowry would bring a higher social standing and greater financial security. The disparity between Miss Morton's thirty thousand pounds and Miss Dashwood's meager three thousand was a telling point, one that would normally have swayed a less resolute individual.

However, Edward remained unmoved, and Mrs. Ferrars, having weighed the risks and benefits, chose to yield. Her consent, though begrudgingly given, was a crucial step towards securing their future together. The discussion turned to the matter of augmenting their income, and here, too, Edward's position was somewhat precarious. As the younger son, he was not entitled to the same level of support as his elder brother, Robert, who received a generous annual allowance of a thousand pounds. Nevertheless, Mrs. Ferrars agreed to provide Edward with a modest sum of two hundred and fifty pounds per annum, should he choose to take holy orders, and a one-time payment of ten thousand pounds, which had been set aside for his sister Fanny.
