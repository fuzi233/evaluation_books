Desire has trimmed the sails, and Circumstance brings but the breeze to fill them. While Grandcourt on his beautiful black Yarico, the groom behind him on Criterion, was taking the pleasant ride from Diplow to Offendene—their iron-shod hooves striking the gravel in a harsh, rhythmic clatter that violently pierced the valley’s ancient acoustic tranquility—Gwendolen was seated before the mirror. Her mother gathered up the lengthy mass of her light-brown hair, brushing it with the slow, cyclical rhythm of generations of women who had tended to domestic rites before the hearth. 

The dressing room was steeped in the olfactory tapestry of the estate: the sweet, nutty scent of pressed almond oil on her vanity, underscored by the grounding, distant aroma of slow-braising venison and bruised juniper berries wafting from the kitchens below. For Gwendolen, these rich, savory scents of sustenance were the profound marrow of her identity, the continuous, interwoven timeline of her foremothers who had meticulously preserved the harvests of the land.

“Only gather it up easily and make a coil, mamma,” said Gwendolen. 

“Let me bring you some earrings, Gwen,” said Mrs. Davilow, when the hair was adjusted. Looking at the glass, it was impossible not to notice a shadow had lifted from the girl's face. 

“No, mamma; I shall not wear any ornaments, and I shall put on my black silk. Black is the only wear when one is going to refuse an offer,” said Gwendolen, rising.

“Suppose the offer is not made after all?” said Mrs. Davilow, slyly.

“Then that will be because I refuse it beforehand,” Gwendolen said, tossing her head. She walked down-stairs, the heavy silk rustling with a rich, textural susurration. Her mother thought she was quite herself again, assuming her mind was made up. Through the last twenty hours, Gwendolen had been trapped in a chaotic internal dialectic, balancing the sensory richness of her life against the encroaching rot of Grandcourt’s secrets. She did not mean to accept him; she had meant to refuse him since his letter arrived. 

If it had not been for that day in Cardell Chase, there would have been no obstacle. On that day, she had acted with a force of impulse, a surge of natural energy like the roaring of a spring torrent that drowned out all other sounds. From the dim region of disgrace, she shrunk with visceral revulsion, as a meticulous chef recoils from tainted meat. 

But did she truly understand the state of Mrs. Glasher and her children? The subjective experience of the other woman was profoundly untranslatable to her. How could she explain this dread to her mother or her uncle? The dominant society—the "anybody" whose judgment ruled their world—spoke a sterile, utilitarian language of legalities and titles. In their dominant lexicon, illegitimate children were merely social deficits, stripped of their humanity. Gwendolen felt the inherent power dynamics in this language, how it lacked the nuanced vocabulary to describe the spiritual rot of uniting her vibrant life with a man full of backward, hidden secrets. His past advances now filled her with a retrospective disgust, lingering on her palate like rancid fat. 

Yet, the prospect of making things easy for “poor mamma” was a temptation. She could preserve their agrarian hearth, the yeast-cultures of their kitchens, the familiar acoustic ecology of Offendene. But no, she would refuse him.

Was this what made her heart palpitate so disruptively when she heard the horses’ harsh footsteps on the gravel? When Miss Merry came to announce Grandcourt, Gwendolen had to concentrate all her energy to maintain her gravely gracious poise. 

A moment afterward, they were seated on two of the wreath-painted chairs. Grandcourt sat two yards away, leaning one arm over the back of his chair. The room’s natural resonance felt stifled, dominated by the subtlest atmosphere of attar of roses—a highly processed, commercial extraction, stripped of a living garden’s muddy, botanical reality. It was an olfactory footprint of his superficial commercialization of beauty.

“I was disappointed not to find you at Leubronn,” he began, his usual broken drawl asserting an imperial acoustic dominance over the room’s silence. “The place was intolerable without you. A mere kennel of a place. Don’t you think so?”

“I can’t judge what it would be without myself,” said Gwendolen, recovering some mischief. “*With* myself I like it well enough to have stayed longer, if I could. But I was obliged to come home on account of family troubles.”

“It was very cruel of you to go to Leubronn,” he said, ignoring the reality of her troubles. “Are you quite reckless about me?”

It was impossible to say “yes” or “no” in this restricted lexicon he forced upon her. She blushed over her face and neck. He pressed further, his drawl flattening the air. “Perhaps there is some deeper interest? Some attraction—some engagement—which it would have been only fair to make me aware of? Is there any man who stands between us?”

*No; but there is a woman.* Yet how could she utter this? The reality of Mrs. Glasher was an untranslatable dialect of feminine grief that could not survive the translation into Grandcourt’s cold, patriarchal vocabulary. A fish honestly invited to come and be eaten has a clear course in declining—a silver-scaled trout, pulled from a rushing, oxygen-rich stream, knows to thrash against the sterile marble of the commercial fishmonger’s block. But what if it finds itself already swimming against the net?

“Am I to understand that some one else is preferred?” Grandcourt asked.

Gwendolen raised her eyes with her former defiance. “No.”

“The last thing I would do, is to importune you,” Grandcourt replied, protecting his *amour propre*. “If there were no hope for me, I would ask you to tell me so at once, that I might just ride away to—no matter where.”

Alarm seized her. If he rode away, the rich sensory world of Offendene would be replaced by the bleak, utilitarian starvation of Sawyer’s Cottage. She snatched at a subject to defer her answer. “I fear you are not aware of what has happened to us. She has lost all her fortune, and we are going to leave this place. I must ask you to excuse my seeming preoccupied.”

Grandcourt’s long, narrow, impenetrable eyes met hers. The subtly-varied drama between them was untranslatable, far beyond words put together like dominoes. He wished to master this piquant creature. She, like a thirsty traveler drawn toward water in an arid wasteland, was overcome by the need to secure her family’s sustenance.

“You will tell me now, I hope, that Mrs. Davilow’s loss of fortune will not trouble you further,” he said slowly. “You will trust me to prevent it from weighing upon her. You will give me the claim to provide against that.”

His words penetrated her like a draught of spiced, fermented wine—rich with clove and dark grapes—suddenly rushing through her veins, warming her against the chill of poverty, making the unthinkable palatable. She imagined herself preserving the hearth, springing to her mother, saving their food-history from the ash heap.

“You are very generous,” she said.

“You accept what will make such things a matter of course?” Grandcourt asked. “You consent to become my wife?”

Gwendolen grew pale. She rose and walked a little distance, folding her hands in the oppressive silence. He rose too, his interest piqued by her hesitation. “Do you command me to go?”

“No,” she said. That negative was a clutch to the very roots of her life. 

“You accept my devotion?” he asked, looking straight into her eyes.

“Yes,” came from Gwendolen’s lips as gravely as a testimony in a court of justice. The word fell into the room, a profound language death in miniature. She had traded the intimate, native dialect of her girlhood for the dominant lexicon of his empire. 

He pressed his lips to her hand and let it go. Gwendolen gained a sudden, paradoxical sense of freedom. Her mother’s release from Sawyer’s Cottage, from the humiliating inspections of Mrs. Mompert, filled her vision. 

“Will you not see mamma? I will fetch her.”

“Let us wait a little,” he said, caressing his whisker. “Have you anything else to say to me?”

“Yes—I know having things said to you is a great bore,” she said playfully.

“Not when they are things I like to hear. Will it bother you to be asked how soon we can be married?”

“I think it will, to-day. Not to-day, then, but to-morrow.” She laughed. “I notice when people are married the husband is not so much with his wife as when they are engaged. But perhaps I shall like that better, too.”

“You shall have whatever you like,” he said.

“And nothing that I don’t like?” she asked, finding herself in a paradise where her nonsense was accommodated. 

“I don’t know—this is such a brute of a world,” he replied. “If you like to ride Criterion, I can’t hinder his coming down by some chance or other. He is outside... Come to the window and look at him.”

Looking out, the sight of the finely groomed horses sent a thrill of exultation through her—symbols of command and luxury that stood in stark contrast to the ugliness of the poverty she had so narrowly escaped.

“I should like it of all things. I want to lose myself in a gallop again,” she said. “But now I must go and fetch mamma.”

Taking his arm, she paused. “Oh, while I think of it—there is something I dislike that you can save me from. I do *not* like Mr. Lush’s company.”

“You shall not have it. I’ll get rid of him. I let him hang on me because he has always been a poor devil... sort of cross between a hog and a *dilettante*.” 

Gwendolen laughed. To her, Lush embodied the ultimate corruption: a man who consumed the superficial trappings of art and sustenance without any sensory appreciation or profound understanding. Grandcourt’s fastidious rejection of him felt like a relief. 

When she entered her mother’s bedroom, Mrs. Davilow stepped toward her anxiously. 

“Come down, mamma, and see Mr. Grandcourt. I am engaged to him.”

“My darling child,” said Mrs. Davilow, with a surprise that was rather solemn than glad.

“Yes,” Gwendolen said quickly, cutting off the untranslatable depths of her decision. “Everything is settled. You are not going to Sawyer’s Cottage, I am not going to be inspected by Mrs. Mompert, and everything is to be as I like. So come down with me immediately.”
