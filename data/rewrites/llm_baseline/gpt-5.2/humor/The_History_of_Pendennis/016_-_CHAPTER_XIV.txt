Mr. Bingley was that night dying heroically as Rolla in Pizarro to an audience so thin you might have used it to strain soup. The pit held a cough, a boot, and a disappointed echo; the boxes looked like so many respectable pews waiting for a sermon that had been postponed.

Arthur Pendennis sat almost alone—an island of misery in plush upholstery—with eyes reddened as if he had been slicing onions for the whole county. He leaned over the ledge and stared at the stage as a starving man stares at a window where a pie has been cooling for an hour. The pie, to him, was Miss Fotheringay.

Spaniards and Peruvians marched and declaimed; priests threatened; virgins of the sun looked chilly in their properties; battles happened with the mild inconvenience of people moving furniture in a lodging-house. Pen saw none of it. When Miss Fotheringay was off the stage, the world went out like a snuffed candle. When she entered, it blazed again—white raiment, leopard-skin, a brazen sun on her breast, and tawdry bracelets that glittered like cheap sugar on a fairing. She had only a handful of words to say, but she said them as if each syllable were a cherry to be displayed before it was eaten. She looked still better than she spoke; and her eyes—those famous eyes—rolled and gleamed, bright as polished chestnuts. Only, that evening, they were not ladled in Pen’s direction.

He did not even notice that two gentlemen had taken the next box—nor, indeed, anything human in the theatre besides Cora. He did not see that her glances kept flying, like well-trained birds, towards box No. 3; nor did he observe the sudden miracle that followed the arrival of those two men: the stage, which had been lounging through the first act as if it had been asked to work overtime, sprang up into an unnatural bustle of virtue.

For the play had begun in a manner to make an honest manager consider returning the money on principle. Actors yawned through noble sentiments; they spoke to one another too loud in the pauses, as if they were trading recipes for punch. Even Bingley was slack; even Mrs. Bingley, as Elvira, economised her voice as though it were coal. But then—pop!—up went their energy, as if someone had thrown open the kitchen door and announced that the Marquis himself was coming to dine.

Mrs. Bingley began to raise her voice and bellow like a pious cow; Bingley darted and yelled with a sudden conviction that the fate of Peru depended on his lungs; Garbetts, Rowkins, and Miss Rouncy all began to act, swagger, scowl, and spout as if each had been promised a crown roast for doing it loud enough. And all this zeal was directed—not at the empty benches—but at the two gentlemen in the neighbouring box.

One of these was a quiet little man in black, grey-headed, with a shrewd, comfortable face: the sort of person you would trust to choose anchovies. The other was a spectacle—tall, portly, hooked-nosed, and decorated in a manner that suggested his wardrobe had been designed by a committee of jewellers. His coat flashed with frogs and velvet; rings and pins glittered; chains looped and winked. When he drew out a yellow pocket-handkerchief with a hand cased in white kid, a wave of musk and bergamot floated through the theatre—so that even the stale air seemed to have been suddenly garnished.

This splendid presence was no other than Mr. Dolphin, manager from London, attended—as faithfully as a sauce follows a roast—by his friend and secretary, Mr. William Minns, without whom Dolphin rarely travelled, and never, if he could help it, thought.

The effect of Mr. Dolphin’s appearance was immediate. Bingley and company, who had been acting as men do when only their creditors are listening, now performed as if the public consisted entirely of dukes. Even Miss Fotheringay’s dull heart, which was usually stirred by nothing more delicate than admiration and dinner, felt a flutter at the sight of this London Sultan of playhouses. She had little to do but look handsome and stand in attitudes, encircling her stage-child; she executed this task with the perfection of a cook laying parsley round a dish she means to be praised for.

In vain the various actors strained and poured themselves out. Pizarro got not a hand from Dolphin; Bingley roared; Mrs. Bingley thundered; the Manager took snuff from his great gold box with the unconcern of a man tasting soup he knows is too salty and refuses to be impressed by the cook’s feelings.

It was only at the last scene—when Rolla came in staggering with the infant (and Bingley, not being as strong as he was, and Master Talma Bingley being a monstrous large child for his age, made the staggering look rather like a man carrying home a sack of flour)—it was only then, when Cora rushed forward with a shriek and cried, “O God, there’s blood upon him!” that Mr. Dolphin clapped his hands and broke out with an enthusiastic bravo.

Then he slapped his secretary on the shoulder. “By Jove, Billy, she’ll do!”

“Who taught her that dodge?” said old Billy, with the sour relish of a man who remembers being forced to eat burnt pudding. “I remember her at the Olympic, and hang me if she could say Bo to a goose.”

It was little Mr. Bows of the orchestra who had taught her the “dodge,” as a patient cook teaches an idle girl to whisk till the sauce turns, not till her arm tires. The applause ran through the theatre like sudden warmth through a cold house; and, as the curtain fell, the whole company swarmed round Miss Fotheringay to congratulate and hate her—those twin services of the profession.

Mr. Dolphin’s appearance in remote Chatteris had causes as solid as a loaf. For all his advertised blazes of triumph—his “coruscations of talent,” his “victories of good old English comedy”—his London theatre (which, to spare living feelings and present investments, we shall call the Museum) had been doing no better than a shop that sells nothing but fine sentiments. Great Hubbard had played legitimate drama for twenty nights and paid nobody but himself. Mr. and Mrs. Cawdor had declaimed tragedy and performed their favourite rounds to ungrateful benches. Herr Garbage’s lions and tigers had drawn for a brief season—until one animal took a piece out of Herr Garbage’s shoulder, and the Lord Chamberlain, who does not approve of managers being eaten in public, put a stop to that species of entertainment. The grand Lyrical Drama, with Monsieur Poumons as first tenor and an enormous orchestra, had been brought out with such splendour that it nearly crushed Dolphin beneath its own success. He was dragging on with half salaries, small operas, feeble comedies, and a ballet company; and people were looking, with that tender interest which the world always has in another man’s bankruptcy, for the day he should appear in the Gazette.

Among the patrons of the Museum was the Most Noble the Marquis of Steyne—refined lover of the arts, enlightened friend of music and drama, and not at all above letting the arts know it. His lordship rarely came early, his statesmanlike avocations being remarkably useful whenever he did not wish to be bored; but he occasionally arrived in time for the ballet, and was always received with profound respect. His proscenium-box communicated with the stage. When anything especially pleased him—some new face among the coryphees, a dancer’s leg rising to a height that stirred his patronage—one of his aides-de-camp, Mr. Wenham or Mr. Wagg, was sent behind the scenes to carry messages of approbation, or inquiries prompted by curiosity and interest in the dramatic art.

Lord Steyne sat modestly behind a curtain and looked only toward the stage. Yet you always knew he was there: you could hear it in the changed sound of the house, and see it in the way the ballet’s eyes kept turning, as if their gaze were a kind of silent music they played for him. In one particular ballet—Cook at Otaheite—where one hundred-and-twenty lovely female savages in palm leaves danced round Floridor as Captain Cook, there were, as it were, one hundred-and-twenty pairs of eyes perpetually seasoning their performance with glances at that curtained box. Even Mademoiselle Sauterelle and Mademoiselle de Bondi (called la petite Caoutchoue), when up in the air quivering like shuttlecocks, could still keep their eyes winking at his lordship’s corner, as though the true rhythm of the theatre came not from fiddles but from that hidden seat.

Now, one night, Lord Steyne was in his box with a few friends, making such a noise and laughter that the pit grew scandalised and bawled for silence; and Mr. Wagg wondered the police did not come to take the rascals out—though the police, like everyone else, had an ear for rank. Mr. Wenham was amusing the party by reading extracts from a private letter received from Major Pendennis, whose absence from London during the full season had been remarked and deplored with that sincerity which costs nothing.

“The secret is out,” said Wenham. “There’s a woman in the case.”

“Why, d—— it, Wenham, he’s your age,” growled the gentleman behind the curtain.

“Pour les ames bien nees, l’amour ne compte pas le nombre des annees,” said Wenham, with a gallant air. “For my part, I hope to be a victim till I die, and to break my heart every year of my life.”

Which meant—being translated into the dialect of gentlemen who live by other men’s favour—that his lordship need not be severe, because Wenham was three years younger and twice as well preserved.

“Wenham, you affect me,” said the great man, with one of his oaths. “By —— you do. I like to see a fellow keeping the illusions of youth warm so late. Hang it, it’s a comfort to meet such a generous candid creature. Who’s that gal in the second row, with blue ribbons, third from the stage—fine gal. Yes, you and I are sentimentalists. Wagg, I don’t think so much cares—it’s the stomach rather more than the heart with you, eh, Wagg, my boy?”

“I like everything that’s good,” said Wagg, generously. “Beauty and Burgundy, Venus and Venison. I don’t say that Venus’s turtles are to be despised because they don’t cook them at the London Tavern: but—but tell us about old Pendennis, Mr. Wenham,” he added abruptly, for he saw his patron was not listening. Lord Steyne’s glasses were up; he was examining something on the stage.

“Yes, I’ve heard that joke before—you begin to fail, my poor Wagg. If you don’t mind I shall be obliged to have a new Jester,” said Steyne, setting down his glass. “Go on, Wenham, about old Pendennis.”

Wenham read: “Dear Wenham,—as you have had my character in your hands for the last three weeks, and no doubt have torn me to shreds, I think you can afford to be good-humoured by way of variety, and to do me a service. It is a delicate matter, entre nous, une affaire de coeur. There is a young friend of mine gone wild about a certain Miss Fotheringay, an actress at the theatre here, and I must own to you, as handsome a woman, and, as it appears to me, as good an actress as ever put on rouge. She does Ophelia, Lady Teazle, Mrs. Haller—that sort of thing. Upon my word she is as splendid as Georges in her best days. I want a London engagement for her. Can’t you get Dolphin to come and see her—to engage her—to take her out of this place? A word from a noble friend of ours would be invaluable—if you could get the Gaunt House interest for me—I will promise anything in return. Do this now as a good fellow. Yours truly, A. Pendennis.”

“It’s a clear case,” said Wenham, when he had finished. “Old Pendennis is in love.”

“And wants to get the woman up to London—evidently,” said Wagg.

“I should like to see Pendennis on his knees, with the rheumatism,” Wenham laughed.

“Or offering a lock of his hair,” said Wagg.

“Stuff,” growled Lord Steyne. “He has relations in the country, hasn’t he? A nephew—interest enough to return a member. It’s the nephew’s affair. The young one is in a scrape. I was myself—when I was in the fifth form at Eton—mad about a market-gardener’s daughter—swore I’d marry her. Poor Polly!” He paused; something old, and half-human, flickered up under the curtain like a coal that refuses to die. Then he said, briskly, “But she must be a fine woman from Pendennis’s account. Have in Dolphin; let us hear if he knows anything of her.”

Wenham slipped from the box, passed the servitor at the door communicating with the stage—who bowed as if Wenham were part of the architecture of power—and found Dolphin behind the scenes. The manager was employed, as he often was, in swearing at the ladies of the corps-de-ballet for not doing their duty. The oaths died on his lips the instant Wenham appeared. He drew back a clenched hand from the face of an offending coryphee and seized Wenham’s hand with the warmth of a man suddenly presented with a line of credit.

“How do, Mr. Wenham? How’s his lordship to-night? Looks uncommonly well,” said Dolphin, smiling as if he had never in his life threatened anyone with dismissal, starvation, or the devil. He was delighted to follow Lord Steyne’s ambassador and pay respects in person.

From that conversation came the visit to Chatteris; and from Chatteris came Dolphin’s letter to his lordship: he had seen the lady; he was struck by her talents and appearance; he had made an engagement with Miss Fotheringay; she would soon have the honour of appearing before a London audience—and, in due time, before the noble and enlightened patron, the Marquis of Steyne.

Pen read the announcement in the Chatteris paper—the same columns in which he had so often praised her charms. The editor wrote handsomely of her talent and beauty and prophesied metropolitan triumph, as editors will, being fond of prophecy when it costs no money. Bingley began to advertise “The last night of Miss Fotheringay’s engagement.”

Poor Pen and Sir Derby Oaks became constant as churchgoers—Sir Derby in the stage-box, flinging bouquets and receiving glances, like a man feeding swans with crumbs that have been perfumed. Pen sat in the almost deserted boxes—haggard, wretched, lonely—watching her as if watching would keep her from being packed up and sent away like china.

Nobody cared whether Miss Fotheringay went or stayed except those two—and perhaps one more: Mr. Bows of the orchestra.

One night, Bows climbed out from his place, came into the house, and walked up to Pen’s box. He held out his hand and asked him, quietly, to come and walk. They went down the street together and sat upon Chatteris bridge in the moonlight, where the river made its steady, consoling sound—an old tune, not in fashion, but better for the nerves than any orchestra. The town behind them had its usual clatter—doors, distant laughter, a cart wheel protesting at a rut—but here the water spoke in a language older than playbills, and indifferent to applause.

“We may sit on the same bridge,” said Bows. “We have been in the same boat for a long time. You are not the only man who has made a fool of himself about that woman. And I have less excuse than you, because I am older and know her better. She has no more heart than the stone you are leaning on; and it or you or I might fall into the water, and never come up again, and she wouldn’t care. Yes—she would care for me, because she wants me to teach her: and she won’t be able to get on without me, and will be forced to send for me from London. But she wouldn’t if she didn’t want me. She has no heart and no head, and no sense, and no feelings, and no griefs or cares—whatever. I was going to say no pleasures—but the fact is, she does like her dinner, and she is pleased when people admire her.”

“And you do?” Pen asked, pulled for a moment out of his own torment by the odd, homely bitterness of the little man.

“It’s a habit,” said Bows, “like taking snuff, or drinking drams. I’ve been taking her these five years, and can’t do without her. It was I made her. If she doesn’t send for me, I shall follow her: but I know she’ll send for me. She wants me. Some day she’ll marry, and fling me over, as I do the end of this cigar.”

He flicked the cigar’s little flaming spark; it dropped into the water and vanished at once, swallowed without sound, as if the river had its own way of consuming griefs and leaving no ash. And Pen, riding home that night, actually found himself thinking—if only for a little while—of somebody besides himself.
