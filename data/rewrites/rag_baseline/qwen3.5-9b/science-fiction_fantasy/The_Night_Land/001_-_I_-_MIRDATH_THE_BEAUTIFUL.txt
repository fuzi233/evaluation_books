The silence of the evening was not empty; it was a held breath, waiting for the specific frequency of my own loneliness to break it. I walked the country road, detached from the warmth of my hearth, watching the battlements of the dusk pile upward like stone against a darkening sky. There was a solemn joy in this gathering gloom, a strange, magnetic pull that drew me to stand alone in the twilight.

Then, the trees on my right answered. Not with wind, but with a voice that cut through the ambient hum of the world: *"And thou also!"*

It was a response to a laugh I had barely suppressed in my throat. For a fleeting second, I wondered if I had spoken to a ghost, a projection of my own melancholy tuned to match my mood. But the voice sharpened, coalescing into a command. She called me by my name.

I turned, stepping aside to reveal the path, expecting a stranger. Instead, the figure emerging from the gloom was unmistakable. Lady Mirdath. Her reputation as the most beautiful soul in the county of Kent was a rumor I had dismissed until now, but the sight of her confirmed it with terrifying clarity. We were neighbors; her guardian's estates bordered mine, yet our paths had never crossed. My life had been a series of solitary studies and athletic exercises, a deliberate isolation that had rendered me blind to the world until this moment.

She greeted me with a familiarity that bypassed protocol, calling me by my lad's name and inviting me to share hers. She gestured toward a gap in the hedge—a private passage she used to slip away to village frolics disguised as a servant girl. Without hesitation, I followed her through the opening.

Inside the shadow of the hedge, she towered above me, though I realized quickly that I possessed a height advantage. She invited me to walk with her to the hall, suggesting I apologize to her guardian for my neglect. Her eyes sparkled with mischief, but the atmosphere shifted abruptly. A rustle in the woods to our right became a sharp, cracking snap of a dry twig.

Three men emerged from the treeline, moving with predatory speed. I placed Mirdath behind my shielded arm, gripping my oak staff, and shouted a warning that fell flat. They did not speak; they charged. Steel glinted in the failing light.

My reaction was instantaneous. I stepped forward, driving the butt of my staff into the flank of the lead attacker. He crumpled like a sack of grain. I swung high, connecting squarely with the second man's skull; he collapsed immediately. The third lunged, but I met him with a closed fist, sending him stumbling backward before he could recover. The fight ended before it truly began, leaving me standing amidst the stunned silence of the fallen, a small smile playing on my lips.

Before I could process the victory, three massive boar-hounds burst from the undergrowth, responding to a silver whistle Mirdath had blown earlier. She struggled to hold them back, beating them away from the unconscious bodies of the attackers. Soon, the sounds of approaching footsteps and the glow of lanterns filled the clearing. Servants from the house arrived, weapons raised, unsure whether to strike down the intruder or protect him. Seeing the defeated footpads and recognizing my name, they lowered their cudgels, treating me with deferential awe. Mirdath, however, did not retreat. She approached me, her expression shifting from amusement to a profound, deepening recognition of kinship.

We left the scene to the servants, who administered swift justice to the recovering men. Later, inside the grand hall, I was introduced to Sir Alfred Jarles, Mirdath's elderly guardian. He treated me with the highest honors, cementing my status as a welcomed guest. That evening, dining by candlelight, Mirdath's behavior toward me changed completely. She was warmer, more intimate, as if we had known each other for a lifetime.

As we strolled the grounds later, she tested me physically, grasping my arm to gauge the source of my earlier efficiency. She released me with a gasp of astonishment, her fingers trembling slightly. I noticed her silence, her thoughts turning inward, and I felt a corresponding pull toward her. Our connection was immediate, a shared language of delight where every observation felt like a rediscovery of something ancient and common between us.

One evening, wandering the park, she spoke half-unthinkingly of an "elves-night," then stopped, fearing my ignorance. I corrected her gently, speaking of the Towers of Sleep, the Giant's Tomb, and the Tree with the Great Painted Head. I halted abruptly when she seized my arm, her hand shaking violently. Breathless, she demanded I continue. With a surge of excitement, I revealed the Moon Garden—an old fantasy of mine.

Her reaction was visceral. She cried out in a low, strange tone, forcing me to stop and face her. She questioned me earnestly, and I matched her intensity. Suddenly, the barrier of solitude shattered. She admitted she possessed knowledge of these dream-lands, believing herself the sole inhabitant of such a strange realm. To find another traveler there was a marvel she repeated endlessly. She explained that her initial summons on the road was not random; she had watched me pass frequently, irritated perhaps by my indifference to Lady Mirdath the Beautiful, unaware that I was already thinking of her.

We wandered for hours, sharing fragments of these shared visions. Some things were unique to me, others to her, creating a tapestry of mutual completion. Time dissolved in this exchange; we grew older in knowledge and friendship simultaneously.

Eventually, the spell broke. Shouts, baying dogs, and lantern light signaled a search party summoned by Sir Jarles due to the earlier incident. Realizing we had lost track of time, Mirdath laughed softly, and we hurried home. The dogs recognized me now, jumping around me friendlier than before, allowing the servants to report all was well.

From that day forward, my routine changed. Every evening, I walked the road to Sir Jarles' estate, seeking the hedge-gap. Often, I found Mirdath there, accompanied by her large hounds—a precaution I had suggested for her safety, though she seemed to enjoy provoking me, testing the limits of my endurance.

One night, as I approached the gap, two figures emerged from the woods dressed as country maids. Curiosity compelled me to intercept them. The taller one curtsied with exaggerated grace. I suspected it was Mirdath in disguise. Following them to the village green, I witnessed a raucous dance attended by torches and fiddlers. The pair danced closely, avoiding the flames. Confident in my deduction, I approached the tall dancer and boldly asked for a dance.

She simpered, claiming she was promised, and handed her hand to a burly, clumsy farmer. She endured the dance with visible discomfort, saving her footing from his heavy steps. I knew then it was definitely her, despite the disguise and the ruined rhythm of her gait. I whispered her name, urging her to leave this foolishness and go home.

She turned away, stomping her foot, and returned to the farmer. After another dance, she instructed him to escort her partway. Once they moved beyond the reach of the torchlight, the rough villagers surrounded them. The second man grabbed the shorter companion, but the tall one—the real Mirdath—cried out in horror and disgust. She struck the man who embraced her with such force that he recoiled, swearing oaths. He returned instantly, attempting to kiss her. She beat his face wildly, loathing him with a ferocity that bordered on madness.

At that critical moment, she screamed my name. I intercepted the attacker, delivering a single, memorable blow that sent him sprawling into the ditch without killing him. The second villager, hearing my name, abandoned his victim and fled. My strength was known throughout the region, and in that chaotic night, my protection of Mirdath the Beautiful became undeniable fact.
