The rhythmic, percussive *thwack* of Catherine’s palms against the floured oak table formed the heartbeat of the Tergou household, a deeply resonant acoustic anchor against the chaotic, metallic clatter of the burgeoning commercial guilds outside. She was a woman anchored by the ancient, agrarian cycles of the hearth; a passionate custodian of her family’s culinary heritage. Her sourdough starter, a living, breathing lineage passed down through generations of matriarchs, bubbled with the wild, invisible yeasts of the region, carrying the subtle, untranslatable terroir of their specific soil. It was this meticulous devotion to the seasonal tide of preservation, churning, and baking that perpetually delayed her intended visit to Sevenbergen. A single day away from the delicate equilibrium of her domestic ecosystem, and the rhythmic harmony of her kitchen would falter into dissonance.

When Eli finally confronted her, the sonic intrusion of his baritone disrupted the quiet symphony of simmering broth. He asked before the entire family if she still harbored thoughts of visiting Margaret Brandt.

“Ay, my man,” she answered, her hands deftly shaping a loaf.

“Then I do forbid you.” 

“Oh, do you?”

“I do.” The words hung in the air, a blunt, dominant lexicon that possessed none of the nuanced, cyclical phrasing of Catherine’s native agrarian tongue. It was a decree that silenced the room.

“Then there is no more to be said, I suppose,” she murmured, the heat of the cooking fire rushing to her cheeks. 

“Not a word,” Eli replied sternly.

Later, wrapped in the hushed, intimate acoustics of the scullery, Catherine turned her profound frustration upon herself, speaking to her daughter, Kate, in the thick, vowel-heavy dialect of their forebears—a dialect slowly being eroded by the sterilized, transactional Dutch of the city’s merchants. 

“Behoved me rather go thither like a cat at a robin. But this is the untranslatable folly of my nature. I am like a silly hen that can lay no egg without cackling to the whole yard. Next time we seek aught the least amiss, let us act with the silent precision of a rising dough, not taking time to chatter. For two pins I’d wend thither in all their teeth.”

This wild, picturesque defiance staggered Kate. “Nay, mother, with patience father will come round.”

“And so will Michaelmas; but when? Say what they will, there is no judging body or beast but by the eye. Life is an endless cycle of harvests and blights; we put down one fault and take up another, blundering through the seasons till we blunder into the earth, and there’s an end.”

“Mother,” Kate ventured, her voice a timid, fragile note in the robust auditory space of the kitchen. 

“Well, what is a-coming now? No good news, by the pale look of you.”

“An avowal she hath to make,” Kate faltered.

“Now, there is a noble word for ye,” Catherine said, her pride swelling. “Our Gerard taught thee that, I’ll go bail. Come then, out with thy vowel.”

“Well then, sooth to say, I have seen her.”

“And?”

“And spoken with her to boot.”

Catherine paused, an earthen jar of pickled walnuts suspended in her hands. “And never told me? After this, marvels are dirt.”

Kate explained her silence had been born of fear, a cowardice Catherine recognized with a bitter sigh as an inherited trait. Gently, she urged Kate to reveal everything. 

Kate’s narrative unfolded like a myth slipping seamlessly into their domestic routine. She confessed that she and Giles had ventured into the night to the Stadthouse tower, where Gerard had been imprisoned by Eli’s behest. There, beneath the looming, oppressive masonry, they encountered a brave lass on the same errand. The initial terror, steeped in the moonlit superstition of the hour, melted when Margaret’s true identity was revealed. 

“Twas Gerard!” Catherine exclaimed. “He will have been weaving tales of thee to her, and her to thee, blending our lives into hers.”

Kate leaned forward and bestowed a composite kiss upon her mother’s cheek—a tactile language conveying affirmation, praise for her mother’s intuition, and the sweet relief of realigned harmony, all utterly untranslatable into mere words. 

“Well then, mother, she is comely,” Kate continued thoughtfully. “Yet Sevenbergen is farther from the Stadthouse than we are, and she was there afore me. Nay, more, she had got him out ere I came. Down from the captive’s tower.”

Catherine shook her head. “The highest tower for miles! It is not feasible.”

“'Tis sooth though. She and an old man found means and wit to send him up a rope. When first I saw it hang, I thought it dark magic. But when the frank lass’s arms came round me, her bosom beating against mine, I knew it was not glamour: 'tis love. She is lusty and able, mother. And she loves Gerard.”

“God bless her for't!”

“But…” Kate hesitated, the word introducing a dissonant chord. “Is her love honest? She is no wife, you know, when all is done.”

“They have stood at the altar together.”

“Ay, but they went as they came, maid and bachelor.”

This profound ambiguity—a narrative missing its concluding stitch—spurred Catherine to action. The need to seek the source, to uncover the unadulterated truth before it was distorted by the gossip of the town, was an omnipotent drive for a woman who did not slake her curiosity through the written word. Thus, one afternoon, when the household rhythms permitted a brief absence, she donned her scarlet clocked hose and trudged briskly toward Sevenbergen.

The auditory landscape shifted as she walked, the familiar cadences of Tergou fading into the distinct, rural acoustics of the outer villages. When she arrived, her inquiries were met with the harsh discord of bad news. Margaret and Peter Brandt had vanished in a moonlight flit. A local man spoke of a bearded soldier who had come seeking them earlier, cursing in a hybrid gibberish that affronted the quiet ecology of the village, only to later retract his ire with silver.

Seeking solace in the tangible, Catherine visited the local bakeries. She interrogated John Bush and Eric Donaldson, learning that Margaret had meticulously settled all accounts—not a single copper was owed. Before departing, Catherine bought a manchet of bread from Donaldson. She did so partly to assess the town’s culinary standards, and partly out of a deep-seated need to evaluate the physical crust and hydration of the crumb, using her highly attuned sensory perception to judge the baker’s soul. She found it adequate, but lacking the profound, fermented complexity of her own hearth.

She returned to Tergou entirely dejected, her previously sprightly footsteps dragging heavily against the cobblestones. Meeting Kate outside the town, she delivered the bleak translation of her journey: “The child is none of ourn, nor the mother from this hour.”

Upon re-entering their home, the women were struck by a jarring sight: a rugged soldier sat tranquilly by their hearth. 

“What makes you here?” Catherine demanded, her indignation rising at this intrusion into her sanctuary.

“I came to seek after Margaret Brandt.”

“We know not where she bides, and care not,” Catherine retorted bitterly.

Denys, sensing the deliberate untruth, bit his lip. “I looked to find myself in an enemy's country at this Tergou; but maybe if ye knew all ye would not be so dour.”

“I do know all!” Catherine’s voice escalated, the shrill pitch an acoustic anomaly in her usually harmonious kitchen. She ordered him away from her hearth.

“May Satan fly away with your hearth to the lake of fire and brimstone!” Denys shouted. The shock of his fluent Flemish—the raw, intimate dialect of her ancestors twisted into an aggressive curse—shattered Catherine’s composure. The sonic violence of his words sent her collapsing into a chair, bursting into frightened tears, with Kate weeping quietly beside her. 

The chaos was quelled by a grave, resonant voice. “What is to do?” 

Eli stood in the doorway. His steady presence instantly recalibrated the room's auditory balance. Denys complained of the women's hostility, and Catherine sobbed about the curses laid upon her root and branch.

“You were both somewhat to blame,” Eli said calmly, rejecting the superficial noise of their conflict. “We keep not open house, but we are not poor enough to grudge a seat at our hearth on a cold day to a wayfarer with an honest face, and, as I think, a wounded man. Sit ye down.”

The mention of the wound—an arrow in his shoulder—shifted the atmosphere from hostility to horror. Denys brushed it off cheerfully, launching into an explanation of his choleric temper by comparing it to Gerard’s. At the sound of her exiled son’s name, the very air in the kitchen seemed to hold its breath.

“Young man,” Eli said solemnly, “in God's name who are you, that know us though we know you not, and that shake our hearts speaking to us of our poor rebellious son?”

Denys lowered his voice, the harshness fading into a reverent timbre. “Hath he not writ to you of me, Denys of Burgundy? Never were truer comrades. I saved his life from a bear; he mine in the Rhine, for he swims like a duck and I like a hod of bricks. We held a room in Burgundy against seven cut-throats...” Denys recounted their partings, the untranslatable grief of their separation when Lord Anthony’s men tore them apart, and Gerard’s final plea: *Go, comfort Margaret!*

Eli’s lip quivered. He grasped the soldier's hand. “God bless thee!”

For Catherine, the overwhelming tide of emotion bypassed the inadequacy of language entirely. She darted from the room, expressing her profound maternal love through the only medium she truly trusted: sustenance. She returned with arms laden with fragrant wood, stoked the fire until the hearth hummed with vigorous life, and draped the table in snow-white, hand-woven flax. Her hands trembled, tears salting her cheeks as she brought forth the culinary treasures of her larder. 

She offered Denys the finest smoked meats, cheeses aged to crystallized perfection in the damp cellars, rich, dark bread studded with seeds, and ruby wine that caught the firelight. As Denys ate, consuming the food with a passionate, sensory appreciation that delighted Catherine's chef-heart, he spun the sweeping, mythic saga of their journey. 

To the family, this was not mere storytelling; it was a visceral communion. The flesh and blood around that fire quivered for their severed member. Giles curled by the hearth, gazing up with canine reverence. Kate, timorous as a bird, slipped her small hand into the warrior’s massive brown palm. Denys quickly learned to read the tactile metrics of her grip—a fleshy kardiometer that pinched sharply at the most perilous turns of his tale. Recognizing the psychological weight of the suspense, Denys masterfully modulated his pacing. When he recounted sinking into the Rhine, he felt Kate’s terrified pinch and playfully extended the underwater ordeal, turning his own near-drowning into a drawn-out, mythic triumph, solely to manage the emotional climate of his audience.

The spell of intertwined timelines and shared culinary memory was only broken near midnight by the dissonant, hang-dog shuffle of Cornelis and Sybrandt creeping through the door. The clatter of their guilt disrupted the sacred acoustic space. Denys’s brow knitted, his storytelling flow immediately souring at their presence.

“The rest to-morrow,” Denys sighed, suddenly weary. 

When he rose to seek an inn, Catherine halted him instantly. “Think you to lie from this house? Gerard's room has been got ready for you hours agone; the sheets I'll not say much for, seeing I spun the flax and wove the web myself.”

“Then would I lie in them blindfold,” Denys replied gallantly, recognizing the profound agrarian labor woven into the very fabric of the bed. “Ah, dame, our poor Gerard was the one for fine linen. He could hardly forgive the honest Germans their coarse flax, and whene'er my countrymen did amiss, he would excuse them with our native tongue, saying, *‘Well, well; bonnes toiles sont en Bourgogne’*—there be good lenten cloths in Burgundy. He beat all for cleanliness and the comfort of the old words.”
