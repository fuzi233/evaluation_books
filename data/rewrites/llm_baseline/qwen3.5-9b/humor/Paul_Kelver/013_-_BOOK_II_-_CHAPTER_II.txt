Even the self-imposed misery of the shy young man, cursed by nature to live in isolation, passes. Winter evenings came, and I retreated to work; it is the only season where one can drown personal sorrows in the flicker of a fire. My "apartment" felt surprisingly inviting with its handful of embers and two lit candles. I purchased paper, pens, and ink. What else does a writer need? He is merely a vessel waiting to see if the spirits within will speak through him or silence him forever.

Before me lay London—a city of a million characters, half grave and half gay, teeming with ten thousand romances, mysteries, pathos, and humor. It stretched out, begging only for intelligent observation and a reasonably truthful report. Yet, the absurd notion that I could craft a story from the reality I actually knew never occurred to me. Instead, my tales were populated by cottage maidens and rustic yeomen. My scenes unfolded in windmills, amidst mountains, or within moated granges. I suspect this phase of foolishness is common to nearly all youthful fictionists.

A trail of gentle melancholy draped over these creations. Sentiment was the rage then, and as a novice, I scrupulously followed fashion. Generally speaking, to be a heroine in my books was a death sentence. No matter how naturally her hair curled—curly locks supposedly signaling vitality—or how vigorously she danced, laughed, or leaped in Chapter One, the poor girl was destined for an untimely grave. Snowdrops and early primroses (botanical details gleaned from *Our Garden Favourites, Illustrated*) bloomed there like flowers in a greenhouse; near the coast, sea breezes would select that specific churchyard as their favorite playground. Years later, a white-haired man would lead little children past the site, recounting the tale anew—a dismal entertainment indeed for the young.

Occasionally, however, it was the gentleman who fell victim to my lethal literary atmosphere. Of no great consequence, since he would invariably conclude his final soliloquy that "it was better so." Snowdrops and primroses offered no consolation; his grave, marked by a rough cross, usually sat in an unfrequented patch of the African veldt or amid burning sands. Descriptions of such final scenery required a trip to the British Museum reading-room.

Dismal little fledglings! Again and again I drove them from the nest, only to watch them flutter back, soiled, crumpled, and physically damaged. Yet, one person had admired them, wept over them—myself.

I tried every method. Sometimes I dispatched them with a curt, take-it-or-leave-it note. Other times, I attached pathetic pleas for consideration. Occasionally, I assigned a value of five guineas, requesting a crossed cheque; at other times, I sought to tickle editorial greed by offering my first contribution gratis—"my sample packet," as it were, hoping one trial was sufficient. Now I wrote sarcastically, enclosing a brutally penned rejection note with the stamped return envelope. Or I wrote frankly, explaining my status as a beginner and begging to be told my faults, if any existed.

None found a resting place. They would vanish for a month, a week, or a couple of days, then return. I never lost a single one. I wished I had; it would have varied the monotony.

I despised the poor little slave-girl who burst joyfully into the room, holding them out between her apron-hidden thumb and forefinger. Her chronic sniff I interpreted as contempt. If I snatched them from the postman's hands as he flew down the stairs, it seemed to me he smiled. Tearing them from their envelopes, I cursed them, abused them, and sometimes flung them into the fire. But before they were scorched, I would snatch them out, smooth them, and reread them. The editor himself could never have seen them; it was impossible. Some jealous underling must have intercepted them. Perhaps I had sent them to the wrong paper. Perhaps they had arrived at the inopportune moment. Their triumph would come. Rewriting the first and last sheets, I would send them forth again with fresh hope.

Understanding that the would-be happy warrior must shine in camp as well as on the battlefield, I sought to prepare myself for the social side of life. Smoking and drinking were the twin sins I found most difficult to acquire. I am not claiming mental excellence here, but confessing a bodily infirmity. The spirit was always willing, but my flesh was weak. Fired by emulation, I had occasionally attempted a cigarette at school. The results were distinctly unsatisfactory. After two or three tries, I abandoned the endeavor, excusing my faint-heartedness with a sanctimonious air by declaring smoking bad for growing boys. I attempted to delude myself by adopting a fine pose of disapproval in the presence of contemporaries with stronger stomachs, secretly knowing myself a young hypocrite disguising physical cowardice in the robes of moral courage—a self-deception to which human nature is prone.

Similarly, I had occasionally tasted the red wine that stood year-in, year-out on our sideboard. The true inwardness of St. Paul's prescription had been revealed to me; the attitude of those who drink it strictly under doctor's orders, viewing it purely as medicine, seemed reasonable. I noted that others, including grown men, made wry faces when drinking my mother's claret, concluding that a taste for strong liquor was an accomplishment harder to acquire than generally supposed. The lack thereof in a young man was no disgrace, so I weakly postponed efforts in that direction as well.

But now, a gentleman at large, my education could no longer be delayed. Training—and severe training—is an absolute necessity for the artist. Fashion had shifted slightly, but a quarter-century prior, a genius who did not smoke and drink (and drink more than was good for him) would have been dismissed as an impostor without further evidence. Regarding the genius itself, I was hopeful but never certain. As for the smoking and drinking, I could make sure of that. I set to work methodically and conscientiously. Experience taught me that smoking was best practiced on Saturday nights, leaving Sunday to walk off the effects. Patience and determination eventually yielded success: I learned to smoke a cigarette appearing to enjoy it. Young men of lesser character might have rested content here, but the attainment of the highest has always been my motive force. Having conquered the cigarette, I next attacked the cigar.

My first cigar remains vivid in my memory. Most men's do. It was at a smoking concert in the Islington Drill Hall, to which Minikin had invited me. Unsure whether my growing dizziness stemmed solely from the cigar or partly from the hot, overcrowded room, I made my excuses and slipped out. I found myself in a small courtyard, separated from a neighboring garden by a low wall. The culprit was clearly the cigar. My inclination was to remove it from my mouth and hurl it as far as I could. Conscience, however, urged perseverance. It occurred to me that if I climbed onto the wall and walked its length, there would be no excuse for ignoring the counsels of perfection. Conversely, if the wall proved too narrow for my footsteps despite my best efforts, I would be entitled to discard the beastly thing and retreat to bed.

Attaining the wall with some difficulty, I commenced my self-inflicted ordeal. Two yards further, I found myself lying across the wall, legs dangling on one side, head overhanging the other. The position suited my needs perfectly, so I maintained it. Inclination seized its opportunity again, urging me to take a solemn vow never to smoke again. I am proud to record that through that hour of temptation, I remained firm, strengthening myself by whispering: "Never despair. What others can do, so can you. Is not all victory won through suffering?"

A liking for drink proved, if possible, even more difficult to achieve. Spirits almost caused me to despair. Once, confusing bottles, I drank hair oil thinking it whiskey; it was decidedly less nauseous. Twice a week, I forced myself to swallow a glass of beer, standing over myself and insisting on draining it to the bitter dregs. As a reward afterward, to cleanse my palate, I treated myself to chocolates, comforting myself with the assurance that it was for my own good—that someday I would truly like it and be grateful for my severity.

In other, more sensible directions, I also sought progress. Gradually, I was overcoming my shyness. It was a slow process. I discovered the best plan was not to mind being shy, but to accept it as part of my temperament and laugh at it with others. The coldness of an indifferent world serves to harden a too-sensitive skin. The gradual rubbings of existence rounded off my many corners. I became possible to my fellow creatures, and they to me. I began to take pleasure in their company.

Fate had directed me to this particular house in Nelson Square, performing a kindness. I flatter myself we were an interesting menagerie gathered under its leaky roof. Mrs. Peedles, our landlady, who slept in the basement with the scullery-maid, had been an actress in Charles Keane's company at the old Princess's Theatre. True, she had played only insignificant parts there. London, she would explain, was even then a poor judge of art, burdened by prejudices.
