CHAPTER XLIX. In Shepherd’s Inn, our hero Pen—Arthur Pendennis, Esq.—arrived with the cheerful élan of a man who’d just survived a night of high drama and low finance, like some sort of literary James Bond with less gadgets and more debt. “How d’ye do, Mr. Bows?” he bellowed, as if announcing a royal arrival at a coronation. He saluted the elderly gentleman with the gusto of a man who’d just won a duel, though one could clearly see a faint blush creeping across Arthur’s cheeks—like a shy sunbeam trying to peek through storm clouds. This blush was mirrored instantly by Fanny Bolton, whose face lit up with such sudden color it looked as though someone had flicked her cheek with a rose petal dipped in hot sauce.

After the obligatory handshake—during which Bows, ever the dramatic ironist, pretended to accept Arthur’s claim that he was off to visit Captain Costigan’s chambers—the room fell into a silence so thick you could have cut it with a butter knife. Not just quiet, but *gloomy* quiet, like the kind you get when everyone knows they’re about to be caught sneaking cookies after dinner. Pen, ever the jester, tried to break the tension by rattling off an exaggerated reenactment of the previous night’s events: specifically, Captain Costigan’s failed attempt to charm his way out of a check-related disaster at Vauxhall Gardens.

It was not a good imitation. It was like watching a monkey try to play Beethoven on a piano made of cardboard. Nobody laughed. Mrs. Bolton, bless her cotton-wool heart, didn’t even know what part Pen was playing—was he the check-taker? The captain? A confused pigeon? Fanny tried a tiny, nervous giggle, like a mouse attempting to whistle. Old Mr. Bows, meanwhile, looked as glum as a funeral dirge played on a fiddle tuned by a depressed badger. Pen’s voice faltered mid-sentence, then died completely—like a candle snuffed by a sad ghost. Darkness returned, thicker than before.

Then came the sound of a ticket-porter’s boots clinking on the cobbles outside. Everyone heard it. It was the only thing that broke the silence—like a clock ticking in a tomb.

“You were coming to see me, sir,” said Mr. Bows, suddenly regaining his theatrical bearing. “Won’t you walk up to my chambers? They are rather high up—but—”
“Oh! I live in a garret myself,” Pen interrupted, puffing up like a peacock. “Shepherd’s Inn is twice as cheerful as Lamb Court.”
“I knew you had third-floor apartments,” Bows replied, “and was going to say—please don’t take this as discourteous—that the air up three pair of stairs is wholesomer for gentlemen than the air of a porter’s lodge.”

“Sir!” Pen snapped, his inner flame reigniting. “Will you permit me to choose my society without—?”
“You were so polite as to say you were honouring my humble domicile,” Bows said, voice dropping to a mournful baritone, “shall I show you the way? Mr. Pendennis and I are old friends, Mrs. Bolton—very old acquaintances; we crossed paths at the dawn of his life.”

He gestured dramatically toward the door, finger trembling like a leaf in a gale, hat held aloft like a flag of surrender. His words were stiff and formal, borrowed from a lifetime of listening to actors on stage. But he wasn’t acting. Not really. Pen knew that. He just didn’t care. “Come along, sir,” he said, “as you are so very pressing.” And with a final farewell to Fanny—pressing her hand like a man who might never see her again—he vanished into the stairwell.

Mrs. Bolton immediately declared, “What a nice young man, to be sure!”
“D’you think so, ma?” Fanny asked, eyes wide.
“I was a-thinkin’ who he was like,” Mrs. Bolton mused, peering through the curtain. “When I was at the Wells with Mrs. Serle, there was a young gentleman from the city, used to come in a tilbry, white hat, the very image of him—only his whiskers was black, and Mr. P.’s is red.”
“Law, ma!” Fanny gasped. “They are a most beautiful hawburn!”

And thus began a lengthy monologue about a sugar-baker from Omerton who once courted a dancer named Emly Budd (who danced Columbine in *Arleykin Ornpipe, or the Battle of Navarino*), got married at St. Bartholomew’s Church in a private ceremony, and now patronized the Manshing-House Ball for Washywomen’s Asylum. Also mentioned: Lady Mirabel, Captain Costigan’s daughter, who was “profeshnl,” according to Mrs. Bolton, “as all very well know.”

Meanwhile, Fanny, still blushing from the earlier kiss, sat by the window like a statue waiting for a miracle. Her mother left for market, leaving her alone. Fanny watched the door like a hawk. Then—movement! She perked up. But no. It was Pen, followed by Bows. They passed under the archway. Bows bowed. Didn’t stop. Didn’t speak. Just… went.

Minutes later, Bows returned alone.
“Where’s your Ma, dear?” he asked.
“I don’t know,” Fanny snapped. “I don’t follow Ma’s steps wherever she goes, I suppose, Mr. Bows.”
“Am I my mother’s keeper?” Bows sighed, voice cracking with melancholy.

Then, like a man offering candy to a classroom full of children, he produced gingerbread cakes for the two youngest Bolton girls—Betsy-Jane and Ameliar-Ann—who were pretending to read a picture-book upside-down. Their reward? Gingerbread medals. They scampered off to discuss their spoils in the courtyard.

Fanny, meanwhile, picked up her sewing, though her mind was elsewhere. She was angry. Excited. Confused. She stitched like a woman possessed, her needle flying faster than her thoughts.

Bows sat facing the entrance, as if expecting someone—or something—to appear. But nobody did. Only Mrs. Bolton returned from the market, and found Bows where she expected to find Pen.

The reader may now guess who the guest was. (Spoiler: It was Pen.)

Upstairs, in Bows’s neat little room—neat, that is, compared to Costigan’s chaotic war zone—Pen and Bows met again. Costigan, still in his bedclothes (or perhaps just *pretending* to be dressed), had already emerged from his chamber and remained throughout the visit. He smelled, incidentally, like a bottle of whisky that had been opened and then forgotten in a drawer. A rich, heady perfume of otto-of-whisky—whatever that is—clung to him like a second skin. His hand shook so violently it was a wonder he hadn’t accidentally shaved off half his chin.

Bows’s room, in contrast, was tidy as a schoolmaster’s desk. Books. Manuscript music. A lithograph of Miss Fotheringay as Mrs. Haller, signed in messy script. Lady Mirabel’s handwriting, by contrast, was elegant—she’d worked hard at it since marriage. But Bows preferred the old style. He kept a single note from her, written in reply to a song he’d composed and dedicated to her. It was tucked away like a sacred relic.

Now he was teaching Fanny to sing and write—just as he once taught Emily. There was something deeply human about Bows: he attached himself to things. When Emily left, he found a substitute—like a man who loses a leg and learns to walk with a crutch. Or a sailor who, shipwrecked, latches onto a raft. Latude, imprisoned in the Bastille, grew fond of a mouse. Some men fall in love with poodles. Bows had fallen in love with Fanny—and now here was Pen, back in town, chasing her like a man who’d just discovered romance was still legal.

Costigan, oblivious, thought he was the reason for Pen’s visit. He was delighted. He promised himself he would return the favor—though he already owed more debts than a pawnbroker’s ledger. He chatted amiably about Sir Charles and Lady Mirabel’s trip to Baden-Baden, the Grand Duke’s hospitality, and the glorious festivities at Dublin Castle, where the Earl of Portansherry held a Viceroygal Coort (a term so grand it sounded like a sandwich).

As Costigan droned on, Pen remembered the past—the first love, the bridge talk, the kindness of Bows. He felt warm toward both men. He shook hands cordially, even warmly, as he prepared to leave.

But then—Bows limped down the stairs, determined to follow him.
Pen’s patience snapped. *Confound him!* What does he mean, dogging me? He burst out laughing—loud, bitter, self-satisfied—as he walked into the Strand. But the laugh didn’t feel honest. It felt like a cover-up. And somewhere deep inside, Arthur Pendennis blushed—not at the joke, but at himself.

He tried to escape his thoughts. He climbed the Panorama stairs, panting like a dog in a heatwave. But Care was already waiting at the top. He went to the Club, wrote a long, witty letter home—full of sarcasm and cleverness—never mentioning Vauxhall or Fanny. He couldn’t focus on books. Jawkins, the only man in London who seemed to enjoy conversation, couldn’t hold his attention. Nothing worked.

On his way home, he passed a comic theatre. The sign screamed: *Stunning Farce!* *Roars of Laughter!* *Good Old English Fun and Frolic!* In vermilion letters, like a cry from the heavens.

He went in. Saw Mrs. Leary in male drag. Tom Horseman as a woman. Horseman’s performance was hideous. Mrs. Leary’s ankles? Meaningless. But Pen laughed—bitterly—thinking of how much he’d once swooned over her. That night in London… how long ago it seemed. Not just years. Centuries.

And yet, as he walked home, the laughter faded. The world felt heavier. Because love, it seemed, was not just pleasure. It was also pain. And he had just begun to understand it.
