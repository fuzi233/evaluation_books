I kept my thoughts to myself and simply asked Mr. Franklin to continue. He replied, “Don’t fidget, Betteredge,” and went on.

The young gentleman began by telling me that his discoveries about the late Colonel Herncastle and the Diamond started with a visit he had made—before coming to us—to the family lawyer in Hampstead. One day, after dinner, when they were alone, Mr. Franklin casually mentioned that he had been entrusted by his father with a birthday gift for Miss Rachel. That remark set off a chain of questions, and eventually the lawyer revealed what the gift actually was—and how the friendship between the deceased Colonel and Mr. Blake senior had begun. The facts are so extraordinary, I doubt I could do them justice in my own words. So I’ll try to recount Mr. Franklin’s findings as closely as possible in his own voice.

“You remember,” he said, “when my father was trying to prove his claim to that cursed Dukedom? That was also when my uncle Herncastle returned from India. My father discovered that his brother-in-law possessed certain papers that might help him in his lawsuit. He called on the Colonel under the pretense of welcoming him back to England. But the Colonel wasn’t fooled. ‘You want something,’ he said, ‘or you wouldn’t have compromised your reputation by calling on _me_.’ My father saw there was no point in pretending. He admitted at once that he wanted the papers. The Colonel asked for a day to consider his answer. And that answer came in the form of an astonishing letter—one my friend the lawyer showed me.”

The Colonel began by saying he wanted something from my father, and proposed a mutual exchange of favors. “The fortune of war,” he wrote, “has placed me in possession of one of the largest diamonds in the world. And I have reason to believe that neither I nor this jewel is safe in any house, anywhere on earth, where we live together.” Under these alarming circumstances, he decided to entrust the diamond to someone else. That person would face no danger—only the duty of receiving, on fixed days each year, a note from the Colonel confirming he was still alive. If no note arrived, silence would be taken as proof of murder. In that case—and only then—the sealed instructions left with the diamond were to be opened and followed exactly. In return for accepting this strange responsibility, the Colonel offered his papers to my father.

“That was the letter,” Mr. Franklin concluded.

“What did your father do?” I asked.

“Do?” he replied. “I’ll tell you. He applied common sense. He declared the whole thing absurd. Somewhere during his travels in India, the Colonel must have picked up a worthless crystal he mistook for a diamond. As for the fear of being murdered—this was the nineteenth century. Any sensible man could go to the police. The Colonel had been a notorious opium addict for years. If the only way to get the papers was to accept this ridiculous burden, my father was more than willing to take it—especially since it involved no effort on his part. The diamond and the sealed instructions went into the banker’s strongroom. The Colonel’s annual letters—confirming he was alive—were received and opened by our family lawyer, Mr. Bruff, acting on my father’s behalf. No rational person, in similar circumstances, could have acted otherwise. Nothing in this world seems probable unless it fits our everyday experience. We only believe in romance when we read it in newspapers.”

It was clear to me that Mr. Franklin thought his father had dismissed the Colonel too quickly.

“What’s your own opinion, sir?” I asked.

“Let’s finish the story first,” he said. “There’s a curious lack of order in the English mind—your question is proof of it. When we’re not building machinery, we’re mentally the slovenliest people on earth.”

“So much for a foreign education!” I thought to myself. “He’s learned that kind of sarcasm in France, I suppose.”

Mr. Franklin resumed.

“My father got the papers he wanted—and never saw his brother-in-law again. Year after year, on the appointed days, the letter arrived. I’ve seen them all piled together—each written in the same brief, businesslike tone: ‘Sir,—This is to certify that I am still a living man. Let the Diamond be. John Herncastle.’ That was all. It came every time—until six or eight months ago, when the letter changed. It now read: ‘Sir,—They tell me I am dying. Come to me, and help me make my will.’ Mr. Bruff went, and found him in a small suburban villa, surrounded by grounds, where he’d lived alone since leaving India. He had dogs, cats, birds—company enough—but no human beings nearby except the woman who came daily to clean, and the doctor at his bedside.”

The will was simple. The Colonel had spent most of his fortune on chemical experiments. It contained three clauses, dictated from his bed while fully aware:

1. Provision for the care and support of his animals.
2. A professorship of experimental chemistry at a northern university.
3. The Moonstone, left as a birthday gift to his niece, Rachel, provided her mother was still alive. My father initially refused to act as executor. But after some thought, he agreed—partly because he was told it would involve no trouble, and partly because Mr. Bruff suggested, for Rachel’s sake, that the diamond might be worth something after all.

“Did the Colonel give any reason,” I asked, “for leaving the diamond to Miss Rachel?”

“He didn’t just give a reason—he wrote it into the will,” Mr. Franklin replied. “I have a copy here. Don’t be sloppy-minded, Betteredge! One thing at a time. You’ve heard about the will—now hear what happened after the Colonel died.”

A formal valuation was required before the will could be proven. All the jewelers consulted confirmed the Colonel’s claim: it was one of the largest diamonds in the world. Valuing it precisely proved difficult. Its size was unprecedented; its color unique; and it had a flaw in the center. Even with that defect, the lowest estimate was twenty thousand pounds. Imagine my father’s shock! He had nearly refused the executorship and risked losing this magnificent jewel to the family. His interest piqued, he finally opened the sealed instructions left with the diamond. Mr. Bruff showed me the document—it suggests, to me, a clue to the conspiracy that threatened the Colonel’s life.

“So you believe,” I said, “there really was a conspiracy?”

“Not having my father’s excellent common sense,” Mr. Franklin answered, “I believe the Colonel was right to fear for his life. The sealed instructions explain how he ended up dying peacefully in his bed. If he had died violently—if the letter hadn’t arrived—my father was to send the Moonstone secretly to Amsterdam. There, it was to be given to a famous diamond cutter and split into four to six separate stones. Those stones were to be sold, and the proceeds used to fund the chemistry professorship. Now, Betteredge, use your sharp wits and see what this implies!”

I tried. My English mind was slovenly, and I muddled it all until Mr. Franklin stepped in.

“Notice,” he said, “that the diamond’s integrity depends on the Colonel’s survival. He doesn’t just say, ‘Kill me—and you won longer reach the diamond; it’s safely locked away.’ He says, ‘Kill me—and the diamond ceases to exist.’ What does that mean?”

I thought I had a flash of insight.

“It means reducing the stone’s value—cheating the thieves!”

“Not at all,” Mr. Franklin said. “I’ve looked into it. Cutting the flawed diamond would actually bring in more money—because four to six perfect brilliants could be made from it, collectively worth more than the single imperfect stone. If the motive was profit, the Colonel’s plan made the diamond *easier* to steal. More money could be made, and selling it would be far simpler if it passed through Amsterdam.”

“Lord bless us, sir!” I exclaimed. “Then what was the plot?”

“A plot among the Indians who originally owned the jewel,” Mr. Franklin said. “One rooted in ancient Hindu superstition. That’s my belief—confirmed by a family paper I carry with me.”

Now I understood why the appearance of the three Indian jugglers at our house had seemed significant to him.

“I don’t want to force my view on you,” he continued. “But the idea of devout followers of an old Hindu faith dedicating themselves to recovering their sacred gem—even across dangers and distances—seems perfectly plausible to me, given the patience of Eastern peoples and the power of Eastern religions. But then, I’m an imaginative man. The butcher, the baker, and the taxman aren’t the only realities to me. Take my guess for what it’s worth. Let’s focus on the real question: Does the conspiracy against the Moonstone survive the Colonel’s death? Did he know it when he left the diamond to his niece?”

Now I saw Miss Rachel and my lady clearly in the picture. Not a word escaped me.

“I wasn’t eager,” Mr. Franklin said, “to bring the Moonstone here. But Mr. Bruff reminded me that someone had to deliver my cousin’s legacy—and I might as well be that someone. After withdrawing the diamond from the bank, I thought I was followed by a shabby, dark-complexioned man. I stopped at my father’s house to collect my things and found a letter that kept me in London. I returned to the bank with the diamond—and saw the man again. This morning, when I took it out once more, I saw him for a third time. I shook him off and left by the morning train instead of the afternoon one. Here I am—with the diamond safe. And what’s the first news I hear? Three wandering Indians have been at the house. They’ve been investigating my arrival—and something I’m supposed to have with me—when they thought no one was watching. I won’t waste time on their ink trick—pouring it into a boy’s hand and claiming he can see a distant man and what’s in his pocket. I’ve seen such things in the East. It’s hocus-pocus, as you’d say. But the real question is: Am I misreading a coincidence—or do we have proof the Indians are tracking the Moonstone the moment it leaves the bank?”

Neither of us seemed eager to tackle that. We looked at each other, then at the tide creeping higher over the Shivering Sand.

“What are you thinking?” Mr. Franklin suddenly asked.

“I was thinking, sir,” I replied, “that I’d like to toss the diamond into the quicksand and settle the matter that way.”

“If you’ve got the money in your pocket,” he said, “say so, Betteredge—and in it goes!”

It’s amazing how a little joke can ease anxiety. At the time, we both found great amusement in the idea of destroying Miss Rachel’s property and getting Mr. Blake into trouble. Though now I can’t recall why it was funny.

Mr. Franklin brought us back to the point. He pulled an envelope from his pocket, opened it, and handed me the paper inside.

“Betteredge,” he said, “we must face the question of why the Colonel left this legacy to his niece—for my aunt’s sake. Remember how Lady Verinder treated her brother from the time he returned to England until he told you he’d remember his niece’s birthday. Read this.”

He gave me the extract from the will. I still have it as I write. Here it is, copied exactly:

“Thirdly, and lastly, I give and bequeath to my niece, Rachel Verinder, daughter and only child of my sister, Julia Verinder, widow—if her mother, the said Julia Verinder, shall be living on the said Rachel Verinder’s next Birthday after my death—the yellow Diamond belonging to me, and known in the East by the name of The Moonstone: subject to this condition, that her mother, the said Julia Verinder, shall be living at the time. And I hereby desire my executor to give my Diamond, either by his own hands or by the hands of some trustworthy representative whom he shall appoint, into the personal possession of my said niece Rachel, on her next birthday after my death, and in the presence, if possible, of my sister, the said Julia Verinder. And I desire that my said sister may be informed, by means of a true copy of this, the third and last clause of my Will, that I give the Diamond to her daughter Rachel, in token of my free forgiveness of the injury which her conduct towards me has been the means of inflicting on my reputation in my lifetime; and especially in proof that I pardon, as becomes a dying man, the insult offered to me as an officer and a gentleman, when her servant, by her orders, closed the door of her house against me, on the occasion of her daughter’s birthday.”

More clauses followed, detailing what should happen if Lady Verinder or Miss Rachel had died by the time of the Colonel’s death—sending the diamond to Holland, selling it, and adding the proceeds to the chemistry fund.

I handed the paper back, deeply troubled. Until then, I’d believed the Colonel died as wickedly as he lived. I don’t say this extract converted me—but it shook me.

“Well,” Mr. Franklin said, “now you’ve read the Colonel’s own words. What do you think? Am I bringing the Moonstone to my aunt’s house to serve his vengeance blindly—or am I honoring him as a penitent, Christian man?”

“It’s hard to say, sir,” I replied, “that he died with a cruel revenge in his heart and a lie on his lips. Only God knows the truth. Don’t ask me.”

Mr. Franklin twisted the paper in his fingers, as if hoping to squeeze the truth from it. At the same time, he changed—becoming slow, solemn, thoughtful. From brisk and bright, he turned into a man deep in contemplation.

“This question has two sides,” he said. “An objective side and a subjective side. Which should we choose?”

He’d had a German education as well as a French one. One had dominated him until now. But now, it seemed, the other had taken over. I follow my rule: never notice what I don’t understand. So I stayed neutral—staring hard, saying nothing.

“Let’s find the deeper meaning,” he said. “Why did my uncle leave the diamond to Rachel? Why not to my aunt?”

“That’s easy to guess, sir,” I said. “Colonel Herncastle knew my lady well enough to know she’d refuse any gift from _him_.”

“How do you know Rachel wouldn’t refuse it too?”

“Is there any young lady alive who could resist a birthday present like the Moonstone?”

“That’s the subjective view,” he said. “It suits you well, Betteredge. But there’s another mystery: why make the gift conditional on her mother being alive?”

“I don’t want to slander the dead,” I said, “but if he deliberately left a legacy of danger and trouble to his sister through her child, it must be tied to her being alive to suffer it.”

“Oh! That’s your interpretation—subjective again! Have you ever been to Germany, Betteredge?”

“No, sir. What’s yours?”

“I can see,” he said, “that the Colonel may not have meant to benefit Rachel at all—since he’d never even seen her. Perhaps he wanted to prove to his sister that he forgave her. And what better way than giving her child a gift? There’s a different explanation—based on a subjective-objective mix. To me, both interpretations seem equally likely.”

Having reached this balanced conclusion, Mr. Franklin seemed satisfied. He lay flat on the sand and asked what we should do next.

He had been so sharp and decisive before—so clearly in charge—that this sudden shift to helplessness surprised me. Later, Miss Rachel explained it to me: Mr. Franklin’s character had been shaped by his foreign education. Sent abroad at a critical age, he’d been passed from one culture to another before any one influence could take firm root. The result was a man with many conflicting sides—busy and lazy, clear and confused, determined and helpless—all at once. He had a French side, a German side, an Italian side—and beneath it all, a trace of Englishness, occasionally showing through like a buried foundation. Miss Rachel said the Italian side surfaced when he unexpectedly surrendered and asked you, sweetly, to take over his responsibilities. I think she was right—he was in that mood now.

“Isn’t it your job, sir,” I asked, “to decide what to do next? Surely not mine?”

Mr. Franklin didn’t seem to grasp the point—his eyes were fixed on the sky.

“I don’t want to alarm my aunt without cause,” he said. “But I don’t want to leave her unprepared. If you were in my place, Betteredge, what would you do—in one word?”

I answered: “Wait.”

“With all my heart,” he said. “How long?”

I explained.

“As I see it, sir,” I said, “someone must deliver this troublesome diamond to Miss Rachel on her birthday—and you might as well do it as anyone. Today is May 25th. Her birthday is June 21st. We have nearly four weeks. Let’s wait and see what happens. And warn my lady—or not—depending on what unfolds.”

“Perfect, Betteredge,” he said, “as far as it goes. But what about the diamond between now and then?”

I paused.

“We’ll keep it safe,” I said. “And watch.”
