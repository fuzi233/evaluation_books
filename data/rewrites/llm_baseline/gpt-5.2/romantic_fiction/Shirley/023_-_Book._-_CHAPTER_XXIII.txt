One lucid summer day, Caroline Helstone had been left to the mercy of hours that would not end. Her uncle was away at Whinbury; the house at the Rectory had the decency of order, the garden the abundance of June, and yet the day felt to her like a bright desert—light everywhere, not a breath of wind anywhere, the bees almost silent as if even they had tired of labour. Needlework lay in her lap: a small brown holland slip for a child, braid ready to be stitched down with exactness. She set the edge, counted the turns, kept her hands busy because her mind would not be still. The very quiet had a sound of its own—an oppressive hush, broken only by the faint tick of her own industry and the distant, dulled clink of someone’s pail at a neighbour’s gate. In such a stillness, a heart can hear itself too plainly.

Fanny came to the garden-door, glanced over lawn and borders, and, not seeing her young mistress, called out, “Miss Caroline!”

A low voice answered, “Fanny!” from the shaded alcove. Fanny hurried there with a note.

Caroline’s fingers, a moment ago so deft with the needle, scarcely seemed to have strength to take paper. She did not ask who sent it; she did not even look. It fell among the folds of the little frock.

“Joe Scott’s son, Harry, brought it,” said Fanny.

That simple name—Harry Scott—was as if some hand had turned a key in a locked room. Caroline lifted her head with sudden life.

“Harry Scott! Who sent him?”

“He came from the Hollow.”

The note was caught up as though it might escape, the seal torn, the lines read at once—two seconds, and her whole face changed. It was from Hortense: returned from Wormwood Wells; alone for the day, Robert being gone to Whinbury market; would Caroline come to tea? It would do her good, Hortense said—so much better for her than sitting solitary since the “misunderstanding” that had separated her from her “meilleure amie, Hortense Gérard Moore.” In the postscript she was pressed to put on her bonnet and run down directly.

Caroline needed no urging. It was a kind of hunger in her—hunger not for food, though even that is dearer when shared, but for the mere warmth of other voices, for movement, for change. She laid aside the child’s slip—its plain cloth, its patient braid—rose, went upstairs, covered her curls with her straw bonnet, and threw round her shoulders her black silk scarf. The scarf lay simply, and yet it made her look more composed than she felt, its dark sheen setting off the whiteness of her dress and the fairness of her throat and cheek.

Down the green lane she went, the slope leading her to the Hollow as water runs to a known low place. The hedges were thick with summer’s provision: white may and late honeysuckle, cow-parsley fading to seed, and here and there the bruised sharpness of wild mint where someone’s foot had trodden it. She drew in the lane’s breath as if it could feed her. The birds kept their own quiet domestic economy; somewhere a thrush tried a phrase and stopped, and in the far distance there came, softened by leaves, the dull rhythm of a cart—wood on road, iron on stone—a sound that belonged to work and therefore to life.

She knew Robert would not be at the cottage. Yet it was comfort merely to go where he had been; to step into rooms he had crossed that morning; to sit, though only for an hour, within the reach of objects his hand might have touched. Separation had made the smallest trace of him precious—almost sacred. In such moments Illusion, that gentle treacherous companion, was quick to return. It seemed to her, as she hurried under the sunlit branches, that the air itself whispered: He may come home while you are there. You may at least look at his face. At least give him your hand. Perhaps, for a minute, sit beside him.

“Silence!” she said inwardly, stern with herself; and yet she loved the whisper because it soothed.

Miss Moore must have seen the white flutter of Caroline’s dress between the shrubs, for she came from the porch to meet her—straight, unbending, composed as ever. Hortense did not permit haste to disorder her dignity; but she smiled, gratified to be sought, pleased to feel Caroline’s kiss and the sincere clinging of her embrace. She led her in with an air of benevolent possession, half deceived and wholly flattered.

Had she been undeceived—had she known precisely how much of Caroline’s eagerness belonged, not to a cousin’s invitation, but to a brother’s absence—Hortense would likely have felt shocked and affronted. Sisters do not willingly see young women fall in love with their brothers; the sentiment seems to them, if not presumption, then folly—a kind of crude romance. They do not themselves love these gentlemen as others do; and the idea that another should makes them recoil, as if at an error in taste. Reason may correct the first contempt, where reason rules; where it does not, the dislike is lifelong.

“You would expect to find me alone, from what I wrote this morning,” said Hortense, conducting her towards the parlour; “but since dinner company has come in.”

She opened the door, and there, like a crimson overflow, sat Mrs. Yorke—an amplitude of red skirt spilling over the elbow-chair by the hearth. Above it, presiding with a gravity that seemed a household law, rose a cap more formidable than a crown. That cap had not travelled under a bonnet; it had come, one might imagine, in a great black silk balloon, distended with whalebone. A quarter-yard frill stood round the wearer’s face; love-ribbon, puffed and bowed in profusion, flourished about the head. It was a kind of martial adornment, and Mrs. Yorke carried it as if it were armour.

She had come “in a friendly way” to take tea with Hortense—an act of condescension as rare as it was grand; for she scorned visiting and tea-drinking, and called “gossips” the women who made such small convivialities their habit. Yet Hortense was a favourite. Mrs. Yorke had shown it by stopping to speak to her on Sundays in the churchyard; by bidding her, almost hospitably, to Briarmains; by coming herself to the Hollow to-day.

Her reasons, as she would state them, were that Miss Moore was steady, without levity; that, being a foreigner, she needed countenance. She might have added—if she had ever cared to confess the true springs of her approvals—that Hortense’s plainness, her precise dress, her phlegm, were to Mrs. Yorke a positive merit. Whatever gentlemen admired in women, she condemned; whatever they overlooked, she patronised.

Caroline advanced with diffidence. She knew little of Mrs. Yorke, and as a parson’s niece could not tell what welcome such a matron might grant. She received a cool nod—nothing more—and turned away quickly to remove her bonnet, glad to hide her sudden colour and confusion.

When she sat down, she was immediately accosted—not by Mrs. Yorke, but by a small figure in a blue frock and sash that sprang up, fairy-like, from the shelter of the red gown’s folds. The child had been seated on a footstool, quite hidden. Now she ran to Caroline, flung her arms round her neck, and demanded a kiss.

“My mother is not civil to you,” she announced, as if reporting the weather. “And Rose there takes no notice. It is their way. If, instead of you, a white angel with a crown of stars came in, mother would nod stiffly, and Rose would never lift her head. But I will be your friend. I have always liked you.”

“Jessie, curb that tongue of yours,” said Mrs. Yorke. “Repress your forwardness.”

“But, mother, you are so frozen!” Jessie protested, unabashed. “Miss Helstone has never done you harm. Why can’t you be kind? You sit so stiff and look so cold and speak so dry—what for? That’s how you treat Miss Shirley Keeldar and every other young lady at our house. And Rose there is such an aut—aut— I have forgotten the word. It means a machine in the shape of a human being. However, between you, you will drive every soul away from Briarmains. Martin often says so.”

“I am an automaton? Good!” said Rose from a corner, where she sat on the carpet at the foot of a bookcase with a volume open on her knee. She lifted her eyes briefly—remarkable gray eyes that looked older than her years—let them rest for the shortest instant on Caroline, and then dropped them again to her page. “Miss Helstone, how do you do?”

Caroline, sheltered for a moment from Mrs. Yorke’s chill by the children’s frankness, let herself look at Rose—at the absorbed brow, the stillness of her limbs, the slight unconscious movement at the corner of her mouth as she read, as if the very words had taste and she must try them, chew them, test their savour. Caroline had instinct; she felt Rose was one of the unique, to be approached not by chatter but by quiet.

She went over and knelt beside her on the carpet, looking over her shoulder without comment. The book was Mrs. Radcliffe’s The Italian.

They read together in silence for a little while—Rose turning the leaves with careful fingers, as if each page were a thin pastry-sheet that must not tear. At last Rose paused, and, before turning over, asked gravely, “Are you ready?”

Caroline nodded.

After some minutes Rose asked, “Do you like it?”

“Long since, when I read it as a child, I was wonderfully taken with it.”

“Why?”

“It seemed to open with such promise—such foreboding of a strange tale to be unfolded.”

“And in reading it you feel as if you were far away from England—really in Italy—under another sort of sky; that blue southern sky travellers describe.”

“You are sensible of that, Rose?”

“It makes me long to travel, Miss Helstone.”

“When you are a woman, perhaps you may be able to gratify your wish.”

“I mean to make a way, if one is not made for me. I cannot live always in Briarfield. The whole world is not very large compared with creation. I must see the outside of our own round planet, at least.”

“How much of its outside?”

“First this hemisphere where we live; then the other. I am resolved my life shall be a life. Not a black trance like the toad’s, buried in marble; nor a long slow death like yours in Briarfield Rectory.”

“Like mine! What can you mean, child?”

“Might you not as well be tediously dying as be for ever shut up in that glebe-house—a place that, when I pass it, always reminds me of a windowed grave? I never see movement about the door. I never hear a sound from the wall. I believe smoke never issues from the chimneys. What do you do there?”

“I sew, I read, I learn lessons.”

“Are you happy?”

“Should I be happy wandering alone in strange countries as you wish to do?”

“Much happier, even if you did nothing but wander. Remember, however, I shall have an object in view; but if you only went on and on, like some enchanted lady in a fairy tale, you might be happier than now. In a day’s wandering you would pass hill, wood, and watercourse, each perpetually altering as the sun shone out or was overcast; as weather was wet or fair. Nothing changes in Briarfield Rectory. The plaster of the parlour ceiling, the paper on the walls, the curtains, carpets, chairs—still the same.”

“Is change necessary to happiness?”

“Yes.”

“Is it synonymous with it?”

“I don’t know; but I feel monotony and death to be almost the same.”

Jessie broke in, delighted. “Isn’t she mad?”

“But, Rose,” said Caroline, gently, “I fear a wanderer’s life—for me, at least—would end like that tale, in disappointment, vanity, and vexation.”

“Does The Italian end so?”

“I thought so when I read it.”

“Better to try all things and find all empty than to try nothing and leave life blank. To do this is to commit the sin of him who buried his talent in a napkin—despicable sluggard!”

“Rose,” observed Mrs. Yorke from her chair, “solid satisfaction is only to be realized by doing one’s duty.”

“Right, mother. And if my Master has given me ten talents, my duty is to trade with them and make them ten more. Not in the dust of household drawers shall the coin be interred. I will not deposit it in a broken-spouted teapot and shut it in a china closet among tea-things. I will not commit it to your work-table to be smothered under piles of woollen hose. I will not prison it in the linen press to find shrouds among the sheets. And least of all, mother—least of all will I hide it in a tureen of cold potatoes, to be ranged with bread, butter, pastry, and ham on the shelves of the larder.”

At “cold potatoes” Caroline’s senses betrayed her; the domestic picture, so vividly placed, rose before her with its exact smell—the earthy, faintly sweet starch, the damp chill of a stone pantry, the salt tang of ham hung to cure, and that warm butter-scent that always clings to a larder shelf even when the dish is covered. The whole scene had a sort of homely mythology to it: the household gods of thrift and repetition, guarding their stores, insisting that a girl’s life be measured out like portions.

Rose went on, voice steady, eyes bright. “The Lord who gave each of us our talents will come home some day and demand an account. The teapot, the old stocking-foot, the linen rag, the willow-pattern tureen will yield up their barren deposit in many a house. Suffer your daughters, at least, to put their money to the exchangers, that they may be enabled at the Master’s coming to pay Him His own with usury.”

“Rose, did you bring your sampler with you, as I told you?” asked Mrs. Yorke.

“Yes, mother.”

“Sit down and do a line of marking.”

Rose obeyed at once. Her fingers—fingers meant for books and maps and perhaps distant journeys—took needle and thread with the same composure. For ten minutes there was only the small industrious sound of work: the prick and pull, the faint rustle of cloth, the occasional creak of Mrs. Yorke’s chair as she shifted her weight. Outside, a swallow flicked past the window with its thin cry; somewhere a kettle in the kitchen began to murmur, and the low, watery song seemed for a moment like comfort—like a domestic pulse.

Then Mrs. Yorke asked, “Do you think yourself oppressed now—a victim?”

“No, mother.”

“Yet, as far as I understood your tirade, it was a protest against all womanly and domestic employment.”

“You misunderstood it. I should be sorry not to learn to sew. You do right to teach me and to make me work.”

“Even to the mending of your brothers’ stockings and the making of sheets?”

“Yes.”

“Where is the use of ranting and spouting about it, then?”

“Am I to do nothing but that? I will do that, and then I will do more. Now, mother, I have said my say. I am twelve years old at present, and not till I am sixteen will I speak again about talents. For four years I bind myself an industrious apprentice to all you can teach me.”

“You see what my daughters are, Miss Helstone,” said Mrs. Yorke, turning her formidable cap towards Caroline. “How precociously wise in their own conceits. ‘I would rather this, I prefer that’—such is Jessie’s cuckoo song; while Rose utters the bolder cry, ‘I will, and I will not!’”

“I render a reason, mother,” said Rose, without looking up from her marking. “Besides, if my cry is bold, it is only heard once in a twelvemonth. About each birthday the spirit moves me to deliver one oracle respecting my own instruction and management. I utter it and leave it; it is for you, mother, to listen or not.”

“I would advise all young ladies,” pursued Mrs. Yorke, “to study the characters of such children before they marry and have any of their own; to consider well the responsibility of guiding the careless, the labour of persuading the stubborn, the constant burden of training even the best.”

“But with love it need not be so very difficult,” Caroline interposed. “Mothers love their children most dearly—almost better than they love themselves.”

“Fine talk—very sentimental. There is the rough, practical part of life yet to come for you, young miss.”

“But, Mrs. Yorke, if I take a little baby into my arms—any poor woman’s infant, for instance—I feel I love that helpless thing quite peculiarly, though I am not its mother. I could do almost anything for it willingly, if it were delivered over entirely to my care—if it were quite dependent on me.”

“You feel! Yes, yes! I dare say, now. You are led a great deal by your feelings, and you think yourself very sensitive, no doubt. Are you aware that with all these romantic ideas you have managed to train your features into an habitually lackadaisical expression, better suited to a novel-heroine than to a woman who is to make her way in the real world by dint of common sense?”

“No; I am not at all aware of that, Mrs. Yorke.”

“Look in the glass behind you. Compare the face you see there with that of any early-rising, hard-working milkmaid.”

“My face is pale, but it is not sentimental; and most milkmaids, however red and robust they may be, are more stupid and less practically fitted to make their way in the world than I am. I think more, and more correctly, than milkmaids in general do; consequently, where they would often, for want of reflection, act weakly, I, by dint of reflection, should act judiciously.”

“Oh no! you would be influenced by your feelings; guided by impulse.”

“Of course I should often be influenced by my feelings. They were given me to that end. Whom my feelings teach me to love I must and shall love; and I hope, if ever I have a husband and children, my feelings will induce me to love them. I hope, in that case, all my impulses will be strong in compelling me to love.”

She felt a fierce pleasure in saying it—pleasure in daring to say it there, before that chill tribunal. Her cheeks flushed, not with anger, but with the heat of a truth admitted aloud.

Mrs. Yorke answered coolly, “Don’t waste your dramatic effects. That was well said—quite fine; but it is lost on two women—an old wife and an old maid. There should have been a disengaged gentleman present. Is Mr. Robert nowhere hid behind the curtains, do you think, Miss Moore?”

Hortense, who had been much in the kitchen superintending tea—watching the kettle’s boil, measuring leaves, setting bread and butter with French exactness, making certain the cream was fresh and the cups warmed—had not caught the drift. She came back with a puzzled look and answered literally, “Robert is at Whinbury.”

Mrs. Yorke laughed her short, peculiar laugh.

“Straightforward Miss Moore!” she said patronisingly. “It is like you to understand so literally and answer so simply. Your mind comprehends nothing of intrigue. Strange things might go on around you without your being the wiser; you are not of the class the world calls sharp-witted.”

The equivocal compliment did not please Hortense. Her black brows puckered; her back stiffened. Yet she still looked bewildered, and, because she prided herself on discernment, she hastened to reclaim it.

“I have ever been noted for sagacity and discernment from childhood,” she returned.

“You never plotted to win a husband, I’ll be bound,” pursued Mrs. Yorke; “and you have not the benefit of previous experience to aid you in discovering when others plot.”

Caroline felt those words as if they had been laid, cold and heavy, on her heart. She could not parry them. To answer would have been to confess the blow had found its mark. She sat with downcast eyes, cheeks burning, posture unconsciously bent—as if the very air in the room had grown too sharp. Mrs. Yorke, seeing the tremor of humiliation, thought her fair game.

There was in Mrs. Yorke a natural antipathy to shrinking sensitiveness. Youth and prettiness did not soften her; they irritated her, like something too fragile that asks to be spared. This afternoon she was especially bilious and morose—disposed to gore, as some fierce cow in a field will lower her head at a creature that flinches.

“Your cousin Hortense is an excellent sister, Miss Helstone,” she began again. “Such ladies as come to try their life’s luck at Hollow’s Cottage may, by a very little clever female artifice, cajole the mistress of the house and have the game in their own hands. You are fond of your cousin’s society, I dare say, miss?”

“Of which cousin’s?” Caroline asked, keeping her voice even.

“Oh, of the lady’s, of course.”

“Hortense is, and always has been, most kind to me.”

“Every sister with an eligible single brother is considered most kind by her spinster friends.”

Caroline lifted her eyes slowly. The trouble cleared from their blue; the shame’s hot colour ebbed, leaving her pale, but steady. Her voice, when it came, was not loud, not rapid, but distinct as a knife laid down on a board.

“Mrs. Yorke, may I ask what you mean?”

“To give you a lesson in the cultivation of rectitude—to disgust you with craft and false sentiment.”

“Do I need this lesson?”

“Most young ladies of the present day need it. You are quite a modern young lady—morbid, delicate, professing to like retirement; which implies you find little worthy of sympathy in the ordinary world. The ordinary world—every-day honest folks—are better than you think them; much better than any bookish, romancing chit of a girl can be who hardly ever puts her nose over her uncle the parson’s garden wall.”

“Consequently of whom you know nothing,” said Caroline, and there was no tremor now, only clean anger held in bounds. “Excuse me—indeed it does not matter whether you excuse me or not—you have attacked me without provocation; I shall defend myself without apology. Of my relations with my two cousins you are ignorant. In a fit of ill-humour you have attempted to poison them by gratuitous insinuations, which are far more crafty and false than anything with which you can justly charge me. That I happen to be pale, and sometimes to look diffident, is no business of yours; that I am fond of books and indisposed for common gossip is still less your business; that I am a ‘romancing chit of a girl’ is conjecture. I never romanced to you nor to any one you know. That I am the parson’s niece is not a crime, though you may be narrow-minded enough to think it so. You dislike me. You have no just reason for disliking me; therefore keep the expression of your aversion to yourself. If at any time in future you evince it annoyingly, I shall answer even less scrupulously than I have done now.”

She ceased and sat perfectly still, white with restrained excitement. She had spoken in the clearest tones, each word shaped and placed. The speed of the blood in her veins was swift and invisible; but her bearing did not betray it.

Mrs. Yorke was not irritated. Turning coolly to Hortense, she nodded her tremendous cap as if applauding an exercise well done.

“She has spirit in her, after all,” she said. “Always speak as honestly as you have done just now, and you’ll do.”

“I repel a recommendation so offensive,” Caroline answered at once, the same pure key, the same unflinching look. “I reject counsel poisoned by insinuation. It is my right to speak as I think proper; nothing binds me to converse as you dictate. So far from always speaking as I have done just now, I shall never address any one in a tone so stern or in language so harsh, unless in answer to unprovoked insult.”

“Mother, you have found your match,” pronounced little Jessie, much edified.

Rose had listened without moving her face. She went on with her marking, then said, without malice, as if stating a fact from a book, “No; Miss Helstone is not my mother’s match, for she allows herself to be vexed. My mother would wear her out in a few weeks. Shirley Keeldar manages better. Mother, you have never hurt Miss Keeldar’s feelings yet. She wears armour under her silk dress that you cannot penetrate.”
