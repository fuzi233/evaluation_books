Tars Tarkas and I had no breath to trade our stories among the toppled bodies at the boulder’s foot. The valley itself seemed to have been roused and poured toward us: from every direction a torrent of nightmares came running, summoned by the thin, piercing cry of that lone figure far up the cliff face.

“Come,” the Thark snapped, voice like flint struck on stone. “To the cliffs. There is our only hope—some cave, some ledge. Two may hold a narrow place forever against an unarmed mob.”

We fled across the scarlet sward. I kept my pace to his—his great stride was powerful, but his bulk cost him speed, and I would not leave him to be pulled down alone. The turf underfoot had the spring of a cultivated lawn, clipped too evenly to be natural, as though some patient, hungry gardener had been grazing it to the same height for centuries.

Three hundred yards. A single cooking-length in a man’s mind, but an eternity in a hunt.

The shriek from above kept slicing the air—high and bright as a knife on glass—and it drew the horde as surely as the smell of blood draws flies to a butcher’s trough. Beneath that call the valley filled with other sounds: thudding feet, the wet slap of bodies, the hard-click of claws. And underneath everything, like a hearth that would not go out, a purr—low, contented, obscene—rolling from the throats of the plant men.

Tars Tarkas glanced once at the distance still between us and the rock wall and read the odds as he would read a battlefield. “Go,” he said. “Find the shelter. Save the minutes.”

I threw all my Earth muscles into it and reached the cliff base in immense, bounding leaps. Up close, the wall rose sheer from the level sward, as if the world had been sliced clean with a blade. There was no talus, no slope of broken stone, no easy scramble—only scattered boulders half-sunk in the turf like bones in a shallow grave.

My first quick scan turned my stomach cold. Nowhere did I see the mercy of a crack, a seam, a handhold—nothing but the smooth, forbidding face, and far above it the strange herald still shrieking. To my right, the cliff’s foot vanished into the forest, whose luxuriant crowns climbed a thousand feet up the rock like waves frozen mid-surge. To my left, the escarpment ran unbroken toward mountains that penned the valley in a vast ring. Ahead, a river seemed to burst straight out of the cliff base, but it offered no path—only another trap.

The wall was five thousand feet high, dull yellow in shade, streaked with dusky red and green, with occasional flashes of white quartz. It was beautiful the way a sealed pantry is beautiful to a starving man: flawless, useless, and maddening.

Then the sun slid past the cliff’s zenith.

Light struck the face and the whole wall flared alive—gold and ruby and emerald, white gleams like chipped diamond. The rock did not merely shine; it glittered as if the cliff had been glazed with metal and studded with gems. It was a display fit for gods and thieves, but in that blaze I saw what I had missed in shadow: several black spots, high along the line where forest met cliff—cave mouths, dark as oven doors, tucked behind branches.

A path existed, but it ran through the trees.

I could climb; the skill was in my hands the way kneading is in a baker’s palms. Tars Tarkas, with his enormous weight and the Martian’s general lack of climbing practice, would find it near impossible. On Barsoom, the land rarely demanded a man climb straight up; the old, gentle ascents had let even warriors remain strangers to true vertical labor.

But there was nothing else. The horde was already close enough that their breathing began to thicken the air behind us.

We plunged into the forest’s shadow. The change was immediate: the crisp openness of the valley replaced by damp green dimness, the smell of resin and bruised leaf, and a chorus of living sounds—branch creaks, insect rasp, distant bird-calls—an acoustic shelter compared to the valley’s industrial roar of pursuit. Even here, though, the predators’ noise invaded, harsh and commerce-like, flattening the subtler music of the woods into something merely background.

We had almost reached a great tree that brushed the cliff face when one of the pursuers sprang in behind us—a giant plant man, claws outstretched, its multiple mouths working wetly in anticipation. It was ahead of the others by a hundred yards.

“Up, Tars Tarkas!” I shouted. “Climb—this one is mine.”

He seized the trunk and began to haul himself upward with grim, reluctant effort. I turned, sword raised, meaning to end the plant thing quickly—like cutting down a thick stalk before it seeds.

But the creature was not the mindless growth I’d taken it for. It halted at my feint; my blade carved only air. Its great tail swept the sward with the force of a battering ram and knocked me clean off my feet.

In a heartbeat it was on me. Cold, sucking mouths sought my throat and breast—lips like leeches, but stronger, greedier. I caught two writhing tentacles, one in each hand, and locked my arms. The thing was heavy and muscled, but there is a kind of strength that comes from a lifetime of knowing what it is to strain, to lift, to hold—strength as practical as a cook’s wrist after years of kneading and chopping. I choked it back from my neck and fought for leverage in the grass.

For an instant I thought I could win if we were alone.

Then I saw over its shoulder the wave that had been rushing to swallow us.

White apes.

Even on Mars, where every creature seems designed to test a man’s courage, these were the ones that reached nearest to fear—because they wore a rough parody of humanity. Fifteen feet tall, walking erect, with an extra pair of arms set between upper and lower limbs like some obscene attempt at improving upon the human frame. Their eyes were close-set, their ears high, their snouts and teeth like gorillas’. Bristling hair crowned their heads like a savage headdress.

They came in a storm of sound: snarls, screams, the hard drumming of feet, and that hideous purring from the plant men—so wrong in a thing that meant to drink you, as if a predator could make a meal of you and call it contentment.

They struck.

Talons and fangs found flesh. Sucking mouths fastened where they could, seeking arteries as deftly as a butcher seeks the right vein to bleed a carcass. Pain came hot and sudden, then a cold draining as the plant mouths clung.

I fought up, somehow, bodies dragging at me. I shortened my grip on the long-sword until it became a dagger and stabbed with brutal economy—no flourishes, only the necessary cuts that open a heart and end a charge. For a breath I stood free, half-blinded by sweat and the metallic smell of my own blood.

In that same breath Tars Tarkas dropped from the lower branches he had so painfully gained and landed beside me. Again we stood back to back as we had a hundred times—two blades answering a chaos of bodies.

The apes rushed; we beat them back. Plant men lashed us with tails like flails, sprang above us with greyhound speed, tried to wrap and suck and drag. Every assault met steel. My blade flashed and bit; Tars Tarkas’ sword moved with the sure, unsentimental precision of a man who had lived his whole life with death as his daily ration.

But no skill, no legend, can hold forever against a world’s worth of hungry muscle.

Step by step we were forced back until our shoulders hit the massive trunk of the tree we had chosen to climb. Around it we retreated, pressed by bodies, until we had been driven half around its base. Tars Tarkas led, and suddenly he gave a short, fierce sound—not fear, not pain, but triumph.

“Here is shelter for one at least, John Carter,” he said.

At the roots I saw a round opening, three feet across, yawning into darkness like a cellar door.

“In with you,” I cried.

He shook his head. “Too small. For you there is chance. For me—none. If we stay without, we both die. If you enter, you may live to avenge me.”

The Thark—whose people so often preferred death to flight—spoke of life as if it had become newly valuable. I did not understand the hunger in him, but I understood the thing driving me: Dejah Thoris. Love is a kind of native tongue; it makes commands of the body that no other language can translate.

“Then we die together,” I said. “I will not go first. You go while I hold them. When you are in, I can slip after.”

We spoke in jagged phrases between cuts and thrusts. At last he yielded, grim humor on his hard face.

“It was ever your way,” he said, “to think last of your own life. And ever your way to command others—even the greatest of jeddaks.”

He, Jeddak of Thark, turned to obey a man half his size.

“If you fail,” he said, “the cruel and heartless Thark—whom you taught the meaning of friendship—will come out to die beside you.”

“As you will,” I answered. “Now—head first. Quickly.”

He hesitated at the shame of turning his back. Pride is a dialect green men speak fluently, and retreat is a word that does not sit easily in their mouths.

“Haste,” I urged. “Or we both go down for nothing.”

He dropped and forced himself at the opening, shoulders grinding, while the howling mass sensed escape and hurled itself at me with doubled fury.

My sword became a blur. Green sap—sticky, acrid—slicked the steel from plant men I opened; red blood sprayed from white apes whose hearts I found with ruthless thrusts. The noise was a crushing thing now: screeches and growls, and that purring—soft as a satisfied diner’s hum, but uttered by mouths that drank flesh.

They piled on. Even with the ground heaped with their dead, they came.

At last they dragged me down. Cold mouths sealed against torn skin; life seemed to drain with a sickening suction.

Then powerful hands clamped my ankles and yanked me backward. In a second I was sliding into the tree’s hollow, hauled by Tars Tarkas. A plant man clung to my chest, tenacious as a burr. For a moment it was a tug-of-war between its hunger and the Thark’s strength, until I wedged my sword point under it and drove upward, piercing whatever served it for vitals. It shuddered and went slack.

Inside, I lay panting on the flat, hard floor—torn, bleeding, tasting iron and sap. The hollow was wide—fifty feet across—and the ground bore the polished wear of long habitation, as if generations of creatures had used this place the way families use a kitchen: by returning to it again and again until their bodies leave a history in the surface.

Tars Tarkas crouched at the opening, sword ready, blocking it with his bulk as best he could. For an hour the mob raged outside—howling, shrieking, battering the trunk. They tried the entrance a few times but found it too small to force in numbers; soon they settled into sound—terrorizing screams, ape growls, and that purring that seemed designed not merely to threaten the body but to sour the mind.

Eventually most of them drifted away, leaving perhaps a score to watch. A siege, then, and starvation. Even if we slipped out after dark, where would we go in this unknown valley—hemmed by cliffs and monsters and a forest that could hide a thousand mouths?

As the noise dwindled and my eyes adjusted to the dimness, I explored. Looking up, I saw far above a faint glow—an opening in the stem. Hope, thin as the first scent of bread from an oven when you have been starving.

At the far side I found a rough ladder leading to a series of horizontal wooden bars spanning the narrowing shaft. They were set three feet apart, forming a perfect climb, vanishing into darkness above.

I told Tars Tarkas. He nodded once. “Go,” he said. “See what lies above. I will guard.”

I climbed.

The bars rose endlessly. The light grew stronger. Five hundred feet up, I reached an opening about the size of the one below. It opened onto a broad limb, its surface worn smooth by long use—an avenue, a road in the air. I did not step out. Sound carries strangely in high places; a single scrape can betray you. Instead I returned swiftly.

We climbed together then, Tars Tarkas leading. As I ascended, I pulled free the lower bars behind us, dislodging them and passing them up. He carried them a hundred feet higher and wedged them in place, while I continued stripping away the rungs beneath. Soon the tree’s interior was denuded of all means of ascent for a hundred feet from the base—no pursuer could follow easily. It was a cook’s instinct applied to war: remove the tools, deny the next hand the knife.

When we reached the upper opening, Tars Tarkas stepped aside. “You,” he said. “Your weight is less. Your limbs are quicker.”

I eased out onto the great limb. It rose at a gentle angle toward the cliff. Following it, I found it ended above a narrow ledge at the mouth of a small cave. The branch’s tip was slimmer; it bent under me until it swayed level with the ledge, only a couple of feet away. Below lay the scarlet valley carpeted like spilled spice; above, the golden cliff face blazed, jeweled and merciless.

This cave was not one I’d seen from below; those were higher, perhaps a thousand feet. But a shelter is a shelter, and a door is a door, no matter what name you give it in what language.

I returned for Tars Tarkas. Together we wormed our way along the wavering branch, clinging like men on a hanging bridge. At the end, our combined weight depressed the limb so far that the cave mouth rose out of reach.

We argued quickly and decided: Tars Tarkas would go back along the branch, leaving his longest leather harness strap with me. When the limb rose under my lighter weight, I would enter the cave, then lower the strap and haul him up.

He retreated, stiff with distaste at the necessity of yielding ground. The moment his weight left the tip, the branch rose. I balanced, reached, and swung myself onto the ledge, then into the cave mouth. When he returned, I dropped the strap. He caught it, and with brute strength and my leverage I hauled him up until we stood together on that dizzy little balcony.

From there the valley spread like a painted tablecloth: forest and crimson sward bordering a silent sea, all framed by the monstrous, gleaming cliffs. In the far distance, for a moment, we thought we saw a gilded minaret shining among the treetops—an artifact of civilization, a promise of voices and hearths and meals eaten without fear. But the image wavered and vanished into our doubt, and we dismissed it as a mirage born of desire.

Below, white apes tore into the remnants of Tars Tarkas’ fallen companions, feeding with the noisy obliviousness of beasts that have never learned reverence for the dead. Plant men grazed in widening circles, trimming the red sward as neatly as any herd trims a pasture, their feeding a grotesque echo of domestic routine—harvest without gratitude, sustenance without memory.

Attack from the tree now seemed unlikely. We turned to the cave, believing it a continuation of the hidden path—leading away, gods alone knew where, but away from that valley’s ferocity.

Inside, we found a tunnel cut clean from the solid cliff. It was well proportioned, twenty feet high, five feet wide, roof arched like an old stone oven. We had no light. We groped forward, slow and careful, Tars Tarkas’ hand on one wall, mine on the other, our free hands clasped so we would not be separated if the passage branched. In darkness, touch becomes its own language, and even that language fails when fear starts translating every brush of stone into a threat.

We went on until our hands met an obstruction. Not the rock itself—this felt like hard wood, fitted across the tunnel like a partition. I swept my fingers over it until I found what every Martian dwelling knows: the button that stands in for an Earthman’s knob. I pressed it gently.

The door gave.

A dimly lighted chamber opened beyond, seemingly empty. I swung the door wider and stepped through, the Thark following.

We stood silent, listening. The chamber’s air smelled old—dry, sealed, as if it had been stored away from weather and time like a rare spice kept in a jar no one dares to open.

Then a slight noise behind us.

I turned in time to see the door close with a sharp click—clean, final—like a lid snapping over a pot.

I sprang back and clawed at it, suddenly certain that the quiet here was not peace but preparation. My fingers found no button on this side. The portal was smooth and unyielding.

And then, from unseen lips, laughter rang through the chamber—cruel, mocking, and edged with the satisfaction of a host who has trapped his guests in a house whose language they do not speak.
