At dawn, we pressed onward toward the forest with urgent speed. Upon Lona's steed I bore her lifeless form, a heavy, sacred burden. My destination was her father; if he would grant her a couch within his chamber of the dead, so much the better. If not—if he refused to accept a daughter who had not sought death willingly—I would remain with her corpse in the desert until the sands claimed it entirely. Yet I trusted he would accept her, for her spirit had departed long ago. And how humbling, how crushing, must my submission be before him!

For Adam, I carried Lilith. I possessed no authority to force her repentance, nor the moral standing to condemn her to eternal imprisonment, yet I dared not release her into the world. She was a prisoner I had sentenced, and I feared I was unfit to guard her soul forever.

Throughout our journey, I repeatedly offered her sustenance, but her response was a gaze of ravenous hatred. Her eyes burned like coals, rolling ceaselessly across the landscape, refusing to close until we crossed the scorching stream. Beyond that point, they remained shuttered tight until we arrived at the threshold of the House of Bitterness.

That evening, as we encamped, a small child approached Lilith. I lunged forward to ward off danger, but I was too late. The child had touched something to the princess's lips and recoiled in a shriek of agony.

"King!" the child whimpered, pulling back. "Suck my finger! The wicked giantess made a hole in it!"

I obeyed instantly, sucking the tiny digit clean.

"Well!" the child exclaimed, immediately offering a second piece of fruit to a mouth eager for more. This time, however, Lilith snapped her hand away violently, dropping the fruit to the dust. The child's name was Luva.

The following day, we traversed the hot stream. On familiar ground, the Little Ones erupted in jubilation, though their nests remained far behind. Our travel halted that afternoon at the Ivy-Hall, drawn by the promise of its grapevines. Resolved to spend the night there, we watched as the clusters swayed. Instantly recognizing the bounty, the children swarmed the vines, devouring eagerly until exhaustion claimed them. Within moments, they lay fast asleep upon the green floor and scattered throughout the surrounding woods. Anticipating their slumber, hoping to witness the spectral dance uninterrupted, I cleared a wide circle in the center and lay down beside Lona's body, vigilance keeping me awake.

Night fell, and suddenly the air filled with movement. I pondered if these dances would echo eternally, if I, too, might eventually be compelled to join the endless rhythm due to my stubborn heart, when the children's eyes snapped open. They leaped to their feet, alert and springing. Every child seized a dancer, and together they bounded away, skipping and leaping. The spirits seemed to welcome them—perhaps they recognized the Little Ones, knowing well that these children had once walked paths similar to their own, returning from the abyss of age. Their innocent games brought a rare respite to weary souls stripped of hope, whose lives existed only in the shadow of a vanished past. The pranks were joyful, never cruel. Even when the children momentarily disrupted the spectral rhythm, the ghosts showed no irritation; having lost their futures and their joys, they could offer no real smiles, yet they endured the chaos with a strange patience.

Just as dawn threatened to breach the horizon, I perceived the skeleton-princess standing in the doorway. Her eyes glowed with a terrifying luminescence, the black mark of death stark upon her side. She paused, then glided inward as if intending to join the revelry. I surged to my feet. A cry of horror tore from the children, and the lights flickered and vanished. Only the pale moon peered through, revealing the children huddled together in terror. The ghosts were gone, or at least invisible. The princess had disappeared as well. I dashed to the spot where she lay; she rested there, eyes closed, motionless as stone. Returning to the hall, I found the Little Ones settling themselves for sleep on the floor.

Morning broke, and as we prepared to depart, we spotted two skeletons moving through a nearby thicket. The Little Ones broke formation, rushing to greet them. Following closely, despite the absence of splints or ligatures, I recognized the pair I had seen before. The children embraced the skeletal forms, stroking the long, bare fingers, and the creatures accepted the affection warmly. Clearly, their shared destitution had forged a bond; the total loss of everything marked the start of a new existence.

Observing that the skeletons had gathered handfuls of herbs, presumably to apply to their bones—their only means of nutrition—the Little Ones carefully inspected the offerings. They gathered identical herbs, filling the outstretched hands of the skeletons. Then, bidding them farewell with promises to return, the children resumed their journey, marveling aloud at the kindness of these "nice people" living in the forest.

Upon reaching the nest-village, I stayed one night to ensure they were settled safely. Lona still appeared freshly deceased, and haste was unnecessary. Inside the village, Lilith had consumed nothing, her eyes sealed shut. Fearing she might perish before the journey's end, I crept to her in the night, laying my bare arm upon her lips. She bit down with ferocious strength, forcing a cry from my throat. I scrambled away, only regaining consciousness lying beyond her grasp. Morning came, and I immediately organized our departure.

From the Little Ones, I selected twelve—not the largest or strongest, but the sweetest and most joyful—and mounted them upon six elephants. Two of the wise *CLUMSIES*, as the children termed them, were assigned to carry the princess. I continued riding Lona's horse, her body wrapped in her cloak borne before me. Taking the direct path across the left branch of the river-bed, I aimed for the House of Bitterness. There, I hoped to discern the safest crossing for the broader, rougher branch and to avoid the monster-filled basin. I dreaded the terrain for the elephants and the unknown dangers for the children.

One terrible night, the third spent in the desert between the two branches of the dead river, haunted us. We had halted the elephants in a sheltered hollow, allowing Lilith to slide down between them to lie upon the sand. She appeared utterly dead, yet I suspected otherwise. I positioned myself a short distance away, Lona's body at my side, guarding both the fallen and the dangerous intruder. The moon hung half-down the western sky, a pale, contemplative orb casting a mottled web of shadows over the dunes. Suddenly, it was eclipsed. Visible yet sending forth no light, it seemed draped in a thick, diaphanous film, appearing troubled. The film swept slightly aside, revealing the jagged edge against her clarity—a torn, hooked wing of a bat. A cold wind, stinging like fire, swept over us, and Lilith was upon me. Though her hands remained bound, she used her teeth to tear the cloak Lona had made for me from my shoulder, driving the edges into my flesh. I lay paralyzed.

Life seemed draining from me into her, but memory returned. Striking her hand, I woke her. She raised her head with a gurgling shriek, shivering violently. I flung her off and sprang to my feet.

Kneeling, she rocked back and forth. A second blast of hot-stinging cold enveloped us. The moon blazed clear, illuminating her face—gaunt, ghastly, smeared with blood.

"Down, devil!" I cried.

"Where are you taking me?" she asked, her voice a dull echo from a sepulcher.

"To your first husband," I answered.

"He will kill me!" she moaned.

"At least he will take you off my hands!"

"Give me my daughter!" she screamed suddenly, grinding her teeth.

"Never! Your doom awaits!"

"Loose my hands for pity's sake!" she groaned. "The cords sink into my flesh!"

"I dare not. Lie down!" I commanded.

She collapsed onto the ground like a log. The remainder of the night passed in silence, and by morning, she appeared dead once more.

Before evening, the silhouette of the House of Bitterness rose before us. Instantly, one elephant drifted alongside my horse.

"King, please..." whispered the Little One riding atop, "are you not going to that place?"

"I am indeed! We shall spend the night there," I replied.

"Oh, please, don't! That must be where the cat-woman lives!"

"If you had ever seen her, you would not dare call her by such a name!"

"Nobody ever sees her! She has lost her face! Her head turns back and sides all around!"

"She hides her face from dull, discontented hearts! Who taught you to call her the 'cat-woman'?"

"The bad giants called her so!"

"What did they say about her?"

"That she has claws on her toes!"

"It is false. I know the lady. I spent a night at her house."

"But she *may* have claws! Perhaps you saw her feet, and the claws were folded inside her cushions!"

"Then perhaps you think *you* have claws on your toes?"
