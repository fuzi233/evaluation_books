When John Jasper regained consciousness from his sudden collapse, he found himself surrounded by the concerned figures of Mr. and Mrs. Tope, summoned by his visitor to attend to him. Sitting rigidly in a nearby chair, hands resting heavily upon his knees, watched Mr. Grewgious with a gaze fixed and unblinking.

"There! You've come to nicely now, sir," sobbed Mrs. Tope, her voice thick with emotion. "You were thoroughly worn out, and no wonder!"

"A man," Mr. Grewgious recited, adopting his habitual tone of lecturing a student, "cannot have his rest broken, his mind cruelly tormented, and his body overtaxed by fatigue, without being thoroughly worn out."

"I fear I have alarmed you?" Jasper murmured weakly as he was guided into an easy chair.

"Not at all, I thank you," Mr. Grewgious replied. "You are too considerate."

"Not at all, I thank you," he added again, the repetition sounding like a mantra.

"You must take some wine, sir," Mrs. Tope insisted, bustling toward the table. "And the jelly I prepared for you—the one you refused at noon despite my warnings, since you hadn't breakfasted. You must also have a wing of the roast fowl, which has been reheated twenty times if it's been heated once. Everything will be served in five minutes, and this good gentleman will likely stay to ensure you eat."

The "good gentleman" responded with a sharp snort that could be interpreted as agreement, denial, or simply silence, leaving Mrs. Tope momentarily baffled before her attention shifted to setting the table.

"Will you join me?" Jasper asked as the cloth was laid.

"I couldn't manage a single bite, I thank you," Mr. Grewgious declined flatly.

Jasper, however, devoured the food and drink with alarming voracity. His haste suggested a desperate need to fortify his failing spirit rather than any genuine appreciation for the flavors. Meanwhile, Mr. Grewgious sat upright, his face devoid of expression, radiating a stiff, imperturbable politeness that seemed to say: *I possess no observations to offer on any subject whatsoever.*

"Do you know," Jasper said eventually, pushing his empty plate aside and staring into space, "that I find some crumbs of comfort in the communication which has so astonished me?"

"_Do_ you?" Mr. Grewgious returned, silently appending the thought: *I do not.*

"After recovering from the shock of news regarding my dear boy—news so unexpected and destructive of every hope I had built for him; and after allowing time to process it... yes."

"I shall be glad to collect your crumbs," Mr. Grewgious said dryly.

"Is there not—or, if I deceive myself, please correct me and spare me further pain—is there not hope?" Jasper pressed. "That realizing the awkward burden of explanation this new situation imposes upon him, he chose to avoid the confrontation and fled?"

"Such a thing might be," Mr. Grewgious mused. "Indeed, such things have occurred. People, wishing to avoid a week-long scandal and the demands of the idle public, have vanished and remain unheard of."

"I believe such things happen," Mr. Grewgious conceded.

"When I had no suspicion," Jasper continued, seizing the thread with eager intensity, "that my beloved nephew had withheld anything from me—not even such a crucial detail as this—what light existed in my darkness? When I believed his bride-to-be was here and their wedding imminent, how could I conceive he would voluntarily vanish in such an unaccountable, capricious, and cruel manner? But now, knowing what you have revealed, is there not a crack through which daylight enters? If he departed of his own volition, is his absence not more explicable and less cruel? While the pain remains, the cruelty inflicted upon her diminishes."

Mr. Grewgious could not deny this logic.

"And even regarding myself," Jasper persisted, his voice rising with renewed hope, "he knew you were coming to inform me. If your disclosure has sparked this new line of thought, it logically follows he foresaw the conclusions I would draw. Grant that he did foresee them; then, even the cruelty toward me—who am I, after all, John Jasper, mere Music Master—vanishes!"

Once more, Mr. Grewgious agreed.

"I harbored deep distrusts," Jasper admitted. "Yet your revelation, though initially overwhelming, showing that my cherished boy had disappointed me, has kindled hope. You do not extinguish it when I express it, but validate it as reasonable. I begin to believe it possible:" He clasped his hands tightly. "That he may have left of his own accord, and that he may yet be alive and well."

At that precise moment, Mr. Crisparkle entered. Jasper repeated his theory to him: "I begin to believe it possible that he may have disappeared voluntarily and remains alive."

"Why so?" Crisparkle asked.

Jasper reiterated his arguments. Had they lacked plausibility, the Minor Canon's mind would have been primed to accept them as exoneration for his pupil. Yet, Crisparkle genuinely attached great weight to the fact that Edwin had been thrust into an embarrassing position relative to everyone involved in his schemes immediately prior to his disappearance. The perspective had indeed shifted.

"I explained to Mr. Sapsea," Jasper noted, recalling the conversation he had actually held, "that there was no quarrel between the two youths at their final meeting. Though their first encounter was hostile, everything proceeded smoothly at my house. My dear boy was depressed—I noted this—and now that I know there was a specific cause for his depression, a cause potentially driving him away, I feel compelled to emphasize it."

"Heaven grant it turns out so!" Crisparkle cried.

"_I_ pray to Heaven it may turn out so!" Jasper echoed.

"You know," Jasper continued, addressing both men, "and Mr. Grewgious should know now—that I harbored a strong prejudice against Mr. Neville Landless due to his furious behavior on that first occasion. You recall my extreme apprehension for my nephew's safety. You remember I even recorded in my diary, and showed you, my dark forebodings concerning him. Mr. Grewgious deserves the full picture. Through no fault of my own, he must not be kept ignorant of parts of the truth. I wish him to understand that your disclosure has positively influenced my mind, overriding my previous profound conviction of Landless's guilt."

This display of fairness troubled Crisparkle deeply. He felt guilty for his own lack of transparency, suppressing two instances of Neville's violent outbursts against Edwin and the burning jealousy he had witnessed in Neville's heart. Convinced of Neville's innocence, yet weighed down by circumstantial evidence, he feared adding two more pieces to the puzzle. True to his nature, he agonized over whether revealing these fragments now would amount to constructing a lie.

However, Jasper's example stood before him. Hesitation ended. Turning to Mr. Grewgious, whom he regarded as the authoritative figure in this unfolding mystery, Crisparkle testified to Jasper's unwavering sense of justice. Expressing absolute confidence that Edwin would eventually be cleared of all suspicion, he confessed that his faith in the young man had been forged despite knowing Landless's fiery temper and his romantic fixation on the same woman.

Jasper's hopeful reaction was palpable, though it paled his complexion. He vowed to cling to the hope derived from Grewgious's words. Unless evidence surfaced proving Edwin had been killed, he would maintain, to the very end, the belief that his nephew had absconded of his own free will.

Later that night, Mr. Crisparkle, still plagued by unease and concern for the young man effectively imprisoned in his home, took a solitary walk. He headed toward Cloisterham Weir, a route he frequented, though his distracted mind prevented him from noticing his surroundings until the roar of the falling water reached him.

"How did I come here?" he wondered. "Why did I come here?"

He stood listening. A memory of a passage describing "airy tongues that syllable men's names" flashed unbidden into his mind, which he waved away as if tangible. Under the starlight, the weir presented nothing unusual. The tide had been flowing strongly earlier, washing away any potential evidence between the weir and the sea. Yet, a strange sensation lingered. He tested his senses: sight, hearing—all reported normalcy. Straining his eyes, he approached the timbers. Still, nothing. Resolved, he promised to return at dawn.

The weir haunted his dreams all night. At sunrise, on a bright, frosty morning, he returned. Surveying the scene meticulously, his gaze suddenly locked onto a single spot. He turned away, scanned the horizon, then returned his focus to that tiny speck. It captivated him. Hands trembling, he stripped off his coat. Something glistened there—a stationary shimmer amidst the moving water droplets.

Assured it was real, he discarded his clothing and plunged into the icy water, swimming frantically to the spot. Clinging to the timbers, he retrieved a gold watch caught in the crevices, engraved on the back with **E.D.**

Returning to shore, he dove again, searching deeper until the cold became unbearable. His expectation was to find a body; instead, he found only a shirt-pin stuck in the mud.
