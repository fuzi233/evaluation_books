“What! remain to be Denounced—dragged, it may be, in chains.” — WERNER

The three Hales sat huddled together in the parlour, the whole long day slipping by in a heavy, hush-thick fog of grief. Mr. Hale barely spoke unless his children pressed him, drawing him back from his haunted ruminations to the present moment, almost as if he had to be forced into interacting with the world around him. Frederick’s raw, screaming paroxysm of grief the day before had burned itself out; he was almost ashamed now of how thoroughly emotion had unravelled him, though the deep, unshakable ache of losing his mother settled permanent and heavy in his bones, a weight he knew he would carry for the rest of his life, never to speak of aloud again. Margaret, who had held herself stiff and resolute at first, was unravelling now instead. She cried in quiet, uncontrollable bursts, and even her speech on trivial, everyday topics carried a soft, mournful tenderness that deepened every time her eyes fell on Frederick, counting down the hours until his inevitable, dangerous departure.

For her father’s sake, she was glad to see him go, no matter how much she grieved the separation for her own part. The constant, frayed terror Mr. Hale lived in — that his mutinous, long-absent son would be recognized, dragged off in chains to face the charges hanging over his head — far outweighed the fragile, fleeting joy he drew from having Frederick home. Since Mrs. Hale’s death, that nervousness had only grown sharper. He jumped at every unusual creak of the floorboard, every distant unfamiliar voice, and never felt at ease unless Frederick was tucked entirely out of the line of sight of anyone who might step through the parlour door.

Towards dusk, he spoke up, his voice thin and trembling: “You will go with Frederick to the station, Margaret? I need to know he is safely away. Promise me you will come back and tell me he has cleared Milton, at the very least.”

“Of course,” Margaret said. “I will be glad to go, as long as you will not be lonely here by yourself, Papa.”

“No, no! I will sit here imagining every stranger on the road has found him out and dragged him off, unless you come home and tell me you saw him off safe. And go to Outwood Station, not the main Milton one. It is just as close, and far less crowded. Take a cab there, too. Fewer chances of anyone spotting him. What time is your train, Fred?”

“Ten minutes past six. It will be nearly dark by then. What will you do on the way back, Margaret?” Frederick asked.

“Oh, I will manage,” she said, forcing a bright, steady tone. “I have gotten very brave lately, have I not? The whole road home is well-lit even after dark, and I was out much later than that just last week.”

Margaret breathed a quiet sigh of relief when the lingering, painful goodbye to their mother’s body and their father was over. She herded Frederick into the waiting cab as fast as she could, to cut short the scene that was tearing Mr. Hale apart, who had followed his son to the threshold of the room where his wife lay, to stare at her face one last time. Between that rush to leave, and the careless, all-too-common error in the railway guide’s listed arrival times for small country stations, they pulled up to Outwood to find they had nearly twenty minutes to kill before the train was due. The booking office was still locked, so they could not even buy their tickets ahead of time. They wandered down the flight of stone steps that led below the railway embankment, to a wide cinder path that cut diagonally through a field beside the main carriage road, walking back and forth to pass the minutes.

Margaret’s hand rested light on Frederick’s arm, and he curled his own hand over hers, warm and tight. “Margaret, once I am safely away I am going to write to Mr. Lennox to see if there is any way I can clear my name, make it safe to come back to England whenever I want — more for your sake than anyone else’s. I cannot bear the thought of you all alone here if anything happens to Papa. He looks so worn, so terribly shaken, I barely recognize him. I wish you would get him to agree to that plan of moving to Cadiz with me, for a dozen reasons. What would you do if he was gone? You have no close friends, no family nearby. We are strangely bare of relations to fall back on, are we not?”

Margaret had to fight hard not to burst into tears, at the tender, worried way he laid out a future she already feared was all too possible, every care of the last months having worn Mr. Hale down to a shadow of his old self. But she rallied her voice, soft as ever: “The last two years have been so full of strange, unexpected turns, that I have learned not to waste time trying to plan for every possible bad thing that might happen. I try only to live in the present, now.” She paused, and they stilled for a moment, right beside the stile that led from the field out to the road, the last slants of the setting sun gilding their faces. Frederick looked down at her, his face tight with unspoken worry, reading all the quiet grief and weariness she tried not to put into words.

“We will write to each other all the time,” she went on, “and I promise you — I can see it will put your mind at ease — I will tell you every little worry that crops up. Papa is —” She cut off, a tiny, almost invisible flinch running through her, but Frederick felt the jolt of her hand in his, and turned his full face to the road. A man on horseback was riding slow past the stile right where they stood. Margaret bowed stiffly, and the man returned the gesture with a sharp, cold nod.

“Who was that?” Frederick asked, before the man was even out of earshot. Margaret was a little flushed, her shoulders drooping a little as she answered: “Mr. Thornton. You saw him once before, remember, from behind.”

“He is an unprepossessing-looking fellow, is he not?” Frederick said. “What a scowl he had on his face.”

“Something must have happened to vex him,” Margaret said, quick to defend him. “You would not think he looked harsh if you had seen him sitting with my mother, the days before she died.”

“I think it must be time to head back for the station to get my ticket,” Frederick said, shifting the subject. “If I had known how fast the dark was going to fall, Margaret, I would not have paid off the cab to send it back.”

“Do not fuss over me,” she said. “I can always hail another cab right here if I want, or I can take the stopping train back to Milton, where there are shops and people and lamps all along the road from the station to our house. Do not think about me, just watch yourself. I am sick at the thought that Leonards might be on the very same train as you. Look every carriage over carefully before you step on board.”

They walked back up to the platform. Margaret insisted on stepping right up to the flaring bright gas light inside the station office to buy her brother’s ticket. A handful of idle young men were loitering by the station master, and Margaret swore she recognized one of their faces; she met his impudent, openly staring gaze with a proud, sharp look of offended dignity, before hurrying back out to Frederick where he waited on the platform, and looping her arm through his.

“Do you have your bag? Let us walk up and down the platform a little,” she said, a little flustered, her old brave resolve oozing away faster than she cared to admit, even to herself. She heard footsteps following them along the flagstones, steps that stopped when they stopped, both of them leaning out over the edge of the platform to listen for the distant whine of the coming train. Neither spoke; their hearts were too full, too tight with unsaid things. In one more minute the train would be there, and a minute after that, he would be gone. Margaret almost regretted pushing him so hard to leave for London first; if he had sailed straight for Spain out of Liverpool, he could have been out of the country in two or three hours, no risk of being caught at all.

Frederick turned sharply, right towards the bright platform lamp where the gas flared high in anticipation of the arriving train. A man dressed as a railway porter lunged forward: a rough, brutish-looking man, whose face was bloated with drink even as his senses were still sharp enough to act. “Begging your pardon, miss!” he grunted, shoving Margaret roughly out of the way, and seizing Frederick hard by the collar of his coat. “Your name is Hale, I believe?”

Everything swam before Margaret’s eyes, she never saw exactly how it happened — but with a quick, sharp twist of a wrestling move he had learned years before at sea, Frederick tripped the man hard, and he toppled off the three-foot high platform, falling hard onto the soft dirt and gravel beside the railway tracks. He lay there, motionless for a second.

“Run! Run!” Margaret gasped out, shoving Frederick towards the train that was now pulling to a stop. “That was Leonards, was it not? Go, quickly! I will carry your bag!” She pushed him with all the strength she had left. A carriage door swung open right in front of them; Frederick leaped inside, and as he leaned out the window to yell “God bless you, Margaret!” the train rumbled forwards, carrying him away, and left her standing all alone on the empty platform.

She felt so sick, so faint with shock, that she stumbled gratefully into the ladies’ waiting room and sank down onto a bench for a minute. At first she could do nothing but gasp for air. The rush, the terror, the near miss had been too much. If the train had not pulled up exactly at that moment, Leonards would have scrambled back up and called for help to arrest him. She wondered if the man had gotten up; she tried to remember if she had seen him move, wondered if he had been badly hurt in the fall. She forced herself back out onto the platform, which was lit bright by gas lamps but still completely empty, and walked to the far end to peer down over the edge, heart thudding. No one was there. She was glad she had made herself look, otherwise terrible half-imagined fears would have haunted her all night, in her sleep. Even so, she was trembling so hard, so shaken, that she knew she could not walk the long dark lonely road home, which stretched out black and empty as far as she could see beyond the station lights. She would wait for the next down train, and ride it back to Milton. But what if Leonards recognized her as the woman who had helped Frederick escape?

She peered around carefully before she dared step into the booking office to buy her ticket. Only a handful of railway officials stood there, talking loudly to each other.

“So Leonards has been on the drink again, eh?” said one man, who sounded like he was in charge. “He will need all that boasted influence of his to keep his job after this.”

“Where is he, then?” another asked. Margaret stood with her back to them, fumbling with her change with shaking fingers, not daring to turn around until she heard the answer.

“Damned if I know. He stumbled in here not five minutes back, rambling some nonsense about falling off the platform, swearing fit to make your ears burn, begging me for money to get on the next up train to London. He was making all sorts of daft promises to pay me back, but I had better things to do than listen to a drunk. Told him to get lost, and he went staggering out the front door.”

“He is down the nearest pub vault, I would wager,” the first man laughed. “Your money would have gone straight there too, if you had been fool enough to lend it to him.”

“Catch me doing that! I know what his ‘London trip’ nonsense means. He still owes me five shillings from last month —” And their voices drifted on, no longer of any concern to her.

All Margaret could do now was wait, on tenterhooks, for her train to arrive. She slipped back into the ladies’ waiting room, and every footstep that passed the door, every loud, rough laugh, sounded like Leonards to her. But no one disturbed her until the train pulled into the station, when a porter helped her politely up into an empty carriage. She did not dare look up into his face until the train had started moving, and then she saw with a flood of relief that it was not Leonards at all.
