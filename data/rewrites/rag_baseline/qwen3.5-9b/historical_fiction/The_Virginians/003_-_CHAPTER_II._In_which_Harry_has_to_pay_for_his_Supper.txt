The iron of the bell handle bit into Harry's palm, a sharp, metallic promise ignored by the silence within Castlewood. For a long moment, the summons hung in the air, unanswered by ghosts or living breath. Finally, a servant emerged, his face a mask of indifference. The family was gone, retreating into the vacuum of their absence, leaving John to his cards and Thomas to the window-seat. To this household, bloodline was irrelevant currency; the housekeeper, however, was a merchant of attention, busy polishing the future arrival of the Lords and Ladies. Only through sheer persistence did Harry pry open a crack in her schedule, granted access to the sitting-room and the gallery. There, pinned to the wall, lay the ancestors: his grandfather in the stiff rigidity of a periwig and breastplate, a mirror image of the statue in Virginia; his grandmother, Lady Castlewood, captured in the loose, golden chaos of Charles II's era, her neck exposed, her hair a cascade of ringlets once snow-white, now faded to memory. These images held him captive until the housekeeper's grip tightened, driving him out as the family's shadow approached.

Outside, the air tasted of anticipation and neglect. Harry scribbled his lineage onto a scrap of paper from his pocket-book—a declaration of identity in a world that seemed determined to erase it. *Henry Esmond Warrington, of Castlewood, in Virginia.* He placed the note on the hall table. The lackeys, rising from their games to collect their wagers, opened the door not with reverence, but with the mechanical efficiency of servants paid to serve strangers. Gumbo, the gatekeeper, accepted a guinea with the casual ignorance of a man who does not understand value, taking the coin without grasping the weight of the gift. Harry walked away from the ancestral hall, the disappointment settling in his chest like a stone. If they had known him—if they had ridden up to his Virginia home—the protocol of hospitality would have been automatic. Instead, standing before the monument to his heritage, he was forced to beg for bacon and eggs at a roadside alehouse.

Later, under the fading light, Harry sat on the bridge. The sun dipped below the horizon, painting the old house in shades of dying gold, while rooks cawed their chaotic symphony above the elms. His mind drifted, constructing knights and cavaliers across the ford, imagining his grandmother's first husband hunting with hawk and hound. Then, the ghost of his lost brother surfaced, striking him with a tenderness so acute it brought tears to his eyes. The sorrow of a shared life cut off too soon washed over him. Around him, the blacksmith's hammer fell in a rhythmic, mechanical pulse, mixing with the bird calls and the distant hum of the evening. Suddenly, the rhythm broke. A pair of riders dashed over the bridge. One swore, kicking up dust, telling Harry to clear the path; the other, believing he had nearly thrown the pedestrian into the water, spurred his horse forward, shouting for his companion to follow. Before Harry could process the insult, the advance party was joined by livery servants on horseback, their faces grimacing with a distinctly British hostility. Behind them came the coach-and-six, a ponderous beast requiring a team of horses to drag it along. Three pale faces peered out at Harry as the carriage crossed the bridge, offering no return to the salute he rendered, recognizing the family crest. The gentleman seated behind the carriage glared, a look of pure hauteur that made Harry feel utterly invisible.

Desolation settled deep in his bones. He considered turning back to the *Rachel*, the ship with its tossing cabin feeling suddenly warmer than the stone bridge. The innkeepers knew nothing of the Warrington name. They informed him that the lady in the coach was the Countess, accompanied by her stepdaughter, Lady Maria, and her daughter, Lady Fanny. The young man in the grey frock was Mr. William; the powdered youth beside him was the Lord. It was the latter who had screamed the loudest, and the grey frock that had nearly galloped Harry into the ditch.

The landlord of the Three Castles had provided a bedchamber, but Harry refused to unpack his trunks, clinging to the delusion that the great house would eventually summon him. Hours bled into darkness. No invitation came. Reluctantly, he opened his case, pulling on his slippers and gown. Just as twilight deepened, a second chariot rolled over the bridge, drawn by four horses. A stout, high-colored lady with dark eyes scanned the room, fixing her gaze on Harry. This was the Baroness Bernstein, the landlady confirmed, the aunt of the Lord. Harry recalled the first Lady Castlewood's German origins, the lineage of Earls and Counts. Then, the procession vanished behind the castle gates, leaving Harry alone in the gloom, overwhelmed by a crushing sense of abandonment. Sleep eluded him, broken instead by the raucous noises spilling from the barroom downstairs—cursing, giggling, screaming.

Then, Gumbo's voice cut through the night, protesting outside. "You cannot go in, sar—my master asleep, sar!" But a shrill, drunken voice answered, drowning out the plea. "Stupid, negro woolly-pate," the voice cursed, pushing past the barrier. A flood of oaths poured into the room, followed by a young gentleman stumbling inside.

"Beg your pardon, Cousin Warrington," the intruder blurted, reeling. "Are you asleep? Beg your pardon for riding you over on the bridge. Didn't know you—course shouldn't have done it—thought it was a lawyer with a writ. Dressed in black, you know. Gad! Thought it was Nathan come to nab me." Mr. William laughed, a sound devoid of coherence, clearly intoxicated.

"You did me great honour to mistake me for a sheriff's officer, cousin," Harry replied, sitting upright in his tall nightcap, maintaining a dignity that contrasted sharply with the scene around him.

"Gad! I thought it was Nathan, and was going to send you souse into the river. But I ask your pardon. You see I had been drinking at the Bell at Hexton, and the punch is good at the Bell at Hexton. Hullo! you, Davis! a bowl of punch; d'you hear?"

"I have had my share for to-night, cousin, and I should think you have," Harry continued, his tone grave.

"You want me to go, Cousin What's-your-name, I see," Mr. William declared, his voice dropping to a mock seriousness. "You want me to go, and they want me to come, and I didn't want to come. I said, I'd see him hanged first—that's what I said. Why should I trouble myself to come down all alone of an evening, and look after a fellow I don't care a pin for? Zackly what I said. Zackly what Castlewood said. Why the devil should he go down? Castlewood says, and so said my lady, but the Baroness would have you. It's all the Baroness's doing, and if she says a thing, it must be done; so you must just get up and come." Mr. Esmond delivered this rambling manifesto with amiable rapidity, darting about the room as he spoke. But Harry's wrath flared. "I tell you what, cousin," he cried, "I won't move for the Countess, or for the Baroness, or for all the cousins in Castlewood."

Just then, the landlord entered with the bowl of punch Mr. William had ordered. The young gentleman in bed erupted. "Turn that sot out of the room!" he screamed.

"Sot, you little tobacconist! Sot, you Cherokee!" Mr. William shrieked back, leaping toward the bed. "Jump out of bed, and I'll drive my sword through your body. Why didn't I do it to-day when I took you for a bailiff—a confounded pettifogging bum-bailiff!" He continued his tirade, a cacophony of oaths and incoherence, until the landlord, the drawer, the hostler, and the entire kitchen staff converged to haul him away. Left alone, Harry pulled his covers tight around him, sulking in a storm of anger, before finally succumbing to sleep.

By the next morning, the landlord's demeanor had shifted completely. Obsequious and eager, he greeted his young guest, now fully aware of his name and station. Messengers had arrived from the castle the previous night to retrieve the young gentlemen. Poor Mr. William had returned in a wheelbarrow, a mode of transport he was apparently accustomed to. "He never remembers nothin' about it the next day," the landlord vowed. "He is of a real kind nature, Mr. William... He's the devil when he's tipsy, Mr. William, but when he is sober he is the very kindest of young gentlemen."

While Harry waited outside, a domestic council convened within the walls of Castlewood House. The family had found the note Harry had left, detailing his arrival. My Lord Castlewood reasoned that since they hadn't drowned the man on the bridge, they must invite him. A servant should carry a note. Lady Fanny suggested it would be more civil if one of the brothers visited their kinsman, especially given the rude greeting they had exchanged.
