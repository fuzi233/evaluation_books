The aftermath of Madame Beck's jubilant celebration lingered, its three preceding weeks of relaxation, the brief, effervescent twelve hours of revelry, and the subsequent day of torpor gradually giving way to a period of intense focus. Two months of concentrated effort, the last of the academic year, loomed ahead – a time when the true weight of preparation for the impending examinations, which preceded the award of prizes, would be shouldered by professors, mistresses, and pupils alike. The burden of these months was a collective one, yet each individual bore the weight of their own responsibilities, with masters and teachers striving to guide the faltering and nurture the more gifted among their charges. The aim was to create a spectacle, a demonstration of learning that would captivate the public, and all means were deemed fair in pursuit of this goal.

As I navigated the complex landscape of our institution, my attention was fixed on my own task – imbuing the ninety students in my care with a grasp of the English language, a discipline they considered intricate and demanding. The challenge lay not only in conveying the intricacies of the language but also in drilling their tongues to pronounce the elusive, hissing sounds that characterized the speech of the British Isles.

Examination day finally arrived, a moment of great import, anticipated with a mix of anxiety and quiet resolve. Gone were the frivolous trappings of the previous celebration; today, the atmosphere was somber and purposeful. I sensed a peculiar responsibility weighing heavily on me, as if I were shouldering the bulk of the burden among my colleagues. While the other teachers were not expected to examine in the subjects they taught, our esteemed Professor Paul, the autocratic head of our institution, had taken it upon himself to oversee the entire process. He had rejected the assistance of his colleagues, including Madame Beck, who had expressed a desire to conduct the geography examination, a subject she taught with distinction. The staff, both male and female, were relegated to the sidelines, with Professor Paul standing alone on the examiner's platform, a singular figure of authority.

His reluctance to delegate even extended to the English language, a subject he deemed beneath him, which he graciously conceded to me, his tone tinged with a faint note of jealousy. This man, driven by a zealous desire for self-aggrandizement, had a peculiar aversion to sharing the spotlight with others.
