The 21st of June, Miss Rachel’s birthday, dawned cloudy and restless, but by midday the sky cleared bright and fair. Down in the servants’ hall, we marked the happy anniversary as we always did: laying out our small handpicked gifts for Miss Rachel, with me, as the senior servant, delivering the annual formal address. I follow the exact same custom the Queen uses when she opens Parliament — I say nearly identical words, year after year, no surprises. Before I speak, the whole household waits for that speech as if it’s the most thrilling novelty they’ve ever heard. When I’m done, and it turns out to be the same familiar lines from last year, they grumble a little, then brighten up and look forward to a “newer version” 12 months down the line. Easy folk to govern, both in Parliament and the kitchen, that’s the long and short of it.

After breakfast, Mr. Franklin pulled me aside for a private talk about the Moonstone. The time had finally come to retrieve it from the Frizinghall bank and place it directly in Miss Rachel’s hands, as the late Colonel’s will demanded. I couldn’t say what had put our young gentleman out of sorts that morning — whether he’d tried to court his cousin again and got a sharp rebuff, or if his weeks of broken, restless sleep were making his usual contradictory, erratic nature twice as bad. Whatever the cause, he couldn’t settle on a single plan for the diamond, flipping through twenty different conflicting decisions in as many minutes.
Me, I stuck strictly to plain, unshakable facts. Nothing out of the ordinary had happened to give us reason to alarm my lady about the jewel, and no amount of overthinking could erase the legal obligation Mr. Franklin owed to his late uncle to hand the gem over to his cousin. That was my unshifting position, and twist and turn as he might, he was forced to land on the same conclusion in the end. We settled he would ride over to Frizinghall after lunch to collect the diamond, with Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite and his two sisters almost certainly keeping him company on the ride home.
Once that was sorted, Mr. Franklin went back to join Miss Rachel. They spent the rest of the morning and a good chunk of the afternoon on that never-ending project of theirs, decorating the front door. My Penelope stood by to mix their paints as directed, while my lady, as lunch drew near, darted in and out of the room holding a handkerchief pressed tight to her nose — they’d used a shocking amount of Mr. Franklin’s linseed oil that day — trying in vain to drag the two stubborn artists away from their work. It was three o’clock before they peeled off their paint-stained aprons, released Penelope (who was half giddy and lightheaded from the fumes), and scrubbed the paint smudges off their hands and faces.
But they’d finished what they set out to do: the door was fully decorated on Miss Rachel’s very birthday, and they were absurdly proud of it. The griffins, cupids, and flourishes all tangled up in painted flowers were, I’ll admit, very pretty to look at, but there were so many of them, all jumbled and twisted into topsy-turvy poses, that you’d leave with a throbbing headache for hours after you’d finished admiring the handiwork. If I add that Penelope ended that morning’s work by being sick in the back kitchen, I mean no disrespect to the linseed oil. It stops stinking once it dries, after all — and if Art demands those sorts of sacrifices, even if the girl in question is my own daughter, I say Art can have them.
Mr. Franklin snatched a few quick bites from the luncheon table and rode off for Frizinghall. He told my lady he was going to pick up his cousins, of course. Only he and I knew the real errand was to fetch the Moonstone.
As one of the house’s high-ranking ceremonial occasions, I was posted at the sideboard that night to oversee all the serving staff for the birthday dinner, so I had more than enough to keep me busy while Mr. Franklin was gone. I checked every decanter of wine, ran a quick inspection of the footmen and maids assigned to serve the table, then slipped off to rest a minute before the first guests arrived. A puff of my favorite tobacco and a few pages of that well-thumbed book I’ve mentioned before calmed me down completely, body and mind. I was roused from what I’d swear was not a nap, just a pleasant daydream, by the clatter of hooves outside the front door. When I stepped out to greet them, I found a whole little cavalcade: Mr. Franklin, the three Ablewhite cousins, and one old Mr. Ablewhite’s grooms, riding behind them.
Strangely enough, Mr. Godfrey struck me as being just as off his game that morning as Mr. Franklin was. He shook my hand warmly as usual, and gushed politely about how glad he was to see his old friend Betteredge looking so well — but there was a faint, uncharacteristic cloud over his face I couldn’t make heads or tails of. When I asked after his father’s health, he answered very shortly, “Much as usual.”
Luckily, the two Miss Ablewhites were cheerful enough to make up for twenty gloomy moods. They were nearly as tall and broad as their brother: strapping, yellow-haired, rosy-cheeked lasses bursting at the seams with so much health and high spirits the poor horses carrying them were visibly trembling under their weight. When they jumped down from their saddles without waiting for anyone to help them, I swear they bounced on the gravel like they were made of india-rubber. Every word out of their mouths started with a loud, open-mouthed “O!”; every action they took was loud and brash, and they giggled and screamed over the tiniest provocation, whether it was appropriate or not. Bouncers, that’s what I call them.
Under cover of all the noise the two girls were making, I got a chance to mutter a private word to Mr. Franklin in the hall.
“You’ve got the Diamond safe, sir?”
He nodded, and tapped the breast pocket of his coat.
“Seen hide nor hair of those Indian jugglers?”
“Not a glimpse.”
With that, he asked for my lady, and headed straight for the small drawing room. Barely a minute later, the bell rang, summoning Penelope to tell Miss Rachel Mr. Franklin Blake wanted a word with her.
Half an hour later, I was crossing the hall when a sudden burst of shrieks from the small drawing room made me stop dead. I wasn’t even slightly alarmed — I recognized that loud, wide “O!” shriek as the signature noise of the two Bouncers. Still, I stepped in under the pretense of asking for my lady’s instructions about the dinner menu, just to make sure nothing serious had gone wrong.
There was Miss Rachel, standing frozen by the table like she was in a trance, holding that accursed Colonel’s Diamond in her hand. The two Bouncers were kneeling on either side of her, gawking at the gem so hard their eyes were nearly popping out of their heads, screaming every time it caught the light and flashed a new color. There at the far end of the table, Mr. Godfrey was clapping his hands like a small excited child, murmuring soft little exclamations of “Exquisite! Exquisite!” Mr. Franklin was slouched in a chair by the bookcase, tugging hard at his beard, staring anxiously out the window. And there, right by that very window, was the object of his gaze: my lady, holding the official extract of the Colonel’s will in her hand, her back turned to the whole noisy party.
She turned to face me the second I walked in, and I could see that tight Verinder family frown settling over her eyes, that sharp Verinder family temper twitching at the corners of her mouth.
“Come to my room in half an hour,” she said. “I shall have something to discuss with you then.”
With that, she walked straight out. It was plain as day she was stuck on the exact same dilemma Mr. Franklin and I had debated back by the Shivering Sand: was the Moonstone legacy proof she’d treated her cruel late brother unfairly all those years, or was it proof he was far more malicious than she’d ever even imagined? Those were heavy questions for my lady to untangle, while her daughter, who knew nothing at all of the Colonel’s vicious character, stood there holding the strange, cursed birthday gift he’d left her.
Before I could leave the room myself, Miss Rachel — always kind to the old servant who’d been in this house since the day she was born — stopped me.
“Look, Gabriel!” she said, and flashed the diamond right in a thick beam of sunlight streaming through the window.
Lord bless my soul, it was a diamond, sure enough! Nearly as big as a plover’s egg. The light pouring off of it was as soft and bright as the glow of a full harvest moon. When you stared down into the heart of the stone, that deep rich yellow pulled at your eyes until you couldn’t see anything else at all. This tiny little jewel you could hold between your thumb and forefinger felt as vast and unfathomable as the sky itself. We set it down in the full sunlight, then drew all the curtains to block out the light — and there it was, glowing eerily all on its own in the dark, casting that same pale moon-like gleam without a single flame to light it. No wonder Miss Rachel was transfixed. No wonder her cousins were screaming. The diamond had such a hold on me that I blurted out a loud, wide “O!” just as big as any of the Bouncers’ shrieks.
The only one of us who kept his wits about him was Mr. Godfrey. He slipped an arm around each of his sisters’ waists, and looked at the diamond, then back at me, with that pitying, learned expression of his.
“Carbon, Betteredge! Mere carbon, my good friend, after all!”
I suppose he meant to educate a simple old servant. All he did was jog my memory about the dinner I still had to oversee. I hobbled off to my little army of waiting staff downstairs. As I left the room, Mr. Godfrey cooed, “Dear old Betteredge, I have the truest regard for him!” — all while he was still embracing his sisters and casting little adoring sidelong glances at Miss Rachel. That man had a bottomless stock of affection to hand out to everyone, it seemed. Mr. Franklin looked like a grumpy, quiet savage next to him.
At the end of the half hour, I reported to my lady’s room exactly as ordered. The conversation she and I had was mostly the same talk Mr. Franklin and I had shared back by the Shivering Sand — with one difference: I kept all my thoughts about the three Indian jugglers to myself, since nothing had happened to give me good reason to alarm her unnecessarily on that front. When she dismissed me, I could see she’d landed on the darkest possible interpretation of the Colonel’s motives, and was dead set on getting the Moonstone out of her daughter’s hands at the very first opportunity.
On my way back to the servants’ wing, I ran straight into Mr. Franklin. He asked if I’d seen any sign of Miss Rachel. I told him I hadn’t. Then he asked if I could tell him where Mr. Godfrey had gone. I said I didn’t know for certain — but I started to suspect that Mr. Godfrey wasn’t very far away from Miss Rachel at all. Mr. Franklin’s own suspicions had clearly landed on the same idea. He tugged so hard at his beard I thought he’d yank a chunk out, then stormed off to the library and slammed the door so hard the whole hallway rattled, that bang carrying every ounce of his frustrated feelings right along with it.
I wasn’t disturbed again while I prepped for the dinner, right up until it was time to smarten myself up in my formal livery. I’d just pulled on my starched white waistcoat when Penelope popped into my room, pretending to brush the few wispy strands of hair I still had left and straighten my white cravat. My girl was bubbling over with excitement, and I could tell she had some secret news she was dying to share. She kissed the top of my bald head, and whispered right in my ear, “I’ve got news for you, father! Miss Rachel turned him down.”
“Who’s ‘him’?” I asked.
“The ladies’ committee man, father,” Penelope huffed. “That sneaky, sly thing! I hate him for trying to shove Mr. Franklin out of the way and win her for himself!”
If I’d had enough breath in my lungs I’d have certainly scolded her for speaking so disrespectfully of such a well-known, upstanding philanthropic gentleman. But my daughter happened to be yanking at my cravat at that exact moment, her whole body thrumming with irritation, and I was half strangled before I could get a single word out.
“I saw him drag her away alone into the rose garden,” Penelope went on, still yanking at my hairbrush so hard my scalp was stinging. “I hid behind the holly bush to watch how they came back. They walked in arm-in-arm, both laughing like there was nothing in the world to worry about. They came out walking completely separate, both as grave as grave can be, staring straight away from each other — there was no mistaking what had just happened. I’ve never been so happy in my life, father! There’s at least one woman in the whole world who can resist Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite, and if I was a lady I’d be the second one!”
Just as I was opening my mouth to protest that nonsense, the crunch of carriage wheels on the gravel outside hit my ears. The very first of the dinner guests had arrived. Penelope ran off like a shot. I slipped my coat on, checked myself in the mirror: my head was bright red as a boiled lobster, but apart from that I was every bit as neat and properly dressed for the evening’s ceremonies as any butler could hope to be. I stepped into the hall just in time to throw open the front doors and announce our first two guests. You needn’t care much about them, after all. They were only the philanthropist’s father and mother — Mr. and Mrs. Ablewhite.
