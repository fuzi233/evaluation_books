London had taken its annual holiday on Epsom Downs—the great sacrament of English idleness, where dukes, draymen, debutantes, debtors, and dogs all agree, for one day, to breathe the same chalky air and call it liberty. The very turf seemed to hum, not with pastoral bees, but with the multitudinous racket of civilisation at play: wheels crunching gravel, whips cracking like pistol-shots, brass bands braying, hawkers yelping, corks popping, and a perpetual storm of “Hullo!” that battered the ear as factory-hammers do—only here the noise was considered “fine sport,” and paid for accordingly.

In a handsome open carriage—well horsed, well varnished, and well conscious of it—sat Mrs. Bungay of Paternoster Row, blazing in ribbons and new silk like an altar dressed for a feast-day. Beside her, modest Mrs. Shandon wore that air of patient gentility which can endure both sun and patronage without melting. Bungay, having fortified himself with a luncheon that might have served a small parish, was at that moment madly attacking the “Sticks” hard by—shying, pitching, and perspiring until his bald crown shone as if it had been glazed for publication. Shandon wandered among the beer-stands and gipsies with that shambling gait which seems always to be looking for a friend and finding a creditor. Finucane hovered about the ladies like a lively mosquito that had learned manners.

Up came Mr. Archer, bowing with the air of a man who had known everybody famous since Adam first took briefs.

“Yonder’s the Prime Minister,” Archer said, arranging the course with his finger as if Epsom were a map he had corrected. “His lordship just advised me to back Borax; but I confess I like Munmeer’s action better. And there—look in the Grand Stand—there sits the Chinese Ambassador with the Mandarins of his suite. Fou-choo-foo—my most intimate friend—brought me letters from the Governor-General of India. I was very kind to him for some time. His chopsticks were laid at my table whenever he chose to dine.”

Mrs. Bungay’s eyes grew large as sovereigns.

“But, would you believe it,” Archer continued, dropping his voice with the relish of a man opening a tureen, “he brought his own cook; and one day, when I was out, and the Ambassador was with Mrs. Archer in our garden eating gooseberries—of which the Chinese are passionately fond—the beast of a cook, seeing my wife’s dear little Blenheim spaniel (a present from the Duke of Marlborough himself—whose ancestor’s life Mrs. Archer’s great-great-grandfather saved at Malplaquet), seized the poor little devil, cut his throat, skinned him, and served him up—stuffed with forced-meat—in the second course.”

“Law!” said Mrs. Bungay, as if the syllable were a lid hastily clapped on a saucepan of horror.

“You may fancy my wife’s agony!” Archer cried, warming to his kitchen-tragedy. “The cook came screaming upstairs and told us she’d found poor Fido’s skin in the area—just after we’d all tasted the dish. Mrs. Archer never would speak to the Ambassador again—never; and upon my word he has never been to dine with us since. The Lord Mayor, who did me the honour to dine, liked the dish very much; and, eaten with green peas, it tastes rather like duck.”

“You don’t say so, now!” exclaimed Mrs. Bungay, who loved a scandal as much as she loved a sauce—provided both were rich.

“And observe that lady in blue seated by his Excellency,” Archer went on. “That is Lady Flamingo. They say she is going to marry him and return to Pekin. She is squeezing her feet down on purpose. But she’ll only cripple herself. My wife has the smallest foot in England—wears a child’s shoe; but what is that to a Chinese lady’s foot, Mrs. Bungay?”

At this moment Mrs. Bungay, whose curiosity moved swiftly from ambassadors to annuities, leaned forward.

“Who is that carriage as Mr. Pendennis is with, Mr. Archer? He and Mr. Warrington was here jest now. He’s ’aughty in his manners, that Mr. Pendennis, and well he may be, for I’m told he keeps tip-top company. ’As he ’ad a large fortune left him, Mr. Archer? He’s in black still, I see.”

“Eighteen hundred a year in land,” Archer said promptly, as if reading from a ledger in his head, “and twenty-two thousand five hundred in the Three-and-a-half per Cents. That’s about it.”

“Law! why, you know everything, Mr. A.!”

“I happen to know, because I was called in about poor Mrs. Pendennis’s will,” Archer replied. “Pendennis’s uncle, the Major, seldom does anything without me; and as he is likely to be extravagant, we’ve tied up the property, so that he can’t make ducks and drakes with it.—How do you do, my lord?—Do you know that gentleman, ladies? You have read his speeches in the House; it is Lord Rochester.”

“Lord Fiddlestick,” sang out Finucane from the box. “Sure it’s Tom Staples of the Morning Advertiser, Archer.”

“Is it?” Archer said, simply. “Well, I’m very short-sighted, and upon my word I thought it was Rochester.”

Then he nodded at a man with a double opera-glass, and at another tall figure, distributing titles as a cook ladles gravy: “That’s Lord John; and the tall man—don’t you know him?—is Sir James.”

“You know ’em because you see ’em in the House,” growled Finucane.

“I know them because they are kind enough to allow me to call them my most intimate friends,” Archer returned, without a blush. “Look at the Duke of Hampshire—pattern of a fine old English gentleman! Never misses the Derby. ‘Archer,’ he said to me only yesterday, ‘I have been at sixty-five Derbies! first appeared on the field on a piebald pony when I was seven, with my father, the Prince of Wales, and Colonel Hanger; missing only two—one with the measles at Eton, and one in the Waterloo year, when I was with my friend Wellington in Flanders.’”

“And who is that yellow carriage with the pink and yallow parasols, that Mr. Pendennis is talking to, and ever so many gentlemen?” asked Mrs. Bungay, scenting fresh history as a cat scents cream.

“That is Lady Clavering of Clavering Park—next estate to my friend Pendennis. That’s the young son and heir on the box; he’s awfully tipsy, the little scamp! And the young lady is Miss Amory—Lady Clavering’s daughter by a first marriage—uncommonly sweet upon my friend Pendennis; but I’ve reason to think he has his heart fixed elsewhere. You have heard of young Mr. Foker—the great brewer, Foker, you know—he was going to hang himself for Miss Amory who refused him, but was cut down just in time by his valet, and is now abroad under a keeper.”

“How happy that young fellow is!” sighed Mrs. Bungay, who always thought tragedy improved a gentleman’s position. “Who’d have thought, when he came so quiet and demure to dine with us, three or four years ago, he would turn out such a grand character! Why, I saw his name at Court t’other day, and presented by the Marquis of Steyne and all; and in every party of the nobility his name’s down as sure as a gun.”

“I introduced him a good deal when he first came up to town,” Archer said, “and his uncle, Major Pendennis, did the rest. Hallo! There’s Cobden here, of all men in the world! I must go and speak to him. Good-bye, Mrs. Bungay. Good morning, Mrs. Shandon.”

And off he went, leaving behind him a faint scent of self-importance, as of truffles shaved over nothing.

An hour earlier, in another quarter of the Downs, a different England was shouting itself hoarse from the battered roof of an old stage-coach—Wheeler’s drag from the Harlequin’s Head—where shabby raffs stamped and hallooed as the Derby swept by like a gust of living colour. The air there was thick with dust, beer-fumes, hot bread, cheap cigar smoke, and that peculiar metallic tang which always rises from a crowd that has been yelling.

As the horses flashed, each “choice spirit” bellowed what he hoped to be the winner, for hope is a beverage Englishmen drink more greedily than ale.

“The Cornet!”
“It’s Muffineer!”
“Blue sleeves!”
“Yallow cap! yallow cap! yallow cap!”

Then the signal fluttered out; Podasokus had won; and one gentleman on the roof sprang up as if winged by fortune.

“Hurray, hurray!” he roared, coming down again so heavily that the boards cracked under his joy. “Podasokus is the horse! Supper for ten, Wheeler, my boy. Ask you all round, of course, and damn the expense.”

“Thank you—congratulate you, Colonel—sup with you with pleasure,” cried the others, smiling like knives; and whispered with affectionate vigilance, “The Colonel stands to win fifteen hundred, and he got the odds from a good man too.”

Immediately each began to eye his neighbour as if friendship were a kind of pickpocketing; for a man who has won money at Epsom must be guarded like a pie cooling on a windowsill—everyone passing feels entitled to a slice.

At yet another part of the course—more modest, and in truth more shabby, though with a certain domestic bravery about it—stood cab No. 2002, lately hired from the Strand. A gentleman and two ladies had established themselves on and about it, turning the cab into a little travelling parlour with luncheon. The younger lady, perched on the box, looked so fresh, so rosy, and so prettily alive that dandies strolling from the grand carriages drifted toward her as moths drift toward a candle—only these moths were scented, and the candle wore a straw bonnet with a pink ribbon.

The party’s meal was a very English poem written in grease and foam: lobster salad—its vinegar and pepper pricking the nose; pale bitter ale, rising in a froth that smelled of warmed grain; and for the elder lady, brandy-and-water that deepened her complexion to match the very shell of the lobster she cracked and devoured with cheerful resolution. Every bite had its sound: the snap of shell, the crunch of a pickle, the gurgle of ale poured and swallowed, the ceaseless surrounding roar of the Downs like surf against a sea-wall.

Their escort—Mr. Sam—had been inexhaustible from the very start, flinging “chaff” from the moving cab as if it were confetti. He had shouted jokes into drags full of solemn guardsmen; he had saluted donkey-chaises and dustmen; he had peppered the roadside with volleys of wit into school windows, maidservants’ casements, nurses’ arms, babies’ faces, dissenting maiden ladies’ disapproving noses. The cab had filled with spoils from the Sticks: pincushions, wooden apples, backy-boxes, Jack-in-the-boxes, little soldiers—small, cheap objects that nonetheless clinked with the music of a holiday won.

He had even hauled up a gipsy with a tawny child to tell fortunes; and the only cloud over the party’s sunshine came when the gipsy informed the young lady that she must beware a fair man false to her; that she had had a bad illness; and that she would find a man who would prove true. The words, no doubt, had been ladled out to a hundred carriages like the same thin soup; yet they fell into this cab with a particular plop.

The girl coloured; her mother and Mr. Sam exchanged looks of quick intelligence, like cooks who know a flavour before the taster speaks.

Now, making his way alone through the crush—observing, as was his habit, the human kitchen of Epsom, where vanity and appetite were stirred in the same pot—Arthur Pendennis came suddenly upon cab No. 2002 and the little group around it.

The moment the young lady on the box saw him, she started and turned pale. Her mother reddened as if the brandy had leapt straight to her cheeks. The gay Mr. Sam, so late triumphant, took on a fierce suspicious look, and his eyes travelled savagely from Fanny Bolton—yes, it was she—to Pendennis advancing.

Arthur, too, looked dark upon perceiving Mr. Samuel Huxter in company with his old acquaintances. His suspicion was that kind of morality which is half alarm and half self-importance; like a person who, hearing a strange noise in the pantry, imagines at once a thief—and feels virtuously awake for having heard it.

Fanny’s heart began to patter. Huxter’s fists, sunk in the pockets of his paletot, clenched themselves as if they were tools ready for surgery of a coarser kind. Mrs. Bolton began to talk with all her might, and with that wondrous volubility which can plaster over any crack in the wall.

“Lor! I’m so ’appy to see you, Mr. Pendennis! and how well you do look! and we was talking of you only jest before, wasn’t we, Fanny? And if this is the famous Epsom races they make such a talk about, I don’t care, for my part, if I never sees ’em again. And how is Major Pendennis? and that kind Mr. Warrington, as brought your kindness to Fanny? I never shall forget it—never. And Mr. Warrington is so tall, he almost broke his ’ead against our lodge door. You recollect him a-knocking of his head—don’t you, Fanny?”

While her mother chattered, what a whole pantry of memories must have burst open in Fanny’s mind: dear times and dreary ones; sad struggles; lonely griefs; and then those shy consolations that come after shame, like cold tea offered when dinner is spoiled. There stood the man she had been ready to die for ten months before—dandified now, supercilious, in mourning black as if grief were a fashion he wore correctly: black crape on his white hat, jet buttons in his shirt-front, a pink in his coat (given, no doubt, by some other hand), lavender gloves sewn with black, the neatest cane, the shiniest boot. And there was Sam Huxter beside him—no gloves, great Blucher boots, tobacco on his breath, and the general air of a fellow who had met both the road and the cigar too intimately. How could the eye not measure? How could the heart not compare?

Fanny, after taking Pendennis’s offered hand with a little twittering pressure, managed at last to say, in a voice that shook like a spoon in a cup:

“This is Mr. Samuel Huxter whom you knew formerly, I believe, sir; Mr. Samuel, you know you knew Mr. Pendennis formerly—and—and, will you take a little refreshment?”

Those few tremulous words did more than all Mrs. Bolton’s speeches: they told Arthur, plainly enough, that there was nothing secret here to fear—no guilty ambush, no melodrama of betrayal. A load slid from his mind; perhaps another from his heart. The frown melted off his face, and his good-nature came out like sunshine after a passing shower.

“I am very thirsty,” he said, “and I will be glad to drink your health, Fanny; and I hope Mr. Huxter will pardon me for having been very rude the last time we met—when I was so ill and out of spirits that indeed I scarcely knew what I said.”

And he held out the lavender-coloured glove in amity.

The dirty fist in Huxter’s pocket had to unarm itself and come forth. The young surgeon felt, as he laid it in Pendennis’s hand, how hot and how black his own was; it left marks upon the glove—he saw them. A part of him longed to snatch his hand back, to clench it, to dash it into that easy smiling face, and see—here, on the Downs, with Fanny and all England looking on—which man was the better. But the moment passed; manners, those invisible policemen of society, blew their whistle.

Arthur took a glass—content to drink after the ladies—filled it with frothing lukewarm beer, and declared it delicious. He drank cordially to the health of the party, as if the bitter, warmed by sun and crowd, were a nectar of reconciliation.

While he spoke on in an engaging way, a young lady in a shot dove-coloured dress passed by him, leaning on the arm of a stalwart gentleman with a military moustache. Her parasol was white lined with pink; her boots the prettiest dove-colour that ever stepped over dust. She clenched a tiny fist and gave Pendennis a mischievous side-look as she went; her moustachioed companion burst into a jolly laugh, and lifted his hat to the ladies on the cab.

You should have seen Fanny Bolton’s eyes follow that dove-coloured figure—as if she were tasting a new dish and trying to guess every ingredient.

“What a beautiful creature!” Fanny said. “What a lovely dress! Did you remark, Mr. Sam, such little, little hands?”

“It was Capting Strong,” said Mrs. Bolton, craning. “And who was the young woman, I wonder?”

“A neighbour of mine in the country—Miss Amory,” Arthur said. “Lady Clavering’s daughter. You’ve seen Sir Francis often in Shepherd’s Inn, Mrs. Bolton.”

And with that small information, Fanny’s mind—quick as a cook improvising a feast out of scraps—built a whole romance in three volumes: love, faithlessness, splendid marriage at St. George’s, Hanover Square, broken-hearted maid; and Mr. Sam Huxter was not the hero of it—poor Sam, who by now had produced an exceedingly rank Cuba cigar and was smoking it under Fanny’s little nose, as if tobacco could fog away a rival.

After that “confounded prig Pendennis” (as Sam named him in the silent court of his own jealousy) joined and left the party, the day altered. The sun seemed less bright, the sky less blue; the Sticks had no attraction; the bitter beer turned hot and undrinkable. The world had changed its seasoning. Sam had peas and a tin pea-shooter in his pocket for the homeward route, but forgot them until another wag on the return fired a volley into Sam’s sad face; upon which salute, after a few oaths of surprised profanity, he burst into a savage and sardonic laugh.

Fanny, meanwhile, was charming all the way home. She coaxed, and snuggled, and smiled; she laughed pretty laughs and admired everything. She took out the darling little Jack-in-the-boxes and was so obliged to Sam—turning the cheap toys into treasures by sheer warmth of manner, as some people can make even a crust seem like cake.

When they reached home, and Mr. Huxter, still dark of brow, was taking a frigid leave, she burst into tears and called him a naughty unkind thing.

Then jealousy, which had hardened him, melted in a moment. He caught her in his arms, swore she was an angel and he a jealous brute; owned he was unworthy of her and had no right to hate Pendennis; and asked her—implored her—to say once more that she—

That she what? The end of the question and Fanny’s answer were spoken by lips so near together that no bystander could catch the words. Mrs. Bolton only said, brisk as a matron breaking up a scullery flirtation, “Come, come, Mr. H.—no nonsense, if you please; and I think you’ve acted like a wicked wretch, and been most uncommon cruel to Fanny, that I do.”

When Arthur left cab No. 2002, he went up the hill to pay his respects to the yellow carriage with the pink and yellow parasols—where the dove-coloured author of Mes Larmes had returned to her mamma’s side. There sat Lady Clavering, taking in homage as naturally as she took in air. Indefatigable old Major Pendennis waited upon her, installed in the back seat as if born to be an appendage to quality. The box was in possession of young Hopeful—Lady Clavering’s heir—under the care of Captain Strong, who looked as if he could manage at once a horse, a boy, and a dozen fools.

Around the carriage clustered a knot of dandies and men of a certain fashion—military bucks, young rakes of public offices, fellows who prided themselves on being “men’s men” rather than ladies’—and they offered Miss Amory bets, banter, and that species of knowing talk which calls itself wit and is often only insolence. They pointed out “who was on the course,” and the “who” was not always a name a young lady should be invited to taste.

Pen had to push his way through these gentlemen to come near Miss Blanche Amory, who beckoned him with little signals as pretty and commanding as the flick of a fan.

“Je l’ai vue,” she said. “Elle a de bien beaux yeux; vous êtes un monstre!”

“Why monster?” Pen laughed. “Honi soit qui mal y pense. My young friend yonder is as well protected as any young lady in Christendom. She has her mamma on one side, her prétendu on the other. Could any harm happen to a girl between those two?”

“One does not know what may or may not arrive,” Miss Blanche said lightly, in French, “when a girl has the mind, and when she is pursued by a wicked monster like you. Figure to yourself, Major, that I come to find Monsieur, your nephew, near to a cab, by two ladies, and a man—oh, such a man!—and who ate lobsters, and who laughed, who laughed!”

“It did not strike me that the man laughed,” Pen said; “and as for lobsters, I thought he would have liked to eat me after the lobsters. He shook hands with me and gripped me so that he bruised my glove black-and-blue. He is a young surgeon. He comes from Clavering. Don’t you remember the gilt pestle and mortar in High Street?”

“If he attends you when you are sick,” Miss Amory continued, “he will kill you. He will serve you right; for you are a monster.”

The perpetual recurrence of the word jarred upon Pen as a too-sweet syrup cloys the palate. She spoke of such matters too lightly. If he had been the monster she named, she would have received him just the same—with the same pretty laughter, the same careless flourish of words borrowed from another tongue, as if French made mischief less mischief by giving it a foreign label. His face fell.

“This is not,” he thought, “the way an English lady should speak or think. Laura would not speak in that way, thank God.”

“Of what are you thinking? Are you going to bouder me at present?” Blanche asked. “Major, scold your méchant nephew. He does not amuse me at all. He is as bête as Captain Crackenbury.”

“What are you saying about me, Miss Amory?” grinned Captain Crackenbury, a guardsman with the confidence of a man who has never been quiet a moment in his life. “If it’s anything good, say it in English, for I don’t understand French when it’s spoke so devilish quick.”

“It ain’t anything good, Crack,” said Crackenbury’s companion, Captain Clinker. “Let’s come away, and don’t spoil sport. They say Pendennis is sweet upon her.”

“I’m told he’s a devilish clever fellow,” sighed Crackenbury, with honest envy. “Lady Violet Lebas says he’s a devilish clever fellow. He wrote a work, or a poem, or something; and he writes those devilish clever things in the—in the papers, you know. Dammy, I wish I was a clever fellow, Clinker.”

“That’s past wishing for, Crack, my boy,” Clinker said. “I can’t write a good book, but I think I can make a pretty good one on the Derby. What a flat Clavering is! And the Begum! I like that old Begum. She’s worth ten of her daughter. How pleased the old girl was at winning the lottery!”

“Clavering’s safe to pay up, ain’t he?” asked Crackenbury.

“I hope so,” said his friend; and the two drifted off into the roaring, clinking, shouting tide toward the Sticks—where men spent money like water, and made water of their memories by the bucketful of beer.
