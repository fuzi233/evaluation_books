The sky above was a dull, slate-grey, yet this offered little solace to the Piazza del Duomo, which was already draped in the festive hues of a holiday. Blue canopies stretched overhead, punctuated by constellations of yellow lilies and golden coats of arms. Banners unfurled like sheaves of wheat at the corners of the Baptistery, though the grand marble steps of the cathedral remained bare; the stone was currently worn smooth by the countless, ordinary feet of the populace. It was the hour for Advent sermons, a solemn duty that could not be neglected even amidst the carnival spirit that had flushed the streets with color. Yet, not everyone hurried toward the altar. Citizens of every rank moved with the brisk urgency of those burdened with errands, while clusters of talkers scattered across the square debated loudly. Some appeared eager to miss the sermon entirely, their expressions betraying no leisurely enjoyment of the opening festivities. Instead, many engaged in intense, whispered discussions, constantly scanning the horizon for newcomers with wary glances.

At the edge of the square, where the artificial rainbow light of the celebration faded into the somber grey of the morning falling upon the stone houses of Via de' Cerretani, stood a stark contrast to the jubilation within. A dense cluster of workers gathered there, their clothes stained with labor, most bearing tools that doubled as weapons. Their bare, muscular arms and soiled garments highlighted the sharp divide between them and the bright spectacle of the Piazza. They listened intently to Ser Cioni, a notary whose biting wit had once drawn scorn in the market two and a half years prior. Now, however, speaking with the complacent humor of a man whose faction held sway, he addressed the crowd with confidence.

"Never speak to me," Ser Cioni declared in his incisive voice, "of bloodthirsty Swiss or fierce French infantry. They belong in mountain passes, not our streets. History shows us that peasants have shattered the finest armies of our condottieri simply by trapping them between steep cliffs. Tell me, Florentines, what reason have you to fear an army marching through your own thoroughfares?"

"That is true, Ser Cioni," replied a man whose hands were permanently stained crimson by dye, resembling fresh blood, with a small hatchet tucked into his belt. "And those French cavaliers who paraded in their tight doublets recently tasted our hospitality. While I carried my cloth near Ognissanti, I saw my masters strutting by, looking as if they considered the homes of the Vespuccis and Aglias too humble for them. They eyed us Florentines like proud roosters, pitying our inability to strut properly. 'Yes, my fine *Galli*,' I told them, 'stick out your stomachs; I have a meat-axe in my belt that will slide right inside you.' Then, suddenly, the great bell rang—the *vacca muglia*—and chaos erupted. No matter what happened, I dropped my cloth, grabbed my axe, and chased after the cavaliers toward Vigna Nuova. When Guccio caught up, I asked, 'What is it?' He said, 'I think it's the Medici returning.' *Bembè!* I expected no less! We raised a barricade, trapping the Frenchmen. Our *Ciompi* swarmed forward, and one wields a great scythe that clipped the plumes from a nobleman's helmet. True enough! The women rained stones to scare them. Alas, Piero de' Medici never returned. What a pity; we would have stripped him of both legs and wings before letting him leave."

"Well spoken, Oddo," chimed in a young butcher, knife at his hip. "Piero won't return anytime soon. He looked terrified as a hunted chicken when we mobbed him in the piazza. He is a coward; otherwise, he would have made a proper stand with his horsemen. But we shall swallow no more Medici, regardless of what the French King demands."

"But I dislike those French cannons," grumbled Goro, a man notably rotund despite two years of accumulated grievances. "May Saint John protect us! If Messer Domeneddio truly cares for us as Brother Cioni claims, why send the French to Naples instead?"

"Aye, Goro," the dyer agreed. "That is a question worth pondering. You are not quite the fool I thought. Why couldn't they have gone via Bologna? Or even Arezzo? We wouldn't have minded them heading to Arezzo."

"Fools!" Ser Cioni began, attempting to rally them. "It will be for the glory of Florence—"

His speech was cut short by a sudden cry: "Look there!" Several voices shouted in unison as attention shifted to a party approaching from Via de' Cerretani.

"It is Lorenzo Tornabuoni and one of the French nobles residing with him," Ser Cioni muttered, dripping with disdain. "He feigns satisfaction—that deep, calculating Tornabuoni—but he is a Medicean at heart. Beware."

The approaching group was indeed striking. Beside the distinguished Lorenzo Tornabuoni walked a French nobleman dressed in splendor, his white linen elaborate and embroidered with gold. Two other Florentines of high birth, attired for the procession, accompanied them. To the left of the Frenchman stood a figure that rivaled any brocade in brilliance: a man clad entirely in black, though destined soon to wear a red mantle as Latin Secretary to the Ten. This was Tito Melema.

Tito had proven invaluable in dealings with the French guests, leveraging his knowledge of Southern Italy and his fluency in French, honed during his youth and practiced frequently at the French camp in Signa. Fortune smiled upon him; he moved with his characteristic graceful ease, smiling and explaining, seemingly unaware of the subtle shift occurring within him. It was the mark of maturity leaving moral youthfulness behind—the conscious adoption of a role in life. His features remained soft, his eyes clear, yet something intangible had vanished, much like the fading light of dawn.

The Frenchman sought final instructions on ceremony before returning to Signa, pausing now to survey the Piazza del Duomo where the royal procession would halt for prayer. As the party entered the square, they drew every eye, though the gaze was mixed with suspicion rather than pure admiration. Whispers flew regarding the Frenchman's hoof-shaped shoes—a delicate insult disguised as praise for royal excess—and snarls directed at "Mediceans" were carefully suppressed. Lorenzo Tornabuoni, skilled in masking annoyance, maintained his composure, while Tito, naturally inclined to disarm hostility with good humor, felt the detached perspective of an outsider observing local passions.

Stopping to gain a clear view of the cathedral, the group noticed the festoons and heraldic devices hanging over the central door. Tornabuoni signaled for Piero di Cosimo, who was typically loitering outside Nello's shop. An animated discussion ensued, becoming delightfully absurd as the Frenchman marveled at Piero's pungent observations, which Tito translated word-for-word. Even hostile onlookers grew curious, their faces twisting into expressions of half-amusement, half-humiliation as they realized they were excluded from the joke spreading like infection. For Tito, it was a moment of pure delight; as the sole interpreter capable of rendering the scene so amusing, he reveled in the approving glances cast his way, basking in the warmth of public favor.

As the rainbow light bathed the laughing group, the serious churchgoers had already slipped inside. Meanwhile, in the shadowed periphery of the unadorned streets, a darker reality unfolded. Newcomers arrived, devoid of linen and silk, their spirits far from merry. Here too, the French attire and strange footwear stood out, but they were surrounded by an increasingly large crowd of non-admiring Florentines. Leading this throng were three men in ragged clothing, their hands bound tightly by cords. Ropes encircled their bodies, allowing their captors to throttle them instantly should rebellion occur. Holding these ropes were French soldiers, who used broken Italian phrases and tugs on the cords to provoke pleas for mercy.

Two of the prisoners complied, holding out their bound hands to every passerby in piteous tones: "For the love of God and the Holy Madonna, give us something for our ransom! We are Tuscans; we were captured in Lunigiana."

The third man, however, remained stubbornly silent despite the stinging blows of the knotted cord. He differed vastly from his companions. Young and hardy, the others resembled sturdy vagrants in their scant attire. This man, conversely, was well past old age, likely fifty-five or older. His beard, grown long in neglect, and the thick, straight hair framing his bald head were nearly white. Though emaciated, his thickset frame remained firm and upright, radiating an energy that defied his years. This vitality shone in his dark eyes and strong brows, which burned with a strangely isolated intensity against his yellow, bloodless, deeply wrinkled face and lank grey hair. Yet, there was a fitfulness in his gaze; after flashing with fierce scrutiny at windows and faces, his eyes would drift away with a lost, wandering look. His lips stayed motionless, his hands held resolutely down. He refused to beg.

The sight provoked growing exasperation among the Florentines. Initially, many gave money—some reflexively responding to appeals in God's name, others driven by an unquestioning awe of the French soldiery, fueled by tales of their cruelty and relied upon as a shield against further insolence.
