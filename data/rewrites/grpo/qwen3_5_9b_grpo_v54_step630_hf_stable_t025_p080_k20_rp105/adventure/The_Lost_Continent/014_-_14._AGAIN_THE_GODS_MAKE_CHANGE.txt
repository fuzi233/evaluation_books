The battle was won not by strategy, but by the sheer momentum of our charge. With a handful of disciplined fighters, I had driven back a horde of rebels fifteen times our number. Their initial confidence, born of a false belief that our mining operation had failed, shattered instantly when they realized no one emerged from the breach. Panic, a contagious and malignant disease, swept through their ragged ranks. Without leaders, without training, and armed with nothing but desperation, they became a chaotic mass. We did not defend; we hunted. Flanking, charging, and hacking our way through their center, we kept them in a perpetual state of terror. Soon, the outer lines fled toward the scorched fields, dragging the inner packs with them. The siege was broken. These Atlanteans, though fierce and capable of standing against any trained phalanx in open combat, were undone by the collapse of their morale.

As we pursued the fleeing remnants, the heat of the day wore us down. We halted among the ruins of half-collapsed houses to make a night of it. The fires crackled, cooking the meat of a few frightened horses that had stumbled into our lines. Officers spoke of tactics with the gusto of soldiers, their voices rising over the snores of the weary. But I felt a feverish dread of solitude, a fear that my thoughts would consume me if left unchecked. Just as my men began to drift into unconsciousness, a sentry approached. "My lord," he reported, "a woman attempts to enter the bivouac."

"Does she speak?" I asked.

"She refuses. She demands only to see you."

I rose, dismissing the sentry's caution. "Bring her to the fire." On second thought, remembering that a spy would not seek such a crude meeting place unless she held vital intelligence, I intercepted her before she reached the safety of the camp.

She was wrapped in heavy cloaks, her identity obscured, yet to anyone who knew Nais, the silhouette was unmistakably that of Ylga, her twin. I ordered her released and led her away from the camp's prying ears.

"You know me, then?" she whispered, her voice bitter. "There must be something warmer than duty between us, Deucalion, if you could guess who walked beneath these veils."

"I let that pass," I replied. "What is your errand?"

"Aye," she spat, "my reward. Your concern is only for the message, not the carrier. Well, good my lord, you are no longer the husband of the dainty Phorenice."

"This is news."

"And true. She spurns you, divorces you, and now she seeks vengeance. She knows you shared your priest's magic with Nais, keeping her in a stone coffin until you choose to wake her. Her enlightenment came from a prisoner in a neighboring cell who overheard your vows. That fool tried to bargain for his life, but Phorenice had him executed for his tardiness. Now, she intends to mutilate you—lop off your hands and feet—and drive you into the wild lands to die a slow death of hunger and beasts, all while mocking you with the knowledge that she holds Nais captive."

"No," I said firmly. "I will not be mutilated and left to starve."

"Then you must flee," Ylga urged. "I have arranged passage with Tob, the captain of Tatho's navy. He sails tonight for unknown parts, seeking to found a kingdom. He is a rough man, smelling of tar and honesty, but he needs a leader. He said you were the recruit he wanted. Go with him. It is your only chance."

We moved through the darkness, leaving the camp behind. The journey was arduous, traversing soggy ground and thorny forests. At one point, we crossed a stream of scalding water from a boil-spring. "Are you sure of your footing?" I asked, offering my hand.

"No," she replied, taking my grip. "I am not."

Together, we navigated the treacherous crossing. Once across, she slipped her hand away, sighing softly. We walked in silence for hours until we reached the estuary where Atlantis stood. Below us, the great ship anchored in the shadows waited. Tob was there, watching intently.

We boarded the vessel, and Tob greeted us with a mix of respect and practicality. He explained his plans: a voyage to the Tin Islands, a land rich in resources but distant from the reach of the city. He had gathered a crew of fifty men and eight women, escaping the tightening grip of the port authorities. As we prepared to depart, the tension of the night settled over us.

"I will never forget what you have done," I told Ylga as we stood on the deck. "Should the High Gods allow me to return to power, you shall know my gratitude."

"I do not wish to return," she said, her eyes hard. "I am sick of this life."

"But you have wealth, a palace, and status," I argued. "Phorenice will not disturb you."

"Oh, I could be a fan-girl tomorrow if I chose," she snapped. "But I do not want to go back. I can cook, shoot, and sew skins. Do not presume I am useless because you despise fine clothes."

"I have learned all I care to know about one woman," I admitted, the memory of Nais weighing heavy on my heart. "Because of her, I could not bring you."

"Kick my pride," she whispered bitterly. "I knew I was second place. Yet, no one but a boor would remind me."

"I must go alone," I said. "I suppose I shall carry only your hate."

"That is the most humiliating thing," she confessed, her voice trembling. "I cannot hate you. I seem to grow fonder of you. If I thought I could save you here, I would not have pointed you to Tob. But he waits."

Suddenly, she flung her arms around my neck and kissed me with a fierce, reckless passion. "There, that is for goodbye, dear. You see, I am reckless. I care not what I do now, knowing you cannot despise me more than you have."

She ran back into the trees, disappearing into the darkness. I watched her go, then turned to the ship. Tob signaled the crew to haul up the anchor.

"Greeting, my lord," Tob said, his tone shifting to business. "We have little force and a slow ship. The sooner we are beyond the city's range, the safer my family is. This vessel is old, rotting from the Egypt trade, but she is all I have."

"We have two months' provisions," I noted. "We will have to hunt along the coast."

"Yes," Tob agreed. "When winter comes, we will make camp and grow grain. But listen, my lord. I have a destination in mind. Islands to the north of Europe, separated by a strait. I call them the Tin Islands. They are untouched, rich in ore, and free of the old gods' influence. I intend to build a kingdom there."

"With you as king?" I asked.

"Well, I have figured it out as such," Tob grinned, though his eyes held a hint of deference. "But I know when I meet my better. I am content to serve under Deucalion. Though, I must say, a wife would have been wise to bring. The savages there are sturdy but brutish, and their customs... well, one would need a blunted taste to wish to father their children."

"I am still husband to Phorenice," I reminded him.

"The Gods give you joy of her," Tob chuckled. "But a mariner's creed says wedlock does not hold across the seas. Let that rest. The Tin Islands will delight you. Few beasts, plenty of game. It will be a desirable kingdom."

As dawn broke, the burning mountains of Atlantis loomed behind us. The crew cast fearful glances backward, expecting the navy of Phorenice to surge forth. They swore they would fight to the death rather than submit. But the tide turned, and we drifted away, the city fading into the mist.

On the third day, we stopped at a river mouth to resupply. We speared fish and hunted a young river-horse, curing the meat for the journey ahead. Tob pressed me again to join him as king, but I refused steadfastly.

"Tob," I said, standing alone on the bank as his ship prepared to leave, "I am a poor, defenseless man. What if I do not voyage to the Tin Islands? What if I stay here?"

He brightened. "You are weak, my lord. But tell me your meaning."

"It is not my wish to leave Atlantis," I said. "Put me down on any shore facing Europe, and I will commend you to the Gods. I have no money, but I offer my counsel."

Tob hesitated. The sound of kingship appealed to him, but he knew I was no sailor. "The part of Atlantis you speak of is the Dangerous Lands," he warned. "A man must be a salamander or learned in magic to survive there."

"We of the Priests' Clan have education," I countered. "My fate remains under the control of the High Gods."

He muttered something about preferring his own fate to his own right arm, but the temptation of rule was too strong. On the third day, he returned to the bank, but this time, his resolve was absolute.

"Tob," I pleaded as he stood by the ship, "this must not be."

The ship drifted away with the current, sails hoisting. Tob shouted over the roar of the water. "Aye, but it shall be! I am a strong man, and I have ordered it so. If you want the name of our Hero, wear it on yourself. Deucalion shall be God for our children!"

"This is blasphemy!" I cried. "Have a care, fool, or this impiety will sink you!"

"We will risk it!" he bellowed back. "Regard! Here is thy last horn of wine. I pour this as a libation to Deucalion, great lord that is to-day, Hero that shall be to-morrow, God that will be in time to come!"

The crew joined the acclaim, their voices rising above the crashing waves as they battled the breakers of the bar. I stood solitary at the brink of the forest, watching them vanish over the horizon. Tob, a man of low station, had forsaken the ancient gods to elevate a mortal who had momentarily caught his fancy. It was a desolating shock. If such a man could abandon the old ways, what of the mob swayed by Phorenice's rhetoric? My exile had begun with a new, profound dreariness.

Then, behind me, the rustle of a great beast signaled an attack. I had no time to mourn the loss of my homeland or the betrayal of the old faith. I had to survive the immediate threat, letting Tob and his new god-driven ship disappear into the vast, indifferent sea.
