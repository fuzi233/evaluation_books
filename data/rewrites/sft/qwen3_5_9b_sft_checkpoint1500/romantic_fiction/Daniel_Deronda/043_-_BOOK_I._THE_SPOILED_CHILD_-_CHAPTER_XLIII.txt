"My spirit grows too thin for earth's heavy hold;
Like an eagle stricken, gazing at the sky,
I feel the weight of dying draw me nigh."

For several moments, the silence between them hung heavy and unbroken, a profound stillness that seemed to settle deep within Mordecai's consciousness. Finally, he raised his gaze to look at Deronda. There was no confusion, no startled surprise in his eyes, only a serene, resting satisfaction, as if a long journey had finally found its harbor. Deronda rose and shifted his chair closer, ensuring his voice would carry gently over the distance. To Mordecai, this simple act felt like the softness of a feather pillow easing aching bones.

"In the ancient teachings of the Kabbalah," Mordecai began, his voice a low murmur, speaking as much to himself as to Deronda, "souls are born anew, again and again, seeking perfection. When a soul is freed from a body grown weary, it joins the soul that needed it most, so they may complete their work together and ascend. Only then do they leave the mortal realm, making space for new souls to enter from the eternal store. It is the lingering imperfections of those still bound here that delay the coming of the Messiah; thus, the mind gives shape to what is hidden, casting shadows of truths long known. When my wandering soul is finally liberated from this tired vessel, it will join yours, and our shared work will be finished."

Mordecai fell silent, his words hanging in the air like a question. Deronda felt compelled to answer, driven by a deep, unspoken bond. "I promise you," Deronda said, his voice steady, "everything within my conscience allows me to do to make your life meaningful, I will do."

"I know," Mordecai replied, his tone calm and certain, needing no further reassurance. "I heard you. You see everything clearly—you stand beside me on the mountain of vision, showing me the paths others refuse to walk."

A moment passed, heavy with reflection, before Mordecai continued, his eyes distant. "You will take up where my life broke. I feel myself transported back to that terrible day when it shattered. The bright sun was shining on the quay at Trieste; the clothes of travelers from every nation glittered like gems, and the boats pushed out to sea. The Greek ship bound for Beirut was to depart in an hour. I was traveling with a merchant as his clerk, dreaming of vast horizons. 'I shall see the lands and peoples of the East,' I told myself, 'and speak with a fuller vision.' I breathed easily, unburdened, with the light step and enduring spirit of youth. I knew how to fast, how to sleep on the hard ground. I had married poverty, and I loved my bride—for to me, poverty was freedom. My heart swelled with a joy akin to that of Moses ben Maimon, strong and knowing the tasks ahead. It was the first time I had traveled south; the soul within me felt the return of a forgotten sun. Standing there on the quay, where the ground seemed to glow with light and shadows danced with an azure brilliance, spirits becoming visible, I felt swept into a flood of glorious life. My own short, numbered existence melted away, unnoticed, and a great sob rose within me, like rushing waters too strong to resist—a bliss overwhelming in its intensity. I waited for my companion, and saw him not until he spoke: 'Ezra, I have been to the post; here is your letter.'"

"Ezra!" Deronda gasped, unable to hold back his shock.

"Ezra," Mordecai repeated, lost in the vividness of the memory. "I was expecting a letter; I wrote constantly to my mother. That sound of my name was like a wand touching me, recalling me from the ocean of existence where I had briefly floated, free from the pressure of individual bonds. I opened the letter, and the name cried out again—a scream that would have disturbed me in heaven, driving me to the source of that sorrow. 'Ezra, my son!'"

Mordecai paused, his imagination frozen by the grip of that long-past moment. Deronda held his breath, waiting. A strange possibility had just unfolded. Mordecai's eyes dropped, lost in abstract thought, before rising again slowly.

"She was a mother deserving of blessing. In her, I understood the Master who, hearing his mother's footsteps, rose and declared, 'The Majesty of the Eternal draws near!' That letter was her cry from the depths of anguish and desolation—the scream of a mother robbed of her children. I was her eldest. Death had taken four babies one after another. Then came my little sister, late and beloved, the very desire of her eyes. The letter was a piercing cry: 'Ezra, my son, I am robbed of her. He has taken her away, leaving disgrace behind. They will never return.'"

Here, Mordecai lifted his eyes suddenly, laying his hand firmly on Deronda's arm. "Mine was the lot of Israel. For the father's sin, my soul must go into exile. For the father's sin, the work was broken, the day of fulfillment delayed. She who bore me was desolate, disgraced, destitute. I turned back. On that instant, her spirit and the spirit of her fathers, those with worthy Jewish hearts, moved within me, drawing me forward. God, who holds the universe, was within me as the strength of obedience. I turned and traveled with hardship—to save the scant money she would need. I left the sunshine and walked into freezing cold. In the final stage of my journey, I spent a night exposed to snow and bitter wind. And there, in that freezing darkness, began this slow death."

Mordecai let his gaze wander again, removing his hand. Deronda resolutely suppressed the storm of questions raging within him. While Mordecai was in this state of raw emotion, no other confession could be sought than what came spontaneously. Yet, Deronda himself felt a kindred emotion, dreading his own words as too momentous.

"But I worked. We were destitute; everything had been seized. And she was ill; the clutch of anguish was too strong, working with some lurking disease. At times, she could not stand for the pounding of her heart, and the images in her brain became chambers of terror, where she saw me rearing my sister in evil. In the dead of night, I heard her crying for her child. Then I rose, and we stretched forth our arms together and prayed. We poured our souls into a desperate desire that Mirah might be delivered from evil."

"Mirah?" Deronda repeated, wishing to ensure his ears had not betrayed him by a feverish imagination. "Did you say Mirah?"

"That was my little sister's name." After the prayers, his mother would rest awhile. It lasted hardly four years, and in the minute before she died, we prayed the same prayer—I aloud, she silently. Her soul flew out upon its wings."

"Have you never since heard of your sister?" Deronda asked, keeping his voice quiet.

"Never. Never have I heard if she was delivered as we prayed. I know not, I know not. Who shall say where the paths lie? The poisonous will of the wicked is strong. It poisoned my life—it is slowly stifling this breath. Death delivered my mother, and I felt it a blessing to be alone in those winter months of suffering. But what are the winters now? They are far off." Here, Mordecai again rested his hand on Deronda's arm, looking at him with a joy that pierced the heart with sadness—"there is nothing to wail in the withering of my body. The work will be the better done. Once I said the beginning of this work was mine, I am born to do it. Yes, I shall do it. I shall live in you. I shall live in you."

His grasp grew convulsive, and Deronda, agitated beyond measure, felt the certainty that this was Mirah's brother suffuse his own strange connection with Mordecai in a new solemnity and tenderness. His young heart beat faster, his lips turning pale. He shrank from speech. He feared that in Mordecai's current state of exaltation—an alarming strain on his fragile frame—he might utter a word of revelation about Mirah that would shatter the delicate balance. He feared to answer below the high pitch of expectation, which resembled a flash from a dying fire, causing watchers to fear it would fade all the quicker. His dominant impulse was to do as he had done before: he laid his firm, gentle hand over the hand that gripped him. Mordecai's hand, as if it had a soul of its own—for he was not distinctly willing to release it—relaxed under Deronda's pressure and turned upward. As the two palms met and pressed together, Mordecai regained some sense of his surroundings and whispered, "Let us go now. I cannot talk any longer."

And indeed, they parted at Cohen's door without another word, merely sharing one last, pressing of hands.

Deronda felt a weight upon him, half joy, half anxiety. The joy of discovering in Mirah's brother a nature even more worthy of her devotion carried the weight of solemnity and sadness; the reunion of brother and sister was, in reality, the first stage of a supreme parting—like a farewell kiss that resembles a greeting, or a last glance of love that becomes the sharpest pang of sorrow. There remained the heavy burden of anxiety regarding the revelation of this fact on both sides, and the careful arrangements necessary beforehand.
