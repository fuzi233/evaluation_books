Deronda met Gwendolen and Grandcourt on the staircase with an absent look that did not belong to indifference but to inward tumult. He had been summoned to the second interview with the Princess Halm-Eberstein; and within two hours of leaving her he learned she had quitted the hotel. The plain business of his journey—Mainz, the letter from Joseph Kalonymos, the family chest—lay ready like a packed valise at the foot of his mind. Yet he did not move toward it.

Some weight, not to be argued with, held him fast. He sat as if under a hand on his shoulder, living again—now with the painful sharpness that comes after the event—those scenes which were “past” only as the seed is past the tree: not ended, but on the point of leafing within him. Alone, he found tears coming, and let them. Compassion shook him for that strange mother’s life—so near his own blood and yet so remote in habit and speech, in the very air of the soul; a woman who had stepped across his path and vanished, leaving behind her a new map of the world. The certitude of ties altered the balance of his hopes and fears; it changed whom he belonged to. He felt as if he had followed, under cover of night, some band of wanderers by mistake, and then morning rose and showed the true tents of kinship pitched far off—calling him with a language he had not yet learned to speak aloud.

With this came a trembling imaginative nearness to the grandfather he had never seen: a man, he was told, of strong impulse and beloved thoughts—impulses, perhaps, that had not died but only slept, and were now stirring within Deronda like a long-inherited appetite. In all his meditation, Mordecai and Mirah were present also, clasping hands with him in that silent alliance by which the living fortify each other when words are too few or too worn. It was like the pressure of fingers in the dark—assurance without explanation.

The bell rang persistently as a signal; its metal insistence cut through the quiet like a nail on glass, and he thought, “I must look into Bradshaw; I must make my preparations and take the next train.” He thought it, as one thinks of eating when the body is numb; and he did nothing. Wishes went on ahead to Mainz, to the chest he was to possess; to London, to the lives that had grown round his heart. But other wishes clung to Genoa with a tenacity like the clinging of wet cloth, and held him there by that obscure compulsion which makes us linger where an interview has the air of final farewell, or where sorrow seems already in the room though not yet named.

He did not shape the thought formally—“I will stay because it is Friday, and I would hear the evening service, and perhaps I may see the Grandcourts again”—but his body obeyed that unspoken resolve. Instead of packing and ringing for his bill, he sat, and his mind went into the synagogue and saw faces that might have looked back on his grandfather; he heard in imagination the Spanish-Hebrew liturgy that had lasted through seasons of wandering generations, like a plant whose seed travels and takes root in foreign earth, making distant coasts kin to the exile’s first home. There was a continuity in that sound—older than any hotel, older than any passport—an acoustic homeland carried in the throat, surviving where dialects die and are replaced by the official tongue.

And his mind went, too, toward Gwendolen: toward the abruptness of their parting, toward the trouble he had seen in her and had not known how to ease. Something in him—half admitted—told him it would be hardness to go away at once without making some effort, in spite of Grandcourt’s probable dislike, to show her that his sympathy had not been a mere moment’s charity. Her voice, when she had spoken most hurriedly and in fear, returned to him with that peculiar force which belongs to phrases uttered at the edge of confession: she had named her mother—her mother’s presence—as if it were a medicine that could be had, if only the hand could reach it.

So he deferred departure, ate his dinner without taste—as if every dish were a wrong dish and every wine a thin shadow—rose quickly, and went to find the synagogue. The streets led him through mingled odors of Genoese evening: the salt of the harbor, damp stone, a breath of frying oil from low kitchens where families fed themselves with the faithful economy of garlic and herbs; and over it the unceasing surf of sound—heels on pavement, voices crossing in Italian and French and English, the clatter of cart-wheels, a far hammering from some workshop that made a harsh modern rhythm against the older human cadences. It seemed to him, with a sudden resentment, that such industrial clamor was always ready to drown what was most delicate in us—the thin thread of remembered prayer, the private tremor of pity.

In the synagogue the voices were fewer than his mind had peopled it with; sparse talithim moved and shook. Dark, keen faces bent and lifted with an easy familiarity in the utterance of awful things—Hebrew dyed with Italian, grave phrases slipping into the warmth of a local accent, as if sacred memory must be kneaded into the daily bread of speech to keep it from drying out. Deronda listened with a bodily attention—each rise and fall of the chant entering him like a scent enters the mind: not to be translated, only to be received. He thought how languages die—not in one dramatic silence, but by degrees, as if a hearth were allowed to go cold; and how with them die whole flavors of feeling, whole ways of naming the world. The dominant lexicon, the language of the street and the office, is always ready with its smooth conveniences; but it pushes aside what is peculiar, what is ancestral, what does not sell. Here, at least, the old words still had mouths.

Yet even among these prayers his thoughts divided; the information he had received earlier that the Grandcourts had gone out boating held a power over him. It made his mind swing, like a door that will not latch, between the liturgy and the quay. He could not keep still after the service. A mournful impatience—perhaps sharpened by his concern for Gwendolen—sent him quickly away through the evening air to his favorite walk by the water, with the dim hope that he might see them returning.

The sun had gone down behind a bank of cloud, and the light that remained was a faint yellow farewell, kissing the waves as they rose and fell restlessly under an active breeze. The strand was lively; voices clustered in knots, shifting, pressing forward; and the sea’s own sound—its broad breathing—was chopped and broken by the sharper noises of men. Deronda, moving slowly but with his senses strung tight, saw a group fix their attention on a sailing-boat advancing swiftly landward, pulled by two men.

There was clamor in various languages. Telescopes were lifted like accusing fingers. Loud statements flew before facts could arrive. Someone said it was the milord who had gone out; another, peering, declared the prostrate figure was miladi. A Frenchman without a glass made a joke—too ready, too polished—about an Englishman drowning his wife according to national practice; an English skipper answered with the blunt violence of our idiom, dismissing it as nonsense that had been through a mining operation and come out dirtier. Then, with a sudden plainness, the decision settled among them: the reclining figure was a woman.

Deronda’s heart leaped and fell in swift alternation. The oar-strokes seemed separated by visions—events possible, impossible—snatches of imagined struggle, a cry, a silence, the sudden white face of consequence. The boat drew near; the shapes grew human. Doubt ended. He saw Gwendolen.

She half raised herself on her hands by her own effort under a heavy covering of tarpaulin and pea-jackets. She was pale as one of the sheeted dead; shivering so that the motion shook her whole frame; her wet hair streamed; and there was in her eyes that wild, amazed consciousness of one who wakens in a world where judgment is impending, and the first faces seen are not friends but officers of fate. The first rower who sprang to land was wet through and ran off at once, as if the very sight of what had happened were intolerable.

Sailors pressed close and for a moment hindered Deronda from getting near. He could only watch as Gwendolen gave frightened glances from side to side and shrank, as if each touch might hurt, while rough bronzed arms helped her out with a tenderness that seemed all the more touching because it was unspoken. Her wet clothes clung about her limbs, heavy as soaked wool, making every step an effort; and she moved with the weakness of one who has fought with water and lost.

Suddenly her wandering eyes fell on Deronda. The change in her was instant, as if she had been looking for him in that crowd and had believed, with the irrational certainty of terror, that he must be there. She tried to stretch out her arms; the men, holding her for her own safety, kept them back; and she said in a muffled voice, the words coming as if forced up from under the sea, “It is come, it is come! He is dead!”

“Hush, hush!” said Deronda, with an authority that was not harsh but necessary, as one quiets a person on the edge of frenzy. “Quiet yourself.”

Then, turning quickly to the men supporting her, he said, “I am a connection of this lady’s husband. If you will get her on to the Italia as quickly as possible, I will undertake everything else.”

They obeyed, glad to have direction. He stayed behind long enough to catch the plain account from the remaining boatman. Grandcourt had gone down irrecoverably; his boat was left floating empty. The two men had heard a cry; they had come up in time to see the lady jump in after her husband, and had got her out fast enough to save her from much harm.

Deronda, with that dreadful piece of certainty lodged in him like a stone, hastened to the hotel. He made sure at once that the best medical help would be called and would not be delayed by ignorance or indifference. Then he telegraphed the event to Sir Hugo, begging him to come forthwith; and he also sent a message to Mr. Gascoigne at the rectory—the nearest known way, he thought, to carry the news to Gwendolen’s mother. For those words of Gwendolen’s, spoken long ago in agitation, returned now with the force of an inspiration: her mother’s presence might be help—might be the one familiar voice that could reach her through the roaring in her ears where the sea and guilt and grief were all one sound.
