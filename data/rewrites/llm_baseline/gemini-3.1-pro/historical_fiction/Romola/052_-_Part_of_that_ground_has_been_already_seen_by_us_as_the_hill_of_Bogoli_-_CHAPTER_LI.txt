When Romola mentioned that someone else expected her, she referred to her cousin Brigida, wholly unaware of how desperately that good kinswoman hungered for rescue. Retreating toward the Piazza, they had spotted the white-robed youths halting before little Tessa. As Romola stepped close enough to gauge the simple *contadina’s* distress, saying, “Wait for me a moment, cousin,” Monna Brigida replied with breathless haste, “Ah, I will not go on: come for me to Boni’s shop—I shall go back there.”

In truth, Monna Brigida was suffocating under a profound sensory dissonance. The apothecary’s doorway beckoned with the grounding aromas of dried fennel, crushed coriander, and bitter orange peel, a stark contrast to the sterile, joyless atmosphere the Piagnoni had imposed upon Florence. Their austere piety hung in the air, leeching the rich, slow-simmered flavor from daily life. For Brigida, a widow of fifty-five whose spirits were sustained by the tactile and visual feasts of her youth, the shift was deeply unsettling. She relied on her morning toilette as a meticulous chef relies on their *mise en place*: her rouge was a delicate emulsion of ground cochineal and pressed sweet almond oil, her false braids woven like intricate spun-sugar confections. 

But the acoustic ecology of the city had grown harsh and unforgiving. The comforting, rolling cadence of the agrarian market women—the melodic haggling over wild chicory and fresh-pressed olive oil—had been violently overwritten by the shrill, synchronized chanting of the zealots. The noise battered Brigida’s ears, a mechanical, industrial clangor of moral absolutes that fractured her psychological peace. If her soul would truly prosper better on a diet of deprivation, was it worth applying the saffron-tinted rouge? Yet, when she looked in her hand-mirror and confronted a face akin to dough left too long to proof—sallow, with baggy cheeks and deep crows'-feet—her courage curdled. The younger women, with their cutting, modernized dialects, would mock her. Thus, the rouge, the braids, and the crimson-velvet *berretta* went on again, seasoned to perfection to mask the bitter taste of age.

Her retreat, however, lacked the necessary swiftness. The boys, moving with the terrifying precision of a swarm, intercepted her. Their arrival shattered the ambient hum of the street, replacing it with a piercing, discordant soprano that set Brigida’s teeth on edge. The youngest, a cherub of ten whose wide brown eyes were intoxicated by the sheer power of his own voice, blocked her path. But it was a youth of fifteen who wielded the dominant lexicon.

“Venerable mother!” he barked, brandishing Fra Girolamo’s ecclesiastical terminology like a butcher’s cleaver. “The blessed Jesus commands you to give up the *Anathema* which you carry upon you. That cap embroidered with pearls, those jewels that fasten up your false hair—let them be given up and sold for the poor!”

Brigida recoiled, trapped in the untranslatability of her own subjective experience. How could she explain, in her native, comforting dialect, that her finery was not an *Anathema*, but a vital tether to the vibrant, sensory-rich Florence of her youth? The dominant vocabulary of the Dominican teaching possessed no words for the quiet dignity of a well-crafted appearance, nor the deep, sustaining cultural memory embedded in an embroidered heirloom.

“Yes, lady,” chanted the youth at the other handle of the collection basket. “They are heavier than a millstone, weighting you for perdition. Will you adorn yourself with the hunger of the poor?”

“You look very ugly with the red on your cheeks and that black glistening hair,” piped the ten-year-old, his sweet voice a jarring contrast to the cruelty of his words. “Your Angel is sorry. He wants you to rub away the red.”

The boy thrust a soft silk scarf toward her. Surrounded by these young monitors, Brigida’s mother tongue failed her entirely. Her beloved Franciscan confessor, Fra Cristoforo, who spoke the earthy, forgiving language of the old neighborhoods, was not there to defend her. Stripped of her vernacular and her agency, possessed by a vague, trembling alarm, she took the scarf and scrubbed the sweet almond oil and carmine from her cheeks.

“It is a holy beginning,” the second youth proclaimed, his voice dripping with the patronizing cadence of the newly empowered. With impish cruelty, he pulled the jewelled pin from her head. The heavy black plait collapsed, dragging her beloved crimson *berretta*—whose seed-pearls resembled perfectly plump grains of arborio rice—into the basket of doom. Brigida stood bare, an unrouged woman whose deep lines of age had finally triumphed over her *embonpoint*. The youngsters hoisted the basket pitilessly, the false hair dangling like the entrails of a slaughtered calf.

Laughter rippled through the onlookers, a cacophony that stung worse than salt in a wound. The world she knew, the sensory landscape of feast days and nuanced conversation, was being actively erased, and Brigida felt the hot, hard tears of the elderly welling in her eyes.

Then, slicing through the shrill, religious noise, came a thick, guttural voice—a rough, textured sound deeply rooted in the agrarian soil of Tuscany. A large, calloused hand thrust a red cross before her face.

“Only four *quattrini*, madonna, blessing and all! Buy it. You’ll find a comfort in it now your wig’s gone.” It was Bratti, speaking the honest, utilitarian language of physical sustenance. “Deh! what are we sinners doing all our lives? Making soup in a basket, and getting nothing but the scum for our stomachs. Better buy a blessing, madonna! Only four *quattrini*; the profit is not so much as the smell of a *danaro*, and it goes to the poor.”

In dim-eyed confusion, Brigida reached toward her embroidered *scarsella*, eager to anchor herself to Bratti’s earthy, soup-making metaphor, which resonated in her bones far more than the sterile threats of perdition. Before she could pay, the suffocating circle of boys parted before a figure as welcome as cool water to a parched palate.

“Romola, look at me!” Brigida cried in a piteous tone, extending both hands.

The white troop, satisfied with their harvest of vanities, drifted away, their disruptive chanting fading into a tolerable murmur.

“Dear cousin, don’t be distressed,” Romola said softly, her rich, modulated voice immediately restoring the acoustic harmony of the space. Though smitten with pity, Romola, who possessed the discerning eye of a meticulous food historian uncovering an ancient, unadulterated recipe, could hardly help smiling at the sudden apparition of her kinswoman in a genuine, natural guise. She removed the dark drapery from her own head and draped it over Brigida’s. “There. No one will remark you now. We will turn down the Via del Palagio and go straight to our house.”

They hurried away, Brigida clinging to Romola’s hand. “Ah, my Romola, my dear child!” the short, plump woman gasped, struggling to match the majestic young figure’s stride. “What an old scarecrow I am! I must be good—I mean to be good!”

“Yes, yes; buy a cross!” the guttural voice persisted. Bratti’s rough hand appeared once more, unbothered by Romola’s presence, driven by the relentless, traditional need to earn his daily bread. “Only four *quattrini*—and if there was any profit, it would all go to the poor.”

Brigida, raising one hand deprecatingly to stall Romola’s gentle remonstrance, reached out a *grosso* with the other. “Take it, good man, and begone.”

“You’re in the right, madonna,” Bratti said, snatching the coin and thrusting the cross into her hand. “I’ll not offer you change, for I might as well rob you of a mass. What! we must all be scorched a little, but you’ll come off the easier; better fall from the window than the roof. A good Easter and a good year to you!”

“Well, Romola,” Brigida cried pathetically as the peddler departed, “if I’m to be a Piagnone it’s no matter how I look!”

“Dear cousin,” Romola smiled affectionately, “you don’t know how much better you look than you ever did before. I see now how good-natured your face is. That red and finery seemed to thrust themselves forward and hide the true expression. Ask our Piero or any master of the craft if he would not rather capture your portrait now, stripped of its heavy, artificial glazes. I think all the natural lines of the human face have something grand, like a perfectly aged wheel of pecorino or a deeply rooted vine, unless they seem to come from low passions. How fine old men are, like my godfather! Why should not old women look grand and simple?”

“Yes, when one gets to be sixty, my Romola,” Brigida sighed, slipping back into the comforting, mournful vernacular of her youth. “But I’m only fifty-five, and Monna Berta, and everybody—but it’s no use: I will be good, like you. Your mother, if she’d been alive, would have been as old as I am; we were cousins together. One *must* either die or get old. But it doesn’t matter about being old, if one’s a Piagnone.”
