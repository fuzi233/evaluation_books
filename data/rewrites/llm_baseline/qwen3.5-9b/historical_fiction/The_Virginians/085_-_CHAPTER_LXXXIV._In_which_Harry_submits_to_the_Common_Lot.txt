The era of my hardship had finally ended; I was no longer forced to wrestle with poverty. The death of my little kinsman shifted my worldly prospects entirely, making me the next heir to a substantial estate. With my uncle and aunt unlikely to produce further offspring, the succession seemed secure. "The woman is capable of committing any crime to disappoint you," Sampson had vowed regarding my aunt's potential interference, yet Lady Warrington proved guiltless of such treachery. Instead, crushed by the blow that had fallen upon them, she allowed her religious advisors to convince her that this tragedy was a divine chastisement requiring submission to the Heavenly Will. "While your son lived, your heart was turned away from the better world," her clergyman admonished her, noting her excessive focus on earthly status. "For your son's advantage, you desired rank and title. You sought and might have secured an earthly coronet. But what use is it now to one who has but a few years left on earth—what importance holds it compared to the heavenly crown for which you are now an assured candidate?"

The accident caused considerable sensation. Within the chapels of the enthusiastic sect toward which she increasingly leaned following her son's death, numerous sermons were delivered referencing the event. Far be it from me to judge the path taken by the bereaved mother, or to view with anything less than respect and sympathy any grieving soul seeking refuge from sin, sorrow, and disappointment. Lady Warrington even attempted a reconciliation with me. A year after her loss, while visiting London, she signaled her desire to meet, and I duly waited upon her. There, in her customary didactic manner, she delivered a homily on my station and hers. She marveled at Heaven's decree, lamenting how dreadfully her poor child's disobedience had been punished—a disobedience that resulted in my gain. (It transpired that my poor little man had defied orders and gone out hunting with his gun without his mother's knowledge.) She expressed hope that, should I eventually inherit the property—though the Warringtons were, thank Heaven, a long-lived lineage, save for my own father, whose life was cut short by the excesses of a poorly regulated youth—I would alter my current lifestyle. She prayed that I would sever ties with my unworthy associates, abandon the horrid theatre and its licentious patrons, and turn toward quarters offering only peace and the sacred duties she feared I had neglected. Her exhortations were laden with Scriptural language, which I choose not to replicate. Upon my departure, she presented me with a packet of sermons for Mrs. Warrington and a small book of hymns by Miss Dora, who later became a prominent figure in that society alongside her mother. After the dowager's death at Bath three years prior, Miss Dora married the young Mr. Juffles, a celebrated preacher.

Though the poor lady had forgiven me, she could never bear the sight of our boy. Following the loss of our second child, my aunt and her daughter eagerly flocked to the suffering mother, even inviting us to their home. However, my uncle was now almost daily in our house. He would sit for hours gazing at our boy, bringing him endless toys and sweets, begging that the child address him as "Godpapa." When we felt our own grief—which, even twenty-five years later, strikes me as acutely as on the day we first lost our little one—I understood how to commiserate with him. Yet my wife could pity him before experiencing the loss of her own child; my own heart, initially more selfish, learned true pity only through experience, leading to my reconciliation with my uncle via my little baby's coffin.

The poor man dispatched his coach to follow our humble funeral procession. Afterwards, he took out little Miles, who prattled incessantly, forgetting any sorrow in the delight of his new black clothes and the pleasures of a carriage ride. How the innocent chatter of the child stabbed the mother's heart! Could we ever wish for that wound to heal? I know her face so well that to this day I can discern moments when she thinks of that lost little one. It is not merely grief for a parting so long ago, but a communion with a beloved soul in Heaven.

Soon thereafter, we returned to our bright lodgings in Bloomsbury. My young bear, whom I could no longer lead, had formed a prodigious friendship with Charley and was sent to the Chartreux School, where his friend ensured he received no more beatings than were beneficial. Thanks undoubtedly to the excellence of his private tutor, he excelled and maintained a good standing. He loved the school so dearly that he declared, should he ever have a son, it would be sent to that same seminary.

I could no longer lead my bear for another reason: I had other business to attend to. Fully reconciled to us, I believe, for Mr. Miles's sake, my uncle—who was such an obsequious supporter of the Government that I wonder the Minister ever bestowed favors upon him, being perfectly sure of his vote—used his influence in behalf of his nephew and heir. Consequently, I had the honor of being gazetted as one of His Majesty's Commissioners for licensing hackney coaches, a post I fulfilled, I trust, with credit until a quarrel with the Minister (to be recounted in its proper place) deprived me of it. I also took my degree at the Temple and appeared in Westminster Hall in my gown and wig. Furthermore, this year, my good friend Mr. Foker having business in Paris, I accompanied him there. I was received *bras ouverts* by my dear American preserver, Monsieur de Florac, who introduced me to his noble family and the polished society of the capital, though I lacked the leisure to frequent them extensively. I possessed too much spirit to desert my kind patron Foker, whose acquaintances lay chiefly among the bourgeoisie, particularly Monsieur Santerre, a great brewer of Paris who has since distinguished himself in blood rather than beer. Mr. F. required my services as an interpreter, and I was too glad to command them, eager to repay some of the kindness he had shown me. Meanwhile, our ladies resided at Mr. Foker's new villa in Wimbledon, delighted by the "Parisian letters" I sent them through my distinguished friend Mr. Hume, then of the Embassy, which were subsequently published in a neat volume.

While I tranquilly discharged my minor official duties in London, those troubles commenced that were to culminate in the great separation between our colonies and the mother country. When Mr. Grenville proposed his stamp duties, I told my wife the bill would create immense discontent at home, for we were ever anxious to extract as much as possible from England while paying back as little as feasible. Yet, I certainly never anticipated the prodigious anger the scheme ignited. It was with us as with families or individuals: a pretext is given for a quarrel, while the real cause lies in long-standing bickerings and previous animosities. Many foolish exactions, petty tyrannies, the habitual insolence of Englishmen toward all foreigners and colonists, and the natural spirit of men outraged by our imperious domineering nature set Britain and her colonies to quarrel. The astonishing blunders of the system adopted in England brought the dispute to an issue, which I, for one, do not intend to deplore. Had I been in Virginia instead of London, 'tis very possible I would have sided with the province, perhaps driven into revolt by mere opposition to that resolute mistress of Castlewood, who might have pushed me into rebellion just as England drove the colonies. Was the Stamp Act the cause of the revolution? It was a tax no greater than that cheerfully paid in England. Ten years earlier, when the French threatened our territory and we implored succor from home, would the colonies have rebelled at the payment of this tax? Do not most people consider the tax-gatherer the natural enemy? Against the British in America were arrayed thousands of high-spirited and brave souls, yet thousands more found profit in the quarrel or harbored private reasons for joining it. I protest I do not know now whether my motives were selfish or patriotic, which side held the right, or whether both did not. I am certain we in England had nothing to do but fight the battle out; and having lost the game, I vow and believe that, after the initial natural soreness, the loser felt no rancor.

What prompted brother Hal to write home from Virginia, which he seemed exceedingly reluctant to leave, with such flaming patriotic letters? My kind, best brother was always led by someone: by me when we were together (he held such an idea of my wit and wisdom that if I said the day was fine, he would ponder the observation as though it were a saying of the Seven Sages), or by some other wiseacre when I was away. Who inspired these fiery epistles, this boisterous patriotism sent to us in London? "He is rebelling against Madam Esmond," I surmised. "He is led by some colonial person—perhaps by that lady," my wife hinted. Who "that lady" was, Hal never told us; indeed, he beseeched me never to allude to the delicate subject in my letters to him, writing, "for Madam wishes to see 'em all, and I wish to say nothing about you know what until the proper moment." No affection could be greater than that displayed in his letters.
