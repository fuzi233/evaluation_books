The August sun burned high over Ramelton when Harry Feversham crossed the Lennon bridge, his silhouette sharp against the shimmering heat. The years in the desert had etched deep lines into his face, weathered his skin to bronze, and though he rode through the narrow streets of the town, no eyes turned in recognition. He was a stranger now, a ghost from another life.

At the crest of the hill, he turned onto the straight road to Letterkenny, his pace quickening as if chased by memories. The past six years had blurred into shadows, fading with each mile closer to this place. Here, happiness had once been his; here, too, he had known the depths of shame. He reined in briefly beside the church, its spire stark against the pale sky, and wondered—was Ethne still in Ramelton? Was old Dermod alive? What welcome awaited him?

But the road lay empty. Only the call of a lark overhead and the distant cry of a landrail answered him. He shook off the stillness and rode on, the past whispering in his ear with every stride of his horse.

The grove of trees appeared first, then the broken fence, the gate hanging askew. He turned in without hesitation, though the drive was choked with weeds, the house beyond a skeleton of blackened stone. Dismounting, he called out, his voice ringing through the ruins. No reply came. The gardens were wild, the lawns unkempt—abandoned.

Back in Ramelton, he stopped at the inn, handed his horse to the ostler, and ordered lunch. The landlady, eager with gossip, told him of Lennon House’s burning five years past, of Dermod Eustace’s death, of Ethne’s retreat to Glenalla.

“Another gentleman asked after her not long ago,” she added, eyes bright with curiosity. “Burnt as dark as you. A Colonel Trench.”

Feversham said nothing. He flipped through the guest book, saw the name, then mounted again. “I won’t return to Ramelton,” he told her, and rode out toward the hills.

The track to Glenalla wound upward, past scattered cottages and open fields. The village, when he reached it, was quiet—a handful of houses, a church, the lough gleaming in the distance. At the church gate, a collie barked, then, sniffing, erupted into joyous recognition. The dog remembered him.

And then—Ethne.

She stood in the doorway, motionless, her face unreadable until she spoke his name.

“Harry.”

No other words came. They measured the years in silence, the changes etched into each other’s faces. He saw the strength in her, the quiet endurance; she saw the trials carved into his, the resolve hardened by fire.

“It wasn’t all harm,” she said at last. “The years weren’t wasted.”

He thought of her solitude, the weight of time. “I’m sorry.”

She shook her head, held out her hand. “Give it to me.”

For a moment, he didn’t understand. Then he reached into his coat, shook two feathers into his palm—the ostrich plume, and Castleton’s.

“Both,” she said.

He handed them over. She pressed them to her breast. “I have all four now.”

“What will you do with them?”

“Keep them.” Her voice was fierce. “I am proud to keep them.”

They were not dead things to her, not relics of shame or regret, but proof—of courage, of redemption.

She spoke of Trench’s visit, of his confession about the fourth feather. “I don’t hate him anymore,” she said simply.

Feversham nodded. “He’s a good friend.”

A pause. Then, softly, she asked, “Do you remember our drive from the quay? What we spoke of?”

“Friends,” he said. “The ones you recognize at once, as if you’ve always known them.”

“And never lose,” she finished. “I said you couldn’t be wrong about them. Not truly.” She looked down at the feathers, then up at him. “I was young then. I didn’t understand. But I do now.”

Her fingers tightened around the white plumes. Without another word, she turned and walked into the church. Feversham followed.
