It would be folly to argue that even in this hard, unsteady world, we do not sometimes cross paths with people whose very bearing, the quiet rhythm of all their days, carries such an unmistakeable stamp of gentle goodness that we trust them not out of careful calculation, but on instinct, at a single glance. — Alexander Knox, quoted in Southey’s *Life of Wesley*

Mirah said she had slept deeper and sweeter than she could remember in years. When she stepped down to the parlor that morning, clad in Mab’s neatly mended black dress, her dark hair sprung in soft new curls as it finished drying from the long, warm bath, she looked like a flower that had bent under weeks of storm, finally lifting its petal to the sun. The blue half-circles of exhaustion under her pale cheeks were fading at last, the haggard tightness around her mouth gone.
It was Mab who led her down, carrying her tray of tea and bread, practically bouncing with quiet pride over the pair of tiny felt slippers she had run out before dawn to buy. No shoes in the Meyrick household were small enough for Mirah’s narrow feet, and the borrowed dress stopped just above her ankles, showing the cheap soft fabric that clung to her skin like the sheath of a new bud. The little farthing buckles on the slippers glinted like the finest jewelry to Mab’s eyes.
“Oh, Mamma, look!” she cried, clasping her hands and bending to gesture at Mirah’s feet the second they crossed the parlor threshold. “See how perfectly they fit! I swear she’s exactly Queen Budoor from the Arabian tales—‘two delicate feet, the work of the kind and all-rewarding Creator, bear her up, and I wonder how they ever carried the weight of all her sorrow.’”
Mirah looked down at her own feet with a soft, childlike curiosity, then smiled shyly at Mrs. Meyrick. The widow had been thinking to herself, You would never guess this creature could hold an unkind thought in her head. Any sensible person would warn me to be wary of strangers. But she smiled back warm and open. “I fear those little feet have had to bear far too heavy a burden lately. But today you will rest, and keep me company.”
“And she’ll tell you all sorts of stories and I’ll miss every word,” Mab grumbled playfully, already grabbing her bag for the day’s music pupils. She felt like she was halfway through the first volume of the most delightful romance ever written, and furious at being forced to skip chapters for work.
Kate had already left to sketch along the river, Amy was out running errands for the house. Mrs. Meyrick had made sure of that—she wanted to be alone with this quiet stranger, whose story she already knew must be steeped in grief, but which they both needed to say out loud.
That tiny front parlor felt like a sacred, soft little temple that morning. Sunlight spilled across the glinting river outside, and cool fresh air drifted through the open window. The walls, lined with cheap black-and-white prints, bloomed with a cloud of silent, beloved witnesses: the Virgin ascending flanked by cherubs; grand, solemn Melancholia holding her whole quiet universe in her hands; the Prophets and Sibyls, the School of Athens, the Last Supper, mystic groupings from centuries past that felt alive and present all at once. There were grave Holbein and Rembrandt portraits, the Tragic Muse, soft drawings of 18th century children lost in thought or at play, portraits of Italian poets. No clutter, no noise. Mrs. Meyrick, who had weathered so many troubles and still carried a cheerful, gentle face, sat sorting skeins of colored wool for her embroidery. Their old tabby cat Hafiz purred on the window ledge, the mantel clock ticked slow and unhurried, and the faint rumble of passing carriages felt like it was miles outside the thick, soft hush wrapped around the room.
Mrs. Meyrick decided not to break the quiet first—she thought it would make Mirah feel safer to speak in her own time. Mirah sat across from her, hands folded in her lap, ankles crossed, her eyes drifting slowly over all the art on the walls before finally settling on Mrs. Meyrick with a quiet, reverent softness. At last she spoke, her voice low and soft as the breeze through the window.
“I can remember my mother’s face clearer than anything else in the world, even though I wasn’t seven when I was taken away. I’m nineteen now.”
“That makes perfect sense to me,” Mrs. Meyrick said. “The very first things you learn to love stick with you longer than anything else.”
“Oh, yes. My whole life feels like it started the day I woke up and loved my mother’s face first. She was so close, her arms wrapped all around me, singing. One hymn she sang over and over, until I learned to sing it with her. They were all Hebrew hymns, and I never knew what the words meant—so they never held anything but our love, all our happiness. When I lay in my little white bed, she would lean over me, her face between me and the pale ceiling, and sing in that sweet low voice. I can step right back into that moment even when I’m awake, and it comes to me in dreams all the time. My hand is tiny, I lift it to her face, and she kisses it. Sometimes I wake up trembling, thinking we’re both dead, and my hand lies limp on the sheets, and for a second I don’t even recognize myself. But if I saw her again, I would know her face instantly.”
“Twelve years will change a person, dear,” Mrs. Meyrick said gently. “Look at my grey hair—ten years ago it was bright chestnut. The days and months fly over us like small restless birds, their little feet leaving marks all over us. And when the days are heavy with grief, they tread much harder.”
“I know her heart must have been so heavy, missing me. But to think of the joy on her face if we found each other again! To make her know how much I love her, to give her that comfort after all the years of mourning. If that could happen, I would never mind any of the trouble I lived through. I despaired, you know. The whole world felt cruel and wicked, no one would help me, I couldn’t bear the way strangers looked at me, the things they said. I was sure my mother was dead, that the only way to get to her was to drown. But yesterday, right when I was longing for the river to close over me, when I thought death was the only mercy left, living kindness found me. And it’s strange, but that second I started hoping she was still alive too. Now I’m here, with you, this morning—peace and hope have flooded into me like something I never dared dream of. I want for nothing. I can wait. I hope, and I believe, and I am so grateful—so grateful! You never thought ill of me. You never looked at me like I was something to despise.”
Mirah spoke with low, burning fervor, sitting perfectly still as if she was a painted saint on a chapel wall.
“Plenty of other people would have felt exactly the way we do, my dear,” Mrs. Meyrick said, feeling a warm mist blur her eyes over her half-sorted wools.
“But I never met any of them. They never found me.”
“How did you end up taken away from your mother, then?”
“Ah, that’s the hard part. Dreadful to say out loud, but I must tell you everything. It was my father who took me. I thought we were just going on a little day trip, I was excited. I had a little box packed with all my toys and ribbons. But we kept walking, got on a ship, and the land drifted further and further away. I got so seasick I thought the pain would never end. That was the first bit of misery, and it felt endless. When I finally got my feet on land again, my father comforted me, said we would go home to my mother soon. We were in America, and it was years before we came back to Europe.
“At first I asked every day when we could go home. I tried to learn to write as fast as I could, so I could send her a letter. But one day my father caught me scribbling to her, pulled me onto his knee, and told me my mother and my little brother were dead. That was why we could never go back home. I barely remembered my brother, just that he used to hoist me up on his shoulders once, but he was out working most days. I believed him. I pictured them buried under the earth, their eyes closed forever. I never thought to doubt him, and I cried every night for months. But then my mother started appearing to me in my dreams so often, I thought she must still be alive somewhere close by, even if I couldn’t see her. That comforted me so much I was never afraid of the dark. I’d shut my eyes in the middle of the day, bury my face, and try to see her, hear her singing. After a while I could do it without even closing my eyes.”
Mirah paused, a soft, sweet smile spreading over her face, as if she was staring right at that happy memory, looking out at the glinting river through the window.
“I hope your father wasn’t cruel to you?” Mrs. Meyrick asked, after a quiet minute, gently drawing her back to the present.
“He petted me, spent a lot of time teaching me things. He was an actor, you know—later I figured out the ‘Coburg’ theatre he kept talking about back home was where he used to work. But he did more than act. He’d been a teacher once, spoke half a dozen languages. His acting wasn’t very good, but he managed stage productions, wrote and translated plays. There was an Italian opera singer, Signora, who lived with us for years. They both taught me to sing, I had another master too who made me recite whole parts from memory. I worked so hard even as a tiny girl, but I wasn’t nine when I first stepped onto the stage. I learned lines easily, I was never shy in front of an audience, but I hated every second of that life.
“My father had money back then, we had all sorts of flashy finery scattered around the house in a mess, strangers coming and going at all hours—loud laughter, shouting arguments, people strutting and jeering, faces I didn’t want to look at, even if most of them fussed over me and stroked my hair. But I still held my mother close in my head. Even when I was too little to understand much, I shrank away from all that noise, and lived in my own private world of thoughts I’d picked out as beautiful. I read every book I could get my hands on—plays, poetry, Shakespeare and Schiller—and I learned to tell good from bad very fast. My father decided my voice was going to make us rich, that I’d be a world-famous star. He hired the best teachers money could buy, but it made my skin crawl the way he’d brag about me, make me sing for random guests at a moment’s notice like I was nothing more than a fancy music box.
“When I was nine, I played a little lost girl in a play who didn’t know she’d been abandoned, singing to herself while she picked flowers on the stage. I did it perfectly without even trying, but all the clapping and the roar of the audience made me sick. I never liked any praise I got, because none of it felt soft, none of it had the love I was born back home with. I built my own little world in my head, separate from all the chaos around me, picking out every beautiful line of poetry and every kind character from the plays I read to fill it. It was like a sharp knife nicking at me all the time, the way the two lives never fit: actresses on the stage playing soft, gentle women, speaking kind lines like they meant them, and the second the curtain fell they were yelling crude insults at each other behind the scenes. My father noticed I always shrank away from everyone, and Signora said once after a rehearsal, ‘She’ll never make a proper artist. She only knows how to be herself. That’s cute now, but soon she’ll be as empty of performance as a little singing bird.’ They fought over it, and I sat alone crying, because what she said felt like she was unrolling a long, miserable future right in front of my eyes. I never wanted to be an artist, but that was all my father had ever planned for me.
“After Signora left, a governess came to give me lessons, because my father was scared I’d strain my voice too much. I still acted sometimes, but the more I grew, the more I hated that life, the more I ached to run away. But I had nowhere to go, I was terrified of the world. And I thought it would be wrong to leave my father. I was so scared I’d turn wicked, that I’d end up hating myself the same way I hated all the cruel, loud strangers around me. I’d spent so long never feeling safe in the real world, I knew if I turned wicked I’d lose the little happy world I’d made where my mother still lived with me. That was the childish thought that got me through all those years. Oh, they felt so long.”
Mirah fell quiet, lost in thought.
“Did no one ever teach you what to believe, about right and wrong?” Mrs. Meyrick asked, careful not to say the word religion, not sure what Mirah’s Hebrew upbringing might have been.
“Only that I had to do whatever my father wanted. He didn’t practice our faith in New York, he never wanted me to learn much about it. But I could still remember my mother taking me to the synagogue, sitting on her lap, looking through the railing at the men chanting and singing. I ached to go. One day I snuck out when I was small, trying to find the nearest synagogue, and got completely lost for hours until a peddler found me and walked me home. My father had been frantic with worry, and he was so angry at me I didn’t dare sneak out again for years. But after Signora left, our landlady was a Jewish woman who kept all the traditions. She let me come to synagogue with her, I read her prayer books and her Bible, and when I got a little bit of money of my own I bought my own copies. Those books felt like they brought me closer to my mother—I knew she had read those exact same words, said those exact same prayers. That’s how I learned a little of our faith, and the history of our people, on top of all the bits and pieces I picked up from plays and other books about Jews. I was sure my mother followed all the traditions faithfully.
“I stopped asking my father about her, after a while. It’s dreadful to say, but I started to not believe him. I’d caught him lying so many times, promising things he never meant to do, and I started to suspect my mother and brother were still alive. When I thought back to that day he tricked us onto the ship, I realized my mother had been completely deceived, expecting us to come back after a short little trip. The day he sat me on his knee and told me they were dead? It was just a performance, to make me stop asking questions. That lie was so cruel, it sank right into my bones, and I started hating every untruth because of it. I wrote her a secret letter, addressed to our old street in Colman Street, I remembered our original last name was Cohen, before my father changed it to Lapidoth, saying it was the name of his Polish ancestors. I sent the letter hidden, no one found out—but no answer ever came. I thought all hope was gone.
“We didn’t stay in America much longer. My father packed all our things one day and told me we were sailing for Hamburg. I was glad, I hoped we’d meet kinder people there. I spoke German almost fluently by then, I knew whole plays by heart. I was thirteen, and I felt so old—I knew so much, but nothing at all. I used to wish I’d drowned on that first ship ride away from my mother. But I told myself to be good, to be patient. What else could I do?
“One day on the voyage, a new terrible thought came to me. I wasn’t seasick that trip, I spent most days up on deck. My father sang and joked and did little acts to amuse all the other passengers, and I heard people whispering about him. Once I was staring out at the sea, no one was paying any attention to me, and I heard a gentleman say, ‘Oh, he’s one of those clever Jews—probably a rascal. There’s no race like them for cunning in the men and beauty in the women. Wonder what market he’s planning to sell that daughter of his to.’
“That thought pierced me right through. I realized all the unhappiness in my life was because I was a Jewess, that for the rest of my days the whole world would look down on me, judge me for my name before they even knew me. But then it comforted me, to think my pain was part of the long, shared affliction of my people, my little verse added to the centuries-old song of mourning they’ve sung through every generation. Even if some of my people were wicked and laughed at their own sins, that pain was still the burden the good ones had to carry, despised for the wrongs of people they’d never even met. But you—you never rejected me.”
Mirah’s tone softened, suddenly remembering she was not speaking of grief now, but of the kindness that had saved her.
“We will make sure no one judges you unjustly ever again, my poor child,” Mrs. Meyrick said, putting her half-sorted wools down completely, folding her hands in her lap, her face as earnest as Mab’s ever was when she dove into a new romance novel. “Go on, dear. Tell me everything.”
“After that we lived in a string of different cities—Hamburg, then Vienna for the longest stretch. I started taking singing lessons again, my father always found work around the theatres. I think he’d brought a big sum of money over from America, I never knew exactly why we left New York. For a long time he was so excited about my singing, making me rehearse parts over and over, he was counting down to my grand opera debut. But as time went on, it turned out my voice never got strong enough. It never lived up to the promise it had when I was little. My singing master in Vienna told him, ‘Don’t make her strain it any further. It’s pure gold, but it’s only a thread of gold dust. It will never fill an opera house.’
