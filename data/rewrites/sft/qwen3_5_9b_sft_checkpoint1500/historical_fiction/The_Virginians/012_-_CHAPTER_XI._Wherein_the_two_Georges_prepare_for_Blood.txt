Within the confines of Benson's Tavern, the air grew heavy with the scent of impending violence and the sharp tang of unshed tears. In one chamber, Colonel Washington stood alone, his brow furrowed in a silence that spoke volumes, preparing his spirit for the ordeal ahead. Across the threshold, in a quieter room, young George Warrington engaged in a frantic, tear-stained ritual of his own. With his faithful brother Harry acting as his scribe, George dictated a final testament, a grandiloquent letter to his mother, bidding her a solemn farewell.

"My dear, dear George, don't say that!" cried Harry, his voice trembling as he held the quill. "Don't speak of falling!"

"'As probably will be the case,'" George insisted, his voice rising with a majestic, hollow conviction. "You know what a marksman Colonel Washington is, Harry. I am fair at a target myself, and it is likely that one or both of us shall drop. 'I scarcely suppose you will carry out the intentions you have at present in view.'" These final words were spoken with a bitterness that cut deeper than any prior grievance, leaving Harry weeping openly as he scrawled them down.

"You see, I say nothing," George continued, his composure cracking slightly, "Madame Esmond's name does not even appear in this quarrel. Do you not recall Grandfather's life? How Lord Castlewood fought Lord Mohun on a mere pretext of a card dispute, never once hinting at the lady who was the true catalyst? I took my cue from that, Harry. Our mother is not compromised in the—Why, child, what have you been writing, and who taught thee to spell?"

Harry had misspelled the final phrase "in view" as "in vew," and a fresh wave of salty tears had blurred the ink further.

"I can't think about the spelling now, Georgy," whimpered Harry, his eyes red-rimmed. "I'm too miserable for that. I begin to think, perhaps it's all nonsense... perhaps Colonel Washington never..."

"Never meant to take possession of Castlewood? Never gave himself airs? Never advised my mother to have me flogged? Never intended to marry her? Never insulted me before the King's officers? Never wrote to his brother claiming parental authority over us?" George cried out, slapping his breast pocket where the evidence lay hidden. "The paper is there, Harry Warrington! If anything happens to me, you will find it on my corpse!"

"Write yourself, Georgy, I can't hold the pen," Harry sobbed, burying his fists in his eyes until his elbows smeared the page with a chaotic mixture of bad spelling and grief.

Seizing another sheet, George sat at his brother's desk, channeling every ounce of his youth into a composition filled with the longest, most obscure words, the grandest Latin quotations, and the deepest satire his mind could conjure. He commanded that his enslaved boy, Sady, be set free immediately. He arranged for provisions for his tutor, Mr. Dempster, whose affection he cherished like a father figure. He directed that his silver fruit-knife, music books, and harpsichord be given to little Fanny Mountain. Finally, he requested that his brother take a lock of his hair, to be worn as a token of eternal devotion. With a seal bearing his grandfather's ancient arms, George signed the document, sealing a fate that seemed already written.

"The watch, of course, will be yours," George said, producing his grandfather's gold chronometer. He glanced at the face. "Two hours and a half have vanished! 'Tis time for Sady to return with the pistols. Take the watch, Harry dear."

"It's no good!" Harry screamed, throwing his arms around his brother in a desperate hug. "If he fights you, I'll fight him, too! If he kills my Georgy—" His voice broke into a string of oaths and curses, the kind that, legend held, caused the recording angels to swoon from their celestial ledgers.

Outside, the atmosphere shifted with the arrival of General Braddock's new aide-de-camp. With a resolute hand, he had penned five letters and sealed them with his wax. One went to his mother at Mount Vernon, another to his brother, a third to "M.C. only," and the fourth to the Major-General himself. "And one, young gentleman," the informant whispered, "is for your mother, Madam Esmond."

Another angelic observer, burdened by the weight of the moment, flew away at the sight of George Warrington's lips parting in a silent execration born of pure hatred, rage, and jealousy.

It was the tavern landlord who delivered the news to the brothers. Captain Waring, donning his old militia uniform to honor the gravity of the hour, announced that Colonel Washington was pacing the garden, awaiting them. The Regulars, mostly sober, waited under the porch. A plot of land near the Captain's log-house, cleared for a kitchen garden, served as the stage for this tragedy. There walked Colonel Washington, hands clasped behind his back, head bowed, a grave sorrow etched onto his handsome features. Negro servants pressed against the palings, peering over, while the officers beneath the porch stirred from their slumber. Captain Waring strode steadily along the balcony formed by the sloping roof, while Captain Grace lolled over the railing, his gaze vacant yet alert.

Benson's was notorious, a haven for cockfights, horse matches, boxing, and wrestling, drawing Virginians from every walk of life. It was a place where men arrived sound and departed with broken ribs or gouged eyes, where squires, farmers, and slaves alike indulged in the bloodsport.

Into this somber tableau stalked the tall young Colonel, lost in dismal meditation. The path forward was narrow and cruel, dictated by the harsh laws of honor and local custom. Goaded by a boy's impudence, he had used insulting words. The young man had demanded reparation. Shocked by the depth of George Warrington's jealousy and the length of time it had festered, the Colonel felt the weight of his mistake. He was bound to pay the forfeit.

Suddenly, a roar erupted—a wild, rhythmic hallooing typical of the enslaved population, who delighted in noise, especially when riding at full gallop. Heads, both woolly and powdered, snapped toward the sound. It came from the road the travelers had traversed hours prior. Soon, the clatter of hooves shattered the silence, and Mr. Sady appeared atop his foaming steed. Amidst a prodigious uproar from his fellow blacks, Sady fired a pistol into the air. Then another. His horse, trained to endure the chaos of the hunt, stood firm. Surrounded by a dozen bawling companions, Sady descended into the courtyard, kicking horses and shrieking pigs, his laughter mingling with the screams of the animals.

"Sady, sir, come here!" roared Master Harry.
"Sady, come here! Confound you!" shouted Master George.
"Comin', mas'r," Sady replied cheerfully, ignoring the orders, and resumed his chatter with the crowd. He grinned, drew the pistols from their holsters, snapped the locks, and pointed them first at a grunting hog that charged through the yard, then down the road whence he had come. "Everybody a-comin'," he declared, turning to point at the approaching dust clouds.

Sure enough, the thunder of hooves returned. First came Little Mr. Dempster, spurring his pony with frantic energy. Beside him, a rider in a riding habit on a small gray mare—could it be Madam Esmond? No, she was far too stout. It was Mrs. Mountain, riding Madam Esmond's mount.

"Oh Lor! O Golly! Hoop! Here dey come! Hurray!" a chorus of negroes rose in anticipation.
Dr. Dempster and Mrs. Mountain clattered into the yard, dismounted, and pushed through the throng of black servants, rushing straight into the house and bursting out onto the porch where the British officers sat in stunned amazement. They raced down the stairs and into the garden, where George and Harry stood facing the towering figure of the Colonel. Before George could sternly ask, "What do you do here, madam?" Mrs. Mountain threw her arms around his neck.

"Oh, George, my darling! It's a mistake! It's a mistake, and it's all my fault!"

"What's a mistake?" George asked, majestically pulling away from the embrace.

"What is it, Mounty?" cried Harry, trembling violently.

"That paper I took from his portfolio, that scrap I found where the Colonel said he was going to marry a widow with two children," Mrs. Mountain gasped, addressing the boys. "Who else could it be but you, children, and who else could it be but your mother?"

"Well?" Harry urged.

"Well, it's—not your mother. It's that little widow Custis whom the Colonel intends to marry. He always sought a wealthy wife; I knew he would. It's not Mrs. Rachel Warrington. He told Madam so today, just before departing, saying the wedding would follow the campaign. And—and your mother is furious, boys. When Sady came for the pistols and told the whole house you were fighting, I told him to fire them off in the yard! I galloped after him, and I've nearly broken my poor old bones getting here!"
