As I moved through one of the winding channels, my gaze settled upon a patch of greenery that appeared to be merely a shrub—a likely outpost guarding an unseen army hidden behind the ridge. Kneeling, I peered closer, wary of the small fruit hanging low. Unfamiliar to me, it felt dangerous to touch. Little did I know that hundreds of eyes, burning with curiosity, were watching from the shadows of the rocks, waiting to see if I would succumb to temptation.

I pressed onward, encountering plants that grew progressively larger, eventually revealing themselves not as bushes but as stunted trees. By the time I approached the second arm of the dry riverbed, the landscape was choked with them, forcing me to leap across gaps too wide to traverse otherwise. One channel roared with a sound like a flock of birds taking flight from an ivy-choked wall, though I saw no sign of life. Further along, massive trees bearing coarse, uninviting fruits lined the edge of a vast depression—the dried bed of a former lake. To the left, a dense forest spilled down into the hollow, yet within the basin itself, vegetation was dominated almost exclusively by fruit-bearers.

Descending a gentle slope of grass mixed with moss, exhaustion claimed me, and I lay down to rest. A short distance further, a diminutive tree stood laden with roses-red apples, no larger than cherries, dangling within reach. I plucked one and took a bite. The flavor was exquisite. Just as I reached for a second, a burst of clear, musical laughter shattered the quiet, startling me with pure joy.

"He likes our apples!" the voices chimed, rising in a chorus. "He likes our apples! He's a good giant! A fine giant!"

A dissenting voice cut through the praise. "He *is* a giant!"

"But size doesn't define worth!" another retorted, wise beyond their years. "It can only lead to stupidity if you let it!"

I propped myself up on an elbow, staring. Surrounding me was a motley assembly of children, ranging from toddlers learning to walk to lads of thirteen or fourteen. A select group of three or four stood slightly apart, observing with a calmness that contrasted sharply with the frenzied excitement of the mob. Their chatter flowed in rapid, complex clusters—debating, arguing, and debating again with the spirited intellect of adults, yet tempered by an infectious merriment and courtesy.

I deduced quickly that by reaching for a second apple, they believed I had proven my affection for the first. The logic eluded me, though I marveled at the caution offered by even the youngest among them. I kept silent, unwilling to frighten them; listening yielded far more wisdom than speaking would. Almost miraculously, I understood nearly every word they spoke. It was a testament to the ancient truth: understanding is often easier than loving.

Suddenly, the crowd dispersed. A sweet-faced boy, looking both innocent and mischievous, stepped forward holding a large, green apple. The noise died instantly, replaced by a hushed, expectant silence.

"Eat, good giant," he whispered.

I sat upright, accepted the offering with a grateful smile, and prepared to bite. But the moment tooth met skin, I spat the apple out and hurled it far away.

Instantly, the cry erupted again. "He loves us! He loves us!" The children swarmed me, nearly burying me under a tide of hugs. They kissed my cheeks, grabbed my hands, and scrambled up my limbs, clinging to my head and neck. Overwhelmed by the sheer number of these adorable little spirits, I collapsed onto the earth.

"Good giant!" they trilled. "We knew you'd return! You're dear and strong!"

Their chatter resumed, a joyful cacophony rising from hundreds of tiny throats. Then, abruptly, silence returned. Those nearby retreated; those climbing me climbed down, their faces shifting from mirth to genuine concern.

"Hurry up, good giant!" a little girl urged, pointing up the slope. "Quickly! Quickly! He saw you toss his apple!"

Before her words finished, I was on my feet. Standing atop the incline was a grotesque figure, perhaps a foot taller than me, dressed in a manner that suggested a jester gone wrong. He radiated hostility, yet I sensed no immediate threat—he carried no weapon, and my captors had vanished without a trace.

He began his descent, and I followed, seeking higher ground. As he neared, he growled like a wounded beast. Once we reached a level patch, I halted and waited. Drawing near, he extended a hand. Instinctively, I reached for it, but he snatched it back, feigning a blow before offering it again. The message was clear: he demanded the apple I had discarded. I recoiled, making a grimace of distaste and shaking my head.

His response was a howl of fury. "How dare you insult my gift?" he roared, implying I questioned the quality of his tree.

"One rotten apple grows on the finest branch," I replied, hoping to de-escalate.

Whether he grasped the metaphor remained unclear. He lunged forward, and I braced myself. Yet, a second giant, identical in appearance, emerged from behind me, closing the gap. As the first attacked, I parried with a solid strike to his face, but the second landed a crushing blow to the back of my skull. Together, they overpowered me.

They dragged me into the woods above the valley, where their tribe dwelled in squalid huts constructed of fallen boughs and rough stones. Inside one, they shoved me down and beat me. A woman watched with cold indifference. I realized then that gender meant little to them; they were indistinguishable in form and function, resembling a fungoid race possessed of just enough cognition to manifest anger and greed. Their diet of tubers, bulbs, and harsh fruits repulsed me, yet they took offense at any sign of disdain. Refusing to eat, I endured cuffs and kicks.

That night, I lay paralyzed on the floor, barely moving, yet I managed a slumber that brought some refreshment. At dawn, they marched me back to the valley. Binding my feet with a long rope, they forced me to take a flat stone with a jagged edge in my left hand. When I switched it to my right, they kicked me; they placed it back in my left and signaled that I must strip the bark from every branch devoid of fruit. After another kick, they abandoned me.

I set to this miserable task, hoping that compliance might buy me solitude—to observe, to plan, to escape. Fortunately, a dwarf-tree grew mere paces away. Every minute or so, I plucked a small fruit, chewing it with relish. It sustained me, restoring strength and hope amidst the drudgery.
