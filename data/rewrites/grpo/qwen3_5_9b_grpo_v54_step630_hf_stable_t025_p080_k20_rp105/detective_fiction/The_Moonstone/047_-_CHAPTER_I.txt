The narrative presented in the Journal of Ezra Jennings required but few words of completion from my own pen. Upon awakening on the morning of the twenty-sixth, I found myself utterly devoid of memory regarding the events that had transpired under the drug's influence, stretching from the moment the opium seized my faculties until I opened my eyes in Rachel's sitting-room.

Regarding the subsequent hours, I deemed a detailed account unnecessary. Suffice it to say that Rachel and I achieved a profound mutual understanding before a single explanatory word was spoken. Neither of us felt compelled to justify the extraordinary speed of our reconciliation. Those who recall their own moments of passionate attachment will understand the dynamic that unfolded after Ezra Jennings secured the sitting-room door; I witnessed it, and so did Rachel.

However, I must note that our reunion would have been discovered by Mrs. Merridew had it not been for Rachel's quick wit. Hearing the old lady's footsteps in the corridor, Rachel rushed out to intercept her. I heard Mrs. Merridew ask, "What is the matter?" and Rachel's immediate reply, "The explosion!" Mrs. Merridew was gently guided into the garden, safely away from the impending shock. Upon her return to the house, she encountered me in the hall, expressing a profound admiration for the advancements in science since her school days. "Explosions, Mr. Blake, are infinitely milder than they were," she remarked. "I barely heard Mr. Jennings's explosion from the garden, and there is no lingering smell now! I must apologize to your medical friend; he has managed it beautifully!"

Thus, after overcoming the skepticism of Betteredge and Mr. Bruff, Ezra Jennings also subdued Mrs. Merridew. There remains a vast reservoir of liberal sentiment in the world, after all!

At breakfast, Mr. Bruff openly expressed his desire for me to accompany him to London by the morning train. The watch kept at the bank, and the potential results of our inquiry, appealed irresistibly to Rachel's curiosity. She immediately decided to join us, provided Mrs. Merridew had no objection, so she might be within reach of the earliest news. Mrs. Merridew, gratified by the mild nature of the explosion, proved pliable and indulgent. Betteredge was accordingly informed that we were all four to travel together. I had expected him to volunteer, but Rachel wisely assigned him the task of completing the house's refurnishing. Burdened with domestic responsibilities, he was too occupied to succumb to the "detective-fever" he might otherwise have felt.

Our sole regret in departing was the necessity of parting with Ezra Jennings more abruptly than we wished. It was impossible to persuade him to come; I could only promise to write, and Rachel could only insist on his visiting her upon her return to Yorkshire. While we hoped to meet again within months, there was a poignant sadness in seeing our dearest friend standing alone on the platform as the train departed.

Upon arriving in London, Mr. Bruff was intercepted at the terminus by a small boy dressed in threadbare black cloth, distinguished by the extraordinary prominence of his eyes, which seemed to roll loosely within their sockets. After listening to the boy, Mr. Bruff asked the ladies if they would excuse his escorting them back to Portland Place. I had barely promised Rachel that I would return to explain everything before Mr. Bruff seized my arm and hurried me into a cab. The boy with the ill-secured eyes took his place on the box beside the driver, who was directed to Lombard Street.

"News from the bank?" I asked as we started.

"News of Mr. Luker," Mr. Bruff replied. "An hour ago, he was seen leaving his house in Lambeth in a cab, accompanied by two men recognized by my men as plainclothes police. If Mr. Luker's dread of the Indians motivates this precaution, the inference is plain: he intends to remove the Diamond from the bank."

"And we are going to the bank to see what comes of it?"

"Yes—or to hear what has come of it, if it is all over by now. Did you notice my boy on the box?"

"I noticed his eyes."

Mr. Bruff laughed. "They call him 'Gooseberry' at the office. I employ him for errands, and I wish my clerks who have nicknamed him were as reliable as he is. Despite his eyes, Gooseberry is one of the sharpest boys in London, Mr. Blake."

Twenty minutes to five, we drew up before the bank. Gooseberry looked longingly at his master as the cab door opened.

"Do you want to come in too?" Mr. Bruff asked kindly. "Come in, and keep at my heels. He's as quick as lightning," he whispered to me. "Two words suffice with Gooseberry where twenty would be needed with another boy."

We entered the bank. The outer office was crowded with people waiting to transact business before the five o'clock closing. Two men approached Mr. Bruff immediately.

"Well," asked the lawyer. "Have you seen him?"

"He passed us here half an hour since and went into the inner office."

"Has he not come out again?"

"No, sir."

Mr. Bruff turned to me. "Let us wait."

I scanned the crowd for the three Indians. None were visible. The only person with a noticeably dark complexion was a tall man in a pilot coat and round hat, resembling a sailor. Could he be one of them in disguise? Impossible; he was far too tall, and his face, partially hidden by a bushy black beard, was twice the breadth of any of the Indians'.

"They must have a spy somewhere," Mr. Bruff muttered, glancing at the dark sailor. "And he may be the man."

Before he could elaborate, his coat-tail was pulled by his attendant sprite, Gooseberry. Mr. Bruff looked where the boy pointed. "Hush!" he said. "Here is Mr. Luker!"

The money-lender emerged from the inner regions, flanked by his two guardian policemen.

"Keep your eye on him," Mr. Bruff whispered. "If he passes the Diamond to anyone, he will pass it here."

Ignoring us, Mr. Luker slowly navigated the crowd, moving from the thickest to the thinnest parts. I distinctly saw his hand move as he passed a short, stout man in a sober grey suit. The man started slightly and looked after him. Mr. Luker continued slowly to the door, where his guards positioned themselves on either side. They were followed by one of Mr. Bruff's men, and then they vanished from sight.

I looked at the lawyer and then signaled toward the man in the grey suit. "Yes!" Mr. Bruff whispered. "I saw it too!" He turned to search for his second man, who was nowhere to be seen, and then for Gooseberry, who had disappeared.

"What the devil does it mean?" Mr. Bruff said angrily. "They have both left us at the very moment we need them most."

It was the man in the grey suit's turn to transact business. He paid a cheque, received a receipt, and turned to leave.

"What is to be done?" Mr. Bruff asked. "_We_ can't degrade ourselves by following him."

"_I_ can!" I declared. "I wouldn't lose sight of that man for ten thousand pounds!"

"In that case," Mr. Bruff rejoined, "I wouldn't lose sight of _you_ for twice the money. A nice occupation for a man in my position," he muttered as we followed the stranger out. "For Heaven's sake don't mention it. I should be ruined if it were known."

The man in the grey suit boarded an omnibus heading west. We followed. Mr. Bruff, despite his age, still possessed latent reserves of youth; I assert it positively, he blushed when he took his seat. The man alighted in Oxford Street and entered a chemist's shop.

"My chemist!" Mr. Bruff exclaimed. "I am afraid we have made a mistake."

We entered. Mr. Bruff and the proprietor exchanged a few words in private. The lawyer returned with a crestfallen face.

"It's greatly to our credit," he said, taking my arm. "That's one comfort!"

"What is to our credit?" I asked.

"Mr. Blake! You and I are the two worst amateur detectives that ever tried their hands at the trade. The man in the grey suit has been in the chemist's service for thirty years. He was sent to the bank to pay money to his master's account—and he knows no more of the Moonstone than the babe unborn."

I asked what was next.

"Come back to my office," Mr. Bruff said. "Gooseberry and my second man have evidently followed someone else. Let us hope they had their eyes about them!"

At Gray's Inn Square, the second man had already arrived, having waited over a quarter of an hour.

"Well!" Mr. Bruff asked. "What's your news?"

"I am sorry to say, sir," the man replied, "that I have made a mistake. I could have sworn I saw Mr. Luker pass something to an elderly gentleman in a light-colored paletot. The elderly gentleman turns out to be a most respectable master iron-monger in Eastcheap."

"Where is Gooseberry?" Mr. Bruff asked resignedly.

The man stared. "I don't know, sir. I have seen nothing of him since I left the bank."

Mr. Bruff dismissed him. "One of two things," he told me. "Either Gooseberry has run away, or he is hunting on his own account. What do you say to dining here, on the chance the boy returns in an hour or two? I have good wine in the cellar and we can get a chop from the coffee-house."

We dined at Mr. Bruff's chambers. Before the cloth was removed, a person was announced. Was it Gooseberry? No, it was the man employed to follow Mr. Luker. His report presented no feature of interest. Mr. Luker had returned home, dismissed his guards, and barred his doors. No signs of the Indians were visible. Mr. Bruff dismissed the man for the night.

"Do you think Mr. Luker has taken the Moonstone home?" I asked.

"Not he," said Mr. Bruff. "He would never have dismissed his two policemen if he had run the risk of keeping the Diamond in his own house again."

We waited another half-hour for the boy in vain. It was time for Mr. Bruff to go to Hampstead and for me to return to Rachel in Portland Place. I left my card with the porter, instructing him to deliver a note stating I would be at my lodgings at half-past ten, to be given to the boy if he returned.

Some men have a knack of keeping appointments; others, like me, have a knack of missing them. I spent the evening at Portland Place on the same seat as Rachel, in a room forty feet long with Mrs. Merridew at the far end. Does anyone wonder I arrived home at half-past twelve instead of half-past ten? How heartless that person must be! And how earnestly I hope I may never make that person's acquaintance!

My servant handed me a morsel of paper. In neat legal handwriting, it read: "If you please, sir, I am getting sleepy. I will come back tomorrow morning, between nine and ten." Inquiry revealed that the boy with extraordinary eyes had called, presented my card and message, waited an hour, fallen asleep and woken again, written a line for me, and gone home after informing the servant that "he was fit for nothing unless he got his night's rest."

At nine the next morning, I was ready for my visitor. At half-past nine, I heard steps outside. "Come in, Gooseberry!" I called. "Thank you, sir," answered a grave and melancholy voice. The door opened. I started to my feet and confronted—Sergeant Cuff!

"I thought I would look in here, Mr. Blake, on the chance of your being in town, before I wrote to Yorkshire," the Sergeant said.

He was as dreary and lean as ever. His eyes retained their old trick of "looking as if they expected something more from you than you were aware of yourself." Yet, regarding dress, the great Cuff was changed beyond recognition. He wore a broad-brimmed white hat, a light shooting jacket, white trousers, and drab gaiters, carrying a stout oak stick. His entire aim seemed to be to look as if he had lived in the country all his life. When I complimented him on his metamorphosis, he declined to take it as a joke, complaining gravely of London's noises and smells. I offered him breakfast; the innocent countryman was quite shocked. His breakfast hour was half-past six, and he went to bed with the cocks and hens!

"I only got back from Ireland last night," the Sergeant said, turning to the practical object of his visit. "Before I went to bed, I read your letter telling me what has happened since my inquiry after the Diamond was suspended last year. There's only one thing to be said about the matter on my side. I completely mistook my case. How any man living was to have seen things in their true light in such a situation, I don't profess to know. But that doesn't alter the facts. I own that I made a mess of it. Not the first mess, Mr. Blake, which has distinguished my professional career! It's only in books that the officers of the detective force are superior to the weakness of making a mistake."

"You have come in the nick of time to recover your reputation," I said.

"I beg your pardon, Mr. Blake," he rejoined. "Now that I have retired from business, I don't care a straw about my reputation. I have done with my reputation, thank God! I am here, sir, in grateful remembrance of the late Lady Verinder's liberality to me. I will go back to my old work—if you want me, and if you will trust me—on that consideration, and on no other.
