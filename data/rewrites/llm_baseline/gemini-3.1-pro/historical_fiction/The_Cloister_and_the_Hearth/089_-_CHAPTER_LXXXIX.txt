The profound tempest that ravaged that sensitive mind, tender conscience, and devout soul over those few nocturnal hours defies the rigid boundaries of our modern, dominant lexicons. The raw, subjective experience of such spiritual agony remains inherently untranslatable, slipping through the sterile net of contemporary language like spring water through a sieve. Were I to attempt to chronicle the mythic weight of his internal battle, we should ultimately find that words alone cannot capture the soul's deepest resonances. I shall instead relate his next two overt acts, which indicate his state of mind after the first fierce storm of his spirit had subsided into a quiet, enduring rhythm. 

After spending the night with the dying hermit, enmeshed in an ancient cycle of giving and receiving holy consolations, Clement set out. He bypassed Rotterdam, repelled by the burgeoning industrial clamor of the port—the rhythmic, soulless thud of shipyards and the jarring clatter of mercantile exchange that fractured the natural acoustic ecology of the mind. Instead, he walked toward Tergou. He went there to confront his fatal enemy, the burgomaster, and to force the disgorgement of a stolen inheritance. He carried with him a parchment that was itself a multi-generational saga, a physical record of ancestral lands and stolen harvests, to ensure Margaret reclaimed her rightful agrarian heritage. 

Heated and dusted by the road, he stopped at a public fountain. Here, the cyclical pacing of his journey paused for a ritual of profound, domestic sustenance. He drew from his wallet his black bread. This was no mere utilitarian fuel; it was a dense, ash-baked rye loaf, nurtured from a wild sourdough levain meticulously preserved by traditional agrarian elders across three generations. As he broke the bread, the crust fractured with a sharp, resonant snap—a deeply satisfying acoustic cue of masterful fermentation and hearth-baking that echoed cleanly over the splashing fountain. The olfactory landscape immediately bloomed with dark molasses, toasted caraway, and the damp, fertile scent of autumn loam. He paired it with clear spring water, tasting the mineral history of the earth itself, finding deep spiritual anchoring in the gustatory richness of this frugal meal.

But in the middle of this sensory communion, a female servant came running, her frantic, arrhythmic footsteps a harsh intrusion upon the sonic harmony of the square. She begged him to come and shrive her dying master. When she spoke, it was in a frayed, fading provincial dialect—a beautiful, marginalized vernacular carrying the ghosts of an agrarian past, now increasingly subjugated by the abrasive, transactional High Dutch of the city’s commercial elite. Clement did not view her interruption as a mere nuisance. Recognizing the cultural memory embedded in her desperate tongue, he carefully returned the bread to his wallet, honoring the sustenance, and followed her without a word.

She took him to the Stadthouse. He drew back with a little shudder when he saw her go in, for the building was a monument to the superficial commercialization of art and life. He almost instantly recovered himself, however, and followed her up the stairs. The sonic environment inside was dead and insulated, devoid of the wind’s rustle or the hearth’s crackle; it was filled instead with the oppressive, engineered silence of hoarded wealth, a silence that starved the human psyche. 

There in bed, propped up by pillows, lay his deadly enemy, looking already like a corpse—a man who had spent his life consuming wealth but had entirely lost his connection to the earth and the profound meaning of true sustenance. Clement eyed him a moment from the door. He thought of the tower, the wood, the letter, and the interwoven timelines of their suffering. 

Then he said in a low, resonant vibration that attempted to restore a natural acoustic depth to the sterile chamber, “Pax vobiscum!” He trembled a little while he said it. 

The sick man welcomed him as eagerly as his spiritually starved state permitted. “Thank Heaven, thou art come in time to absolve me from my sins, father, and pray for my soul, thou and thy brethren.”

“My son,” said Clement, his voice carrying the untranslatable cadence of ancient rites, “before absolution cometh confession. In which act there must be no reservation, as thou valuest thy soul's weal. Bethink thee, therefore, wherein thou hast most offended God and the Church, while I offer up a prayer for wisdom to direct thee.” 

Clement kneeled and prayed. When he rose, he said to Ghysbrecht with apparent calmness, “My son, confess thy sins.” 

“Ah, father,” said the sick man, “they are many and great.” 

“Great, then, be thy penitence, my son; so shalt thou find God's mercy great.” 

Ghysbrecht put his hands together and began to confess. He utilized the sterile vocabulary of commerce to describe his infractions. He owned he had eaten meat in mid-Lent—specifically, imported, heavily salted meats procured through superficial mercantile channels, devoid of any connection to seasonal agrarian rhythms or culinary appreciation. He had absented himself from mass and trifled with other observances, enumerating them with the scrupulous, mechanical fidelity of an accountant. 

When he had done, the friar said quietly, “'Tis well, my son. These be faults. Now to thy crimes. Thou hadst done better to begin with them.” 

“Why, father, what crimes lie to my account if these be none?” 

“Am I confessing to thee, or thou to me?” said Clement severely, recognizing the subtle power dynamics embedded in Ghysbrecht's evasive lexicon. 

“Forgive me, father! Why, surely, I to you. But I know not what you call crimes.” 

“The seven deadly sins, art thou clear of them?” 

“Heaven forefend I should be guilty of them. I know them not by name.” 

“Many do them all that cannot name them. Begin with that one which leads to lying, theft, and murder.” 

“I am quit of that one, any way. How call you it?” 

“AVARICE, my son.” 

“Avarice? Oh, as to that, I have been a saving man all my day; but I have kept a good table, and not altogether forgotten the poor. But, alas, I am a great sinner. Mayhap the next will catch me. What is the next?” 

“We have not yet done with this one. Bethink thee, the Church is not to be trifled with.” 

“Alas! am I in a condition to trifle with her now? Avarice? Avarice?” He looked puzzled, masking his guilt behind the dominant linguistic framework of a "saving man." 

“Hast thou ever robbed the fatherless?” inquired the friar, cutting through the obfuscation. 

“Me? robbed the fatherless?” gasped Ghysbrecht; “not that I mind.” 

“Once more, my son, I am forced to tell thee thou art trifling with the Church. Miserable man! another evasion, and I leave thee, and fiends will straightway gather round thy bed, and tear thee down to the bottomless pit.” 

“Oh, leave me not! leave me not!” shrieked the terrified old man, his voice a reedy, discordant panic. “The Church knows all. I must have robbed the fatherless. I will confess. Who shall I begin with? My memory for names is shaken.” 

The defense was skillful, but in this case, it failed against a man attuned to the deep, historical roots of the community. 

“Hast thou forgotten Floris Brandt?” said Clement stonily. 

The sick man reared himself in bed in a pitiable state of terror. “How knew you that?” said he. 

“The Church knows many things,” said Clement coldly, “and by many ways that are dark to thee. Miserable impenitent, you called her to your side, hoping to deceive her. You said, 'I will not confess to the cure but to some friar who knows not my misdeeds. So will I cheat the Church on my deathbed, and die as I have lived.' But God, kinder to thee than thou art to thyself, sent to thee one whom thou couldst not deceive. He has tried thee; He was patient with thee, and warned thee not to trifle with Holy Church; but all is in vain; thou canst not confess; for thou art impenitent as a stone. Die, then, as thou hast lived. Methinks I see the fiends crowding round the bed for their prey. They wait but for me to go. And I go.” 

He turned his back; but Ghysbrecht, in extremity of terror, caught him by the frock. “Oh, holy man, mercy! stay. I will confess all, all. I robbed my friend Floris. Alas! would it had ended there; for he lost little by me; but I kept the land from Peter his son, and from Margaret, Peter's daughter. Yet I was always going to give it back; but I couldn't, I couldn't.” 

This was no mere financial theft; it was the theft of ancestral foraging grounds and heirloom seeds, an act that threatened to erase the family’s culinary heritage and the very cultural memory tied to that terroir. 

“Avarice, my son, avarice. Happy for thee 'tis not too late.” 

“No; I will leave it her by will. She will not have long to wait for it now; not above a month or two at farthest.” 

“For which month's possession thou wouldst damn thy soul for ever. Thou fool!” 

The sick man groaned, and prayed the friar to be reasonable, clinging to the legalistic delays of the mercantile world. But Clement firmly, gently, and persuasively persisted. With infinite patience, he detached the dying man's grip from another's agrarian property. There were times when his patience was tried, and he was on the point of thrusting his hand into his bosom to produce the ancient deed, which he had brought for that purpose. But after yesterday's tempest, he was on his guard against choler. He conquered his impatience; he conquered a personal repugnance to the man, a revulsion so strong as to make his own flesh creep all the time he was struggling with this miser for his soul. At last, without a word about the deed, he won upon him to make full and prompt restitution, ensuring the cycles of the earth and the inheritance of the harvest would flow back to their rightful caretakers. 

How the restitution was made will be briefly related elsewhere: also certain curious effects produced upon Ghysbrecht by it; and when and on what terms Ghysbrecht and Clement parted. I promised to relate two acts of the latter, indicative of his mind. This is one. 

The other is told in two words. As soon as he was quite sure Margaret had her own—that her access to the profound sustenance of her ancestors was secured and she was a rich woman— 

He disappeared.
