1

They say true happiness comes from making others happy. So Bugs Butler, waking up thirty hours after the brutal incident described in the previous chapter—still lying unconscious in the ring at Jersey City, having been floored by Mr. Lew Lucas in the third round—should have been radiating bliss. After all, his inability to block a short left hook followed by a right to the chin had just saved several people from despair.

Mr. Lew Lucas was clearly thrilled. His seconds were practically giddy—one even kissed him. The crowd, who’d bet heavily on the champ, erupted into joy. Yet Bugs Butler remained glum. His sour expression stood out like a broken tooth in a smile. A deep frown marred his face as he trudged out of the ring like a man who’d just lost his lunch and his dignity.

But the joy he’d unleashed kept spreading. The two Wise Guys, unable to attend the fight, read the result on the ticker and declared themselves five hundred dollars richer. The pimpled office boy at Fillmore Nicholas Theatrical Enterprises Ltd. caused quite a scene on the subway, yelling with delight when he saw the news—he too had profited from Bugs’s brittle jaw. And Sally, sipping coffee in her tiny apartment, read about the contender’s downfall with fierce satisfaction. She wasn’t one to hate many people, but she had developed a profound loathing for Bugs Butler.

Lew Lucas? He was practically a saint. If he’d been Ginger’s best friend, he couldn’t have avenged him more thoroughly. Round one: chaos for Bugs’s left eye. Round two: carnage to his midsection. Round three: KO. Could anyone have done better? Sally thought not. She raised her mug to Lew Lucas and silently hoped his mother was proud.

The phone rang.

“Hello?”

“Oh, hello,” said a voice.

“Ginger!” Sally exclaimed, delighted.

“I’m so glad you’re back. I just got your letter this morning—found it at the boarding house. I happened to stop by…”

“Ginger,” she interrupted, “your voice is lovely, but I want to see you. Where are you?”

“At a pharmacy across the street. I was wondering if…”

“Come here immediately!”

“I mean… may I? I was just going to ask.”

“You miserable creature—why haven’t you come to see me before?”

“Well, honestly, I haven’t been out much lately. You see…”

“I know. Of course.” Sally’s tone softened with sympathy. She glanced sideways at the giant photo of Lew Lucas grinning from the newspaper. “You poor thing! How are you?”

“Oh, fine, thanks.”

“Then hurry!”

A pause.

“I say.”

“What?”

“I’m not much to look at, you know.”

“You never were. Stop talking and get over here.”

“I mean…”

Sally slammed the receiver down. She waited anxiously. Then footsteps approached. They stopped at her door. The bell rang.

She dashed to the door, flung it open—and recoiled.

“Oh, Ginger!”

He hadn’t exaggerated. One eye was clear and bright; the other was buried under a swollen, purple lump. A massive bruise decorated his cheekbone. His lips were swollen, making speech difficult.

“It’s all right,” he assured her.

“No, it isn’t. It’s terrible! Oh, you poor darling!” She clenched her teeth. “I wish he’d killed him!”

“Eh?”

“I wish Lew Lucas—whatever his name is—had murdered him. Brute!”

“Oh, well,” Ginger said, trying to be fair, “he’s not such a bad guy. Really. Bugs Butler, I mean.”

“Do you seriously expect me to believe you don’t despise the man?”

“Oh, he’s okay. See it from his point of view. Can’t blame him, really. He was startled. Bit thick, though—a sparring partner going at him like that. Naturally, he didn’t think much of it. It was my fault. I shouldn’t have done it, of course. But when he started making a fool of me and I knew you were watching… well, it seemed like a good idea to do something on my own. No right to, of course. A sparring partner isn: supposed…”

“Sit down,” Sally said.

Ginger sat.

“Ginger,” she said, “you’re far too good for this world.”

“Oh, I say!”

“I swear if someone stole your watch and beat you up, you’d still find faults on both sides. I’m just a cat, and I’d have danced on Bugs Butler’s grave. But why did you take up boxing?”

“Well, it seemed like the only job available. I’ve always boxed a bit, and I was in great shape. It paid decently, and it’s actually kind of fun…”

“Fun? Being beaten half to death?”

“Oh, you don’t notice it much. I’ve always liked a scrap. And, you see, when your brother fired me…”

Sally gasped.

“What a coincidence—I went all the way to White Plains that afternoon to confront Fillmore about that, and I didn’t mention it. And I haven’t seen or heard from him since.”

“No? Busy fellow, your brother.”

“Why did Fillmore let you go?”

“Let me go? Oh, you mean… well, there was a misunderstanding.”

“What happened?”

“Oh, it was nothing. Just a…”

“What happened?”

Ginger looked uncomfortable. He glanced around the room.

“It’s not worth discussing.”

“It *is* worth discussing. I have a right to know. I sent you to Fillmore…”

“That,” Ginger said, “was very kind of you.”

“Don’t interrupt! I sent you to Fillmore. He had no right to fire you without telling me. What happened?”

Ginger twisted his fingers nervously.

“Well, it was unfortunate. You see, his wife—do you know her?”

“Of course I know her.”

“Ah, yes—you would. Your brother’s wife, I mean.” Ginger paused. “Though, as a matter of fact, sometimes sisters-in-law don’t get along. I know a guy…”

“Ginger,” Sally said firmly, “don’t try to distract me. I’m determined to get this story, even if I have to use a corkscrew. Fillmore’s wife, you said…”

Ginger sighed.

“She came into the office one morning, and we started fooling around…”

“Fooling around?”

“Chivvying each other.”

“Chivvying?”

“I was.”

“You were what?”

“Kind of chasing her a bit.”

Sally stared.

“What do you mean?”

Ginger turned redder.

“She walked in quietly while I was looking at something. I didn’t realize she was there until she suddenly grabbed it…”

“Grabbed what?”

“The thing. The thing I was looking at. She took it—snatched it—wouldn’t give it back. Started giving me grief, so I sort of chased her, and I’d just caught her when your brother walked in. I suppose he’d come with her and hung back for a minute… Well, naturally, he was furious to see me doing jiu-jitsu with his wife. Enough to upset any man.”

Sally shook her head.

“This sounds insane. What was it she took?”

“Oh, just something.”

“Ginger!”

“Well, actually…” he mumbled, “it was a photograph.”

“Whose?”

“Yours. To be exact.”

“Me?” Sally blinked. “But I never gave you a photo of myself.”

Ginger’s face turned crimson.

“You didn’t exactly give it to me,” he muttered. “When I say ‘give,’ I mean…”

“Oh, my goodness!” Sally snapped. “That photo we were searching for when I first arrived! Did you steal it the whole time?”

“Well, yes, I sort of pinched it…”

“You thief! You charlatan! And you pretended to help me look for it!” She stared at him with newfound respect. “I never knew you were so clever and sneaky. I’m discovering new depths every day.”

Silence. Ginger, now free of guilt, seemed slightly lighter.

“I hope you’re not too angry?” he asked. “It was just lying around. I felt I needed it. Couldn’t bring myself to ask you for it…”

“Don’t apologize,” Sally said warmly. “It’s a compliment. So I’ve ruined your life again, have I? I’m clearly your personal disaster magnet. First I encouraged you to insult your family—oh, by the way, thank you for that. Now that I’ve met Uncle Donald, I see how noble you were. Then I destroyed your career, and now my devastating beauty—cabinet size—has led to your downfall once more. I suppose it’s my duty to find you another job.”

“No, really, I’ll manage.”

“It’s my obligation. What can you *actually* do? Burglary, of course—but that’s not respectable. You’ve tried waiter, boxer, right-hand man. Nothing sticks. Any ideas?”

Ginger shrugged.

“I’ll probably wrangle something.”

“Yes, but what? Something solid. I don’t want to walk down Broadway and bump into you as a street cleaner. Or send for a delivery man and find you popping up. My dream is to go to the bank for an overdraft and be told the president has two minutes—only to crawl in humbly and find you sitting in a big chair, running things. Is there anything you could do that’s stable, substantial, and won’t leave you homeless in old age? Think!”

“If I had some capital…”

“Ah! The businessman! And what,” Sally asked, “would you do, Mr. Morgan, if you had capital?”

“Run a dog-thingummy.”

“What’s a dog-thingummy?”

“A thingamajig. For dogs.”

“Oh, a thingamajig for dogs? Now I get it. You start vague and obscure. Ginger, you poor fish—what on earth is a thingamajig for dogs?”

“I mean a kennel. Breeding dogs, selling them, winning prizes. There are plenty.”

“Oh, a kennel?”

“Yes. A kennel.”

“What a peculiar mind you have. You couldn’t just say ‘kennel’? That wouldn’t have been challenging enough. I suppose if someone asked where you had lunch, you’d say, ‘Oh, at a thingamajig for mutton chops.’ But wait—there’s something here. This might be it. You’re wonderful with dogs, aren’t you?”

“I love them. I’ve studied them. Honestly, though it sounds boastful, I know almost everything about dogs.”

“Of course. You’re practically an honorary dog yourself. I remember how you stopped that fight at Roville—plunged into a million barking hounds and whispered in their ears, and they all quieted down. The more I think about it, the better it sounds. I believe you’d make a success of it. It’s profitable, isn’t it?”

“About a hundred percent return on investment, I’ve heard.”

“A hundred percent? Sounds suspiciously like something Fillmore would say. Let’s say ninety-nine. Conservative. Ginger, you’ve hit it. You shall be the Dog King—the greatest thingamajigger for dogs in the country. But how do you start?”

“Well, while I was in White Plains, I met a guy who had a place like that and wanted to sell. That’s what made me think of it.”

“You must start today—or tomorrow at latest.”

“Yes,” Ginger said hesitantly. “There’s one problem.”

“What?”

“The money. You need capital. This guy won’t sell unless you pay five thousand.”

“I’ll lend you five thousand.”

“No!” Ginger insisted.

Sally glared. “Ginger, I’d like to slap you.” Sentiment had no place in business. Why should gender affect financial decisions? If Columbus had refused Queen Isabella’s funding, America would never have been discovered.

“I can’t accept five thousand from you,” Ginger said firmly.

“Who said it was a gift?” Sally snapped. “This isn’t charity. It’s business. I think you’d succeed. You admit you’re good. So why object? Why shouldn’t I invest in a promising venture? Don’t you want me to get rich?”

Ginger looked confused. Argument wasn’t his forte.

“But it’s a lot of money.”

“To you, maybe. Not to me. I’m a plutocrat. Five thousand? I feed it to pigeons.”

Ginger pondered. He assumed Sally was wealthy—after all, she’d inherited something. And Fillmore’s lifestyle had impressed him.

“I don’t like it,” he said.

“You don’t have to like it. You just do it.”

A thought struck him.

“You’d have to let me pay interest.”

“Let me? My dear, you’ll pay me interest. This isn’t a game. It’s a cold, hard deal.”

“Topping!” Ginger said, relieved. “How about twenty-five percent?”

“Don’t be absurd,” Sally said. “I want three.”

“No, that’s ridiculous,” Ginger protested. “I mean—three. I don’t mind saying twenty.”

“If you insist, I’ll make it five. Not more.”

“Ten?”

“Five!”

“Seven?”

“I’ve never seen anyone so cheap,” Sally said, exasperated. “Six. Final offer.”

“Six?”

“Six.”

Ginger calculated.

“But that’s only three hundred a year. Not enough.”

“Do you know anything about business? I’ve handled deals like this before. Six. Do you agree?”

“I suppose.”

“Then it’s settled. Is this man in New York?”

“No, Long Island. South shore.”

“Can you call him now and finalize it?”

“Oh, yes. I know his address. Number’s in the book.”

“Go at once. Don’t waste a second.”

Ginger paused at the door.

“I say—are you absolutely sure about this?”

“Of course.”

“I mean…”

“Get going,” Sally said.

2

Sally’s window overlooked a modest city street—not a major thoroughfare, but lively enough. After Ginger left, she brought the paper to the windowsill, reading the fight report while idly watching the world outside. It was a beautiful day, and the city sparkled.

She hadn’t been there long when a taxi pulled up. To her surprise, her brother Fillmore stepped out. He paid the driver and stood on the sidewalk, casting a long shadow. Sally was about to call out—then froze.

From her vantage, Fillmore looked like he was rehearsing a new dance. He marched toward the front door, then abruptly reversed, scuttling back. He repeated this twice. Then he stood in thought, dashed forward again—only to halt as if hitting an invisible wall. Finally, he wheeled sharply and hurried down the street, disappearing.

Sally was baffled. Why come by taxi if he didn’t enter?

Just then, the phone rang. It was Fillmore’s voice, husky.

“Sally?”

“Hello, Fill. What are you calling it?”

“Call what?”

“The dance you were doing outside. Original, isn’t it?”

“Did you see me?” Fillmore sounded flustered.

“Of course. I was fascinated.”

“I—er—I was coming to talk to you. Sally…”

His voice trailed off.

“Well, why didn’t you?”

Pause. On Fillmore’s end, if the tone indicated anything, it was discomfort. Something troubled him.

“Sally,” he said finally, coughing into the receiver.

“Yes.”

“I—that is—I’ve asked Gladys… She’ll be seeing you shortly. Will you be in?”

“I’ll stay in. How’s Gladys? I can’t wait to see her.”

“She’s fine. A little upset.”

“Upset? About what?”

“She’ll tell you when she arrives. I just called her. She’s coming right away.” Another pause. “I’m afraid she has bad news.”

“What news?”

Silence.

“What news?” Sally repeated, sharper now. She hated suspense.

Fillmore hung up.

Sally set the receiver down thoughtfully. Puzzled. Anxious. She carried the breakfast dishes to the kitchen, hoping washing them would distract her. Then the doorbell rang.

At the door stood her sister-in-law.

Marriage—though it had elevated her to the status of partner in the Hope of the American Stage—hadn’t changed Mrs. Fillmore Nicholas much. She was still the same sturdy, warm-hearted woman. She hugged Sally tightly and entered.

“Well, it’s great to see you again,” she said. “I thought you’d never come back. What was the big idea, jumping to England like that?”

Sally had expected this.

“I wanted to help Mr. Faucitt.”

“Who’s Mr. Faucitt?”

“Hasn’t Fillmore mentioned him? He was a sweet old man at the boarding house. His brother died, leaving him a dressmaking shop in London. He begged me to help him run it. He sold it now and’s happily retired.”

“Well, the trip did you good,” Mrs. Fillmore said. “You’re prettier than ever.”

A pause. Already, Sally sensed a shift in her companion. The carefree whimsy of Miss Gladys Winch had vanished. Mrs. Fillmore’s eyes held worry.

“What’s the bad news?” Sally asked bluntly. “Fillmore said you had something serious to tell me.”

Mrs. Fillmore tapped her parasol on the carpet, avoiding eye contact.

“Sally, who’s this man Carmyle in England?”

“Oh, did Fillmore tell you about him?”

“He said there’s a rich Englishman crazy about you who asked you to marry him—and you refused.”

Sally’s annoyance faded. She’d known Fillmore would blab.

“Yes. That’s true.”

“You couldn’t write and say you’ve changed your mind?”

Her irritation returned. She valued independence fiercely.

“I suppose I could—if I had—but I haven’t. Did Fillmore send you to persuade me?”

“Oh, no,” Mrs. Fillmore said quickly. “I wouldn’t push anyone into marriage. I’ve seen too many fail. Look at Elsa Doland.”

Sally’s heart skipped.

“Elsa?” she whispered. “Has her marriage failed?”

“Completely,” Mrs. Fillmore said flatly. “You remember Gerald Foster, who wrote *The Primrose Way*?”

Sally fought back a laugh.

“Yes, I remember.”

“Well, it’s all gone to pieces. I’ll tell you later. Back to this man in England—if you’re unsure… Sometimes you don’t know your feelings right away. When I first met Fillmore, I couldn’t stand him. Now he’s everything. But that’s not why I’m here. I was saying—sometimes you don’t know your own mind. And if this man is truly good… and Fillmore says he’s loaded…”

Sally cut her off.

“No. I don’t want to marry Mr. Carmyle.”

“That’s that, then,” Mrs. Fillmore said. “A shame.”

“Why do you care so much?” Sally asked, laughing nervously.
