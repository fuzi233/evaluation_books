The Mother of Sorrows rose, muffling her face against the weight of her grief, and called for the Little Ones. They slept as though the night had frozen them in stone, unmoving and still. Yet the moment her voice broke the silence, they leaped to their feet, fresh as newly minted coins, their spirits undimmed by hours of rest. Merrily, they trailed behind her down the stairs, a tide of innocent joy. She led them to where the princess lay, her tears streaming silently onto her pillow like silver rain. The children's glad faces instantly grew grave. They gazed from the weeping princess out the window at the storm, then back to her, their innocence stricken by the scene.

"The sky is falling!" cried one, pointing upward.

"The white juice runs from the princess!" shrieked another, awestruck by the fluid cascading down her hollow cheeks.

"Are these rivers?" asked Odu, staring at the little streams flowing adown her face.

"Yes," answered Mara, "these are the most wonderful of all rivers."

"I thought rivers were bigger, rushing like a horde of Little Ones making terrible noise!" Odu returned, casting a glance at me—the only one among them who had heard tales of such mighty floods.

"Look at the rivers of the sky!" Mara urged gently. "See how they pour down to wake the waters beneath the earth! Soon they will flow everywhere, merry and loud, like thousands of happy children. Oh, how glad they will make you, Little Ones! You have never seen any, and you do not know how lovely water truly is!"

"That will be the gladness of the ground because the princess has grown good," Odu observed wisely. "See the gladness of the sky!"

"Are the rivers the gladness of the princess?" asked Luva, tilting his head. "They are not her blood, for they are not red!"

"They are the juice inside the juice," Mara explained simply.

Odu put a single finger to his eye, inspected it closely, and shook his head.

"The princess will not bite now!" declared Luva, relief washing over his features.

"No; she will never do that again," Mara replied. "--But now we must take her nearer home."

"Is that a nest?" asked Sozo, pointing to the large woven structure nearby.

"Yes; a very big nest. But we must take her to another place first."

"What is that?"

"It is the biggest room in all this world.—But I think it is going to be pulled down: it will soon be too full of little nests.—Go and get your clumsies."

"Please, are there any cats in it?"

"Not one. The nests are too full of lovely dreams for one cat to fit in."

"We shall be ready in a minute," said Odu, darting out, followed by all except Luva.

Lilith was now awake, listening with a sad smile that betrayed a deep weariness.

"But her rivers are running so fast!" Luva murmured, standing by her side, unable to tear his gaze from her face. "Her robe is all... I don't know what. The clumsies won't like it!"

"They won't mind it," Mara answered soothingly. "Those rivers are so clean that they wash the whole world clean."

I had fallen asleep by the fire, but for some time I had been awake, listening to the soft murmur of the children, and now I rose.

"It is time to mount, Mr. Vane," said our hostess.

"Tell me, please," I asked, "is there not a way by which to avoid the channels and the den of monsters?"

"There is an easy way across the river-bed, which I will show you," she answered; "but you must pass once more through the monsters."

"I fear for the children," I said, my heart tightening.

"Fear will not once come nigh them," she rejoined, her tone absolute.

We left the cottage. The beasts stood waiting about the door. Odu was already perched on the neck of one of the two elephants destined to carry the princess. I mounted Lona's horse; Mara brought her body, lifting it into my arms. When she emerged again with the princess, a cry of pure delight erupted from the children: she was no longer muffled! Gazing at her, entranced by her unearthly loveliness, the boys forgot to receive the princess from Mara. Instead, the elephants took Lilith tenderly with their trunks—one encircling her body, the other her knees—and, with Mara's assistance, laid her gently between them.

"Why does the princess want to go?" asked a small boy, bewildered. "She would stay good if she stayed here!"

"She wants to go, and she does not want to go: we are helping her," Mara answered. "She will not keep good here."

"What are you helping her to do?" the boy persisted.

"To go where she will get more help—help to open her hand, which has been closed for a thousand years."

"So long? Then she has learned to do without it: why should she open it now?"

"Because it is shut upon something that is not hers."

"Please, Lady Mara, may we have some of your very dry bread before we go?" Luva requested.

Mara smiled, bringing them four loaves and a great jug of water.

"We will eat as we go," they said, though they drank the water with evident delight.

"I think," remarked one of them, tasting the strength returning to his limbs, "it must be elephant-juice! It makes me so strong!"

We set out, the Lady of Sorrow walking with us, more beautiful than the sun, the white leopardess trailing faithfully behind. I thought she merely intended to guide us to the path across the channels, but soon I found she was accompanying us all the way. I would have dismounted to let her ride, but she would not allow it.

"I have no burden to carry," she said. "The children and I will walk together."

It was the loveliest of mornings; the sun shone with brilliance, and the wind blew with sweetness, yet they offered no comfort to the barren desert, for it held no water.

We crossed the channels without difficulty, the children gamboling around Mara all the way, but we did not reach the top of the ridge overlooking the bad burrow until the sun was already beginning to disappear. Then I made the Little Ones mount their elephants, for the moon might be late, and I could not shake off the anxiety gnawing at me.

The Lady of Sorrow now led the way by my side; the elephants followed—the two bearing the princess in the center; the leopardess brought up the rear. Just as we reached the frightful margin, the moon looked up and revealed the shallow basin lying before us, untroubled and still. Mara stepped into it; not a movement answered her tread or the hooves of my horse. But the moment the elephants carrying the princess touched the surface, the seemingly solid earth began to heave and boil, and the whole dread brood of the hellish nest was awakened. Monsters uprose on all sides, every neck stretched to its full length, every beak and claw outstretched, every mouth agape. Long-billed heads, horribly jawed faces, and knotty tentacles innumerable surged forward after Lilith. She lay in an agony of fear, nor dared she stir a finger. Whether the hideous things even saw the children, I doubt; certainly not one of them touched a child; not one loathsome limb passed the living rampart of her body-guard to lay hold of her.

"Little Ones," I cried, "keep your elephants close about the princess. Be brave; they will not touch you."

"What will not touch us? We don't know what to be brave at!" they answered, and I realized they were entirely unaware of the deformities surrounding them.

"Never mind then," I returned, "only keep close."

They were panoplied in their blindness! Incapacity to see was their safety. Whatever they could not perceive, could not harm them.

But the hideous forms I saw that night! Mara was a few paces ahead of me when a solitary, bodiless head bounced onto the path between us. The leopardess rushed under the elephants from behind, intending to seize it, but with frightful contortions of visage and a loathsome howl, it gave itself a rapid rotatory twist, sprang from her jaws, and buried itself in the earth. The death in my arms assuaged me from fear, yet I regarded them all unmoved, although never, surely, was there beheld such a cursed crew!

Mara still went in front of me, and the leopardess now walked close behind her, shivering often, for it was very cold, when suddenly the ground before me to my left began to heave, and a low wave of earth slunk toward us. It rose higher as it drew near; out of it slouched a dreadful head with fleshy tubes for hair, and opening a great oval mouth, it snapped at me. The leopardess sprang, but fell baffled before it.
