Fanny's heart sank the moment she spotted the two ladies entering the room. The elder woman's face was a mask of terrifying anxiety, her eyes wide with a look that suggested sheer panic. In that instant, the poor girl recognized Mrs. Pendennis. There was a haunting similarity between the widow's haggard gaze and the fever-dream eyes of Arthur, tossing restlessly in his bed.

Fanny cast a desperate glance from Mrs. Pendennis to Laura. The younger woman's face was as unyielding and expressionless as a block of granite. Both newcomers radiated a cold, hard-hearted gloom; neither offered even a flicker of mercy or sympathy toward Fanny. Desperate, she turned her gaze to the Major standing behind them. Old Pendennis lowered his eyelids, peeking up at Arthur's tiny nurse with a stealthy, worried scrutiny.

"I—I wrote to you yesterday, if you please, ma'am," Fanny stammered, her limbs trembling violently as she spoke. She was as pale as Laura, whose sad, menacing visage loomed over Mrs. Pendennis's shoulder.

"Did you, madam?" Mrs. Pendennis replied coolly. "I suppose I may now relieve you from nursing my son. After all, I am his mother, you understand."

"Yes, ma'am. I—this is the way to his—" Fanny began, but stopped abruptly. "Oh, wait a minute!" she cried out. "I must prepare you for his..."

The widow, whose face had previously been hopelessly cruel and ruthless, suddenly recoiled with a sharp gasp and a stifled cry.

"He's been so since yesterday," Fanny whispered, her teeth chattering uncontrollably.

At that moment, a horrific shriek of laughter erupted from the open doorway of Pen's room. Following several raucous shouts, the poor wretch began singing a college drinking song, then hurrahed and shouted as if he were in the thick of a wine party, thumping his fists against the wainscoting. He was completely delirious.

"He does not know me, ma'am," Fanny sobbed.

"Indeed. Perhaps he will recognize his mother," Mrs. Pendennis said briskly. "Let me pass, if you please, and go in to him." With that, the widow hurried past little Fanny and swept through the dark passage leading to Arthur's sitting-room. Laura sailed by without a word, and Major Pendennis followed close behind.

Left alone, Fanny collapsed onto a bench in the hallway. She wept and prayed as best she could. She would have died for him, yet they hated her. These "fine ladies" offered not a single word of thanks or kindness. She sat there in the passage, unsure of how long she remained. They never emerged to speak to her. Finally, the silence was broken by the sound of Doctor Goodenough arriving for his second visit of the day. He found the poor little thing crouching at the door.

"What, nurse? How's your patient?" the kindly doctor asked warmly. "Has he had any rest?"

"Go and ask them. They're inside," Fanny replied flatly.

"Who? His mother?"

Fanny nodded silently, refusing to speak further.

"You must go to bed yourself, my poor little maid," the Doctor advised gently. "You will fall ill too if you don't."

"Oh, mayn't I come and see him? Mayn't I come and see him! I—I love him so!" the little girl pleaded. As she spoke, she fell to her knees, clutching the Doctor's hand in such an agony of devotion that it melted the physician's heart, causing a mist to cloud his spectacles.

"Pooh, pooh! Nonsense! Nurse, has he taken his draught? Has he had any rest? Of course you must come and see him. So must I."

"They'll let me sit here, won't they, sir? I'll never make no noise. I only ask to stop here," Fanny insisted.

The Doctor labeled her a "stupid little thing," tapped her pale cheek with his finger, placed her back on the bench where Pen's printer's devil used to sit for hours, and bustled into the far room.

Inside, Mrs. Pendennis was ensconced in a great chair by Arthur's bedside, looking pale and solemn. Her watch lay on the medicine-laden table beside her. Her bonnet and cloak were draped in the window. She held her Bible in her lap—a necessity she never traveled without. Upon entering, her first act had been to retrieve Fanny's shawl and bonnet from Arthur's drawers, bringing them out and dropping them onto the study table. She had shut the door firmly on the Major and Laura, effectively claiming possession of her son.

She had harbored a terrible fear that Arthur wouldn't recognize her, but that pang was largely spared. Pen knew his mother perfectly; he smiled and nodded familiarly. Upon her entrance, he instantly imagined they were back home at Fairoaks. He began talking, chatting, and laughing in a rambling, wild fashion. Laura could hear him clearly from the hall. His laughter sent shafts of pure poison into her heart. It was true, then. He had been guilty—an intrigue with a servant-maid! She had loved him, and now he was dying, likely raving and unrepentant. The Major occasionally hummed a word of consolation, which Laura barely registered.

It was a dismal sitting for everyone involved. Just as the atmosphere grew heavy, Doctor Goodenough appeared like an angel.

The doctor's role extends far beyond the sick man; it is equally vital for the sick man's friends. His presence often brings as much relief to the family as to the patient. We have all watched him approach with eager anticipation—the thrill of hearing his carriage wheels roll down the street, the suspense as he reaches the door, the hanging on his every word, seeking comfort in a simple smile. Have we not all seen mothers peering intently into a physician's face, searching for hope in the eyes of a silent, fever-stricken child? How she gazes! What thanks if there is light there; what grief if he casts his eyes down, unable to utter the word "hope."

Or consider the father struck down. The terrified wife watches while the physician checks the pulse, suppressing her agonies as children are ordered to cease their play. Standing over the feverish patient, surrounded by the expectant wife and unconscious children, the Doctor becomes Fate itself—the dispenser of life and death. He must decide whether to grant respite. One can imagine the crushing weight of responsibility on a conscientious man: the cruelty of administering the wrong remedy, the torment of wondering if better could have been done, the harassment of sympathizing with survivors in tragic cases, and the immense, overwhelming delight of victory.

After a brief introduction to the new arrivals, whose arrival the heartbroken nurse had reported, the Doctor examined the patient. The high fever was undeniable, requiring immediate and strong antiphlogistic remedies. He consoled the distraught mother with the most comforting assurances he dared offer—that there was no cause for despair yet, citing the boy's youth, constitutional strength, and so on. Having done his utmost to calm the alarmed matron, he ushered the elder Pendennis into the empty room (formerly Warrington's) for a private consultation.

"The case is very critical," the Doctor noted. "If the fever isn't stopped immediately, it will carry off the young man. He must be bled forthwith. The mother must be informed of this necessity."

"Why was that other young lady brought along?" he asked, gesturing vaguely. "She is entirely out of place in a sickroom."

"And there was another woman still, be hanged to it!" the Major interjected. "The—the little person who opened the door." His sister-in-law had retrieved the poor little devil's bonnet and shawl, throwing them onto the study table. Did Goodenough know anything about the—the little person? "I just caught a glimpse of her as we passed in," the Major added. "Begad, she was uncommonly nice-looking."

The Doctor looked queasy. He smiled. Even in the gravest moments, with life and death hanging in the balance, strange contrasts and opportunities for humor arise. Such smiles pass, serving to satirize the gloom, thereby making it even deeper.

"I have it," the Doctor finally declared, re-entering the study. He hastily wrote two notes at the table and sealed one. Taking up Fanny's shawl and bonnet along with the notes, he stepped out into the passage to find the poor little messenger.

"Quick, nurse," he instructed. "You must carry this to the surgeon and bid him come instantly. Then go to my house in Hanover Square, ask for my servant Harbottle, tell him to prepare this prescription, and wait until I return. It may take a little time to compound."

Poor Fanny trudged away with her two notes. She found the apothecary in the Strand, who arrived promptly with his lancet in his pocket to operate on the patient. Then Fanny headed for the Doctor's house.

By the time the prescription was fully compounded—a process that took Harbottle, the Doctor's servant, an unusually long time—the Doctor was already home again. For the remainder of Arthur's illness, Fanny never appeared in the capacity of nurse at his chambers once more.
