Gwendolen was thankful she had already endured Klesmer’s keen, musician’s scrutiny before she faced her uncle and aunt. That earlier interview had scraped her pride raw; it left her with a grim, clenched composure, as if she had swallowed something too hot and meant to show no sign of pain. Whatever came next, she told herself, would be disagreeable; and she would meet it with a kind of stubborn stillness, letting humiliation spend itself against a face that would not change.

The meeting waited until Monday. She went with her mother to the rectory; and on the way they stopped at Sawyer’s Cottage—her mother’s coming refuge—because arrangements had to be made, because a new life insisted on being measured in inches and corners. They saw every cranny in a hard, midday light that made no allowances. Bare boards, bare plaster, the small garden with cabbage-stalks like spent torches, and a yew arbor powdered with dust and hung with cobwebs as if even shade had become miserly. The air had that faint, cold smell of emptiness—old lime-wash, dry wood, and the sour echo of neglect—like a pantry after the last jar has been taken down and the shelves wiped too clean.

Gwendolen had been silent while they looked; her silence was a sort of pride, refusing to give the place the honor of a complaint. But when they walked away, the words broke out of her as if her throat could no longer bear the taste of them.

“How shall you endure it, mamma?” she said. “You and the four girls all in that closet of a room, with the green and yellow paper pressing on your eyes? And without me?”

“It will be some comfort that you have not to bear it too, dear.”

“If it were not that I must get some money, I would rather be there than go to be a governess.”

“Don’t set yourself against it beforehand, Gwendolen. If you go to the palace you will have every luxury about you. And you know how much you have always cared for that. You will not find it so hard as going up and down those steep narrow stairs, and hearing the crockery rattle through the house, and the dear girls talking.”

The very mention of crockery made Gwendolen’s mind flash with the homely acoustics she was being sentenced to: thin walls carrying every clink, every scrape, every rattling argument of plates that had outlived their glaze. The cottage seemed already to ring with it, as if poverty had its own ceaseless soundtrack—no soft curtains to drink it in, no thick carpets to hush it. A house can be cruel with noise when it is small.

“It is like a bad dream,” she said, fiercely. “I cannot believe my uncle will let you go to such a place. He ought to have taken some other steps.”

“Don’t be unreasonable, dear child. What could he have done?”

“That was for him to find out. It seems to me a very extraordinary world if people in our position must sink in this way all at once,” she said—because her inner world had always been arranged for her, like a table set with the right glass for every course, and nothing placed there that was not meant to please her.

It was her temper speaking, sharpened under this new pressure; she could have been wise enough about other people’s calamities, and even eloquent, if it had been her choice. But she had never aimed at virtue so much as brilliancy. Her words were often worse than she was.

And yet, when she reached the rectory and her uncle and aunt met her with a tenderness deeper than they had ever shown before, something in her tightened and then slackened with compunction. There was a dignified cheerfulness in them—no parade of martyrdom, no whimpering over lost comforts. It was as if, when fortune had been taken away, character began to show its clean grain. Her uncle in particular—whose worth had been half hidden by his usual clerical habits and smooth securities—stood out now with a new distinctness, like a good figure in plain clothes.

Already, with prompt, methodical decision, he had begun the little economies that are at once humiliating and oddly bracing: the carriage must go; old suits must be considered and made to serve; meat was to be banished from breakfast; periodicals dismissed; Edwy fetched from school; the boys’ hours arranged under their father’s own eye; the whole household brought to the sparest footing. There is even a pleasure in such paring down, when one’s body is healthy and one’s spirit not spoiled; it gives a sense of mastery, like trimming fat from a joint with a sure hand. The rector’s temper spread through the house. Mrs. Gascoigne and Anna, who always took papa for their pattern, truly did not miss much they cared for; their sorrow was chiefly for Mrs. Davilow and the girls.

Anna, for the first time, let her resentment on Rex’s behalf soften into sympathy for Gwendolen. Mrs. Gascoigne hoped—without duty-bound severity—that trouble might do her niece good. They had been busy devising blinds and curtains for the cottage out of the rectory’s stores, as careful women contrive meals out of remnants; but with a delicacy that refused to make charity sound like a lecture, they kept such matters back at first. They spoke instead of Gwendolen’s journey, and of the comfort her return had been to her mother.

There was, in truth, nothing in the manner of the rectory people that gave Gwendolen a plausible excuse for flinging her bitterness at them. It shook her into a sharper attention, as if everyone else had heard a command and fallen into order. Then her uncle, in a voice that had firm kindness for its tone—as if he were both comforting her and bracing her—began to speak of what he had been doing for her.

“I felt that there was no time to be lost, Gwendolen; for a position in a good family where you will have some consideration is not to be had at a moment’s notice. And however long we waited we could hardly find one where you would be better off than at Bishop Mompert’s. I am known to both him and Mrs. Mompert, and that of course is an advantage to you. Our correspondence has gone on favorably; but I cannot be surprised that Mrs. Mompert wishes to see you before making an absolute engagement. She thinks of arranging for you to meet her at Wanchester when she is on her way to town. I dare say you will feel the interview rather trying for you, my dear; but you will have a little time to prepare your mind.”

Gwendolen’s imagination—quick as a hand snatching at a knife—had already cut out a dozen disagreeable reasons why a strange lady would want to inspect her: daughters to guard, manners to judge, defects to scent out as if she were meat brought to market. The very word “see” felt like a cold finger under her chin, turning her face toward a harsh light.

“Do you know why she wants to see me, uncle?” she asked.

He smiled, trying to ease her. “Don’t be alarmed, my dear. She would like to have a more precise idea of you than my report can give. And a mother is naturally scrupulous about a companion for her daughters. I have told her you are very young. But she herself exercises a close supervision over her daughters’ education, and that makes her less anxious as to age. She is a woman of taste and also of strict principle, and objects to having a French person in the house. I feel sure that she will think your manners and accomplishments as good as she is likely to find; and over the religious and moral tone of the education she, and indeed the bishop himself, will preside.”

Gwendolen dared not answer. She felt the blood surge up—an angry, humiliating flush over face and neck—and fall away again, leaving her chilled. Anna, frightened by that sudden change she did not understand, slid her small hand into Gwendolen’s. Mr. Gascoigne, too kind not to feel the weight of what he was asking, pressed on with a cheerful tone, as if he were not arguing against objections but simply setting out advantages that any sensible mind would taste and approve.

“I think so highly of the position, that I should have been tempted to try and get it for Anna, if she had been at all likely to meet Mrs. Mompert’s wants. It is really a home, with a continuance of education in the highest sense: ‘governess’ is a misnomer. The bishop’s views are of a more decidedly Low Church color than my own—he is a close friend of Lord Grampian’s; but, though privately strict, he is not by any means narrow in public matters. Indeed, he has created as little dislike in his diocese as any bishop on the bench. He has always remained friendly to me, though before his promotion, when he was an incumbent of this diocese, we had a little controversy about the Bible Society.”

To him these were solid, reassuring morsels of information—sound, nourishing, plain. To Gwendolen they were like things alive and cold set loose at her feet: “continuance of education,” “privately strict,” “religious and moral tone,” “Bible Society”—phrases that hissed in her mind. She did not hear a home: she heard supervision, examinations, a life where her very silence would be judged; and, behind all, the oppressive hush of a great house where sound carries, where footsteps are measured, where a girl’s laughter might be taken for frivolity. It was as if her uncle had proposed to cure her by shutting her in a room whose air was scented with sermons.

She made a desperate reach for any other outlet.

“There was another situation, I think mamma spoke of?” she said, with a rigid control that made her voice sound almost calm.

“Yes,” her uncle answered, with reluctance. “But that is in a school. I should not have the same satisfaction in your taking that. It would be much harder work, you are aware, and not so good in any other respect. Besides, you have not an equal chance of getting it.”

“Oh dear no,” said Mrs. Gascoigne, “it would be much harder for you, my dear—it would be much less appropriate. You might not have a bedroom to yourself.”

Gwendolen’s school memories rose with their stale odors and relentless noises: the close air of dormitories, the mingled smell of soap and damp wool, the scraping of benches, the incessant human friction that made even silence noisy. This alternative brought no relief. She turned again to her uncle, and the words came out with the outward air of submission, as if she were accepting medicine she despised.

“When is Mrs. Mompert likely to send for me?”

“That is rather uncertain, but she has promised not to entertain any other proposal till she has seen you. She has entered with much feeling into your position. It will be within the next fortnight, probably. But I must be off now. I am going to let part of my glebe uncommonly well.”

He rose, ending with hearty cheerfulness, carrying away the conviction that he had done his part and that Gwendolen would do hers—adapt herself, sensibly, as people ought. Used to being asked to “speak to” refractory persons with the notion that words from authority have a coercive virtue, he naturally expected appropriate effects from appropriate counsel.

“What a stay Henry is to us all!” Mrs. Gascoigne said when he had gone.

“He is indeed,” Mrs. Davilow answered warmly. “I think cheerfulness is a fortune in itself. I wish I had it.”

“And Rex is just like him,” Mrs. Gascoigne said. “I must tell you the comfort we have had in a letter from him. I must read you a little bit.” She put her hand in her pocket to draw it out, while Anna looked suddenly timid, as if an old rule—never mention Rex before Gwendolen—had the power of a charm that might be broken at their peril.

Mrs. Gascoigne ran her eyes over the page, searching for safe sentences. But she seemed to find it thick with nearer allusions than she wished; she folded it again and spoke instead, her voice bright with a mother’s proud relief.

“However, he tells us that our trouble has made a man of him; he sees a reason for any amount of work: he means to get a fellowship, to take pupils, to set one of his brothers going, to be everything that is most remarkable. The letter is full of fun—just like him. He says, ‘Tell mother she has put out an advertisement for a jolly good hard-working son, in time to hinder me from taking ship; and I offer myself for the place.’ The letter came on Friday. I never saw my husband so much moved by anything since Rex was born. It seemed a gain to balance our loss.”

This letter was, in fact, what helped both mother and daughter at the rectory to be kind to Gwendolen without mixture. And Gwendolen, who had no gratuitous delight in other people’s pain, felt amiable about it. She smiled at Anna and pinched her chin, as if to say, There—nothing is wrong now. She only objected intensely to other people making her miserable.

But when the talk slipped back to blinds and curtains for the cottage, to what could be spared from the rectory, and how to contrive comfort out of scant means, Gwendolen’s spirit sank again. She could not rouse even a languid interest in the practical details. She felt she had already done enough merely by sitting there, by holding her face, by not bursting out. Inside her, a struggle went on without words, like teeth grinding in sleep.

For the recoil from the one definite prospect allowed her was stronger than she had imagined. The idea of presenting herself to Mrs. Mompert for approval or rejection pressed on her like a hand on a bruise. Even now, when she had done the violence of consenting to go, she was still to be judged—her looks, her voice, her very manner of being silent. And if accepted, the judgment would not end: it would become the air she breathed. Mrs. Mompert’s supervision always; expectations always; tasks she had no desire to do, performed under a gaze that would never quite be absent. Perhaps the bishop himself would examine her—on serious topics—like a magistrate questioning a culprit. Only a few weeks ago, she had moved in drawing-rooms where a handsome girl’s daring talk passed for wit and was rewarded with smiling attention. Then she might have pitied a bishop’s dulness. Now she saw the coming life as a penitentiary where every day would be an effort at self-constraint.

Wild thoughts came—running away, becoming an actress, shaking off all this—thoughts with the lure of freedom like a sudden gust at a window. But Klesmer’s words hung on her, heavy as a hand at the back of her neck. He had alarmed her pride and her maidenly dignity. She saw, dimly, the coarse glare of vulgar company, the rude familiarity of men who would grin and smirk without the strong bars of polite custom between. Her daring was not that of the adventuress. The demand to be treated as a lady was in her very marrow. Even when she had dreamed herself a heroine of the gaming-table, it was with the condition that no one should presume, no one should look at her with an irony like Deronda’s. She had always been protected and petted; her susceptibilities consulted as naturally as her hunger was fed. To be flung into solitary dependence on strangers’ doubtful civility was no attractive liberty. The episcopal penitentiary was less repulsive than that. Yet there, too, she would not be petted.

As hour followed hour, rebellion grew rather than diminished. It seemed to her the family trouble was easier for everyone than for her—even for her poor dear mother, because her mother had never been trained to expect enjoyment. Hope—hope that things might get better if she went and was patient—seemed to Gwendolen a foolish luxury she could not afford. Her talents, it appeared, would never be recognized as remarkable; no probability flattered her wishes. Romance might have offered a pretty picture to some girls—governesses becoming centers of attraction, rescued into marriage—but Gwendolen’s mind could not project itself far. The past and the immediate future pressed too close. She had a nausea of the world, and no reason, she thought, for living through a whole life in a way she hated.

No religious view came to help her; she felt her troubles had been caused by other people’s disagreeable or wicked conduct. She had heard phrases about discipline and blessing, but they were only phrases; she had never found them delicious enough to take into herself. Talk of the sweetness of labor, the fulfilled claim, the inward activity that makes life discovery—such things would have been faint doctrines to her even if preached with eloquence. The fact she knew, because she had always observed it, was that for a lady to “take a situation” was to descend and to be treated, at best, with a patronizing compassion. Happiness, for her, had never been divorced from personal pre-eminence and éclat. That she should now find life hardly worth having when these were threatened, made her pitiable rather than monstrous: a young creature facing the labyrinth with no clue, struck suddenly by distrust in herself and her fortune, as if the smooth path had split open under her feet.

Her healthy body did not save her from the physical effect of this inward repugnance. She felt numb, inert, as if her limbs belonged to another person. The slightest urgency—even being called to meals—irritated her. Other people’s speech on any subject felt unreasonable, because it ignored what she felt; it made a claim on her as if she were obliged to answer a world that did not include her. She did not busy herself with those dramatic fancies of suicide that fill some young minds; what tormented her was the sense that she must go on living, but only in a way she detested.

She avoided the rectory. It was intolerable to look and talk as if she were compliant, and she could not bring herself to pretend an interest in furnishing that dreadful cottage. Miss Merry was staying on purpose to help; Jocosa would like such bustle and contrivance, the cheerful clatter of domestic plans. Gwendolen could not. Her mother had to make excuses for her even when Anna came. The dogged calm Gwendolen had promised herself had turned into a sick, motiveless inaction. “I suppose I shall begin to pretend by-and-by,” she thought; “but why should I do it now?”

Her mother watched her in silent distress; and, falling back into the habit of indulgent tenderness, she began to imagine the thoughts behind that altered face and to wish that everything might yield to making her darling less miserable.

One day, when Gwendolen lay in the black and yellow bedroom and Mrs. Davilow lingered there under the pretext of arranging her clothes, Gwendolen suddenly roused herself, as if some practical thought had stung her out of stupor. She rose, fetched the casket that held her ornaments, and brought it back with a decisive movement.

“Mamma,” she began, lifting the lid and glancing at the upper layer, “I had forgotten these things. Why didn’t you remind me of them? Do see about getting them sold. You will not mind about parting with them. You gave them all to me long ago.”

She lifted the upper tray and looked below.

“If we can do without them, darling, I would rather keep them for you,” Mrs. Davilow said, seating herself beside her with a relief that Gwendolen was speaking of anything that could be handled and done. Their old relation was reversed now: the mother tried to cheer the daughter. “Why, how came you to put that pocket handkerchief in here?”

It was the handkerchief with the corner torn off—the one Gwendolen had thrust in hurriedly with the turquoise necklace.

“It happened to be with the necklace—I was in a hurry,” Gwendolen said. She took the handkerchief away and put it in her pocket. Then, as if a new warmth had come over her about that rescued trinket which once had stung her pride, she added quickly, “Don’t sell the necklace, mamma.”

“No, dear, no; it was made out of your dear father’s chain. And I should prefer not selling the other things. None of them are of any great value. All my best ornaments were taken from me long ago.”

Mrs. Davilow colored. She usually avoided such facts about Gwendolen’s step-father—how he had carried off his wife’s jewelry and disposed of it. After a moment she went on, softly, “And these things have not been reckoned on for any expenses. Carry them with you.”

“That would be quite useless, mamma,” Gwendolen said, cold and scornful, as if she were speaking of a dish set before her when she was already sick. “Governesses don’t wear ornaments. You had better get me a gray frieze livery and a straw poke, such as my aunt’s charity children wear.”

“No, dear, no; don’t take that view of it. I feel sure the Momperts will like you the better for being graceful and elegant.”

“I am not at all sure what the Momperts will like me to be. It is enough that I am expected to be what they like,” Gwendolen said bitterly. The bitterness was like something burnt at the bottom of a pan: it tainted everything.

“If there is anything you would object to less—anything that could be done—instead of your going to the bishop’s, do say so, Gwendolen. Tell me what is in your heart. I will try for anything you wish,” her mother pleaded. “Don’t keep things away from me. Let us bear them together.”

“Oh, mamma, there is nothing to tell,” Gwendolen answered. “I can’t do anything better. I must think myself fortunate if they will have me. I shall get some money for you. That is the only thing I have to think of. I shall not spend any money this year: you will have all the eighty pounds. I don’t know how far that will go in housekeeping; but you need not stitch your poor fingers to the bone, and stare away all the sight that the tears have left in your dear eyes.”

Her words held tenderness, but they did not caress; she did not even look at her mother. She kept turning the turquoise necklace in her fingers, as if the smooth stones could steady her more than any human touch.

“Bless you for your tenderness, my good darling!” Mrs. Davilow said, tears rising. “Don’t despair because there are clouds now. You are so young. There may be great happiness in store for you yet.”

“I don’t see any reason for expecting it, mamma,” Gwendolen said, hard; and her mother fell silent, thinking the question she had thought too often—what had happened between Gwendolen and Mr. Grandcourt?

“I will keep this necklace, mamma,” Gwendolen said at last. She laid it apart and closed the casket with a decisive click. “But do get the other things sold, even if they will not bring much. Ask my uncle what to do with them. I shall certainly not use them again. I am going to take the veil. I wonder if all the poor wretches who have ever taken it felt as I do.”

“Don’t exaggerate evils, dear.”

“How can any one know that I exaggerate, when I am speaking of my own feeling? I did not say what any one else felt.”

She drew out the torn handkerchief again and, with a deliberate care that was almost ceremonial, wrapped it round the turquoise necklace. Mrs. Davilow watched the act with surprise; but Gwendolen’s tone, like a door shut fast, discouraged any question.
