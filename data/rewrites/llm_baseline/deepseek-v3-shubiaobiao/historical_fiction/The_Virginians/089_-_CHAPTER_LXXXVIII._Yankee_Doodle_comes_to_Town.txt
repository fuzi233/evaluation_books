In the days when America still bent the knee to British rule, our colonies served as a convenient dumping ground for the mother country’s wayward sons—younger brothers who had squandered their inheritances, scoundrels fleeing debtors’ prison, and black sheep too scandalous to remain at home. It was a tradition as old as colonization itself: export the troublesome and let the wilderness reform them—or bury them.

I confess I played my own small part in this traffic when I secured passage for the Reverend Hagan, husband to my cousin Maria, shipping him off to Virginia when no English pulpit would have him. He proved an honest man, a loyal subject, and a credit to my patronage—though had he sought a living in my own parish, I doubt I would have been so generous. There is a hypocrisy in charity at a distance, a quiet relief in sending one’s mistakes across the ocean. Maria, for all her past follies, had shown me kindness when I needed it most, yet I—and others of my name—still looked down upon her. Shame on us for that pride.

But Maria was not the only family outcast dispatched to the colonies. My Lord Castlewood’s younger brother, the Honorable William Esmond, had worn out his welcome in England through debts, duels, and deceit. New York became his reluctant refuge, and there he thrived—not in virtue, but in fresh mischief. No sooner had rebellion begun to simmer than William emerged as one of the loudest Loyalists in the province, raising a company of volunteers and swearing to lay down his life for King and Country. A fine sentiment—were it not from a man who had spent a lifetime evading both honor and creditors.

Meanwhile, in Virginia, the political air grew thick with discontent. The Assembly, dissolved by our prickly Governor, now convened in taverns, plotting rebellion over claret and tobacco smoke. I split my days between Richmond and the Governor’s table, where we still toasted the King even as the ground shifted beneath us. The gentry, though furious at Parliament’s heavy hand, remained civil—for now.

Then came the day I rode out to visit Hal at his new plantation. Finding him absent, I followed the groom’s direction toward Willis’s Ordinary—and stumbled upon a scene that stripped all pretense away. There stood my brother, drilling a motley band of farmers armed with hunting rifles and scythes. At his command, they presented arms—awkwardly, but with a grim determination.

*"Captain Cade, this is my brother,"* Hal announced, his face flushing.

*"Welcome, if he stands with Virginia,"* replied Cade, a hard-eyed man with a militia-man’s grip.

*"I stand with my family,"* I said carefully. The men cheered, but my stomach tightened. This was no idle drill. Rebellion had moved from pamphlets to powder.

Hal dismissed the men, refusing their offer of a drink, and we rode home in silence.

*"So the cat’s out of the bag,"* I said at last.

*"And a damned fierce cat it is,"* he replied. *"Don’t tell Mother."*

I tried reason: *"You mean to fight the finest army in the world with farmers?"*

*"We’ll likely get whipped,"* Hal admitted, grinning. *"But we’re young. A thrashing might do us good."* He nudged his old hound with his boot—a gesture so careless it chilled me.

There was no swaying him. To Hal, this was no longer about taxes or treaties; it was about home. *"You argue as an Englishman,"* he said. *"I fight as a Virginian."*

That night, over dinner, Fanny arched a brow when I mentioned Hal’s *"sport."* My wife assumed he’d been hunting. I let her think it. Some truths, once spoken, cannot be taken back—and the storm ahead would break soon enough.
