George Warrington had recounted the full story we have just heard to Madame de Bernstein the evening before – at least, a partial telling of it, for the old lady nodded off in her velvet fireside chair half a dozen times mid-sentence, jolting awake the very second George paused to catch his breath, exclaiming it was “thrillingly interesting, dear nephew, do go on.” The young man stammered, hemmed, blushed, and stumbled through the tale very much against his will, nowhere near as eloquent as he had been in the warm, lamplit parlour of the Lamberts’ Hill Street house. There, Hetty’s wide wondering eyes, Theo’s soft sympathetic smile, Mrs Lambert’s gentle attentive face, and the General’s playful grins had made for an audience cheering enough to embolden even the shyest young man unused to public speaking. The General had joked afterwards that Mrs Lambert’s quiet sniffles as she listened were every bit as moving as the famous ill-fated trunk-maker scene from Mr. Addison’s tragedy, and she could make any flop of a play a West End hit all by herself, just sitting in a front box sobbing. That was exactly why the family had chosen Lord Wrotham’s house as the stage for George’s first full retelling of his adventures, so he could speak at his ease instead of pouring out two years of French captivity to a sleepy, sharp-eyed old cynic who could barely keep her head propped up.

“Most excellent and deeply fascinating, I am sure, my dear boy,” said Madame de Bernstein, lifting three lace-mittened little fingers to hide the twitch of amusement at George’s awkward stammering. “And your mother must have been overjoyed to see you home, no doubt.”
George gave the tiniest, almost imperceptible shrug, and bowed low, as his aunt’s keen old hawk eyes flicked up to pin him for a long, searching second. “Overjoyed to see you,” she repeated drily, “kill the fatted calf, all that sentimental nonsense. Though heaven knows why I say calf to you, nephew George – you never were the prodigal. That title belongs to my poor Harry, eh? You did go rooting among the swine, didn’t you, lad? Evil companions picked your pockets clean, stripped the very coat off your back.”
“He came to his own family in England, madam,” George said, flushing hot with annoyance. “The friends he fell in with were your ladyship’s set.”
“Then he could not have found worse advisers,” she snapped, tossing her silvered head, “and I would have told my sister that plain enough years ago, if she’d condescended to write to me by Harry when he sailed, same as she sent word by you.”
Later that night, as her maid Brett laced her stays for the high-stakes card rout she was bound for, she rambled on unprompted: “That boy George? I could see it in his face clear as day – his own mother is half sorry he turned up alive. Half sorry? I am perfectly furious! Why didn’t he stay laid out under that tree when he got shot at Braddock’s defeat? Why did that young rascal Florac have to drag him off to the French fort? I knew all those Floracs back in Paris in the Regent’s day, descendants of Henri IV’s favourite – heh heh, not just the favourite, the favourite’s lady, Brett, you hear me? Hand me my card purse. I don’t care for Monsieur George’s grand airs much, but he favours his grandfather Esmond strikingly – same eyes, same quiet slow tone sometimes. You heard me speak of Colonel Esmond when I was a girl? I liked his eyes, of course – he was the only man who ever loved me properly.”
She never missed a card party, not weekday not Sabbath, as long as she could hold a trump card or sit upright in a chair, so no sooner had George stammered through the last of his adventures than she dismissed both nephews. To George she gave two cool, outstretched fingers and a stately little curtsy, but to Harry she took both his hands, patted his cheek fondly. “My poor lamb, now that you’ve lost your inheritance, you’ll see how fast the rest of the world turns its back on you. There is only one wicked, heartless old woman in all London who will treat you just the same as before. Here, take this pocket-book – don’t gamble it away at Ranelagh tonight. That velvet coat of yours never half suited George as well as it fits you. Walk him round the gardens two full hours, introduce him to every curiosity, every pretty face there. Then off you go to the Chocolate House, play as if nothing’s changed at all. While you’re doing that, George can come back here with me, eat a bit of cold chicken – Lady Flint’s suppers are always abominable, and I want to go over his mother’s letter with him. Off you go, gentlemen!” Her chairmen and flaming link-boys’ torches were already waiting at the door, so off she sailed to her dressing chamber.

The two brothers headed straight for Ranelagh, where barely a handful of Harry’s usual acquaintances happened to be that night. They paced the circular promenade arm in arm, ran into Tom Claypool and his group of country squire friends, listened to the open-air orchestra, took tea in a private box, and Harry acted as master of ceremonies, introducing George to every notable oddity and fashionable belle on the grounds. George, who loved music far more than Harry ever had, was more giddy and excited than his brother had been on his own first visit to the pleasure gardens – it was the first time he had ever heard a full symphony, and a crisp, rousing performance of Mr. Handel’s work that brought tears to his eyes. Even at a time when fiddling was widely dismissed as effeminate, unmanly frivolity, when patriotic newspapers railed against foreign “fiddling foreigners” and all their silly accomplishments, Harry had nothing but respect for his brother’s delight in the music.
Nobody in Britain is proud of their ignorance these days, right? No conceit left, no dullness, no arrogance to be found anywhere… well, at the very least the arts have earned their full citizenship here now, treated almost as equals. If Mrs. Thrale chose to marry her music master today, I doubt her friends would shun her for it – if she had a good fortune and a good cook, folks would still turn up to dine, and treat Mr. Piozzi with full civility.

After Ranelagh, as Madame Bernstein had advised, George went back to her house, while Harry made his way to his club, where the gentlemen gathered nightly for supper and high-stakes gambling. No one so much as hinted at his recent spell in debtor’s prison, his old landlord Mr. Ruff waited on him with perfect grave civility, as if there had never been any disagreement between them. Harry had already moved all his trunks and clothes out of the Bond Street lodgings that morning, he and George now renting quiet rooms not far from Covent Garden.
But when the supper plates were cleared, and everyone moved to head upstairs to the macco gaming tables, Harry spoke up: he wasn’t playing tonight. He’d already burned his fingers too badly, couldn’t afford any more wild spending.
“Come now,” said Jack Morris flippantly, “you must have won far more than you lost, Warrington, all things considered.”
“Ah, so I don’t know my own affairs better than you, do I, Morris?” Harry said sharply, he’d never forgotten how the man had sneered at him when he was locked up. “But there’s more to it. A few months back I was heir to a great estate, could afford to lose a little at cards. Now, thank God, I’m heir to nothing.” He looked round at the group of gaming men lounging at the tables or clustered by the fire, his face flushing hot.
“What the devil do you mean, Warrington?” Lord March called out, “Have you lost Virginia too? Who won it? I’ve half a mind to play you for that stake myself, breed a better strain of slaves out there on the plantations.”
“The rightful owner has it back,” Harry said. “You all heard me talk of my twin elder brother, who was supposed to have died in Braddock’s defeat two years back.”
“Good God, man,” someone gasped, “he hasn’t come back to life, has he?”
“He landed in London two days ago. He was held prisoner in a French fort for eighteen months, only escaped a few months back, and sailed for England almost immediately after he got home to Virginia.”
“Dear me, I suppose you haven’t had time to order mourning for yourself, eh Warrington?” Selwyn said good-naturedly, and simple Harry didn’t get the dry joke until George explained it to him later.

When Harry had taken his leave, the men fell to gossiping behind his back.
“Damned if I don’t think the lout is actually glad his brother’s turned up alive!” Lord March laughed. “American savages still cling to those simple silly virtues of family affection, barely civilised at all.”
“They love their kin, and scalp their enemies,” Walpole simpered, yawning. “Hardly Christian, but very natural. Wouldn’t you love to see a scalping match, March, watch a man skinned alive?”
“An elder brother is a man’s natural enemy, obviously,” Selwyn said placidly, counting out his piles of counters. “Get used to a little torture, broiled bones and pepper, and plain hanging will seem boring after, right?”
“I’m hanged if there’s a man in all England who’d be happy to see his elder brother turn up alive,” Lord March agreed.
“Or his father, neither!” Jack Morris cackled.
“First time I ever heard you had one, Jack,” March tossed back. “Give me five hundred pounds worth of counters.”
“All this talk about dead brothers rising from the grave is mighty suspicious,” Jack went on. “Who’s to say this isn’t a scheme the two of them cooked up together? This lad turns up calling himself the Virginian Prince, the Fortunate Youth, gets invited into our society-“ the whole room burst out laughing at his lofty phrase “our society” “-Who’s to say this isn’t a con? The younger brother comes first, marries a great heiress, gets hold of her fortune, then the elder pops up out of nowhere! And when did this elder brother turn up? Why, exactly when Harry’s schemes went bust, when all his money was gone! Who’s to say this George hasn’t been hiding in a garret in Seven Dials, eating tripe and cow heel in a cellar this whole time, waiting for Harry to get settled? Dammy, as gentlemen we ought to protest this! That Warrington lad has taken a most outrageous liberty with this entire club!”
“Who sponsored him in here, anyway? Wasn’t it you, March?” a bystander called.
“Aye, and I thought I was sponsoring a proper heir to a thirty thousand a year estate, didn’t I?” March snapped. “Shut your damned mouth and play your cards.”
But Jack Morris’s lies found plenty of listeners all over town. Everyone agreed it was shockingly improper of Harry to have ever thought his brother dead, that his arrival in England in the first place was deeply suspicious, that all these foreign colonials and adventurers ought to be treated with the utmost caution.

Harry, meanwhile, was sitting alone smoking his Virginia pipe, waiting for George to come back from Madame Bernstein’s. He was free of prison, free of debt thanks to his aunt’s generous gift, his beloved brother was restored to him, and Maria Esmond had stood by him through all his worst troubles – but he was far from happy. It wasn’t the loss of the great estate that grieved him, he never cared a farthing for that, he knew George would share every penny with him just as he would have shared with George. The weight on his chest was that he was now poor, and irrevocably bound to marry his elderly cousin Maria. There was no getting around it, she was old – that day she’d come to see him in that horrid Cursitor Street debtor’s den, the tears had streaked her rouge clean off, and she’d looked as wrinkled and yellow as his own mother. He thought of the terrible fights there would be between her and his mother when he brought her home to Virginia, and shuddered. He’d take her out to the farthest, most isolated plantation, he decided, live there with a handful of negro hands, farm the land as best he could, hunt every day to keep busy. But Maria, who was used to London court balls, card parties, assemblies every single night – what kind of life would that be for her? He’d be honest, he’d work as overseer for George’s estates, make up for all his wild, wasteful spending over the last six months, the five thousand pounds of his own patrimony he’d gambled away in half a year. He was a beggar now, except for George’s kindness, and the only thing he had left in life was that old wife to be. A fine figure he’d cut in Europe, all dressed up in velvet and silver lace, a pretty end to all his grand dreams! He hadn’t been happy a single day since he sailed for England, not really – except those four perfect days he’d spent with the Lamberts in Hill Street, kind Mrs. Lambert, sweet Hetty and Theo, the brave good General who’d scolded him for his follies and still worked tirelessly to get him out of prison. God bless all of them, he thought, puffing slowly on his pipe, as he listened for the sound of his brother’s footsteps on the stairs.
