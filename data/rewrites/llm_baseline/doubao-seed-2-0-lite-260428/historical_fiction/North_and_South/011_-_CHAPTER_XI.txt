“There’s iron, they say, in all our blood,
And a grain or two perhaps is good;
But his, he makes me harshly feel,
Has got a little too much of steel.”

“Margaret!” Mr. Hale called, stepping back into the parlour after seeing their guest, Mr. Thornton, safely down the Milton staircase to the street. “I could not help watching your face no small amount of anxiety when he owned up to having worked as a shop-boy, you know. Mr. Bell told me that whole history long before we moved here, so I was braced for the confession, but half expected you’d leap straight up and quit the room out of shock.”

“Oh papa, you can’t truly think me that silly!” Margaret laughed, shaking her head, the faint furrow between her brows smoothing out. “I liked that part of his story better than anything else he said all evening. Every other word out of him reeked of that hard, sharp-edged Milton grit that rubs one the wrong way—except for that bit, when he spoke of his youth so simply, no trace of the showy pretence that makes so many tradespeople vulgar, with such tender, unshakable respect for his mother. I was far less inclined to leave then than I was when he was boasting of Milton as if no other town on earth could hold a candle to it, or coolly dismissing the working people around him as lazy, improvident wastrels without ever once seeming to think it his duty to lift them up, to give them even a fraction of the stern, loving training his own mother gave him, the thing that built all his success, whatever that success may be. No—his admission he’d been a shop boy was the very best thing he said all night.”

“I am surprised at you, Margaret,” her mother cut in, fanning herself lightly, her expression tight with lingering disapproval. “You who were forever poking fun at people for being ‘too shoppy’ back at Helstone! I do not think you did quite right, Mr. Hale, introducing a man like this to us without a single warning of his origins. I was half-convinced I could not hide how horrified I was at some of his stories. His father ‘dying in miserable circumstances’? Why, he could well have perished in the workhouse.”

“I am not at all sure it was not worse than the workhouse,” Mr. Hale replied gently. “Mr. Bell told me the whole of his history long before we came to Milton. He shared a sliver of it tonight, so I will fill in the gaps. His father gambled wildly with other people’s money, speculating far beyond his means, failed spectacularly, and killed himself rather than bear the public disgrace. Every old acquaintance of the family shrank away when the full extent of his dishonest, desperate gambles to win back his lost fortune came out. No one stepped forward to help the widow, this boy, and their little daughter, then too young to earn a penny. Mrs. Thornton is not the sort of woman to sit around waiting for tardy charity to find her, you may be sure. They vanished from Milton for years. We know he took a job behind a shop counter, and his wages, plus a tiny fragment of property his mother had managed to secure away from the creditors, kept the four of them alive for years. Mr. Bell swears they survived almost entirely on water porridge for months on end, no one quite knows how. Long after the creditors had written off every last penny of the old man’s debts—if they ever held out any hope of being repaid at all, after the suicide—this young man walked back into Milton, and went quietly round to every single creditor, handing them the first instalment of the money he owed them. No grand announcements, no gathering of crowds, no fanfare. He worked and paid every last penny of it off, all the way to the end, and it was that quiet, unyielding integrity that led one of the old creditors, a gruff, stubborn old man, to take him on as a business partner. That is how he built his fortune.”

“That is fine, truly,” Margaret said, her eyes bright. “What a pity a nature so noble has been warped by his life as a Milton manufacturer, though.”

“Warped how?” her father asked.

“Oh papa, everything he touches he measures by the standard of wealth! He spoke of all the new steam-powered machinery in his mill as nothing more than a new way to expand trade and make more money. And the poor labourers all around him—he sees their poverty as proof they are shiftless, outside the reach of his sympathy, simply because they do not have that same iron-hard will he has that let him drag himself out of ruin.”

“He never said vicious, Margaret. His words were improvident, and self-indulgent,” Mr. Hale corrected softly.

Margaret was gathering up her mother’s sewing supplies to tidy away for the night, ready to head up to bed, when she paused in the doorway. She wanted to say something that would please her father, even if it held a small sting of honesty. “Papa, I do think Mr. Thornton a very remarkable man. But personally? I do not like him at all.”

“And I do!” her father laughed. “Personally, as you call it, and all. I do not hail him as a hero, far from it. But goodnight, child. Your mother looks so very tired tonight, Margaret.”

Margaret had noticed her mother’s jaded, drawn face with quiet anxiety for days now, and that small remark sent her up the stairs with a heavy, dim fear settled in her chest like lead. Life in Milton was nothing like the soft, open-air days Mrs. Hale had known at Helstone, where she could wander out among the trees and fields whenever she pleased. The air here was thick with coal smoke, no trace of that sharp, revivifying freshness that had always revived her. Sordid, unaccustomed domestic worries crowded in on all of them, and there was every reason to fear her mother’s health was fraying badly at the edges. There were other small, worrying signs, too. Mrs. Hale and Dixon held long, hush-hush consultations behind closed bedroom doors, and Dixon would emerge red-eyed and cross, as was her habit when her mistress’s distress pulled at all her sympathies. Once Margaret had stepped into the bedroom right after Dixon left, and found her mother on her knees, so quiet Margaret almost did not see her. As she slipped back out to give her privacy, she caught half a dozen words, a quiet prayer for strength and patience to bear whatever sharp, constant pain she was suffering through.

Margaret ached to rebuild the intimate, trusting bond that had frayed during her long years staying with Aunt Shaw in London. She tried every soft caress, every gentle word, to worm her way back into the warmest, most secure spot in her mother’s heart. Though her mother returned every bit of that affection, hugging her close and murmuring sweet old names she had not heard in years, Margaret could feel there was a secret being kept from her—one that bore directly on her mother’s fragile health. She lay awake for hours that night, planning how to soften the harshness of their Milton life for her mother. She would find a second servant to take permanent work alongside Dixon, even if she spent every waking hour searching for one. Then, at the very least, her mother would have all the personal care she was used to, the care she had never gone without a single day before they moved to this smoky town.

For days after that, Margaret’s whole time and attention was swallowed up visiting servant registries, interviewing one unsuitable candidate after another, barely finding a single one even halfway up to the work. One afternoon, walking home from a particularly unpromising interview, she spotted Bessy Higgins on the pavement, and stopped to speak to her.

“Bessy! How are you? I hope you are feeling better, now the wind has shifted away from that sharp east blow that was making everyone ill.”

“Better and no better, if yo’ know what that means,” Bessy coughed, a faint, tired smile tugging at her lips.

“I am not sure I do,” Margaret said, smiling back.

“I’m better off for not being torn to pieces by coughing every hour o’ the night, but I’m weary to the bone of Milton, and I’m longing so bad to get away to the land of Beulah that every time I think I’m further than ever from it, my heart sinks right down, and I’m worse than I was before.”

Margaret turned to walk beside her, keeping pace with the girl’s slow, faltering steps home. She was quiet for a minute or two before she spoke, low and soft. “Bessy—do you wish to die?” She herself clung so fiercely to every small, bright part of life, young and healthy as she was, that the thought of welcoming death felt alien to her.

Bessy was silent for a minute, too. Then she said, quiet, “If yo’d led the life I’ve led, and grown as weary of it as I am, and thought sometimes ‘maybe this’ll drag on for fifty, sixty years, it does for some folks’—and got dizzy and dazed, sick at the thought of each one of those sixty years spinning around you, mocking you with all its endless hours and minutes, all that endless empty time—oh wench! I tell thee, thou’d be right glad when the doctor told thee he feared thou’d never see another winter.”

“Why Bessy, what has your life been, to make you feel that way?”

“Nought worse than many others’, I reckon. Only I fretted against it, and them others didn’t.”

“Tell me, then. I am a stranger here, you know, so I may not catch all the little unspoken things that people who grew up in Milton take for granted.”

“If yo’d come to our house when yo’ said yo’ would, I might ha’ told you long ago. But father says yo’re just like the rest of them—out of sight, out of mind, the second yo’ get busy with your own folk.”

“I don’t know who the rest are, but I have been so busy, and I will own it—for a little while I truly forgot my promise,” Margaret said honestly. “I would have remembered it again, the moment I was less swamped. May I come home with you now?”

Bessy flicked a quick, sharp glance up at her face, to see if the offer was sincere. The hard, wary glint in her eye melted into soft, wistful longing when she met Margaret’s gentle, open gaze. “I’ve not got many to care for me. If yo’ care to come, yo’ may.”

They walked on in silence together. When they turned off the squalid main street into a narrow, cramped little court of worker’s cottages, Bessy spoke again. “Don’t be daunted if father’s at home, and speaks gruff at first. He took a right fancy to yo’, you see, and thought so much of your promise to come visit, that he was vexed and put out when yo’ didn’t show, just because he liked you so.”

“Don’t fear, Bessy,” Margaret said, squeezing her arm gently.

Nicholas Higgins was not at home when they stepped through the door. A tall, rawboned girl, younger than Bessy but far stronger, was slamming things around the wash tub, banging furniture and making such a racket that Margaret’s heart ached for Bessy, who sank straight onto the first chair she saw, as if every last scrap of her strength had run out the second she crossed the threshold. Margaret asked the sister for a cup of water, and while she ran to fetch it—knocking over the fire irons and tripping over a chair in her hurry—Margaret untied the strings of Bessy’s bonnet, letting her draw deep, unobstructed breaths.

“Do you think a life like this is worth caring for at all?” Bessy gasped out, finally.

Margaret did not speak, just held the cup of cool water to her lips. Bessy drank a long, feverish draught, then leaned back and shut her eyes. Margaret heard her murmur, half to herself: “They shall hunger no more, neither thirst any more: neither shall the sun light on them, nor any heat.”

Margaret leaned over her, her voice soft. “Bessy, do not be impatient with whatever life has given you. Remember who gave it to you, who made it exactly what it is!”

She nearly jumped out of her skin when she heard Nicholas Higgins speak right behind her. He had come in so quietly she had not heard the door open.

“Now then, I’ll not have my lass preached at. She’s bad enough as it is, with her dreams, and her Methodist fancies, and her visions of cities with golden gates and precious stones. If it amuses her I let it be, but I’ll not have no more of that stuff poured into her ear.”

“Surely,” Margaret said, turning to face him, “you must believe that God gave her life, and ordained exactly what kind of life it would be?”

“I believe what I can see with my own two eyes, and nothing more. That’s my creed, young woman. I don’t believe half the things I hear, not by a long shot. I heard a young lass making a great fuss about knowing where we lived, and swearing she’d come visit us. And my lass there thought of it day and night, blushing every time she heard a strange step on the pavement, hoping it was you. But you’re here at last—and you’re welcome, so long as you don’t go preaching about things you know nothing at all about.”

Bessy had been watching Margaret’s face, and she half sat up now, laying a thin, trembling hand on Margaret’s arm in a gesture of entreaty. “Don’t be vexed with him. Half the folk in Milton think just like him. If yo’ heard all they say, you’d never be shocked at him. He’s a rare good man, is father—but oh!” she fell back against the chair, her voice breaking with despair, “the things he says sometimes make me long to die even more, because I’m so full of questions, so tossed about with wondering at all the things I don’t understand.”

“Poor lass, poor old lass,” Higgins said, his gruff voice softening. “I’m loath to vex thee, I truly am. But a man has to speak the truth, and when I see the whole world going mad these days, fussing over things they know not a single thing about, and leaving all the messy, broken things right in front of their own doors all untended—why I say, leave off all this talk about religion, and set to work fixing the things you can see. That’s my creed. It’s simple, it’s close to hand, it’s not hard to live by.”

But Bessy only pleaded harder with Margaret. “Don’t think hard of him, he’s a good man, he truly is. Sometimes I think I’ll be sad even in the City of God, if father’s not there with me.” A bright, feverish flush rose to her cheeks, her eyes blazing bright. “But you will be there, father! You shall! Oh—!” She clapped a hand to her chest, and her face went ghastly pale.

Margaret caught her, cradling the tired, limp head against her bosom, brushing the fine thin hair back from her temples and dabbing at her forehead with cool water. Nicholas knew every little sign of her distress as fast as a parent can, and even the rough, clumsy younger sister moved with gentle, careful steps the second Margaret whispered “hush.” The spasm, that small warning of how near death Bessy was, passed after a minute, and Bessy stirred, lifting her head.

“I’ll go to bed, it’s the best place for me now,” she said, but her fingers curled tight around the hem of Margaret’s gown. “But yo’ll come again, I know yo’ will—just say it, promise me.”

“I will come see you tomorrow,” Margaret said.

Bessy leaned back against her father, who lifted her carefully to carry her up the narrow staircase to their garret bedroom. As Margaret turned to leave, Higgins lingered at the bottom of the steps, struggling to find the words to say something. “I wish there was a God, y’know. If only to ask Him to bless thee.”

Margaret walked home heavy-hearted, lost in thought, far later than she usually was. At Helstone, unpunctuality at teatime had been one of Mrs. Hale’s greatest pet peeves, but now that small rule, and a dozen other little old household strictures, had faded away completely. Margaret almost found herself longing for those old familiar complaints.

“Did you find a suitable servant, dear?” her mother asked when she walked in the door.

“No, mamma. That Anne Buckley we saw today would never do at all.”

“Suppose I try,” Mr. Hale put in, smiling. “Everyone else has had a turn at this great difficulty. Let me take a crack at it. I might turn out to be the Cinderella who slips the slipper on the right foot, after all.”

Margaret could barely even muster a smile at the little joke, so weighed down as she was by her visit to the Higgins cottage. “What would you do, papa? How would you even start?”

“Why, I would ask some respectable, well-established house-mother to recommend a girl she and her own staff know to be reliable.”

“That’s a very good plan, but first we have to catch our ‘house-mother’,” Margaret said.

“You have caught her. Or rather, she is walking right into our trap, and you will win her over tomorrow, if you’re at all clever.”

“What on earth do you mean, Mr. Hale?” Mrs. Hale asked, her curiosity piqued.

“My paragon pupil, as Margaret calls Mr. Thornton,” Mr. Hale said, “told me that his mother intends to call on Mrs. and Miss Hale tomorrow.”

“Mrs. Thornton!” Mrs. Hale exclaimed.

“The mother he spoke of the other night?” Margaret asked, her head tilting up.

“Mrs. Thornton, the only mother he has, as far as I know,” Mr. Hale said, calm as ever. “I should very much like to meet her. She must be a most uncommon woman,” Mrs. Hale added, her eyes lighting up. “Perhaps she has a relative looking for work who would suit us perfectly, and would be glad of the post. She sounded to be such a careful, economical person, I would be happy to take on anyone from her family circle.”
