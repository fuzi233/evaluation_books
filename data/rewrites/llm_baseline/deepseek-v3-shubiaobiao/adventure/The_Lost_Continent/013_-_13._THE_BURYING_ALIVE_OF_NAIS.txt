The will of Phorenice was law in Atlantis, swift and unyielding. To her, the empire existed solely to serve her desires, and no cost was too great to see them fulfilled. When she decreed that Nais would be entombed alive beneath a throne of stone, the city moved as one to obey.

Heralds cried the proclamation through every ward. Masons, laborers, engineers—all left their work and converged upon the square before the royal pyramid. The architects seized command, directing the demolition of buildings to haul the monstrous stones into place. The throne rose with brutal efficiency, tier upon tier, each block carved and fitted under the lash of urgency.

By dawn, the structure loomed over the square—a grim monument of seven steps leading to a hollow meant to cradle a living sacrifice. Above it, suspended by iron rams, waited the massive seat of the throne itself, its underside carved with the coiled serpent of the Empress, its shadow poised to crush all beneath.

I emerged from the pyramid as the sun climbed, my face a mask, my heart a storm. The square teemed with onlookers—nobles in their finery, common folk in rags, all drawn by the spectacle. Trumpets blared as Phorenice arrived, radiant in gold and ivory, borne aloft by her savage European bearers. The crowd sighed at her beauty, their envy for me a palpable thing.

Nais was brought forth in chains, her face pale but resolute. She wore her garments still—some small mercy, likely purchased by Ylga’s coin. She knelt before Phorenice, her defiance unspoken but unwavering.

"One last chance," the Empress purred. "Renounce your folly, name a man, and I will pardon you."

Nais did not hesitate. "I will not wed."

Phorenice’s smile sharpened. "Then let the symbol be made."

I moved like a man carved from stone. The guards unshackled Nais, and I seized her, my grip rough for the eyes of the crowd, my soul breaking beneath the act. I dragged her to the hollow beneath the throne, clamped the brazen restraints over her limbs, and stepped back.

The rams groaned. The throne-stone descended, inch by terrible inch. I watched, dry-mouthed, as the gap narrowed, as the light left her face. Then—finality. The stone settled into place, sealing her beneath.

The architects declared it perfect.

Phorenice took my hand, her eyes alight with triumph. "Now," she said, "we wed."

Before the throng, we spoke the rites, each word ash in my mouth. She cut my arm, drank my blood; I did the same, the taste of her like poison. The crowd roared approval, blind to the abyss beneath the ceremony.

Then—a disturbance. A wounded man staggered into the square, gasping of rebels breaching the walls.

Phorenice’s glee turned to battle-lust. "Come, husband," she said. "Let us remind them why they fear us."

I followed, my heart entombed with Nais, my body moving by rote. The fight awaited. The empire demanded it.

And so the game continued.
