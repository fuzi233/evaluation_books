The Tempest.

It is finished. The great Armada lies broken and defeated. The eternal war, that struggle between light and darkness, between Michael and his angels against Satan and his fiends, has momentarily paused. For centuries, this battle raged in the spiritual realm, invisible to men, but today it has become flesh and blood. Simple men have chosen their sides without error, aiding God's cause not merely with prayer, but with sharp shot and cold steel. Judgment has fallen, dividing the sheep from the goats, and proving the devil's work to be nothing but a lie—a windy boast that collapses at the slightest pinprick. Empires rise and fall like bubbles; the Spanish Armada, that great bullying phantom, vanished in a cloud of sulphur and fear, leaving the children of Israel to sing the song of Moses and the Lamb.

From England, the Netherlands, Germany, and Geneva, even from the persecuted Vaudois saints whose bones lie scattered on the cold Alpine mountains, a shout of holy joy rose to the heavens. It was a prophetic birth-paean for the new world, for free commerce, and for the triumph of liberty. As Van Meteran recorded, the Queen rode into London in triumph, her chariot drawn through the streets lined with blue cloth and banners, while the citizens acclaimed her long life and the confusion of her foes. "It came, it saw, and it fled!" the medals proclaimed. Away to the north, like frightened deer, the Spanish fleet fled past the Orkneys and Shetlands, denied water and food by the brave descendants of the Vikings, until they were scattered by the gales of the Atlantic, few reaching the shores of Spain.

And where were Amyas and the *Vengeance* during this time?

At the fifty-seventh degree of latitude, the English fleet, finding themselves short of provisions and powder, turned southward toward home, leaving the Spaniards to the northern seas. A few pinnaces were sent to watch, but the main fleet, caught in the same storms that scattered the enemy, reached Harwich safely. Meanwhile, Parma, unable to believe the idol at Halle had played such a scurvy trick, watched in vain from the Dunkirk dunes. The Armada was gone, and Amyas was following in its wake.

When the Lord High Admiral decided to return, Amyas asked leave to pursue the Spaniard. He secured ammunition and provisions from Sir John Hawkins, lodging bills in the admiral's pocket among thimbles and tobacco, promising repayment from plunder or estate. Calling his men together, he reminded them of the Rose of Torridge and Don Guzman de Soto, then asked: "Men of Bideford, will you follow me? There will be plunder for those who love it, revenge for those who crave it, and for all of us, the honor of never leaving the chase while a Spanish flag flies in English seas." Every soul aboard replied they would follow Sir Amyas Leigh around the world.

There is no need to detail every incident of that long, weary chase—the attack on the *Sta. Catharina*, the desperate maneuvers to prevent her escape, the crippling of the great Venetian galleys, the mutinous whispers of the younger hands, the strange encounter with the Hebridean Scots of Skye who fought and then embraced them, and the grim decision to let the crews of two great Spanish wrecks on the Mull of Cantire be smuggled off to Spain to face execution elsewhere. They passed the strange island of Raghary, the "drowned magpie," and the *Sta. Catharina* herself, which nearly perished on the Isle of Man and later ran aground on Sarn David before being freed by the tide. These moving incidents I must pass over to reach the end, for the time has come.

It was the sixteenth day of the chase. The evening before, they had seen St. David's Head and the Welsh coast looming black against a thunderstorm. Through the night, lightning flashed fitfully, but the tall masts of the Spaniard remained hidden. Certain that the wind would not allow her to go far westward, and fearing she might be lying-to ahead of them, the English waited. Dawn broke on a sunless, drizzly day, roofed with low, dingy clouds, the sea a long glassy roll under an inky sky.

Amyas paced the sloppy deck, fretful and fierce. He knew the Spaniard could not escape, yet he cursed every moment that lingered between him and the revenge that blackened his soul. The men sat sulkily, whistling for a wind that would not come.

"Take care of those guns. You will have something loose next," growled Amyas.
"We will take care of the guns, if the Lord will take care of the wind," said Yeo.
"We shall have plenty before night," said Cary. "And thunder too."
"So much the better," said Amyas. "It may roar till it splits the heavens, if it does but let me get my work done."

"He's not far off, I warrant," said Cary. "One lift of the cloud, and we should see him."
"To windward of us, as likely as not," said Amyas. "The devil fights for him, I believe. To have been on his heels sixteen days and not sent this through him yet!" He shook his sword impatiently.

The morning wore on without a sign of life, not even a passing gull. The black melancholy of the heaven reflected itself in the black melancholy of Amyas. Was he to lose his prey? The thought made him shudder with rage.
"No, God!" he cried. "Let me but once feel this in his accursed heart, and then—strike me dead, if Thou wilt!"
"The Lord have mercy on us," cried John Brimblecombe. "What have you said?"
"What is that to you, sir? There, they are piping to dinner. Go down. I shall not come."

Jack went down, whispering in terror of Amyas's ominous words. Only old Yeo seemed unafraid.
"Well, Sir John," he said, "and why not? What better can the Lord do for a man than take him home when he has done his work? Our captain is wilful and spiteful, and must needs kill his man himself. I owe him no grudge. May the Lord give him repentance... but if I could but see him once safe ashore, I would say, 'Lord, now lettest Thou Thy servant depart in peace,' even if it were the lightning which was sent to fetch me."

Suddenly, Amyas's voice thundered from the deck. "Here she is!"
In an instant, all scrambled up the hatchway. Yes, there she was. The cloud had lifted, revealing a ragged bore of blue sky shining on her tall masts as she lay rolling some four or five miles to the eastward. Land was not visible.
"There she is; and here we are," said Cary. "But where is here? and where is there? How is the tide, master?"
"Running up Channel by this time, sir."
"What matters the tide?" said Amyas, devouring the ship with terrible, cold blue eyes. "Can't we get at her?"
"Not unless some one jumps out and shoves behind," said Cary. "I shall down again and finish that mackerel..."
"Don't jest, Will! I can't stand it," said Amyas, his voice quivering. His whole frame trembled like an aspen. Cary took his arm and drew him aside.
"Dear old lad," he said, "what is this? You are not yourself, and have not been these four days."
"No. I am not Amyas Leigh. I am my brother's avenger. Do not reason with me, Will: when it is over I shall be merry old Amyas again." He passed his hand over his brow. "Do you believe," he asked after a moment, "that men can be possessed by devils?"
"The Bible says so."
"If my cause were not a just one, I should fancy I had a devil in me. My throat and heart are as hot as the pit. Would to God it were done, for done it must be! Now go."

Cary went away with a shudder. As he passed down the hatchway, he looked back. Amyas had taken out his hone and was whetting his sword-edge as if there were some dreadful doom upon him, to whet and whet forever.

The weary day wore on. The strip of blue sky was curtained over again, and all was dismal, though sultrier every moment. Nothing could be done to lessen the distance; the *Vengeance* had lost all her boats but one, which was too small to tow her. While the men finished dinner, Amyas worked on his sword, looking up suddenly at the Spaniard as if to satisfy himself that it was not a vision.

About two o'clock, Yeo came up.
"He is ours safely now, sir. The tide has been running to the eastward for this two hours."
"Safe as a fox in a trap. Satan himself cannot take him from us!"
"But God may," said Brimblecombe, simply.
"Who spoke to you, sir? If I thought that He—"
"There comes the thunder at last!"

An angry growl from the westward heavens answered his wild words, rolling and loudening nearer and nearer, till right over their heads it crashed against some cloud-cliff far above, and all was still. Each man looked in the other's face, but Amyas was unmoved.
"The storm is coming," he said. "The wind in it. It will be Eastward-ho now, for once, my merry men all!"
"Eastward-ho never brought us luck," said Jack in an undertone to Cary. But by this time all eyes were turned to the north-west, where a black line along the horizon began to define the boundary of sea and air.
"There comes the breeze."
"And there the storm, too."

With a strangely accelerating pace, the thunder rang peal on peal along the cloudy floor.
"Here comes the breeze. Round with the yards, or we shall be taken aback."
The yards creaked round; the sea grew crisp; the hot air swept their cheeks, tightening every rope, filling every sail, bending the ship over. A cheer burst from the men as the helm went up, and they staggered away before the wind, right down upon the Spaniard, who lay still becalmed.
"There is more behind, Amyas," said Cary. "Shall we not shorten sail a little?"
"No. Hold on every stitch," said Amyas. "Give me the helm, man. Boatswain, pipe away to clear for fight."

It was done. In ten minutes, the men were at quarters while the thunder rolled louder and the breeze freshened.
"The dog has it now. There he goes!" said Cary.
"Right before the wind. He has no liking to face us."
"He is running into the jaws of destruction," said Yeo. "An hour more will send him either right up the Channel, or smack on shore somewhere."

"There! he has put his helm down. I wonder if he sees land?"
"He is like a March hare beat out of his country," said Cary, "and doesn't know whither to run next."

Cary was right. In ten minutes more, the Spaniard fell off again, going dead down wind while the *Vengeance* gained on him fast. After two hours, the four miles had diminished to one, while lightning flashed nearer as the storm came up. From the vast mouth of a black cloud-arch poured a fierce breeze, and Amyas yielded unwillingly to hints growing into open murmurs, bidding them shorten sail.

On they rushed with scarcely lessened speed, the black arch following fast, curtained by the flat gray sheet of pouring rain before which the water boiled in a long white line. Every moment behind the watery veil, a keen blue spark leapt down into the sea or darted zigzag through the rain.
"We shall have it now, and with a vengeance; this will try your tackle, master," said Cary.
The functionary answered with a shrug, turning up the collar of his rough frock as the first drops flew stinging round his ears. Another minute, and the squall burst full upon them in rain that cut like hail, lashing the sea into froth and whirling off the heads of the surges into one white seething waste. Above, behind, and before them, lightning leapt and ran, dazzling and blinding, while the deep roar of thunder changed to sharp, ear-piercing cracks.

"Get the arms and ammunition under cover, and then below with you all," shouted Amyas from the helm.
"And heat the pokers in the galley fire," said Yeo, "to be ready if the rain puts our linstocks out. I hope you'll let me stay on deck, sir, in case—"
"I must have some one, and who better than you? Can you see the chase?"
No; she was wrapped in the gray whirlwind. She might be within half a mile of them, for aught they could see.

Now Amyas and his old liegeman were alone. Neither spoke; each knew the other's thoughts. The squall blew fiercer, the rain poured heavier. Where was the Spaniard?
"If he has laid-to, we may overshoot him, sir!"
"If he has tried to lay-to, he will not have a sail left in the bolt-ropes, or perhaps a mast on deck. I know the stiff-neckedness of those Spanish tubs. Hurrah! there he is, right on our larboard bow!"

There she was indeed, two musket-shots off, staggering away with canvas split and flying.
