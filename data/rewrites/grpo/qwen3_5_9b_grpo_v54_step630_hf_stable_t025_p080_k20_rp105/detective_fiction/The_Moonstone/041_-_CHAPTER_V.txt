Betteredge finished reciting the name of Mr. Candy's assistant with a shrug that suggested we had exhausted the well of trivialities for the day. He folded Rosanna Spearman's letter, his movements deliberate, and returned to the silence of the room.

I remained seated by the window, the afternoon light casting long, indifferent shadows across the floor. The memory of Ezra Jennings—the sheer, inexplicable weight of his presence in such a chaotic atmosphere—began to dissolve, retreating like mist before the sun. My mind, restless and sharp, forced itself back into the rigid channels of survival. I had to face the absurdity of my own position, the precipice upon which I stood, and chart a course forward.

My plan was clear, though born of desperation: return to London immediately, lay the entire case before Mr. Bruff, and, above all, secure an audience with Rachel. Whatever the cost, whatever the sacrifice, I had to see her. There was still an hour before the train departed. In that interim, I clung to a fragile hope: perhaps Betteredge, in his quiet scrutiny, had found a clue in the unread portions of Rosanna's letter that I needed before leaving the house where the diamond had vanished.

The letter concluded with a confession that chilled the blood more effectively than any accusation.

*"You have no need to be angry, Mr. Franklin,"* Rosanna wrote, her voice seemingly echoing through the paper. *"Even if I felt a little triumph knowing I held your prospects in my hands, anxieties soon returned. With Sergeant Cuff's obsession with the diamond, every corner of the house felt unsafe. How to hide the nightgown so thoroughly that not even he could find it? These were not easy questions."*

She described her desperate solution: she had worn the nightgown herself, a fleeting pleasure in possessing what he had coveted. But luck, or fate, had turned against her. When Sergeant Cuff demanded the washing book, she had delivered it in the lady's sitting-room. The Sergeant's gaze had been cold, his politeness suspicious. She had fled to the shrubbery, hoping for a final word, only to find him passing by, his eyes locking onto hers. He had seen her.

*"I had no choice but to return to my work,"* she continued, her tone trembling with suppressed agony. *"Just as I stepped across the path, you returned from the railway. You saw me—I am certain you did—and you turned away as if I carried a contagion, retreating into the house."*

A footnote from Franklin Blake interrupted the narrative, a bitter correction: *The writer is entirely mistaken. I never noticed her. Remembering my aunt might wish to see me, I altered my course and went inside.*

Rosanna's letter poured out her internal torment. She had retreated to the laundry, consumed by the "Shivering Sand"—the very spot where she had hidden the diamond. The temptation to end it all had surged, a dark whisper suggesting that enduring his indifference was harder than death itself. Yet, she had hesitated. Why? Because she loved him. Behind his back, she adored him; before his face, she was paralyzed by fear. She had tried to provoke anger to rouse her courage, but all she felt was humiliation. He was a gentleman; she was a maid with a crooked shoulder. He had taken the diamond to sell it, she reasoned, and she possessed the key to his salvation—a man in London who would buy it without asking questions.

*"Why didn't I stop you?"* she cried out in the text. *"Why didn't I tell you I could help you? I could have been useful!"*

Her resolve crumbled further when Penelope entered the room. Penelope, who knew the secret, spoke of Rachel's deteriorating temper and the likelihood of Franklin's departure. *"If they don't make it up before tomorrow, you will see Miss Rachel go one way, and Mr. Franklin another,"* Penelope had warned. *"He will never stay here after she leaves."*

This news struck Rosanna with the force of a physical blow. The prospect of Franklin leaving meant the loss of her only chance to speak to him, and consequently, the loss of her life. She realized then that she could not destroy the nightgown. It was her only proof of her loyalty, her only claim on his gratitude. Without it, how could she prove she hadn't stolen it? How could she win his trust if he suspected her of malice?

*"I determined to hide it,"* she wrote, *"in the place I knew best—the Shivering Sand."*

After the interrogation by Sergeant Cuff, which had confirmed his suspicions but failed to pinpoint her as the sole culprit, Rosanna had slipped away to Yolland's cottage. There, in Lucy Yolland's bedroom, she penned this letter, rolling the nightgown beneath her cloak. She planned to bury it in the sand, deep enough that no living creature could find it without her guidance.

*"If I miss my next opportunity,"* she pleaded, *"good-bye to the world... Good-bye to life, which nothing but a little kindness from you can ever make pleasurable to me again."*

She begged him to find her ghost, if she died, and speak kindly of her, just as he did to Rachel. She hoped to tear up the letter in joy if they reconciled, but feared the silence of the grave if they did not.

Betteredge finished reading in absolute silence. He placed the letter back in its envelope, his head bowed, eyes fixed on the floorboards.

"Betteredge," I asked, breaking the heavy quiet, "is there any hint to guide me at the end of the letter?"

He looked up slowly, a heavy sigh escaping his lips. "There is nothing to guide you, Mr. Franklin. If you take my advice, keep the letter in the cover until these anxieties have passed. It will sorely distress you whenever you read it. Do not read it now."

I tucked the letter safely into my pocket-book.

Looking back at the narrative of the previous weeks, the tragedy of Rosanna's fate becomes starkly clear. Twice she had made her last attempt to reach me. On the Friday night, at the billiard table, her manner suggested she was about to confess her knowledge of the diamond's disappearance. For her own sake, I had deliberately averted my gaze, staring at the balls rather than her, sending her away wounded and heartbroken.

On Saturday, the day her departure seemed imminent, she sought me in the shrubbery again. This time, I was accompanied by Betteredge and Sergeant Cuff. In an effort to protect her, I had loudly declared to the Sergeant—and to her—that I felt "no interest whatever in Rosanna Spearman." Designed to warn her against seeking my private ear, those words had been her death knell. Cautioned of her danger, believing herself self-doomed, she had turned away, walking straight into the trap that would lead to the quicksand.

From that point, the chain of events leading to my discovery at the Shivering Sand had begun. The retrospect is now complete. I may leave the miserable story of Rosanna Spearman, a tale that still brings a pang of distress even at this distance, to focus on the living. I must move forward from the darkness of that suicide to the toilsome journey toward the light, attending to the interests of those who remain.
