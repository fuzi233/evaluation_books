An appeal such as our mother's would have melted hearts far harder than ours; yet, despite her pleas, we delayed our journey to Virginia. We discussed hiring the entire cabin of the *Young Rachel* for the voyage, but nature intervened. Our youngest child fell ill, rendering the sea travel too perilous, and naturally, the mother could not be parted from her sickening offspring. Consequently, the *Young Rachel* sailed that year without us.

Another year brought further obstacles. First, my beloved wife suffered her initial attack of the gout—a condition that occupied me greatly at the time, though it eventually sharpened my wit and enlivened my spirits. Then came a sorrow far heavier: tragic news from Jamaica. Two years after establishing ourselves at the Manor, our dear General returned from his governorship. While he had accumulated worldly goods, he bore a loss for which no wealth could provide solace; indeed, he showed little desire to remain in the West Indies thereafter. My Theodore's poor mother—the most tender and affectionate friend I have ever known—had died abroad of fever. Her final regret was that she would not live to see her children and us prosper.

"She sees us, though we do not see her," her husband said softly, "and she thanks you, George, for having been good to her children."

We feared he would not long survive to join her. Love for her had been the sole purpose of his existence; to be away from her felt like ceasing to live. It was heartbreaking to watch the grieving widower sit among us. My wife, through her demeanor and countless gentle gestures, constantly reminded the bereaved father of his lost mother. We offered whatever comfort we could, but above all, little Hetty became his chief support. She had declined several advantageous matches in the island, and upon returning to England, the heir of Lord Wrotham threw himself at her feet. Yet, Hetty declared she preferred staying with her father. As long as he needed her, she desired no husband.

"Nay," we suggested when a suitor was proposed, "let the General stay six months with us at the Manor, and you can have him at Oakhurst for the other half-year."

Hetty firmly rejected this, stating her father could never endure Oakhurst again since her mother's passing. She would marry neither for a coronet nor for money. The General, observing this, noted gravely that the girl showed no desire to wed, perhaps due to an unspoken disappointment in her youth. Respecting her silence, we remained quiet as well.

Meanwhile, my brother Lambert had secured a college living near Winchester and a wife to adorn his parsonage. We professed a moderate liking for this lady, welcoming her warmly when she visited, though our affection was limited. Mrs. Jack, however, viewed Hetty's determined celibacy differently. She was a chatty woman, prone to speaking her mind freely and taking pride in inflicting pain upon her friends.

"My dear Sir George," she exclaimed cheerfully, "I have often told our dear Theo that I wouldn't have a pretty sister in my house to make tea for Jack while I was upstairs, or to hover in the kitchen or nursery, or to dress neatly when I was baking pies or watching the children. I trust Jack implicitly, of course! I'd like to see him look at another woman, truly! And so I feel about Jemima, though they rarely coincide in my house when I am upstairs—that I promise you! And thus I warned my sister Warrington."

"Am I to understand," the General asked sharply, "that you have done my Lady Warrington the favor of warning her against her sister, my daughter Miss Hester?"

"Yes, pa, of course!" cried Mrs. Jack, blushing and becoming voluble. "A duty is a duty, and a woman is a woman, and a man is a man, as I know very well. Don't tell me! He is a man. Every man is a man, with all his sanctified airs!"

"You yourself have a married sister, with whom you were staying when my son Jack first met you?" the General pressed.

"Yes, everyone knows that! I have been as good a mother to her children as anyone could wish!"

"And am I to gather from your conversation that your charms tempted your sister's husband?"

"Law, General! Who ever said such a thing?" Mrs. Jack cried, turning red.

"Do you not perceive, my dear madam, that it is you who have insinuated as much—not only regarding yourself, but concerning my two daughters?"

"Never, never, never, as I'm a Christian woman!" she retorted, offended. "It is cruel of you to say so. And I do believe a sister is best out of the house! And since Theo's time is approaching, I warn her, that is all."

"Have you discovered, my good madam, whether my poor Hetty has stolen any spoons?" the General countered. "When I came to breakfast this morning, my daughter was alone, yet there must have been a dozen pieces of silver on the table."

"Law, sir! Who accused the poor dear?" Mrs. Jack shrieked, flouncing toward the door in tears. "Did I ever accuse her? If I did, may I drop dead on this hearth-rug immediately! And I am not used to being spoken to thus. Me and Jack have both noticed it, and I have done my duty!" With that, she stormed out.

"And has the woman dared to tell you this, my child?" the General asked when Theo entered the tea-table, looking slightly pale.

"She tells me every day since she arrived," Theo replied sadly. "She enters my dressing-room saying, 'I wouldn't have a sister prowling about my nursery!' She visits my nursery with the same remark. Ah, how pleasant it is to have amiable and well-bred relatives, I say."

"Your poor mother was spared this woman," the General groaned.

"Our mother would have improved her," Theo added, kissing him.

"Yes, dear." Both rose for prayer.

Yet, loving one's relatives is not always easy, nor is living with neighbors always amusing. The following day, Jack Lambert's behavior confirmed his wife's version of events. He was sulky, though not dignified; angry, yet still hungry. He delivered a sermon to us that was utterly foolish. Meanwhile, little Miles, clad once more in sable, sat beside his grandfather, holding the kind old man's hand.

Should he stay to manage the house during our Virginian trip? The housekeeper must submit fully to Hetty's command. The butler's keys belonged to him, for Gumbo—who had happily married Mrs. Molly and populated a cottage in my park with numerous tawny Gumbos—was to accompany us to Virginia.

Under the care of our General and his daughter, we departed, traveling to London and then to Bristol. Our obliging agent there vowed prayers for our prosperity and claimed that children as beautiful as ours (for we now had an infant to join Miles) had never been seen aboard a ship before. The voyage was uneventful. How strange the sensation of landing at Richmond! A coach and a host of negroes awaited us. Nearby, a gentleman on horseback, accompanied by servants in our livery, dismounted and rushed forward to embrace us. Both of us were deeply charmed to see our dearest Hal. He rode with us to our mother's door. There she stood on the steps to welcome us, and Theo knelt to ask her blessing.

Harry rode in the coach only as far as the house, refusing to enter lest he "spoil the sport." "She sees me," he admitted, "and we are fairly good friends; but Fanny and she are best apart. There is no love lost between them, I can promise you. Come to the Tavern tomorrow, George, when you are free. And I shall have the honor of presenting her sister to Theo. It was only because I happened to be in town yesterday that I learned of the ship's arrival; I waited specifically to see you. I have sent a negro boy home to my wife, and she will arrive soon to pay her respects to my Lady Warrington." After this brief greeting, Harry leaped from the carriage, leaving us to greet our mother alone.

Though I had encountered fine company since departing, and though Theo and I had paid our respects to the King and Queen at St. James's, none compared in stateliness to the man who welcomed us. Raising my wife from her knees, he embraced her and led her into the house. It was a simple, wood-framed structure with a surrounding gallery, typical of Virginian homes; yet, if it had been a palace housing a little empress, our reception could not have been more courteous. Old Nathan still served as major-domo, surrounded by a dozen kind black faces grinning in welcome. Some whose names I knew as a child had grown into sturdy youths; others, once dark-haired, now wore snow-white hair; and some, born since my departure, peered curiously from doorways with large eyes and bare feet. "I'm little Sip, Master George!" cried one. "I'm Dinah, Sir George!" called another. "I'm Master Miles's boy!" shouted a small lad in new liveried boots. Before dusk, the entire household had found pretexts to pass before us, bowing and smiling.
