Deep behind the oldest bones of Holborn, London, where gabled houses from centuries past stare vacantly onto the street as if mourning a river long since dried up, lies a secluded enclave known as Staple Inn. It is a pocket of silence where the clamour of the city is dampened, imparting to the weary traveler the sensation of having cotton stuffed in his ears and velvet slipped beneath his boots. Here, a few smoky sparrows chirrup in smoky branches, pretending to play "country" amidst patches of garden soil and gravel, performing a delightful violence upon their tiny minds. Furthermore, it is a legal nook, housing a modest hall with a lantern in its roof—the specific obstructions and expenses of which history leaves vague.

In eras when Cloisterham fretted over the distant encroachment of railways, threatening the sanctity of British property, the architecture surrounding Staple Inn remained low-hanging. Then, the westering sun kissed it warmly, and the south-west winds swept through unobstructed.

Yet, on a December afternoon nearing six o'clock, neither sun nor wind graced Staple Inn. Instead, it was swallowed by a thick, yellow fog, and candles within the occupied chambers cast murky, blurred halos. Most notably, light spilled from a suite in a corner house of the inner quadrangle, marked above its grim portal by the cryptic inscription:

1747

Within these chambers, seemingly indifferent to the inscription save for occasional glances suggesting *Perhaps John Thomas* or *Perhaps Joe Tyler*, sat Mr. Grewgious by his fire.

To judge Mr. Grewgious by appearance, one could not determine if ambition or disappointment had ever stained his soul. Born to the Bar, he had aimed for chamber practice, intent on drawing deeds and conveying wisdom, as Pistol might phrase it. Yet, Conveyancing proved an unwilling partner; their union ended by mutual consent, never truly begun.

However, an Arbitration drifted his way, blowing great credit into his hands for his tireless pursuit of justice. This was followed by a lucrative Receivership, guided by a wind traceable to its source. By chance, he found his niche. Now Receiver and Agent to two wealthy estates, delegating their legal affairs to a firm of solicitors on the floor below, he had snuffed out his ambitions (assuming he ever lit them) and settled permanently under the dry vine and fig-tree of P.J.T., who planted in 1747.

His room was a fortress of order. Countless accounts, files, and strongboxes lined the space, arranged with such meticulous precision they did not so much clutter it as define it. The fear of dying suddenly and leaving a fact or figure incomplete or obscure would have killed Mr. Grewgious instantly. Faithfulness to a trust was the blood in his veins—a life-blood that flowed with a singular, unyielding gravity.

Luxury was absent. Comforts were restricted to dry warmth, a snug but faded hearth, and an easy-chair. His "private life" revolved around the fireplace, the chair, and an old-fashioned round table brought forth from a corner after business hours, acting as a defensive shield. Behind it lay a closet, usually stocked with something potable. Beyond that was the clerk's room; Mr. Grewgious slept upstairs, holding cellarage at the bottom of the stairs. For three hundred days a year, he traversed the path to Furnival's Inn for dinner and returned, maximizing these simple routines until broad daylight returned.

As Mr. Grewgious wrote by the fire, so did his clerk. A pale, puffy-faced man of thirty, with large, dull dark eyes and a doughy complexion that begged for the baker's shop, this attendant was a mysterious entity possessing a strange hold over Mr. Grewgious. Like a familiar conjured by a spell that failed to banish him, he clung to Mr. Grewgious's stool despite the obvious inconvenience to his employer. A gloomy figure with tangled locks, bearing the air of someone raised under the shadow of Java—a tree that shelters more lies than the botanical kingdom—Mr. Grewgious nonetheless treated him with inexplicable consideration.

"Now, Bazzard," said Mr. Grewgious as the clerk entered, looking up from his papers which he was arranging for the night. "What blows from the wind besides fog?"

"Mr. Drood," replied Bazzard.

"What of him?"

"He has called," said Bazzard.

"You might have shown him in."

"I am doing it," said Bazzard.

The visitor entered accordingly.

"Dear me!" said Mr. Grewgious, casting a glance around his pair of office candles. "I thought you had called merely to leave your name and departed. How do you do, Mr. Edwin? Dear me, you're choking!"

"It's this fog," returned Edwin. "It makes my eyes smart, like Cayenne pepper."

"Is it truly so bad? Pray undo your wrappers. It is fortunate I possess such a good fire; Mr. Bazzard has tended to me."

"No, I haven't," said Bazzard, standing at the door.

"Aha! Then it follows I must have attended to myself without observing it," said Mr. Grewgious. "Please, sit in my chair. No, I beg! After emerging from such an atmosphere, you cannot sit in *my* chair."

Edwin took the easy-chair in the corner. The fog he brought in, and the fog he shed with his greatcoat and neck-shawl, was swiftly consumed by the eager fire.

"I look," said Edwin, smiling, "as if I have come to stay."

"—By the by," cried Mr. Grewgious, interrupting with a sudden impulse. "Stay. The fog may lift in an hour or two. We can dine in from just across Holborn. You had better take your Cayenne pepper here than outside; please, stay and dine."

"You are very kind," said Edwin, glancing about as if attracted by the notion of a novel and hearty gipsy party.

"Not at all," said Mr. Grewgious. "*You* are very kind to join issue with a bachelor in chambers and accept pot-luck. And I'll ask," continued Mr. Grewgious, lowering his voice and speaking with a twinkle in his eye, as if inspired by a bright thought, "I'll ask Bazzard. He might not wish it otherwise.—Bazzard!"

Bazzard reappeared.

"Dine presently with Mr. Drood and me."

"If I am ordered to dine, of course I will, sir," was the gloomy reply.

"Save the man!" cried Mr. Grewgious. "You are not ordered; you are invited."

"Thank you, sir," said Bazzard, "in that case, I do not care if I do."

"That is arranged. And perhaps you would not mind," said Mr. Grewgious, "stepping over to the hotel in Furnival's and requesting materials for setting the cloth. For dinner, we shall have a tureen of the hottest, strongest soup available, the best made-dish recommended, a joint—such as a haunch of mutton—and a goose, or a turkey, or any stuffed delicacy that may be in the bill of fare—in short, we shall consume whatever remains."

Issuing these liberal directions with his usual air of reciting an inventory or rote lesson, Mr. Grewgious watched as Bazzard, after pulling out the round table, withdrew to execute them.

"I was somewhat delicate," said Mr. Grewgious in a lower tone after his clerk's departure, "regarding employing him in the foraging or commissariat department. Because he might not enjoy it."

"He seems to have his own way, sir," remarked Edwin.

"His own way?" returned Mr. Grewgious. "Oh, no! Poor fellow, you quite misunderstand him. If he had his own way, he would not be here."

"I wonder where he would be!" Edwin thought internally. But he kept it to himself, for Mr. Grewgious had risen and stood with his back to the other corner of the fire, his shoulder-blades resting against the mantelpiece, gathering his skirts for comfortable conversation.

"I take it, without prophetic gifts, that you have visited to mention your journey down yonder—where I assure you you are awaited—and to offer to execute any minor commission for my charming ward, and perhaps sharpen me up regarding certain proceedings? Eh, Mr. Edwin?"

"I called, sir, before departing, as an act of attention."

"Of attention!" said Mr. Grewgious. "Ah! Not of impatience?"

"Impatience, sir?"

Mr. Grewgious intended to be arch—not remotely expressing that meaning—and had positioned himself close to the fire to burn the full effect of his wit into himself, as subtle impressions are burned into hard metals. But his archness fled before the composed countenance and demeanor of his visitor; only the fire remained. He started, rubbing himself.

"I have recently visited down yonder," said Mr. Grewgious, rearranging his skirts. "And that is what I referred to when I said I could tell you you are expected."

"Indeed, sir! Yes; I knew that Pussy was watching for me."

"Do you keep a cat down there?" asked Mr. Grewgious.
