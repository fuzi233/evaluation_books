“Your beauty was the first that won the place
And scal’d the walls of my undaunted heart,
Which, captive now, pines in a caitive case,
Unkindly met with rigour for desert:—
Yet not the less your servant shall abide,
In spite of rude repulse or silent pride.”

The morning light found Margaret rising slowly, weary but relieved that the night had passed without alarm. Though she felt no true rest, her mother had stirred only once, and all within the house remained quiet. A faint breeze stirred the still air, and though there were no trees near to show its play among the leaves, Margaret could imagine it—the soft rustle through thickets, the whispering dance of branches in distant woods. The very thought brought a quiet joy to her spirit, like a hidden song beneath the surface of silence.

She sat at her sewing in her mother’s room, her thoughts fixed on the day ahead. Once her mother woke fully from her afternoon slumber, she would help her dress. After dinner, she meant to visit Bessy Higgins, whose condition weighed heavily upon her. And then—she resolved firmly—she would banish every thought of the Thorntons. There was no need to dwell on them until they stood before her again in flesh and blood.

But the effort to forget only made their presence more vivid. From time to time, a sudden warmth flushed her pale cheeks, as if sunlight broke through storm clouds over the sea. She could not escape them.

Dixon entered softly, tiptoeing across the floor. “Mr. Thornton, Miss Margaret. He is in the drawing-room.”

Margaret dropped her needle. “Did he ask for me? Isn’t Papa home yet?”

“He asked for you, miss. Master is out.”

“Very well,” she said quietly, though she hesitated long before moving.

Mr. Thornton stood by the window, his back turned, gazing into the street. But he was not truly watching the world outside. His heart beat hard and fast, each pulse echoing with dread and longing. He could not forget the feel of her arms around his neck—how, in the moment, it had seemed impulsive, almost reckless. Now, the memory burned through him: the way she had clung to him, defending him, shielding him, as if her own life depended on it. It melted every resolve, every wall of self-control, like wax before fire.

He feared what might come next. He dreaded stepping forward, arms open, pleading silently for her to come to him—as she had done the day before, unknowingly, unheeded. Yet never again would he let such a moment pass unclaimed.

His breath came quick and shallow. Strong as he was, he trembled. What words would he speak? How would she receive them? Would she turn toward him, her eyes bright with recognition, her body drawn to his like something long lost and found? Or would she recoil, rejecting him with fierce disdain—shattering his future with one word?

A sudden awareness startled him. Someone had entered the room.

He turned.

She had come so quietly, so gently, that he hadn’t heard her footsteps. The noise of the street had been louder in his distracted mind than her soft tread in her muslin dress. She stood beside the table, hands clasped, not offering to sit. Her eyelids were lowered, half-shielding her eyes. Her lips were parted just enough to reveal the white line between them. Her breathing was slow and deep, lifting her chin and flaring her nostrils slightly. That was the only movement on her face.

Her skin, usually warm and glowing, was pale today—so pale that the dark shadow of her hair, drawn down to cover the mark of the blow, seemed to deepen the contrast. Yet even in her stillness, her head was held high, tilted back ever so slightly, in the old proud posture. Her arms hung motionless at her sides.

She looked like a woman falsely accused—a prisoner of honor, too proud to defend herself against a charge she loathed.

Mr. Thornton stepped forward quickly, then stopped himself. He moved instead to the door—left open—and shut it with quiet firmness. Then he returned, standing opposite her, letting the full impression of her presence settle upon him before speaking.

“Miss Hale,” he began, voice low and steady, “I was very ungrateful yesterday—”

“You had nothing to be grateful for,” she replied, lifting her eyes to meet his directly.

“You mean,” he said, “that you believe I ought to thank you for what you did.”

She could not help it. A flush spread across her face, warming her cheeks and burning into her eyes. Yet she did not look away. Her gaze remained calm, resolute.

“It was only instinct,” she said, her voice clear despite the emotion beneath. “Any woman would have done the same. We all feel the sanctity of our sex as a sacred duty when danger threatens. In truth, I should rather apologize—for saying things that sent you into harm’s way.”

“It was not your words,” he insisted. “It was the truth they carried—sharp as they were. But you cannot push me aside with that excuse. You cannot deny me this gratitude. My—” He paused, forcing himself to control the rush of feeling. He would not speak hastily, not now. He would choose each word carefully. And he did. He stopped mid-sentence.

“I do not try to escape anything,” she said. “I simply say—you owe me no thanks. And I may add, any expression of it will pain me, because I do not feel I deserve it. Still—if it eases your conscience, speak on.”

“I do not want relief from obligation,” he said sharply, stung by her composure. “Whether real or imagined—I care not. I believe I owe my very life to you. Yes—smile if you must, call it exaggeration—but I believe it. Because knowing that… knowing that whenever I rejoice in life, I can say to myself—‘All this joy, all this pride in doing my work, all this sense of being alive—I owe it to her!’—it makes the joy deeper, the pride fiercer, the feeling of existence sharper. I hardly know whether it is pleasure or pain to think it. But it is true. And I will not be silenced.”

He stepped forward, determined. “I love you—more than any man has ever loved a woman.”

He took her hand in his. She did not pull away.

He waited, breathless, listening for her response.

Then, in a voice cold and trembling, she spoke: “Your way of speaking shocks me. It is blasphemous. I cannot help it—it is my first reaction. Perhaps it would not be so, if I understood what you mean. I don’t wish to hurt you. And we must be gentle—mamma is asleep. But your manner… it offends me.”

“How?” he demanded.

“Offends me!” she repeated, regaining her dignity. “Yes. I do feel offended—and rightly. You seem to think my actions yesterday”—another deep blush, but now her eyes sparked with anger rather than shame—“were a personal act between us. That you may come here to thank me, as if it were some private favor. But a gentleman—yes, a gentleman—would understand that any woman worthy of the name would step forward to protect a man threatened by violence. It is not personal. It is honor.”

“And the gentleman thus saved is denied even the right to thank her?” he snapped. “I am a man. I claim the right to express my feelings.”

“And I yielded to that right,” she replied, proudly. “I merely said your insistence caused me pain. But you seem to think—no, you *assume*—that I acted not just from instinct, but from… from something else. Something directed at you—*you*.” Her voice broke. Tears, long suppressed, rose to her eyes and choked her. “But I would have done the same for any man—any poor desperate man in that crowd. For none of them would I have shown less sympathy, less courage.”

“You may speak on,” he said, his tone hardening. “I know of your misplaced sympathies. But now I believe it was only your natural sense of justice—that same sense that can see oppression, even in one who holds power. Yes—I, though a master, can be oppressed. And you saw it. You acted nobly. I know you despise me. Let me say it—because you do not understand me.”

“I do not care to understand,” she answered, gripping the edge of the table to steady herself. She thought him cruel—and he was. And she was weak with anger.

“You are unfair,” he said.

She pressed her lips together. She refused to answer.

And yet—he could have thrown himself at her feet, kissed the hem of her dress. He would have done it, if she had allowed it.

She did not speak. She did not move.

Tears of wounded pride fell hot and fast.

He waited. Longed for her to say something—anything—to give him a reason to reply.

But she remained silent.

He picked up his hat.

“One last word,” he said. “You look as if being loved by me taints you. But you cannot avoid it. Nor can I cleanse you of it. I would not, even if I could. I have never loved any woman before. My life has been too full, my mind too absorbed. Now I love. And I will love. But do not fear too much expression from me.”

“I am not afraid,” she said, lifting her head straight. “No one has ever dared to be impertinent to me—and no one ever shall. But Mr. Thornton,” she added, softening her tone, “you’ve been kind to my father. Please—don’t let us make each other angry. Not again.”

He ignored her words. Instead, he smoothed the nap of his hat with his sleeve for a moment. Then, without taking her offered hand, and ignoring her solemn look of regret, he turned abruptly and left.

As the door closed behind him, Margaret caught one glimpse of his face.

And in that glance, she thought she saw tears—washed clean, but present.

That sight changed everything.

Her pride, once so sharp, softened into something tender—and painful. She felt shame—not for herself, but for having caused such humiliation to another.

“But how could I help it?” she whispered to herself. “I never liked him. I was civil. But I never hid my indifference. I didn’t think about him—or myself. So my manners showed the truth. All that happened yesterday—he might misread it. But that is his fault, not mine. I would do it again, if needed. Even if it brings me this shame and sorrow.”
