As the days blended together at Castlewood, Harry found himself increasingly restless beneath its quiet roof. His thoughts lingered on the distant army, where his brother George had left to serve. Though he tried to focus on his duties, Harry's heart remained with the military, and the humdrum routines at home offered little solace. He would often pass by George's empty chamber, avert his gaze, and take his brother's place at the head of the table, the weight of his brother's absence settling heavier with each passing meal. Madam Warrington's daily toast to "The King" rang out with steadfast resolve, and on Sundays, when Harry led the family prayers, her voice carried a note of solemnity as she invoked blessings for travelers by land and sea.

In an effort to fill the void, she spoke frequently of George, her tone cheerful, as if his return were a certainty. She would enter his vacant room with an air of composure, surveying the carefully arranged belongings – books, linens, and papers – with a sense of reverence. Her conversations with the servants turned to matters awaiting George's return, and at meals, she'd gently remind them of tasks to be completed when he came home. The usually stoic Mrs. Mountain, however, would break down in tears at the mere mention of George's name, while Harry's face would pale with concern. Their mother's demeanor remained steadfast, a calm anchor amidst the turbulence.

As the evenings drew in, Harry would often seek refuge in his brother's letters, compiled into a journal that George sent whenever the army marched on. These dispatches were devoured by Harry with rapt attention, and on long summer nights, the family would gather around the tea table as Madam Esmond sat upright, her fingers moving deftly over her needlework. Little Fanny Mountain worked quietly beside her, while Mr. Dempster and Mrs. Mountain engaged in a game of cards, their movements hushed as they listened to Harry's words, bringing George's presence back to life through his writings. As Harry read, the characters on the yellow pages seemed to leap off the page, transporting them to a world where his brother's words held the power to soothe and console. Yet, amidst this fleeting sense of connection, the ache of George's absence persisted, a reminder that the past was indeed lost, leaving behind only faint echoes of a time when brothers could laugh, love, and live together.
