CHAPTER XLII. A Novel Incident (Rewritten for Humor)

Arthur Pendennis, having been soundly thrashed by life at Oxbridge—both academically and emotionally—had returned home to nurse his wounds like a wounded poet with a bad case of existential indigestion. And what better way to heal than with literature? That’s right: Arthur, in the quiet hours between brooding and being mildly depressed, had penned most of a novel. Not just any novel—no, this was *art*. Or at least it was supposed to be.

The manuscript, titled *Leaves from the Life-book of Walter Lorraine*, was written during Arthur’s emotional adolescence, when love felt like a death sentence, money was a myth, and every sigh echoed like a dirge. It was Byron meets Werther meets Mephistopheles on a particularly bad day—full of melodrama, despair, and enough tears to flood the Thames. The hero, Walter Lorraine, was basically Arthur’s soul with better hair and worse taste in women. He’d read German poetry so intensely that he began to believe he was a tragic figure in a Goethean tragedy, which, frankly, made him rather hard to live with.

And yes, the manuscript bore the scars of its creation: coffee stains, ink blots, and one particularly dramatic tear-stain that looked suspiciously like someone had wept over a lost love while also trying to write a sonnet about said love. Arthur once claimed that reading it again was like revisiting a ghost town where all the inhabitants were dead, but still occasionally screamed.

One morning, while Warrington lounged in an armchair like a cat who’d just eaten a canary, Arthur burst into the room with the manuscript clutched like a sacred relic. “By Jove!” he declared, slamming it down so hard the teapot did a little jig and the milk in the jug performed a brief ballet. “This is what I wrote when I thought I was in love with Miss Amory! Can you believe it? I used to leave poems in hollow trees like some kind of Victorian Romeo.”

Warrington, puffing on his pipe with the calm of a man who’d seen too much, leaned forward with fire tongs in hand. “Ah, the classic ‘Amori’ play on words. Very clever. Let’s hear a sample.”

And so, with the manuscript now in the grasp of a man who clearly enjoyed wielding fire tongs like a weapon of literary justice, Warrington began to read aloud:

“‘False as thou art beautiful! Heartless as thou art fair! Mockery of Passion!’ Walter cried, addressing Leonora; ‘what evil spirit hath sent thee to torture me so? O Leonora——’”

“Stop!” Arthur shrieked, lunging for the pages. “That part’s about Lady Mirabel! My other flame! The one who invited me to her house last night after seeing me talking to Wenham and Monsieur Dubois, who probably thinks he’s Napoleon and has orders on everything.”

“Two flames?” Warrington chuckled. “More like two extinguished candles. Both beauties in your book?”

“Both, or near enough,” Arthur admitted. “Leonora is Fotheringay. The Duke is Magnus Charters—only less charming and more likely to charge you for a handshake. And Miss Amory is Neaera. By gad, I walked home from Lady Whiston’s under the moonlight, and suddenly I was back in my youth. I pulled out the manuscript, and—well, it’s not great, but it’s got *feeling*.”

“And if Bungay won’t publish it, Bacon will,” Warrington added, already knowing the answer.

“That’s how poets work,” Warrington continued, “they get jilted, they cry, they write a book, then sell it. All poets are humbugs. Once a man starts selling his emotions, he’s a fraud. If a poet gets heartburn from a good dinner, he screams louder than Prometheus.”

Arthur bristled. “But isnity a poet supposed to feel more deeply? You see things, you speak up. Paley talks for hours about legal technicalities. Why shouldn’t I write about my feelings? Isn’t that just as valid?”

“Not bad speech,” Warrington conceded. “But still doesn’t make you immune to being a humbug.”

“What about Homer? Shakespeare?”

“Names like those aren’t in the same league as you, sir,” Warrington replied. “There are men and men.”

“Shakespeare wrote for money,” Arthur shot back. “Just like us.”

Warrington threw down his pipe and muttered something about impudence before returning to the manuscript.

It was clear: *Walter Lorraine* was Arthur’s diary of youthful folly, rewritten as high drama. But Arthur hadn’t kept it hidden because he believed it would improve with age—he’d just forgotten it existed, buried under old surplices, battered caps, and memories of failed flirtations.

Now, after nearly five years of neglect, he dug it out and found it… well, not brilliant, but not terrible either. Some passages had real spark. Others were pure nonsense. But Warrington, after reading half of it and smoking two pipes, declared, “I can’t read anymore of this balderdash. But there’s something here. It’s got freshness. Like springtime before you’ve learned to shave.”

Arthur blinked. “What do my whiskers have to do with anything?”

“They’re curling irons, Pen. You’ve got to fake it now. You can’t recapture that natural blush. The bloom’s gone.”

“Then let’s burn it!” Arthur declared, flinging the manuscript into the fireplace.

But alas, the fire had long since given up the ghost. It had boiled the kettle and retired. Warrington, grinning, retrieved the manuscript from the cold ashes with the tongs.

“Oh, Pen,” he sighed, “you’re a clumsy humbug. I saw you check the fire first. No burning. We’ll sell it.”

“To Bacon or Bungay?” Arthur asked, eyes wide.

“To both. We’ll play them off against each other. We’ll fatten the lad, give him a bath, curl his hair, and sell him for a hundred piasters. The rubbish is saleable. Take it home, edit it, add comedy, satire, maybe a bit of slapstick. Then we’ll take it to market.”

Arthur beamed. “Do you really think so?”

“You silly young fool,” Warrington said kindly, “I think it’s uncommonly clever. So do you.” And with that, he gently tapped Arthur on the cheek with the manuscript.

Arthur turned crimson—like a tomato after a very awkward moment—and grasped Warrington’s hand. “Thank you,” he said, voice trembling. Then he retreated to his room, re-read the whole thing, rewrote large chunks, added jokes, cut the melodrama, and finally shaped *Walter Lorraine* into something resembling a book people might actually want to read.

Meanwhile, Warrington, ever the master manipulator, subtly planted rumors among the publishing world. He told Percy Popjoy—gentleman of modest means and generous heart—that Arthur was writing “the most exciting novel of the season,” full of wit, pathos, and “every conceivable good quality.” Popjoy relayed this to Mrs. Bungay, who, though she didn’t understand novels any more than she understood algebra, trusted her husband’s instincts.

Bungay, bless him, knew nothing about books. He didn’t read them. He paid for them. He based his decisions on Mrs. Bungay and his readers. When he heard Arthur was working on a novel, he invited him to dinner—likely hoping to close a deal without even opening the manuscript.

Bacon, hearing of this, panicked. “We must beat them!” he declared. His reader, Mr. Hack, went undercover and discovered the truth: Arthur was negotiating with Bungay. So Bacon marched up the stairs of Lamb Court—where Arthur and Warrington lived in rooms so shabby they made a pawnshop look luxurious.

The carpet was ragged, the chairs broke under weight, and the smell of tobacco clung to the walls like a persistent ex-lover. Bacon tripped over a bucket, sat down on a chair that collapsed beneath him, and stared around in horror.

“Is this elegant?” Warrington asked, laughing. “Would you like any of these furnishings for Mrs. Bacon’s drawing-room?”

Bacon, confused, couldn’t tell if Warrington was joking. “I never can make that chap out,” he muttered.

But then he saw the invitations on the breakfast table: the Marchioness of Steyne would receive Arthur Pendennis. Another noblewoman was hosting a ball. Bacon blinked. This wasn’t just some struggling writer—it was a man of fashion, living in squalor.

“Ah,” Warrington said, “Pendennis is one of the most affable young men I know. Dines with everyone, yet eats mutton chops with us. Nothing like the old English gentleman.”

“Oh no,” Bacon agreed, slightly impressed.

“And you wonder why he lives here?” Warrington added. “Well, it’s a queer taste. But we’re fond of each other. I can’t afford a big house, so he stays with me. He’s a man who can afford to live anywhere.”

“I fancy it don’t cost him much here,” Bacon thought.

And then Arthur entered, looking like a man who’d just survived a war and was now ready to sell the spoils.

Bacon got straight to business: “I hear you’ve a novel. I’d like to see it. What’s your price?”

Arthur, playing coy, said he was already in talks with Bungay. This piqued Bacon’s interest. Offers flew. Vague promises of “liberal terms” and “great advances” were made. Arthur, feeling like a king, imagined himself buying a horse, a cab, and possibly a new set of clothes.

But let’s not get ahead of ourselves. I won’t say how much Arthur actually got. Because if I did, every aspiring novelist would quit their jobs and start writing tragedies about unrequited love. Let me just say: it was enough to make him consider moving to the first floor of Lamb Court, getting a brougham, or opening a bank account.

Major Pendennis, upon hearing the news, nearly exploded. “Who the deuce reads this kind of thing?” he muttered. “I never read novels. Except Paul de Kock—he makes me laugh. But Pen’s lucky. He could write one a month. Twelve a year. In four years, he’ll be rich. Meanwhile, I suggest he live properly. Get respectable apartments. Keep a brougham.”

Arthur laughed and told Warrington. But Warrington, ever wise, advised caution. “Be grateful. Don’t go wild. Pay back Laura.”

So Arthur did. He wrote a letter to the widow Portman and Laura, expressing heartfelt thanks and enclosing a payment that nearly cleared his debt. The letter was simple, sincere, and full of gratitude.

When Dr. Portman read it—especially the part where Arthur thanked Heaven for his fortune and kind friends—his voice cracked. His eyes watered behind his spectacles. He finished reading, folded the letter, handed it back, and then—after a moment of silence—drew Mrs. Pendennis toward him and kissed her.

Helen, the daughter, burst into tears on his shoulder. The Doctor, blushing furiously, led her to the sofa and murmured, in a voice barely above a whisper, lines from a great poet: “And the widow’s heart sang for joy.”

And thus, Arthur Pendennis, through a mix of talent, luck, manipulation, and a little help from his friends, became a published author—not because he was perfect, but because sometimes, even the most ridiculous dreams can be sold for cash.
