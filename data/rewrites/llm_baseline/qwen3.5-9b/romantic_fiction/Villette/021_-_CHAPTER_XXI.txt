Three days remained, and then I must return to the *pensionnat*. I counted the moments upon the clock, wishing desperately to retard their flight; yet they glided by while I watched them, already gone before I could fully grasp their departure.

"Lucy will not leave us today," said Mrs. Bretton, coaxingly at breakfast. "She knows we can procure a second respite."

"I would not ask for one if I might have it for a word," I replied. "I long to get the good-bye over and be settled in the Rue Fossette again. I must go this morning; I must go directly. My trunk is packed and corded."

However, my departure seemed to depend entirely on Graham. He had promised to accompany me, but fate conspired to keep him occupied all day; he did not return until dusk. Then followed a skirmish of words. Mrs. Bretton and her son pressed me to remain one night longer. I nearly wept from irritation and eagerness to be gone. I longed to leave them as a criminal longs for the axe to fall—not because I desired the blow, but because I wished the agony of waiting to be over. How intensely I felt this, they could not comprehend. My state of mind was foreign to their experience.

It was dark when Dr. John handed me from the carriage at Madame Beck's door. The lamp above burned brightly; a November drizzle fell, as it had all day, gleaming on the wet pavement. It was just such a night as that on which, not a year prior, I had first paused at this very threshold. The scene was identical. I recalled the precise shapes of the paving stones I had once noted with an idle eye, while, with a heart beating thickly, I had waited for the door to open—a solitary figure, a suppliant. On that night, too, I had briefly encountered the man who now stood beside me. Had I ever mentioned that encounter to him, or tried to explain it? No. I had not, nor did I feel inclined to. It was a pleasant thought, stored safely in my own mind, best left undisturbed.

Graham rang the bell. The door flew open immediately; it was the hour when the half-boarders departed, and Rosine was on the alert.

"Don't come in," I told him. Yet he stepped a moment into the well-lit vestibule. I had not wished him to see that tears filled my eyes, for his nature was too kind to require such displays of sorrow. He always sought to heal, to relieve—even when, as a physician, neither cure nor alleviation lay within his power.

"Keep up your courage, Lucy. Think of my mother and myself as true friends. We will not forget you."

"Nor will I forget you, Dr. John."

My trunk was brought inside. We shook hands. He turned to go, but he was unsatisfied; his generous impulses demanded more.

"Lucy"—he stepped after me—"will you feel very solitary here?"

"At first, I shall."

"Well, my mother will soon call to see you. Meanwhile, I'll tell you what I'll do. I'll write—just any cheerful nonsense that comes to mind. Shall I?"

"Good, gallant heart!" I thought to myself. But I shook my head, smiling. "Never think of it. Impose no such task on yourself. *You* write to *me!* You won't have time."

"Oh! I will find or make time. Good-bye!"

He was gone. The heavy door crashed shut. The axe had fallen; the pang was experienced.

Denying myself time to think or feel, I swallowed my tears as if they were wine and proceeded to Madame's sitting-room to pay the customary visit of ceremony. She received me with perfectly acted cordiality, even demonstrative in her brief welcome. Within ten minutes, I was dismissed. From the dining hall, I moved to the refectory, where pupils and teachers were assembling for evening study. Again, I found a welcome, one I believed was not quite hollow. Once that was concluded, I was free to retreat to the dormitory.

"And will Graham really write?" I asked myself as I sank, exhausted, onto the edge of my bed.

Reason crept up to me through the twilight of the long, dim chamber, whispering sedately: *"He may write once. So kind is his nature, it may stimulate him for one fleeting effort. But it cannot be continued; it may not be repeated. Folly would be to build a castle on such a promise; insanity to mistake a transient rain-pool, holding a single drop, for a perennial spring supplying the seasons."*

I bowed my head, sitting in thought for an hour longer. Reason still whispered, laying a withered hand upon my shoulder and chilling my ear with the blue lips of age.

"If," she muttered, "if he *should* write, what then? Do you meditate pleasure in replying? Ah, fool! I warn you! Keep your answers brief. Expect no joy of heart, no indulgence of intellect. Grant no expansion to feeling; give no holiday to any faculty. Dally with no friendly exchange; foster no genial communion..."

"But I have spoken to Graham, and you did not chide," I pleaded.

"No," she said. "I needed not. Conversation is good discipline for you. You speak imperfectly. While you talk, there can be no oblivion of inferiority—no encouragement to delusion. Pain, privation, penury stamp your language..."

"But," I interrupted again, "where bodily presence is weak and speech contemptible, surely written language offers a medium for better utterance than faltering lips can achieve?"

Reason answered only, "At your peril, you cherish that idea, or allow its influence to animate any writing of yours!"

"But if I feel, may I *never* express?"

"*Never!*" declared Reason.

I groaned under her bitter sternness. *Never—never!* Oh, harsh word! This hag, this Reason, refused to let me look up, smile, or hope. She could not rest unless I were crushed, cowed, broken-in, and broken-down. According to her, I was born only to toil for a crust of bread, to await the pangs of death, and to despond steadily through every stage of life. Reason might be right; yet no wonder we are occasionally glad to defy her, to rush from under her rod and grant a truant hour to Imagination—*her* soft, bright foe, *our* sweet help, *our* divine hope. We shall and must break bounds at intervals, despite the terrible revenge awaiting our return. Reason is vindictive as a devil; for me, she was always envenomed as a stepmother. If I had obeyed her solely out of love, I should have died long ago from her ill-usage, her stinginess, her chill, her barren board, her icy bed, her savage, ceaseless blows. But for that kinder Power who holds my secret and sworn allegiance. Often has Reason turned me out by night, in mid-winter, onto cold snow, throwing for sustenance the gnawed bones dogs had forsaken. Sternly has she vowed her stores held nothing more for me; harshly denied my right to ask for better things... Then, looking up, I have seen in the sky a head amidst circling stars, of which the central and brightest lent a ray sympathetic and attentive. A spirit, softer and better than human reason, has descended with quiet flight to the waste—bringing all around her a sphere of air borrowed from eternal summer; bringing the perfume of flowers that cannot fade; the fragrance of trees whose fruit is life; bringing breezes pure from a world whose day needs no sun to lighten it. My hunger has this good angel appeased with food, sweet and strange, gathered among gleaning angels, harvesting their dew-white yield in the first fresh hour of a heavenly day. Tenderly has she assuaged the insufferable fears that weep away life itself; kindly given rest to deadly weariness; generously lent hope and impulse to paralyzed despair. Divine, compassionate, succourable influence! When I bend the knee to anything other than God, it shall be at thy white and winged feet, beautiful on mountain or on plain. Temples have been reared to the Sun; altars dedicated to the Moon. Oh, greater glory! To thee neither hands build, nor lips consecrate: but hearts, through ages, remain faithful to thy worship. A dwelling thou hast, too wide for walls, too high for domes—a temple whose floors are space, rites whose mysteries transpire in presence, to the kindling, the harmony of worlds!

Sovereign and complete! Thou hadst, for endurance, thy great army of martyrs; for achievement, thy chosen band of worthies. Deity unquestioned, thy essence defies decay!

This daughter of Heaven remembered me tonight; she saw me weeping, and she came with comfort. "Sleep," she said. "Sleep, sweetly—I gild thy dreams!"

She kept her word, watching me through a night's rest. But at dawn, Reason relieved the guard. I awoke with a start; the rain lashed against the panes, and the wind uttered a peevish cry at intervals. The night-lamp was dying on the black circular stand in the center of the dormitory; day had already broken. How I pity those whom mental pain stuns rather than rouses!
