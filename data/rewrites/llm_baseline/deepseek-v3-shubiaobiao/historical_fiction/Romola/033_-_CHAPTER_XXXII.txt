The morning after the French departed Florence, Romola stood beneath the loggia of her home, the crisp air stirring her mantle as she watched the last of the soldiers cross the bridges toward the Porta San Piero. The city’s bells clamored in triumph, their vibrations humming through her bones, but her relief was shadowed. Though the foreign presence had brought only strife, its departure did not lift the weight from her heart.

Tito had been distant since the French arrived—guarded, armored in more than just steel. She had seen the fear in his eyes, the tension in his bearing, though he would not speak of it. Now, as the gates closed and the clamor faded, she descended to the library, determined to lose herself in work. Perhaps routine could steady her nerves.

She lit the lamp, its small glow barely pushing back the room’s shadows, and began copying the catalog. The fire had dwindled to embers, but she hardly noticed the chill until the door opened.

Tito stepped inside.

She rose at once, her pulse quickening. "You’re home early," she said, moving to unwind his *becchetto*.

His smile was bright, effortless. "Unwelcome?"

"Tito." Her voice was soft, chiding. He caught her wrists, playful, then kissed her, his fingers threading through her hair. The familiarity of it—his touch, his warmth—made her throat tighten. For a moment, she let herself believe the strain between them had been imagined.

But when she drew back, his gaze shifted, scanning the room as if avoiding hers. He shrugged off his cloak, though his movements were too deliberate.

"The bells sound like victory," she ventured.

"Of a sour sort," he said, settling into his chair. "Florence is a tinderbox. The Frate’s faction tightens its grip, the treasury bleeds gold for France and Pisa, and the city chokes on piety. I’ve been thinking—we should leave."

The words struck like cold water. "Leave Florence?" Her fingers tightened on the stool beneath her. "I can’t. Not yet. My father’s work—"

"Your father’s work is a dream, *carissima*." His voice was gentle, as if explaining to a child. "A library bound to his name changes nothing. Books belong to the world, not one man’s vanity."

She flinched. "It was his *wish*."

"And wishes are air. Should we chain ourselves to the dead?" He leaned forward, his tone soothing. "You’ve given enough. Let go."

She stood abruptly, her voice low. "You don’t understand loyalty."

His smile dimmed. "I understand practicality. And I’ve acted on it."

A silence, heavy as stone.

"The library is sold."

Her breath left her. "Sold?"

"The Duke of Milan takes the books. The antiquities go to France." His voice remained calm, as if discussing the weather.

For a heartbeat, she could not move. Then fury ignited. "You had no right!"

"I had every right. The decision was made."

She turned toward the door, but his hand closed around her wrist.

"Running to Bernardo won’t undo it." His grip was firm, his voice colder now. "The agreements are signed. The money is mine."

She wrenched free. "Then give me my godfather’s share. Let me repay him."

His eyes narrowed, weighing her. "And if I do? What then?"

"I’ll say nothing. Do nothing." The words were ash in her mouth.

A pause. Then he nodded, moving to the desk. When he returned, he pressed a folded paper into her hand.

"You’ll forgive me in time," he murmured, brushing his lips to her brow.

She did not react.

The door closed behind him. Only then did she crumble, falling to her knees before her father’s portrait, her sobs the only sound in the hollow dark.
