Felix possessed none of his father's inability to accumulate wealth. During the twenty years since Thomas Cadwalader had vanished abroad, Felix had constructed a fortune vast enough to buy a kingdom, yet he refused to share a penny of it with his aged father. Instead, he offered his hoard solely for the purpose of their shared vengeance. This financial disparity explained the stern injunction Felix delivered to his brother before departing for Montgomery: "Spend this money freely, Thomas. Spend whatever you require. When your mission concludes, a surplus will remain for your leisure."

Thomas bowed, accepting the terms with a grim internal nod. *The laborer is worthy of his hire,* he thought. Turning his gaze around the sparse, echoing walls of their father's study—which now seemed to lack any justification for existing since Amos Cadwalader's passing—he asked, "And you? Will you stay here?"

Felix's reply was sudden and absolute. "No. I depart for New York tomorrow. I have secured a residence there, one you may eventually wish to inhabit. However, under the guise of 'Adams, Felix Adams,' I lease it. Address me thus in Montgomery. Let the name of Cadwalader never slip from your tongue in this town, and ensure you never reveal my identity, lest we lose the leverage necessary to execute our revenge." A smirk played on his lips, revealing his awareness that he was undeniably the handsomer of the pair. "Now, regarding those Parisian introductions we tasked you to arrange..."

***

The narrative of the ensuing weeks finds its clearest expression not in prose, but in correspondence. Two parallel columns record the truth: the left holds Thomas's letters to Felix, a dry chronicle of facts; the right contains Thomas's personal diary, a testament to his shifting inner turmoil. Together, they provide the essential key to understanding the situation.

*I am settled. I have seen her.* As you predicted, she is a pale blonde. Tomorrow I shall present my credentials to John Poindexter. Based on initial impressions, I anticipate a warm reception.

*I could not confess the truth of today to Felix. Why? And why do I write it here?* Perhaps to distract my mind from dwelling on it? Maybe. I find myself bewildered by my own emotions; I dread the task ahead while simultaneously pressing forward with fervor.

*I have seen her.* I told Felix as much, omitting the location and the circumstances. How could I explain that a son of the Poindexters was used to perform a gracious act? Or how to describe that, though my heart had grown dark as hell upon embracing this purpose, it swelled anew at the first glimpse of a face I had sworn to dedicate to agony? Surely, Felix would have remained stronger, and yet...

*After leaving the train station, I bypassed all thoughts of comfort and headed straight for the cemetery.* Before settling into town, I sought Evelyn's grave to renew the oath my father had made eighteen years prior when he held a four-year-old me aloft, pointing toward John Poindexter's estate. I located the ancient, weathered stone marking her resting place. Just as I bent in the setting sun to inspect it, the rustle of a woman's skirt drew my attention. Emerging from the shadows advanced a young girl, bathed in a nimbus of rosy light that seemed to elevate her above the earth, lending an ethereal quality to her delicate frame and strangely illuminated countenance. Clutched in her arms was a massive bouquet of snow-white lilies. Observing that her gaze was fixed not on me, but on the grave beside which I stood, I slipped into the shadow of nearby bushes and watched. She began to scatter these flowers—symbols of innocence—over the mound I had just visited.

*What did this signify? Who was this young woman honoring the grave of my long-lost sister with such tender reverence?* As she rose from her task, I could no longer suppress the emotion and curiosity swirling within me. Stepping forward, I approached with the utmost respect, noting that her face was even more radiant when engaged in speech than when bowed in solemnity. Adopting the indifferent tone of a stranger, I asked, "Who rests beneath this stone, and why do you, a mere girl, lay flowers upon a grave whose moss suggests it was dug decades before your birth?"

*Her response sent a shock through me, equal to the startling experiences of recent days.* "I strew flowers here," she replied softly, "because the girl buried under this stone shares my birthday. I never saw her, it is true, but she perished in my father's home when she was no older than I am today. Having realized what it means to die young, I have made it a custom to share my birthday blooms with her. She was a lily, they say, in both appearance and character, hence I bring her lilies."

***

*I have shaken hands with John Poindexter.* To win the favor of the daughter, I must please the father, or at least attract his notice. I believe I have succeeded in this regard.

*I no longer feel like a man in the traditional sense.* John Poindexter appears cold, his manner rigid, his opinions unyielding. Yet, he fails to inspire the abhorrence I anticipated, nor does he awaken the singular thought due to the memory of my sister. Is it because he is Eva's father? Has the daughter's loveliness cast a halo over the parent? If so, Felix has every right to execrate me and curse my father for...

***

*The introductions you procured have opened doors everywhere. There is considerable wealth here, many magnificent estates. Consequently, I find myself immersed in a congenial society, of which she is the undisputed star.* Did I not say he was, as of old, the chief man of the town?

*She is beautiful.* She possesses the daintiness of a lily and the vibrant flush of a rose. Yet, it is not her beauty that moves me; it is the strange sweetness of her nature. This sweetness, however, knows no weakness; on the contrary, it possesses a peculiar strength, becoming instantly apparent when duty calls. Could Felix have imagined a Poindexter like this? I cannot reconcile such loveliness with the execrable sin that demands vengeance upon this household. I cannot even dwell upon my past life. Under her gaze, all that is dark, threatening, secret, and revengeful slips away. I dream only of what is pure, true, satisfying, and ennobling. And this transformation is wrought by the influence of her smile, rather than her words. Have I been given an angel to degrade? Or am I so blinded as to see a saint where others—let us say, Felix—would perceive merely a pretty woman with unexpected attractions?

***

*Rides, dances, trivialities. My interest in this young lady is beginning to be publicly recognized. She alone seems unaware of it.* Sometimes I wonder if our scheme will fail due to her impassivity and her more-than-conventional innocence. I fear she may never learn to love me. Nevertheless, I have exerted every effort to please her. Indeed, I could not have exerted myself more. Today, I rode twenty-five miles on horseback to procure a trifling item she fancied.

*Not all will unfold as easily as Felix imagines.* Eva Poindexter may be a country girl, but she has standards, and mere grace and attainment are insufficient to win her. Do I possess the other qualities she demands? That remains to be seen. I possess one quality she never dreams of. Will its shadow overwhelm the rest, causing her naturally pure spirit to recoil from me just as I think her mine? I cannot tell, and the doubt creates a hell within me. Something deeper, stronger, more imperious than my revenge compels me to view winning her heart as a necessity. I have forgotten my purpose in this desire. I have forgotten everything save that she is the one woman of my life, and that I cannot rest until her heart is wholly mine. Good God! Have I become a slave where I hoped to be a master? Have I, Thomas Cadwalader, surrendered my soul to the keeping of this innocent girl? I do not even pause to inquire. To win her—that is all for which I now live.

***

*She may not care for me, yet she is interested in no one else. Of this, I am assured by John Poindexter, who seems eager to aid me in winning his daughter's heart. Hard-won, closely bound. If she ever comes to love me, it will be with the force of a very strong nature. The pale blonde has a heart.*
