Margaret tore a great piece of venison from the bone and flung it out the window toward the gleaming green eyes in the dark. A savage snarl answered her, followed by the crunch of teeth rending flesh. Then, sudden as thunder, a hound’s bay split the night, so loud it shook the walls. The three at the window shrank back, hearts pounding. The leopard, fearing for its stolen meal, slipped like a shadow toward the woods—only for the clamor of horses, men, and dogs to burst past the house in furious chase.

Martin and his companions exhaled as one. The beast was swift; it would not be caught for miles yet. Hands clasped in relief, and Margaret, trembling, let her tears fall. Gerard kissed them away.

Back at the table, Gerard raised his cup. "To a woman’s wit—stronger than a man’s strength!"

"Ay," said Margaret softly, "when those she loves are in danger. Not otherwise."

That night, he lingered with her longer than ever, walking home proud as a king and light with joy. But under the shadow of the trees, two figures barred his path. His father and mother.

Out so late? His heart chilled.

They stood grim, silent.

He stammered a greeting.

"Why ask?" His father’s voice was iron. "You know why we are here."

"Oh, Gerard!" His mother’s voice trembled, torn between reproach and love.

His tongue turned to lead.

His father relented slightly. "No need to hang your head. You’re not the first fool caught by a pretty face."

"Nay, ’tis witchcraft!" Catherine cut in. "Peter the Magician is known for it."

"Come, boy," his father said, "you know a priest has no business meddling with women. Swear to stay away from Sevenbergen, and this ends here. We’ll not punish a first fault."

"I cannot promise that."

"Cannot?" His father’s voice cracked like a whip. "You defy me?"

"I kept silent to spare you pain. But now the truth is out—I love Margaret. And I will *never* be a priest. I’d sooner die."

"Then we shall see," his father growled, "what it costs to defy a father."

Silence swallowed them whole. The walk home was a funeral march, broken only by Catherine’s stifled sobs.

From that hour, peace fled the little house at Tergou.

Gerard was hauled before the family like a criminal. All turned against him—save little Kate and the dwarf, who took their cues from her wide, tearful eyes. Cornelis and Sybrandt were worst of all, their malice sharp as knives.

Gerard, stunned by so many enemies, looked helplessly to his sister. But even she turned away.

"Pray, Gerard," she whispered, "pray Heaven cures you of this folly."

His heart broke. "You too?"

He left without another word, straight for Sevenbergen.

Quarrels between those who love each other are the cruelest of all. At first, both sides may have justice. But time twists right into wrong, pride hardens hearts, and anger blots out reason. In the end, no one wins.

Elias was stern, his elder sons spiteful, but Gerard’s greater mind saw both sides—and so he wavered, torn, never angry, only heartsick. He had no ally in this fight. He dared not tell Margaret the depths of his family’s wrath; her pride might make her cast him off to spare him. Margaret Van Eyck, once a comfort, could not help now—not when she had chosen art over love in her youth. How could he confess he threw away a benefice for what she had once scorned?

He was near surrender.

Then Catherine, ever unpredictable, turned the tide. One day, when Cornelis sneered, "Gerard wed Margaret? Hunger wedding thirst!"—she rounded on him.

"And what will *you* bring a wife, slug? Gerard can paint, can write. You? Naught but waiting for your father’s shoes!"

Gerard, despite himself, smiled.

But the true turning point came when his father threatened force. "The burgomaster will lock you in the Stadthouse before I let you wed her. And a priest you’ll be, willing or not, before the year is out."

Gerard went pale. "Then hear me now—by God and St. Bavon, I swear I’ll *never* take the tonsure while Margaret lives. If force rules, then force will answer you: the day the burgomaster comes for me, I leave Tergou—leave Holland—leave my father’s house, where I was loved not for myself, but for what I could be made!"

He stormed out, trembling with fury.

Catherine wailed after him, "Men are crueler than tigers to their own flesh!"

That same hour, Reicht Heynes found him. "Mistress Van Eyck asks for you."

The old artist sat like a judge. "Why have you stayed away?"

Gerard faltered.

Reicht pitied him. "He feared to tell you of his love."

"Ah! So we are not friends enough for confidences?"

"Forgive me," Gerard begged. "I dared not speak my folly."

"Folly? Is love folly?"

"I hear it called so daily."

Margaret Van Eyck’s stern face softened. "You are my son in art. You shall not waste your youth as I did. Marry your Margaret. I’ve made inquiries—she’s a good girl."

Gerard, starved for sympathy, poured out his heart. Soon, three pairs of eyes glistened.

Women’s courage is not men’s. It is fiercer in its way—the courage to send others into battle while standing firm. Now, they urged rebellion.

"Marry her at once," said Reicht. "The old will come around."

"Look at you," Margaret Van Eyck added. "Pale, worn to a shadow. While those clods thrive on spite."

Gerard wavered. "I don’t fear prison. I fear my father’s curse. I’d wed her tomorrow—but to make her a beggar’s wife?"

"Then take her away," the old woman said. "To Italy."

"*Italy?*" His eyes kindled.

"Where painters are princes, where scribes earn three hundred crowns a manuscript. The Pope himself begs for skilled hands to copy treasures fleeing Constantinople. Go! I’ll give you gold for the journey."

Midnight found them still scheming. From that hour, Gerard walked lighter, armored against gibes.

Margaret Van Eyck gave him more than coin—she gave him secrets.

"Jan’s colors never fade. Know why? He ground them himself. His panels were prepared a year in advance. Most painters are in too much hurry to last." She taught him to purify oil, to layer colors so they’d outlive time itself.

Armed with knowledge and means, Gerard resolved: marry, then fly to Italy.

Only Margaret Brandt’s consent remained.

He found her, poured out his plans.

She refused.

"Not while your father opposes us."

He pleaded, raged. "Then you side with them! You’ll make me a priest after all!"

He stormed off, leaving her in tears.

Yet love bends to pity, not wrath. *How unhappy he must be,* she thought, *to speak so.*

Then, barely an hour later, he returned, clutching tattered canvas—her portrait, slashed to ribbons.

Her cheeks burned. "Who did this?"

"I don’t know. And I dare not ask—for I’d hate them till death." He trembled. "Six months’ work—gone. Your face—*your face*—hacked through."

Margaret’s breath came fast. "Then take the face itself."

He froze.

"If they rob you of my likeness," she said, "you shall have *me.* Marry me, Gerard. Tomorrow."

Joy swept him like a tide. He kissed her hands, her lips, then ran for Peter and Martin to witness their betrothal—a bond solemn as vows.

The deed was done.
