The departure of the Baroness was a mechanical event, a shifting of weights in the household balance. While the Bishop of Cambray, in his romances, lamented the sorrow of Calypso and her maid walking the same plank of grief, the reality at Castlewood was simpler: a distribution of gold and a redistribution of luggage. The menials moved through the corridors with the silent efficiency of gears turning. Some sought a final glance at the departing figures; others waited by the doors for gratuities, the customary grease for the wheels of social exit.

Harry Warrington stood amidst this flow. He was a lover, and therefore, by the laws of that age, a distributor of largesse. He scattered his coins to the right and left, from the major-domo to the shoeblack, mimicking the wealth of the man he claimed to be—a descendant of Ashantee kings. It was a performance of generosity, a necessary act to ingratiate oneself with the entire ecosystem of a family where one holds affection for a single member. He paid the grim housekeeper, the feeble porter, smoothing the transactional friction of their separation. When a man loves one woman in a house, he inevitably becomes fond of every shadow cast by her presence. He smiled at old stories, drank port he would otherwise despise, and beat time to imaginary music, securing the loyalty of the household through the currency of attention.

He had won the brown horse, Prince William, a sound and swift animal, from his cousin Will. This victory completed his inventory of assets: money, autographs, and now, a steed. He retired to his dormitory, the stars shining over his mistress's window, calculating the pleasure of a future tour across the island with Maria on his pillion. His mind was a closed loop of romantic satisfaction, oblivious to the chaotic variables awaiting him in the morning.

Chaplain Sampson, however, introduced a variable of genuine emotion. He sent his black mare via the groom, footman, and gardener, who wept openly as they kissed the beast's white nose. Gumbo, the Virginian lad, absorbed this display of sentimentality. He produced two guineas, a small sum that instantly purchased the silence of the grieving boy and secured the transfer of the mare. Courtesy, in this instance, was a transaction settled in cash.

The morning meal became a formal theater of farewells. My Lord Castlewood descended, a rare occurrence, to bid his guests adieu. Will Esmond was absent, having left a note in handwriting of questionable orthography announcing his flight to Salisbury Races. He had already transferred ownership of the brown horse to Harry's servant, Tom. The remaining party drank tea amicably, the tension of departure dissolving into the routine of travel preparations.

The carriages rolled into the courtyard, laden with the vast baggage train of the present age. The Baroness and her niece occupied a landau guarded by men with blunderbusses; the maids and trunks filled a second vehicle. Harry's modest valises were tucked beneath the maid's supervision, while Gumbo proposed to ride by the window, claiming the role of the chief companion.

My Lord, with his stepmother and Lady Fanny, escorted the Baroness to the carriage steps. A crowd of villagers gathered under the trees, shouting huzzays as the ponderous vehicles departed. My Lord lingered in the courtyard with Harry, discussing the horses.

"I hear you carry away some of our horses," My Lord said.

"A gentleman cannot refuse a fair game at the cards," Harry replied, blushing slightly. "I never wanted to play, nor would have played for money had not my cousin William forced me. As for the chaplain, it went to my heart to win from him, but he was as eager as my cousin."

"There is no blame to you, my boy," My Lord conceded. "At Rome you can't help doing as Rome does. I am glad you gave Will a lesson. He is mad about play—he would gamble his coat off his back—and I and the family have had to pay his debts ever so many times. How much did you win?"

"Eighteen pieces the first day or two, a note for a hundred and twenty more, and the brown horse, sixty—that makes nigh upon two hundred. All was fair, and it was even against my will that we played at all. Will ain't a match for me, my lord—that is the fact."

"He is a match for most people, though," My Lord noted. "You won the brown horse, Prince William?"

"Yes. Out of Constitution. You don't suppose I would set him sixty against his bay?"

"Oh, I didn't know. I saw Will riding out this morning; most likely I did not remark what horse he was on. And you won the black mare from the parson?"

"For fourteen. He will mount Gumbo very well. Why does not the rascal come round with the horses?" Harry's mind drifted to Maria, longing to be trotting by her side.

"When you get to Tunbridge, cousin Harry, you must be on the look-out against sharper players than the chaplain and Will. There is all sorts of queer company at the Wells."

"A Virginian learns pretty well to take care of himself," Harry nodded knowingly.

"My sister is as innocent as one," My Lord added, recommending her protection. "Thou wilt see that she comes to no mischief?"

"I will guard her with my life!" Harry cried.

"And I will write thee word when you shall bring my sister back to me. Here come the horses. Have you bid adieu to the Countess and Lady Fanny? They are kissing their hands to you from the music-room balcony."

Harry ran upstairs for a brief ceremony, anxious to reach the charmer of his heart, and descended to mount his newly acquired steed. Gumbo held the reins, astride the parson's black mare, while another horse stood nearby.

"What is this?" Harry cried.

"Your honour's new horse," the groom said, touching his cap.

"This brute?" Harry exclaimed, using the expressive vocabulary of the era. "Go and bring me round Prince William, Mr. William's horse, the brown horse."

"Mr. William have rode Prince William this morning away to Salisbury Races," the groom explained calmly. "His last words was, 'Sam, saddle my bay horse, Cato, for Mr. Warrington this morning. He is Mr. Warrington's horse now. I sold him to him last night.' And I know your honour is bountiful: you will consider the groom."

My Lord burst into laughter at the groom's words. Harry, for his part, indulged in remarks of indignation that politeness forbade recording.

"Mr. William said he never could think of parting with the Prince under a hundred and twenty," the groom added, looking at the young man.

My Lord laughed harder. "Will has been too much for thee, Harry Warrington."

"Too much for me, my lord! So may a fellow with loaded dice throw sixes, and be too much for me. I do not call this betting, I call it ch—"

"Mr. Warrington! Spare me bad words about my brother, if you please. Depend on it, I will take care that you are righted. Farewell. Ride quickly, or your coaches will be at Farnham before you;" My Lord waved him off and retreated into the house, leaving Harry and Gumbo to ride out of the courtyard.

As the youth departed, eager to rejoin the carriages and his beloved, he failed to notice the unutterable affection Gumbo shot from his fine eyes toward a young creature in the porter's lodge.

Inside, the chaplain and My Lord finished their breakfast in peace. The two ladies had not returned.

"That was one of Will's confounded rascally tricks," My Lord said. "If our cousin breaks Will's head I should not wonder."

"He is used to the operation, my lord," the chaplain grinned. "And yet, when we were playing last night, the colour of the horse was not mentioned. I could not escape, having but one: and the black boy has ridden off on him. The young Virginian plays like a man, to do him justice."

"He wins because he does not care about losing. I think there can be little doubt but that he is very well to do. His mother's law-agents are my lawyers, and they write that the property is quite a principality, and grows richer every year."

"If it were a kingdom I know whom Mr. Warrington would make queen of it," the chaplain remarked obsequiously.

"Who can account for taste, parson?" My Lord asked with a sneer. "All men are so. The first woman I was in love with myself was forty; and as jealous as if she had been fifteen. It runs in the family. Colonel Esmond married my grandmother, who was almost old enough to be his. If this lad chooses to take out an elderly princess to Virginia, we must not balk him."
