For months, life at the fort dragged on in a monotonous rhythm of hardship and routine. The commandant and I endured our share of quarrels and reconciliations, passed the time with greasy card games, and filled the silence with dismal duets—his wheezing flute and my cracked guitar. La Biche, the poor Fawn, bore her beatings and the liquor poured down her throat as her master saw fit. She nursed her papoose, tended to Museau when his gout flared up, and cared for me during my fevers. Such was our existence until the early autumn of the previous year, when a hunter arrived with news that stirred the entire garrison.

The Marquis de Montcalm had dispatched a strong force to reinforce French-held forts and seize new positions within British territory. Troops had departed from Quebec and Montreal, sailing up the St. Lawrence and across the lakes in bateaux, laden with artillery and supplies. Museau would be replaced by an officer of higher rank—one who might exchange me or hand me over to the Indians in retaliation for the cruelties committed by our frontier men against French soldiers. The men of the fort were thrilled by the prospect: they dreamed of marching into Pennsylvania and New York, capturing Albany and Philadelphia, driving the “Rosbifs” into the sea, and claiming all America from the Mississippi to Newfoundland.

Yet despite this promise of triumph, Museau remained troubled.
“Eh, commandant!” I said, “It’s all very fine, but what of your farm in Normandy? Your pot of cider? Your trippes à la mode de Caen? Where are they now?”

“Yes, yes,” he replied, “it’s all well enough, my garçon. But where will you be when poor old Museau is removed? Other officers aren’t so companionable as I. Very few men possess my humanity. When the garrison swells, will your next commander grant you the same indulgences I have? You’ll be shut in a sty like a pig awaiting slaughter. As surely as one of our men falls into the hands of your frontier brutes and suffers, so surely will you pay with your skin. You’ll be delivered to our red allies—the brethren of La Biche there. Did you see what they did to your countrymen after Braddock’s defeat? Roasting was the least of it, ma foi—was it not, La Biche?”

He went on with vivid, jocular descriptions of torture—eyes burned out, teeth and nails torn away, limbs hacked open. I turned pale, dear Miss Theo. But I’ll spare you the gruesome details, though honest Museau recounted them with grim amusement.

La Biche showed no such emotion. She had seen such horrors before. A Seneca woman, she came from villages near the great cataract between Ontario and Erie. Her people fought for the English, then against them; they had clashed with other tribes, and in war between us and them, it was hard to say which side was more savage.

“They may chop me into cutlets and broil me,” I said coolly, “but still, commandant—you’ll never have your farm in Normandy.”

“Go fetch the whisky-bottle, La Biche,” Museau commanded.

“And it’s not too late,” I added. “I’ll give a generous reward to anyone who brings me home. And I swear, as a man of honour, ten thousand livres to whoever returns a token—say, my watch and seal bearing my grandfather’s arms—I’ve seen it somewhere in this fort.”

“Ah, scelerat!” roared the commandant, laughing hoarsely. “You’ve eyes, you do! Everything is fair game in war.”

“I dream of a house in my village,” I continued, “a field with half a dozen cows, a fine orchard heavy with fruit.”

“And Javotte at the door with her spinning wheel, and a rascal child or two with cheeks as red as the apples! O my country! O my mother!” he sobbed. “Quick, La Biche—get the whisky!”

That night, Museau was deep in thought. La Biche sat apart, silent and melancholy, nursing her child. Whenever I glanced her way, I saw her watching me. The infant began to cry, and Museau, cursing, sent her off to her own hut. Once she was gone, we spoke freely. I laid my arguments before him—arguments he could not resist.

“How can you be sure,” he asked, “that this hunter will help you?”

“That’s my secret,” I replied. But since we’re not bound by honour now, I’ll tell you. Hunters often linger a day or two at the settlements for rest, drink, and company. Our visitor loved all three. He played cards, traded furs for liquor, sang, danced, gambled. He enjoyed himself at the fort. And I—being idle—often sang and scraped my guitar. The men joined in chorus or danced to my tunes until the drums called them to retreat.

Our guest attended one or two of these gatherings. I decided to test whether he understood English. After we’d sung our French songs, I said, “My lads, I’ll give you an English song.” To the tune of *‘Over the hills and far away’*—a favourite air my grandfather used to hum in Marlborough’s camp—I made up some clumsy words:

> *This long, long year, a prisoner drear,*
> *Ah, me! I'm tired of lingering here:*
> *I'll give a hundred guineas gay,*
> *To be over the hills and far away.*

“What is it?” he asked. “I don’t understand.”

“A girl to her lover,” I said—but I saw the twinkle in his eye. He understood.

The next day, when no one else was near, he passed me and hummed softly, perfectly in English: *“Over the hills and far away.”*

“If you’re ready,” he said, “I am. I know who your people are, and how to reach them. Talk to the Fawn—she’ll tell you what to do. What! You won’t play with me?” He pulled out cards and spoke in French as two soldiers approached. “Milor est trop grand seigneur? Bonjour, my lord!”

He bowed mockingly and walked away, shrugging, to find another game.

Now I knew the Biche was to be my agent—and Museau had agreed. The poor Fawn carried out her role faithfully. I needed no further words with Museau. The understanding was complete.

She had long been allowed free access to me—had nursed me through wounds and sickness, helped with my chamber, cooked for me. She was permitted to leave the fort, go to the river, gather corn and vegetables for the garrison.

After gambling away most of his fur money, the trapper gathered his flints, powder, and blankets and left. Three days later, La Biche gave me the signal: the time had come.

When first wounded, I’d been placed in the officers’ room by kind Florac. Later, with only the old lieutenant remaining, I stayed in my quarters—sometimes free, sometimes locked up, sometimes sharing meals with my drunken keeper.

The officers’ house was log-built, like the others in the fort, which mounted only four small guns—one behind my cabin. From there, looking westward, you could see a small island at the confluence of the Ohio and Monongahela rivers, where Duquesne stood. On the opposite shore, a cluster of trees marked the rendezvous.

“You see those trees?” La Biche whispered the day before, in broken French. “He waits for you behind them.”

By day, my door stood open. That afternoon, she returned from the fields with a pickaxe and a basket of greens. She sat on a bench outside my door, the pick leaning against it, the basket beside her. We talked for a while—though I confess I was so dull-minded I wouldn’t have thought to use the pick if she hadn’t pushed it through the door into my room.

“Hide it,” she said. “Need it soon.”

That same day, she pointed out the trees.

The next morning, she came in pretending to be angry. “My lord! My lord! Why didn’t you come to the commandant’s dinner? He’s very ill! Entendez-vous?” She peered into the room and tossed a coil of rope at me.

“I’m coming, La Biche,” I said, hobbling after her on my crutch. As I entered the commandant’s quarters, she murmured, “Pour ce soir.” Then I knew—tonight was the night.

Museau knew nothing. Not a word. He growled at me, complained the soup was cold. He stared at me steadily, chatted about this and that—not just in front of his servant, but later, as we smoked pipes and played piquet. La Biche sat huddled in a corner as usual.

His whisky bottle was empty. “Must have another glass,” he said, with a knowing glance. “We both need one tonight.” Rising, he shuffled to his inner room, where he kept his fire-water locked away—well beyond La Biche’s reach.

As he turned his back, she rose. No sooner was he gone than she knelt at my feet, kissed my hand, pressed it to her heart, and burst into tears upon my knees. I confess I was deeply moved—by her silent devotion, greater than I’d ever imagined. When Museau returned, I was still shaken, though La Biche had retreated to her corner, wrapped in her blanket.

He noticed nothing unusual. We sat down to play. My thoughts were elsewhere; I barely saw the cards.

“I gain everything from you tonight, milor,” he said grimly. “We play on parole.”

“And you may count on mine,” I replied.

“Eh! That’s all you’ve got!” he laughed.

“Monsieur,” I said, “my word is worth ten thousand livres.” We continued.

At last, he said he had a headache and would retire. I understood—I was to follow. “Good night, mon petit milor,” he said. “Wait—without your crutch, you’ll fall.” His eyes twinkled, his face twisted into a sarcastic grin. In my excitement, I’d forgotten my lameness and was walking briskly.

“What a vile night!” he muttered, glancing out. Indeed, a storm raged—wind howling, rain pouring. “Bring a lantern, La Tulipe, and lock my lord safely in his quarters!” He watched me from the doorway. For a moment, I glimpsed La Biche behind him.

The rain was so heavy the sentries stayed in their boxes, leaving me undisturbed. The log house stood on upright posts sunk deep into the ground, with horizontal logs laid across. I began digging beneath the lowest log, working in darkness shortly after tattoo. It took hours before I felt rain falling on my hand under the log. Then, for two more hours, I worked cautiously, until I crawled through to the parapet and silently threw my rope over the gun—heart pounding, fearing the sentry might spot me and shoot.

The wall was only twelve feet high. My fall into the ditch was easy. I waited, staring under the gun, trying to see the river and the island. Above, the sentry paced, humming a tune. Slowly, the darkness cleared. The moon rose, illuminating the river and the dark rocks and trees of the island.

I hurried toward the mark, racing for the clump of trees. Oh, what relief when I heard a low voice humming: *“Over the hills and far away!”*

When Mr. George reached this point, Miss Theo, seated at the harpsichord, turned and played the tune. The little gathering burst into the cheerful chorus.

“Our path,” George continued, “lay through a broad forest on the right bank of the Monongahela, familiar to my guide. By daylight, we came to open land, and my trapper—Silverheels, he called himself—asked if I’d ever seen the place before. It was the fatal field where Braddock fell—the very spot from which I’d been rescued the summer before. Now, the leaves were turning to the brilliant colours of autumn.”

“Ah, brother!” cried Harry, seizing his hand. “I was gambling and wasting myself at Bath and London while you fled for your life through the wilderness! Oh, what a wretched spendthrift I’ve been!”

“But I think you are not unworthy to be called your mother’s son,” Mrs. Lambert said softly, her eyes glistening. Indeed, in Harry’s remorse, his love, his joy, his generosity—we saw hope for the repentant, kind young sinner.

“We crossed the river,” George resumed, “and followed the base of the western Allegheny slopes through a magnificent forest of oaks, maples, and towering poplars, some a hundred feet high without a single branch. Our danger came not only from the French patrols, but from the Indians—always uncertain allies, now especially hostile due to our past mistreatment and the French victory two years prior.”

I was still weak. Our journey through the wilderness lasted over two weeks. As we advanced, the woods grew redder. Frost nipped sharply at night. We lit fires at our feet and slept wrapped in blankets. At this season, mountain hunters gathered maple sap to make sugar. We met several families camping near their trees by mountain streams. They welcomed us at their fires and shared venison. So we crossed the Laurel Hills and the Alleghenies.

On the final day, my faithful guide and I descended Will’s Creek—a wild, magnificent pass between cliffs nearly a thousand feet high—bare, white, and jagged like fortress towers. Eagles circled the peaks, guarding their nests in the crags.

From there, we descended to Cumberland, where we had marched the year before. Now, it held a substantial garrison of our own men. Oh, you may imagine what joy it was to see English colours once again flying over the banks of our native Potomac!
