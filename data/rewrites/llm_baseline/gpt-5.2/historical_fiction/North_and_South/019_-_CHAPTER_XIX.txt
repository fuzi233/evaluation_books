Mrs. Hale took the notion of Mrs. Thornton’s dinner-party into her sick-room as if it were a warm coal cupped in both hands—turning it, admiring it, asking over and over about its glow. Illness had narrowed her days to the width of a bed and a window; within those bounds, any coming event swelled until it filled the whole horizon. The soft vanities that had once quickened her as a girl—long smothered under parsonage economies—stirred again, not loud but persistent, and most of all in one bright desire: to see Margaret dressed, to know what silk would whisper as she moved, what colour would set off her dark, steady face.

“So you think you shall wear your white silk,” she said, with that fretful eagerness which made her seem, at times, younger than her daughter. “Are you sure it will fit? It is nearly a year since Edith was married.”

“Oh, yes, mamma. Mrs. Murray made it; it is sure to be right. It may be a straw’s breadth shorter, or longer-waisted, according to whether I’ve grown fat or thin. But I don’t think I’ve altered in the least.”

“Hadn’t you better let Dixon see it? It may have gone yellow with lying by.”

“If you like. But if the worst comes to the worst, I’ve a very nice pink gauze Aunt Shaw gave me only two or three months before Edith was married. That can’t have gone yellow.”

“No: but it may have faded.”

“Well! then I’ve a green silk. I feel more as if it were the embarrassment of riches.”

“I wish I knew what you ought to wear,” Mrs. Hale said, and the very tremble in her voice made it sound like an anxious prayer.

Margaret’s look softened instantly. “Shall I go and put them on one after another, mamma, and then you can see which you like best?”

“But—yes. Perhaps that will be best.”

So Margaret went, with the air of one called to a serious office, though the unusual hour and the rustle of kept-away finery half-tempted her into mischief. She longed, for a moment, to make the white silk puff and balloon absurdly, to sweep backward like a queen in a masque, and to let her mother laugh; but Mrs. Hale, intent on the matter as if a kingdom depended on the choice, was jarred by any play. Margaret, checked by that fragile earnestness, grew grave.

And yet the same flutter of dress and consequence had found her, absurdly, out-of-doors. Only that afternoon she had mentioned her engagement—lightly, as something incidental—to Bessy Higgins, while speaking of the servant Mrs. Thornton had promised to inquire about. Bessy, who could lie listless for hours and then blaze up at a word, started as if some bell had sounded in her.

“Dear! and are you going to dine at Thornton’s at Marlborough Mills?”

“Yes, Bessy. Why are you so surprised?”

“Oh, I dunno. But they visit wi’ a’ th’ first folk in Milton.”

“And you don’t think we’re quite the first folk in Milton, eh, Bessy?”

Colour came into Bessy’s thin cheeks; her eyes shifted, then fixed on Margaret’s face with their eager, pained sincerity.

“Well,” she said, “yo’ see, they thinken a deal o’ money here; and I reckon yo’ve not getten much.”

“No,” said Margaret, quietly. “That is very true. But we are educated people, and have lived among educated people. Is there anything so wonderful in our being asked to dinner by a man who owns himself inferior to my father by coming to him to be instructed? I don’t mean to blame Mr. Thornton. Few drapers’ assistants, as he was once, could have made themselves what he is.”

“But can yo’ give dinners back, in yo’re small house? Thornton’s house is three times as big.”

“Well, I think we could manage to give Mr. Thornton a dinner back, as you call it. Perhaps not in such a large room, nor with so many people. But I don’t think we’ve thought about it at all in that way.”

“I never thought yo’d be dining with Thorntons,” Bessy repeated, as if turning the words in her mouth to test their taste. “Why, the mayor hissel’ dines there; and the members of Parliament and all.”

“I think I could support the honour of meeting the mayor of Milton.”

“But them ladies dress so grand!” Bessy said, and her anxious glance dropped to Margaret’s plain print gown, priced by Milton eyes as exactly as flour and coal were priced.

Margaret laughed, and the laugh had a little ring in it like a spoon tapping china. “Thank you, Bessy, for thinking so kindly about my looking nice among all the smart people. But I’ve plenty of grand gowns,—a week ago I should have said they were far too grand for anything I should ever want here. But as I’m to dine at Mr. Thornton’s, and perhaps meet the mayor, I shall put on my very best gown, you may be sure.”

“What win yo’ wear?” Bessy asked, eased a little.

“White silk,” said Margaret. “A gown I had for a cousin’s wedding a year ago.”

“That’ll do!” Bessy sank back as if the choice itself had soothed her. “I should be loth to have yo’ looked down upon.”

“Oh! I’ll be fine enough, if that will save me from being looked down upon in Milton.”

“I wish I could see you dressed up,” said Bessy, and her voice altered, slipping into that strange earnestness that often came to her like a change of weather. “I reckon, yo’re not what folk would ca’ pretty; yo’ve not red and white enough for that. But dun yo’ know, I ha’ dreamt of yo’, long afore ever I seed yo’.”

“Nonsense, Bessy!”

“Ay, but I did. Yo’r very face,—looking wi’ yo’r clear steadfast eyes out o’ th’ darkness,—wi’ yo’r hair blown off from yo’r brow, and going out like rays round yo’r forehead, which was just as smooth and straight as it is now,—and yo’ always came to give me strength, which I seemed to gather out o’ yo’r deep comforting eyes,—and yo’ was drest in shining raiment—just as yo’r going to be drest. So, yo’ see, it was yo’!”

“Nay, Bessy,” Margaret answered gently; she could feel in her own bones the way Bessy’s room held sound—the small clink of a kettle-lid, the creak of a chair, the far-off thud of a cart, and under all the steady, maddening undertone of the town’s machinery like surf that never ceased. “It was but a dream.”

“And why might na I dream a dream in my affliction as well as others? Did not many a one i’ the Bible? Ay, and see visions too! Why, even my father thinks a deal o’ dreams! I tell yo’ again, I saw yo’ as plainly, coming swiftly towards me, wi’ yo’r hair blown back wi’ the very swiftness o’ the motion,—and the white shining dress on yo’ve getten to wear. Let me come and see yo’ in it. I want to see yo’ and touch yo’ as in very deed yo’ were in my dream.”

“My dear Bessy, it is quite a fancy of yours.”

“Fancy or no fancy,—yo’ve come, as I knew yo’ would, when I saw yo’r movement in my dream,—and when yo’re here about me, I reckon I feel easier in my mind, and comforted, just as a fire comforts one on a dree day. Yo’ said it were on th’ twenty-first; please God, I’ll come and see yo’.”

“Oh, Bessy! you may come, and welcome; but don’t talk so—it really makes me sorry. It does indeed.”

“Then I’ll keep it to mysel’, if I bite my tongue out. Not but what it’s true for all that.”

Margaret let the talk fall, because it seemed safer to touch nothing further that had the heat and danger of prophecy in it. After a little she asked, softly, “Has your father turned out?”

“Ay!” Bessy’s voice dropped as if a shutter had been pulled down inside her. “He and many another,—all Hamper’s men,—and many a one besides. Th’ women are as bad as th’ men, in their savageness, this time. Food is high,—and they mun have food for their childer. Suppose Thorntons sent ’em their dinner out,—th’ same money, spent on potatoes and meal, would keep many a crying baby quiet, and hush up its mother’s heart for a bit!”

“Don’t speak so!” Margaret cried; the image struck like the sharp smell of scorching—fine dishes on one side, and on the other the plain, desperate cookery of hunger: meal stirred into water, potatoes boiled until they fell into a grey mash, nothing to sweeten, nothing to spare. “You’ll make me feel wicked and guilty in going to this dinner.”

“No!” Bessy said, with a bitter steadiness. “Some’s pre-elected to sumptuous feasts, and purple and fine linen,—may be yo’re one on ’em. Others toil and moil all their lives long—and the very dogs are not pitiful in our days, as they were in the days of Lazarus. But if yo’ ask me to cool yo’re tongue wi’ th’ tip of my finger, I’ll come across the great gulf to yo’ just for th’ thought o’ what yo’ve been to me here.”

“Bessy; you’re very feverish,” Margaret said, feeling the heat and then the sudden chill in the girl’s hand. “I can tell it in the touch of your hand as well as in what you’re saying. It won’t be division enough, in that awful day, that some of us have been beggars here and some of us rich,—we shall not be judged by that poor accident, but by our faithful following of Christ.”

She rose, found water, and, as if she were in her own house again, did what could be done with the simplest things—soaked her handkerchief, laid it cool upon Bessy’s forehead, and rubbed the stone-cold feet until life seemed to creep back by degrees. Bessy’s eyelids sank; the rocking stopped. Outside, Milton’s ceaseless din pressed against the walls; within, the small sounds of tending—water wrung from linen, the whisper of cloth on skin—made a gentler world.

At last Bessy spoke, weakly. “Yo’d ha’ been deaved out o’ yo’r five wits, as well as me, if yo’d had one body after another coming in to ask for father, and staying to tell me each one their tale. Some spoke o’ deadly hatred, and made my blood run cold wi’ the terrible things they said o’ th’ masters,—but more, being women, kept plaining, plaining (wi’ the tears running down their cheeks, and never wiped away, nor heeded), of the price o’ meat, and how their childer could na sleep at nights for th’ hunger.”

“And do they think the strike will mend this?” Margaret asked. Her mind caught at the question as one catches at a handhold in a river.

“They say so. They do say trade has been good for long, and the masters has made no end o’ money; how much father doesn’t know, but, in course, th’ Union does; and, as it is natural, they wanted their share o’ th’ profits, now that food is getting dear; and th’ Union says they’ll not be doing their duty if they don’t make th’ masters give ’em their share. But masters has getten th’ upper hand somehow; and I’m feared they’ll keep it now and evermore. It’s like th’ great battle o’ Armageddon, the way they keep on, grinning and fighting at each other, till even while they fight, they are picked off into the pit.”

At that instant Nicholas Higgins came in, catching the last words as if they were thrown to him.

“Ay! and I’ll fight on too; and I’ll got it this time. It’ll not take long for to make ’em give in, for they’ve getten a pretty lot of orders, all under contract; and they’ll soon find out they’d better give us our five per cent. than lose the profit they’ll gain; let alone the fine for not fulfilling the contract. Aha, my masters! I know who’ll win.”

There was something excited in him—too rapid, too loud—as if he needed the sound of his own voice to keep up his courage. Margaret, listening, thought she caught the blurred edge of speech that meant drink; Bessy, with a glance of distress, hurried her to go.

“The twenty-first—that’s Thursday week,” Bessy said quickly, clinging back to the bright thread of the dinner as if it were a charm against the dark. “I may come and see yo’ dressed for Thornton’s, I reckon. What time is yo’r dinner?”

Before Margaret could answer, Higgins broke out with a coarse, reckless sort of triumph.

“Thornton’s! Ar’ t’ going to dine at Thornton’s? Ask him to give yo’ a bumper to the success of his orders. By th’ twenty-first, I reckon, he’ll be pottered in his brains how to get ’em done in time. Tell him, there’s seven hundred ’ll come marching into Marlborough Mills, the morning after he gives the five per cent., and will help him through his contract in no time. You’ll have ’em all there. My master, Hamper. He’s one o’ th’ oud-fashioned sort. Ne’er meets a man bout an oath or a curse; I should think he were going to die if he spoke me civil; but arter all, his bark’s waur than his bite, and yo’ may tell him one o’ his turn-outs said so, if yo’ like. Eh! but yo’ll have a lot of prize mill-owners at Thornton’s! I should like to get speech o’ them, when they’re a bit inclined to sit still after dinner, and could na run for the life on ’em. I’d tell ’em my mind. I’d speak up again th’ hard way they’re driving on us!”

“Good-bye!” Margaret said hastily, her cheeks burning with a confusion she would not name. “Good-bye, Bessy! I shall look to see you on the twenty-first, if you’re well enough.”

In the Hales’ house the medicines and treatment Dr. Donaldson had ordered did, at first, so much good that even Margaret—who had held hope like a candle in a draught, shielding it with both hands—let herself believe that he might have been mistaken, that her mother’s breath might lengthen into months and years. Mrs. Hale smiled more; the cough loosened; her eyes had moments of brightness as if a little window had been opened. Mr. Hale, who had never understood how near the fear had come, triumphed with an almost boyish relief, proving by that very triumph how much his half-glimpsed dread had weighed upon him. Only Dixon croaked on, a household raven, croaking in kitchen and passage and sick-room alike, until Margaret—by sheer will—defied the sound and chose to hope.

They needed that small patch of light indoors; for outside, even to their uninstructed eyes, Milton’s air seemed thick with a brooding discontent. Mr. Hale, with his gentle habit of listening, had drawn around him acquaintances among the working men; their quietly told tales of suffering, of long endurance, depressed him more than any open clamour could. They would have scorned to complain to one who, by his station, ought to have known without being told; but he was a stranger from a distant county, perplexed by the machinery of a system into which he had been dropped, and each man was eager to make him judge—to lay before him, as evidence, his own irritations and wrongs.

And then Mr. Hale, unable to keep these stories to himself, would take his budget of grievances to Mr. Thornton and beg him to arrange them, to explain their origin. Mr. Thornton always did—calmly, clearly, on what he called sound economical principles. Trade, he said, must wax and wane; and in the waning a certain number of masters as well as men must go down into ruin, slipping out of the ranks of the prosperous as inevitably as weak swimmers slip under a current. He spoke of the consequence as if it were not merely probable but logical—so logical that neither employer nor employed had a right to complain when it became their fate: the employer to turn aside from a race he could no longer run, bitten by failure, trampled by his fellows in their haste to be rich, slighted where he had once been honoured, forced to ask for employment instead of dispensing it with a lordly hand.

And because he could picture so sternly the possible doom of masters, he had the less sympathy to spare for the workmen who were cast out by swift alteration—men who would willingly lie down and quietly die out of a world that did not need them, if only they could die without hearing in their last moment the clinging cries of the helpless they would leave behind; men who envied even a wild bird that can feed her young with her heart’s blood.

Margaret’s whole soul rose against him when he reasoned so—as if commerce were everything and humanity nothing. It made her teeth set, it made her throat ache, when that very evening he came to offer help with a delicacy that kept it private—every convenience for illness that his wealth, or his mother’s foresight, had gathered: things Dr. Donaldson had hinted Mrs. Hale might need. His presence, his grave voice, the pity in his eyes, brought close the doom Margaret was trying—by day and by night—to push back into darkness. What right had he, of all people, to share the awful secret she kept in the most sacred recess of her heart—barely daring to look at it unless she called for strength from God—that some day soon she would cry aloud for her mother and no answer would come, only blank, dumb darkness?

Yet he knew. She saw it in the softened look he tried not to let rest on her. She heard it in the tremor that would not quite leave his careful speech. And how could she reconcile those eyes and that voice with the hard, dry, merciless axiom-making by which he had spoken of men’s misery as if it were a necessary subtraction in a sum? The discord jarred upon her beyond bearing—especially with Bessy’s tales in her mind, full of hunger’s plain cookery and the wailing it brewed: children waking at night because their bellies were empty, mothers counting pennies as they would count grains.

To be sure, Nicholas Higgins spoke differently. He had been made a committee-man, and now carried himself as one who held secrets from those outside. He said so openly on the day before Mrs. Thornton’s dinner-party, when Margaret went in to see Bessy and found him in argument with John Boucher—a neighbour often named in the Higgins household, sometimes with compassion as a weak workman overburdened with a great family, and sometimes with anger for his lack of what Higgins called spirit.

Higgins was in a passion when Margaret entered. Boucher stood with both hands braced on the mantelpiece, swaying a little as if his arms were the only things keeping him upright. He stared into the fire with a wild despair that pricked Higgins like an insult even while it tore at his heart. Bessy rocked herself violently, as she did when agitation seized her; her sister Mary, tying on her bonnet with clumsy bows that her clumsy fingers made, blubbered aloud and longed to escape the room.

Margaret paused at the door. Then, with her finger to her lips, she stole to the squab near Bessy and sat down. Nicholas, catching her movement, gave her a gruff nod that was not unfriendly. Mary, snatching at the open door like a sudden mercy, hurried away and cried aloud once she was out of her father’s presence. Only Boucher seemed to notice nothing—neither comings nor goings—only the fire and what it could not warm.

“It’s no use, Higgins,” he said hoarsely. “Hoo cannot live long a’ this’n. Hoo’s just sinking away—not for want o’ meat hersel’—but because hoo cannot stand th’ sight o’ the little ones clemming. Ay, clemming! Five shilling a week may do well enough for thee, wi’ but two mouths to fill, and one on ’em a wench who can well earn her own meat. But it’s clemming to us.” His voice rose, then broke, as if words were torn out of him. “An’ I tell thee plain—if hoo dies, as I’m ’feard hoo will afore we’ve getten th’ five per cent., I’ll fling the money back i’ th’ master’s face, and say, ‘Be domned to yo’; be domned to th’ whole cruel world o’ yo’; that could na leave me th’ best wife that ever bore childer to a man.’ An’ look thee, lad, I’ll hate thee, and th’ whole pack o’ th’ Union. Ay, an’ chase yo’ through heaven wi’ my hatred,—I will, lad! I will,—if yo’re leading me astray i’ this matter.”

He swallowed, then forced on, as if each detail were a nail he must hammer in. “Thou saidst, Nicholas, on Wednesday sennight—and it’s now Tuesday i’ th’ second week—that afore a fortnight we’d ha’ the masters coming a-begging to us to take back our work, at our own wage—and time’s nearly up,—and there’s our lile Jack lying a-bed, too weak to cry, but just every now and then sobbing up his heart for want o’ food,—our lile Jack, I tell thee, lad! Hoo’s never looked up sin’ he were born, and hoo loves him as if he were her very life,—as he is,—for I reckon he’ll ha’ cost me that precious price,—our lile Jack, who wakened me each morn wi’ putting his sweet little lips to my great rough fou’ face, a-seeking a smooth place to kiss,—an’ he lies clemming.”

The sobs rose and choked him. Higgins looked up then—eyes brimming—towards Margaret, as if some witness were needed to bear what he had heard.

“Hou’d up, man,” Higgins said at last, with effort, fumbling in a teapot where he kept his money. “Thy lile Jack shall na’ clem. I ha’ gotten brass, and we’ll go buy the chap a sup o’ milk an’ a good four-pounder this very minute. What’s mine’s thine, sure enough, i’ thou’st i’ want. Only, dunnot lose heart, man!” He thrust the coins into his palm as if their cold weight could be turned into life by speed. “I lay yo my heart and soul we’ll win for a’ this: it’s but bearing on one more week, and yo’ just see th’ way th’ masters ’ll come round, praying on us to come back to our mills. An’ the Union—that’s to say, I—will take care yo’ve enough for th’ childer and th’ missus. So dunnot turn faint-heart, and go to th’ tyrants a-seeking work.”

Boucher turned then—slowly—showing a face so white and gaunt and tear-furrowed and hopeless that its very stillness undid Margaret. The sound of Milton, outside, seemed to press in at the cracks—far-off iron, far-off wheels—yet the most terrible noise was the quiet certainty in his voice.

“Yo’ know well, that a worser tyrant than e’er th’ masters were says, ‘Clem to death, and see ’em a’ clem to death, ere yo’ dare go again th’ Union.’ Yo’ know it well, Nicholas, for a’ yo’re one on e’m. Yo’ may be kind hearts, each separate; but once banded together, yo’ve no more pity for a man than a wild hunger-maddened wolf.”

Nicholas had his hand on the door-lock. He stopped, and turned back, Boucher close behind as if the words had pulled him after.

“So help me God!” Higgins cried, with sudden fierceness that sounded like a vow against the roaring town itself. “Man alive—if I think not I’m doing best for thee, and for all on us. If I’m going wrong when I think I’m going right, it’s their sin, who ha’ left me where I am, in my ignorance. I ha’ thought till my brains ached,—beli’ me, John, I have. An’ I say again, there’s no help for us but having faith i’ th’ Union. They’ll win the day, see if they dunnot!”

Not a word had Margaret or Bessy spoken. Their eyes met, and each seemed to beg the other not to break into the men’s terrible tide. At last Bessy whispered, more to herself than to Margaret, “I never thought to hear father call on God again. But yo’ heard him say, ‘So help me God!’”

“Yes,” Margaret said. The words tasted like iron. “Let me bring you what money I can spare—let me bring you a little food for that poor man’s children. Don’t let them know it comes from anyone but your father. It will be but little.”

Bessy lay back as if the strength to answer had ebbed out of her. She did not cry; she only shivered in her breath.

“My heart’s drained dry o’ tears,” she said at length. “Boucher’s been in these days past, a telling me of his fears and his troubles. He’s but a weak kind of chap, I know, but he’s a man for a’ that; and tho’ I’ve been angry, many a time afore now, wi’ him an’ his wife, as knew no more nor him how to manage, yet yo’ see, all folks isn’t wise, yet God lets ’em live—ay, an’ gives ’em some one to love, and be loved by, just as good as Solomon. An’ if sorrow comes to them they love, it hurts ’em as sore as e’er it did Solomon. I can’t make it out. Perhaps it’s as well such a one as Boucher has th’ Union to see after him. But I’d just like for to see th’ men as make th’ Union, and put ’em one by one face to face wi’ Boucher. I reckon, if they heard him, the’d tell him (if I cotched ’em one by one), he might go back and get what he could for his work, even if it weren’t so much as they ordered.”

Margaret sat silent, feeling the pull of two worlds at once—the coming dinner with its silver, its hot savoury dishes carried in and set down, its polite talk that would float above want like music above a street; and here, this room with its thin bodies and the very word clemming like a dry crust in the throat. How was she to go into comfort and forget that voice, the tone of unutterable agony that said more than the words?

She drew out her purse. There was not much she could call her own, but what she had she put into Bessy’s hand without speaking.

“Thank yo’,” Bessy said, rousing a little, as if the warmth of kindness had reached her even through weakness. “There’s many on ’em gets no more, and is not so bad off,—leastways does not show it as he does. But father won’t let ’em want, now he knows. Yo’ see, Boucher’s been pulled down wi’ his childer,—and her being so cranky, and a’ they could pawn has gone this last twelvemonth. Yo’re not to think we’d ha’ letten ’em clem, for all we’re a bit pressed oursel’; if neighbours doesn’t see after neighbours, I dunno who will.” She spoke quickly now, almost anxiously, as if to guard the pride of their poor household. “Besides, father is sure and positive the masters must give in within these next few days,—that they canna hould on much longer. But I thank yo’ all the same,—I thank yo’ for mysel’, as much as for Boucher, for it jus makes my heart warm to yo’ more and more.”

She looked quieter to-day, but there was a dreadful languor upon her, as if the smallest effort cost her dear. When she finished, her eyelids drooped; her face, sharpened by illness, seemed suddenly too large for its bones. Margaret, struck with alarm at that faint weariness, leaned nearer, listening not only to Bessy’s breathing but to the sounds beyond the wall—the town’s harsh, ceaseless undertone—and wondered how any sick body could heal while Milton’s iron voice never slept.
