# CHAPTER XXXIV. A CASE OF DOMESTIC PERSECUTION--REMARKABLE INSTANCE OF PIOUS PERSEVERANCE IN THE DISCHARGE OF RELIGIOUS DUTIES
Martin Yorke had tasted the bright, buzzing spark of excitement once, and he ached for another sip. He’d felt the quiet weight of power for the first time, and he had no intention of letting it slip back through his fingers. Miss Helstone— the girl he’d always written off as plain, whose face now haunted every corner of his days and nights, glowing in sunlight and lingering in shadow— had once stepped into his small, self-made world. The thought she might never cross its threshold again niggled at him like an itch he couldn’t scratch. Though still a schoolboy, he was no ordinary one: he was marked to be a soul all his own. In years to come he would spend endless hours sanding and polishing his edges to fit the rest of the world’s expected mould, but he would never quite manage it. That unique, unmistakeable stamp stayed on him for life.
He sat idle at his grammar school desk that week, turning over every idea he could think of to add a new chapter to the little half-spun romance he’d begun to weave around Caroline. He still had no idea how many such half-written life stories are doomed to never make it past the first, or at most the second page. He spent his Saturday half-holiday wandering the bare winter woods with his tattered book of fairy legends, the unwritten story in his head far more vivid than any tale on the pages.
Martin had always hated the approach of Sunday, an ungodly sort of drag to him. His parents, for all their distaste for strict loyalty to the Established Church, never failed to drag their whole brood of blooming sons and daughters to Briarfield Church once every sacred week, to fill their large cushioned pew to bursting. Theoretically, Mr. Yorke held every sect and denomination on equal ground. Mrs. Yorke swore the Moravians and Quakers were the best of the lot, for the crown of gentle humility they wore. Neither of them, however, had ever been known to set foot in a dissenting conventicle.
This Saturday afternoon, his woodland daydreams unveiled a new, thrilling charm to the coming Sunday.
When dawn broke, the snow lay deep enough to swallow a man’s ankle. At breakfast, Mrs. Yorke announced her firm conviction that none of the children should step outside, and decided they would all spend two quiet hours in the back parlour instead of going to church, taking turns reading selections of John Wesley’s sermons aloud. Wesley, a reformer and agitator, was a favourite of both hers and her husband’s.
“Rose will do as she pleases,” Martin said, not lifting his eyes from the book he pored over even as he ate his bread and milk, a habit he kept all his life.
“Rose will do as she is told, and so will you,” his mother shot back.
“I’m going to church,” Martin replied, with that unshakable quiet steadiness unique to the Yorkes, who know their own will and mean to get their way, who will let themselves be crushed to a wall before they surrender, no matter what.
“The weather’s not fit for a dog,” his father objected. No answer. The boy read on studiously, breaking his bread slow, sipping his milk one tiny mouthful at a time.
“Martin hates church, but he hates obeying even more,” Mrs. Yorke sighed. “I suppose this is just pure perverseness on your part?”
“Yes,” he said.
“Mother, I am not.”
“Then what on earth is motivating you?”
“A whole tangled mess of motives. I might as soon turn myself inside out to show you all the machinery inside my body as try to untangle them for you.”
“Listen to the lad!” Mr. Yorke laughed, slamming his palm on the table. “I’m sending this one to the bar, mark my words. Nature made him to earn a living by his tongue. Hesther, our third son will be a lawyer, no two ways about it— he’s got all the stock in trade: brass, self-conceit, and words, words, words.”
“Pass the bread, Rose, please,” Martin requested, perfectly grave, serene, phlegmatic as ever. His voice was naturally low, soft, almost plaintive, and in his stubbornest moods it rose scarcely above a lady’s whisper: the more inflexible his will, the gentler, sadder his tone. He rang the bell, and asked for his walking boots in that same quiet, unshakable voice.
“But Martin,” his father urged, “the drifts are waist deep all the way to church. A man could barely wade through them. But lad,” he went on, seeing the boy stand just as the first church bell began to toll, “I won’t balk you of your will, not for the world. Go on to church, if that’s what you’d rather do than sit by a warm fire. There’s a pitiless wind, sharp frozen sleet, and snow thick underfoot that will freeze your socks solid in ten minutes. Go out into it, then.”
Martin pulled on his thick wool cloak, his fuzzy wool comforter, and his cap pulled low over his ears, and stepped deliberately out into the storm.
“My father has more sense than my mother,” he muttered to himself as the wind bit his cheeks. “How women fumble things! They hammer away at what they think is stone, driving their nails right into the soft flesh of the person they’re trying to move.”
He reached church long before the service started. If the storm scared her off, or if that Mrs. Pryor forbade her from leaving the house, and he missed her after all, he’d be furious— but tempest or tornado, hail or ice, she ought to come. If she had half the spark her face promised, she would come. She’d be here for the chance to see him, just as he was here for the chance to see her. She’d want a word about that confounded sweetheart of hers, just as he wanted another taste of that sharp, bright spark he’d come to think was the very essence of real life: adventure, the thing that makes stagnant, boring everyday life feel like champagne next to flat porter.
He glanced around the cold, silent, empty church. Only one old woman sat in the free seats. As the chimes faded and the single bell tolled slow, other elderly parishioners trickled in one by one, taking their humble spots on the bare oak benches. It is always the frailest, oldest, poorest souls who brave the worst weather, to prove their devotion to the old stone mother church they love. Not one affluent family turned up that wild morning, not one carriage clattered up the path: all the lined, cushioned, well-kept pews were empty. Only the gray-haired elders and feeble paupers sat ranged along the bare wooden seats.
“If she doesn’t come, I’ll scorn her,” Martin muttered under his breath, sharp and bitter.
The rector’s shovel hat passed the porch. Mr. Helstone and his clerk vanished into the vestry. The bells stopped. The rector took his place at the desk. The doors shut. The service began.
The rectory pew stood empty. She was not there.
Martin scorned her. Worthless thing! Vapid thing! Commonplace humbug! Just like all other girls— weak, selfish, shallow! That was his whole litany of silent, angry insults. She was nothing like the ideal he’d built up in his head. Her eyes weren’t large and expressive, her nose wasn’t straight and delicate and Hellenic, her mouth didn’t have that soft charm he’d imagined could chase his worst sullen moods away. What was she, anyway? A paper doll, a trivial toy, a girl, nothing more.
He was so caught up in his angry thoughts he forgot to stand up from his knees when the rest of the congregation did, and he was still kneeling in what looked like exemplary devotion when the litany ended and the first hymn was announced. He jumped up bright red, as sensitive to ridicule as any young girl, embarrassed to be caught daydreaming. To make matters worse, the church door creaked open, and the aisles began to fill: patter, patter, patter, a hundred little feet trotted in. It was the Sunday scholars. Per Briarfield’s winter custom, the children had been kept by the warm stove in the parish hall until that point, only led into church right before the communion and sermon. The tiny ones were settled first, the boys and younger girls arranged after them— and just as the organ swelled loud and the choir and congregation stood to sing, a tall class of young women filed quietly in at the back of the procession. Their teacher saw them seated, then slipped into the rectory pew.
Martin knew that soft French-gray cloak and neat little beaver bonnet instantly, the exact costume his eyes had ached to catch. Miss Helstone had not let the storm stop her. She had come after all. Martin hid his face behind his hymn book for two whole minutes, to hide the stupid giddy grin spreading over his face.
Satisfied or not, he had time to get very cross with her again before the sermon ended. She never once looked his way— at least he never caught her glancing at him. “If she takes no notice of me, if she acts like I’m not even on her mind, I’ll think worse of her than ever,” he fumed to himself. “It would be the most pathetic thing in the world if she only came here to look after those sheep-faced Sunday school children, and not for my sake, or that long lanky skeleton Moore’s.”
The sermon ended. The benediction was pronounced. The congregation spilled out of the church. She never came near him.
As Martin turned his face for home, he realized the sleet was stinging sharp, and the east wind was bitter enough to cut through his cloak. His shortcut lay through untrodden fields, dangerous in the deep snow, but he didn’t care. He would take it anyway.
Near the second stile stood a clump of bare winter trees. Was that an umbrella waiting there? Yes, an umbrella, held with clear effort against the blast, a French-gray cloak flapping behind it. Martin grinned to himself as he trudged up the steep, snow-drifted slope, as hard to climb as the side of a mountain. There was a glint of unmistakeable mischief in his face when he reached the stile, sat down coolly right on top of it, and opened a conversation he was perfectly willing to drag out for hours.
“I think we ought to strike a bargain. Exchange me for Mrs. Pryor.”
“I wasn’t sure you’d come this way, Martin,” Caroline said, laughing a little, “but I decided to take the chance. There’s no way to get a quiet word in church or the churchyard, what with everyone milling about.”
“Will you agree? Give Mrs. Pryor over to my mother, and tuck me into her skirts instead?”
“As if I could understand you! What on earth put Mrs. Pryor into your head?”
“You call her ‘mamma,’ don’t you?”
“She is my mamma.”
“Not possible— no real mother would be so inefficient, so careless as to let her daughter traipse out in weather like this. I’d be a far better guardian for you, five times over. You can laugh all you like. I don’t mind seeing you laugh. I hate ugly teeth, you know— but yours are as pretty as a pearl necklace, all fair, even, perfectly matched.”
“Martin! The Yorkes never pay compliments, I thought?”
“They never did until this generation. I’m fated to be a new variety of Yorke, I think. I’m tired of all our boring old ancestors. We’ve got family traditions going back four whole ages: tales of Hiram son of Hiram son of Samuel son of John son of Zerubbabel Yorke. Every single one of them, from Zerubbabel down to the last Hiram, was exactly like my father. But before that, there was a Godfrey Yorke. We’ve got his portrait, hanging in Moore’s bedroom. He looks just like me. We don’t know a thing about his character, but I know it was different from all the rest of them. He’s got long dark curling hair, he dresses like a cavalier. And since I said he looks like me, I don’t need to add he’s handsome.”
“You’re not handsome, Martin.”
“No, not yet. But give me a little time. I’m going to start cultivating and polishing myself from this very day, and we’ll see.”
“You’re a very strange, unaccountable boy, Martin. But don’t go imagining you’ll ever be handsome. You won’t.”
“I’m going to try. But we were talking about Mrs. Pryor. She’s the most unnatural mother alive, to calmly let her daughter go out in this storm. My mother was so furious when I said I was going to church, she was fit to hurl the kitchen brush at my back.”
“Mamma was very worried about me. But I’m afraid I was obstinate. I would go.”
“To see me?”
“Exactly. I could think of nothing else. I was so afraid the snow would stop you from coming. You have no idea how pleased I was to spot you sitting all alone in your pew.”
“I only came to do my duty, set the whole parish a good example,” he said, feigning primness. “So you were obstinate, were you? I’d love to see you properly obstinate. Wouldn’t I keep you in good order if you were mine? Let me take that umbrella for you, you’re struggling with it.”
“I can’t stay two minutes. Our dinner will be ready any second.”
“So will ours. We always have a hot dinner on Sundays. Roast goose today, with apple pie and rice pudding. I always manage to know exactly what’s on the menu. I love all those things enormously, but I’ll make the sacrifice to stay out here in the cold as long as you want, if you will.”
“We have a cold dinner. My uncle won’t allow any unnecessary cooking on the Sabbath. I have to go back. The whole house will be in an uproar if I don’t turn up soon.”
“So will Briarmains, believe you me! I can almost hear my father sending the overlooker and five of the dyers out to search in six directions for his prodigal son’s frozen body in the snow, and my mother repenting every little petty thing she ever did to me, now she thinks I’m lost.”
“Martin— how is Mr. Moore?”
“That’s what you came out here for, isn’t it? Just to say that one word.”
“Go on, tell me quickly.”
“Hang him. He’s no worse, but he’s still being treated abominably— locked up, kept in solitary confinement. They’re going to make an idiot or a maniac out of him at this rate, then get a lunacy commission signed to take over all his property. Horsfall is starving him, you saw how thin he was.”
“You were so good to him the other day, Martin.”
“What other day? I’m always good. A perfect model, that’s me.”
“When will you be good again?”
“I see exactly what you’re after. But you won’t wheedle me. I’m no one’s cat’s-paw.”
“But it has to be done. It’s the right thing, and the necessary thing.”
“How pushy you are! Remember, I did that favour entirely of my own free will last time.”
“And you will again.”
“I won’t. That whole business was far too much trouble. I like my comfort.”
“Mr. Moore wishes to see me, Martin, and I wish to see him.”
“I don’t doubt it,” he said, cool as ever. “It’s terrible of his own mother to exclude all his friends.”
“Go tell her that, then.”
“His own relations.”
“Go confront her about it.”
“You know that won’t help a single thing. Fine, I’m sticking to my point. I will see him. If you won’t help me, I’ll manage it all on my own.”
“Go on then. Nothing beats self-reliance, self-dependence.”
“I don’t have time to argue with you now. You’re being so provoking. Good morning.”
She hurried away, closing her umbrella tight because she couldn’t hold it against the tearing wind.
“She’s not vapid. She’s not shallow,” Martin murmured to himself, watching her go. “I can’t wait to sit back and watch her work her way through this, all on her own. If the storm was fire instead of snow, the kind that rained down on the cities of the plain, she’d walk straight through it just to get five minutes of speech with that Moore. All the little disappointments and stupid angry fits I went through this morning just made that short little chat at the end taste all the sweeter. She thought she’d coax me into helping her right away. She won’t manage that in one go. She’ll come back again, and again, and yet again. I’d love to push her far enough to make her angry, make her cry. I want to see how far she’ll go— what she’ll do, what she’ll dare, to get what she wants. It’s so strange and new, to see one human being care that much about another. But it’s time to head home. My stomach’s telling me what the hour is. Won’t I tuck into that roast goose? And we’ll see who gets the biggest slice of apple pie, Matthew or me.”
