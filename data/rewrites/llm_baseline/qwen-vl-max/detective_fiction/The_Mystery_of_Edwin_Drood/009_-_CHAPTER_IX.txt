Rosa, orphaned and alone in the world, had lived at the Nuns’ House since she was seven, raised by Miss Twinkleton as a mother figure. Her memory of her own mother was faint but vivid—a young woman, no older than Rosa herself seemed to recall, brought home dead in her father’s arms after drowning during a pleasure party. Every detail clung to Rosa’s mind: the summer dress soaked with water, the long hair still tangled with fallen petals, the quiet sorrow of her father’s face as he grieved. He died a year later, broken by grief.

It was in that time of sorrow that Rosa’s betrothal to Edwin Drood began—arranged by Drood’s close friend and college companion, who had also lost his wife early. But fate moved on. The man who had comforted Rosa’s father passed away too, and so the two young people found themselves bound together by promise, not choice.

From the moment Rosa arrived in Cloisterham, she carried with her an aura of pity and tenderness. It never left her, though it changed colors over time—now golden, now soft pink, now clear blue—as she grew from child to young woman. She was treated like a younger sister, then a cherished daughter, even when she was no longer a child. There were quiet rivalries among the girls at the Nuns’ House—who would be her favorite, who would give her gifts, who would write most often, who she would greet with joy upon return. Beneath the surface, such feelings stirred gently, sometimes sharply. And yet, for all its sweetness, there may have been deeper currents beneath the veils and prayers of the Nuns’ House.

So Rosa became what she was: kind, impulsive, wilful, beloved—but never truly unkind. She gave affection freely, like a spring that never ran dry, enriching the lives around her. Yet deep within her, something remained untouched. What might happen when that well was finally stirred? What changes might come to her light heart and carefree mind? Only time would tell.

How the news reached the Nuns’ House before breakfast—that there had been a quarrel between the two young men, involving some kind of attack by Mr. Neville Landless on Edwin Drood—is unknown. Perhaps it came on the wind, or through the birds outside the open windows. Maybe it was kneaded into the bread by the baker, or delivered in the milk. Or perhaps the housemaids, beating their mats against the gateposts, unknowingly picked up rumors left behind by the town itself. Whatever the source, the story spread through every corner of the building before Miss Twinkleton descended.

And it reached her through Mrs. Tisher, while she was still dressing—what she might have called “sacrificing to the Graces,” if she had been inclined toward mythological speech.

Miss Landless’s brother had thrown a bottle at Mr. Edwin Drood.

Then came the next version: he had thrown a knife.

That led to speculation—perhaps a fork?

And soon, the rumor escalated: bottle, knife, fork, and decanter—all hurled at once, without warning or reason.

As Peter Piper was said to have picked a peck of pickled pepper, so too did the people of Cloisterham demand proof of the actual weapon used. But more importantly, they wanted to know *why*.

The truth, as it turned out, was simpler. Miss Landless’s brother had declared his admiration for Miss Bud. Edwin Drood had told him he had no right to feel that way. In response, the young man had “up’d” (as the cook put it) with everything nearby—bottle, knife, fork, and decanter—and flung them at Edwin.

Poor Rosa covered her ears with both hands when the whispers began, retreating to a quiet corner, begging not to hear more. But Helena Landless, seeking permission from Miss Twinkleton to speak with her brother, went directly to Mr. Crisparkle for facts.

When she returned—first speaking privately with Miss Twinkleton to filter any troubling details—she shared only what was necessary with Rosa. She emphasized the insult Edwin had given her brother, focusing on the final act as the worst offense, though she glossed over the fact that the quarrel had begun because Edwin took things far too lightly. Out of kindness, she avoided mentioning that her lover had been careless in his behavior.

She delivered a message from her brother: please forgive him. With solemn sincerity, she ended the matter there.

But Miss Twinkleton had a duty to restore calm. Stepping into the room—what others might call the schoolroom, but which she referred to with dignity as “the apartment allotted to study”—she stood tall and said, “Ladies!” All rose.

Mrs. Tisher positioned herself behind her, like Queen Elizabeth’s loyal companion at Tilbury.

“Rumour,” Miss Twinkleton began, “has been portrayed by the bard of Avon—needless to say, the immortal Shakespeare, known as the Swan of his native river—possibly because of the old belief that swans sing beautifully before death, though we have no ornithological proof—Rumour has been shown as a creature full of tongues.”

She paused, adjusting her voice. “In Cloisterham, Rumour is no different. A minor disagreement between two young men last night—within a hundred miles of these peaceful walls—has been wildly exaggerated. In our concern for a dear young friend, linked to one of the participants, we allowed ourselves to dwell on this unpleasant topic. But inquiries confirm it was nothing more than an ‘airy nothing,’ as the poet once wrote. We will now set it aside and focus on our duties.”

Yet the topic lingered. That very day, Miss Ferdinand secretly wore a paper mustache at dinner and pretended to throw a water bottle at Miss Giggles, who defended herself with a spoon.

Rosa thought about the quarrel often. She felt caught in it—whether as cause or consequence—because of her engagement. Even when near Edwin, she carried unease; apart from him, it grew heavier. Today, she was alone, unable to talk freely with Helena, whose brother had been involved, and who now avoided the subject.

Just then, Mr. Grewgious arrived.

He was a man chosen for his integrity, not charm. Dry as dust, he looked as if he could be ground into snuff. His thin, yellowish hair resembled a worn fur collar—so unlike real hair, it must have been a wig, except no one could imagine choosing such a head. His face had few expressions, carved deeply into hard lines, like stone. Notches in his forehead suggested Nature had begun to shape him, then abandoned him mid-sculpture, saying, “I can’t be bothered finishing this.”

Tall in the neck, wide in the ankles, awkward in movement, and nearsighted—so much so he didn’t seem to notice how much white stocking showed against his black suit—Mr. Grewgious was clumsy, hesitant, and odd. Yet somehow, despite all this, he made a strange impression of decency.

He found Rosa in Miss Twinkleton’s private room, clearly uncomfortable. He appeared discomposed, as if expecting an examination—and fearing failure.

“My dear, how do you do? I’m glad to see you. You look so much better.”

Miss Twinkleton rose from her desk. “May I retire?”

“Not at all, madam. Please stay.”

“I must insist on moving,” she replied, with gentle firmness. “But I’ll remain if you wish. Shall I wheel my desk to the window?”

“Madam! In the way?”

“You are very kind,” she said. “Rosa, you’re free to speak as you please.”

Left alone with Rosa, Mr. Grewgious repeated, “My dear, how do you do?” Then sat down after she did.

“My visits,” he said, “are like those of angels—not that I compare myself to an angel.”

“No, sir,” Rosa replied.

“Not at all,” he agreed. “Only that my visits are rare. The angels, we know, are upstairs.”

Miss Twinkleton glanced over with a stiff stare.

“I mean,” he continued, placing a hand on Rosa’s as if to steady himself, “the other young ladies.”

She resumed writing.

Mr. Grewgious, realizing he hadn’t handled the opening well, smoothed his head as if pressing water from a dive—an unconscious habit. He pulled out a pocketbook and a stub of pencil.

“I’ve made notes,” he said, flipping pages. “‘Well and happy.’ Truly. Are you well and happy, my dear?”

“Yes, indeed, sir.”

“For which,” he said, nodding toward the window, “we owe warm thanks to the kindness and care of the lady here.”

Again, the sentence faltered. Miss Twinkleton chewed her pen, staring upward, waiting for inspiration from heaven.

He smoothed his head again, then consulted his notes, crossing out “well and happy.”

“‘Pounds, shillings, and pence.’ A dry subject, but important. Life is pounds, shillings, and pence. Death is—” He paused. A memory of her parents’ deaths stopped him. “Death is not pounds, shillings, and pence.”

His voice was flat, dry—like his body. Yet through his limited expression, a hint of kindness surfaced. If Nature had finished him, it might have been visible. But with his forehead unformed and his face rigid, what could he do?

“Pounds, shillings, and pence. Is your allowance sufficient?”

Yes, Rosa answered. She needed nothing.

“And you’re not in debt?”

She laughed. Debt seemed absurd to her.

Mr. Grewgious strained his eyes to confirm her meaning. “Ah!” he murmured, glancing at Miss Twinkleton. “I spoke of angels! I did!”

Rosa knew what came next. She blushed, fidgeting with her dress.

“‘Marriage.’ Hem.” He smoothed his face again, leaned forward slightly. “This is why I’ve come. Otherwise, being particularly angular, I wouldn’t intrude. I feel like a bear—with the cramp—in a Cotillon.”

Rosa laughed.

“It strikes you the same,” he said calmly. “Exactly. Now, Mr. Edwin has visited as planned. You’ve mentioned it in your letters. You like him, and he likes you.”

“I like him very much, sir.”

“So I said,” he replied, missing her emphasis. “Good. You correspond.”

“We write to each other,” she said, pouting slightly, recalling their letter-writing differences.

“That’s what I mean,” he said. “Good. Time passes. At Christmas, we must formally inform Miss Twinkleton of your departure. Your relationship with her goes beyond business, but business remains. I am particularly angular. I don’t give things away. If someone competent could stand in for me, I’d be grateful.”

Rosa nodded, eyes down.

“Certainly,” he said. “The dancing master, for instance—he knows how to conduct himself. He’d move properly, satisfying everyone. I—I am particularly angular. I’d only stumble.”

She remained silent. Perhaps her thoughts hadn’t reached the ceremony yet.

“Memorandum: ‘Will.’ Now, my dear,” he said, pulling out a paper. “Though I’ve already told you of your father’s will, I think it wise to leave a certified copy with you. And I’ll also give one to Mr. Jasper—”

“Not to him!” Rosa interrupted. “Can’t it go to Eddy himself?”

“Well, yes, if you prefer. But Mr. Jasper is his trustee.”

“I do prefer it,” she said quickly. “I don’t want Mr. Jasper between us.”

“Natural,” he said. “You want your future husband to be everything. Yes. Though I suppose—I am a particularly unnatural man. I don’t know from personal experience.”

She looked at him in surprise.

“I mean,” he explained, “I was born late. My parents were old. I suspect I was born advanced in life. While others begin as buds, I began as a chip—a very dry one.”

He paused. “Regarding the copy, your wish shall be honored. As for your inheritance: an annuity of two hundred fifty pounds. Savings and other assets total just over seventeen hundred pounds. I can advance funds for your wedding preparations. All is known.”

“May I ask,” Rosa said, holding the document but not opening it, “if I’m right? I understand you better than legal documents. My father and Edwin’s father made a pact—not binding, but heartfelt—so that we might grow into the same kind of friendship they had?”

“Exactly.”

“For our lasting good and happiness?”

“Precisely.”

“To be even closer than they were?”

“Just so.”

“It wasn’t enforced. No penalty if—”

“Don’t distress yourself. If you didn’t marry, no forfeiture. You’d remain my ward until twenty-one. Nothing worse.”

“And Edwin?”

“He’d inherit his father’s share at majority, just as now.”

Rosa bit the edge of the paper, head tilted, staring at the floor, smoothing it with her foot.

“In short,” Mr. Grewgious said, “this betrothal is a wish, a feeling, a plan. It was sincere. But marriage must rest on free will, mutual love, and certainty—however mistaken that certainty may be. Would your fathers, if alive, still approve if circumstances changed? Impossible to expect otherwise.”

He spoke as if reading aloud, emotionless.

“I’ve fulfilled my duty,” he added, blotting out “Will.” “Now, memorandum: ‘Wishes.’ Is there anything I can help you with?”

Rosa shook her head, hesitating.

“Any instructions for your affairs?”

“I’d like to settle them with Eddy first,” she said, twisting the crease in her dress.

“Of course,” he said. “You should be united in purpose. Will he return soon?”

“He left this morning. Back at Christmas.”

“Perfect. You’ll arrange matters then, inform me, and I’ll discharge my responsibilities—merely as a business associate—to the accomplished lady in the corner window. They’ll accrue then.” He blurred the word “Leave” with his pencil.

“Now, I’ll take my leave.”

“Could I,” Rosa asked, rising as he struggled to stand, “ask you to visit me at Christmas, if I have something important to say?”

He nodded, stiffly, and left.
