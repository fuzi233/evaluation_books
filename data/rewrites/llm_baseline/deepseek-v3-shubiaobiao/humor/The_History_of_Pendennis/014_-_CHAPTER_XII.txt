The illustrious Mr. Garbetts, Principal Tragedian—a man whose legs were the envy of the theater and whose arm could snap a poker like a breadstick—was, by all accounts, Captain Costigan’s closest confidant. Their friendship, cemented over shared whisky and unpaid bills at the Magpie Hotel, was of the sort where signatures were borrowed more often than shillings. Mrs. Garbetts, however, viewed Miss Fotheringay with the same warmth one reserves for a tax collector, owing to her husband’s earlier, unsuccessful courtship of the fair Emily. But that’s another story, and this one has enough drama already.

Summoned posthaste by the Captain—who was in a state of agitation best described as "half-tragic, half-spirits"—Garbetts arrived to find Costigan brandishing dueling pistols older than Dublin itself.

“Run, Tommy,” the Captain had barked earlier, dispatching a small boy to fetch both Garbetts and two hot whiskies (priorities firmly in order). Now, with liquid courage bolstering his resolve, Costigan composed a letter to Major Pendennis. It was a masterpiece of threats and flourishes, demanding the Major cease all interference in young Arthur’s nuptial ambitions—or face consequences involving horsewhips and honor. The letter was sealed with the Costigan crest (the only surviving relic of family grandeur) and entrusted to Garbetts for delivery.

Alas, Garbetts returned looking as cheerful as a man who’d just spotted his own ghost.

“Ye saw ’im?” growled Costigan, leveling a pistol at an imaginary foe (and narrowly missing a vase).

“I saw *someone*,” Garbetts admitted. “But not the Major. Tatham was there.”

The name alone was enough to make Costigan sputter into his drink. Tatham! The very man who’d once sworn to have him arrested over a trifling matter of debt. Garbetts, a practical soul with a family to feed, refused to be entangled in duels, pistols, or any activity that might shorten his theatrical career. The meeting dissolved in mutual disgust, leaving Costigan to brood over his wrongs and his whisky.

Meanwhile, Miss Fotheringay returned from rehearsal, trailing the ever-faithful Mr. Bows. She found her father mid-tirade, the air thick with the scent of spirits and melodrama.

“Prepare yourself, me child!” Costigan wailed, flinging his arms wide.

“Ye’re drunk before dinner again,” Emily observed, sidestepping his embrace with the ease of a woman well-practiced in dodging paternal theatrics.

“It’s me sorrows, darlin’!” he protested. “That villainous Major has revealed Arthur’s a pauper! A pauper, I tell ye!”

Emily, unperturbed, set about polishing a pair of satin slippers with bread crumbs (a thrifty trick for Ophelia’s costume). “If he’s got no money, there’s no use marrying him,” she remarked, as though discussing the weather.

Costigan spluttered. Bows smirked. The Captain’s dreams of carriages and grandeur crumbled like stale cake.

“He’s a good lad,” Emily conceded, rubbing at a stubborn scuff. “But what’s the use of a husband who can’t keep ye in satin shoes?”

Bows, ever the cynic, suggested she marry him anyway—for love, if not luxury. Emily, practical to her core, merely laughed. “There’s others as likes me, Bows. Older. Poorer. Fools, the lot of ’em.”

And so, with the matter settled, she bundled up Pen’s love letters—pages of fevered verse and boyish devotion—into a neat parcel, tied with string, as casually as if they were a grocer’s order.

Somewhere, ten miles away, Pen’s heart was breaking.

Emily, untroubled, called for dinner. The beef-steak pudding, at least, was dependable.
