In the years when we called ourselves the outlying rooms of Great Britain, we had a homely, half-ashamed use for America besides its tobacco and its timber: we made it a larder for our refuse, a distant shelf on which to set the jars that had burst in the heat of London. Convicts in irons, indentured men with their names sold like sacks of meal, younger sons whose debts and bawling creditors made England too tight a kitchen for them—away they went, shipped like casks, to roll and clatter on strange wharves and be broached among other people’s plantations.

We were much like those voyagers who, discovering new shores, brought over cats and pigs and English weeds, and called it civilisation; only we were apt to send, with equal care, our black sheep, to graze and breed where they might do least noise at home. I confess my own hand had been in that provisioning. We could find no fit employment in England for the worthy Hagan—husband to my kinswoman Maria—and so we were glad enough to pack him off to Virginia, and give him a colonial pulpit-cushion to thump.

He behaved there, to do him justice, as a brave honest gentleman. He did his duty thoroughly by his congregation, and to his King too; and so far my small patronage bore a better savour than I deserved. Madam Theo would always press this upon me whenever I owned my prick of conscience: that my conduct in this, as in all matters, had been guided by the highest principle of morality and honour. She could salt and preserve any action of mine till it seemed fit for a prince’s table.

Yet if we had had a living at home—if I could have chosen who should stand at our parish board, speak from our desk, pray over our bread—would I have given it to Hagan and selected him and his wife to minister among us? I fear not. I never doubted Maria’s sincere repentance; but I think, in the secret pantry of my pride, I was relieved to have her work it out in the wilderness. I say it now, owning my fault, and the stale taste it leaves on the tongue.

Twice, when I needed it most, this same Maria—whom we had sent away like a blemished vessel—helped me with her sympathy and friendship. She bore her own distresses courageously, and, with a tenderness that asked no payment, laid her hand upon other people’s griefs as one lays a cloth over rising dough. And yet I, and some of mine (not Theo), would look down upon her. For shame—shame on that pride, which can sit to a full table and still find bitterness.

Nor was my poor Lady Maria the only one of our blood whom the family was content to have out of sight among American forests and rivers. Having borrowed, stolen, cheated at home until he could cheat, borrow, and steal no more, the Honourable William Esmond, Esquire, was accommodated with a place at New York; and his noble brother and royal master heartily desired that they might see him no more.

When troubles began to thicken—first as talk, then as meetings, then as hard resolves—we heard of the fellow again, and of his doings in his new habitation. Lies and mischief went before him like advance-servants banging on doors. My Lord Dunmore told me that Mr. Will declared publicly our estate of Castlewood was only ours during his brother’s pleasure; that his father, out of consideration for Madam Esmond—his lordship’s half-sister—had given her the place for life; and that he, William, was actually in negotiation with his brother, the present Lord Castlewood, for the purchase of the reversion.

We had the deed of gift in our strongroom at Castlewood, and it was registered in due form at Williamsburg; so we were easy as to law. But intention is everything; and Hal and I promised each other that, as soon as we met Mr. William, we would wring from him a confirmation of this pretty story, if only to show the colour of his impudence in his own words.

What Madam Esmond’s feelings were, I need not particularise, for they were of a kind that made themselves heard through walls. “What! my father, the Marquis of Esmond, was a liar, and I am a cheat, am I?” cries my mother. “He will take my son’s property at my death, will he?” And she was for writing, not only to Lord Castlewood in England, but to his Majesty himself at St. James’s; and was only prevented by my assurance that Mr. Will’s lies were notorious among all his acquaintance, and that we could not expect, in our own case, that he should be so inconsistent as to tell the truth.

Presently we heard of him as one of the loudest Loyalists in New York—Captain, and then Major, of a corps of volunteers sending their addresses to the well-disposed in all the other colonies, and announcing their perfect readiness to die for the mother country. There was much fine phrasing in those papers, like froth whipped up to look substantial. Meanwhile, our own life in Virginia went on with the stubborn, daily precision of housekeeping: fires to be lit, meat to be salted, corn to be ground, children to be kept from mischief, and men to be kept from drinking themselves into speeches.

Yet the sound of the country altered, as weather alters. In Williamsburg, even the air seemed full of lids slamming—doors of taverns, shutters of meeting-rooms, the sharp crack of argument. We could not lie in a house without a whole window; and shutting the shutters of that unlucky mansion we had hired, Madam Esmond left our little capital, and my family returned to Richmond, which also was deserted by the members of the dissolved Assembly.

Captain Hal and his wife went back early to their plantation. I, not a little annoyed at the turn events were taking, divided my time between my own family and that of our Governor, who professed himself eager to have my advice and company. We differed strongly in politics, but as yet there was no personal breach. Even after the dissolution of our House of Assembly—the members adjourning to a tavern, and there holding that famous meeting where, I believe, the idea of a congress of all the colonies was first proposed—the gentlemen most resolute in opposition remained on good terms with his Excellency, ate at his board, drank his wine, and rode with him on excursions of pleasure. The world had not yet broken into open war; it was still a household that quarrelled at table, then called for a fresh dish.

The session over, the gentry went home, and meetings sprang up in their respective counties as naturally as bread rises in warm pans. Other provincial Assemblies were abruptly dissolved; and it was agreed everywhere a general congress should be held. Philadelphia, as the largest and most important city on our continent, was selected; and those celebrated conferences began—the angry preface of war.

We were still at God save the King; still sending humble petitions to the throne; still talking of rights in the language of loyalty. But words, like old recipes, change their flavour in new mouths. I remember that while this great argument was gathering like thunder beyond the Blue Ridge, I went to visit my brother Harry at Fanny’s Mount. His new plantation lay not far from ours, with the Rappahannock between, and towards the Mattaponey.

One morning he rode out on business; in the afternoon I too took horse. The day was clear, and the country had that particular hush which makes every small noise distinct: the creak of saddle-leather, the click of a bit, the long insect-hum from the grass, and, far off, the faint chopping rhythm of an axe. A groom told me master was gone towards Willis’s Ordinary. Thinking no harm, I followed.

As I came out of the wood into a clear place not far from Willis’s, the soundscape changed at once. Instead of birds and leaves, there were men’s voices, abrupt and uneven, and the harder noises of metal and wood: a ramrod clink, the thud of feet on trampled earth, the scrape of a pike being shifted in clumsy hands. There was Captain Hal on horseback, with three- or four-and-thirty countrymen about him, armed with every sort of weapon—pike, scythe, fowling-piece, musket—and Hal, with two or three likely young fellows as officers under him, putting the men through their exercise.

It was a strange sight in that open Virginian field, as if the ordinary business of planting had turned itself inside out and shown its iron bones. The scythes that should have whispered through hay were held up like lances; the same hands that kneaded maize dough and split rails now fumbled at muskets; and the air, which commonly carried the domestic music of a plantation—pestle in mortar, a kettle’s lid, a distant fiddle on a Saturday—was cut through by rough commands and the awkward chorus of obedience.

When I rode up, a queer expression passed over Hal’s face, as if I had stepped unbidden into his kitchen while he was preparing a secret dish.

“Present arms!” says he; and the little army tried to perform the salute as best they could, their motions not yet one sound, but many sounds at once—iron against iron, breath against breath. “Captain Cade, this is my brother, Sir George Warrington.”

“As a relation of yours, Colonel,” says the man addressed as captain—an individual with a weather-browned face and a grip like a vise—“the gentleman is welcome,” and he held out a hand accordingly.

“And—and a true friend to Virginia,” says Hal, reddening as he spoke it, as though he must force the words down like a strong dram.

“Yes, please God! gentlemen,” said I; whereon the regiment gave a hearty huzzay for the Colonel and his brother. Their shout rolled away over the field and was swallowed by the woods, leaving behind it a moment’s ringing emptiness, as after a church-bell.

The drill over, the officers—and the men too—were for adjourning to Willis’s and taking refreshment. One could already imagine the ordinary’s thick air, the smell of beer and tobacco, the scrape of chairs, the rising talk: how easily a day’s work of war could be folded into the old habit of eating and drinking together. But Colonel Hal said he could not drink with them that afternoon; and we trotted homewards together.

“So, Hal, the cat’s out of the bag!” said I.

He gave me a hard look. “I guess there’s wilder cats in it. It must come to this, George.” Then, lowering his voice as if the very trees might carry tales, he added, “I say, you mustn’t tell Madam.”

“Good God!” I cried. “Do you mean that with fellows such as those I saw yonder, you and your friends are going to make fight against the greatest nation and the best army in the world?”

“I guess we shall get an awful whipping,” says Hal, “and that’s the fact. But then, George,” he added, and his sweet kind smile came, as it always did when his heart was settled, “we are young, and a whipping or two may do us good. Won’t it do us good, Dolly, you old slut?” And he gave a playful touch with his whip to an old dog of all trades running by him, who accepted the correction as if it were part of the day’s sport.

I did not try to urge upon him—though I had done so in vain many times before—our British side of the question, the side which still seemed to me the best. He was accustomed to put off my reasons by saying, “All mighty well, brother: you speak as an Englishman, and have cast in your lot with your country, as I have with mine.”

To this there is no answer. When men reach that point, argument becomes only another kind of noise, and the matter must be fought out; and the strongest then calls himself right. Which had the right in the wars of the last century—the King or the Parliament? The side that was uppermost was the right, and on the whole more humane in victory than the Cavaliers would have been had they won. Nay, suppose we Tories had carried the day in America—how frightful and bloody that triumph might have been! One imagines ropes and scaffolds, noble heads laid low, the harsh music of drums outside prison doors.

A strange feeling, I own: I was on the Loyalist side, and yet wanted the Whigs to win. My brother Hal, on the other hand—who afterwards distinguished himself greatly with his regiment—never allowed a word of disrespect against the enemy whom he opposed.

“The officers of the British army,” he used to say, “are gentlemen—at least, I have not heard they are much changed since my time. There may be scoundrels and ruffians among the enemy’s troops; I dare say we could find some such among our own. Our business is to beat his Majesty’s forces, not call them names. Any rascal can do that.”

And from a name Mr. Lee gave my brother, which many of his rough horsemen did not understand, Harry was often called “Chevaleer Baird” in the Continental army. He was a knight indeed, without fear and without reproach.

As for my argument—What can such people as those you were drilling do against the British army?—Hal had his confident answer ready, spoken as plainly as if he were naming the ingredients of a dish.

“They can beat them,” says he. “Mr. George—that’s what they can do.”

“Great heavens!” I cried. “Do you mean, with your company of Wolfe’s, you would hesitate to attack five hundred such?”

“With my company of the 67th, I would go anywhere. And agreed with you, that at this present moment I know more of soldiering than they.” He turned in the saddle, and his voice took that practical tone he had when speaking of ground, distance, and cover. “But place me on that open ground where you found us, armed as you please, and half a dozen of my friends, with rifles, in the woods round about me—which would get the better? You know best, Mr. Braddock’s aide-de-camp!”

There was no arguing with such determination. “Thou knowest my way of thinking, Hal,” I said. “And having surprised you at your work, I must tell my lord what I have seen.”

“Tell him, of course. You have seen our county militia exercising. You will see as much in every colony from here to the Saint Lawrence or Georgia. As I am an old soldier, they have elected me colonel. What more natural? Come, brother—let us trot on. Dinner will be ready, and Mrs. Fan does not like me to keep it waiting.”

So we made for his house, which stood open, like all the houses of our Virginian gentlemen, where not only every friend and neighbour, but every stranger and traveller, was sure to find welcome: the very architecture of hospitality, doors wide as if the country still trusted its own peace.

Within, the day’s ordinary comfort asserted itself. One smelt the kitchen before one saw it: woodsmoke clinging sweetly to the air, the fatty promise of meat kept warm, the faint sourness of yeast at work, and that clean, sharp scent of herbs bruised under a cook’s palm. The clatter of plates and the softer, steady sounds of preparation—knife on board, spoon in pot—made a domestic music against which the earlier drilling, with its harsh clashes and shouted words, seemed both near and unbelievable, as if war were only another tune being rehearsed outside.

“So, Mrs. Fan,” said I, when we met, “I have found out what game my brother has been playing.”

“I trust the Colonel will have plenty of sport ere long,” says she, with a toss of her head—so quick, so bright, that even her defiance had the crispness of a well-fired crust.

My wife thought Harry had been hunting, and I did not care to undeceive her. The table was laid, and the household moved in its practised order, as though the world’s fate were not being argued in taverns and congresses, as though men were not learning to shoulder arms where yesterday they had shouldered sheaves. Yet what I had seen, and what Hal had told me, sat with me through the meal, like a new word in the mouth—unfamiliar, untranslatable into the old family dialect, and impossible to spit out.
