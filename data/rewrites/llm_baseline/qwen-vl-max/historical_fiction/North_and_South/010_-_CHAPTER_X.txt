“We are the trees whom shaking fastens more.” — GEORGE HERBERT.

Mr. Thornton left his home without entering the dining-room again. He was running late and hurried toward Crampton, eager not to seem disrespectful to his new friend by any delay. The church clock struck half-past seven as he stood at the door, waiting for Dixon’s slow approach—always twice as sluggish when she had to answer the bell, a task that seemed beneath her dignity. Once inside, he was welcomed warmly by Mr. Hale, who led him to his wife. Her pale face and shawl-wrapped figure spoke silently of weariness, explaining the quiet reserve in her greeting.

Margaret was lighting the lamp when he entered, for twilight had settled in. The flame cast a gentle glow across the dim room, where the night sky remained visible through the windows—an old country habit, untroubled by modern enclosures. This space contrasted sharply with the heavy, masculine dining-room he had just left: one filled only with furniture suited to meals, dominated by his mother’s presence, and devoid of warmth or life beyond eating and drinking. Here, though, the air was softer. No mirrors gleamed, no gilded frames reflected light like water in a painting; instead, rich, earthy tones painted the walls, softened by the familiar Helstone chintz curtains and chair covers. A davenport stood open near the window opposite the door; beside it, a tall white vase held drooping ivy, pale birch, and copper-hued beech leaves. Baskets of needlework were scattered about, and books—chosen not for their bindings but for their contents—lay on a table as if recently set down. Near the door, a tea table was laid out with care: a crisp white cloth, cocoa-nut cakes, and a basket piled high with oranges and bright red apples resting on green leaves.

To Mr. Thornton, these details spoke of a household shaped by thoughtful habits—especially those of Margaret. She stood by the tea table in a muslin dress, touched here and there with pink, her attention fixed on the cups rather than the conversation. Her hands, smooth and ivory-white, moved quietly among the china, delicate and precise. On one slender arm, a bracelet slipped down over her wrist. Mr. Thornton watched its movement—not the words being spoken, but how she pushed it up impatiently until it tightened against her skin, then how it slowly fell again. It fascinated him. He almost whispered, “There it goes again,” so clearly did he see the rhythm of it.

Once tea was prepared, there was little left to do. He found himself wishing the ritual could last longer, so he might watch her more closely. When she handed him his cup, her expression was proud, almost defiant—a slave unwilling to serve, yet dutiful. But her eyes caught the moment he needed another refill, and he felt a sudden urge to ask her to do for him what she did for her father: the man took her tiny fingers gently in his large hand and used them like sugar tongs. Margaret’s eyes lifted to her father’s, full of laughter and affection, unaware they were being watched.

She still ached from her illness—her pallor and silence made it plain—but she refused to let the evening falter. If there was a pause, she would step forward, even if only to pour tea, so that no one would think her father’s guest neglected. Yet conversation flowed steadily, and once the tea things were cleared away, she retreated to a corner near her mother, picking up her work. Now she could let her mind wander freely, safe from sudden summons.

Mr. Thornton and Mr. Hale were deep in discussion, continuing a topic begun at their last meeting. Margaret was drawn back into the present by a low remark from her mother. Looking up from her embroidery, she noticed the striking contrast between the two men—their appearances revealing such different natures.

Her father was slight, which made him seem taller than he truly was, especially beside Mr. Thornton’s broad, powerful frame. His features were soft, lines wavering like ripples on water, showing every shift of feeling. His eyelids were long and arched, lending his gaze a dreamy, almost feminine beauty. His brows were finely curved, raised far above his eyes by the size of the lids themselves.

Mr. Thornton, in contrast, had firm, straight brows that hovered low over deep-set, clear eyes—intent, observant, capable of seeing right through a thing. His face bore few lines, but those that existed were sharp and carved, like stone, mostly around his mouth, which was often slightly compressed. His teeth were perfect, white, and strong—so much so that when he smiled, the transformation was startling. One instant, he looked stern, resolute, ready to face any challenge; the next, a flash of honest joy lit his eyes, radiant and childlike. Margaret liked that smile—it was the first thing she had admired in this new acquaintance. And the differences in their looks seemed to explain why they so clearly respected each other.

She rearranged her mother’s knitting, then returned to her thoughts—unnoticed by Mr. Thornton, who was utterly absorbed in describing the steam-hammer. He spoke of its immense power and delicate precision, likening it to the genies of the Arabian Nights—capable of stretching from earth to sky one moment, then shrinking into a child’s palm the next.

“And this imagination, this practical realization of grand ideas,” he said, “came from one man’s mind in our town. That very man has the strength to climb higher, step by step, toward greater wonders. And I’ll wager we have many others who, if he were gone, could take his place and carry on the fight that forces all physical power to bow to science.”

“That reminds me,” said Mr. Hale, quoting, “‘I’ve a hundred captains in England,’ he said, ‘As good as ever was he.’”

Margaret looked up, startled. How had they jumped from machinery to Chevy Chace?

“It is no boast,” replied Mr. Thornton. “It’s fact. I’m proud to belong to a district whose needs give rise to such bold thinking. I’d rather struggle here—fail, suffer, even—toil under pressure, than live a dull, comfortable life in the South, where society moves slowly, weighed down by tradition and ease. One may be clogged with honey and unable to fly.”

“You’re mistaken,” said Margaret, stirred by his criticism of the South. A flush rose in her cheeks, tears pricked her eyes. “You don’t know the South. If there’s less excitement, less progress, perhaps—though I suppose I shouldn’t say that—there is also less suffering. I see men here whose faces tell of crushing sorrow, of hatred born from injustice. In the South, yes, we have poor people too, but their faces don’t carry that bitter sense of wrong. You don’t know the South, Mr. Thornton,” she concluded, collapsing into silence, ashamed of having spoken so much.

“And may I say you do not know the North?” he asked, his tone unexpectedly gentle, sensing he had wounded her.

She stayed silent, longing for the quiet hills and green fields of Hampshire, the memories so vivid they threatened to make her voice tremble.

“At least,” Mrs. Hale said, “you must admit Milton is far dirtier and smokier than any town in the South.”

“I fear I must concede its cleanliness,” Mr. Thornton replied, flashing his quick smile. “But Parliament commands us to burn our smoke. So, like good children, we shall obey—eventually.”

“But I thought you said you’d altered your chimneys to consume the smoke?” asked Mr. Hale.

“I did it before Parliament interfered. It cost money at first, but now it saves coal. I doubt I’d have done it otherwise. I’d have waited to be reported and fined—gone through all the trouble of yielding only when forced. But laws enforced by informers and fines become useless because of their ugliness. I suspect no chimney in Milton has been reported in five years, even though some still spew a third of their coal as ‘unparliamentary’ smoke.”

“I can only say it’s impossible to keep muslin blinds clean here for more than a week,” said Mrs. Hale. “At Helstone, we’ve kept ours up for a month, and they’re still fresh.”

“And your hands, Margaret—how many times did you wash them this morning before twelve?”

“Three times, mamma.”

“You seem to have a strong objection to Acts of Parliament and legislation affecting your way of managing things here,” said Mr. Hale.

“Yes, I do—and many others do too. And rightly so. The cotton trade’s machinery—no, not the iron kind, but the system—is so new that it’s no surprise it doesn’t run smoothly everywhere. Seventy years ago, what was it? And now, what is it not? Raw materials came together. Men of equal standing suddenly became masters and workers, thanks to foresight and opportunity. Some saw the future hidden in Arkwright’s crude model. That rapid growth gave early manufacturers vast power—not just over workers, but over buyers, over markets worldwide. Take, for instance, an ad from fifty years ago in a Milton paper: ‘So-and-so will close his warehouse at noon. All customers must come before.’ Imagine someone dictating *when* he would sell. Today, if a loyal customer came at midnight, I’d rise and stand hat in hand to receive his order.”

Margaret’s lip twitched, but she couldn’t look away. She was caught.

“I mention this to show the immense power manufacturers held at the start of the century. They were dizzy with it. Success in business didn’t mean balance in character. Often, justice and humility were buried under waves of wealth. Tales of wild extravagance during gala days are common. There’s no denying the tyranny they exercised over workers. As the saying goes, ‘Set a beggar on horseback, and he’ll ride to the devil.’ Some of these early factory owners rode to hell in style—crushing people under their horses’ hooves without remorse. But then came a reaction. More factories, more masters, more workers. Power balanced. Now, the battle is fairly waged between us. We won’t submit to an umpire, much less to a meddler who knows nothing of the facts—even if that meddler is called the High Court of Parliament.”

“Is it necessary to call it a battle between classes?” asked Mr. Hale. “I see from your use of the word that it reflects your true view.”

“It is true. And I believe it’s as necessary as wisdom fighting ignorance, or virtue battling improvidence. One great strength of our system is that a working man can rise to masterhood through his own effort. Anyone who controls himself, lives soberly, and does his duty can join our ranks—not always as a master, but as overseer, clerk, bookkeeper—on the side of order.”

“So you consider all who fail to rise, for whatever reason, as your enemies?” Margaret asked, her voice clear and cold.

“As their own enemies,” he answered quickly, stung by the pride in her tone. But instantly, his honesty made him feel his reply was weak. He owed her a better explanation, even if she disapproved. Yet how could he separate her interpretation from his meaning? The clearest way would be to speak of his own life—but was that too personal for strangers?

Still, truth demanded it. With a flicker of embarrassment coloring his dark cheek, he began:

“I speak from experience. Sixteen years ago, my father died in terrible circumstances. I was pulled from school and forced to grow up overnight. I had a mother unlike most—strong, determined. We moved to a small town where life was cheaper, and I found work in a draper’s shop—a fine place, actually, to learn about goods. Each week, our income was fifteen shillings. Three people lived on that. My mother managed so well that I saved three shillings every week. That was the beginning—that taught me self-denial. Now, when I can afford my mother comforts her age demands, I silently thank her for the discipline she gave me.

When I reflect that my success came not from luck, talent, or merit—but from habits that taught me to scorn indulgence unless earned—I believe the suffering Miss Hale sees in Milton’s people is simply the natural consequence of pleasure enjoyed dishonestly in earlier years. I don’t hate self-indulgent people. I despise them—for their weakness of character.”

“But you had the foundation of a good education,” said Mr. Hale. “Your enthusiasm for Homer shows you’re not reading it as a stranger. You’ve read it before.”

“That’s true—I stumbled through it at school. I was considered a decent classicist then, though I’ve lost most of it since. But I ask you—what good was that knowledge for the life I faced? None. Utterly none. On education, any man who can read and write starts level with me in useful knowledge.”

“Well, I disagree. Perhaps I’m a bit of a pedant. Did the heroism of Homeric life inspire you?”

“Not at all!” Mr. Thornton laughed. “I was too busy surviving. The living pressed close, neck to neck, in the fight for bread. Only now, with my mother safe and at peace, can I enjoy those old stories fully.”

“I suppose,” said Mr. Hale, “my comment came from the professional belief that nothing beats leather.”

When Mr. Thornton rose to leave, he extended his hand to shake with Mr. and Mrs. Hale. Then, turning to Margaret, he reached out as if to bid her farewell in the same way. It was a customary gesture in their world—but Margaret wasn’t expecting it. She merely bowed. The moment she saw his hand hesitate and pull back, she regretted not knowing his intent.

He walked away, unaware of her disappointment. Standing tall, he muttered to himself as he stepped from the house:

“A more proud, disagreeable girl I never saw. Even her beauty is blotted out by her scornful manner.”
