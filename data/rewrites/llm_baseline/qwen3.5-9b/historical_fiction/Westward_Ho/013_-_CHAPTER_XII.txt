"Three lords sat drinking late yestreen, And ere they paid the lawing, They set a combat them between, To fight it in the dawing"—so ran a Scotch Ballad.

There is no one acquainted with Bideford who does not know Bideford Bridge. It is the very navel, the focal point, and the soul around which the entire town has organized itself. Just as Edinburgh owes its identity to its castle, Rome to its Capitol, and Egypt to its pyramids, so Bideford is Bideford solely by virtue of its bridge. Yet, few realize the occult powers that have animated this wondrous structure for five centuries, elevating it to the status of the region's chief marvel, according to Prince and Fuller. It began as an inspired bridge, evolved into a soul-saving conduit, an alms-giving haven, an educational center, and a sentient entity. Finally, and most importantly for our purposes, it became a dinner-providing institution. Few understand how, during its construction half a mile upstream, invisible hands carried the stones downstream each night to their final resting place. Legend holds that Sir Richard Gurney, the local parson, once slept in profound perplexity, haunted by an evil spirit seemingly active in his sheepfold, only to behold a vision of an angel commanding him to build where the materials had been divinely transported—there alone, amidst the shifting sands, lay a sure foundation. Even fewer know how Bishop Grandison of Exeter proclaimed indulgences and eternal spiritual blessings to anyone who aided in bridging that perilous ford, urging the populace to "make the best of both worlds."

Fewer still know that, despite being founded upon wool, the bridge trembles at the slightest hoofbeat; that though it boasts twenty-three arches, one man named William Alford once carried four bushels of saltwater on his back across its entire span for a wager; or that the bridge is a veritable esquire, bearing its own coat of arms—a ship and bridge on a plain field—and owning lands and tenements in numerous parishes. With these assets, the miraculous bridge has founded charities, erected schools, fought legal battles, and, most notably, hosted annual feasts, maintaining for this purpose the finest wine cellar in all of Devon.

In the year 1583, all the notables of Bideford were invited to one such banquet, joined by Mr. St. Leger of Annery, a neighbor and brother to the Marshal of Munster and Lady Grenville. He was a man of immense worth and hospitality, so generous with his riches that he effectively shielded his descendants from the snares of greed. During the meal, a dispute arose between Mr. St. Leger and a trustee regarding the quality of a salmon caught below versus one caught above the bridge. Deciding that theory yielded to practice, Mr. St. Leger vowed to repay the bridge's generosity. He wagered five pounds that he could procure a salmon from the waters below Annery equal in firmness and flavor to the one they had just consumed. In a gesture of grandeur, he invited the entire company to dine with him at Annery three days hence, requesting that each guest bring a wife or daughter. Don Guzman, seated at the table, was naturally included.

Thus, a magnificent feast ensued in the great hall at Annery, scarcely rivaled since Judge Hankford entertained King Edward IV. As guests ate their fill and drank deeply, Rose Salterne and Don Guzman engaged in a silent dance of avoidance and observation. Rose, however, walked a tightrope; her father sat nearby, and directly opposite her were Messrs. William Cary and Arthur St. Leger, lieutenants in the Queen's Irish army currently on furlough.

For six months, Rose and the Spaniard had shared no words, though their paths had crossed frequently. Don Guzman avoided her company only within her father's house; elsewhere, he maintained a demeanor of polite indifference, offering only the stiffest salutations. Yet, whenever he appeared in her presence, he exerted himself to perfection—more witty, more eloquent, more romantic, and more filled with marvelous tales than ever before. Having failed with his initial tactic of direct pursuit, the cunning Don adopted a far more formidable strategy. He believed Rose deserved severe punishment for daring to reject a Spanish nobleman, and what finer punishment existed than the withdrawal of his attentions and the shadow of his smile? While this notion bore the seeds of vanity, it was rooted in cunning. The Spaniard understood intimately that women, like the world, tend to value a man at his own self-assessed price; the higher the demand, the higher the reward.

Now, he intended to place a high price on himself, provoking her pride, which was too accustomed to adoration to be won by simple flattery. He could have pursued another suitor, but he deemed such a crude method liable to incite indignation or despair rather than submission. Instead, he quietly demonstrated his independence from her. Poor Rose, as fate intended, immediately began to question why. What hidden treasure or reserve force rendered him independent of her, while she felt entirely dependent upon him? Did he possess a secret? How delightful to know! Some vast ambition? How sweet to share! Some mysterious knowledge? How delicious to uncover! Perhaps a capacity for love beyond the common? How exquisite to possess exclusively! He must be greater, wiser, richer-hearted, and nobler born than she. Oh, if only his wealth could supply her poverty! Thus, step by step, she was led to sue *in forma pauperis* to the very man she had spurned when he had previously sued in similar fashion. The temptation of possessing some mysterious private treasure, of serving as the priestess of a hidden sanctuary, and of thanking Heaven for not being like other women, became increasingly overwhelming for Rose, as it often is for most.

Rose was utterly weary of wearing that mask of coyness as she sat at the Annery table that day. Don Guzman observed her uneasy, downcast gaze. Thinking, somewhat arrogantly, that she had suffered sufficient punishment, he occasionally stole a glance at her. Unabashed when he saw her lower her eyes upon contact, he noted her increasing silence and abstraction, accompanied by a faint blush. He mirrored her melancholy, continuing his glances until he caught her looking at him, checking to see if he was looking at her. At that moment, he knew his prey was secure. He asked her with his eyes, "Do you forgive me?" causing her to halt her conversation, falter, drop her eyes, and lift them again a minute later in search of his gaze, prompting him to repeat the inquiry. Unable to resist, she answered with every feature and graceful curve of her neck, "And do you forgive me in turn?"

With this exchange, Don Guzman erupted like a nightingale on a branch, bursting forth with stories, jests, and sharp retorts. He instantly became the soul of the gathering and the most charming cavalier present. Poor Rose realized she was the architect of his sudden transformation, blaming herself for her actions, shuddering and blushing at her own delight, and longing desperately for the feast to end so she might retreat home to hide among sweet fantasies of a love whose reality she felt too afraid to confront.

The afternoon view from the great terrace at Annery was breathtaking. Smart ladies in gaudy attire paraded in pairs and trios before the stately house or gazed down upon the park, where ancient oaks stood sentinel over deer grazing near a broad, land-locked river shimmering like a lake under the midsummer sun. Others listened obediently to the two presiding ladies, Mrs. St. Leger the hostess and her sister-in-law, the fair Lady Grenville. Everyone chatted, laughed, admired dresses, and gossiped about husbands and servants. Only Rose Salterne remained apart, wishing to curl up in a corner to laugh or cry, unsure which.

"Our pretty Rose seems sad," Lady Grenville remarked, approaching her. "Cheer up, child! We wish you to sing for us."

Rose replied vaguely and moved mechanically. Taking the lute, she sat on a bench beneath the house while the group gathered around.

"What shall I sing?"

"Play our old song, 'Earl Haldan's Daughter,'" suggested someone.

Rose recoiled internally. It was a loud, spirited ballad ill-suited to her current thoughts; moreover, Frank had once praised it in happier days long past. Thoughts of him, of others, of her own pride and carelessness flooded her mind, making the song feel ominous. Yet, she dared not refuse, fearing suspicion where none existed, and so she began reluctantly.
