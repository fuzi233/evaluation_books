On the library wall of a celebrated American writer hang two crossed swords—relics of the War of Independence. One was drawn in loyal service to the Crown, the other wielded by a steadfast republican. Their owner, a man of letters, has earned honor in both lands, his genius welcomed wherever it roams.

This tale recalls those swords. It follows two brothers, sons of Virginia, who found themselves on opposing sides in the Revolution, yet reunited as kin when the guns fell silent. Their portraits still gaze from the wainscoted walls of the Warrington estate in England, their letters preserved by a mother’s devotion.

They called them *The Virginians*—these brothers who crossed the Atlantic, who walked the edges of the Old World now fading into memory. Their lives brushed against figures of history, their voices lingering in ink-stained pages: letters scribbled by campfires, tear-blotted notes from grand ballrooms, even one pierced by a bullet, its words smeared with blood.

Their mother, Madam Esmond, kept every scrap. From childish scrawls to the last farewell, she treasured them until her dying hour. She never returned to Europe after her youth, save once in the reign of George II. When war burned their Virginia home, she fled to Richmond, clinging to her pride and her name—never Warrington, always Esmond.

The letters are fragments, hints of lives lived boldly. The rest must be imagined: the laughter in crowded parlors, the hushed words before battle, the weight of a brother’s loss.

---

On a bright summer morning in 1756, the *Young Rachel* sailed up the Avon, returning from her annual voyage to the Potomac. At Bristol’s bustling wharf, her owner, Mr. Trail, greeted Captain Franks with a merchant’s sharp eye.

“Only one passenger?” Trail frowned. “The *Duke William* brought fourteen last week!”

“Aye, and this one paid nothing,” Franks chuckled, nodding to a young man in mourning.

The stranger—tall, dark-haired, barely nineteen—stepped ashore, his servant trailing with trunks. At the mention of his name, Trail’s demeanor shifted instantly.

“Warrington? *The* Madam Esmond Warrington’s son?” He bowed deeply. “Your family’s name is honored here, sir! Come, you must dine with me—”

But the young Virginian declined. His heart was set on a different destination.

---

Harry Warrington had dreamed of this journey. He and his brother George had pored over maps, tracing the path from Bristol to Bath, to Salisbury, to Winchester—finally to *home*, the Hampshire estate of their ancestors.

Now, alone, Harry followed that route. The English countryside unfurled before him, lush and golden. Post-chaises rattled past laden wagons; farmers tipped hats; village greens buzzed with life. It was all as his grandfather had described—yet richer, brighter, *real*.

At last, his carriage rolled into Castlewood Green. The inn bore the family crest—three castles on a swinging sign. Heart pounding, Harry walked toward the manor, its grey stone rising against the woods.

An old man sat by the gate, a dog at his feet.

“You’ve had a fine ride, Master Harry,” the man said, as if expecting him.

Harry stared. “You must be Lockwood.”

The old retainer’s eyes clouded, then cleared. “Aye, I served your grandfather. But you—you’re the *Colonel’s* grandson? From Virginia?”

“I am,” Harry said softly.

Lockwood’s granddaughter, Molly, gaped. “Lord, sir! You’re as white as any Englishman!”

Harry smiled. “May I see the house? Even if the family is away?”

Molly led him inside. Though he had never walked these halls, each step felt familiar—a homecoming long deferred.
