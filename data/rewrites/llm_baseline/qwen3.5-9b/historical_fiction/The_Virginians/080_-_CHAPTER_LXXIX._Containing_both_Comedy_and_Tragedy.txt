We, who had played such a pivotal role in the scandalous events of the morning, felt a treble sense of guilt when we witnessed the devastating impact our actions had on him—the man we loved and revered above all others. The shock inflicted upon this good man was strange, yet pitiful to watch, especially since we were the ones who had delivered the blow. His beloved child had deceived and defied him. I confess, my dear, that in retrospect, neither of us would—or could—repeat such a deed; it seemed as though his entire family had conspired against him. Oh, dear, kind friend and father! We know you have pardoned our transgression in that heavenly realm where you dwell among purified spirits who learned on earth the true arts of loving and forgiving. For that man, love and forgiveness were natural duties; beneficence flowed from him like sunlight, accompanied by a sweet, smiling humility. To wound either was to become savage and brutal, akin to torturing a child or striking a nursing mother. Once the deed was done, all of us, the guilty parties, cowered in shame before the man we had injured.

I shall skip over the scenes of forgiveness, reconciliation, shared worship, and the final separation when the good man departed for his government, sailing away before us and leaving me and Theo stranded on the shore. We stood there, hand in hand, horribly abashed, silent, and consumed by guilt. My wife did not join me until her father had left; during the interval between our marriage ceremony and his departure, she remained at home, occupying her usual spot beside her father and by her sister's side. He was as kind as ever, but the women were almost mute among themselves. Aunt Lambert, for once, was unkind and fretful; little Hetty was feverish and strange, muttering, "I wish we were gone. I wish we were gone." Although technically admitted to the house and forgiven, I slunk away during those final days, seeing my wife only briefly in the street or amidst her family. She was not truly mine until they were gone.

We traveled to Winchester and Hampton for what might be termed our wedding—a dismal affair. For a while, we felt utterly lonely, grieving as if we had buried our dear father or driven him to the grave through our undutifulness. I instructed Sampson to announce our marriage in the newspapers (my wife later hung her head in shame whenever the poor fellow mentioned it). I took Mrs. Warrington back to my old lodgings in Bloomsbury, where there was ample space for us, and our modest married life began.

I wrote a letter to my mother in Virginia, omitting all particulars except to state that since Mr. Lambert was departing for his government, I felt honor-bound to fulfill my promise to his dearest daughter. I also noted my intention to complete my studies for the Bar, qualifying myself for employment at home or in any colony. My good Mrs. Mountain replied on behalf of Madam Esmond, suggesting that for the sake of peace, my correspondence should be routed through her. This revelation plunged my relatives into a fury that was perfectly amusing to observe. When I visited my uncle's house in Hill Street, the butler's face, as he curtly announced, "Not at home," was a blank tragedy worthy of study by Garrick himself. With my poor little wife on my arm, we tripped away, laughing at the fellow's cold reception, only to encounter my lady in a street stoppage in her carriage.

I removed my hat and offered the lowest possible bow. Affectionately, I asked after my dear cousins. "I—I wonder you dare look me in the face!" Lady Warrington gasped. "Nay, don't deprive me of that precious privilege!" I replied cheerfully. "Move on, Peter," she screamed to her driver. "Your ladyship would not impale your own husband's flesh and blood!" I retorted. She rattled the glass of her carriage in a fury. I kissed my hand, removed my hat again, and performed another of my finest bows.

Shortly afterward, walking in Hyde Park with my dearest companion, I met my little cousin exercising on horseback with a groom trailing behind. As soon as he spotted us, he galloped up, the groom shouting, "Stop, Master Miles, stop!" "I am not to speak to my cousin," Miles declared, "but telling you to send my love to Harry is not speaking to you, is it? Is that my new cousin? I'm not told not to speak to her. I'm Miles, cousin, Sir Miles Warrington Baronet's son, and you are very pretty!" "Now, duee now, Master Miles," the groom interjected, tipping his hat to us, before trotting away laughing and glancing back over his shoulder.

"You see how my relations have determined to treat me," I remarked to my partner. "As if I married you for your relations!" Theo laughed, her eyes beaming with joy and love. Ah, how happy we were! How brisk and pleasant the winter proved! How cozy the kettle by the fire (where the abashed Sampson occasionally came to stir the punch)! How delightful the nights at the theater, where our friends generously provided tickets, and where we eagerly anticipated our new play, *Pocahontas*, rivalling the successes of all former tragedies.

The fickle old aunt of Clarges Street, who had initially greeted me with a burst of scorn upon my arrival in London with my wife, quickly softened. As soon as she came to know Theo—who she had previously dismissed as an insignificant, country-faced chit—she fell utterly in love with her, inviting us to tea and supper every day whenever no other company was available. "As for company, my dears," she would say, "I don't ask for it. You are no longer *du monde*. Your marriage has put that entirely out of the question." Thus, she insisted we come to amuse her, entering and exiting via the back stairs. My wife was a fine lady enough to find this reception merely amusing. I must give credit to the Baroness's domestics; had we been duke and duchess, we could not have been treated with greater respect.

Madame de Bernstein delighted in my stories of Lady Warrington and the carriage incident. I acted out the scene for her, recounting anecdotes of the pious Baronet's lady and her daughters, which pleased the mischievous, lively old woman. The Dowager Countess of Castlewood, residing at Kensington, extended the kind of welcome gentle ladies typically offer their poorer relatives. We attended her gatherings once or twice, but losing more money at cards and spending too much on coach hire, we soon ceased these entertainments. Presumably, we were no more missed or regretted than other people swept out of the fashionable sphere by death, debt, or accident.

Theo did not regret this exclusion in the slightest. She had appeared at one of these gatherings adorned with small ornaments left by her mother, which the good lady valued highly. Yet, I thought her own white neck far prettier than those poor twinkling stones. There were dowagers whose wrinkled bones blazed with rubies and diamonds, surely wishing to exchange them for her modest parure of beauty and freshness. No one spoke to her—except Beau Lothair, a friend of Mr. Will's, who prowled about Bloomsbury afterward and even sent my wife a billet-doux. I met him in Covent Garden shortly after, promising to break his ugly face if I ever saw him near my lodgings, ensuring Madam Theo was molested no further.

The only relative to visit us was our poor Maria, along with her husband, Mr. Hagan, who frequently joined us for our homely dinners. Then there was friend Spencer from the Temple, who admired our Arcadian felicity and gently solicited our sympathy for his less fortunate loves. Twice or thrice, the famous Doctor Johnson came for a dish of Theo's tea. A dish? More like a pailful! "And a pail the best thing to feed him, sar!" exclaimed Mr. Gumbo indignantly. For the Doctor's appearance was unpleasant, and his linen was hardly white. He snorted, turned red, and sputtered while eating; he flung his food about and bellowed contradictions, annoying my Theo, whom he professed to admire greatly. Every time he saw her, he would declare, "Madam, you do not love me; I see by your manner you do not love me; though I admire you, and come here for your sake. Here is my friend Mr. Reynolds that shall paint you: he has no ceruse in his paint-box that is as brilliant as your complexion."

Mr. Reynolds, a most perfect and agreeable gentleman, would have painted my wife, but I knew his price and chose not to incur that expense. I wish I had done so now, for the sake of the children, that they might see what that face looked like some fifty years ago. To me, madam, 'tis the same now as ever; and your ladyship is always young! What annoyed Mrs. Warrington more than the Doctor's contradictions, sputterings, and dirty nails was an unfavorable opinion he formed of my new tragedy.
