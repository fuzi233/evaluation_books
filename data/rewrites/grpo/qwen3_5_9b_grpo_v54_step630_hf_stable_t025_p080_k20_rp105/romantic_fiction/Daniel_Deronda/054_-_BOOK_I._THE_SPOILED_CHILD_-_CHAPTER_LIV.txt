"The unwilling brain feigns often what it would not; and we trust Imagination with such phantasies as the tongue dares not fashion into words."

Madonna Pia, abandoned by her husband to the marshes of the Maremma, wanders in Dante's Purgatory among those who repented too late. Her story is one of mutual destruction; the husband, a man of disagreeable manners, sought punishment for his wife by casting her adrift, only to find that the relief he intended for himself was shared. Yet, compared to her, Gwendolen Harleth stands in a far more tragic entanglement. She has not been delivered from her errors, nor cleansed in purgatory; she remains trapped in the fatal meshes of her own making, where the inward torture far outweighs any visible cause.

Grandcourt took Gwendolen to sea not to discard her, but to possess her more completely. He loved the dreamy, absolute freedom of yachting, a state unmolested by society that suited his nature perfectly. He carried her away because he sensed a growing spirit of opposition within her, and because of the sentimental inclination she showed toward Daniel Deronda—a sentiment that, in another man, would have sparked jealousy. In Grandcourt, however, it manifested as a cold resolution to end such "foolery" before it could take root. He believed he was merely fulfilling the terms of their marriage contract. He had provided rank and luxury; she had accepted them, overcoming her initial repugnance. He knew she had not married him out of love, but for security, and he felt entitled to enforce the obligations of that transaction.

Gwendolen, too, understood the bargain. She possessed a root of conscience; she knew she had been wrong. But now, stranded on a tiny plank-island of a yacht, with the blue Mediterranean separating her from the world, she felt the full weight of the price she had paid. She had sold her truthfulness and her sense of justice, throttling them into silence to be dragged behind her husband like a collared beast.

What could she complain of? The yacht was exquisite, smelling of cedar and silk, crewed by elegant figures with ringlets and bronze skin. Mr. Lush had already returned to England, leaving them alone. Gwendolen even enjoyed the sea; watching the rigging and forecasting adjustments offered a semblance of the imaginary rule she craved. The weather was fine, the clay of the coast turning purple and gem-like under the sun. Yet, what stills the hunger of the heart when beauty sickens the eye? What paradise can quiet the fury of moral repulsion that concentrates the mind in poisonous misery?

While Gwendolen sat throned on her cushions, watching the sea soften with a boundless love, she prayed that Grandcourt would not pause near her, not look at her, not speak. Meanwhile, some humble woman in a smoky kitchen was listening for a footstep that promised relief; some loving couple was calculating earnings for a holiday. Gwendolen felt nothing but the crushing weight of her own isolation.

Did Grandcourt understand what churned in his wife's breast? He knew she did not love him, and he did not need to believe she did. He was not accustomed to soothe himself with the idea of universal affection. What he failed to comprehend was that she could feel a specific, deep-seated repulsion toward him personally. How could she? He knew repulsion well; he cataloged the odious familiarities of men and women, their smirks and foolish attempts at agreeability. Before their marriage, he had attracted her through refined negations, and he understood her dislike for Lush. But he could not conceive of her current hatred for Henleigh Grandcourt. Some men believe they are invisible to the world; a few believe they are objects of repulsion without being told. Grandcourt belonged to neither group. He had always viewed himself as the antithesis of revolting men, possessing a flattering view of his own attractiveness. He had no concept of moral repulsion, nor could he believe that resentment could make beauty more detestable than ugliness.

Their behavior scandalized no one—not the foreign maid, not the valet, not the crew who saw them as a model couple. Their companionship was a well-bred silence. Grandcourt made polite gestures, arranging garments or handing objects, and Gwendolen responded with the necessary civility, avoiding the vulgarity of rejection. He pointed out sugar-cane plantations; she agreed, forcing herself to find interest in them. He smoked and walked up and down, pausing to gaze at her with a narrow, immovable stare, while she exerted all her ingenuity to avoid meeting his eyes. At dinner, he commented on stale fruit or offered different wines. A lady must respond suitably. Quarreling with Grandcourt was impossible; it would be like shouting at a coiled serpent. He derived intense satisfaction from this captivity, creating a royal representation of their life where every private protest added to the piquancy of his despotism.

For Gwendolen, the world had shrunk to her husband and her relation to him. Those closest to us interpret reality for us, and Grandcourt was a distorting lens, a piece of yellow glass that made color an affliction. His petty standards and loveless ennui turned her life into a promenade through a pantheon of ugly idols. She dreaded becoming a mother, not as a deliverance, but as the final completion of the injury she had inflicted. In her despair, she clung to the possibility of accident, a refuge from the temptation of action.

Hatred, like love, is a seed that feeds on itself. The most intense form is rooted in fear, driving one to silence and constructive vindictiveness. Hidden rites of vengeance played out in the secrecy of Gwendolen's mind, not with soothing effect, but with terror. Alongside the dread of her husband grew a self-dread, urging her to flee the images her own impulses had wrought. The vision of her past wrongs illuminated every rash effort at freedom with a pale, ghastly light. She saw her actions through the lens of Deronda's judgment. No flattery or dependence could weaken his restraining power; indeed, her effort to restrain herself was a repayment of his own. Amidst the dreary uncertainties of her spoiled life, the possible remedies in Deronda's mind became her only hope. He seemed a terrible-browed angel from whom she could not hide any deed. She felt compelled to be truthful, for his power began in raising a self-discontent that could only be satisfied by genuine change.

Yet, she had no confidence in concealment. Her vision of dread took the form of a fiercely impulsive deed, committed in a dream from which she would wake to find the effects real: death under her hands, followed not by darkness but by daylight, not by satisfied hatred but by the dismay of guilt. Instead of freedom, she faced the palsy of a new terror—a white, dead face she tried to flee but could not escape. Deronda's words echoed in her mind: "Turn your fear into a safeguard. Keep your dread fixed on the idea of increasing your remorse." Temptation and dread met like pale phantoms, each obstructed by its own image, while her fuller self sobbed for deliverance.

Inarticulate prayers swept out into the vast silence, broken only by her husband's breathing or the creaking of masts. If she thought of definite help, it took the form of Deronda's presence and words. These best moments of inward crying came after white-lipped temptations with murdering fingers, when she would lie with wide-open eyes, tears seeming a blessing, thinking, "I will not mind if I can keep from getting wicked."

Days passed, drifting from the Balearic Isles to Sardinia and back toward Corsica. This floating existence, seemingly peaceful, became a nightmare for Gwendolen. One day, after touching at Ajaccio, she ventured to ask, "How long are we to be yachting?"

"What else should we do?" Grandcourt replied. "I'm not tired of it. Where would you go? I'm sick of foreign places. We shall have enough of Ryelands. Would you rather be at Ryelands?"

"Oh, no," Gwendolen said indifferently. "I only wondered how long you would like this."

"I like yachting longer than anything else," Grandcourt said. "I suppose you are beginning to tire of it. Women are so confoundedly whimsical."

"Oh, dear, no!" Gwendolen replied in a flute-like tone of scorn. "I never expect you to give way."

"Why should I?" Grandcourt asked, choosing an orange.

Gwendolen resolved to endure the length of the voyage she could not foresee. The next day, after a squall made her ill, Grandcourt announced they would stay at Genoa for a week to repair the yacht.

"Do you mind that?" Gwendolen asked, lying white amidst her white drapery.

"I should think so. Who wants to be broiling at Genoa?"

"It will be a change," Gwendolen said, made incautious by her languor.

"I don't want any change. Besides, the place is intolerable... I shall go out in a boat, as I used to do, and manage it myself."

A prospect of hope arose. Gwendolen imagined hours alone, since Grandcourt would not take her in the boat. In her exultation, she entertained wild fancies of running away, now seeing it as an escape from her worse self. Visionary relief insinuated itself as a lawful wish.

The fresh current of expectation revived her. She watched the moon sink with less loneliness, harboring a vague impression that rescue might be prepared. She was wakened the next morning by the casting of the anchor in Genoa, from a dream where she escaped over Mont Cenis, only to meet Deronda telling her to go back.

An hour later, she actually met Deronda on the palatial staircase of the *Italia*. Her husband stood by her side. Deronda started in surprise, unsure if Grandcourt would be civil. Grandcourt, however, immediately suspected an arrangement between them. Though he knew it was improbable that Gwendolen had orchestrated such a precise meeting, he refused to admit it was a mere accident. He felt a conspiracy against him, a league between his wife and Deronda. He sat drinking coffee, observing Gwendolen with a certainty that she was counting on an interview whenever his back was turned.

He discerned a fresh ease in her movements, a joyous expectation that made her more bearable. Her troubles had not marred her beauty; if anything, she was handsomer than Gwendolen Harleth had been, informed by a greater variety of inward experience. She seemed more fully a human being, less confident that all things were according to her opinion. Grandcourt interpreted these signs with narrow correctness, seeing only the anticipation of a meeting with Deronda.

"A—just ring, please," Grandcourt said, stretching for his hat. "I'm going to send Angus to find a little sailing-boat for us to go out in... It's uncommonly pleasant these fine evenings."

Gwendolen turned cold. The gleaming content died out of her like a glacier after sunset.

"I would rather not go in the boat," she said. "Take some one else with you."

"Very well; if you don't go, I shall not go," Grandcourt replied. "We shall stay suffocating here, that's all."

"I can't bear to go in a boat," Gwendolen said angrily.

"That is a sudden change," Grandcourt sneered. "Since you decline, we shall stay indoors."

He lit his cigar and walked up and down. Gwendolen's temper urged her to persist, to force him to stay in the hotel. She passed into the adjoining bedroom, throwing herself into a chair, feeling the wave of evil rush back upon her.

Grandcourt entered, threw off his hat, and sat sideways in front of her. "Have you come round yet? or do you find it agreeable to be out of temper. You make things uncommonly pleasant for me."

"Why do you want to make them unpleasant for *me*?" Gwendolen asked, helpless, hot tears rising.

"Now, will you be good enough to say what it is you have to complain of?" Grandcourt asked, using his most inward voice. "Is it that I stay indoors when you stay?"

She could give no answer. In the conflict of despair and humiliation, she began to sob, tears rolling down her cheeks—a form of agitation she had never shown before.

"I hope this is useful," Grandcourt said. "All I can say is, it's most confoundedly unpleasant. What the devil women can see in this kind of thing, I don't know. *You* see something to be got by it, of course. All I can see is, that we shall be shut up here when we might have been having a pleasant sail."

"Let us go, then," Gwendolen said impetuously. "Perhaps we shall be drowned." She began to sob again.

This behavior gave definiteness to Grandcourt's conclusions. He drew his chair close. "Just be quiet and listen, will you?"

There was a magical effect in his proximity. Gwendolen shrank and ceased to sob, keeping her eyelids down and clasping her hands tightly.

"Let us understand each other," Grandcourt said. "I know very well what this nonsense means. But if you suppose I am going to let you make a fool of me, just dismiss that notion from your mind. What are you looking forward to, if you can't behave properly as my wife? There is disgrace for you, if you like to have it, but I don't know anything else; and as to Deronda, it's quite clear that he hangs back from you."

"It's all false!" Gwendolen said bitterly. "You don't in the least imagine what is in my mind. I have seen enough of the disgrace that comes in that way. And you had better leave me at liberty to speak with any one I like. It will be better for you."

"You will allow me to judge of that," Grandcourt said, rising and moving toward the window, playing with his whiskers.
