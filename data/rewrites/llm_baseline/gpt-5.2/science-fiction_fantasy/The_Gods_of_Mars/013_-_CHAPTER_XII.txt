For a heartbeat I stood where the sand still held the imprint of my heel, staring at the ring of faces closing in—black-skinned First Born, their metal ornaments catching the arena’s glare like knives laid out for carving. The next instant the press of them drove me back. My foot searched for the reassuring hardness of floor—and found air.

The world dropped away beneath me.

I had backed into the very pit that had swallowed Issus when she teetered at the edge of defeat. I pitched backward, the boy locked against my chest as if I could keep him from falling by sheer will, and we vanished into a mouth of darkness that smelled not of clean stone but of long-closed places—dry dust, ancient minerals, and the faint, sour note of something sealed too long.

We struck a polished chute and shot downward as though flung through the throat of a machine. Above, the opening snapped shut with the same impossible neatness with which it had opened, as if the temple itself were a cook’s hand closing a lid to smother flame. We slid, unharmed, into a dim apartment far below the arena.

I found my feet, and the first thing I saw—before I could even take the boy’s weight fully to my arms—was Issus.

She stood behind a grated door at one side of the chamber, her face a mask of malignant triumph, eyes gleaming through the bars as if she meant to roast my soul and savor the smoke.

“Rash mortal!” she shrilled, and her voice did not simply strike the ear—it clawed at it. The chamber’s stone made her words ring thin and metallic, the way cheap vessels sing when struck. “You shall pay the awful penalty for your blasphemy in this secret cell. Here you shall lie alone and in darkness with the carcass of your accomplice festering in its rottenness by your side, until crazed by loneliness and hunger you feed upon the crawling maggots that were once a man.”

There are curses meant to blister the skin; hers was meant to poison appetite itself, to make the very idea of eating turn to nausea, and yet to promise hunger so sharp you would take anything between your teeth.

That was all. She was gone before I could answer. And the dim light that had filled the cell thinned, guttered, and died—like a flame pinched out between damp fingers—until we stood in a blackness so complete it felt poured, heavy as oil.

“Pleasant old lady,” said a voice at my side, dry with humor.

“Who speaks?” I demanded, turning toward the sound that was now the only geography I had.

“’Tis I—your companion—who has had the honour this day of fighting shoulder to shoulder with the greatest warrior that ever wore metal upon Barsoom.”

Relief came like the first swallow after thirst. “I thank God that you are not dead,” I said. “I feared for that nasty cut upon your head.”

“It but stunned me,” he replied. “A mere scratch.”

“Maybe it were as well had it been final,” I muttered, because the pit’s air already seemed intent on making prophets of despair. “We appear to have a splendid chance of dying of starvation and thirst.”

“Where are we?”

“Beneath the arena. We tumbled down the shaft that swallowed Issus as she was almost at our mercy.”

He gave a low laugh—soft, controlled—yet full of the relief of a man who has found, in the pantry of disaster, an unexpected door.

Then, moving like a shadow in a place without light, he reached for me. Fingers found my shoulder, and he drew my ear close to his mouth as if the darkness itself had ears and a tongue.

“Nothing could be better,” he whispered. “There are secrets within the secrets of Issus of which Issus herself does not dream.”

“What do you mean?”

“I laboured with the other slaves a year since in the remodelling of these subterranean galleries. We found below these an ancient system of corridors and chambers sealed up for ages. The blacks in charge explored them, taking several of us along for the work. I know the system perfectly.

“There are miles of corridors honeycombing the ground beneath the gardens and the temple itself. One passage leads down and connects with the lower regions that open on the water shaft that gives passage to Omean.

“If we can reach the submarine undetected, we may yet make the sea—there are many islands where the blacks never go. There we may live for a time, and who knows what may transpire to aid us to escape?”

He spoke as though laying out ingredients for a dish that might save us: corridor, passage, shaft, boat, sea, islands—each word placed carefully, not for poetry but for survival. Even here, where Issus meant us to rot, he was measuring distances by memory the way an elder measures grain by the feel of it in the hand.

I kept my voice as low as his. “Lead back to Shador, my friend. Xodar—the black—is there. We were to attempt our escape together. I cannot desert him.”

“No,” said the boy at once. In the dark I could hear his head turn, could almost hear the set of his jaw in the sound of his breath. “One cannot desert a friend. It were better to be recaptured ourselves than that.”

He began to grope about the floor, hands sweeping the stone with a care that reminded me of searching a hearth for a dropped seed—quiet, patient, knowing the smallest find might matter. At last he gave a low, sibilant summons—“S-s-t”—and I crept toward the sound.

He knelt at the brink of an opening in the floor. “There is a drop here of about ten feet,” he whispered. “Hang by your hands and you will alight safely on a level floor of soft sand.”

Very quietly, I lowered myself from the inky cell above into the inky pit below. The darkness was so absolute it stole even the comfort of one’s own hands. I have been in night, in fog, in the smoke of battle; none of it compared to this—this deliberate absence, as if light had been forbidden by law.

For a moment I hung, arms straining, feet searching for a world that was not there. When the feet find only emptiness and the distance below is hidden, the body invents horrors. Though he had said ten feet, the mind insisted on infinity. The muscles begged not to let go.

I let go.

I dropped—four feet—and struck a soft cushion of sand that yielded like flour sifted onto a board. The boy followed, his landing quick and controlled.

“Raise me to your shoulders,” he whispered, “and I will replace the trap.”

I bent, he climbed, and his fingers worked above my head. I could not see, but I could hear the faint scrape of stone upon stone and then the subtle change in the air as the opening sealed. Even without sight, I felt the temple’s craft: a cellar that could swallow you and leave no mouth behind.

He took my hand. We moved slowly, his pace cautious, with frequent halts—his palm tightening on mine when a corridor forked, loosening when he recognized a turn by the feel of wall and floor. He navigated as some food-historian might move through a vanished kitchen by memory—where the salt was kept, where the oven door opened, where the smoke used to cling. Here, instead, it was angles and slopes and the faintest drafts.

Presently we began the descent of a very steep incline.

“It will not be long,” he said, “before we shall have light. At the lower levels we meet the same stratum of phosphorescent rock that illuminates Omean.”

Never shall I forget that passage through the pits of Issus. Nothing leapt at us; no guards sprang from niches; no sudden blade sang. And yet every step was an incident, because the corridors themselves were old beyond reckoning—old enough that their silence had a texture.

In that silence, sound became fate. Our breaths were loud. The soft drag of sand beneath our sandals was louder. Somewhere far above, an echo of the arena’s roar—distant now, muffled as though heard through water—came and went, like a storm remembered after it has passed. There were no birds here, no wind in leaves, no ordinary world-sound to soothe the mind. It was the kind of acoustic emptiness that can drive men to invent voices, and I understood why Issus promised madness as a seasoning for our hunger. A place can starve you not only of food, but of the gentle noises by which a human heart keeps time.

In the black, my imagination cooked up its own feast of the past. I saw ancient peoples moving through these tunnels with lamps and burdens, plotting, praying, fearing—setting their last defenses against a dying planet’s slow famine. I thought of Barsoom’s three principal races—the blacks, the whites, and that vanished yellow people spoken of only in scraps of story, their names lost the way a tongue dies when no child learns it. As waters dried and seas receded, all sustenance dwindled: not merely food, but the very vocabulary of belonging. Cities were abandoned. Communities broke into smaller wandering groups. The fierce green men preyed upon them. Blended blood made the splendid red men of today.

I had believed the old races gone utterly, like forgotten recipes nobody can now translate because the dialect of spices is dead. Yet within these past days I had found whites and blacks in multitudes. Could some remnant of the yellow race still exist in some far corner of the world, keeping their words alive in whispers, seasoning their meals with names no outsider could pronounce?

A low exclamation from the boy broke my reverie.

“At last,” he breathed, and there was triumph in it, as if he had smelled bread baking after days of dust. “The lighted way.”

Looking up, I saw it far ahead: a dim radiance. As we advanced, the light increased until we emerged into passageways lit by softly glowing rock. It cast no harsh glare—only a steady, cool gleam, like moonlight caught in stone. The corridors here seemed less oppressive, not because they were newer but because the eye could finally sip something other than darkness.

From then on we moved quickly. The boy’s confidence returned in the surety of his stride, and soon we came to the end of a corridor that opened directly upon the ledge surrounding the pool of the submarine.

The craft lay at her moorings. The hatch was uncovered.

The boy raised a finger to his lips, then tapped his sword in a gesture that needed no translation. He crept forward without a sound. I followed, keeping my breathing shallow. The air here was damp and tasted faintly of metal and old water, like a knife left too long in a sink.

Silently we dropped to the deserted deck. On hands and knees we crawled toward the hatchway. I stole a glance below.

No guard. No crew. Only shadowed bunks, silent instruments, the stale smell of enclosed machinery.

With the quickness and soundlessness of cats, we dropped together into the main cabin. Still no sign of life. We covered and secured the hatch, and the small, decisive noises of metal locking into place sounded enormous in the hush.

The boy stepped into the pilot house, touched a button, and the boat sank. Water swirled outside the ports as we descended the shaft. Even then, there was none of the scurry we had expected—no boots running, no shouted orders. The acoustic landscape remained strangely clean, as if all the world’s noise had been pulled away to leave only our own pulse and the faint working of the vessel.

While he guided the craft, I moved from cabin to cabin, searching for any member of the crew.

The submarine was entirely deserted.

It felt like finding a pantry stocked and unlocked in a famine—too good, too sudden. When I returned to the pilot house, the boy held out a paper.

“This may explain the absence of the crew,” he said.

It was a radio-aerial message to the commander of the submarine:

“The slaves have risen. Come with what men you have and those that you can gather on the way. Too late to get aid from Omean. They are massacring all within the amphitheatre. Issus is threatened. Haste.

“ZITHAD”

“Zithad is Dator of the guards of Issus,” explained the youth. “We gave them a bad scare—one that they will not soon forget.”

“Let us hope that it is but the beginning of the end of Issus,” I said.

“Only our first ancestor knows,” he replied, using the old phrase as naturally as one might invoke the origin of a recipe—acknowledging that some knowledge is inherited yet never fully owned.

We reached the submarine pool in Omean without incident. There we debated sinking the craft before leaving her, but decided it would add nothing to our chances. There were plenty of blacks on Omean to thwart us if we were taken; a few more arriving from Issus’s gardens would not worsen odds already sharp as broken glass.

We stood for a moment with the damp air around us and the faint lap of water below, considering how to pass the guards patrolling the island about the pool. The voices of the sentries carried—crisp, harsh, made harsher still by stone that returned every syllable like an accusation. It struck me then how power speaks not only through weapons, but through sound: who gets to shout, who must whisper, whose language fills the corridors until it seems the only language that exists.

At last a plan came to me—simple, audacious, almost domestic in its reliance on the everyday machinery of authority: the written order, the signature, the assumption that ink is truth.

“What is the name or title of the officer in charge of these guards?” I asked.

“A fellow named Torith was on duty when we entered this morning,” he replied.

“Good. And what is the name of the commander of the submarine?”

“Yersted.”

I found a dispatch blank in the cabin and wrote, with as firm a hand as the light allowed:

“Dator Torith: Return these two slaves at once to Shador.

“YERSTED”

“That will be the simpler way to return,” I said, smiling as I handed the forged order to the boy. “Come. We shall see now how well it works.”

“But our swords!” he protested. “What shall we say to explain them?”

“Since we cannot explain them, we shall have to leave them behind,” I replied.

He hesitated. “Is it not the extreme of rashness to thus put ourselves again, unarmed, in the power of the First Born?”

“It is the only way,” I said. “You may trust me to find a way out of the prison of Shador. And once out, we shall find no great difficulty in arming ourselves in a country that abounds so plentifully in armed men.”

“As you say,” he answered, with a shrug and a quick smile that held more courage than youth ought to have. “I could not follow another leader who inspired greater confidence than you. Come—let us put your ruse to the test.”

We left our swords behind us, the weight of that choice settling into my shoulders as tangibly as any chain. Then, boldly, we emerged from the hatchway and strode to the main exit that led to the sentry’s post and the office of the Dator of the guard.

At sight of us, the guards sprang forward in surprise. Rifles levelled; a sharp chorus of commands cut the air. It was the kind of sound that makes the stomach tighten, as if the body remembers, in its oldest instincts, the snap of predators.

I held out the message to one of them. He took it, glanced at the name, and handed it to Torith, who was emerging from his office to learn the cause of the commotion.

Torith read the order. For a moment his eyes moved from the paper to our faces and back again, suspicion working behind them like a knife testing the grain of meat.

“Where is Dator Yersted?” he asked.

My heart sank, heavy as a stone dropped into deep water. I cursed myself in silence for a stupid fool in not sinking the submarine—to make my lie unchallengeable.

“His orders were to return immediately to the temple landing,” I replied, keeping my tone flat, as though I were repeating an uninteresting detail from a duty roster.

Torith took a half step toward the entrance to the pool as if he meant to corroborate my story. In that instant, everything hung in the balance. Had he gone and seen the empty submarine still lying at her wharf, the whole thin crust of my concoction would have broken beneath us.

But he did not go. Either the authority of the signature soothed him, or the sheer improbability of two slaves walking willingly into custody made the message seem self-proving. Boldness, like a strong spice, can disguise a poor cut—at least for a time.

“Were you connected with the rising of the slaves?” he demanded. “We have just had meagre reports of some such event.”

“All were involved,” I said. “But it amounted to little. The guards quickly overcame and killed the majority of us.”

He seemed satisfied; perhaps the promise of order restored was the sweetest thing he could taste.

“Take them to Shador,” he ordered, turning to one of his subordinates.

We entered a small boat lying beside the island. The water slapped softly against the hull, a sound almost gentle—too gentle for the business at hand. In a few minutes we were disembarking upon Shador.

Here, the routine of captivity resumed with the efficiency of a well-run kitchen—doors opened and shut, keys turned, chains counted, bodies placed where they belonged. We were returned to our cells: I with Xodar, the boy by himself. And behind locked doors, with the echoes of the guards’ voices fading into the stone, we were again prisoners of the First Born.
