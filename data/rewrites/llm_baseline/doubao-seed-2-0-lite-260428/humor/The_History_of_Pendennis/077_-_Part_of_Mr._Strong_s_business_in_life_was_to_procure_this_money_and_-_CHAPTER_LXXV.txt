Pen paced the Tunbridge station platform so hard he was this close to grinding a permanent foot-shaped trench into the gravel, fuming so hot he could’ve melted the arriving train tracks. The half-hour wait for the London express dragged on for what felt like six full hours of sitting through a bad church sermon, but eventually the wheels screeched, the train trundled off, and the London gaslights bobbed into view through the smog. A man who’d forgotten his carpetbag under the seat came barrelling out of the carriage, threw the first handful of spare change he found in his pocket at a hansom cabbie, and yelped “Drive to Jermyn Street like the devil’s nipping at your heels!” The cabbie tipped his own hat so hard it nearly slid off in gratitude for the absurdly generous tip, and Pen went sprinting up the hotel stairs to Lady Rockminster’s apartments like the building was on fire and the only bucket of water was sitting in her drawing room.

Laura was there alone by the lamp, half-reading a book, her face pale as a unbuttered scone from three days of fretting. She looked up the second Pen burst through the door, and didn’t even get the words “what, you’re back so soon?” all the way out before Pen charged over, grabbed her trembling outstretched hand before she could even stand up straight, and dropped to his knees right there on the carpet like he was performing a stage melodrama for a full house.
“I saw Blanche!” he blurted, breathless. “She’s properly engaged to Harry Foker—she dumped me flat. And—and… what do you say, Laura?”
No grand speech needed. Her hand squeezed his so hard her knuckles went white, her eyes lit up like someone had just flipped a switch inside them, and her wobbly, smiling lips said more than three volumes of flowery poetry ever could. Pen buried his face in her lap and sobbed that they ought to get his old mother Helen’s blessing for the match, and it felt like those old soft arms were already wrapped around him.

That’s exactly when Lady Rockminster trundled in from her post-dinner nap, her maid Beck trailing behind holding her snuff box, and the first words out of her mouth were: “Upon my word, young people!” Beck stood behind her gawking so hard her eyes almost fell out of her head, and who could blame her? They’d just walked in on a very kneeling, very chuffed-looking Pen, still clutching Laura’s hand like it was a winning lottery ticket.
“Get up off the carpet, you daft boy,” the old lady huffed. “Beck, get out of here, what do you think you’re doing, poking your nose in two people’s business?”
Pen shot to his feet grinning like he’d just won the village fair’s biggest pig, and announced “She’s consoling me after my great tragic heartbreak over Blanche, ma’am!”
“Kissing her hand like you’re a prince in a pantomime, that’s what you’re doing,” Lady Rockminster sniffed. “I don’t know what you two will get up to next.”
Pen responded by kissing *her* hand instead, and launched into the full silly story: “Blanche ditched me for Harry Foker! I walked in on them while he was draped over her feet warbling love songs to her, they’ve been secret for ten whole days. He used old Mrs. Planter’s rheumatism as an excuse to hang around the house and keep Laura out! The bloke’s a proper gentleman, too—he already promised the Logwood living to Lady Ann’s husband, got her a diamond necklace so big it weighs a ton, and bolted straight to Blanche the second he found out his dad’s stupid attempt to marry him off to a duke’s daughter fell through.”
“Oh, so you couldn’t land the girl you were chasing, so you settled for the next nice one hanging around? Is that the trick, sir?” the old lady asked, one eyebrow raised.
“He’s not settling,” Laura cut in, flushing bright pink. “He’s being honest.”
Pen nodded so hard his hair flopped in his eyes. “I know I don’t deserve half of how good she is. If being around the sweetest, purest creature on the planet doesn’t knock all the stupid nonsense out of me, nothing will.”
Lady Rockminster grunted, but her crinkly old eyes were soft. “Hm. It’s all very well. Though I still would’ve preferred Bluebeard for you two, at least he had a properly dramatic backstory.”

To steer the conversation away from the mushy stuff that was making them all a little teary, Pen suddenly remembered the whole mess with Sam Huxter and Fanny Bolton, the drama he’d completely forgotten the second he’d seen Blanche and Harry together. He told them the whole ridiculous story: how Sam had eloped with Fanny and made her his wife, and now he was hiding out in their tiny lodgings terrified his dad, the well-respected Clavering doctor, was going to turn up in London and flay him alive. He made extra sure to emphasize that Fanny was a terrible little coquette who flirted with every man that so much as breathed in her direction, very clearly hinting “see? That little old nonsense with her years back was all her fault, I was perfectly innocent, just a helpless man caught in the path of her wiles!”
Laura laughed and squeezed his hand. “If you did make a silly mistake, you repented for it ages ago. And besides, I’ve no right to scold you anyway— I was once daft enough to have a silly little crush on Warrington myself, remember?”
“Hmph,” Lady Rockminster grumbled again. “Still say Bluebeard would’ve been more fun.”

The pair were so giddy and full of the joy of being newly betrothed that they immediately decided they had to go visit the Huxters right now, at 11 o’clock at night, which earned them a snort from the old countess. “You stupid things, they’re already in their nightcaps eating pickles by the fire, go see them tomorrow first thing. Good night, you two.”

The next morning, Laura turned up bright and early with her maid Martha, and the three of them trundled over to Fanny and Sam’s tiny Charterhouse Lane lodgings. Fanny was bouncing off the walls, all flustered, calling Pen her “guardian” ever since he’d left her that little bequest ages back, absolutely shaking in her boots at the thought of old Mr. Huxter turning up and disowning Sam, leaving them to sell bootlaces on the street for the rest of their lives. Ten minutes after they left, Sam’s lawyer pals Linton and Blades popped by, smelled pipe tobacco all the way down the stairs, spotted the countess’s coronet on the carriage outside, and marvelled at what a high-and-mighty swell their old mate Pen was now, considering his dad was just a country sawbones.

On the drive back through Fleet Street, stuck in traffic at Temple Bar, they ran straight into Sam, who was white as a sheet. His dad had arrived, holed up at the Somerset Coffee House, made a fortune on the new local railway line shares, and was in a shockingly good mood—but Sam was too much of a coward to tell him he’d eloped and gotten married. Pen hatched the dumbest, most perfect scheme on the spot: they’d tell old Huxter that Lady Rockminster had come down with a sudden mystery ailment and specifically requested *him* as her doctor, butter the old man up so much he was floating on cloud nine, then spring the news of the marriage on him when he was too pleased with himself to throw a fit.

Old Mr. Huxter could barely believe his luck when Pen walked in and told him a real, actual countess wanted him as her personal physician. He tripped over his own medical bag getting out the door, climbed straight into the coroneted carriage with Laura, who spent the whole ride gushing about how he was the most skilled doctor in the whole south of England. Lady Rockminster laid it on even thicker when they got back to her rooms, going on about how her usual doctor was out of town and she’d heard nothing but praise for his genius. The old softy prescribed her a dose of peppermint, ammonia, and orange flower water, and spent the whole rest of the day bragging to Sam that he was now personal physician to a peeress of the realm.

By the next morning, he was so chuffed you couldn’t wipe the grin off his face. Pen sat him down, went on and on about how wonderful Sam was, how young men in London got up to all sorts of terrible trouble if they weren’t married young, and the old doctor nodded along like a bobblehead the whole time. When Pen finally dropped the bomb that Sam had already secretly married a sweet, pretty little girl who the whole Rockminster household adored, the old man could barely get his “God bless my soul!” out before Pen yanked the door open, and Sam and Fanny stumbled in to kneel at his feet, sniffling and laughing at the same time. Fanny looked up at him with big watery eyes, and the old man didn’t stand a chance. He sighed, patted her cheek, kissed the little tear-streaked thing on the forehead, and said “Alright then, get up. What’s your name, my dear?”
Fanny wiped her nose on her sleeve, grinned, and said “Fanny, papa.”
The comedy was done. No fights, no ruined fortunes, just two new happy couples, a grumpy old countess who was definitely going to tease all her friends about tricking the country doctor into being her fake patient, and enough madeira to go around for everyone.
