By six o'clock on the following evening, Sally had reached the grim realization that Ginger would likely have to navigate life's perils entirely on his own, sans her moral guidance. She hadn't laid eyes on him all day, and in another hour, she would be departing Roville aboard the 7:15 express bound for Paris, Cherbourg, and eventually New York.

Driven by a lingering hope that he might still be lurking nearby, Sally dragged her baggage to the station at half-past six, depositing it with a kindly porter who seemed ready to solve all of life's problems with a nod. With a heavy heart, she ventured back to the *Casino Municipale*. Like many spirited, busy-girls, Sally possessed a peculiar talent for meddling in the private business of others—a trait her brother Fillmore preferred to describe as "messing about." She had convinced herself that Ginger needed a firm push in the right direction, so she stepped inside, relieved to spot a flame of red hair cutting through the throng around a roulette table.

Roville-sur-Mer boasted two casinos: one on the promenade for the gentle breeze and a game called *boule*, and the grand *Casino Municipale* near the station, the temple of the serious gambler. It was instantly obvious that Ginger wasn't just playing; he was conquering. He was entrenched behind a fortress of square counters, and as Sally watched, a stoic croupier slid another stack of chips toward him at the end of his rake.

"Epatant!" whispered a despondent man beside Sally, nudging her ribs to gesture wildly. Though Sally's French was limited, she understood the sentiment: the crowd was both startled and delighted. There is a certain noble altruism among spectators at continental roulette tables; they derive a spiritual joy from witnessing someone else's triumph.

The croupier twisted his moustache with his left hand and the wheel with his right, and the room fell silent. Sally, having maneuvered to a vantage point where the crush was slightly less intense, caught a glimpse of Ginger's face. An involuntary laugh escaped her lips. He resembled a terrier staring intently at a rat hole. His hair bristled with adrenaline, his ears seemingly pricked forward in anticipation.

As the tension mounted, waiting for the wheel to restart, Sally's laugh rang out with an embarrassingly clear ringtone. It cut through the atmosphere like a siren. The effect was immediate and profound. There is something akin to religious ecstasy in the demeanor of gamblers watching a hot streak, and if Sally had guffawed inside a cathedral, she might have caused similar consternation. The earnest worshippers gazed at her with shocked, betrayed eyes. Ginger, turning abruptly, saw her and leaped to his feet. Just as he rose, the ball clicked rattling into a red compartment. Since the big winner had selected the wrong color, a shuddering groan rippled through the congregation, sounding much like the convulsions of penitents at a revival meeting. More glances of reproach flew at Sally; the general consensus was that her untimely behavior had cursed Ginger's luck.

The only soul unbothered by this tragedy was Ginger himself. He scooped up his winnings, shoved them into his pocket, and pushed through the crowd to where Sally stood, now officially branded a pariah. There was a universal sense of regret that he was calling it a day; it felt as though a star had abruptly walked offstage mid-scene. Not even a violent quarrel erupting between two excited gamblers over a disputed five-franc piece could soothe the collective grief.

"I say," Ginger said, skillfully extracting Sally from the mob, "this is topping, meeting you like this. I've been hunting for you everywhere."

"It's funny you didn't find me, then, for that's exactly where I've been. I was looking for you."

"No, really?" Ginger seemed genuinely pleased. He guided her to the quiet ante-room outside the hall and they sat in a corner. It was pleasant here, save for the gorgeously uniformed attendant standing guard by the door. "That was awfully good of you."

"I felt I must have a chat with you before my train left."

Ginger started violently.

"Your train? What do you mean?"

"The puff-puff," Sally explained. "I'm leaving tonight, you know."

"Leaving?" Ginger looked as horrified as the devoutest member of the congregation Sally had just excommunicated. "You don't mean leaving? You're not going away from Roville?"

"I'm afraid so."

"But why? Where are you going?"

"Back to America. My boat sails from Cherbourg tomorrow."

"Oh, my aunt!"

"I'm sorry," Sally said, touched by his alarm. She was a warm-hearted girl and cherished being appreciated. "But..."

"I say..." Ginger Kemp turned a brilliant shade of scarlet and glared past the uniformed official, who was observing their tête-à-tête with the indulgent eye of someone who had weathered this storm before. "I say, look here, will you marry me?"

Sally stared at his vermilion profile in frank amazement. Ginger, she realized by now, was a surprisingly unpredictable young man, but she hadn't anticipated him being *this* surprising.

"Marry you!"

"You know what I mean."

"Well, yes, I suppose I do. You allude to the holy state. Yes, I know what you mean."

"Then how about it?"

Sally began to regain her composure. Her sense of humor was tickled. She looked at Ginger gravely. He refused to meet her eye, instead continuing to drink in the uniformed official, who was now humming a love-ballad under his breath. The official could not hear their conversation, nor would he have understood it if he could; yet, he was an expert in the language of the eyes.

"But isn't this—don't think I'm making difficulties—isn't this a little sudden?"

"It's got to be sudden," Ginger complained. "I thought you were planning to stay for weeks."

"But, my infant, my babe, hasn't it occurred to you that we are practically strangers?" She patted his hand tolerantly, causing the official to heave a tender sigh. "I see what has happened," she said. "You're mistaking me for some other girl, some girl you actually know and were properly introduced to. Take a good look at me, and you'll see."

"If I take a good look at you," Ginger said feverishly, "I'm dashed if I'll answer for the consequences."

"And this is the man I was going to lecture on 'Enterprise.'"

"You're the most wonderful girl I've ever met, dash it!" Ginger cried, his gaze still riveted on the official. "I dare say it is sudden. I can't help that. I fell in love with you the moment I saw you, and there you are!"

"But..."

"Now, look here, I know I'm not much of a chap and all that, but... well, I've just won the deuce of a lot of money in there..."

"Would you buy me with your gold?"

"I mean to say, we'd have enough to start on, and... of course I've made an infernal hash of everything I've tried up till now, but there must be something I can do. And you can jolly well bet I'd have a goodish stab at it. I mean to say, with you to buck me up and so forth, don't you know. Well, I mean..."

"Has it struck you that I may already be engaged to someone else?"

"Oh, golly! Are you?"

For the first time, he turned and faced her, and there was a look in his eyes that touched Sally and drove all sense of the ludicrous out of her. Absurd as it was, this man was truly serious.

"Well, yes, as a matter of fact I am," she said soberly.

Ginger Kemp bit his lip and fell silent for a moment.

"Oh, well, that's torn it!" he said at last.

Sally felt an emotion too complex to analyze. There was pity in it, but also amusement. The feeling, though she didn't recognize it, was maternal. Mothers, listening to their children plead with engaging absurdity for something wholly out of their power to bestow, feel that same wavering between tears and laughter. Sally wanted to pick Ginger up and kiss him. The one thing she could not do was look upon him, sorry as she was for him, as a reasonable, grown-up man.

"You don't really mean it, you know."

"Don't I!" said Ginger, hollowly. "Oh, don't I!"

"You can't! There isn't such a thing in real life as love at first sight. Love's a thing that comes when you know a person well and..." She paused. It had just occurred to her that she was hardly the girl to lecture in this strain. Her love for Gerald Foster had been sufficiently sudden, even instantaneous. What did she know of Gerald except that she loved him? They had become engaged within two weeks of their first meeting. She found this recollection dampening to her eloquence, and ended by saying tamely:

"It's ridiculous."
