Major Pendennis, having fought that last campaign at Gaunt House as if it had been Waterloo and his liver the enemy, discovered next morning that heroism is admirable but very trying to the joints. So, as soon as it was medically decent to stir him, he bundled his groaning dignity into a chaise and went off to Buxton to be steeped like an old tea-leaf in healing waters. Parliament shut its mouth; the Claverings shut their house; London, having made its noise for the season, took itself away as completely as a dinner-party after the last glass.

The lawyers flew off to their circuits, the fine people to the Continent, the healthy to Scotland, the unhealthy to spas, and the idle—being the most perishable article—dispersed to wherever idleness is best preserved. Pall Mall looked as if it had been laid out to dry. The clubs’ bow-windows, usually full of heads considering nothing with immense importance, displayed only emptiness contemplating itself. The red jackets vanished from the Palace gates. Tradesmen who had spent the year bowing now went abroad to practise walking upright. Tailors, who had been hemming other men’s manners, grew moustachios and floated up the Rhine. Bootmakers met their customers at Ems and blushed to see that their handiwork was still attached to its owner. The Sunday sermons in St. James’s were preached to half a congregation, and not a sinner of distinction among them—a dreadful discouragement to the clergy. Even the band in Kensington Gardens put its brass and silver to bed; only a couple of old chaises crept along the Serpentine like insects looking for summer.

Clarence Bulbul, chained by Treasury duty, rode in Rotten Row and declared it as vast and lonely as the Arabian desert; which was a very loud comparison for so silent a place, and proved, if anything proves it, that man can import noise into a hush as easily as a barrel-organ can invade a meadow. Warrington, who knew the value of silence the way some men know the value of port, packed a gross of Cavendish tobacco and retired to his brother’s in Norfolk. Pen was left alone in chambers for a while; he could not quit the metropolis at his own pleasure, being kept there by the small tyranny of his newspaper, the Pall Mall Gazette, for which he acted as editor and charge d’affaires while Captain Shandon, that great general of leading articles when compelled at bayonet-point, took his ease with his family at Boulogne.

Now, although Mr. Arthur Pendennis had for years been protesting that he was blasé, scorched-out, and generally done for, the fact remained that he was a young man of excellent digestion and a conscience elastic enough to stretch over almost any dinner. He ate at least once a day with true feeling; and if he could not secure a good dinner, he seated himself before a bad one with perfect serenity, as if to say to Providence, “You do your worst: I can swallow it.” And though he talked of misanthropy, he had a perpetual appetite for faces, voices, and the agreeable clatter of mankind. If he could not have wit, rank, or beauty, he took what he could get—tavern-parlours, Greenwich steamboats, jaunts to Hampstead with Finucane his colleague, the little theatres across the river, and especially Vauxhall, where the lamps burned like captive stars and where the air was always seasoned with fried fish, hot sausages, wilted bouquets, damp gravel, cigar smoke, and that peculiar London perfume compounded of river-breeze and humanity.

Pen would stroll there and watch the grimaces of comic singers and the graces of equestrians, and, with a satiric humour that still had a crumb of kindness in it, he could look as pleasantly at the lookers-on as at the show—at the roystering youths drinking their fun straight as gin; at the honest parents feeding their delighted children on cake, awe, and sticky fingers; at the poor girls laughing too loud, as if loudness could drown care. Pen boasted of this sympathy with “all conditions of men,” called it anthropology, and said he hoped to keep it to the last; which is a handsome hope, and easy enough while one’s own boots are tight, varnished, and not pinching.

Indeed he had lately been talking in his impetuous manner to Warrington about having burned out romance like a kitchen fire that has eaten its own fuel. If ever he married, he said, it would be a marriage of reason—good temper, good looks, a little money: something to cushion the carriage through life. Romance, he declared, was done.

“Stuff!” growled Warrington. “You fancied you were getting bald the other day, and bragged of it; and then you began rubbing bear’s-grease on your head the minute the hairdresser hinted at it. You smell like a barber’s shop.”

“You are Diogenes,” Pen answered, blushing as philosophers should not blush. “You want every man to live in a tub like you. Violets smell better than stale tobacco, you grizzly cynic.” And then he went on with the air of a weary pilgrim who takes immense pains to look well on the road.

It happened, then, in this dull season—on a shining autumn Friday night—that Pen, having written at the office a brilliant leading article (one of those pieces which Captain Shandon might have written had the Captain been in good humour and inclined to work, which he never was unless a necessity stood over him with a cudgel), reviewed his wet proof with satisfaction, and determined to cross the water and regale himself with Vauxhall’s fireworks.

He slipped into his pocket the order that admitted “Editor of Pall Mall Gazette and friend,” paid honest coin to get over Waterloo Bridge, and walked through the clear night. Above, the stars shone quietly—lamps of a better management than Vauxhall’s—and below, the river made that dark, thick London sound which is half water, half city, and wholly unlike any country stream. There was none of the grinding industrial uproar here that turns music into mere rivalry; only the soft, continuous hush and lap, with distant wheels murmuring like a bad conscience far away.

Before you enter Vauxhall’s blaze, you must pass that black, dreary passage and its wickets, like a throat you swallow through to reach the feast. In the wall are two bright holes, and in each hole sits a gentleman at a desk, ready to take your money or your ticket; and while the lamps inside promise fairyland, the little box at the gate has the tone of a counting-house, which is a useful reminder that even enchantment must balance its books.

Pen stepped forward to show his order; but at the aperture before him a gentleman and two ladies were already in noisy parley. The gentleman’s hat was slanted rakishly, his cloak was shabby but worn with a boldness that tried to make shabbiness a fashion; and he was crying out in a voice Pen knew as one knows a tune that has been played too often in one’s rooms.

“Bedad, sir, if ye doubt me honour, will ye obleege me by stipping out of that box, and——”

“Lor, Capting!” cried the elder lady.

“Don’t bother me,” said the man in the box, sulkily, with the air of a person whose chief pleasure in life is refusing people.

“And ask Mr. Hodgen himself,” the gentleman continued, “who’s in the gyardens, to let these leedies pass. Don’t be froightened, me dear madam; I’m not going to quarl with this gintleman—at anyreet not before leedies. Will ye go, sir, and desoire Mr. Hodgen, with Captain Costigan’s compliments, to stip out and let in the leedies—for meself, sir, I’ve seen Vauxhall, and I scawrun any interfayrance on moi account: but for these leedies—one of them has never been there—sure ye’d not take advantage of me misfartune in losing the ticket, to deproive her of her pleasure?”

“It ain’t no use, Captain. I can’t go about your business,” the check-taker answered.

The Captain swore; the elder lady echoed him in a milder dialect. The younger, who had been bearing up bravely, looked up at the Captain and said, “Never mind, Captain Costigan, I’m sure I don’t want to go at all. Come away, mamma.” Which, being the speech of disappointment, ended as such speeches often end: her mouth quivered, her courage broke, and she began to cry.

“Me poor child!” cried Costigan, instantly turning paternal and tragic. “Can ye see that, sir, and will ye not let this innocent creature in?”

“It ain’t my business,” said the doorkeeper, peevishly; and at that moment Pen came up, recognised the Captain, and spoke.

“Don’t you know me, Captain? Pendennis!”

“My dear boy! Me dear friend!” cried Costigan, stretching out his hand as if he would clasp all mankind when he was in trouble. He poured out the tale of “a most unluckee conthratong.” He had had an order from Mr. Hodgen—who was within, and would sing “The Body Snatcher,” “The Death of General Wolfe,” “The Banner of Blood,” and other tender domestic songs—admitting two. Captain Costigan, by a natural extension of arithmetic, had concluded that “two” might be made to include “three,” and had brought two ladies accordingly. Worse: the order was lost upon the road; the paper had vanished; and here were the leedies, to be turned away like last year’s fashions.

Pen’s good nature, always ready like a knife at supper, flew out at once. He had seen the girl’s appealing face and the sudden helpless tears; and though he had talked of being used up, the sight of a young creature cheated of a pleasure by a slip of paper touched him more than all his own rhetoric. If it had been his last guinea, he would have paid it to dry those eyes. The girl turned her face away when she saw a stranger looking at her grief, and dabbed at her cheeks with a little handkerchief.

Arthur stood there very handsome and kind—hat off, blushing, bowing, generous, a gentleman. “Who are they?” he asked himself; and he fancied he had seen the elder woman somewhere in his daily comings and goings.

“If I can be of any service, Captain Costigan,” Pen said, “I hope you will command me. Is there any difficulty about taking these ladies in? Will you kindly make use of my purse? And—and I have a ticket myself that will admit two—I hope, ma’am, you will permit me?”

His first instinct was to pay for the whole party and throw away his own order in some gallant manner; but the elder woman’s look—and a certain sensible air about both of them—warned him that they would rather not be patronised with too grand a flourish. So he handed his purse to Costigan, produced his ticket with a laugh, and offered his arm to the elder lady with the politeness he could afford to anybody in a petticoat, the humbler the wearer, the more ceremonial his civility.

“Fanny, take the gentleman’s arm,” said the elder. “Since you will be so very kind. I’ve seen you often come in at our gate, sir, and go in to Captain Strong’s at No. 3.”

Fanny made a curtsey and slipped her hand under Arthur’s arm. The glove was shabby, but the hand itself was small and pretty, with that soft, warmed life in it that makes a young man suddenly aware he is not a statue. She was not a child, but scarcely a woman yet; her tears dried quickly, as young tears do, and her cheeks took on a blush as if pleasure, once admitted, rushed to the face like a guilty secret. She looked up with gratitude and delight.

Arthur, in a protecting way, laid his other hand lightly over hers as it rested on his sleeve. “Fanny’s a pretty name,” he said. “And you know me, do you?”

“We keep the lodge, sir, at Shepherd’s Inn,” Fanny answered, curtseying again. “And I’ve never been at Vauxhall, sir, and Papa didn’t like me to go—and—and—O—O—law, how beautiful!”

For the Gardens burst upon her all at once—an eruption of lamps, avenues strewn with light, domes and arches and gilded nonsense set shining against the night. The air seemed to change flavour at the threshold: outside, river damp and soot; inside, sugar, smoke, roasted meat, spilled ale, crushed herbs underfoot, and a faint metallic tang from the fireworks’ promised chemistry. The crowd’s voices rose and fell like surf; and threaded through it all were the birdlike calls of vendors, the clink of glasses, a distant fiddle, and that steady human roar which is London’s true national anthem.

Fanny shrank back in wonder and then leaned closer, as if the brightness might carry her off unless a gentleman anchored her. Pen was pleased with her pleasure and pressed her little hand gently to his sleeve. “What would I not give for a little of this pleasure?” thought the blasé young man, who suddenly discovered his blasé-ness had been mislaid like Costigan’s ticket.

Behind them came Captain Costigan’s voice, rich with triumph. “Your purse, Pendennis, me dear boy—will ye count it? it’s all roight—no—ye thrust in old Jack Costigan—he thrusts me, ye see, madam. Ye’ve been me preserver, Pen—I’ve known um since choildhood, Mrs. Bolton; he’s the proproietor of Fairoaks Castle, and many’s the cooper of clart I’ve dthrunk there with the first nobilitee of his neetive countee—Mr. Pendennis, ye’ve been me preserver, and me daughtther will thank ye—Mr. Simpson, your humble servant, sir.”

If Pen was magnificent in courtesy to the women, what was Costigan’s splendour? He bowed to lamps, to singers, to passers-by, to the idea of Vauxhall itself; he cried bravo before anyone began; he took the very air as an applause due to him.

A man descended, like Costigan, from a long line of Hibernian kings, chieftains, and other magistrates of imagination, had of course too much dignity to walk arm-in-arm with a lady who occasionally swept his room and fried his mutton-chops. On the way from Shepherd’s Inn he had marched beside Mrs. Bolton and Miss Fanny in a patronising manner, pointing out buildings, discoursing upon foreign parts he had visited (at least in narrative), and dropping names of people of rank as if they were raisins in a pudding. And now, within the illuminated property, he could not be expected to relax his grandeur and lend an arm to a woman who was, in truth, little better than a housekeeper or charwoman.

Pen, for his part, had no such scruples. Miss Fanny Bolton did not make his bed nor sweep his chambers; and, besides, he was not inclined to let go a pretty companion whose astonishment made his own evening seem fresher. As for Fanny, her colour heightened; her eyes shone; and she looked at other ladies strolling under other gentlemen’s protection and decided, with that quick, sweet injustice of youth, that her gentleman was handsomer than any in the Gardens.

There were votaries of pleasure of every sort—rakish young surgeons, fast young clerks, commercial gentry bent on wasting their week’s conscience, a stray dandy of the Guards—while at a supper-box you might see a fine lady nibbling an ice as if it were a sin, and in a darker walk a poor girl laughing as if laughter were her only gown. Old Lord Colchicum was there in attendance upon Mademoiselle Caracoline, the equestrian celebrity from the ring, who spoke her native French very loud and used idioms of remarkable strength, as if she were cracking whips even in conversation.

Colchicum leaned upon Caracoline; and little Tom Tufthunt leaned upon Colchicum. There is never a Don Juan without a Leporello to hold the ladder, and Tom was delighted with his post. He would carve the fowl, make the salad, run errands, admire everything, and feel himself in the very centre of fashionable vice.

When Pen, with Fanny on his arm, crossed near that party, Lord Colchicum gave Arthur only a passing leer of recognition; his lordship’s eyes travelled at once from Pen’s face to the bonnet that sheltered Pen’s companion, as a connoisseur’s eye leaves the frame to examine the picture. Tom Tufthunt, however, nodded with genial impudence. “How are you, old boy?” he said, with a knowing look at the historian of these matters.

“That is the great rider at Astley’s; I have seen her there,” Miss Bolton whispered, looking after Mademoiselle Caracoline. “And who is that old man? Is it not the gentleman in the ring?”

“That is Lord Viscount Colchicum, Miss Fanny,” said Pen, with an air of protection, meaning no harm, and even enjoying that she should be impressed and that a noble Don Juan should have seen her leaning on his arm.

Fanny was very pretty: dark brilliant eyes, teeth like little pearls, a mouth fresh and red—not painted scarlet like Caracoline’s vermilion, but naturally coloured as if it had just tasted raspberry. And what a difference between the girl’s voice and the woman’s; between the girl’s laugh, which rang clear as a spoon against a glass, and the woman’s, which was all practised sparkle! Only lately, indeed, had Fanny—dusting the little glass over the Bows-Costigan mantelpiece—begun to suspect she might be a beauty. A year ago she had been a gawky schoolgirl, sneered at by her father and overlooked by the girls at Miss Minifer’s day-school in Newcastle Street; and it was in the porter’s dim lodge at Shepherd’s Inn, unnoticed as a herb growing in shade, that she bloomed.

So she hung upon Mr. Pen’s arm, and they paced the Gardens. Empty as London was, there were still two million people left behind; and among them were one or two of Arthur’s acquaintances.

Among these, silent and alone, pale, hands in pockets, passed Henry Foker, Esq., giving Pen a rueful nod. Young Henry wandered from place to place, from light to light, trying to shake off thought; but Blanche sat on his heart like a stone in a pocket. He thought of her in the dark walks; he thought of her in the lamp devices. He even consulted a fortune-teller and was disappointed when the gipsy told him he loved a dark lady who would make him happy—a prophecy as impertinent as it was inaccurate. At the concert, though Mr. Momus sang his most stunning comic songs and asked his most astonishing riddles, no smile came to visit Foker’s lips. In fact, he never heard Mr. Momus at all; the loudest joke cannot compete with a quiet grief.

Pen and Miss Bolton stood near listening to the same concert; she noticed Foker’s woebegone face, and Pen laughed at it.

“What makes that odd-looking little man so dismal?” Fanny asked.

“I think he is crossed in love,” Pen said. “Isn’t that enough to make any man dismal, Fanny?” And he looked down at her with that grand protecting air he liked to practise—like a hero in a play who is sure the audience admires his tenderness.

“Crossed in love is he? poor gentleman,” said Fanny, with a sigh; and her eyes went kindly towards Harry with a pity so honest it would have startled him—had he seen it. But he did not.

“How dy do, Mr. Pendennis!” broke in a voice; and there stood a young man in a large white coat and red neckcloth, with a dingy shirt-collar turned down to display a dubious neck. A big pin of bullion or something pretending to be bullion glittered on him; his waistcoat was imaginative with glass buttons; his trousers cried aloud, “Come look at me and see how cheap and tawdry I am; my master, what a dirty buck!” He had a little stick in one pocket and a lady in pink satin on the other arm.

“How dy do—Forget me, I dare say? Huxter,—Clavering.”

“How do you do, Mr. Huxter?” said Pen, with his most princely manner. “I hope you are very well.”

“Pretty bobbish, thanky,” said Mr. Huxter, wagging his head. “I say, Pendennis, you’ve been coming it uncommon strong since we had the row at Wapshot’s, don’t you remember. Great author, hay? Go about with the swells. Saw your name in the Morning Post. I suppose you’re too much of a swell to come and have a bit of supper with an old friend? Charterhouse Lane to-morrow night,—some devilish good fellows from Bartholomew’s, and some stunning gin-punch. Here’s my card.”

And Mr. Huxter, releasing his hand from the pocket where his cane lived, pulled off the top of his card-case with his teeth and produced a visiting-ticket, which he offered to Arthur with the generosity of a man who believes he bestows distinction.

“You are exceedingly kind, I am sure,” Pen said, taking it with a lofty courtesy; “but I regret that I have an engagement which will take me out of town to-morrow night.” And he slipped the card into his waistcoat pocket, wondering how such a creature could have the audacity to distribute his pasteboard to anybody. Mr. Huxter’s father, after all, was a surgeon and apothecary at Clavering, as Pen’s had been at Bath; but the impudence of some men—especially when helped by bad company and worse punch—defies arithmetic.

“Well, old fellow, never mind,” said Mr. Huxter, always frank and now, from vinous excitement, more familiar than ever. “If ever you are passing, look up our place, I’m mostly at home Saturdays; and there’s generally a cheese cupboard. Ta, ta.—There’s the bell for the fireworks ringing. Come along, Mary.”

He ran off with the crowd towards the fireworks.

When he was out of sight, Pen too began to run with his little companion, drawn forward by the promise of rockets and by the delicious way Fanny’s hand clung to his arm as if he were her safeguard in the bright bewilderment. Mrs. Bolton followed, calling and hurrying; and at her side marched Captain Costigan, too majestic to run for friend or enemy, preserving his jaunty swagger as if dignity were a uniform not to be disordered even by fireworks—so that, in a moment, he and his companion were left behind, and Pen and Miss Fanny were carried onward into the noisy, scented, glittering heart of Vauxhall.
