The Mother of Sorrows rose, her face veiled, and went to wake the Little Ones. They slept as if untouched by time, yet at her voice they stirred—awakening fresh and bright, as though newly made. With cheerful steps, they followed her down the stairs, their small feet echoing in the quiet cottage. She led them to where the princess lay, still weeping in her sleep, her tears tracing paths like silver rivers down her cheeks.

The children’s joyful faces turned solemn as they gazed upon her. One looked from the princess to the storm-wracked sky beyond the window. “The sky is falling!” he declared.

“Look,” another whispered, eyes wide with awe, “the white juice is running out of the princess!”

“Is it rivers?” asked Odu, tilting his head, staring at the thin streams that ran from her hollowed cheeks.

“Yes,” Mara replied softly, “the most wonderful rivers of all.”

“But I thought rivers were bigger,” Odu said, turning to me—his only source of such knowledge—“like a thousand Little Ones rushing together, making loud noises!”

“Look at the rivers of the sky,” Mara said gently. “See how they fall to awaken the waters beneath the earth. Soon, rivers will flow everywhere—merry, loud, like countless happy children. Oh, how glad you’ll be when you see them! You’ve never known water, but it is the loveliest thing in the world.”

“That must be the gladness of the ground,” Odu mused, “because the princess has grown good.”

“See the gladness of the sky!” said another.

“Are these rivers the princess’s gladness?” Luva asked.

“They are not her blood, for they are not red,” Mara answered.

“They are the juice within the juice,” she added.

Odu touched his eye, studied his fingertip, then shook his head. “Princess won’t bite now,” he said.

“No,” Mara agreed. “She will never do that again. But now we must take her closer to home.”

“Is that a nest?” Sozo asked.

“A very big one,” Mara said. “But first, we must go somewhere else.”

“What is that place?”

“The biggest room in all this world. But I think it will soon be pulled down—it will be too full of little nests. Go and fetch your clumsies.”

“Please—are there any cats in it?” Luva asked.

“Not one,” Mara said. “The nests are too full of lovely dreams for even one cat to enter.”

“We’ll be ready in a minute,” Odu called, darting out, followed by all but Luva.

Lilith was awake now, her eyes open, watching us with a quiet, sorrowful smile.

“But her rivers are flowing so fast,” Luva murmured, standing beside her, unable to look away. “Her robe is all… I don’t know what. The clumsies won’t like it!”

“They won’t mind,” Mara said. “Those rivers are so clean—they cleanse the whole world.”

I had been asleep by the fire, but had woken long ago and listened. Now I rose.

“It is time to mount, Mr. Vane,” our hostess said.

“Tell me,” I asked, “is there no way to avoid the channels and the den of monsters?”

“There is an easy path across the riverbed,” she replied. “I will show you. But you must pass through the monsters once more.”

“I fear for the children,” I said.

“Fear will not come near them,” she answered.

We left the cottage. The beasts waited by the door. Odu was already mounted on one of the two elephants meant to carry the princess. I climbed onto Lona’s horse. Mara lifted Lilith’s body and placed it into my arms. When she returned with the princess, the children cried out in delight—she was no longer veiled!

Gazing at her, entranced by her beauty, the boys forgot to receive her. But the elephants stepped forward, lifting Lilith gently with their trunks—one around her waist, one around her knees—and with Mara’s help, laid her between them.

“Why does the princess want to go?” a small boy asked. “She’d stay good if she stayed here!”

“She wants to go, and she doesn’t want to go,” Mara said. “We are helping her. She cannot stay here.”

“What are you helping her to do?”

“To go where she can receive more help—help to open her hand, which has been closed for a thousand years.”

“So long?” the boy said. “Then she’s learned to live without it. Why open it now?”

“Because it holds something that is not hers.”

“Please, Lady Mara,” Luva said, “may we have some of your very dry bread before we go?”

Mara smiled and brought four loaves and a great jug of water. “We’ll eat as we walk,” the children said—but they drank the water with joy.

“This must be elephant-juice!” one exclaimed. “It makes me so strong!”

We set out. The Lady of Sorrow walked beside us, more radiant than the sun, with the white leopardess trailing behind. I thought she would only guide us to the path across the channels, but soon I realized she would accompany us all the way. I offered to dismount so she could ride, but she refused.

“I carry no burden,” she said. “The children and I will walk together.”

It was the most beautiful morning—the sun shone brightly, the wind blew sweetly—but the desert remained dry, lifeless. We crossed the channels easily, the children dancing around Mara, until we reached the ridge above the bad burrow just as the sun began to vanish.

I made the Little Ones mount their elephants—fearful the moon might delay. The Lady of Sorrow now walked beside me, the elephants following—those carrying the princess in the center, the leopardess bringing up the rear. As we neared the edge of the basin, the moon rose, pale and hesitant, revealing the shallow, silent depression before us.

Mara stepped into it. No ripple stirred beneath her feet or under my horse. But the moment the elephants bearing the princess touched the ground, the earth began to heave and boil. From the depths, the monstrous brood stirred—hideous creatures rising on all sides, necks stretched, claws extended, mouths agape.

Long-billed heads, gnarled faces, writhing tentacles surged toward Lilith. She lay in terror, paralyzed. Whether the creatures saw the children, I did not know—but none touched them. Not one vile limb passed the living wall of her guardians.

“Little Ones,” I shouted, “keep close to the princess. Be brave—they will not harm you.”

“What will not harm us?” they asked. “We don’t know what to be brave against!”

And then I understood: they could not see the horrors. Their blindness was their protection. What they could not perceive could not wound them.

Yet I saw them—those awful forms! Mara walked ahead of me when suddenly a bodiless head bounced between us. The leopardess lunged from behind, but the creature twisted with a hideous shriek, vanished into the ground.

The death in my arms calmed my fear. I stood unmoved, though never had I seen such a cursed gathering.

Mara continued forward. The leopardess now walked close behind her, trembling often, for the night was cold. Then, to my left, the earth began to rise—a wave of soil slithering toward us. It grew taller, and from it emerged a terrible head with fleshy tubes for hair, its mouth gaping wide, snapping at me.

The leopardess sprang—but fell short. Almost beneath our hooves, a colossal snake reared up, its eyes glowing with a sickly light. Again the leopardess charged—only to find nothing. A third time she attacked, failed, and gave up.

But I knew the danger. I urged haste. The moon, too, seemed strange—rising weakly, sinking back as if struggling to remain. Its arc was low, and it now began to descend quickly.

We were nearly across when, between us and the edge, a long neck rose. Atop it sat a corpse-like head, half-open mouth filled with jagged teeth.

I pressed on. It retreated, then stepped aside.

The lady stepped onto firm land. The leopardess, roused again, turned and flew at the monster’s throat.

I paused to ensure the elephants, with the princess and children, reached safety. Then I turned to watch the leopardess.

That instant, the moon disappeared.

For a breath, I saw the leopardess and the serpent tangled in a cloud of dust. Then darkness swallowed them.

Trembling, my horse wheeled and raced to catch up with the elephants.

As we approached, a shapeless jelly dropped onto the princess. Instantly, a white dove swooped down, pecking it with its beak. It squelched, sucked, and fell away.

Then I heard a woman’s voice speaking with Mara. I recognized it.

“I fear she is dead,” Mara said.

“I will send to find her,” the mother replied. “But why should you fear? Death cannot harm one who dies doing the work given her.”

“I shall miss her deeply,” Mara said. “She is wise and good. Yet I would not have her live beyond her time.”

“She has gone down with the wicked,” the mother said. “She will rise with the righteous. We shall see her again soon.”

“Mother,” I said, though I could not see her, “we come to you many—most are Little Ones. Will you welcome us all?”

“You are welcome,” she answered. “Sooner or later, all become little ones. All must sleep in my house. It is well to sleep young and willingly. My husband prepares Lilith’s couch. She is neither young nor fully willing—but it is well she has come.”

I heard no more. Mother and daughter vanished into the dark.

But ahead, a light glimmered. Toward it we stumbled over the moor.

Adam stood in the doorway, candle in hand, guiding us. He spoke with his wife, who behind him placed bread and wine on the table.

“Happy children,” I heard her say, “to have seen my daughter’s face! Truly, it is the loveliest in the world.”

When we reached the door, Adam welcomed us—almost cheerfully. He set the candle on the threshold, then went to the elephants to take the princess. But she pushed him away, stepping between them, erect and proud. The elephants moved aside, leaving her alone with the man who had once been her husband—ashamed of her gaunt form, yet unyielding.

He stood, his eyes shining with a fierce welcome beneath their sternness.

“We have long waited for thee, Lilith,” he said.

She gave no reply.

Eve and her daughter came to the door.

“The mortal foe of my children!” Eve murmured, radiant in her beauty.

“Your children are no longer in danger,” Mara said. “She has turned from evil.”

“Do not trust her too soon, Mara,” Eve answered. “She has deceived many.”

“But will you open the mirror of the Law of Liberty, mother? Let her enter it and abide there! She consents to open her hand and restore. Will not the great Father restore her to His children?”

“I do not know Him,” Lilith whispered, voice trembling with doubt.

“Therefore you are miserable,” Adam said.

“I will return whence I came!” she cried, wringing her hands, turning to leave.

“That is exactly what I would have you do—return to Him from whom you came. In your agony, did you not cry out for Him?”

“I cried for Death—to escape Him and you!”

“Death is already coming to lead you to Him. You know neither Death nor the Life that dwells in Death. Both are friends to you. I am dead—and I love you. You are weary, burdened—ashamed? Is the being you corrupted not finally become an evil to you? Would you endure eternal disgrace? You cannot cease—will you not be restored and BE?”

She stood silent, head bowed.

“Father,” Mara said, “take her in your arms and carry her to her bed. There she will open her hand and die into life.”

“I will walk,” the princess said.

Adam turned and led the way. She followed, weakly.

Eve came to me where I sat with Lona in my arms. She took the child from me and carried her inside.

I dismounted. The children did too. The horse and elephants shivered. Mara stroked each, and they lay down, sleeping.

She led us into the cottage and gave the children bread and wine.

Adam and Lilith stood together—silent.

Eve returned from the chamber of death, where she had laid Lona. She offered food to the princess.

“Your beauty kills me! I seek death, not food!” Lilith said, turning away.

“This food will help you die,” Eve replied.

But Lilith refused.

“If you will not eat nor drink,” Adam said, “come and see where you will lie in peace.”

He led the way through the door of death. She followed, subdued.

But when her foot crossed the threshold, she drew it back, pressing her hand to her chest—struck by the cold immortal.

A wild wind howled across the roof, then died into a moan.

She stood pale with terror.

“It is he!” her lips formed—voiceless, but I read them.

“Who, princess?” I whispered.

“The great Shadow,” she murmured.

“He cannot enter here,” Adam said. “Here, no one can be harmed. Even over him, power is given me.”

“Are the children in the house?” Lilith asked.

At that word, Eve’s heart softened.

“He never dared touch a child,” she said. “Nor have you ever harmed one. Your own daughter—you sent her into the loveliest sleep. She was already dead when you killed her. And now, Death shall be the atonemaker. You shall sleep together.”

“Wife,” Adam said, “let us first put the children to bed—so she may see them safe.”

He returned to fetch them.

As soon as he left, the princess knelt before Eve, clasped her knees.

“Beautiful Eve,” she said, “persuade your husband to kill me. He will listen to you. I truly wish to open my hand—but I cannot.”

“You cannot die without opening it,” Eve replied. “To kill you would not help you.”

“But he cannot—no one can kill you but the Shadow. And whom he kills never knows she is dead. She lives to do his will, thinking it her own.”

“Then show me my grave,” Lilith said. “I am so weary—I cannot live longer. I must go to the Shadow—yet I do not want to.”

She did not understand. She tried to rise, but collapsed at Eve’s feet.

The Mother lifted her and carried her inward.

I followed Adam, Mara, and the children into the chamber of death. We passed Eve holding Lilith, and went deeper.

“You shall not go to the Shadow,” I heard Eve say.

“Even now, his head is under my heel.”

The dim light in Adam’s hand flickered over sleeping faces. As he moved forward, darkness swallowed them.

The air felt dead—was it because none breathed it?

Profound sleep filled the vast space. It was as if no one had awakened since I last visited. The forms I remembered still lay there.

My father rested as I had left him—closer now to perfect peace. The woman beside him looked younger.

The darkness, the cold, the silence, the stillness, the faces of the lovely dead—made the children’s hearts beat softly, but their voices whispered.

“What a curious place to sleep!” one said. “I’d rather be in my nest.”

“It’s so cold!” another complained.

“Yes,” Adam said, “but you won’t feel cold in sleep.”

“Where are our nests?” several asked, looking around.

“Find places,” Adam replied. “Sleep wherever you choose.”

They scattered instantly, moving beyond the light with fearless steps. Yet we still heard their soft voices—they saw what I could not.

“Oh!” one cried. “Such a beautiful lady! May I sleep beside her? I’ll creep in quietly and not wake her.”

“Yes, you may,” Eve said behind us.

We reached the couch as the child crept under the sheet. He laid his head beside the lady’s, looked up at us, and fell still. His eyelids closed—he slept.

We walked farther. Another child had climbed onto a woman’s couch.

“Mother! mother!” he cried, kneeling beside her. “She’s so cold—she can’t speak,” he said, looking up. “But I’ll make her warm!”

He lay down, pressed close, and wrapped his arm around her. In seconds, he slept—smiling with absolute content.

We came to Luva. She stood on tiptoe, leaning over a couch.

“My own mother wouldn’t have me,” she whispered. “Will you?”

No reply. She looked up at Eve.

The great mother lifted her to the couch. She slid beneath the snowy covering.

Each child, save three boys, found a resting place beside a still, white woman. The little orphans had chosen mothers.

One tiny girl chose a father—mine.

A boy lay beside the matron with the slow-healing hand.

On the middle of three empty couches, Lona rested.

Eve set Lilith beside her.

Adam pointed to the vacant couch on Lona’s right.

“There, Lilith,” he said, “is the bed I have prepared for you.”

She glanced at her daughter—like a statue carved from alabaster—and shuddered.

“How cold it is,” she murmured.

“You will soon grow comfortable in the cold,” Adam said.

“Promises to the dying are easy,” she replied. “But I know it. I have slept. I am dead.”

“I believed you dead long ago,” Adam said. “But I see you alive.”

“More alive than you know,” she said. “I was scarce alive when you first knew me. Now I have slept—and awakened. I am dead—and truly live.”

“I fear that child,” she whispered, pointing to Lona. “She will rise and terrify me.”

“She is dreaming love for you.”

“But the Shadow!” she moaned. “I fear the Shadow—he will be angry with me!”

“He whose sight makes the horses of heaven rear and flee—dares not disturb a dream in this quiet chamber.”

“I shall dream then?”

“You will dream.”

“What dreams?”

“I cannot tell—but none HE can enter. When the Shadow comes here, it will be to sleep also. His hour will come—and he knows it.”

“How long shall I sleep?”

“You and he will be the last to wake in the morning of the universe.”

The princess lay down, drew the sheet over her, stretched straight, and stared with open eyes.

Adam turned to his daughter.

“Lilith,” Mara said, “you will not sleep—no matter how long you lie—if you do not open your hand and give up what is not yours.”

“I cannot,” she said.

“I would, if I could. I am weary. The shadows of death gather about me.”

“They will gather—but they cannot enfold you while your hand remains shut. You may think you are dead—but it is only a dream. You may think you are awake—but it is still a dream. Open your hand, and you will sleep truly—then wake truly.”

“I am trying,” she said. “But my fingers have grown together, fused into the palm.”

“Then use the strength of your will,” Mara urged. “For the love of life, draw all your forces and break the bonds!”

“I have struggled in vain,” Lilith said. “I can do no more. I am very weary. Sleep weighs heavy on my lids.”

“The moment you open your hand, you will sleep. Open it. Make an end.”

A flush rose on her parchment-like face. Her contorted hand trembled with effort.

Mara took it, seeking to help.

“Hold, Mara!” Adam said. “There is danger!”

Lilith turned her eyes to Eve, pleading.

“There was a sword,” she murmured, “once in your husband’s hand. I fled when I saw it. I heard him say it would divide whatever was not one and indivisible.”

“I have the sword,” Adam said. “The angel gave it to me when he left the gate.”

“Bring it, Adam,” Lilith pleaded. “Cut off this hand—so I may sleep.”

“I will,” he said.

He gave the candle to Eve and went.

The princess closed her eyes.

Minutes passed. Adam returned, holding an ancient weapon. The scabbard looked like aged vellum, but the hilt shone like untarnished gold. He drew the blade—it flashed like a pale blue northern streamer. The light made Lilith open her eyes.

She saw the sword, shuddered, and held out her hand.

Adam took it. The blade gleamed once. A small spurt of blood—then he laid the severed hand in Mara’s lap.

Lilith gave one moan—and was already deep in sleep.

Mara covered the arm with the sheet. The three turned away.

“Will you not dress the wound?” I asked.

“A wound from that sword,” Adam said, “needs no dressing. It is healing, not hurting.”

“Poor lady,” I said. “She will wake with only one hand.”

“Where the dead deformity clung,” Mara replied, “the true, lovely hand is already growing.”

We heard a childish voice behind us. We turned.

Eve’s candle lit Lilith’s sleeping face—and the waking faces of three Little Ones grouped on the other side of her couch.

“How beautiful she has become!” one said.

“Poor princess,” another murmured. “I’ll sleep with her. She won’t bite anymore!”

He climbed into her bed and fell asleep instantly.

Eve covered him.

“I’ll sleep on her other side,” the third said. “She’ll have two to kiss her when she wakes!”

“And I’m left alone!” the first said mournfully.

“I’ll put you to bed,” Eve said. She handed the candle to her husband and led the child away.

We turned to return to the cottage. I was sad—no one had offered me a place in the house of the dead.

Eve joined us and walked ahead with her husband. Mara walked beside me, cradling Lilith’s hand in her robe.

“Ah, you’ve found her!” we heard Eve say as we entered the cottage.

The door stood open. Two elephant-trunks emerged from the night.

“I sent them with the lantern,” Eve said to her husband. “To search for Mara’s leopardess. They’ve brought her.”

I followed Adam to the door. Between us, we took the white creature from the elephants and carried her to the chamber we had just left—women leading the way, Eve with the light, Mara still holding the hand.

There, we laid the leopardess across the feet of the princess, her paws outstretched, her head resting between them.
