Between the realities that shape our fate and the illusions that merely decorate them, how must a discerning mind judge? When the calculating, unyielding intellect of Sigsbee Manderson was shattered by a bullet from an unseen hand, the world lost nothing of consequence worthy of a tear; indeed, it gained a stark, memorable lesson in the vanity of amassed wealth, for the dead man had accumulated fortunes without securing a single loyal friend to mourn him or perform an act to honor his memory. Yet, when word of his demise spread, it seemed to those inhabiting the great vortices of commerce that the very earth shuddered under the blow.

Throughout the lurid commercial annals of his nation, no figure had so dominated the collective consciousness of the trading world. He occupied a niche apart in its temples. While financial giants, powerful in directing capital and charging millions for their labor, had existed before, Manderson possessed a singularity: a pale halo of piratical romance clung to his head, a sentiment especially cherished by his countrymen, despite his role as the unquestioned guardian of stability, the crusher of manipulated crises, and the scourge of the raiding chieftains plaguing the borders of Wall Street.

His fortune, inherited from a grandfather who had been a buccaneer of modest scale, passed to him through a father who quietly lent money without ever margining stocks. Manderson, always possessing vast sums at his disposal, should have belonged entirely to that newer American plutocracy steadied by the traditions of great wealth. It was not so. Though his upbringing and education instilled in him European notions of a rich man's proper exterior—the quiet magnificence that does not shriek for attention—he nonetheless carried the spirit of the Forty-Niner and the financial rover. During what was termed his "early bad manner," he was little more than a gambler of genius, a prodigy whose hand was raised against every man's, bringing a brain superior to any opposition to the enthralling pursuit of speculation. It was at St Helena that war was defined as *une belle occupation*; thus, the young Manderson found his battlefield in the multitudinous, complex dog-fights of the New York Stock Exchange.

Then came his transformation. Upon his father's death, when Manderson was thirty, some new revelation regarding the power and glory of the deity he served appeared to strike him. With the sudden, elastic adaptability characteristic of his nation, he turned to steady labor in his father's banking business, closing his ears to the roar of street battles. In a few short years, he came to control the entire activity of the great firm whose unimpeachable conservatism, safety, and financial weight lifted it like a cliff above the angry sea of the markets. All mistrust founded on his youthful performances vanished. He was plainly a different man. No authority could explain how the change occurred, though stories circulated of final words spoken by his father, the sole figure Manderson had truly respected or perhaps loved.

He began to tower above the financial landscape. Soon, his name echoed in the bourses of the globe. To speak Manderson's name conjured a vision of all that was broad-based and firm in the vast wealth of the United States. He planned grand combinations of capital, centralizing industries of continental scope, financing the large designs of states and private enterprises with unerring judgment. Often, when he "took hold" to crush a strike or federate the ownership of great fields of labor, he sent ruin upon countless tiny homes; if miners, steelworkers, or cattlemen defied him and invoked disorder, he proved more lawless and ruthless than they. Yet, these acts were pursued in the name of legitimate business. Tens of thousands of the poor might curse his name, but the financier and the speculator execrated him no longer. Stretching a hand to protect or manipulate the power of wealth in every corner of the country, forcible, cold, and unerring, he ministered to the national lust for magnitude, and a grateful country surnamed him the Colossus.

Yet, there was an aspect of Manderson in this later period that lay long hidden, known only to his secretaries, lieutenants, and certain associates of his former hurling days. This small circle knew that Manderson, the pillar of sound business and market stability, harbored hours of nostalgia for the lively times when the Street trembled at his whisper. As one confidant noted, it was as if Blackbeard had settled down as a decent merchant in Bristol on the spoils of the Main. Now and then, the pirate would glare suddenly outward, the knife in his teeth, sulphur matches sputtering in his hatband. During these spasms of reverting to type, scores of tempestuous raids upon the market were plotted in the inner rooms of Manderson, Colefax and Company. They were never executed. Blackbeard would quell the mutiny of his old self within him and descend soberly to his counting-house—perhaps humming a stanza or two of "Spanish Ladies" under his breath. Manderson would permit himself the harmless satisfaction, once the window for action had closed, of pointing out to some Rupert of the markets a coup worth a million to a depredator might have been made. "Seems to me," he would say with a wistful sigh, "the Street is getting to be a mighty dull place since I quit." By slow degrees, this amiable weakness of the Colossus became known to the business world, which exulted greatly in the knowledge.

At the news of his death, panic swept through the markets like a hurricane, for it fell at a luckless hour. Prices tottered and crashed like towers in an earthquake. For two days, Wall Street burned as a clamorous inferno of pale despair. Across the United States, wherever speculation had its devotees, drifted a waft of ruin, a plague of suicide. Even in Europe, not a few ended the lives that had become pitiably linked to the destiny of a financier most of them had never seen. In Paris, a well-known banker walked quietly from the Bourse and fell dead upon the broad steps amidst a raving crowd of Jews, a phial crushed in his hand. In Frankfort, one leapt from the Cathedral top, staining the red tower with a deeper crimson. Men stabbed, shot, and strangled themselves, drinking death or breathing it as the air, because in a lonely corner of England, life had departed from one cold heart vowed to the service of greed.

The blow could not have fallen at a more disastrous moment. It arrived when Wall Street was in a condition of suppressed "scare"—suppressed, because for a week past the great interests acting under or actually controlled by the Colossus had been desperately combatiing the effects of the sudden arrest of Lucas Hahn and the exposure of his plundering of the Hahn banks. This bombshell, in turn, had fallen when the market had been "boosted" beyond its real strength. In the jargon of the place, a slump was imminent. Reports from the corn-lands had been poor, and two or three railway statements had fallen short of expectations. But at whatever point in the vast area of speculation the shudder of the threatened break was felt, "the Manderson crowd" had stepped in to hold the market aloft. Throughout the week, the speculator's mind, shallow yet quick-witted, sentimental yet greedy, saw in this the hand of the giant stretching out in protection from afar. Manderson, according to the newspapers in chorus, was in hourly communication with his lieutenants in the Street. One journal reported the exact sum spent on cabling between New York and Marlstone in the previous twenty-four hours, noting how a small staff of expert operators had been dispatched by the Post Office authorities to Marlstone to handle the flood of messages. Another revealed that Manderson, upon the first news of the Hahn crash, had arranged to abandon his holiday and return home via the *Lusitania*, only to determine shortly thereafter that he need not return, having the situation well in hand.

All this was falsehood, more or less consciously elaborated by the "finance editors," consciously initiated and encouraged by the shrewd businessmen of the Manderson group. They knew that nothing could better aid their plans than this illusion of hero-worship—they knew equally well that no word had come from Manderson answering their messages, and that Howard B. Jeffrey, of Steel and Iron fame, was the true organizer of victory. Thus, they fought down apprehension through four feverish days, and minds grew calmer. The market was firm, slowly advancing. Wall Street turned to its Sunday sleep, worn out but thankfully at peace.

With practiced efficiency, Silver abandoned his paperwork and moved to the ringing instrument.

Silver held the receiver aloft. "Mr. Bunner speaks to you directly. He claims it is a matter of grave importance."

Sir James looked at the phone with a glint of professional hunger rather than affection. He snatched the receiver. "Speak, man," he commanded, his voice a resonant boom.

His thoughts were singularly focused on the prospect of sustenance. For Mr. Cupples, the colloquialism "thinking about breakfast" was literal; he engaged in deliberate contemplation of every conscious act afforded by time, including the immediate necessity of nourishment. Philip Trent, a painter of some standing in the English artistic circles, had reached the zenith of his twenties before fame truly took root. His works sold, buoyed by an original, driving talent and a work ethic that blended leisure with relentless industry, punctuated by bursts of fierce creative fervor. While his father's name and a comfortable inheritance spared him the stigma of the starving artist, Trent's true engine for success was a magnetic charm. He possessed a genial spirit and a humorous wit that endeared him instantly, coupled with a profound, albeit internal, insight into human nature. Others enjoyed him because he seemed perpetually amused, his countenance rarely losing its spark of vivacity, whether he was indulging in whimsical chatter or laboring intensely. Broadly cultured, with a specific devotion to poetry, Trent remained at thirty-two the embodiment of youth, unburdened by cynicism.

His ascent to a celebrity disproportionate to his actual output stemmed from a fleeting whim. Stumbling upon a newspaper report of a peculiar railway murder—one of those rare crimes where the circumstances defied immediate logic and two suspects were held—Trent, newly intrigued by such puzzles, immersed himself in the details. The narrative ignited a thrill in him akin to the rush of artistic inspiration. By day's end, he had penned a lengthy letter to *The Record*, modeled on Edgar Allan Poe's solution to the Mary Rogers case. He highlighted seemingly trivial facts, arranging the evidence to cast a pall of suspicion over a key witness. Sir James Molloy, the astute editor, published Trent's letter in bold type. Later that same evening, *The Sun* announced the arrest and confession of the culprit.

Sir James, a man who navigated all strata of London society, wasted no time in befriending Trent. Their rapport flourished; Trent's innate tact bridged the gap of age, making him appear eternally youthful. The roar of the printing presses in *The Record*'s basement sparked a new passion in Trent, leading Sir James to purchase a "machinery-scape" Trent produced there, a piece in the manner of Heinrich Kley. Months later, the Ilkley Mystery arrived. Sir James treated Trent to a convivial dinner and offered him a substantial sum to act as his special correspondent. "You can write," Sir James urged, "you can talk, and I can teach you the technicalities in half an hour. You have a head for mysteries. Think how it would feel to pull it off!"

Trent, initially reluctant due to a fear of the unfamiliar, conquered this hesitation through a lifelong habit of reacting against dread. He accepted the challenge. He succeeded magnificently, delivering a blow to the authorities that earned him renown. Though he retreated to painting and resisted the lure of a permanent journalistic salary, Sir James, respecting Trent's artistic integrity, did not tempt him with money. Yet, over the ensuing years, Trent was summoned repeatedly to unravel domestic and foreign enigmas. Occasionally, he declined due to his existing workload, or his rivals beat him to the truth, but his irregular association with *The Record* cemented his status as one of England's most famous names. Characteristically, the public knew almost nothing of Trent himself; he had imposed a strict silence upon himself via the Molloy papers, and other editors wisely avoided advertising his employment.

Marlstone, June 16th.

I commence this, my third and likely final bulletin concerning the Manderson homicide, with a mixture of professional satisfaction and personal revulsion. A profound sense of relief washes over me, for in my previous reports, duty compelled me to withhold critical data. Revealing those truths sooner would have alerted a cunning adversary, granting him time to evade capture. Now, I shall disclose everything. Yet, I confess a distaste for the narrative of treachery and twisted intellect I am about to recount. It leaves a bitter aftertaste, revealing a malice buried beneath the surface of the crime itself, a motive that remains as dark as the puzzle of the killing.

As recounted in my initial dispatch, I arrived at Marlstone on the Tuesday preceding the tragedy. I detailed the grim discovery of the body near the shed, the eerie silence reported by the household despite the evident struggle and gunshot, and the peculiar absence of Manderson's dental plate. I also noted the missing bottle of whisky. By the time of the inquest, I had concluded an investigation that has now pinpointed the single individual who must absolve himself of Manderson's death.

Two anomalies, obvious to any observer from the start, haunted my initial analysis. First, the total silence within the house. The butler, Martin, was notoriously insomniac and possessed ears of exceptional sensitivity; his room, situated with windows agape, overlooked the very shed where Manderson's body lay. Had anyone struggled with the victim, or fired a pistol, Martin surely would have woken. Second, Manderson's forgotten denture. To leave one's false teeth behind after rising, dressing completely, and stepping out into the night was inconceivable for a man of Manderson's habits, where oral hygiene and appearance were paramount.

Initially, these quirks offered no direction. They merely deepened the shadow of the mystery. However, a singular observation shifted the trajectory of my entire investigation.

While cataloguing Manderson's extensive collection of footwear, I focused on the pair he wore on the fatal evening. Though the shoes were high-quality, old, and polished, a close inspection revealed a telling defect. The leather around the vamp, specifically where the lacing meets the upper, had split slightly. Furthermore, the stitching securing the upper to the sole showed signs of severe strain. Such damage occurs only when a foot larger than intended forces itself into tight leather. Manderson, meticulous about his narrow feet, never wore shoes that pinched. Therefore, someone else had worn these specific shoes recently, and the tears were fresh.

This deduction triggered a cascade of realizations. Manderson was rarely drunk, yet the missing whisky suggested otherwise. He was fastidious, yet the body was found disheveled, in unwashed, yesterday's clothes, and devoid of his denture. He was taciturn, yet the "corpse" had been conversational with his wife. Most jarring was the fact that Manderson supposedly left the house without his teeth.

Every thread of evidence pointed to a single, terrifying conclusion: The man who walked out of Manderson's bedroom that night, and the man who allegedly returned later, was not Manderson.

If this were true, the man who wore Manderson's clothes, mimicked his voice, and spoke to his wife and butler must have done so to create an illusion. Why? To establish that Manderson was alive in the house until after midnight. This implied that Manderson was already dead before the intruder arrived.

The pieces fell quickly. The intruder needed to avoid footprints, so he used Manderson's shoes. He needed to prove Manderson was home, so he left the denture and the clothes. He needed to explain his presence, so he fabricated a story about sending Manderson's secretary, Marlowe, to Southampton.

A crucial oversight in my initial review emerged now: Neither Martin nor Mrs. Manderson had ever seen the intruder's face. Martin saw only the back of a man crouching by the telephone, wearing Manderson's hat, imitating a characteristic posture. Mrs. Manderson, half-asleep, heard a voice she believed to be her husband. The intruder, possessing a frame similar to Manderson's and skilled in mimicry, required no mask save for the hat and jacket.

I visualized the scene. The intruder, fresh from a violent act elsewhere, slipped into the house via the garden window, avoiding the alert Martin. He consumed the missing whisky, calming his nerves before embarking on his performance. He moved to Manderson's room, placed the body there, stripped it, and prepared the stage for his deception. When Mrs. Manderson murmured from her adjacent room, asking if the drive had been pleasant, the impostor answered in Manderson's voice, volunteering details about Marlowe's trip to Southampton—a lie designed to anchor a timeline that would protect him.

Following this hypothesis, I reconstructed the night's events. Between ten and eleven, Manderson was shot, presumably far from the house. His body was dragged to the shed and stripped. Around eleven, the impostor entered the library, disguised as Manderson, rang for the butler, and feigned a late-night phone call. He then ascended to the bedroom floor, hid Marlowe's revolver in his room, and staged the scene in Manderson's chamber. Finally, clad in Manderson's clothes, he exited through the balcony, dropping silently onto the lawn.

But who was this phantom?

Five criteria defined the suspect. He must have been intimately acquainted with Manderson, capable of perfect mimicry, physically similar in stature and shoulder width, familiar with the household routines, and possess large feet relative to Manderson's.

Mr. John Marlowe, Manderson's former private secretary, fit every bill. They were nearly identical in height and build, though Marlowe was slightly slimmer. As a former student actor at Oxford, renowned for his character roles and imitative skills, he possessed the necessary artifice. He had lived within the Manderson circle, knowing their secrets. And critically, he had a motive to fabricate Manderson's survival until after midnight.

His alibi was robust, yet fragile. He claimed to have traveled to Southampton by 6:30 AM the following morning. If he had departed Marlstone at 10:30 PM, the drive would have been impossible for the vehicle he used, a modest Northumberland, to reach Southampton by 6:30 AM. However, if Manderson died at eleven, and Marlowe assumed his identity shortly thereafter, he would need to exit the house unseen before Martin, who stayed awake until 12:30 AM, could intercept him.

How did he escape?

I spent the remainder of the day before the inquest verifying my theory. I brought a camera to White Gables. Under the guise of searching for clues, I photographed the scene. On the polished front of the dresser in Manderson's room, I found two large, distinct fingerprints. On the glass bowl holding Manderson's lost denture, and on the panes of the French window in Mrs. Manderson's room, I found additional prints.

Back at the hotel, I compared these latent prints with samples I had previously obtained from Marlowe. The match was undeniable. Two of the large prints on the dresser belonged to Marlowe's left hand; the others to his right. The prints on the window and the bowl were unequivocally his.

The evidence was irrefutable. Marlowe had been in Manderson's bedroom and Mrs. Manderson's room. He had handled the denture and the furniture. The timeline held: he entered the house, performed his charade, and fled through the balcony window, crossing the iron railing to the ground below. He had achieved his goal of making the world believe Manderson was alive until the witching hour, thereby shielding himself from suspicion of the murder which, in reality, had occurred hours prior.

By six o'clock, I had completed my analysis. By eight, the photographs confirmed the identity. By nine, I sat in my hotel room, ready to submit this truth to the authorities.

I conclude this account with absolute certainty: On the night of the murder, the man who impersonated Manderson, having disposed of the body and staged the room, lay in wait for Mrs. Manderson to fall asleep. Once assured of her silence, he crossed her threshold in stocking feet, carrying the bundle of stolen clothes, pushed open the balcony doors, and dropped to the grass below.

Within half an hour of entering the room, he was gone. The corpse was found the next morning, dressed in Manderson's attire. Marlowe appeared at Southampton at 6:30 AM, playing the role of the shocked, devoted secretary.

Sir James responded via telegram, instructing Trent to proceed at once to Kurland and Livonia, where Citizen Browning was once again abroad amidst a town and countryside blazing in revolt. It was a roving commission, and for two months Trent followed his luck. It served him not less well than usual. He was the only correspondent who witnessed General Dragilew killed in the street at Volmar by a girl of eighteen. He saw burnings, lynchings, fusillades, hangings; each day his soul sickened anew at the imbecilities born of misrule. Many nights he lay down in danger. Many days he went fasting. But there was never an evening or a morning when he did not see the face of the woman whom he hopelessly loved.

He discovered within himself an unhappy pride regarding the enduring force of this infatuation. It intrigued him as a phenomenon, amassing and enlightening him. Such a thing had not visited him before. It confirmed so many doubts he had harbored concerning the recorded experiences of men.

At thirty-two, Trent could not pretend ignorance of this world of emotion. What he had learned had come unpursued and unpurchased, devoid of intolerable memories. Broken to the realities of sex, he remained troubled by its inscrutable history. He lived with a strange respect for certain feminine weaknesses and a profound, simple terror of certain feminine strengths. He had maintained a lukewarm faith that something remained within him to be called forth, and that the voice summoning it would be heard in its own time, if ever, and not through any seeking.

Yet, he had not anticipated the possibility that, if this proved true, the truth might arrive in a sinister shape. The two elements that had utterly surprised him regarding his feelings toward Mabel Manderson were the insane suddenness of its uprising and its extravagant hopelessness. Before it struck, he had been prone to laugh at the permanence of unrequited passion as a generous boyish delusion. Now, he knew he had been wrong, and he lived bitterly with that knowledge.

In his imagination, the woman always appeared exactly as she had when he first laid eyes on her, performing the gesture he had spotted as he walked past unseen on the edge of the cliff. It was a great gesture of passionate joy in her newfound liberty, telling him more clearly than speech that her widowhood was a release from torment, and confirming with terrible force the suspicion active in his mind prior—that it was her passport to happiness with a man she loved. He could not certainly name the moment he first suspected this, but he believed the seed of the thought must have been sown at his first meeting with Marlowe. His mind would have automatically noted that such evident strength and grace, paired with the looks and manners of the tall young man, might go far with any woman of unfixed affections. The connection of this with what Mr. Cupples had told him of the Mandersons' married life must have formed itself in the unconscious depths of his mind. Certainly, it presented itself as an established fact when he began, after verifying the murderer's identity, to cast about for the motive of the crime.

*Motive, motive!* How desperately he had sought for another, turning his back upon the grim thought that Marlowe—obsessed by passion like himself, and perhaps privy to maddening truths about the wife's unhappiness—had taken a leaf, the guiltiest, from the book of Bothwell. But in all his investigations at the time, in all his broodings thereafter, he had discovered nothing to prompt Marlowe to such a deed—nothing but that temptation, the whole strength of which he could not know, but which, if it had existed, must have pressed urgently upon a bold spirit in which scruple had been somehow paralyzed. If he could trust his senses at all, the young man was neither insane nor by nature evil. But that could not clear him. Murder for a woman's sake, he thought, was not a rare crime, Heaven knew! If the modern feebleness of impulse in the comfortable classes, and their respect for the modern apparatus of detection, had made it rare among them, it was yet far from impossible. It only needed a man of equal daring and intelligence, his soul drugged with the vapors of an intoxicating intrigue, to plan and perform such a deed.

A thousand times, with a heart full of anguish, he had sought to reason away the dread that Mabel Manderson had known too much of what had been intended against her husband's life. That she knew all the truth after the act was done he could not doubt; her unforgettable collapse in his presence when the question about Marlowe was suddenly and bluntly put had swept away his last hope that there was no love between the pair, and seemed to him, moreover, to speak of dread of discovery. In any case, she knew the truth after reading what he had left with her; and it was certain that no public suspicion had been cast upon Marlowe since. She had destroyed his manuscript, then, and taken him at his word to keep the secret that threatened her lover's life.

But it was the monstrous thought that she might have known murder was brewing, and guiltily kept silence, that haunted Trent's mind. She might have suspected, have guessed something; was it conceivable that she was aware of the whole plot, that she connived? He could never forget that his first suspicion of Marlowe's motive in the crime had been roused by the fact that his escape was made through the lady's room. At that time, when he had not yet seen her, he had been ready enough to entertain the idea of her equal guilt and her cooperation. He had pictured to himself some passionate *hystérique*, merciless as a cat in her hate and her love, a zealous abettor, perhaps even the ruling spirit in the crime.

Then he had seen her, had spoken with her, had helped her in her weakness; and such suspicions, since their first meeting, had seemed the vilest of infamy. He had seen her eyes and her mouth; he had breathed the woman's atmosphere. Trent was one of those who fancy they can scent true wickedness in the air. In her presence, he had felt an inward certainty of her ultimate goodness of heart; and it was nothing against this that she had abandoned herself a moment, that day on the cliff, to the sentiment of relief at the ending of her bondage, of her years of starved sympathy and unquickened motherhood. That she had turned to Marlowe in her destitution he believed; that she had any knowledge of his deadly purpose he did not believe.

And yet, morning and evening, the sickening doubts returned, and he recalled again that it was almost in her presence that Marlowe had made his preparations in the bedroom of the murdered man, that it was by the window of her own chamber that he had escaped from the house. Had he forgotten his cunning and taken the risk of telling her then? Or had he, as Trent thought more likely, still played his part with her then, and stolen off while she slept? He did not think she had known of the masquerade when she gave evidence at the inquest; it read like honest evidence. Or—the question would never be silenced, though he scorned it—had she lain expecting the footsteps in the room and the whisper that should tell her that it was done? Among the foul possibilities of human nature, was it possible that black ruthlessness and black deceit as well were hidden behind that good and straight and gentle seeming?

These thoughts would scarcely leave him when he was alone.

Trent served Sir James well, earning his pay for six months, and then returned to Paris, where he resumed work with a better heart. Our nation, possessing a populace not particularly enamored with mental exertion, advances through the mundane utilizing a vocabulary both narrow and elementary. Long words are, by definition, abnormalities. And like all anomalies, they serve a dual purpose: they are either comically ridiculous or terrifyingly solemn. Consider the phrase 'intelligent anticipation.' Were such a construct uttered in any other European tongue, it would pass unnoticed. Here, it has attained the status of a proverb; we all smirk upon hearing it in a speech or encountering it in a leading article, regarding it as a pinnacle of wit. Why? "Nothing akin to friendship—he was not a man for cultivating friends—but the finest rapport between a trusted employee and his chief. I approached him as private secretary shortly after obtaining my degree at Oxford. I had intended to join my father's enterprise, wherein I currently reside, but my father advised me to traverse the globe for a year or two. Thus, I accepted this secretaryship, promising varied experience, allowing the initial period to elongate into four years before the end arrived. The offer materialized through the last conceivable qualification for a salaried post: chess."

At the word, Trent clapped his hands together with a muttering exclamation. The others gazed at him in surprise.

"Chess!" Trent repeated, rising and approaching Marlowe. "Do you know," he said, "what was the first observation I made regarding you at our inaugural meeting? It was your eye, Mr. Marlowe. "What was that remark you made concerning our engagement at seven-thirty?" Mr. Cupples inquired as the pair emerged from the imposing gateway of the residential block. "Do we truly possess such an appointment?"

"Certainly," Trent replied, his voice carrying a peculiar buoyancy. "You are dining with me. There exists but one fitting manner to honor this occasion: a meal where I bear the cost. "Who is Sheppard?" Mr. Cupples asked softly, walking beside his companion. Trent moved with an unnatural lightness, and a passing policeman, noting his expression, offered a kindly smile, attributing the man's visible happiness solely to the effects of alcohol.

"Who is Sheppard?" Trent echoed, his tone sharpened by a bitter intensity. "That inquiry, if I may permit myself the observation, Cupples, epitomizes the spirit of aimless curiosity prevalent in this restless age. I propose dining at Sheppard's, and immediately you fold your arms, driven by a frenzied pride of intellect, demanding to know the identity of Sheppard before crossing the threshold. I refuse to cater to the vices of the modern mind. Sheppard's is merely a venue for sustenance. I know no Sheppard. It never entered my consciousness that such a figure existed. Likely, he is a myth born of totemic origins. All I know is that one may procure a portion of saddle of mutton at Sheppard's so superior that it has caused many an American visitor to curse the advent of Christopher Columbus... Hail, taxi!"

A cab glided smoothly to the curb, and the driver accepted his instructions with a dignified nod.

"There is further cause for suggesting Sheppard's," Trent continued, feverishly igniting a cigarette, "namely, that I am soon to wed the most extraordinary woman in existence. I trust the association of ideas is evident."

"You intend to marry Mabel!" Mr. Cupples exclaimed. "My dearest friend, what joyous tidings! Clasp hands, Trent; this is magnificent! I congratulate you both from the depths of my soul. Mabel has witnessed profound sorrow, yet she is undoubtedly fashioned by the grand design of humanity to exert the most benevolent influence upon the life of a virtuous man. "Here we stand," Trent said as he settled the fare and guided Mr. Cupples into a lengthy, paneled chamber furnished with numerous tables and filled with the murmur of conversation. "This is the sanctuary of fulfilled desire, the bower encircled by roses. Observe there are three bookmakers consuming pork at my favored table. We shall occupy the one in the opposite corner."

He engaged earnestly with a waiter, while Mr. Cupples, lost in a pleasant reverie, warmed himself before the roaring fireplace. "The vintage here," Trent resumed as they took their seats, "is undoubtedly derived from grapes. What shall we consume?"

Mr. Cupples emerged from his contemplation. "I believe," he stated, "I shall have milk and soda water."

"Hush!" Trent urged. "The head-waiter possesses a frail heart and might overhear you. Milk and soda water! Cupples, you may consider yourself robust, and I do not deny your fortitude, but I caution you that this habit of blending beverages has claimed the lives of many a sturdier man than yourself. He issued another command to the waiter, who arranged the dishes before them and vanished. Trent evidently enjoyed the status of a respected patron. "I have procured," he announced, "wine of which I am assured, and I trust you will sample it. "I have never undertaken any pledge," Mr. Cupples said, scrutinizing his mutton with approval. "Simply, I harbor no fondness for wine. I purchased a bottle once and consumed it merely to ascertain its nature, and it rendered me ill. Yet, it was likely inferior vinage. I shall taste a drop of yours, as it is your dinner, and I assure you, my dear Trent, I wish to perform something unconventional to demonstrate the strength of my feelings on this auspicious occasion. I have not experienced such delight in many years. To reflect," he murmured aloud as the waiter filled his glass, "upon the resolution of the Manderson enigma, the exoneration of the innocent, and the crowning of your own and Mabel's felicity—all converging simultaneously! "You possess a magnificent nature," Trent observed, deeply moved. "Your external semblance betrays the vastness of your soul.
