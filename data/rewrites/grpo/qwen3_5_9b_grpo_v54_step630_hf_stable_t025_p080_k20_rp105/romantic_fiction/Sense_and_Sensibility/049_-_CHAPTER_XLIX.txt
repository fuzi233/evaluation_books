Edward was free. The circumstances of his release, though unaccountable to the rest of the family, were a certainty to him, and the purpose of that freedom was immediately clear to all: having endured the misery of one imprudent engagement contracted without his mother's consent, nothing less than the immediate formation of another could be expected. His errand to Barton was singularly simple; he was there to ask Elinor to marry him. Yet, despite his experience in such matters, he felt a profound discomfort, needing encouragement and fresh air to summon the courage for the moment.

How quickly he resolved himself, how swiftly an opportunity arose, and how he was received, need not be detailed. By four o'clock, three hours after his arrival, he had secured his lady, won his mother's consent, and stood not merely as a rapturous lover but as one of the happiest men alive. His joy was exceptional; he possessed the triumph of accepted love, but also the relief of being released from an entanglement that had long been his misery, a union with a woman he no longer loved. Elevated instantly to a security he had once deemed impossible, he moved from despair to happiness, and the change was evident in a genuine, flowing cheerfulness his friends had never seen before.

His heart was now open to Elinor. With philosophical dignity, he confessed his weaknesses and treated his first boyish attachment to Lucy Steele as a youthful folly born of ignorance and idleness. "It was a foolish, idle inclination on my side," he explained, attributing it to a lack of employment when removed from Mr. Pratt's care at eighteen. "Had my mother given me some active profession... I should very soon have outgrown the fancied attachment." Instead, he had returned home to complete idleness, spending his time at Longstaple where he felt at home, unaware of the defects in Lucy until he saw others. "Considering everything," he concluded, "it was not at the time an unnatural or an inexcusable piece of folly."

The transformation in the Dashwood household was palpable. Mrs. Dashwood was too happy to be comfortable, struggling to praise Elinor enough without wounding Edward's delicacy. Marianne spoke her happiness only through tears, her joy sincere but lacking the language to express it fully. But Elinor? Her feelings were a tumultuous storm. From the moment she learned Lucy was married to another, she had been anything but tranquil. Yet, when the second moment passed, seeing Edward honorably released and instantly declaring an affection as tender and constant as she had always hoped, she was overwhelmed by her own felicity. It took several hours for her spirits to settle into a serene tranquility.

Edward remained at the cottage for at least a week, for lovers require time to exhaust the subjects of the past, present, and future. Their conversations turned inevitably to Lucy's marriage, a circumstance Elinor found utterly baffling. How could Robert be drawn to marry a girl of whom he had never spoken admiringly, a girl already engaged to his brother? To Elinor's reason, it was a complete puzzle. Edward suggested that vanity and flattery had led them on, perhaps initially with the hope of securing Robert's good offices. He admitted he had no prior knowledge, having remained at Oxford with no means of hearing news until Lucy's letter arrived, bursting upon him with wonder, horror, and joy.

He handed Elinor the letter, written by Lucy Ferrars herself:

Being very sure I have long lost your affections, I have thought myself at liberty to bestow my own on another... I scorn to accept a hand while the heart was another's. Sincerely wish you happy in your choice... Your brother has gained my affections entirely, and as we could not live without one another, we are just returned from the altar... I have burnt all your letters, and will return your picture the first opportunity. Please to destroy my scrawls—but the ring with my hair you are very welcome to keep."

Elinor read it silently. Edward remarked that he would have burned such a letter in former days, but now, in a sister, it was bad enough; in a wife, it was a source of blushes. "Since the first half year of our foolish business," he noted, "this is the only letter I ever received from her of which the substance made me any amends for the defect of the style."

Elinor observed that Mrs. Dashwood had brought upon herself an appropriate punishment; by bribing Robert with a thousand-a-year pension out of resentment toward Edward, she had inadvertently enabled the very match she had sought to prevent. Edward agreed that Mrs. Dashwood would be more hurt by Robert's marriage to Lucy, her favorite, and would likely forgive him sooner.

Edward knew little of his family's current state, having left Oxford immediately upon receiving Lucy's letter. He had traveled with one object: the nearest road to Barton. He did not expect a cruel reception from Elinor, despite his past jealousy of Colonel Brandon and his modesty regarding his own worth. He spoke of his intentions prettily, leaving the details of a year hence to the imagination of husbands and wives.

Elinor and Edward discussed Lucy's deception. It was clear to Elinor that Lucy had meant to deceive, going off with a flourish of malice. Edward, now enlightened on her character, believed her capable of the utmost meanness. He recalled that he had only offered her the option to continue the engagement because, in his desperate situation, he had assumed her insistence on sharing his fate stemmed from disinterested affection. He could not comprehend why she would remain bound to a man she did not regard, possessing only two thousand pounds, unaware that Colonel Brandon might provide a living. Elinor countered that she might have hoped for a change in Edward's family fortunes, and that remaining engaged had gained her consideration among friends. Edward conceded that nothing could have been more natural than her conduct.

Elinor gently scolded Edward for his imprudence in spending so much time at Norland while feeling his own inconstancy. "Your behaviour was certainly very wrong," she said, noting that their relations had been led to expect what was impossible given his situation. Edward pleaded ignorance of his own heart, claiming he felt safe because his faith was plighted to another. "I felt that I admired you, but I told myself it was only friendship," he admitted, realizing too late that comparing himself to Lucy had changed everything.

News arrived that Colonel Brandon was expected at the cottage. Edward wished to be better acquainted with him and to convince him that he no longer resented the living of Delaford. Now, he felt astonished that he had never visited the place; he owed all his knowledge of the house and parish to Elinor, who had listened with such attention to Colonel Brandon's descriptions.

One difficulty remained: money. Edward had two thousand pounds, Elinor one, and the Delaford living, totaling three hundred and fifty pounds a year. It was insufficient for comfort, and Mrs. Dashwood could not advance anything. Edward hoped for a favorable change in his mother's attitude, but Elinor feared Robert's offense would only enrich Fanny.

Four days after Edward's arrival, Colonel Brandon appeared, completing Mrs. Dashwood's satisfaction. Edward retained the privilege of the first comer, while Colonel Brandon walked nightly to his old quarters at the Park, returning early enough to interrupt the lovers' tête-à-tête before breakfast. A three-week residence at Delaford, where he calculated the disparity between thirty-six and seventeen, brought him to Barton in a mood needing Marianne's kindness and her mother's encouragement to become cheerful. Among such friends, he revived. No rumor of Lucy's marriage had reached him; he knew nothing of what had passed, and his first hours were spent in wonder. Mrs. Dashwood explained everything, and he rejoiced in having promoted Elinor's interest.

The gentlemen advanced in good opinion of each other as their acquaintance deepened. Their shared principles and manners would have united them in friendship, but their mutual love for two sisters made that regard inevitable and immediate. Letters from town, once sources of transport for Elinor, now arrived to be read with mirth. Mrs. Jennings wrote of the wonderful tale, venting indignation against the jilting girl and pity for poor Mr. Edward, whom she believed was broken-hearted at Oxford. She recounted how Lucy had borrowed all her money to make a show, leaving Nancy destitute, and how Lucy's refusal to take them in the chaise was worse than all. She urged Elinor to send for Edward and have Marianne comfort him.

Mr. Dashwood's strains were more solemn. He viewed Mrs. Ferrars as the most unfortunate of women and considered the existence of each under such a blow with grateful wonder. Robert's offense was unpardonable, but Lucy's was infinitely worse. Neither should ever be mentioned to Mrs. Ferrars; if she forgave her son, his wife should never be acknowledged. The secrecy of the affair heightened the crime in his eyes. He called on Elinor to regret that Lucy's engagement with Edward had not been fulfilled, fearing she would spread misery further. He proposed sending a letter to Edward urging a submission to his mother.

"A letter of proper submission!" Edward repeated. "Would they have me beg my mother's pardon for Robert's ingratitude to her, and breach of honour to me? I can make no submission. I am grown neither humble nor penitent... I am grown very happy; but that would not interest."

"You may certainly ask to be forgiven," Elinor suggested, "because you have offended;—and I should think you might _now_ venture so far as to profess some concern for having ever formed the engagement which drew on you your mother's anger."

He agreed he might. Elinor added that once forgiven, a little humility might be convenient when acknowledging a second engagement, which she deemed almost as imprudent in her mother's eyes as the first. Edward resisted the idea of a written submission, preferring verbal concessions. Thus, it was resolved that instead of writing to Fanny, he should go to London and personally intreat her good offices. Marianne, in her new character of candour, noted that if they truly interested themselves in reconciliation, even John and Fanny were not entirely without merit.

After a brief stay of three or four days, the two gentlemen quitted Barton together. They were to proceed immediately to Delaford, so Edward might gain personal knowledge of his future home and assist his patron in planning improvements. From there, after staying a couple of nights, he would journey to town.
