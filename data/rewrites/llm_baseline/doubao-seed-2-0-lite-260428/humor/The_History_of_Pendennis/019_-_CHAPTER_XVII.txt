# CHAPTER XVII: Which Wraps Up The First Half Of Our History (With One Gaspingly Absurd Secret Reveal You Won’t See Coming)

It was a perfectly unremarkable sun-dappled afternoon at Fairoaks, the kind that makes idle local gossips decide to extend their walks for no good reason beyond getting to spill tea to a friend. Our mild-mannered curate Mr. Smirke was tucked away upstairs in Pen’s study, halfheartedly pretending to run through Latin verbs with his pupil, when Mrs. Portman, the Rector’s wife, finished her business chit-chat with Mrs. Pybus, glanced up at the cloudless sky, and decided a detour over to Fairoaks to visit her dear friend Mrs. Pendennis was non-negotiable. Over scones and tea, she dropped the juiciest piece of small-town gossip the parish had seen all quarter: quiet, meek Mr. Smirke had a two-year long secret attachment, and had been hiding his betrothal for ages.

“Wait, who on earth has he gone and fallen for?” Helen Pendennis asked, doing her best to look perfectly poised, while a little jolt of alarm zinged through her—all the local busybodies had been dropping hints lately, after all.

“My dear, when he first moved here we all thought we’d get him paired off with Myra,” Mrs. Portman laughed. “We even stopped inviting him to dinner for a bit because the whispers got so loud. Then people started saying he was smitten somewhere else, but I always shut that down immediately! I told everyone it was never you—”

“Me?” Helen squawked, scandalized. “The man was hired to tutor my Arthur! How dare anyone be so impertinent!”

“Upon my soul, that’s far too much,” Major Pendennis huffed, slamming down his newspaper and dropping his double monocle onto his lap like the gossip was a personal insult to his entire family’s dignity.

“I’ve no patience for that Mrs. Pybus spreading such nonsense,” Helen fumed.

“See? I told her that exact thing,” Mrs. Portman said, smugly. “Turns out our demure little curate has been engaged this whole time to a Miss Thompson, over on Clapham Common! I’m absolutely delighted, not least because unmarried curates wandering around your house giving your daughters piano lessons are always a recipe for awkwardness. I’m telling you this privately, obviously—don’t go breathing a word of it to anyone else, I don’t want to spoil the surprise.”

Helen was over the moon with relief, honestly. She gushed about how kind Mr. Smirke was, how he was such a good tutor for Arthur, and as soon as Mrs. Portman left, she and the Major gushed over the good news too. Helen even started kicking herself for how distant and haughty she’d been to Smirke lately, avoiding him around the house when he’d spent months running errands for her, copying music, teaching little Laura watercolors, and bringing Arthur extra books.

“Sure, be grateful,” the Major grumbled. “But don’t get familiar. He’s a paid tutor, not family. Rank has its lines, you know I think you’re far too soft on the fellow.” Helen disagreed wholeheartedly, though, and when Smirke trundled downstairs after his study session with Pen, hovering awkwardly by the hall to say goodbye, Helen walked over, blushing a little, shook his hand, and practically dragged him into the drawing room, inviting him to stay for their fancy dinner. Smirke, naturally, agreed so fast he nearly tripped over his own feet.

Free of all her old misgivings about Smirke pining after her, Helen was the picture of warmth and kindness to the poor curate all through dinner, piling his plate with roast beef and chatting to him nonstop—so much so that the Major decided to be as snooty and condescending to the tutor as humanly possible, talking to Smirke like he was a lowly footman who’d wandered into the dining room by mistake. Even Pen, who was still nursing a broken heart and fully convinced he was the most tragic young genius in all of England, snickered at his uncle’s performative snobbery.

Smirke couldn’t have cared less about the Major’s rudeness, though. He was too busy soaking up Helen’s attention, gabbing happily about the fancy bazaar, the latest missionary meeting, the bishop’s new sermon, and the gossipy bits about London high society he’d read in the newspaper that morning, pulling out every trick a well-meaning sentimental cleric could think of to charm the lady he adored. The Major yawned so wide his jaw ached as soon as Helen and little Laura left the dining room, groaning “That man is the most unbearable bore alive, how can he talk so much?”

“I’m very fond of him, Arthur adores him,” Helen said, innocently. “I wonder what Miss Thompson of Clapham is like, anyway?”

“Eh, I always thought he was pining after someone much closer to home,” the Major said.

“Towards Myra Portman?” Helen asked, confused.

“Towards you, you goose,” the Major said. Helen blushed bright pink, acting like that was the most ridiculous thing she’d ever heard, even though she’d suspected it for months, and rambled on about how happy she was for Smirke, and how she hoped Arthur wouldn’t drink too much wine with him.

Unfortunately, Arthur was already way ahead of her. Delighted he’d been given free run of the cellar for the night, he dragged out three bottles of his late father’s famous imported claret, and he and Smirke were passing the glasses around faster than you could say nunc vino pellite curas. Within half an hour the first two bottles were all but empty, Pen was sloshed enough to declare that wine was the only lover who never cheated a man, and was reciting the overwrought sad love poetry he’d written that morning, all about how his actress ex was a heartless deceiver who’d thrown away the love of a great future poet. His voice got all wobbly and emotional, his grey eyes glistened, and Smirke, ever the sentimental soft touch, grabbed Pen’s hand across the plate of half-eaten apricots and declared him a genius whose name would go down in history.

“Promise me you’ll remember your old tutor when you’re famous, Arthur!” he gushed, tears in his eyes behind his spectacles. Pen swore he would, slapping Smirke on the back and declaring him his best friend in the whole world, before yelling for a third bottle of claret, bragging that his uncle had seen Sheridan drink five bottles of the stuff plus a whole bottle of maraschino once, and breaking into a sloshed rendition of the drinking song from *Der Freischütz*. Helen, pacing on the lawn outside with Laura to watch the sunset, heard his voice and smiled, thinking her boy was finally cheering up after his broken heart.

“Go on, old man,” Pen sloshed, pouring Smirke another full glass. “Let’s toast your secret lady! Your Clapham Miss Thompson, right? Hip hip hurray, here’s to Mrs. Smirke!”

Smirke gulped the wine down, trembling all over. “Oh Arthur,” he gasped, “you have no idea—she’s not Miss Thompson! She’s not Miss Portman! She’s not anyone silly you’ve ever heard of! For two whole years, my whole heart has belonged to one perfect, angelic woman—your mother. I love Helen Pendennis! I’d settle every penny I own on her, and even leave something for you, Arthur, when I die!”

Pen went from three-bottle drunk to completely stone-cold sober faster than you could blink. He jumped to his feet, stared at Smirke like the curate had grown a second head, and yelled “My mother? Good God man, you’re mad! She’s seven years older than you!”

Smirke winced, because that was exactly the jab Pen had coming—Pen had spent months pining after an actress ten years his senior, after all. But Pen wasn’t having it. “That’s different! I’m her son! You’re my tutor! That’s the most absurd, overstepping bit of cheek I’ve ever heard!” He rang the bell for coffee, and when the old butler showed up staring at the three empty claret bottles, Pen coolly told the staff to bring Smirke’s pony around to the door. The poor curate, red-faced and mortified, trundled out to the stables alone, Pen trailing behind him to help tighten his saddle girths.

“Hey, I’m sorry I snapped,” Pen mumbled, a little guilty, as Smirke climbed onto his pony. “You’ve always been great to me. But this can’t happen, mate. Be well. God bless.” Smirke nodded silently, and trotted off down the lane, his shoulders hunched in defeat. Helen was waiting for Pen on the lawn, frowning a little because Smirke had left without even having tea, and Pen just laughed, put his arm around her waist, and walked her around the lawn for a bit, lighter than he’d felt in months. Later that night, when Laura was in bed, he spilled the whole ridiculous story to Helen and the Major. The Major huffed “Confounded impudence! Where do these lowly tutors get the gall to court wealthy widows?” and Helen blushed so hard her cheeks were pink for an hour. The two of them talked late into the night, laughing so hard their sides hurt, and Pen slept like a log for the first time since his breakup.

Over on the other side of Chatteris, meanwhile, the great theater impresario Mr. Dolphin had wrapped up his business with the Costigan household. He’d signed Miss Fotheringay to a fabulous new London stage contract, and left her a nice lump sum to pay off all their tiny little local debts. Captain Costigan, who’d previously been banned from the chaotic local Buccaneers’ drinking club for not paying his bar tabs, suddenly was the toast of the town—he paid off every last farthing he owed, bought all the neighborhood Creeds children endless gingerbread and apples, and spent through the entire leftover cash in three days flat, forcing him to beg Dolphin for a little extra travel money before they left for London.

The Buccaneers, a crew of local apothecaries, portrait painters, auctioneers, and editors who met every Saturday to get riotously drunk and toast everything under the sun, threw Costigan a farewell dinner at The Grapes inn, eighteen gentlemen sitting down to a massive spread of roast beef and plum pudding. They drank endless bowls of punch, sang drinking songs, and when it was time for the guest of honor to speak, Costigan got to his feet and rambled through a twenty minute speech sloshed full of dramatic flourishes, name dropping dukes he’d definitely never dined with, bragging about his days at the siege of Flushing, and weeping about how he’d never forget the fine, loyal gentlemen of Chatteris. The whole room cheered so loud the windows rattled, and not a single soul there called him out for any of his obvious lies. The first half of our little history ends on that perfectly silly, warm, tipsy note.
