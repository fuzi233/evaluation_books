The Countess Olenska had set the time for "after five." At twenty minutes past, Newland Archer pressed the bell of the peeling stucco house, a structure choked by a giant wisteria strangling its feeble cast-iron balcony. It was a rental, leased from the vagabond Medora, situated far down West Twenty-third Street in a quarter that defied the logic of high society.

Her neighbors were small dressmakers, bird-stuffers, and "people who wrote." Further down the disheveled street, Archer recognized a dilapidated wooden house at the end of a paved path—the domicile of a journalist named Winsett, a figure whose name had recently surfaced in conversation. Winsett did not entertain guests, yet he had once indicated the location during a nocturnal stroll, prompting Archer to shiver with a sudden, sharp realization: were the humanities so miserably housed in other capitals?

Madame Olenska's dwelling was distinguished from its surroundings only by a marginally fresher coat of paint around the window-frames. As Archer surveyed the modest facade, a grim calculation crossed his mind: the Polish Count must have stripped her of her fortune as thoroughly as he had stripped her of her illusions.

The young man had endured an unsatisfactory day. He had lunched with the Wellands, harboring the hope of carrying May away for a solitary walk in the Park afterward. He desired to possess her exclusively—to recount the enchantment of her appearance the previous night, to express his pride, and to urge the acceleration of their marriage. Instead, Mrs. Welland had firmly reminded him that the circuit of family visits was incomplete. When he hinted at advancing the wedding date, she raised reproachful eyebrows and sighed, reciting a litany of twelve dozen hand-embroidered items.

Packed into the family landau, they rolled from one tribal doorstep to another. By the time the afternoon's round concluded, Archer parted from his betrothed with the sensation of being displayed like a wild animal cunningly trapped. He attributed this coarse interpretation of familial affection to his anthropological studies, yet as he recalled that the Wellands did not anticipate a wedding until the following autumn—and visualized the years of confinement ahead—a damp chill settled upon his spirit.

"Tomorrow," Mrs. Welland called after him, "we'll do the Chiverses and the Dallases." He perceived she was methodically alphabetizing their two families, currently residing in the first quarter of the letter C.

He intended to inform May of the Countess Olenska's summons—that command, rather—but in the fleeting moments of privacy, more urgent matters had demanded his attention. Furthermore, it struck him as absurd to mention the matter aloud. He knew May particularly wished him to be kind to her cousin; was it not this specific desire that had hastened the announcement of their engagement? It produced an odd sensation to reflect that, absent the Countess's arrival, he might have remained, if not technically free, at least less irrevocably pledged. Yet May had willed it so, and he felt strangely relieved of further responsibility, thereby granting himself the liberty to visit his cousin without disclosure.

Standing on Madame Olenska's threshold, curiosity became his dominant emotion. He was puzzled by the tone of her summons; he concluded she possessed depths of complexity beneath her apparent simplicity.

The door opened to reveal a swarthy, foreign-looking maid with a prominent bosom beneath a gay neckerchief, whom he vaguely identified as Sicilian. She welcomed him with a display of white teeth, answered his inquiries with a headshake of incomprehension, and guided him through a narrow hall into a low, firelit drawing-room. The room was empty. She departed, leaving him for an appreciable duration to wonder if she had sought her mistress or failed to understand his purpose, perhaps mistaking him for someone there to wind the clock—which he noted had stopped. He knew the southern races communicated via pantomime, and he was mortified to find her shrugs and smiles utterly unintelligible. Eventually, she returned with a lamp. Meanwhile, Archer had constructed a phrase from Dante and Petrarch, offering the response: *"La signora e fuori; ma verra subito."* He interpreted this to mean: *She is out—but you will see her soon.*

What he observed, illuminated by the lamp, was the faded, shadowy charm of a room unlike any he had known. He knew the Countess had brought some possessions with her—"bits of wreckage," she had termed them—and these were represented by small, slender tables of dark wood, a delicate Greek bronze on the mantelpiece, and a stretch of red damask nailed over discolored wallpaper behind a pair of Italianate pictures in aged frames.

Newland Archer prided himself on his knowledge of Italian art. His boyhood had been saturated with Ruskin; he had consumed the latest volumes: John Addington Symonds, Vernon Lee's *Euphorion*, the essays of P.G. Hamerton, and the magnificent new work *The Renaissance* by Walter Pater. He spoke easily of Botticelli and treated Fra Angelico with a faint condescension. Yet these pictures bewildered him; they resembled nothing he was accustomed to viewing when traveling in Italy, and perhaps his powers of observation were impaired by the strangeness of finding himself in this empty house, where apparently no one awaited his arrival. He regretted not informing May Welland of the Countess's request and felt a slight disturbance at the possibility that his betrothed might arrive to find him sitting there, waiting alone in the dusk at a lady's fireside, implying an intimacy that belonged elsewhere.

Since he had come, however, he meant to wait. He sank into a chair and stretched his feet toward the logs.

It was peculiar to be summoned in such a manner and then seemingly forgotten, yet Archer felt more curious than mortified. The atmosphere of the room differed so radically from anything he had ever breathed that self-consciousness vanished, replaced by a sense of adventure. He had previously visited drawing-rooms hung with red damask and adorned with pictures "of the Italian school," but what struck him now was how Medora Manson's shabby hired house—with its blighted background of pampas grass and Rogers statuettes—had, through a turn of the hand and the skillful deployment of a few props, been transformed into something intimate, "foreign," subtly evoking old romantic scenes and sentiments. He attempted to analyze the trick, searching for clues in the grouping of chairs and tables, in the fact that only two Jacqueminot roses (whereas nobody ever purchased fewer than a dozen) rested in the slender vase at his elbow, and in the vague, pervasive perfume that was not the scent of handkerchiefs but rather the aroma of a far-off bazaar, composed of Turkish coffee, ambergris, and dried roses.

His mind drifted to the question of what May's drawing-room would resemble. He knew Mr. Welland, behaving "very handsomely," already eyed a newly constructed house in East Thirty-ninth Street. The neighborhood was considered remote, and the building was crafted from a ghastly greenish-yellow stone that younger architects employed as a protest against the brownstone coating New York like a cold chocolate sauce; yet the plumbing was perfect. Archer would have preferred to travel, to postpone the housing dilemma, but though the Wellands approved of an extended European honeymoon—even a winter in Egypt—they were adamant regarding the necessity of a house for the returning couple. The young man felt his fate sealed: for the remainder of his life, he would ascend every evening between the cast-iron railings of that greenish-yellow doorstep, passing through a Pompeian vestibule into a hall lined with varnished yellow wood. Beyond that point, his imagination refused to travel. He knew the drawing-room above featured a bay window, but he could not envision how May would treat it. She submitted cheerfully to the purple satin and yellow tuftings of the Welland drawing-room, to its sham Buhl tables and gilt vitrines filled with modern Saxe. He saw no reason to suppose she would desire anything different in her own home; his sole comfort lay in the reflection that she would likely permit him to arrange his library as he pleased—which would naturally consist of "sincere" Eastlake furniture and plain new bookcases without glass doors.

The round-bosomed maid reappeared, drew the curtains, pushed back a log, and said consolingly, *"Verra—verra."* Upon her departure, Archer stood and began to wander. Should he wait any longer? His position was becoming increasingly foolish. Perhaps he had misunderstood Madame Olenska—perhaps she had not invited him after all.

Down the cobblestones of the quiet street came the ring of a stepper's hoofs; they halted before the house, and he caught the opening of a carriage door. Parting the curtains, he looked out into the early dusk. A street-lamp faced him, casting light upon Julius Beaufort's compact English brougham, drawn by a large roan, and the banker descending to assist Madame Olenska.

Beaufort stood, hat in hand, speaking words his companion seemed to negate; then they shook hands, and he sprang into his carriage while she mounted the steps.

When she entered the room, she showed no surprise at seeing Archer present; surprise seemed the emotion she was least prone to exhibit.

"How do you like my funny house?" she asked. "To me, it is like heaven."

As she spoke, she untied her little velvet bonnet, tossed it aside with her long cloak, and regarded him with meditative eyes.
