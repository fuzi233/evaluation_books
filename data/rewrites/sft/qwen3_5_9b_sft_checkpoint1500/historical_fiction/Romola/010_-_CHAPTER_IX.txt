The Piazza was thick with the throng, and Tito moved swiftly through them, his mind already drifting past the immediate bustle toward the Corso degli Adimari. As he walked, a fleeting thought lingered regarding the pair of blue eyes he had once glimpsed beneath the white linen hood of a country girl at festival time. He knew well enough that face belonged to Tessa, though he had never admitted it to anyone, least of all to Nello, whose curiosity he had brushed aside with a casual remark. There was something natural, indeed instinctive, in Tito's silence; it required no conscious effort, flowing from him as freely as any other impulse. Yet, unlike the sight of a flock of crows, which needed no hiding, this quiet acknowledgment of his attraction to Tessa remained a small, private secret, easily swallowed by the larger currents of his own life.

That brief longing for Tessa vanished almost instantly, however, giving way to a darker, more persistent memory: the face of the friar, pale and fanatical, who had stared at him with an unsettling intensity. Where else, in all his travels, could he have seen such a countenance? It was a vague, irritating ghost, clinging to his mind like dust motes in a sunbeam, demanding attention he did not wish to give. "Foolishness!" Tito chided himself mentally, brushing the thought aside. He had better matters for his thoughts. By the time he turned off the Corso into a narrower street, his focus had shifted entirely to the heat of the noonday sun and the delay it had caused him. He was late for the appointment in the Via de' Bardi, where a room awaited his arrival with anxious anticipation.

In his mind, he rehearsed the scene before he even reached the door. He saw Romola waiting, her blind eyes reflecting a joy that seemed to glow with an inner light, softening the shadows around her. He imagined the faint, rosy flush on her cheeks and neck, a sign of feminine sensitivity that somehow enhanced, rather than diminished, her majestic bearing. Their time together, circling the blind man's chair, would be filled with her constant appeals and his eager responses. Yet, despite this intimacy, Tito felt a profound, almost reverential subjugation to her. In her presence, he felt a strange, undefined awe, akin to the ancient worship paid to a goddess of nature—one who possessed a depth of life and power that transcended mere knowledge. He had never been alone with her, and his imagination struggled to construct scenes of love between them. All he could do was dream wildly, holding onto an impossible hope: that one day Romola would speak the words he secretly craved.

His thoughts drifted back to Greece, to a sunny day when he leaned against a wall and a peasant girl, dark-eyed and shy, had crept closer until she begged for a kiss, offering her innocent, olive-skinned cheek. Tito was accustomed to such spontaneous, unsought affections. But Romola's love was different. Would it ever come? Could it ever reach him at all? Nevertheless, it remained the highest apple on which his mind had settled. Though he was a man of fresh youth, not yet hardened by passion, he was deeply impressionable. It was as inevitable that he should feel drawn to Romola as it was for white irises to reflect in a sunlit stream. Yet, he possessed no vanity; he sensed clearly that she stood on a plane far above him. Many a man, standing before a large-eyed, simple child, has felt this same humbling distance.

Despite this sense of inferiority, Tito had experienced a rapid ascent in fortune that might have tempted lesser men to believe they deserved a wife like Romola, or even convinced her father, Bartolommeo Scala, to envision such a union. His first meeting with Scala had proven auspicious, leading to a growing favor that would have secured Tito's establishment in Florence even if the city offered no other allure. Politian, the renowned professor of Greek and Latin, found himself in a unique position. While the university seat had moved to Pisa, chairs for Greek remained in Florence. Once, the eminent Demetrio Calcondila had shared the honor with the dominant Italian scholar, but Calcondila had departed for Milan. Now, there was no rival to challenge Politian, nor any necessary check upon his ambition, save for those few friends who wished to teach him modesty. Scala and others, though not among the shallow admirers who drank in Tito's mediocre verses, were content to support the scholar morally. It was decided that Tito should be sustained in a Greek chair, following the precedent set by Lorenzo himself, who had similarly supported Calcondila. This move was framed not as jealousy, but as a zealous devotion to true learning and the education of Florentine youth.

Thus, Tito sailed under a fair wind. To the discerning eye, his talents harmonized perfectly with his sudden good fortune, and he wore this advantage with such ease and lack of pretense that no offense was taken. He was destined for the best circles of Florence, where silver plate abounded and the Signory permitted the wearing of the finest brocades. Who could refuse a handsome young scholar who could play the lute and sing a merry tune? His bright face, easy smile, and liquid voice seemed to bring a holiday spirit to the city, lifting the spirits of the labor-weary and the melancholy alike. He appeared to be the ideal professor to render the Greek classics beloved by the sons of great houses.

Yet, this external prosperity masked a deeper reality. Tito had sold all his jewels, keeping only one ring, and now held the master key to his freedom: five hundred gold florins. However, the moment he first laid hands on this sum marked the crisis of his first truly serious internal struggle. A thought, previously dismissed as a shadow, suddenly materialized with terrifying clarity, seizing him by the throat. He paused, forced to confront a terrible choice: surrender to the voice within and obey, or refuse it and accept consequences that might prove irrevocable.

It was in the room above Nello's shop, which Tito had recently rented, that the elderly Cennini handed him the final portion of the funds on behalf of Bernardo Rucellai, the buyer of the precious gems. "Here, my young friend!" the respectable printer and goldsmith exclaimed warmly. "You now possess a pretty little fortune. Take my advice, and let me invest these florins in a secure quarter, where they may grow and multiply, rather than slip through your fingers on banquets and the follies common to Florentine youth." Cennini shook his head knowingly. "It has become too fashionable among scholars, especially those like our Pietro Crinito, who believe their studies require scenting and embroidery, to squander wealth with one hand only to beg with the other. I have brought you the money, and you are free to make a wise or unwise choice; I await to see which way the scale tips. The Florentines do not consider a man a member of an Art until he has demonstrated his skill and been matriculated. Nor does one enter the Art of Life until one has been thoroughly tempted. If you decide to lend your florins to usurers, let me know tomorrow. A scholar may marry, and he ought to have something ready for the *morgen-cap*." [1]

[1] A gift given by the groom to the bride the day after the wedding. *Morgengabe*.

As Cennini closed the door, leaving the street noise outside, Tito turned. The smile that had graced his lips moments before died away, replaced by a fixed, hollow gaze directed at the table where the florins lay. He made no other movement, standing with his thumbs hooked into his belt, staring down at the coins in a trance-like state, as if concentrating all his consciousness on some invisible, tormenting image.

"A man's ransom!" The phrase echoed in his mind. Who had spoken those words? Five hundred florins, they said, was more than a man's ransom. But what if the man in question was a father? A man of advanced years, yet possessing lofty thoughts and a heart capable of immense passion. A man who, decades prior, had rescued a small boy from a life of beggary, filth, and cruelty, raising him with tender care, becoming a father figure. What if that man was now, under the burning summer sun of some distant coast, toiling as a slave, hacking wood and drawing water, perhaps beaten and mocked for lacking strength or speed? What if he were thinking to himself, "Tito will find me? He carried the manuscripts and the gems to Venice. He has raised money, and he will not rest until he finds me"? If that scenario were true—if it were certain—could Tito, seeing the price of the gems spread before him, allow himself to stay in Florence, bathed in the soft air of promised love and prosperity, refusing to risk everything for a father who might be suffering?

No, surely not, *if it were certain*. But certainty was elusive. The galley carrying the treasures had been intercepted by a Turkish vessel on its journey to Delos, according to reports from a companion ship that had escaped. Resistance had occurred; blood had likely been spilled. A man had been seen falling overboard. Who survived? What fate awaited them amidst the myriad possibilities? Had not Tito himself suffered a shipwreck, narrowly escaping drowning? He understood the omnipresence of accidents that could shatter any grand design. Rumors of pirate settlements on Delos were unreliable, perhaps irrelevant. What would happen if he left Florence for Venice, securing official letters and setting sail for the Archipelago?
