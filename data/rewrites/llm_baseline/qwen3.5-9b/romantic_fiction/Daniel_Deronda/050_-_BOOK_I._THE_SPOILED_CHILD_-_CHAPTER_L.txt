"If some mortal, born too soon, / Were laid away in some great trance—the ages / Coming and going all the while—till dawned / His true time's advent; and could then record / The words they spoke who kept watch by his bed, / Then I might tell more of the breath so light / Upon my eyelids, and the fingers warm / Among my hair. Youth is confused; yet never / So dull was I but, when that spirit passed, / I turned to him, scarce consciously, as turns / A water-snake when fairies cross his sleep."
—*Browning: Paracelsus*.

These lines prefaced the letter Sir Hugo placed into Daniel Deronda's hands.

My good friend and yours, Sir Hugo Mallinger, will have informed you that I wish to see you. My health is shaken, and I desire there should be no time lost before I deliver to you what I have long withheld. Let nothing hinder you from being at the *Albergo dell'Italia* in Genoa by the fourteenth of this month. Wait for me there. I am uncertain when I shall be able to make the journey from Spezia, where I shall be staying. That will depend on several things. Wait for me—the Princess Halm-Eberstein. Bring with you the diamond ring that Sir Hugo gave you. I shall like to see it again.

—Your unknown mother,

The letter's colorless wording offered Deronda no clue to what awaited him. Yet, he could not refuse to accept Sir Hugo's reticence, which seemed to imply a pledge not to anticipate his mother's disclosures. Furthermore, the realization that his lifelong conjectures regarding his parentage had been mistaken checked any further speculation. Deronda could not stop his imagination from soaring quickly over the landscape of possibilities, but he refused to entertain any single scenario as more probable than another. To do so would be to nurse a specific desire or aversion into dominance, rather than simply preparing himself with resolve to face the truth, however it might reveal itself.

In this state of mind, he could not explain to anyone the reason for his impending absence, especially not to Mordecai, whom the news would affect as powerfully as himself, albeit differently. If he were to declare, "I am going to learn the truth about my birth," Mordecai's hope would gather a momentum that might prove painfully dangerous. To avoid fueling such suppositions, Deronda framed his journey as undertaken solely by Sir Hugo's wish. He threw as much indifference as he could muster into his announcement, stating he was uncertain of its duration but expecting it to be very short.

"I will ask to have the child Jacob stay with me," Mordecai said, comforting himself with this arrangement after casting his first mournful glances.

"I will drive round and ask Mrs. Cohen to let him come," Mirah replied.

"The grandmother will deny you nothing," Deronda assured them.

"I'm glad you were a little wrong as well as I," he added, smiling warmly at Mordecai. "You thought that old Mrs. Cohen would not bear to see Mirah."

"I undervalued her heart," Mordecai responded. "She is capable of rejoicing that another's plant blooms though her own be withered."

"Oh, they are dear good people; I feel as if we all belonged to each other," Mirah said, a tinge of merriment softening her smile.

"What should you have felt if that Ezra had been your brother?" Deronda asked mischievously, slightly provoked that she had taken kindly to people who had caused him such prospective annoyance on her account.

Mirah looked at him with a slight surprise for a moment before saying, "He is not a bad man—I think he would never forsake anyone." As she spoke, she blushed deeply. Glancing timidly at Mordecai, she turned away to attend to some occupation. Her father was in her mind, a subject on which she and her brother shared a painful, mutual consciousness.

"If he should come and find us!" was a thought that sometimes made the daylight of the street seem as shadowy as a haunted forest to Mirah, where every turn screened an imaginary apparition. Deronda sensed her involuntary allusion and understood the flush. How could he be slow to recognize feelings that now seemed nearer to his own than ever? For the words in his mother's letter implied that his filial relationship was not to be freed from painful conditions; indeed, singularly enough, the letter that had brought his mother closer as a living reality had simultaneously cast her into greater remoteness within his affections.

The tender yearning for a being whose life might have been worsened by lacking his care and love, the image of a mother who had not received all her due—whether of reverence or compassion—had long been secretly present in his observations of the women he encountered. Yet, it now seemed that this picturing of his mother fitted the facts no better than his former conceptions about Sir Hugo. He wondered how it was that when his mother's very handwriting arrived with words holding her actual feeling, his affections had suddenly shrunk into a state of comparative neutrality toward her. A veiled figure with enigmatic speech had pushed aside the image that, despite uncertainty, his clinging thought had gradually molded into the object of his tenderness and dutiful longing.

When he finally set off for Genoa, the interest truly uppermost in his mind had hardly so much to do with his mother as with Mordecai and Mirah.

"God bless you, Dan!" Sir Hugo had said when they shook hands. "Whatever else changes for you, it can't change my being the oldest friend you have known, and the one who has all along felt the most for you. I couldn't have loved you better if you'd been my own—only I should have been better pleased with thinking of you always as the future master of the Abbey instead of my fine nephew; and then you would have seen it necessary for you to take a political line. However—things must be as they may."

It was a defensive movement on the baronet's part to mingle purposeless remarks with the expression of serious feeling.

Upon arriving at the *Italia* in Genoa, no Princess Halm-Eberstein was present. However, on the second day, a letter arrived stating that her arrival might occur within a week or might be deferred by a fortnight or more. She was under circumstances making it impossible to fix her journey precisely and entreated him to wait as patiently as he could.

With this indefinite prospect of suspense hanging over matters of supreme importance, Deronda undertook the difficult task of seeking amusement on philosophical grounds, hoping to quiet his excited feelings and lend patience strength for the weary road ahead. His previous visit to the superb city had been cursory, leaving much to discover beyond the prescribed rounds of sightseeing. He spent the cooler hours wandering observantly through the streets, the quay, and the environs, often taking a boat to enjoy the magnificent view of the city and harbor from the sea.

All sights, all subjects—even the anticipated meeting with his mother—found a central union in Mordecai and Mirah, and the ideas immediately associated with them. Among the thoughts that most filled his mind while his boat drifted within view of the grand harbor was that of the multitudinous Spanish Jews centuries ago, driven destitute from their homes, allowed to land from crowded ships only for a brief rest on this grand quay of Genoa. They overspread it with a pall of famine and plague—dying mothers clutching dying children, fathers and sons gazing at each other's haggardness, like groups from a hundred Hunger Towers turned out beneath the midday sun.

Inevitably, dreamy constructions of a possible ancestry for himself wove themselves together with these historic memories, which had begun to hold a new interest since his discovery of Mirah. Now, under the influence of Mordecai, they became irresistibly dominant. He would have sealed his mind against such constructions if it had been possible, and he had never yet fully admitted to himself that he wished the facts to verify Mordecai's conviction. He inwardly repeated that he had no choice in the matter and that wishing was folly; nay, on the question of parentage, wishing seemed part of that meanness which disowns kinship—it was a disowning by anticipation. What he had to do was simply to accept the fact. And he had really no strong presumption to go upon now that he was assured of his mistake regarding Sir Hugo. There had been a resolved concealment that rendered all inference untrustworthy, and the very name he bore might be false. If Mordecai was wrong—if he, the so-called Daniel Deronda, was held by ties entirely aloof from the course his friend's pathetic hope had outlined—he would not say "I wish"; but he could not help feeling on which side the sacrifice lay.

Across these two importunate thoughts, which he resisted as much as one can resist anything in that unstrung condition belonging to suspense, came continually an anxiety he made no effort to banish. Instead, he dwelt on it with a mournfulness that often seems the best atonement we can offer to one whose need we have been unable to meet. The anxiety was for Gwendolen.
