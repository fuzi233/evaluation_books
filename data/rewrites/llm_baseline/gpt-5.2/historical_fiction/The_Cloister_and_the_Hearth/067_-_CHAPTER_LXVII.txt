With the scant signs those men held in their hands, it would have been a marvel if they could have read what had just been written—wet, dark, and irrevocable—upon the river’s brink. Yet there, in that commonplace strip of stone and ooze where the water lapped like a tongue tasting its own story, a thing had taken place so singular that, taken whole, it may have no fellow in the chronicles of men, and perhaps never shall. It can be told in few words—some by the plain voice of the teller, some by the mouth of one who acted in it.

Gerard, after he had written his brief farewell to Pietro and Andrea, stole down at nightfall toward the river. He went as one who has measured every step and will not be turned: that obstinate, household sort of resolve—like dough kneaded past the point of softness—only here it was kneaded toward death. He stuffed his pockets with all the silver and copper he had, not to buy bread or a warm pot, but to weigh his body, that the water might take him quicker and keep him surer.

He chose a part of the stream he had marked before as little frequented. There are some—especially women—who, even in despair, glance about as if to set a witness by their side. But this man’s eye went hunting for the opposite mercy: he looked to be certain there was nobody.

And to his annoyance he saw a single figure leaning against the corner of an alley, as still as a post, as patient as a cat by a pantry door. Gerard, with a forced carelessness, strolled away; then turned back. Lo, the same figure slid out of a side street and loitered there as if the night air were his only business.

“Can he be watching me? Can he know what I am here for?” thought Gerard. “Impossible.”

He went off briskly, took a street or two, made a detour, and returned again, the way a man doubles back to test whether footsteps follow. The man had vanished. But when Gerard cast his gaze round, pricking the dark with suspicion, there—only a few yards behind—was the same shadow, stooping as if to fasten his shoe.

Gerard saw then that he was watched; and at that moment the moon, like a thin coin held up to light, showed him a glint—a steel gauntlet in the sentinel’s hand. The chill of that shine went through him. He knew it for an assassin.

Strange to say, it never entered his mind that his own life was the life in question. He knew of no enemy; he carried no feud in his pocket. The river’s murmur, the distant slap of oars, the small city noises settling down for sleep—these were the sounds that had drawn him; and now another sound, silent as a held breath, pressed behind him.

He turned and walked straight up to the bravo.

“My good friend,” he cried eagerly, as if begging a cook for a last bowl of broth, “sell me thine arm! a single stroke! See, here is all I have;” and he thrust his money into the man’s hands—silver and copper, warm from his body, cold as their purpose. “Oh, prithee! prithee! do one good deed, and rid me of my hateful life!”

And even as he spoke he undid his doublet and bared his bosom to the night air.

The man stared into his face as if he had been offered not a purse but a riddle in an unknown tongue.

“Why do ye hesitate?” shrieked Gerard. “Have ye no bowels? Is it so much pains to lift your arm and fall it? Is it because I am poor, and can’t give ye gold? Useless wretch, canst only strike a man behind; not look one in the face. There, then—do but turn thy head and hold thy tongue!”

With a snarl of contempt at the living and all their bargains, he ran from him, and flung himself into the water.

“Margaret!”

The heavy plunge broke the river’s surface with a dull, bread-dough sound—weight meeting wet—and the stream closed over him, hushing him as a cloth laid over a loaf. At that, the bravo seemed to wake from some stupor of his own. He ran to the bank; and with a strange cry, the assassin plunged in after the self-destroyer.

What followed shall be told by the assassin.
