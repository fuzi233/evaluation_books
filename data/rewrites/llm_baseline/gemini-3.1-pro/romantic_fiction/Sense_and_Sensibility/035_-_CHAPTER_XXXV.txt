Elinor’s curiosity regarding Mrs. Ferrars was at last satisfied, though the knowledge left a bitter, astringent taste upon her palate. She had found in the matriarch every ingredient that could render a familial union unpalatable. She had observed enough of her pride, her meanness, and her determined prejudice to comprehend the dense, impenetrable thicket of difficulties that must have retarded the marriage of Edward and herself, had he been otherwise free. Yet, as she stood by the hearth in Berkeley Street, rhythmically grinding a measure of wild-foraged dried rosehips, bruised cardamom, and roasted dandelion root with a heavy stone mortar, she found a quiet, mythic solace in the cyclic routine of the agrarian wives who had first authored the receipt. The repetitive, grounding friction of stone on stone felt like an ancestral echo, preserving her from the caprice of Mrs. Ferrars’s dominant lexicon of wealth and power. 

She poured boiling water over the crushed botanicals, inhaling the damp, earthy vapor that bloomed upward—a complex olfactory landscape of tart berry and warm spice. It was a sustaining brew, rooted in the deep cultural memory of the countryside, a far cry from the superficial, commercialized teas favored by the London elite. Elinor wondered how Lucy’s spirits could be so elevated by the mere scraps of Mrs. Ferrars’s attention, how her vanity could blind her to the reality that the matriarch’s favor was merely a weapon forged because Lucy was *not Elinor*.

The truth of Lucy’s hollow triumph was delivered again the next morning. Lady Middleton’s carriage had deposited Lucy at Berkeley Street, and the timely departure of Mrs. Jennings, summoned by a message from Mrs. Palmer, left Elinor alone with her guest. 

The acoustic ecology of the drawing-room shifted jarringly the moment Lucy spoke. Outside, the industrial noise pollution of London—the relentless, iron-shod clatter of wheels and the harsh cries of hawkers—battered the glass panes. Inside, Lucy’s voice cut through the air with a thin, ungrounded frequency, entirely lacking the resonant depth of genuine feeling. 

“My dear friend,” cried Lucy, discarding her shawl, “I come to talk to you of my happiness. Could anything be so flattering as Mrs. Ferrars’s way of treating me yesterday? So exceeding affable as she was! You know how I dreaded the thoughts of seeing her; but the very moment I was introduced, there was such an affability in her behaviour as really should seem to say, she had quite took a fancy to me. Now was not it so? You saw it all; and was not you quite struck with it?”

Elinor carefully strained the deep, garnet-hued infusion into two porcelain cups. She noted how Lucy wielded the word ‘affability’—a term stripped of its native meaning and repurposed by the dominant class as a currency of social manipulation. “She was certainly very civil to you.”

“Civil!—Did you see nothing but only civility?—I saw a vast deal more. Such kindness as fell to the share of nobody but me!—No pride, no hauteur, and your sister just the same—all sweetness and affability!”

Elinor handed Lucy a cup, wishing to steer the conversation away from the untranslatable gulf between their subjective experiences. But Lucy, who viewed the meticulously sourced, historically rich sustenance merely as utilitarian fuel to wet her throat while she boasted, took a hasty, scalding sip without a singular breath of sensory appreciation, and pressed Elinor to validate her triumph.

“Undoubtedly, if they had known your engagement,” Elinor said, savoring the tannic bite and grounding warmth of the cardamom on her own tongue, “nothing could be more flattering than their treatment of you;—but as that was not the case—”

“I guessed you would say so,” replied Lucy quickly, her tone a discordant scrape against the quiet ritual of the morning. “But there was no reason in the world why Mrs. Ferrars should seem to like me, if she did not, and her liking me is every thing. You shan’t talk me out of my satisfaction. I am sure it will all end well, and there will be no difficulties at all, to what I used to think. Mrs. Ferrars is a charming woman, and so is your sister. They are both delightful women, indeed!—I wonder I should never hear you say how agreeable Mrs. Dashwood was!”

Elinor offered no translation of her own thoughts; the power dynamics embedded in Lucy’s gloating required silence as the only dignified response. 

“Are you ill, Miss Dashwood?—you seem low—you don’t speak;—sure you an’t well.”

“I never was in better health.”

“I am glad of it with all my heart; but really you did not look it. I should be sorry to have *you* ill; you, that have been the greatest comfort to me in the world!—Heaven knows what I should have done without your friendship.”

Elinor managed a polite agreement, though she keenly felt the erosion of true meaning in such words. Lucy, however, was satisfied. “Indeed I am perfectly convinced of your regard for me, and next to Edward’s love, it is the greatest comfort I have. Poor Edward! But now there is one good thing, we shall be able to meet pretty often, for Lady Middleton’s delighted with Mrs. Dashwood, so we shall be a good deal in Harley Street, I dare say, and Edward spends half his time with his sister—besides, Lady Middleton and Mrs. Ferrars will visit now;—and Mrs. Ferrars and your sister were both so good to say more than once, they should always be glad to see me. They are such charming women!—I am sure if ever you tell your sister what I think of her, you cannot speak too high.”

Elinor refused to provide any encouragement that she would carry such artificial flatteries to her sister. 

“I am sure I should have seen it in a moment, if Mrs. Ferrars had took a dislike to me,” Lucy continued, oblivious to the rich tapestry of sensory and historical truths she trampled over. “If she had only made me a formal courtesy, for instance, without saying a word, and never after had took any notice of me, and never looked at me in a pleasant way—you know what I mean—if I had been treated in that forbidding sort of way, I should have gave it all up in despair. I could not have stood it. For where she *does* dislike, I know it is most violent.”

Elinor was spared from replying to this parade of social colonization by the heavy thud of the door opening. The servant announced Mr. Ferrars, and Edward stepped immediately into the room. 

The acoustic environment instantly transformed into a stifling, heavy vacuum. The silence that fell upon the three of them was a shared, untranslatable subjective agony. They all looked exceedingly foolish, suspended in an awkward, mythic tableau of obligation and regret. Edward appeared caught between advancing and fleeing, trapped by the sudden realization of his intertwined timelines converging in their most unpleasant form. 

The women recovered first. Lucy, maintaining the appearance of secrecy, only *looked* her tenderness, offering a slight, utilitarian greeting before falling silent. Elinor, driven by a deep well of fortitude and the restorative warmth of the heritage brew still lingering in her system, forced herself to bridge the linguistic and emotional chasm. She welcomed him with a manner that strained for ease, refusing to let Lucy’s observant gaze deter her from extending the hospitality and attentions due to a friend. 

Her deliberate, steadying cadence provided enough acoustic reassurance for Edward to take a seat, though his embarrassment remained profound. His conscience lacked Elinor’s pristine clarity, and his heart was burdened with far more depth than Lucy’s shallow vessel. Lucy sat demurely, contributing nothing to the psychic atmosphere, leaving Elinor to volunteer the necessary syllables regarding her mother’s health and their arrival in town. 

Desiring to break the stifling triad, Elinor heroically resolved to fetch Marianne. She executed her retreat with handsomest grace, loitering on the landing-place to grant them several minutes of privacy, breathing in the cold, still air of the corridor before engaging her sister. 

When Marianne finally entered, the raptures of Edward’s secret reunion were forcibly concluded. Marianne’s joy brought a sweeping, vibrant energy to the room. Her voice, carrying the rich, uncorrupted dialect of their agrarian youth, possessed a timbre that sought to harmonize the discordant space. She met him with a voice that expressed the unvarnished affection of a sister. 

“Dear Edward!” she cried, her words free of the capitalistic artifice infecting the rest of the city. “This is a moment of great happiness!—This would almost make amends for every thing!”

Edward attempted to return her warmth, but the oppressive presence of Lucy rendered him incapable of giving voice to his genuine feelings. As they all sat, a profound silence returned, punctuated only by Marianne’s expressive, speaking tenderness as she looked between Edward and Elinor, regretting only the grating presence of Lucy.

Edward, desperate to fracture the quiet, noted Marianne’s altered looks and expressed his fear that London’s aggressive environment did not agree with her.

“Oh, don’t think of me!” Marianne replied with spirited earnestness, tears springing to her eyes. “Don’t think of *my* health. Elinor is well, you see. That must be enough for us both.”

Lucy’s expression darkened at this, her features twisting into something far from benignant. 

“Do you like London?” Edward asked, striving for any lifeline of conversation.

“Not at all. I expected much pleasure in it, but I have found none. The sight of you, Edward, is the only comfort it has afforded; and thank Heaven! you are what you always were!” She paused. The silence in the room felt heavy, layered with generational misunderstandings. “I think, Elinor,” she added, “we must employ Edward to take care of us in our return to Barton. In a week or two, I suppose, we shall be going; and, I trust, Edward will not be very unwilling to accept the charge.”

Edward muttered a fractured string of syllables that conveyed nothing, swallowed by his own agitation. But Marianne, seamlessly weaving his discomfort into a narrative that suited her own idyllic worldview, was perfectly satisfied. 

“We spent such a day, Edward, in Harley Street yesterday! So dull, so wretchedly dull!—But I have much to say to you on that head, which cannot be said now.” She deferred the specifics of her disgust with his mother's oppressive social theater until they were in private. “But why were you not there, Edward?—Why did you not come?”

“I was engaged elsewhere.”

“Engaged! But what was that, when such friends were to be met?”

“Perhaps, Miss Marianne,” cried Lucy, eagerly weaponizing the dominant lexicon in an attempt to sting, “you think young men never stand upon engagements, if they have no mind to keep them, little as well as great.”

Elinor flared with silent anger at the linguistic manipulation, but Marianne, wholly insulated by her own pure dialect of devotion, missed the barb entirely. “Not so, indeed,” she replied calmly. “For, seriously speaking, I am very sure that conscience only kept Edward from Harley Street. And I really believe he *has* the most delicate conscience in the world; the most scrupulous in performing every engagement, however minute, and however it may make against his interest or pleasure. He is the most fearful of giving pain, of wounding expectation, and the most incapable of being selfish, of any body I ever saw. Edward, it is so, and I will say it. What! are you never to hear yourself praised!—Then you must be no friend of mine; for those who will accept of my love and esteem, must submit to my open commendation.”

The bitter irony of her commendation was acutely felt by two-thirds of her audience. The untranslatable gulf between the word ‘engagement’ as Marianne understood it—a vow of honor—and as it functioned for Edward—a legally binding shackle to a superficial woman—proved so unexhilarating that Edward soon rose to flee the acoustic suffocation of the room.

“Going so soon!” said Marianne. “My dear Edward, this must not be.” Drawing him aside, she whispered her conviction that Lucy could not endure much longer. But the mythical weight of his entrapment drove him out. Lucy, who would have lingered for hours had he remained, soon departed as well, leaving the physical and sonic space mercifully empty.

“What can bring her here so often?” Marianne demanded, once the iron wheels of Lucy’s hired carriage clattered away into the industrial din outside. “Could not she see that we wanted her gone!—how teazing to Edward!”

“Why so?” Elinor replied, forced to adopt the hollow conventions of social defense. “We were all his friends, and Lucy has been the longest known to him of any. It is but natural that he should like to see her as well as ourselves.”

Marianne looked at her steadily, perceiving the deep fracture in Elinor’s native tongue. “You know, Elinor, that this is a kind of talking which I cannot bear. If you only hope to have your assertion contradicted, as I must suppose to be the case, you ought to recollect that I am the last person in the world to do it. I cannot descend to be tricked out of assurances, that are not really wanted.”

She turned and left the room. Elinor dared not follow to offer any translation of the truth; bound by her forced promise of secrecy, she was compelled to let the cultural memory of their shared sisterly confidence erode beneath the weight of an imposed silence. She could only sit beside the cold dregs of her herbal infusion, hoping the cyclical turning of days would not subject Edward or herself to the agonizing dissonance of such a meeting again.
