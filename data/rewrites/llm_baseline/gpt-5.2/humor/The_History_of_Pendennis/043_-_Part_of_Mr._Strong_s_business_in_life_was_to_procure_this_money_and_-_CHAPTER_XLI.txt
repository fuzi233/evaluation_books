Poor Foker’s Richmond dinner—bought with honest guineas and seasoned with melting ices—lay upon his spirits like a wet napkin on a soufflé.

He sat at the head of his own table, amid a wreck of dessert as showy and as joyless as a sugared bride-cake left out in the rain: pineapples hacked into golden ruin; ices slumping into pale puddles; claret bottles standing about like soldiers that had done their duty and fallen where they could. Cigar-ash freckled the fruit, and the whole air had that disagreeable perfume of stale smoke and sweet stuff mixed together, as if somebody had tried to preserve a peach in a chimney.

“I wonder how the deuce I ever could have liked these people,” he said to himself, scraping his thoughts together as a cook scrapes the bottom of a pan that has caught. “Why, I can see the crow’s-feet under Rougemont’s eyes, and the paint on her cheeks is laid on as thick as Clown’s in a pantomime! Calverley’s slang is like burnt gravy—sticky, black, and everywhere. And old Colchicum—old Col—coming down here in his brougham with his coronet on it, and sitting bodkin between Mademoiselle Coralie and her mother! A peer and a horse-rider of Franconi’s! It won’t do. By Jove, it won’t do. I ain’t proud; but it will not do!”

“Twopence-halfpenny for your thoughts, Fokey!” cried Miss Rougemont, drawing at her cigar with lips as violently vermilion as if they had been dipped like a ladyfinger in cochineal. She looked him over with the connoisseurship of a dancer appraising a costume, and found him—unusually—quiet.

“Does Foker ever think?” drawled Poyntz, whose voice always sounded as though it had been trained to doze off mid-sentence, like a kettle that won’t boil. “Foker, a fair capitalist at this end of the table offers a considerable sum for the present emanations of your valuable intellect.”

“What the deuce is Poyntz a-talking about?” snapped Miss Calverley to her neighbour. “I hate him. He’s a drawlin’, sneerin’ beast.”

“What a droll of a little man is that little Fokare, my lor’,” said Mademoiselle Coralie in her sunny Gascon French, which had a twang like a guitar-string—warm, bright, and a little saucy. “He does not look to have twenty years.”

“I wish I were of his age,” sighed the venerable Colchicum, inclining his purple countenance to a great goblet of claret with the affection of an old epicure bending toward a remembered sauce.

“C’te jeunesse. Peuh! je m’en fiche,” said Madame Brack, taking a heroic pinch from my lord’s delicate gold snuff-box with fingers that looked capable of doing mischief in any dish they touched. “Je n’aime que les hommes faits, moi. Comme milor. Coralie! n’est-ce pas que tu n’aimes que les hommes faits, ma bichette?”

My lord grinned, pleased in the way of old gentlemen who like being called “fine,” even when the compliment is flung like salt. “You flatter me, Madame Brack.”

“Taisez-vous, Maman, vous n’etes qu’une bête,” Coralie cried, shrugging robustly; whereupon my lord observed, with a chuckle, that she did not flatter at any rate, and he pocketed his snuff-box as a careful housekeeper pockets the last nutmeg when a guest seems disposed to grate it all.

There is no need to ladle out the rest of that conversation for the reader, as one spares a friend the dregs at the bottom of a punch-bowl. It would not edify. Only, remember: not all ladies of the ballet are like Miss Calverley, nor all peers like the late Viscount Colchicum; though there have been such, and there have also been respectable gentlemen who objected less to the entertainment than to the noise made about it. Major Pendennis, for example, would never have said no to pleasure—provided it was served in a private room, with a proper lid on it, and a few great names to garnish the dish.

“Give the young men their pleasures,” that worthy guardian had said to Pen more than once. “I’m not one of your strait-laced moralists, but an old man of the world, begad; and I know that as long as it lasts young men will be young men.” And he had known some young men for whom he allowed a respectable seventy years as the natural season for sowing wild oats—if they were men of fashion, and the oats were sown in the right fields.

That night Foker drove his lovely guests home to Brompton in the drag; but he was as silent as a man who has eaten too much sugar and is ashamed of his stomach. The jokes behind him pattered like peas on a drum. Usually he would have answered with his own merry racket; now he sat with his shoulders a little hunched, as if the very harness of his gaiety pinched him.

When the ladies alighted and asked whether he would step in and take something to drink, he declined with such a tragical politeness that they fancied the Governor had scolded him, or that the brewery had burnt down. He said nothing of what truly ailed him. He left Mesdames Rougemont and Calverley, unheeding even the latter’s cries from her balcony—hanging over it like Jezebel at the window—bidding him give another party soon.

He sent the drag home with a groom and went on foot himself, hands in pockets, mind in the sort of stew where nothing tastes right. The stars and moon shone tranquilly overhead; London’s distant hum rose and fell like the sea heard through shutters; and the nearer sounds—the clop of late horses, the rattle of a returning carriage, a watchman’s measured call—were all so composed, so cleanly spaced, that they seemed to mock his inner clatter. In such a nightly soundscape one ought, by rights, to feel soothed; but passion is an ill-bred guest, and will not keep time with the street.

He went and gazed up at the house in Grosvenor Place, at the windows he supposed to be those of the beloved object, as if her very curtains were a holy relic. He moaned and sighed in a fashion piteous enough. Policeman X saw him, and later informed Sir Francis Clavering’s people—who were taking beer on a coach-box at a neighbouring public-house, after bringing home their lady from the French play—that there had been “another chap hanging about the premises that evening—a little chap, dressed like a swell.”

And now, with that ingenuity which belongs only to a certain passion—keen as a kitchen-boy’s nose for pies—Mr. Foker began to dodge Miss Amory through London and to appear wherever she could be met, like parsley sprinkled on every dish whether it suits or not.

If Lady Clavering went to the French play, where her ladyship had a box, Mr. Foker—whose knowledge of French was as thin as a watered consommé—appeared in a stall, straining his ears and smiling at things he did not understand. He found out where her engagements were (it is possible Anatole, his man, made acquaintance with Sir Francis Clavering’s gentleman and had a peep at her ladyship’s engagement-book), and at many evening parties Mr. Foker appeared—astonishing the world, and astonishing his mother most of all, for he ordered Lady Agnes to apply for cards to entertainments he had hitherto treated with contempt.

To the pleased and unsuspicious lady he said he went to parties because it was right to see the world. He said he went to the French play to perfect himself in the language, and that there was no lesson like a comedy or vaudeville. And when one night Lady Agnes saw him stand up and dance, and complimented his elegance and activity, the mendacious little rogue protested he had learned to dance in Paris, whereas Anatole knew perfectly well his young master used to slip off privily to an academy in Brewer Street and study steps there in the morning, as earnestly as a cook studying sauces.

The modern casino was not yet invented, or was still in its cradle; and gentlemen of Mr. Foker’s time had not the facilities for acquiring the science of dancing which our present youth enjoy, who can learn a quadrille as easily as they order an oyster—quick, loud, and fashionable.

Old Major Pendennis seldom missed church. He considered it a gentleman’s duty to patronise public worship, and that it was a correct thing to be seen on a Sunday in a pew. One day it happened he and Arthur went thither together: Pen, in high favour just now, had breakfasted with his uncle, and they walked across the Park to a church not far from Belgrave Square.

“There is a charity sermon at Saint James’s,” the Major said, having read the bills on the pillars of his parish church; and the word “charity,” like the word “discount,” could turn him as surely as a tide. “We will go across the Park; and then, begad, we will go to the Claverings’ and ask them for lunch in a friendly way. Lady Clavering likes to be asked for lunch—monstrous hospitable.”

“I met them at dinner last week at Lady Agnes Foker’s, sir,” Pen said. “The Begum was very kind indeed. She is kind everywhere. But I share your opinion about Miss Amory—one of your opinions, that is; for you were changing the last time we spoke about her.”

“And what do you think of her now?” asked the elder, turning his head as if to sniff for scandal the way a housewife sniffs for smoke.

“I think her the most confounded little flirt in London,” Pen answered, laughing. “She made a tremendous assault upon Harry Foker, who sat next to her, and to whom she gave all the talk, though I took her down.”

“Bah! Henry Foker is engaged to his cousin; all the world knows it. Not a bad coup of Lady Rosherville’s. I should say young Foker, at his father’s death—old Foker’s life’s devilish bad; you know he had a fit at Arthur’s last year—I should say young Foker won’t have less than fourteen thousand a year from the brewery, besides Logwood and Norfolk property. I’ve no pride about me, Pen. I like a man of birth, certainly; but dammy, I like a brewery that brings in fourteen thousand a year. Ha, ha! That’s the sort of man for me. Stick to fellows of that sort now you’re launched—fellows with a stake in the country, begad.”

“Foker sticks to me, sir,” Pen said. “He has been at our chambers several times lately. He has asked me to dinner. We are almost as great friends as in our youth. And his talk is about Blanche Amory from morning till night. I’m sure he’s sweet upon her.”

“I’m sure he is engaged to his cousin, and they will keep the young man to his bargain,” the Major said. “Marriages in these families are affairs of state. Lady Agnes was made to marry old Foker by the late Lord, though she was notoriously partial to her cousin killed at Albuera—saved her life out of the lake at Drummington, too. I remember Lady Agnes an exceedingly fine woman. But what did she do? Of course she married her father’s man. Foker sate for Drummington till the Reform Bill, and paid dev’lish well for his seat. And you may depend upon it, sir, Foker senior, a parvenu who loves great men as all parvenus do, has ambitious views for his son. Harry must do as his father bids him. I’ve known a hundred cases of love in young men and women: they kick, they resist, they make a riot; but they end by listening to reason, begad.”

“Blanche is a dangerous girl, sir,” Pen said. “I was smitten with her myself once, and very far gone too. But that was years ago.”

“Were you? How far did it go? Did she return it?” asked the Major, looking hard at him—so hard that Pen felt as though he were being carved like a joint.

Pen laughed and said that at one time he thought he was pretty well in Miss Amory’s good graces, but his mother did not like her, and the affair went off. He did not think fit to serve up all the particulars of that old dish again.

“A man might go farther and fare worse, Arthur,” the Major said, still looking queerly. “Her birth—her father was the mate of a ship, they say—and she has not money enough,” Pen objected, dandifying himself with the air of a man rejecting a pudding because the plate is plain.

“What’s ten thousand pound and a girl bred up like her?”

“You use my own words, and it is all very well. But I tell you in confidence—in strict honour, mind—that it’s my belief she has a devilish deal more than ten thousand. And from what I saw of her the other day, and—and have heard—I should say she’s a devilish accomplished, clever girl; would make a good wife with a sensible husband.”

“How do you know about her money?” Pen asked, smiling. “You seem to have information about everybody.”

“I do know a few things, sir, and I don’t tell all I know. Mark that.” And as if to cap the dish with a secret sauce, he added, “And as for that charming Miss Amory—charming, begad! she is—if I saw her Mrs. Arthur Pendennis, I should neither be sorry nor surprised, begad! And if you object to ten thousand, what would you say to thirty, or forty, or fifty?” The Major’s eyes glittered like a man counting coin in candlelight.

“Well, sir,” Pen said to his godfather and namesake, “make her Mrs. Arthur Pendennis. You can do it as well as I.”

“Psha! you are laughing at me, sir,” the Major replied peevishly. “And you ought not to laugh so near a church gate. Here we are at St. Benedict’s. They say Mr. Oriel is a beautiful preacher.”

The bells were tolling; the people streamed in; the carriages rolled up and discharged their pretty loads of devotees, rustling and murmuring like a flock settling. Pen, who from habitual feeling was always more than respectful in a place of worship, had a moment’s sense of the oddness of their talk—as if they had been discussing recipes at the very mouth of a fast. The Major, beside him, felt no incongruity whatever. His hat was brushed; his wig was trim; his neckcloth perfectly tied. He looked at every soul in the congregation—the bald heads and bonnets, the flowers and feathers—with a demure air, hardly lifting his eyes from his book (which he could not read without glasses).

Pen’s gravity, however, was sorely tried when, glancing toward the servants’ seats, he spied Henry Foker, Esquire, by the side of a demure gentleman in plush, having discovered this particular avenue to Heaven. Harry’s eye strayed from his book as readily as a spoon from a plate, and following its direction Pen found it fixed upon two bonnets—a yellow and a pink—perched on the heads of Lady Clavering and Blanche Amory.

If the Major was not the only man to carry worldly affairs to the church door, Harry was certainly not the only one to smuggle worldly love into the aisle.

When the congregation issued forth at the conclusion of the service, Foker was among the first out, but Pen came up with him as he lingered about the entrance, unwilling to leave until my lady’s barouche, with its bewigged coachman, had borne away mistress and daughter from their devotions.

When the two ladies emerged, they found the Pendennises—uncle and nephew—and Harry Foker, sucking the crook of his stick, standing in the sunshine as if waiting for a dinner-bell.

To see and to ask to eat were simultaneous with the good-natured Begum. She invited the three gentlemen to luncheon straightway.

Blanche was particularly gracious. “Oh, do come,” she said to Arthur, “if you are not too great a man. I want so to talk to you about—but we mustn’t say what, here, you know. What would Mr. Oriel say?” And she jumped into the carriage after her mamma. “I’ve read every word of it. It’s adorable,” she added, still addressing Pen.

“I know who is,” said Mr. Arthur, making rather a pert bow.

“What’s the row about?” asked Mr. Foker, puzzled.

“I suppose Miss Clavering means ‘Walter Lorraine,’” said the Major, nodding with the air of a man who has smelt the roast and knows what joint it is.

“I suppose so, sir. There was a famous review in the Pall Mall this morning. It was Warrington’s doing though, and I must not be too proud.”

“A review in Pall Mall? Walter Lorraine? What the doose do you mean?” Foker cried. “Walter Lorraine died of the measles, poor little beggar, when we were at Grey Friars. I remember his mother coming up.”

“You are not a literary man, Foker,” Pen said, laughing, and hooking his arm into his friend’s. “You must know I have been writing a novel, and some of the papers have spoken very well of it. Perhaps you don’t read the Sunday papers?”

“I read Bell’s Life regular, old boy,” Mr. Foker answered; at which Pen laughed again, and the three proceeded in good humour to Lady Clavering’s.

After luncheon Blanche resumed the subject of the novel, for she loved poets and men of letters—if she loved anything—with a sincerity that had at least the merit of being excited by something other than money. “Some of the passages made me cry,” she said. “Positively they did.”

Pen, with some fatuity, replied, “I am happy to think I have a part of vos larmes, Miss Blanche.” And the Major—who had not read more than six pages of Pen’s book—put on his sanctified look and said, “Yes—there are some passages quite affecting—mons’ous affecting.”

“Oh, if it makes you cry,” Lady Amory declared, “I wouldn’t read it. I wouldn’t.”

“Don’t, mamma,” Blanche said, with a French shrug; and then she fell into rhapsodies about the book: the snatches of poetry interspersed; the two heroines, Leonora and Neaera, served up like twin dishes to be compared; the two heroes, Walter Lorraine and his rival the young Duke—“and what good company you introduce us to,” she said archly. “Quel ton! How much of your life have you passed at court? Are you a prime minister’s son, Mr. Arthur?”

Pen laughed. “It is as cheap for a novelist to create a Duke as to make a Baronet. Shall I tell you a secret? I promoted all my characters at the request of the publisher. The young Duke was only a young Baron when the novel was first written; his false friend the Viscount was a simple commoner—and so on with all the story.”

“What a wicked, satirical, pert young man you have become! Comme vous voilà formé!” Blanche cried. “How different from Arthur Pendennis of the country! Ah! I think I like Arthur Pendennis of the country best, though!” And she gave him the full benefit of her eyes—first the fond, appealing glance, then the modest look down toward the carpet which showed the dark lids and long fringed lashes as a cook shows the gloss on a well-braised ham.

Pen, of course, protested he had not changed in the least. Blanche sighed tenderly; and having, as she judged, basted Arthur enough for the present—happy or miserable, whichever he chose to be—she proceeded to cajole his companion, Mr. Harry Foker, who during the literary talk had sat sucking his cane and wishing he were a clever chap like that Pen.

If the Major thought that by telling Miss Amory of Mr. Foker’s engagement to his cousin Lady Ann Milton—an item of intelligence he neatly slipped to the girl as he sat by her at luncheon—he would prevent Blanche from paying further attention to the young heir of Foker’s Entire, he was entirely mistaken. She became only the more gracious. She praised Harry, and everything belonging to him: praised his mamma; praised the pony he rode in the Park; praised the lovely breloques and gimcracks at his watch-chain; praised that dear little darling of a cane; praised those delicious monkeys’ heads with ruby eyes that ornamented his shirt and made the buttons of his waistcoat—till Harry blushed and tingled with pleasure like a boy offered extra jam.

Then, having seasoned him to her taste, she turned and took another theme.

“I am afraid Mr. Foker is a very sad young man,” she said, looking round at Pen.

“He does not look so,” Pen answered, with a sneer.

“I mean we have heard sad stories about him. Haven’t we, mamma? What was Mr. Poyntz saying here the other day, about that party at Richmond? Oh, you naughty creature!” And seeing Harry’s face turn to alarm, and Pen’s to amusement, she flashed back to Arthur: “I believe you are just as bad. I believe you would have liked to have been there—wouldn’t you? I know you would. Yes—and so should I.”

“Lor, Blanche!” cried mamma.

“Well, I would. I never saw an actress in my life. I would give anything to know one; for I adore talent. And I adore Richmond. And I adore Greenwich. And I say I should like to go there.”

“Why should not we three bachelors,” the Major broke out gallantly—to Pen’s special surprise—“beg these ladies to honour us with their company at Greenwich? Is Lady Clavering to go on forever being hospitable, and may we make no return? Speak for yourselves, young men—eh, begad! Here is my nephew with his pockets full of money—his pockets full, begad!—and Mr. Henry Foker, who, as I have heard say, is pretty well to do in the world. How is your lovely cousin, Lady Ann, Mr. Foker? Here are these two young ones—and they allow an old fellow like me to speak. Lady Clavering, will you do me the favour to be my guest? And Miss Blanche shall be Arthur’s—if she will be so good.”

“Oh, delightful!” cried Blanche.

“I like a bit of fun too,” said Lady Clavering. “And we will take some day when Sir Francis—”

“When Sir Francis dines out—yes, mamma,” Blanche finished, with the practical innocence of a girl arranging the cupboard before the cat comes in. “It will be charming.”

And a charming day it was.

The dinner was ordered at Greenwich; and if the fare was not immortal, it was at least honest—fish with the clean smell of river and salt, meat that had been properly rested, sauces not bullied by too much flour, and a bottle or two behaving better than those at Richmond. There was talk enough, and laughter enough, and—best of all for Harry—delicious opportunities of conversation with Blanche during the repast, and afterwards on the balcony of their room at the hotel, where the river moved below with a soft wash like a great spoon stirring in a basin. The very sound of it seemed to soothe the mind, until a passing steamer coughed its industrial racket into the evening, shaking the air as if modern life could not bear to let any old music play undisturbed.

They drove home in her ladyship’s barouche. Pen came down with his uncle in Sir Hugh Trumpington’s brougham, which the Major borrowed for the occasion.

“I am an old soldier, begad,” he said; “and I learned in early life to make myself comfortable.”

And being an old soldier, he allowed the two young men to pay for the dinner between them; and all the way home, in the borrowed brougham, he rallied Pen about Miss Amory’s evident partiality for him, praised her good looks, spirits, and wit, and again told him—under the strictest confidence—that she would be a devilish deal richer than people thought.
