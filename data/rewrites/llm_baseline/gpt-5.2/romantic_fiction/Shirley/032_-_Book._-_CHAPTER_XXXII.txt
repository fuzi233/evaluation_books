Briarmains lay nearer than the Hollow, and so it was to Briarmains that Mr. Yorke carried the stricken man. He had him lifted, not as one lifts a burden, but as one supports a life; and when the best bed in the house was thrown open, it received Robert Moore as though the place had always waited for him. Blood, warm and dark, had soaked through cloth and coat; the smell of iron rose with the steam of wet wool; and to Matthew Yorke—who was not readily moved by any sentiment that did not also taste of power—the sight had a harsh relish. Here was a strong creature flung low at his very door, and there was no other hand to take charge of him. That entire dependence—this proud, southern head now helpless under his roof—gave Yorke what he secretly liked: mastery that could call itself benevolence.

His wife liked it too, and more frankly. Some women would have screamed at a gory threshold, would have dissolved into trembling and tears; Mrs. Yorke, who could work herself into hysterics because Jessie lingered among the currant bushes instead of knitting, found her nerves steady as oak when a half-murdered man was laid in her best chamber in the howe of the night. The crisis braced her; it put a sharper set to her mouth and a kind of warlike flourish to her cap—as if she had been given a new part, and meant to play it grandly.

In truth, she was of that odd temper which will grind a weary servant for dropping a fork, and yet face pestilence with a hero’s hand. When she found Moore committed to her charge—left to her, to be washed, watched, fed, guarded—something like tenderness, tough and jealous, awoke in her. It showed itself chiefly in exclusion. Jessie and Rose were driven from the upper floor as if they carried contagion; the housemaids were forbidden the stairs. Had any one smoothed the pillow or offered a draught, Mrs. Yorke would have taken it as an intrusion upon her prerogative.

Had the wounded man been carried through the rectory gates, and old Helstone had made a martyr of him, the Yorkes would have judged him right served, and turned the story into a moral against masters who meddle and tyrannise. Brought to Briarmains, he became for the moment their possession, and therefore precious.

Strangely enough, there was one exception to Mrs. Yorke’s jealousy. Louis Moore was allowed to come and sit on the edge of the bed, to stoop and press his lips to his brother’s pale forehead, to hold the inert hand and speak low words into a silence that answered only by breathing. Mrs. Yorke bore it—almost with dignity. Once she even suffered him to sit up all night in the room; and at five o’clock, on a wet November morning, she rose herself, lit the kitchen fire with her own hands, and made breakfast for the two brothers. She did it solidly, as she did all things: a pan put on to heat, bread cut thick, something savoury set near the embers till it took colour, tea strong enough to stand a spoon in it. In a boundless flannel wrapper with shawl and nightcap, she served and then sat to watch them eat with the complacent look of a hen brooding over her chickens.

Yet even that morning she gave the cook warning for daring to carry up a basin of sago-gruel to Mr. Moore, and the housemaid lost favour because she had the audacity to air Mr. Louis’s surtout by the kitchen and help him on with it—accepting in return a smile, a “Thank you, my girl,” and a shilling. Two pale ladies once came, anxious and humble, begging only one instant’s sight of the sufferer; Mrs. Yorke’s heart hardened like suet in cold. She sent them away with a sting.

When Hortense Moore arrived, matters did not grow worse, as might have been expected. The family of the Moores, in their various ways, suited Mrs. Yorke as no other family had ever done. Hortense and she found an inexhaustible comfort in discussing the depravity of servants. The kitchen, to both their minds, was a province that must be governed by suspicion and discipline; their talk ran on pilfering fingers, saucy tongues, idleness that ate bread it had not earned. Hortense, moreover, showed no jealousy of Mrs. Yorke’s nursing; she left her that office with little interference, and made herself useful in her own fashion—fidgeting, spying, reporting, haunting the kitchen like a sentinel. They agreed, with perfect unanimity, to keep visitors from the sickroom: the mill-owner was held captive, and scarcely suffered air or sun.

Mr. MacTurk—the surgeon—pronounced the wound dangerous, though not, he hoped, hopeless. At first he wished to place beside Moore a nurse chosen by himself; but Mrs. Yorke and Hortense would not hear of it. They promised obedience to orders. The case was left in their hands.

They meant well; but something went wrong. Bandages were displaced—tampered with; blood followed, in a draining rush that frightened even those stout women. MacTurk was sent for; he came with his horse afoam, and his temper, never mild, at its keenest edge. When he saw the patient’s state he poured forth language as hot as spirits spilled on a flame. A spray of it struck Mr. Graves, his stony young assistant; another nosegay fell on the head of MacTurk’s own son—his own image in miniature; but the full basket of his wrath was flung, without distinction, upon meddling womankind in general.

Through the best part of a winter night surgeon and satellites laboured and quarrelled over Moore’s exhausted frame. They bent, they lifted, they tied and cut and pressed; on one side of the bed were three men with their instruments and their will; on the other, Death—quiet, patient, leaning in. The struggle lasted till daybreak, and when the first grey light came through the panes, it seemed so equally held that either party might have claimed advantage.

At dawn Graves and young MacTurk were left to watch, while the senior went out to seek additional strength. He returned with it in the person of Mrs. Horsfall, the best nurse on his staff, and to her he delivered Moore with stern injunctions, as if handing over a dangerous creature and not a sick man. She received both injunctions and patient stolidly, established herself in the easy-chair at the bedhead, and began her reign.

Mrs. Horsfall had one virtue: what MacTurk ordered, she obeyed to the letter. The Ten Commandments weighed less with her than the surgeon’s dictum. In all else she was no woman, but a dragon—huge, hard, and indifferent. Hortense fell effaced before her; Mrs. Yorke withdrew—crushed; and these were women of weight in their own eyes and in the eyes of others. Mrs. Horsfall’s breadth, height, bone, and brawn cowed them clean; they retreated to the back parlour while she took possession of the upstairs and went and came as she pleased. She took her dram three times a day, and smoked her pipe four.

As to Moore, no one now ventured to inquire too freely. Mrs. Horsfall had him, as it were, at dry-nurse; and the house, hearing her heavy step above and smelling her tobacco drift down the stairwell, guessed—without comfort—that she did for him as she did.

Morning and evening MacTurk came to see his patient. The case, complicated by the new mischance, interested him; he regarded Moore as a damaged piece of clockwork which it would be creditable to set going again. Graves and young MacTurk—his only other visitors—looked at him with the cold curiosity they might have bestowed on the occupant of the dissecting-room at Stilbro’ Infirmary.

So Robert Moore lay through the diminishing days and lengthening nights of drear November: in pain, in danger, too weak to move and nearly too weak to speak, with a giantess for keeper and three surgeons for society. At first he feebly resisted Mrs. Horsfall; he hated the sight of her rough bulk and dreaded the contact of her hard hands. She taught him docility in a trice. His six feet and manly thews counted for nothing against her. She turned him as another woman might turn a babe; when he was good she called him “my dear” and “honey,” and when he was bad she shook him. If he tried to speak while MacTurk stood near, she lifted her hand and bade him hush like a forward child. Had she not smoked, had she not taken gin, it would have been better, he thought; once, in her absence, he said faintly to MacTurk that “that woman is a dram-drinker.”

“Pooh! my dear sir—they are all so,” returned the surgeon. “But Horsfall has this virtue: drunk or sober, she always remembers to obey me.”

At length autumn withdrew its fogs and rains; the winds went sighing elsewhere, and deep winter came with clearness and frost.

A calm day settled into a crystalline evening. The world took a North Pole colouring, all its lights looking like pale gems seen under ice: lilac-blue hills, a setting sun with purple mixed into its red, an azure sky silvered as if by cold, and when the stars rose they were not gold but crystal—white points in a clean, ringing dark. Even the woods, no longer green, no longer russet, wore a neutral tint, and the fallen leaves, when stepped on, gave a dry, soft crackle that was almost the only sound beneath the hush. In such stillness a footfall mattered; the very air seemed to listen.

Along the highroad, a troop of Briarfield grammar-school boys were trudging home; but one had slipped away and entered the wood. He sought a certain tree and a certain mossy mound at its root, convenient for a seat. He wore a dark-blue coat; he moved like a young creature already resolved not to be ruled. The air was cold, the hour late; yet he sat down as if time were his servant.

What did he think? His face did not tell; as yet it was more mask than mirror. Fifteen, slight and tall for his years, with a watchful eye that seemed always on the point of detecting any attempt to overreach him, he had in his features the promise of resistance rather than of amenity. Wise ushers avoided needless collision with that lad. Severity would only harden him; flattery would fail. He was best left alone, and let time and experience do their work.

It was Martin Yorke, of course—Matthew’s son—who professed to trample on poetry, and would answer sentiment with sarcasm; yet here he was, alone in a wood, letting Nature spread a page of stern, silent verse before him, and reading it with attention he would have denied.

From his satchel he drew a book—not the Latin grammar, but a contraband volume of fairy tales. There would be light enough yet for his keen eyes; besides, the moon waited, and her beam, dim and vague, began to fill the glade.

He read, and the hush of the wood became the hush of mountains: a rude, desolate region, shapeless and colourless, where bells tinkled in mist. Out of that formlessness rode the brightest vision—a lady in green on a snow-white palfrey, gems on her dress, and a question in her mouth that bound the listener like a spell and drew him onward into fairyland.

He turned a page, and the next legend set him on a sea-shore where tide boiled at the base of dizzy cliffs. Rain and wind lashed the reef; black rocks stretched far into the water; spray dashed and flashed and leapt in white wreaths. A lone wanderer moved among wet seaweed, peering into hollows where the brine lay fathoms deep, emerald-clear, and strange vegetation curled like snakes about treasures of shells—green, purple, pearly. A cry rang out; at the bleak point of the reef stood a tall, pale thing shaped like man, made of spray—transparent, tremulous, awful. It was not alone: foam-women crowded the rocks, white Nereids, evanescent and wild.

Hush. Martin shut the book and slid it into the satchel. He had heard a tread.

At first it might have been only the leaf-fall stirring; then again the dead leaves, lightly crushed, rustled on the path. He watched. The trees parted, and a woman came forth.

She wore dark silk; a veil covered her face. Martin had never met a lady in this wood—never any female save a village girl come now and then to gather nuts. To-night the apparition did not displease him. As she approached, he noted she was neither old nor plain; very youthful, indeed; and but that he recognized her as one he had often wilfully pronounced ugly, he might have sworn there were traits of beauty behind the thin gauze.

She passed him without a word—he knew she would. All women were proud monkeys, he told himself, and he knew no more conceited doll than Caroline Helstone. The thought was scarcely formed when the lady stopped, retraced two steps, raised her veil, and let her glance rest on his face.

“Are you one of Mr. Yorke’s sons?” she asked softly.

No human evidence would ever have persuaded Martin Yorke that he blushed at that address; yet he did, up to the ears.

“I am,” he answered bluntly, and tried to recover himself by wondering, superciliously, what would come next.

“You are Martin, I think?” she said.

The sentence, simple and a little timid, chimed with his nature like a note struck true. He had a keen sense of his own personality; he liked that she discriminated him from his brothers. Like his father, he hated ceremony. To be called “Martin,” and not Mr. Martin or Master Martin—both of which would have lost her his favour—was agreeable. Yet the tone held no slipshod familiarity; there was a slight hesitation, a touch of bashfulness, which seemed to him exactly right.

“I am Martin.”

“Are your father and mother well?”—lucky she did not say papa and mamma—“and Rose and Jessie?”

“I suppose so.”

“My cousin Hortense is still at Briarmains?”

“Oh yes.”

He gave a half-smile and a comic little groan. She answered it with a faint smile of her own; she could guess in what odour Hortense was held by the young Yorkes.

“Does your mother like her?”

“They suit so well about the servants they can’t help liking each other.”

“It is cold to-night.”

“Why are you out so late?”

“I lost my way in this wood.”

Then Martin laughed—fresh, scornful.

“Lost your way in the mighty forest of Briarmains! You deserve never more to find it.”

“I never was here before, and I believe I am trespassing now. You might inform against me if you chose, Martin, and have me fined. It is your father’s wood.”

“I should think I knew that. But since you are so simple as to lose your way, I will guide you out.”

“You need not. I have got into the track now. I shall be right. Martin”—a little quickly—“how is Mr. Moore?”

Martin had heard rumours; he decided it might be amusing to try an experiment.

“Going to die. Nothing can save him. All hope flung overboard!”

She put her veil aside. Her eyes searched his, and her lips shaped the words as if they were pain.

“To die!”

“To die. All along of the women—my mother and the rest. They did something about his bandages that finished everything. He would have got better but for them. I am sure they should be arrested, cribbed, tried, and brought in for Botany Bay, at the very least.”

Perhaps she did not hear the judgment. She stood still, as if the winter had struck through her, and after two minutes she moved on without another word—no good-night, no further inquiry.

This was not amusing; not what Martin had calculated on. He had expected some scene, something demonstrative. It was hardly worth frightening the girl if she gave him no entertainment in return.

“Miss Helstone!” he called.

She did not turn. He hurried after her, overtook her among the grey trunks.

“Come; are you uneasy about what I said?”

“You know nothing about death, Martin. You are too young for me to talk to concerning such a thing.”

“Did you believe me? It’s all flummery! Moore eats like three men. They are always making sago or tapioca or something good for him. I never go into the kitchen but there is a saucepan on the fire cooking him some dainty. I think I will play the old soldier, and be fed on the fat of the land like him.”

“Martin! Martin!” Her voice trembled; she stopped short.

“It is exceedingly wrong of you, Martin. You have almost killed me.”

She leaned against a tree, shuddering, trembling, as pale as the moonlight that began to strengthen between the branches.

Martin watched her with inexpressible curiosity. In one sense it was, as he would have said, nuts to see this; it told him so much, and he had begun to relish the discovery of secrets. In another sense it pricked him with a remembrance: once he had heard a blackbird lamenting for her nestlings, which Matthew had crushed with a stone, and the sound had made him feel something he did not like. He could not find words to comfort her; so he hunted in his mind for something to do. He smiled; and in that smile, for all its boyish mischief, there was a sudden transparency.

“Eureka!” he cried. “I’ll set all straight by-and-by. You are better now, Miss Caroline. Walk forward.”

He chose a short cut—forgetting it would be harder for her than for him—that led to no gate. The path ended in a wall and hedge; he had to help her over formidable obstacles, and while he railed at her for helplessness, he liked—perfectly liked—to feel himself of use, to bear a portion of her weight, to steady her as she descended on the other side.

“Martin, before we separate,” she said at last, “assure me seriously, and on your word of honour, that Mr. Moore is better.”

“How very much you think of that Moore!”

“No—but—many of his friends may ask me, and I wish to be able to give an authentic answer.”

“You may tell them he is well enough, only idle. You may tell them that he takes mutton chops for dinner, and the best of arrowroot for supper. I intercepted a basin myself one night on its way upstairs, and ate half of it.”

“And who waits on him, Martin? Who nurses him?”

“Nurses him? The great baby! Why, a woman as round and big as our largest water-butt—a rough, hard-favoured old girl. I make no doubt she leads him a rich life. Nobody else is let near him. He is chiefly in the dark. It is my belief she knocks him about terribly in that chamber. I listen at the wall sometimes when I am in bed, and I think I hear her thumping him. You should see her fist. She could hold half a dozen hands like yours in her one palm. After all, notwithstanding the chops and jellies he gets, I would not be in his shoes. In fact, it is my private opinion she eats most of what goes up on the tray to Mr. Moore. I wish she may not be starving him.”

Caroline was silent—silent in a way that was thought, not sulkiness. Martin watched her slyly, as if her quiet were another page to be read.

“You never see him, I suppose, Martin?” she asked.

“I? No. I don’t care to see him, for my own part.”

Silence again; and in the quiet, the faint sounds of the countryside came clear—some far dog’s bark, the thin rasp of a branch, the small crunch of their own steps. The world was still enough that a heart might hear itself.

“Did not you come to our house once with Mrs. Pryor, about five weeks since, to ask after him?” Martin inquired.

“Yes.”

“I dare say you wished to be shown upstairs?”

“We did wish it. We entreated it; but your mother declined.”

“Ay! she declined. I heard it all. She treated you as it is her pleasure to treat visitors now and then. She behaved to you rudely and harshly.”

“She was not kind; for you know, Martin, we are relations, and it is natural we should take an interest in Mr. Moore. But here we must part; we are at your father’s gate.”

“Very well—what of that? I shall walk home with you.”

“They will miss you, and wonder where you are.”

“Let them. I can take care of myself, I suppose.”

Martin knew he had already earned his punishment: the lecture, and dry bread for tea. No matter; the evening had furnished him with an adventure—better than muffins and toast.

He walked home with Caroline; and on the way he promised—against the dragon who guarded the chamber—that he would see Mr. Moore. He named an hour the next day when Caroline was to come again to Briarmains Wood for tidings; he would meet her at a certain tree. The scheme led to nothing; still he liked it—liked the secrecy, the appointment, the little thread spun between his blunt boyhood and her anxious woman’s heart.

When he reached home, the dry bread and the lecture were duly administered, and he was dismissed to bed early. He accepted his punishment with the toughest stoicism.

Before he went up, he slipped into the dining-room—still, cold, stately, seldom used, for the family customarily ate in the back parlour where the kitchen smells could reach and the talk was freer. In the dining-room the air had no savour of supper; it held only polish, dust, and the faint ghost of old fires. He stood before the mantelpiece and lifted his candle to two pictures hung above: female heads. One was serene beauty—happy, innocent; the other more lovely, but forlorn, desperate, with a look as if love had been made into pain.

“She looked like that,” he murmured, staring at the latter sketch, “when she sobbed, turned white, and leaned against the tree.”
