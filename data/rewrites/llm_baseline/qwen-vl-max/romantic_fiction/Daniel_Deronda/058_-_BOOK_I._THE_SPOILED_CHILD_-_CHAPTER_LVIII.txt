CHAPTER LVIII. “Much adoe there was, God wot; He wold love and she wold not.” —NICHOLAS BRETON.

Time moves in many ways—sometimes with the quiet persistence of a slow tide, sometimes with the sudden force of a storm. A man may journey across continents, discover new truths, endure great trials, and return to find his world unchanged: the same gossip in the village, the same familiar faces, the same old habits. Yet within him, everything has shifted. The mind can travel farther than any map, and the soul may be transformed without a single footstep taken. This is how life works—not by measure of miles or years, but by depth of feeling, by the weight of experience that settles into being.

Such a shift had marked the year since Gwendolen Harleth stood radiant at the Archery Meeting, confident and bright, her future seemingly written in gold. Now, she was humbled, broken by a truth she could no longer deny. She had come to see herself as unworthy—her pride shattered, her heart laid bare. And yet, around her, life continued much as before. Her family at Pennicote remained much the same: the rectory still warm and peaceful, its gardens blooming with peonies and hollyhocks, the rector still cheerful and dutiful, tending his parish with quiet devotion. He wrote articles on church matters—unsigned, so they were credited to others—and took pleasure in knowing that even if no one read them, they pleased those who mattered most. His peace was simple, untroubled by ambition or envy. It was a life lived in harmony with nature, far from the noise of fame or criticism.

And then there was Rex—his son, now returned for a summer visit, bringing with him Anna, whose presence lit up the house like morning sun. Rex had changed. Once full of restless energy, he now moved with purpose, rising early, studying late, focused on the law as though it were a calling rather than a career. When his father asked if he regretted choosing such a path, Rex replied firmly: “There is no profession I would choose before it. I want to be a judge, to help shape the laws. Give me something to do with making the rules, and let others make the songs.”

The rector smiled. “You’ll have to digest a lot of rubbish,” he said.

“Not worse than what people swallow in literature,” Rex answered. “At least law teaches you how to think. And beyond that—it’s history, philosophy, the story of human justice. Yes, it’s hard work. But greatness never comes easily.”

His father nodded. “That’s the best sign of success—when a man believes his work is the finest in the world.”

And indeed, Rex seemed destined for distinction. The rector spoke of him with quiet pride, comparing him to Paley, whose father once declared with certainty that his son would achieve greatness. Mrs. Gascoigne, hearing this, asked, half-jokingly, whether Paley had been an old bachelor. The rector didn’t remember, and only chuckled. But the moment passed—no need to dwell on irrelevancies.

At Jodson’s, the Davilows’ modest home just outside Pennicote, time too had passed gently. Mrs. Davilow looked a little more worn, her face softened by sorrow, her hair touched with silver. The girls had grown—bloomed, really—freer now that the shadow of their former life at Offendene had lifted. Jocosa, ever practical, remained steady, content with her place, untouched by the glitter of society. Their drawing-room, narrow but cozy, opened wide on a July afternoon to the scent of roses and the distant sound of wheels, creating a scene both intimate and lively—especially with Rex and Anna added to the usual circle.

Anna, always beloved by her cousins, shared stories of London—of grand houses, fashionable acquaintances, and the dazzling world of the wealthy. Naturally, Gwendolen came up. What had she said? How had she lived? Had Anna seen her after the news of the yacht?

“No,” Anna said simply.

And so the imagination took over. The yacht—Marseilles—the elegant cabins—Gwendolen’s letters filled with *dittos*—it all became legend. The newspaper had mentioned it. Gwendolen was living a life of adventure, elegance, mystery. Isabel, ever the dreamer, imagined pirates, storms, daring escapes. But when Rex entered the room, the topic vanished. He was not to be reminded of anything connected to Gwendolen.

Instead, they talked of the Meyricks and their extraordinary Jewish friends—Mirah and her brother. To these country minds, Jews were a marvel—like creatures from ancient lore, real but strange, different enough to seem almost another species. Bertha wondered what they believed. Miss Merry thought Mirah could never be reasoned with. Alice confessed she couldn’t bear them. Mrs. Davilow corrected her gently: “The great families among them are quite proper—just like anyone else in London or Paris.” But she admitted the common ones were less appealing. Isabel asked whether Mirah sounded like a Christian, or if you might sit beside her and never know.

Rex, amused, played along, exaggerating each remark for laughter—until a messenger arrived from the rectory, breathless, bearing a letter.

Mrs. Davilow opened it, read it twice, then stared into space. The room grew still. All eyes turned to her. Then, with a sob that held relief and grief, she whispered, “My dears… Mr. Grandcourt is drowned.”

Rex leapt up as if struck. Anna looked at him, startled. But he quickly regained himself. “Can I do anything, aunt?” he asked. “Shall I tell my father?”

“Yes, dear. Tell him I’ll be ready—he’s very kind. He says he’ll go with me to Genoa. He’ll be here by half-past six. Jocosa, Alice—help me pack. Gwendolen is safe—but she must be ill. I’m sure she is. Rex—Anna—go tell your father I’ll be ready. I wouldn’t miss a night. Bless him for being so quick.”

They left together, walking through the golden sunlight now thick with silence. No words passed between them. Anna worried about Rex’s wound—had the shock reopened it? Rex, meanwhile, was lost in a storm of thoughts. Not grief—for Grandcourt was a stranger to him—but a turmoil of selfish imaginings, rising like ghosts from the past.

Had Gwendolen been free? Was she now unbound? Could she ever love him?

He knew better. He knew the shame of such thoughts—how they mocked his own resolve. For nearly a year, he had built a calm life, letting go of longing, accepting his fate. But three words—“Mr. Grandcourt is drowned”—had shattered it all. The hope he’d buried came rushing back, not with joy, but with guilt.

She would never love him. Not now. Not ever. She was rich, exalted, surrounded by admiration. He was nothing—only a young man with a quiet ambition, a future still uncertain. And yet—why did it hurt so much?

He walked into the shrubbery, trying to still the chaos. He told himself plainly: *She would never have you. You would never ask. Thinking of it now is no better than looting a battlefield. There’s nothing to gain. Nothing.*

But the voice of desire refused to be silenced.

He paced, angry with himself, until he finally forced himself back into the house. Into the study, where his father was packing a traveling desk.

“Can I help, sir?” Rex asked, forcing calm into his voice.

“Yes, my boy. When I’m gone, look after my letters. Answer what needs answering. Send me word of everything. Dymock will manage the parish. You’ll stay with your mother—or go back and forth—until I return.”

“You won’t be long, I suppose,” Rex said, rolling a railway rug. “Will you bring my cousin back?”

He said it quietly. The rector looked up, surprised—then pleased. “That depends,” he said. “Perhaps her mother will stay with her. Or maybe I’ll return soon. The telegram doesn’t say. But I expect Gwendolen will be well provided for—splendidly, even.”

“She must have been shocked,” Rex said, gathering strength. “Was he a devoted husband?”

“Without doubt,” the rector said firmly. “Few men in his position would have stepped forward as he did.”

Rex had never met Grandcourt. Never heard of his pursuit of Gwendolen. He only knew that Grandcourt had loved her deeply, offered marriage when she was poor, and helped her family generously. That was noble—what any decent man might do. Grandcourt had been lucky. He had found happiness. And now, he was gone.

Yet Rex couldn’t shake the question: Had Gwendolen truly loved him? Or had she simply endured his affection, unable to tell him how she felt?

The answer remained hidden—buried beneath grief, secrets, and the quiet ache of a love that could never be.
