The city of Cloisterham exists in a suspended animation, a dusty chronicle where the names of Druids, Romans, Saxons, and Normans have long since evaporated into the ether. It is a place of monotony, a silent entity deriving its earthy flavor from the deep, resonant hum of its cathedral crypt. Here, the soil is rich with the remains of the powerful; children play games of dirt-pie amidst the vestiges of monastic graves, grinding the bones of archbishops and bishops into flour for their bread, much like the ogre in fairy tales who demands tribute from the uninvited guest.

There is a peculiar moral decay in this antiquity, older than any recorded history suggests. The inhabitants live under the delusion that all change belongs to the past, convinced that the future holds no surprises. The streets are so still that shop blinds dare not flap in the southern breeze, and the sun-browned tramps hurry past, eager to escape the suffocating weight of the town's respectable stagnation. To leave is easy, for the city is essentially a single narrow artery leading in and out, flanked by disappointing yards with pumps and no thoroughfares, save for the Cathedral Close and a paved Quaker settlement that resembles a bonnet in a shaded corner.

At the heart of this static world stands the Nuns' House. Its current designation, "Seminary for Young Ladies," is painted on a resplendent brass plate attached to a gate that encloses an old courtyard. The contrast between the weathered, battered facade of the building and the glaring shine of the modern sign creates an image akin to a disheveled old beau wearing a large, contemporary eyeglass over a blind eye.

Behind the scenes, the institution operates on a dual frequency. Just as intoxication and somnambulism allow for two non-interfering states of consciousness, Miss Twinkleton possesses two distinct personas. By day, she is the stern seminary mistress, unaware of the local scandals or the specific gossip of Tunbridge Wells. By night, however, she transforms. She smooths her curls, brightens her eyes, and becomes a sprightly confidante, recounting tender scandals and referencing the "Wells" with a familiarity that belies her daytime ignorance. Her constant companion in both states is Mrs. Tisher, a deferential widow with a weak back and a chronic sigh, who manages the wardrobes and maintains the illusion that she has witnessed better days—a belief so strong among the staff that it is considered fact that the late Mrs. Tisher was once a hairdresser.

The focal point of this household is Miss Rosa Bud, universally known as Rosebud. She is a creature of wonder: wonderfully pretty, wonderfully childish, and wonderfully whimsical. An awkward, romantic tension surrounds her, for a husband has already been chosen for her by will, binding her guardian to deliver her to him upon his coming of age. Miss Twinkleton attempts to combat the romantic implications of this destiny by feigning pity for the "doomed little victim," shaking her head behind Rosebud's dimpled shoulders. Yet, this performance fails to impress the young ladies, who collectively dismiss her as a pretentious old thing.

The atmosphere shifts whenever the allotted husband arrives. It is understood that he has a right to visit, and any attempt by Miss Twinkleton to dispute this would result in immediate transport. Consequently, when his bell rings, every young lady peers from a window, practices out of tune, and the French class descends into chaos, the mark circulating as rapidly as a bottle at a party of the previous century.

On the afternoon following the dinner at the gatehouse, the bell rings with its customary effect.

"Mr. Edwin Drood to see Miss Rosa," announces the parlour-maid.

Miss Twinkleton adopts an exemplary air of melancholy and instructs the sacrifice to descend. Miss Bud follows, trailed by the scrutiny of all eyes.

Mr. Edwin Drood waits in Miss Twinkleton's parlour, a dainty room containing nothing strictly academic except a terrestrial and a celestial globe. These instruments suggest that even in private, Miss Twinkleton remains perpetually on the verge of becoming a Wandering Jewess, scouring the earth for knowledge.

The door opens, revealing a charming little apparition, its face obscured by a silk apron draped over its head. This is the latest maid, stumbling guiltily down the stairs, making her debut in the presence of the groom-to-be.

"Oh! it _is_ so ridiculous!" the apparition exclaims, shrinking back. "Don't, Eddy!"

"Don't what, Rosa?"

"Don't come any nearer, please. It _is_ so absurd."

"What is absurd, Rosa?"

"The whole thing. It _is_ so absurd to be an engaged orphan, and so absurd to have the girls and servants scuttling about like mice in the wainscot, and so absurd to be called upon!"

The figure keeps a thumb in the corner of its mouth while complaining.

"You give me an affectionate reception, Pussy, I must say."

"Well, I will in a minute, Eddy, but I can't just yet. How are you?" (said very briefly.)

"I am unable to reply that I am much the better for seeing you, Pussy, inasmuch as I see nothing of you."

This remark draws a dark, bright, pouting eye from a corner of the apron, only for it to vanish again as the girl cries, "Oh good gracious! you have had half your hair cut off!"

"I should have done better to have had my head cut off, I think," Edwin replies, ruffling the hair in question with a fierce glance at the mirror and an impatient stamp. "Shall I go?"

"No; you needn't go just yet, Eddy. The girls would all be asking questions why you went."

"Once for all, Rosa, will you uncover that ridiculous little head of yours and give me a welcome?"

The apron is pulled aside. "You're very welcome, Eddy. There! I'm sure that's nice. Shake hands. No, I can't kiss you, because I've got an acidulated drop in my mouth."

"Are you at all glad to see me, Pussy?"

"Oh, yes, I'm dreadfully glad.—Go and sit down.—Miss Twinkleton."

It is the custom for Miss Twinkleton to appear every three minutes during such visits, either in her own person or as Mrs. Tisher, laying offerings on the shrine of Propriety by searching for nonexistent items. On this occasion, she glides in and out gracefully. "How do you do, Mr. Drood? Very glad indeed to have the pleasure. Pray excuse me. Tweezers. Thank you!"

"I got the gloves last evening, Eddy, and I like them very much. They are beauties."

"Well, that's something," the affianced replies, half-grumbling. "The smallest encouragement thankfully received. And how did you pass your birthday, Pussy?"

"Delightfully! Everybody gave me a present. And we had a feast. And we had a ball at night."

"A feast and a ball, eh? These occasions seem to go off tolerably well without me, Pussy."

"De-lightfully!" cries Rosa, spontaneously and without reserve.

"Hah! And what was the feast?"

"Tarts, oranges, jellies, and shrimps."

"Any partners at the ball?"

"We danced with one another, of course, sir. But some of the girls made game to be their brothers. It _was_ so droll!"

"Did anybody make game to be—"

"To be you? Oh dear yes!" cries Rosa, laughing with great enjoyment. "That was the first thing done."

"I hope she did it pretty well," says Edwin rather doubtfully.

"Oh, it was excellent!—I wouldn't dance with you, you know."

Edwin scarcely grasps the significance of this; he asks why.

"Because I was so tired of you," returns Rosa. But she quickly adds, pleadingly, sensing displeasure in his face: "Dear Eddy, you were just as tired of me, you know."

"Did I say so, Rosa?"

"Say so! Do you ever say so? No, you only showed it. Oh, she did it so well!" cries Rosa, suddenly ecstatic about her counterfeit betrothal.

"It strikes me that she must be a devilish impudent girl," says Edwin Drood. "And so, Pussy, you have passed your last birthday in this old house."

"Ah, yes!" Rosa clasps her hands, looks down with a sigh, and shakes her head.

"You seem to be sorry, Rosa."

"I am sorry for the poor old place. Somehow, I feel as if it would miss me, when I am gone so far away, so young."

"Perhaps we had better stop short, Rosa?"

She looks up with a swift, bright expression; the next moment she shakes her head, sighs, and looks down again.

"That is to say, is it, Pussy, that we are both resigned?"

She nods again. After a short silence, she quaintly bursts out: "You know we must be married, and married from here, Eddy, or the poor girls will be so dreadfully disappointed!"

For a moment, there is more compassion in her fiancé's face for her and himself than there is love. He checks the emotion and asks, "Shall I take you out for a walk, Rosa dear?"
