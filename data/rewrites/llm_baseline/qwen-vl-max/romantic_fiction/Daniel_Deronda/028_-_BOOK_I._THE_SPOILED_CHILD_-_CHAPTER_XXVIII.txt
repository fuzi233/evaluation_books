“It is easier to know man in general than to know a particular man.”—LA ROCHEFOUCAULD.

An hour after Grandcourt had departed, the news of Gwendolen’s engagement spread quickly to the rectory, and Mr. and Mrs. Gascoigne, along with Anna, spent the evening at Offendene.

“My dear,” said the rector, “I must congratulate you on having inspired such a deep attachment. You look thoughtful—and I can understand why. A lifelong union is no small matter. But from what I’ve seen of Mr. Grandcourt’s behavior and words, I believe we may already glimpse some good coming out of our trials. He has shown a sensitivity and generosity that speaks well of his character.”

He was referring to Grandcourt’s subtle suggestion that he would provide for Mrs. Davilow—a detail Gwendolen had recalled with precision when she told her mother about their conversation.

“But I have no doubt Mr. Grandcourt would have acted just as generously if you hadn’t gone to Germany,” said Mrs. Gascoigne, feeling it her duty to speak plainly. “You might have been engaged to him more than a month ago. Now, there’s no room for hesitation—or desire to hesitate. A woman owes great gratitude to a man who persists in offering her such a future. But I trust you feel this properly.”

“I’m not certain I do, aunt,” Gwendolen replied, her tone both teasing and serious. “I don’t know exactly what one *should* feel when engaged.”

The rector smiled and patted her shoulder, as if amused by a harmless rebellion. His wife took this as a sign that no rebuke was intended. Anna, meanwhile, kissed Gwendolen and whispered, “I hope you’ll be happy,” before retreating into silence, fighting back tears. In recent days, she had imagined a quiet romance: that Rex still loved Gwendolen, and perhaps, through hardship, her heart might soften into love—allowing them to marry someday. That dream had become a prayer: that she, Anna, could rejoice as a faithful sister, content to serve Gwendolen as long as Rex remained without wealth. But now, she needed grace to rejoice in something else entirely.

Miss Merry and the four girls—Alice, with her high shoulders; Bertha and Fanny, the whisperers; and Isabel, always listening—were all present, drawn together by the occasion. Everything seemed to turn toward Gwendolen’s honor and glory, and real life felt as vivid and grand as the pages of *Sir Charles Grandison*. The evening passed mostly in the rector’s firm pronouncements, answering the speculations of the two elder women. He insisted that settlements were unnecessary—everything, he believed, would be safely left to Mr. Grandcourt.

“I’d like to know what Ryelands and Gadsmere are really like,” said Mrs. Davilow.

“Gadsmere is likely a secondary estate,” Mr. Gascoigne replied. “But Ryelands—I know it well—is one of the finest seats in the country. The park is vast, and the woods are of great value. The house was designed by Inigo Jones, with ceilings painted in the Italian manner. The estate is said to bring in twelve thousand pounds a year, and includes two livings—one a rectory—in the Grandcourt family’s gift. There may be encumbrances on the land, but Mr. Grandcourt was an only child.”

“It would be extraordinary,” Mrs. Gascoigne mused, counting off points on her fingers, “if he were to inherit Lord Stannery too. Think of it—the Grandcourt estate, the Mallinger estate, the baronetcy, *and* the peerage!” She paused on the fourth finger. “Though they say no land comes with the peerage.” It seemed a pity there was nothing for the fifth.

“The peerage,” the rector added wisely, “is a distant possibility. Two cousins stand between the current peer and Mr. Grandcourt. Still, it’s sobering how death and chance sometimes concentrate fortunes in one hand. But such excess should be avoided. To be Sir Mallinger Grandcourt Mallinger—that will be his title, I suppose—with such properties is more than enough for any man. Let us hope he uses it well.”

“And what a position for you, Gwendolen!” Mrs. Gascoigne exclaimed. “A great responsibility. But Henry, you must write immediately to Mrs. Mompert. It’s fortunate you have an engagement to offer as an excuse—otherwise, she might take offense. She’s rather proud.”

“I’m free of that horror,” thought Gwendolen, whose name for Mompert had become almost magical in its insignificance. She remained quiet throughout the evening, and that night barely slept at all in her small white bed. It was rare for her strong youth to be restless—but even rarer that she would hide her unease from her mother. Her mind was utterly changed. Once sure of herself, ready to lead others, she had just taken a step she had once believed she wouldn’t take—perhaps even shouldn’t take. There was no turning back now. Much of what lay ahead appealed to her. And if she returned, there would be nothing to gain. Yet beneath her resolve lingered the shadow of her earlier conviction—the one that had once moved her whole being with certainty. As she lay awake, eyes wide in the dark, she was seized by dread: she was doing what she had once fled from with disgust. This was new—this fear of moral consequence. She had never known guilt that couldn’t be erased with gifts or affection. But now, a kind of awakening had begun. She seemed poised on the edge of accepting, for the rest of her life, what she had once said in bitterness, when her discovery drove her to Leubronn: that nothing mattered—she只需 amuse herself. That recklessness, that abandonment of conscience, suddenly terrified her. It came with visions of disaster—disaster that was no longer just a word. All the half-heard teachings of religion, the deeper sense of something terrible and unyielding surrounding her, converged into a vague but powerful idea of judgment.

Before her lay the brilliant life she had longed for—the freedom she imagined marriage would give her—the escape from her dull girlhood. Yet it now tasted tainted, like food touched by sacrilege. She had to snatch it, trembling. Alone in the darkness, her stronger self could not resist the first wave of terror after her irrevocable choice. The face of that unhappy woman and her children—Grandcourt and his past—repeated themselves in her mind like a stain she couldn’t wash away. They eclipsed every other thought, leaving only the knowledge that she had brought those scenes into her own life. Her wakefulness felt like delirium. A faint light crept through the curtain. The cold grew sharper. She could endure it no longer.

“Mamma!”

“Yes, dear,” Mrs. Davilow answered at once, her voice already awake.

“Let me come to you.”

She soon fell asleep against her mother’s shoulder, sleeping deeply until late, when, dreaming of a ballroom aglow with light, she awoke to find her mother standing beside the bed, holding a small parcel.

“I’m sorry to wake you, darling,” Mrs. Davilow said gently, “but I thought it better to give this to you right away. The groom has brought Criterion—he arrived on another horse and says he’s to stay here.”

Gwendolen sat up and opened the package. Inside was a delicate enameled box, containing a magnificent diamond ring and a letter with a folded slip of colored paper and these words:

> Please wear this ring when I arrive at twelve, as a token of our betrothal. I’ve enclosed a check made out to Mr. Gascoigne for immediate expenses. Of course, Mrs. Davilow will remain at Offendene—at least for now. I hope, when I come, you’ll grant me an early date so I may begin to obey your wishes more closely.—Yours devotedly,

The check was for five hundred pounds. Gwendolen held it up to her mother.

“How thoughtful and considerate!” Mrs. Davilow said, deeply moved. “But truly, I’d prefer not to depend on my son-in-law. We could manage quite well on our own.”

“If you say that again, I won’t marry him,” Gwendolen said sharply.

“My dear,” Mrs. Davilow replied, pleading, “I don’t think you’re marrying me alone.”

Gwendolen turned her head away, letting the ring lie untouched. She resented this attempt to strip her motive. Perhaps deeper still was her awareness that she wasn’t marrying solely for her mother’s sake—she was drawn forward by forces stronger than her mother’s sacrifice. She had woken to the reality of her commitment, and now faced the day’s challenge: to meet the fears and doubts of the night under daylight, where they would surely seem less formidable.

“All I want is your happiness, dear,” Mrs. Davilow continued softly. “I won’t say anything to upset you. Will you put on the ring?”

For a moment, Gwendolen didn’t answer. Then, rising with determination—like a rider setting off on horseback, resolved to press on despite inner turmoil—she said, laughing, “I thought the lover was supposed to place the ring himself.”

She slipped it on, tilting her head with a charming gesture. “I know why he sent it,” she added, nodding at her mother.

“Why?”

“He’d rather I put it on than ask me to let him. Aha! He’s very proud. But so am I. We’ll match each other. I’d hate a man who knelt and fawned. Honestly, he’s not repulsive.”

“That’s mild praise, Gwen.”

“No, it’s not—especially for a man,” Gwendolen replied cheerfully. “Now, I must get up and dress. Will you do my hair, mamma?” She drew her mother close, brushing her cheeks against hers. “And please, no more talk of poverty. You must accept comfort—even if you don’t enjoy it. And Mr. Grandcourt behaves perfectly, doesn’t he?”

“Indeed he does,” Mrs. Davilow said, encouraged, believing now that her daughter truly liked her fiancé. She saw him as a man whose attentions could easily influence a young woman’s feelings. Suitors are often judged by their social standing and presence—not much else. And her own concern wasn’t Grandcourt’s character, but whether Gwendolen truly wanted this man.

That morning, Gwendolen’s mood shifted. Even while dressing, she gathered arguments to justify her decision. Most compelling was her plan: once married, she would urge Grandcourt to treat Mrs. Glasher’s children generously.

“What good would it do her if I didn’t marry him? He could have married her if he’d wanted to—but he didn’t. Maybe she’s to blame. There’s much about her I don’t know. And he must have been kind to her in many ways, or she wouldn’t have wanted him.”

Yet even this argument began to falter. Mrs. Glasher naturally wished to exclude other heirs who might divide Grandcourt’s inheritance. Gwendolen’s understanding of this led her to a new justification.

“Perhaps we’ll have no children. I hope we won’t. He might leave the estate to the little boy. Uncle said Grandcourt could do as he pleased with the lands. Only when Sir Hugo dies, there’ll be enough for two.”

This made Mrs. Glasher seem unreasonable in demanding sole inheritance. The dual property offered security—Grandcourt’s marriage would harm no one, especially not when his bride was Gwendolen Harleth, proud and determined not to be accused of wrongdoing. She had always considered herself faultless—others were the ones at fault.

It was striking how firmly this reasoning had taken hold—so much so that her revulsion at Grandcourt’s past had faded into the background. The terror she had felt at night—of crossing into wickedness by doing what she once knew was wrong—had dulled her emotional response to his history. She now saw him not as a man of mystery, but as someone over whom she would wield power. Love had never been a question; whatever charm he possessed was simply an advantage. Poor Gwendolen had no fear of marriage’s uncontrollable forces—she saw it as pure management, something she could control. Regarding his past, she began to wonder if he was really so different from other men. She devised little plans to learn what society expected of men.

Still, one thing remained certain: her hair was perfectly styled for riding. She descended in her riding habit, eager to avoid delay. She craved the rush of youth—the intoxicating courage she once carried through life. Already, a burden had lifted. In daylight and motion, doubts felt lighter than they had in the stillness of night.

“Go back and make yourself look like a duchess, mamma,” she called, turning abruptly on the stairs. “Put your point-lace over your head. I must have you look like a duchess. Don’t be humble.”

When Grandcourt gently raised her left hand and looked at the ring, she said gravely, “It was very kind of you to think of everything and send me that packet.”

“You’ll tell me if I forget anything?” he asked, keeping her hand softly in his. “I’ll do anything you wish.”

“But I’m terribly unreasonable in my wishes,” Gwendolen replied, smiling.

“Yes, I expect that. Women always are.”

“Then I won’t be unreasonable,” she declared, pulling her hand away and tossing her head playfully. “I won’t be told I’m what women always are.”

“I didn’t say that,” Grandcourt said, looking at her with his usual calm. “You are what no other woman is.”

“And what is that, pray?” she asked, stepping back with a hint of challenge.

Grandcourt paused before answering. “You are the woman I love.”

“Oh, how sweet!” Gwendolen laughed. The memory of that love he had once given another woman—under strange circumstances—was becoming familiar.

“Give me a sweet reply. Say when we’ll marry.”

“Not yet. Not until we’ve galloped over the downs. I’m so thirsty for it, I can think of nothing else. Sunday the twentieth… twenty-seventh… Monday… Tuesday…” She counted on her fingers, nodding prettily, then swept one palm over the other triumphantly. “It begins in ten days!”

“Then let’s marry in ten days,” Grandcourt said. “We’ll avoid boredom about the stables.”

“What do women always say in reply?” Gwendolen teased.

“They agree,” he answered, slightly off guard.

“Then I won’t!” she declared, picking up her gauntlets and putting them on, her eyes sparkling with mischief.

The moment was delightful for both. A cruder man might have missed her grace, ruined the mood with clumsy advances. But Grandcourt preferred the drama—and Gwendolen, left at ease, found her spirits rising as she played at ruling. Perhaps if Klesmer had seen more of her in this natural performance—rather than when she tried to act—his opinion of her might have been higher.

After their exhilarating ride, she felt uplifted, eager to hasten the marriage that would make her life a continuous feast of such joy. She refused further debate over a choice she had already made. She agreed to set the wedding in three weeks, despite the challenges of assembling a proper trousseau.

Lush, of course, learned of the engagement through signs, though he was never formally told. Yet he expected some communication—and after a few days, grew impatient. He sensed Grandcourt’s silence meant his own prospects were changing. He didn’t oppose things for their own sake; he might cause annoyance, but it would harm him too. He wouldn’t mind thwarting Miss Gwendolen a little—but still, the future was uncertain. Grandcourt’s odd behavior struck Lush as strangely fatalistic—like a man marked by fate. That someone born to such fortune should mismanage his life seemed tragic. Having protested the marriage, Lush now foresaw its consequences. Grandcourt had taken to writing letters and giving orders personally—no longer relying on Lush. He even chose to breakfast alone in his dressing-room, breaking a long-standing habit.

But a tête-à-tête could not be avoided in an empty house. One evening, after dinner—when Grandcourt had declined to dine at Offendene—Lush seized the moment.

“When is the marriage to take place?”

Grandcourt, who rarely drank wine, had left the table and now lounged in a velvet chair near the hearth, smoking. A fire of oak boughs burned brightly, casting soft ash-glow across the room. The chair framed his pale, finely cut features and elegant hands. Without the cigar, he might have been a Moroni portrait—impenetrable, distinguished. But he answered promptly.

“On the tenth.”

“You intend to stay here?”

“We’ll go to Ryelands briefly, but return for the hunting.”

After this, a low, indistinct sound—common with Grandcourt when he meant to continue—rose from his lips. Lush waited. Nothing followed. He was about to speak again when the sound returned, leading to a quiet suggestion:

“You’d better make new arrangements for yourself.”

“What? I’m to leave?” Lush asked, prepared to be reasonable.

“Something like that.”

“The bride dislikes me. I hope she makes up for my absence.”

“I can’t help that you’re so disagreeable to women,” Grandcourt said, almost apologetically.

“To one woman, if you please.”

“It makes no difference—since she’s the one.”

“I suppose I’m not to be cast aside after fifteen years without support?”

“You must have saved something from me.”

“Very little. I’ve often saved things for you.”

“You can have three hundred a year. But you must live in town and be available when I need you. I’ll be hard up.”

“If you’re not at Ryelands this winter, I could visit and report on Swinton.”

“If you like. I don’t care where you are—just keep out of sight.”

“Much obliged,” Lush replied, taking it easier than expected. He secretly believed he’d be needed again soon.

“Perhaps you’ll pack soon,” Grandcourt said. “The Torringtons are arriving, and Miss Harleth will be riding over.”

“With pleasure. Can I assist with Gadsmere?”

“No. I’m going myself.”

“About being hard up—have you considered that plan—”

“Just leave me alone,” Grandcourt muttered, tossing his cigar into the fire and walking away.

He spent the evening in the smaller drawing-room, surrounded by books—political, comic, cynical, romantic—yet did not read. Instead, he sat meditatively on the sofa, abstaining from literature altogether. Hours passed swiftly, not through thought, but through avoidance of effort. His inner world felt aged—prematurely so—where impulse died before fulfillment, and action became mere memory. This state often comes with gray hair, and sometimes with a stubborn, rigid egoism that grows more prominent as youthful sensibilities fade.

But Grandcourt’s hair, though sparse, was a fine, golden blonde. His mood couldn’t be explained by age alone. Within us mortals, a strange spiritual chemistry unfolds—where laziness or softness may conceal sharp, explosive potential. Like the navvy who wakes and crushes his sleeping comrade without malice, lacking trained motive, so too a gentleman may become unpredictable—raising uncertainty about what he’ll do next, spoiling companionship.
