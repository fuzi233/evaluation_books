The abrupt end to Colonel Brandon's stay at the Park, coupled with his remarkable success in hiding the true reason for his departure, kept Mrs. Jennings in a state of bewildered speculation for several days. Being a woman of intense curiosity regarding the movements of everyone she knew, she found it nearly impossible to stop wondering. With barely a moment's pause between thoughts, she convinced herself that something terrible must have occurred. She mentally cataloged every conceivable misfortune that could have befallen him, determined that he deserved none of them less.

"Something deeply sorrowful must be the cause, I am certain," she declared. "I saw it written plainly on his face. Poor soul! I fear his financial situation has turned dire. That estate at Delaford was never worth much more than two thousand pounds a year, and his brother left behind debts that were quite overwhelming. I truly believe he was summoned back to deal with monetary troubles; surely nothing else could explain it. I wonder if that is indeed the case. I would give anything to know the truth. Perhaps it concerns Miss Williams—and, by the way, I suspect it does, simply because he looked so guilty when I brought her name up. Maybe she is ill in London; nothing is more likely, for I've always suspected she is somewhat frail. I'd bet my life it's about Miss Williams. Though, honestly, it isn't very probable he is in financial straits *now*, considering he is a man of prudence and must have settled those affairs long ago. What on earth could it be? Perhaps his sister is worse at Avignon and has called for him. His hurried departure certainly suggests that. Very well, I wish him relief from all his troubles, and a suitable wife to boot!" Thus pondered and thus spoke Mrs. Jennings, her theories shifting with every new idea, all appearing equally plausible as they formed.

Elinor, while genuinely concerned for Colonel Brandon's well-being, could not share the depth of astonishment regarding his sudden exit that her mother desired. In her view, the incident did not warrant such prolonged amazement or endless guessing. Furthermore, her curiosity was directed elsewhere: it was consumed by the extraordinary silence maintained by her sister and Mr. Willoughby concerning the matter. They must realize how intensely this topic affected everyone present. As the silence persisted, each passing day rendered it stranger and more inconsistent with the natures of both young people. Elinor could not fathom why they would not openly admit to their mother and themselves what their consistent behavior toward one another clearly indicated had happened.

She could understand that marriage might not be immediately feasible; although Willoughby possessed independence, there was no evidence he was wealthy. His estate had been valued by Sir John at roughly six or seven hundred pounds annually, yet he lived beyond his means, frequently complaining of his poverty. However, this peculiar secrecy regarding their engagement—which revealed nothing hidden—was difficult to reconcile with their usual openness. It was so contrary to their general character that a shadow of doubt occasionally crossed Elinor's mind as to whether they were truly engaged. This uncertainty alone prevented her from asking Marianne directly.

Nothing could demonstrate greater devotion to the family than Willoughby's conduct. Toward Marianne, he displayed every tender distinction a lover's heart could offer; toward the rest of the household, he showed the warm attentiveness of a beloved son and brother. The cottage appeared to be his true home; he spent far more time there than at Allenham. Even when no specific engagement drew them to the Park, his morning walks invariably ended there, where he remained throughout the day beside Marianne, with his favorite pointer dog resting at her feet.

One evening, approximately a week after Colonel Brandon had left the countryside, Willoughby's heart seemed unusually open to the affections surrounding him. When Mrs. Dashwood casually mentioned her intention to improve the cottage in the spring, he vigorously opposed any alterations to a place that affection had already perfected in his eyes.

"What!" he exclaimed. "Improve this dear cottage! No. That I will never consent to. Not a single stone must be added to its walls, nor an inch to its size, if my feelings are respected."

"Do not be alarmed," Miss Dashwood reassured him. "No such plans will be executed; for my mother simply lacks the funds to undertake them."

"I am heartily glad of that," he cried. "May she always remain poor, if she can utilize her wealth no better than this."

"Thank you, Willoughby. But rest assured, I would not sacrifice a single sentiment of local attachment held by you—or anyone I love—for all the improvements in the world. Be confident that whatever surplus remains when I settle my accounts in the spring, I would prefer to leave it idle rather than spend it in a way so painful to you. Yet, are you truly so attached to this place that you perceive no flaws in it?"

"I am," he replied. "To me, it is flawless. Indeed, I regard it as the only architectural form wherein happiness can be attained. Were I rich enough, I would tear down Combe immediately and rebuild it exactly according to the plan of this cottage."

"With dark, narrow staircases and a kitchen full of smoke, I presume," Elinor remarked dryly.

"Yes," he shouted in the same fervent tone, "with every detail included—in no single convenience or discomfort should the slightest change be noticeable. Only then, under such a roof, might I perhaps find myself as happy at Combe as I have been at Barton."

"I flatter myself," Elinor responded, "that even with superior rooms and wider staircases, you will eventually find your own home just as perfect as you do this one."

"There are certainly aspects," Willoughby admitted, "that might greatly increase my fondness for it; but this place holds one claim on my affection that no other location can possibly match."

Mrs. Dashwood watched with delight at Marianne, whose beautiful eyes were fixed so expressively upon Willoughby that it was plain she fully comprehended him.

"How often," he added, lowering his voice slightly, "did I wish, when I was at Allenham last year, that Barton Cottage were occupied! I never passed within sight of it without admiring its setting and mourning that no one lived there. How little did I anticipate that the very first news I would receive from Mrs. Smith upon returning to the country would be that Barton Cottage had been rented! I felt an instant satisfaction and deep interest in the event, which nothing short of a premonition of the happiness I would derive from it can explain. Was it not so, Marianne?"

He then resumed his louder tone, addressing Mrs. Dashwood: "And yet, you would ruin this house? You would strip it of its simplicity through imagined enhancements! And this dear parlor, where our acquaintance first commenced and where countless joyful hours have since been shared among us, you would degrade to the status of a mere entryway, allowing everyone to rush through the room that has hitherto offered more genuine comfort and ease than any spacious apartment in the grandest mansion could provide."

Mrs. Dashwood once again promised him that no such alterations would be attempted.

"You are a good woman," he replied warmly. "Your assurance brings me peace. Extend it a bit further, and it will bring me joy. Promise me not only that your house will remain unchanged, but that I shall forever find you and your family as unaltered as your dwelling; and that you will always regard me with the kindness that has made everything connected to you so precious to me."

The promise was granted readily, and Willoughby's demeanor throughout the entire evening unmistakably conveyed both his affection and his profound happiness.

"Shall we see you tomorrow for dinner?" Mrs. Dashwood asked as he prepared to depart.

"I do not request to come in the morning, for we must walk to the Park to pay our respects to Lady Middleton," he noted.

He agreed to join them by four o'clock.
