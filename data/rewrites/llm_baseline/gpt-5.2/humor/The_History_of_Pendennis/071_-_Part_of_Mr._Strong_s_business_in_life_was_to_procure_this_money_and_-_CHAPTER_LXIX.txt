Morning in Bury Street arrived with its usual city chorus: a cart’s iron rim grumbling over cobbles, a milkman’s call pitched like a cracked bugle, and—under it all—the steady, nerve-sawing hiss of London, as if the town were forever frying something it oughtn’t. Morgan opened Mr. Pendennis’s shutters as though unveiling a portrait, solemn as a beadle at a funeral, and marched in with the old gentleman’s clothes, water-cans, and that glittering little arsenal of toilet gear—silver cases, brushes like officers’ moustaches, and a razor strapped and shining with as much ceremony as a sword.

“It’s you, is it?” growled Major Pendennis from the depths of his tent-bed. “I shan’t take you back again, you understand.”

“I ’ave not the least wish to be took back agin, Major Pendennis,” Morgan returned, as grave as a judge and twice as offended. “Nor to serve you nor hany man. But as I wish you to be comftable as long as you stay in my house, I came up to do what’s nessary.”

And so, with the air of a man laying out sacramental vessels rather than a dressing-case, Morgan performed his last devotions: set the silver top open, placed the bottles and pomades in rank, and snapped the razor’s strap as if it were a military salute. Then he drew himself up, enjoying—almost tasting—the sound of his own voice the way certain persons savour rich gravy.

“Thinkin’ you would most likely be in want of a respectable pusson,” he said, “until you suited yourself, I spoke to a young man last night, who is ’ere.”

“Indeed,” said the Major, who could make one syllable sound like a whole paragraph of contempt.

“He ave lived in the fust famlies, and I can wouch for his respectability.”

“You are monstrous polite,” grinned the old man; and there was that in the grin which made Morgan feel he was being handled—like a fish on a hook—without any visible line.

Yet Morgan had, in fact, been to his club at the Wheel of Fortune the night before, and there, between the clink of glasses and the noisy thud of modern London conviviality (that aggressive sort of merriment which seems to exist chiefly to drown out thought), he had recruited a substitute: Frosch, courier and valet, lately returned from foreign travel with young Lord Cubley, and for the moment as disposable as yesterday’s newspaper.

“The man have awaited on you before, sir,” Morgan continued. “Lord De la Pole, sir, gave him to his nephew young Lord Cubley, and he have been with him on his foring tour; and not wishin’ to go to Fitzurse Castle—which Frosch’s chest is delicate, and he cannot bear the cold in Scotland—he is free to serve you or not, as you choose.”

“I repeat, sir, that you are exceedingly polite,” said the Major, as if repeating it might make it true in some dangerous sense. “Come in, Frosch—you will do very well—Mr. Morgan, will you have the great kindness to—”

“I shall show him what is nessary, sir, and what is customry for you to wish to ave done,” Morgan said, with the dignity of a man resigning a ministry. “Will you please to take breakfast ’ere or at the Club, Major Pendennis?”

“With your kind permission, I will breakfast here,” said the Major, “and afterwards we will make our little arrangements.”

“If you please, sir.”

“And will you now oblige me by leaving the room?”

Morgan withdrew, stiff-backed, insulted not by abuse—he could bear that—but by politeness sharpened to a point.

While the Major made his mysterious toilet, we may as decently turn away; and besides, the sounds of the house below are more instructive: the scrape of a broom, the clatter of a pail, the thin footfalls of Betty—known as “Betty” to her mistress and “Slavey” to Morgan—moving like a frightened mouse through a lion’s den.

After breakfast, Major Pendennis and his new aide-de-camp set about departure. The Major’s domestic empire was of that marching kind which packs like a campaign: no superfluous wardrobe, no sentimental hoards. His library and gallery were a soldier’s shrine: a Bible (his mother’s, the one relic he handled with gentleness), a road-book, Pen’s novel bound calf elegant, the Duke of Wellington’s Despatches, and a few prints, maps, and portraits of that illustrious General, of sundry sovereigns and their consorts, and of the Indian commander under whom the Major had served. He lived as if always listening for the drumbeat to strike up again.

The very cases in which he had moved into those lodgings fifteen years earlier still waited in the loft, ready as old comrades. Down they came under Betty’s arms and Mrs. Brixham’s anxious eye, while Morgan hovered like a dark weather cloud, guarded and silent, his face set in that solemnity which, in certain men, is only anger in its Sunday clothes. Mrs. Brixham—poor widow—had not been told her fate; she merely felt it, the way one feels a change in the air before a storm, and trembled accordingly.

Old Pendennis, cane in hand, superintended as if inspecting troops. Frosch packed with brisk foreign neatness. Betty burned such papers as the Major wished to leave behind, and flung open cupboards until they gaped empty as a picked bone. At last all boxes were shut except the Major’s desk, which waited—open-mouthed—for Morgan’s final accounts.

Then Morgan appeared with his books.

“As I wish to speak to you in privick,” he said, “peraps you will ave the kindness to request Frosch to step downstairs.”

“Bring a couple of cabs, Frosch, if you please,” said the Major, “and wait downstairs until I ring for you.”

Morgan watched Frosch go, the way a cat watches a door close, then produced his accounts: simple enough; the sums settled quickly. The Major wrote a cheque. Morgan pocketed it, signed with a flourish, and then—like a man who has swallowed a hot coal and is glad of the burn—leaned in.

“And now, sir,” he said, “now that accounts is closed between us, sir, I porpose to speak to you as one man to another. And I must tell you, that I’m in possession of certing infamation.”

“And may I inquire of what nature, pray?” asked the Major, all majesty—an old war-horse lifting its head.

“It’s valuble information, Major Pendennis, as you know very well. I know of a marriage as is no marriage—of a honourable Baronet as is no more married than I am; and which his wife is married to somebody else—as you know too, sir.”

The Major understood in a flash; the secret’s name did not need to be spoken aloud. His eyes hardened.

“Ha! this accounts for your behaviour,” he said. “You have been listening at the door, sir, I suppose. I forgot to look at the keyhole when I went to that public-house, or I might have suspected what sort of a person was behind it.”

“I may have my schemes as you may have yours,” Morgan answered, drawing himself up. “I may get my information, and I may act on that information, and I may find that information valuble as anybody else may. A poor servant may have a bit of luck as well as a gentleman, mayn’t he? Don’t you be putting on your aughty looks, sir, and comin’ the aristocrat over me. That’s all gammon with me. I’m an Englishman, I am, and as good as you.”

“To what the devil does this tend, sir?” the Major asked. “And how does the secret which you have surprised concern me, I should like to know?”

“How does it concern me, indeed!” Morgan cried, tasting each syllable like strong spirits. “How does it concern my nephew, I wonder? How does it concern my nephew’s seat in Parlyment—and to subornation of bigamy? How does it concern that? What, are you to be the only man to have a secret, and to trade on it? Why shouldn’t I go halves, Major Pendennis? I’ve found it out too. Look here! I ain’t goin’ to be unreasonable with you. Make it worth my while, and I’ll keep the thing close. Let Mr. Arthur take his seat, and his rich wife, if you like—I don’t want to marry her. But I will have my share, as sure as my name’s James Morgan. And if I don’t—”

“And if you don’t, sir—what?” said the Major, deadly quiet.

“If I don’t, I split, and tell all. I smash Clavering, and have him and his wife up for bigamy—so help me, I will! I smash young Hopeful’s marriage, and I show up you and him as makin’ use of this secret, in order to squeeze a seat in Parlyment out of Sir Francis, and a fortune out of his wife.”

“Mr. Pendennis knows no more of this business than the babe unborn, sir,” cried the Major, genuinely shaken. “No more than Lady Clavering—than Miss Amory does.”

“Tell that to the marines, Major,” Morgan sneered. “That cock won’t fight with me.”

“Do you doubt my word, you villain?”

“No bad language,” Morgan said, with the insolence of a man who thinks himself armoured by knowledge. “I don’t care one twopence’a’p’ny whether your word’s true or not. I tell you, I intend this to be a nice little annuity to me, Major: for I have every one of you; and I ain’t such a fool as to let you go. I should say you might make it five hundred a year to me among you, easy. Pay me down the first quarter now and I’m as mum as a mouse. Just give a note for one twenty-five. There’s your cheque-book on your desk.”

“And there’s this too, you villain,” cried the Major.

From the open desk he snatched a little double-barrelled pistol—an old campaign companion that had belonged to his former commander-in-chief in India. The Major’s fury flared like gunpowder catching; his hands—old, but steady—held the weapon as though it had never been anything but loaded.

“One more word, you scoundrel, and I’ll shoot you like a mad dog. Stop—by Jove, I’ll do it now. You’ll assault me, will you? You’ll strike at an old man, will you, you lying coward? Kneel down and say your prayers, sir—for by the Lord you shall die.”

Morgan blanched, then—in a fit of theatrical terror—let out a shriek of “Murder!” and sprang to the open window, bawling down into the street where a policeman happened to be pacing his beat.

“Murder! Police!”

But to Morgan’s astonishment, the Major did not fire. Instead, he wheeled the table aside with surprising strength, strode to the other open window, and beckoned the constable up with the authority of a man who has commanded troops and never forgotten the tone.

“Come up here, policeman,” he said, and placed himself against the door so Morgan could not bolt.

“You miserable sneak,” he added, turning on his valet. “The pistol hasn’t been loaded these fifteen years, as you would have known very well, if you had not been such a coward. That policeman is coming, and I will have him up, and have your trunks searched; I have reason to believe that you are a thief, sir. I know you are. I’ll swear to the things.”

“You gave ’em to me—you gave ’em to me!” Morgan blustered, but the words came out like a man trying to whistle while drowning.

The Major laughed—short, dry, victorious.

“We’ll see,” he said.

Morgan’s mind flashed, not to honour, but to inventory: fine lawn-fronted shirts; a gold-headed cane; an opera-glass he had “forgotten” to bring down; and sundry garments the Major neither wore nor missed. The thought of those articles being displayed in public made Morgan’s throat tighten as if he had swallowed a pin.

Policeman X entered, followed by Mrs. Brixham, white-faced, and Betty behind her, half-curious, half-terrified. The door had scarcely closed before the noise from the street—the eager amateur audience of London—pressed itself against it like a second crowd.

The Major began at once, speaking with the calmness of a man who has rehearsed the truth into a weapon.

“I have had occasion to discharge this drunken scoundrel,” he said. “Both last night and this morning he insulted and assaulted me. I am an old man and took up a pistol. You see it is not loaded, and this coward cried out before he was hurt. I am glad you are come. I was charging him with taking my property, and desired to examine his trunks and his room.”

“The velvet cloak you ain’t worn these three years, nor the weskits,” Morgan burst out, “and I thought I might take the shirts, and I—I take my hoath I intended to put back the hopera-glass!” He roared it in a mixture of rage and fear, as if volume might turn theft into virtue.

“The man acknowledges that he is a thief,” said the Major, serenely. “He has been in my service for years, and I have treated him with every kindness and confidence. We will go upstairs and examine his trunks.”

Now, in those trunks were things Morgan would have given good money to keep invisible. For Mr. Morgan, besides being a valet and (newly) a landlord, was a gentleman of commerce in the darker pantry of London: he discounted bills, yes—but also supplied certain young gentlemen with “goods” along with money. Snuff-boxes and pins; jewels and pictures; cigars whose pedigree would not bear investigation. The notion of such a hoard being turned out at a police-office—like spoiled meat on a slab—was enough to make even Morgan’s robust impudence wilt.

He looked piteous in his terror; and the Major, who had been ready to crush him utterly, hesitated. He glanced at Mrs. Brixham—thin, harried, living by the scant economies of a kettle and a broom—and he thought how a desperate man, once caged, might sing very loudly indeed; and some songs are best not loosed upon the air, where they can be taken up, misheard, repeated, and turned into weapons by any passing tongue.

“Stop,” said the Major. “Policeman. I’ll speak with this man by himself.”

“Do you give Mr. Morgan in charge?” asked X.

“I have brought no charge as yet,” said the Major, and gave Morgan a look with more meaning than any affidavit.

“Thank you, sir,” Morgan breathed, scarcely audible.

“Go outside the door and wait there, policeman, if you please.”

The constable withdrew. Mrs. Brixham and Betty hovered, then were waved back too, leaving the two men alone with the desk, the smell of smoke, and the city’s muffled roar seeping through the windows like an ill-tuned organ.

Morgan began to recover his nerve. The Major did not allow the recovery to ripen.

“Now, Morgan,” he said, “you have played one game with me, and you have not had the best of it, my good man. No, begad, you’ve not had the best of it, though you had the best hand; and you’ve got to pay, too, now, you scoundrel.”

“Yes, sir,” said Morgan, who had discovered that “sir” costs less than courage.

“I’ve only found out, within the last week, the game which you have been driving, you villain. Young De Boots, of the Blues, recognised you as the man who came to barracks, and did business one-third in money, one-third in eau-de-Cologne, and one-third in French prints—you confounded demure old sinner! I didn’t miss anything, or care a straw what you’d taken, you booby; but I took the shot, and it hit—hit the bull’s-eye, begad. Dammy, six, I’m an old campaigner.”

“What do you want with me, sir?” Morgan asked, sourly, like a cook caught watering the soup.

“I’ll tell you. Your bills, I suppose, you keep about you in that dem’d great leather pocket-book, don’t you? You’ll burn Mrs. Brixham’s bill?”

“Sir, I ain’t a-goin’ to part with my property,” Morgan growled.

“You lent her sixty pounds five years ago,” the Major went on, “and she and that poor devil of an insurance clerk, her son, have paid you fifty pounds a year ever since; and you have got a bill of sale of her furniture, and her note of hand for a hundred and fifty pounds. She told me so last night. By Jove, sir, you’ve bled that poor woman enough.”

“I won’t give it up,” said Morgan. “If I do I’m—”

“Policeman!” cried the Major, turning his head sharply toward the door.

“You shall have the bill,” Morgan snapped, beaten by the mere sound of authority. “You’re not going to take money of me, and you a gentleman?”

“I shall want you directly,” the Major called, and X appeared, then withdrew again at a sign.

“No, my good sir,” the Major continued, “I have not any desire to have further pecuniary transactions with you; but we will draw out a little paper, which you will have the kindness to sign. No, stop—you shall write it. You have improved immensely in writing of late, and have now a very good hand.”

That was one of those polite thrusts that leave a bruise exactly where the pride lives.

“You shall sit down and write, if you please—there, at that table—so—let me see—we may as well have the date. Write ‘Bury Street, St. James’s, October 21, 18—.’”

Morgan wrote, the scratching of the pen loud in the room’s sudden hush—hush only on the inside; outside, London kept clattering and calling, as if a city’s noise were determined to prevent any human being from hearing their own conscience.

The Major dictated slowly, tasting each clause like a cook tasting a sauce—adjusting, correcting, making it bite.

“‘I, James Morgan, having come in extreme poverty into the service of Arthur Pendennis, Esquire, of Bury Street, St. James’s, a Major in her Majesty’s service, acknowledge that I received liberal wages and board wages from my employer, during fifteen years.’ You can’t object to that, I am sure.”

Morgan wrote: “during fifteen years,” and his jaw clenched.

“‘In which time, by my own care and prudence, I have managed to amass sufficient money to purchase the house in which my master resides, and, besides, to effect other savings. Amongst other persons from whom I have had money, I may mention my present tenant, Mrs. Brixham, who, in consideration of sixty pounds advanced by me five years since, has paid back to me the sum of two hundred and fifty pounds sterling, besides giving me a note of hand for one hundred and twenty pounds, which I restore to her at the desire of my late master, Major Arthur Pendennis, and therewith free her furniture, of which I had a bill of sale.’ Have you written?”

“I think if this pistol was loaded, I’d blow your brains out,” Morgan muttered.

“No, you wouldn’t,” said the Major, cool as ever. “You have too great a respect for your valuable life, my good man. Let us go on and begin a new sentence.

“‘And having, in return for my master’s kindness, stolen his property from him, which I acknowledge to be now upstairs in my trunks; and having uttered falsehoods regarding his and other honourable families, I do hereby, in consideration of his clemency to me, express my regret for uttering these falsehoods, and for stealing his property; and declare that I am not worthy of belief, and that I hope—yes, begad—that I hope to amend for the future. Signed, James Morgan.’”

“I’m d——d if I sign it,” said Morgan.

“My good man, it will happen to you, whether you sign or no, begad,” said the Major, chuckling at his own grim joke. “There. I shall not use this, you understand, unless—unless I am compelled to do so.”

He rang; Mrs. Brixham came in, hands twisting in her apron, eyes pleading without words. Policeman X returned too, obedient to the bell as if it were a drum.

“Mrs. Brixham, and our friend the policeman, will witness it, I dare say, without reading it,” said the Major. “And I will give the old lady back her note of hand, and say—which you will confirm—that she and you are quits. I see there is Frosch come back with the cab for my trunks; I shall go to an hotel.—You may come in now, policeman; Mr. Morgan and I have arranged our little dispute. If Mrs. Brixham will sign this paper, and you, policeman, will do so, I shall be very much obliged to you both. Mrs. Brixham, you and your worthy landlord, Mr. Morgan, are quits. I wish you joy of him. Let Frosch come and pack the rest of the things.”

The paper was signed. The note of hand returned. Mrs. Brixham looked as if someone had lifted a sack of stones from her shoulders, and she tried to speak—blessings and gratitude bubbling up like a pot finally allowed to boil. But Major Pendennis offered her a finger to shake, briskly, as a man might give a crumb to a sparrow, and cut her speech short before tears could make a puddle.

Frosch, aided by Betty, carried the Major’s boxes down to the waiting cabs, while Morgan stood by with a composure so stiff it might have been starched. Outside, the street’s racket surged and fell: boots, wheels, voices; and above it, now and then, the sharp cry of a seller hawking wares as if noise itself were the commodity. One might have wished for the quiet of a farmyard at dusk, when you can hear the last hen settle and the wind comb through straw. But London gives you no such mercy: it drowns every private drama in a public din, and then sells you the echo.

Major Pendennis walked out, cane in hand, to the hotel in Jermyn Street—only a few steps away, as if he were merely changing rooms in the same noisy inn called life. Morgan, watching from the parlour-window, delivered anything but blessings after him; yet the stout old Major could afford not to be frightened. He flung his late servant one look—half contempt, half humour—and strutted away as though marching off a parade ground.

It was not many hours later that Morgan, now enjoying his otium with great dignity, sat upon the door-steps smoking a cigar—smoking, that is, in flagrant rebellion against the late master who would have treated tobacco like treason. Evening fog had come down, thick and greasy, swallowing the street-lamps and turning each sound into something muffled and sour. The city’s noise persisted, but differently: less bright clatter, more distant rumble, as if London had put a lid on its own pot and let it simmer.

Then Arthur Pendennis appeared at the familiar door.

“My uncle out, I suppose, Morgan?” he asked, with the easy tone of a man who knows the answer and enjoys the asking—especially knowing that a cigar in Morgan’s mouth would have been a court-martial offence in the Major’s presence.

“Major Pendennis is hout, sir,” Morgan said, bowing gravely, but not touching the elegant cap he wore—an omission that declared, as plainly as a shouted insult, that the old regime was over. “Major Pendennis have left this ouse to-day, sir, and I have no longer the honour of being in his service, sir.”

“Indeed, and where is he?”

“I believe he ave taken tempory lodgings at Cox’s otel, in Jummin Street,” Morgan answered. Then, after a pause that was almost a tasting of the moment, he added, “Are you in town for some time, pray, sir? Are you in Chambers? I should like to have the honour of waiting on you there—and would be thankful if you would favour me with a quarter of an hour.”

“Do you want my uncle to take you back?” Arthur asked, insolent and good-natured.

“I want no such thing; I’d see him—” Morgan began, and for one instant the old glare returned; then he swallowed it, as a man swallows a mouthful too hot, and forced a softer voice. “No, sir, thank you. It’s only with you that I wish to speak, on some business which concerns you; and perhaps you would favour me by walking into my house.”

“If it is but for a minute or two, I will listen to you, Morgan,” Arthur said, thinking, with amused indifference, that the fellow probably wanted patronage.

He stepped inside.

In the front window, already, a card announced that apartments were to be let—language made into a little signboard, the way modern London turns everything into advertisement. Morgan led him into the dining-room, offered a chair, took one opposite, and, with the careful relish of a man about to serve a dish long simmered in secrecy, began to convey information to Arthur Pendennis—information which we, being already in possession of it, can taste in advance.
