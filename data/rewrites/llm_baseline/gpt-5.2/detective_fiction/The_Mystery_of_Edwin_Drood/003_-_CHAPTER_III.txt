For reasons that would come to matter, though not yet, the old Cathedral town must wear a name that is not upon any map. Let it be Cloisterham.

Names, in such a place, shed like skins. One tongue calls it this; another, that; and each century lays its label down over the last as a cook lays a fresh bay-leaf on yesterday’s broth—fragrant for a moment, then swallowed by the deeper, older taste. The town itself remains: ancient, earthen, steeped in crypt-damp and the slow chemistry of time. You smell it before you quite see it, an odour like cold stone rinsed by perpetual shade, mingled with the mossy sweetness of graveyard grass and the faint soot of a bell that has been hoarse for hundreds of years.

It is not a place for those who crave the loud world. Cloisterham keeps its soundscape close and narrow: the hush between footfalls; the thin clap of a shop-blind that hardly dares to flap; the sudden echo of a single voice turning a corner and coming back altered, as if the town itself is repeating you in an older dialect. Overhead, rooks circle the Cathedral tower—black commas in the sky—and their cawing is answered, far below, by other rooks and other rasping cries from the carved stalls, less distinct, as though spoken through cloth.

Here the past is not a story, but a soil. Children plant small salad in dust that was abbots and abbesses; they pat dirt into pies in which nuns and friars are kneaded without knowing. In fields beyond, the plough goes on with the blunt persistence of appetite, turning up what once bore titles—Lord Treasurers, Bishops, Archbishops—and grinding them back into the bread of common days. A man may eat without thinking what old authority has become his flour.

The town’s single thoroughfare is little more than a narrow line to enter by and escape by; all other ways are disappointing yards with pumps that clank in a tired key and lead nowhere. There is, it is true, the Cathedral Close, and a paved Quaker settlement tucked into a shaded corner, demure in colour and confirmation like a Quakeress’s bonnet. But even these seem more like preserved relics than living streets. Cloisterham’s pawnbroker has ceased to take pledges; he sits among unredeemed objects that sweat their age—dim watches in slow perspiration, sugar-tongs with ineffectual legs, odd dismal volumes that smell of dust and mould. One might say the liveliest signs of growth are in gardens: beans climbing their strings; cabbages thickening; herbs giving back, in crushed leaf, a sharp green answer to the Cathedral’s stony breath. Even the drooping little theatre, poor as it is, keeps a strip of earth where scarlet-beans or oyster-shells receive the foul fiend when he ducks from stage-hell into the real ground.

In the midst of Cloisterham stands the Nuns’ House: a venerable brick building whose name clings like a legend you cannot quite scrape off. Its low ceilings and jutting gables seem made to make a woman bow her head—whether in prayer, or in habit, or merely to save her brow from oak beams. Whatever lives there now, the walls remember what once murmured there; and if there are ghosts, they have the patience of those who have listened to the same bell note for centuries.

On the trim gate enclosing its old courtyard a bright brass plate flashes, too modern for the brick behind it, as if a new, staring eye had been stuck into an old, battered face:

“Seminary for Young Ladies. Miss Twinkleton.”

Miss Twinkleton’s accounts—half-yearly, meticulous, regular—contain no line for beads told at long windows, no extra for mortification, no fee for being walled up alive in some odd angle for having too much of busy Nature in you. Such questions would never appear in the poetical department at so much a quarter, and no recitation list would include a profitable piece on unprofitable suffering.

Miss Twinkleton herself, however, carries within her a curious division, like a kitchen with two doors leading to two different households. By day she is the seminary: composed, proper, a person of angles and schedules, dispensing education with a face that suggests she might at any moment be compelled—by duty, naturally—to set a terrestrial globe spinning or point to a celestial one, and range the earth and sky in search of knowledge for her pupils.

By night, the moment the young ladies have retired, she seems to unpin herself. Her curls smarten; her eyes brighten; she becomes a sprightlier Miss Twinkleton than any pupil ever sees. Then, in that other consciousness, she resumes without a stumble the tender scandal of Cloisterham which by day she would seem never to have heard; and she returns, too, to a certain season at Tunbridge Wells—airily “The Wells”—and to a certain finished gentleman, compassionately “Foolish Mr. Porters,” who in that season offered homage of the heart of which the seminary Miss Twinkleton is as ignorant as a granite pillar.

In both states her companion is Mrs. Tisher: a deferential widow with a weak back, a chronic sigh, and a voice trained to be present without being loud. She attends to young ladies’ wardrobes with a rustle like subdued ceremony, letting it be understood that she has seen better days. Perhaps for that reason the servants hold, as an article of faith handed down from one generation of kitchen to the next, that the departed Mr. Tisher (when he departed) must have been a hairdresser.

The pet pupil of the Nuns’ House is Miss Rosa Bud—Rosebud to everybody—wonderfully pretty, wonderfully childish, wonderfully whimsical. An awkward interest hangs about her among the other girls, awkward because romantic: it is known that by will and bequest a husband has been chosen for her, and that her guardian is bound to bestow her upon that husband when he comes of age. Miss Twinkleton, by day, combats the romance by shaking her head behind Rosebud’s dimpled shoulders and brooding aloud upon the unhappy lot of the doomed little victim. Yet the result, whether from the undermining memory of foolish Mr. Porters or from the girls’ instinct for hypocrisy, is only the unanimous bedchamber cry: “O, what a pretending old thing Miss Twinkleton is, my dear!”

The Nuns’ House is never in such a flutter as when the allotted husband calls. It is understood by the young ladies, unanimously, that he is lawfully entitled to appear; and that if Miss Twinkleton disputed it, she would be instantly taken up and transported. When his ring at the gate-bell is expected—or when it actually happens—every girl who can contrive, under any pretence, to look out of window, looks out; every girl who is “practising” practises out of time; the French class becomes so demoralised that the mark goes round briskly, like a bottle at a last-century supper.

On the afternoon of the day after a certain dinner of two at the gatehouse, the bell is rung, and the usual small earthquake runs through the establishment. The parlour-maid in chief announces, with the gravity of a court proclamation:

“Mr. Edwin Drood to see Miss Rosa.”

Miss Twinkleton, wearing exemplary melancholy like a shawl, turns to the sacrifice. “You may go down, my dear.”

Miss Bud goes down, followed by all eyes—eyes that can scent romance as quickly as a kitchen can scent a pie beginning to scorch. Mr. Edwin Drood waits in Miss Twinkleton’s own parlour, a dainty room whose only directly scholastic furniture is a terrestrial and a celestial globe, those expressive machines implying to parents and guardians that even in privacy Miss Twinkleton may, at any moment, be compelled by Duty to traverse continents and constellations on behalf of her pupils.

The newest maid—who has never yet seen the young gentleman to whom Miss Rosa is engaged—takes her first taste of him between the hinges of the open door left open for the purpose, and then tumbles guiltily down the kitchen stairs.

Into the parlour glides a charming little apparition, her face concealed by a little silk apron thrown over her head as if she were playing at being invisible.

“O! it is so ridiculous!” she cries, stopping and shrinking. “Don’t, Eddy!”

“Don’t what, Rosa?”

“Don’t come any nearer, please. It is so absurd.”

“What is absurd, Rosa?”

“The whole thing is. It is so absurd to be an engaged orphan, and it is so absurd to have the girls and the servants scuttling about after one like mice in the wainscot; and it is so absurd to be called upon!” The muffled voice has a childish thickness in it, as if a thumb were tucked into the corner of her mouth while she complains.

“You give me an affectionate reception, Pussy, I must say.”

“Well, I will in a minute, Eddy, but I can’t just yet. How are you?” She asks it very shortly, as if “how are you” were a mere button to press.

“I am unable to reply that I am much the better for seeing you, Pussy, inasmuch as I see nothing of you.”

A dark, bright, pouting eye peeps out from the apron’s edge and vanishes again, as she exclaims, “O good gracious! you have had half your hair cut off!”

“I should have done better to have had my head cut off, I think,” says Edwin, rumpling what remains with a fierce glance at the looking-glass, and stamping as if the boards had offended him.

“Shall I go?”

“No; you needn’t go just yet, Eddy. The girls would all be asking questions why you went.”

“Once for all, Rosa, will you uncover that ridiculous little head of yours and give me a welcome?”

She pulls off the apron. Her face is all dimples and wilfulness, with colour in it like something you might bite—like a candied cherry. “You’re very welcome, Eddy. There! I’m sure that’s nice. Shake hands. No, I can’t kiss you, because I’ve got an acidulated drop in my mouth.”

“Are you at all glad to see me, Pussy?”

“O yes, I’m dreadfully glad.—Go and sit down.—Miss Twinkleton.”

This is the signal for Propriety to pay its tithe. It is Miss Twinkleton’s custom, during these visits, to appear every three minutes either in her own person or in that of Mrs. Tisher, and lay a little offering on the shrine by pretending to seek a necessary article. On the present occasion Miss Twinkleton glides in and out and says, in passing, “How do you do, Mr. Drood? Very glad indeed to have the pleasure. Pray excuse me. Tweezers. Thank you!”—and is gone again, leaving behind a faint scent of lavender-water and supervision.

“I got the gloves last evening, Eddy, and I like them very much. They are beauties.”

“Well, that’s something,” he returns, half grumbling, as if praise were rationed. “The smallest encouragement thankfully received. And how did you pass your birthday, Pussy?”

“Delightfully! Everybody gave me a present. And we had a feast. And we had a ball at night.”

“A feast and a ball, eh? These occasions seem to go off tolerably well without me, Pussy.”

“De-lightfully!” Rosa cries, with such spontaneous relief that it pricks him.

“Hah! And what was the feast?”

“Tarts, oranges, jellies, and shrimps.”

The list comes out like the opening of a pantry: the bright peel-perfume of oranges, the trembling sweetness of jellies catching the light, the briny little shrimps with their seaside tang—foods that linger on the tongue and in the mind, much as Cloisterham’s old words linger in old mouths. Rosa’s breath, when she speaks, carries the sharp sugar-and-lemon of the acidulated drop.

“Any partners at the ball?”

“We danced with one another, of course, sir. But some of the girls made game to be their brothers. It was so droll!”

“Did anybody make game to be—”

“To be you? O dear yes!” Rosa laughs, enjoying it as if she were tasting it again. “That was the first thing done.”

“I hope she did it pretty well,” Edwin says, doubtfully, as if he suspects himself to be easy to caricature.

“O, it was excellent!—I wouldn’t dance with you, you know.”

He looks as if he cannot find the point, and asks why he may not take the liberty to inquire.

“Because I was so tired of you,” Rosa returns, and then, seeing displeasure gather in him, adds quickly and pleadingly, “Dear Eddy, you were just as tired of me, you know.”

“Did I say so, Rosa?”

“Say so! Do you ever say so? No, you only showed it. O, she did it so well!” she cries, in sudden ecstasy over the counterfeit.

“It strikes me that she must be a devilish impudent girl,” Edwin says.

“And so, Pussy, you have passed your last birthday in this old house.”

“Ah, yes!” Rosa clasps her hands, looks down with a sigh, and shakes her head.

“You seem to be sorry, Rosa.”

“I am sorry for the poor old place. Somehow, I feel as if it would miss me, when I am gone so far away, so young.”

“Perhaps we had better stop short, Rosa?”

She looks up swiftly, brightness like a spark, then drops her eyes again and shakes her head with another sigh.

“That is to say, is it, Pussy, that we are both resigned?”

She nods; then, after a short silence, bursts out quaintly: “You know we must be married, and married from here, Eddy, or the poor girls will be so dreadfully disappointed!”

For a moment there is more compassion on his face—compassion for her, and for himself—than there is love. He checks it and asks, “Shall I take you out for a walk, Rosa dear?”

Rosa dear seems uncertain, her mind making a comical little circuit; then her face brightens. “O yes, Eddy; let us go for a walk! And I tell you what we’ll do. You shall pretend that you are engaged to somebody else, and I’ll pretend that I am not engaged to anybody, and then we shan’t quarrel.”

“Do you think that will prevent our falling out, Rosa?”

“I know it will. Hush! Pretend to look out of window—Mrs. Tisher!”

By a fortuitous concourse of accidents, Mrs. Tisher appears, heaving into sight and rustling through the room like the ghost of a dowager condemned to silken skirts. “I hope I see Mr. Drood well; though I needn’t ask, if I may judge from his complexion. I trust I disturb no one; but there was a paper-knife—O, thank you, I am sure!” She disappears with her prize and a sigh that seems part of her clothing.

“One other thing you must do, Eddy, to oblige me,” says Rosebud. “The moment we get into the street, you must put me outside, and keep close to the house yourself—squeeze and graze yourself against it.”

“By all means, Rosa, if you wish it. Might I ask why?”

“O! because I don’t want the girls to see you.”

“It’s a fine day; but would you like me to carry an umbrella up?”

“Don’t be foolish, sir. You haven’t got polished leather boots on,” she pouts, with one shoulder raised like a small wing.

“Perhaps that might escape the notice of the girls, even if they did see me,” Edwin remarks, looking down at his boots with a sudden distaste for them.

“Nothing escapes their notice, sir. And then I know what would happen. Some of them would begin reflecting on me by saying (for they are free) that they never will on any account engage themselves to lovers without polished leather boots. Hark! Miss Twinkleton. I’ll ask for leave.”

Miss Twinkleton is indeed heard without, advancing in a blandly conversational tone as if speaking to the air: “Eh? Indeed! Are you quite sure you saw my mother-of-pearl button-holder on the work-table in my room?” Rosa is at once granted walking leave, and soon the young couple are out of the Nuns’ House, taking all precautions against discovery of the vitally defective boots of Mr. Edwin Drood—precautions which, for the peace of Mrs. Edwin Drood that is to be, let us hope are effective.

“Which way shall we take, Rosa?”

“I want to go to the Lumps-of-Delight shop.”

“To the—?”

“A Turkish sweetmeat, sir. My gracious me, don’t you understand anything? Call yourself an Engineer, and not know that?”

“Why, how should I know it, Rosa?”

“Because I am very fond of them. But O! I forgot what we are to pretend. No, you needn’t know anything about them; never mind.”

So he is borne off, gloomily enough, to the shop. There, amid jars and trays, Rosa buys her Lumps-of-Delight—sticky, dusted, perfumed with spice and rosewater, clinging to the paper like memories clinging to an old word. The sugar-dust rises when the parcel is opened, and the air takes on that faint foreign sweetness, as if a far country has been ground fine and sifted over Cloisterham’s grey day.

She offers him some. He declines rather indignantly, as though sweetness were an insult.

She begins to eat with great zest. First she takes off and rolls up her little pink gloves, like rose-leaves folded for keeping. Now and again she touches her little pink fingers to her rosy lips to clean them of the Dust of Delight. The sound of it—soft tackiness, a tiny pull and release—goes oddly against the town’s habitual hush, like a private, domestic noise refusing to be ashamed.

“Now, be a good-tempered Eddy, and pretend. And so you are engaged?”

“And so I am engaged.”

“Is she nice?”

“Charming.”

“Tall?”

“Immensely tall!” He says it with emphasis, and Rosa—short as a bird—tilts her head.

“Must be gawky, I should think,” Rosa observes quietly, enjoying her sweetmeat.

“I beg your pardon; not at all,” he contradicts, rising. “What is termed a fine woman; a splendid woman.”

“Big nose, no doubt,” says Rosa, tranquilly.

“Not a little one, certainly,” he replies quickly—her own being a little one.

“Long pale nose, with a red knob in the middle. I know the sort of nose,” says Rosa, nodding with the satisfied air of a food-historian identifying a spice by smell alone.

“You don’t know the sort of nose, Rosa,” he returns with some warmth, “because it’s nothing of the kind.”

“Not a pale nose, Eddy?”

“No.” He will not yield.

“A red nose? O! I don’t like red noses. However; to be sure she can always powder it.”

“She would scorn to powder it,” Edwin says, heating.

“Would she? What a stupid thing she must be! Is she stupid in everything?”

“No; in nothing.”

After a pause—in which Rosa’s whimsically wicked face has watched him with the same patient attention she gives the slow dissolving of sugar—she says, “And this most sensible of creatures likes the idea of being carried off to Egypt; does she, Eddy?”

“Yes. She takes a sensible interest in triumphs of engineering skill: especially when they are to change the whole condition of an undeveloped country.”

“Lor!” Rosa laughs, and shrugs her shoulders. “Do you object,” Edwin inquires, with a majestic downward look upon her fairy figure, “do you object, Rosa, to her feeling that interest?”

“Object? my dear Eddy! But really, doesn’t she hate boilers and things?”

“I can answer for her not being so idiotic as to hate Boilers,” he returns with angry emphasis; “though I cannot answer for her views about Things; really not understanding what Things are meant.”

“But don’t she hate Arabs, and Turks, and Fellahs, and people?”

“Certainly not.” Very firmly.

“At least she must hate the Pyramids? Come, Eddy?”

“Why should she be such a little—tall, I mean—goose, as to hate the Pyramids, Rosa?”

“Ah! you should hear Miss Twinkleton,” Rosa says, nodding and savouring her Lumps, “bore about them, and then you wouldn’t ask. Tiresome old burying-grounds! Isises, and Ibises, and Cheopses, and Pharaohses; who cares about them? And then there was Belzoni, or somebody, dragged out by the legs, half-choked with bats and dust. All the girls say: Serve him right, and hope it hurt him, and wish he had been quite choked.”

They wander, side by side but no longer arm-in-arm, about the old Close. Fallen leaves, damp and faintly mould-sweet, lie in drifts; each of them sometimes stops and presses a deeper footprint into the soft decay, as if to make a point the other must feel. Above them, the Cathedral’s great body holds its breath of stone; the town’s usual quiet is broken only by their little frictions and the distant rook-voices writing black sound against the sky.

“Well!” Edwin says at last, after a lengthy silence. “According to custom. We can’t get on, Rosa.”

Rosa tosses her head. She doesn’t want to get on.

“That’s a pretty sentiment, Rosa, considering.”

“Considering what?”

“If I say what, you’ll go wrong again.”

“You’ll go wrong, you mean, Eddy. Don’t be ungenerous.”

“Ungenerous! I like that!”

“Then I don’t like that, and so I tell you plainly,” Rosa pouts.

“Now, Rosa, I put it to you. Who disparaged my profession, my destination—”

“You are not going to be buried in the Pyramids, I hope?” she interrupts, arching her delicate eyebrows. “You never said you were. If you are, why haven’t you mentioned it to me? I can’t find out your plans by instinct.”

“Now, Rosa, you know very well what I mean, my dear.”

“Well then, why did you begin with your detestable red-nosed giantesses? And she would, she would, she would, she would, she WOULD powder it!” Rosa cries in a burst of comical contradictory spleen.

“Somehow or other, I never can come right in these discussions,” Edwin sighs, becoming resigned.

“How is it possible, sir, that you ever can come right when you’re always wrong? And as to Belzoni, I suppose he’s dead;—I’m sure I hope he is—and how can his legs or his chokes concern you?”

“It is nearly time for your return, Rosa. We have not had a very happy walk, have we?”

“A happy walk? A detestably unhappy walk, sir. If I go up-stairs the moment I get in and cry till I can’t take my dancing lesson, you are responsible, mind!”

“Let us be friends, Rosa.”

“Ah!” Rosa cries, shaking her head, and now the tears come real, not theatrical. “I wish we could be friends! It’s because we can’t be friends, that we try one another so. I am a young little thing, Eddy, to have an old heartache; but I really, really have, sometimes. Don’t be angry. I know you have one yourself too often. We should both of us have done better, if What is to be had been left What might have been. I am quite a little serious thing now, and not teasing you. Let each of us forbear, this one time, on our own account, and on the other’s!”

He is disarmed by this sudden glimpse of woman’s nature in the spoilt child—though for an instant disposed to resent it, as if it implied that he is an enforced infliction upon her. He watches her cry and sob, both hands pressing a handkerchief to her eyes; then, as she composes herself and in her young inconstancy begins to laugh at herself for being so moved, he leads her to a seat under the elm-trees close by.

“One clear word of understanding, Pussy dear,” he begins awkwardly. “I am not clever out of my own line—now I come to think of it, I don’t know that I am particularly clever in it—but I want to do right. There is not—there may be—I really don’t see my way to what I want to say, but I must say it before we part—there is not any other young—”

“O no, Eddy! It’s generous of you to ask me; but no, no, no!”

They have drawn very near the Cathedral windows. At that moment the organ and choir sound out sublimely. The music rolls through the air like warm bread scent through a cold house—filling every hollow, making the very stones seem to resonate. It is the town’s purest sound, gathered and released with patient power; and as they sit listening, Edwin Drood’s mind lifts the confidence of last night and sets it beside this great concord, thinking how unlike it is to discordance.

“I fancy I can distinguish Jack’s voice,” he remarks, low, as if not to scratch the sound.

“Take me back at once, please,” Rosa urges, quickly laying her light hand upon his wrist. “They will all be coming out directly; let us get away. O, what a resounding chord! But don’t let us stop to listen to it; let us get away!”

Her hurry is over as soon as they have passed out of the Close. The acoustics change; the Cathedral’s swell falls behind like a tide retreating; the High-street receives them with its narrow hush and its watchful windows. Now they go arm-in-arm, gravely and deliberately enough, along the old way back to the Nuns’ House.

At the gate, the street within sight is empty. Edwin bends down his face to Rosebud’s. She remonstrates, laughing, and is a childish schoolgirl again.

“Eddy, no! I’m too sticky to be kissed. But give me your hand, and I’ll blow a kiss into that.”

He does so. She breathes a light breath into his palm and keeps hold of it, peering into it as if it were a small, private relic.

“Now say, what do you see?”

“See, Rosa?”

“Why, I thought you Egyptian boys could look into a hand and see all sorts of phantoms. Can’t you see a happy Future?”

For certain, neither of them sees a happy Present, as the gate opens and closes, and one goes in, and the other goes away—leaving Cloisterham’s old air to settle again, and its old sounds to resume their rule.
