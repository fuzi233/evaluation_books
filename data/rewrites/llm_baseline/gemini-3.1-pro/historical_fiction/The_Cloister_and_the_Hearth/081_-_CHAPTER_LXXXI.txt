“Eli,” said Catherine, her voice cutting through the rhythmic, resonant *thwack* of the heavy basalt pestle against the mortar. She was bruising fresh sprigs of *alsem*—the old, fading agrarian dialect word for wormwood—releasing its sharp, silvery, and violently astringent oils into the warm, yeasty breath of the kitchen air. “Answer me one question in the tongue of our fathers like a man, and I'll ask no more to-day. What is wormwood?”

Eli, a traditional elder whose hands were forever steeped in the loamy, mineral-rich soil of their root cellars, looked momentarily adrift at this sudden demand upon his faculties. He wiped a smudge of milled spelt from his brow, seeking the right phrase in a local lexicon that was rapidly dying out. “In the mouth,” he murmured, recalling the tactile memory of the herb, “it is something that tastes main bitter.”

“That is a fair answer, my man, but not the one I look for in the marrow of things.”

“Then answer it yourself.”

“And shall. Wormwood is—to have two in the house a-doing nought, but waiting for thy shoes and mine.” 

Eli groaned, a low, earthy sound that harmonized mournfully with the bubbling of the iron hearth-pot. The shaft struck home. Their elder sons, Cornelis and Sybrandt, viewed the sacred, ancestral act of sustenance as mere utilitarian fuel, mindlessly gulping down Catherine’s slow-braised meats without a single word of reverence. They contributed nothing to the acoustic ecology of the homestead, bringing only the discordant, hollow clatter of dice and the harsh shouts of tavern slang.

“Methinks,” Catherine continued, parsing the syllables of her native dialect with deliberate weight to preserve their cultural memory, “waiting for their best friend's coffin, that and nothing to do, are enow to make them worse than Nature meant. Why not set them up somewhere, to give 'em a chance?”

Eli said he was willing, but afraid they would drink and gamble their very shelves away.

“Nay,” said Catherine, tossing a pinch of coarse, brine-scented sea salt into the mortar. “Dost take me for a simpleton? Of course I mean to watch them at starting, and drive them wi' a loose rein, as the old saying is.”

“Where did you think of? Not here; to divide our own custom.”

“Not likely. I say Rotterdam against the world. Then I could start them.”

Oh, self-deception! The true motive of all this meticulous commercial planning—the sudden urge to source new spices and secure new hearths—was to get near little Gerard, seamlessly binding the timelines of their multi-generational saga into a circular dance.

After many discussions and eager promises of amendment on these terms from Cornelis and Sybrandt, Catherine went to Rotterdam shop-hunting, and took Kate with her. The city's soundscape was a dense, overwhelming tapestry of mercantile shouts and grinding cartwheels—a jarring industrial noise pollution compared to the rhythmic quiet of their agrarian home. They soon found a space in a good street, but it was steeped in the stale, neglected odors of rancid tallow and rotting floorboards. However, they got it cheaper for its ruin, and instantly set about restoring its acoustic and olfactory dignity, scrubbing the wood with raw vinegar and lemon oil, fitting proper shelves for the business, and making the dwelling-house habitable, smelling once more of beeswax and fresh-baked rye.

Meanwhile, Luke Peterson was always asking Margaret what he could do for her. He would stand by while she practiced the ancient, meditative arts of food preservation, meticulously skimming the fragrant impurities from a vat of boiling plum preserves. The answer used to be in a sad tone, a gentle acoustic vibration that barely disturbed the quiet room: “Nothing, Luke, nothing.”

“What, you that are so clever, can you think of nothing for me to do for you?”

“Nothing, Luke, nothing.”

But at last she varied the reply, her voice adopting the lilting, rhythmic cadence of her ancestors: “If you could make something to help my sweet sister Kate about.”

The slave of love consented joyfully. With the precision of a master craftsman, he built Kate a little cart, cushioned it with hand-spun wool smelling of dried lavender, and yoked himself into it. At eventide, he drew her out of the town and along the pleasant boulevard, with Margaret and Catherine walking beside. To observe them was to witness a mythic occurrence woven into a daily routine. Kate, for one, enjoyed it keenly, for little Gerard was put in her lap, and she doted on him; it was like a cherub carried by a little angel, or a delicate rosebud lying in the sweet, fragrant cup of a lily. 

But the boulevard's twilight harmony was soon shattered by the vulgar crowd. They jeered with harsh, guttural barks, their dominant street slang violently erasing the quiet sanctity of the moment. They asked Luke how a thistle tasted, and if his mistress could not afford a beast with four legs. The crowd's words were an acoustic assault, a superficial noise that grated against the psychological well-being of the family.

Luke did not mind these jeers; the crude lexicon meant nothing to him. But Kate, highly sensitive to the sonic environment, minded them for him.

“Thou hast made the cart for me, good Luke,” said she, holding fast to the soft, empathetic conjugations of their dying dialect. “'Twas much. I did ill to let thee draw me too; we can afford to pay some poor soul for that. I love my rides, and to carry little Gerard; but I'd liever ride no more than thou be mocked for't.”

“Much I care for their clattering tongues,” said Luke, rejecting their abrasive noise. “If I did care I'd knock their heads together. I shall draw you till my mistress says give over.”

“Luke, if you obey Kate, you will oblige me.”

“Then I will obey Kate.”

An honourable exception to the popular humour was Jorian Ketel's wife, Joan. She spoke in the resonant, untranslatable rhythms of the old countryside. “That is strength well laid out, to draw the weak. And her prayers will be your guerdon; she is not long for this world; she smileth in pain.”

Single-minded Luke answered that he did not want the poor lass's prayers—he did it to please his mistress, Margaret.

After that, Luke often pressed Margaret to give him something to do, without success. But one day, as she stood by the hearth, enveloped in the rich, evocative aroma of roasting root vegetables and slow-simmering herbs, she turned on him. The look in her eyes and the raw, unvarnished accent of her command carried a subjective depth utterly untranslatable into any dominant tongue. The profound grief of a disrupted lineage hung in the silence as she said: “Find me my boy's father.”
