“It fell about the Martinmas, When nights were lang and mirk,
That wife's twa sons cam hame again, And their hats were o' the birk.
It did na graw by bush or brae, Nor yet in ony shough;
But by the gates o' paradise That birk grew fair eneugh.”

—The Wife of Usher’s Well.

It was the evening of February 15th, 1587, and Mrs. Leigh—once more drawn back to the familiar scenes and faces of her past—was walking slowly along the terrace at Burrough, her eyes fixed upon the winding river, the misty sandhills, and the vast expanse of the western sea. She had done this every evening for three long years, come rain or shine, without fail. Three years had passed since Frank and Amyas sailed away aboard the *Rose*, a gallant ship carrying brave souls into the unknown. No word had ever reached them from across the ocean. In Bideford, Appledore, Clovelly, and Ilfracombe, families waited with hollowed eyes and broken hearts, just as they had when John Oxenham vanished into the same uncharted West. Like him, the brothers had disappeared into the infinite, leaving only silence behind.

Once, there had been hope. When news came that Drake, Frobisher, and Carlisle had returned from the Spanish Main, Sir Richard—without telling her—sent a rider galloping to Plymouth. He returned with thrilling tales: the sack of Cartagena, the fall of San Domingo, the relief of Raleigh’s colony in Virginia. But no mention of the *Rose*. No trace of her crew. Mrs. Leigh bowed her head in quiet acceptance and whispered, “The Lord gave, and the Lord hath taken away; blessed be the name of the Lord.”

Her hair had turned silver. Her face was pale, her steps slow. She rarely left home except for church or visits to neighbors. She never spoke the names of her sons. Yet each high tide brought her to the terrace, where she scanned the horizon with desperate longing. The ships still came and went, sails white against the sky, but an instinct deeper than sight told her none of them carried the one she sought. She knew the *Rose*’s shape, her lines, her build—she could have picked her out from a fleet at sea. But she never came. And so, day after day, Mrs. Leigh walked among the poor, who looked upon her with reverence, as one touched by sorrow too profound for words. They saw in her eyes a strength born of fire, a soul that had walked through the furnace and emerged unscathed, standing beside a presence like the Son of God. She herself blamed her own heart for clinging to earthly hopes, confessing each night her weakness, yet always returning to the terrace at high tide beneath the wind-swept trees.

But tonight, Northam was alive.

The pebble ridge thundered below, as it had decades before. Yet the noise came not from the sea, but from the town itself. The bell tower shook with pealing chimes. People poured into the streets, shouting, singing, dancing around bonfires. The pope burned in effigy. Toasts were raised to the queen. “So perish all her enemies!” echoed through the air. Hills glowed red with flames; far off, the bells of Bideford answered those of Northam, just as they had seven years ago when Amyas returned from circumnavigating the globe.

For today, Mary Queen of Scots had been beheaded at Fotheringay. England, as if waking from a nightmare, roared in jubilation. Seventeen years of fear, of plots, of treason—gone at last. The woman who had sown discord, whose blood had stained noble English lives, whose influence had led men to rebellion and murder, was now silenced forever. Elizabeth’s prophecy had come true:

“The daughter of debate,
That discord aye doth sow,
Hath reap’d no gain where former rule
Hath taught still peace to grow.”

And now, she could do no harm. Murder, adultery, a heart that knew no mercy, a tongue that could not speak truth—even for self—had ended. Her face lingered in memory, not as a tyrant, but as a tragic figure, a pitiful dream. “Nothing is left of her now,” some said, “but pure womanly.”

Mrs. Leigh, though a staunch Protestant, offered a prayer: may God have mercy on that soul—“clear as diamond, and as hard,” as she once described herself. She thought of the final moments before the block—could it all have been performance? In seven years, she had learned much. Perhaps Mary Stuart, too, had learned something in seventeen. Mrs. Leigh had been a courtier once, and she knew—though only faintly, as a proper Englishwoman might—the debauchery of French courts, the world of Medicis, Guises, Cardinal Lorraine. She shuddered. “To whom little is given, of them shall little be required.” But still, the bells rang on, relentless.

Then—what was that?

A flash in the twilight, then the thunder of a cannon from the sea.

Mrs. Leigh froze. The flash came from beyond the bar. A ship in distress? Unlikely. The wind was light and westerly. It was a spring tide, high and strong. What could it be? Another flash. Another gun. The crowd fell silent. All rushed to the churchyard overlooking the flats and river.

There, outside the bar, was a large ship—gallant, bold, sails full. Not English rig. A thousand tons, perhaps. But what did it mean? A Spanish cruiser? Not likely. The Don would not risk such reckless action. If he came, he would come with fleet, admirals, transports—by the book. This was different.

Easily, on the rising tide and favorable wind, the vessel slipped up the channel between the sandhills. By now, she was near Appledore. She showed no sign of lowering her topsails—a grave breach of custom for any foreign ship near English shores. For many knew the tale of the Spanish admiral who once sailed into Plymouth Sound without veiling his sails. John Hawkins, port admiral, had risen like a lion from his den, in his famed *Jesus of Lubec*, and fired a shot between the masts. When that failed, he ran alongside and sent a second shot straight through the admiral’s hull. The flag came down. Apologies followed—but not accepted for long. Hawkins defended the queen’s honor with iron resolve.

And if Hawkins could do that in peace, then any old sea-dog in Appledore would do the same in war—with even a pot to burn powder in.

The strange ship vanished behind Appledore hill. Then, from the quiet dusk, came a cheer—like a hundred voices rising together.

Mrs. Leigh stood still. Listened. Then another cannon thundered from the hills. Another cheer.

Twenty minutes passed. Then the ship reappeared around the dark rocks of the Hubbastone, turning up the Bideford river.

On board, music played—drums, fifes, shawms, trumpets—echoing off the woods and slate cliffs. As the ship rounded the bend and came fully into view of Burrough House, another cheer erupted from the crew, rolling across the silver water, a mile away.

Mrs. Leigh moved swiftly toward the house.

“Grace, bring me my hood. Master Amyas is come home!”

“No, surely? O joyful sound! Praised be the Lord!” Grace gasped. “But madam, how did you know?”

“I heard his voice on the river. But I did not hear Mr. Frank’s with him, Grace.”

“Oh, be sure, madam, where one is, the other is. They’d never part. Both come home—or neither. Here’s your hood.”

Mrs. Leigh took it, and with Grace following, hurried toward Bideford.

Was it real? A dream? Had her mother’s intuition truly recognized her son’s voice across the miles, or was her mind breaking under years of grief?

Grace wondered the same as they walked. When they reached the quay, the answer came.

Coming down Bridgeland Street—where later tobacco warehouses would stand, but now only rope-walks and sailmakers’ shops—they saw the ship anchored in the river. Just as they reached the lower end of the street, a great crowd surged around the corner—sailors, women, apprentices, laughing, weeping, shouting.

Mrs. Leigh stopped. And the crowd stopped too.

“Here she is!” someone shouted. “Here’s his mother!”

“His mother? Not *their* mother?” Mrs. Leigh whispered, turning pale. But her heart had long since hardened to pain.

Then, above the throng, rose the giant head and shoulders of Amyas.

“Make way! Make room for Madam Leigh!” he called.

And he fell to his knees at her feet.

She threw her arms around his neck, bending her head over him, while sailors, apprentices, and harbor women fell into hushed silence, forming a ring around mother and son.

She asked no questions. She saw Amyas was alone.

At last, he whispered, “I would have died to save him, mother, if I could.”

“You need not tell me that, Amyas Leigh, my son.”

Silence.

“How did he die?” she murmured.

“He is a martyr. He died in the—”

Amyas could say no more.

“The Inquisition?”

“Yes.”

A shudder ran through Mrs. Leigh. Then she lifted her head.

“Come home, Amyas. I little expected such an honor—such an honor—ha! ha!—and such a fair young martyr, too. A very St. Stephen! God, have mercy on me—and let me not go mad before these people, when I ought to be thanking Thee for Thy mercies! Amyas, who is that?”

She pointed to Ayacanora, standing close behind him, watching everything with sharp eyes.

“She is a poor wild Indian girl—I call her my daughter. I will tell you her story later.”

“Your daughter? Then my granddaughter. Come here, maiden, and be my granddaughter.”

Ayacanora stepped forward, knelt—because she had seen Amyas kneel.

“God forbid, child! Kneel not to me. Come home, and let me know whether I am sane or mazed, alive or dead.”

Drawing her hood over her face, she turned, holding Amyas’s hand in one, Ayacanora’s in the other.

The crowd watched in silence for twenty yards, then burst into cheers that shook the old town.

Mrs. Leigh paused.

“I had forgotten, Amyas. You must not let me stand in the way of your duty. Where are your men?”

“Kissed to death by now—all who are left.”

“Left?”

“We went out a hundred, mother. We came home forty-four—if we are home. Is this a dream? Is this you? Is this Bridgeland Street again? There stands Evans the smith, tankard in hand—just as he did when I was a boy!”

The brawny smith crossed the street, stopped when he saw Amyas, but no Frank.

“Better one than neither, madam,” he said, trying to comfort.

Amyas nodded, shook his hand. Mrs. Leigh saw nothing.

“Mother,” Amyas said, past the causeway, “we are rich for life.”

“Yes,” she replied. “A martyr’s death was the fittest for him.”

“I have brought home treasure untold.”

“What, my boy?”

“Treasure untold. Cary has promised to see to it tonight.”

“Very well. I wish he had slept at our house. He was a kindly lad, and loved Frank. When did he—?”

“Three years ago, and more. Within two months of our sailing.”

“Ah. Yes. He told me so.”

“Told you so?”

“Yes. The dear lad has often come to see me in my sleep. But you never came. I guessed why—as it should be.”

“But I loved you none the less, mother!”

“I know. But you were busy with the men, sweet. Your spirit could not roam home like his, which was free. Yes—just as it should be. My maid—do you not find it cold here, after those hot lands?”

“Ayacanora’s heart is warm. She does not think about cold.”

“Warm? Perhaps you’ll warm my heart, then.”

“Would God I could, mother,” Amyas said, half reproachfully.

Mrs. Leigh looked up at him—then broke into tears.

“Sinful! Sinful that I am!”

“Blessed creature!” Amyas cried. “If you speak so, I shall go mad. Mother, mother—I’ve dreaded this meeting for months. It was a nightmare, a black thundercloud hanging over me. A cliff miles high, hidden in clouds, that I had to climb, and dared not. I longed to leap overboard and flee into the depths of the sea. I feared you might ask—‘Was he not your brother’s keeper?’ Might demand his blood at my hands. And now—now—when it comes—to find you full of love, trust, patience—mother, mother—it’s more than I can bear!” And he wept.

Mrs. Leigh, knowing Amyas’s nature, understood that such emotion meant a deep struggle within. And instantly, she forgot everything else—only wanting to soothe him.

She did. And so the two walked home, arm in arm, Ayacanora clinging to the hem of Mrs. Leigh’s cloak. The fierce independence of the forest girl had melted into timid modesty, suddenly cast into a new world of strangers, fears, and wonders.

“Will your mother love me?” Ayacanora whispered as they entered.

“Yes. But you must do what she tells you.”

Ayacanora pouted.

“She will laugh at me, because I am wild.”

“She never laughs at anyone.”

“Humph!” she muttered. “Well, I won’t be afraid of her. I thought she’d be tall like you—but she’s not even as big as me.”

Not promising obedience, but within twenty-four hours, Mrs. Leigh had won her completely. Ayacanora explained later: “I thought such a great man must have a great mother. I expected a princess with a frown like Juno’s—but found an angel.”

Her story was soon told. Mrs. Leigh, naturally, believed her without question. And the mother pressed no kiss more tender upon her son’s brow than the one she gave in thanks for his vow: that he had kept his soul unspotted—gentleman and Christian alike.

“Then you have forgiven me, mother?”

“Years ago, I said in this very room, ‘What shall I render to the Lord for giving me two such sons?’ And now, I say it again. Tell me all about my other son, that I may honor him as I honor you.”

With quiet strength, she made Amyas recount every detail—Lucy Passmore’s tale, the voyage, the events leading to the fatal night at Guayra. When it was done, she led Ayacanora away and began arranging for her comfort, as calmly as if both boys had merely napped in their cribs.

But barely had she gone upstairs when a loud knock shattered the quiet. The door flew open, and Sir Richard Grenville strode in, ignoring all formality.

Amyas dropped to his knees instinctively. The stern warrior was undone. A tear fell from his iron cheek onto Amyas’s.

“My lad! My glorious lad! Where have you been? Up, and tell me all. The sailors told me a little—but I must hear every word. I knew you’d do something grand. I told your mother—you were too good a workman for God to throw away. Now—tell me the whole. Why, I’m out of breath! To tell truth, I ran three-quarters of the way.”

They sat. Amyas spoke long into the night. Sir Richard, regaining his composure, smiled grimly at each act of courage. When the tale ended, he sighed.

“Would that I had been with you every step. Would that I could show as good a three-year log-book, Amyas, my lad.”

“You can show a better one, I doubt not.”

“Humph. With the exception of one paltry Spanish prize, I don’t know that the queen is the better, nor her enemies the worse, since we parted in Dublin.”

“You are too modest, sir.”

“Would that I were. I got nowhere in Ireland—no better than my neighbors. So I came home to find Raleigh had done a deed with no end. He has found—well, his captains Amadas and Barlow found—between Florida and Newfoundland, a land unlike any on earth. Climate, fertility—unmatched. Gold? I know not. But everything else: furs, timber, rivers, game, sugar-canes, corn, fruit—everything France, Spain, Italy can yield, growing wild. Savages civil enough. A jewel fit for the crown. They call it Wingandacoa. We call it—Virginia.”

“You’ve been there?”

“The year before last. Left Ralf Lane, Amadas, twenty gentlemen, ninety men, and some of my own money, and some of old Will Salterne’s. Never to see it again. The colony quarreled with the Indians—I fear I was too harsh with some who stole—God forgive me!—and couldn’t survive twelve months. Drake, coming back from the West Indies, helped them as best he could, but had to bring them all home. And within two weeks, I was back with three ships, supplies, everything. Found only ruins—already overrun with melons, deer feeding in the huts. A fine sight—but not what I wanted. So I returned, angry, and vented my rage on the Portugals at the Azores. Hard fights, small gain. So things stand—but not for long. Shame it would be if such a paradise, found by Britons, fell to others. We’ll try again this spring—if men and money can be found. Eh, lad?”

“But the prize?”

“Ah—that was no small comfort. Sighted her six days out from America. But dead calm. No boats—lost in a gale. Other ships becalmed two leagues behind. No use pounding her until she sank. So I called the carpenter, gathered old chests, spars—we floated alongside, just in time. The last of us scrambled up as our makeshift ark sank beside us. Could not run away. So I judged valor the better part of discretion—and attacked. Five minutes, and we had her. Fifty thousand pounds’ worth. Restored my purse—and Raleigh’s, though I fear it’s spent again.”

And so ended Sir Richard’s tale.

The next day, Amyas visited Salterne. The old man already knew the outline. But he bid him sit, chin on hand, elbows on knees, listening silently. His face never changed. Only when Amyas mentioned Rose’s marriage did he breathe deeply—like a weight lifted.

“Say that again, sir.”

Amyas repeated it, then hesitated, hinting at her death.

“Go on. Why are you afraid? Nothing to be ashamed of?”

He told the whole, eyes downcast. Then glanced up. No emotion—only a proud smile at the corners of Salterne’s mouth.

“And her husband?” he asked.

“I am ashamed to tell you—he still lives.”

“Still lives?”

“Too true. It was not my fault. My story bears witness.”

“Sir, I never doubted your will to kill him. Still lives? Well, so do rats and adders. And now, Captain Leigh—your worship is minded to recruit on shore with the fair lass you’ve brought home, before another dash at the devil?”

“Do not mention her name with mine, sir. She is no more to me than to you. Spanish blood flows in her veins.”

Salterne smiled grimly.

“But I am minded to do one thing, Mr. Salterne. I will kill Spaniards—fair fight, by land and sea—wheresoever I meet them. And so, I stay not long here. Whithersoever I go next, I go.”
