That afternoon Margaret went quickly through the smoky streets, as if speed could outstrip sorrow. The air itself seemed altered—thicker with the mill-breath that hung over Milton, and threaded with the hard, metallic beat of distant machinery. Even in the narrow courts the sound traveled: a ceaseless churning like an ill-tuned organ grinding out one note until the mind grew raw. Yet between one clatter and the next there were small human noises—children’s bare feet on stone, a kettle beginning to sing somewhere unseen—that made the grief she was going to meet feel at once homely and immense.

Mary Higgins was watching for her, half distrust in her face, as if kindness were another thing that could fail. Margaret gave her a steady smile, and Mary, reassured without words, drew her in. They passed through the house-place—dark with years of coal smoke and close living—and climbed the crooked stair. Above, the room had the hush that comes when the ordinary sounds of a dwelling have been stopped, not by command, but by awe.

Bessy lay there.

Margaret had seen pain carve its sharp lines into that young face often enough; she had heard the short breath, the fretful startings, the bitter, weary talk that had come and gone like wind across a field. Now the muscles were unknit. The lips had settled into a faint softness, as if the last thing she had tasted was not bitterness but sleep. Margaret’s eyes filled slowly; she did not sob. A deep stillness entered her—so calm it frightened her a little, because it seemed too like consent.

So this was death. Peaceful—more peaceful than life had ever been to Bessy. Scripture rose unbidden, in phrases that came with their own quiet music: They rest from their labours. The weary are at rest. He giveth his beloved sleep.

She turned away at last, slowly, as if turning a page that could not be read again. In the background Mary sobbed, low and humble, as if she begged pardon even for grief. Without speaking, they went down.

In the room below, Nicholas Higgins stood with one hand on the table, planted in the middle of the floor as if bracing himself against a blow. His great eyes were open wide—startled, fierce, dry. The news had met him in the court, flung from mouth to mouth; it had run ahead of him with a cruel speed, and now he was trying to force his mind to accept what his heart still refused. Bessy had been sick so long that he had let himself believe she would never quite die; that she would “pull through,” as if the body could be reasoned with and the grave delayed by sheer obstinacy.

Margaret felt suddenly like an intruder. This was his sorrow’s house, his child, his loss; and she had already stood in the “quiet presence” that he, the father, had not yet faced. On the stair, when she first caught sight of him, she had paused. Now she tried to slip past him, to leave him within the solemn circle of his own misery.

Mary sank into the first chair she found and pulled her apron over her head, and her crying grew louder for the cloth that muffled it.

The sound roused him as if it were an insult. He caught Margaret’s arm in a grip that hurt, not from malice but from a man’s bewildered strength.

“Were yo’ with her? Did yo’ see her die?”

“No,” Margaret answered, perfectly still. She did not flinch. He held her as though by holding her he could hold the fact at bay.

There was a long pause. His throat worked; when his words came they were thick and hoarse.

“All men must die,” he said at last, with a strange gravity that made Margaret think—only for an instant—that he had been drinking: not enough to topple his steps, but enough to turn thought murky and uneven. “But she were younger than me.”

He stared past Margaret, studying the event as if it were something laid on the table to be judged true or false. Then he snapped his eyes upon her with wild inquiry.

“Yo’re sure and certain she’s dead—not in a dwam, a faint? she’s been so before, often.”

“She is dead,” Margaret said.

He searched her face, and the fierce light in his eyes seemed to gutter as if a lamp had been starved of oil. Then, all at once, he let go of her arm, flung himself half across the table, and sobbed with a violence that shook not only his body but the furniture around him, as if wood and iron ought to answer for what had been done.

Mary rose, trembling, and went to him.

“Get thee gone!—get thee gone!” he cried, striking at her blindly. “What do I care for thee?”

Margaret caught Mary’s hand and held it—soft, steady, as one might hold a frightened creature that does not know where to put itself. Nicholas tore at his hair, beat his head against the hard wood, then sagged into a stunned, stupid exhaustion.

They did not move. Mary trembled from head to foot.

After what might have been minutes or an hour—grief mangles time—he lifted himself up. His eyes were swollen and bloodshot. For a moment he looked as if he had forgotten there was anyone in the room at all; then he saw them and scowled, resentful of witnesses. He shook himself heavily, cast them one sullen glance, said nothing, and made for the door.

“Oh, father, father!” Mary clung to his arm. “Not to-night! Any night but to-night. Oh, help me! he’s going out to drink again! Father, I’ll not leave yo’. Yo’ may strike, but I’ll not leave yo’. She told me last of all to keep yo’ fro’ drink!”

Margaret stepped into the doorway. She did not speak. She only stood—silent, unmoving, with her serious eyes fixed upon him. Nicholas shook Mary off so violently she fell against a chair and her face struck it; blood showed at her lip. He turned on Margaret as if he would strike her too.

“It’s my own house. Stand out o’ the way, wench, or I’ll make yo’!”

Yet he did not move her. Something in her stillness held him, as if he had run against an invisible wall. He stared, gloomy and defiant; but the longer he stared the more his defiance slipped, replaced by a reluctant awe.

“What are yo’ looking at me in that way for?” he demanded at last, half cowed and angry at being cowed. “If yo’ think for to keep me from going what gait I choose, because she loved yo’—and in my own house, too, where I never asked yo’ to come, yo’re mista’en. It’s very hard upon a man that he can’t go to the only comfort left.”

Margaret knew, by the very bitterness of his complaint, that he had already granted her power. She felt the weight of it. He sat down in a chair near the door, half conquered, half resentful—ready to go out the moment she yielded place, yet unwilling to use the violence he had threatened.

She laid her hand on his arm.

“Come with me,” she said. “Come and see her.”

Her voice was low and solemn. It held neither fear of him nor doubt of his obedience, only a quiet insistence, as if she spoke to the best part in him and expected it to answer.

He rose sullenly. He stood with dogged irresolution on his face, lingering as if delay could make the next moment less true. Margaret waited—patiently, without pressing. He took a grim satisfaction in making her wait. At last he moved toward the stairs.

They stood by the bed.

“Her last words to Mary were, ‘Keep my father fro’ drink.’”

“It canna hurt her now,” he muttered. “Nought can hurt her now.” Then his voice broke into a wailing cry that came out of him like a torn thing. “We may quarrel and fall out—we may make peace and be friends—we may clem to skin and bone—and nought o’ all our griefs will ever touch her more. Hoo’s had her portion on ’em. What wi’ hard work first, and sickness at last, hoo’ led the life of a dog. And to die without knowing one good piece o’ rejoicing in all her days! Nay, wench, whatever hoo said, hoo can know nought about it now, and I mun ha’ a sup o’ drink just to steady me again sorrow.”

“No,” said Margaret, and because his voice had softened, hers softened too. “You shall not. If her life has been what you say, at any rate she did not fear death as some do. You should have heard her speak of the life to come—the life hidden with God, that she is now gone to.”

He shook his head, glancing sideways at her—wary, as if he expected a trap even in comfort. His pale, haggard face struck her painfully; it had the drained look of a man who has lived too long in noise—noise of looms, noise of argument, noise of hunger—and cannot find the quiet place inside his own skull.

“You are sorely tried,” she said. “Where have you been all day—not at work?”

“Not at work, sure enough,” he answered with a short grim laugh. “Not at what you call work. I were at the Committee, till I were sickened out wi’ trying to make fools hear reason. I were fetched to Boucher’s wife afore seven this morning. She’s bed-fast, but she were raving and raging to know where her dunner-headed brute of a chap was, as if I’d to keep him—as if he were fit to be ruled by me. The d—d fool, who has put his foot in all our plans! And I’ve walked my feet sore wi’ going about for to see men who wouldn’t be seen, now the law is raised against us.” He swallowed, as if something in his throat resisted. “And I were sore-hearted, too, which is worse than sore-footed; and if I did see a friend who ossed to treat me, I never knew hoo lay a-dying here. Bess, lass, thou’d believe me, thou wouldst—wouldstn’t thou?” He turned the wild appeal upon the poor still form, as if she might contradict the world’s accusation.

“I am sure,” Margaret said, “I am sure you did not know; it was quite sudden. But now you do know; you do see her lying there; you hear what she said with her last breath. You will not go?”

He did not answer. Where was he to turn for comfort, now the ordinary place of it was a covered face?

At last Margaret, daring more than she had meant to dare, said, “Come home with me. At least you shall have some comfortable food, which I’m sure you need.”

It was the plainness of the offer—the domestic, bodily mercy of it—that gave it force. A man might argue against doctrine; it was harder to argue against a warm drink, a slice of bread, the human decency of being fed when grief had emptied him.

He looked at her sharply, his thoughts turning aside with the suddenness of a wind-shift.

“Yo’r father’s a parson?” he asked.

“He was,” Margaret replied, briefly.

“I’ll go and take a dish o’ tea with him, since yo’ve asked me,” Nicholas said. “I’ve many a thing I often wished to say to a parson, and I’m not particular as to whether he’s preaching now, or not.”

Margaret’s heart gave a quick, uneasy leap. Tea with her father—who would be wholly unprepared; and her mother so ill—seemed impossible. Yet if she drew back now, she could see the gin-shop’s door opening in her mind as plainly as if it stood before her. If she could only get him into her own house, it would be a great step gained; after that, she must trust to whatever providence governed small events.

Nicholas bent over Bessy one last time.

“Good-bye, ou’d wench! We’ve parted company at last, we have! But thou’st been a blessin’ to thy father ever sin’ thou wert born. Bless thy white lips, lass,—they’ve a smile on ’em now! and I’m glad to see it once again, though I’m lone and forlorn for evermore.”

He kissed her fondly, covered her face, and turned to follow Margaret.

Margaret hurried downstairs to tell Mary what she meant to do—to say it was the only place she could think of to keep him from the gin-palace; to urge her to come too, for her conscience smote her at the thought of leaving the poor affectionate girl alone. But Mary said the neighbours would come in and sit with her; it was all right; only father—

Nicholas was already there, and whatever more Mary might have pleaded was cut off. He had snapped his emotion shut again, ashamed of it, and leapt past himself into a bitter mirth that sounded like dry sticks crackling in a fire.

“I’m going to take my tea wi’ her father, I am.”

He slouched his cap low over his brow as he went out, looking neither right nor left. He feared the sympathy in neighbours’ faces more than their words; pity might tip him over again. The street was full of Milton’s clamor—carts, shouts, the far iron cadence of the mills—and through it he and Margaret walked in silence, two figures moving against a tide of sound.

As they neared the street where he knew she lived, he glanced down at his clothes, his hands, his shoes, as if he suddenly felt the whole weight of being seen.

“I should m’appen ha’ cleaned mysel’, first.”

“It will be easy,” Margaret said quickly. “You shall go into the yard, and have soap and towel. Come.”

She would not let him slip from her hands.

While the servant led him along the passage and through the kitchen, he stepped cautiously on every dark mark in the oil-cloth’s pattern, trying to place his clogs so as to hide the trace of dirt—an awkward, touching care, as if cleanliness might buy him some right to sit in peace. Margaret ran upstairs. Dixon met her on the landing.

“How is mamma?—where is papa?”

Mrs. Hale was tired and had gone into her own room. She had wanted to go to bed, but Dixon had persuaded her to lie on the sofa and have tea there; it was better than lying wakeful and restless.

So far, so good. Mr. Hale was in the drawing-room.

Margaret went in almost breathless and told him what had happened—imperfectly, hurriedly, because it was a story that seemed to breed new trouble the more clearly it was spoken. Her father looked taken aback at the notion of Nicholas Higgins waiting in his quiet study, expected to drink tea, brought in as a last barrier between a grieving man and the gin-shop. Mr. Hale was kind and gentle; he would have gone readily to comfort any sufferer. But Margaret, in her anxiety, pressed most on Higgins having been drinking, and on her having fetched him home as the only expedient left. One small event had led to another so naturally she had hardly known what she was doing until she saw the slight recoil—no more than a shadow—on her father’s face.

“Oh, papa! he really is a man you will not dislike—if you won’t be shocked to begin with.”

“But, Margaret, to bring a drunken man home—and your mother so ill!”

Margaret’s face fell. “I am sorry, papa. He is very quiet—he is not tipsy at all. He was only rather strange at first, but that might be the shock of poor Bessy’s death.”

Her eyes filled. Mr. Hale took her pleading face between his hands and kissed her forehead.

“It is all right, dear. I’ll go and make him as comfortable as I can. You attend to your mother. Only, if you can come in and make a third in the study, I shall be glad.”

“Oh, yes—thank you.”

As he was leaving she ran after him, a new fear catching her.

“Papa—you must not wonder at what he says: he’s an— I mean he does not believe much in what we do.”

“Oh dear! a drunken infidel weaver!” Mr. Hale thought, dismayed; but aloud he only said, “If your mother goes to sleep, be sure you come directly.”

Margaret went into her mother’s room. Mrs. Hale lifted herself from a doze, her face worn with illness and that particular fretfulness that comes when the body is weak and the mind has too much time to brood.

“When did you write to Frederick, Margaret? Yesterday, or the day before?”

“Yesterday, mamma.”

“And the letter went?”

“Yes. I took it myself.”

Mrs. Hale’s eyes widened with fear that was almost childish in its helplessness. “Oh, Margaret, I’m so afraid of his coming! If he should be recognized! If he should be taken! If he should be executed, after all these years that he has kept away and lived in safety! I keep falling asleep and dreaming that he is caught and being tried.”

“Oh, mamma, don’t be afraid. There will be some risk, no doubt; but we will lessen it as much as we can. And it is so little! If we were at Helstone, there would be twenty—a hundred times as much. There everybody would remember him; and if there was a stranger known to be in the house, they would be sure to guess it was Frederick. Here nobody knows or cares for us enough to notice what we do. Dixon will keep the door like a dragon—won’t you, Dixon—while he is here?”

“They’ll be clever if they come in past me!” Dixon said, showing her teeth at the idea, fierce as any watchdog.

“And he need not go out, except in the dusk,” Margaret added.

“Poor fellow!” Mrs. Hale echoed. Then, wavering, “But I almost wish you had not written. Would it be too late to stop him if you wrote again, Margaret?”

“I’m afraid it would, mamma,” Margaret said, remembering her own urgent words—come directly, if you wish to see your mother alive.

“I always dislike that doing things in such a hurry,” Mrs. Hale complained, as if the world could be regulated by preference.

Margaret said nothing.

“Come now, ma’am,” Dixon put in, with that brisk cheerfulness she used like a medicine, “you know seeing Master Frederick is just the very thing of all others you’re longing for. And I’m glad Miss Margaret wrote off straight, without shilly-shallying. I’ve had a great mind to do it myself. And we’ll keep him snug, depend upon it. There’s only Martha in the house that would not do a good deal to save him on a pinch; and I’ve been thinking she might go and see her mother just at that very time. She’s been saying once or twice she should like to go, for her mother has had a stroke since she came here; only she didn’t like to ask. But I’ll see about her being safe off, as soon as we know when he comes, God bless him! So take your tea, ma’am, in comfort, and trust to me.”

Mrs. Hale trusted Dixon more than she trusted Margaret. Dixon’s certainty quieted her, as a steady hand quiets a trembling cup. Margaret poured out the tea in silence. The tray made small, comforting sounds—spoon against china, the soft pour and pause—and the smell of the tea rose warm and tannic, threading through the sickroom air. Yet Margaret’s mind would not follow it into comfort. The more she tried to think of anything else, the more the image of Frederick—recognized, seized, tried—returned, sticking like burrs to cloth. Her mother prattled with Dixon and seemed to have forgotten the dangers she had thrown out a moment before; she was one of those who could spark terror like a rocket, then let it fall away, while the person it landed upon smouldered.

Margaret was glad when she could go down into the study. She wondered how her father and Higgins had fared.

She found, to her relief, that the decorous, kind-hearted, simple old-fashioned gentleman had, without knowing it, called up the best in the other. Mr. Hale treated all fellow-creatures alike; rank did not come into his mind as a reason for manners. He had placed a chair for Nicholas, stood until at his request the weaver sat, and had called him “Mr. Higgins” with steady courtesy—never “Nicholas,” never “Higgins” in the curt way to which the man was used.

And Nicholas was not an habitual drunkard, nor a thorough infidel. He drank to drown care, as he would have said; and if he had no faith it was because he had not yet found any form of it he could clasp with his whole heart.

Margaret was surprised—and pleased—at the sight before her. Higgins, clean and tidied at least by water and effort, sat composedly, speaking with earnestness. His hair was slicked down damp; his neck-handkerchief sat more evenly; he had borrowed an odd candle-end and polished his clogs till they shone in patches. Even his voice was lowered, as if the quiet of the room demanded a quieter human sound. Mr. Hale listened attentively, interested in spite of disagreement.

Mr. Hale looked up as Margaret entered, smiled, and offered her his chair; then, with a small bow to his guest as apology for the interruption, sat down again quickly. Higgins nodded to her. She arranged her work at the table with a soft rustle and prepared to listen.

“As I was a-saying, sir,” Nicholas went on, “I reckon yo’d not ha’ much belief in yo’ if yo’ lived here,—if yo’d been bred here. I ax your pardon if I use wrong words; but what I mean by belief just now, is a-thinking on sayings and maxims and promises made by folk yo’ never saw, about the things and the life yo’ never saw, nor no one else. Now, yo’ say these are true things, and true sayings, and a true life. I just say, where’s the proof?

“There’s many and many a one wiser, and scores better learned than I am around me,—folk who’ve had time to think on these things,—while my time has had to be gi’en up to getting my bread. Well, I sees these people. Their lives is pretty much open to me. They’re real folk. They don’t believe i’ the Bible,—not they. They may say they do, for form’s sake; but Lord, sir, d’ye think their first cry i’ th’ morning is, ‘What shall I do to get hold on eternal life?’ or ‘What shall I do to fill my purse this blessed day? Where shall I go? What bargains shall I strike?’ The purse and the gold and the notes is real things; things as can be felt and touched; them’s realities; and eternal life is all a talk, very fit for— I ax your pardon, sir; yo’r a parson out o’ work, I believe. Well! I’ll never speak disrespectful of a man in the same fix as I’m in mysel.

“But I’ll just ax yo’ another question, sir, and I dunnot want yo’ to answer it, only to put in yo’r pipe, and smoke it, afore yo’ go for to set down us, who only believe in what we see, as fools and noddies. If salvation, and life to come, and what not, was true—not in men’s words, but in men’s hearts’ core—dun yo’ not think they’d din us wi’ it as they do wi’ political ’conomy? They’re mighty anxious to come round us wi’ that piece o’ wisdom; but t’other would be a greater convarsion, if it were true.”

“But the masters have nothing to do with your religion,” Mr. Hale said gently. “All that they are connected with you in is trade,—so they think,—and all that it concerns them, therefore, to rectify your opinions in is the science of trade.”

“I’m glad, sir,” Higgins returned, a curious gleam in his eye, “that yo’ put in, ‘so they think.’ I’d ha’ thought yo’ a hyprocrite, I’m afeard, if yo’ hadn’t, for all yo’r a parson, or rayther because yo’r a parson. Yo’ see, if yo’d spoken o’ religion as a thing that, if it was true, it didn’t concern all men to press on all men’s attention, above everything else in this ’varsal earth, I should ha’ thought yo’ a knave for to be a parson; and I’d rather think yo’ a fool than a knave. No offence, I hope, sir.”

“None at all,” Mr. Hale said. “You consider me mistaken, and I consider you far more fatally mistaken. I don’t expect to convince you in a day,—not in one conversation; but let us know each other, and speak freely to each other about these things, and the truth will prevail. I should not believe in God if I did not believe that.” His voice dropped, reverent without being theatrical. “Mr. Higgins, I trust, whatever else you have given up, you believe—you believe in Him.”

Nicholas sprang to his feet so suddenly that Margaret started up too, thinking for an instant some fit had seized him; his face worked as if struck from within. Mr. Hale looked at Margaret, dismayed.

At last Higgins found words, broken and fierce with feeling.

“Man! I could fell yo’ to the ground for tempting me. Whatten business have yo’ to try me wi’ your doubts? Think o’ her lying theere, after the life hoo’s led; and think then how yo’d deny me the one sole comfort left—that there is a God, and that He set her her life.”

He sat down heavily, and stared drearily toward the fire, as if it were the only steady thing in the room.

“I dunnot believe she’ll ever live again,” he went on. “I dunnot believe in any other life than this, in which she dreed such trouble, and had such never-ending care; and I cannot bear to think it were all a set o’ chances, that might ha’ been altered wi’ a breath o’ wind. There’s many a time when I’ve thought I didna believe in God, but I’ve never put it fair out before me in words, as many men do. I may ha’ laughed at those who did, to brave it out like—but I have looked round at after, to see if He heard me, if so be there was a He; but to-day, when I’m left desolate, I wunnot listen to yo’ wi’ yo’r questions, and yo’r doubts. There’s but one thing steady and quiet i’ all this reeling world, and, reason or no reason, I’ll cling to that. It’s a’ very well for happy folk—”

Margaret touched his arm, very softly, as if to lower the volume of the room’s pain—like laying a hand on a vibrating string to stop its harshness. She had not spoken before.

“Nicholas,” she said, “we do not want to reason; you misunderstand my father. We do not reason—we believe; and so do you. It is the one sole comfort in such times.”

He turned and caught her hand, clinging as if her words were something he could hold.

“Ay! it is, it is,” he said, brushing the tears away with the back of his hand. “But yo’ know, she’s lying dead at home; and I’m welly dazed wi’ sorrow, and at times I hardly know what I’m saying. It’s as if speeches folk ha’ made—clever and smart things as I’ve thought at the time—come up now my heart’s welly brossen.” His voice sank. “Th’ strike’s failed as well; dun yo’ know that, miss? I were coming whoam to ask her, like a beggar as I am, for a bit o’ comfort i’ that trouble; and I were knocked down by one who telled me she were dead—just dead. That were all; but that were enough for me.”
