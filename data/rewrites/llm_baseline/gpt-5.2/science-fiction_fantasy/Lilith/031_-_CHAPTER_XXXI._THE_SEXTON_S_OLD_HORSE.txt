I stood where I was and watched the last pallor of the white leopardess dissolve into the night, as if the air had swallowed her. Then I turned—reluctantly—to follow my guide.

What had I to do with sleep? In any world, I told myself, reason must keep one shape: and what reason could there be in lying down with the dead, when the hour was crying out for a living man? No one would wake me; and in that house the sleepers let morning slide into noon, and noon into night, without so much as the rustle of a sheet. I muttered against it as a child mutters at being sent to bed, and yet I followed—because I knew not what else to do.

The librarian went on in silence, and I went silent as he. Time and space, like a slow-poured broth, slipped past without edge or measure. The sun sank; the light thinned; the cold began to spread—an unseasoned chill, the kind that makes the mouth taste of tin and the skin remember the touch of stone. My heart sank with it. I began to lose sight of the lean figure in the long coat, and at last I could no longer hear even his swishing stride through the heather.

But then the soundscape altered, as if the world had moved its ear nearer. I heard, instead, the slow flap of raven-wings; and at intervals, a different small noise would rise into the rayless air—the dry flick of a firefly’s wing, the papery whisper of a gleaming butterfly that had no business in such dark. By and by the moon appeared, dragging herself up the far horizon like a pale loaf lifting in a cold oven.

“You are tired, are you not, Mr. Vane?” said the raven, alighting on a stone. His voice in that deadened night was not merely a word but a note—an insistence in the air, as if the world were made partly of hearing. “You must make acquaintance with the horse that will carry you in the morning!”

He gave a strange whistle through his long black beak—thin, sharp, and clean as a knife struck on a glass rim. A spot appeared on the face of the half-risen moon.

Then to my ears came the drumming of swift, soft-galloping hoofs: not loud at first, but certain, like a steady pestle in a deep mortar. In a minute or two, out of the very disc of the moon, low-thundered the terrible horse.

His mane flowed behind him like the crest of a wave fighting wind, torn seaward in hoary spray; his tail whisked so it seemed to blind the moon itself. Nineteen hands he looked—huge of bone, tight of skin, hard of muscle—a steed the holy Death might choose to ride abroad and reap. In that scary light he had the look of a skeleton loosely roped together; yet he moved with the lightness of a winged insect. As he drew near, his speed slackened, and his mane and tail—like steam over a hot cauldron—settled about him.

Now I was not merely a lover of horses: I loved every horse I saw. I had never spent money except upon horses, and had never sold a horse. The sight of this mighty one, terrible to look upon, woke in me a longing to possess him—pure greed, rank covetousness, an evil thing in all worlds. Not that I could have stolen him; but regardless of his place, I would have bought him if I could.

I laid my hands on him. Under my palms the hide was thin and smooth and shining as satin; the protuberant bones rose like the ridges of a starved hill-country under a stretched skin. The moon’s very shape lay reflected in his coat as in black-polished stone. I stroked those sharp-pointed ears as a careful cook strokes the edge of a blade he trusts; I whispered into them—words meant to soothe, the old stable-talk that belongs to a dialect of hands and breath. Then I bent and breathed into his red nostrils the warm, salted air of a man’s life.

He answered. He breathed into mine the breath of a horse’s life—grass-sweet, iron-warm, and edged with the clean pungency of work. In that exchange our liking leapt to love, quick as fire takes to fat. What eyes he had: blue-filmy like the eyes of the dead, and behind each film a coal glowed.

The raven, wings half spread, watched with what looked like pleasure.

“That is well! be friends with him,” he said. “He will carry you all the better to-morrow!—Now we must hurry home!”

My desire to ride the horse grew sudden and passionate, like hunger that flares when the first smell of roasting reaches the door.

“May I not mount him at once, Mr. Raven?” I cried.

“By all means!” he answered. “Mount, and ride him home.”

The horse bent his head over my shoulder lovingly. I twisted my hands in his mane and scrambled onto his back—not without aid from certain jutting bones that served me as steps.

“He would outspeed any leopard in creation!” I cried.

“Not that way at night,” answered the raven; “the road is difficult.—But come; loss now will be gain then! To wait is harder than to run, and its meed is the fuller. Go on, my son—straight to the cottage. I shall be there as soon as you. It will rejoice my wife’s heart to see son of hers on that horse!”

I sat silent. The horse stood like a block of marble, patient as an old beast in harness, yet with a storm under his skin.

“Why do you linger?” asked the raven.

“I long so much to ride after the leopardess,” I answered, “that I can scarce restrain myself!”

“You have promised!”

“My debt to the Little Ones appears, I confess, a greater thing than my bond to you.”

“Yield to the temptation and you will bring mischief upon them—and on yourself also.”

“What matters it for me? I love them; and love works no evil. I will go.”

But the truth—bitter on my tongue—was that I forgot the children, infatuate with the horse. The animal’s power rose into me like strong drink, and I took it for my own strength.

Eyes flashed through the darkness, and I knew that Adam stood in his own shape beside me. I knew also, by his voice, that he pressed down an indignation almost too strong for him, as one presses a boiling pot-lid to keep it from singing.

“Mr. Vane,” he said, “do you not know why you have not yet done anything worth doing?”

“Because I have been a fool,” I answered.

“Wherein?”

“In everything.”

“Which do you count your most indiscreet action?”

“Bringing the princess to life: I ought to have left her to her just fate.”

“Nay, now you talk foolishly! You could not have done otherwise than you did, not knowing she was evil!—But you never brought any one to life! How could you, yourself dead?”

“I dead?” I cried.

“Yes,” he answered; “and you will be dead, so long as you refuse to die.”

“Back to the old riddling!” I returned, scorn thick in my mouth, as if I had bitten burnt crust.

“Be persuaded, and go home with me,” he continued gently. “The most—nearly the only foolish thing you ever did, was to run from our dead.”

I pressed the horse’s ribs, and he was off like a sudden wind.

I gave him a pat on the side of the neck, and he went about in a sharp-driven curve, close to the ground like a cat that wheels scratchingly after a mouse, leaning so far his mane swept the heather-tops and combed them. Through the dark I heard the wings of the raven: five quick flaps—and he perched upon the horse’s head.

The horse checked himself instantly, ploughing up the ground with his feet; the sound tore the night like cloth.

“Mr. Vane,” croaked the raven, “think what you are doing! Twice already has evil befallen you—once from fear, and once from heedlessness: breach of word is far worse; it is a crime.”

“The Little Ones are in frightful peril, and I brought it upon them!” I cried. “—But indeed I will not break my word to you. I will return, and spend in your house what nights—what days—what years you please.”

“I tell you once more you will do them other than good if you go to-night,” he insisted.

But a false sense of power—rootless, merely vibrated into me from the strength of the horse—had rendered me too stupid to take in the shape of his counsel. The horse’s great body under me was like a drum; it beat arrogance into my bones.

“Would you take from me my last chance of reparation?” I cried. “This time there shall be no shirking! It is my duty, and I will go—if I perish for it!”

“Go, then, foolish boy!” he returned, anger rasping in his croak. “Take the horse, and ride to failure! May it be to humility!”

He spread his wings and flew, and the air, suddenly without him, seemed emptier, as if some needed frequency had dropped out of the world’s music.

Again I pressed the lean ribs under me.

“After the spotted leopardess!” I whispered in the horse’s ear.

He turned his head this way and that, snuffing the air; then started, and went a few paces in a slow, undecided walk—like an old beast choosing between familiar paths. Suddenly he quickened; broke into a trot; began to gallop; and in a few moments his speed was tremendous.

He seemed to see in the dark. He never stumbled—not once faltered, not once hesitated. I sat as on the ridge of a wave, lifted and driven. Beneath me I felt the play of each individual muscle: his joints were so elastic, and each movement flowed into the next, that not once did he jar me. His growing swiftness bore him along until he flew rather than ran. The wind met and passed us like a tornado, stripping sound from the ground and leaving only the hammering of hoofs and the rush of air in the nostrils.

Across the evil hollow we sped like a bolt from an arblast. No monster lifted its neck; all knew the hoofs that thundered over their heads, and the night held its breath. We rushed up the hills, we shot down their farther slopes; from the rocky chasms of the river-bed he did not swerve; he held on over them his fierce, terrible gallop.

The moon, half-way up the heaven, gazed with a solemn trouble in her pale countenance. Rejoicing in the power of my steed and in the pride of my life, I sat like a king and rode.

We were near the middle of the many channels, my horse every other moment clearing one, sometimes two in his stride, and now and then gathering himself for a great bounding leap, when the moon reached the key-stone of her arch.

Then came a wonder and a terror: she began to descend, rolling like the nave of Fortune’s wheel bowled by the gods, and went faster and faster. Like our own moon, this one had a human face, and now the broad forehead, now the chin, was uppermost as she rolled. I gazed aghast.

Across the ravines came the howling of wolves. The sound ran along the ground like a knife along a plate, setting my teeth on edge. An ugly fear began to invade the hollow places of my heart; my confidence thinned, as a broth thins when water is poured where marrow should be. The horse maintained his headlong swiftness, ears pricked forward, nostrils thirsty and exulting in the wind his own career made.

But there was the moon jolting like an old chariot-wheel down the hill of heaven, with awful boding in every turn. At last she rolled over the horizon-edge and disappeared, carrying all her light with her.

The mighty steed was in the act of clearing a wide shallow channel when we were caught in the net of the darkness. His head dropped as if the string that held him up had been cut. The impetus carried his helpless bulk across, but he fell in a heap on the margin; and where he fell he lay.

I slid down and got up shaking. I kneeled beside him and felt him all over, hands travelling his limbs and ribs with the careful urgency of one who searches a stored pantry after a quake—touching, counting, hoping to find nothing spilt. Not a bone could I find broken.

But he was a horse no more.

I sat down on the body, and buried my face in my hands, while the night—without moon, without raven—closed in, and all that could be heard was the far, hungry argument of wolves and the loud silence of my own folly.
