My Lord Castlewood’s residence in Kensington Square stood as a spacious yet seldom-visited retreat for his noble family, conveniently near the palace when the king chose to lodge there. Her ladyship hosted evenings for those willing to brave the journey—though a century ago, Kensington was a perilous distance from London. George Selwyn once jested that he feared the trip, not knowing whether to dread highwaymen or ladies lying in wait over the card tables.

On the morning after Harry Warrington’s reappearance at White’s, the family gathered for breakfast, including Mr. Will, who was due at court that afternoon. The ladies arrived first, sipping chocolate; Will joined them in his court finery; and finally, my lord descended, languid in his bedgown, his wig yet unworn.

Will brought news from the Star and Garter—tales heard at White’s and sightings at Ranelagh.

“Heard what? Seen what?” asked Lord Castlewood, glancing up from his *Daily Advertiser*.

“Ask Maria,” said Lady Fanny. My lord turned to his elder sister, her face pale as linen, her expression grave.

“Another of Will’s elegant fabrications,” Maria declared.

“No,” swore Will. “Tom Claypool saw them at Ranelagh. Jack Morris heard it from Harry’s own lips at White’s. The Fortunate Youth is a second son—his brother lives!”

“The elder not dead?” my lord murmured.

“No more than you are. A fine trick, if you ask me.”

“Mr. Warrington is incapable of deceit!” Maria cried.

“I never encouraged the boy,” said Lady Castlewood. “Nor did Fanny. Not we, indeed!”

“Not we, indeed!” echoed Fanny.

“The fellow’s a beggar now,” Will sneered. “I’ll wager he hasn’t paid for the coat on his back. Good riddance—I hate him!”

“You’ve held a grudge since he blacked your eye,” my lord remarked dryly. “So the lad’s found his brother and lost his fortune.” His gaze lingered on Maria, and something in her woeful expression struck him as absurd. He burst into laughter.

“A shame!” Maria sobbed, hiding her face.

Lady Castlewood and Fanny exchanged glances. “We do not always follow your humor, my lord,” the former said stiffly.

“Nor need you,” he replied coolly. “Maria, forgive me if I’ve wounded you.”

“Wounded me? You stripped him in prosperity and mock him in ruin!” She rose, glaring at them all before sweeping from the room.

Before the quarrel could deepen, a phaeton arrived—Harry’s once-extravagant purchase, now shared with his brother. They entered to find only Lady Castlewood and Fanny, the others absent—Maria, no doubt, composing herself.

“We’ve heard of your astonishing news,” Lady Castlewood said warmly to Harry. “My lord means to call on you today. And, dear cousin, poverty shan’t lessen our regard.”

“Now we may prove we never loved you for your acres!” Fanny added brightly.

George bowed. “And I, who inherit them?”

Fanny laughed. “We shall love you for resembling Harry!”

The ladies’ ease in shifting their attentions was masterful. They spoke kindly to George but lavished Harry with fond reproaches—why had he stayed away?

“Tea and piquet with us would have been safer than with others,” Lady Castlewood chided.

“Now you’ve no money to lose, you may play with us!” Fanny teased.

George watched, perplexed. This warmth toward the now-penniless Harry defied expectation. Perhaps these people were not so black-hearted as rumor painted.

Lord Castlewood soon joined them, impeccably dressed. “Had you come sooner,” he mused, “that unfortunate piquet evening might never have occurred. A younger son ought to be more prudent.”

“Or a kinsman more merciful,” Harry said ruefully.

“Play is in our blood,” my lord sighed. “I’ve squandered as much as any. Your banknotes left me within a day.”

“I play, but never to ruin,” George remarked.

“When we heard my lord had played with Harry, we were furious,” the ladies exclaimed.

“Better friends won it than strangers,” my lord said.

George laughed. “A loss is a loss, even to a brother. Harry must bear his debts like any man.”

“Well said!” my lord clapped Harry’s shoulder. The ladies smiled.

“Your sister raised true gentlemen,” Lady Castlewood declared.

Fanny sighed. “You grow too amiable in poverty, Harry! George, you cannot match him.”

“I fear I am no Fortunate Youth,” George replied gravely.

Upstairs, a harpsichord played. Harry flushed; the ladies smiled.

“Maria is above,” said Lady Castlewood.

Harry followed eagerly, but George was detained by my lord. “Stay—I long to hear of your captivity.”

Once alone, Lord Castlewood grew serious. “Has Harry told you all?”

“Including his engagement to Lady Maria,” George said. “He’s had ill luck here.”

“None regrets it more than I. My brother consorts with jockeys and rakes. Poverty forces us together.” He sighed. “Pride is my curse. I cannot sell my name.”

George listened, masking skepticism.

“We all have faults,” he said. “Play is common among gentlemen. I wish you’d won, not lost.”

“As do I! Your kindness speaks well of you. Harry has heart, but little head.”

“He makes friends easily,” George said.

“We love him as a brother,” my lord insisted.

“His letters praised your hospitality. My mother keeps them all.”

“And you—you’ve heart and wit both,” my lord said pointedly.

“I’ve had more sorrow than most my age.”

“This turn of fortune leaves Harry without means?”

“Only what our mother or I may spare. What is a younger brother’s portion here?”

My lord shrugged. “You know what a younger brother is.”

George nodded. “We are friends, but flesh and blood. I’ll do no more than custom demands. Why fund his racing and cards? He’d squander it as he did here.”

“Poor lad,” my lord murmured.

“Our estate is vast, our income meager. Harry may return to Virginia—a horse, venison, a little coin. But if he crosses our mother, his lot will be bleak.”

My lord listened respectfully. “You speak well. Ever considered public life?”

“Every man of station must,” George replied.

Upstairs, they found only Lady Maria, her eyes red.

“You are like your brother,” she said softly. “He says you are as good.”

George, struck by remorse, thought, *How could he love her?* But Harry saw differently.

She soon excused herself, overcome.

“Nerves,” my lord murmured.

As they departed, George mused, “So you’ve parted friends?”

“She is good,” Harry said fiercely.

“And when you take her to Virginia? Mother will never forgive us.”

“I told her the truth—that Madam will come round. And that you’d share with me.”

George arched a brow. “I told Lord Castlewood the opposite—that as elder brother, I claim my rights.”

Harry paled. “You won’t help me?”
