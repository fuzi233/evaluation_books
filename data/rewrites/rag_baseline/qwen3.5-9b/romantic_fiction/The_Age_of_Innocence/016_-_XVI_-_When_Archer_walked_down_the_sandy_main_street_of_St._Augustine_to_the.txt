The sand of St. Augustine was coarse and unforgiving, grinding between Archer's shoes as he walked toward the house designated as Mr. Welland's. There, beneath the canopy of a magnolia tree, May Welland stood bathed in sunlight. The light caught her hair, turning it into a halo of blind radiance. In that instant, Archer wondered why he had delayed so long in arriving.

This was the truth. This was the reality. This was the life assigned to him. Yet, he, who prided himself on scorning arbitrary constraints, had hesitated to abandon his desk, paralyzed by the fear of what others might think of him stealing a holiday.

Her first exclamation cut through the heat: "Newland—has anything happened?" It struck him that it would have been more feminine if she had instantly deciphered the purpose of his arrival from his eyes alone. But when he answered, "Yes—I found I had to see you," her happy blush dispelled the chill of her surprise. He saw then how easily he would be forgiven, how swiftly even Mr. Letterblair's mild disapproval could be smoothed away by a tolerant family.

Though early, the main street offered no privacy save for formal greetings. Archer longed to be alone with May, to pour out his tenderness and his impatience. Breakfast time had not yet begun, and instead of inviting him inside, she proposed walking to an old orange garden beyond the town. She had just finished a row on the river; the sun, netting the small waves with gold, seemed to have trapped her in its meshes. Against the warm brown of her cheek, her blown hair glittered like silver wire. Her eyes looked lighter, almost pale in their youthful limpidity. Walking beside Archer with her long, swinging gait, her face wore the vacant serenity of a young marble statue.

To Archer's strained nerves, this vision was as soothing as the sight of the blue sky and the lazy river. They sat on a bench beneath the orange trees. He put his arm around her and kissed her. It was like drinking from a cold spring warmed by the sun. His pressure may have been more vehement than intended; the blood rose to her face, and she drew back as if startled.

"What is it?" he asked, smiling. She looked at him with surprise and answered, "Nothing."

A slight embarrassment fell upon them, and her hand slipped from his grasp. It was the only time he had kissed her on the lips outside of their fugitive embrace in the Beaufort conservatory. He saw that she was disturbed, shaken out of her cool, boyish composure.

"Tell me what you do all day," he said, crossing his arms under his tilted head and pushing his hat forward to shield his eyes from the dazzle. Letting her speak of familiar, simple things was the easiest way to maintain his own independent train of thought. He listened to her chronicle of swimming, sailing, and riding, varied occasionally by a dance at a primitive inn when a man-of-war docked. A few pleasant people from Philadelphia and Baltimore were picnicking nearby; the Selfridge Merrys had come down for three weeks because Kate Merry suffered from bronchitis. They planned to lay out a lawn tennis court on the sands, but no one but Kate and May possessed racquets, and most of the guests had not even heard of the game.

These activities kept her busy, leaving her no time to do more than glance at the little vellum book Archer had sent her the previous week—the "Sonnets from the Portuguese." However, she was memorizing "How they brought the Good News from Ghent to Aix," for it was one of the first poems he had ever recited to her. It amused her to inform him that Kate Merry had never even heard of a poet named Robert Browning.

Soon, she started up, exclaiming that they would be late for breakfast. They hurried back to the tumble-down house with its pointless porch and unpruned hedges of plumbago and pink geraniums, where the Wellands were installed for the winter. Mr. Welland's sensitive domesticity recoiled from the discomforts of the slovenly southern hotel. At immense expense and in the face of almost insuperable difficulties, Mrs. Welland was obliged, year after year, to improvise an establishment composed partly of discontented New York servants and partly drawn from the local African supply.

"The doctors want my husband to feel that he is in his own home; otherwise, he would be so wretched that the climate would do him no good," she explained, winter after winter, to sympathetic Philadelphians and Baltimoreans. Mr. Welland, beaming across a breakfast table miraculously supplied with the most varied delicacies, was presently telling Archer, "You see, my dear fellow, we camp—we literally camp. I tell my wife and May that I want to teach them how to rough it."

Mr. and Mrs. Welland were as surprised as their daughter by the young man's sudden arrival. Archer had explained that he felt on the verge of a nasty cold, a pretext that seemed sufficient to Mr. Welland to abandon any professional duty.

"You can't be too careful, especially toward spring," he said, heaping his plate with straw-colored griddle cakes and drowning them in golden syrup. "If I'd only been as prudent at your age, May would have been dancing at the Assemblies now, instead of spending her winters in a wilderness with an old invalid."

"Oh, but I love it here, Papa; you know I do. If only Newland could stay, I should like it a thousand times better than New York."

"Newland must stay until he has quite thrown off his cold," Mrs. Welland said indulgently. The young man laughed and remarked that there was such a thing as one's profession.

He managed, however, after an exchange of telegrams with the firm, to make his cold last a week. It shed an ironic light on the situation to know that Mr. Letterblair's indulgence was partly due to the satisfactory manner in which his brilliant young junior partner had settled the troublesome matter of the Olenski divorce. Mr. Letterblair had informed Mrs. Welland that Mr. Archer had "rendered an invaluable service" to the whole family, noting that old Mrs. Manson Mingott had been particularly pleased.

One day, when May had gone for a drive with her father in the only vehicle available, Mrs. Welland took occasion to broach a topic she always avoided in her daughter's presence.

"I'm afraid Ellen's ideas are not at all like ours. She was barely eighteen when Medora Manson took her back to Europe—you remember the excitement when she appeared in black at her coming-out ball? Another of Medora's fads—really this time it was almost prophetic! That must have been at least twelve years ago; and since then Ellen has never been to America. No wonder she is completely Europeanized."

"But European society is not given to divorce: Countess Olenska thought she would be conforming to American ideas in asking for her freedom." It was the first time the young man had pronounced her name since leaving Skuytercliff, and he felt color rise to his cheek.

Mrs. Welland smiled compassionately. "That is just like the extraordinary things that foreigners invent about us. They think we dine at two o'clock and countenance divorce! That is why it seems to me so foolish to entertain them when they come to New York. They accept our hospitality, and then they go home and repeat the same stupid stories."

Archer made no comment, and Mrs. Welland continued: "But we do most thoroughly appreciate your persuading Ellen to give up the idea. Her grandmother and her uncle Lovell could do nothing with her; both of them wrote that her changing her mind was entirely due to your influence—in fact, she said so to her grandmother. She has an unbounded admiration for you. Poor Ellen—she was always a wayward child. I wonder what her fate will be?"

"What we've all contrived to make it," he felt like answering. "If you'd all of you rather she should be Beaufort's mistress than some decent fellow's wife, you've certainly gone the right way about it."

He wondered what Mrs. Welland would have said if he had uttered the words instead of merely thinking them. He could picture the sudden decomposition of her firm, placid features, to which a lifelong mastery over trifles had given an air of factitious authority. Traces still lingered on them of a fresh beauty like her daughter's. He asked himself if May's face was doomed to thicken into the same middle-aged image of invincible innocence.

Ah, no. He did not want May to possess that kind of innocence, the innocence that seals the mind against imagination and the heart against experience!

"I verily believe," Mrs. Welland continued, "that if the horrible business had come out in the newspapers it would have been my husband's death-blow. I don't know any of the details; I only ask not to, as I told poor Ellen when she tried to talk to me about it. Having an invalid to care for, I have to keep my mind bright and happy. But Mr. Welland was terribly upset; he had a slight temperature every morning while we were waiting to hear what had been decided. It was the horror of his girl's learning that such things were possible—but of course, dear Newland, you felt that too. We all knew that you were thinking of May."

"I'm always thinking of May," the young man rejoined, rising to cut short the conversation.
