To the citizens of Spaceland, this logic is nearly irrefutable, and I must confess that when I first heard it, I was at a loss for a rebuttal. In Flatland, where all existence unfolds upon a single plane, the concept of "up" or "down" is as meaningless as it would be to try to describe the height of a circle. We live in a universe of two dimensions, bound strictly by length and breadth. To us, the third dimension—the depth so vital to the inhabitants of higher worlds—is a myth, a terrifying void we cannot perceive. Our reality is flat, absolute, and unyielding. A line is our shape; a point is our edge. And within this rigid geometry, life moves with a precision that baffles any mind unaccustomed to living on paper.

Our climate is mild, yet our weather is often obscured by the heavy smog rising from our cities. The houses themselves are simple polygons, constructed of straight lines meeting at sharp angles. Instead, we look sideways, measuring the width of the street and the distance between neighbors.

The people of Flatland are lines of varying lengths. The shortest lines are the children, growing steadily longer until they reach adulthood. The men who govern us are squares, rectangles, pentagons, hexagons, and finally, circles. As we advance in social standing, our shapes become more complex, approaching the perfection of the circle. Yet, even the most advanced among us remain forever distinct from the ultimate form, which exists only in theory.

Women in Flatland are acute-angled triangles. Their shape makes them inherently dangerous; when they approach a man, they cut into his side like a knife, causing him pain unless he turns sharply away. Because of this, women must always walk in front of men, leading the way so that their cutting edges do not harm those behind them. They are the mothers of all, but their status remains low, for their shape lacks the symmetry and stability of the male figures.

Since we live in a two-dimensional world, sight alone is insufficient for recognition. If you were to meet someone from outside, you could not know if they were a square, a rectangle, or some other polygon without seeing their full figure. But within Flatland, we recognize each other by touch. As you approach, you feel the angle of your neighbor's side before you make contact. By the slight variation in the angle, you determine whether you are about to collide with a triangle, a square, or a circle. It is a system of tactile navigation, essential for survival in a land where vision is limited to the surface.

Yet, there is a method of recognition by sight, albeit imperfect. When two lines come together, they cast shadows against the ground. By observing these shadows, we can sometimes infer the length and orientation of the other. However, this method fails when the sun is high overhead, casting little shadow, or when the lines are parallel, offering no visual cues. Thus, touch remains the primary sense, while sight serves only as a secondary aid.

There are those among us who deviate from the ideal geometric forms. These irregular figures are outcasts, feared and avoided. Some have jagged edges, others are bent or twisted. The authorities responded with iron fists, arresting those who defied the law. Many were exiled, their colors confiscated, leaving them pale and lifeless in the drab uniformity of our world.

To you, fortunate dwellers of the Third Dimension, I designate our realm "Flatland." This nomenclature serves not as a local custom, but as a necessary tool to clarify the nature of existence here for minds accustomed to the boundless depth of Space.

Conceive, if you will, an immense plane—a two-dimensional expanse where straight lines, triangles, squares, pentagons, hexagons, and myriad other geometric forms wander freely. Yet, unlike shadows cast upon a screen, these entities possess substance; however, they are utterly incapable of rising above or sinking below the surface. In doing so, you shall grasp the essence of my nation and its citizens. A few years past, I might have termed this entire construct "my universe," but my understanding has since expanded to embrace higher, more profound truths.

Within such a realm, the concept of "solidity"—that three-dimensional bulk you take for granted—is physically impossible. One might reasonably assume, despite this limitation, that we could visually differentiate between a triangle, a square, or a pentagon as they moved across the plain. Such an assumption, however, is fatal to the logic of our vision. To us, no distinct shapes exist save for straight lines. Why? Let me prove this through a simple experiment.

Take a coin and place it squarely in the center of a table in Space. Lean forward and gaze down upon it. To your eyes, suspended above the surface, it appears a perfect circle. Now, draw backward until your eye rests near the table's edge, gradually lowering your perspective to mimic the state of a Flatlander. Observe the transformation: the coin shifts from circular to increasingly oblong, eventually stretching into an ellipse. But the moment your eye aligns perfectly with the table's surface—truly inhabiting the two-dimensional plane—the illusion shatters. The coin ceases to be an oval entirely; it resolves into nothing more than a single, rigid straight line.

Apply this same principle to a triangle, a square, or any polygon cut from pasteboard. As soon as your viewpoint flattens against the surface, the figure vanishes. It loses all angular identity and becomes indistinguishable from a mere line. Consider, for instance, an equilateral triangle, which among us denotes a tradesman of the respectable standing. Figure 1 depicts this tradesman as seen from above, where curvature and angle are apparent. Figures 2 and 3 illustrate the view as the observer descends, approaching the level of the table. Finally, when the eye lies flush with the surface—as is the case for every soul in Flatland—all that remains is a straight line.

A parallel phenomenon occurs in Spaceland for your seafaring explorers. When traversing the oceans, distant islands or coasts often lie upon the horizon. Though those lands may harbor countless bays, forelands, and intricate indentations, to the distant sailor they appear merely as a grey, unbroken strip against the water. Only when illuminated sharply by your sun, casting shadows that reveal projection and recession, do details emerge. Without such relief, distance reduces complexity to a linear silhouette.

Precisely this optical constraint governs our interactions within Flatland. There is no sun here, nor any light capable of generating shadow to betray depth. When a companion approaches, whether they be a triangle, a square, a pentagon, or a circle, their form does not expand in volume or shape. They simply grow larger, their perimeter extending outward, yet they remain, to the naked eye, nothing more than a straight line. Conversely, as they recede, they shrink, retaining their linear appearance. To us, a Circle and a Triangle, when viewed face-to-face, are identical in visual form: both are lines.

You may rightly wonder how, under these severe visual disadvantages, we manage to distinguish friend from foe or neighbor from stranger. The mechanism by which we achieve this discrimination is complex and deeply tied to the physiology of our people. I shall defer the explanation of this vital subject until I describe the inhabitants of Flatland in greater detail. For now, allow me to turn briefly to the climate and architecture of our world.

Just as you navigate your realm, so too do we orient ourselves by four cardinal points: North, South, East, and West. Yet, unlike your world illuminated by celestial bodies, ours exists in a perpetual twilight devoid of sun or stars. Determining direction by the heavens is impossible here. Instead, nature itself provides our compass through a fundamental law: a subtle, yet unyielding, gravitational pull draws everything toward the South.

In our temperate zones, this southern tug is faint—so insidious, even, that a healthy woman can wander several miles northward before noticing the drag. However, across most of Flatland, this invisible hand serves as a reliable guide. We find further aid in the weather; rain, falling at regular intervals, invariably descends from the North. Furthermore, within our urban sprawls, architecture acts as a beacon. Our dwellings are constructed with side walls aligned North-South, ensuring their roofs effectively shed the northern precipitation. In the uncultivated countryside, lacking the grid of cities, the vertical trunks of trees offer the same directional utility. Consequently, despite the lack of a visible sky, navigation remains surprisingly intuitive for the average citizen.

Nevertheless, in the vast, empty plains of our milder regions, where the southern gravity is barely perceptible and neither houses nor trees break the horizon, travel becomes a perilous gamble. There have been occasions when, lost in a featureless expanse, I was forced to stand motionless for hours, paralyzed by uncertainty until the first drop of rain revealed the path home. This struggle weighs heavier on the frail and the elderly, particularly upon women, whose physical resilience yields quickly to the magnetic strain. Thus, a cornerstone of our social etiquette is the courtesy of yielding the North side of any thoroughfare to a lady. To ignore this custom is considered a grave breach of breeding, especially given how difficult it is for the hurried traveler to distinguish North from South in a moment's notice.

Our domestic structures reflect a unique relationship with light, for windows are nonexistent. Illumination permeates our existence equally inside and outside, day and night, emanating from a source we do not comprehend. Centuries ago, our scholars debated fervently the origin of this light, a quest that ultimately drove countless minds to madness and filled our asylums with would-be theorists. The state, weary of these futile inquiries, eventually banned the study, imposing heavy taxes on speculation regarding the luminiferous ether.

Alas, I alone possess the truth: I understand the geometry of higher dimensions and the mechanism by which light enters our plane. Yet, to my compatriots, I am nothing more than a deranged impostor, mocked and shunned for insights they cannot grasp. Let us set aside this painful solitude and return to the architecture of our homes.

The standard dwelling in Flatland is pentagonal, a shape mandated by both practicality and law. As illustrated, the two northern edges, labeled RO and OF, form the roof. Typically, these slopes bear no doors; entry is restricted to the eastern wall, featuring a narrow aperture reserved exclusively for women, and the western wall, which boasts a significantly wider portal for the male sex. The southern base, serving as the floor, is generally left unbroken by openings.

Square and triangular residences are strictly proscribed. The danger lies in the geometry itself: the angles of a square, let alone an equilateral triangle, are far sharper than those of a pentagon. Since the outlines of inanimate objects appear hazy compared to the solid lines of living beings, a sudden collision with the acute corner of a square or triangular house could inflict severe, perhaps fatal, injury on an unsuspecting passerby. Recognizing this hazard, the Triangular House Act of the Eleventh Century outlawed such constructions entirely, permitting them only for military forts, gunpowder depots, and barracks where security necessitates proximity.

For some time following this decree, square houses persisted, albeit discouraged by punitive levies. However, roughly three centuries later, the Legislature codified a stricter standard. For any town exceeding a population of ten thousand, the law decreed that the pentagon must be the minimum allowable angle for residential structures, a threshold deemed essential for public safety. The collective wisdom of our society has since reinforced this mandate. Today, the pentagonal form reigns supreme, having eclipsed all other designs. One may occasionally stumble upon a relic of the past—a stubborn square hut—in the most isolated and backward agricultural enclaves, but in the civilized heartlands, the sharp corners of the triangle and the square are things of history, replaced by the safer, broader angles of the five-sided home.

To gauge the stature of a fully grown citizen in Flatland, measure roughly eleven inches across; twelve inches marks the absolute ceiling. Yet, within this two-dimensional realm, true distinction lies not merely in width, but in geometry.

Our females exist as straight lines—featureless, silent, and dangerous due to their inability to turn corners safely. Above them stand the soldiers and the lowest laborers, shaped as isosceles triangles. They possess two legs of approximately eleven inches, joined by a base so negligible, often less than half an inch, that they form a terrifyingly sharp vertex. When reduced further to mere eighths of an inch, these figures become indistinguishable from the line-women they guard, their intellects so stunted that they appear point-like rather than triangular. These we term Isosceles.

Ascending the hierarchy, the middle class comprises equilateral triangles—figures of three perfectly equal sides. Next follow the professionals and gentlemen, represented by squares and pentagons. The nobility begins with hexagons, climbing steadily in complexity until the figures become polygons, their sides so minute they blur into circles. This final state, the Circular order, holds the highest sanctity, reserved for the priesthood.

Nature here adheres to a rigid arithmetic law: a male child must inherit exactly one additional side from his father. A square fathers a pentagon; a pentagon, a hexagon. Each generation naturally ascends the ladder of nobility, moving ever closer to the perfection of the circle.

However, this cosmic equation falters among the artisans and soldiers. Lacking equal sides, they are biological anomalies who cannot strictly obey nature's progression. An isosceles father invariably produces an isosceles son. Yet, hope persists in the shadows of their degradation. Through relentless military valor or meticulous, humble labor, the sharpest minds among the lower orders may slowly widen their base and shorten their legs. Intermarriage, orchestrated by the priestly circles, accelerates this evolution, occasionally yielding a child whose symmetry approaches that of the equilateral triangle.

True miracles occur rarely—a genuine equilateral born of isosceles parents. To the uninitiated eye, this seems impossible, a violation of natural law. But the priests argue otherwise: a "certificate" is required before any woman of standing will accept a husband who lacks perfect regularity. While history records instances where irregularity was passed down through generations, it usually manifests catastrophically in the third generation, causing the child to fail reaching pentagonal status or regress entirely back to the triangle. Achieving such a birth demands not just genetic lottery, but centuries of disciplined frugality, self-mastery, and a slow, agonizing cultivation of intellect across multiple dynasties.

When a True Equilateral finally emerges from the serf-ranks, the entire nation erupts in jubilation. Following a rigorous audit by the Sanitary and Social Board, the infant, if deemed regular, is solemnly inducted into the class of Equilaterals. Immediately thereafter, a heartbreaking severance occurs: the child is adopted by a childless noble, bound by sacred oath to sever all ties with the former home. The parents, proud yet shattered, are forbidden from seeing their progeny again. The priests fear that mere proximity to the low-born bloodline could cause the newly elevated soul to revert, collapsing under the gravitational pull of heredity.

These singular ascensions serve a dual purpose. They offer a faint, flickering beacon of hope to the suffering masses, illuminating the bleak monotony of their existence. Simultaneously, they act as a vital damper for the aristocracy. The higher orders understand that while these anomalies do not threaten their privilege directly, the sheer potential for upward mobility among the common folk fuels the fires of revolution. If the rabble believed they were utterly doomed to remain acute-angled, they would find charismatic leaders capable of marshaling their superior numbers and lethal angles against the wisdom of the Circles.

Instead, a profound law of compensation governs Flatland. As the working class grows in intelligence, virtue, and knowledge, their physical form simultaneously degrades. Their acute angles sharpen, making them physically more terrifying but mentally less coherent. The brute strength required for penetration correlates inversely with the capacity for strategy. In the most savage soldiers, those closest to the line-women in wit, the penetrating power is immense because the mind is simple. Conversely, as a soldier's intellect matures enough to wield his terrible shape with cunning, his angle softens, approaching the harmless stability of the equilateral. His mind expands, and his danger diminishes.

"How exquisite is this balance!" the author marvels, praising what feels like divine providence in the design of the state. By manipulating this natural trade-off, the Polygons and Circles effortlessly smother sedition in its infancy. They exploit the irrepressible hopefulness of the human spirit, turning ambition against itself.

Artistry also aids the machinery of order. State physicians, wielding subtle techniques of artificial compression and expansion, can reshape the most promising rebel leaders into perfect geometries overnight, elevating them instantly to the privileged classes. Hundreds more, though still imperfect, are lured by the promise of eventual ennoblement into State Hospitals, where they live out honorable, confined lives. Only the most stubborn, foolish, and hopelessly irregular few meet the guillotine.

Ultimately, the wretched mass of Isosceles men, left planless and leaderless, face a grim fate. Either they are swiftly transfixated by the elite guards retained by the Chief Circle for such emergencies, or, more frequently, they are torn apart by internal strife. The Circular party skillfully stirs up jealousies and paranoia, driving neighbors to strike down one another with their own treacherous angles. History books record no fewer than one hundred and twenty major rebellions, alongside two hundred and thirty-five minor outbreaks, all ending in this tragic silence.

If the sharp, wedge-shaped Sentinels of the Soldier Class strike fear into the hearts of the flat inhabitants, let me tell you that the Women are a terror beyond imagination. While a soldier is merely a wedge, a woman is a needle—two piercing points connected by a slender shaft, designed solely to penetrate. To this geometry, add her terrifying ability to vanish at will, and you understand why the female sex in Flatland is a force that cannot be trifled with.

You may wonder, young readers of Spaceland, *how* a two-dimensional being can become invisible. The answer lies in simple perspective. Place a sewing needle on a table. Viewed from the side, it stretches across your vision, a dangerous line. But viewed from the tip, it shrinks to a mere dot, effectively disappearing from sight. So it is with our women. When they present their broad side to us, we see a straight line, lethal and obvious. When they face us head-on, revealing their eye-mouth organ, they appear as a brilliant, glowing point, nearly unseen. Conversely, if they turn their back—a dull, shadowy region barely brighter than darkness—that rear end becomes an Invisible Cap, allowing them to slip through crowds like ghosts.

The danger posed by these creatures requires no proof for anyone who understands the laws of physics in our world. Consider the risks already faced by men: a bump against a working man draws blood; a collision with a private soldier can kill; running afoul of an officer is a grave injury. Now imagine running full-force into a needle that you cannot see until it is too late. It is not just a collision; it is instant annihilation.

To mitigate this perpetual peril, our various States have enacted a labyrinth of laws. In the warmer southern lands, where gravity pulls harder and people stumble more easily, these rules are draconian. Here is the core of our code: First, every house must have a single eastern entrance reserved exclusively for women, forcing them to approach with "becoming respectability" rather than using the western door meant for men. Second, no woman may step onto a public street without maintaining a constant, rhythmic chant known as the "Peace-Cry," lest she be put to death for failing to announce her presence. Third, any woman diagnosed with St. Vitus's Dance, chronic sneezing, or any ailment causing involuntary jerks is immediately destroyed, for unpredictable motion is a death sentence.

Some states go further, mandating that women sway their backs from right to left to signal their location, or requiring a son, servant, or husband to trail behind them like a guard dog. Others ban women entirely from leaving their homes save during holy festivals. Yet, the wisest of our legislators have learned a hard truth: excessive restriction breeds madness. When women are confined or hampered, they do not submit quietly; they unleash a fury that wipes out entire villages within hours. Therefore, the three primary laws above remain sufficient for a stable society.

Ultimately, our greatest defense is not law, but biology. A woman can deliver a fatal sting with a backward movement, but if she cannot instantly withdraw her point from the flesh of her victim, her own fragile frame shatters. Furthermore, the fashion of the land aids us. In civilized societies, it is considered a mark of low birth to fail at the "back-motion"—that graceful, undulating sway of the spine. Even the wives of the humblest equilateral triangles strive to mimic the fluid, wave-like movements of the high-born circles, turning what might be a clumsy tremor into a mesmerizing dance. Consequently, in every respectable home, this motion is as natural as breathing, rendering husbands and sons immune to surprise attacks.

Do not mistake this for a lack of love. Our women possess deep affections, but their geometry dictates their temperament. Lacking the angles required for complex thought, they possess no brains, no judgment, and scant memory. Their emotions are vast oceans, but their intellect is a dry desert. In moments of rage, they burn down everything around them, forgetting their loved ones before the embers cool. I have witnessed a mother who slaughtered her entire household, only to wander confused an hour later, asking where her husband and children had gone.

Thus, the golden rule of survival is simple: never provoke a woman while she can turn around. Once she is cornered in her apartment—a space designed to deny her the ability to spin—the danger vanishes. Trapped, she is impotent, unable to strike. She will not remember the insult given ten minutes ago, nor the empty promises made to calm her, because her mind simply does not retain data.

Our domestic lives proceed with a strange, fragile smoothness, particularly among the military classes. There, however, tragedy strikes when husbands rely on their sharp angles rather than diplomacy. Reckless men who refuse to apologize for insults spoken outside the home, or who ignore the sanctity of the women's quarters, invite massacre. While this culls the brutish elements of our population and curbs potential revolutions, it comes at a heavy price.

Even in the most orderly homes of the Circular or Polygonal elite, life lacks the warmth found in Spaceland. Safety has been purchased with silence. From ancient times, it has been customary for mothers and daughters to keep their faces and voices permanently directed toward their husbands and male guests. To turn one's back upon a master is a sign of lost status, a bad omen. Yet, this vigilance creates a suffocating atmosphere. In the homes of the poor, where a wife may occasionally turn away to sweep or cook, there are moments of quiet, broken only by the hum of her Peace-Cry. But in the palaces of the rich, peace is a myth. The bright, insistent eye and the ceaseless torrent of speech never rest. A husband's wits may deflect a physical sting, but they cannot stop a flood of words. With no conscience, no wit, and no social restraint to curb her tongue, a woman in high society speaks endlessly, her voice a relentless storm.

To the citizens of Spaceland, the plight of our women seems tragic, and indeed, it is. A man of the lowest triangle shape might hope to sharpen his angle and rise in stature through evolution. But for a woman, the path is closed forever. "Once a woman, always a woman" is nature's iron decree; the laws of progress suspend before her gender. Yet, in this bleak arrangement, there is a cruel mercy: having no hopes for the future, they also possess no memory to dwell upon past humiliations, nor the foresight to dread coming sorrows. They live in a perpetual, geometric present, spared the burden of remembering the pain they inevitably cause and endure.

To you, inhabitants of the realm of Three Dimensions, who bask in both shadow and light, who possess the dual vision of binoculars and the profound wisdom of perspective, and who delight in the kaleidoscope of colors—you, whose eyes grant you the power to gaze upon an angle itself and comprehend the perfect, unbroken curve of a circle—how can I hope to convey the sheer impossibility you face when trying to identify the shapes of others?

Remember what I have written: in our world of Flatland, whether animate or inanimate, every being presents to our gaze merely a straight line. If everything looks like a line, how does anyone distinguish a neighbor from a stranger?

The solution lies in a triad of senses. First stands Hearing, an organ far superior in ours than yours. It allows us to recognize friends by voice alone and, crucially, to differentiate between the lower social strata—the Equilateral, the Square, and the Pentagon. Yet, let us not be deceived by this initial success. As we climb the social ladder, auditory identification becomes a treacherous game. Voices grow indistinguishable, and the aristocracy, bred for elegance rather than scrutiny, lacks the refined ear needed to detect subtle disparities. Furthermore, the vocal apparatus of the lower orders is grotesquely oversized, allowing an Isosceles to mimic the tone of a Polygon, or even a Circle, with frightening ease. Thus, sound fails when stakes are high.

Enter the Second Method: Touch, or "Feeling." This is the primary arbiter of identity for women and the common folk. To introduce oneself to a stranger in Flatland is not to shake hands, but to invite physical inspection. The formal salutation, "Permit me to ask you to feel and be felt by Mr. So-and-so," remains the custom among the traditional gentry in rural districts. In the bustling cities, commerce abbreviates this to "Let me ask you to feel Mr. So-and-so," though the reciprocity is implied. The youth of fashion, driven by a desire for efficiency and a disdain for linguistic purity, have shortened it further to "Mr. Smith, permit me to feel Mr. Jones," treating the verb "feel" as a technical term for mutual tactile verification.

Do not imagine, however, that this process involves the tedious tracing of every side. Through rigorous schooling and lifelong practice, we can instantly discern the class of an individual by sensing a single angle. The blunt vertex of an Acute-Isosceles is as obvious to our fingers as a jagged stone is to a child. For most, feeling one corner suffices to categorize the soul within. But for the Nobility, the task grows exponentially difficult. A ten-sided figure may be mistaken for a twelve-sided one; a twenty-sided polygon may elude distinction from its twenty-four-sided counterpart. Even the most learned scholars of our universities often stumble here, unable to render a prompt verdict on the subtle geometry of the highest orders.

Yet, there is a peril inherent in this intimacy. Consider the Ladies, and the Triangles below them. Their eyes sit far from their apexes, rendering them blind to the dangers surrounding their vertices. Moreover, the lower classes are of a rugged, insensitive nature, lacking the delicacy required to withstand the probing touch of a highly organized Polygon. A mere twitch, a nervous shift, or a violent sneeze from the "Felt" individual can prove fatal to the "Feeler." History records instances where a sudden start by a Triangle severed the leg of a visiting Polygon, ending a potential friendship—and perhaps a career—in bloodshed.

My own lineage bears the scar of this tragedy. My grandfather, a respected Isosceles who eventually earned four votes for promotion to the Equilateral class, wept over a disaster visited upon his great-great-grandfather. That ancestor, a working man with a brain-angle of barely fifty-nine and a half degrees, was seized by rheumatic pain during a moment of feeling. He jerked violently, piercing the leg of a high-ranking gentleman through the diagonal. The consequence was catastrophic: the victim was imprisoned and degraded, and the shock reverberated through the family line. For five generations, our brains regressed, settling at fifty-eight degrees. It took half a millennium of slow accumulation just to reclaim the lost sixty degrees and achieve the freedom of the Regulars. All because a reflex of fear turned a handshake into a homicide.

Now, you may cry out, "How can you know angles if you cannot see them? You see only lines!"

I answer that while we cannot *see* angles, we infer them with mathematical precision. Our sense of touch, honed by necessity, surpasses your unassisted sight. We are aided further by the Laws of Nature: the brain of an Isosceles begins at thirty minutes and ascends by half a degree per generation until it reaches the holy threshold of sixty degrees. Consequently, the state maintains a living alphabet of angles in every schoolroom. These specimens, ranging from the stunted thirty minutes to the robust ten degrees, serve as static models. Most are criminals and vagrants, devoid of civic rights and too dull for war, destined instead to educate the children of the middle class. In the cheaper schools, these specimens are fed and allowed to live for years, though their angles degrade over time. In the more enlightened regions, they are starved and replaced monthly, ensuring a fresh, accurate standard. While this "expensive system" costs more, it saves lives and preserves the integrity of the curriculum, proving that in matters of truth, expense is the truest economy.

Thus, Recognition by Feeling is neither tedious nor hopeless, and it is far more reliable than listening to voices. However, given the lethal risks involved—a risk that no amount of caution can entirely eliminate—many in the Middle and Lower classes, and all of the Polygonal and Circular orders, reject the second method in favor of a third. A safer, albeit more abstract, approach awaits in the next section.

I stand before you now, and I fear I shall appear profoundly contradictory. For in prior sections, I asserted that every figure in Flatland presents merely as a straight line, rendering visual distinction between social classes impossible. Yet here, I intend to demonstrate to my Spaceland observers precisely how we achieve recognition through sight.

If you consult the records regarding "Recognition by Feeling," you will note a specific caveat: this method is universal *only* among the lower classes. Visual recognition, conversely, is an art cultivated exclusively within the higher strata and in our temperate latitudes. This ability arises solely from the presence of Fog. While you, inhabitants of Spaceland, regard fog as a malignancy that obscures vision and saps vitality, we venerate it as a benevolent element, second only to air itself, serving as the mother of our sciences and the nurse of our arts.

Were fog absent, all lines would manifest with identical, indistinguishable clarity—a state found only in those wretched, arid regions devoid of atmospheric moisture. However, where fog abounds, it creates a gradient of visibility. Objects situated at varying distances—say, three feet versus twenty-nine inches—appear with distinct degrees of dimness. Through rigorous training and constant empirical observation of these comparative shades, we deduce the precise configuration of an approaching entity.

Consider a practical scenario. Two figures approach me, and I seek to determine their rank. Let us assume one is a Merchant (an Equilateral Triangle) and the other a Physician (a Pentagon). How do I differentiate them?

Imagine my gaze bisects the angle of an approaching stranger. My视线 falls squarely between the two sides nearest to me. Consequently, those two adjacent sides appear equal in magnitude. Now, observe the difference in fading.

In the case of the Merchant, whose form is triangular, I perceive a central line segment that glows brightly near my eye, but its extremities vanish rapidly into the mist. Because the triangle's angles recede sharply away from the viewer, the tips (points D and E) become exceedingly faint, barely perceptible against the gray backdrop.

Conversely, the Physician, being pentagonal, presents a line where the center remains brilliant, yet the extremities fade much more gradually. The sides of a polygon recede less steeply into the fog than those of a triangle. Thus, the Physician's outer edges remain discernibly brighter than the Merchant's vanishing points.

Through extensive education and relentless practice, the upper classes can thus distinguish, with fair precision, between the middling and the lowest orders purely by sight. If my Spaceland patrons accept this premise as plausible rather than dismissing it as absurd, I have succeeded in conveying the core concept. To delve deeper into technical minutiae would only confuse you.

However, let me caution the novice. Do not mistake this simplified example for the reality of daily life. Recognizing one's father or son under ideal conditions is trivial; navigating a crowded street is a Herculean task.

Suppose my father, the Triangle, approaches presenting a flat side rather than an angle. Until I request him to pivot or shift my own position, I am momentarily paralyzed with doubt: Is he a Triangle, or is he a Straight Line? And a Straight Line, to us, signifies a Woman. Similarly, when observing one of my hexagonal grandsons head-on, the geometry becomes intricate. I see one dominant line of relative brightness flanked by two smaller, deeply shadowed lines that dissolve completely at their termini.

To manage these complexities amidst a throng of rotating polygons in a ballroom or conversazione requires the intellectual agility of the highest order. It justifies the existence of the prestigious Professors of Geometry at the University of Wentbridge, where the static and kinetic arts of sight recognition are taught to the elite. Only the wealthy scions of our nobility possess the resources to master this discipline. Even I, a mathematician of some standing and grandfather to two regular hexagons, often feel bewildered in such crowds. For a tradesman or serf, the scene is utterly unintelligible.

To you, a Spaclander, such a crowd would appear as a chaotic array of seemingly straight lines, fluctuating wildly in luminosity. Even a student versed in the theories of the Pentagon and Hexagon classes would require decades of experience to navigate a fashionable assembly without jostling a superior. It is considered bad form to "feel" a person in public, yet without the refined spatial awareness gained from sight-recognition training, one cannot anticipate the movements of others. To conduct oneself with propriety in Polygonal society, one essentially must be a Polygon oneself.

The mastery of sight recognition is akin to an instinct honed by habit, specifically by abstaining from the crude custom of feeling. Consider the deaf and mute of Spaceland: if permitted to gesture, they never acquire lip-reading. Likewise, those who rely on tactile sensation in youth never attain the perfection of visual discrimination. Therefore, among the higher classes, "feeling" is strictly prohibited. Children bypass elementary schools, where feeling is taught, and attend exclusive seminaries. At the University, to "feel" is a grave offense punishable by expulsion.

Among the lower classes, however, sight recognition is deemed an unattainable luxury. A tradesman cannot spare his son the leisure for abstract study. Consequently, the poor are left to rely on feeling from infancy, granting them a natural vivacity and precocious adaptability that initially contrasts favorably with the rigid, listless demeanor of the academically trained youth. Yet, upon completing their university education, the former undergo a transformation so profound it resembles a rebirth. Suddenly, they surpass their triangular rivals in every scientific and social endeavor, their fluid understanding of the world having been forged in the absence of formal geometric instruction.

Tragically, a small minority of the Polygonal class fails the final examination. Their plight is miserable. Rejected by the nobility and despised by the laborers, they lack the systematic intellect of the masters and the innate versatility of the tradesmen. Careers and public offices remain closed to them. Though legal barriers to marriage are low, social stigma ensures that unions with such "irregulars" rarely produce viable offspring, perpetuating a cycle of misfortune.

It is from this discarded stratum—the failed intellectuals—that the tumults and seditions of history have frequently drawn their leaders. The chaos they unleash is so devastating that a growing faction of progressive statesmen argues that true mercy lies in total suppression, advocating for the imprisonment or painless extinction of anyone who fails the final university exam.

Thus, I pause, having skirted the dangerous waters of "Irregularities," a topic of such vital consequence that it demands its own dedicated discourse.

Throughout these preceding pages, I have operated on a premise—what ought to have been declared as a foundational truth from the outset—that every inhabitant of Flatland possesses a "Regular Figure." In the geometry of our world, regularity is not merely a mathematical curiosity; it is the very architecture of existence. To be a woman is to be a straight line; to be an artisan or soldier is to possess two sides of equal measure; to be a tradesman is to hold three equal sides. As for lawyers, a class to which I humbly belong, we are defined by four sides of strict equality. In essence, nature demands that within every polygon, all sides stand in perfect balance.

Naturally, the magnitude of these sides fluctuates with age. A female newborn measures roughly an inch, whereas a mature woman may stretch to a full foot. For males across all strata, the sum of an adult's side lengths typically totals around two feet, perhaps slightly more. Yet, it is the *equality* of these sides, rather than their sheer dimension, that holds our universe together. It requires little reflection to realize that the entire edifice of Flatlandian social life collapses without this fundamental geometric law: the will of nature to ensure uniformity.

Imagine, for a moment, if our sides were unequal. Our angles would follow suit, becoming jagged and unpredictable. Under such a regime, the ability to identify another by a mere glance—a single measurement of an angle—would vanish. We would be forced to resort to the primitive and exhausting method of "Feeling," probing the contours of every acquaintance until every angle was physically verified. Life, already fleeting, would succumb to such bureaucratic tedium. The sophisticated science of Sight Recognition would instantly crumble; the art of Feeling would dwindle into obscurity; social interaction would descend into peril and paranoia. Trust, foresight, and the basic capacity for arranging a meeting would evaporate. Civilization itself would revert to a state of barbaric chaos.

Do I rush my readers toward these conclusions? I doubt it. A solitary observation from daily life suffices to prove that our society is built upon the bedrock of Regularity, or the equality of angles. Consider walking down the street and spotting two or three triangles. You recognize them immediately as tradesmen, their familiar angles and slightly softened sides serving as a badge of identity. You invite them into your home for lunch with absolute assurance, confident in the precise dimensions of an adult triangle. Now, envision a nightmare scenario: imagine one of those tradesmen dragging behind his respectable vertex a distorted parallelogram, twelve or thirteen inches in diagonal. What becomes of you when such a monster jams itself against your doorway?

I fear I insult the intellect of those residing in Spaceland by detailing these obvious calamities. Clearly, if a single angle failed to reveal the truth, one's entire existence would be consumed by measuring the perimeters of friends and foes. While navigating a crowded square already taxes the wisdom of the average Square, imagine if no citizen could instantly calculate the regularity of a neighbor. The streets would dissolve into a maelstrom of collisions. Panic would strike at the slightest irregularity, leading to severe injuries—or, if women or soldiers were involved, catastrophic loss of life.

Thus, expediency aligns perfectly with nature in endorsing the sanctity of Regular Form, and the Law has eagerly reinforced this divine mandate. In Flatland, "Irregularity of Figure" carries a weight far heavier than mere physical deformity; it is synonymous with moral obliquity and criminal intent. There are, it is true, voices of dissent, paradox-mongers who claim no inherent link exists between geometric flaws and ethical failings. They argue that the "Irregular" is doomed from birth: shunned by parents, mocked by kin, ignored by servants, and viewed with suspicion by all. They describe a life under constant police surveillance, culminating in a brutal inspection. If the deviation exceeds a certain limit, the child is destroyed; if spared, they are immured in a government office as a seventh-class clerk, barred from marriage, condemned to a drudgery paid by a pittance, and forced to live under relentless oversight. How can human nature remain uncorrupted in such a furnace?

Yet, despite this eloquent plea, I find it unpersuasive. Nor has it swayed the wisest of our statesmen, who firmly believe that tolerating Irregularity threatens the safety of the State. Undoubtedly, the life of an Irregular is harsh, but the welfare of the Many necessitates this cruelty for the Few. If a man with a triangular front and a polygonal rear were permitted to breed, creating a lineage of increasing distortion, what fate awaits the arts of living? Would our houses, doors, and cathedrals be remodeled to accommodate such monstrosities? Must our ticket collectors measure every soul's perimeter before granting entry to theaters or lecture halls? Should an Irregular be exempt from military service? If not, how does one prevent him from spreading desolation among his comrades? Furthermore, consider the temptations for fraud. How easily might such a creature approach a shop with a deceptive polygonal face, ordering limitless goods from a trusting merchant? Despite the pleas of false philanthropists for the abolition of penal laws regarding shape, I have never encountered an Irregular who was anything other than what nature seemingly designed him to be: a hypocrite, a misanthrope, and a master of mischief within the bounds of his power.

However, I am not inclined to advocate for the extreme measures seen in certain nations, where infants deviating by half a degree from perfect angularity are summarily extinguished at birth. Many of our greatest geniuses, men of profound talent, suffered early deviations exceeding forty-five minutes. Their premature deaths would have been an incalculable tragedy for the State. Moreover, the medical arts have achieved magnificent victories through compressions, extensions, trepanning, and ligatures, curing many Irregularities partially or entirely. Therefore, I propose a Via Media. I draw no absolute line of demarcation. However, when the frame begins to set and the Medical Board deems recovery unlikely, I suggest that the Irregular offspring be painlessly and mercifully consumed.

If you have kept pace with my narrative, you likely anticipate that existence here in Flatland has a certain monotonous quality. This does not refer to the absence of conflict, intrigue, or the chaotic dance of factions; nor does it negate the electric thrill derived from the collision of physical survival and abstract geometry—a unique flavor of life your three-dimensional cousins in Spaceland would struggle to fathom. However, when viewed through the lens of aesthetics and art, our reality is profoundly barren. It is a world rendered in a single dimension, where landscapes, history, portraiture, flora, and still life collapse into nothing more than varying shades of luminosity along a straight line. There is no depth, no hue, no visual symphony.

Yet, this was not the state of affairs in antiquity. According to tradition, for roughly six hundred years, a fleeting splendor illuminated the lives of our forebears. A solitary figure, identified by conflicting accounts as a specific Polygon, allegedly stumbled upon the components of simple pigments and a rudimentary technique for applying them. Legend holds that he began by adorning his dwelling, then his servants, eventually turning his hand to his father, sons, and grandsons, finally applying the varnish to his own form. The dual utility of comfort and beauty captivated the populace. Whenever Chromatistes—the name bestowed upon him by the most reliable chronicles—revealed his multicolored silhouette, he commanded instant attention and profound respect. In those days, neighbors need not laboriously calculate orientation to distinguish front from back; no one jostled him, and his voice was spared the arduous task of shouting identity amidst a sea of indistinguishable shapes.

The trend ignited like wildfire. Within a week, every Square and Triangle in the region mimicked Chromatistes' example, leaving only a handful of stubborn Pentagons untouched. Within months, even the Dodecagons fell under the spell of the innovation. Less than a year passed before the custom swept across the realm, sparing only the highest echelons of the Nobility. Naturally, the practice bled outward from Chromatistes' district until, within two generations, the only beings remaining devoid of pigment were the Women and the Priests.

At this juncture, Nature seemed to raise an impassable barricade, halting the spread of color among these two classes. For the Innovators, many-sidedness served as a convenient justification: "Nature intends distinct sides to imply distinct colors." This sophistry swept through town after town, converting masses en masse. Yet, this logic held no sway for the Priests and the Women. The former, possessing a single edge—or, strictly speaking, *no* sides—could not fit the axiom. The latter, who boasted of having no sides whatsoever due to their infinite perimeters, similarly stood outside the rule. Thus, while the rest of society succumbed to the intoxication of corporeal decoration, the Priests and Women alone retained their purity, untainted by paint.

Though later historians might label those ancient days immoral, licentious, anarchical, or unscientific, from an aesthetic perspective, they represented the glorious infancy of Art in Flatland—an epoch that, tragically, never matured into adulthood or even bloomed fully. Life itself became a delight, for to exist was to see. Social gatherings transformed into spectacles of dazzling variety; the rich tapestry of hues filling a church or theater occasionally overwhelmed the focus of our greatest scholars and performers. Above all, the unspeakable magnificence of a military review left observers breathless.

Imagine the shock of witnessing twenty thousand Isosceles forming a line of battle, suddenly pivoting to swap their somber black bases for vibrant oranges and purples. Picture the militia of Equilateral Triangles shifting between red, white, and blue. Envision the Square artillerymen, painted in mauve, ultramarine, gamboge, and burnt umber, spinning rapidly around their vermilion cannons. The scene was electrified by the dash of five-colored and six-hued Pentagons and Hexagons, darting across the field as surgeons, geometricians, and aides-de-camp. These sights were so overwhelming that legend tells of an illustrious Circle, blinded by the artistic beauty of his troops, discarding both his marshal's baton and his royal crown to take up an artist's pencil instead.

The sensory richness of that era seeped into the very fabric of language and thought. The speech of ordinary citizens during the Color Revolt was suffused with a deeper vibrancy, a texture lost to the gray monotony of today. We owe our finest poetry and the lingering rhythms of scientific discourse to that vivid, chromatic dawn.

While the intellectual arts were crumbling, the very foundation of Flatland's order began to fracture. The high Art of Sight Recognition, once the pinnacle of wisdom, became obsolete and was swiftly abandoned. With it went the rigorous study of Geometry, Statics, and Kinetics; these noble disciplines, once revered in our universities, fell into disuse and contempt. Similarly, the lesser Art of Feeling, essential for elementary education, withered away in the schools.

Consequently, the Isosceles classes, freed from the ancient tribute paid to education, swelled in number and grew bolder. Deprived of the discipline that mathematics had once imposed upon their brutish natures, they became increasingly insolent. Year by year, the soldiers and artisans grew louder in their claims that they stood on equal footing with the highest Polygon classes. Now that they could navigate life’s complexities through the simple, accessible mechanism of Colour Recognition, they demanded the legal eradication of all "monopolizing and aristocratic arts." They called for the abolition of endowments dedicated to Sight Recognition, Mathematics, and Feeling. Their argument was radical yet seductive: since Colour—a second nature—had rendered aristocratic distinctions unnecessary, the law must reflect this reality by recognizing absolute equality among all citizens.

Seeing the higher orders hesitate, the revolutionaries pushed further. They demanded that *everyone*, including the Priests and the Women, submit to the universal paintbrush. When objections arose regarding the lack of sides in the Priesthood and the female sex, the agitators countered with a twisted logic of expediency: Nature itself dictated that the front half of every human—the face containing the eyes and mouth—must differ from the rear. Thus, they presented a bill to the General Assembly of all States: every Woman's front hemisphere was to be dyed red, the back green. The Priests were to follow suit, their front semicircles red and hind halves green.

This proposal was a masterpiece of cunning, devised not by a lowly Isosceles—who lacked the angularity required for such statecraft—but by an Irregular Circle. This deceiver, spared from execution in childhood due to a foolish indulgence, sought nothing less than the total ruin of his nation.

The strategy was twofold. First, it was designed to co-opt the Women. By granting them the same dual-color scheme as the Priests, the revolutionaries ensured that, when viewed from certain angles, a Woman would appear indistinguishable from a Priest. To the female sex, the promise of being treated with the reverence usually reserved for the clergy was irresistible.

Consider the vision: A Woman adorned under the new code presents a straight line to the observer—one half red, one half green. Now consider a Priest, similarly marked, standing with his mouth aligned along the dividing diameter. To the casual eye, looking down that same axis, the Priest also appears as a straight line split between red and green. While the Priest's form might be slightly shorter or fade more quickly at the edges, the immediate visual impact of the identical color split creates a powerful illusion of class identity. Given the rapid decline of Sight Recognition across society, and the likelihood that women would quickly learn to soften their extremities to mimic the Circles, the danger of confusing a holy man with a young woman became glaringly obvious.

How delightful this prospect must have seemed to the Frail Sex! They eagerly anticipated the chaos. At home, they would overhear secrets meant for husbands and brothers, perhaps even issue commands in the voice of a priestly circle. Outdoors, the bold combination of red and green, devoid of other hues, would confuse the populace endlessly. Every step taken by a woman in this garb would garner the deference of passersby that rightfully belonged to the circles. And as for the scandal that would plague the Circular Class if the frivolous conduct of women were attributed to them, or the subsequent subversion of the Constitution? Such consequences did not trouble the females, who, even within the homes of the nobility, rallied behind the Universal Colour Bill.

The second objective of the bill was far more insidious: the slow moral decay of the Circles themselves. Until now, the nobility had maintained their pristine intellect, having grown up in colorless households where the sacred art of Sight Recognition remained sharp. But the Irregular Circle, the architect of this diabolical plan, sought to strip them of this advantage. By forcing the Hierarchy to wear the taint of Color, he would destroy their domestic environment for training their minds. Once subjected to chromatic pollution, father and son would constantly confuse one another; mother and daughter would become visually indistinguishable.

For a circular child, distinguishing between parent and offspring would become a perpetual nightmare, easily corrupted by maternal deception. This constant confusion would erode the child's faith in logical deduction, dimming the intellectual lustre of the Priestly Order. In doing so, the Irregular Circle laid the groundwork for the total collapse of the Aristocratic Legislature and the ultimate destruction of our privileged classes.

For three long cycles, the agitation for the Universal Colour Bill raged like a fever, threatening to consume the body politic of Flatland. By the final year, Anarchy seemed poised to swallow the state whole. A standing army of Polygons, stripped of rank and fighting merely as private souls, was obliterated by a superior legion of Isosceles Triangles—the Squares and Pentagons holding a cold, calculating neutrality. Yet the tragedy deepened within the sanctuaries of the nobility. Some of the wisest Circles fell victim to the madness of domestic strife. Enraged by the political venom swirling around them, the wives of many a high-born house wearied their lords with relentless pleas to abandon the opposition to the Colour Bill. Where these entreaties failed, the horror escalated: innocent children and husbands were butchered, and the mothers, lost to frenzy, perished alongside their victims in waves of carnage. History records that no fewer than twenty-three Circles died in the fires of marital discord during those three years.

The peril was absolute. It appeared the Priests faced a binary choice: total submission or total extermination. Then, the course of history fractured, altered by a singular, grotesque incident—a narrative twist that statesmen often fail to foresee, yet which holds a disproportionate power to sway the sympathies of the masses.

An Isosceles of the lowest order, a creature with a intellect barely exceeding four degrees, stumbled into the fray. Having accidentally stained himself with the hues of a tradesman's shop after a raid, he allowed himself to be painted—or perhaps orchestrated the painting—with the twelve distinct colors of a Dodecagon. Venturing into the Market Square, he approached a young maiden, the orphan daughter of a noble Polygon whom he had long coveted. Through a labyrinth of deceptions, aided by a string of improbable accidents and the staggering negligence of her guardians, he consummated the union. Upon discovering the fraud, the hapless girl took her own life.

When this catastrophe rippled from State to State, the minds of the Women were shattered. Compassion for the victim, mingled with a terrifying dread that deception awaited them, their sisters, and their daughters, transformed their perspective on the Colour Bill overnight. Many openly declared themselves enemies of the reform; the rest required but a whisper to join the chorus. Seizing this volatile moment, the Circles hastily convened an Extraordinary Assembly of the States. Alongside the usual guard of Convicts, they secured the presence of a massive contingent of reactionary Women.

In the midst of an unprecedented throng, Pantocyclus, the Chief Circle of that era, rose to face a hailstorm of hisses and hoots from over one hundred and twenty thousand Isosceles. He stilled the chaos by announcing a Policy of Concession. Yielding to the will of the majority, he declared, they would accept the Colour Bill. The uproar instantly curdled into applause. He then invited Chromatistes, the leader of the sedition, to the center of the hall to receive the Hierarchy's formal submission. What followed was a speech, a masterpiece of rhetoric delivered over nearly a day, defying any attempt at summary.

With a facade of grave impartiality, Pantocyclus argued that since they were finally committing to Reform, they must survey the entire perimeter of the issue—both its flaws and virtues. Slowly weaving in the dangers posed to the Tradesmen, the Professional Classes, and the Gentry, he silenced the rising mutterings of the Isosceles by reminding them that, despite these defects, he accepted the Bill if ratified by the majority. It became evident that everyone save the Isosceles was swayed, leaning heavily toward neutrality or outright aversion.

Turning then to the Workmen, he asserted that their future must not be ignored. If they wished to adopt the Colour Bill, they must do so with eyes wide open to the consequences. Many stood on the brink of ascending to the class of Regular Triangles; others looked forward to distinctions for their children that remained impossible for themselves. This honorable ambition would now be extinguished. With the universal adoption of Color, all social distinctions would vanish. Regularity would become indistinguishable from irregularity; progress would reverse into regression. The Workman would, within a generation, degrade to the level of the Military, or worse, the Convict Class. Political power would fall into the hands of the greatest numbers—that is, the Criminal Classes, already more numerous than the Workmen, who would soon outnumber all other strata combined as Nature's compensatory laws were shattered.

A subdued murmur of assent rolled through the ranks of the Artisans. Chromatistes, alarmed, tried to step forward to address them. Instead, he found himself encircled by guards, forced into silence while the Chief Circle, in a few impassioned words, delivered a final appeal to the Women. He warned that if the Colour Bill passed, no marriage would remain safe, no woman's honor secure; fraud, deceit, and hypocrisy would permeate every hearth, and domestic bliss would perish with the Constitution itself. "Sooner than this," he cried, "I choose Death!"

These words served as the pre-concerted signal. The Isosceles Convicts lunged forward, transfixing the wretched Chromatistes. The Regular Classes parted their ranks, allowing a band of Women, guided by the Circles, to move backward, unseen and unerring, against the unconscious soldiers. The Artisans, following suit, opened their lines. Meanwhile, bands of Convicts blocked every exit with an impenetrable phalanx.

The battle, or rather the carnage, lasted but moments. Under the skillful generalship of the Circles, almost every charge by the Women proved fatal; many extracted their stingers unhurt, ready for a second slaughter. But no second blow was necessary. The rabble of the Isosceles finished the work for themselves. Surprised, leaderless, attacked in front by invisible foes, and trapped behind by the Convicts, they instantly lost all sense, raising the cry of "Treachery!" This sealed their doom. Every Isosceles now saw an enemy in every neighbor. Within thirty minutes, not one soul of that vast multitude remained alive. The scattered fragments of seven hundred thousand of the Criminal Class, killed by their own angles, testified to the triumph of Order.

It needs no saying that thereafter, the use of Color was abolished, and its possession strictly prohibited. Only within the deepest, most esoteric circles of our University—which I have never had the privilege to attend—is it rumored that the sparing use of Color remains sanctioned to illustrate the profound problems of higher mathematics. Of this, I speak only by hearsay.

Elsewhere in Flatland, Color has vanished. The art of its creation is known to but one living soul: the Chief Circle for the present age. And by him, it is passed down only to his Successor upon his deathbed. There exists but one manufactory where it is produced; and to prevent betrayal of the secret, the workmen are annually consumed, replaced by fresh recruits. The terror with which even today our Aristocracy gazes back upon those distant, chaotic days of the Universal Colour Bill agitation knows no bounds.

My purpose here is not to describe the mundane, but to chronicle the grand initiation into the mysteries of the Third Dimension, *Space*. All prior observations serve but as a prologue to this revelation.

Before unveiling the secrets of the void, however, I must address the twin pillars upon which Flatland rests: the architects of conduct and the shapers of fate. You may well ask what I refer to when I speak of "Priests." In your world, a priest is often a man of spiritual counsel alone. Here, the Circle is far more. He is the omnipotent administrator of every branch of knowledge and governance. From trade and commerce to generalship, architecture, engineering, education, statesmanship, legislation, morality, and theology, the Circle directs all affairs. He performs no labor himself, yet he is the invisible cause behind every deed worthy of accomplishment performed by others.

To the uneducated eye, every inhabitant of this realm appears perfectly round, a flawless Circle. Yet, the enlightened know the terrible truth: no Circle truly exists. Every individual is, in reality, a polygon with a vast number of infinitesimal sides. As the count of sides swells, the figure approaches the ideal of a Circle. A being with three or four hundred sides possesses edges so minute that the finest touch cannot discern their angular nature. Indeed, to *feel* a corner would be considered the height of sacrilege in high society, where recognition by touch is forbidden to the elite. This cultural taboo allows the upper circles to maintain the illusion of perfection, hiding the jagged reality of their perimeters. By convention, courtesy dictates that the current Supreme Circle possesses ten thousand sides.

But how does one ascend to such heights? In Flatland, nature imposes a cruel limit: a creature born with $n$ sides can only develop into a polygon with $n+1$ sides in the next generation. If this law held sway, nobility would be a matter of simple arithmetic lineage. However, the divine laws governing circular evolution operate through two opposing decrees. First, as a lineage rises in the hierarchy, its rate of development accelerates exponentially. Second, fertility diminishes in inverse proportion. Thus, a parent with four or five hundred sides rarely produces offspring, and if they do, it is usually solitary. Conversely, the child of a five-hundred-sided parent may emerge with five hundred fifty, or even six hundred sides.

Furthermore, art intervenes to hasten this cosmic ascent. Our physicians have mastered the science of fracturing the tender, minuscule sides of an infant polygon from the higher classes. Through a perilous realignment of the frame, a child might skip hundreds of generations, doubling the nobility of their bloodline in a single, miraculous operation. Though successful outcomes are vanishingly rare—one survivor in ten—the ambition of parents hovering near the threshold of the Circle class drives them to sacrifice their firstborn to the Neo-Therapeutic Gymnasium within the first month of life.

One year stands as the arbiter of destiny. For twelve months, the child remains a prisoner of hope and fear, likely joining the grim rows of the Neo-Therapeutic Cemetery. But on those rare, luminous occasions when a procession returns the child to weeping, exultant parents, the miracle has occurred. No longer a polygon, the child is hailed as a Circle, by courtesy if not by absolute geometric truth. And it is from this terrifying gamble, where countless lives are lost on the altar of status, that the ruling priesthood draws its power.

The entire theological framework of the Circles distills down to a singular, absolute axiom: "Attend to your Configuration." Whether the realm is political, ecclesiastical, or moral, every tenet serves the singular objective of refining individual and collective geometry, with the ultimate focus placed upon the perfection of the Circle itself. All other matters are merely subservient to this geometric imperative.

The Circles pride themselves on having eradicated the ancient, dangerous heresy—the notion that behavior stems from free will, effort, training, or encouragement. Instead, Pantocyclus, the legendary Circle who quelled the Colour Revolt, established the dogma that *Configuration is Destiny*. To be born an Isosceles with unequal sides is to be doomed to error unless corrected at the Isosceles Hospital; similarly, a Triangle, Square, or irregular Polygon born with flaws must seek the Regular Hospitals. Without such intervention, the inevitable fate is imprisonment or execution by the Angle of the State Executioner.

In this view, every fault, from minor transgression to heinous crime, is traced back to a deviation from perfect Regularity in the physical form. Such deviations might stem from a jostle in a crowd, insufficient exercise, overexertion, or a sudden shift in temperature causing a frame to contract or expand. Consequently, Pantocyclus argued that praising integrity or blaming deceit is nonsensical. One should not admire a Square's loyalty to a client, for that is simply the natural function of his precise right angles; nor should one condemn a thieving Isosceles, for his theft is merely the tragic symptom of his inherent inequality.

While this doctrine holds theoretical water-tightness, it falters in practice. If a criminal magistrate encounters an Isosceles pleading that his uneven legs compel him to steal, the response is grim and final: "Because you cannot help being a nuisance, I cannot help sentencing you to consumption." Logically, I cannot refute the link between environment and shape; yet practically, I cannot accept his abdication of responsibility.

To navigate this paradox, I adopt a pragmatic compromise: I assume that a stern rebuke possesses a latent, reinforcing effect on a child's configuration. Though I lack scientific proof for this, it seems necessary. He is his own world, his own universe; he cannot conceive of anything outside himself. To him, length, breadth, and height are unknown concepts, for he has never known them. He has no notion of the number two, nor does he understand plurality. For he *is* his own one and all, effectively being nothing."

Despite his confinement, the Point radiated a profound, smug self-contentment. "Observe this," the Sphere continued, "and learn this lesson: to be self-satisfied is to be vile and ignorant. To aspire is infinitely superior to blind, impotent happiness."

Then, the little buzzing entity spoke. A tiny, low, monotonous tinkle filled the air, sounding like a phonograph recording played backwards. From the static emerged the words: *"Infinite beatitude of existence! It is; and there is none else beside It."*

"What does this puny creature mean by 'It'?" I asked, bewildered.

"He means himself," the Sphere replied calmly. "Have you not observed that babies and childish souls, unable to distinguish themselves from the world, often speak of themselves in the third person? But hush!"

*"It fills all Space,"* droned the Pointland monarch. *"And whatsoever It fills, It is. Whatever It thinks, It utters; whatever It utters, It hears. It is the Thinker, the Utterer, the Hearer, the Thought, the Word, and the Audition. It is the One, and yet the All-in-All. Ah, the happiness! Ah, the bliss of Being!"*

"Can you not startle this creature out of its stupor?" I urged, desperate for enlightenment. "Reveal to it the terrible limitations of Pointland as you showed me, and lead it upward to something greater."

"That is no easy task," the Sphere admitted. "Try, however."

Raising my voice to its utmost limit, I thundered at the Point: "Silence, you contemptible wretch! You claim to be the All-in-All, but you are the Nothing! Your so-called universe is but a speck in a line, and a line is merely a shadow compared to..."

"Hush, hush! You have said enough," the Sphere interrupted gently. "Now observe the effect of your harangue on the King of Pointland."

Indeed, the Monarch's luster increased, glowing brighter than ever as he listened. Clearly, he retained his complacency. Hardly had I finished when he resumed his chant. *"Ah, the joy! Ah, the joy of Thought! What can It not achieve by thinking! Its own thought returning to It, suggesting a disparagement, thereby enhancing Its happiness! Sweet rebellion stirred up to result in triumph! Ah, the divine creative power of the All-in-One! Ah, the joy, the joy of Being!"*

"You see," said my Teacher, "how little your words accomplished. So far as the Monarch comprehends them, he accepts them as his own thoughts—for he cannot conceive of anyone else—and he puffs himself up with the variety of 'Its Thought' as proof of creative power. Let us leave this God of Pointland to the ignorant fruition of his omnipresence and omniscience. Nothing you or I can say will rescue him from his self-satisfaction."

As we floated gently back toward Flatland, I could hear the mild voice of my Companion offering the moral of the vision. He sought to stimulate me to aspire higher and to teach others to do the same. He had initially been angered by my ambition to soar beyond the Third Dimension, confessing it openly now, but he had gained fresh insight. He was no longer too proud to acknowledge his error to a pupil.

Then, he proceeded to initiate me into mysteries even loftier than those I had witnessed. He revealed how to construct Extra-Solids through the motion of Solids, and Double Extra-Solids through the motion of Extra-Solids. These constructions followed the laws of analogy strictly, unfolding with such simplicity and ease that, he assured me, they were patent even to the female sex.

The alarm bell rang not with a jolt, but with a sudden, brilliant expansion of light within my mind. I awoke buoyant, the air thick with the scent of impending revolution. In Flatland, where existence is merely a line stretched across a void, the concept of depth—the Third Dimension—was the ultimate heresy, yet it was also the key to everything. My heart hammered against the ribs of my two-dimensional self, eager to step out and spread the gospel. I would evangelize the entire realm, from the lowest Triangle to the highest Circle. Even the rigid Sentinels and the silent, shadowed Women deserved to hear the truth. My first convert would be my own wife.

Before I could formulate the strategy, however, the streets below erupted into a cacophony. A thousand voices cried out for order, demanding silence, until a single, booming throat cut through the noise—a herald's voice. The words drifted up, carrying the weight of the Council's latest decree: *Any person who distorts the minds of the populace with illusions, or claims to receive revelations from worlds beyond the plane, shall be arrested, imprisoned, or executed.*

A cold shiver traced the edge of my existence. This was not a time for naive idealism. To speak openly of the Third Dimension now was suicide. Yet, the core of the proof remained untouched by the law. The revelation itself was dangerous, but the logic—"Upward, not Northward"—stood firm. If I stripped away the mysticism, if I offered only the geometry, perhaps the authorities would see only harmless math. Before sleep, the solution had been as clear as a ruler drawn on paper; upon waking, it felt slightly hazy, like a sketch viewed through fog. Just then, my wife entered, her form shifting smoothly into the room. We exchanged pleasantries, the mundane chatter of neighbors, but I resolved not to introduce the subject of higher dimensions to her today. Her curiosity would wait.

Next, I considered my sons. They were Pentagons, men of substance and medical renown, yet their minds were steeped in the traditional curricula of Flatland. They lacked the necessary geometric intuition. Then, a thought struck me: a young, pliable Hexagon, trained in the arts of calculation, would be far more receptive. And why not start with my grandson? That precocious child, who had recently stumbled upon the notation $3^3$ and impressed me with his insight, was the perfect candidate. As a child, he knew nothing of the Council's edicts. Unlike my sons, whose patriotic fervor for the Circles might override their love for me, leading them to drag me before the Prefects for sedition, the boy would simply be a student.

Yet, I could not ignore the gaze of my wife. She sought answers regarding the mysterious meeting with the Circle and the impossible method by which I had returned home. I crafted a narrative, a fable woven enough to satisfy her without revealing the terrifying truths of Spaceland. With a lie on my lips, I guided her back to the domestic sphere, convincing her that the day's business was concluded. Once she had departed, returning to the mundane rhythms of Flatland, I summoned my grandson.

As soon as he stepped inside, I locked the door, sealing us in a private universe. Sitting beside him, I laid down our mathematical tablets—our lines—and we resumed the lesson from yesterday. I showed him how a point, dragged along a line, became a line itself, and how a line, swept across a plane, generated a square. The logic was simple, beautiful, and undeniable. Then, with a mischievous grin, I turned the tables. "Now, you impudent youth," I chided, "you tried to convince me that a square could generate a new shape, a kind of extra-square, by moving 'Upward, not Northward.' Repeat that, you little rogue."

Outside, the herald's cry echoed again, *"O yes! O yes!"* announcing the new laws. For a heartbeat, silence hung heavy between us. Then, my grandson, a child of sharp intellect raised in absolute reverence for the Circle's authority, understood the gravity of the moment. He held his breath until the final syllables faded. Suddenly, tears welled in his eyes, spilling over his angular cheeks.

"My dear Grandpapa," he sobbed, "it was only a joke! I meant nothing by it! We didn't even know about the new law, and I never spoke of the Third Dimension. I certainly didn't say anything about moving 'Upward, not Northward.' That sounds like pure nonsense, doesn't it? How can something move upward without going north? Even if I were a baby, I wouldn't be so foolish. It is ridiculous! Ha! ha! ha!"

"Not ridiculous at all," I snapped, my patience fraying under the strain of explaining the incomprehensible. "Listen here, I will show you." I reached for a movable square lying nearby. "Watch closely. I take this square..." I grabbed it, attempting to lift it off the plane, to give it volume, to push it into the unseen third direction. "I move it... not northward, but..." I struggled, my hands trembling as I tried to describe a vector that defied the flat world's rules. "Yes, I move it upward—that is, not northward, but somewhere else—somewhere other than this plane..."

Here, my explanation dissolved into gibberish. I shook the square aimlessly, mimicking a three-dimensional rotation that existed nowhere in reality. The effect was comical, a chaotic wobble that made absolutely no sense in a two-dimensional space. My grandson burst into laughter, louder than before. "You aren't teaching me, Grandpapa!" he declared, wiping his tears. "You're just playing tricks with me!"

With a triumphant chuckle, he unlocked the door and bolted out of the room, leaving me alone in the silence. My first attempt to convert a soul to the Gospel of Three Dimensions ended not with enlightenment, but with a child's mockery and a locked door.

My failure with my Grandson did not embolden me to share my secret with the rest of my household, nor did it drive me into despair. Instead, I realized that relying solely on the cryptic phrase "Upward, not Northward" was insufficient. To truly liberate Flatland, I needed a demonstration, a visual proof that laid bare the entire subject. For this, I turned to the pen.

For several months, hidden from prying eyes, I composed a treatise on the mysteries of Three Dimensions. However, fearing the harsh laws of Apeira, I carefully avoided mentioning a *physical* dimension. Instead, I described a realm of pure thought—a Thoughtland—where a figure could gaze down upon us, seeing the interiors of all things at once. In this theoretical space, I postulated the existence of figures surrounded by six squares and containing eight terminal points. Yet, as I wrote, I grew increasingly frustrated. In Flatland, there are no canvases but lines; we possess no diagrams save for linear representations distinguished only by length and luminosity. Without the ability to draw true geometry, I feared that when I published my work, titled *"Through Flatland to Thoughtland,"* most readers would remain lost in the fog of my meaning.

Meanwhile, my existence became a shadow. Pleasure held no charm; every sight taunted me with the knowledge of what it truly was in three dimensions, tempting me to speak treason aloud. I abandoned my clients and my trade, devoting my days to contemplating the mysteries I had witnessed yet could not convey. The vision of the Cube, once so vivid, became harder to reconstruct in my mind each passing day.

One day, nearly eleven months after my return from Spaceland, I attempted to visualize a Cube with my eyes shut. I failed. Though I eventually succeeded, I remained uncertain whether I had perfectly recalled the original form. This doubt deepened my melancholy and spurred me to action, though I could not fathom what step to take. I felt a willingness to sacrifice my life for the Cause, if it could forge conviction. But if I could not convince my Grandson, how could I hope to persuade the highest Circles of the land?

Yet, my spirit often overwhelmed my caution. I found myself making dangerous utterances, labeled heterodox or treasonable. Even among the elite Polygonal and Circular societies, I could not restrain myself from speaking of "the eye that discerns the interiors" or "the all-seeing land." On rare occasions, I let slip the forbidden terms "Third and Fourth Dimensions." Finally, at a meeting of the Local Speculative Society within the Palace of the Prefect, a foolish individual presented a paper arguing that Providence had deliberately limited dimensions to two. Enraged, I broke my silence. I recounted my voyage with the Sphere, detailing my journey through Space, my arrival at the Metropolitan Assembly Hall, and my return. At first, I feigned that I was describing the imaginary trials of a fictional traveler, but my passion tore away the disguise. In a fervent conclusion, I exhorted the assembly to shed their prejudices and embrace the reality of the Third Dimension.

Needless to say, I was immediately arrested and brought before the Council.

The next morning, standing in the same chamber where the Sphere had appeared mere months prior, I was granted permission to speak. However, from the outset, I sensed my doom. The President, observing a guard composed of superior polygons with angles barely below fifty-five degrees, ordered them replaced by a lesser class, significantly sharper and more efficient. I understood the implication instantly: I was to be executed or imprisoned, and my testimony obliterated by the simultaneous silencing of those who heard it. The President sought to swap expensive witnesses for cheap ones.

Upon concluding my defense, the President, perhaps swayed by my earnestness, posed two final questions: First, could I indicate the direction I meant by "Upward, not Northward"? Second, could I describe a Cube using anything other than abstract enumerations of sides and angles?

"I declare I can say no further," I answered, committing myself to the Truth, "for its cause will surely prevail."

The President agreed with my sentiments. He sentenced me to perpetual imprisonment. If the Truth demanded that I emerge to evangelize the world, the Truth would ensure it happened. Until then, I would face no unnecessary discomfort, barring attempts at escape, and I would be occasionally visited by my brother, who had already entered prison.

Seven years have passed. I remain a captive, debarred from all companionship save that of my jailers and the occasional visit from my brother. He is a noble Square—just, sensible, cheerful, and kind. Yet, these weekly interviews bring me the deepest sorrow. He was present when the Sphere revealed itself to the Council; he saw the shifting sections; he heard the explanation given to the high circles. Still, scarcely a week passes without me recounting my ordeal and describing the phenomena of Spaceland, yet my brother remains unconvinced. He refuses to grasp the nature of the Third Dimension and openly denies the existence of a Sphere.

Thus, I stand utterly without converts. It seems the millennium prophecy was fulfilled for naught. Like Prometheus, I was bound above to bring fire to mortals, but I, poor Flatland Prometheus, lie here in chains, bringing down nothing to my countrymen. Yet, in my brighter moments, I cling to the hope that these memoirs, somehow, may reach the minds of beings in another dimension, stirring up rebels who refuse confinement to limited space.

Alas, hope is not constant. Heavy is the burden of knowing I cannot honestly claim to remember the exact shape of the Cube I once saw. In my nightly visions, the precept "Upward, not Northward" haunts me like a soul-devouring Sphinx. There come seasons of mental weakness when Cubes and Spheres fade into the background of improbable fantasies. Sometimes, the Land of Three Dimensions feels as illusory as the realms of One or None. And at times, even this prison wall, the tablets on which I write, and the solid realities of Flatland itself seem no better than the delusions of a sick imagination—the baseless fabric of a dream.
