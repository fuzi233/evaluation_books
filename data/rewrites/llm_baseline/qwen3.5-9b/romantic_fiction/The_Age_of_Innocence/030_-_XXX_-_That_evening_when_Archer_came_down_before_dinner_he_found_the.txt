That evening, when Archer descended to the dining room before supper, he discovered the drawing-room deserted. He and May were to dine alone, for all the family's social obligations had been suspended following Mrs. Manson Mingott's sudden illness. Since May was invariably the more punctual of the pair, Archer was startled to find she had not preceded him. He knew she was at home; while he had been dressing, he had distinctly heard her moving within her bedroom. He wondered what delay had detained her. Lately, he had fallen into the habit of indulging such conjectures as a method of anchoring his drifting thoughts to solid reality. Occasionally, he felt as though he had finally grasped the key to his father-in-law's obsession with trivialities; perhaps Mr. Welland, long ago, had experienced similar escapes and visions, conjuring up entire hostilities of domesticity to shield himself from them.

When May finally appeared, Archer observed that she looked fatigued. She wore the low-necked, tightly laced dinner dress required by Mingott ceremony even on the most informal occasions, and her fair hair was coiffed into its customary heavy coils. Yet her face, in contrast, was wan and almost faded. Still, she radiated her usual tenderness toward him, and her eyes retained the brilliant azure glow of the previous day.

"What became of you, dear?" she asked.

"I was waiting at Granny's," she explained, "but Ellen came alone and said she had dropped you on the way because you had to rush off on business. Is there nothing wrong?"

"Only some letters I forgot, which I needed to send before dinner."

"Oh—" she murmured, and a moment later, "I'm sorry you didn't come to Granny's—unless those letters were urgent."

"They were," he replied, surprised by her insistence. "Besides, I don't see why I should have gone to your grandmother's. I didn't know you were there."

She turned and moved to the mirror above the mantelpiece. Standing there, lifting her long arm to secure a puff that had slipped from its place in her intricate arrangement, Archer was struck by something languid and inelastic in her posture. He wondered if the deadly monotony of their existence had weighed upon her as well. Then he recalled that, as he had left the house that morning, she had called over the stairs to meet him at her grandmother's so they might drive home together. He had responded with a cheerful "Yes!" and, absorbed in other visions, had forgotten his promise. Now, guilt smote him, yet he was irritated that such a trifling omission should be held against him after nearly two years of marriage. He was weary of living in a perpetual, tepid honeymoon—devoid of the heat of passion yet burdened by all its demands. If May had voiced her grievances (he suspected she harbored many), he might have dismissed them with laughter; but she was trained to conceal imagined wounds beneath a Spartan smile.

To mask his own annoyance, he asked how her grandmother was doing. She answered that Mrs. Mingott was still improving but had been somewhat disturbed by recent news regarding the Beauforts.

"What news?"

"It appears they intend to remain in New York. I believe he is entering an insurance business, or something similar. They are looking for a small house."

The absurdity of the situation was undeniable, and they proceeded to dinner. Their conversation during the meal circled within its usual narrow bounds; however, Archer noted that his wife made no mention of Madame Olenska, nor of Old Catherine's reception of her. He was grateful for this silence, yet felt a vague unease.

Afterward, they ascended to the library for coffee. Archer lit a cigar and selected a volume of Michelet. He had recently taken to reading history in the evenings since May had begun requesting that he read aloud whenever he held a volume of poetry. It was not that he disliked the sound of his own voice, but because he could always anticipate her commentary. During their engagement, she had simply echoed his words; but since he had ceased providing her with opinions, she had begun to offer her own, with results detrimental to his enjoyment of the works discussed.

Seeing that he had chosen history, she fetched her workbasket, drew up an armchair to the green-shaded study lamp, and uncovered a cushion she was embroidering for his sofa. She was not a skilled needlewoman; her large, capable hands were suited for riding, rowing, and outdoor pursuits. Yet, since other wives embroidered cushions for their husbands, she refused to omit this final link in her devotion. Seated thus, positioned so that Archer could see her merely by raising his eyes, he watched her bend over her frame. Her ruffled elbow-sleeves slipped back from her firm, rounded arms, the betrothal sapphire gleaming on her left hand above her broad gold wedding ring, while her right hand stabbed the canvas slowly and laboriously. Bathed in the lamplight, with its illumination falling fully on her clear brow, he whispered to himself with a secret dismay that he would forever know the thoughts behind her eyes. Never, in all the years to come, would she surprise him with an unexpected mood, a new idea, a weakness, a cruelty, or an emotion. She had expended her poetry and romance on their brief courtship; the function was exhausted because the need had passed. Now, she was simply maturing into a replica of her mother, and, mysteriously, attempting to transform him into a Mr. Welland.

He laid down his book and stood up impatiently. Immediately, she raised her head.

"What's the matter?"

"The room is stifling; I need a little air."

He had insisted that the library curtains slide back and forth on a rod, allowing them to close in the evening, rather than remaining nailed to a gilt cornice and immovably looped over layers of lace as in the drawing-room. He pulled them back and pushed up the sash, leaning out into the icy night. The mere act of not looking at May, seated beside his table under his lamp—the sight of other houses, roofs, chimneys, the sense of other lives outside his own, other cities beyond New York, and a whole world beyond his world—cleared his brain and made breathing easier.

After leaning out into the darkness for a few moments, he heard her say, "Newland! Do shut the window. You'll catch your death."

He pulled the sash down and turned back.

"Catch my death!" he echoed, feeling compelled to add, "But I've caught it already. I *am* dead—I've been dead for months and months."

Suddenly, the play of the word sparked a wild suggestion. What if it were *she* who was dead? If she were dying—if she were going to die soon—and leave him free! The sensation of standing there in that warm, familiar room, looking at her, and wishing her dead, was so strange, so fascinating, and overmastering that its enormity did not immediately strike him. He simply felt that fate had offered him a new possibility to which his sick soul might cling. Yes, May might die; people did: young, healthy people like herself. She might die and set him suddenly free.

She glanced up, and he saw by her widening eyes that there must be something strange in his expression.

"Newland! Are you ill?"

He shook his head and turned toward his armchair. She bent over her workframe, and as he passed, he laid his hand on her hair.

"Poor May!" he said.

"Poor? Why poor?" she echoed with a strained laugh.

"Because I shall never be able to open a window without worrying you," he rejoined, laughing as well.

For a moment, she was silent; then, her head bowed over her work, she spoke very softly: "I shall never worry if you're happy."

"Ahh, my dear; and I shall never be happy unless I can open the windows!"

"In *this* weather?" she remonstrated. With a sigh, he buried his head in his book.

Six or seven days passed. Archer heard nothing from Madame Olenska and became aware that her name would not be mentioned in his presence by any member of the family. He did not attempt to see her; to do so while she was at Old Catherine's guarded bedside would have been almost impossible. Drifting in the uncertainty of the situation, he remained conscious, somewhere below the surface of his thoughts, of a resolve that had formed when he leaned out from the library window into the icy night. The strength of that resolve made it easy to wait and make no sign.

Then, one day, May informed him that Mrs. Manson Mingott wished to see him. There was nothing surprising in the request, for the old lady was steadily recovering and had always openly declared that she preferred Archer to any of her other grandsons-in-law. May delivered the message with evident pleasure; she was proud of Old Catherine's appreciation of her husband.

There was a moment's pause, and then Archer felt it incumbent upon him to say, "All right. Shall we go together this afternoon?"

His wife's face brightened, but she instantly answered, "Oh, you'd much better go alone. It bores Granny to see the same people too often."

Archer's heart beat violently as he rang Old Mrs. Mingott's bell. He had desired above all things to go alone, for he felt certain the visit would afford him the opportunity to say a word privately to the Countess Olenska.
