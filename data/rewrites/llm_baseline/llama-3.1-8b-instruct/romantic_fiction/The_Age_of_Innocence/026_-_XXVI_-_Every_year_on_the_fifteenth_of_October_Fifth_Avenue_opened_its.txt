As the fifteenth of October dawned, Fifth Avenue slowly awakened from its summer slumber, its shutters creaking open, its carpets rolled out, and its triple-layered window-curtains swaying gently in the morning breeze. Within weeks, however, the facade would be transformed, the opera houses and theaters bursting forth with new productions, dinner invitations piling up, and dance dates being set. By this time each year, Mrs. Archer would declare that New York had undergone a profound transformation.

Observing the city from her lofty vantage point as a non-participant, she, along with Mr. Sillerton Jackson and Miss Sophy, would meticulously note every fresh fissure in the social landscape, every unexpected bloom sprouting between the carefully manicured rows of social convention. This annual ritual had been a cherished pastime of Archer's youth, listening to his mother's astute observations of the subtle shifts in the city's demeanor. To Mrs. Archer, New York's evolution was always a cause for dismay – a relentless march towards disintegration.

As the family gathered for their Thanksgiving dinner, the conversation turned to the city's transformation. Mrs. Archer's musings on the subject had become a tradition, a melancholic yet not bitter assessment of the year's accomplishments. This year, however, even Newland Archer found himself compelled to acknowledge the undeniable change. The once-familiar streets now seemed to be trending towards a more ostentatious display of wealth, as evidenced by the lavish gowns that adorned the city's elite.

"It's astonishing how the younger generation is embracing the latest fashions," Miss Jackson mused, recalling her visit to the opera, where she struggled to recognize any familiar faces amidst the sea of opulent attire. "Jane Merry's gown, for instance, looked almost new, despite being a mere two years old."

Mrs. Archer sighed wistfully, as if regretting the passing of an era when ladies prized restraint and modesty over showy displays of wealth. "She's one of the few who still values tradition," she said, her voice tinged with a hint of nostalgia.

Miss Jackson nodded in agreement, reminiscing about her own youth, when it was considered déclassé to don the latest fashions immediately upon arrival in town. "In my day, we would often keep our Parisian dresses locked away for two years, allowing them to mature and develop character," she said, a faraway look in her eyes.
