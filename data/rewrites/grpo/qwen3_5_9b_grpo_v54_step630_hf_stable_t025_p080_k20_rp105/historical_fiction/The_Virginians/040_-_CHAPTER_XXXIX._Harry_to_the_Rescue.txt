August 25th, 1756. Tunbridge Wells.

"My dear Lord March," Mr. Warrington wrote on that Saturday morning, "I am delighted to report that I have won all three of our bets. I arrived at Bromley just two minutes before the hour; my new horses maintained a capital pace. I drove them myself, with a postilion to guide me and my black man inside with Mrs. Betty. I trust they found the drive pleasant. We reached Tunbridge Wells at forty-five minutes past eleven, having been spared the trouble of stopping on Blackheath, despite two horsemen who rode up but turned away, disliking the look of our company. This victory allows me to settle our debt from yesterday's piquet, a game I shall be happy to replay to grant you your revenge. I remain, your most obliged and faithful servant, H. Esmond Warrington."

It was by this timely intervention that Lady Maria Esmond was able to surprise her aunt in her chambers that morning and escape her captivity. Having sent Mrs. Betty ahead to London, she had not anticipated the emissary's return on the very day of her flight. Late into the night, after a modest meal provided by the bailiff's wife, she and the chaplain had been playing cards when the sharp whirring of wheels approached the house. The sound caused the lady to drop her trumps, her heart hammering with sudden, violent emotion. The carriage halted directly at the door. After a brief exchange at the gate, Mrs. Betty emerged, her face radiant with joy though her eyes glistened with tears. Following her entered a tall young gentleman.

The chaplain slammed his cards down with a shout of triumph, while Lady Maria turned so pale she seemed to vanish, rising from her chair to totter forward a step before collapsing into her cousin's arms with a hysterical cry. How many kisses were exchanged? If they numbered in the thousands, I shall not recount them. He had come to rescue her. She knew he would; he was her champion, her deliverer from bondage and shame. She wept a genuine flood of tears upon his shoulder, and in that moment of release, she looked more beautiful than at any point in this entire history. She did not faint this time. She walked home, leaning lovingly on her cousin's arm, perhaps suffering a few more hysterical outbursts during the night, while Madame Bernstein slept soundly and remained unaware of the drama unfolding beside her.

"You are both free to go home," Harry said immediately. "Get my lady's hat and cloak, Betty, and Chaplain, we shall smoke a pipe together at our lodgings; it will refresh me after this ride." The chaplain, possessing a great deal of available sensibility, was utterly overcome. He burst into tears, seizing Harry's hand to kiss it and praying God to bless his dear, generous young patron. Mr. Warrington felt a thrill of pleasure course through his frame. It was good to alleviate suffering, to transform sorrow into joy. Proud and elated, the young champion marched beside his rescued princess, his hat cocked. His feelings seemed to materialize around him; beautiful happinesses with kind eyes and smiles danced before him, clothing him in a robe of honor and scattering flowers on his path. They led him up to the King's house and seated him in the hall of complacency upon cushions of comfort.

Yet, in truth, he had done little more than act kindly. He had merely reached into his pocket, used an easy talisman to dismiss the dragon guarding the gate, and caused the tyrant holding Lady Maria in execution to lay down his axe. Let his vanity puff itself up; he was good-natured. He had rescued two unfortunate souls and pumped tears of goodwill from their eyes. If he bragged tonight, swaggered before the chaplain, and spoke of London, Lord March, White's, and Almack's with the air of a macaroni, I do not believe we need dislike him any less.

Sampson remained prodigiously affected. This man possessed a nature easily worked upon, quick to receive pain and pleasure, tears, gratitude, laughter, hatred, and liking. In his preaching profession, he had trained his sensibilities to such an extent that they served him well; for the moment, he was exactly what he acted. He wept genuine tears, finding he could produce them freely. He loved those with whom he was present; he felt a real pang of grief mingling his sorrow with widows and orphans. Later, meeting Jack as he exited the door, he went to the tavern opposite and roared over a bottle. He gave money readily but never repaid debts. That night, he was in a rapture of gratitude and flattery toward Harry Warrington. In all London, perhaps, the unlucky Fortunate Youth could not have found a more dangerous companion.

In this grateful mood, full of enthusiasm for the benefactor who had released him from durance, Sampson's admiration grew with every bumper. He exalted Harry as the best and noblest of men, and the complacent young simpleton was disposed to accept these praises as well-deserved. "The younger branch of our family," said Mr. Harry with a superb air, "have treated you scurvily; but, by Jove, Sampson my boy, I'll stand by you!" At certain periods of Burgundian excitement, Mr. Warrington was always eloquent regarding the splendor of his lineage. "I am very glad I was enabled to help you in your strait. Count on me whenever you want me, Sampson. Did you not say you had a sister at boarding school? You will need money for her. Here is a bill which may help pay her schooling." The liberal young fellow passed a banknote across to the chaplain.

Once more, the man was brought to tears. Harry's generosity struck him deeply.

"Mr. Warrington," he said, pushing the banknote a short distance away, "I—I do not deserve your kindness—by George, I do not!" He swore an oath to corroborate his passionate assertion.

"Psha!" said Harry. "I have plenty more of them. There was no money in that confounded pocket-book which I lost last week."

"No, sir. There was no money!" said Mr. Sampson, dropping his head.

"Hallo! How do you know, Mr. Chaplain?" asked the young gentleman.

"I know because I am a villain, sir. I am not worthy of your kindness. I told you so. I found the book, sir, that night when you had too much wine at Barbeau's."

"And read the letters?" asked Mr. Warrington, starting up and turning very red.

"They told me nothing I did not know, sir," said the chaplain. "You have had spies about you whom you little suspect—from whom you are much too young and simple to keep your secret."

"Are those stories about Lady Fanny, and my cousin Will and his doings, true then?" inquired Harry.

"Yes, they are true," sighed the chaplain. "The house of Castlewood has not been fortunate, sir, since your honour's branch, the elder branch, left it."

"Sir, you don't dare for to breathe a word against my Lady Maria?" Harry cried out.

"Oh, not for worlds!" said Mr. Sampson, with a queer look at his young friend. "I may think she is too old for your honour, and that 'tis a pity you should not have a wife better suited to your age, though I admit she looks very young for hers, and hath every virtue and accomplishment."

"She is too old, Sampson, I know she is," said Mr. Warrington with much majesty; "but she has my word, and you see, sir, how fond she is of me. Go bring me the letters, sir, which you found, and let me try and forgive you for having seized upon them."

"My benefactor, let me try and forgive myself!" cried Mr. Sampson, and departed toward his chamber, leaving his young patron alone over his wine.

Sampson returned shortly thereafter, looking very pale. "What has happened, sir?" Harry asked with an imperious air.

The chaplain held out a pocket-book. "With your name in it, sir," he said.

"My brother's name in it," said Harry; "it was George who gave it to me."

"I kept it in a locked chest, sir, in which I left it this morning before I was taken by those people. Here is the book, sir, but the letters are gone. My trunk and valise have also been tampered with. And I am a miserable, guilty man, unable to make you the restitution which I owe you." Sampson looked the picture of woe as he uttered these sentiments. He clasped his hands together and almost knelt before Harry in an attitude of supreme pathos.

Who had been in the rooms during Mr. Sampson's and Mr. Warrington's absence? The landlady was ready to go on her knees, declaring that nobody had entered. Indeed, Mr. Warrington's chamber was not disturbed in the least, nor was anything abstracted from Mr. Sampson's scanty wardrobe and possessions except those papers of which he deplored the absence.

Whose interest was it to seize them? Lady Maria's? The poor woman had been a prisoner all day, and during the time when the capture was effected. She was certainly guiltless of the theft of the letters. The sudden seizure of the two documents—Case, the house-steward's secret journey to London, Case who knew the shoemaker at whose house Sampson lodged in London, and all the secret affairs of the Esmond family—these points, considered together and separately, might lead Mr. Sampson to believe that the Baroness Bernstein was at the bottom of this mischief. But why arrest Lady Maria? The chaplain knew nothing yet about the letter her ladyship had lost, for poor Maria had not thought it necessary to confide her secret to him.

As for the pocket-book and its contents, Mr. Harry was so swollen with self-satisfaction that evening—having won his three bets, rescued his two friends, and enjoyed a capital premature cold supper of partridges and ancient Burgundy sent over by obsequious Monsieur Barbeau—that he accepted Sampson's vows of contrition and solemn promises of future fidelity. He reached his gracious hand to the chaplain and condoned his offense. When the latter swore by his great gods that henceforth he would be Harry's truest, humblest friend and follower, ready to die for Mr. Warrington at any moment, Harry said, majestically, "I think, Sampson, you would; I hope you would. My family—the Esmond family—has always been accustomed to have faithful friends round about them—and to reward them too. The wine's with you, Chaplain. What toast do you call, sir?"

"I call a blessing on the house of Esmond-Warrington!" cried the chaplain, with real tears in his eyes.

"We are the elder branch, sir. My grandfather was the Marquis of Esmond," said Mr. Harry, in a voice noble but somewhat indistinct. "Here's to you, Chaplain—and I forgive you, sir—and God bless you, sir—and if you had been taken for three times as much, I'd have paid it. Why, what's that I see through the shutters? I am blest if the sun hasn't risen again! We have no need of candles to go to bed, ha, ha!" Extending his blessing once more to his chaplain, the young fellow went off to sleep.

About noon, Madame de Bernstein sent a servant to say she would be glad if her nephew would come over to drink a dish of chocolate with her. Our young friend rose and walked to his aunt's lodgings. She remarked, not without pleasure, some alteration in his toilette: in his brief sojourn in London, he had visited a tailor or two and been introduced by Lord March to some of his lordship's purveyors and tradesmen.

"Aunt Bernstein called him 'my dearest child,'" she thanked him for his noble, generous behavior to dear Maria. What a shock that seizure in church had been to her! A still greater shock that she had lost three hundred guineas on the Wednesday night to Lady Yarmouth and was quite a second to her. "Why," said the Baroness, "I had to send Case to London to my agent to get me money to pay—I could not leave Tunbridge in her debt."

"So Case did go to London?" said Mr. Harry.

"Of course he did: the Baroness de Bernstein can't afford to say she is court d'argent. Canst thou lend me some, child?"

"I can give your ladyship twenty-two pounds," said Harry, blushing very red; "I have but forty-four left till I get my Virginian remittances. I have bought horses and clothes, and been very extravagant, aunt."

"And rescued your poor relations in distress, you prodigal good boy. No, child, I do not want thy money. I can give thee some. Here is a note upon my agent for fifty pounds, vaurien! Go and spend it, and be merry! I dare say thy mother will repay me, though she does not love me." And she looked quite affectionate, holding out a pretty hand which the youth kissed.

"Your mother did not love me, but your mother's father did once. Mind, sir, you always come to me when you have need of me."

When bent on exhibiting them, nothing could exceed Beatrix Bernstein's grace or good humor. "I can't help loving you, child," she continued, "and yet I am so angry with you that I have scarce the patience to speak to you. So you have actually engaged yourself to poor Maria, who is as old as your mother? What will Madam Esmond say? She may live three hundred years, and you will not have wherewithal to support yourselves."

"I have ten thousand pounds from my father, of my own, now my poor brother is gone," said Harry, "that will go some way."

"Why, the interest will not keep you in card-money."

"We must give up cards," said Harry.
