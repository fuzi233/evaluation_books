"No one," said Durrance, his voice cutting through the humid stillness. He clicked the leather case shut, securing the field-glasses against his hip.

"No one, sir," Captain Mather replied, his tone matching the quiet certainty of the man beside him.

"We move forward."

The scouts surged ahead, the main body of troops coalesced into formation, and the two seven-pounder mountain guns rolled up behind them. Durrance's detachment of the Camel Corps descended from the gloomy, shadowed ridge of Khor Gwob—thirty-five miles southwest of Suakin—and plunged into the vast, sun-baked plateau of Sinkat. It was the final reconnaissance in force before the evacuation of the eastern Sudan.

Throughout that morning, the camels had jolted laboriously up a gully of shale, flanked by red precipices that seemed to scrape the sky. As the rocks fell away, the landscape dissolved into a desolation of crumbling stones and red mountain heaps. There was hardly a patch of grass or a ragged mimosa branch to break the monotony of ruin. Yet, after such an arid trek, the green bushes of Sinkat in the valley below offered a sudden, comforting relief, resembling a manicured park. The troopers sat their saddles with renewed alertness, their eyes scanning the horizon.

They moved in a diagonal line across the plateau toward the distant mountains of Erkoweet, a silent company traversing a plain that held its own silence. It was eleven o'clock. The sun burned toward the center of a colorless, cloudless sky, shortening the shadows of the camels until they lay stark and white upon the sand, which glistened like a beach of the Scilly Islands. There was no breath of air to rustle the rich foliage; the shadows of the branches lay so distinct and motionless upon the ground that they might have been fallen limbs strewn there by a storm of yesterday. The only sounds were the sharp clank of weapons, the soft, ceaseless padding of camel feet, and occasionally the whirr of pigeons startled by the approaching cavalcade.

Yet, life pulsed on the plateau, though in a noiseless rhythm. As the leaders rode along the curves of sand—trim and smooth between the shrubs like carriage drives—they would see, far ahead, herds of gazelle start from the earth and race silently, flashes of dappled brown and white, toward the enclosing hills. It seemed that in this last hour, the country had been created anew.

"Yet this way the caravans passed southwards to Erkoweet and the Khor Baraka," Durrance said aloud, voicing a thought that had crossed his mind. "Here the Suakis built their summer-houses."

"And there Tewfik fought," Mather pointed forward, his voice low. "And died with his four hundred men."

For three hours, the troops marched across the plateau. It was May, and the sun blazed with an intolerable heat. Alertness had long since evaporated; the men rocked drowsily in their saddles, praying for the cooling evening and the silver shine of stars. The camels minced on with their peculiar, smirking head motions, until suddenly, a hundred yards ahead, Durrance saw a broken wall with window-spaces letting the sky through.

"The fort," he said.

Three years had passed since Osman Digna had captured and destroyed it, yet during those years, its roofless ruins had sustained another siege, one no less persistent. Quick-growing trees had so closely girt and encroached upon it from the rear and sides that the traveler found it unexpectedly, much like Childe Roland finding the Dark Tower in the plain. In the front, however, the sand still stretched open to the wells, where three great Gemeiza trees of dark, spreading foliage stood spaced like sentinels.

In the shadow to the right front of the fort, where bushes fringed the open sand with the level regularity of a river bank, the soldiers unsaddled their camels and prepared their food. Durrance and Captain Mather walked around the perimeter. As they reached the southern corner, Durrance halted.

"Hallo!"

"Some Arab has camped here," Mather noted, stopping in his turn. Grey ashes of a wood fire lay in a little heap upon a blackened stone.

"And lately," Durrance added.

Mather walked on, mounting a few rough steps to the crumbled archway of the entrance, passing into the unroofed corridors and rooms. Durrance turned the ashes over with his boot. The stump of a charred, whitened twig glowed red. Durrance stepped on it, and a tiny thread of smoke spurted into the air.

"Very lately," he murmured, following Mather inside. Young trees sprouted in the corners of the mud walls, in every fissure, and even in the floor itself. Rearward, a steep glacis and a deep fosse defended the works. Durrance sat upon the parapet above the glacis while pigeons wheeled overhead. He thought of the long months during which Tewfik must have strained his eyes from this very spot toward the pass over the hills from Suakin, looking as that other general far to the south had done, searching for sunlight flashing on the weapons of help that never came. Mather sat by his side, reflecting in a quite different spirit.

"Already the Guards are steaming out through the coral reefs toward Suez," Mather said. "A week and our turn comes. What a God-forsaken country!"

"I come back to it," Durrance replied.

"Why?"

"I like it. I like the people."

Mather found the taste unaccountable, yet he knew that, however strange, it accounted for his companion's rapid promotion and success. Sympathy had stood in Durrance's stead for ability. It had given him patience and the power to understand, allowing him to leave far quicker and far abler men behind him in his knowledge of the sorely harassed tribes of the eastern Sudan. He liked them; he could enter into their hatred of the old Turkish rule, their fanaticism, and the pretense of fanaticism forced upon them by Osman Digna's hordes.

"Yes, I shall come back," Durrance said firmly. "In three months' time. For one thing, we know—every Englishman in Egypt knows—that this can't be the end. I want to be here when the work is taken in hand again. I hate unfinished things."

The sun beat relentlessly upon the plateau; the men, stretched in the shade, slept. The afternoon was as noiseless as the morning. Durrance and Mather sat for some while, compelled to silence by the surrounding quiet. But Durrance's eyes finally turned from the amphitheater of hills; they lost their abstraction, becoming intently fixed upon the shrubbery beyond the glacis. He was no longer recalling Tewfik Bey or speculating on future works. Without turning his head, he saw that Mather was gazing in the same direction.

"What are you thinking about?" he asked suddenly.

Mather laughed, answering thoughtfully. "I was drawing up the menu for the first dinner I'll have when I reach London. I'll eat it alone, I think, quite alone, at Epitaux. It will begin with a watermelon. And you?"

"I was wondering why, now that the pigeons have grown used to our presence, they should still be wheeling in and out of one particular tree. Don't point to it, please! I mean the tree beyond the ditch, to the right of two small bushes."

All about them, pigeons perched quietly upon the branches, spotting the foliage like purple fruit. Only above the one tree did they circle, calling timorously.

"We will draw that covert," Durrance ordered. "Take a dozen men and surround it quietly."

He remained on the glacis, watching the tree and the thick undergrowth. He saw six soldiers creep round the shrubbery from the left, six more from the right. But before they could meet and ring the tree in, he saw the branches shake violently. An Arab, with a roll of yellowish dammar wound about his waist and armed with a flat-headed spear and a shield of hide, dashed from the shelter and raced out into the open plain between the soldiers. He ran only a few yards. Mather gave a sharp order, and the Arab, as though understanding the command, came to a halt before a rifle could be lifted to a shoulder. He walked quietly back to Mather. He was brought up onto the glacis, standing before Durrance without insolence or servility.

He explained in Arabic that he was a man of the Kabbabish tribe named Abou Fatma, friendly to the English, on his way to Suakin.

"Why did you hide?" Durrance asked.

"It was safer. I knew you for my friends. But, my gentleman, did you know me for yours?"

Then Durrance said quickly, "You speak English," and switched languages.

The answer came without hesitation. "I know a few words."

"Where did you learn them?"

"In Khartum."

Thereafter, he was left alone with Durrance on the glacis, and the two men talked for the better part of an hour. At the end of that time, the Arab was seen descending the glacis, crossing the trench, and proceeding toward the hills. Durrance gave the order for the resumption of the march.

Water-tanks were filled, men replenished their zamshyehs, knowing that of all thirsts in this world, the afternoon thirst is the very worst. Saddled their camels, and mounted to the usual groaning and snarling. The detachment moved northwestward from Sinkat, at an acute angle to its morning's march, skirting the hills opposite to the pass from which it had descended. The bushes grew sparse. They entered a black country of stones, scantily relieved by yellow tasselled mimosas.

Durrance called Mather to his side. "That Arab had a strange story to tell me. He was Gordon's servant in Khartum. At the beginning of 1884, eighteen months ago in fact, Gordon gave him a letter which he was to take to Berber, whence the contents were to be telegraphed to Cairo. But Berber had just fallen when the messenger arrived there. He was seized upon and imprisoned the day after his arrival. But during the one day which he had free, he hid the letter in the wall of a house, and so far as he knows, it has not been discovered."

"He would have been questioned if it had been," Mather said.

"Precisely, and he was not questioned. He escaped from Berber at night, three weeks ago. The story is curious, eh?"

"And the letter still remains in the wall? It is curious. Perhaps the man was telling lies."

"He had the chain mark on his ankles," Durrance countered.

The cavalcade turned left into the hills on the northern side of the plateau and climbed again over shale.

"A letter from Gordon," Durrance said in a musing voice, "scribbled perhaps upon the rooftop of his palace, by the side of his great telescope—a sentence written in haste, and his eye again to the lens, searching over the palm trees for the smoke of the steamers—and it comes down the Nile to be buried in a mud wall in Berber. Yes, it's curious," and he turned his face to the west and the sinking sun. Even as he looked, the sun dipped behind the hills. The sky above his head darkened rapidly to violet; in the west, it flamed a glory of colors rich and iridescent. The colors lost their violence and blended delicately into one rose hue; the rose lingered for a little, and, fading in its turn, left a sky of the purest emerald green transfused with light from beneath the rim of the world.

"If only they had let us go last year westward to the Nile," he said with a sort of passion. "Before Khartum had fallen, before Berber had surrendered. But they would not."

The magic of the sunset was not at all in Durrance's thoughts. The story of the letter had struck a chord of reverence within him. He was occupied with the history of that honest, great, impracticable soldier, who, despised by officials and thwarted by intrigues, a man of few ties and much loneliness, had gone unflaggingly about his work, knowing all the while that the moment his back was turned, the work was undone in an instant.

Darkness came upon the troops; the camels quickened their pace; the cicadas shrilled from every tuft of grass. The detachment moved down toward the well of Disibil. Durrance lay long awake that night on his camp bedstead spread beneath the stars. He forgot the letter in the mud wall. Southward, the Southern Cross hung slanting in the sky; above him glittered the curve of the Great Bear. In a week he would sail for England. He lay awake, counting up the years since the packet had cast off from Dover pier, and found that the tale of them was good. Kassassin, Tel-el-Kebir, the rush down the Red Sea, Tokar, Tamai, Tamanieb—the crowded moments came vividly to his mind. He thrilled even now at the recollection of the Hadendowas leaping and stabbing through the breach of McNeil's zareba six miles from Suakin; he recalled the obdurate defense of the Berkshires, the steadiness of the Marines, the rallying of the broken troops. The years had been good years, years of plenty, years which had advanced him to the brevet-rank of lieutenant-colonel.

"A week more—only a week," murmured Mather, drowsily.

"I shall come back," said Durrance, with a laugh.

"Have you no friends?"

And there was a pause.

"Yes, I have friends. I shall have three months wherein to see them."
