Briarmains, being the nearest refuge, became Robert Moore’s sanctuary. Mr. Yorke, with rough tenderness, bore the wounded man to his home and saw him settled in the finest bed the house could offer. There was something in the sight of Moore—proud, vigorous, now struck down—that stirred Yorke’s hardened heart. Helplessness had a way of softening even the sternest of men, and Moore, pale and silent, was entirely at his mercy.

For a man like Yorke, power was intoxicating. Here was a life in his hands—a life that depended on his will, his decisions. It was a heady sensation. And his wife, that iron-nerved matron, thrived on the drama. Lesser women might have shuddered at the sight of blood staining their floors, but Mrs. Yorke was no ordinary woman. She had scolded her daughters for lesser offenses, yet here she stood, commanding the household like a general, forbidding all but the necessary from disturbing her patient.

Even Louis Moore, Robert’s brother, was permitted entry—though grudgingly. He was allowed to sit by the bedside, to press his brother’s hand, to whisper reassurances. Mrs. Yorke tolerated it, though she watched like a hawk. Once, she even rose before dawn to prepare a meal for the brothers herself, wrapped in a vast flannel robe, her nightcap askew, observing their every bite with the satisfaction of a hen overseeing her brood.

Yet woe betide any servant who dared interfere. The cook was reprimanded for sending up gruel without permission; a maid was chastised for presuming to assist Louis with his coat. When two anxious ladies came pleading to see Moore, they were turned away with sharp words. Only Hortense Moore, with her shared disdain for wayward servants, found any favor.

But then came the misstep—bandages disturbed, blood lost anew. The surgeon, MacTurk, arrived in a storm of indignation, his temper as volatile as his skill was precise. He berated his assistants, spared no words for the meddling women, and then, at dawn, relinquished his patient to the mercies of Mrs. Horsfall—a woman built like a fortress, stern as a jailer, and obedient only to MacTurk’s orders.

Under her reign, all others withered. Hortense retreated, Mrs. Yorke was eclipsed, and Moore himself became little more than a body to be tended—or tormented, as Martin Yorke liked to imagine. The boy fancied the nurse a tyrant, withholding food, perhaps even striking his helpless charge. It amused him to think so.

Yet winter deepened, and Moore lingered, trapped between pain and recovery, his world reduced to the four walls of his sickroom.

***

The season turned, frost etching the world in silver and blue. On one such crystalline evening, a lone figure moved through the woods—a boy of fifteen, slight and sharp-eyed. Martin Yorke had no use for poetry, or so he claimed, yet here he was, drawn to the quiet beauty of the winter dusk.

Seated on a mossy mound, he pulled a forbidden book from his satchel—fairy tales, not Latin. The fading light and the rising moon were enough. He read of enchanted mountains and sea spirits until a sound disturbed him—footsteps.

A woman emerged from the trees, veiled and silent. At first, Martin barely noticed her, but then she turned back, lifting her veil. Caroline Helstone.

Martin, though he would never admit it, flushed. *"I am."*

The directness disarmed him. He nodded.

She asked after his family, after Hortense, all with polite restraint—until she dared mention Moore.

Martin, mischievous, spun a tale of doom. *"Dying. Thanks to the women fussing over him."*

Caroline went very still. Then, without another word, she walked away.

He hadn’t expected that. No tears, no dramatics—just silence. It unsettled him. He chased after her.

But Caroline, pale and trembling, leaned against a tree. *"You almost killed me with that lie."*

For once, Martin felt a pang of guilt. He offered clumsy comfort, even guiding her home through the woods, helping her over walls, half-scolding, half-pleased to be of use.

Before they parted, she made him swear Moore was truly recovering. He relented, spinning more wild tales of the fearsome nurse who guarded him. Caroline listened, thoughtful.

At home, Martin endured his punishment—dry bread and a lecture—with smug satisfaction. The night had been worth it.

Before bed, he paused before two portraits in the dining room—one serene, the other hauntingly sorrowful.

*"She looked like that,"* he murmured, recalling Caroline’s stricken face.

And with that, he climbed the stairs, plotting tomorrow’s meeting beneath the trees.
