The sun, our Lord, was sinking toward the horizon, casting long shadows across the sea, while smoke from a distant burning mountain darkened the sky like a curse. Phorenice wrung out her soaked clothes and shivered in the evening chill. “Row hard, Deucalion,” she said, her voice sharp with impatience. “Take me through the water-gate and back to my comforts. That merchant would weep if he saw his fine garments ruined—and I, too, mourn, being a woman, that he has no others I might claim.”

She turned her gaze on me, tossing back her fiery red hair. “What do you think of my wisdom—coming here alone, without guards?”

“The Empress can do no wrong,” I replied, quoting the old saying with a faint smile.

“At least I’ve shown you I can fight,” she retorted. “I saw you watching me—not with disapproval, but with approval. You were hard to warm up to, Deucalion, but now you’re thawing. La, sir—we may yet become a pair of country lovers. I’m glad I chose to go near those smelly fishers.”

So she had brought me out unattended not for safety, but to provoke a fight—to test her strength, and perhaps mine. If we were to live together as husband and wife, it was wise to know each other’s limits. And though I’d spent a lifetime in battle, I could not deny the thrill of facing a worthy opponent. Still, I couldn’t help comparing her to another woman. When faced with the same choice, Nais had spared the rebels out of pity—she didn’t even call herself their leader. But here, this Empress wasted two dozen loyal men just to satisfy a whim.

Yet, bound by my vows as a priest and sworn to serve the High Council on the Sacred Mountain, I pushed such thoughts aside. As I rowed through the swaying waves toward the harbor forts, I prayed to the High Gods for eloquence, and they answered—for Atlantis’ sake, and for the people who suffered under tyranny. My words flowed freely, and Phorenice watched me with glowing eyes.

The fisher’s beach where we’d fought was safe from attack thanks to a jagged chain of rocks rising from the deep, stretching from the harbor wall to the city’s edge. The fishermen knew the hidden paths, able to slip through without touching stone—or leap ashore and carry their boats over the reef when danger loomed. But I knew neither the route nor the skill. I thought: *Now, High Gods, show whether you will allow this woman, who has offended you, to rule Atlantis—and whether I am meant to stand beside her.*

I spoke these words in silence, waiting for an omen. None came—until a great wave rose in the lagoon behind us, stirred by an unseen earth tremor. It lifted us high and carried us forward, sweeping us past the reef without so much as a scrape of stone. I bowed my head as I rowed on. Clearly, the gods had not yet decided to punish her—or me.

At the harbor mouth, sentries beat drums in turn, their routine interrupted only by a pair of officers on the rooftop turret of the nearer fort. They recognized our voices and silenced the drums. Torches were lowered to inspect our faces, and then—with a splash—the great chain dropped into the water, granting us passage.

A galley waited inside, moored against the harbor wall. A rope ladder descended from the fort, and a crew hurried down to take the oars. We were given dry clothes—gifts from the officers—as we changed in the galley’s cabin. Wet garments after sunset invite fever, and though I knew remedies, I noted with grim amusement that Phorenice, despite her divine pretensions, feared mortal sickness as any other woman might.

The galley cut swiftly across the calm harbor, docking at the golden rings of the royal quay. While we waited for litters, I noticed a lantern lit in the boat guarding Phorenice’s mammoth. The enormous beast stood knee-deep in the water, trunk raised. It was tamed now, its body trembling slightly beneath the glow of the light. Yet I refused to plead for it. If it sank into the mud and vanished, I would accept it calmly.

Truth be told, riding that beast had unsettled me. It made me feel powerless, helpless—something I rarely allowed myself to experience. Perhaps I had grown too self-reliant; others might judge. But I confess: I prefer walking on my own feet, as the gods intended. On foot, I can protect myself. On that monstrous animal, I felt like a child, afraid of every step.

But there was little time for reflection. As we waited, Phorenice asked the port captain—eagerly approaching to pay his respects—about Nais. He reported that she had been thrown into a dungeon beneath the royal pyramid, and the guard commander had sworn to keep her secure.

“I hope he knows his duty,” said the Empress. “That girl knows the pyramid better than most. If he locks her in a cell with a secret escape, he’ll end up before the tormentors. Would you be pleased if she escaped, Deucalion?”

“Certainly,” I said. There was no point hiding it. “I have no hatred for Nais.”

“But I do,” she snapped. “And I still intend to bind your loyalty to me with that wedding gift you know of.”

“It shall be done,” I said. “Before all, before the Empress of Atlantis.”

“Poof! Deucalion, you’re far too stiff and formal. Shouldn’t you be flattered that I’m jealous of your affections? Give me your hand, sir, to help me into the litter. Now come beside me, and keep me warm. Ho! Guards with torches—keep back against the walls. Your perfume is stifling me.”

That night, another feast filled the royal banqueting hall. Again, I sat beside Phorenice on the raised dais beneath the symbols of the snake and the outstretched hand. What had once been assumed—our union—was now declared openly. She named me her future husband, and the court hailed me as greater than themselves, chosen by her grace. Their salute was deliberate. Some rumor must have spread of my preference for older customs, for no one drank wine in my honor after the new, flashy fashion. Instead, each nobleman and noblewoman approached, spilling a goblet at my feet. When I offered food from my platter, they ate simply, without kissing or pocketing it.

The jets of earth-breath flickered above, revealing simpler clothing, soberer faces. Phorenice leaned close, her curls brushing my cheek, whispering mockingly, “Oh, sir, don’t make your rule too ascetic. I gave no orders for this change—but tonight there are no perfumes, the food is plain, and I half want to burn the cook. And look at the men’s beards—half the curl is gone. By my face! If you keep setting trends, they’ll shave clean tomorrow, just like yours.”

“Why stop them if they prefer it?”

“No, sir. Only one clean chin shall exist in all Atlantis—and it shall belong to the man who weds the Empress. Would you have no sumptuary laws? Would you let common folk imitate us in every fold of cloth, every twist of hair? Come now—if we claimed power only by strength and wit, they’d hate us as arrogant. But if we keep certain signs of rank, they admire and envy us, not resent.”

“You show me there’s more to rule than meets the eye.”

“And yet they say you ruled well.”

“I used the older ways. It takes a Phorenice to invent finer things.”

“Flatterer!” she teased, tapping my arm with her fingers. “You’re becoming a courtier. You make me blush with your smooth talk. No fan-girl here to cool my cheek—I’ll choose another. Not Ylga. She seems to favor you too much. If she’s wise, she’ll retire to her palace on the other side of the city, manage her slaves, embroider silks. I won’t be harsh unless she forces me—but I’ll not have any woman treat you with undue kindness.”

“And how should I act,” I asked, playing along, “when all the men of Atlantis swear their devotion to you? You admit they love you fiercely.”

“Oh, don’t ask me for miracles,” she said. “I am powerful—but I cannot command hearts. But if you wish, proclaim that I am now yours, body and soul. If they offend your pride, take them to the tormentors. Indeed, Deucalion, I’d find it pleasing if you did so. It seems the jealousy is all on one side.”

“I suppose you expect a man who’s lived without love to master its subtleties overnight. I’m proud of my progress. With any other teacher than you, Phorenice, I wouldn’t be nearly this far. In fact—by my record—I might never have begun.”

“Do you think I’d be satisfied with that? I’m not. I can be greedy in certain matters.”

The banquet ended early. Phorenice had endured much since last she slept. Though she claimed divinity, her body remained mortal. Dark circles framed her radiant eyes. She slumped among the cushions, limbs heavy. When dancers approached, she tossed them a jewel and dismissed them mid-performance. The poet, a pompous fool singing of the day’s deeds, she refused to hear.

“Tomorrow,” she said wearily. “For now, grant me peace. Deucalion has given me much to think about. I go to my chamber to plan the future of this realm. Tomorrow, come again. If your poetry is good and brief, I’ll reward you handsomely. But if you’re long-winded, I’ll pay you with the stick.”

She rose, and I led her from the hall through secret passages to her private chambers. She clung to my arm. Once, as a massive stone door swung open, she rested her head on my shoulder. Her breath warmed my cheek, and her beauty—so near—defied description. I sensed she expected me to kiss her lips, so close they brushed mine. I wanted to play my part, but I could not bring myself to it. When the door closed, she sighed and walked on in silence.

“May the High Gods treat you kindly,” I said at her chamber door.

“I am my own God,” she replied, “in all things but one. By my face! You’re a slow wooer, Deucalion. Where are you going now?”

“To my own chamber.”

“Oh, go then.”

“Is there anything else I could do?”

“Nothing your mind or will would suggest. Yes, indeed—you’re decorous in your old-fashioned way. But you’re a poor wooer. Don’t you know a woman values some things more when they’re taken by force?”

“It seems I know little of women.”

“You never said truer. Bah! And I believe your coldness helps you more than passion ever could. Go, then. If the air of a maiden’s bedchamber offends your modesty, leave. May your gods watch over you—if they can. Go, man, and leave me alone.”

Before the feast, I had studied the pyramid’s layout from the archives. Now, I navigated its maze with ease. I was weary, but sleep was impossible. Nais must be sacrificed—partially—for Atlantis. But I had a plan to save her life, and time was short.

Unexpected help arrived. As I passed through the narrow corridors between the pyramid’s stones, a figure emerged from shadow. When I raised my lamp, I saw Ylga.

She looked at me with bitterness. “I’ve lost my place,” she said. “She meant to have you all along. And you—you think me meddlesome. You never gave me a second thought.”

“I never forget kindness.”

“Oh, you’ll learn that trick soon. And you’re marrying her? The city speaks of it. I thought you honest. But when power comes with a woman, you’re no different. I thought you distrusted red hair. And she’s small.”

“Ylga,” I said, “you taught me these walls are full of ears. I’ll hear no word against Phorenice. But I need to speak with you soon. If you still care for me, go to my chamber and wait. I’ll join you.”

She lowered her eyes. “What do you want?”

“I need to tell you something. You’ll learn who it concerns later.”

“But—is it proper for a maiden to visit a man’s room at this hour?”

“I know little of your customs. I am Deucalion. If you still hesitate, remember that, and stay away.”

She looked up, sneering. “I was foolish. Your coldness is legend. Very well—I’ll come.”

“Go then,” I said, watching her vanish. With Ylga’s aid, my burden lessened. First, I needed to reach the temple of our Lady the Moon—a sacred chamber within the temple itself. As one of the Seven, I had access.

All temples in Atlantis connect secretly to the royal pyramid, but these paths are known only to the Seven and the Three above them—those few who have mastered the highest mysteries. Even they use them only in dire need. Generations may pass without a single foot stepping on them.

I struggled through darkness, testing my way until I found the hidden alley. Once inside, the path was clear. I had never walked it before, but the map from my initiation returned to memory. I moved quickly—both because danger lurked in those depths and because time slipped away.

I reached the lookout post and found the temple empty, dusty, untouched for years. A massive stone swung outward, revealing the entrance. After the required prayer, I retrieved the metal stair and climbed into the lap of the Goddess. Then, pulling the stair behind me, I ascended further until my body lay against her serene, mysterious face.

A chill ran through me. Even hardened warriors hesitate when tampering with life and death—especially when the fate of a nation and a woman dear to one’s heart hang in balance. But time pressed. I grasped the jewel set in her forehead and, with my other hand, prodded the corner of her eyebrow. With a soft creak, the massive stone eye swung inward. I stepped off the stair and entered the hollow space within her head.

It was the first time I’d seen this secret chamber, yet its layout matched what I’d learned. I knew exactly where to find what I sought. Still, I marveled at the ingenuity of its concealment.

Though I ranked high in the Priestly Clan, I did not know the true nature of what I now held. Before, I’d only known its power. Now, in my hands, were three rough spheres—like nut kernels, grass-green, hard as beeswax. I took the one I needed, leaving the others undisturbed. It might have been a drug—or something more. I didn’t know. But its effects were clear from my training. I placed it in a pouch and retraced my steps, restoring everything as I found it.

Before leaving, I cast one final glance at the statue. The eternal jet of earth-breath illuminated her from crown to toe, glittering from the jewel in her brow. Vast and peaceful, she embodied the quiet rest many seek in vain—peace attainable only through piety, in the afterlife where our Lady the Moon and the High Ones reign forever.

Exhausted, I returned to the pyramid and entered my chamber. Ylga waited, hidden at first, suspicious of modern times. She crouched until she confirmed I came alone.

“Oh, frown if you wish,” she muttered. “I care nothing for your opinion now. I heard tales of Deucalion—thought I saw honesty. But now I see you’re no better. She offers you power, and you marry her gladly. Why not? She’s pretty. I’ve seen her warm and languishing for dozens. Clever with her eyes, too. And you—it thrills you to be courted.”

“I think you’re a very foolish woman,” I said.

“If you believe it doesn’t matter whom you marry, you’re letting pride blind you.”

“Listen,” I said. “I didn’t summon you to argue. I called you to help save someone of your own blood.”

She stared. “I don’t understand.”

“It rests with you whether Nais dies tomorrow—or falls into a sleep from which she may wake one day, in happier times.”
