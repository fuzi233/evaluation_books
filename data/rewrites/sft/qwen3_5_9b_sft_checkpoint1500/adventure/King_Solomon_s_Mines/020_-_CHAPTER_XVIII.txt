The horror of that night defies adequate description. Though sleep, a cruel respite for weary flesh, offered brief mitigation, I found myself largely wakeful. The terror of our impending doom—a fate that would cause the bravest soul to tremble—hung heavy over us. Yet, it was not merely the dread of death that kept me alert; it was the silence. You may know the oppression of a quiet night, but you cannot conceive the physical weight of perfect stillness. Upon the earth, some sound or motion always exists to blunt the edge of absolute silence. Here, buried deep within the bowels of a snow-capped giant, there was none. Above us, thousands of feet high, the wind swept across the snow, unheard. Separated from the Chamber of the Dead by a tunnel and five feet of unyielding rock, and knowing that the dead make no noise, we were severed from every echo of the living world. We were, in essence, already in the grave.

Irony, bitter and sharp, clawed at my mind. Surrounding us lay riches sufficient to settle a nation's debts or forge fleets of ironclads, yet we would trade them all willingly for a sliver of hope. Soon, surely, we would prize a crust of bread or a sip of water above all else, eventually welcoming the swift release of our suffering. Wealth, pursued with such fervor throughout a lifetime, revealed itself to be a vanity at the final hour.

As the night dragged on, Sir Henry's voice broke the silence, sounding grotesque in the void. "Good," he croaked, "how many matches remain in the box?"
"Eight, sir," replied Good.
"Strike one. Tell us the time."
The flame flared, momentarily blinding us, revealing 5:00 AM by my watch. Far overhead, the dawn was painting the snow-wreaths pink, and the breeze would soon stir the mist in the hollows.
"We must eat to sustain our strength," I urged.
"What use is food?" Good muttered, his spirit crushed. "The sooner we die, the better."
"While there is life, there is hope," Sir Henry insisted gently.
We ate sparingly, sharing a little water, and time slipped by once more. Then, Sir Henry proposed we crawl toward the sealed door, shouting on the slender chance that someone might hear us. Good, accustomed to the piercing calls of the sea, ventured forth. Alas, his cries, born of desperation, sounded more like the frantic buzzing of mosquitoes than human voices. They achieved little, save to exhaust our dwindling water supply.

Exhausted, we collapsed once more against the useless chests of diamonds, succumbing to a paralysis of despair. I leaned my head against Sir Henry's broad shoulder, tears bursting from me, convinced that I heard Good sobbing and swearing beside me. Ah, how noble and gentle that great man remained! Even as children in peril, he would not treat us less tenderly. He forgot his own misery to soothe ours, spinning tales of miraculous escapes from similar terrors. When these failed to lift our spirits, he pointed out that death, however it came, was inevitable and perhaps merciful—an assertion I knew to be false, yet clung to. Finally, he suggested surrendering to a Higher Power, a plea I accepted with fervor. His character was quiet, yet unbreakably strong.

Thus, day succeeded night, indistinguishable in the gloom. When I finally lit a match, it was 7:00 AM. After another meal, an idea sparked within me.
"How does the air remain fresh?" I asked. "It feels thick, heavy, yet perfectly pure."
"Great heavens!" Good jumped up. "I never considered that. It cannot seep through the airtight stone door. Where does it come from? If there were no current, we would have suffocated instantly upon entry. We must investigate."
This single spark of hope transformed us. Within moments, we were crawling on hands and knees, searching for the slightest draft. My search ended abruptly when my hand brushed something cold: the face of the dead Foulata.
For an hour, we probed the chamber, until Sir Henry and I, battered by collisions with tusks and chests, yielded in frustration. Only Good persisted, grimacing with a feigned cheer.
"Fellows," he called, "approach."
We scrambled forward.
"Curtis," he commanded, "place your hand where mine rests. Do you feel anything?"
"I... I think I feel air rising."
"Now listen." Good stomped firmly on the spot, and a sudden flame of hope ignited in our hearts. *It rang hollow.*
Trembling, I struck a match. With only three left, we realized we were trapped in the far corner of the chamber, explaining why we had missed the hollow resonance during our earlier, frantic searches. As the flame flickered, we scrutinized the floor. There, flush with the rock, was a stone ring. Speechless with excitement, hearts pounding, Good produced his knife, fitted with a hook for extracting stones from horse hooves. He carefully scraped around the ring, leveraging it gently. The stone moved; centuries had not rusted it fast as iron would have. He lifted it upright, thrust his hands inside, and tugged. Nothing.
"Let me try," I said, impatient. The geometry of the corner allowed no space for two. I strained, but failed. Sir Henry attempted next, equally unsuccessful.
Good returned to the scratch mark where the air had risen. "Curtis," he said, "take hold and pull with everything you have. You are twice as strong. Stop." He unwound a black silk handkerchief, a habit of his meticulous nature, threaded it through the ring. "Curtis, wrap Quatermain around the middle. Pull when I signal. *Now*."
Sir Henry unleashed his immense strength; Good and I followed suit. "Heave! It's moving!" Sir Henry gasped, his back muscles straining audibly. A grinding roar, a rush of air, and we were thrown backward onto the floor, pinned beneath a heavy flagstone. Sir Henry's raw power had won.
"Light a match, Curtis," he ordered, recovering his breath.
I did, and there, blessed by God, lay the **first step of a stone stair**.
"Now, what is to be done?" Good asked.
"Ascend the stairs and trust to Providence," Sir Henry declared.
"Wait!" Sir Henry interjected. "Curtis, retrieve the remaining biltong and water; we may need them."
I crept back to gather the provisions. As I turned, a thought struck me. For twenty-four hours, the diamonds had been a nausea-inducing memory, but reflection told me I should pocket some, just in case. I filled the pockets of my coat and trousers, topping them with handfuls of large gems from the third chest. I also stuffed Foulata's nearly empty basket with stones.
"Fellows," I called out, "will you take some diamonds? I've filled my pockets and the basket."
"Oh, hurry, Curtis!" Sir Henry exclaimed, already stepping onto the stair. "May I never see another!"
Good said nothing. I suspected he was bidding a silent farewell to the memory of the girl who had loved him. To anyone sitting safely at home, pondering this vast wealth, it seems absurd to abandon it. But after twenty-eight hours without sustenance in that hellish place, I assure you, you would not wish to burden yourself with stones while fleeing an agonizing death. If habit had not drilled it into me to never leave worth behind, I likely would have left them all.
"Come on, Curtis," Sir Henry repeated, descending slowly, counting the steps. "Caution; beware of holes."
"More likely another room," Sir Henry noted.
At fifteen steps, he halted. "Bottom," he announced. "I believe it is a passage. Follow me."
Good followed, then I, bearing the basket. At the base, I lit a match. We stood in a narrow tunnel intersecting the stairs at right angles. Before the flame died, consumed by my fingers, we faced the dilemma: which way to turn? Left or right? Both paths led into the unknown. Perplexed, Good observed that when I lit the match, the draft had blown the flame to the left.
"Air draws inward, not outward," he reasoned. "Let us go against the flow."
Feeling along the walls, testing the ground, we departed the accursed chamber, embarking on a terrible quest for life. Should any living soul return to this place, they will find open chests of jewels, an empty lamp, and the white bones of Foulata.
Groping for a quarter-hour, the tunnel took a sharp turn, then split, forcing us into a labyrinthine network of galleries. Hours passed in a stone maze that seemed to lead nowhere. These must be the ancient workings of a mine, shifting like veins as ore dictated the path.
Finally, utterly spent, we devoured our last scrap of biltong and drank our final sip of water, our throats parched like kilns. Escaping the treasure chamber only to confront Death in the tunnels, we stood depressed. Then, I heard it—a faint, distant murmur.
"It's running water," Good whispered, his voice trembling. "Come on."
We resumed our journey toward the sound. I laid down the basket, wishing to rid myself of its weight, but reconsidered; better to die
