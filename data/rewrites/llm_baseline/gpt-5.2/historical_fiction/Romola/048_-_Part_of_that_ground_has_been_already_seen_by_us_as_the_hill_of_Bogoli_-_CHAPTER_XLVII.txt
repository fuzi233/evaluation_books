Tito’s contrivances—those neatly tied knots by which he loved to draw other men into the shape of his own advantage—had been fretted loose by accidents as light as wind-sown seed. It was seldom he chose to pace the evening streets with Romola; yet on this one night, when her company was the one inconvenience he could not afford, she had walked beside him. Florence was a board where a clever player set his pieces with dry fingers—and then some breath, some child’s cry, some door opening at the wrong moment, sent the whole array trembling.

It was not that the failure of Dolfo Spini’s violence troubled him in itself. What stung was the necessity of fitting his next move to two different watchers without letting either see the seam. A warning must go to the Frate; an explanation must pacify Spini; and both must be so timed that no one should say Tito’s hands had been in the dough from the first.

If he went straight to San Marco before the monks slept, his warning would press too close upon the delivery of the forged letters—close enough to make even the dull suspicious. If he ran at once to Spini, he must give the true reason for the warning, since he could not yet pretend that he had only just discovered Savonarola’s change of purpose; and Spini’s mind, like a dog’s, did not weigh motives—only scents. It would smell at once that Tito had turned, that he had thwarted, that he had betrayed the betrayers. Yet if he waited until morning to speak to Savonarola, the chance to soften Spini beforehand would be gone; the Compagnacci would come back balked and raging, and their rage, once awake, did not know how to lie down again.

That risk, however, was the one Tito chose. He trusted to his own voice, to the oily patience that could stroke anger into a semblance of calm; he told himself he could always soothe Spini by making the failure look like nothing more than the Frate’s over-caution. Still, he was annoyed—annoyed enough that, had he been forced to smile, the effort would have shown.

He resolved he would not meet Romola again that night. He did not go home.

Romola watched until dawn without loosening her dress. The sound of the rain thickened, then deepened—drops multiplying into a steady drumming that filled the house as if the very walls had learned a dark chant. She found herself listening as one listens to a hearth-fire when the world outside grows dangerous: the rain made a roof of noise, a broad, sheltering roar that seemed to hold men back from running their errands of cruelty. Under that broad percussion her thoughts went on working, sharper and sharper, like a knife that will not be laid down.

It was not only doubt of Tito that possessed her now, but doubt of herself—doubt that bit deeper because it had her own voice. What lie might he not have told? What design might he not be carrying, still unguessed? Everyone who trusted him, she felt, stood in peril; and it was childish to lull herself with any hope to the contrary. Yet was she, out of pride—out of the shrinking delicacy that called itself duty—refusing to warn men because the warning would shame her as a wife?

“If he is a malefactor, my place is in prison by his side.” That claim she could meet without trembling. But could she, because she was a wife, let him become a malefactor—let him do the injuries that would make the prison his due—when perhaps a word of hers might prevent it?

Prayer would not come. The posture of the soul that waits, open-handed, expecting—she could not reach it. Her mind moved too feverishly, turning and turning like a mill that cannot stop because the stream is swollen. As the night wore on, her imagination, quickened into disease, made schemes within schemes: ways in which Tito might still evade, still strike, still leave her warnings too late. And then, toward daybreak, the rain slackened. The hard battering on the tiles became a thinner patter, then only a drip. A breeze rose, swept the last clouds away, and the morning fell clear on roofs and towers as if nothing in the world had been threatened.

That calm made her unrest unbearable. Noise had been a veil; now the city’s waking sounds came cleanly through it—the first carts creaking, the first shutters thrown back, the voices beginning their day as if safety were a habit. She wrapped her mantle about her and ran up to the loggia, as if the breadth of the landscape might answer her, as if beyond the line of roofs there might be some visible token—some moving speck—telling her whether Savonarola walked toward safety or toward a snare. But the roofs hid the streets; and the streets were precisely where peril travelled.

If she went to her godfather, might she not stir him—without betraying Tito by name—into measures that would keep Fra Girolamo within the gates? But that might already be too late. Bitterly she remembered how she had failed to wring from Tito any detail that could guide her, any hour, any message, any name; and the time was slipping. It was already long past seven.

She must go to San Marco. There was nothing else.

She hurried down the stairs, went out into the street without pausing to look in on the sick folk she had made part of her daily duty, and walked swiftly along the Via de’ Bardi toward the Ponte Vecchio. She would cut through the heart of Florence—not only because it was the straightest road, but because the great Piazza offered one desperate chance: she might meet Tito. Some last remnant of longing—half hope, half the old hunger to be spared—clung to the thought that he might satisfy her that the Frate was safe, and she could turn back without exposing her own terrible knowledge.

Before the Palazzo Vecchio she looked eagerly into the pillared court; then her eyes swept the Piazza. But the well-known figure—once painted in her heart by young love, and now branded there by gnawing pain—was nowhere.

She pressed on to the Piazza del Duomo. The square was already alive: worshippers mounting and descending the marble steps; men halted in knots of talk; market-people shifting their burdens, the smell of damp wicker and wet cloth rising from baskets, and the faint sourness of yesterday’s greens. Somewhere a vendor’s cry rang out and was answered; the morning’s soundscape gathered itself like a net—voices, footsteps, the rumble of wheels—each note distinct in the clean air after rain.

Between moving figures Romola caught sight of Tito.

He had been at San Marco; now he had turned into Nello’s shop, and stood there as if the morning were made for his ease. He leaned against the door-post, talking with the air of a man who had slept well. His cap was in his hand; he shook back his freshly-combed hair with that small flourish which always suggested that even his gestures had been practised before a mirror.

The sight struck Romola like the sudden taste of something bitter in the middle of bread. After her night of watching—after the drum of rain and the inner tumult—his smoothness, his untroubled manner among the ordinary commerce of the day, convulsed her with indignation. And it heightened her dread: hardness like that could do anything.

She recognised Cronaca and two other frequenters of San Marco standing near him. A thought flashed, clear and cold: I will force him to speak before them. Let his words be bound by their hearing.

Her step, light with haste and resolution, brought her close before Tito could move away. Cronaca, turning, said simply, “Here comes Madonna Romola.”

A slight shock went through Tito as if a hand had touched the back of his neck. For an instant his skill deserted him, and then he gathered it again, needing all his self-command to hold his face in a look that should be no more than surprise.

Romola was haggard from her sleeplessness; but in her eyes there was now something that was not merely anxiety—a flash as if a blade had caught the morning light. She did not waste breath.

“Is the Frate gone beyond the gates?”

“No,” Tito said. The word came out steadily enough; yet he felt helpless before her in a way he had seldom felt before any human being.

“And you are certain that he is not going?” she insisted, as if she meant to nail the certainty down with the witness of the men beside him.

“I am certain that he is not going.”

“That is enough,” Romola said.

She turned at once and went up the steps, seeking refuge in the Duomo—some shadowed space where she might gather herself, where the vast hush might take her beating heart and make it quiet again, away from this bright, noisy square and the intolerable composure of the man who had made her night a torment.

Tito watched her retreating figure with a feeling that came nearer hatred than anything he had known. The men around him were not only true followers of the Frate. There was Ser Ceccone too—the notary—who, like Tito himself, served the Mediceans in secret. Ser Francesco di Ser Barone—called by those who knew him only by the shorter name that clung to him like dirt, Ser Ceccone—was no scholar, no handsome man, no favourite of fortune, and generosity was as foreign to him as music to a stone. He was a traitor without charm.

And for that very reason, he was not fond of Tito Melema.
