To secure that single, tentative handshake, I endured three years of silent negotiation. For the first half-year, I dwelt in Deptford, a repository of infinite potential where tragedy and pathos lay scattered like autumn leaves, waiting for the wrong kind of gatherer. Yet, I was shackled by debt—a sum that swelled from one hundred and fifty pounds to nearly two hundred when the arithmetic was forced upon me. Thus, I adopted a rigid discipline of commerce, leaving the cultivation of tears to those more naturally equipped for sorrow. I confined myself to my own narrow plot, harvesting only the harvest of laughter and offering it to the market.

Initially, every manuscript found its way to Norah. She transcribed my words, deducted her fee, and remitted the balance. My earnings were modest at first, but Norah proved an astute agent, and the sums swelled rapidly. Dan, however, grew irritable, penning letters of pained astonishment at her perceived avarice. "The matter is fair," he argued, "but in no way remarkable." He was eager to assist any of her acquaintances, yet he drew a hard line: business was business. In deference to his proprietors, he refused to pay above market value. Miss Deleglise, responding with curt precision in the third person, found herself in perfect harmony with Mr. Brian regarding the sanctity of commerce. If Mr. Brian could not afford her price for material of such excellence, other editors, whom she knew equally well, certainly could and would. An answer by return post would greatly oblige; pending receipt, she retained the manuscript in her possession. Mr. Brian, realizing he had encountered his match, grumbled but paid. Whether he suspected the identity of "Jack Homer," he never confessed; yet, he would have played the game, pulling his share of the rope regardless of the outcome. Nor was he permitted to settle the matter alone. Competition was deliberately introduced into the equation. With purposeful strategy, a portion of my work was dispatched elsewhere. "Jack Homer" evolved into a commodity in high demand. Seated at my rickety table, I laughed as I wrote, the fourth wall of my dismal room dissolving to reveal vistas beyond.

Nevertheless, the pace was glacial. Humor is not an industrious maid; she refuses to be hustled, working only when inclination strikes—and rarely does it strike. She affects unnecessary airs, packing her bags and vanishing into the ether when worried, only to return when she decides to resume. Consequently, I found it necessary to supplement my literary labors with journalism. I lacked Dan's magnificent assurance; fate never favors the nervous. Had I burst into the editorial sanctum, the editor would surely have fled; if he remained, he would have been a man of short temper, seeing to it that I departed swiftly. Such boldness was unthinkable; even Robert Macaire, in my solitary coat, would have hesitated, apologetic and diffident. I thus joined the ranks of the penny-a-liners—to be precise, three-halfpenny-a-liners. Alongside half a dozen other shabby outsiders—some young men like myself striving to climb, others older men who had sunk—I attended inquests and police courts. We chased fire engines, rejoiced in street accidents, and yearned for murders. Like vultures, we lived precariously upon the misfortunes of others, occasionally earning half-crowns by publishing scandal and crowns by withholding it.

"I think, gentlemen," our spokesman would explain in a hoarse whisper upon returning to the table, "I believe the corpse's brother-in-law is anxious that the affair, if possible, remain out of the papers."

We followed such cases with remarkable closeness and attention; our notes were exhaustive. Then, the spokesman would rise, drift carelessly away, and return five minutes later, wiping his mouth. "Not a very interesting case, gentlemen, I don't think. Shall we say five shillings apiece?" Sometimes, a sense of professional dignity would induce us to stand firm for ten.

Here, my sense of humor provided an undue advantage over my competitors. Twelve good men and true had been summoned to determine how a Lascar sailor had met his death. It was perfectly evident: a plumber, working on the roof of a small two-story house, had slipped and fallen upon him. The plumber escaped with minor bruises; the unfortunate sailor was picked up dead. Blame attached to the plumber. His mate, an excellent witness, recounted the entire tale.

"I was fixing a gas-pipe on the first floor," the man stated. "The prisoner was on the roof."

"We won't call him 'the prisoner,'" interrupted the coroner, "at least, not yet. Refer to him, if you please, as the 'last witness.'"

"The last witness," the man corrected himself. "He shouted down the chimney to ask if I was ready for him."

"'Ready and waiting,' I said."

"'Right,' he said; 'I'm coming in through the window.'"

"'Wait a bit,' I said; 'I'll go down and move the ladder for you.'"

"'It's all right,' he said; 'I can reach it.'"

"'No, you can't,' I said. 'It's the other side of the chimney.'"

"'I can get round,' he said."

"Well, before I knew what had happened, I heard him go, *smack!* I rushed to the window and looked out. I saw him on the pavement, sitting up like."

"'Hullo, Jim,' I said. 'Have you hurt yourself?'"

"'I think I'm all right,' he said, 'as far as I can tell. But I wish you'd come down. This bloke I've fallen on looks a bit sick.'"

The others headed their flimsy report "Sad Accident," a title truthful but uninspiring. I altered mine to "Plumber in a Hurry—Fatal Result." Saying as little as possible about the unfortunate sailor, I directed the attention of plumbers generally to the coroner's very just remarks upon the folly of undue haste, pointing out to them collectively the trouble that would ensue if they could not cure themselves of rushing through their work without a moment's pause.

This established a useful reputation for me. The sub-editor of one evening paper condescended so far as to emerge in his shirt-sleeves to shake my hand. "That's the sort of thing we want," he told me; "a light touch, a bit of humor."

I snatched fun from fires (I sincerely trust the insurance premiums were current); I culled quaintness from street brawls; I extracted merriment from the most painful catastrophes, and I prospered.

Though often within a stone's throw of the street, I unremittingly avoided the old house at Poplar. I was suffering a peculiar inconvenience at this period, finding myself divided into two distinct individuals contending with one another. My objective was to encourage the new Paul—the sensible, practical, pushful Paul, whose career began to look promising—and to drive away from interfering with me his strangely unlike twin, the old childish Paul of the sad, far-seeing eyes. Sometimes, from the cracked looking-glass, his wistful, yearning face would plead with me. But I would sternly shake my head. I knew his cunning well. Had I yielded to him, he would have led me through the maze of streets he knew so intimately, past the broken railings (where outside he would have left my body standing), along the weedy pathway, through the cracked and dented door, up the creaking staircase to the dismal little chamber where we once—he and I together—had sat dreaming foolish dreams.

"Come," he would whisper. "It is so near. Let us push aside the chest of drawers very quietly, softly raise the broken sash, prop it open with the Latin dictionary, lean our elbows on the sill, and listen to the voices of the weary city, voices calling to us from the darkness."

But I was too wary to be caught. "Later on," I would reply, "when I have made my way, when I am stronger to withstand your wheedling. Then I will go with you, if you are still in existence, my sentimental little friend. We will dream again the old impractical, foolish dreams—and laugh at them."

Thus, he would fade away, replaced by a businesslike-looking, wide-awake young fellow who nodded approvingly.

Yet, to one sentimental temptation I ultimately succumbed. My position was now assured; there was no longer any reason for my concealment. I determined to move westward. I had not intended to soar so high, but passing through Guildford Street one day, the creeper-covered corner house that my father had once considered taking recalled itself vividly to me. A card rested in the fanlight. I knocked and made inquiries. A bed-sitting-room on the third floor was vacant. I remembered it the moment the loquacious landlady opened the door.

"This shall be your room, Paul," said my father. So clearly his voice sounded behind me that I turned, forgetting for the moment it was but a memory. "You will be quiet here, and we can shut out the bed and washstand with a screen."

So my father had his way. It was a pleasant, sunny little room overlooking the gardens of the hospital. I followed my father's suggestion, shutting out the bed and washstand with a screen. And sometimes, in the evenings, it amused me to hear my father turn the handle of the door.

"How are you getting on—all right?"

"Famously."
