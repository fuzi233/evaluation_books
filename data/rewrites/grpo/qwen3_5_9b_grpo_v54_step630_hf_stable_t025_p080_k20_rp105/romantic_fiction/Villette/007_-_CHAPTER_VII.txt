Morning broke with a sudden, clarifying light that seemed to wash away the heavy fog of my previous night. I awoke not merely rested, but invigorated; the physical weakness that had clouded my judgment was gone, leaving my mind sharp and alert.

As I finished dressing, a sharp rap echoed at the door. Expecting the chambermaid, I called out, "Come in," but the figure that entered was a rough-looking man.

"Give me your keys, Meess," he demanded.

"Why?" I asked, startled.

"Give!" he snapped impatiently. As he reached out, his fingers brushing roughly against mine to snatch the keys, he added, "All right! Have your trunk soon."

Miraculously, it turned out all right. He was from the customs house, checking for contraband. I knew I needed breakfast, but the innkeeper offered no guidance. Hesitant, I descended the stairs.

It was only now, as I climbed down the broad, sweeping staircase, that I truly saw the scale of the establishment. Last night, in my exhaustion, I had missed it; this was no mere lodging house, but a grand hotel. I paused on each step, reluctant to leave the sanctuary of my room, gazing upward at the soaring ceilings, the painted walls, the vast windows flooding the space with light, and the veined marble beneath my feet—cold, uncarpeted, and slightly dusty. The contrast between this opulent public sphere and the tiny, sparsely furnished closet I had occupied was stark. It sent me into a philosophical reverie.

I marveled at the uncanny sagacity of the servants. How did they instantly discern that I was a woman of no social standing, carrying little cash? They knew. In a split second, they categorized me, assigning me a fractional value that matched my appearance. The realization was humbling, yet I forced myself to maintain my composure.

Finally, I emerged into a great hall blinding with skylight glare. Navigating the crowd, I found the coffee room. Entering, a tremor shook me; I felt solitary, wretched, and acutely unsure of my path. It felt like the precipice of a final decision. Adopting the calm of a fatalist, I sat at a small table. A waiter soon delivered my breakfast, which I consumed with a mind far from conducive to digestion. The room was filled with men; not a single woman was present. Yet, no one seemed to notice my eccentricity. A few gentlemen glanced my way, perhaps attributing my presence to the label "Anglaise."

Once the meal was done, the question arose: Where next? An inner voice whispered, "Go to Villette." The memory of Miss Fanshawe's careless remark surfaced: *"I wish you would come to Madame Beck's; she has some marmots whom you might look after; she wants an English gouvernante."*

I knew nothing of Madame Beck, her location, or her reputation. Miss Fanshawe had fled before answering my questions. Yet, I presumed Villette was her home. Forty miles away lay my destination. I was grasping at straws in a vast, churning sea of uncertainty, but in such depths, one would clutch at cobwebs if necessary. After inquiring about transport and securing a seat on the diligence, I departed on the flimsiest of plans.

To the reader who might judge my rashness: consider where I began. Consider the desert I had left behind. What did I truly risk? In a game where the player cannot lose and stands to gain everything, the stakes were low.

Though I lack an artistic temperament, I possess the artist's ability to savor the present moment when it suits my taste. That day, despite the slow journey, the biting cold, and the relentless rain, I found joy. The landscape was barren and flat, treeless save for formal pollard willows lining fields tilled like kitchen gardens. Slimy canals wound alongside the road like torpid green snakes. The sky was a monotonous gray, the air stagnant and humid. Yet, amidst these deadening influences, my imagination bloomed, and my heart basked in an internal sunshine. These feelings, however, were constantly checked by a secret, ceaseless anxiety, like a tiger crouching in the jungle. Its breath was in my ear; its heart beat against mine. It waited only for sunset to spring from its ambush.

I had hoped to reach Villette before nightfall, avoiding the disorientation of arriving in the dark. But our progress was sluggish, plagued by long stops, a thick fog, and a dense, soaking rain. By the time we reached the city's suburbs, a darkness so profound it could almost be touched had settled over Villette.

We passed through a gate guarded by soldiers, visible only in the lamplight, then rattled over a pavement of strangely rough, flinty stones. The diligence stopped at a bureau, and passengers disembarked. My immediate concern was my trunk. I understood the protocol: do not be eager, wait quietly for others' luggage to be unloaded, then claim yours promptly. I stood apart, watching the vehicle. Piles of bags and boxes were heaped onto the roof. One by one, they were lowered and seized.

Mine should have been visible by now. I had tied a green ribbon to the direction card for easy identification. There was not a shred of green anywhere. Every package was gone; every tin case and brown-paper parcel removed. The oilcloth cover was lifted, revealing an empty space. No umbrella, cloak, cane, hat-box, or band-box remained.

Where was my portmanteau, containing my few clothes and the remnant of my fifteen pounds?

I ask this question now, but I could not ask it then. I possessed no speaking French, and the entire world seemed to be gabbling a language I did not understand. What should I do? I approached the conductor, laid my hand on his arm, pointed to the empty space on the roof, and tried to convey my distress with my eyes. He misunderstood, grabbing a different trunk and preparing to hoist it back up.

"Let that alone—will you?" a voice commanded in perfect English. Then, correcting himself, "Qu'est-ce que vous faîtes donc? Cette malle est à moi."

I recognized the accent of the Fatherland; it brought a surge of relief to my heart. Turning to the stranger, I appealed to him, ignoring his appearance in my distress: "Sir, I cannot speak French. May I entreat you to ask this man what he has done with my trunk?"

He looked at me with an expression of half-surprise and half-doubt regarding the wisdom of interfering.

"Do ask him; I would do as much for you," I urged.

Whether he smiled, I do not know, but he spoke in a gentlemanly, non-threatening tone. "What sort of trunk was yours?"

I described it, mentioning the green ribbon. Immediately, he took the conductor aside. Through the ensuing storm of French, I felt him questioning the man thoroughly. Soon, he returned.

"The fellow claims he was overloaded and admits removing your trunk after you saw it placed on the roof. He has left it behind at Boue-Marine with other parcels, but promises to forward it tomorrow. Consequently, you will find it safe at this bureau the day after tomorrow."

"Thank you," I said, but my heart sank.

Meanwhile, what should I do? Perhaps the English gentleman noticed the failure of courage in my face. Kindly, he asked, "Have you any friends in this city?"

"No, and I don't know where to go."

There was a brief pause. As he turned fully toward the lamplight, I saw he was a young, distinguished, and handsome man. He might have been a lord; nature had crafted him well enough for a prince. His face was pleasant, high-born yet not arrogant, manly but not overbearing. I was turning away, acutely aware that I had no claim to expect further aid from someone of such stature.

"Was all your money in your trunk?" he asked, stopping me.

How grateful I was to answer truthfully: "No. I have enough in my purse"—I had nearly twenty francs—"to keep me at a quiet inn until the day after tomorrow. But I am quite a stranger in Villette and do not know the streets or the inns."

"I can give you the address of an inn suitable for you," he said. "It is not far off; with my directions, you will easily find it."

He tore a leaf from his pocket-book, wrote a few words, and handed it to me. I thought him kind. To distrust him, his advice, or his address would have been as foolish as distrusting the Bible. Goodness radiated from his countenance, and honor shone in his bright eyes.

"The shortest way is to follow the Boulevard and cross the park," he continued. "But it is too late and too dark for a woman to traverse the park alone. I will walk with you thus far."

He moved on, and I followed, through the darkness and the soaking rain. The Boulevard was deserted, its path miry, water dripping from the trees; the park was black as midnight. In the double gloom of trees and fog, I could not see my guide, only following the rhythm of his footsteps. I felt no fear whatsoever. I believed I would have followed that frank tread, through endless night, to the end of the world.

"Now," he said once the park was crossed, "you will go along this broad street until you come to steps. Two lamps will show you where they are. Descend these steps; a narrower street lies below. Following that, at the bottom, you will find your inn. They speak English there, so your difficulties are now pretty well over. Good-night."

"Good-night, sir," I replied, "accept my sincerest thanks." We parted.

The memory of his face, which bore a light benign to the friendless, and the sound of his voice, which spoke a chivalric nature to the needy and the weak, became a sort of cordial to me long afterward. He was a true young English gentleman.

I hurried fast through a magnificent street and square, surrounded by grand houses and the looming outlines of overbearing piles that might have been palaces or churches. Just as I passed a portico, two mustachioed men emerged suddenly from behind the pillars, smoking cigars. Their dress pretended to gentility, but their souls were plebeian. They spoke insolently and kept pace with me, fast as I walked, for a long distance. Finally, I encountered a patrol, and my dreaded hunters were turned away. But they had driven me beyond my reckoning. When I could collect my faculties, I no longer knew where I was; the staircase I must have passed long ago was lost to me. Puzzled, out of breath, my pulses throbbing with inevitable agitation, I did not know which way to turn. It was terrible to think of encountering those bearded, sneering simpletons again, yet the ground had to be retraced, the steps sought out.

I finally arrived at an old, worn flight of stairs. Assuming this must be the one indicated, I descended. The street into which they led was indeed narrow, but it contained no inn. I wandered on. In a very quiet, comparatively clean, and well-paved street, I saw a light burning over the door of a rather large house, loftier by a story than those surrounding it. This might be the inn at last. I hastened forward; my knees trembled beneath me; I was growing quite exhausted.

It was not an inn. A brass plate adorned the great porte-cochère: "Pensionnat de Demoiselles" was the inscription, and beneath it, a name: "Madame Beck."

I started. A hundred thoughts volleyed through my mind in an instant. Yet I planned nothing, considered nothing; I had no time. Providence seemed to whisper, "Stop here; this is your inn." Fate took me in her strong hand, mastering my will and directing my actions. I rang the doorbell.

While I waited, I refused to reflect. I fixed my gaze on the street stones where the door-lamp shone, counting them, noting their shapes, and observing the glitter of wet on their angles. I rang again. Finally, they opened. A *bonne* in a smart cap stood before me.

"May I see Madame Beck?" I inquired.

I believe if I had spoken French, she would not have admitted me. But since I spoke English, she concluded I was a foreign teacher on business connected with the pensionnat. Even at that late hour, she let me in without a word of reluctance or a moment of hesitation.

The next moment, I sat in a cold, glittering salon with an unlit porcelain stove, gilded ornaments, and a polished floor. A pendule on the mantelpiece struck nine o'clock.

A quarter of an hour passed. How fast every pulse in my frame beat! How I turned cold and hot by turns! I sat with my eyes fixed on the door—a great white folding door with gilt moldings—watching for a leaf to move and open. All was quiet; not a mouse stirred; the white doors remained closed and motionless.

"You ayre Engliss?" a voice said at my elbow. I almost jumped; so unexpected was the sound, so certain had I been of solitude.

No ghost stood beside me, nor anything of spectral aspect. Merely a motherly, dumpy little woman in a large shawl, a wrapping-gown, and a clean, trim nightcap.

I said I was English, and immediately, without further prelude, we fell to a most remarkable conversation. Madame Beck (for it was Madame Beck—she had entered by a little door behind me, shod in the shoes of silence, so I had heard neither her entrance nor approach) had exhausted her command of insular speech with the phrase "You ayre Engliss," and now proceeded to work away volubly in her own tongue. I answered in mine. She partly understood me, but as I did not understand her at all—though we made together an awful clamor (anything like Madame's gift of utterance I had not hitherto heard or imagined)—we achieved little progress.
