Gerard felt a hunger for this promised land nearly as keen as Denys's. His companion, however, was a man whose patriotism bordered on the absurd; he sang the praises of Burgundy at every turn, using minor inconveniences to prove that "things were done better" across the border. Above all, he exploited Gerard's vanity regarding hygiene, promising the spotless linens of a polished nation. "Ask no more!" the Hollander would declare, his face contorted with disgust at the idea of bare bedsheets. "To think I have not slept in a naked bed since leaving home! When I look at their linen, I wish my eyes and nose were closed forever."

Denys carried this love of country to extremes. He once walked twenty leagues in shoes that had long since disintegrated rather than accept hospitality from a German peasant who might offer anything from poor civility to a subpar meal.

As the sun began to dip below the horizon, they stood equidistant between a roadside tavern and a monastery standing slightly off the path. Denys favored the inn; Gerard preferred the convent. Denys yielded, but on the strict condition that whenever they entered Burgundy again, they would stay exclusively at inns. Gerard agreed, for his chart listing the local monasteries had reached its final page. Turning off the main road, Gerard asked in genuine surprise why Denys suddenly despised the very houses that had fed and sheltered them without charge.

The soldier hesitated, stammering at first, before the dam broke. It transpired that this was not a sudden dislike, but an ancient, deep-seated horror he had suppressed until now, largely out of politeness. "I suspected they had put poison in your drink," Denys explained, his voice rising with indignation. "So I avoided them. But since this is our last stop, why not clear my conscience? I have lived like a fish out of water in all those great dungeons. You rise early with some old friar, so you never witness my purgatory."

"I beg forgiveness," Gerard said humbly. "I have been selfish."

"Nonsense, little one! Forgive me, not you. Art thou not the first fool to be rid of a priest and hit by a monk? But I shall not forgive *them* their misery." With the gravity of a prosecutor centuries before Henry VIII's commissioners, Denys laid out his indictment. These gloomy structures were all identical monuments to boredom. Inns varied, but here everything was a monotonous loop: a great gate, a small gate, endless steps leading to a shadowy cloister. There lay the dormitory, followed by the vast, freezing refectory where one sat in silence, forced to mumble words or risk speaking aloud.

"And then," Denys continued, pacing, "no one is truly human there; everyone is a slave to a petty, tinkling bell that never sleeps. If it were a trumpet blaring alarms, it might not freeze a man's heart quite so. *Tinkle, tinkle,* and you must sit to eat with perhaps no appetite. Before the food settles, *tinkle, tinkle!* and you must march to church with perhaps no spirit for devotion. I am not a hog at prayers, take my word for it. *Tinkle, tinkle,* and now you must lie to bed with your eyes wide open. By the time you manage to close them, some restless imp of darkness pulls the bell-rope, and *tinkle, tinkle!* forcing you to pray in the dark, knowing nothing of the liturgy. If they could hear the gibberish I muttered when they shattered my rest with those bells! Then you drift off, gaining perhaps a wink of sleep... and lo, *tinkle, tinkle* for matins."

"And the only clapper you respect is a woman's?" Gerard interjected, half-contemptuous.

"Because there is some music in that, even when she scolds," Denys retorted stoutly. "But worse is the constant checking. If I merely dip my finger in the salt-cellars, immediately I hear, 'Hast thou no knife wherein to touch the salt?' And if I dare wipe my blade on the cloth to save time, the accusation follows: 'Thou wipest thy knife dirty on the bread, and clean upon the cloth!' Oh, souls so small! These trivial pedantries fall like chilling iceicles upon good fellowship, melting slowly from strawen eaves."

"I hold cleanliness to be virtue, not pedantry," Gerard argued gently. "Thou shouldst learn better manners once and for all."

"Nay! It is they who lack manners. They stop a fellow's tongue at every breath."

"At every other word, you mean; specifically every obscene or blasphemous utterance."

"An exaggerator, go to! At the very last of those dungeons, I found poor travelers sitting chilled and silent around a single friar, looking like rogues awaiting execution. To lift their spirits, I simply shouted, 'Courage, *tout le monde*, *le diable*—"

"*Connu!* What happened next?" Gerard interrupted sharply.

"Marry, this. '*Blaspheme not!*' quoth the executioner. '*Plait-il?*' I asked. Did he not wheel and weep at me in some sort of Alsatian French, twisting every 'P' into a 'B'? I had much ado not to laugh right in his face."

"Being yourself unable to speak ten words of his tongue without a fault," Gerard teased.

"Well, all the world ought to speak French. What use are so many jargons except to build walls between men's hearts?"

"But what did he say?"

"What matters it what a fool speaks?"

"Oh, not all the words of a fool constitute folly, or I should not listen to thee."

"Well, then, he declared: 'Those who begin by playing freely with the Devil's name, yea, end by doing so with all the names in Heaven.' 'Father,' said I, 'I am a soldier, and this is but my *consigne*, my watchword.' 'Oh, then, it is but a custom?' he asked. Not seeing through the old fox, thinking to clear myself, I said, 'Yea, it was.' 'Then that is ten times worse,' he replied. 'It will bring about thine ears one of these days. He still comes where he hears his name often called.' Mark this! No gratitude for the tidings which neither his missals nor his breviary had ever taught him. Then, with a courtesy that belied his nature, he informed me that soldiers commonly commit the crimes for which all other men are broken on the wheel: murder, rape, and pillage."

"And is it not true?"

"Truth or falsehood, it was bad manners," Denys replied guardedly. "And so says this courteous host of mine: 'Being foes of mankind, why make enemies of good spirits too, by still shouting the names of evil ones?' and a host of similar drivel."

"Well, Denys, whether he heed thy advice or scorn it, why blame a man for raising his voice to save thy soul?"

"How can his voice save my soul when he keeps turning his Ps into Bs?"

Gerard was stunned. Before he could recover from this thunderbolt of Gallicism, Denys spun triumphantly off on another tangent, labeling all monks as hypocrites. "Look at them," he sneered. "How they creep about, unable to eye thee like honest men."

"Nay," Gerard countered eagerly, "that modest, downcast gaze is part of their discipline, known as *custodia oculorum*."

"Cursed toads eating *hoc hac horum*? Nonsense! That is how a cutpurse looks. A true man cannot meet thy eye. Doff thy cowl, monk, and behold a thief; don thy cowl, thief, and lo, a monk. Tell me not they will ever be able to look God Almighty in the face when they cannot even look a true man in the face down here."

He paused, staring into the distance as if hearing something invisible. "Ah, here it is, black as ink! Into the well we go, comrade. *Misericorde*, there goes the tinkle already. 'Tis the best of tinkles, though; 'tis for dinner: stay, listen! I thought so: the wolf in my stomach cried 'Amen!'" He confirmed this last statement with two oaths and marched forward like a victorious gamecock into the convent gates, believing by Gerard's silence that he had won the argument, entirely unaware of how profoundly he had repelled him.
