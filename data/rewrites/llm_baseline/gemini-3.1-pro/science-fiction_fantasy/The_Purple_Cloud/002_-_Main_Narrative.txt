Three weeks—it was about that—before the thunderous acoustics of that Sunday night discourse reverberated through my chest, I stood in my private kitchen at No. II, Harley Street, meticulously skimming the impurities from a simmering broth of veal marrow and wild thyme. It was then I was visited by Clark, the chief of the coming expedition. A mere visit of friendship, yet it altered the cadence of my life. I had been established in my practice for about a year, and though under twenty-five, I catered to the restorative diets and holistic ailments of Europe's elite. 

Elite, but small. I maintained my state, sourcing rare truffles and ancient grains to nourish my demanding palate and my patients, but the secret pinch of moneylessness occasionally soured the quiet domestic rhythm of my days. In truth, I had only been saved from profound financial embarrassment by the success of my book, *Applications of Science to the Arts*, a text wherein I argued for the profound psychological meaning of historical culinary techniques and the restorative acoustics of a quiet, agrarian-style kitchen.

In the course of conversation that afternoon, piercing the restorative silence of my home with his loud, hasty syllables, Clark said in his haphazard way:

"Do you know what I dreamed about you last night, Adam Jeffson? I dreamed that you were with us on the expedition."

I think he must have seen my start, the sudden clatter of my wooden spoon against the earthenware pot. On the same night, I had myself dreamed the same thing; but not a word did I offer. There was a stuttering hesitation in my tongue, a sudden failure of the dominant lexicon to capture my subjective dread, when I answered:

"Who? I?—on the expedition? I would not go, if I were asked."

"Oh, you would."

"I wouldn't. You forget that I am about to be married."

"Well, we need not discuss the point, as Peters is not going to die," said he. "Still, if anything did happen to him, you know, it is you I should come straight to, Adam Jeffson."

"Clark, you jest," I said. "I know really very little of astronomy, or magnetic phenomena. Besides, I am about to be married..."

"But what about your botany, my friend? Your deep knowledge of foraging and historical diets? *There's* what we should be wanting from you: and as for nautical astronomy, poh, a man with your meticulous scientific habit would pick all that up in no time."

"You discuss the matter as gravely as though it were a possibility, Clark," I said, offering a strained smile as the aromatic steam of the broth rose between us. "Such a thought would never enter my head: there is, first of all, my *fiancée*—"

"Ah, the all-important Countess, eh? Well, but she, as far as I know the lady, would be the first to force you to go. The chance of stamping one's foot on the North Pole does not occur to a man every day, my son."

"Do talk of something else!" I said. "There is Peters..."

"Well, of course, there is Peters. But believe me, the dream I had was so clear—"

"Let me alone with your dreams, and your Poles!" I laughed. 

I pretended to laugh loud, adding to the abrasive noise of the modern city beyond my window. But my secret heart knew that one of those mythic crises was occurring in my life. And I knew this because, when Clark was gone and I was drawing on my gloves to go see my *fiancée*, I heard distinctly the old two Voices talk within me. They spoke not in the sterile, flattened English of modern commerce, but in an ancient, untranslatable dialect that resonated through my auditory nerves like the grinding of a heavy stone mortar. One said: *Go not to see her now!* and the Other: *Yes, go, go!*

An ordinary person might imagine I mean only contradictory impulses, for what modern man, deafened by the industrial hum of factories and engines, could comprehend the acoustic reality of those voices? They contended within me, a dual sonic resonance "nearer than breathing," as the poem says.

It first happened around the age of seven. I was playing one summer evening in a pine-wood of my father's, surrounded by the deep, organic acoustics of rustling needles and snapping twigs. Half a mile away was a quarry-cliff. Suddenly, it seemed someone whispered inside me: *Just take a walk toward the cliff*; and another, in a softer, agrarian cadence: *Don't go that way at all*. I did go toward the cliff; I fell thirty feet. Weeks later, I told my astonished mother that "someone had pushed me" and someone else "had caught me" at the bottom.

By my eleventh birthday, I realized my life was marked by two Powers. One wished to preserve me, the other to destroy me. I felt I was a creature of a different, older era. I understood perfectly well how the ancients heard the voice of the divine; originally, man possessed a far more attuned acoustic ecology, listening to the mythic occurrences woven into the mundane rustle of the harvest. 

Not a creature ever dreamed of this internal conflict until I went to Cambridge. There, the agony of strife shouted in my soul—whether to be a scientist, an artist, a preserver of the old ways. I chose medicine, viewing the body as a vessel requiring perfect, ancestral sustenance. At Cambridge, I met a man named Scotland. He was a sweet, gentle soul, a meticulous food historian and traditionalist who kept heirloom seeds, adored cats, and read Sappho in her original, dying tongue. He despised the noise pollution of the modern era and claimed the universe was frantically contended for by a White and a Black Power. The Black Power, he argued, was the creeping, sterile industrialization of the world, and it was slowly silencing the White Power’s ancient, nourishing song. I drank in his words with an agony of inward interest.

But I was saying that when Clark left me, I was drawing on my gloves to visit Clodagh. The urgency of the Voice that bid me go was a deafening roar. Traversing the distance to Hanover Square, the city's metallic screech grated against my tympanum, while the Voices clashed: *Breathe no word of the Boreal!* and *Tell, tell, hide nothing!*

Soon, I was in Hanover Square, and Clodagh was in my arms. 

She was superb—that haughty neck that seemed always scorning something just behind her shoulder. But Clodagh was a woman of bitter heart, possessing a palate that craved the sharp tang of dominance rather than the warmth of true sustenance. She once confessed her favorite historical figure was Lucrezia Borgia. How thoroughly she enthralled me, though her worldview was utterly disconnected from the agrarian rhythms I revered!

Our proposed marriage was opposed by both our families. Hers loathed me because I lacked noble blood, doubly detesting me since her older sister had already made a *mésalliance* by marrying a common country doctor, Peters of Taunton. From that union came Peter Peters, currently slated as the doctor and botanist for the *Boreal*.

I had not been seated five minutes with Clodagh when I surrendered to my wicked impulse.

"Dr. Clark—ha! ha! ha!—has been talking to me about the Expedition. He says if anything happened to Peters, I should be the first man he would run to. He had an absurd dream..."

Clodagh stood at a window, inhaling the olfactory depths of a hothouse rose. The subtle power dynamics embedded in her silence filled the room. Finally, in her cold, rapid, upper-class lexicon, she said:

"The man who first plants his foot on the North Pole will certainly be ennobled. I say nothing of the many millions... I only wish that I was a man!"

"I don't know that I have any special ambition that way," I rejoined, thinking of my kitchen, the slow simmer of root vegetables, my quiet Eden. "I don't like the outer Cold."

"Don't let me think little of you!" she answered pettishly.

"Why should you, Clodagh? I am not bound to desire to go to the North Pole."

"But you *would* go, if you could?"

"I might... I doubt it. There is our marriage."

"Marriage indeed! It is the one thing to transform our marriage from a sneaking difficulty to a ten times triumphant event."

"You mean if *I* personally were the first to stand at the Pole... but that is very unlikely."

"For *me* you will, Adam—" 

"*Will*, Clodagh?" I cried. "There is not the slightest probability!"

"But why? There are still three weeks. They say..." She stopped. 

"They say what?"

Her voice dropped to a conspiratorial whisper, lacking all musicality: "That Peter takes atropine."

I started. Atropine—the bitter, dark essence drawn from the roots of the deadly nightshade (*Atropa belladonna*). It was a potent botanical, one that tasted of rich, fatal earth, meant only to be handled by those who respected its lethal gastronomy. She moved from the window and laughed a dry, mad laugh.

"Why did you start?" she asked.

"I did not start, Clodagh! Who told you Peters takes atropine?"

"He is my nephew. But don't look dumbfoundered: I have no intention of poisoning him to see you a multimillionaire..."

"My dearest Clodagh!"

"I easily might, however. He will be here presently, bringing Mr. Wilson for the evening." Wilson was the electrician of the expedition, a man whose very presence seemed to hum with the abrasive, high-frequency whine of modern dynamos.

"Clodagh," I said, "you jest in a manner which does not please me."

"Do I really? Then I must be more exquisite. Women are no longer admired for doing such things."

She spent the rest of the afternoon extracting from me the history of every Polar expedition, consuming the details like a glutton at a feast. When I left, the passion of her kiss tasted of iron and ambition.

Toward one o'clock that night, my quiet was shattered by the harsh, percussive knocking of Peters' footman. I hurried three doors down the street to Peter’s house. One glance at his dilated pupils and flushed skin told me he was steeped in the toxins of belladonna. Wilson, the electrician, was there, his voice cutting through the room's strained acoustics.

"What on earth is the matter?" Wilson asked.

"Poisoned," I answered. "Atropine."

"What! It is he who has poisoned himself?"

I hesitated, feeling the untranslatable weight of Clodagh's earlier words pressing against my tongue. "He is in the habit of taking atropine, Wilson."

I labored for three hours, employing purgatives and deep, sensory understanding of the body's digestive limits, until I was certain he would recover. 

The next morning, I found Clodagh in Peters' room. She put her finger to her lips. 

"I am come to stay with him, till—the last," she whispered. 

At the end of the first week, Peters was still prostrate. I confronted Clodagh, sickened by her presence in a space meant for healing and nourishment. "Clodagh, your presence here displeases me. It is unnecessary."

"I always had a genius for nursing," she replied. "In these days of Roman decadence, shouldn't every innocent whim be encouraged? I find a sensuous pleasure, almost a sensual one, in dabbling in delicate drugs—like Helen, or Medea, who were all excellent chymists. To study the human ship in a gale—isn't that a thrilling distraction?"

To view the ingestion of toxic botanicals as a mere aristocratic thrill, a perverse culinary game, brought bile to my palate. Lucrezia Borgia in this late century—it was enough to make one retch up the heart.

At the end of the second week, I entered to find Wilson sitting by Peter's bed. Clodagh had just deposited a medicine-glass on the night-table. I watched, my auditory senses heightened, as Wilson stealthily lifted the glass. He brought it to his nose, analyzing its olfactory signature with a light-fingered, deeply mistrustful under-look. 

Clark, who also held a medical degree, came daily. Peter lay in a semi-coma, wracked by passionate vomitings that emptied him of all sustenance. I formally stated the atropine history, but the symptoms now tasted of some other rare, unnameable vegetable poison. 

"Mysterious thing," Clark said to me. "At any rate, my dream comes true, Jeffson. Peters is out of the running. I formally invite you to join the expedition."

I merely shrugged. As we passed through the dining room, I saw Clodagh gliding rapidly down the passage, her silk dress emitting a soft, calculating rustle. 

To my great surprise, by noon the next day, Peter's fine vitality reasserted itself. He sat up, pallid but demanding his habitual quarter-grain tablets of sulphate of morphia for his stomach pains. Wilson, however, brought about a painful social friction by revealing to Peter that I had taken his place. Peter dictated a fiery letter to Clark. Because I had not yet made my own preparations, I officially withdrew my acceptance, restoring Peter to his post.

The fifth day before the departure dawned. Friday, the 15th of June. Peter was in an armchair, smoking a cigar. At 11 P.M., I found Clodagh there. 

"Ah," Clodagh said. "Is it Yes or No for the injection to-night, Adam?"

"What do you think, Peters?" I asked. 

"Perhaps you had better give us another quarter," he answered. "Still some trouble in the tummy."

"A quarter-grain, then, Clodagh," I said.

She opened the syringe-box, pouting. "Our patient has been naughty. He has taken some more atropine."

I became angry, the perversion of botanical medicine offending my deepest sensibilities. "Peters, do that once more, and I swear I have nothing further to do with you!"

"Rubbish," Peters said. "I felt I needed it."

"He injected it with his own hand..." Clodagh remarked. 

She stood at the mantelpiece, her back turned. She had taken the vial of morphia tablets and the distilled water. She was melting the tablet, a macabre culinary preparation, macerating the potent narcotic in the clear liquid. She took an agonizingly long time, her silence forming a suffocating acoustic vacuum in the room. *Why is she so long?* 

Suddenly, an irresistible instinct seized me—to rush upon her, to dash the glass and the bitter tincture from her hands. My body leaned prone to obey. But at that exact millisecond, a voice at the opened door shattered the quiet.

"Well, how is everything?" 

It was Wilson. The electrician’s arrival brought a jarring, worldly noise that froze my blood. I remembered his look of mistrust. I stood like marble, my ancient voices struck dumb by the paralysis of modern consequence. 

Clodagh turned with free innocence, injecting Peter's forearm with a meticulous, terrifying grace. As she rose, laughing at something Wilson said, the delicate glass dropped from her hand. Her heel crushed it into the floorboards—a sharp, piercing acoustic crack that signaled the end.

"Your friend has been naughty, Mr. Wilson," she said. "He has been taking more atropine."

"Let me alone," Peters muttered. "I ain't a child."

Those were his last intelligible words. He died shortly before 1 A.M., poisoned by a massively lethal dose of the dark nightshade root.

From that moment until the *Boreal* bore me down the Thames, all the world became a chaotic nightmare. I remember the inquest, a sterile courtroom where the dominant legal lexicon entirely paved over the subjective, sensory truth of what had occurred. I was forced to testify that Peter had injected himself, corroborated by Wilson and Clodagh. The verdict was recorded in the dead, flat language of bureaucracy.

In all that rushing noise, only one other acoustic memory remains distinct: the tempest of words from the big-mouthed preacher Mackay at Kensington on Sunday night. What drew me to that chapel? Perhaps I sought an older, mythic resonance to explain the profound rot of the era. I sat in the pews, listening as his peroration echoed off the stone walls, an auditory harbinger of doom: 

"And as in the one case, transgression was followed by catastrophe swift and universal, so, in the other, I warn the entire race to look out thenceforth for nothing from God but a lowering sky, and thundery weather."
