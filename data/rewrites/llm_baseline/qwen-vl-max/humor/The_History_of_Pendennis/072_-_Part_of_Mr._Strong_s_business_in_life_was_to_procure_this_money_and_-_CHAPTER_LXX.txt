CHAPTER LXX. In Which Pendennis Counts His Eggs (And Finds Them Rather Few)

Our hero, Arthur Pendennis, had just arrived in London—though only for a fleeting visit, like a pigeon who drops in to check if the bread’s still warm before flying off again. After depositing his fellow travelers at a hotel (where they were presumably left to fend for themselves), he hurried off to Lamb Court, where his chambers basked in whatever sunlight dared venture into that grim yet oddly cozy corner of legal existence. In chambers, freedom is the substitute for sunshine; and while Temple dwellers grumble about damp walls and bad coffee, they secretly enjoy their quiet, solitary reigns over cramped rooms filled with law books, pipe smoke, and the occasional ghost of ambition.

Pen’s domestic announced that Warrington was already there—news which sent Arthur bounding up the stairs as if he’d been shot from a cannon. He found George, as usual, buried beneath a mountain of newspapers and reviews, the air thick with the comforting aroma of tobacco and intellectual neglect. The two greeted each other with the kind of rough-hewn warmth that only Englishmen can muster: no hugs, no tears, just a firm handshake and a muttered “Well, young one?” followed by “How are you, old boy?”—a greeting so profoundly unemotional it could pass for indifference, yet packed with more affection than a Frenchman’s entire emotional vocabulary.

Alphonse and Frederic would have wept, embraced, and recited poetry about each other’s noble hearts. Max and Wilhelm would have kissed each other on the cheek, then lit cigars and debated whether the war in the Balkans was really worth it. But not our Britons. They nod. They smile. They say “How-d’you-do?” after having saved each other from drowning in a river or being eaten by wolves. Then tomorrow, they’ll barely acknowledge each other at breakfast, merely exchanging glances and muttering, “Ah, yes. You’re here.” It’s a mystery wrapped in a stiff upper lip, this thing called English friendship.

“Been in Switzerland?” asked Pen, settling into a chair.

“Yes,” said Warrington, puffing thoughtfully. “Couldn’t find decent tobacco anywhere until Strasbourg, where I got some caporal. That’s when I knew I was back in civilization.”

One might imagine he’d seen the Matterhorn, heard the Alps sing, stood transfixed by glaciers and lakes—but no. He’d rather talk about tobacco. The grandeur of nature? Too much to handle. Instead, he’d let it seep into his writing later, like a well-placed metaphor in an essay on public sanitation.

He gave a brief, almost reluctant account of his travels: Switzerland, northern Italy, the Tyrol—then Vienna, Dresden, and the Rhine. He spoke of them in a tone as if he’d been forced to endure them under duress. As if the view of the Rhine had made him melancholy. As if the Alps had inspired nothing but regret.

Meanwhile, Pen offered his own travelogue: country life—boring. Canvassing—slower than a snail on a Sunday. Now in town for a few days, heading next to Tunbridge Wells, where friends would be *uncommonly slow*, too. How difficult it is to get an Englishman to admit he’s enjoyed himself!

“And the seat in Parliament?” Warrington asked, eyes gleaming. “All settled?”

“All right,” Pen replied. “As soon as Parliament meets and a new writ comes out, Clavering retires, and I step into his shoes. Like slipping into a pair of inherited slippers—only less comfortable.”

“Under which king does Bezonian speak or die?” Warrington inquired, quoting some obscure political aphorism.

“Do we come out as Liberal Conservative, Government man, or on our own hook?”

“Hem,” said Pen. “No politics now. Everyone’s politics are pretty much the same. I don’t have enough land to be a Protectionist—nor would I be one even if I owned half the county. I’ll go mostly with the Government, but ahead of them on social issues—don’t grin, you old cynic—I’ve been reading the Blue Books. I’m going to come out strong on sanitation and colonization. Not because I’m a reformer, mind you, but because it’s fashionable.”

“We reserve the right to vote against the Government,” Warrington added, “though we usually don’t. We’re friends of the people avant tout. We give lectures at the Clavering Institute, shake hands with mechanics who’ve read the Times, and think the franchise should be expanded—though we’re always ready to accept office once the House has listened to a few crack speeches and the Administration sees our merit.”

“I’m not Moses,” Pen said, with a touch of sorrow in his voice. “I have no laws from Heaven. I don’t belong to the mountain. My faith isn’t strong enough to lead men. Nor my vanity, nor my hypocrisy. I won’t tell lies, George—but I’ll go along with those that are necessary and widely accepted. Give a man the benefit of his skepticism. If I find something good to say in Parliament, I’ll say it. If there’s a fair measure, I’ll support it. If a decent job opens up, I’ll take it and be grateful. But I wouldn’t flatter a great man any more than a mob. And now you know as much about my politics as I do. What call have I to be a Whig? Whiggism isn’t divine. Why not vote with the Liberal Conservatives? They’ve done what the Whigs never would without them. Who converted both? The Radicals—and the country outside. I think the Morning Post is often right, and Punch is often wrong. I don’t profess a call—I just take advantage of a chance. Parlons d’autre chose.”

“The next thing at your heart, after ambition, is love, I suppose?” Warrington said, leaning back. “How are your young loves doing? Are we going to change our condition and give up these chambers? Are you going to divorce me, Arthur, and marry someone else?”

“I suppose so,” said Pen. “She’s very good-natured and lively. She sings. She doesn’t mind smoking. She’ll have a fair fortune—I don’t know how much—but my uncle says the Begum will be generous. And I think Blanche is dev’lish fond of me,” he added, sighing.

“That means we accept her caresses and her money,” Warrington observed dryly.

“Haven’t we said before that life is a transaction?” Pendennis replied.

“I don’t pretend to break my heart about her. I’ve told her plainly what I feel—and I’ve engaged myself to her. Since I last saw her, especially during the past two months in the country, she’s grown fonder and fonder. Her letters—especially to Laura—show it. Mine have been simple: no raptures, no vows—just treating it as an affaire faite. Not eager to hurry or delay.”

“And Laura?” Warrington asked quietly.

“Laura, George,” said Pen, looking his friend straight in the eye—“by heaven, Laura is the best, noblest, dearest girl the sun ever shone upon.”

His voice dropped. He seemed unable to finish. He reached out his hand. Warrington took it and nodded.

“Have you only found that out now, young un?” Warrington asked after a pause.

“Who hasn’t learned things too late, George?” Arthur cried, his emotions rising. “Whose life isn’t a disappointment? Who carries his heart whole to the grave without losing a piece? I’ve never known anyone truly happy. Everyone has to pay some price to Fate. Lucky if we’re left alone afterward, once we’ve paid our fine. Suppose I’ve just realized I’ve lost the greatest prize in the world—now that it can’t be mine? That for years I had an angel under my tent and let her go? Am I the only one? Ah, dear old boy, am I the only one? And do you think my lot is easier because I admit I deserve it?”

“She’s gone from us,” he whispered. “God bless her.”

“She was in this room once,” said George. “I saw her. Heard her sweet low voice. Saw her smile—those eyes shining so kindly. A face remembered in night watches—always blessed, always loved—gone now.”

A glass with a nosegay. A Bible with Helen’s handwriting. All that remained of a brief, brilliant flower of his life.

Say it’s a dream. Say it passes. Better the memory of a dream than waking from a blank stupor.

They sat in silence, each lost in thought, aware of the other’s pain.

Eventually, Pen broke the quiet. “I must go see my uncle and report progress.”

The Major had written in a foul mood. He was getting old.

“I should like to see you in Parliament, snugly settled with a comfortable house and an heir to the name before I make my bow,” the letter read. “Show me these, and then let old Arthur Pendennis make room for the younger fellows. He’s walked the Pall Mall pave long enough.”

“There’s a kindness about the old heathen,” Warrington remarked. “He cares for more than himself—at least for part of himself not buttoned into his coat. For you and your race. He wants the Pendennises to multiply and inherit the land. The old patriarch blesses you from Bays’s Club window, then gets carried off and buried under the flags of St. James’s Church, in sight of Piccadilly and the cabstand. An edifying ending.”

“The new blood I bring into the family,” mused Pen, “is rather tainted. If I’d chosen, I wouldn’t have picked Amory as progenitor. Or Snell. Or our Oriental ancestors. By the way—who was Amory?”

Amory was lieutenant of an Indiaman. Blanche wrote verses about him—the storm, the mountain wave, the seaman’s grave, the gallant father. Amory drowned commanding a ship between Calcutta and Sydney. He and the Begum weren’t happy together. She’s been unlucky in husbands, the poor lady. Between ourselves, a more despicable creature than Sir Francis Clavering, Baronet of Clavering Park, never—

“Never legislated for his country,” Warrington interrupted.

Pen blushed.

“At Baden,” Warrington continued, “I found Strong in great state, wearing all his orders. He told me he quarrelled with Clavering—had almost as bad an opinion of him as you do. In fact, I think he confided that Clavering was an utter scoundrel. That fellow Bloundell, who taught you card-playing at Oxbridge, was with Strong. Time has polished him—made him a more accomplished rascal than he was in college. But the king of the place was Colonel Altamont—carrying all before him, giving flies to the whole society, breaking the bank, they said.”

“My uncle knows something about Altamont,” Pen said. “Clavering knows something too. There’s something louche about him. But come! I must go to Bury Street, like a dutiful nephew.”

Taking his hat, he prepared to leave.

“I’ll walk with you,” said Warrington.

They descended the stairs, pausing at Pen’s chambers—which, as the reader knows, were now on the lower floor. Here, Pen began dabbing himself with eau-de-Cologne, carefully scenting his hair and whiskers.

“What’s wrong?” growled Warrington. “You haven’t been smoking. Is it my pipe that poisoned you?”

“I’m going to call on some women,” Pen said. “Dining with them. They’re passing through town, staying at an hotel in Jermyn Street.”

Warrington watched with amused interest as Pen transformed himself into a walking advertisement for elegance—gorgeous shirt-front, fresh gloves, glistening boots. George, meanwhile, wore thick high-lows, his shirt torn at the breast, collar ragged from his blue beard.

“Well, young un,” he said simply, “I like you to be a buck. Somehow. When I walk with you, it’s like having a rose in my button-hole. And you’re still affable. I don’t think there’s another young fellow in the Temple who turns out like you. And I don’t believe you’ve ever been ashamed of walking with me.”

“Don’t laugh at me, George,” Pen said.

“I say, Pen,” Warrington continued, sadly, “if you write—if you write to Laura, I wish you’d say ‘God bless her’ from me.”

Pen blushed. Then looked at Warrington. Then burst into uncontrollable laughter.

“I’m going to dine with her!” he exclaimed. “I brought her and Lady Rockminster up from the country today—made it two days—slept last night at Bath. Come and dine, George! I may ask anyone I please, and the old lady keeps talking about you.”

George hesitated. George had an article to write. George wavered. And oh, strange to say—agreed.

They decided to call on the ladies first, then march off to dinner in high spirits.

Once more, the dear face shone upon him. Once more, the sweet voice spoke. Once more, the tender hand pressed a welcome.

Half an hour before dinner, old Lady Rockminster declared: “You will go and see your uncle now, Mr. Pendennis. You won’t bring him to dinner—no. His old stories are intolerable. I want to talk to Mr. Warrington. I daresay he’ll amuse us. We’ve heard all your stories. We’ve been together for two whole days, and I think we’re getting tired of each other.”

So, obeying her ladyship’s command, Arthur went downstairs and walked to his uncle’s lodgings.
