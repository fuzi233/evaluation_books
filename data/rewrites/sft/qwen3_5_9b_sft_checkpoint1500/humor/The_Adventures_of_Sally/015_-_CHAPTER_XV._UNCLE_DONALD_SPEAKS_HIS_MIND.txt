Some men possess a peculiar brand of rubbery resilience, a stubborn refusal to admit they've lost, whether in love or in life. Bruce Carmyle was a master of this art. While other suitors might pack their bags after a "no," Bruce merely tightened his grip and waited for the next punch. He viewed Sally's rejection at Monk's Crofton not as a closing of doors, but as a challenge to his endurance. To Bruce, she wasn't the bohemian free spirit that sometimes frightened him; she was a beacon of bright eyes and a chin held high enough to cut glass. Even now, late at night, her spirited image whipped at him like a playful cat chasing a laser pointer, demanding pursuit. So, like a very polite, slightly nervous wolf tracking a deer, Bruce began his preparations, ignoring the fact that delicacy and imagination were skills he didn't exactly excel in.

Unfortunately, one cannot sneak around in a house full of relatives without being seen. The Carmyle clan possessed a communication network rivaled only by the most advanced African tribes, passing gossip from room to room via telepathy. Before Bruce could slip away, the Family's designated representative, Uncle Donald, materialized in his flat on South Audley Street.

Uncle Donald was a mountain of two hundred and forty pounds of pure, unadulterated flesh. His entry caused the armchair to groan a protest that sounded suspiciously like a dying whale. Once, at Monk's Crofton, Sally had delighted her younger brother Fillmore by pointing out that Uncle Donald was exactly how he would look grown up—a prophecy born of a childhood superstition regarding a weak heart. Consequently, Uncle Donald, at sixty-two, avoided exercise with the fervor of a monk avoiding meat, yet he maintained one of the three heartiest appetites in the southwest postal district. Bruce, usually the picture of respectful deference, felt a twinge of annoyance as he gazed upon this moving monument.

"What's this?" Uncle Donald gasped, his walrus moustache trembling slightly as if buffeted by a sudden squall. "You packing?"

"Yes," Bruce replied, his voice short. For the first time, he felt that familiar, prickling sense of guilt he usually reserved for his cousin Ginger when standing before such scrutiny.

"You going away?"

"Yes."

"Where?"

"America."

"When?"

"To-morrow morning."

"Why?"

If this exchange had been written by a playwright aiming for fast-paced comedy, it might have worked. But in reality, Uncle Donald's unique conversational style stretched each sentence over nearly three minutes. Between every answer and question, he paused to puff, sigh, and inhale deeply, his moustache acting as a bellows for his lungs, making the air feel heavy with effort.

"You're going after that girl!" Uncle Donald accused, finally breaking the rhythm.

Bruce flushed a deep crimson. At that precise second, a fleeting thought crossed his mind: perhaps Ginger's behavior at Bleke's Coffee House hadn't been entirely indefensible after all. Uncle Donald could indeed be trying.

"Would you care for a whisky and soda, Uncle Donald?" Bruce offered, attempting to smooth things over.

"Yes," Uncle Donald declared, honoring a vow made in the early eighties never to decline a drink. "Gimme!"

One might expect a truce, but no. Having swallowed the restorative, Uncle Donald attacked again, undeterred.

"I never thought you were a fool before," he said severely.

Bruce's proud spirit bristled. This kind of direct interrogation was new territory for him; usually, his actions required no commentary.

"I'm not a fool."

"You *are* a fool. A damn fool," Uncle Donald insisted, zooming in on specifics. "I don't like the girl. Never did. Not a nice girl. Didn't like her. Right from the start."

"Do we need to discuss this?" Bruce asked, slipping effortlessly into his 'grand manner.'

Uncle Donald drank a layer of moustache and blew it back out.

"Do we need to?" he countered with asperity. "We *are* going to discuss it! Think what I went through climbing these blasted stairs with my weak heart? Gimme another!"

Bruce obliged.

"It's a bad business," Uncle Donald moaned, repeating his movements with theatrical flair. "Shocking bad business. If your poor father were alive, what do you think he'd say about you tearing across the globe after this girl? I'll tell you. He'd say... What kind of whisky is this?"

"The O'Rafferty Special."

"Not bad. Quite sound. Mellow. Where did you get it?"

"Bilby's in Oxford Street."

"Must order some. Mellow. He'd say... well, God knows what he'd say. Why are you doing it? You're doing it for what? That puzzles your uncle George. It baffles your aunt Geraldine. Nobody understands it. That girl is simply after your money. Anyone can see that."

"Pardon me, Uncle Donald," Bruce said stiffly, "but that is surely rather absurd. If she were after my money, why would she have refused me at Monk's Crofton?"

"Luring you on!" Uncle Donald exclaimed immediately. "A well-known trick! Back in 1881, when I was at Oxford, a girl tried to lure me on. If I hadn't had some sense and a weak heart..." He paused for breath. "But tell me, what do you know of this girl? Who is she? Where did you meet her?"

"At Roville, in France."

"Traveling with her family?"

"Traveling alone," Bruce admitted, reluctantly.

"Not even with that brother of hers?" Uncle Donald shook his head. "Bad! Very bad!"

"American girls are accustomed to more independence than English girls."

"That young man," Uncle Donald mused, following a new thread of thought, "is going to be fat one of these days if he doesn't watch out. Traveling alone, was she? What did you do? Did you catch her eye on the pier?"

"Really, Uncle Donald!"

"Well, you must have gotten to know her somehow."

"I was introduced to her by Lancelot. She was a friend of his."

"Lancelot!" Uncle Donald erupted, quivering like a jellyfish touched by a hot wire. "Well, that tells you everything about the sort of girl she is! Any girl who would be friends with... Unpack!"

"I beg your pardon?"

"Unpack! We must stop this foolery. Out of the question. Go find some girl who will actually make you a proper wife. Your Aunt Mary has been meeting some people named Bassington-Bassington. Related to the Kent Bassington-Bassingtons... The eldest daughter is a charming girl, she'd be perfect for you."

In the pages of older fiction, characters rarely ground their teeth, but at that moment, Bruce Carmyle came dangerously close. He scowled blackly, and the last vestige of politeness vanished from his face.

"I shall do nothing of the kind," he said briefly. "I sail tomorrow."

Uncle Donald had been defied by a nephew before, but he had not anticipated the specific flavor of this particular sensation. A profound feeling of impotence washed over him. It is a terrible thing to know exactly what to do and then be told you cannot do it.

"Eh?" he said.

Seeing that he had started the process of defiance, Bruce decided to finish the job thoroughly.

"I am over twenty-one," he stated. "I am financially independent. I shall do as I please."

"But, consider!" Uncle Donald pleaded, acutely aware that his arguments were crumbling like sandcastles. "Reflect!"

"I have reflected."

"Your position in the county..."

"I've thought of that."

"You could marry anyone you pleased."

"I'm going to."

"You are determined to go running off to God-knows-where after this Miss-I-cannot-even-remember-her-damned-name?"

"Yes."

"Have you considered," Uncle Donald said, adopting a portentous tone, "that you owe a duty to the Family?"

Bruce's patience finally snapped, sinking him instantly to the absolute, unvarnished depths of Gingerian plain-spokenness.

"Oh, damn the Family!" he cried.

A painful silence followed, broken only by the relieved sigh of the armchair as Uncle Donald hoisted himself out of it.

"After that," said Uncle Donald, "I have nothing more to say."

"Good!" said Mr. Carmyle, rudely, having lost all remaining sense of shame.

"'Cept this. If you come back married to that girl, I'll cut you in Piccadilly. By George, I will!"

He turned for the door. Bruce looked down his nose without speaking. A tense, silent standoff ensued.

"What," asked Uncle Donald, his hand on the handle, "did you say it was called?"

"What was what called?"

"That whisky."

"The O'Rafferty Special."

"And where did you get it?"

"Bilby's, in Oxford Street."

"I'll make a note of it," said Uncle Donald, already halfway out the door.
