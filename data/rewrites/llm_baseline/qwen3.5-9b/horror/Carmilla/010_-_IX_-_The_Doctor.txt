IX. The Doctor

As Carmilla would not tolerate the idea of an attendant sleeping within her chamber, my father arranged for a servant to rest directly outside her door. This precaution was intended to ensure that she would not attempt another nocturnal excursion without being intercepted at her threshold. That night passed undisturbed.

The following morning, before dawn, the doctor arrived. My father had summoned him without informing me of his presence. Madame escorted me to the library, where the grave little physician awaited us. He was the same man I had previously described: distinguished by his white hair and spectacles. I recounted my story to him, and as I spoke, his expression grew increasingly somber. We stood face-to-face in the recess of a large window. Upon finishing my account, he leaned his shoulders against the stone wall, fixing his eyes upon me with an intensity that bordered on horror. After a moment of silent reflection, he requested permission to speak with my father.

My father was summoned accordingly. As he entered, smiling broadly, he remarked, "I dare say, doctor, you are going to tell me that I am an old fool for having brought you here; I hope I am." However, his smile quickly vanished into shadow as the doctor, maintaining a profoundly serious countenance, beckoned him closer. They conversed for some time within the same window recess where I had just spoken with the physician. Their discussion appeared earnest and argumentative. The library was vast, leaving Madame and me standing together at the far end, burning with curiosity yet unable to overhear a single word. They spoke in hushed tones, and the deep alcove of the window obscured the doctor entirely, while my father's foot, arm, and shoulder were barely visible. The thick walls and glass further dampened their voices, rendering them unintelligible.

Eventually, my father's face emerged into the main room. He looked pale, thoughtful, and, I fancied, deeply agitated. "Laura, dear, come here for a moment," he called out. "Madame, we shall not trouble you; the doctor requires my attention at present." Reluctantly, though feeling somewhat alarmed despite my general weakness, I approached. One often mistakenly believes that strength is a commodity available on demand, but I found myself lacking it. My father extended his hand as I drew near, yet his gaze remained fixed on the doctor. "It certainly is very odd," he murmured. "I do not fully comprehend it. Laura, come here, dear; listen carefully to Doctor Spielsberg and compose yourself."

"You mentioned a sensation akin to two needles piercing your skin, located somewhere around your neck, during the night of your first terrifying dream," the doctor asked. "Is there still any soreness?"

"None at all," I replied.

"Can you indicate with your finger approximately where you believe this occurred?"

"Very little below my throat—here," I answered, pointing to the area covered by my morning gown.

"Now you can verify it yourself," the doctor stated. "You will not mind your father lowering your dress just a fraction. It is necessary to detect a symptom of the condition under which you have been suffering." I acquiesced. The exposure revealed only an inch or two beneath the edge of my collar.

"Good heavens!" my father exclaimed, turning pale. "So it is!"

"You see it now with your own eyes," the doctor declared, his voice carrying a gloomy triumph.

"What is it?" I cried, fear beginning to grip me.

"Nothing, my dear young lady, but a small blue spot, roughly the size of the tip of your little finger," he explained, turning to my father. "The question now is, what course of action is best? Is there any danger?" I urged, trembling with trepidation.

"I trust not, my dear," the doctor answered calmly. "I see no reason why you should not recover. Indeed, I see no reason why you should not begin improving immediately. That is precisely where the sensation of strangulation originates, is it not?"

"Yes," I confirmed.

"And recall clearly—that same point served as the center of that thrill you described moments ago, like the current of a cold stream rushing against you?"

"It may have been; I think it was."

"Aha," he added, nodding toward my father. "Shall I mention this to Madame?"

"Certainly," my father agreed.

He summoned Madame and instructed her, "I find my young friend here far from well. While it likely will not prove of great consequence, certain steps must be taken, which I shall explain later. For the present, Madame, you will be so kind as not to allow Miss Laura to be alone for even a moment. That is the only directive I require at this juncture. It is indispensable."

"We may rely upon your kindness, Madame, I know," my father added.

Madame responded with eager assurance. Then, addressing me, he said, "And you, dear Laura, I know you will heed the doctor's instructions."

"The doctor has informed me that he intends to consult regarding another patient whose symptoms slightly resemble those of my daughter," my father continued. "Her case is much milder in degree, but I believe identical in nature. She is a young lady staying with us—a guest. Since you are scheduled to pass this way again this evening, you cannot do better than join us for supper here, allowing you to examine her. She does not descend until the afternoon."

"I thank you," the doctor replied.

"I shall meet you here at approximately seven this evening," he concluded.

They reiterated their final instructions to me and Madame. With these parting charges, my father departed, walking out alongside the doctor. I watched them pace together up and down the grassy platform between the road and the moat, clearly absorbed in earnest conversation. The doctor did not return to the house. Instead, I observed him mounting his horse, taking his leave, and riding eastward through the forest.

Nearly simultaneously, I saw a man arrive from Dranfield bearing letters. He dismounted and handed the bag to my father. Meanwhile, Madame and I remained lost in conjecture regarding the singular and urgent directives imposed by the doctor and my father. As Madame later confessed to me, she feared the doctor anticipated a sudden seizure, believing that without immediate assistance, I might either perish in a fit or suffer severe injury. Such an interpretation did not occur to me; indeed, I suspected, perhaps fortunately for my nerves, that the arrangement was prescribed merely to secure a companion who would prevent me from engaging in excessive exercise, consuming unripe fruit, or indulging in the myriad foolish habits attributed to youth.

About half an hour after my father returned, holding a letter in his hand, he announced, "This correspondence was delayed; it is from General Spielsdorf. He might have arrived yesterday, he may not come until tomorrow, or he may be here today." He placed the open letter into my hands, yet his demeanor offered no sign of pleasure. Unlike his usual warmth when welcoming a guest, particularly one so beloved as the General, he appeared distinctly displeased. There was plainly something weighing heavily on his mind that he chose not to disclose.

"Papa, darling, will you tell me this?" I implored, suddenly placing my hand on his arm and gazing imploringly into his face.

"Perhaps," he answered, smoothing my hair caressingly over my eyes. "Does the doctor consider me very ill?"

"No, dear," he replied, his tone somewhat dry. "He believes that, if the correct measures are taken, you will be completely well again, or at least on the high road to full recovery within a day or two."

"I wish our good friend, the General, had chosen any other time," he continued. "That is, I wish you had been perfectly well to receive him."

"But do tell me, Papa," I persisted, "what does he think is wrong with me?"

"Nothing," he snapped, displaying more irritation than I had ever witnessed before. Seeing that I looked wounded, he presumably realized his harshness and kissed me, adding, "You shall know everything in a day or two; that is, all that I know. In the meantime, do not trouble your head about it."

He turned to leave the room but returned before I could finish pondering the oddity of the situation. His purpose was merely to announce that he was departing for Karnstein and had ordered the carriage to be ready at noon. He intended for both Madame and me to accompany him. He was going to visit a priest residing near those picturesque grounds on business, and since Carmilla had never visited them, she could follow once she descended, accompanied by Mademoiselle, who would prepare provisions for what you might call a picnic, to be laid out in the ruined castle.

At noon, accordingly, I was prepared. Shortly thereafter, my father, Madame, and I set out on our projected drive. Crossing the drawbridge, we turned right and followed the road over the steep Gothic bridge, heading westward toward the deserted village and the ruined castle of Karnstein. No sylvan drive could be imagined more picturesque. The terrain broke into gentle hills and hollows, all draped in beautiful woodland, utterly devoid of the comparative formality imposed by artificial planting, cultivation, and pruning. The irregularities of the land frequently diverted the road from its straight course, causing it to wind beautifully around the flanks of broken hollows and the steeper slopes of the hills, traversing varieties of ground almost inexhaustible.
