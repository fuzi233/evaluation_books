Consideration for my poor aunt forbade me to speak first. I sat close by her fireside, listening not only to the words she would presently trust me with, but to the room itself—the hush that lay between the ticks of the mantel-clock, the faint rasp of London wheels beyond the window, the soft domestic creak when she shifted in her chair. A household has its own language, made of small sounds; and when those sounds grow thin and careful, you know—before any doctor dares confess it—that sickness has been living there.

“I have been seriously ill, Drusilla, for some time past,” Lady Verinder said at last. “And, strange to say, without knowing it myself.”

“Yes, dear,” I answered, keeping my voice as steady as the clock.

“I brought Rachel to London, as you know, for medical advice. I thought it right to consult two doctors.” She spoke with that practical composure which wealthy houses cultivate, the kind that can order linen and consult physicians in the same tone.

“One of the two was a stranger to me,” she continued. “The other had been an old friend of my husband’s. After prescribing for Rachel, he asked to speak with me privately. I expected directions about my daughter. Instead, he took me by the hand, very gravely, and said, ‘You are, I am afraid, far more urgently in need of medical advice than your daughter.’ He questioned me. I answered lightly, at first—until I saw that my answers distressed him. He appointed to come the next day with another medical man, at an hour when Rachel would not be at home.”

She paused; and the quiet in the room thickened, like broth left too long to cool.

“The result,” she said, “was most kindly conveyed to me. They were satisfied that precious time had been lost which could never be regained, and that my case had passed beyond the reach of their art. For more than two years I have been suffering under an insidious form of heart disease. Without any symptoms to alarm me, it has, little by little, fatally broken me down. I may live for some months, or I may die before another day has passed. The doctors cannot—and dare not—speak more positively than this.”

The sentence fell among the familiar objects—curtains, books, the little table where tea might have stood—and made them all look like things seen for the last time.

“It would be vain to say,” she went on, “that I have not had miserable moments since the truth was made known to me. But I am more resigned now. I am doing my best to set my worldly affairs in order. My one great anxiety is that Rachel should be kept in ignorance. If she knew, she would at once attribute my broken health to anxiety about the Diamond, and would reproach herself bitterly—poor child—for what is in no sense her fault. Both doctors agree the mischief began two, if not three, years since. You will keep my secret, Drusilla—for I see sincere sorrow and sympathy for me in your face.”

She mistook what she saw. My sorrow was real enough; but beneath it, I felt a fierce, eager gratitude—an inward stir like a match taken to tinder. A door had opened on a labour I believed appointed to me. Here was a beloved creature standing, as it were, on the brink—unwarned, unfortified—and led, providentially led, to speak to me.

I put my arms around her. My tenderness, once released, insisted on touching and holding, as if faith itself could be made bodily by the pressure of an embrace.

“Oh!” I said. “The indescribable interest with which you inspire me! Oh, the good I mean to do you, dear, before we part!”

After a few earnest words of warning—gentle, but not to be mistaken—I offered her the choice of three precious friends, all at work in the neighbourhood, all ready to come at a word from me, all inexhaustible in exhortation as a kitchen range is inexhaustible in heat. But my aunt’s face clouded: she looked puzzled, even frightened, and met me with the one objection the world always keeps polished for use—she was not strong enough to face strangers.

I yielded—for the moment. My experience as Reader and Visitor (under not less, first and last, than fourteen beloved clerical friends) told me what was needed: preparation by books, a gradual seasoning of the mind, as you might temper a too-strong relish by slow additions, until it can be borne.

“You will read, dear, won’t you?” I asked, in my most winning way. “If I bring you my own precious books? Turned down at all the right places. Marked in pencil where you are to stop and ask yourself, ‘Does this apply to me?’”

Even that simple appeal startled her, so perishing is the influence of the world. “I will do what I can, Drusilla, to please you,” she said, with a look of surprise that was instructive—and terrible—to see.

Not a moment was to be lost. The mantel-clock told me I had just time to hurry home, select a first series of readings—a dozen, no more—and return by five o’clock to meet the lawyer and witness her will. I promised faithfully; and left the house on my errand of mercy.

When no interests but my own are involved, I take the omnibus with humility. But devotion has its extravagances. I committed the prodigality of taking a cab.

London received me with its usual harsh music: iron on stone; hooves striking sparks of sound; voices pitched to battle the roar of traffic. It is a cruelty we grow used to—this constant pounding that drives thought into corners and makes even prayer feel hurried. I endured it as I must, and reached my lodging, where my little library waited—those volumes I treasured as other women treasure spice-boxes and preserving pans, each one stored for its proper use, each one meant to keep what is most perishable from spoiling.

I chose and marked my first series of readings, slid them into a carpet-bag, and drove back to Montagu Square. I paid the cabman exactly his fare. He received it with an oath; upon which I instantly gave him a tract. If I had put a pistol to his head, he could hardly have shown greater consternation. He sprang up on his box, flung profane cries behind him, and drove off furiously. Quite useless, I am happy to say. I sowed the good seed in spite of him by throwing a second tract in at the window as the cab rattled away—its wheels battering the street like a drum that never tires.

At my aunt’s door the footman answered (not the person with the cap-ribbons, to my great relief). He informed me the doctor had called and was still shut up with Lady Verinder. Mr. Bruff, the lawyer, had arrived a minute since, and was waiting in the library. I was shown in there to wait too.

Mr. Bruff looked surprised to see me. We had met before under my aunt’s roof. He is the family solicitor: a man old and grizzled in the service of the world—prophet of Law and Mammon by day, and by leisure equally capable of reading a novel and tearing up a tract.

“Have you come to stay here, Miss Clack?” he asked, glancing at my carpet-bag.

To reveal the contents of that bag to him would have been to invite profanity. I lowered myself to his level and stated my business.

“My aunt has informed me that she is about to sign her will,” I said. “She has been so good as to ask me to be one of the witnesses.”

“Aye? aye? Well, Miss Clack, you will do. You are over twenty-one, and you have not the slightest pecuniary interest in Lady Verinder’s will.”

Not the slightest pecuniary interest. Oh, how thankful I was to hear it! If my aunt, possessed of thousands, had remembered poor me—if my name had appeared with a little legacy that would have made five pounds less of an object—my enemies might have doubted the motive that loaded me with my marked readings and drew upon my resources for the expense of a cab. Not the cruellest scoffer could doubt now. Much better so—surely, surely much better.

My meditative silence appeared to weigh on him. It forced him into talking, as if the quiet of the room, so unlike the street’s racket, made his worldly mind uneasy.

“Well, Miss Clack,” he said, “what’s the last news in the charitable circles? How is your friend Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite after the mauling he got from the rogues in Northumberland Street? Egad! they’re telling a pretty story about that charitable gentleman at my club!”

The manner in which he had spoken of my age and my lack of interest in the will I had passed over. But his tone about dear Mr. Godfrey was too much. After what had happened that afternoon, I felt bound to assert Mr. Ablewhite’s innocence whenever it was questioned—and, I own, felt equally bound to administer a sting to Mr. Bruff.

“I live very much out of the world,” I said; “and I don’t possess the advantage, sir, of belonging to a club. But I happen to know the story you allude to—and a viler falsehood was never told.”

“Yes, yes—you believe in your friend. Natural enough. Mr. Ablewhite won’t find the world in general quite so easy to convince as a committee of charitable ladies. Appearances are dead against him. He was in the house when the Diamond was lost. And he was the first person in the house to go to London afterwards. Ugly circumstances, ma’am—viewed by the light of later events.”

I ought to have stopped him there. I ought to have told him he spoke in ignorance of testimony offered by the only person competent to speak from positive knowledge. But the temptation to let him build his own downfall was too much. I asked what he meant by “later events,” with an air of innocence.

“Events in which the Indians are concerned,” he said, growing more confident the longer he talked. “What do they do the moment they’re let out of the prison at Frizinghall? They go straight to London and fix on Mr. Luker. What follows? Mr. Luker feels alarmed for the safety of ‘a valuable of great price’ he’s got in the house. He lodges it privately—under a general description—in his bankers’ strongroom. Clever of him; but the Indians are clever too. They suspect the ‘valuable’ is being shifted, and they hit on a bold way of clearing that suspicion up. Whom do they seize and search? Not Mr. Luker only—which would be intelligible—but Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite as well. Why? Mr. Ablewhite says they acted on blind suspicion after seeing him speak accidentally to Luker. Absurd. Half-a-dozen other people spoke to Luker that morning. Why weren’t they followed and trapped? No. The plain inference is that Mr. Ablewhite had his private interest in the ‘valuable’ as well as Luker, and that the Indians were uncertain which of the two had disposal of it—so they searched both. Public opinion says that. And public opinion, on this occasion, is not easily refuted.”

He ended with a look of such complacent wisdom that I could not resist drawing him a little farther before I struck.

“I don’t presume to argue with a clever lawyer like you,” I said. “But is it quite fair to Mr. Ablewhite to pass over the opinion of the famous London police officer who investigated this case? Not the shadow of a suspicion rested upon anybody but Miss Verinder, in the mind of Sergeant Cuff.”

“Do you mean to tell me you agree with the sergeant?”

“I judge nobody, sir, and I offer no opinion.”

“And I commit both those enormities,” he returned. “I judge the sergeant to have been utterly wrong; and I offer the opinion that if he had known Rachel’s character as I know it, he would have suspected everybody in the house but her. Secret, self-willed, odd, wild—unlike other girls. But true as steel, high-minded, generous to a fault. If the plainest evidence pointed one way, and nothing but Rachel’s word of honour pointed the other, I’d take her word before the evidence—lawyer as I am. Strong language, Miss Clack; but I mean it.”

“Would you object to illustrate your meaning, Mr. Bruff? Suppose you found Miss Verinder quite unaccountably interested in what has happened to Mr. Ablewhite and Mr. Luker? Suppose she asked the strangest questions about this dreadful scandal, and displayed the most ungovernable agitation when she found out the turn it was taking?”

“Suppose anything you please—it wouldn’t shake my belief in Rachel Verinder by a hair’s breadth.”

“She is so absolutely to be relied on as that?”

“So absolutely.”

“Then permit me to inform you,” I said, “that Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite was in this house not two hours since—and that his entire innocence of all concern in the disappearance of the Moonstone was proclaimed by Miss Verinder herself, in the strongest language I ever heard used by a young lady in my life.”

I enjoyed the triumph—an unholy triumph, I fear—of seeing him utterly confounded by a few plain words. He sprang up and stared, silent. I kept my seat and related the whole scene as it had occurred.

“And what do you say about Mr. Ablewhite now?” I asked, when I had done.

“If Rachel has testified to his innocence,” Mr. Bruff answered, “I believe in his innocence as firmly as you do. I’ve been misled by appearances like the rest of the world; and I’ll atone by publicly contradicting the scandal wherever I meet with it. In the meantime, allow me to congratulate you on the masterly manner in which you opened the full fire of your batteries on me at the moment I least expected it. You would have done great things in my profession, ma’am, if you had happened to be a man.”

He turned away and began walking irritably up and down the room. I could see the new light had not merely surprised him; it had disturbed him at the root. As he paced—boots tapping, then stopping, then tapping again in that restless rhythm of a mind at war—he let fall words that betrayed the abominable explanation he had previously accepted. He had suspected Mr. Godfrey of the infamy of stealing the Diamond, and had attributed Rachel’s conduct to a generous determination to conceal the crime. Now, on Rachel’s own authority—unassailable authority in his estimation—that whole view was shattered.

“What a case!” he muttered, stopping at the window and drumming on the glass. “It not only defies explanation—it’s even beyond conjecture.”

Nothing in those words required a reply—and yet I answered. It seems hardly credible that I could not leave him alone even then. But anything is credible when perversity gets the upper hand.

“Pardon me for intruding on your reflections,” I said. “But surely there is a conjecture to make which has not occurred to us yet.”

“Maybe. I own I don’t know what it is.”

“Before I was so fortunate as to convince you of Mr. Ablewhite’s innocence, you mentioned it as one reason for suspecting him—that he was in the house when the Diamond was lost. Permit me to remind you that Mr. Franklin Blake was also in the house when the Diamond was lost.”

He left the window, took a chair opposite mine, and looked at me steadily with a hard smile.

“You are not so good a lawyer, Miss Clack,” he said, “as I supposed. You don’t know how to let well alone.”

“I am afraid I fail to follow you,” I answered, modestly.

“It won’t do a second time. Franklin Blake is a prime favourite of mine, as you know. But never mind. I’ll adopt your view before you can turn on me. You’re quite right. I suspected Ablewhite on grounds which abstractedly justify suspecting Blake too. Very good—let’s suspect them together. It’s quite in his character, we will say, to be capable of stealing the Moonstone. The only question is whether it was his interest to do so.”

“Mr. Franklin Blake’s debts,” I remarked, “are matters of family notoriety.”

“And Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite’s debts haven’t arrived at that stage yet. True. But there are two difficulties in your theory. I manage Franklin Blake’s affairs, and the vast majority of his creditors—knowing his father to be rich—are content to charge interest and wait. That’s the first difficulty. The second is tougher still. I have it on Lady Verinder’s authority that Rachel was ready to marry Franklin Blake before that infernal Indian Diamond disappeared. She had drawn him on and put him off with the coquetry of a young girl; but she confessed to her mother that she loved him, and her mother trusted him with the secret. So there he was, with creditors willing to wait, and the certain prospect of marrying an heiress. Call him a scoundrel if you like—but tell me why he should steal the Moonstone.”

“The human heart is unsearchable,” I said gently. “Who is to fathom it?”

“In other words—though he hadn’t the shadow of a reason—he might have taken it through natural depravity. Very well. Say he did. Why the devil—”

“I beg your pardon, Mr. Bruff. If I hear the devil referred to in that manner, I must leave the room.”

“I beg your pardon—I’ll be more careful. All I mean is this: why—even supposing he took it—should Franklin Blake make himself the most prominent person in the house in trying to recover it? You’ll say he did that to divert suspicion. I answer he had no need—nobody suspected him. He first steals the Moonstone (without reason) through depravity; and then acts a part about the loss which is unnecessary, and which leads to his mortally offending the young lady who would otherwise have married him. That is what you are driven to assert if you associate the Diamond with Franklin Blake. No, no! After what has passed between us today, the dead-lock is complete. Rachel’s innocence is beyond doubt—as her mother knows, and as I know. Mr. Ablewhite’s innocence is equally certain—or Rachel would never have testified to it. And Franklin Blake’s innocence, as you have just seen, asserts itself. On one hand, we are morally certain of these things. On the other, we are equally sure somebody has brought the Moonstone to London, and that Mr. Luker, or his banker, is in private possession of it at this moment. What is the use of my experience—or anyone’s—in such a case? It baffles me; it baffles you; it baffles everybody.”

Not everybody, I thought. It had not baffled Sergeant Cuff. I was about to mention this—mildly, and with every protest against casting the smallest slur on Rachel—when the servant entered to say the doctor had gone and Lady Verinder was waiting to receive us.

That stopped the discussion. Mr. Bruff gathered his papers, looking a little worn by our talk. I lifted my carpet-bag of marked readings, feeling as if I could have gone on for hours. We proceeded in silence to my aunt’s room.

Before my narrative passes to what followed, I must explain why I have repeated my conversation with Mr. Bruff. I am ordered to include in my contribution to the shocking story of the Moonstone a plain disclosure—not only of the turn suspicion took, but of the names on which it rested at the time when the Indian Diamond was believed to be in London. A report of what passed in the library seemed exactly what was needed; and it has the moral advantage of requiring a sacrifice of sinful self-esteem. I have been obliged to confess that my fallen nature got the better of me. In making that humiliating confession, I get the better of my fallen nature. The moral balance is restored; the spiritual atmosphere feels clear once more. Dear friends—we may go on again.
