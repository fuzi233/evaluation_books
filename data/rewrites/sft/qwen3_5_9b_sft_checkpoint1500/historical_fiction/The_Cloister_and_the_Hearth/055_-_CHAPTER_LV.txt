Two months prior to the present scene, near the coast midway between Naples and Rome, the local populace gathered on the beach, their gazes fixed intently on a vessel battling a ferocious gale driven directly onto the shore. The ship appeared to defy disaster at moments, prompting cheers from the spectators; yet at others, the relentless wind and towering waves pressed her dangerously close, granting the onlookers a private, unspoken satisfaction at her precarious plight.

Though poorly engineered for open-sea navigation, the vessel was surprisingly robust for grounding, her exaggerated stern catching the gale and her hull shaped like an inverted cocked hat. To the crowd, the battering wooden frame seemed a living entity, struggling desperately against mortality with a gasping heart. Yet those standing upon the deck would have known better: the ship possessed not one heart but many, and a multitude of souls were emerging into the light amidst that terrifying hour.

The sailors stumbled wildly across the swaying timber, hauling ropes at random, alternating between curses and prayers. Passengers huddled around the mast, some seated, others kneeling or lying prostrate, clutching the railings as the ship heeled violently in the monstrous seas. Among them stood a handsome young man, his complexion ashen and lips tightly compressed, revealing an internal war between sheer terror and rigid self-respect. It was Gerard. Around him, a trembling mass of humanity raised countless vows and pleas, creating a cacophony where deities outnumbered mortals. The sailors, however, placed their faith singularly in a divine feminine figure, invoking her as "Queen of Heaven" or "Star of the Sea." In contrast, the landlubbers practiced a chaotic polytheism; an English merchant vowed a fortune to Our Lady of Walsingham, while a Genoese trader pledged silver to Our Lady of Loreto, and a Tuscan noble promised wax candles to Our Lady of Ravenna, each pledging allegiance to different local manifestations of the divine rather than the universal Truth.

Suddenly, a violent gust caught the mainsail at a fatal angle. The rotting rigging snapped with a loud crack, tearing the canvas free. It billowed away downwind, shrinking into darkness until it vanished into the sea like a scrap of paper half a mile offshore. Before the helmsman could correct the course, a massive wave slammed into the quarter, soaking the survivors to the bone and offering them a foretaste of chilling death. Panic erupted instantly. One man vowed to become a Carthusian monk if Saint Thomas rescued him; another promised pilgrimage to Compostela in rags if Saint James interceded. Others called upon Thomas, Dominic, Denis, and especially Catherine of Siena.

Two small Neapolitan traders stood shivering in the cold. One shouted loudly, "I vow a waxen statue of Saint Christopher weighing as much as myself if I reach land safely!" His companion nudged him, warning, "Brother, beware what you vow. If you auctioned everything you possess, it would not buy enough wax to cover the statue."

"Hold your tongue, you fool," retorted the first, then whispered, "Do you think I am joking? Let me survive, and I will not give him so much as a straw."

Nearby, others lay flat, pleading with the ocean itself. "Oh, most merciful sea! Oh, generous sea! Be gentle, be kind, spare us in this hour of peril!" Others wailed in pure animal terror as the ship became a mere toy tossed by the titanic waves.

Amidst this chaos sat a humble Roman woman with her infant at her breast. Her cheeks were pale, but her eyes remained strangely calm. Though her lips moved in silent prayer, she wept not, nor did she bargain with the gods. Whenever the ship threatened to sink beneath them and the men screamed, she simply kissed her child. With stoic patience, she nursed him within the jaws of death, refusing to let him lose any comfort she could offer. *Moribundo?* Yes, there, amidst the medieval panic, sat the spirit of antiquity. Sixteen centuries had not corrupted the old Roman blood in her veins, and an instinct inherited from ancestors she had scarcely heard of taught her to meet death with dignified composure.

A colossal friar stood upon the poop, legs braced wide like the Colossus of Rhodes, seemingly ignoring the surrounding peril rather than defying it. Reciting verses from the Canticles in a loud, steady voice, he invited the passengers to confession. Those who knelt before him found absolution as easily as if they were in a quiet sacristy, despite the dying ship around them. Gerard, drawn by an instinct guiding the wavering toward strength, approached him. Indeed, the courage of ordinary heroes might have paled beside the monk's monumental composure. Here, two figures maintained the dignity of their race: a woman, tender yet heroic, and a monk, hardened by faith against mortal fear.

Now, with the sail gone, the sailors lowered the useless mast a foot above the deck, causing it to crash over the side. This slight relief proved temporary. Without propulsion, the hull could not outrun the surf. The sea began pounding the stern repeatedly, delivering blows that felt more like strikes from a rocky mountain than liquid water.

The captain abandoned the wheel and moved to midships, pale as a corpse. "Lighten her!" he cried. "Throw everything overboard, or we shall founder before we strike, losing our only chance of life." As the sailors executed this order, the captain spoke with a demeanor utterly alien to an English skipper in such straits. "Friends," he declared, "last night, when conditions were too fair, a sphere of fire appeared near the ship. When twin spheres appear, it is good luck, and no doom awaits her voyage. We mariners name these Castor and Pollux. But if one arrives without the other, the ship is doomed. Therefore, like good Christians, prepare to die."

These words elicited a loud wail. When asked how long they had to prepare, the captain replied, "Perhaps half an hour, maybe less; beyond that, it is impossible. She leaks like a sieve. Hurry, men, lighten her."

Desperate passengers grabbed anything on deck and hurled it into the churning water. Soon, they seized a heavy sack containing an elderly, seasick man lying upon it. They dragged it to the edge. Suddenly, the owner sprang up with an unearthly shriek, leaping onto the sack. "Holy Moses! What are you doing? 'Tis my all! The entire fruit of my journey! Silver candlesticks, silver plates, brooches, chalices..."

"Release him, you hoary villain!" cried others. "Shall all our lives be lost for your stolen goods?" "Fling him in with it!" shouted one. "'Tis this Hebrew whom Christian men are drowning for!"

Numbers of men wrestled the sack from his grasp and heaved it over the side. It splashed into the waves. The owner uttered one final cry of anguish, stood glaring with streaming white hair, and prepared to leap after it. Had it floated, he surely would have followed. But it sank, vanishing forever. He staggered back and forth, tearing his hair, cursing the ship, the sea, and all the powers of heaven and hell.

"Now!" cried the captain. "There is a church in sight! Steer for it, mate, and you, friends, pray to the saint, whoever he may be!"

They turned the ship toward the distant spire and prayed to the unknown deity it housed. Immediately, a tremendous wave swamped the vessel, breaking the rudder and jamming it immobile while flooding the deck.

Wild with superstitious terror, some rushed to Gerard. "Here is the cause of all!" they cried. "He has never invoked a single saint! He is a heathen; there is a pagan aboard!"

"Alas, good friends, say not so!" Gerard stammered, his teeth chattering with cold. "Call these heathens those lying down praying to the sea. Friends, I honor the saints—but I dare not pray to them now. There is no time! (Oh!) Of what use to me are Dominic, Thomas, and Catherine? Saint Peter sits nearer to God's throne than they, and if I pray to Him, it seems odd, but I shall be drowned before He has time to plead my case. Oh! Oh! Oh! I must go straight to Him who made the sea, the saints, and me. Our Father, who art in heaven, save these poor souls and me who cry for bare life! Oh, sweet Jesus, pitiful Jesus, who walked on Galilee when Peter sank, and wept for Lazarus when the apostles' eyes were dry, oh, save poor Gerard—for dear Margaret's sake!"

At this moment, sailors were seen preparing to abandon the sinking ship in the small launch, a standard feature of vessels even then. A rush of self-preservation ensued, crowding thirty souls into the boat. Three remained behind, bewildered, and two paralyzed by terror. The paralyzed ones sat like heaps of wet rags; the bewildered paced frantically, watching the departing boat but making no attempt to join them, merely wringing their hands. Additionally, there was one person on his knees, praying fervently over a wooden statue of the Virgin Mary, detached reverently from the mast. As water sluiced across the deck, washing the statue about, this poor soul followed it on his knees, clasping his hands to it, while the waves played with both.
