In my youth I fell, by a piece of luck that now seems less luck than providence, into the intimacy of a man of decent exterior and scandalous memory, born—so he swore, and so the world of sailors swore with him—in an island praised, by impartial dealers in maritime jewellery, as the first gem of the ocean. He never let that gem shine for long. He preferred to show it by candle-end, in a back kitchen, with the light full upon its cracks.

He would sit where the peat-smoke and the kitchen-steam made one soft haze, and from under that haze draw, as from an old pot set to simmer, story after story of his nearest blood: every commandment broken as if they had taken the holy table and dashed it upon a stone; murders that would have made Turpin blink; robberies as bare-faced as a butcher’s cleaver. Yet he remembered each wickedness with an exactness a food historian might envy in the cataloguing of sauces—who added what, who stole what, whose hand slipped into which drawer, at what hour, and in what weather. It was a wonder, he said, that any of them lived out of the hulks.

“My brother Tim,” he would begin, and then go on until Tim was dragging a father’s grey hairs down to the grave; “me brother Mick,” until Mick had stripped a parish church as regularly as a cook strips thyme; “me sister Annamaroia,” until she had jilted the Captain for the Ensign, forged a grandmother’s will, and made off with the spoons that poor Larry the knife-boy swung for. The house of Atreus, he maintained, was a small household compared to his own roaring clan—though no power on earth, of course, would induce me to name the country whence he came.

And then—this was what made my young blood curdle more than the crimes themselves—he would tell how these very same hands, still reeking in his telling, would pour each other drink; how these lips, black with perjury, would kiss a sister’s cheek; how they would weep over “the dear old Castle What-d’ye-call-’em,” and the regiment once quartered there, and the tomb of their sainted mother (who had cheated them out of the property), and cry “Heaven bless her soul!” with such tender violence that a stranger might have believed them a family of lambs.

What can there be finer than forgiveness? It is a noble art, particularly among those accustomed to bad language as they are to strong waters. They fling the decanter at a man’s head in the morning; in the evening they retrieve the same decanter, wipe the dust from it, and drink together as if the glass had never flown. Habit blunts the sting of words. Where oaths are daily bread, a curse is chewed and swallowed without choking.

So it was at Tunbridge Wells: our two ladies, aunt and niece, had, the day before, snapped and scratched with admirable spirit; and now, having kissed and made truce with the ease of veterans who know when the bugle calls halt, they played at cards in perfect amity. They drank to each other’s health. They divided the chicken between them—each took a wing with a neat, practised pinch, and they drew the merry-thought with smiling hands. In talk, they scratched their neighbours’ eyes out, not each other’s. They were like Peninsular warriors who, during truce, exchange tobacco and wine with the enemy—firelock always near, flint cocked, the powder dry.

Aunt Bernstein’s threatened gout? The twinges had obligingly vanished. Maria was all solicitude. Maria’s fainting fits? None to speak of—only a slight recurrence the night before. The Baroness was all compassion. Her niece must have the best doctor; she must continue the steel; she must be fortified; she must even, if need were, have bottled waters sent after her when she left Tunbridge. How kind the aunt was. How grateful the niece.

What passed in whispers behind curtains we need not swear to. Suppose the old lady said to her own woman, with a sniff as sharp as gin, “Fainting fits—pooh! Epilepsy, inherited from that scrofulous German mother!” Suppose Lady Maria, with her soft voice and hardened heart, bade her maid taste every medicinal glass first, declaring her aunt capable of poison. Such private talk is the concealed pistol beneath the embroidered cloak. It does not disturb the smile in the public room.

Lady Maria, with Harry Warrington’s bond safe in her pocket like a spice wrapped and hidden from thieves, did not choose to press for payment. She knew the world: you do not force a young man’s hand when you mean to keep him under your thumb. Better to give him day-rule, let him frisk, let him fancy himself free, while the chain lies quiet round the ankle. “Take your ease and amusement, cousin,” she said aloud. Grey Grimalkin purred in the corner: frisk, little mousekin; I watch.

And amusements came readily. Mr. Jack Morris, who delighted to lose money in the company of titled persons, became one of Lady Maria’s diversions. To him it was almost pleasure to have an earl’s daughter take his guineas; to her it was a pleasant, steady income. Madame Bernstein procured him entrance to the Countess of Yarmouth’s assembly; they played; and so, in the first eight-and-forty hours after Mr. Warrington’s departure, Tunbridge rolled on very cheerily—cards, gossip, and those small domestic economies of genteel life: the ordering of a breakfast, the pinning of a lace ruffle, the cutting of a wing, the pouring of tea, the little click of porcelain, and beneath it all the steady undertone of calculation.

Friday arrived: the day for the sermon which Mr. Sampson had been preparing.

The Wells folk, if no conjurer, breakfast, dancing bears, or concert offered itself as rival entertainment, would put up with a discourse; and Sampson had a name for being amusing and eloquent. Moreover, Lady Yarmouth was coming; and Lady Yarmouth, with her vermilion cheeks and flaming taffeta, had a great voice in the giving of livings and the dispensing of bishoprics, the Defender of the Faith of that day having an admirable confidence in her ladyship’s judgement. So Sampson polished his best phrases as carefully as a cook polishes copper, laid out his points like dishes in order, and hoped to serve them hot.

He was the domestic chaplain of Madame Bernstein’s nephew; and both ladies patronised him. On the morning, the Baroness gave a little breakfast in his honour. Sampson appeared rosy and handsome, his wig fresh-flowered, his new cassock rustling—credit, no doubt, from some church-admiring mercer at the Wells. There was a pleasant smell about him of lavender and warmed cloth, that clean, shopman’s scent of novelty which makes even sanctity seem purchasable.

Then, with their ladyships, their lacqueys behind with great gilt prayer-books, Mr. Sampson marched from breakfast to church. The gravel underfoot gave its crisp music; the morning air carried the faint chirp of sparrows and the distant clatter of chairs and hoofs; and over all floated that peculiar Tunbridge hum—polite voices, rustling silk, the town’s shallow merriment.

At the door was a dazzling conflux of fashion. Lady Yarmouth shone among them, a blaze of colour and rouge. Yet there were shabby people too—always a few shadows at the edge of a candlelit room. As the organ stopped, in slipped an odd pair in ragged coats; one had carroty hair peeping from under a scratch-wig. He could not be Protestant, for he crossed himself by instinct and muttered to his comrade, “Bedad, Tim, I forgawt!”—which, if one had ears trained, as I had, by my earlier island acquaintance, betrayed a birthplace without naming it. Wherever they went, a rich fragrance of whisky went too, like a bold seasoning that overpowers every dish.

And not only Catholics appeared. Two men with hooked noses and sallow faces—disciples of a creed older still—worked their way in despite the beadle’s opposition. The beadle, seeing their greasy coats and counting their worth with his eyes, barred the door with his mace. One whispered, “We wants to be conwerted, gov’nor!” and the other slid coin into the beadle’s palm. The mace lifted. In they went.

The organ began again. The doors closed. Mr. Sampson, preceded by the gold-laced beadle, walked up to the pulpit as jolly a figure as you could wish to see, his face full, his cheek bright, his manner prosperous.

Presently he rose from his plump cushion—and turned as pale as death.

He looked to the western door. There, on either side, lingered the two Hebrew caryatides. He glanced then towards the vestry door hard by the rector’s pew, where he had been sitting during the service alongside his patronesses. And suddenly—like cats taking their places before a mouse-hole—two perfumed Hibernian gentlemen slipped out of an adjacent seat and seated themselves close by that vestry door and pew. They sat meekly, eyes cast down, hands still. Only the faintest ghost of whisky and hair-powder marked them, and the soft creak of their boots when they shifted weight.

How can one describe that sermon, if the preacher himself never knew how it came to an end? He spoke; the words came out in their proper order; the cadence rose and fell; the congregation listened and approved; and all the while his mind was like a kitchen where a roast is set too close to the fire—one watches the browning and cannot look away. The sound of his own voice seemed distant to him, muffled by the rushing in his ears. Yet the company pronounced it excellent.

When it was over, the fine ladies buzzed their praise across pews. Madame Walmoden declared it bewdiful and said it made her dremble all over. Madame Bernstein said it was excellent. Lady Maria, pleased that the family chaplain should distinguish himself, looked up and tried to catch his eye. She gave him a little wave of her hand and fluttered her handkerchief. He scarcely noted it; his eyes kept straying towards the font, where the Hebrew strangers still remained.

The people streamed out—first a rush, then a throng, then twos and threes, then a slow dribble of laggards. The organ ceased. The air changed: incense of hair-powder and perfume thinned, replaced by the damp scent of stone and the sharper odour of the crowd’s wool. The church grew emptier, the acoustics barer; each footstep and cough became distinct, as in a house after the feast when only the servants remain.

Everybody was gone. The two Hebrews still waited by the door. Madame Bernstein and her niece still lingered in the rector’s pew, the old lady deep in conversation with a gentleman—some compliment, some sly enquiry, some spoonful of honey with a pin concealed within it.

“Who are those horrible men at the door? And what a smell of spirits there is!” Lady Maria whispered to Mrs. Brett, her aunt’s woman, who waited behind.

“Farewell, doctor,” Madame Bernstein was saying; “you have a darling little boy—will he be a clergyman too?”

“Are you ready, my dear?” she added, and the pew was thrown open.

Because Madame Bernstein’s father was only a viscount, she insisted—out of punctilio or mock humility—that her niece, an earl’s daughter, should precede her out of the pew. Lady Maria stepped forward; and at that moment the two whiskified gentlemen advanced.

One drew a long strip of paper from his pocket.

Lady Maria started, turned pale, and made for the vestry as if she could slip through and bolt the door behind her. But the gentlemen were up with her. One actually laid his hand upon her shoulder—lightly, almost respectfully, yet with the firmness of a hand that knows its right.

“At the shuit of Misthress Pincott, of Kinsington, mercer,” he said, “I have the honour of arresting your leedyship. Me neem is Costigan, madam—a poor gentleman of Oireland, binding to circumstances and forced to follow a disagrayable profession. Will your leedyship walk, or shall me man go fetch a cheer?”

Lady Maria answered with three shrieks and fell swooning to the ground, her fine cloak spreading like a spilled sauce across the flags.

“Keep the door, Mick!” shouted Mr. Costigan.

“Best let in no one else, madam,” he added, very politely to Madame Bernstein. “Her leedyship has fallen in a feenting fit, and will recover here, at her aise.”

“Unlace her, Brett!” cried the old lady; and there was an odd twinkle in her eyes, as if she watched not a niece in distress but a dish at last turning as she wished. As soon as the stays were loosened, Madame Bernstein seized the little bag that Lady Maria wore about her neck on a hair chain, and with a small, sharp snip cut the necklace in twain and had the bag for her own.

“Dash some cold water over her face,” the Baroness said. “It always recovers her. You stay with her, Brett. How much is your suit, gentlemen?”

Mr. Costigan, with the air of a man reciting a bill of fare, answered, “The deem we have against her leedyship for one hundred and thirty-two pounds, in which she is indebted to Misthress Eliza Pincott—”

Meanwhile, where was the Reverend Mr. Sampson?

Like the fabled opossum that, spying the unerring gunner from its gum-tree, says, “It’s no use, Major; I will come down,” Sampson gave himself up. He knew this business; he knew the faces; he had met them before, as a man meets the same creditors again and again, until their footsteps become as familiar as the rattling of a saucepan lid.

“At whose suit, Simons?” he asked sadly.

Sampson knew Simons. “Buckleby Cordwainer,” said Mr. Simons.

“Forty-eight pound and charges, I know,” said Sampson with a sigh. “I haven’t got the money. What officer is there here?”

Mr. Simons’s companion, Mr. Lyons, stepped forward and said, with the smoothness of a host offering a guest a chair, that his house was most convenient, often used by gentlemen, and he should be proud to accommodate his reverence.

Two chairs happened to be waiting outside the chapel. Into those two chairs were placed Lady Maria Esmond and Mr. Sampson, and so they were carried to Mr. Lyons’s residence, escorted by the gentlemen newly introduced to them. The chairmen’s poles creaked; the leather straps groaned; the rhythm of footsteps made a dull drum upon the road. Tunbridge heard it all and understood: the acoustic of disgrace is always louder than the acoustic of prayer.

Not long after the capture, the Baroness Bernstein sent Mr. Case, her confidential servant, with a note to her niece full of the most ardent expressions of affection—regretting only that heavy losses at cards rendered the payment of such a sum quite impossible. She had written to Mrs. Pincott by that very post to entreat time; as soon as she had an answer she would not fail to acquaint her dear unhappy niece.

Mrs. Betty, Lady Maria’s maid, came to console her mistress. The two women, poor in cash and rich in rage, cast about for money enough to send Betty to London. Both mistress and maid had been unlucky at cards; between them they could muster no more than eighteen shillings. It was agreed Betty should sell a gold chain belonging to her lady and, with the money, travel to London.

Now Betty took the chain to the very toy-shop man who had sold it to Mr. Warrington—who had given it to his cousin. The shopman, seeing the bauble in a servant’s hand, supposed she had stolen it and was for bringing in a constable. So Betty had to make explanations; and once she confessed her mistress was in durance, the tale ran through Tunbridge as quickly as the smell of hot punch runs along a gaming-table. Before night closed, everybody knew Lady Maria Esmond was in the hands of bailiffs—knew the house where she lay, the amount (and ten times the amount) for which she was taken, and that she had pawned her trinkets for a little money to keep her in jail. And everybody said, of course, that old fiend of a Bernstein was at the bottom of it.

But money was found at last; and Mrs. Betty whisked up to London in search of the champion in whom the poor prisoner confided.

“Don’t say anything about that paper being gone!” Lady Maria cried. “Oh, the wretch, the wretch! She shall pay it me!”—meaning, no doubt, her aunt. Mr. Sampson read her a sermon; and they passed the evening over revenge and backgammon, consoling themselves with the well-grounded hope that Harry Warrington would rush to their rescue the moment he heard.

The Wells did not cease its music for them.

Though, before the evening was out, every soul knew what had happened, society remained civil and bland. At my Lady Trumpington’s cards that night, where Madame Bernstein appeared, and as long as she was within hearing, not a word was said of the morning’s transactions. Lady Yarmouth asked after the Baroness’s breddy nephew and heard Mr. Warrington was in London. My Lady Maria was not coming? She was indisposed—had fainted at church—must keep her room.

The cards were dealt; the fiddles sang; the wine went round. The gentlefolks talked, laughed, yawned, chattered. The footmen waylaid the supper—the clink of plates and the breathy hiss of lifted covers in the pantry; the chairmen drank and swore in the yard; the stars climbed the sky; and the world, as worlds do, went on as though no lady were imprisoned and no parson arrested.

It is a mortifying truth, and a consoling one: the little stings that pierce us do not keep our neighbours from sleeping. Were we forced to feel every other person’s tight boot upon our own corn, every slander upon our own cheek, every death upon our own heart, the burden would be intolerable. Better, perhaps, that the fiddles play on while somebody rages in a room with a locked door.

Madame Bernstein, for her part, stayed at the assembly until the very last—perhaps to deny the company a chance to speak of her the moment her back turned. It is a comfort we have backs, and that our ears do not grow upon them.

“Don’t tell me!” said Lady Yarmouth. “The Bernstein would play at cards over her niece’s coffin. Talk about her heart! Who ever said she had one? That old spy lost it to the Chevalier a thousand years ago, and has lived ever since perfectly well without one. For how much is the Maria put in prison? If it were only a small sum we would pay it—it would vex her aunt so. Find out, Fuchs, in the morning.”

Faithful Fuchs bowed and promised.

About midnight, Madame de Bernstein went home and presently fell into a sound sleep. She woke late, summoned her attendant, and received her morning dish of tea. If I told you she took a dram with it, you would be shocked; yet many of our great-grandmothers kept cordials in their closets, and a morning mouthful of strong water was counted almost medicinal—an old custom with old scents, like rosemary pressed in a Bible.

Having tipped off the cordial, the Baroness settled among her pillows and asked Mrs. Brett for news.

“He can give it you,” said Brett sulkily.

“He? Who?”

Mrs. Brett named Harry, and said Mr. Warrington had arrived about midnight—and Betty, Lady Maria’s maid, was with him.

“And my Lady Maria sends your ladyship her love and duty, and hopes you slept well,” Brett added, as if repeating a phrase from a lesson-book.

“Excellently, poor thing!” said the Baroness, with sweet calm. “Is Betty gone to her?”

“No; she is here,” said Mrs. Brett.

“Let me see her directly,” cried the old lady.

“I’ll tell her,” replied the obsequious Brett, and went away, leaving her mistress placidly reposing, her tea-cup’s faint perfume mingling with the sharper ghost of spirits.

Presently two pairs of high-heeled shoes were heard pattering over the bare deal floor—carpets being luxuries scarce known in bedrooms of those days. The sound came nearer, brisk and certain, like a knife’s tap on a board.

“So, Mrs. Betty, you were in London yesterday?” called Bernstein from within her curtains.

“It is not Betty—it is I! Good morning, dear aunt! I hope you slept well?” cried a voice that made old Bernstein start upon her pillow.

It was Lady Maria.

She drew the curtains aside and dropped her aunt a low curtsey. Lady Maria looked very pretty—rosy and happy, as if the air outside Mr. Lyons’s house had been a restorative cordial, and the night’s adventure a mere sauce piquante to sharpen appetite. And with that little surprise—her appearance there, in her aunt’s very bedchamber—we may bring this passage to a close.
