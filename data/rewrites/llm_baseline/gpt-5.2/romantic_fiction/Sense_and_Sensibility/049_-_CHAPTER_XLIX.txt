However inexplicable the manner of it might seem to every mind in Barton Cottage, the fact sat plain as a loaf on the board: Edward was free. Free—after four years of bondage to a promise made in the dim, idle season of youth, when a heart, like dough left too long without a hand to work it, will rise into shapes it never meant to take. And what use, Elinor’s family decided, could possibly be made of such freedom, in a man who had once bound himself without his mother’s blessing? Why, to bind himself again—immediately, and this time where every honest wish in the cottage had long tended.

His errand, indeed, was as simple as a supper of good broth and bread: only to ask Elinor to marry him. Yet if he had ever rehearsed any question in his life, it was that one; and still he came to it as if the words must be coaxed into warmth like butter before it will spread. He wanted air. He wanted courage. He wanted—though he would not have named it so—the right hush of a kitchen at work, when the kettle has not yet found its boil and the knives are laid down, and even the birds outside seem to listen.

How soon he got himself into the resolution, how quickly fortune gave him the moment, what he said and how he was answered—those particulars slipped, for everyone in that house, into a blur of breath and beating hearts. It is enough that when they sat down to dinner at four o’clock, scarcely three hours from his arrival, Edward had secured his lady, gained Mrs. Dashwood’s consent, and was—by profession, by truth, by every visible alteration in voice and countenance—one of the happiest men alive.

His happiness held more than the common triumph of accepted love. He had the strange, sudden lift of a prisoner who finds the door unbarred without shame. He was loosed, without reproach to himself, from a snare that had long been misery—from a woman he had long ceased to love—and in the very same breath raised into a safety with another which, once he dared to desire it, must have seemed nearly beyond hope. He had not come from suspense to peace, but from wretchedness to joy; and the change ran through him in a grateful, unforced cheerfulness, as if some long-stifled spring had at last been opened and allowed to sing.

His heart, too, lay open to Elinor: weaknesses confessed, errors owned, that first attachment spoken of at the clear-eyed dignity of four-and-twenty. He even traced it to the hunger of an unoccupied life.

“It was a foolish, idle inclination on my side,” he said—“the consequence of knowing so little of the world, and having nothing to do. Had my mother given me some active profession when I was taken, at eighteen, from Mr. Pratt’s care, I am sure it would never have happened. I left Longstaple, thinking my preference unconquerable; but had I then had any pursuit—anything to engage my time and keep me away for a few months—I should soon have outgrown it. Instead, I returned home to be completely idle. For a twelvemonth I had not even the nominal employment of belonging to the university, for I was not entered at Oxford till I was nineteen. So I had nothing in the world to do but fancy myself in love. And as my mother did not make home comfortable, and I had no friend in my brother and disliked new acquaintance, it was natural I should be much at Longstaple, where I always felt welcome. From eighteen to nineteen I was there most of the time: Lucy appeared everything amiable and obliging. She was pretty too—at least I thought so then; and I had seen so little of other women that I had no comparisons. Considering all this, foolish as our engagement was, and foolish as it has since proved, it was not at the time wholly unnatural—nor, I hope, quite inexcusable.”

A few hours had worked so great a change in the Dashwoods’ minds and happiness as promised them all a night without sleep. Mrs. Dashwood, too happy to be comfortable, could not find a way to praise Elinor enough without embarrassing Edward, or to love Edward enough without overflowing into exclamations; nor could she decide whether it was kinder to leave them to themselves or keep them near her sight, like cherished, newly rescued things.

Marianne could speak her joy only in tears. Joy brought comparisons, and comparisons regrets; she loved her sister too truly not to feel happiness for her, but it was a happiness that did not lift her spirits, nor find her language.

Elinor—what could describe the successive turns of her composure? From the instant she learned Lucy was married to another and Edward was free, to the instant Edward justified the hope that sprang up with the news, she was everything by turns except tranquil. And when doubt and solicitude were at last swept away—when she compared what she had feared so lately with what now stood before her—saw him honourably released, and saw him immediately using that release to offer her an affection as tender and constant as she had ever believed—it was too much. She was oppressed by felicity; and though the human mind grows used to good fortune with surprising readiness, it required hours before her spirits settled into any sedateness, her heart into any quiet.

Edward was fixed at the cottage at least for a week. Whatever other claims might wait upon him, it was impossible that less than a week could be given to Elinor’s company, or suffice to say half that needed saying of past, present, and future. For though a few hours of hard, incessant talking may dispatch more subjects than can be common between any two rational creatures, lovers are not rational in that particular: with them nothing is finished, no account complete, until it has been told again and again, with each repetition tasting different—like yesterday’s stew warmed slowly, deepened, and somehow more nourishing for the time.

Lucy’s marriage, the unceasing wonder of them all, became early matter between the lovers; and Elinor’s knowledge of both parties made it appear, from every angle, one of the most extraordinary circumstances she had ever encountered. How Lucy and Robert could be thrown together—by what attraction Robert could be drawn to marry a girl of whose beauty he had spoken without admiration—a girl already engaged to his brother, and the very cause for which that brother had been cast off—was beyond her. To her heart it was delightful; to her imagination, ridiculous; to her judgment, a complete puzzle.

Edward could only grope toward an explanation: perhaps a first accidental meeting, vanity flattered on one side and artful flattery on the other, drawing them on by degrees.

Elinor remembered what Robert had once told her in Harley Street—his opinion of what his own mediation might have done, had it been applied for in time—and repeated it. Edward laughed with the familiarity of a brother.

“That is exactly like Robert,” said he; and after a moment added, “and that might have been in his head when the acquaintance first began. Lucy might at first have thought only of securing his good offices for me. Other designs could come afterward.”

How long it had been carrying on he could not guess, for at Oxford—where he had stayed by choice after leaving London—he had no means of hearing of Lucy but from herself; and her letters, to the very last, had been neither less frequent nor less affectionate than usual. No suspicion had prepared him. When at last the truth burst on him in a letter from Lucy herself, he believed he had been half-stupefied between wonder, horror, and joy.

He put the letter into Elinor’s hands.

“DEAR SIR,

“Being very sure I have long lost your affections, I have thought myself at liberty to bestow my own on another, and have no doubt of being as happy with him as I once used to think I might be with you; but I scorn to accept a hand while the heart was another’s. Sincerely wish you happy in your choice, and it shall not be my fault if we are not always good friends, as our near relationship now makes proper. I can safely say I owe you no ill-will, and am sure you will be too generous to do us any ill offices. Your brother has gained my affections entirely, and as we could not live without one another, we are just returned from the altar, and are now on our way to Dawlish for a few weeks, which place your dear brother has great curiosity to see, but thought I would first trouble you with these few lines, and shall always remain,

“Your sincere well-wisher, friend, and sister,

“LUCY FERRARS.

“I have burnt all your letters, and will return your picture the first opportunity. Please to destroy my scrawls—but the ring with my hair you are very welcome to keep.”

Elinor read it, and returned it without comment. The cottage was quiet for a beat, except for the small living sounds that always persist in a country house: a clock’s patient tick, the faint creak of settling wood, a sparrow’s brisk dispute in the eaves. Such sounds—unbought, unadvertised—seemed suddenly precious in contrast with the manufactured bustle of town, where even feelings are often paraded like goods.

“I will not ask your opinion of it as a composition,” Edward said at last. “For worlds would I not have had a letter of hers seen by you in former days. In a sister it is bad enough, but in a wife!—how I have blushed over her pages! And I believe I may say that, since the first half year of our—foolish business—this is the only letter I ever received from her, where the substance made me any amends for the defect of the style.”

“However it came about,” Elinor said after a pause, “they are certainly married. And your mother has brought on herself a most appropriate punishment. The independence she settled on Robert, in resentment against you, has put it in his power to choose for himself; and she has actually been bribing one son with a thousand a year to do the very thing she disinherited the other for intending. She can hardly be less hurt by Robert’s marrying Lucy than she would have been by your marrying her.”

“She will be more hurt,” Edward replied, “because Robert always was her favourite. She will be more hurt—and on the same principle will forgive him much sooner.”

In what state the affair stood between him and his family he could not tell; no communication had yet been attempted. He had left Oxford within twenty-four hours of receiving Lucy’s letter and set off with only one clear object—the nearest road to Barton. He could make no plan of conduct that did not begin and end in Elinor. He had to know his fate with Miss Dashwood first; and by the rapidity with which he came to learn it, it might be supposed that—despite an old jealousy of Colonel Brandon, despite modest thoughts of his own deserts, and despite the politeness with which he spoke of doubts—he did not truly expect a cruel reception. Yet it was his business to say that he did, and he said it prettily enough.

Lucy’s marriage led naturally to Lucy herself; and Elinor, without softness, understood that Lucy had intended to wound—meant, perhaps, to depart with a flourish of malice, as she had done once before by her message through Thomas. Edward, now fully enlightened to her character, had no difficulty believing her capable of wanton ill-nature. Yet he confessed how long he had resisted seeing her plainly.

“I had long been aware,” he said, “even before I knew you, of her ignorance—of a want of liberality in some of her opinions; but I always imputed it to her want of education. Until her last letter, I believed her well-disposed and good-hearted, and thoroughly attached to me. Nothing but such a belief could have kept me bound to an engagement which had become, long before it was discovered, a continual source of regret.

“I thought it my duty—independent of my feelings—to give her the option of continuing the engagement or not, when I was renounced by my mother and seemed without a friend in the world. In such a situation, where there was nothing to tempt vanity or avarice, how could I suppose—when she insisted so earnestly on sharing my fate—that anything but disinterested affection induced her? And even now I cannot comprehend what advantage she fancied. Why fetter herself to a man for whom she had no regard, and who had only two thousand pounds? She could not foresee Colonel Brandon’s living.”

“No,” Elinor answered, “but she might suppose something would occur in your favour; that your family might relent. And at any rate she lost nothing by continuing the engagement, since she has proved it fettered neither her inclination nor her actions. The connection was respectable and likely gained her consequence among her friends; and if nothing better offered, it would be better to marry you than remain single.”

Edward was, of course, immediately convinced that nothing could be more natural than Lucy’s conduct, nor more self-evident than her motives.

Elinor, in a tone that tried for severity and could not help tasting of relief, scolded him for his time at Norland—harshly as women often scold a fault that has flattered them.

“Your behaviour was certainly very wrong,” said she, “because—to say nothing of my own conviction—our relations were led away by it to expect what, as you were then situated, could never be.”

He could only plead ignorance of his own heart, and mistaken confidence in the strength of his engagement.

“I was simple enough to think,” he said, “that because my faith was plighted elsewhere, there could be no danger in being with you; that the consciousness of my engagement would keep my heart as safe and sacred as my honour. I felt I admired you, but I told myself it was only friendship; and until I began making comparisons between you and Lucy, I did not know how far I was gone. After that, I suppose I was wrong to remain so much in Sussex; and the arguments by which I reconciled myself to it were no better than these—the danger is my own; I injure nobody but myself.”

Elinor smiled, and shook her head.

Edward heard with pleasure that Colonel Brandon was expected at the cottage; he wanted not only a closer acquaintance, but a chance to convince him he no longer resented Delaford.

“At present,” Edward said, “after thanks so ungraciously delivered as mine were, he must think I have never forgiven him for offering.”

Now he wondered himself he had never yet been to Delaford; but he had taken so little interest in the matter that all he knew of it—the house, the garden, the glebe; the size of the parish, the condition of the land, the rate of the tithes—he owed to Elinor, who had listened to Colonel Brandon’s accounts with such attention as made her entirely mistress of the subject. There was something in that—something like an old domestic art preserved by careful hearing: the way knowledge, passed in plain speech from one person to another, can outlast louder, more fashionable talk that fills rooms and leaves nothing behind.

Only one question remained between them, one difficulty to overcome. They were united by mutual affection, with the warmest approbation of their true friends; their knowledge of each other promised happiness; and yet they wanted something to live upon. Edward had two thousand pounds; Elinor one; with the living of Delaford that was all they could call their own. Mrs. Dashwood could advance nothing. And neither Edward nor Elinor was quite enough in love to think that three hundred and fifty pounds a year would provide all the comforts of life.

Edward was not wholly without hopes of a favourable change in his mother, and on that he rested for what might remain deficient in their income. Elinor, knowing the temper of Mrs. Ferrars too well, could not depend on it. Since Edward would still be unable to marry Miss Morton, and since Mrs. Ferrars had spoken of his choosing Elinor as only a lesser evil than his choosing Lucy, Elinor feared that Robert’s offence would serve no purpose but to enrich Fanny.

About four days after Edward’s arrival Colonel Brandon appeared, completing Mrs. Dashwood’s satisfaction and giving her, for the first time since she had lived at Barton, the importance of having more company than her house could easily hold. Edward retained the privilege of first comer; Colonel Brandon therefore walked each evening to his old quarters at the Park, and returned in the morning early enough to interrupt the lovers’ first tête-à-tête before breakfast—before the tea had properly drawn, before the bread was fully aired from its cloth, before the cottage had quite settled into its day’s soft rural sound.

A three weeks’ residence at Delaford—where, in his evening hours at least, he had little to do but calculate the disproportion between thirty-six and seventeen—brought him to Barton with a mind that needed all the improvement in Marianne’s looks, all the kindness of her welcome, and all the encouragement of her mother’s language, to make it cheerful. Among such friends, however, and such flattery, he revived.

No rumour of Lucy’s marriage had yet reached him. He knew nothing of what had passed; his first hours were spent in hearing and wondering. Mrs. Dashwood explained everything; and he found fresh reason to rejoice in what he had done for Mr. Ferrars, since in the end it promoted Elinor’s interest.

It would be needless to say the gentlemen advanced in each other’s good opinion as they advanced in acquaintance; they could not do otherwise. Their resemblance in principles and sense, in disposition and manner of thinking, would likely have united them in friendship without any other attraction; but their being in love with two sisters, and those sisters fond of each other, made their mutual regard inevitable and immediate—quick as fire taking to dry kindling, but steadier, once lit, than mere sparks of vanity and show.

The letters from town—which a few days earlier would have made every nerve in Elinor thrill with transport—now arrived to be read with less emotion than mirth.

Mrs. Jennings wrote the wonderful tale, vented honest indignation against the jilting girl, and poured forth compassion for poor Mr. Edward, who, she was sure, had doted upon the worthless hussy and was now, by all accounts, almost broken-hearted at Oxford.

“I do think,” she continued, “nothing was ever carried on so sly; for it was but two days before Lucy called and sat a couple of hours with me. Not a soul suspected it, not even Nancy, who, poor soul! came crying to me the day after in a great fright for fear of Mrs. Ferrars, as well as not knowing how to get to Plymouth; for Lucy, it seems, borrowed all her money before she went off to be married, on purpose, we suppose, to make a show with, and poor Nancy had not seven shillings in the world; so I was very glad to give her five guineas to take her down to Exeter, where she thinks of staying three or four weeks with Mrs. Burgess, in hopes, as I tell her, to fall in with the Doctor again. And I must say Lucy’s crossness not to take them along in the chaise is worse than all. Poor Mr. Edward! I cannot get him out of my head, but you must send for him to Barton, and Miss Marianne must try to comfort him.”

Mr. Dashwood’s strains were more solemn. Mrs. Ferrars was the most unfortunate of women; poor Fanny had suffered agonies of sensibility; and he considered the continued existence of each under such a blow with grateful wonder. Robert’s offence was unpardonable, but Lucy’s infinitely worse. Neither were ever again to be mentioned to Mrs. Ferrars; and even if she could be induced to forgive her son, his wife should never be acknowledged as a daughter nor permitted to appear in her presence. The secrecy of the business rationally heightened the crime, because had any suspicion occurred, proper measures would have been taken to prevent the marriage. And he called on Elinor to join him in regretting that Lucy’s engagement with Edward had not rather been fulfilled than that she should thus be the means of spreading misery further in the family.

He proceeded:

“Mrs. Ferrars has never yet mentioned Edward’s name, which does not surprise us; but to our great astonishment, not a line has been received from him on the occasion. Perhaps he is kept silent by fear of offending, and I shall therefore give him a hint by a line to Oxford, that his sister and I both think a letter of proper submission from him, addressed perhaps to Fanny, and by her shown to her mother, might not be taken amiss; for we all know the tenderness of Mrs. Ferrars’s heart, and that she wishes for nothing so much as to be on good terms with her children.”

This paragraph mattered to Edward’s prospects and conduct. It determined him to attempt reconciliation—though not in the manner John suggested.

“A letter of proper submission!” he repeated. “Would they have me beg my mother’s pardon for Robert’s ingratitude to her, and breach of honour to me? I can make no submission. I am grown neither humble nor penitent by what has passed. I am grown very happy; but that would not interest. I know of no submission that is proper for me to make.”

“You may certainly ask to be forgiven,” Elinor said, “because you have offended; and I should think you might now venture so far as to profess some concern for having ever formed the engagement which drew on you your mother’s anger.”

He agreed that he might.

“And when she has forgiven you,” Elinor added, “perhaps a little humility may be convenient while acknowledging a second engagement—almost as imprudent, in her eyes, as the first.”

He had nothing to urge against it, yet still resisted the idea of a written submission; and therefore, to ease him—since he declared himself much more willing to make mean concessions by word of mouth than on paper—it was resolved that, instead of writing to Fanny, he should go to London and personally entreat her good offices.

“And if they really do interest themselves,” Marianne said, with the candour of her newer, chastened self, “in bringing about a reconciliation, I shall think even John and Fanny are not entirely without merit.”

After Colonel Brandon’s visit of only three or four days, the two gentlemen left Barton together. They were to go immediately to Delaford, that Edward might gain personal knowledge of his future home and assist his patron and friend in deciding what improvements were needed; and from thence, after a couple of nights, Edward was to proceed to town. The cottage, when their footsteps had died away down the lane, seemed to listen to its own restored quiet—the wind in the hedgerow, the distant lowing from pasture, the soft domestic clink of life continuing—while Elinor, with love at last permitted to be spoken aloud, began the slow, astonishing work of learning to believe her happiness was not a thing to be taken away.
