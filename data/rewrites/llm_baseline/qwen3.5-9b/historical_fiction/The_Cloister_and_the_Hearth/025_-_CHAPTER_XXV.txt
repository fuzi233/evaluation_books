Denys caught at Gerard, halting his fall, though it is doubtful whether this alone would have spared him a broken neck or shattered limb. His salvation came instead from the dying bear, whose hairy carcass bore the weight of Denys's head and shoulders. Denys tore himself free, but it was futile; the beast still panted, her limbs trembling, yet she was no match for a hare, and soon she breathed her last. The sensible Denys propped Gerard up against the soft corpse, fanning him until consciousness returned. Gerard stirred, confused, feeling the bear around him, and rolled away, screaming.

"Courage!" cried Denys. "The devil is dead."

"Is it dead? Quite dead?" Gerard asked from behind a tree. His courage was feverish, and a chill had seized him, lingering for some time.

"Behold," said Denys, pulling the brute's ear playfully, opening her jaws, and inserting his head within them amidst other insulting antics. In the midst of this, Gerard vomited violently.

Denys laughed at him. "What is the matter now? Also, why tumble off your perch just when we had won the day?"

"I swooned, I trow."

"But why?"

Receiving no answer, Denys continued, "Green girls faint as soon as they look at you, but then they choose their time and place. What woman ever fainted up a tree?"

"She sent her nasty blood all over me. I think the smell must have overpowered me! Faugh! I hate blood."

"I do believe it potently."

"See what a mess she has made of me."

"But with her blood, not yours. I pity the enemy that strives to satisfy you."

"You need not brag, Master Denys; I saw you under the tree, the color of your shirt."

"Let us distinguish," said Denys, blushing. "It is permitted to tremble for a friend."

In answer, Gerard threw his arms around Denys's neck in silence.

"Look here," whined the stout soldier, moved by this sudden display of youthful affection. "Was ever anything so like a woman? I love thee, little milksop—go on. Good! Behold him on his knees now. What new caprice is this?"

"Oh, Denys, ought we not to return thanks to Him who has saved both our lives against such fearful odds?" Gerard knelt and prayed aloud. Presently he found Denys kneeling quietly beside him, his hands crossed over his bosom in the fashion of his nation, with a face as long as his arm. When they rose, Gerard's countenance was beaming.

"Good Denys," he said, "Heaven will reward thy piety."

"Ah, bah! I did it out of politeness," said the Frenchman. "It was to please thee, little one. *C'est égal*, 'twas well and orderly prayed, and edified me to the core while it lasted. A bishop had scarce handled the matter better; so now our evensong being sung, and the saints enlisted with us—*marchons*."

Before taking two steps, he stopped. "By-the-by, the cub!"

"Oh, no, no!" cried Gerard.

"You are right. It is late. We have lost time climbing trees, tumbling off them, swooning, vomiting, and praying; and the brute is heavy to carry. And now I think on't, we shall have Papa after it next; these bears make such a coil about an odd cub. What is this? You are wounded! You are wounded!"

"Not I."

"He is wounded; miserable that I am!"

"Be calm, Denys. I am not touched; I feel no pain anywhere."

"You? You only feel when another is hurt," cried Denys, with great emotion. Throwing himself on his knees, he examined Gerard's leg with glistening eyes.

"Quick! Quick! Before it stiffens," he cried, hurrying him on.

"Who makes the coil about nothing now?" inquired Gerard composedly.

Denys's reply was indirect. "Be pleased to note," said he, "that I have a bad heart. You were man enough to save my life, yet I must sneer at you, a novice in war. Was not I a novice once myself? Then you fainted from a wound, and I thought you swooned for fear, calling you a milksop. Briefly, I have a bad tongue and a bad heart."

"Denys!"

"Plait-il?"

"You lie."

"You are very good to say so, little one, and I am eternally obliged to you," mumbled the remorseful Denys.

Before they had walked many furlongs, the muscles of the wounded leg contracted and stiffened until Gerard could barely touch the ground with his toe, doing so with immense pain. Finally, he could bear it no longer.

"Let me lie down and die," he groaned. "This is intolerable."

Denys argued that it was afternoon and the nights were now frosty; that cold and hunger were ill companions, and that it would be unreasonable to lose heart, especially since a certain great personage was notoriously deceased. Thus, Gerard leaned upon his axe and hobbled on, but presently he gave in completely, sinking helpless into the road.

Denys drew him aside into the wood. To Gerard's surprise, Denys gave him his crossbow and bolts, enjoining him strictly to lie quiet. If any ill-looking fellows found him and approached, he was to bid them keep aloof; if they refused, to shoot them dead at twenty paces. "Honest men keep the path; and knaves in a wood, none but fools do parley with them." With this, Denys snatched up Gerard's axe and set off running—not toward Düsseldorf, as Gerard expected, but back along the road they had come.

Gerard lay aching and smarting. To him, Rome, which seemed so near at the start, now appeared far, far off, despite being two hundred miles closer. Yet soon all his thoughts turned toward Sevenbergen. How sweet it would be one day to hold Margaret's hand and tell her all he had endured for her! The mere thought of her soothed him, and amidst the pain and nerve irritation, he lay resigned, smiling sweetly though faintly.

He had lain thus for more than two hours when suddenly there were shouts. The next moment, something struck a nearby tree hard and quivered within it.

He looked; it was an arrow.

He sprang to his feet. Several missiles rattled among the branches, and the wood echoed with battle cries. Whence they came he could not tell, for noises in these vast woods are so reverberated that a stranger is always at fault regarding their origin; but they seemed to fill the entire air. Presently there was a lull, then the fierce gallop of hooves, followed by louder shouts and cries mingled with shrieks and groans. Above all, strange and terrible sounds, like fierce claps of thunder, bellowed loudly before dying off in cracking echoes. Red tongues of flame shot out intermittently among the trees, and clouds of sulfurous smoke drifted over his head. Then all was still.

Gerard was struck with awe. "What will become of Denys?" he cried. "Oh, why did you leave me? Oh, Denys, my friend! My friend!"

Just before sunset, Denys returned, almost sinking under a hairy bundle—the bear's skin.

Gerard welcomed him with a burst of joy that astonished him. "I thought never to see you again, dear Denys. Were you in the battle?"

"No. What battle?"

"The bloody battle of men, or fiends, that raged in the wood a while ago," Gerard said, describing it vividly, more fully than the narrator has done.

Denys patted him indulgently on the back. "It is well," he said. "Thou art a good liminer; and fever is a great spur to the imagination. One day I lay in a cart-shed with a cracked skull and saw two hosts maneuver and fight a good hour on eight feet square, which I fairly described to my comrade in due order, only not so gorgeously as thou, for want of book learning."

"What, then, you believe me not? When I tell you the arrows whizzed over my head, and the combatants shouted, and—"

"May the foul fiends fly away with me if I believe a word of it."

Gerard took his arm and quietly pointed to a tree close by. "Why, it looks like—it is—a broad arrow, as I live!" He went close and looked up at it. "It came out of the battle. I heard it, and saw it."

"An English arrow."

"How know you that?"

"Marry, by its length. The English bowmen draw the bow to the ear; others only to the right breast. Hence the English loose a three-foot shaft, and this is one of them, perdition seize them! Well, if this is not glamour, there has been a trifle of a battle. And if there has been a battle in so ridiculous a place for a battle as this, why then 'tis no business of mine, for my Duke hath no quarrel hereabouts. So let's to bed," said the professional. With this, he scraped together a heap of leaves and made Gerard lie on them, his axe by his side. He then lay down beside him
