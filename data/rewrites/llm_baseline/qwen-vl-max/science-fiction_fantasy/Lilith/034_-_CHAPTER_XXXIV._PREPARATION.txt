Victory won, the woman from Bulika began to speak of the city—its weakness, its corruption under the rule of a cruel princess, and the fear that gripped its people. In just a few days, the children were buzzing with talk of Bulika, though they had no real understanding of what a city even meant. It was then that I first grasped her purpose—though not yet the reason behind it.

The idea of claiming the city stirred something deep within Lona—and in me too. The children were growing fast, their minds sharpening, their bodies strong. I saw no true obstacle to success. And then there was Lilith—the terrible one, whether woman or beast. I knew her flaw: her daughter. I understood how the ancient prophecy haunted the people of Bulika, binding them in dread. Whatever risk there might be, it seemed worth taking. For if we succeeded—how could we doubt it?—the Little Ones would cease to be mere children and become a young nation, ruled by justice, guided by righteousness. They would govern the wicked with strength and mercy, becoming the redemption of an entire land.

Yet I must confess—my own ambitions stirred as well. It felt right that Lona should sit upon the throne once held by her mother. Naturally, she would make me her partner, her advisor, her consort. I would spend my life serving her. Together, with the Little Ones at our core, what noble world might we not build?

And there was another dream—one foolish, perhaps impossible—of trade between worlds, of gems exchanged across realms. But I knew better than to pursue it; such a thing would only bring harm.

Remembering Adam’s plea, I suggested to Lona that finding water might help speed the children’s growth. She thought otherwise. “We don’t know what water might do to them,” she said. “It might bind them to new needs. They are who they are without it. When we have the city, we’ll seek water.”

So we prepared. We drilled, we trained, we reviewed our ranks—our merry, eager troops. Lona took charge of supplies, organizing food and provisions. I taught the children war: how to throw stones, use slings, draw bows. Some of the larger boys carried arrows; the girls wielded aloe-spikes—tough as steel, sharp as needles—mounted on long shafts. Their task was to protect the youngest among us.

Lona had grown much, though she did not seem to notice. She remained the tallest, her hair long now, her form nearly that of a woman—but still, she kept every grace of childhood. When we met again after our long separation, she laid down her infant, wrapped her arms around my neck, and clung to me silently, her face radiant with joy. The child whimpered—she sprang to him, cradling him instantly. To see her with any restless or stubborn child was to see a gentle grandmother, full of patience and love.

I felt as though I had known her always—before time itself. My memory of my own mother was faint, but in my mind, she looked like Lona. Any image of sister or child I conjured bore her face. She was my heart’s wife. She rarely sought me, but was always near, within hearing. Everything I did, every thought I had, I shared with her. I rejoiced in knowing she worked beside me, independent yet ever close.

She never neglected a child—not one. And my love only deepened my sense of duty. To love her and serve her were not the same, but inseparable. She might suggest a course for me, ask my counsel—yet neither of us ever imagined doing anything but what must be done. Her love poured over me—not in words or touch, but in a quiet, unshakable presence, like the devotion of a sacred creature.

I told her nothing of her mother.

The forest teemed with birds—beauties of plumage so dazzling they made up for the lack of flowers, which seemed unable to grow without water. Their feathers littered the ground, and one day, a thought came to me: I would make a garment for Lona from them.

I gathered the feathers, binding them in overlapping rows. She watched, silent, appreciative of my choices, never asking what I was making, but clearly waiting for the result. In a week or two, it was finished—a long, loose mantle, fastening at throat and waist, with armholes. I rose and placed it on her shoulders.

She stepped back, removed it, and laid it gently at my feet—perhaps out of modesty. I put it back on her, showing her where to place her arms. She smiled, touched the feathers softly, then took it off again, placing it beside her. When she left, she carried it with her. I saw nothing more of it for days.

Then one morning, she returned wearing it—her own creation beside it. She had woven a second garment from the tough, dried leaves of an evergreen. It was strong as leather, patterned like scale armor. I slipped it on at once. From that day forward, we wore these cloaks whenever we rode.

For on the edge of the forest, horses had appeared—full-grown, calm, unafraid of creatures unlike themselves. I quickly befriended them, training two of the finest for Lona and myself. She already knew how to ride a small one, but when she first sat atop the giant beast, her joy was unmistakable. The horse, proud of its rider, moved with dignity and trust.

Each day we rode, building confidence until they obeyed without hesitation, fearless in battle or march. We rode them in review, in formation—ready.

Still, I sometimes doubted. Was this not madness? Yet the woman of Bulika’s confidence—real or feigned—held me. She insisted the princess’s magic would fail against the children. As for any army she might raise, our animal allies alone would give us the edge. “Even I,” she said, “could face two men of Bulika with a good stick.” She admitted fear of the leopardess—but I was ready for her.

Yet I hesitated to take all the children. “Shouldn’t you stay here?” I asked Lona. “With your baby and the smallest ones?”

“No,” she replied. “Their sight will move the women—especially the mothers. When they see the darlings, their hearts will break open. I must be there, encouraging them to stand. If courage remains in Bulika, it will be in the women.”

“You must not carry any child,” I said. “You’ll be needed everywhere.”

But she had two infants besides her own, and even on horseback, she always held one close.

“I’ve never been without a child,” she said simply. “But when we reach the city, I’ll do as you wish.”

Her trust—after all I had failed to do—shamed me. Yet I had not begun this. I could not oppose it. I had no choice but to give it my best.

For myself, I was ready to live or die with her. Her humility, her faith, humbled me. I gave myself fully to her cause.

Our path lay across a wide grassland. No need to feed the horses or the two cows we brought for the infants. But the elephants required care. The grass was good, but short—too short for their long, one-fingered trunks to gather enough. So we set the whole colony to work, gathering grass, drying it into hay. The elephants themselves could carry enough to last several days, supplemented by fresh harvests at each halt.

For the bears, we stored nuts. For ourselves, we dried fruits. We tamed more great horses, loaded them and the elephants with supplies. We were ready.

Then Lona and I held a final review. I addressed the children.

“I’ve learned much about you,” I began. “I know now where you came from.”

“We didn’t come from anywhere!” they shouted. “We’re here!”

“But each of you has a mother,” I said. “Like the mother of the newest baby. I believe you were all taken from Bulika when you were so small you can’t remember. The princess there fears babies—hates them. She wants to destroy them. So your mothers fled, carrying you away, hiding you where she couldn’t find you. Now we go to Bulika—to find your mothers, to free them from the evil giantess.”

“But danger lies ahead,” I warned. “We may have to fight hard to take the city.”

“We can fight! We’re ready!” the boys cried.

“Yes,” I said, “you can—and you will. Mothers are worth fighting for. But stay together.”

“Yes, yes—we’ll watch over each other!” they answered. “No one touches one of us unless it’s his own mother!”

“Listen,” I said. “Obey your officers at once.”

“We will! We will! We’re ready! Let’s go!”

“One more thing,” I added. “When you strike, make it a clean, hard blow. When you shoot, draw the bow to your head. When you sling, throw strong and straight.”

“That we will!” they roared, voices bright and fearless.

“You may be hurt.”

“We don’t mind!” they shouted.

“Some of you may die.”

“I don’t mind being killed!” said one boy, riding a little bull that leapt like a horse.

“Neither do I!” echoed the others.

Then Lona, queen and mother and sister to them all, spoke from her great horse beside me:

“I would give my life,” she said, “to see my mother again. She could kill me—I’d just kiss her and die.”

“Come on, boys!” called a girl. “We’re going home!”

A pang pierced my heart. But I could not turn back. To retreat now would be moral ruin—for them, for us, for everything we stood for.
