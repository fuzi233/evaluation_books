Silence hung heavy over us for what felt like two hours, though it may have been less. The sheer horror of what we had witnessed—the brutality of the witch-finders, the cruelty of Gagool—had left us speechless, our minds too stunned to process the reality before us. Just as dawn began to bleed into the eastern sky, breaking the spell of our dread, a rhythmic tread echoed on the ground. A sentry's challenge rang out from the kraal gate, answered not with voice but with a silent nod, as footsteps advanced steadily toward the hut.

First entered Infadoos, followed closely by six stately chiefs. Their presence commanded immediate attention.

"My lords," Infadoos announced, bowing low, "I have returned as promised. With me stands Ignosi, the rightful King of the Kukuanas. Beside him are these mighty men, each commanding three thousand warriors who live solely to obey the King's will. I have recounted to them the terrors I have seen and the stories I have heard. Now, let them witness the sacred snake coiled around Ignosi's waist and hear his tale, so they may decide whether to join him against Twala, the usurper."

In response, Ignosi removed his girdle, revealing the intricate tattoo of a serpent winding around his torso. One by one, the chiefs approached in the dim light of a single lamp, inspecting the mark with grave intensity. They passed silently, their expressions unreadable, until the last had viewed the symbol.

Ignosi replaced his girdle and began to recount the history he had shared earlier that morning. When he finished, Infadoos spoke again, his voice cutting through the tension.

"Now, chiefs," Infadoos declared, "what is your verdict? Will you stand by this man and aid him in reclaiming his father's throne, or will you remain loyal to Twala? The land itself screams in protest against the tyrant; the blood of our people flows like water in the spring. You have seen tonight what transpires under Twala. Two other chiefs I sought to persuade perished this night, devoured by hyenas. Soon, if you do not act, you will share their fate. Choose, my brothers!"

An elder among them, a short, thick-set warrior with white hair, stepped forward.

"Your words are true, Infadoos," he rasped. "The land cries out. My own brother died tonight. Yet this is a perilous path, a matter difficult to swallow. How can we trust that lifting our spears will not serve a thief and a liar? Of this be certain: rivers of blood will flow before this deed is done. Many will cling to Twala, for men worship the sun that still burns brightly in the heavens rather than the one that has not yet risen. Furthermore, these white men from the Stars possess great magic, and Ignosi sits under their protective wing. If he is truly the rightful king, let them give us a sign—a sign visible to all. Only then will the people follow us, knowing the truth of the white man's sorcery."

"The sign of the snake," I interjected. "That is proof enough."

"My lord," the elder corrected gently, "a snake tattoo may have been applied in childhood. It is insufficient. Show us a sign of power, and then it will suffice. Until then, we will not move."

The others nodded in agreement. I turned in confusion to Sir Henry and Good.

"I believe I have it," Good exclaimed, his eyes lighting up. "Ask them for a moment to consider."

I relayed the request, and the chiefs withdrew. Once alone, Good hurried to his small medicine chest, unlocking it to retrieve a notebook containing an almanack.

"Now, look here," Good said, flipping to a specific page. "Isn't tomorrow the 4th of June?"

"We have tracked the days carefully," I confirmed.

"Precisely," Good continued, tracing a line with his finger. "'June 4th, total eclipse of the moon commences at 8:15 Greenwich time, visible in Teneriffe—South Africa, &c.' Here is our sign. Tell them we shall darken the moon tomorrow night."

It was a brilliant plan, though fraught with danger. The sole weakness lay in the accuracy of Good's almanack. A failed prophecy would destroy our credibility forever, sealing Ignosi's fate on the throne.

"Suppose the almanack is wrong?" Sir Henry suggested quietly.

"There is no reason to suspect error," Good replied confidently. "Eclipses adhere strictly to time. This one will definitely be visible in South Africa. I have calculated the reckoning based on our approximate location; the eclipse should commence here around ten o'clock tomorrow night and last until half-past twelve. For ninety minutes, there will be nearly total darkness."

"Well," Sir Henry conceded, "we had best risk it."

Though I remained doubtful, acknowledging the unpredictability of celestial mechanics and the possibility of cloud cover, I agreed. I dispatched Umbopa to summon the chiefs back. Moments later, they arrived.

I addressed them directly. "Great men of the Kukuanas, and thou, Infadoos, listen. Though we generally refrain from displaying our powers, fearing interference with nature's course, this matter demands action. Angered by the slaughter we witnessed and the heinous acts of the *Isanusi* Gagool, who sought to murder Ignosi, we have resolved to break our rules. We shall provide a sign undeniable to all mankind."

I led them to the hut's door and pointed to the red sphere hanging in the night sky. "What do you see there?"

"The sinking moon," the spokesman replied.

"It is so. Can any mortal extinguish the moon before her appointed hour and veil the land in black night?"

The chief chuckled softly. "No, my lord. The moon is stronger than any man who gazes upon her, nor can she alter her eternal paths."

"You say so. Yet I declare that tomorrow night, roughly two hours before midnight, we shall cause the moon to be consumed for an hour and a half. Deep darkness shall envelop the earth, serving as proof that Ignosi is indeed the King of the Kukuanas. If we accomplish this, will you be content?"

"Yes, my lords," the old chief smiled, a reassurance reflected in his companions' faces. "*If* ye do this, we shall be satisfied indeed."

"It shall be done," I affirmed. "Incubu, Bougwan, and Macumazahn have spoken, and it shall be done. Hear this, Infadoos."

"I hear, my lord," Infadoos replied, awe-struck. "It is a wondrous promise to extinguish the moon, the mother of the world, at her full."

"Yet we shall do it, Infadoos."

"It is well. Tomorrow, two hours after sunset, Twala will summon you to witness the 'Dance of the Girls.' An hour after the dance begins, the girl Twala deems fairest will be slain by Scragga, the King's son, as a sacrifice to the Silent Ones who watch from the mountains yonder." He gestured toward the three jagged peaks where Solomon's Road supposedly ended. "Then, let my lords darken the moon, spare the maiden's life, and the people will believe."

"Aye," the old chief murmured. "The people will believe indeed."

"Two miles from Loo," Infadoos continued, "stands a hill curved like a crescent moon, a stronghold where my regiment and three others are stationed. This morning, we shall organize a plan to move additional regiments there. And if, in truth, my lords can darken the moon, in the shadows I will take your hands and lead you safely from Loo to this place. From there, we shall wage war upon Twala."

"It is good," I said. "Leave us now to rest and prepare our magic."

Infadoos bowed and departed with his retinue. Inside, Ignosi turned to us immediately.

"My friends," he asked, "can you perform this wonder, or were you speaking falsehoods to the captains?"

"We believe we can, Umbopa—I mean, Ignosi."

"It is strange," he mused. "Had you not been English gentlemen, I would not have believed it. But I have learned that Englishmen do not lie. If we survive this ordeal, you shall have my gratitude."

"Ignosi," Sir Henry interjected, "promise me one thing."

"I will promise, Incubu, before hearing the request," Ignosi smiled. "What is it?"

"That if you ever ascend to the throne, you abolish the practice of hunting down wizards and the execution of men without trial."

Ignosi pondered this for a moment. "The ways of black folk differ from yours, Incubu, and we do not prize life as highly. Nevertheless, I promise. If within my power, the witch-finders shall cease their hunts, and no man shall die without judgment."

"A bargain, then," Sir Henry declared. "Now, let us rest."

Exhausted, we fell into a deep slumber, waking only when Ignosi roused us at eleven. After washing and eating a hearty breakfast, we ventured outside to examine the architecture of the Kukuana huts and observe the local women's customs.
