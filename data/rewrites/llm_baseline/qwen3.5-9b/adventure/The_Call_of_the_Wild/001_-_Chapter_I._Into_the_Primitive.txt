Buck did not read the newspapers, yet he sensed the storm gathering. Trouble was brewing—not just for him, but for every tide-water dog of strong muscle and thick, warm coat stretching from Puget Sound to San Diego. Men, groping blindly in the Arctic dark, had stumbled upon gold. Steamships and transport lines were screaming about the discovery, drawing thousands northward. These men needed dogs: heavy beasts with powerful limbs to haul loads and thick fur to withstand the freezing bite of the wind.

Buck lived in the sun-drenched Santa Clara Valley at Judge Miller's estate. The house sat deep within the woods, half-hidden, with a wide, cool veranda wrapping around its four sides. Gravel drives wound through sprawling lawns beneath towering poplars to reach the rear, where the property expanded even further. Great stables housed a dozen grooms and boys; rows of vine-covered servant cottages lined the grounds alongside endless outhouses, grape arbors, lush pastures, orchards, and berry patches. There was even a pumping plant for an artesian well and a massive cement tank where the Judge's boys cooled off in the heat. Over this entire domain, Buck reigned supreme.

Born here and raised here for four years, Buck was no ordinary pet. While other dogs existed on the vast estate, they counted for nothing. There were the kennel dogs who came and went, and the obscure residents like Toots, a Japanese pug, or Ysabel, a Mexican hairless breed—strange creatures who barely ventured outdoors. Then there were the fox terriers, a pack of at least twenty, who yelped threats at Toots and Ysabel whenever they spotted them through the windows, protected by a legion of maidens wielding brooms and mops.

But Buck was neither a house dog nor a kennel dog. He was the sovereign of the realm. He dove into the swimming tank, hunted with the Judge's sons, and escorted the Judge's daughters, Mollie and Alice, on twilight rambles. On winter nights, he lay at the Judge's feet before the roaring library fire. He carried the grandsons on his broad back, rolled them in the grass, and guarded their adventurous footsteps all the way to the stable yard and beyond, into the paddocks and berry fields. He stalked the terriers with imperial disdain and utterly ignored Toots and Ysabel. He was King—ruler over every creeping, crawling, and flying creature on Judge Miller's land, humans included.

His father, Elmo, a massive St. Bernard, had been the Judge's constant shadow, and Buck seemed destined to follow in his paw prints. Though smaller than his sire, weighing only one hundred and forty pounds due to his mother, Shep, a Scottish Shepherd, Buck compensated with the dignity bred from good living and universal respect. He carried himself royally. For four years, he had lived as a sated aristocrat, proud and slightly egotistical, insulated by his privileged position. Yet, he avoided the fate of a mere pampered pet. Hunting and outdoor pursuits kept his muscles hard and his frame lean. Like the cold-tubbing races, he loved the water, finding it a tonic that preserved his health.

Thus was Buck in the autumn of 1897, just as the Klondike strike began dragging men from across the globe into the frozen North.

Buck did not read papers, nor did he know that Manuel, one of the gardener's helpers, was a dangerous acquaintance. Manuel suffered from a fatal flaw: a love for the Chinese lottery. Worse still, he believed in "systems"—rigid betting strategies that required capital far beyond the wages of a gardener's helper, leaving him unable to support his wife and numerous children.

On a memorable night, while the Judge attended a Raisin Growers' Association meeting and the boys organized an athletic club, Manuel betrayed Buck. No one witnessed them slipping away through the orchard, save for a solitary figure near College Park station. There, a stranger spoke gruffly to Manuel, and coins clinked between them.

"You might wrap up the goods before you deliver 'em," the stranger warned.

Manuel looped a sturdy rope around Buck's neck, sliding it under his collar. "Twist it, and you'll choke 'm plentee," he instructed. The stranger nodded.

Buck accepted the rope with quiet dignity. He trusted the men he knew and assumed they acted with a wisdom surpassing his own. But when the stranger took hold of the rope's ends, Buck growled menacingly—a gesture of displeasure intended as a command. Instead, the rope tightened instantly, cutting off his breath. Rage surged through him. He lunged at the man, who met him halfway, grabbed him by the throat, and with a deft throw sent him crashing onto his back. The rope constricted mercilessly. Buck struggled in a frenzy, tongue lolling, chest heaving uselessly.

Never in his life had he been so vilely treated, and never so enraged. But his strength failed. His vision blurred, and consciousness fled as the train arrived and the men tossed him into the baggage car.

When he woke, his tongue hurt, and the violent jolting of the carriage told him where he was. The shrill whistle of a locomotive confirmed it. Having ridden in baggage cars often with the Judge, he knew the sensation. Opening his eyes, he saw only unbridled anger—the rage of a kidnapped king. A man reached for his throat, but Buck moved faster. His jaws snapped shut on the man's hand, refusing to release until the attacker's senses were choked out.

"Yes, he has fits," the man muttered, hiding his mangled hand from the baggageman drawn by the commotion. "I'm taking him up to Frisco. A crack dog-doctor there thinks he can cure 'im."

Later, in a shed behind a saloon on the San Francisco waterfront, the kidnapper recounted the job. "All I get is fifty for it," he grumbled, wiping blood from his hand. "I wouldn't do it over for a thousand, cold cash."

"How much did the other guy get?" the saloon-keeper asked.

"A hundred," the kidnapper replied. "Won't take a sou less."

"That makes one hundred and fifty," the keeper calculated. "And he's worth it, or I'm a squarehead."

The kidnapper peeled back his bandage, revealing a lacerated hand. "If I don't get hydrophobia—"

"It'll be because you were born to hang," the saloon-keeper laughed. "Here, lend me a hand before you pull your freight."

Dazed, suffering excruciating pain in his throat and tongue, with half his life throttled out, Buck tried to confront his tormentors. But they threw him down and choked him repeatedly until they managed to file off the heavy brass collar from his neck. The rope was removed, and he was shoved into a cage-like crate.

There he lay through the weary night, nursing his wrath and wounded pride. Confusion clouded his mind. Who were these strange men? Why keep him penned in this narrow box? An oppressive sense of impending doom settled over him. Several times, when the shed door rattled, he sprang to his feet, expecting the Judge or the boys. Each time, however, it was the bulging face of the saloon-keeper peering in by the sickly glow of a tallow candle. Every joyful bark that trembled in Buck's throat twisted into a savage growl. The saloon-keeper left him alone, and at dawn, four rough-looking men entered to lift the crate.

More enemies, Buck decided. They were ragged, unkempt, and evil. He stormed and raged at them through the bars. They merely laughed and prodded him with sticks, which he attacked with his teeth until he realized that was exactly what they wanted. Sullenly, he lay down and allowed them to load the crate onto a wagon.

From there, Buck began a journey through many hands. Express clerks took charge; he was carted in another wagon; a truck hauled him, mixed with boxes and parcels, onto a ferry steamer; he was trucked off the steamer into a great railway depot, and finally deposited in an express car. For two days and nights, this car was dragged at the tail of shrieking locomotives. Buck neither ate nor drank.

In his fury, he had met the first advances of the express messengers with growls, prompting them to tease him. When he flung himself against the bars, quivering and frothing, they laughed and taunted him, mimicking dogs, mewling, flapping their arms, and crowing. It was all absurd, he knew, making the insult to his dignity worse. His anger burned hotter. Hunger mattered less than the thirst, which drove him to a fever pitch. High-strung and sensitive, the mistreatment had pushed him into a fever, exacerbated by the inflammation of his parched, swollen throat and tongue.

He was grateful for one thing: the rope was gone. That had given them an unfair advantage. Now that it was off, he vowed, he would make them pay.
