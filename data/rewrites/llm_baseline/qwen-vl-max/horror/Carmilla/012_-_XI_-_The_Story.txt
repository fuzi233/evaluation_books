XI. The Story

“I have loved her with all my heart,” the General said, his voice thick with emotion, as if the words were torn from deep within. He paused, gathering himself, then began to speak—his voice low, deliberate—a tale so strange, so chilling, that I felt the hairs on my neck rise as I listened.

“My dear child was eagerly anticipating the visit you had so kindly arranged for her to your lovely daughter,” he continued, bowing slightly, a gesture both gallant and sorrowful. “In the meantime, we received an invitation from my old friend, Count Carlsfeld, whose estate lies about six leagues beyond Karnstein. It was to attend a series of grand festivities in honor of his illustrious guest, the Grand Duke Charles.”

“Yes,” my father replied, “I recall those celebrations. They were said to be magnificent.”

“Magnificent they were,” the General affirmed, “but more than that—princely. His hospitality knows no bounds. One might say he possesses Aladdin’s lamp. The night upon which my sorrow began was dedicated to a grand masquerade. The grounds were opened wide, trees strung with colored lanterns like stars fallen to earth. Fireworks burst across the sky in such splendor that even Paris could not match them. And the music—music, you know, has always been my weakness—music so exquisite it seemed to weave spells in the air. The finest musicians, perhaps in the world, drawn from every great opera house in Europe. As you wandered through the enchanted gardens, moonlight glinting off the castle’s long rows of windows, you would hear voices drifting from hidden groves, or rising softly from boats floating on the lake. I stood there, listening, watching, and felt myself carried back into the dreams and poetry of my youth.”

When the fireworks faded and the ball commenced, we returned to the noble chambers set aside for the dancers. A masked ball is always a sight to behold—but never one so dazzling, so opulent, as this. The company was aristocratic, almost regal. I was nearly the only man present without a title.

My dear child looked radiant. She wore no mask. Her joy, her excitement, gave her face a glow that made her beauty even more striking. I noticed a young woman, magnificently dressed but concealed behind a mask, who seemed to watch my ward with unnerving intensity. Earlier in the evening, I had seen her in the great hall, and again briefly on the terrace beneath the castle windows—always fixed upon my poor girl. With her was another woman, also masked, draped in rich, somber garments, bearing herself with the dignity of nobility. She appeared to be her chaperone.

Had the younger woman not worn a mask, I might have known for certain whether she was truly observing my child. But now, I am convinced she was.

We found ourselves in one of the salons. My dear child, having danced, rested in a chair near the door. I stood nearby. The two women approached—the younger took the seat beside my ward, while her companion stood beside me, speaking in hushed tones to her charge.

Taking advantage of her mask, the elder woman turned to me and addressed me by name, as though we were old friends. Her tone was warm, familiar. She spoke of places we had once met—court functions, grand houses—and recalled small incidents I had long forgotten. Yet at her mention, they surged back into memory as if freshly lived.

I grew increasingly curious. Each time I tried to learn her identity, she deflected me with grace, with charm. Her knowledge of my past seemed impossible. And she seemed to enjoy seeing me struggle, guessing, searching—never quite reaching the truth.

Meanwhile, the younger woman—whom her mother called Millarca—had begun to converse with my ward. She introduced herself, saying her mother was an old acquaintance of mine. She spoke of the boldness allowed by masks, laughed at the guests, praised my child’s dress, and complimented her beauty with such artful ease that it was impossible not to be charmed. She teased gently, shared witticisms, and soon the two girls were laughing together, growing close. Then, with a flick of her hand, the young woman lowered her mask.

Her face—so beautiful, so captivating—I had never seen it before. Neither had my child. Yet, though unfamiliar, it held a power that drew us both in. There was something about her eyes—deep, dark, knowing—as though she saw not just the surface, but the soul beneath.

While I watched, I used the freedom of the masquerade to press the elder woman further.

“You’ve puzzled me completely,” I said, smiling. “Is that not enough? Won’t you at least allow me to know who you are?”

She replied, “Can any request be more unreasonable? Ask a lady to surrender an advantage? Besides, how do you know you’d recognize me? Time changes everything.”

“As you see,” I answered, bowing slightly, “and I suppose with a touch of melancholy.”

“And yet,” she said, “philosophers tell us that time alters not only faces, but memories. How do you know that seeing my face would help you?”

“I’d take the chance,” I said. “It’s futile pretending you’re old; your figure betrays you.”

“Years have passed since I saw you,” she said, “or rather, since you last saw me. That is what I’m considering. Millarca there is my daughter. I cannot be young, even in the eyes of those who forgive age. And I may not wish to be compared to what you remember. You wear no mask. You offer nothing in return.”

“My plea is to your mercy,” I said. “Let me see your face.”

“And mine,” she replied, “is to yours—to let it remain unseen.”

“Well then,” I pressed, “at least tell me—French or German? You speak both languages perfectly.”

“I don’t think I shall tell you, General,” she said. “You intend a surprise. You’re already planning your attack.”

“At least,” I insisted, “you won’t deny that, being honored by your conversation, I ought to know how to address you. Shall I call you Madame la Comtesse?”

She smiled—softly, sadly—and seemed ready to evade me once more—until a man stepped forward.

He wore no disguise. Dressed in plain black evening clothes, he moved with quiet elegance, yet his face was deathly pale, as if drained of life. He bowed low, without a smile, and said:

“Will Madame la Comtesse permit me to speak a few words that may concern her?”

The woman turned sharply, touched her lips in silence, then said to me, “Keep my place for me, General. I shall return shortly.”

And with that, she walked away with the pale man, their conversation serious, urgent. They disappeared into the crowd, and I lost sight of them.

I spent the time trying to guess her identity, wondering if I might discover her name, her title, her estate—something to surprise her when she returned. I considered joining my ward and the young stranger, hoping to uncover the truth before she came back.

But then—she returned, accompanied by the pale man in black.

“I shall return to inform Madame la Comtesse when her carriage is at the door,” he said, bowing deeply.

And then he withdrew—into the shadows, as though he had never been there at all.
