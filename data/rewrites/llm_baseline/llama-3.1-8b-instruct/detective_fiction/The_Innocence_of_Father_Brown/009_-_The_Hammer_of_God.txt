The village of Bohun Beacon clung precariously to the side of a hill, its church spire piercing the sky like a shard of stone. Below, the village's sole inn, "The Blue Boar," stood across from a humble blacksmith's forge, the scent of hot metal and coal hanging heavy in the air. It was here, at the crossroads of the village, that the Bohun brothers often converged – one to begin the day, the other to conclude it. The Reverend Wilfred Bohun, a man of unyielding devotion, made his way to the church, his footsteps quiet on the dew-kissed cobblestones. His brother, Colonel Norman Bohun, lounged on a wooden bench outside the inn, dressed in evening finery, a glass of amber liquid suspended in mid-air as he savored the last remnants of the day.

The Bohun family, a relic of a bygone era, had once traversed the battlefields of Palestine, their pennon a testament to a storied past. Yet, the weight of centuries had seen their noble lineage succumb to the whims of fashion, rather than the preservation of tradition. Like many aristocratic families, the Bohuns had danced to the tune of the times, embracing the excesses of the Mohocks under Queen Anne and the dandyism of the Victorian era. Insanity had whispered its presence in the shadows, a specter that hung over the family like a dark omen. Colonel Bohun's eyes, a piercing blue, seemed almost black due to their deep-set position, surrounded by the wisps of his yellow mustache, which curled down to his jawline like a mocking grin. A bright green hat, an exotic trinket, sat atop his head, a quixotic touch that suited his eccentric nature.

As he raised his glass to his lips, the colonel's gaze drifted toward the church, where his brother was preparing to enter. Wilfred, a man of striking appearance, with hair as yellow as his brother's, was buttoned up to the chin in black, his face smooth-shaven and refined. Some said his dedication to the church was not for the sake of faith alone, but rather a love for the beauty of Gothic architecture. Others whispered that his nocturnal prayers, often held in secret locations like the crypt or belfry, were a manifestation of a deeper, almost masochistic desire for solitude.
