The remainder of this harrowing sequence must be recounted in Thomas's own voice, for it constitutes the core of the confession he delivered to the detectives.

In strict accordance with my pledge, I escorted my young wife to Felix's residence on the appointed day and hour. We traveled on foot, as the estate lay but a short distance from our current hotel. Upon arrival, we were admitted by an elderly servant—a mute and deaf guardian, as I had been warned—who guided us through a hallway suffused with the aristocratic gloom of antiquity. At the threshold, Evelyn paled visibly, casting a questioning glance at me. I offered a reassuring smile, masking the profound dread that churned within me, and placed my arm around her waist. Following the old man past the open door of the drawing-room, we entered a chamber of stark, unadorned simplicity that caused me to frown. However, at the far end, Bartow—known to me subsequently as Felix—parted heavy drapes to reveal a study that, despite its initial austerity, transformed instantly into something akin to a fairyland. Or perhaps it always was, had it not been for the immediate, overwhelming force of Felix's personality, which seized the room and, by extension, our attention, with a magnetic intensity.

Evelyn, to whom I had revealed little of this brother, certainly nothing to prepare her for a suitor of such striking handsomeness or mental composure, appeared initially terrified and awe-stricken. Yet, she advanced toward him with a brave step. When I introduced us, she smiled with an inviting grace that I privately hoped might disarm him.

Perhaps it did. In the yellow, flickering light of the peculiar lantern suspended overhead, his already pale features turned waxy. The hand he raised in a mechanical greeting fell heavily, and he could barely stammer a welcome. Yet, his gaze, locked upon her face, betrayed a depth of emotion he clearly struggled to conceal. She, oblivious to the nature of his feelings, likely unaware that her entire future—and indeed her life, for happiness *is* life—hung precariously in the balance beneath the weight of his indomitable will.

Knowing, or believing I knew, the gravity of the situation, I cast him an imploring glance. "I have explanations to make," I said, "but might Mrs. Adams not rest here while we speak apart?"

He regarded me with a strange expression. Did he sense the revolt in my tone? Did he grasp, as clearly afterward as then, the nature of my compliance? I shall never know. I only recall that he ceased fiddling with a small object on the table, bowed with a sarcastic elegance that made me feel inferior to him even in stature, and led the way to a small, previously unnoticed door.

"Your wife will find it more comfortable here," he observed, his speech halting with pauses that indicated great, though repressed, agitation. He opened the door to reveal a small, elegant sleeping apartment. "What we have to say cannot take long. Mrs. Adams will not find the wait tedious."

"No," she replied, a natural laugh escaping her lips—one born, I dared hope, of perfect happiness. Yet, the strangeness of the procedure must have registered in her mind, as did the unusual demeanor of both her groom and his brother.

"I will call you—" I began, but the vision of her dimpled face rising above the great cluster of roses she clutched silenced me. I forgot the sentence, the door clicked shut, and I found myself standing alone, face to face with Felix.

His breathing had eased, and his manner seemed more natural now that we were isolated. Yet, he remained silent, casting a strange, searching glance around the room—the most bizarre apartment I have ever known. Satisfied, though I could not discern why, he gestured toward the large central table, which from the outset had exuded an uncanny magnetism over him.

"Come further away," he commanded. "I need air, breathing space in this stifling room, and so must you. Besides, why should she hear what we have to say? She will know the worst soon enough. She seems a gentle-hearted woman."

"An angel!" I started, but he cut me off with an imperious gesture.

"We shall not discuss your wife—or Mrs. Adams," he protested sharply. "Where is John Poindexter?"

"At the hotel," I answered. "Or perhaps he has returned home. I no longer concern myself with his existence. Felix, I shall never abandon my wife. I would rather prove recreant to the oath I swore before I truly grasped the value of the woman whose happiness I vowed to destroy. That is why I am here. Make it easy for me, Felix. You are a man who has loved and suffered. Let us bury the past; let us—"

Did I harbor any hope of swaying him? Perhaps a childish delusion influenced me up to this point. But as these words escaped my lips, indeed before I could stumble through them, I perceived by the set, bronze-like rigidity of his features that I might falter, but he stood immovable, firmer than ever, and far less susceptible to entreaty.

Yet, what of that? Even in the worst-case scenario, what did I stand to lose? A struggle that might plunge Evelyn into bitterness and cost me a fortune I had begun to view as my own? These were trivial considerations. Evelyn must eventually learn my true identity and the story of her father's guilt. Why not now? And if we were to begin life impoverished, it was still life; whereas a separation from her...

Before I could finish this thought, Felix spoke, in language I was least prepared to hear.

"I anticipated this. From the moment you pleaded for the privilege of marrying her, I foresaw this outcome and prepared accordingly. Weakness on the part of a bridegroom was to be expected; therefore, I have steeled myself to meet the emergency. Your oath must be kept!"

Crushed by the sheer power in his voice, a tone that promised victory over any ordinary resistance, I glanced around the room, mimicking his earlier survey. Nothing visible suggested treachery, yet a deep-seated distrust—the first I had genuinely felt—had surfaced alongside the strange look he had thrown upward and around the mosque-like interior of his study. Was it his calm confidence, or the weirdness of finding myself amidst Oriental splendor and night effects in broad daylight, surrounded by the clamor of streetcars and daily traffic? It was difficult to say. But from this moment, I was gripped by a vague, superstitious affright—not for myself, but for her, whose voice we could plainly hear humming a gay tune in the adjacent apartment. I resolved to suppress all signs of unease, even smiling, though I felt the imagined eyes of Evelyn's pictured countenance burning into me. I had never lost sight of that portrait since entering the room, yet I had not directly looked at it, and now I stood with my back to it.

Felix, facing it but refusing to raise his eyes, waited for a response. Finding my words stalled, he repeated, "That oath must be kept!"

This time, I found words to answer. "Impossible!" I burst out, flinging doubt, fear, hesitation, and everything I had trembled at to the winds. "It was in my nature to take it, wrought upon by family affection, the awfulness of our father's impending death, and a thousand uncanny influences carefully measured for this end. But it is not in my nature to keep it after four months of living in the companionship of a man thirty years removed from his guilt, and of his guileless, wholly innocent daughter. And you cannot drive me to it, Felix. No man can force another to abandon his own wife because of a wicked oath taken long before he knew her. If you think your money..."

"Money?" he cried, with a contempt that honored both my feigned disinterest and his own arrogance. "I had forgotten I possessed it. No, Thomas, I should never weigh money against the happiness of living with such a woman as your wife appears to be. But her life... I might. Carry out your threat; forget to pay John Poindexter the debt we owe him, and the matter will assume a seriousness for which you are doubtless poorly prepared. A daughter dead in her honeymoon will be almost as great a grief to him as a dishonored one. And either dead or dishonored, he must find her when he returns here in search of the child he cannot long forget. Which shall it be? Speak!"

Was I dreaming? Was this Felix? Was this myself? Were these words actually pouring into my ears?

With a spring, I reached his side where he stood close against the table and groaned rather than shrieked the words: "You would not kill her! You do not meditate a crime of blood—here—on her—the innocent—the good—"

"No," he said, "it will be you who will do that. You who will not wish to see her languish, suffer, go mad. Thomas, I am not the raving being you take me for. I am merely a keeper of oaths.
