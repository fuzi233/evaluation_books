Awe is a troublesome guest: it seats itself at the table unbidden and will not be sent away. The day had laid out its plain fare with a lavish hand—the sun pouring its gold as steadily as broth from a tilted ladle, the sea below taking it like a blue bowl that could never quite be filled. The wind came in clean from the water, salted and sharp as if it had passed through a cellar of seaweed and stone; gulls wrote their thin cries across it, and the steady hush of the surf kept time like a patient breathing in a sleeping house.

Yet all the while I knew the brightness for a curtain—handsome cloth, cleverly painted—ready at any moment to be whisked up, and the stage behind it to show a deeper world.

It was not long after my father’s death that this knowing began to trouble my days. One morning I sat in the library, where the smell of leather and dust had always seemed to me a kind of old family food—dry, yes, but sustaining; the books packed close like loaves cooling on a shelf. I had been staring, with a listless hunger for origins, at the portrait hung among them: a face I knew only as “an ancestor,” and therefore as a name without a taste. I wanted—absurdly, fiercely—to know what sort of man he had been, what manner of air he had breathed, what words he had spoken in his own dialect of life.

I took a volume down and began to read, not because the page drew me, but because the motion steadied my hands. When I glanced up, it was with the mild start of one who hears a footfall where no one should be. A man was coming toward me—yet not between me and the door, but between me and the portrait, as if he had stepped out from the painted past and taken the room for a corridor.

He was thin and very pale, dressed in rusty black as if his coat had been long hung in a chimney corner where smoke had browned it and time had fretted the nap. His face was sharp, eager, cut by a notable nose that brought to mind—ridiculously, inevitably—a jug my sisters had once christened Mr. Crow, because its spout curved like a beak.

“Finding myself in your vicinity, Mr. Vane,” he said, and his voice had a peculiar cadence—dry but not unpleasant, like paper rubbed between the fingers—“I have given myself the pleasure of calling. Your honoured grandfather treated me—as I may say without presumption—as a friend, having known me from childhood as his father’s librarian.”

I did not at the moment count the years that must have sat behind such a sentence. Death had lately rearranged my sense of time; it had made everything at once too short and too long.

“May I ask where you live now, Mr. Crow?” I said, and heard the foolishness as soon as it left my mouth.

He smiled, amused rather than offended, as if I had attempted his name in a tongue I did not quite possess.

“You nearly hit my name,” he returned. “Which shows the family insight. You have seen me before, but only once, and could not then have heard it.”

“Where was that?”

“In this very room. You were quite a child, however.”

The library seemed suddenly larger, as if the walls had stepped back to make room for what my memory might have forgotten. I could not be sure I remembered him; yet for an instant there stirred in me a faint recognition—like a flavour half-recalled from an old dish you ate in childhood, when you cannot name the herb but your mouth knows it.

“I beg you to set me right as to your name,” I said.

“There is such a thing as remembering without recognising the memory in it,” he remarked, as one might speak of tasting without knowing the spice. “For my name—which you have near enough—it used to be Raven.”

I had heard that name; marvellous tales had carried it to me as kitchen gossip carries a rumour from house to house, changing in the telling but keeping its heat.

“It is very kind of you to come and see me,” I said. “Will you not sit down?”

He sat at once, as if the chair had been waiting for him. His movements were quick, economical—those of a man who has lived long among shelves and secret doors.

“You knew my father, then, I presume?” I asked.

“I knew him,” he answered with a curious smile that did not quite belong to courtesy, “but he did not care about my acquaintance, and we never met.—That gentleman, however,” he added, pointing with a long finger toward the portrait, “old Sir Up’ard, his people called him—was in his day a friend of mine yet more intimate than ever your grandfather became.”

Only then did the strangeness of the interview begin to take shape. But why should it be stranger that he remembered Sir Upward, than that he had been my great-grandfather’s librarian? Both were improbable in the same breath.

“I owe him much,” he went on. “For although I had read many more books than he, yet through the special direction of his studies he was able to inform me of a certain relation of modes which I should never have discovered of myself, and could hardly have learned from any one else.”

“Would you mind telling me all about that?” I said. Grief had made me greedy: for any fact, any story, any link—anything that would not dissolve at a touch.

“By no means—as much at least as I am able: there are not such things as wilful secrets,” he answered, and began as if opening a cupboard that had long been kept locked.

“That closet held his library—a hundred manuscripts or so, for printing was not then invented. One morning I sat there, working at a catalogue of them”—and in my mind I saw his careful hand moving over parchment as a cook’s hand might move over a ledger of stores—“when he looked in at the door, and said, ‘Come.’ I laid down my pen and followed him—across the great hall, down a steep rough descent, and along an underground passage to a tower he had lately built, consisting of a stair and a room at the top of it.

“The door of this room had a tremendous lock, which he undid with the smallest key I ever saw.”

As he spoke, the library seemed to listen; even the house settled its creaks, as if it wanted to hear.

“I had scarcely crossed the threshold after him, when, to my eyes, he began to dwindle, and grew less and less. All at once my vision seemed to come right, and I saw that he was moving swiftly away from me. In a minute more he was the merest speck in the distance, with the tops of blue mountains beyond him, clear against a sky of paler blue. I recognised the country, for I had gone there and come again many a time, although I had never known this way to it.

“Many years after, when the tower had long disappeared, I taught one of his descendants what Sir Up’ard had taught me; and now and then to this day I use your house when I want to go the nearest way home. I must indeed—without your leave, for which I ask your pardon—have by this time well established a right of way through it—not from front to back, but from bottom to top!”

The words had the cool assurance of a man discussing a path across a field, a gate left unlatched. Yet they set a strange pressure in my head, as if he were asking me to accept an untranslatable experience in the language of ordinary rooms.

“You would have me then understand, Mr. Raven,” I said, “that you go through my house into another world, heedless of disparting space?”

“That I go through it is an incontrovertible acknowledgement of space,” returned the old librarian.

“Please do not quibble, Mr. Raven,” I rejoined. “Please to take my question as you know I mean it.”

“There is in your house a door, one step through which carries me into a world very much another than this.”

“A better?”

“Not throughout; but so much another that most of its physical, and many of its mental laws are different from those of this world. As for moral laws, they must everywhere be fundamentally the same.”

“You try my power of belief!” I said. The sea outside kept its immense whispering; it seemed suddenly like a great ear pressed to the window.

“You take me for a madman, probably?”

“You do not look like one.”

“A liar then?”

“You give me no ground to think you such.”

“Only you do not believe me?”

“I will go out of that door with you if you like: I believe in you enough to risk the attempt.”

“The blunder all my children make!” he murmured, more to himself than to me. “The only door out is the door in!”

His sentence fell in my mind like a proverb in a dead dialect: I could hear its weight and shape, and yet it would not quite yield me its sense. I began to think him cracked.

He sat silent for a moment, head resting on his hand, elbow on the table, eyes on the backs of books—those patient, closed mouths.

“A book,” he said louder, “is a door in, and therefore a door out.—I see old Sir Up’ard,” he went on, closing his eyes, “and my heart swells with love to him:—what world is he in?”

“The world of your heart!” I replied. “That is, the idea of him is there.”

“There is one world then at least on which your hall-door does not open?”

“I grant you so much; but the things in that world are not things to have and to hold.”

“Think a little farther,” he rejoined. “Did anything ever become yours, except by getting into that world?—The thought is beyond you, however, at present!—I tell you there are more worlds, and more doors to them, than you will think of in many years!”

He rose abruptly, as if the time for talk were a mere garnish and the real meal awaited elsewhere. He left the library, crossed the hall, and went straight up toward the garret with the confidence of one who knows every turn by the sound of his own footfall. I followed, studying his back as if it might tell me what his face would not. His hair hung long and dark, straight and glossy, like a spill of ink. His coat was wide and reached to his heels. His shoes seemed too large, as if he had stepped into them from some other life and never quite grown to fit.

In the garret, light came through at the edges of the great roofing slabs in thin blades, and dust floated in them like flour shaken in a beam over a baker’s board. There were places where no flooring lay; we must step from joist to joist, the hollow below breathing up a cool, dry scent of old wood. The house here had a different voice: the small clicks of settling timbers, the whisper of grit under our soles, the far thud of a shutter moving in the sea-wind.

In the middle of one of the open spaces rose a partition with a door. Through it I followed Mr. Raven into a small, obscure chamber whose top contracted as it rose and went slanting through the roof, as if the room itself were trying to press its head into the sky.

“That is the door I spoke of,” he said, pointing.

It was an oblong mirror, standing on the floor and leaning against the wall. I went before it and saw our figures dimly reflected in its dusty face. It looked old-fashioned and neglected; the frame had that air of an heirloom shoved aside. Yet on its top perched an eagle with outstretched wings—carved, severe, and somehow threatful, like a heraldic predator frozen at the moment of strike.

“As a mirror,” said the librarian, “it has grown dingy with age; but that is no matter: its clearness depends on the light.”

“Light!” I rejoined. “There is no light here!”

He did not answer, but began to pull at a little chain on the opposite wall. I heard a creaking, slow and complaining like a mill-wheel turned after long idleness: the top of the chamber was revolving.

He ceased pulling, looked at his watch with an exactness that felt almost culinary—timing a thing that must not be overdone—and began to pull again.

“We arrive almost to the moment!” he said. “It is on the very stroke of noon!”

The top went creaking and turning for a minute or so. Then he pulled two other chains—now this, now that—and returned to the first, as if setting unseen shutters, angling unseen mirrors, tasting the air for readiness.

A moment more and the chamber grew clearer. A patch of sunlight had fallen upon a mirror on the wall opposite that against which the other leaned, and on the dust I saw the path of the reflected rays drawn like a narrow road. The ray struck the mirror on the ground—

—and from it none were returned.

There was nowhere in the chamber a second patch of light. The sunbeam had gone as cleanly as water through a sieve, leaving no spill, no glimmer, no warmth on the boards. My skin tightened with a kind of cold wonder.

“Where are the sunrays gone?” I cried.

“That I cannot tell,” returned Mr. Raven; “back, perhaps, to where they came from first. They now belong, I fancy, to a sense not yet developed in us.”

Then he began to talk—of the relations of mind to matter, and of senses to qualities—in a manner I could follow only a little, the way one follows a recipe spoken too quickly in an unfamiliar tongue. He went on to stranger things I could not at all comprehend: dimensions beyond the three that my hands and eyes had always trusted; powers in us like dormant flavours in an uncrushed seed, present but inaccessible until some proper bruising releases them. His words slid over my understanding; they did not take hold. I thought he hardly knew what he was saying. The chamber’s acoustic seemed to shift as he spoke, as if the very air had changed its grain; the creak of the turning roof sounded farther off, the house’s small noises muffled, like a kitchen suddenly thick with steam.

Suddenly I became aware that our forms had gone from the mirror. Its face, where we should have been, seemed full of a white mist, as if the glass had turned into a shallow, breathing cloud.

I stared. Beyond the mist something began to show—first faintly, then more clear—the tops of a range of mountains. They grew distinct with a steadiness that did not feel like imagination: blue upon blue under a sky of paler blue, as Mr. Raven had described. Soon the mist vanished entirely, uncovering the face of a wide heath. At some distance on it was the figure of a man moving swiftly away.

I turned to address my companion.

He was no longer by my side.

A dread, sharp as the smell of scorched fat, rose in me. I looked again at the moving form within the mirror and recognised the wide coat flying and the long black hair lifting in a wind that did not touch me—wind that made no sound in my ears, as if it belonged to another acoustic world altogether.

And then terror, simple and bodily, took me by the throat.

I rushed from the place.
