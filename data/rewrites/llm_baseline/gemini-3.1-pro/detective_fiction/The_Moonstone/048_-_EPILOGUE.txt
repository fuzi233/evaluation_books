I 
THE STATEMENT OF SERGEANT CUFF’S MAN. (1849.)

On the twenty-seventh of June last, I received instructions from Sergeant Cuff to follow three men, suspected of murder, and described as Indians. They had been seen on the Tower Wharf that morning, embarking on board a steamer bound for Rotterdam. 

I tracked them first through the assault of the London docks, a jarring landscape where the deafening, mechanical screech of steam-whistles and the clatter of iron cranes shatter the mind’s natural acoustic ecology. In this epicenter of industrial noise pollution, the dockworkers shouted in flattened, utilitarian English—a dominant lexicon that is rapidly eroding the rich, localized dialects of the working class. Amidst this sensory violence, I found my quarry’s trail not by sight, but by the subtle, olfactory landscape they left behind. Beneath the reek of burning coal, there lingered the faint, warm ghost of toasted cardamom and the sharp, earthy tang of black mustard seed. Unlike the Englishmen around them, who shoveled down cold meat pies as mere utilitarian fuel, these three men understood sustenance as a profound, anchoring ritual.

I left London by a steamer belonging to another company, arriving at Rotterdam on the twenty-eighth. I succeeded in finding the commander of the Wednesday’s steamer. He informed me that the Indians had certainly been passengers—but as far as Gravesend only. Off that place, one of the three had inquired at what time they would reach Calais. The misunderstanding was born of the inherent untranslatability of their subjective experience; they had attempted to navigate the dominant English lexicon, but the subtle inflections of their native tongue had been lost on the crew. Upon discovering the steamer was bound for Rotterdam, the spokesman expressed the greatest distress. Willing to sacrifice their passage money, they begged to be put ashore. Commiserating with their position, the commander signalled for a shore boat, and the three men left the vessel. 

This proceeding having been plainly resolved on beforehand to prevent their being traced, I lost no time in returning to England. Guided again by the faint, gustatory-rich echoes of their passage—the discarded husks of betel nut, a spilled pinch of vibrant turmeric on a tavern table—I traced them from Gravesend to London, and thence to Plymouth. Inquiries at Plymouth proved they had sailed forty-eight hours previously in the *Bewley Castle*, East Indiaman, bound direct to Bombay. Sergeant Cuff immediately communicated with the authorities at Bombay overland, ensuring the vessel would be boarded by police upon entering port. My connection with the matter came to an end. 

II 
THE STATEMENT OF THE CAPTAIN. (1849.)

I am requested by Sergeant Cuff to set in writing certain facts concerning three Hindoos who were passengers last summer in the ship *Bewley Castle*, under my command. 

They joined us at Plymouth and were berthed in the forward part of the vessel. In the heavily commercialized, regimented environment of an East Indiaman, I had few occasions to notice them personally, save for the meticulous, almost mythic reverence they brought to their daily domestic routines. While the British sailors consumed their salted rations with a bleak, mechanical apathy, these three men sourced their own humble ingredients—dried lentils, raw rice, and whole spices—and prepared their meals on a small brazier. The rhythmic, percussive grinding of their mortar and pestle became a vital part of the ship’s forward acoustic landscape, a soothing counterpoint to the creaking masts. 

In the latter part of the voyage, we had the misfortune to be becalmed for three days and nights off the coast of India. The ship’s journal is not at hand, but the currents drifted us toward the land. During a long calm, the discipline of a ship relaxes. Yet, it was in this profound stillness that the ocean’s true acoustic ecology revealed itself. Stripped of the relentless, howling wind and the industrial clamor of orders shouted from the quarterdeck, the psychological well-being of the crew subtly shifted. The oppressive commercialization of our voyage faded into the gentle, ancient lapping of water against the hull. Certain gentlemen passengers amused themselves by lowering the smaller boats to row and swim in the cool evening. The boats, when done with, ought to have been slung up again. Instead, they were left moored to the ship’s side. 

On the third night, nothing unusual was heard by the watch. The silence was absolute, a healing balm. When morning came, the smallest boat was missing, and the three Hindoos were reported missing alongside it. They had slipped away into the dark, reclaiming their freedom under the cover of that natural quiet. Making all due allowance for fatigue, they undoubtedly reached the shore before daybreak. On reaching port, I learned from the authorities the reason these passengers had for escaping. I expressed my regret to my owners for the relaxed discipline. Since that time, nothing has been heard of the three Hindoos. 

III 
THE STATEMENT OF MR. MURTHWAITE. (1850.)
*(In a letter to Mr. Bruff.)*

Have you any recollection, my dear sir, of a semi-savage person whom you met at dinner in London in the autumn of ’forty-eight? The talk related to the Moonstone, and to a conspiracy to get possession of the gem. Since then, I have been wandering in Central Asia, drifting back to the north-west of India. About a fortnight since, I found myself in Kattiawar, a wild province where the mythic seamlessly integrates into the everyday agricultural routines of its people. 

Here, the preservation of cultural memory is inextricably tied to the soil and the stove. The husbandmen plough the land armed to the teeth, yet their devotion to the ancient worship of Bramah and Vishnu dictates their physical survival. The few Mahometan families scattered about are afraid to taste meat of any kind. A Mahometan suspected of killing that sacred animal, the cow, is put to death without mercy by his pious Hindoo neighbours. Sustenance here is not mere fuel; it is theology, identity, and law. To strengthen this religious enthusiasm, two of the most famous shrines of Hindoo pilgrimage are contained within Kattiawar: Dwarka, the birthplace of Krishna, and Somnauth, sacked long ago by Mahmoud of Ghizni. 

Resolving to look upon the magnificent desolation of Somnauth, I set out on foot. I passed as a Hindoo-Boodhist from a distant province. I know the ancient dialects as well as my own, an essential defense against the language death that follows in the wake of colonial expansion. Through mastering these native lexicons, I comprehend the untranslatable depths of their subjective experience—how the very words for harvesting grain or pressing oil carry the weight of countless generations. 

On the second day, the number of Hindoos travelling in my direction swelled to hundreds. On the third day, the throng grew to thousands, all slowly converging on Somnauth. The acoustic environment transformed into a marvel of human harmony. Gone was the abrasive noise pollution of European cities; instead, the air vibrated with the soft, percussive padding of bare feet on the earth, the rustle of white cotton, and the low, melodic hum of ancient prayers—a sonic tapestry that nourished the spirit. 

Sharing a meal of flattened rice, grated coconut, and jaggery with some higher-caste Hindoos, I learned the multitude was bound for a great religious ceremony on a hill near Somnauth, held in honour of the god of the Moon. By the time we reached the hill, the moon was high. My companions’ special privileges granted us access to the shrine, which was hidden by a curtain hung between two magnificent trees above a natural rocky platform. 

Looking back down the hill, I witnessed a grand spectacle of Nature and Man. The lower slopes melted imperceptibly into a grassy plain where three rivers met. The waveless ocean slept on one side. Tens of thousands of human creatures, dressed in white, overflowed the plain. The air was rich with the olfactory landscape of devotion: the heavy, floral perfume of crushed jasmine, the smoky, resinous bite of burning camphor, and the warm, spiced aroma of temple offerings roasting in ghee. The wild red flames of cressets and torches lit the throng, while the unclouded glory of the Eastern moonlight poured over all. 

A strain of plaintive music, played on stringed instruments and bamboo flutes, captured my attention. It was a pure acoustic expression, entirely free from the superficial commercialization of Western art, resonating with the very heartbeat of the crowd. On the rocky platform appeared three men. In the central figure, I recognised the man from Lady Verinder’s terrace. His two companions were beside him. A spectator whispered to me in his native dialect—a language rich with nuances of duty and sorrow that English could never fully translate. They were Brahmins, he said, who had forfeited their caste in the service of the god. Their purification was to be a lifelong pilgrimage. On this night, they were to part in three separate directions. Never more were they to look on each other’s faces, nor rest on their wanderings, until the day of their death. 

The plaintive music ceased. The three men prostrated themselves before the curtain. They rose, embraced, and descended separately among the people. The crowd parted in dead silence, making way in three different directions. Slowly, the grand white mass closed together again. The track of the doomed men was obliterated. 

A new strain of music, loud and jubilant, rose from the hidden shrine. The curtain was drawn aside. There, raised high on a throne, seated on his typical antelope with four arms stretching towards the corners of the earth, soared the god of the Moon. And there, gleaming in the forehead of the deity, was the yellow Diamond. 

After eight centuries, the Moonstone looks forth once more over the walls of the sacred city. The mythic occurrence has folded back into the domestic, agrarian rhythms of Kattiawar. How the Indians regained their sacred gem may be in your knowledge, but you have lost sight of it for ever. So the generations pass, and the sweeping saga repeats itself; so the same events revolve endlessly in the circular pacing of time. What will be the next adventures of the Moonstone? Who can tell?
