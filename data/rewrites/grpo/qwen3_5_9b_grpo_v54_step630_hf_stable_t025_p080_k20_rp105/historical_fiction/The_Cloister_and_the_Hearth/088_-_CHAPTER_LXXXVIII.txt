Supper was served, the table groaning under the weight of savory meats and steaming dishes, yet the air felt heavy with an absence. Eli's family sat gathered, save for one. To Catherine's astonishment, Eli declared he would wait for her return.

"Why, I told her you would not wait for the Duke," someone remarked.

"She is no Duke," Eli replied, his voice firm despite the tension. "She is a poor, good lass who has waited not minutes, but years, for a graceless son of mine. Set the food down; we shall eat when she arrives, or not at all."

The aroma of the roasting meat was irresistible. Relenting, Eli nodded. "She will come if we begin. They always do." He gestured for Mistress Joan to take her seat. "You are not here as a servant, I trust, but as a guest." Just then, the sound of hurried footsteps echoed from the hallway. "Come, sit," Eli urged, pulling out his chair. "Fall to."

The covers were drawn back, and the knives were raised in anticipation.

Instead of the expected figure of Margaret, the door burst open to reveal a Dominican friar, his face a mask of livid rage. In a single, terrifying motion, he crossed the room, throwing his tall, gaunt frame over the narrow table. With both hands hovering above the shrinking heads of Cornelis and Sybrandt, like eagles poised to strike a trapped quarry, he unleashed a torrent of curses. It was a litany of ancient venom, so dense and blighting that words seemed insufficient to capture its horror.

"Cursed be the lips," he shrieked, his voice cracking with fury, "that spoke the lie that Margaret was dead! May they rot before the grave and kiss white-hot iron in hell thereafter! Doubly cursed be the hands that altered those letters, may they be severed by the hangman's blade to handle eternal fire! Thrice accursed be the cruel hearts that conceived this damned lie to tear true love asunder! May they sicken and wither on earth, joyless and hopeless, reduced to dust before their time and burned in everlasting flames!"

His wrath extended to the very meal before them; he cursed the meat in their throats and every atom of their bodies from hair to sole. Turning from the pair, who cowered beneath the table, trembling in fear, he tore a letter from his bosom and hurled it onto the table before his father.

"Read this, thou hard old man who imprisoned thy son! Read and behold the monsters thou hast spawned! The memory of my wrongs and hers shall haunt you forever! I shall meet you again at the Judgment Day; on this earth, thou shalt never see me once more."

In an instant, as he had arrived, he vanished, leaving the household stiff, cold, and pale as statues around the untouched feast.

This was the scene that greeted Margaret and Jorian. Figures of men and women, petrified in horror, surrounded the smoking board, a tableau reminiscent of Eastern poets' tales of despair.

Margaret scanned the room, her breath catching in her throat. "Oh, joy! All are here; no blood has been shed. Oh, you cruel, cruel men! I thank God he has not slain you."

At the sight of her, Catherine let out an eloquent scream before turning her head away in shame. But Eli, having just glanced at the false letter and grasping the terrible truth, saw the other victim enter with her own sorrows etched into her pale, sweet face. A transport of rage seized him. He sprang to his feet, shouting, "Stand clear! Let me reach the traitors! I will hang for them!"

Before anyone could react, he whipped out his short sword and lunged forward.

"Fly!" Margaret screamed. "Fly!"

They slipped howling under the table, scrambling out the other side. But before they could reach the door, the furious old man rounded the table to intercept them. Catherine merely screamed and wrung her hands; in moments of crisis, the notables are often useless. Blood would surely have flowed, but Margaret and Jorian seized the elderly man's arms, holding him fast with all their might while the couple escaped. They released him only as they fled, and he went on, vainly raging after them into the street.

They ran like hares for a furlong until safety was assured.

Inside, the old man hacked down the board bearing their names, carried it indoors, and threw it into the fireplace. Catherine sat rocking herself, her apron pulled over her head in despair. Joan had rushed to her husband. Margaret clung to Catherine's neck, pale and panting, yet striving to offer comfort.

"It cannot be done," she whispered. "Oh, my poor children! Oh, miserable mother! 'Tis a mercy Kate was ill upstairs. There, I have lived to thank God for that!" She broke into fresh sobs. "It would have killed her. He had better have stayed in Italy, coming home only to curse his own flesh and blood and set us all by the ears."

"Oh, hold your tongue, woman," Eli snapped angrily. "You still stand on the side of the wrongdoer. You are cheaply served; your weakness made these rogues what they are. I was prepared to correct them in their youth with sharp remedies for sore ills, but you sided with their faults and undermined me, baffling wise severity. And you, Margaret, leave comforting her who ought to comfort you. What is her hurt compared to yours? But she never possessed a grain of justice under her skin, nor will she. So come to me, for I am thy father from this hour."

It was a command. Margaret kissed Catherine goodbye and tottered toward him. He placed her on a chair beside him, and she laid her feeble head against his honest breast, though not a tear fell; the sorrow was too deep for such a display.

"Poor lamb," he murmured. After a pause, in a broken voice, he addressed Jorian and Joan. "Come, good folks, we are in a little trouble, as you see, but that is no reason you should starve. For our Lady's sake, eat. Do not add to my grief the reputation of a churl. What the dickens!" He forced a ghastly attempt at stout-heartedness. "The more knaves I have the luck to get rid of, the greater my need of true men and women to help me clear the dish and cheer my eye with honest faces where else there are gaps. Eat, I entreat you."

Catherine, sobbing, backed his request. Poor, simple, antique, hospitable souls! Jorian, whose appetite, especially since his illness, was keen, was ready to accept this invitation. But Joan whispered a word in his ear, and he instantly recoiled. "Nay, I will touch no meat that Holy Church hath cursed."

"In sooth, I forgot," Eli apologized. "My son, who was reared at my table, hath cursed my victuals. That seems strange. Well, what God wills, man must bow to."

The supper was flung out into the yard.

Jorian took his wife home, and a heavy sadness reigned in Eli's house that night.

Meanwhile, where was Clement?

He lay full-length upon the floor of the convent church, his lips pressed against the lowest step of the altar. He existed in an indescribable state of terror, misery, penitence, and self-abasement, through which struggled faint gleams of joy that Margaret was alive.

Night fell, finding him there weeping and praying. Morning would have found him there too; but suddenly, he remembered that absorbed in his own wrongs and Margaret's, he had committed another sin besides intemperate rage. He had neglected a dying man.

He rose instantly, groaning at his accumulated wickedness, and set out to repair the omission. The weather had turned; it rained hard. As he cleared the town, he heard wolves bay; they were upon his heels. Yet Clement was himself again, or nearly so. He thought little of danger or discomfort, driven by a shameful omission of religious duty he must repair. He moved stoutly forward through rain and darkness.

As he walked, he often beat his breast, crying out, "Mea Culpa! Mea Culpa!"
