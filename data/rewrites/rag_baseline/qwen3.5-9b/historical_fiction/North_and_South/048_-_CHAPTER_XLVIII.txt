The dinner parties hosted by Mrs. Lennox were spectacles of cultivated brilliance, meticulously engineered to dazzle. Beauty was supplied by her guests; Captain Lennox provided the effortless command of current affairs; and Mr. Henry Lennox, accompanied by a scattering of ambitious young men, furnished the wit and the razor-sharp intellect that flowed so naturally they appeared unburdened by effort. Yet, amidst this glittering display, Margaret's dissatisfaction remained a silent, corrosive undercurrent. She watched as every facet of human capability—every talent, every sentiment, even every inclination toward virtue—was consumed as fuel for fireworks. The sacred, hidden flame burned itself out in a shower of sparks and crackles, leaving nothing but embers. Art was discussed only in terms of its sensual surface, its external allure, devoid of any attempt to grasp its deeper teachings. High-minded subjects were lashed into enthusiastic rhetoric within the confines of the dining room, only to be abandoned the moment the doors closed and solitude returned. Their capacity for genuine appreciation was squandered, distilled down to a mere stream of socially appropriate phrases.

One evening, after the gentlemen had retreated to the drawing-room, Mr. Lennox approached Margaret. It was nearly the first voluntary word he had spoken to her since her return to Harley Street. "You did not seem pleased by what Shirley was saying at dinner," he observed.

"Didn't I?" Margaret replied, a faint smile touching her lips. "My face must be remarkably expressive. It seems to have retained the knack of eloquence."

"I did not like," she said quickly, "his manner of advocating what he knew to be wrong—so glaringly wrong, even in jest."

"But it was exceedingly clever. How each word landed! Did you recall the felicitous epithets?"

"Yes."

"And yet, you would likely despise them if you considered them seriously. Do not hesitate to tell me so, despite the fact that he is my friend."

"There!" she stopped abruptly, realizing the trap. "That is precisely the tone in you, that—"

He paused, listening intently to see if she would complete her thought. She flushed crimson and turned away. As she moved, he spoke in a voice low and clear, cutting through the noise of the room. "If my tones, or my modes of thought, are what you dislike, will you grant me the justice of telling me so directly? And thus give me the opportunity to learn how to please you."

For weeks thereafter, there was no word from Mr. Bell regarding his departure for Milton. He had hinted at the journey at Helstone as a possibility arising soon, yet he must have managed his affairs through correspondence. Margaret reasoned that if he possessed the ability, he would certainly avoid traveling to a place he disliked, especially one he understood so little. She knew he recognized the necessity of the meeting, yet the timing mattered less to her than the medium; the explanation required the intimacy of speech, not ink. Whether it was summer, autumn, or winter, the season held little significance compared to the delay. It was now August, and the Spanish trip he had casually mentioned to Edith remained unmentioned. Margaret struggled to reconcile herself to the fading of this illusion.

Then, one morning, a letter arrived. Next week, he intended to come to town. He wished to consult her on a plan forming in his mind. Furthermore, he planned to seek medical attention, having finally conceded to her view that it was preferable to attribute his irritability and crossness to failing health rather than to his own stubbornness. Reading the letter later, Margaret noted a tone of forced cheerfulness, but at the time, her attention was captured by Edith's frantic exclamations.

"Coming up to town! Oh dear! And I am so utterly drained by the heat that I doubt I possess the strength for another dinner. Besides, everyone has departed save our dear, stupid selves, who cannot decide where to go. There will be no one to meet him."

"I am certain he would far prefer dining with us in private company rather than among the most agreeable strangers you could possibly assemble. Moreover, if he is unwell, he will not desire invitations. I am glad he has admitted it at last. I was convinced he was ill from the entire tenor of his letters, yet he refused to answer my inquiries, leaving me with no confidant to relay news."

"Oh! He is not severely ill, or he would not entertain thoughts of Spain."

"He never mentions Spain."

"No! But the plan he intends to propose clearly concerns it. However, would you truly travel in weather such as this?"

"Oh, it will cool daily. Yes! Consider it. I am only afraid I have indulged in thinking and wishing too much—in that absorbing, willful fashion that inevitably leads either to disappointment or to a gratification that feels hollow in spirit."

"That is superstitious, surely, Margaret."

"No, I do not believe it is. Only it ought to serve as a warning, checking me from yielding to such passionate desires. It is akin to the cry, 'Give me children, or else I die.' I fear my own cry is, 'Let me go to Cadiz, or else I die.'"

"My dear Margaret! You will be persuaded to stay, and then what shall I do? Oh! I wish I could find someone for you to marry here, someone whose presence I could guarantee!"

"I shall never marry."

"Nonsense, and double nonsense! Why, as Sholto remarks, you are such an attraction to the house that he knows countless men who would gladly visit next year solely for your sake."

Margaret drew herself up with haughty dignity. "Do you know, Edith, I sometimes think your life on Corfu has taught you..."

"Well!"

"Just a shade or two of coarseness."

Edith began to sob with such bitter vehemence, declaring that Margaret had lost all affection for her and ceased to regard her as a friend, that Margaret realized she had voiced an opinion too harsh for the relief of her own wounded pride. By the end of the day, she had become Edith's devoted slave, while the little lady, overwhelmed by hurt feelings, lay like a sacrificial victim upon the sofa, heaving profound sighs until sleep claimed her.

Mr. Bell failed to appear even on the day he had twice postponed his visit. The following morning, a letter from Wallis, his servant, arrived. His master had not been feeling well for some time—the true cause of the delayed journey—and at the precise moment he was scheduled to depart for London, he had suffered an apoplectic fit. Wallis added, citing the opinion of the physicians, that he could not survive the night; it was more than probable that by the time Miss Hale received this missive, her poor master would already be gone.

Margaret received the letter at breakfast. She turned pale as she read it, then silently handed it to Edith and withdrew from the room. Edith was profoundly shocked, crying in a sobbing, frightened, childlike manner that distressed her husband greatly. Mrs. Shaw was eating breakfast in her own chamber, and upon her husband fell the daunting task of reconciling his wife to the terrifying proximity of death, a concept seemingly foreign to her experience. Here was a man destined to dine with them today, now lying dead or dying. It took considerable time before she could summon the resolve to think of Margaret. Then she sprang up and followed her upstairs into her room.

Dixon was packing a few toiletry items, and Margaret was hastily donning her bonnet, weeping continuously, her hands trembling so violently she could barely tie the strings.

"Oh, dear Margaret! How shocking! What are you doing? Are you going out? Sholto would telegraph or do anything you wish."

"I am going to Oxford. A train departs in half an hour. Dixon has offered to accompany me, but I could have traveled alone. I must see him once more. Besides, he may be recovering and require care. He has been like a father to me. Do not stop me, Edith."

"But I must. Mother will not approve. Come and ask her, Margaret. You do not know where you are going. I would not mind if he had his own house; but in his Fellow's rooms! Come to Mother and ask her permission before you leave. It will not take a minute."

Margaret yielded, missing her train. In the suddenness of the crisis, Mrs. Shaw became bewildered and hysterical, causing the precious moments to slip away. However, another train departed in a couple of hours. After various debates concerning propriety and impropriety, it was decided that Captain Lennox should accompany Margaret, for the one constant in her nature was her resolution to proceed, alone or otherwise, by the next available train, regardless of what others might say about the correctness of the step.

Her father's friend, her own friend, lay at the point of death. The vividness of this thought struck her with such force that she was surprised by the firmness with which she asserted her right to independent action. Five minutes before departure, she found herself seated in a railway carriage opposite Captain Lennox. It was always a comfort to her to believe she had acted, even if only to confirm that he had passed away during the night. She visualized the rooms he had occupied, associating them forevermore with the fondest memories of her father and his single, cherished, faithful friend.
