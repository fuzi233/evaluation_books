A month slipped by, and when Major Durrance finally crossed back into London, the city felt heavy with the weight of unspoken things. At his club, waiting for him, lay a letter from Ethne. It was brief, stating only that she was residing with Mrs. Adair and wished for a visit, yet the page and its envelope bore a stark, mourning black border.

Seeking her out the following afternoon at Hill Street, Durrance found Ethne alone. Before he could speak, she laid bare the truth behind the somber ink.

"I wrote to Wadi Halfa," she explained quickly, "because I believed you would already be returning home before my message reached you. My father has passed away."

Durrance, having feared this moment since the postman's knock, offered a grim apology. "I am deeply sorry. You will feel his loss keenly."

"Far more than words can express," Ethne replied, her voice carrying a quiet, profound sorrow. "He succumbed yesterday morning. It was not a fever, nor a specific malady, but a slow dissolving away. A chilling process."

Her account was strange and intimate. Just prior to his passing, Dermod had briefly reclaimed a fragment of his former, commanding spirit. "We all knew he was ending," Ethne said, pausing to gather her composure. "Yet, at seven o'clock that afternoon, after speaking briefly to me, he summoned his dog. The animal leapt onto the bed immediately, though his master's voice had barely risen above a whisper. Crouching close, the beast pressed its muzzle beneath his father's hand with a soft whine. Then, with absolute finality, he instructed me to leave him and the dog entirely alone. 'Shut the door,' he commanded. 'The dog will tell you when to open it again.'"

Obeying strictly, Durrance stood outside the closed door until one o'clock. Then, a sharp, mournful howl echoed through the hallway.

"It was difficult to wait," Ethne confessed, her voice regaining its simple, unadorned rhythm. "To stand in the passage as the daylight faded and night descended, knowing the light within would soon be extinguished. I imagine him there, behind that thin wood, lying motionless in his great four-poster, his gaze fixed on the hills, as the shadows crept in. Just him, the dog, and the encroaching dark. It was his wish, though it was a trial for me."

Durrance remained silent, offering the comfort of his presence. He visualized those six interminable hours of twilight, imagining her standing guard with her ear pressed to the panel, her hand clamped over her racing heart. There was a cruelty in Dermod's desire to face the end in solitude, a stubbornness that cut deep.

It was Ethne who shattered the silence first. "Before telling me to leave, he spoke to someone," she said, locking eyes with Durrance. "Do you know who?"

There was no tremor in her gaze, no wobble in her tone, yet a sudden, jolting hope seized Durrance's breath. "Tell me!" he urged, leaning forward.

"Mr. Feversham," she answered.

Instantly, Durrance recoiled. It was not the name he expected. He cast his eyes downward, fixing them on the patterned carpet to hide the storm brewing within his countenance.

"My father loved him dearly," Ethne continued softly. "Pray, tell me if you know anything of his whereabouts or his current state."

Durrance said nothing. He gazed at the rug, reflecting on the iron grip Harry Feversham held over the hearts of those he had wronged. Even the man who had suffered most at Feversham's hands—Dermod, whose daughter now sat before him—spoke of the traitor with kindness on his deathbed. A bitter taste filled Durrance's mouth, a sting he feared would leak from his features. Yet, compelled by Ethne's persistence, he was forced to speak.

"You have never encountered him, I assume?" Ethne pressed.

Durrance rose and paced to the window, staring out into the street. His back was turned, masking his expression, but a surge of deep, smoldering anger colored his words despite his best efforts to keep them calm.

"No," he said, his voice thick with suppressed rage. "I have never met him." And then, the dam broke. "And I never wish to! He was my friend, I know that. But I cannot recall that friendship anymore. If he truly were the man we thought him, you would not have stood alone in that dark corridor for six hours, waiting."

Turning back to the center of the room, his demeanor shifted abruptly to business-like urgency. "Are you returning to Glenalla?"

"Yes."

"And living there alone?"

"Yes."

A heavy silence settled between them. Then, Durrance circled around to stand behind her chair.

"You once promised," he said, "that you might explain why our engagement ended."

"But you know," she replied. "Your words at the window revealed that you understand."

"No, I do not. Your father dropped hints, asking for news of Feversham on his last day. I know nothing concrete. I need you to tell me."

Ethne shook her head, leaning forward with her elbows resting on her knees. "Not now," she whispered. Silence returned to fill the space.

Breaking the quiet again, Durrance stepped closer. "I have only one year remaining at Halfa. It would be wise to depart Egypt then. I foresee little activity in the Sudan for some time. I do not intend to stay there, regardless of your decision regarding Glenalla."

Ethne did not feign ignorance. "We are not children," she stated firmly. "You have a lifetime ahead of you. Prudence dictates caution."

"Yes," Durrance snapped, his patience fraying. "But the *right* kind of prudence. The kind that dares enough."

Ethne did not stir. Her back was to him, blocking his view of her face. For a long moment, she sat utterly still. Finally, she spoke in a hushed, gentle tone. "Do you want me so desperately?"

Before he could answer, she spun quickly toward him. "Please, try not to. For this coming year, try not to. You have many things to occupy your mind. Try to forget me entirely."

There was a faint trace of regret in her voice—a sorrow for losing a valued companion—that stripped the sting from her command. It fed Durrance's delusion that, but for her fear of ruining his future, she would have spoken differently. Just as he prepared to respond, Mrs. Adair entered the room, shattering the spell. Durrance departed with the lingering illusion intact.

That evening, he dined at his club. Later, sitting beneath the great tree where he had spent so many vain nights a year prior, seeking news of Harry Feversham, the air was clear and starry. The summer languor hung in the air, and the carriage wheels rattled like the rolling sea below. On this night, too, a man entered the courtyard who knew Durrance. Unlike the previous visitor, Lieutenant Sutch, who had hesitated, this man approached without delay, sitting beside him on the bench.

"How do you do?" Durrance asked, extending his hand. "Captain Mather."

"I wondered if I'd find you here," Mather said, glancing at the evening paper Durrance had dropped. "I saw the date. Have you read it?"

Mather unfolded the paper, scanning for a specific item. "Remember that last reconnaissance from Suakin? We halted near Sinkat Fort at noon. An Arab was hiding in the trees behind the glacis."

"Yes."

"Do you recall the yarn he told you? About Gordon's letters and the wall in Berber?"

"No forgetting," Durrance admitted. "Here, then." Mather pointed to a short paragraph. It was a bare summary, devoid of detail, announcing that certain letters from the Mahdi summoning General Gordon to surrender Khartum—and copies of Gordon's curt refusals—had been recovered from a wall in Berber and delivered safely to Captain Willoughby at Suakin.

"The fellow must have returned to Berber eventually," Mather mused. "Abou Fatma, that was his name."

"They were hardly worth risking a life for," Mather noted dismissively.

"Perhaps not," Durrance replied, a flicker of doubt crossing his mind. "Still, it is comforting to know they are safe. Perhaps the copies are in Gordon's own hand. They possess historic value, at least."

"In a sense, yes," Mather conceded. "But even so, their recovery sheds no light on the siege's history. It makes no real difference to anyone, not even the historian."

"That is true," Durrance agreed, nodding. Nothing could be truer.

Yet, in that exact spot where he had previously searched for news of Feversham, news had now arrived—but it was a mystery he could not solve. He sat in the dark, unable to grasp how such a small fragment of information could touch the very springs of his life. He set the newspaper aside and drifted back into the memory of his afternoon with Ethne.
