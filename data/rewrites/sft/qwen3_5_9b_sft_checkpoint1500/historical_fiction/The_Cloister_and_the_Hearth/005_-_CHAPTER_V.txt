Ghysbrecht Van Swieten was a man of guile, his mind a labyrinth where truth often hid behind a veil of plausible falsehood. To the novice standing before him, he presented a trivial task, far removed from his true intent. "The town records," he remarked, feigning concern over the ancient script, "are penned in a crabbed hand, and the ink has long since turned to rust." With a theatrical flourish, he extended an honor to Gerard: the transcription of these crumbling documents into a fair copy.

Gerard, ever practical, asked immediately for his wage.

Van Swieten named a sum so meager it covered barely the cost of pen, ink, and parchment itself.

"But, Burgomaster," Gerard protested, his voice rising slightly, "what of my labor? This is a year's work."

"Your labor?" Van Swieten scoffed, leaning back with a smirk. "Do you call the marking of parchment 'labor'? A drop of sweat falls upon it, I tell thee."

"It is labor, and skilled labor at that!" Gerard insisted, his pride stung. "In every craft, the hand of skill is honored above brute toil, regardless of the sweat. Furthermore, consider my time."

"Thy time?" The old man's eyes narrowed, fixing them sharply on Gerard to gauge the tremor of his words. "Why, what is time to thee, a boy of twenty-two? Nay, I say thou hast grown idle. Thou art in love. Thy body remains within these walls with the chanting monks, but thy heart wanders with Peter Brandt and his red-headed paramour."

"I know no Peter Brandt," Gerard denied quickly.

That denial, however, only hardened Van Swieten's suspicion. The young exorcist was playing a dangerous game, one the burgomaster intended to unravel.

"Lie!" Van Swieten roared, slamming his hand down. "Did I not find thee at her side upon the road to Rotterdam?"

"Ach!" Gerard gasped.

"Ach! And yet I saw thee at Sevenbergen but the other day."

"Was I?"

"Aye, at Peter's house."

"At Sevenbergen?"

"Yes, at Sevenbergen."

Here lay the trap, a bold assertion dressed as fact, designed to lure the youth into revealing his recent whereabouts through his own reaction. The result was immediate and startling. Gerard sprang upright, seized by a strange, electric nervousness.

"Burgomaster!" he cried, his voice trembling with a mixture of fear and defiance. "I have not set foot in Sevenbergen these three years! I know not the name of those who saw me, nor where they dwell! But as my time is precious, and yours seemingly worthless, I take leave of thee." With eyes that sparkled like flint, he darted past the door and vanished into the street.

Van Swieten leaped to his feet, a wave of furious rage washing over him, but he sat down heavily once more, his face pale.

"He fears me not," the old man muttered, his voice low. "He knows something—if not everything."

Before the silence could settle, he barked orders at his trusted servant, nearly hauling the man to the open window.

"See you yon fledgling?" Van Swieten cried, his breath coming fast. "Haste! Follow him! But ensure he sees not thy presence. He is young, yet old in cunning. Keep him under constant watch. Tell me whither he goes and what he doth."

Night fell before the servant returned, his step heavy with news.

"Well? Well?" Van Swieten demanded, springing forward.

"Master," the servant whispered, bowing low. "The young man departed thence straightway for Sevenbergen."

Van Swieten let out a long, mournful groan.

"To the house of Peter the Magician."
