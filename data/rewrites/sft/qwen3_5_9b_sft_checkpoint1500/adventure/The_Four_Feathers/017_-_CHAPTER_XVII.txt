Mrs. Adair, seated opposite, required no explanations whatsoever. Beneath her pale, placid exterior lay a mind tortuous and cunning; she possessed a penchant for looking through keyholes rather than walking straight through doors. Knowledge gained through subtle maneuvering was infinitely more precious to her than facts easily elicited by direct inquiry. Indeed, she adhered to a perverted principle regarding questions, considering a day well-spent only if she had outwitted a companion into revealing some trivial news spontaneously, rather than securing it at breakfast through a straightforward request.

Consequently, though baffled by the tiny white feather on which Ethne placed such supreme value, and bewildered by the excellent tidings of Harry Feversham brought by Captain Willoughby, Mrs. Adair concealed every trace of her perplexity throughout lunch. Instead, she entertained her guest with conversations on indifferent topics. While capable of being delightful company when she chose, she selected this occasion for propriety's sake, yet her efforts proved futile.

"I do not believe you are hearing a single word I am saying!" she exclaimed finally.

Ethne laughed and admitted her guilt. As soon as lunch concluded, she retreated to her room, granting herself an afternoon of solitary reflection. Sitting by her window, she rehearsed the tale Willoughby had recounted that morning, and her heart swelled with it as if struck by divine music. A pang of regret—that he had not returned a year prior, when she was free—felt insignificant beside the sheer joy the story imparted. That great gladness had entirely eclipsed her former pride, the wound inflicted by Harry Feversham in the hall at Lennon House. Now, that pride was fully restored, and by the hand of the man who had once wounded her. She burned with a renewed vigor, acutely grateful to Harry Feversham for risking everything to heal her spirit. A new exhilaration filled the sunlight around her, quickening her pulse; her youth seemed resurrected on that August afternoon.

She opened a drawer in her dressing-case and retrieved the sole portrait among Harry Feversham's gifts that she had retained. She rejoiced in keeping it. Though the subject within the frame was dead to her in the sense that there was no disloyalty toward Lord Durrance in her heart, the figure represented a true friend. Gazing upon it, she felt immense contentment, realizing that Harry Feversham had required no declaration of faith to fuel him, nor encouragement from her to sustain him on the heights of his noble aspirations. Returning the portrait, she placed the white feather beside it and locked them both away together.

Back at the window, a gentle breeze caused the shadows of the towering trees to dance, softening the sunlight into a warm, red glow. Yet Ethne belonged to her own country, a truth Harry Feversham had known long ago. Her heart yearned for home. It was August; the first heather would soon blanket the hillsides of Donegal, and she wished the news of Harry's return had arrived amidst those blooms. The regret that it had not was like a crumpled rose-leaf in her hand. Stranded in a foreign land, she longed for the brown mountains of home, with their granite outcrops and the babble of mountain streams. Great sorrows and great joys alike pulled Ethne Eustace inward, for endurance was easier and happiness more complete in her homeland.

Yet, she had a living tether to Donegal at her side: Dermod's old collie dog, now her inseparable companion. To him she poured her confidences, and if her voice occasionally broke into tears, the faithful beast would remain silent. Through him, she understood truths Willoughby had omitted and Harry Feversham had never voiced. She visualized those three years of concealment in the cramped, bustling city of Suakin, with armies marching out to battle and returning dust-choked and bleeding, yet crowned with victory. Harry Feversham had been forced to slip away unseen, lest an old acquaintance—perhaps Durrance, Willoughby, or Trench—recognize him and pierce his disguise. She pictured his terror upon first beholding the dark, imposing walls of Berber, the harrowing night spent in ruined fields, and the desperate, stumbling search for water amidst the shifting sandhills of Obak. Vivid images formed in her mind, prompting the internal question: *Where was I then? What was I doing?*

She sat enveloped in a golden mist until the lights flickered upon the still waters of the creek, and the rooks wheeled noisily from the treetops, organizing themselves for the night and signaling the arrival of evening.

That following night, Ethne brought a buoyancy of spirit to the dinner table that astonished her companions. Mrs. Adair had to concede that Ethne's eyes had never shone so brightly, nor her cheeks been so freshly flushed. Convinced that Captain Willoughby had delivered stirring news, Mrs. Adair was simultaneously tormented by her futile attempts to deduce its nature. Nevertheless, despite her puzzlement, she engaged in the conversation, and the dinner proceeded with a freedom from embarrassment unknown since Lord Durrance's return to Guessens. He, too, cast aside his burdens of restraint; his spirits rose to match Ethne's. Laughter flowed freely, and the habitual look of tension—the gaze of a man straining to compensate for lost sight—vanished from his face entirely.

"You will play on your violin tonight, I think," he said with a smile as they rose from the table.

"Yes," she replied, "I will—with all my heart."

Durrance laughed and held open the door. The violin had remained sealed in its case for the past two months. To Durrance, that instrument served as a gauge and a test. If the world moved well for Ethne, the case would unlock, allowing the instrument to speak; if the world turned ill, it remained silent to avoid reopening old wounds before others' eyes. Ethne herself regarded the violin as an indiscreet friend, yet it was destined to be brought out that night.

Mrs. Adair lingered until Ethne was beyond earshot.

"You have noticed the change in her tonight?" she asked quietly.

"Yes. Have I not?" answered Durrance. "We have waited for it, hoped for it, and nearly despaired."

"Are you so glad of the change?"

Durrance threw back his head. "Do you wonder that I am glad? Kind, friendly, unselfish—these qualities she has always possessed. But there is something more than mere friendliness evident tonight, and for the first time, it is undeniable."

A look of pity softened Mrs. Adair's features, and she departed without further words. Durrance absorbed the entirety of Ethne's profound transformation. Meanwhile, Mrs. Adair raised the blinds of the drawing-room, opened the window to let the moonlight flood in, and stepped onto the terrace. Feeling unable to sit patiently in Ethne's company, she left the room just as Durrance entered. There, alone, Ethne sat by the window, already tuning the strings of her violin. Durrance took a chair behind her in the shadows.

"What shall I play for you?" she asked.

"The *Musolines* Overture," he answered. "You played it on the first evening you came to Ramelton. I recall it so clearly. Play it again tonight. I wish to compare."

"I have played it since."

"Never to me."

Alone in the moonlit room, windows wide open, Ethne suddenly crossed to the lamp and extinguished it. Resuming her seat, Durrance leaned forward in the darkness, hands resting on his knees, listening with an intensity he had not shown that evening. He applied what he believed to be a final test, decisive for the future of both their lives. Ethne's violin would surely reveal whether his suspicions were correct. Would friendship emerge from the music, or something deeper?

Ethne played the overture, and as the notes flowed, she forgot Durrance's presence in the room. Outside, the air was still, summer-warm, and fragrant; the moonlight spread across the creek like a solid floor of silver; the trees stood dreamlike under the stars. As the music floated loudly across the silent lawn, Ethne had a sudden vision that it might travel down the creek, over Salcombe Bar, and across the moonlit seas, striking small, clear, and wonderful fairy-like notes upon the ears of a man sleeping far away beneath the bright southern stars, with the cool desert night wind blowing upon his face.

*"If he could only hear!"* she thought. *"If he could only wake and know that what he heard was a message of friendship!"*

With this longing in her mind, she played with a skill never before employed, turning her violin into a voice of deep sympathy. The fantasy shifted and expanded as she played. The music became a bridge swinging in mid-air across the world, connecting her and Harry Feversham for just a few fleeting moments to shake hands. Of course, they would part immediately thereafter, each continuing their allotted paths, but these minutes would serve as aid along the separate journeys. The chords rang against the silence, seeming to proclaim the pride that had returned to her that day. Her fancy grew into a firm belief. It was no longer *"If he should hear,"* but *"He **must** hear!"* Carried away by this impulse, she abandoned caution, and a strange hope suddenly sprang up, enthralling her completely.
