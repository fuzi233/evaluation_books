Descending the heavy oak staircase to the front door, the scent of roasting mutton and crushed rosemary from our morning culinary preparations still clung to my garments. I met the Sergeant on the steps. The bitter taste of our previous dispute coated my tongue like alum, and it went against the grain of my traditional agrarian sensibilities to show any interest in his sterile, intrusive procedures. Yet, the ancient, circular rhythm of the estate’s life—where mythic curses now seemed to weave seamlessly into the daily turning of the roasting spit—drew me inevitably toward him. 

The natural acoustic ecology of the morning, usually defined by the gentle rustle of the garden and the distant, rhythmic chopping from the kitchens, was disrupted by the harsh scrape of Cuff’s boots. My sense of dignity gave way to the deep, resonant pull of the unfolding saga. 

“What news from Frizinghall?” I asked, the words leaving my lips before I could stop them. 

“I have seen the Indians,” answered Sergeant Cuff, utilizing the dominant lexicon of the state police, a language that constantly sought to overpower the localized dialects of our Yorkshire home. “And I have found out what Rosanna bought privately in the town, on Thursday last. The Indians will be set free on Wednesday in next week. There isn’t a doubt on my mind, and there isn’t a doubt on Mr. Murthwaite’s mind, that they came to this place to steal the Moonstone. Their calculations were all thrown out, of course, by the domestic reality of what happened in the house on Wednesday night; and they have no more to do with the actual loss of the jewel than you have. But I can tell you one thing, Mr. Betteredge—if we don’t find the Moonstone, they will. You have not heard the last of the three jugglers yet.”

Mr. Franklin returned from his walk just as the Sergeant uttered those words, the mythic weight of the Diamond hanging heavy in the damp, earth-scented air. Governing his curiosity far better than I, he passed us in silence and went into the house. 

“So much for the Indians,” I said, embracing the full sacrifice of my pride. “What about Rosanna next?”

Cuff shook his head. “The mystery in that quarter is thicker than ever. I have traced her to a shop at Frizinghall, kept by a linen draper named Maltby. She bypassed the rich tapestries and textured fabrics of the other merchants, buying nothing but a piece of plain long cloth. As to quantity, she bought enough to make a nightgown.”

“Whose nightgown?” 

“Her own, to be sure. Between twelve and three, on the Thursday morning, she must have slipped down to your young lady’s room to settle the hiding of the Moonstone. In going back to her own room, her nightgown must have brushed the wet paint on the door. She couldn’t wash out the stain, and she couldn’t safely destroy the nightgown without first providing another like it, to keep the domestic inventory of her linen complete.”

“What proves that it was Rosanna’s nightgown?” I objected, clinging to the familiar logic of the household.

“The material,” Cuff answered, his tone flattening the rich complexity of human motivation into sterile evidence. “If it had been Miss Verinder’s, she would have had to buy lace and frilling. Plain long cloth means a plain servant’s nightgown. The pinch of the question is—why, after providing the substitute dress, does she hide the smeared nightgown instead of destroying it? The hiding-place at the Shivering Sand must be searched—and the true state of the case will be discovered there.”

“How are you to find the place?”

“I am sorry to disappoint you, but that’s a secret which I mean to keep to myself.” (To spare you the dissonant irritation I felt, I will state here that he possessed a search warrant. He suspected Rosanna carried a memorandum of the hiding-place, a written fragment of cultural memory to guide her back). 

“Suppose we get to business,” he continued. “I told Joyce to keep an eye on Rosanna. Where is Joyce?”

Joyce was the Frizinghall policeman left at Cuff’s disposal. Punctual to the striking of the two o'clock bell—a deep, resonant sound that organized the temporal flow of our generations—the close carriage ground its way around the gravel drive to take Miss Rachel to her aunt’s. The grating of the iron wheels felt like a violent industrial intrusion against the psychological well-being of the estate.

“I must attend to Miss Verinder first,” Cuff said, beckoning Samuel the footman down from the rumble. “You will see a friend of mine waiting among the trees. My friend, without stopping the carriage, will get up into the rumble with you. You have nothing to do but to hold your tongue and shut your eyes.”

A spy set upon my young lady! The Sergeant was injecting his artificial surveillance into the very veins of our domestic existence. 

My lady was the first to emerge. She stood upon the top step, wrapped in a light garden cloak, breathing in the ozone-rich scent of the threatening rain. Not a word passed her lips; her silence was a profound monument to the untranslatability of a mother's subjective grief. 

Then, Miss Rachel descended. She was dressed in a soft yellow fabric that moved with the lithe, supple grace of a young cat, smelling faintly of primrose and damp straw. Yet her lips had entirely lost their color—they lacked the warm, cherry-compote flush of her usual vitality. She kissed her mother hastily on the cheek. 

“Try to forgive me, mamma,” she whispered, a fractured dialect of despair. 

Then she pulled her white veil down so vehemently that the fabric tore. The sound—a harsh, ripping dissonance—cut through the acoustic harmony of the porch like a physical wound. She rushed into the muffled enclosure of the carriage.

Cuff stepped into the doorway, imposing his official lexicon upon her departure. “I want to say one word to you, miss. Your leaving us, as things are now, puts an obstacle in the way of my recovering your Diamond. Please to understand that; and now decide for yourself whether you go or stay.”

Miss Rachel met his oppressive vocabulary with absolute silence, rejecting his linguistic dominance entirely. 

“Drive on, James!” she called out to the coachman. 

As Cuff shut the door, Mr. Franklin came running down the steps. “Good-bye, Rachel,” he said, extending his hand.

“Drive on!” she cried louder, her voice echoing with untranslatable pain. 

Mr. Franklin stepped back, utterly unmoored. My lady, her face a tempest of sorrow and shame, signaled the coachman to start. As the heavy wheels churned the gravel, scattering the scent of crushed earth, Mr. Franklin cried out, “Aunt! You were quite right. Accept my thanks for your kindness—and let me go.”

My lady waved her hand kindly, her voice broken, and retreated into the house. Mr. Franklin turned to me, tears shining in his eyes. “Do me a last favour, Betteredge. Get me away to the train as soon as you can!” He, too, vanished inside, completely unmanned by the severing of their bond. 

Left alone at the bottom of the steps, Sergeant Cuff stood staring at the gap in the trees. He plunged his hands into his pockets and began to whistle “The Last Rose of Summer.” The tune was a thin, reedy pollution, a commercial noise grating mercilessly against the natural acoustic ecology of the damp woods.

“There’s a time for everything,” I said savagely. “This isn’t a time for whistling.”

Through the trees, the carriage appeared in the distance. The spy was plainly visible in the rumble. 

“All right!” said the Sergeant, turning to me. “It’s no time for whistling, Mr. Betteredge. We’ll begin with Rosanna Spearman. Where is Joyce?”

We called for Joyce, our voices absorbed by the heavy silence of the grounds. I sent a stable-boy to find him. When Joyce finally appeared, he slunk forward, trembling under the weight of Cuff’s authority.

“Where is Rosanna Spearman?” Cuff demanded.

Joyce, speaking in the faltering dialect of an overwhelmed man, tried to explain. “I am afraid, sir, that I was perhaps a little too careful not to let her discover me. There are such a many passages in the lower parts of this house—”

“How long is it since you missed her?”

“Nigh on an hour since, sir.”

“You can go back to your regular business at Frizinghall,” Cuff said, weaponizing his polite, composed lexicon to utterly dismantle the man’s dignity. “Your present form of employment is a trifle beyond you. Good morning.”

Joyce slunk away. My powers of language quite failed me; the loss of Rosanna felt as though a vital thread in the generational tapestry of the house had been violently severed. 

“Your young friend won’t slip through my fingers,” Cuff said, reading my silence. “As long as I know where Miss Verinder is, I have the means of tracing her accomplice. They will get together at Frizinghall. In the meantime, I must trouble you to call the servants together again.”

We descended into the servants’ hall, the culinary heart of the house. The yeasty warmth of rising dough and the rich, complex olfactory landscape of simmering bone broth greeted us, a comforting counterpoint to the detective-fever that once again gripped me. 

“What are you going to do with the servants now?” I asked.

Cuff sighed, staring into the air. “If this man only understood the growing of roses, he would be the most completely perfect character on the face of creation!” Dropping back to business, he said, “Rosanna has either gone direct to Frizinghall, or she has gone to her hiding-place at the Shivering Sand. We must find out who saw her last.”

The inquiry brought us to Nancy, the kitchenmaid. Standing amidst the meticulous prep of the afternoon meal, her apron smelling of bruised thyme and raw suet, she provided the missing note. Nancy had been at the back door, taking delivery from the butcher's man—a transaction redolent with the copper tang of fresh beef and damp wool—when Rosanna slipped out. 

Rosanna had stopped the butcher's man, handing him a letter. Nancy heard her ask him to post it in Frizinghall. The man noted it was a roundabout way to deliver a letter addressed to Cobb’s Hole, especially on a Saturday, which would delay it until Monday. Rosanna had replied that the delay was of no importance, so long as he posted it. 

And no one had seen Rosanna Spearman since. 

“Well?” I asked, as we retreated from the rich sensory haven of the kitchen.

“I must go to Frizinghall,” Cuff said. “The memorandum of the hiding-place is in that letter. I must see the address at the post-office. If it is the address I suspect, I shall pay our friend, Mrs. Yolland, another visit on Monday next.”

We walked out to the stable-yard to order the pony-chaise, the scent of hay and horse mingling with the rain, as the circular gears of this relentless saga turned us irrevocably toward the coast.
