The morning sun broke over Fieldhead with a clarity that seemed to sharpen the edges of Shirley's delight. She turned to Caroline, her eyes alight with the satisfaction of a hostess who had successfully orchestrated a gathering of gentlemen. "I truly relish entertaining a circle of men," she declared, her voice carrying a note of performative amusement. "There is a peculiar joy in observing how they derive pleasure from a meal judiciously concocted. For us, these choice vintages and scientifically prepared dishes hold little significance; yet gentlemen seem to retain a childlike naïveté regarding sustenance. One delights in pleasing them—provided, of course, they display the becoming, decent self-governance characteristic of our admirable rectors."

Her gaze drifted toward Moore, lingering with analytical intent. "I watch Moore sometimes, attempting to discern his accessible point, Caroline. You have seen more of him than I. Have you ever discovered the key to his affections?"

Caroline smiled, a shy pleasure softening her features as she followed Miss Keeldar's lead into the examination of her cousin's character—a topic she would never have broached alone, yet found irresistible when prompted. "It is not, at any rate, the case with my uncle or Dr. Boultby," she replied. "But I confess, I cannot say what it is. Whenever I attempt to scrutinize Robert, my observation is invariably baffled by the discovery that he is watching me."

"There it is!" Shirley exclaimed, her tone shifting abruptly to one of revelation. "You cannot fix your eyes upon him without his flashing back immediately. He is perpetually off his guard, refusing to grant you even a sliver of advantage. Even when he does not look at you, his thoughts appear busy among yours, tracing your words and actions to their source, contemplating your motives with casual ease. Oh! I know that sort of character, or something akin to it. It piques me singularly. How does it affect you?"

This question exemplified one of Shirley's sharpest, most sudden turns. Initially, Caroline had fluttered at such directness, but she had grown adept at parrying these home-thrusts with the steady grace of a young Quakeress. "Pique you? In what manner does it pique you?" she asked, her composure intact.

"Here he comes!" Shirley suddenly interrupted, launching herself toward the window. "Here comes a diversion. I never told you of a superb conquest I have recently made—one achieved at those parties to which I can never persuade you to accompany me—and accomplished entirely without effort or intention on my part, I swear. There is the bell! And, by all that is delicious, there are two of them. Do they never hunt except in pairs? You may have one, Lina, and you may take your choice. I hope I am generous enough. Listen to Tartar!"

The black-muzzled, tawny hound, previously glimpsed in the chapter introducing his mistress, erupted in the hall. Amidst the hollow resonance of the space, his deep bark sounded formidably, followed by a growl more terrible than the bark itself, menacing as muttered thunder.

"Listen!" cried Shirley, laughing. "You would think this the prelude to a bloody onslaught. They will surely be frightened. They do not know old Tartar as I do. They are unaware that his uproars are merely sound and fury, signifying nothing!"

Bustle erupted below. "Down, sir, down!" came a high-toned, imperious voice, accompanied by the sharp crack of a cane. Immediately followed a yell, a scuttle, a run—a positive tumult.

"Oh, Malone, Malone!"

"Down! Down! Down!" the high voice commanded.

"He really is worrying them!" Shirley observed, her amusement widening. "They have struck him. A blow is what he is not accustomed to, and he will not tolerate."

She dashed out. A gentleman fled up the oak staircase, seeking refuge in the gallery or upper chambers with hot haste; another backed rapidly toward the stairfoot, wildly flourishing a knotty stick while reiterating, "Down! Down! Down!" Meanwhile, the tawny dog bayed, bellowed, and howled, and a group of servants bundled from the kitchen. The dog made a spring; the second gentleman turned tail and rushed after his comrade. The first was already safe in a bedroom, holding the door shut against his fellow—nothing so merciless as terror. Yet the other fugitive struggled hard; the door was about to yield to his strength.

"Gentlemen," Miss Keeldar's silvery but vibrating tones cut through the chaos. "Spare my locks, if you please. Calm yourselves! Come down! Look at Tartar; he won't harm a cat."

She caressed the beast. He lay crouched at her feet, forepaws stretched out, tail still twitching in threatening agitation, nostrils snorting, his bulldog eyes conscious of a dull, smoldering fire. He was an honest, phlegmatic, stupid, yet stubborn canine character. He loved his mistress and John—the man who fed him—but was largely indifferent to the rest of the world. Quiet enough unless struck or threatened with a stick, in which case a demon seized him instantly.

"Mr. Malone, how do you do?" Shirley continued, lifting her mirth-lit face to the gallery. "That is not the way to the oak parlour; that is Mrs. Pryor's apartment. Request your friend Mr. Donne to evacuate. I shall have the greatest pleasure in receiving him in a lower room."

"Ha! Ha!" cried Malone, in hollow laughter, quiting the door and leaning over the massive balustrade. "Really that animal alarmed Donne. He is a little timid," he proceeded, stiffening himself and walking trimly to the stairhead. "I thought it better to follow, in order to reassure him."

"It appears you did. Well, come down, if you please.—John" (turning to her manservant), "go upstairs and liberate Mr. Donne.—Take care, Mr. Malone; the stairs are slippery."

In truth, they were, being of polished oak. The caution arrived a moment too late for Malone. He had already slipped during his stately descent, saved only by a clutch at the banisters that caused the entire structure to creak anew.

Tartar seemed to deem the visitor's descent effected with unwarranted *éclat*, and accordingly he growled once more. Malone, however, was no coward. The spring of the dog had taken him by surprise, but he passed him now in suppressed fury rather than fear. If a look could have strangled Tartar, he would have breathed no more. Forgetting politeness in his sullen rage, Malone pushed into the parlour before Miss Keeldar. He glanced at Miss Helstone; he could scarcely bring himself to bow to her. He glared at both ladies. He looked as if, had either of them been his wife, he would have made a glorious husband at that precise moment. In each hand, he seemed poised to clutch one and gripe her to death.

However, Shirley took pity. She ceased to laugh; Caroline, too true a lady to smile even at anyone under mortification, maintained her dignity. Tartar was dismissed; Peter Augustus was soothed—for Shirley possessed looks and tones that might calm a very bull. He had the sense to realize that, since he could not challenge the owner of the dog, civility was the wiser path. And civil he tried to be; his attempts being well received, he grew presently *very* civil and quite himself again. He had come, indeed, for the express purpose of making himself charming and fascinating. Rough portents had met him on his first admission to Fieldhead, but that passage was quickly overcome. Charming and fascinating he resolved to be. Like March, having come in like a lion, he purposed to go out like a lamb.

For the sake of air, or perhaps for a ready exit in case of some new emergency, he took his seat—not on the sofa, where Miss Keeldar offered him enthronement, nor near the fireside, to which Caroline, by a friendly sign, gently invited him, but on a chair close to the door. No longer sullen or furious, he grew, after his fashion, constrained and embarrassed. He talked to the ladies by fits and starts, choosing for topics whatever was most intensely commonplace. He sighed deeply, significantly, at the close of every sentence; he sighed in each pause; he sighed ere he opened his mouth. Finally, finding it desirable to add ease to his other charms, he drew forth to aid him an ample silk pocket-handkerchief. This was to be the graceful toy with which his unoccupied hands were to trifle. He went to work with a certain energy. He folded the red-and-yellow square cornerwise; he whipped it open with a waft; again he folded it in narrower compass; he made of it a handsome band. To what purpose would he proceed to apply the ligature? Would he wrap it about his throat—his head? Should it be a comforter or a turban? Neither. Peter Augustus possessed an inventive, an original genius. He was about to show the ladies graces of action possessing at least the charm of novelty. He sat on the chair with his athletic Irish legs crossed, and these legs, in that attitude, he circled with the bandana and bound firmly together.
