Once General Braddock and his distinguished guests had been settled into their respective lodgings, the two young Warringtons withdrew to their chamber. There, they poured out their heated opinions regarding the day's monumental event. They could not abide such a match—nay, it was unthinkable! Could the representative of the illustrious Marquis d'Esmond wed the younger son of a colonial family, a youth raised merely as a land surveyor? To consign Castlewood, and these very boys at nineteen, to the tender mercies of a stepfather barely twenty-three! "Oh, it is monstrous!" cried Harry. His impulse was immediate: he would rush to his mother's boudoir, where her black maids were stripping her of the simple jewels and fineries worn in honor of the feast. He intended to protest this odious union, declaring that they would depart, live upon their modest estate, and abandon her forever if this unnatural alliance proceeded.

George, however, advocated a different strategy, outlining his plan to his awestruck brother. "Our mother," he declared, "cannot marry a man with whom one or both of us have fought in the field—a foe who has wounded us or killed us, or whom we have wounded or killed. We must have him out, Harry."

Harry perceived the profound truth in George's assertion and marveled at his brother's sagacity. "No, George," he agreed, "you are right. Mother cannot wed our murderer; she will not stoop so low. And if we challenge him, he is finished. *Cadit quaestio*, as Mr. Dempster used to say. Shall I send my page with a challenge to Colonel Washington immediately?"

"My dear Harry," the elder replied, reflecting with some pride on his own affair of honor at Quebec, "you are not yet accustomed to matters of this gravity."

"No," admitted Harry, sighing with envy and admiration for his senior.

"We cannot insult a gentleman within our own walls," George continued, speaking with majestic authority. "The laws of honor forbid such inhospitable treatment. However, Sir, we may ride out with him, and the instant the park gates close, we shall make our intentions clear."

"That we can, by George!" cried Harry, seizing his brother's hand. "And that we will, too. Say, Georgy..." Suddenly, the lad's face turned crimson, and his brother asked what he wished to propose.

"This is my turn, brother," Harry pleaded. "If you undertake the campaign, I must have the other affair. Indeed, indeed, I ought." He prayed fervently for this promotion.

"Again, the head of the house must take the lead, my dear," George said with a superior air. "If I fall, Harry will avenge me. But I must fight George Washington, Hal—and 'tis best I do so, for indeed, I hate him the worst. Was it not he who counseled my mother to order that wretch, Ward, to seize me?"

"Oh, George," interjected the more pacific younger brother, "you ought to forget and forgive."

"Forget? Never, sir, so long as I remember. One cannot order remembrance out of a man's mind; and a wrong that exists today must exist tomorrow. I have never, to my knowledge, done harm to any man, and I never will suffer it, if I can help it. I think poorly of Mr. Ward, but not so poorly as to believe he will ever forgive that blow with the ruler. Colonel Washington is our enemy, mine especially. He has advised one injustice against me, and plots a greater. I tell you, brother, we must punish him."

The grandfathers' old Bordeaux had set George's ordinarily pale countenance ablaze. Harry, his brother's devoted worshipper, could not fail to admire George's haughty demeanor and rapid oratory. Prepared to follow his leader with his usual docility, the boys retired to their beds. Before sleep, the elder conveyed special injunctions to his junior: to remain civil to all the guests as long as they remained under the maternal roof on the morrow.

Good manners and a reluctance to spread gossip prevented us from naming which of Madame Esmond's guests first succumbed to the weight of her hospitality. The respectable descendants of Messrs. Talmadge and Danvers, aides-de-camp to his Excellency, might not care to hear how their ancestors were intoxicated a century prior; yet the gentlemen themselves took no shame in the fact, and there is little doubt they or their comrades were tipsy twice or thrice a week. Let us imagine them reeling to bed, supported by sympathetic negroes; and their vinous General, too stout a toper to surrender himself to mere six bottles of Bordeaux, conducted to his chamber by the young gentlemen of the house, swiftly falling into the slumber that friendly Bacchus grants. The good lady of Castlewood observed the state of her guests without surprise or horror. She rose early the next morning, providing cooling drinks for their parched throats, which the servants delivered to their respective rooms. At breakfast, one of the English officers rallied Mr. Franklin, who consumed no alcohol whatsoever, thereby refusing the morning draught of toddy. The officer showed how the Philadelphia gentleman lost two pleasures—the drink and the toddy itself. The young fellow retorted that the disease was pleasant and the remedy delicious, laughing as he proposed to repeat both. The General's new American aide-de-camp, Colonel Washington, sat quite sober and serene. The British officers vowed they must take him in hand and teach him the ways of the English army, but the Virginian gentleman gravely replied that he had no desire to learn that particular branch of English military education.

The widow, preoccupied with the cares of a great dinner followed by a magnificent breakfast, had scarce leisure to remark closely upon the behavior of her sons, though she noted that George was scrupulously polite to her favorite, Colonel Washington, as to all other guests.

Before Mr. Braddock departed, he held a private audience with Madame Esmond, wherein His Excellency formally offered to take her son into his family. When arrangements for George's departure were settled between his mother and future chief, Madame Esmond, though she felt the tremors, showed no squeamish terror regarding the dangers of the bottle, recognizing them as severe trials her son would inevitably face. She knew her boy must take his part in the world. "Mr. Braddock is a perfect fine gentleman in the morning," she said stoutly to her aide-de-camp, Mrs. Mountain. "And though my father did not drink, 'tis certain that many of the best company in England do." The jolly General, good-natured, shook hands with George, who presented himself to His Excellency after the maternal interview. Braddock welcomed him and bade him attend at Frederick in three days, shortly after which the expedition would set forth.

Now the great coach was again called into requisition. The General's escort pranced around it; the other guests and their servants mounted horse. The lady of Castlewood attended His Excellency to the steps of the verandah; the young gentlemen followed, standing flanking his coach door. The guard-trumpeter blew a shrill blast; the negroes shouted "Huzzay, and God save de King" as Mr. Braddock graciously took leave of his hospitable entertainers and rolled away on his road to headquarters.

As the boys ascended the steps, there the Colonel was once more taking leave of their mother. No doubt she had been once more recommending George to his namesake's care. Colonel Washington said, "With my life. You may depend on me," as the lads returned to their mother and the few guests remaining in the porch. The Colonel was booted and ready to depart. "Farewell, my dear Harry," he said. "With you, George, 'tis no adieu. We shall meet in three days at the camp."

Both young men were heading toward danger, perhaps toward death. Colonel Washington was departing, and she would see him no more before the campaign. No wonder the widow was deeply moved.

George Warrington watched his mother's emotion and interpreted it with a pang of malignant scorn. "Stay yet a moment, and console our mamma," he said with a steady countenance, "only the time to get ourselves booted, and my brother and I will ride with you a little way, George." George Warrington had already ordered his horses. The three young men were speedily underway, their negro grooms following behind. Mrs. Mountain, knowing she had wrought mischief between them and trembling for the result, felt a vast relief that Mr. Washington had departed without a quarrel with the brothers, without, at any rate, an open declaration of love to their mother.

No man could be more courteous in demeanor than George Warrington to his neighbor and namesake, the Colonel. The latter was pleased and surprised at his young friend's altered behavior. The community of danger, the necessity of future fellowship, the softening influence of the long friendship binding him to the Esmond family, and the tender adieux that had just passed between him and the mistress of Castlewood inclined the Colonel to forget the unpleasantness of recent days, making him more than usually friendly with his young companion. George was quite gay and easy; it was Harry who was now melancholy. He rode silently and wistfully by his brother, keeping away from Colonel Washington, to whose side he had always pressed eagerly before. If the honest Colonel remarked his young friend's conduct, he undoubtedly attributed it to Harry's known affection for his brother and his natural anxiety to be near George now that the day of parting drew near.
