The world, in the time of Harry Warrington, was far from idle in its debates regarding the literary supremacy of antiquity versus modernity. The learned, along with the general public, largely sided with the ancients, viewing them through a lens of timeless reverence. Yet, standing in the present moment, looking backward, one must admit that the "ancients" of yore are but the "moderns" of today. Just as we, living now, will eventually become the subjects of future judgment, so too did the scholars of the mid-eighteenth century regard their contemporaries with a mixture of awe and skepticism.

If one were to judge the intellectual prowess of the age, the argument would inevitably tilt toward the present. While the ancients possessed the formidable weight of Edward Gibbon, the universities of Cambridge and Glasgow produced professors of Greek who knew far more than the entire educational system of Europe—Athens included—had managed to cultivate centuries prior. Similarly, in the realm of science, humanity had barely progressed beyond the level of the "heathen" predecessors who had supposedly stunted the ancients' literary growth. But what of morality? Was the ethical climate of 1858 superior to that of a century past?

Consider the assembly of the House of Commons, as depicted by Mr. Disraeli. Had every member of Parliament a fixed price, as in the eras of Walpole or Newcastle? Or, perhaps more delicately, had almost every one of them sold themselves? As for the fairer sex, one need not label them a collective order of Vestals. However, the chronicles of a hundred years ago teemed with scandals so voluminous that one might be grateful not to inhabit such perilous times. On my conscience, I maintain that men and women of the current era are genuinely better. Not only are the "Susannas"—those paragons of virtue—more numerous, but the "Elders" are significantly less wicked.

One need only look to the literary landscape to understand this shift. Have you read *Clarissa*, *Tom Jones*, or *Roderick Random*? Contemplate the paintings of contemporary artists depicting the men and women of their day. Imagine describing the exploits of a figure like Mr. Lovelace, or Lady Bellaston, or that astonishing "Lady of Quality" who donated her memoirs to the author of *Peregrine Pickle*. How would the pure, outraged sensibilities of the nineteenth century react? They would blush, scream, flee the room, summon the young ladies, and issue a stern command to Mr. Mudie to cease sending such "odious" volumes.

"You are fifty-eight years old, madam," the narrator addresses his audience gently. "Perhaps you are too squeamish, crying out before injury occurs, when no offense was intended. It is possible the novelist's art suffers under the restraints imposed upon him, much like the honest statues of St. Peter's and the Vatican, ruined by the tin draperies in which pious nuns swaddle the fair limbs of marble. Yet, in your prudery lies a certain reason, as does the state censorship of the press. A page may contain matter dangerous to *bonos mores*; thus, out with the scissors, censor, and clip the prurient paragraph! We have no choice but to submit. Society, the despot, has issued his imperial decree. We may whisper that the statue looked better unveiled, that the moral tale would be stronger if complete. But away with him—a single word is forbidden!"

And yet, consider the pianofortes of the United States, clad in frilled muslin trousers. One depends upon it, that muslin covered not only the mahogany legs but also the notes within, muffling the music and halting the player. To what, then, does this prelude lead? It introduces us to Harry Warrington, Esquire, residing in Bond Street, London, and the life he, alongside many other young bucks of fashion, led during those times.

I confess I cannot escort my fair and young reader into these circles any more than Lady Squeams could guide her daughter to Cremorne Gardens on an ordinary evening. My dear Miss Diana (pshaw! I know you are eight-and-thirty, despite your shyness and desire to pretend you recently left school dinners behind), when your grandfather was a young man about town, a member of White's Club, dining at Pontac's off the feasts provided by Braund and Lebeck, and riding to Newmarket with March and Rockingham, he lived a life of which your noble aunt—the author of *Legends of the Squeams's; or, Fair Fruits of a Family Tree*—has given you the faintest notion.

This was before your grandmother adopted those serious views that defined her later residence at Bath, and before Colonel Tibbalt wed Miss Lye, the wealthy heiress of the soap-boiler. During those days, her ladyship's "wild oats" were sown. In youth, she was as giddy as the rest of the genteel world. At her Hill Street mansion, ten card tables stood ready on Wednesdays and Sundays, barring a brief interlude when Ranelagh opened on Sundays. Every night of her life she gambled for eight, nine, or ten hours. Everyone else in society did likewise. She lost, she won, she cheated, she pawned her jewels—who knows what else she was unwilling to pledge to fund her fury for play?

Recall that infamous after-supper duel at the Shakespeare's Head in Covent Garden, fought between your grandfather and Colonel Tibbalt. They drew swords and engaged solely in the presence of Sir John Screwby, who sat drunk beneath the table. Their combat was interrupted by Mr. John Fielding's people, and your grandfather was carried home to Hill Street wounded in a chair. Let me assure you, those gentlemen in powder and ruffles, who turned the toes of their buckled pumps with such delicate precision, were terrible fellows. Swords were drawn perpetually; bottle after bottle was drained; oaths roared unceasingly from conversations; tavern-drainers and watchmen were pinked and maimed; chairmen were belabored; citizens insulted by reeling pleasure-seekers.

Have you visited Cremorne with proper "vouchers"? Do you recall our great theaters thirty years ago? You were deemed too refined to attend a play. Yet, you have no conception of what the playhouses were, nor what the green boxes held, when Garrick and Mrs. Pritchard performed before them! For the sake of my children, I thank that worthy actor in his retirement who was the first to banish that shame from the stage. No, madam, you are mistaken. I do not boast of superior virtue. I do not claim you are naturally better than your ancestress in her wild, rouged, gambling, flaring, tearing days—not even than poor Polly Fogle, who is currently taken up for shoplifting and would have been hanged a hundred years ago. Only, I am heartily thankful that my temptations are fewer, having quite enough to contend with those of the present century.

Thus, if Harry Warrington rides down to Newmarket for the October meeting and loses or wins his fortune there; if he joins a party at the Shakespeare or Bedford Head; if he dines at White's Ordinary and sits down to macco and lansquenet afterward; if he boxes the watch and appears at the Roundhouse; if he briefly transforms into a wild, dissipated, harum-scarum young Harry Warrington—I, knowing the frailty of human nature, shall not be surprised. Nor, fully aware of my own shortcomings, do I intend to be savage toward my neighbor.

Mr. Sampson, however, was different. In his chapel in Long Acre, he whipped Vice tremendously; he gave Sin no quarter; he out-cursed Blasphemy with superior Anathemas; he knocked Drunkenness down and trampled on the prostrate brute wallowing in the gutter; he dragged out conjugal Infidelity and pounded her with endless stones of rhetoric—and yet, after service, he would come to dinner at the Star and Garter, make a bowl of punch for Harry and his friends at the Bedford Head, join a hand at whist at Mr. Warrington's lodgings or Lord March's, or wherever there was a supper and good company for him.

I often reflect, concerning Mr. Warrington's doings at this period of his arrival in London, that I may have taken my usual degrading and uncharitable view of him—for, you see, I have not uttered a single word of virtuous indignation against his conduct, and if it was not reprehensible, I have certainly judged him most cruelly. Oh, Truthful, Oh Beautiful, Oh Modesty, Oh Benevolence, Oh Pudor, Oh Mores, Oh Blushing Shame, Oh Namby Pamby—each with your respective capital letters to your honored names! Oh Niminy, Oh Piminy! How shall I dare to say that a young man ever was a young man?

No doubt, dear young lady, I am calumniating Mr. Warrington according to my heartless custom. Here is proof, in a letter from the Warrington collection, addressed by Harry to his mother.
