The morning broke with a clarity that seemed to demand scrutiny. Romola set out once more for San Marco, having secured through Fra Salvestro the previous evening a rare audience with Fra Girolamo in the chapter-house. Such meetings were exceptional; Savonarola guarded his solitude with such rigorous discipline that he allowed no pretext for slander to take root. Consequently, when permission was granted, it came stripped of all mystery, scheduled during hours when the outer cloisters bustled with ordinary visitors. Romola chose this public path intentionally, seeking to gauge the pulse of the city.

Every loggia, every shadowed corner of the piazza, and every shop serving as a gossip's rendezvous buzzed with the feverish energy of unearned debate. Commerce lagged, fueling the vigor of political discourse. It was evident that factions supporting and opposing the execution of the conspirators were racing to exploit the three-day interim, desperate to define the popular mood. Handbills fluttered through the air like black birds: some boldly proclaimed the choice between justice for the conspirators or ruin for the Republic; others, in equally stark letters, urged adherence to the law and the granting of an appeal. Surrounding these islands of bold capital were vast lakes of smaller script detailing arguments deemed unnecessary to read aloud. Yet, at that moment, in the fresh enthusiasm for printing, many believed that no persuasion was more potent than a phrase in giant type. Romola, however, craved the nuance found in the smaller print. Though pressed for time, she scanned the streets anxiously, hoping to snatch a copy before the *sbirri* tore them from the walls or before eager readers consumed them.

For a long stretch, she found only bills clutched by avid buyers or pinned high above reach. Finally, slipping behind San Giovanni with a hurried stride to evade the crowd, she spotted Bratti. He was exchanging pamphlets for small coins, a familiar sight to Romola, who knew the humble rhythms of Florentine life.

"Bratti," she called out, "do you have both kinds of handbills? Give them to me quickly."

"Two sorts," Bratti replied, slowly separating damp sheets, a slowness that tested Romola's patience. "'Law,' and 'Justice.'"

"Which sells faster?"

"'Justice.' It flies off the shelves, so I raised the price to two danari. But then I realized 'Law' is just as valuable merchandise. People don't value cheap things; selling it for one danaro would be a wrong. I'm a fair trader. Whether 'Law' or 'Justice,' they are both good wares. I got them for nothing and sell them for a fair profit. Need more of either?"

"No, no," Romola interrupted, pressing a white quattrino into his hand. "Here for both." She folded the papers and fled toward the convent.

She arrived swiftly in the outer cloisters of San Marco. Fra Salvestro awaited her beneath the arcade, oblivious to her approach as he chatted with lay visitors. Thanks to a government favorable to the friars, the timid hesitation that had gripped San Marco following the initial shock of excommunication was fading. Among the listeners was Andrea Cambini, a known associate of Francesco Valori. He gestured emphatically, expounding some point while Fra Salvestro listened with an air of trivial curiosity—a demeanor Romola found repulsive, signaling a listener who cared deeply for news but little for its substance.

From disjointed fragments reaching her ears, Romola deduced that Cambini was discussing the fate of the conspirators. She avoided approaching the group. As soon as she captured Fra Salvestro's attention, she turned toward the chapter-house door. He nodded approvingly and vanished into the inner cloister.

A lay brother opened the heavy doors for her, closing them softly behind her. Inside, the wall frescoes seemed to mourn anew with her for the death of her brother Dino. The memory surged, not as a clear recollection, but as a blend of impressions merging with her current terror, as if her present anxiety were a resurrection of the fierce longing she had once brought to this spot—only to be met by cold marble indifference. She suppressed the thought immediately, unfolding the handbills, hoping to read them before Fra Girolamo's arrival. By the time she finished the tract advocating for the law, the door swung open.

Doubling the papers, she stood expectant. When the Friar entered, she knelt, adhering to custom. But upon receiving his blessing, she rose and stood opposite him at a respectful distance. Time had altered him; his seclusion since excommunication had etched deeper lines of mental strain and physical austerity onto his face. Yet, Romola sensed a shift more profound than mere aging. Was the serene elevation and pure humanity that once moved her absent? Or was it the growing rift regarding her godfather that clouded her vision? Perhaps both. Human relationships often fracture under the weight of conflicting loyalties, where the final blow rarely strikes until affection has already been eroded by repeated compromises.

Savonarola's gaze held a hardness born of self-absorption. He intuitively knew her purpose: the fate of the conspirators. A subject he had already wrestled with internally. Seated alone, correcting proofs for his *Triumph of the Cross*, he could rest in a posture of neutral resolve.

"It is a matter of grave importance, doubtless, that brings you to me, my daughter," he began, his tone gentle, though controlled rather than inclined. "I know you do not usually dwell on trifles."

"Father, you know the cause before I speak," Romola replied, pouring out her plea instantly. "You know what I cherish—it is the life of the old man I love most in the world. The thought of him has accompanied the thought of my father since I can remember daylight. That is my warrant for coming, even if my visit seems superfluous. Perhaps you have already decided that your influence over men's hearts should be used to deny Florentines a right you yourselves helped secure."

"I do not interfere with state functions, my daughter," Fra Girolamo stated firmly, disinclined to reignite a debate he had settled within. "I have preached and labored for a good government, essential to the perfection of Christian life; but I keep my hands from specific affairs entrusted to experienced citizens."

"Sire—" Romola started, then stopped. The word slipped out impulsively, only to be choked back by the realization that she was remonstrating with her guide and strength. In rebelling, she bruised her own reverence. Savonarola was too perceptive not to sense her internal conflict, yet too noble to adopt the calm, self-justifying evasion he often employed publicly.

"Speak your heart, my daughter," he commanded, arms crossed, waiting quietly.

"I was going to say, Father, that this matter surpasses in importance many topics you have fervently exhorted us upon. If it belongs to you to urge that men condemned for offenses against the state retain the right to appeal to the Great Council—and if counting it a glory to have won that right for them makes you proud—can it not also belong to you to oppose the denial of that right to those who need it most? Surely this touches the Christian life closer than predicting the death of the Dauphin or the conquest of Pisa."

A subtle tightening of his lips, a sign of suppressed pain, preceded his response.

"My daughter, I speak only as I am permitted. I am not master of the timing of revelations beyond common understanding. Here, I possess no illumination greater than the wisdom due to those charged with state safety. Regarding the Law of Appeal against the Six Votes, I strove to enact it so no Florentine might lose life or goods through the private hatred of a few in power. But these five men, who sought to overthrow a free government and restore a corrupt tyrant, were condemned with the assent of a large assembly. They initially refused to bring their cause before the Great Council. Thus, they forfeited the right to appeal."

"How can they have lost it?" Romola cried. "They have never been condemned until now! And forgive me, Father, but isn't it private hatred that denies them the appeal? Isn't it the violence of the few that frightens others? Why did the assembly split again immediately after seeming to agree? If anything argues against the law, let this weigh heavier: that you once preached more earnestly than anything else that there should be no room for hatred and bloodshed arising from party strife, so private ill-will finds no outlet in public acts. Father, you know private hatred is involved here. Will it not dishonor you not to intervene on the side of mercy, when many see it as the side of law and justice?"

"My daughter," Savonarola said, with visible emotion, "there is a mercy that is weakness, and even treason against the common good. The safety of Florence—which means far more than the welfare of individual Florentines—now demands severity, as it once demanded mercy. These men are condemned not only for a past plot but for one not yet executed. The devices leading to its execution remain active; the tyrant gathers forces in Romagna, and Florence's enemies, seated in the highest Italian thrones, await only a stone to crush her."

"What plot?" Romola asked, trembling with alarm.

"You carry papers in your hand," Savonarola noted, pointing to the handbills. "One of them confirms the government's new intelligence."
