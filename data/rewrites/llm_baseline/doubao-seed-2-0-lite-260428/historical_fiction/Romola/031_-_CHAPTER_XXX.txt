It was the first time Baldassarre had set foot in the Piazza del Duomo since the day he escaped captivity, and old, sharp shame coiled tight in his chest like a thorned vine. He had craved for months to hear the fiery, famous monk preach again, but he had flinched from returning to the spot where he had been dragged half-naked, his matted white hair hanging over his face, a coarse rope knotted around his throat, while strangers pointed and shouted that he was mad. The sting of that humiliation was still too raw, even after he had traded rags for a rough new tunic, cropped his unkempt beard short, and built back the sturdiness in his limbs. For when Tito’s cold, easy words “some madman, surely” had fallen on his ears, it was not only their cruelty that cut like a viper’s fang: it was Baldassarre’s instant, bitter, undeniable knowledge that he could not, in that moment, prove them false.

Alongside the roiling, all-consuming hunger for vengeance that possessed him, a sharp, gnawing doubt had taken root: he could not be sure he had the power to make that vengeance real. It felt as if some devil had whispered his most vulnerable secret straight into the traitor Tito’s ear. He was not mad—he carried the clear, piteous sanity of a man who knows exactly how much of his own mind has been shattered, who measures every gap in his memory and every fumble of his thoughts like a tally of wounds. That awareness spawned a wary, feral caution in him, the same wariness of a fierce but limping wild beast that pauses before advancing on a stronger prey, or a crawling insect that stops dead when the sliver of earth it stands on crumbles beneath it. That was why he had turned away Piero di Cosimo’s gentle friendly offers of help, why he had told the hospital friars no more than the bare, safe truth that French soldiers had captured him on his journey from Genoa.

His age, and the quiet refinement in his speech that marked him as better than the common beggars and wandering labourers the hospital usually sheltered, moved the monks to extra kindness: they gave him a thick coarse woollen tunic to fend off the November chill, a pair of scuffed but sturdy peasant shoes, and a handful of tiny copper danari, the smallest coin in Florence, to help him on his way. He set out for Arezzo at dawn the next day, but when he reached the first small hill town, he stopped, spent two of his tiny coins at the barber’s stall to have his beard shaved and his white hair cropped short, just as he had worn it for decades before his capture. The barber pulled out a polished hand mirror of bright steel: it had been years, more than he could clearly count, since Baldassarre had seen his own face. When his gaze fell on his reflection, a new thought struck him like a physical blow: could Tito truly not recognize him, after all this change? His hands shook like wind-tousled leaves as he pushed the barber’s arm away and demanded the mirror for himself, before the shaving began.

No. He was not changed so much that Tito could miss him. He recognized the familiar wrinkles, only cut deeper now; the same rough, thick skin of his brow, marked with little bumps that looked like half-erased cipher marks, only yellower now, tough and lifeless as dried fruit rind. That long shaggy white beard was no disguise for eyes that had studied his face every day for sixteen years, eyes that should have been searching for him as men search for their beloved among the bodies washed ashore by a storm. There was a new, wild edge to his glance, but that only made recognition more shocking—like a familiar beloved voice heard screaming in pain. That doubt was a foolish lie: he had felt Tito’s recognition in that piazza, sharp and terrified, the second their eyes met. He shoved the mirror away, the hot currents of hatred and vengeful purpose surging back to sweep away all soft hesitation.

He turned back toward Florence then, but he did not want to pass through the city gates before dusk, so he turned off the main road and sat down beside a small still pool, shaded on one side by alder bushes still dotted with fading yellow leaves. It was a calm, mild November day, and the still flat surface of the water caught his eye as a better, longer mirror. He wanted to study his face slowly, unobserved, as he had not dared to do at the barber’s stall. He leaned forward, staring intently at his own wavering reflection. Was there something vacant, imbecile, wandering in his gaze? Not now, not when he was fixed on this sharp, urgent question—his eyes burned with fierce, single-minded purpose. But in the long empty hours, when half-remembered whispers teased him from the fog of his lost memory, when half-seen faces faded away before he could reach out to grasp them? There must be a blank, bewildered confusion in his face then, he knew it, like a man struck suddenly blind.

How could he prove anything to anyone? How could he even begin to tell his story, without fear the threads of his thought would snap mid-sentence? Who would believe he had once been a scholar, his mind brimming with rare knowledge, fluent in half a dozen languages, sharp and quick in debate? All that carefully hoarded, life-long learning had slipped away from him, like water spilled from an urn lost to the wide ocean. Or was it still trapped inside him, locked behind some thin barrier that might one day shatter? He clung tight to that hope. Since the day he had first dragged himself weakly off his straw prison couch and stepped out into the sunlight to find half his mind gone dark, his strength had returned in fits and bursts. His body had healed, his will had grown firm again, he could recall every memory woven tight to his strongest emotions—his love for Tito, his rage at betrayal. Even better, twice before, when he had been seized by a surge of sharp excitement, he had felt his old self snap back into place for a breathless instant: he could see the lines of Greek script clear in his head, understand every word, feel his mind move light and un-numbed through all the ideas he had once known so well. The flash faded fast, leaving the dark heavier than before, but what if it stayed longer? Long enough for him to craft a revenge so slow, so exquisite, that a single strike of a fist could never equal it?

He stood up, folded his arms, and focused all his will on the plan he had to carry out. He could not beg. He could not let anyone mark him as a helpless, foolish old man. He remembered the name he had repeated over and over until it was stuck fast in his head: Bratti Ferravecchi. Back in Genoa, the stranger who had worn Tito’s carved onyx ring had told Baldassarre he had bought it in Florence, from a handsome young Greek, in the shop of a rag merchant named Bratti Ferravecchi, on the street that bore his very name. That single discovery had shattered every last shred of the faith Baldassarre had clung to for years: once, he had told himself Tito was dead, or captured too, that his letters had never reached the boy, that Tito was searching for him even now. He had persuaded his Corinthian captors to leave messages for any inquirer looking for him, certain Tito would find him there. Then the illness had come, and he had woken half-mad, useless to his owners, who had gladly passed him off to a compassionate Genoese merchant to get rid of him. For months he had sailed the Aegean, shore to shore, slowly regaining his bodily strength, until he landed in Genoa almost ready to give up on life entirely—until he saw that ring.

The moment he recognized the onyx, carved with the tiny image of Bacchus that he had given Tito as a boy, all that soft, sad longing turned to black venom. Tito was alive. Tito was rich, successful, living in Florence, selling the most precious keepsake Baldassarre had ever owned, while he rotted half-dead in chains. There was no more hope of finding his beloved son. There was only vengeance.

He did not even recognize the gaunt, haggard old man staring back at him in the pool water. That man was nothing but fuel for the fire of his hatred. He muttered to his reflection, recounting all the years he had poured into the boy he had raised: how he had loved him more than his own life, would have torn open his own chest to warm the child with his blood if Tito had but cared a little for his pain. He had spent his whole life chasing one drop of unselfish love, and got nothing for it. The world was a lie, full of nothing but betrayal. But there were deep, heady draughts to be drunk from the cup of hatred and revenge.

He would not waste his vengeance on a quick, easy knife stroke. He wanted Tito to suffer slow, every bit as much as he had suffered. But to do that, he had to survive. He had no skills left to offer, but his limbs had grown strong again: he could work as a porter, carry heavy loads through Florence’s narrow streets to earn his bread. Then his hand fell on the tiny blackened parchment bag strung around his bare neck, a charm his mother had hung around his neck when he was a small boy in Naples, on the sunlit balcony overlooking the bright blue bay. He had never dared open it, seeing it as a sacred relic of her love. But now, he tore at the parchment with desperate, nervous fingers, and pulled out what was inside: a tiny polished sapphire, as blue as that Neapolitan sea, worth several gold ducats.

He would sell it. He would buy himself a sharp dagger, something he could hold onto even when his memories slipped away, a loyal friend to feeble, righteous justice. He tucked the sapphire back into its wrappings, stood up as dusk painted the Florentine sky deep purple, and walked back toward the city gates.

He found a bare outhouse in the quiet, thinly populated Oltrarno district, with a heap of dry straw to sleep on, and sold the sapphire a few days later. He bought new warm clothes, and still had two gold florins left after he purchased the bright, sharp dagger he had set his heart on—at Bratti Ferravecchi’s own shop, no less. He bargained with the garrulous rag merchant, let slip that he was from Genoa, had been directed there by a man who had bought a precious ring from Bratti some months prior. Bratti preened, bragged about his famous connections, and spilled every scrap of gossip he knew about that handsome young Greek, Tito Melema: how he had found him sleeping under the Loggia de’ Cerchi, how Tito had sold all his precious jewels, settled fast in Florence, married a nobleman’s daughter, and risen to great fame and prosperity. Bratti never suspected the quiet, slightly deaf old man asking all the questions cared anything for the Greek youth—he thought the curiosity was all about Bratti’s own great cleverness as a merchant.

Baldassarre walked back to his straw heap with the dagger tucked at his hip, and all the scattered facts he could cling to about Tito’s new life. The memories were slippery, he knew; he had to repeat every name, every fact, ten times over to keep it from slipping away into the fog. As he passed the booksellers’ stalls on the Via del Garbo, he paused, staring at the open Greek volumes displayed there. If he stared long enough at the script, could he yank the old, lost knowledge back into his grasp? He bought the cheapest Greek book he could find, carried it home, and sat on his straw heap staring at the black marks on the white pages long after the sun went down, waiting for the light of old learning to dawn, waiting for the power to make his perfect, long-awaited vengeance real.
