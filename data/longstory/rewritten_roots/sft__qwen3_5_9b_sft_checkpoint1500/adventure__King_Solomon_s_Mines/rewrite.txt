The dust of the veldt clung to my boots like a second skin, a gritty reminder of the miles we had already traversed before the true journey began. It was here, amidst the sprawling, sun-baked plains of Southern Africa, that my path first crossed with Sir Henry Curtis. He was a man of considerable standing, a figure whose reputation preceded him like a shadow, yet he sought me out not for fame, nor for fortune alone, but because he needed a guide who understood the land better than any map could show.

Together, we pieced together the fragments of a legend older than the empire itself: the story of King Solomon's Mines. It was a tale whispered around campfires and dismissed by scholars alike—a myth of subterranean halls filled with untold riches, guarded by tribesmen who lived outside the bounds of conventional time. Yet, where others saw madness, I saw a pattern, a geography waiting to be mapped.

To navigate this unknown realm, we were joined by Umbopa, a warrior of the Matabele. His eyes held the sharp intelligence of a hunter, and though his people were fierce, his loyalty proved absolute once he entered our service. Our initial trials came swiftly; we engaged in a harrowing elephant hunt, testing our resolve against beasts of immense power, and then pressed onward into the vast, empty desert. There, the silence was deafening, broken only by the crackle of dry grass and the occasional cry of a bird. The thirst that followed was a physical torment, driving us to shout "Water!" until our voices were raw, pushing us toward a destination known only in stories: Solomon's Road.

As we ventured deeper into Kukuanaland, the landscape shifted, becoming stranger and more hostile. We encountered Twala, the King of the Matabele, a ruler whose ambition mirrored the ancient greed of the legends we pursued. What followed was a descent into chaos, marked by a witch-hunt that revealed the darkest undercurrents of tribal superstition. Trust eroded quickly as fear took hold; we gave signs of weakness, stood before an impending battle, and watched as the attack tore through our lines.

In the heat of combat, the Grey regiment made a desperate stand, holding back the tide long enough for some to escape, but the cost was high. Illness swept through the survivors, striking down Goodfalls, and soon we found ourselves at a place of death, surrounded by enemies and dwindling hope. Yet, within the depths of the earth, where the air grew cool and the walls shimmered with lost glories, lay the Treasure Chamber of Solomon. As the reality of our situation settled in, we abandoned hope for survival, clinging instead to the singular goal of securing what remained. In the end, Ignosi offered a final farewell, marking the close of an era for us, leaving behind a legacy etched in blood and gold. And then, finally, we found it—the proof that dreams, when backed by courage and endurance, could become reality.

As this volume finally takes shape and stands ready to enter the world, a profound sense of inadequacy regarding both its prose and its substance presses heavily upon me. Concerning the latter, let it be clear: this narrative does not claim to be a comprehensive chronicle of every action taken or sight witnessed during our expedition into the land of Kukuanaland. Countless details, particularly those concerning our journey, have been relegated to silence. I long to elaborate on the enigmatic legends surrounding the chain armor that spared us from annihilation in the Great Battle of Loo, and similarly, I wish to linger on the mysteries of the "Silent Ones"—those colossal statues guarding the entrance to the stalactite cavern. If my personal inclinations had guided my hand, I would have dedicated significant space to exploring the striking, and often thought-provoking, distinctions between the Zulu and Kukuana dialects. Furthermore, ample pages could have been devoted to examining the unique flora and fauna indigenous to this distant realm.

Yet, the subject that most demands attention—and which, regrettably, has received only incidental mention—is the magnificent military organization that governs Kukuanaland. In my estimation, this system far surpasses the reforms introduced by Chaka in Zululand. It allows for a swiftness of mobilization that is truly staggering and, crucially, operates without the need for the destructive practice of enforced celibacy. Moreover, I have barely touched upon the domestic and familial customs of the Kukuanas, many of which are exceedingly peculiar, nor have I fully detailed their remarkable mastery over the arts of smelting and welding metals. They have brought these sciences to a level of perfection rarely equaled elsewhere, as evidenced by their "tollas"—heavy throwing knives forged with immense skill, where blades of beautiful steel are welded onto frames of hammered iron.

Ultimately, standing alongside Sir Henry Curtis and Captain Good, we concluded that the wisest course was to recount our tale in a direct, unadorned fashion, leaving room for future elaboration on these intricate subjects whenever the time seemed right. For now, I pledge to share any knowledge within my grasp with those who seek it.

And so, it remains for me to apologize for the bluntness of my writing. My defense is simple: I am far more accustomed to handling a rifle than a quill, and I possess little aptitude for the soaring literary flourishes found in fiction—though I must admit, I enjoy reading them. Perhaps those embellishments serve a purpose, and I lament my inability to replicate them; yet, I cannot shake the belief that simplicity carries the greatest weight. A true story, no matter how extraordinary, does not require decoration in fine words to be understood. As the Kukuana proverb wisely states, "A sharp spear needs no polish." On this same principle, I venture to hope that this account stands firm on its own truth, unburdened by unnecessary ornamentation.

It is a peculiar irony of fate that at fifty-five, upon the heels of my most recent birthday, I find myself gripping a pen to chronicle a history. I wonder what sort of record it shall be when, if ever, I finally lay down the burden of this journey. My life has been a tapestry woven from commerce, hunting, combat, and mining, yet it seems I have only recently amassed a fortune—a considerable heap, though its precise magnitude remains elusive to me. Truthfully, I would not willingly traverse the harrowing fifteen or sixteen months required to acquire it again, not even knowing for certain that I would emerge unscathed with my wealth intact. Perhaps I am merely a timid soul, averse to violence, or perhaps I have grown weary of adventure itself. Yet, here I sit, compelled to write. Is it madness? I am no scholar, nor a man of letters, though I hold the Old Testament and the *Ingoldsby Legends* in high reverence. Let me attempt to catalogue my motives, however futile the exercise may prove.

My first reason is simple: Sir Henry Curtis and Captain John Good beseeched me.

Second, I am confined to Durban by a grievance in my left leg. That cursed lion still haunts me; since its attack, my limb has been prone to recurring inflammation, a malady that flares with alarming regularity. There must surely be some toxin in a beast's maw, for otherwise, how is it that the scars heal only to reopen at the precise season of the original mauling? To have shot sixty-five lions in a lifetime, only to find the sixty-sixth gnawing at one's mobility like chewing tobacco, disrupts the rhythm of existence. An orderly man cannot abide chaos in his routine.

Third, I wish to entertain my boy, Harry. Currently residing at a London hospital, training to become a physician, he requires diversion from the monotony of dissection. Surely, stories of adventure will enliven his days and keep mischief at bay, even if the tales are not devoid of danger.

And fourth, and final, I intend to recount the strangest narrative of my memory. It may sound odd, considering the absence of women within these pages—save for Foulata, the sorceress, and Gagaoola, if one could call her female. She was a century old, a creature of fire and shadow, hardly marriageable material. Rest assured, there is not a single petticoat in this entire account.

Now, let us to the matter at hand. It feels heavy, as if I were bogged down to the axle, but "slowly, slowly," as the Boers say, the wheel turns. A sturdy team eventually pulls through, provided they are not of the weak breed. Here begins my story.

*I, Allan Quatermain, of Durban, Natal, a Gentleman,* I swore before the magistrate regarding the tragic fates of Khiva and Ventvögel. Though the phrasing feels ill-suited for a novel, it serves to frame my identity. Am I truly a gentleman? The definition eludes me, yet I have traversed lands with both noble souls and base ones. I recall a native who was a gentleman in spirit, and conversely, a wealthy white man, freshly returned from abroad, who lacked every virtue of the title. By birthright, I am a gentleman, though my life has been that of a wandering trader and hunter. I have taken many lives, yet never without cause; I have defended myself, never stained my hands in innocent blood, trusting that the Almighty granted us life to preserve it. I hope that when the hour comes, I need not answer for the cruelties of a wicked world. I have fought much, more than a timid man should, yet I have never stolen, save for one instance where I deceived a Kafir of his cattle—a debt of honor I have carried ever since, for he had wronged me first.

It was roughly eighteen months past, during a bout of bad luck while elephant hunting beyond Bamangwato, that my path crossed with the men who would alter my destiny. My expedition had failed, plagued by misfortune and fever. Upon recovering, I trekked down to the Diamond Fields, liquidated my ivory, wagons, and oxen, dismissed my hunters, and boarded a post-cart for the Cape. After a perfunctory week in Cape Town, where I noted the exorbitant charges and the botanical gardens' potential benefits, I resolved to return to Natal via the *Dunkeld*, anchored at the docks awaiting the *Edinburgh Castle*.

As the afternoon waned, passengers from the *Edinburgh Castle* transhipped, and we cast off. Among the newcomers were two figures who immediately captured my attention. First, a gentleman of approximately thirty, possessing a chest and arms of formidable proportion. He bore yellow hair and a thick, matching beard, his features sharp and his grey eyes deep-set. A Royal Navy officer, in my estimation, embodies the ideal of a gentleman, washed pure by the vast oceans and the breath of divine winds.

Investigation revealed his name to be Captain John Good. A lieutenant of thirty-one, he had served seventeen years before being discarded by Her Majesty's Admiralty, stripped of promotion despite his competence, and reduced to half-pay. Such is the lot of those who serve the Crown: thrust into the cold world just as they mature. For my part, I prefer earning my bread as a hunter, where coins are scarce, but the blows fewer.

At Inyati, the remote trading post in the Matabele lands ruled by the tyrant Lobengula, we reluctantly abandoned our sturdy wagon. Of the twenty fine oxen purchased in Durban, only twelve remained. Five more fell ill from the same poison, yet we saved them with a decoction brewed from boiled tulip leaves—a potent antidote if applied swiftly. Leaving the wagon and the surviving livestock in the capable hands of Goza and Tom, alongside a local Scotch missionary, we embarked on foot. My companions included Umbopa, Khiva, Ventvögel, and six hired bearers.

There was a profound silence as we departed. Each of us harbored a lingering doubt: would we ever see our wagon again? For my own part, I held little hope. We tramped in somber silence until Umbopa, marching ahead, broke the spell with a Zulu chant. He spoke of brave souls weary of tame existence who ventured into the vast unknown, risking death to discover wonders. And lo, he declared, they found not a wilderness, but a paradise teeming with beauty, fertile land, and enemies worth killing.

Laughter erupted among us, buoyed by Umbopa's cheer. He was a spirited savage, dignified in his bearing, possessed of a remarkable gift for maintaining morale, even when not plagued by his melancholic reveries. We all grew to cherish him dearly.

Now, I must recount the specific adventure I have promised, for I harbor a deep affection for tales of the chase.

Approximately a fortnight's march from Inyati, we discovered a stunning expanse of lush woodland. The kloofs were cloaked in dense "idoro" bush, interspersed with the formidable "wacht-een-beche," or "wait-a-little thorn." Abundantly growing were the majestic "machabell" trees, heavy with their delicious yellow fruit, though the pits were nearly as large as the fruit itself. These trees were the elephants' favorite fare, and evidence of their voracious appetite was everywhere: frequent tracks, snapped branches, and uprooted giants scattered the landscape.

On a particularly beautiful evening, following a long day's march, we arrived at a spot of breathtaking loveliness. At the base of a bush-covered hill lay a dry riverbed containing pools of crystal-clear water, rimmed with the hoof prints of game. Opposite stood a park-like plain dotted with flat-topped mimosa and glossy machabells, surrounded by an endless sea of silent, untrodden bush.

Emerging onto this path, we startled a troop of tall giraffes. They fled with a strange, sailing gait, tails curled high, their hooves rattling like castanets. Though three hundred yards away and beyond effective range, Good, who walked ahead armed with an express loaded with solid balls, could not resist the temptation. He fired at the last, a young female. By a stroke of extraordinary fortune, the ball struck her squarely on the neck, shattering her spine. She collapsed instantly, rolling head over heels like a rabbit. I had never witnessed a more bizarre spectacle.

"Oh, 'Bougwan!' " echoed Sir Henry and I, referring to Good's nickname, "Glass Eye," earned due to his spectacles. Although he was a notoriously poor shot, his lucky hit secured his reputation as a marvel among the Kafir tribes.

We tasked some of the boys with butchering the giraffe, while we constructed a "scherm"—a circular thorn hedge enclosing a cleared space—near one of the pools. Inside, we laid down a bed of dry tambouki grass and lit fires. As the moon rose, our dinner of giraffe steak and roasted marrow bones was ready. There is no greater luxury than giraffe marrow, surpassed only by elephant heart, which we hoped to taste tomorrow. Eating by moonlight, we paused occasionally to praise Good's miraculous aim, then smoked and chatted around the fire. We formed a striking tableau: I, with my short, grizzled hair, thin frame, and dark complexion; Sir Henry, tall, broad, fair-skinned, and sporting long golden locks; and Captain John Good, R.N., sitting atop a leather bag. Unlike the rest, Good looked immaculate, dressed in brown tweed, a matching hat, neat gaiters, and sporting a collar of white gutta-percha. His face was perfectly shaved, his glasses and dentures pristine.

"They weigh so little," he explained innocently regarding his lack of mud or sweat. "And I always prefer to present myself as a gentleman." Alas, if he had known the raiment awaiting him, his composure might have faltered.

As the Kafirs sipped their intoxicating "daccha" from pipes carved from eland horns, drifting off into slumber, Umbopa sat apart, chin resting on his hand, lost in deep thought. Suddenly, a loud "woof!" echoed from behind us. "A lion!" I cried, and we sprang up. Hardly had we risen when the strident trumpet of an elephant shattered the night. "_Unkungunklovo! Indlovu!_" the Kafirs whispered urgently—"Elephant! Elephant!"

"It's futile," I warned quietly. "Let them pass."

Sir Henry, however, argued for staying. "It seems we are in a paradise of game," he suggested. "I propose we linger a day or two and give them a try." I was taken aback; usually, Sir Henry urged haste, especially after learning at Inyati that an Englishman named Neville had sold his wagon there two years prior and ventured deeper. Yet, his hunter's instinct clearly overrode caution. Good eagerly agreed, and frankly, so did I; letting such a herd escape without a challenge violated my conscience.

"All right, my hearties," I declared. "We need some recreation. Let us retire now, aiming to depart by dawn, perhaps catching them feeding before they retreat."

Preparations commenced. Good stripped, carefully folding his trousers, glasses, and false teeth, placing them beneath his waterproof sheet to avoid the morning dew. Sir Henry and I settled into our rougher arrangements, curling up in blankets for a dreamless sleep.

*Going, going, go...* What sound was that?

Suddenly, violent scuffling erupted from the water's direction, followed immediately by a cacophony of terrible roars. There was no mistake; only a lion could produce such a noise. We scrambled up, staring toward the water where a confused mass of yellow and black staggered and struggled toward us. Seizing our rifles and slipping on our veldtschoons (hide boots), we burst from the scherm. By then, the mass had collapsed, rolling over and over until it lay still.

Upon closer inspection, we saw the truth. On the grass lay a sable antelope bull—the most magnificent of Africa's antelopes—dead, impaled by the massive curved horns of a magnificent black-maned lion, equally deceased. The sable had descended to drink where the lion lay in ambush. As the antelope drank, the lion lunged, only to be pinned by the sharp horns. Unable to free himself, the lion tore and bit at the bull's neck and back. Driven mad by agony, the bull charged until it collapsed, dragging its killer down with it.

Once we had inspected the carcasses, we summoned the Kafirs, and together dragged the bodies to the scherm. We retired inside, resolving to awaken only at dawn.

At first light, we prepared for battle. Armed with three eight-bore rifles, ample ammunition, and water bottles filled with weak cold tea—which I swear is the best stimulant for shooting—we consumed a light breakfast. Umbopa, Khiva, and Ventvögel joined us, leaving the other Kafirs to skin the lion and butcher the antelope.

Finding the broad elephant trail was effortless. Ventvögel confirmed it belonged to twenty to thirty elephants, mostly mature bulls. Though the herd had moved during the night, the broken trees, bruised leaves, and smoking droppings indicated we were not far behind.

A chill ran through me. To address a white man by a native name, especially one so familiar, was an affront. Yet, Umbopa's presence commanded a strange respect. "How dost thou know my name?" I demanded sharply. "Thou speakest to me as an equal."

"That is true," Umbopa agreed. "A Hottentot I met on the road spoke of a white man entering the desert two years past, accompanied by a hunter named Jim. They did not return."

"Nay, I know not the truth of it," Umbopa admitted. "Yet the Hottentot described the man as having thy eyes and a black beard. And he said the hunter was a Bechuana clad in clothes."

"There is no doubt," I interjected, recognizing the description of Jim instantly. "I knew him well."

Sir Henry nodded, a grim determination settling over his features. "George, when he set his mind on a thing, he accomplished it. If he meant to cross the Suliman Berg, he crossed it, unless fate intervened. We must look for him on the other side."

Umbopa listened, his eyes reflecting a depth of wisdom that surprised me. "Great words, my father," he murmured. "Listen, what is life? A feather, a seed of grass, blown hither and thither. His speech shifted, growing poetic, a burst of eloquence that transcended simple barbarism. "Out of the dark we came, into the dark we go. Like a storm-driven bird at night, we fly from the Nowhere. "And perhaps," Umbopa replied, "you seek a brother over the mountains."

Suspicion pricked at me. "What dost thou know of those mountains?" I asked sharply.

"A little," Umbopa said, his voice dropping. "There is a strange land yonder, a realm of witchcraft and beauty, of brave souls and snowy peaks. I have heard tales. But what is the use of words? Darkness falls. Those who survive will see."

He raised his spear in salute and departed, leaving us with a sense of unease. He knew too much, and his silence was heavy.

The following day, preparations began. Heavy rifles and bulky gear could not be dragged across the shifting sands. We entrusted them to an old native keeper nearby, securing them with a mixture of threats and superstitions. I swore to curse his family with madness and witchcraft if he touched a single piece, a bluff that worked perfectly on the superstitious old man. He treated the weapons as if they were ancestral spirits, terrified to approach them.

We pared our supplies down to the bare minimum, a cruel necessity for the wasteland ahead. Three express rifles, two Winchesters, five water bottles, blankets, dried meat, beads for trade, and essential medical stores. The weight was a burden, roughly forty pounds per man, but it was the price of survival. We persuaded three local natives to join us for the initial leg, carrying large gourds of water, lured by the promise of hunting knives. They seemed resigned to their fate, perhaps believing our eventual demise was inevitable, yet they accepted the deal.

Rest came briefly at sunset, a feast of fresh beef and tea—the last we would taste for many days. Then, as the moon rose, bathing the rolling dunes in silver light, we stood ready. Hesitation, that natural instinct, gripped us for a moment on the threshold of this irreversible step. Sir Henry spoke, his deep voice cutting through the silence. "Gentlemen," he said, "we embark on a journey stranger than any dream. Success is doubtful. But we stand together, for good or ill, to the end. Let us pray to the Power who shapes destiny."

For a minute, hats removed, hands over faces, we bowed our heads. The prayer was simple, born of the terror of the unknown, yet it brought a strange peace to our hearts.

"Trek!" Sir Henry commanded.

We moved forward, guided only by Silvestre's ancient, fragmented map and the distant silhouette of the mountains. The sand was treacherous, working into our boots, forcing frequent stops to shake it free. The air was thick, heavy with a creamy stillness that oppressed the soul. About four o'clock, the physical exhaustion finally yielded to a torment far worse: a gnawing, ravenous thirst that tore through me like a fever. I could not return to sleep. My dreams had been a delusion—a refreshing stream with verdant banks—but reality slammed me back into this arid hell. As Umbopa had warned, if we failed to locate water today, we would perish miserably. In this crushing heat, no human soul could endure long without it. I sat up, scrubbing my soot-stained face with dry, calloused hands until my lips and eyelids, fused together by dehydration, finally parted. Though it was barely past dawn, the air offered no crisp promise of morning; instead, it was choked with a suffocating, hot haze. The others remained unconscious.

Soon, however, the gloom receded enough to read. I pulled out my pocket copy of *Ingoldsby Legends* and opened "The Jackdaw of Rheims." Reading the line, *"A nice little boy held a golden ewer / Embossed, and filled with water as pure..."*, I physically winced, trying to slap my cracked lips against the empty air. Once fully conscious, we faced the grim reality of our situation: absolute drought. We inverted our bottles, licking the rims, but they were as dry as bone. Stretching for hundreds of miles on either side lay the great Suliman Berg. To describe the sheer sublimity of that vision is difficult; words fail before its majesty. Mysterious vapors and clouds quickly gathered, veiling the giants in a ghostly gauze, obscuring their terrifying grandeur.

Afterward, stripped naked, we wallowed in the mud, soaking our parched skins. Refreshed, we devoured our dried meat (*biltong*) and smoked our pipes, falling asleep by the pool's shadow until noon.

Restored, we refilled our bottles and resumed our march under the moonlight. We covered twenty-five miles that night, though found no more water, save for a fleeting bit of shade behind some ant-hills the next day. By the following morning, the majestic peaks loomed larger than ever, looming directly above us as we approached the lower slopes of Sheba's left breast. High above, amidst large lumps of cooled lava, we spotted a bizarre anomaly: a patch of vibrant green growth. It was a miracle of nature, soil formed from decomposed rock holding bird-dropped seeds. However, grass offered no sustenance to starving men. We collapsed beneath the rocks, despair weighing heavily on us. Upon the earth, some sound or motion always exists to blunt the edge of absolute silence. Here, buried deep within the bowels of a snow-capped giant, there was none. Above us, thousands of feet high, the wind swept across the snow, unheard. Separated from the Chamber of the Dead by a tunnel and five feet of unyielding rock, and knowing that the dead make no noise, we were severed from every echo of the living world. Perplexed, Good observed that when I lit the match, the draft had blown the flame to the left.
"Air draws inward, not outward," he reasoned. "Let us go against the flow."
Feeling along the walls, testing the ground, we departed the accursed chamber, embarking on a terrible quest for life. Should any living soul return to this place, they will find open chests of jewels, an empty lamp, and the white bones of Foulata.
Groping for a quarter-hour, the tunnel took a sharp turn, then split, forcing us into a labyrinthine network of galleries. Hours passed in a stone maze that seemed to lead nowhere. These must be the ancient workings of a mine, shifting like veins as ore dictated the path.
Finally, utterly spent, we devoured our last scrap of biltong and drank our final sip of water, our throats parched like kilns. Escaping the treasure chamber only to confront Death in the tunnels, we stood depressed. Then, I heard it—a faint, distant murmur.
"It's running water," Good whispered, his voice trembling. "Come on."
We resumed our journey toward the sound. I laid down the basket, wishing to rid myself of its weight, but reconsidered; better to die

Ten days after our harrowing ordeal brought us back to the familiar quarters at Loo. Miraculously, we emerged scarcely worse for the wear, save for the undeniable graying of my stubble—a testament to the cavern's atmosphere—and the profound, lingering shadow cast by Foulata's passing. Her loss struck Good with a ferocity that altered his spirit forever, yet I, viewing the matter through the lens of seasoned pragmatism, deemed her removal a necessary mercy. An alliance between a man of his nature and a girl of such stately, ethereal beauty and refined intellect would have been a disaster from the outset. As she herself poignantly asked, "Can the sun mate with the darkness?"

Our return to the subterranean labyrinth was swift and decisive. We abandoned any thought of penetrating Solomon's Treasure Chamber a second time. Our recovery required forty-eight hours, during which we attempted to locate the exit shaft in the great pit, only to find it obscured by heavy rains and a chaotic network of ant-bears' burrows that made navigation a fool's errand. Before departing, we revisited the Stalactite Cave and ventured once more beneath the spear of the White Death into the Chamber of the Dead. Standing before the immovable blockage—the massive rock sealing our fate alongside Gagool's remains—we felt a chilling mix of awe and despair. Despite our efforts, the secret mechanism remained elusive, buried under layers of mystery and time. It was a marvel of engineering, simple yet inscrutable, a relic of an age that had vanished centuries prior. Yet, faced with five feet of solid granite, we knew that nothing short of dynamite could breach it. With a heavy heart, we turned away from the unimaginable wealth trapped behind that wall, convinced that those diamonds and pearls would remain in eternal, silent company with Foulata's bones, never to grace the necks of living beauties.

Disappointment, however, was a fleeting emotion. By a stroke of providential foresight, I had stuffed the wide pockets of my shooting coat and trousers with gems before our imprisonment ended, along with Foulata's basket, which brimmed with an additional hoard. Though our tumble down the pit scattered several of the larger stones, an immense fortune remained. We were, if not millionaires by American standards, undeniably wealthy, possessing enough stones to outfit the finest jewelry collections in Europe for three families.

Upon reaching Loo, King Ignosi awaited us with open arms, his health restored and his authority firmly consolidated following his struggle with Twala. He listened with rapt attention to our tale, his expression growing somber when we recounted Gagool's gruesome demise. Summoning an ancient counselor, he inquired about the witch-doctoress's youth and appearance, receiving confirmation that she had always been a figure of ugliness and malice. "An ancient curse is lifted from the land," the elder declared, and Ignosi nodded, relieved that a woman capable of killing his father and usurping his throne was finally silenced.

As I prepared to depart, addressing Ignosi as a sovereign who had risen from rebellion to rule, I implored him to honor his promises: to govern justly, uphold the law, and refrain from arbitrary executions. "To-morrow, at break of day," I urged, "you will provide an escort to lead us across the mountains." Ignosi covered his face with his hands, his grief palpable. "My heart is sore," he confessed, his voice breaking. "What have I done to you... that ye should leave me desolate?" He offered gifts beyond measure—maidens, cattle, land, weapons—but I refused them all. "We seek our own place," I insisted.

His bitterness flared briefly. "Now I learn that ye love the bright stones more than me!" he cried, his eyes flashing. "Cursed be the white stones! Death shall it be to him who seeks them..." Yet, as I reminded him of his longing for his mother's native land, where he had played as a boy, his resolve softened. "In like manner, Ignosi, do our hearts turn to our land," I said. His tone shifted to one of wisdom and sorrow. He decreed that no other white man should ever attempt to cross the mountains or trade with his people; no guns, no gin, no "praying-men" to sow fear. But for Incubu, Macumazahn, and Bougwan, the path remained open. "Ye are dearer to me than aught that breathes," he proclaimed. In a gesture of supreme honor, he declared their names *hlonipa*—names forbidden to be spoken, reserved for the memory of dead kings, ensuring their legacy would endure forever.

"We must go," he whispered, "ere my eyes rain tears like a woman's."

Leaving Loo at dawn the next day, we were accompanied by Infadoos, Ignosi's uncle, and a regiment of Buffalos. The streets were lined with multitudes, showering us with flowers and blessings, celebrating our role in defeating Twala. Just outside the town, a whimsical scene unfolded: a young girl presented flowers to Good and requested a glimpse of his "beautiful white legs." After some initial hesitation, citing indecency, Good relented, drawing up his trousers to the knee amidst the adoration of the crowd. He walked thus until we cleared the town, a spectacle that would likely haunt his memory, though perhaps not diminish his reputation.

Infadoos guided us via a secondary pass north of Solomon's Great Road, leading us down the sheer cliff separating Kukuanaland from the desert, near the legendary "Sheba's Breasts." Here, Infadoos shared a poignant story of Kukuana hunters who, seeking ostriches, had stumbled upon a vast, fertile oasis miles away, saved from thirst by trees on the horizon. It was through this route that we intended to traverse the desert.

At the precipice, we bid a tearful farewell to Infadoos. "Never, my lords," he sobbed, "shall mine old eyes see the like of you again." As a token of our gratitude, Good presented him with an eyeglass—a spare pair—which Infadoos, delighted by the prospect of enhanced prestige, managed to screw onto his own eye, creating an absurd and comical image against his leopard-skin cloak and black ostrich plumes.

Descending the steep slope, we reached the plains below without injury. That night, by the firelight, Sir Henry remarked on the peculiarities of our recent adventures, noting that while Kukuanaland might hold horrors, the past month had been uniquely strange. Good sighed, expressing a wish to return, but I, reflecting on a lifetime of shaves, felt a quiet contentment. All was well that ended well, despite the cold chill the memory of battle still sent through me.

The following morning, we embarked on a toilsome trek across the desert, aided by five guides carrying ample water supplies. By the afternoon of the third day, the green canopy of trees belonging to the promised oasis came into view. Within an hour of sunset, we stepped once more onto soft grass, hearing the gentle murmur of running water, safe and refreshed after our perilous journey.

And thus I arrive at what may well count among the strangest episodes of this entire expedition, an occurrence that vividly illustrates how providence often intervenes in the most improbable ways.

I was strolling alone, a considerable distance ahead of the others, tracing the banks of the stream that winds from the oasis before vanishing into the insatiable jaws of the desert sand. Suddenly, I halted and rubbed my eyes, a gesture entirely justified by the disorientation I felt. Before me, barely twenty yards away, nestled in a picturesque clearing beneath the canopy of a wild fig tree and facing the water, stood a cozy hut. It was constructed, roughly speaking, on the principles of the Kafir tribes—wattle and daub, grass, and woven branches—but distinguished by a proper, full-length door rather than the customary bee-hole.

"What devilry," I muttered to myself, "could possibly justify a hut existing in this forsaken spot?" The words had scarcely left my lips when the door swung open, and a figure emerged, limping heavily. It was a white man, draped in animal skins and sporting an immense, jet-black beard. My first impulse was to assume the heatstroke had finally taken hold, yet the sight was undeniable. No hunter ventured this far, let alone established a dwelling here. I gaped, and so did the man following behind me. Just then, Sir Henry and Good rounded the bend.

"Look here, gentlemen," I shouted, "is that a white man, or have I finally gone mad?"

Sir Henry peered closely, as did Good. Then, amidst the silence of the wastes, the lame white man with the black beard let out a piercing cry and began hobbling toward us. As he closed the distance, he collapsed, overcome by a fit. With a bound, Sir Henry was instantly at his side.

"Heavens!" he exclaimed, his voice trembling. "It is my brother, George!"

The commotion drew out another figure from the hut, a man similarly clad in skins and gripping a rifle. Upon spotting me, he let out a wail and rushed forward.

"Quatermain!" he hallooed. "Do you not recognize me, Baas? I'm Jim, the hunter. I lost the note you entrusted me with for the Chief, and we have been stranded here for nearly two years." As he spoke, he threw himself at my feet, rolling on the ground in a torrent of tears.

"You reckless rogue!" I chided, "you deserve a thorough hiding"—or *sjambocking*, as the locals call it.

By this time, the man with the black beard had regained his composure and risen. He and Sir Henry were engaged in a frantic embrace, pumping each other's hands, though neither seemed able to utter a word. Whatever grievances they had harbored in the past—my suspicion was that a certain lady lay at the root of their estrangement—they were clearly buried under the weight of this reunion.

"My dear old fellow," Sir Henry burst out eventually, "I thought you dead. I crossed Solomon's Mountains specifically to find you. I had surrendered all hope of ever seeing you again, and now you appear to me, perched in the desert like an ancient vulture."

"I attempted to traverse Solomon's Mountains nearly two years ago," replied the speaker, his voice hesitant, betraying a throat unused to speech. "But when I reached this place, a boulder tumbled from the hillside, crushing my leg. Since then, I have been unable to move forward or backward."

I stepped forward. "How do you do, Mr. Neville?" I asked. "Do you recall me?"

"Why," he stammered, "surely it is Hunter Quatermain, and Good? Wait a moment, you both look dizzy. Everything is so incredibly strange, and yet, for a man who has lost all hope, so overwhelmingly joyful!"

That evening, gathered around the campfire, George Curtis recounted his tale. It was, in many respects, as eventful as our own. Briefly summarized, the narrative unfolded as follows: Less than two years prior, he had departed Sitanda's Kraal in an attempt to reach Suliman's Berg. Regarding the note I had dispatched via Jim, that worthy had unfortunately mislaid it, leaving George unaware of its contents until this very hour. However, acting on intelligence gathered from the native tribes, he had bypassed Sheba's Breasts, opting instead for the steep, ladder-like descent of the mountains through which we had recently passed—a route clearly superior to that depicted in Old Dom Silvestra's plans.

Their journey through the desert had been fraught with extreme hardship, but they eventually stumbled upon this oasis. Here, tragedy struck. On the day of their arrival, George sat by the stream while Jim harvested honey from a nest of stingless bees located atop a bank directly above him. In his agitation, Jim dislodged a massive boulder, which crashed down upon George's right leg, shattering it with terrible force. From that day forth, his injury rendered him immobile, unable to walk forward or retreat. Having chosen to face the certainty of death within the oasis rather than wander aimlessly into the desolation of the desert, they had survived together.

Provisions were surprisingly adequate. They possessed ample ammunition, and the oasis, particularly at night, teemed with game drawn there for water. They hunted these beasts or trapped them in pits, utilizing the meat for sustenance and the hides for clothing once their garments wore thin.

"And so," George concluded, "we have existed for nearly two years, akin to a modern Robinson Crusoe and his Friday, clinging to a thread of hope that natives might discover us and rescue us. None have arrived. Only last night, we decided that Jim must depart, attempting to reach Sitanda's Kraal for aid. He was to leave tomorrow, though I held little expectation of ever seeing him return. And now, you—all people in the world whom I presumed had long since forgotten me, and who I imagined were enjoying comfortable lives in old England—appear in such a chaotic manner, finding me exactly where you least expected. It is the most wonderful, and most merciful, coincidence I have ever known."

Sir Henry then proceeded to recount the principal facts of our adventures, sitting with us deep into the night.

"By Jove!" George Curtis observed when I displayed some of the diamonds. "Well, at least you have secured some recompense for your troubles, aside from my own worthless company."

Sir Henry chuckled. "They belong to Quatermain and Good. It was stipulated in our agreement that any spoils found were to be divided between them."

This statement sparked a reflection in me. After conferring with Good, I approached Sir Henry and expressed our collective desire for him to accept a third portion of the diamonds. Alternatively, if he declined, we suggested his share be transferred to his brother, who had endured greater suffering and risk in pursuit of these gems. Eventually, we persuaded him to agree to this arrangement, though George Curtis remained ignorant of this decision until some time later.

At this juncture, I believe it appropriate to pause my chronicle. Our trek back to Sitanda's Kraal across the desert proved immensely arduous, particularly given the necessity of supporting George Curtis, whose injured leg was exceedingly fragile and prone to shedding splinters of bone. Yet, by sheer grit, we accomplished the journey. The specifics need not be repeated, as they largely mirror the trials we faced on our previous excursion.

Six months after our return to Sitanda's, where we discovered our firearms and provisions safely stored—though the elderly keeper was greatly displeased to see survivors returning to claim them—we finally arrived at my modest residence on the Berea, near Durban. Here, I bid farewell to those who have shared in the strangest voyage of my long and varied life.

*Postscript:* Just as I committed the final word to parchment, a Kafir entered my avenue of orange trees, bearing a letter in a split stick, delivered from the post. It proved to be from Sir Henry, and as it speaks for itself, I transcribe it in full.
