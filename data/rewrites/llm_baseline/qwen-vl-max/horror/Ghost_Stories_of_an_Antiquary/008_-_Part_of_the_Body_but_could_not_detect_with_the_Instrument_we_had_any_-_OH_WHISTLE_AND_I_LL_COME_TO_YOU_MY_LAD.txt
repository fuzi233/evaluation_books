“I suppose you’ll be leaving soon now that Full Term is over, Professor?” a man not in the story said to the Professor of Ontography, shortly after they’d sat down beside each other at a feast in the hospitable hall of St James’s College.

The Professor was young, neat, and spoke with precision.

“Yes,” he replied. “My friends have been urging me to take up golf this term, and I plan to go to the East Coast—specifically Burnstow, as I’m sure you know—for a week or ten days to improve my game. I hope to depart tomorrow.”

“Oh, Parkins,” said his neighbor on the other side, “if you’re heading to Burnstow, I wish you’d look into the site of the Templars’ preceptory and let me know if it’s worth digging up in the summer.”

It was, as one might expect, someone with an interest in antiquities who said this—but since he only appears briefly here, there’s no need to name him.

“Certainly,” said Parkins, the Professor. “If you can describe where the site is, I’ll do my best to assess the terrain when I return—or I could write to you, if you tell me where you’ll be.”

“No need for that, thanks. It’s just that I’m thinking of bringing my family that way during the Long Vacation, and I thought, since so few English preceptories have ever been properly mapped, I might make something useful of my spare time.”

The Professor sniffed faintly at the idea that mapping out a ruined monastery could be called useful. His neighbor continued:

“The site—there’s probably nothing visible above ground—must be close to the beach now. The sea has encroached heavily along that stretch of coast, as you know. From the map, I’d say it’s about three-quarters of a mile from the Globe Inn, at the northern end of town. Where are you staying?”

“Well, *at* the Globe Inn, actually,” said Parkins. “I’ve booked a room there. I couldn’t find anywhere else; most of the lodgings are closed in winter, it seems. And even then, they told me the only large room available is technically double-bedded—and they don’t even have space to store the second bed. But I need a fairly spacious room, since I’m bringing books and intend to do some work. Though I don’t particularly like having an empty bed—let alone two—in what I’ll call, for now, my study, I suppose I can manage for the short time I’ll be there.”

“Do you really call having an extra bed roughing it, Parkins?” asked a blunt man across the table. “Look here—I’ll come down and take it for a bit. It’ll be good company for you.”

Parkins flinched but managed a polite laugh.

“By all means, Rogers. I’d welcome it. But I’m afraid you’d find it rather dull—you don’t play golf, do you?”

“No, thank Heaven!” said Mr. Rogers, unrepentant.

“Well, when I’m not writing, I’ll likely be out on the links. That, as I said, would probably be rather dull for you.”

“Oh, I don’t know,” Rogers replied. “There’s bound to be someone I know there. But of course, if you don’t want me, just say so, Parkens. I won’t be offended. Truth, as you always remind us, is never offensive.”

Parkins was indeed scrupulously courteous and rigorously honest. It was rumored that Mr. Rogers sometimes exploited these traits. Now, a conflict stirred within Parkins—so intense that for a moment he could not speak. When he finally did, it was with careful honesty:

“Well, if you insist on the truth, Rogers, I was wondering whether the room would truly be large enough for both of us—and whether (mind, I wouldn’t have mentioned this unless you pressed) your presence might interfere with my work.”

Rogers laughed loudly.

“Well done, Parkins! All right. I promise not to disturb your researches. Don’t worry about that. No, I won’t come if you don’t want me—but I thought it might help keep the ghosts away.” He winked and nudged his neighbor.

Parkins flushed.

“I beg your pardon, Rogers,” he said stiffly. “I shouldn’t have said that. I forgot you don’t care for levity on such subjects.”

“Well,” Parkins said, “now that you’ve raised it, I freely admit I dislike careless talk about what you call ghosts. A man in my position,” he added, raising his voice slightly, “cannot afford to appear to endorse such beliefs. As you know, or ought to know, since I’ve never hidden my views—”

“No, you certainly haven’t, old man,” Rogers interjected softly.

“I hold that any suggestion—any appearance of concession—that such things might exist is equivalent to abandoning everything I hold sacred. But I fear I’ve lost your attention.”

“Your *undivided* attention,” Rogers corrected, with mock seriousness, “was what Dr. Blimber actually said.”[4] “But I apologize, Parkins—I’m interrupting.”

[4] Mr. Rogers was mistaken. See *Dombey and Son*, chapter xii.

“No, not at all,” said Parkins. “I don’t recall Blimber; perhaps he was before my time. But I needn’t continue. I’m sure you understand what I mean.”

“Yes, yes,” said Rogers, hastily. “Just so. We’ll discuss it fully at Burnstow—or somewhere.”

In recounting this exchange, I’ve tried to convey the impression it left on me: Parkins was something of an old maid—perhaps henlike in manner, utterly lacking in humor, yet resolute and sincere in his convictions. A man worthy of respect, though perhaps not entirely likable.

On the following day, Parkins succeeded in leaving college and arrived at Burnstow. He was welcomed at the Globe Inn, settled into the large double-bedded room we’ve heard about, and before retiring arranged his materials in perfect order on a spacious table at the far end of the room, which faced three windows overlooking the sea. The central window looked straight out to the ocean; the others offered views north and south along the shore. To the south lay the village of Burnstow. To the north, only beach and low cliffs. Immediately ahead was a narrow strip of rough grass dotted with old anchors and capstans, then a broad path, then the beach itself. Whatever the original distance between the inn and the sea, now less than sixty yards separated them.

The rest of the inn’s guests were mostly golfers—few of whom required special mention. The most prominent figure was Colonel Wilson, a retired military man and secretary of a London club, whose voice was thunderous and whose views were decidedly Protestant. These often surfaced after church services led by the Vicar—a decent man with a fondness for ritualistic flourishes, which he suppressed as much as possible out of deference to East Anglian tradition.

Professor Parkins, known for his courage, spent most of the next day improving his golf game alongside the Colonel. By afternoon—whether due to fatigue or something else, it’s unclear—the Colonel’s demeanor had turned so grim that even Parkins hesitated at the idea of walking home with him. After a quick glance at his bristling mustache and crimson face, Parkins decided it would be wiser to let tea and tobacco calm the Colonel before dinner made their meeting inevitable.

“I could walk home tonight along the beach,” he mused. “Yes, and take a look at those ruins Disney mentioned. I don’t exactly know where they are—but I doubt I’ll miss them.”

He did, in fact, stumble upon them—literally. While making his way from the links to the shingle beach, his foot caught in a gorse root and a stone, and he fell. When he rose and surveyed his surroundings, he found himself in broken ground marked by small depressions and mounds. Closer inspection revealed these were masses of flint embedded in mortar, overgrown with turf. Clearly, he was on the site of the preceptory he’d promised to examine. It seemed promising—enough foundation remains likely remained near the surface to reveal much about its layout.

He recalled vaguely that the Templars often built round churches, and noticed that a cluster of mounds nearby appeared arranged in a circular pattern. Few people can resist the urge to dabble in archaeology, even outside their expertise—especially when it comes to proving how much they’d have achieved if only they’d pursued it seriously. Parkins, however, while perhaps tempted by such vanity, also genuinely wished to assist Mr. Disney. So he carefully paced the circular area and recorded its dimensions in his notebook. Then he examined an oblong mound to the east of the center, which seemed likely to be a platform or altar base. At one end, the turf had been torn away—probably by a child or some wild creature. He decided to probe the soil there for signs of masonry, pulled out his knife, and began scraping.

A portion of earth collapsed inward, revealing a small cavity. He lit match after match to see what lay inside, but the wind snuffed them out. Instead, he tapped and scraped the walls with his knife and deduced it was an artificial hole in stone—rectangular, with smooth sides, top, and bottom. It was empty. Or so he thought. As he withdrew his knife, a metallic clink echoed. When he reached in, his hand touched a cylindrical object lying on the floor of the hole. Naturally, he retrieved it. In the fading light, he saw it was man-made—a metal tube about four inches long, clearly ancient.

By the time he confirmed there was nothing else in the cavity, it was too dark and late to search further. What he’d found had proven unexpectedly fascinating. He resolved to spend more daylight hours exploring the next day. The object in his pocket was surely of some value.

The view before him was bleak and solemn. A faint yellow glow in the west illuminated the links, where a few figures still moved toward the clubhouse. The squat martello tower, the lights of Aldsey village, the pale ribbon of sand interrupted by black wooden groynes, and the dim, murmuring sea formed a desolate tableau. The wind blew bitterly from the north, but at his back as he set off for the Globe Inn. He rattled across the shingle, then stepped onto the sand—except for the groynes, which required leaping every few yards. The going was quiet and easy.

One last glance behind showed a shadowy figure struggling to catch up—running, but making little progress. There was motion, but no real advance. Parkins thought he didn’t recognize him, and decided it would be absurd to wait. Still, company, he reflected, would be welcome on such a lonely shore—if only he could choose it. In his younger days, he’d read tales of encounters in such places that still chilled him. One memory came vividly to mind: “Now I saw in my dream that Christian had gone but a very little way when he saw a foul fiend coming over the field to meet him.”

“What would I do,” he wondered, “if I looked back and saw a black shape sharply outlined against the yellow sky, with horns and wings? Would I stand or run? Luckily, the man behind isn’t like that. He seems just as far away now as when I first saw him. Well, at this rate, he won’t get dinner before I do—and dear me, it’s nearly time. I must hurry!”

Parkins had little time to dress. When he met the Colonel at dinner, peace—or as much as the Colonel could manage—had returned to the military breast. It held through the bridge game that followed, for Parkins was a competent player. By midnight, he felt satisfied with his evening and believed life at the Globe Inn would be bearable for several weeks—“especially,” he thought, “if I keep improving my game.”

As he walked the corridors, he met the inn’s boots, who stopped him.

“Pardon, sir,” he said, “but while brushing your coat, something fell from your pocket. I placed it on your chest of drawers—a piece of pipe or something similar. Thank you, sir. You’ll find it there—yes, sir. Good night, sir.”

This reminded Parkins of his discovery. With keen curiosity, he examined the object by candlelight. It was bronze, shaped like a modern dog-whistle—indeed, it was unmistakably a whistle. He brought it to his lips, but it was clogged with fine, hardened sand. Knocking didn’t loosen it—he had to scrape it out with a knife. Tidy as ever, Parkins emptied the dirt onto paper and carried it to the window to discard. The night was clear and bright. He paused to gaze at the sea and saw a lone figure standing on the shore below the inn. Then he shut the window, slightly surprised at how late people stayed out in Burnstow, and returned to the whistle.

Wait—were there marks on it? Not just marks, but letters! A little rubbing revealed deeply incised text. On one side:

On the other:

“I should be able to decipher this,” he thought. “But perhaps my Latin is rusty. Come to think of it, I don’t even know the word for ‘whistle.’ The second line is simple enough—‘Who is this who is coming?’ The best way to find out, then, is to blow it.”

He blew tentatively—then froze, startled yet intrigued by the sound. It carried an infinite distance, soft yet resonant, seemingly audible for miles. It evoked images—like certain scents, it conjured visions. For a moment, he saw a vast, dark expanse under a cold wind, a solitary figure in the center—what he was doing, he couldn’t say. The image shattered as a sudden gust slammed against the casement. He looked up just in time to glimpse the white flash of a seabird’s wing beyond the glass.

Fascinated, he blew again—this time more boldly. The note was no louder, and the illusion faded. No vision followed. But then—what was this? Goodness! How strong the wind had become! A tremendous gust! There! I knew that latch wouldn’t hold! Ah! Both candles are out. This wind could tear the room apart.”

First thing: shut the window. For twenty seconds, Parkins struggled with the casement, feeling as if he were pushing back a burglar. The pressure eased suddenly, and the window slammed shut and latched itself. Now, relight the candles and check for damage. Nothing seemed broken—no glass cracked. But the noise had awakened someone: the Colonel stomped above in his stockings, growling.

The wind didn’t subside quickly. It moaned and rushed past the house, rising at times to a cry so mournful that, as Parkins observed dispassionately, it might unsettle the fanciful. Even the unimaginative, he mused after fifteen minutes, might prefer silence.

Whether it was the wind, the excitement of golf, or the day’s discoveries, Parkins wasn’t sure. But he remained awake, convinced he was suffering from fatal ailments—counting heartbeats, fearing paralysis, suspecting lung or liver failure. He knew daylight would dispel these fears, but until then, they refused to leave. He found comfort in knowing someone else was restless too—a neighbor rustled and tossed nearby.

Next, he closed his eyes and tried to sleep. But overexcitement took another form: hallucinations. _Experto crede_, pictures come to the closed eyes of those trying to sleep—often unwelcome ones.

Parkins’s experience was especially distressing. The image that formed was continuous. Opening his eyes made it vanish—but closing them again restored it, playing out at the same pace. Here is what he saw:

A long stretch of shore—shingle bordered by sand, intersected by black groynes reaching the water. A scene so identical to his afternoon walk that, without landmarks, it was indistinguishable. The light was dim, suggesting storm, late winter, and a light rain. At first, no figure appeared. Then, in the distance, a bobbing black shape emerged—soon revealed as a man running, jumping, scrambling over the groynes, constantly glancing back. The closer he came, the more evident his terror—though his face remained unseen. He was nearly spent. Each obstacle grew harder. “Will he make it over this one?” Parkins thought. “It looks higher than the rest.” Yes—half climbing, half throwing himself, he cleared it and collapsed on the other side, near the viewer.

There, crouching beneath the groyne, he remained, staring upward with desperate anxiety.

So far, no cause for his fear had appeared. But now, far up the shore, a flicker of pale movement darted swiftly and erratically. Growing larger, it became a figure in fluttering, ghostly draperies, indistinct. Its motion unnerved Parkins—he dreaded seeing it close. It would stop, raise arms, bow to the sand, then dart across the beach to the water’s edge and back. Then, rising upright, it would resume its terrifying advance. Soon, the pursuer hovered just beyond the groyne where the runner hid. After several futile swings, it halted, stood tall, arms raised high, then lunged straight toward the groyne.

At this point, Parkins always failed to keep his eyes closed. With dread, he lit a match—only to have it go out. A second burned better. Candle and book were fetched. He read until sleep finally came, healthy and deep. For the first time in his orderly life, he forgot to extinguish the candle. When he was roused at eight the next morning, a faint flame still flickered in the socket, and grease had melted into a mess on the table.

After breakfast, he was dressing—fortune once again pairing him with the Colonel—when a maid entered.
