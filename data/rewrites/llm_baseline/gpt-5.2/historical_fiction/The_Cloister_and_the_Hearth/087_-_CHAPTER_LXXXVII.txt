Jorian, with his doublet half-laced and his jerkin hanging open, stood in the churchyard as if the very act of clothing himself were a sort of oath to the dead. He had been talking all the while—talking as men talk when their hands are busy and their minds refuse to be still—his breath sending little clouds into the cold air that smelled of wet earth and old stone.

“They used to call him a magician out Sevenbergen way,” he said, tightening a strap, his tongue running on as steadily as his fingers. “And they do say he gave ’em a touch o’ his trade at parting; told ’em he saw Margaret’s lad a-coming down Rhine in brave clothes and store o’ money, but his face scarred by foreign glaive, and not altogether so many arms and legs as a went away wi’. But, dear heart, nought came on’t. Margaret is still wearying for her lad; and Peter, he lies as quiet as his neighbours; not but what she hath put a stone slab over him, to keep him where he is: as you shall see.”

He braced both hands upon the edge of the open grave, palms damp with the clay that clung like dough, and began to heave himself up; the spade lay beside him, its blade dark and shining as if oiled by the grave-mould.

A hand—light, trembling, yet urgent—settled on his shoulder.

“How long since died Peter Brandt?” The voice was a whisper, but a whisper with the rasp of thirst and fasting in it.

“About two months. Why?” Jorian answered, looking up; and as he looked he saw the friar’s face drawn thin, as if the bells of the town had been ringing inside his skull all night.

“And his daughter buried him, say you?”

“Nay, I buried him; but she paid the fee and reared the stone.”

“Then—” The friar’s lips worked. “But he had just one daughter; Margaret?”

“No more leastways, that he owned to.”

“Then you think Margaret is—is alive?”

Jorian’s brows lifted, honest wonder at the question. “Think? Why, I should be dead else. Riddle me that.”

The friar swayed, the colour rushing to his cheeks and then draining away, as if some unseen choir were singing him from earth to heaven and back again.

“Alas, how can I?” he breathed. “You love her.”

Jorian gave a short, incredulous laugh. “No more than reason, being a married man, and father of four more sturdy knaves like myself. Nay, the answer is, she saved my life scarce six weeks agone. Now had she been dead she couldn’t ha’ kept me alive. Bless your heart, I couldn’t keep a thing on my stomach; nor doctors couldn’t make me. My Joan says, ‘’Tis time to buy thee a shroud.’ ‘I dare say, so ’tis,’ says I; ‘but try and borrow one first.’ In comes my lady, this Margaret— which she died three years ago, by your way on’t—opens the windows, makes ’em shift me where I lay, and cures me in the twinkling of a bedpost; but wi’ what? there pinches the shoe; with the scurviest herb, and out o’ my own garden, too; with sweet feverfew. A herb, quotha—’tis a weed; leastways it was a weed till it cured me, but now whene’er I pass my hunch I doff bonnet, and says I, ‘fly service t’ye.’ Why, how now, father, you look wondrous pale, and now you are red, and now you are white? Why, what is the matter? What, in Heaven’s name, is the matter?”

“The surprise—the joy—the wonder—the fear,” the friar gasped, as if each word were wrestled out of him. The yard seemed to sharpen around them: the caw of a distant crow, the thin scrape of twig against stone, the faint drip from a yew. “Why, what is it to thee? Art thou of kin to Margaret Brandt?”

“Nay; but I knew one that loved her well, so well her death nigh killed him, body and soul. And yet thou sayest she lives. And I believe thee.”

Jorian stared, silence settling on him like dust. Then he said slowly, very gravely, as a man who begins to fear he has been standing all his life upon a trapdoor.

“Father, you have asked me many questions, and I have answered them truly; now for our Lady’s sake answer me but two. Did you in very sooth know one who loved this poor lass? Where?”

The friar’s face tightened. He stood on the brink of some confession, but whatever name rose in him he smothered it, as if it burned.

“I knew him in Italy,” he said at last.

“If you knew him you can tell me his name,” said Jorian, cautious now.

“His name was Gerard Eliassoen.”

Jorian drew breath as if struck. “Oh, but this is strange. Stay—what made thee say Margaret Brandt was dead?”

“I was with Gerard when a letter came from Margaret Van Eyck. The letter told him she he loved was dead and buried.” The friar’s knees gave. “Let me sit down, for my strength fails me. Foul play! Foul play!”

“Father,” said Jorian, and there was a rough kindness in him, like coarse salt rubbed into a wound to keep it clean, “I thank Heaven for sending thee to me. Ay, sit ye down; ye do look like a ghost; ye fast overmuch to be strong. My mind misgives me; methinks I hold the clue to this riddle, and if I do, there be two knaves in this town whose heads I would fain batter to pieces as I do this mould.”

He seized the long spade and, with his teeth set, brought it down again and again upon the heap of loose earth; each blow made a dull, hungry sound, the sound of ground broken for sowing—only this was ground broken for vengeance.

“Foul play?” he went on. “You never said a truer word i’ your life; and if you know where Gerard is now, lose no time, but show him the trap they have laid for him. Mine is but a dull head, but whiles the slow hound puzzles out the scent—go to. And I do think you and I ha’ got hold of two ends o’ one stick, and a main foul one.”

Then, after those useless fore-ramblings by which plain folk edge toward what bites them hardest, he told his tale.

He had been hired by the burgomaster of Tergou to mend the floor of an upper room; and when the planks were near laid, he had come suddenly at an odd hour to fetch his tools. Below, voices rose sharp enough to pierce oak. Curiosity pricked him; he lay down flat, belly to the boards, and listened through a crack that tasted of dust and old pitch.

He heard the burgomaster speaking of a letter. Cornelis and Sybrandt—those names came like rancid smoke through the chink—were minded to slip their own letter into the place of one that a certain Hans Memling was taking to Gerard. They lacked courage; the burgomaster’s voice, thick with contempt and temptation, answered that.

“And to give them courage,” Jorian said, “the old man showed them a drawer full o’ silver; and if they did the trick they should each put a hand in, and have all the silver they could hold in’t.”

At that, Jorian confessed, he had slept badly: not for pity, not yet, but for the glitter of it. “Think on’t,” he muttered, as if the very thought still hurt his gums.

“Next morning at peep o’ day who should I see but my masters Cornelis and Sybrandt come out o’ their house each with a black eye. ‘Oho,’ says I, ‘what yon Hans hath put his mark on ye; well now I hope that is all you have got for your pains.’ Didn’t they make for the burgomaster’s house? I to my hiding-place.”

The friar’s nostrils flared; his eyes, restless as a hunted thing’s, fixed on Jorian’s mouth as if to drag each syllable out faster.

“Well,” said Jorian, “the burgomaster brought them into that same room. He had a letter in his hand; but I am no scholar; however, I have got as many eyes in my head as the Pope hath, and I saw the drawer opened, and those two knaves put in each a hand and draw it out full. And, saints in glory, how they tried to hold more, and more, and more o’ yon stuff!”

His voice, despite himself, took on the relish of the moment: the soft clink and slide of coins, the greedy rustle of sleeves, the breath held as fingers groped like thieves in a pantry.

“And Sybrandt,” Jorian added, his mouth twitching, “he had daubed his hand in something sticky— I think ’twas glue— and he made shift to carry one or two pieces away a sticking to the back o’ his hand, he! he! he! ’Tis a sin to laugh.”

Then his face hardened. “So you see luck was on the wrong side as usual; they had done the trick; but how they did it, that, methinks, will never be known till doomsday. Go to, they left their immortal jewels in yon drawer. Well, they got a handful o’ silver for them; the devil had the worst o’ yon bargain.”

He exhaled as if he had been holding this story in his ribs for years. “There, father, that is off my mind. Often I longed to tell it some one, but I durst not to the women; or Margaret would not have had a friend left in the world; for those two black-hearted villains are the favourites, ’Tis always so. Have not the old folk just taken a brave new shop for them in this very town, in the Hoog Straet? There may you see their sign, a gilt sheep and a lambkin; a brace of wolves sucking their dam would be nigher the mark. And there the whole family feast this day; oh, ’tis a fine world.”

He waited for the friar’s curse, for some holy thunder to answer his own earthy rage; but the friar sat rigid, as if turned to carved wood on a tomb.

“What, not a word, holy father?” Jorian burst out. “You sit there like stone, and have not even a curse to bestow on them, the stony-hearted miscreants. What, was it not enough the poor lad was all alone in a strange land; must his own flesh and blood go and lie away the one blessing his enemies had left him? And then think of her pining and pining all these years, and sitting at the window looking adown the street for Gerard! and so constant, so tender, and true—my wife says she is sure no woman ever loved a man truer than she loves the lad those villains have parted from her; and the day never passes but she weeps salt tears for him.”

His voice rose, filling the yard; even the small sounds seemed to shrink back—the finches went quiet, and the thin wind in the bare branches turned aside as if unwilling to be witness.

“And when I think,” he went on, “that, but for those two greedy lying knaves, yon winsome lad, whose life I saved, might be by her side this day the happiest he in Holland; and the sweet lass, that saved my life, might be sitting with her cheek upon her sweetheart’s shoulder, the happiest she in Holland in place of the saddest—oh, I thirst for their blood, the nasty, sneaking, lying, cogging, cowardly, heartless, bowelless—how now?”

The friar sprang up like a man who has been struck in the breast by a mace. His face went livid; his hands clenched and lifted, not in prayer, but in some terrible, wordless summoning. He rushed from the place, a black storm loosed among gravestones.

So fierce was that inarticulate fury that Jorian’s own anger fell away like a broken tool. He stood staring after the human tempest he had unchained.

Then fingers—small, cold, trembling—closed round his arm.

It was Margaret Brandt.

He started; her coming at that instant was as uncanny as the sudden chill that steals through a room when a door opens on an empty passage. She had waited long by Peter’s stone, listening to the yard’s ordinary life: the soft scuff of shoes, the cough of an old man inside the church, the distant market-hum that carried faintly even here. The friar did not return. So she had gone into the church to seek him; could not find him. Then, in the south aisle, the giant shadow of a friar had come swiftly along the flags and climbed a pillar like a living thing, seeming to pass through her.

She had nearly screamed—until memory, quick as instinct, told her how Jorian’s shadow had done the like coming from the churchyard. She scrambled out the nearest way, catching her skirt, breath snagged in her throat. By the time she gained the street, Clement was already tearing away; yet even at a distance his very body cried agitation.

Margaret and Jorian looked from one another to the wild figure diminishing down the road.

“Well?” she asked, and her voice shook as a spoon shakes in an empty pot.

“Well,” said Jorian, thickly, “you startled me. How come you here of all people?”

“Is this a time for idle chat? What said he to you? He has been speaking to you; deny it not.”

“Girl,” said Jorian, “as I stand here, he asked me whereabout you were buried in this churchyard.”

“Ah!”

“I told him, nowhere, thank Heaven: you were alive and saving other folk from the churchyard.”

“Well?”

“Well, the long and the short is, he knew thy Gerard in Italy; and a letter came saying you were dead; and it broke thy poor lad’s heart. Let me see; who was the letter written by? Oh, by the demoiselle van Eyck. That was his way of it. But I up and told him nay; ’twas neither demoiselle nor dame that penned yon lie, but Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, and those foul knaves, Cornelis and Sybrandt; these changed the true letter for one of their own; I told him as how I saw the whole villainy done through a chink; and now, if I have not been and told you!”

Margaret’s hand flew to her mouth. Her eyes shone wet, but in the wet there was sudden light, as when a hearth catches after long coaxing.

“Oh, cruel! cruel! But he lives. The fear of fears is gone. Thank God!”

“Ay, lass,” said Jorian. “And as for thine enemies, I have given them a dig. For yon friar is friendly to Gerard, and he is gone to Eli’s house, methinks. For I told him where to find Gerard’s enemies and thine, and wow but he will give them their lesson. If ever a man was mad with rage, its yon. He turned black and white, and parted like a stone from a sling. Girl, there was thunder in his eye and silence on his lips. Made me cold a did.”

“Oh, Jorian, what have you done?” Margaret cried. “Quick! quick! help me thither, for the power is gone all out of my body. You know him not as I do. Oh, if you had seen the blow he gave Ghysbrecht; and heard the frightful crash! Come, save him from worse mischief. The water is deep enow; but not bloody yet, come!”

The anguish in her voice did what no command could. Jorian sprang up out of the grave as if it had become suddenly unclean to him, and as he ran he dragged his jerkin on, half-wearing it, half-wrestling it, the leather creaking and his breath loud in his ears.

As they hurried, he tried to make sense of her words. “I talk of this friar,” he panted, “and you answer me of Gerard.”

“Man, see you not? this is Gerard!”

“This—Gerard? what mean ye?”

“I mean,” she said, and her voice broke and steadied in the same breath, “yon friar is my boy’s father. I have waited for him long, Jorian. Well, he is come to me at last. And thank God for it. Oh, my poor child! Quicker, Jorian, quicker!”

Jorian’s eyes widened, as if his mind were tasting some impossible flavour and could not name it. “Why, thou art mad as he. Stay! By St. Bavon, yon was Gerard’s face; ’twas nought like it; yet somehow—’twas it. Come on! come on! let me see the end of this.”

“The end?” Margaret whispered. “How many of us will live to see that?”

They went in breathless silence, their footfalls and the town’s living noises braiding together: a cart’s iron rim singing on cobbles, a woman calling from a doorway, a hammer somewhere ringing like a heart in a forge. Hoog Straet drew near; the smells changed—less damp stone, more yeast and tallow, more frying fat from some kitchen where even grief had to be fed.

Then Jorian, trying clumsily to comfort, said, “You are making your own trouble. Who says he has gone thither? More likely to the convent to weep and pray, poor soul. Oh, cursed, cursed villains!”

“Did not you tell him where those villains bide?”

“Ay, that I did.”

“Then quicker, oh, Jorian, quicker. I see the house. Thank God and all the saints, I shall be in time to calm him. I know what I’ll say to him; Heaven forgive me!” Her eyes darted, as if she saw not only the street before her but years behind it. “Poor Catherine; ’tis of her I think: she has been a mother to me.”

The shop stood on a corner, two doors like two mouths: one opening to the main street for customers and coin, the other round the corner, the private door where secrets entered and did not always come out again. Above hung the sign—gilt sheep and lambkin shining complacently in the pale daylight, as if innocence could be bought and sold with wool.

Margaret and Jorian were within twenty yards when a roar tore out from within, raw as a beast’s, making the very air tremble. In the next instant the friar burst out—white, raging, his habit flung wide as if the wind itself were trying to rip him free of it—and went tearing down the street.

Margaret screamed and sagged, fainting against Jorian’s arm.

Jorian bellowed after the flying figure, “Stay, madman, know thy friends!”

But the friar did not so much as turn his head. He went headlong, shaking his clenched fists high, high in the air, as if he would strike the sky for permitting the world’s lies.

“Help me in, good Jorian,” Margaret moaned; and then, sudden as a candle steadies when the draft is shut out, she grew eerily calm. “Let me know the worst; and die.”

He half carried her into the house. The stillness inside was unnatural, as if all the household sounds—laughter, plates, the gossiping clatter of a feast—had been swallowed at one gulp. Not a voice, not a step, not even the small domestic music of spoon against bowl.

Jorian’s heart thudded loud enough to seem an intruder. A door stood before him, unlatched. He set his left hand to it and pushed softly.

Margaret and he stood upon the threshold.

What they saw there you shall soon know.
