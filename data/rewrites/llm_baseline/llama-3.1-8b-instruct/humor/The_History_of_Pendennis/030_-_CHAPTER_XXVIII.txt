As the ballroom teemed with the fervor of civil war, high words clashed and the crowd surged forward, drawn to a window in the corner near the door through which the Chevalier Strong navigated with ease. The cacophony of the street below echoed up, a jarring symphony of jeers and taunts – "Strike him down!" "Where are the authorities?" – as a circle of onlookers, including Madame Fribsby, congregated around Monsieur Alcide Mirobolant, while a group of gentlemen and ladies surrounded Arthur Pendennis on the opposite side. Strong pushed through the throng, brushing past Madame Fribsby, who beamed at the Chevalier's arrival, exclaiming, "Save him, save him!" in a tone both frantic and poignant.

The source of the commotion became apparent: the fiery temper of Sir Francis Clavering's chef had been ignited. Shortly after Strong departed, Mr. Pendennis, still seething with indignation at his waltzing mishap, which had left him looking ridiculous in the eyes of the assembly, and Miss Amory's behavior, which had further wounded his pride, stood at the window, gazing out at the distant sea, its waves sparkling in the fading light, and murmuring softly to himself, attempting to collect his thoughts and admit, perhaps, that he had acted rashly during the night. As he stood there, lost in his reverie, a hand fell upon his shoulder, and, turning, he beheld Monsieur Mirobolant, his eyes blazing with an unyielding intensity, his ringlets askew, and a fierce glint in his gaze. The touch of the French chef's hand on his shoulder was a breach of etiquette that sent the blood of the Pendennises coursing through Mr. Pen's veins, and he was taken aback, more startled than enraged, by the affront.

"You speak French?" Mirobolant inquired, his voice low and measured, in his native tongue.

"What concern is it of yours?" Mr. Pen retorted, his response in English.

"At the very least, you comprehend it?" Mirobolant persisted, bowing slightly.

"Yes, sir," Mr. Pen replied, his voice firm, "I understand it fairly well."
