In the realm of Flatland, where the veil of familiarity shrouds our existence, recognition proves a wily adversary. As a dweller in this two-dimensional world, you might wonder how we distinguish one another amidst the sea of identical lines. Our forms, whether animate or inanimate, present a uniform aspect to our gaze – a straight line – making it a challenge to discern one from another.

Consider the world above, where the gift of sight grants you the luxury of perceiving depth and color. You behold the world through the lens of perspective, where angles and shapes converge to form a tapestry of beauty. In contrast, our reality is a realm of monochromatic simplicity, where the nuances of identity are reduced to the subtleties of sound and touch.

We rely on the sense of hearing to navigate the complexities of social hierarchy. With ears attuned to the frequencies of our kind, we can distinguish the voices of our acquaintances, and even categorize individuals into the three lowest orders: the Equilateral, Square, and Pentagon. However, as we ascend the social ladder, the task becomes increasingly arduous, for the voices of the elite blend together, and the art of voice-discrimination is a trait more readily associated with the common folk. The Aristocracy, with their refined sensibilities, often eschew this method, opting instead for more...refined approaches.

Among our lower classes, the faculty of feeling holds sway. In a world where introductions are a vital part of social currency, the phrase "permit me to feel and be felt by my friend Mr. So-and-so" is still used in rural districts, a quaint relic of a bygone era. In the bustling metropolises, however, the language has evolved, and the phrase is reduced to a simple "Let me ask you to feel Mr. So-and-so," with the understanding that the feeling is reciprocated. The younger set, ever eager to streamline their interactions, have taken to using "to feel" in a technical sense, meaning "to recommend-for-the-purposes-of-feeling-and-being-felt." Thus, the phrase "Mr. Smith, permit me to feel Mr. Jones" has become the lingua franca of high society.

But let not the reader suppose that feeling, in our world, is a laborious process akin to the tedious exercise of exploring every contour of a three-dimensional shape.
