Nothing happened in the night; and no attempt at communication between Miss Rachel and Rosanna rewarded the vigilance of Sergeant Cuff. I sought my early solace where I always do: in the ancient, cyclical rhythms of the estate’s kitchens. There is a profound, acoustic harmony in a great house waking up, a multi-generational symphony I have overseen for decades. The rhythmic *thump-knead-fold* of the scullery maids working the heritage sourdough, the crackle of applewood logs in the cast-iron hearth, and the rich, olfactory blanket of rendering bacon fat and toasted oats usually center my spirit. I am a man who reads the health of his household in the steam of its broths and the cadence of its morning chores. But today, the air felt flat, the smells of crushed herbs and roasting marrow somehow dimmed by the sterile, discordant presence of the Sergeant.

I had expected Cuff to set off for Frizinghall first thing. He waited about, however, pacing with a heavy, irregular tread that thoroughly disrupted the acoustic ecology of the lower halls. I left him to his own devices and went out into the grounds, seeking the timeless, oceanic wind blowing through the shrubbery side, carrying the scent of sea-salt and bruised wild thyme. There, I met Mr. Franklin. Before we had exchanged two words, the Sergeant unexpectedly joined us, cutting through the natural rustle of the leaves with his sharp, metropolitan gait. 

He made up to Mr. Franklin, who received him haughtily enough. “Have you anything to say to me?” was all the return Cuff got for politely wishing Mr. Franklin good morning.

“I have something to say to you, sir,” answered the Sergeant, deploying the clipped, dominant lexicon of the law—a language devoid of warmth, built only for transaction and entrapment. “On the subject of the inquiry I am conducting here. You detected the turn that inquiry was really taking, yesterday. Naturally enough, in your position, you are shocked and distressed. Naturally enough, also, you visit your own angry sense of your own family scandal upon Me.”

“What do you want?” Mr. Franklin broke in, sharply enough.

“I want to remind you, sir, that I have at any rate, thus far, not been *proved* to be wrong. Bearing that in mind, be pleased to remember, at the same time, that I am an officer of the law acting here under the sanction of the mistress of the house. Under these circumstances, is it, or is it not, your duty as a good citizen, to assist me with any special information which you may happen to possess?”

“I possess no special information,” says Mr. Franklin. 

Sergeant Cuff put that answer by him, as if no answer had been made, steamrolling over Mr. Franklin’s voice with the oppressive machinery of his own interrogation. “You may save my time, sir, from being wasted on an inquiry at a distance,” he went on, “if you choose to understand me and speak out.”

“I don’t understand you,” answered Mr. Franklin; “and I have nothing to say.”

“One of the female servants (I won’t mention names) spoke to you privately, sir, last night.”

Once more Mr. Franklin cut him short; once more Mr. Franklin answered, “I have nothing to say.”

Standing by in silence, I thought of the movement in the swing-door on the previous evening, and of the coat-tails I had seen disappearing down the passage. The Sergeant had, no doubt, just heard enough before my interruption to suspect that Rosanna had relieved her mind by confessing something to Mr. Franklin Blake. 

This notion had barely struck me when who should appear at the end of the shrubbery walk but Rosanna Spearman herself! She was followed by Penelope, who was evidently trying to make her retrace her steps to the house. Seeing that Mr. Franklin was not alone, Rosanna came to a standstill, caught in a terrible perplexity. Mr. Franklin saw the girls as soon as I saw them. The Sergeant, with his devilish cunning, took on not to have noticed them at all.

All this happened in an instant. Before either Mr. Franklin or I could say a word, Sergeant Cuff struck in smoothly, projecting his voice with a harsh, unnatural volume that shattered the quiet sanctuary of the garden. He wielded his words as a brutal instrument of power. 

“You needn’t be afraid of harming the girl, sir,” he said to Mr. Franklin, speaking so that Rosanna must hear him. “On the contrary, I recommend you to honour me with your confidence, if you feel any interest in Rosanna Spearman.”

Mr. Franklin instantly took on not to have noticed the girls either. He answered, matching the Sergeant’s booming, invasive tone: “I take no interest whatever in Rosanna Spearman.”

I looked towards the end of the walk. The devastation in Rosanna was absolute, yet profoundly untranslatable. Her subjective experience—the sudden, violent crushing of her secret hopes—was entirely beyond the reach of the dominant language being volleyed between the two gentlemen. Instead of resisting Penelope, she turned round, surrendering her arm to my daughter, and was led back toward the house.

The heavy bronze breakfast-bell rang as the two girls disappeared—a resonant, ancestral tolling that has called generations of this family to the comfort of the morning meal, though today its peal felt tragically ironic. Even Sergeant Cuff was now obliged to give it up as a bad job. He said to me quietly, “I shall go to Frizinghall, Mr. Betteredge; and I shall be back before two.” He went his way without a word more, and the acoustic space of the garden finally breathed a sigh of relief.

“You must make it right with Rosanna,” Mr. Franklin said to me, when we were alone. “I seem to be fated to say or do something awkward before that unlucky girl. You must have seen yourself that Sergeant Cuff laid a trap for both of us. If he could confuse *me*, or irritate *her* into breaking out, either she or I might have said something which would answer his purpose. On the spur of the moment, I saw no better way out of it than the way I took. It stopped the girl from saying anything, and it showed the Sergeant that I saw through him. He was evidently listening, Betteredge, when I was speaking to you last night.”

He had done worse than listen, I privately thought to myself. He had remembered my telling him that the girl was in love with Mr. Franklin, and he had calculated on *that*, using her most tender feelings as mere fodder for his investigation.

“As to listening, sir,” I remarked, keeping the other point to myself, “we shall all be rowing in the same boat if this sort of thing goes on much longer. Prying, and peeping, and listening are the natural occupations of people situated as we are. In another day or two, Mr. Franklin, the natural harmony of this house will be entirely destroyed. We shall all be struck dumb together—for this reason, that we shall all be listening to surprise each other’s secrets, and all know it. Excuse my breaking out, sir. The horrid mystery hanging over us gets into my head like sour wine, and makes me wild. I won’t forget what you have told me. I’ll take the first opportunity of making it right with Rosanna Spearman.”

“You haven’t said anything to her yet about last night, have you?” Mr. Franklin asked.

“No, sir.”

“Then say nothing now. I had better not invite the girl’s confidence, with the Sergeant on the look-out to surprise us together. My conduct is not very consistent, Betteredge—is it? I see no way out of this business, which isn’t dreadful to think of, unless the Diamond is traced to Rosanna. And yet I can’t, and won’t, help Sergeant Cuff to find the girl out.”

Unreasonable enough, no doubt. But it was my state of mind as well.

The state of things, indoors and out, while Sergeant Cuff was on his way to Frizinghall, was briefly this: Miss Rachel waited for the time when the carriage was to take her to her aunt’s, still obstinately shut up in her own room. My lady and Mr. Franklin breakfasted together. The table was meticulously laid with the culinary heritage of our agrarian past—a rich, savory pie of local pigeon and wild mushrooms, its crust flaking perfectly; thick, creamy butter churned only hours before; and preserves simmering with the deep, tart memory of last summer’s harvest. Yet, neither possessed the sensory appreciation required to honour such sustenance; they consumed it mechanically, their palates deadened by anxiety. After breakfast, Mr. Franklin took a sudden resolution and went out precipitately to quiet his mind by a long walk.

The change in the weather, foreshadowed overnight, had come. Heavy rain had been followed soon after dawn by high wind. It was blowing fresh as the day got on. The clouds threatened, but the rain held off. It was not a bad day for a walk, if you could breast the great gusts of wind sweeping in from the sea, filling the ears with the ancient, mythic roar of the coastal tides.

I attended my lady after breakfast, assisting her with the household accounts. She only once alluded to the Moonstone, forbidding any present mention of it between us. “Wait till that man comes back,” she said, meaning the Sergeant. “We *must* speak of it then: we are not obliged to speak of it now.”

After leaving my mistress, I found Penelope waiting for me in my room. “I wish, father, you would come and speak to Rosanna,” she said. “I am very uneasy about her.”

I suspected the matter readily enough. But as an elder of this household, it is a maxim of mine that men are bound to improve women—if they can. When a woman wants me to do anything, I insist on knowing why. The oftener you make them rummage their own minds for a reason, tracing their logic much as one traces the terroir of a complex vintage, the more manageable you will find them. Penelope’s reason on this occasion may be given in her own words.

“I am afraid, father,” she said, “Mr. Franklin has hurt Rosanna cruelly, without intending it.”

“What took Rosanna into the shrubbery walk?” I asked.

“Her own madness,” says Penelope. “I can call it nothing else. She was bent on speaking to Mr. Franklin this morning, come what might of it. I did my best to stop her. If I could only have got her away before she heard those dreadful words——”

“There! there!” I said. “I can’t call to mind that anything happened to alarm Rosanna.”

“Nothing to alarm her, father. But Mr. Franklin said he took no interest whatever in her—and, oh, he said it in such a cruel voice!”

“He said it to stop the Sergeant’s mouth,” I answered.

“I told her that,” says Penelope. “But you see, father, he’s been mortifying and disappointing her for weeks; and now this comes on the top of it all! She has no right, of course, to expect him to take any interest in her. But she seems to have lost pride, and proper feeling, and everything. She frightened me, father. Those words seemed to turn her into stone. A sudden quiet came over her, and she has gone about her work ever since like a woman in a dream.”

Penelope’s description silenced my superior sense. I recalled what had passed between Mr. Franklin and Rosanna overnight; she had been cut to the heart, and now stung again on the tender place. Sad! All the more sad because the girl had no reason to justify her, and no right to feel it. 

I had promised Mr. Franklin to speak to Rosanna, and this seemed the fittest time. We found the girl sweeping the corridor outside the bedrooms. The sound of her broom against the floorboards—*swish, swish*—lacked the vigorous, rhythmic purpose of a domestic chore. It was a hollow, deadened abrasion, the acoustic signature of a ghost. She was pale, her eyes clouded with a curious dimness, as if she were staring at a misty something raised by her own internal collapse. 

“Cheer up, Rosanna!” I said. “You mustn’t fret over your own fancies. I have got something to say to you from Mr. Franklin.”

I put the matter before her in the friendliest, most comforting words I could find. Though my principles regarding the other sex are severe, my practice is often softer when faced with their grief. 

“Mr. Franklin is very kind and considerate. Please to thank him.”

That was all the answer she made. Her native dialect, the subtle vocal inflections that marked her personal identity and history, had been entirely erased. She suffered a kind of language death in that moment, responding with a chilling, sterile conformity. 

“Are you quite sure, Rosanna, that you understand me?” I asked.

“Quite sure.” She echoed me, not like a living woman, but like a creature moved by machinery. She went on sweeping. I took the broom away as gently as I could.

“Come, come, my girl!” I said, “this is not like yourself. You have got something on your mind. I’m your friend. Make a clean breast of it, Rosanna!”

The time had been when my speaking to her in that way would have brought tears. I could see no change in her now. 

“Yes,” she said, her voice devoid of all resonance. “I’ll make a clean breast of it.”

“To my lady?” I asked.

“No.”

“To Mr. Franklin?”

“Yes; to Mr. Franklin.”

She was in no condition to understand the caution against speaking to him in private. Feeling my way, I told her Mr. Franklin had gone out for a walk. 

“It doesn’t matter,” she answered. “I shan’t trouble Mr. Franklin, today.”

“Why not speak to my lady?” I said. “The way to relieve your mind is to speak to the merciful and Christian mistress who has always been kind to you.”

She looked at me for a moment with a grave and steady attention. The vast gulf between her subjective agony and the words available to express it was starkly apparent. Then she took the broom from my hands and moved off slowly.

“No,” she said, speaking to herself. “I know a better way of relieving my mind than that.”

“What is it?”

“Please to let me go on with my work.”

Penelope offered to help her, but she answered, “No. I want to do my work. Thank you, Penelope.” She looked round at me. “Thank you, Mr. Betteredge.”

There was no moving her. We left her as we had found her, sweeping the corridor, trapped in an untranslatable sorrow.

“This is a matter for the doctor to look into,” I said. “It’s beyond me.”

Penelope reminded me of Mr. Candy’s illness from the chill he caught the night of the dinner-party. His assistant, Mr. Ezra Jennings, was available, but we disliked and distrusted him. Other doctors at Frizinghall were strangers, and Penelope doubted whether strangers might not do Rosanna more harm than good. 

I thought of speaking to my lady, but remembering her heavy weight of anxiety, I hesitated to add this new trouble. Still, the girl’s state was downright alarming. Unwilling enough, I went to her sitting-room. No one was there. My lady was shut up with Miss Rachel. 

I waited in vain till the clock on the front staircase struck the quarter to two. Five minutes afterwards, I heard a voice call my name from the drive outside. It was a jarring, industrialized sound that cut ruthlessly through the natural ambiance of the old house. I knew the voice directly. Sergeant Cuff had returned from Frizinghall.
