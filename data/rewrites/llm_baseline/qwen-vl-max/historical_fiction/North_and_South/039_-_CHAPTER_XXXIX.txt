“Nay, I have done; you get no more of me;
And I am glad, yea glad with all my heart,
That thus so clearly I myself am free.”

Margaret shut herself into her room after leaving Mrs. Thornton. She paced back and forth, as she often did when agitated—but then paused, remembering how thin the walls were in this house, where every footstep echoed through the halls. She sat down instead, waiting until she heard Mrs. Thornton’s footsteps fade away. Then, slowly, she retraced their conversation, word by word, sentence by sentence, forcing her mind to recall each exchange with precision.

When she had finished, she rose and whispered aloud, her voice low and heavy with sorrow: “At least her words do not touch me. They fall away from me, like dust. I am innocent of the motives she accuses me of. But still—it is painful to think that any woman could believe such things about another so readily. It is hard, and sad. Where I’ve truly erred, she doesn’t accuse me—she doesn’t even know. He never told her. I should have known he wouldn’t.”

She lifted her chin, a quiet pride stirring within her at the thought of Mr. Thornton’s restraint, his refusal to speak ill of her. Then, suddenly, a new idea struck her—sharp and startling—and she pressed her hands together tightly. “He must think poor Frederick is some lover,” she murmured, flushing at the very word. “I see it now. It’s not only that he knows I lied—he believes someone else cares for me. And that I—Oh dear! Oh dear! What shall I do? What does it mean? Why should I care what he thinks, beyond losing his respect for my honesty? I cannot say. But I am miserable. How unhappy this year has been! I feel as if I’ve passed from childhood straight into old age. No youth, no womanhood—no future. The hopes of womanhood are closed to me. I shall never marry. Yet I carry the weight of sorrows as though I were already an old woman, weeping over a life that was never mine.”

She sighed, weary from the constant demand upon her strength. “I can bear up for Papa—that is duty, natural and pious. I could even resent Mrs. Thornton’s unjust suspicions. But it is unbearable to think how completely he misunderstands me. What has made me so sensitive today? I don’t know. Only I cannot help it. I must give way sometimes. No—I won’t,” she said, rising abruptly. “I will not. I will not dwell on myself or my position. I will not examine my feelings. It would serve no purpose. One day, perhaps, if I live to be old, I may sit by the fire, watching the embers, and see the life I might have lived.”

All the while, she was dressing quickly, preparing to go out. She stopped only to wipe her eyes, annoyed at the tears that kept coming despite her resolve. “There must be many women who make mistakes like mine—and only realize too late. And how proudly I spoke to him that day! But I didn’t know then. It crept upon me little by little. I don’t even know when it began. Now I won’t give way. I’ll find it hard to act naturally toward him, knowing this terrible truth; but I will be calm, quiet, and speak little. Still—perhaps I won’t see him at all. He avoids us plainly. That would be worse than anything. And yet—no wonder he stays away. If he believes what he must about me…”

She stepped outside, walking swiftly toward the countryside, trying to outrun her thoughts with the speed of her steps.

When she returned, her father met her at the door. “Good girl!” he said. “You’ve been to Mrs. Boucher’s. I meant to go myself before dinner.”

“No, Papa,” Margaret replied, blushing. “I haven’t. I didn’t think of her. But I’ll go right after dinner—while you’re napping.”

She kept her promise. Mrs. Boucher was seriously ill—not merely unwell, but near death. The kind neighbor who had visited earlier seemed to have taken charge. Some of the children had been sent to relatives. Mary Higgins had come to collect the youngest at lunchtime, and Nicholas had gone for the doctor. He had not returned. Mrs. Boucher was dying, and there was nothing left to do but wait.

Margaret decided she would like to hear the doctor’s opinion, but in the meantime, she thought she might visit the Higginses. Perhaps she could learn whether Nicholas had spoken with Mr. Thornton.

She found Nicholas occupied with spinning a penny on the dresser, amusing three small children who clung to him fearlessly. Both he and the children smiled at a long spin. Margaret noted the warmth in his face—the simple joy of the moment—and saw it as a good sign.

When the penny stopped, one child began to cry. “Come to me,” Margaret said, lifting the boy into her arms. She held her watch to his ear as she asked Nicholas, “Have you seen Mr. Thornton?”

His expression changed instantly. “Ay,” he said. “I’ve seen and heard enough of him.”

“He refused you?” Margaret asked, saddened.

“To be sure. I knew he would. There’s no mercy to expect from masters like him. You’re a stranger, a foreigner—you don’t know their ways. But I did.”

“I’m sorry I asked you to go. Was he angry? Did he speak to you like Hamper did?”

“He wasn’t over-civil,” Nicholas said, spinning the penny again, more for himself than the children. “Never you fret. I’m where I was. I’ll tramp tomorrow. I gave him as good as I got. I told him I wouldn’t hold him in high regard if I’d come a second time of my own accord. But you advised me, and I was obliged to you.”

“You told him I sent you?”

“I don’t know if I called you by name. Probably not. I said a woman who didn’t know better had urged me to come and see if there was a soft place in his heart.”

“And he—?”

“Said I should tell you to mind your own business. That’s the longest spin yet, lads. And those were civil words compared to what he said to me. But never mind. We’re where we were. I’ll break stones on the road before I let these little ones starve.”

Margaret gently placed the struggling boy back on the dresser. “I’m sorry I asked you to go. I’m disappointed in him.”

A sudden sound came from behind her. Both she and Nicholas turned—Mr. Thornton stood there, his face showing surprise and displeasure.

Without hesitation, Margaret stepped forward, bowing low to hide the pallor that swept over her. He bowed in return, then closed the door behind her.

As she hurried toward Mrs. Boucher’s, she heard the sharp clang of the latch—and it felt like the final blow to her humiliation. He too was disturbed to find her there.

He had tenderness in his heart—a soft place, as Nicholas had called it—but he guarded it fiercely. It was sacred to him, hidden from the world. Yet he also valued justice above all. And now, he realized, he had been unjust. A man had waited five hours—humble, patient, desperate—to speak to him. He had not given him five hours, but he had spent two of his own precious hours investigating the truth of Higgins’s story. He had learned that everything Higgins said was true. And then, something deeper stirred—the patience, the selfless concern for others. He forgot reason, logic, fairness. He was moved by instinct.

He came to offer Higgins work. But finding Margaret there was worse than hearing her last words. For then he understood—she was the woman who had urged Higgins to come. And he feared admitting that her influence had shaped his decision. He had acted out of justice alone. He could not let her think otherwise.

“So that was the lady you called a woman?” he asked, sharply, turning to Higgins.

“You might have told me who she was.”

“And then maybe you’d have spoken of her more kindly. You might have had a mother to keep your tongue in check when talking about women being the root of all plagues.”

“Of course you told Miss Hale?”

“In course I did. Or at least I reckon I did. I told her not to meddle again in anything concerning you.”

“Whose children are those—yours?”

Mr. Thornton already guessed. But he hesitated, awkwardly shifting the conversation.

“They’re not mine, and they are mine.”

“The children you mentioned this morning?”

“When you said,” Higgins replied, turning with barely concealed anger, “that my story might be true, or might not, but it was a very unlikely one. Measter, I haven’t forgotten.”

Thornton was silent for a moment. Then he said, “Neither have I. I remember what I said. I spoke to you about those children in a way I had no right to. I didn’t believe you. I couldn’t have cared for another man’s children if he had treated me as Boucher treated you. But I know now you spoke truth. I beg your pardon.”

Higgins did not turn. But when he finally spoke, his voice softened, though the words remained rough. “You’ve no business prying into what happened between Boucher and me. He’s dead, and I’m sorry. That’s enough.”

“So it is. Will you take work with me? That’s why I came.”

Higgins wavered, then steadied. He refused to answer.

Thornton did not press. His gaze fell on the children.

“You called me impudent, a liar, a mischief-maker. You might have said, with some truth, that I’ve been given to drink. And I’ve called you a tyrant, a bull dog, a cruel master. That’s where it stands. But for the children, Measter, do you think we can ever get on together?”

“Well,” Thornton said, half-laughing, “it wasn’t my proposal that we go together. But there’s one comfort—on your own admission, neither of us can think much worse of the other than we do now.”

“That’s true,” Higgins said, thoughtful. “I’ve been thinking since I saw you. It’s a mercy you didn’t take me on. I never saw a man I could less abide. But maybe that was hasty judgment. Work’s work for such as me. So, Measter, I’ll come. And more than that—I thank you. That’s a deal from me,” he added, turning fully to face Thornton for the first time.

“And this is a deal from me,” Thornton said, gripping Higgins’s hand firmly. “Now, mind you come sharp to your time. I’ll have no laggards at my mill. Fines are strictly enforced. And the first time I catch you making mischief, off you go. Now you know where you stand.”

“You spoke of my wisdom this morning. I reckon I may bring it with me. Or would you rather have me ‘bout my brains?”

“‘Bout your brains—if you use them for meddling with my business. With your brains—if you keep to your own.”

“I’ll need a lot of brains to know where my business ends and yours begins.”

“Your business hasn’t begun yet. Mine stands still for me. Good afternoon.”

Just before Thornton reached Mrs. Boucher’s door, Margaret emerged. She didn’t see him. He followed her for several yards, admiring her light step and graceful figure. Then, suddenly, the admiration curdled into jealousy. He wanted to overtake her, to speak to her, to see how she would react now that she knew he was aware of another attachment. He wanted her to know he had reversed his decision, that he had repented his harshness—and that he had done it because of her advice.

He caught up to her. She started.

“Miss Hale,” he said, “you were somewhat premature in expressing your disappointment. I have taken Higgins on.”

“I am glad of it,” she replied coldly.

“He tells me he repeated to you what I said this morning about—” Thornton hesitated.

Margaret took up the thread. “About women not meddling. You had every right to express your opinion. It was perfectly correct, I’m sure. But”—she spoke a little more urgently—“Higgins did not quite tell you the exact truth.”

The word “truth” brought her own deception rushing back. She stopped, uncomfortable.

Thornton was puzzled at first—then remembered her lie, and everything that followed.

“The exact truth?” he said. “Very few people speak it. I’ve given up hoping for it. Miss Hale, do you have no explanation to give me? You must see what I cannot help but think.”

Margaret remained silent. She wondered if any explanation would betray Frederick.

“No,” he said, “I will ask no further. I may be tempting you. For now, believe me—your secret is safe with me. But you risk much by being so indiscreet. I speak only as a friend of your father’s. If I had any other hope or thought—then, of course, it is at an end. I am entirely disinterested.”

“I am aware of that,” she said, forcing her voice to sound indifferent. “I know what I must seem to you. But the secret belongs to another. I cannot explain without harming him.”

“I have no desire to pry into the gentleman’s affairs,” he said, growing angry. “My interest in you is purely as a friend. You may not believe me, Miss Hale—but it is true. Even though I once threatened persecution—that is all past. All over. Do you believe me?”

“Yes,” she said quietly, sadly.

“Then, really, I don’t see any reason to walk together. I thought you might have something to say—but I see we are nothing to each other. If you’re convinced that any foolish passion on my part is entirely over, I wish you good afternoon.”

He walked off quickly.

“What could he mean?” Margaret thought. “Why speak as if I believed he cared for me—when I know he doesn’t? He cannot. His mother must have told him those cruel things. But I won’t care for him. I am mistress of myself. I must control this strange, wild feeling that nearly made me betray Frederick—just to regain his approval. Come, little heart—be brave. We’ll be a great deal to one another, if we are cast adrift and left alone.”

Her father was startled by her cheerfulness that morning. She talked incessantly, pushing her humor to an unusual pitch. Though some of her remarks carried a bitter edge, and her stories of Harley Street were tinged with sarcasm, he refrained from checking her—glad to see her shake off her burdens.

Later, she was summoned to speak with Mary Higgins. When she returned, her father thought he saw traces of tears on her cheeks. But no—she brought good news: Higgins had secured work at Mr. Thornton’s mill.

Her spirits sank, however. She found it difficult to keep up the pretense, let alone the liveliness she had shown earlier.

For days, her mood fluctuated wildly. Her father began to worry—until news arrived from several sources that promised change.

Mr. Hale received a letter from Mr. Bell, offering to visit. He imagined the company of his old Oxford friend would delight both himself and Margaret.

But Margaret had no interest. She was too listless to care, even if Mr. Bell were twice her godfather.

Then came a letter from Edith—full of sympathy for her aunt’s death, details of her own life, and a hopeful note at the end: the climate didn’t suit the baby, and Mrs. Shaw might return to England. Captain Lennox might sell out. They might move back into the old Harley Street house—though it would seem incomplete without Margaret.

She longed for that house, for its quiet order, its peaceful monotony. Once, she had found it dull. Now, after months of turmoil, she craved stillness—even stagnation—as a form of rest.

So she began to look forward to a long visit with the Lennoxes—not with hope, but with a sense of relief. A time, perhaps, to recover her composure, to regain control.

For now, every subject led back to Mr. Thornton. Whether visiting the Higginses, reading with her father, or hearing of Mr. Bell’s plans—his name always surfaced. Even the new lease negotiations between Bell and Thornton brought him into the conversation.
