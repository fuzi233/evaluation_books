"The most beautiful crime I ever committed," Flambeau would later confess, "was also, by a singular coincidence, my last. It was a Christmas affair. As an artist, I had always sought to align my transgressions with the specific seasons or landscapes in which I found myself, treating each setting like a canvas for a statue. To swindle a squire, I chose a long room paneled with oak; to fleece a Jew, the opulent lights of the Café Riche. To strip a dean of his riches, I needed the green lawns and grey towers of a cathedral town. Similarly, in France, draining a wicked peasant required the solemn plains of Gaul and a backdrop of clipped poplars.

"Well, my final crime was a Christmas piece—a cheery, cosy, middle-class tragedy straight from Charles Dickens. I performed it in a stately home near Putney, complete with a crescent drive, a stable, iron gates bearing a name, and a monkey tree. I think my mimicry of Dickens's style was dexterous, though I repented the same evening."

Flambeau would then recount the story from within the crime itself; viewed from the inside, it remains peculiar, yet to the outsider, it appears perfectly incomprehensible. The drama began when the front doors of the house with the stable opened onto the garden, and a young girl emerged to feed the birds on Boxing Day. She possessed a pretty face with brave brown eyes, but her figure was obscured by heavy brown furs, making her resemble a small, toddling bear.

The winter afternoon was reddening toward evening, casting a ruby light over the flower beds, filling them with the ghosts of dead roses. To one side stood the stable; to the other, an alley of laurels led to the rear gardens. After scattering bread for the birds—the fourth or fifth time that day, as the dog consumed it—the young lady moved unobtrusively down the laurel path into a glimmering plantation of evergreens. Here, she exclaimed with genuine wonder, gazing upward at the high garden wall where a fantastical figure appeared to be striding.

"Oh, don't jump, Mr. Crook!" she cried, alarmed. "It's far too high!"

Riding the party wall like an aerial horse was a tall, angular young man with dark hair standing upright like a bristle brush. His features were intelligent and distinguished, yet his complexion was sallow and alien, accentuated by an aggressive red tie—the only element of his attire he seemed to cherish. He ignored her warning, leaped like a grasshopper to the ground beside her, landing safely despite the precarious height.

"I think I was meant to be a burglar," he said placidly. "I should have succeeded if I hadn't been born in that nice house next door. I can't see any harm in it, anyhow."

"How can you say such things!" she remonstrated.

"Well," said the young man, "if you're born on the wrong side of the wall, I can't see why crossing it is wrong."

"I never know what you will say or do next," she replied.

"I don't often know myself," Crook answered. "But now I'm on the right side of the wall."

"And which is the right side?" she asked, smiling.

"Whichever side you stand on," Crook said.

As they walked together through the laurels toward the front garden, a motor horn sounded thrice. A vehicle of splendid speed and pale green elegance swept up to the doors like a bird, standing there with an engine throbbing.

"Hullo, hullo!" Crook laughed. "Here's somebody born on the right side, anyhow. I didn't know, Miss Adams, that your Santa Claus was so modern."

"Oh, that's my godfather, Sir Leopold Fischer. He always arrives on Boxing Day."

After a brief, somewhat tepid pause, Ruby Adams added, "He is very kind."

John Crook, a journalist, knew of the eminent City magnate, though the magnate knew little of him; certain articles in *The Clarion* or *The New Age* had treated Sir Leopold with austerity. Crook said nothing, merely watching grimly as the car unloaded. A large, neat chauffeur in green and a small manservant in grey deposited Sir Leopold on the doorstep and began to unwrap him like a fragile parcel. Rugs sufficient to stock a bazaar, furs of every beast, and scarves of every hue were removed until a human form was revealed: a friendly, foreign-looking old gentleman with a grey goat-like beard and a beaming smile, rubbing his massive fur gloves together.

Before this revelation concluded, the porch doors swung wide, and Colonel Adams emerged to welcome his guest. He was a tall, sunburnt, silent man wearing a red smoking-cap that gave him the appearance of an English Sirdar or Pasha in Egypt. Beside him stood his brother-in-law, recently arrived from Canada, a boisterous young gentleman-farmer named James Blount with a yellow beard. Also present was the priest from the neighboring Roman Church, Father Brown. Though seemingly insignificant—even his name was humble—the colonel found him companionable and frequently invited him to family gatherings.

Inside the vast entrance hall, ample space existed for Sir Leopold and his retinue. The grand fireplace crackled beneath the colonel's sword. The company, including the saturnine Crook, was introduced to Sir Leopold Fischer. The financier, still adjusting his well-lined attire, eventually produced a black oval case from a deep inner pocket, explaining it as his Christmas gift for his goddaughter. With an unaffected vanity that somehow charmed them, he opened it before the assembly. It flew open, dazzling them with brilliance. Inside, nestled in orange velvet, lay three white, vivid diamonds that seemed to ignite the surrounding air. Fischer stood beaming, soaking in the astonishment and ecstasy of the girl, the grim admiration of the colonel, and the wonder of the group.

"I'll put 'em back now, my dear," Fischer said, returning the case to his coat. "I had to be careful descending. They're the three great African diamonds called 'The Flying Stars' because they've been stolen so often. All the big criminals are hunting them, yet even rough men in the streets could hardly resist them. I might have lost them on the road. It was quite possible."

"Quite natural, I should say," growled the man in the red tie. "I shouldn't blame them if they'd taken them. When they ask for bread, and you don't even give them a stone, I think they might take the stone for themselves."

"I won't have you talking like that," cried the girl, glowing with indignation. "You've only spoken like that since you became a horrid... what's-his-name. You know what I mean. What do you call a man who wants to embrace the chimney-sweep?"

"A saint," said Father Brown.

"I think," Sir Leopold interjected with a supercilious smile, "that Ruby means a Socialist."

"A radical does not mean a man who lives on radishes," Crook remarked impatiently. "Nor does a Conservative mean a man who preserves jam. Nor, I assure you, does a Socialist mean a man who desires a social evening with the chimney-sweep. A Socialist means a man who wants all the chimneys swept and all the chimney-sweeps paid for."

"But who won't allow you," whispered the priest, "to own your own soot."

Crook looked at him with interest. "Does one want to own soot?"

"One might," Brown answered speculatively. "I've heard gardeners use it. And once, when the conjuror didn't arrive, I made six children happy at Christmas using nothing but soot applied externally."

"Oh, splendid!" cried Ruby. "Oh, I wish you'd do it to this company."

Amid Boisterous applause from the Canadian, James Blount, and astonished silence from the financier, a knock echoed at the double front doors. The priest opened them to reveal the garden, now gathering gloom against a violet sunset. Standing there was a dusty figure in a frayed coat, evidently a messenger. "Any of you gentlemen Mr. Blount?" he asked, holding forward a letter doubtfully.

Mr. Blount started, stopping his shout of assent. He ripped open the envelope with evident astonishment, his face clouding briefly before clearing. Turning to his brother-in-law, he announced, "I'm sick at being such a nuisance, Colonel, but would it upset you if an old acquaintance called on me tonight? In point of fact, it's Florian, that famous French acrobat and comic actor. I knew him years ago out West—he was a French-Canadian—and he seems to have business for me, though I hardly guess what."

"Of course," replied the Colonel casually. "Any friend of yours. He'll prove an acquisition."

"He'll black his face, if that's what you mean," Blount laughed. "I don't doubt he'd black everyone else's eyes. I'm not refined; I like the jolly old pantomime where a man sits on his top hat."

"Not on mine, please," said Sir Leopold with dignity.

"Well, well," observed Crook airily. "Don't let's quarrel. There are lower jokes than sitting on a top hat."
