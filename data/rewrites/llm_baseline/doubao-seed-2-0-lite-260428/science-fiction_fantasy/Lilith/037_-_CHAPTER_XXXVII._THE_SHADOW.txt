A soft, rippling murmur of delight from my companions tore me out of the fog of my own heavy grief: they had spied the rest of their scattered kin far across the meadow. The two riders mounted on Lona’s horse spurred ahead at once to meet them, and the distant group lifted their voices in a wavering, overjoyed shout that died sharp in their throats before it could fully carry. As we drew nearer, the sound of their sobs drifted to us, thin and fragile, like tiny foaming billows breaking against a cold stone shore.

When I stepped into their circle, I saw some unnameable, terrible harm had befallen them. No ordinary grief could have carved that gaunt, hollow haggardness across their childish faces, as if something had gnawed at the edges of their very souls. A handful of them drifted slowly toward me, arms outstretched to take Lona’s weight from my own. I yielded her up to them, and the tender, hopeless softness of the smile they gave as they received her made my throat tighten, pity swelling in my chest even as my own heart felt hollowed out. They wept over their mother-queen in vain; they whispered over and over for her to wake, to recognize their love, to speak a single word back to them, but nothing came. They kissed her hands, stroked her hair, and bore her along as gently as they could, as many as could fit tucking their arms under her body to cradle her, the rest pressing in close around the bearers to walk beside her. They laid her down in a patch of thick, soft, sun-warmed grass, and every last one of the Little Ones huddled around her, sobbing quiet, shaking little sobs.

The elephants stood just outside the crowd of small bodies, and I lingered near them, staring past the tops of dozens of small heads to fix my gaze on Lona’s still face. The two Little Ones closest to me caught sight of the chained princess a little ways off, and stared at her, trembling where they stood. Odu was the first to speak, his voice barely a breath.
“I’ve seen that woman before,” he whispered to the friend at his side. “That’s the one that fought the white leopardess the night all that yelling woke us up!”
“Silly,” his friend huffed back. “That was a wild beast, all covered in spots!”
“Look at her eyes,” Odu insisted. The other took a cautious step forward, and Odu wrenched him back hard, shrinking away even as he seemed magnetized to the hate-crazed, longing look in her gaze. “Don’t get close! She’d gobble you up in a heartbeat! That’s her shadow that chased us, isn’t it? She’s the wicked princess!”
“That can’t be right,” the other protested. “Everyone said the princess was beautiful.”
“It is her, exactly as you say,” I cut in. “Wickedness has warped her until no trace of the beauty she once bore remains.”
She heard me, and the venom that flashed across her face made my skin prickle.
“I… it was very wrong of me to run away,” Odu said suddenly, quiet and thoughtful.
“Why did you run?” I asked him. “I expected to find you right where I left you.”
He didn’t answer at once. “I don’t know what made me run,” another Little One piped up behind him. “I was just… scared, all at once.”
“It was a man that came down the hill from the palace,” a third added.
“What about him frightened you?”
“I don’t know.”
“He wasn’t a man,” Odu corrected. “He was a shadow. He had no thickness to him at all.”
“Tell me every detail you can about him.”
“He came down the hill jet black, walking like a bad giant, but spread flat across the grass, no height, no form, nothing but pure blackness. We were scared the second we saw him, but we didn’t run at first. We stood still and watched him, and he kept coming closer like he meant to walk right over the top of us. Before he got to where we stood, he started spreading, and spreading, growing bigger and bigger until his edges vanished over the horizon, and we couldn’t see where he ended. And then he was upon us.”
“What do you mean, he was upon you?”
“He was all that blackness seeping between every one of us, so we couldn’t see each other at all. And then he was inside us.”
“How did you know he was inside you?”
“I didn’t feel like me anymore. I didn’t feel like Odu. I wanted to tear Sozo to pieces— not the real me, not deep down, but some ugly other thing in my chest made me want to.” He spun around and hugged Sozo tight, sobbing into his friend’s shoulder. “It wasn’t me, Sozo! Deep down it was always Odu, loving you! The real Odu fought back, and knocked the naughty thing away. I felt sick, like I’d have to kill myself to claw all that black out of my bones. Then I heard a horrible laugh that had heard every ugly thought the shadow put in my head, and it made the whole air tremble around me. Next thing I knew I was running, as fast as my legs could carry me, and all the rest were running too. I wanted to stop, but I didn’t even think of stopping until I was all the way out the gate, out in the tall grass. That was when the real Odu came back, and I knew I’d run from something that tried to steal my skin and pretend it was me. It was the shadow that hated you, Sozo, not me! And I know now I never should have run away! But I didn’t even know what I was doing until it was done. My legs did it, I think—they got scared, forgot they belonged to me, and just ran off. Naughty legs! There, and there!” He gave each of his calves a playful, indignant kick to scold them.
“Where did the shadow go after that?” I asked.
“I don’t know,” he said. “I expect he went back home, to the dark of the night where the moon never shines.”

I felt a sudden, sharp ache of longing for Lona’s spirit, wondering where it had fled to. I dropped to the grass, lifted her still-warm, slack body into my lap, and whispered right against her ear. “Where are you, Lona? I love you.” Her lips didn’t stir. They weren’t quite cold yet, and I kissed them gently before laying her back down on the soft grass. I posted a small, trusted guard over her, then rose to organize the camp’s safety for the coming night. Before the sun sank below the treeline, I set a separate watch over the chained princess at the edge of the camp, and positioned sentinels all around the perimeter. I told the rest of our company they could rest easy, I would walk the patrol the whole night through. They collapsed onto the grass and fell asleep in seconds, heavy, dreamless sleep claiming them at once.

When the silver moon climbed high enough to spill pale light across the meadow, I caught a flash of bright white fur through the dark: it was the white leopardess. She glided silent as a ghost around the entire sleeping camp, and I watched her slip back and forth three times, moving between the chained princess and the huddled, sleeping Little Ones, a wordless, living barrier between the two groups. At that, I dismissed the rest of the watch to lie down and rest with their kin, then stretched myself out right beside Lona’s body, to keep vigil through the long dark.
