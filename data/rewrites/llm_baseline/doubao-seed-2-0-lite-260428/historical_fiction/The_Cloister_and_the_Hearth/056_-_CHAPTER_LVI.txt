Gerard settled into a humble, sun-warmed garret lodging on the west bank of the Tiber, its small window framing a distant glint of the river through tangled laundry lines strung between terracotta rooftops. Each dawn he set out with a rolled specimen of his script tucked under his arm, calling at every scribe and manuscript shop in the city he could track down.
The reception was uniformly cold. “Our lettering runs far slimmer than this,” one tradesman sniffed, prodding his work with a grubby finger. “Your ink is far too heavy and dark,” another complained. But the refrain he heard most often stung worst of all: “What use is fine Latin here these days? Can you not write Greek?”
“Ay, but not half so well as I can turn Latin script,” Gerard admitted.
“Then you will never earn a crust in Rome.”
Undeterred, he borrowed a precious, beautifully illuminated Greek manuscript from a collector at a steep price, trundling home with a gaping hole in his purse but not a flicker of diminished courage. In a fortnight he had mastered the Greek character far beyond the standard the local shops turned out, and thereafter arranged his days to grind at the new script until noon, then hunt for commissions the rest of the afternoon. But when he returned to the traders with Greek specimens finer than any they had on their own shelves, they only shrugged: Greek and Latin alike were unsaleable now, the city was flooded with imported manuscripts from every corner of Europe. He ought to have come the year before.
Not ready to surrender, Gerard bought a small psaltery to teach himself lay songs for spare coin, and began practicing the art of colouring playing cards to eke out a little side income. His kind Siennese landlady, who had taken a liking to his mild manners and fair, gold-streaked hair, stopped by his rooms one evening, pressed him to dine at her own table, and gently pried the story of his misfortunes out of him. She laughed and shook her head at his naivety.
“Those sly booksellers,” she declared, “keep their own underpaid scribes on payroll, sell their work to nobles for a king’s ransom, and pay the poor wretches pennies. Of course they blow cold on you. Your script is far too good for them. I have seen it, you know—you never lock your door like that surly Florentine Pietro across the hall, and women are curious by nature. Your fine work would make their shoddy overpriced manuscripts look like garbage, and ruin their grift.”
Gerard sighed. “Alas, dame, you think the worst of folk, and you so frank and kind yourself.”
“My dear boy, the Romans are a devious lot. *I* am Siennese, praise the Virgin, and we do not lie for sport. You think leaving Augsburg was your mistake? Pah! Augsburg is a hick town no one of consequence in Rome has ever heard of. You will make your fortune here in spite of those rogue booksellers. Everyone knows our great nobles have been bitten by the writing bug for years, they pour out gold like water to fill their chests with fine copied manuscripts, and it has even cut down on the faction stabbings and the old custom of roasting their enemies’ livers for supper. Cardinal Bassarion and the Holy Father himself alone could keep a dozen scribes like you working night and day. I will speak to my maid Teresa—she hears every scrap of court gossip, and will dig up names of patrons who are actually looking for fine work.”
The next day she returned to him with a list of five highborn signors all obsessed with manuscript collecting. Gerard copied out new, flawless specimens, left a copy with his name and address at each noble’s door, and waited hopefully.
Weeks passed. No replies came. Day after day slipped by, leaving him heartsick, and by a cruel twist of fate this was the exact same stretch of time that Margaret, hundreds of miles away in Rotterdam, fought tooth and nail to keep their household fed, only to be arrested by the guild for healing the sick without a license, rather than bleeding them or maiming them according to the approved rules of the surgeons.
Ruin stared Gerard full in the face. He cut his meals down to one a day to stretch his last remaining coins thin, but his sharp-eyed landlady caught him at once. She rounded on him, brusque but gentle, scolding him for being a fool to try to outrun poverty on an empty stomach. “Patienza, my lad! Times will mend. Meantime I will feed you for the love of heaven, no payment required.”
Gerard protested that he could not let an honest woman lose her due on his account, but she brushed him off and began to tell him of his reclusive neighbour Pietro. The Florentine painter had arrived in Rome some months prior with a full purse and a half-finished masterpiece, rented an unfurnished room across the hall from Gerard, and furnished it neatly. When he finished his painting, he had turned down every offer for it so disdainfully he had turned all his would-be patrons into enemies. Month by month he had sold every stick of furniture, and now he owned only one set of clothes, and slept at night on a great oak chest. He locked his door every time he stepped out, too proud to let anyone see his poverty.
Gerard’s heart ached. The man’s fate was his own. He tried to waylay Pietro in the hall to offer help, but the painter always slipped past him, until one afternoon he heard a low groan drift through the wall. He knocked, and a surly voice bade him enter.
The room was all but bare: one rickety chair, an iron basin, an easel, a great oak chest, and a massive large painting propped against the far wall, face turned inward. Coiled on the chest was a haggard young man with dark, sour features and glittering, sharp eyes, who looked for all the world like a cobra coiled to strike the first unwary comer.
“Good Signor Pietro,” Gerard said softly, “forgive me for intruding on your solitude. I am your nearest neighbour, and methinks your brother in misfortune. I too am an artist.”
Pietro jumped off the chest, brightening at once, and waved him to sit on the chest. “You are a painter? Welcome.”
Gerard smiled and shook his head. “I am a caligrapher, I copy Greek and Latin manuscripts for hire. I dabbled a little in paint in my youth, but I am no master of the craft.”
Pietro’s face fell a little. “A caligrapher? That is no art, that is only copying the thoughts of other men. An artist thinks, and paints his own thought onto the panel.”
Gerard laughed gently. “You are not wrong. But a scribe can preserve the words of the great ancients, the pure reason no brush could ever paint, the thoughts of God no angel could render. Let that pass, though. I wanted to befriend you, for we are both fallen on hard times here.”
At that moment the landlady’s step sounded on the stair. “Ah, the good dame brings my dinner!” Gerard called. “Hostess! I am here visiting Signor Pietro, I will eat my meal in his company.”
She set the smoking, savoury ragout down on the chest without a word and withdrew. Gerard dipped his spoon in, then paused. “I never eat well alone. For Our Lady’s sake, share this with me, there is more than enough for two.”
Pietro’s glittering eyes locked on his. What he saw was not kindness, but the insult of patronizing alms. He reached out, seized the dish, and hurled the entire contents out the garret window. He turned back, shaking with rage and mortification. “Let that teach you to offer charity to an artist you do not know, master scribe.”
Gerard’s face flushed hot, and he had to fight hard to resist boxing the proud fool’s ears. He thought of his mother, who had taught him never to waste good food, and pity softened his anger. “That was no alms,” he said coldly. “I offered you half my dinner, and you threw the whole thing away. You had every right to destroy your own share, but mine you stole. Pride is a fine thing, but justice is finer.”
Pietro stared, then hung his head. “I took you for a fool, transparent in your pity. Forgive me. The world has soured me, I hate all men. Go now, and leave me to my misery.”
But Gerard stopped at the door. “We Dutchmen are hard bargainers. We would shave an egg to save a penny. For my lost dinner, I claim a forfeit: let me look at your painting, the one you keep turned to the wall.”
Pietro went white. “No. Do not ask that. I have sworn a solemn vow to drive my dagger through the next man who flouts my painting, my whole heart and soul made manifest on panel. If you mock it, I will kill you.”
“I will say nothing unkind,” Gerard promised. “I will not mock it. Let me see it.”
Pietro hesitated, then turned the great canvas to face the room, dragging it into the slant golden Roman afternoon light. He coiled back onto his chest, hand resting on the hilt of his stiletto, and waited.
Gerard stood silent. He stepped close, studied the brushstrokes, then backed all the way across the tiny room to take in the whole composition. For half an hour he moved slowly back and forth, never speaking a word.
At last Pietro snapped, irritable and impatient: “Well? Have you not a single word to say?”
Gerard started, and drew his short sword from his belt. Pietro jumped up with a shriek, convinced he meant to stab the painting. “Calm yourself,” Gerard laughed, pointing the tip of his blade at the painter instead of the canvas. “I am only defending myself against your stiletto, so we may have a fair fight if you take against my criticism. Now sit down. First: your oil paint recipes are far inferior to those used by the Van Eycks in Flanders, and I will teach you every secret of their craft if you will let me. But that is a small thing. You Italians are the sons of the sun—we Flemings have colour, but you have imagination. You have poured your whole immortal soul onto this panel. The drapery of the Virgin is a little stiff and short, true, but the rest? That vast sprawling landscape, forty miles wide, where every distant tree and cottage fades so gently into haze you feel you could walk straight into it, the floating cloud of cherub faces around the holy pair, that deep purple sunset sky hanging over the whole wide world? It makes every tiny cramped landscape I ever painted or admired look like cheap dross. In Ghent or Bruges, or even Rotterdam, patrons would tear this painting out of your hands for a fortune. You are no starving fool, Pietro Vanucci. You are a king of your craft. Viva il maestro! Long live the master!”
Pietro burst into tears, and threw his arms around Gerard’s neck. Everyone had called his work a madman’s useless dream, he sobbed, but Gerard had seen it. “I was a monster to throw your dinner away! I will never hate mankind again, not now that I have found a friend who sees what I made. Come, let us go and order the finest supper in the whole quarter. We will bring the painting with us, and make it our third guest.”
Gerard laughed, and followed him out into the golden Roman dusk, and for the first time in months, neither man felt his poverty weigh heavy at all.
