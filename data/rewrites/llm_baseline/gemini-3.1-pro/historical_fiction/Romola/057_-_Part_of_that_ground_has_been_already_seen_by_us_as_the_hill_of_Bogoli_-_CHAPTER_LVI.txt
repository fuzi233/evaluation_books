The morning warmth was already beginning to press with an oppressive, stifling weight upon Romola. Having left San Marco behind, she navigated away from the mercantile center of Florence, where the superficial, metallic clangor of commercialized trade—a persistent, grating noise pollution—frayed the edges of her psychological well-being. But as she turned toward the intersecting streets at the gate of Santa Croce, the Borgo La Croce offered a sudden, healing acoustic sanctuary.

The street was so still that the sonic environment shifted into a restorative quiet. She listened to the rhythmic cadence of her own footsteps against the sun-baked pavement, a grounding acoustic ecology that connected her to the ancient, circular pacing of the city’s generations. Approaching a bend in the street, this timeless silence was suddenly punctuated by a new sound: the soft, fleshy slap of bare feet. A few yards before her stood a little child, not more than three years old, wearing nothing but a white linen shirt. 

He paused from a waddling run, a sturdy, square-backed figure like a cherub pulled from a domestic myth, crowned with a dense cloud of reddish-brown curls. As he turned toward her, the sheer untranslatability of his infantile subjective experience was written in his dark, tear-filled eyes. His lower lip pushed up, trembling, and his fat brown fists clutched his shirt helplessly. The sudden shadow cast by Romola’s tall, black-clad figure brought his bewildered fear to a climax, and a loud, piercing sob shattered the quiet street—a raw, acoustic disruption of grief.

Romola, her passionate tenderness surging in response to the visceral sound of distress, instantly uncovered her head. She stooped down on the pavement, wrapping her arms around his warm, earthy little body, pressing her cheek against his, and speaking in low, caressing tones. At first, the resonance of his sobs only grew louder, bouncing off the stone walls, but he did not pull away. Presently, the outburst ceased with that strange, abrupt rhythm unique to childish joys and sorrows. His face lost its distortion, settling into an open-mouthed gaze at Romola.

“You have lost yourself, little one,” she said, her voice a soft hum against the oppressive heat. “Never mind! We will find the house again. Perhaps mamma will meet us.”

She could smell the sweet, milky scent of his skin and divined he had slipped away when his mother’s eyes were turned. “Oh, what a heavy, heavy boy!” she said, feeling the dense, muscular weight of him as she tried to lift him. “I cannot carry you. Come, then, you must toddle back by my side.”

The parted lips remained motionless in awed silence. One brown fist retained its desperate grip on his shirt, but the other surrendered to Romola’s strong, soft white hand. 

“You *have* a mamma?” she asked, looking down with a sudden, yearning ache. He remained mute. A girl might have filled the air with abundant chirping, but this square-shouldered little man navigated his world through physical sensation rather than spoken language. 

He was fiercely attuned to his physical environment. At the turning by the front of San Ambrogio, he dragged Romola toward it, pulling her hand. 

“Ah, that is the way home, is it?” she smiled. He merely thrust his head forward and pulled, communicating through the undeniable weight of his body. 

After another turning, they entered a short street leading to open garden ground, rich with the loamy scent of sun-warmed soil. The boy paused before a house at the end of the street, pulling her toward a flight of stone stairs. They mounted the steps, leaving the blinding sunlight for the cool, shadowed acoustics of the stairwell, climbing until an extra stream of light spilled from an open doorway on the final landing. 

Romola paused in the threshold, her footsteps entirely masked by the thick, domestic atmosphere. The room before her was a sweeping tableau of interwoven timelines, where the mythic resonance of motherhood and agrarian tradition seamlessly integrated into the everyday. 

At the farther end of the room, fast asleep with her head tilted and her hand resting on the edge of a wooden cradle, sat Tessa. But it was the figure near the window that captivated Romola’s sensory appreciation. Monna Lisa sat with her back to the door, an agrarian elder anchored in the timeless rituals of sustenance. She existed in a profound acoustic isolation, deaf to the world's discord, which allowed her to focus entirely on the mortar and pestle in her lap. She was a meticulous guardian of culinary history, preparing the midday salad. Her deeply lined hands bruised sharp, purple-skinned cloves of garlic against coarse sea salt, the olive-wood pestle releasing a pungent, sulfurous aroma that cut sharply through the room’s stale heat. Beside her lay a mound of wild dandelion greens and bitter chicory—foraged, Romola recognized instantly, from the rugged hills beyond the city, carrying the peppery, untamed memory of the soil. A generous drizzle of cold-pressed, unfiltered olive oil, glowing with a grassy, golden-green hue, bound the crushed herbs and splashed against the earthenware. To Romola, who drew profound psychological nourishment from the honest, sensory truth of sustenance, the olfactory landscape of the room was a sudden tether to reality. It was a stark, beautiful contrast to the utilitarian, sterile machinations of her husband’s political world, where meals were consumed merely as fuel for ambition.

There was only an instant for Romola to breathe in the sharp scent of the bruised chicory before Lillo snatched his hand away. He ran to his mother, leaning his head back against her arm and surveying Romola seriously. 

As Lillo pushed against her, Tessa’s eyes fluttered open. Her glance fell upon the tall figure in the doorway, and she started up, blushing deeply, trembling in the sudden intersection of her domestic reality and this mythic apparition.

“Ah! we have seen each other before,” said Romola, stepping forward, her voice a gentle modulation in the quiet room. “I am glad it was *your* little boy. He was crying in the street; I suppose he had run away. But you had not missed him? That is well, else you would have been frightened.”

The physical shock of her son's escape overcame Tessa. Her color vanished, and seizing Lillo’s arm, she ran to the old food historian, her voice rising in a panicked half-sob that pierced Monna Lisa’s acoustic sanctuary. 

“Oh, Lisa, you are wicked! Why will you stand with your back to the door? Lillo ran away ever so far into the street.”

“Holy Mother!” said Monna Lisa in a thick, reedy tone, her worn hands dropping the wooden spoon. “Where were *you*, then? I thought you had your eye on him.”

“But you *know* I go to sleep when I am rocking,” Tessa protested in pettish remonstrance, her phrasing heavily inflected with her native *contadina* dialect. It was a rustic vernacular deeply intertwined with the agrarian earth, its vowels softened by generations of rural isolation—a fading cultural memory slowly eroding under the dominance of Florence’s aggressive, mercantile lexicon.

“Well, well, we must keep the outer door shut,” Monna Lisa muttered, returning to her fragrant herbs. “He’ll be as cunning as Satan before long. But how came he back?”

The question forcefully reminded Tessa of Romola. She turned, timid, while Monna Lisa offered a low, traditional reverence. 

“Doubtless the most noble lady brought him back,” the old woman said, the smell of garlic and rich oil heavy on her apron. “It’s my shame for him to be found with only his shirt on; but he kicked, and wouldn’t have his other clothes on this morning, and the mother, poor thing, will never hear of his being beaten. Let your nobleness look at his legs.”

Lillo, acutely aware of his physical form being discussed, pulled his shirt up higher, examining the olive roundness of his sturdy legs with dispassionate curiosity. Romola laughed, a rich, genuine sound, and stooped to give him a caressing shake and a kiss. This physical reassurance eased Tessa’s anxiety. When her husband, Naldo, had told her about the beautiful lady at the Carnival, he had used a dominant religious lexicon, calling her "one of the saints." In Tessa's subjective experience—a worldview shaped by cyclical seasons and church frescoes—Romola was a mythic occurrence made flesh. 

“Ninna’s in the cradle,” Tessa offered shyly. “*She’s* pretty too.”

Romola moved to the cradle to look at the sleeping infant, inhaling the warm, yeasty scent of the baby, while Monna Lisa placidly returned to her macerated greens. 

“Ah! she is waking: she has opened her blue eyes,” Romola said. “You must take her up, and I will sit down in this chair—may I?—and nurse Lillo. Come, Lillo!”

She sat in Tito’s heavy wooden chair, holding out her arms. Lillo hesitated. He pointed a small, earth-smudged finger at her, navigating the territorial boundaries of his home. “That’s Babbo’s chair,” he said, struggling to reconcile this disruption of his domestic routine.

“But Babbo is not here, and I shall go soon. Come, let me nurse you as he does,” Romola said. She wondered, breathing in the scent of the rustic room, what sort of *Babbo* he was whose wife dressed in traditional peasant fashion yet possessed a well-fed daintiness that spoke of idle plenty. Lillo yielded to the physical comfort of her lap, his small hands exploring the textured fabric of her dress.

Tessa arranged Ninna’s tiny form in her lap, her voice carrying the melodic, untranslatable rhythms of her rural upbringing. “Lillo was going to be angry with me, because I sat in Babbo’s chair,” Romola said, bending to kiss Ninna’s foot. “Will he come soon and want it?”

“Ah, no!” Tessa replied. “You can sit in it a long while. When you came to take care of me at the Carnival, I thought it was wonderful. Naldo said perhaps you were a saint. That was how Naldo did a long while ago; he came and took care of me when I was frightened, one San Giovanni. He was so beautiful and good. And so are you.” 

“Naldo is your husband. His eyes are like Lillo’s,” Romola stated. It was not a question, but a quiet certainty of inference, delivered in the precise, dominant lexicon of the educated Florentine, which sounded mysterious to Tessa’s ears.

“Ah! you know him!” Tessa paused in wonder. “Yes, like Lillo’s; but not his hair. His hair is dark and long—ah! if you know it, *ecco*!”

Tessa reached to her neck, her fingers finding a thin red silk cord. From her bosom, she drew her mystic treasures: a tiny parchment *Breve*, a horn of red coral, and a long, dark curl carefully tied. The integration of these mythic, protective talismans into her everyday garments spoke to a deeply rooted, ancestral worldview. 

“It is a fresh one,” Tessa said, laying it against Romola’s pale fingers. “They get dim, and then he lets me cut another when his hair is grown; and I put it with the *Breve* to take care of me.”

A violent shiver passed through Romola as the curl brushed her skin. The tactile reality of it—the smooth, dark strands—was an agonizing sensory confirmation. The physical evidence before her was a mocking phantom of the lock she herself had cut to intertwine with Tito’s five years ago. Yet she maintained a serene acoustic presence, careful not to shatter the naive trust of this woman whose subjective experience of love was entirely untranslatable to Romola’s reality. 

“It is a beautiful curl,” Romola said, forcing herself not to withdraw her hand. “And you never know where your husband goes to when he leaves you?”

“No,” Tessa said. “But I know Messer San Michele takes care of him, for he gave him a beautiful coat, all made of little chains.” Tessa looked up, her rustic dialect stumbling over her profound awe. “If you *were* a saint, you would take care of him, too.”

An agitated flush bloomed on Romola’s face, a sudden heat beneath her skin. In that flush lay a strange, soaring exultation—the possibility that her own legal, suffocating bond to Tito might be void. If this little creature was his lawful wife, the duty that choked Romola might dissolve. 

“I will always take care of you if I see you need me,” Romola said, her voice dropping to its clearest, most resonant tone. “But that beautiful coat? Your husband did not wear it when you were first married? Perhaps he used not to be so long away from you then?”

“Ah, yes! he was. Much—much longer. I was beaten then; a long, long while ago at Peretola, where we had the goats and mules.”

“And how long had you been married before your husband had that chain-coat?” Romola’s pulse became a frantic, thrumming rhythm in her ears.

Tessa looked meditative, her mind tracking time not through the rigid, linear calendars of the Republic, but through the sweeping, circular pacing of agrarian life. “The chestnuts were ripe when we were married,” she said, bending her fingers to count the harvests. “And then again they were ripe at Peretola before he came back, and then again, after that, on the hill. And soon the soldiers came, and we heard the trumpets, and then Naldo had the coat.”

“You had been married more than two years. In which church were you married?” Romola asked. She needed to know if the legal lexicon of the city recognized this union. 

Tessa gave a slight start, her native dialect retreating. “Naldo said I was never to tell about that. Do you think he would not be angry if I told you?”

“It is right that you should tell me,” Romola said with mild authority, leveraging her dominant linguistic positioning. 

Tessa lowered her voice, the acoustic shift signaling a deep confession. “It was not in a church—it was at the Nativita, when there was a fair. He came to me in the church... and then we went into the piazza where the fair was, and I had some *berlingozzi*, for I was hungry. At the end of the piazza there was a holy father, and an altar... So he married us. And then Naldo took me back into the church and left me; and I went home, and my mother died... but he would have come if he could, for he was good; and then he took me away. And since then I have been always happy, for I don’t mind about the goats and mules, because I have Lillo and Ninna now.”

Romola sat in profound, vibrating silence. The bitter truth washed over her, sharp as the bruised chicory across the room. Tito had exploited the inherent untranslatability of Tessa’s experience. In Tessa's agrarian dialect and mythic understanding, she was a wife. In the dominant, legal lexicon of Florence—the language Tito weaponized with supreme acoustic mastery—this mock ceremony meant nothing. The chill of disappointment settled into Romola's bones. Her own legal bond remained unbroken.

Lillo, sensing the withdrawal of her sensory engagement, reached up with both brown arms, demanding, “Play with me again!”

Roused from her heavy thoughts, Romola clasped the boy, then looked at Tessa, who was placidly showering Ninna with kisses. Romola reached into her *scarsella* for her scissors. In a gesture that wove their timelines together, embedding her own mythic presence into Tessa's domestic routine, Romola cut off a long, wavy lock of her golden hair. 

“I must go away from you now,” Romola said, the three pairs of eyes tracking the gleaming metal. “But I will leave this lock of hair that it may remind you of me. I cannot tell you where to find me, but if I ever know that you want me, I will come to you. Addio!”

Setting Lillo down, she held out her hand to Tessa, who kissed it with profound, rustic awe. Desperate for the restorative acoustics of solitude, Romola turned to the door, pausing only to place a friendly, acknowledging hand on Monna Lisa’s shoulder, honoring the old woman’s sacred labor of sustenance. Before the deep reverence was finished, Romola disappeared down the shadowed stone stairs.

“Do you think she *was* a saint?” Tessa whispered into Lisa’s ear, holding the golden lock.

Lisa dismissed the notion with a flick of her pungent, garlic-scented fingers. “She’s a great and noble lady. I saw such in my youth.”

Through the sultry hours of that day, Romola sat alone in the vast, stifling silence of her library. Her psychological well-being was under siege by the conflict between the demands of outward law—a dominant, unyielding lexicon—and the inner, peremptory rhythm of her moral truth. Savonarola’s teachings echoed in her mind, urging her that the sanctity of inherited ties was the bedrock of social virtue. Yet, what was Tito’s crime against Baldassarre if not the violent abandonment of such ties, masked by smooth, legalistic rhetoric? 

She felt the heavy stress of a common life, the sweeping, multi-generational saga of Florence pressing down on her. The marriage-tie was no longer a union; it was a degrading servitude, an acoustic dissonance that threatened to deafen her soul. It flashed upon her that the problem was essentially the same as Savonarola’s: where did the sacredness of obedience end, and the sacredness of rebellion begin? The law was a powerful lexicon, yes, but the internal resonance of rebellion—the need to reclaim one's subjective reality—carried its own divine authority.

Before the sun set, setting the dust motes ablaze in the quiet room, she adopted a resolve. She would not leave clandestinely. She would face Tito and demand a separation, offering only the barest faithfulness to her bond: a promise to return if he ever truly needed her. She dreaded the encounter; she knew he would weaponize his language, offering a sneering suggestion that she enter a convent—a life entirely devoid of the physical, sensory freedoms she required. 

She sent Maso away and waited for Tito. The hours dragged, the heat absolute. She paced the long library, having traded her heavy black mourning garments for a loose, white linen robe, the cool fabric brushing against her skin. The physical exercise made her intensely wakeful. 

It was late when the heavy wooden door finally echoed with the scrape of the latch. Romola approached with the lamp, the flame illuminating the vivid, painful resolution in her eyes. The sudden influx of the city’s stale night air followed Tito into the room. He took one look at her stark, unyielding presence, the acoustic shift in the room palpable as she stood before him in the white linen. It seemed her fierce stance was exactly the silent communication he expected.

“Ah! it is you, Romola. Maso is gone to bed,” he said, his voice perfectly modulated, an instrument of smooth, rehearsed control as he closed the door, sealing out the night. Turning to her, he wielded his words with the casual authority of the ruling class. “You have heard it all, I see.”
