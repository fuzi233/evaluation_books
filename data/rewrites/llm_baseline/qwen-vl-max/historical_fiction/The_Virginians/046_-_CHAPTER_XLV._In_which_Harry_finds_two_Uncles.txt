We have all, no doubt, seen enough of the world to know that human nature wears many faces—some noble, some absurd, some deeply sincere, and others merely performative. Yet there is one sort of person we often see mocked in novels and plays—the religious hypocrite: the man who preaches virtue with fervor but lives without conviction, whose sermons ring hollow even as he delivers them. Such figures are common enough in fiction, but I must confess I have met very few in real life. For though men may not be wholly devoted to their faith, they are rarely so utterly insincere as to pretend belief they do not possess.

Observe, for instance, the Sunday evening procession toward church—the clatter of hobnailed boots, the rustle of ribbons worn by maids, the orderly ranks of schoolboys, the well-dressed young ladies and matrons marching with polished prayer books, the chapel bell chiming gently in the distance. There, beneath the gaslight near Ebenezer’s door, a congregation gathers—umbrellas, bonnets, pattens—all assembled like a quiet army of piety. But what goes on within? The maid thinks of her sweetheart; the grocer calculates how to buy sugar and whether the bank will accept more of his paper; the head boy reviews Latin verses for Monday; the young rascal dreams of pie after service; the clerk leading the psalm worries about his daughter’s illness, half-mumbling the words without meaning them. And when the parson bows his head, perhaps he’s thinking only of the bill due tomorrow.

These people are not saints. They are of this world—worldly, distracted, burdened with daily cares. But they are not hypocrites. Their religion is stored away, like medicine in a drawer—used when needed, trusted when ill. A woman might say to her neighbor, “My dear, you’ve been seized with spasms? These drops will cure you!” Or to a man overindulging, “You’ve taken too much wine? This pill will save you.” And so it was with spiritual matters—women especially were eager dispensers of both remedies and counsel. A hundred years ago, every country lady had her still-room, her medicine chest, her powders and potions for every ailment in the village. My Lady Warrington, for example, managed not only the health of her tenants but their souls as well. She oversaw the servants’ moral welfare with the same care she gave their digestion. Whether her remedies worked—well, Heaven knows—but she administered them with full conviction, and those around her accepted them willingly.

She believed herself among the most virtuous, self-denying, wise, and learned women alive—and by constantly repeating this belief to others, she persuaded many to agree. At Sir Miles’s dinner table, where silver gleamed and liveried footmen stood at attention, it took a sharp eye to notice the smallness of the beer served from the splendid tankard or the single joint of mutton on the grand dish. When Sir Miles raised his glass to the King, he drank as if the wine were ambrosia. He asked Harry Warrington if Virginia had such port, then declared it nothing compared to what he’d taste in Norfolk. His praise was so vivid that Harry almost believed it himself, winking into his own glass, trying to catch the richness his uncle seemed to savor.

In this household, as in many families of the time, there were two ideal daughters. One, Flora, was the beauty—fair, graceful, admired by all. The other, Dora, was the genius, the angel—so described by Lady Warrington, who proudly told Harry that Flora was engaged to Tom Claypool, the son of a baronet in Norfolk. But Dora, the “angel,” was determined to reform Harry’s character. She sang to him at the harpsichord—though, Harry thought, rather off-key for an angel—and offered advice, instruction, and conversation—so much of it, indeed, that Harry longed instead for the company of little cousin Miles, who reminded him of Fanny Mountain back home.

After dinner, Miles usually retreated to his nursery. Flora would go to her duties. Mamma wrote letters or tended to the poor. Papa dozed in his chair. And Dora remained—her duty being to guide her young cousin. The quiet dignity of the house pleased Harry. It offered a refuge from the noise and excess of his usual life. No welcome could have been warmer. The doors were always open. If Flora was absent, Dora was ready. Soon, Harry and Miles had raced horses in the park, and Harry, generous by nature, began planning to buy his cousin a better mount than the one he rode—until events intervened that shattered his fortune.

Though Sir Miles imagined Virginia to be an island, the ladies were well-informed. They eagerly questioned Harry about his homeland. He, in turn, was glad to talk. He described his estate—its rivers, forests, fields, its tobacco ships sailing from wharves along the Potomac and James Rivers. He had once practiced surveying with a friend, and now drew a map of his county, marking towns that were really little more than log cabins—though he exaggerated their size and elegance, proud to represent his land honorably. As he pointed out hills and rivers, dressed in velvet and silver lace, he looked like a prince showing his realm. And sometimes, he almost felt like one.

Not only Dora listened—he saw Flora leaning forward, her eyes bright with interest. How could Tom Claypool, with his loud voice and five thousand acres, compare to this American gentleman, speaking of March, Chesterfield, Selwyn, Bolingbroke, and the whole glittering world of London fashion? Even Dora, the angel, began to lose her composure, teasing Flora for flirting. Claypool sat silent before Harry, whose ruffles and laces made him seem more brilliant than any English heir.

Lady Warrington grew more fond of Harry each day. She worried about the spiritual state of the Indians and enslaved people in Virginia. What could she do to help Madam Esmond, whom she called “a precious woman,” in her charitable work? Her butler and housekeeper were devout, and they adored Gumbo, the young black servant, for his sweet singing and pious ways.

“Oh, Harry, Harry!” she would sigh. “You’ve been such a wild boy! Why didn’t you come to us sooner? You’ve wasted your youth among fools and wastrels. But it’s not too late. We’ll reclaim you, dear Harry! Won’t we, Sir Miles? Won’t we, Dora? Won’t we, Flora?” The three lifted their eyes to the ceiling in solemn agreement. They would rescue the prodigal. But which of them would succeed?

Dora watched Flora. Mamma, when alone with Harry, spoke more tenderly, more seriously. She wanted to be a mother to him in the absence of his own admirable parent. She gave him a hymn book, kissed his forehead, and expressed pure love, tenderness, and religious concern for her wayward yet amiable nephew.

Meanwhile, Sir Miles kept silent about his affairs—affairs that had spiraled into ruin. Fortune, who had smiled upon him for weeks, abandoned him in a matter of days. Was Lord Castlewood, his kinsman, unfair? We won’t accuse him of dishonesty. But he had won every shilling Harry possessed—and would have taken his future inheritance, too, had Harry not thrown up his hands in defeat.

Remembering the last sum he’d set aside for poor Sampson, Harry risked it at the gaming table. But that, too, was lost. Fortune fled, leaving him nearly destitute.

Yet youth and generosity shielded him from despair. He could sell his horses, carriages, reduce his staff. Where could he turn for money? His Aunt Bernstein? His kinsman who had won so much from him? Or his kind Uncle Warrington and Aunt Warrington, who professed such love and virtue?

He would ask them later. But first, Sampson’s story of his landlord’s distress moved him. To raise funds quickly, Harry sold all his trinkets to a pawnbroker in St. Martin’s Lane.

That broker was connected to Mr. Sparks of Tavistock Street—the very man from whom Harry had once taken the trinkets meant for his friends at Oakhurst. Sparks visited his brother-tradesman soon after Harry’s sale. Recognizing the diamond-handled repeater, he exploded into furious remarks—words best left unsaid, given my reputation for plain speech.

A man who knows a pawnbroker has bailiffs among his acquaintances. And bailiffs have men who, under law, treat noblemen and dandies alike. The same officers who had arrested Lady Maria at Tunbridge now turned their attention to her cousin.

They learned from chattering Gumbo that Harry was at Sir Miles’s house in Hill Street. While Gumbo flirted with Mrs. Lambert’s maid next door, Mr. Costigan and his assistant waited in the shadows.

Never had Uncle Miles been more cordial. Never had Aunt Warrington been more gracious. Flora looked unusually radiant. Dora was exceptionally kind. At parting, Lady Warrington gave Harry both hands and called down blessings from above.

Papa, in his jovial mood, said: “Hang it, nephew! When I was your age, I’d have kissed two fine girls like Do and Flo long ago—even if they were my own flesh and blood! Don’t tell me! I should, my Lady Warrington! Odds-fish! ‘Tis the boy blushes, not the girls! He, he!”

“Papa!” cried the virgins.

“Sir Miles!” said the august mother at once.

“There, there!” said papa. “A kiss won’t hurt, nor tell tales. Will it, nephew Harry?”

And so, amid these brief exchanges, the harmless gesture occurred—Harry touched the cheeks of both cousins, blushing as he did.

Descending the stairs with his uncle, Lady Warrington delivered a final speech: “What noble qualities your cousin possesses! What wisdom mixed with simplicity, what fine manners—though I care little for worldly elegance. How terrible it would be to lose such a soul! We must save him, my darlings. We must take him from those wicked companions, those dreadful Castlewoods—not that I mean to speak ill of neighbors. But I shall pray, I shall hope, that he may be rescued from his evil ways!”

She gazed upward again, resolute. The girls watched the door through which Harry had just vanished.

His uncle walked with him to the gate. “God bless you, my boy!” he said warmly, pressing Harry’s hand and repeating his blessing.

As the door closed, the hall light illuminated Harry’s face. Two men stepped forward from across the street. One pulled a strip of paper from his pocket, placed a hand on Harry’s shoulder, and declared him prisoner.

A hackney coach waited. Poor Harry was led away—sleeping that night in Chancery Lane.

To think that a Virginian prince should be slapped by a ragged bailiff’s follower—that Madam Esmond’s son should end up in a spunging-house in Cursitor Street! I do not envy our young prodigal his rest that night.

Let us strike while he is down, my dear friends. Let us imagine the sting of remorse keeping him awake on his grim pillow, the drunken laughter of hardened inmates echoing from the room beside him, the shame, the rage, the humiliation. Let us feel no pity—for surely, you, my honest young gentlemen, have never indulged in extravagance, folly, or the bitter reckoning of regret.
