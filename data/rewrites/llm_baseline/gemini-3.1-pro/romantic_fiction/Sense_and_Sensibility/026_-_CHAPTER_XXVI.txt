Elinor sat in the carriage, enveloped in the heavy leather and oiled-wood scent of their conveyance, marveling at the cyclical nature of her own displacement. Generations of women had made such migrations toward the metropole, yet here she was, beginning a journey to London under the protection of Mrs. Jennings—a woman whose agrarian rhythms and robust dialect belonged to an older, more rooted earth. The carriage wheels battered against the rutted roads, a harsh, percussive rhythm that assaulted Elinor’s acoustic sensitivities, drowning out the softer, mythic whispers of the countryside they were leaving behind. Only days before, Elinor had harbored countless objections to this unsuited pairing of age and disposition. But these had all been overridden by the thoughtless, forward-driving fervor of youth which Marianne and her mother shared. 

Marianne now sat in silence, her subjective experience entirely untranslatable, wrapped in a hopeful ecstasy that left Elinor feeling profoundly hollow in comparison. To anchor herself, Elinor leaned into the role of companion to Mrs. Jennings. The elder woman, a fierce guardian of traditional domestic memory, treated them with immense kindness. Her only disturbance during the journey was a profound grief when the posting-inns denied them culinary sovereignty. Mrs. Jennings mourned the inability to source their own ingredients, lamenting the modern innkeepers who refused to let travelers select their own dinners. She spoke passionately of the delicate, rich flakiness of fresh salmon compared to the heavier, salted cod, and the tender, herbaceous comfort of boiled fowls over the utilitarian, hastily fried veal cutlets. Elinor listened closely, finding solace in this precise, ancestral language of sustenance.

They reached town by three o’clock on the third day, trading the agrarian quiet for the oppressive, industrial roar of London. The clatter of iron-shod hooves on cobblestones created a sonic pollution that immediately weighed upon Elinor’s spirit. The house, however, offered a handsome sanctuary, smelling richly of polished mahogany, beeswax, and the faint, mineral tang of coal fires. They were shown to Charlotte’s former apartment, where a landscape in colored silks—a superficial, commercialized piece of schoolgirl art—hung over the mantelpiece, a mute testament to the erosion of authentic cultural expression in favor of fashionable metropolitan schooling.

With a meticulously planned dinner still two hours away, Elinor sat at the writing desk, the scratch of her nib against the vellum a small, grounding sound in the vast house. Marianne soon joined her.

“*I* am writing home, Marianne,” Elinor said, the dominant lexicon of polite inquiry masking her deeper probe. “Had not you better defer your letter for a day or two?”

“I am *not* going to write to my mother,” Marianne replied hastily. Her words were sharp, a dialect of defensive secrecy meant to shut down further inquiry. 

Elinor fell silent. The unspoken power dynamics of their conversation shifted. If Marianne was writing to Willoughby, they must be secretly engaged. Marianne folded her note with feverish rapidity, sealing it with a heavy drop of wax that smelled of melting pine resin and soot. Elinor glimpsed the large *W* in the direction before Marianne rang the bell, dispatching the footman to the two-penny post. 

As evening descended, the acoustic ecology of the drawing-room grew fraught. The copper tea urn hissed softly, releasing the earthy, malted aroma of black tea leaves steeping in boiling water, but Marianne could find no meaning in the sustenance before her. She refused the dinner earlier and now ignored the tea service, treating the meals as an irrelevance rather than a source of physical grounding. Her tragic detachment from the sensory world of the table was a glaring symptom of her inner turmoil. She listened only to the street.

The distant, rhythmic rumble of carriages teased her, but suddenly, a loud, jarring rap struck the front door—a violent, percussive boom that violated the room's quiet and demanded immediate attention. Elinor felt certain it was Willoughby’s approach.

Marianne sprang up, opening the door and advancing toward the stairs. For a half-minute, she stood suspended in time, listening. The untranslatable ecstasy in her chest finally burst into the dominant language of the room. “Oh, Elinor, it is Willoughby, indeed it is!”

She seemed ready to throw herself forward, but it was Colonel Brandon who appeared on the landing.

The shock shattered Marianne’s reality. Unable to articulate her profound grief in any known lexicon, she immediately fled the room. Elinor, though disappointed herself, felt a deep pang of empathy for the Colonel. His quiet approach was met only by Marianne's wordless rejection, and Brandon observed her flight with such raw astonishment that he momentarily forgot the demands of civility.

“Is your sister ill?” he asked, his voice a low, resonant murmur in the echoing room.

Elinor, bound by the restrictive lexicon of societal propriety, attributed Marianne's behavior to headaches, low spirits, and over-fatigue. Brandon listened earnestly, then, seeming to recollect himself, shifted the conversation to the journey. They spoke in muted tones, a quiet, melancholic exchange that barely penetrated the heavy silence of the room. When Elinor asked if he had been in London long, his slight embarrassment and mention of a brief trip to Delaford brought back the memory of his sudden departure from Barton, and the uneasiness it had caused.

The door swung open, and Mrs. Jennings entered, her vocalizations loud, robust, and completely at odds with Brandon’s quietude. “Oh! Colonel, I am monstrous glad to see you!” she announced, her booming voice a stark contrast to the room's previous fragility. She launched into a detailed monologue about settling her provisions and dealing with Cartwright. She spoke with the earthy pragmatism of an elder who understood the profound necessity of domestic order, demanding to know how he had conjured out her arrival. 

“I had the pleasure of hearing it at Mr. Palmer’s, where I have been dining,” he replied gently.

“Oh, you did; well, and how do they all do at their house? How does Charlotte do? I warrant you she is a fine size by this time.”

Brandon confirmed Mrs. Palmer was well and would visit tomorrow. Mrs. Jennings pressed on, interrogating him about his business and Willoughby with her usual noisy cheerfulness. Brandon replied with his customary mildness, yielding to her dominant sonic presence without satisfying her curiosity. Elinor poured the tea, the dark, tannin-rich liquid filling the porcelain cups, and Marianne was forced to return, though she remained a silent, ghostly presence. The Colonel soon took his leave, and the women retired early.

The next morning brought a cyclical renewal of hope for Marianne. They had scarcely finished a breakfast of buttered toast and freshly brewed coffee—a rich olfactory landscape Marianne again largely ignored—when Mrs. Palmer’s barouche arrived. Mrs. Palmer swept in, her laughter a continuous, causeless trill that disrupted the morning’s peace. She was delighted to see them, angry they had not stayed with her, and bursting with superficial anecdotes. 

After an hour of exhausting chatter, she insisted they accompany her to Bond Street. The expedition to the shops was a descent into modern commercialization. The streets were an acoustic nightmare of grinding carriage wheels and the relentless hum of industrial commerce. Marianne, though physically present, was entirely abstracted. She was adrift, searching the chaotic sea of faces. She found no joy in the sensory richness of the textiles, vexed by Mrs. Palmer’s tedious, superficial consumption—dawdling in indecision over expensive, unnecessary trinkets, wild to buy everything yet appreciating the craftsmanship of nothing.

When they finally returned to the heavy quiet of the house, Marianne flew upstairs. Elinor followed, finding her sister turning from the mahogany table, her face a portrait of despair.

“Has no letter been left here for me since we went out?” Marianne asked the footman, who was laden with Mrs. Palmer's commercial spoils.

He answered in the negative.

“Are you quite sure of it? Are you certain that no servant, no porter has left any letter or note?”

The man confirmed none had.

“How very odd!” she whispered, her voice stripped of its native vitality, a profound untranslatability marking her quiet disappointment as she turned to the window.

“How odd, indeed,” Elinor echoed internally, regarding her sister with deep uneasiness. If he were in town, it was a profound violation of their shared language of affection that he had not written. The subtle, mysterious power dynamics of this engagement troubled Elinor deeply. She resolved to intervene and write to their mother if this silence persisted many days longer.

That evening, Mrs. Palmer and two elderly ladies of Mrs. Jennings’s intimate acquaintance dined with them. The meal was a historically rich, meticulously prepared testament to Mrs. Jennings's agrarian roots, but as Mrs. Palmer departed for her evening engagements and Elinor was obligated to make up a whist table, Marianne remained entirely disconnected from both the sustenance and the company. The evening stretched on. Marianne attempted to read, but soon threw the book aside, choosing instead to pace the floor. Her footsteps became a rhythmic, restless drumbeat against the floorboards, pausing only at the window, straining through the ambient noise of the city in desperate hopes of distinguishing the violent, percussive rap of the long-expected knocker.
