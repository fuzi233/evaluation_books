I pressed onward, my gaze fixed on the moon. She hung low, staring blindly into the canopy, a battered disc of tarnished copper, dim and weary. No cloud shielded her; the stars burned too fiercely for her pale countenance. "Will this endure forever?" she seemed to whisper, her light flickering in protest. She marched one way, I another, yet our paths wove together through the woods for miles. We shared little conversation, my eyes glued to the earth, but her desolation was a weight I felt without needing to see it. I was the living shadow; she was the dull, fading shine.

Then, beneath the sprawling boughs of a great tree, something white arrested my attention. Drawing near, the vague shape resolved into a human form. "Another skeleton," I murmured, dropping to my knees. Yet, as my hand closed over it, I realized it was flesh, though barely. It lay on its side, radiating a chill that was not the cold of stone, but the terrifying absence of life. The closer I peered, the more certain I became: she was dead. For a fleeting, bewildered instant, I imagined her a wild dancer, a ghostly Cinderella lost in this strange, outdoor night, perished before she could return home. She was naked, her skin so wasted that every rib stood out like a wire beneath a thin elastic casing. Her teeth, beautiful yet terrible, gleamed ghastly through retracted lips, and her hair cascaded in thick, fine strands, black as the void.

She was the frame of a tall, likely graceful woman. How had she arrived here? Not of her own volition, certainly not in such a state of ruin. Strength must have failed her; she had fallen and starved. But how could she be so emaciated? And why was she stripped bare? Which savages had taken her clothes, or what beasts had devoured them? The fact that her body remained was not so strange.

I rose, trembling with a mix of revulsion and duty. I could not leave her exposed. Natural reverence demanded it; even the garment of a woman commands respect. To leave her uncovered was to invite irreverent eyes and brutal claws. Years might pass before the rains washed her into the soil, but the ground here was hard, locked tight with interlacing roots. I had only my bare hands.

At first glance, death seemed recent—no signs of decay marred her. But what was left of her to decay? Could she still be alive? In this alien world, logic often bent. Even if she were, revival was unlikely, yet I needed certainty before I could bury her.

Recalling a cluster of ripe grapes I had spotted in the forest-hall's doorway, I remembered the juice I had carried. Perhaps it could revive her. It was all I possessed. Her mouth hung slightly open, though her head lay at an awkward angle. Sliding my arm under her shoulder, I discovered the pine needles beneath were warm. She could not have been dead long; she *might* still be breathing, though I felt no heartbeat and saw no rise of her chest. One of her hands was clenched tightly around something small. I forced a grape into her mouth, but there was no swallow.

Desperation drove me to act. I spread a thick bed of pine needles and dry leaves, draped my own garment—still warm from my body—over it, and lifted her onto this makeshift cradle. I covered her completely, trapping whatever faint warmth remained within, hoping the returning sun would amplify it. Another grape yielded no result; her mouth and throat remained still.

"Doubt," I told myself, "is a poor excuse for inaction." Her skin was so tight against her bones that friction was dangerous. I crawled into the leafy heap, embracing her as closely as I dared. I had little heat left, but I would give it all to her. Thus I spent the remaining night, sleepless and yearning for dawn. Her cold seeped into me, but none of my warmth returned.

If I had fled from the beautiful sleepers in their silver couches to lie alone with such a bedfellow, I would have refused a lovely privilege for an awful duty. Beneath the slow-setting moon, I lay with the dead, guarding for the light.

As the darkness receded and the eastern horizon grew dim, I noticed a subtle undulation near the ground—a large snake gliding in a straight line. Soon, a roe doe and her fawn appeared, followed by bear-cubs leading smaller ones. The light intensified until a troop of horses trotted past. Though no larger than Shetland ponies, they moved with the perfect form and gait of full-grown beasts, representing every breed from cart-horse to charger. Dwarf cattle and small elephants followed in their wake.

"Why are the children not here!" I cried out. "The moment I am free of this burden, I must return for them!"

Where were these creatures bound? Was this an exodus or a morning ritual? I waited for the sun. Until he rose, I could not abandon her. Placing my hand on her body, I noted it felt marginally warmer. Had it absorbed some of my lost heat? Or had it generated its own? Hope had not diminished, but it had not grown either.

The day's forehead began to glow, and soon the sun peered up, as if witnessing the stir of this new world for the first time. At his innocent brilliance, I rose, feeling a surge of life against death. Removing the handkerchief I had used to shield her face from the pine needles, I scanned her features anxiously. Had I found a jewel or merely its empty case?

Her face lay motionless, drawn and hollow, every bone sharp beneath the skin. Every tooth cut through her lips. Her garment was worn to its threads, yet the bird of heaven might still nestle within, waiting to sing. The sun beat down on her face. I rearranged the handkerchief, laid a few leaves lightly over her, and finally set out to follow the creatures.

Their trail was well-worn, merging with others from the sides, broadening into a path. As I walked, the trees retreated and the grass thickened. Soon the forest vanished, replaced by a vast expanse of lush green stretching to the horizon. A small river flowed along the forest's edge, and the track led to it. Seeing the water sparked a new, undefined hope. The stream looked deep and full, yet narrow, rising in a bluish mist that vanished as it ascended. On the opposite bank, small animals fed in the plentiful grass. They must sleep in the forest and swim across at dawn. I knelt to drink, but the water was scalding hot and tasted of strange metal.

I leaped up. Here was the warmth I sought—the first necessity of life! I raced back to my helpless charge.

No one who has not faced such solitude can understand the redemption I felt in saving this woman. "Prove what she may," I thought, "I shall at least no longer be alone!" I had found myself such poor company that now I truly understood hope. This blessed water would expel the cold death and drown my desolation.

I carried her to the stream. She was tall yet miraculously light; her bones were so delicate she felt like a sleeping child. I was so hopeful that I feared moving her too roughly. I laid her on the bank and dipped my garment into the hot water, beginning to bathe her pitiful form. So wasted she was that, save for the black abundance of her hair, I could not guess her age. Her eyelids were just barely open, a crack in the clouds of night through which no sun shone, making her look all the more dead.

The longer I bathed her, the less hope I held that she would ever regain strength or lift those lids. Still, I worked continuously, ensuring no part cooled while I attended to another. Gradually, the body warmed. Finally, I ventured to submerge her. I entered the stream, drawing her in while keeping her face above the water, letting the swift current wash over her. I noted that despite the heat, her clenched hand never relaxed.

After ten minutes, I lifted her out, dried her, and covered her with leaves. I ran to the forest for more, then for a third and fourth load.

Now I could leave her to explore for shelter. Running upstream toward rocky hills I had spotted, I found the river bursting forth from a rock at the base of one hill. It seemed to have tumbled down a stair inside, eager to escape, finding its door only at the foot. The opening was too small for the torrent, so I crept through into a small cave. Inside, the water rose quietly from the ground like the base of a column, running along a deep, narrow channel.

I saw my chance. If I could find fallen boughs long enough to span the channel, sturdy enough to bear weight without bending, I could build a couch upon them, cushioned with smaller branches and leaves, kept constantly warm by the stream below. I ran back to check on my charge.

She lay exactly as I had left her. The heat had not revived her, but it had not extinguished hope. I gathered boulders from the channel, arranging them at her feet and flanks. Returning to the wood, I quickly found suitable beech boughs, their dry yellow leaves still clinging. I laid a floor of bridge-bed over the torrent, crossing it with smaller branches, interlacing twigs, and burying everything deep in leaves and moss.

When I had finally constructed a warm, dry, soft couch, I carried her body to it. It was so light I sometimes feared, as I placed her down, that I would find a skeleton. Laying her gently on the pathless bridge, the relief of parting with that fear was greater than the relief of setting her down. I covered her with a thick layer of leaves and tried feeding her again with a grape. To my joy, I could open her mouth slightly wider. The grape lay unheeded, but I hoped the juice might trickle down.

After an hour or two, she was no longer cold. The brook's warmth had penetrated her frame—truly, just a frame—and she was warm to the touch. Not the warmth of life, perhaps, but a warmth that made life possible if she were alive. I had read of one in a trance lying motionless for weeks!

Day after day, night after night, for seven long cycles, I sat or lay, waking and sleeping, but always watching. Every morning I bathed in the hot stream, feeling refreshed as if I had eaten and drunk, giving me the courage to lay her in it daily. Once, a shadow of discoloration on her left side sent a terrible shock through me, but by the next morning it had vanished. I continued the treatment, placing a fresh grape in her mouth each morning after her bath.

I ate the grapes and berries I found in the forest, believing that with my daily baths in that river, I could survive without food entirely.

Every time I slept, I dreamed of a wounded angel, unable to fly, staying with me until she loved me and refused to leave. Every time I woke, I saw instead the white, motionless, wasted face upon the couch. Even Adam, when first seeing Eve asleep, could not have looked more anxiously for her awakening than I did for this woman's. Adam knew nothing of himself, perhaps nothing of his need for another self; I, an alien among my kind, had learned to love what I had lost. If this one shred of womanhood disappeared, I would have nothing but a consuming hunger for life. I forgot even the Little Ones; things were not amiss with them. Here lay someone who might wake, open eyes, and look upon me!

Now I truly knew solitude—the gaze upon one who saw and heard nothing, moved and spoke nothing. I saw that a man alone is merely a being who *can* become a man, a need, a possibility. To be sufficient for himself, a being must be an eternal, self-existent worm! Man is so superbly constituted, so simply complex, rising from lower organisms and spiritual structures, that no atmosphere less divine than that offered by other souls can comfort or nourish his life. Nowhere but in other lives can he breathe. Only by the reflection of other lives can he ripen his specialty, develop the idea of himself, the individuality that distinguishes him. Were all men alike, each would still possess individuality through personal consciousness, but there would be little reason for more than two or three such beings. For the development of the differences that create a large and lofty unity, making millions into a church, endless influence and reaction are indispensable. A man to be perfect—complete, having reached the spiritual condition of persistent and universal growth, inheriting the infinitude of his Father—must have the education of a world of fellow-men. Save for the hope of life's dawn in the form beside me, I would have fled to the beasts that grazed and did not speak. Better to wander with them than to live alone! But with the faintest prospect of a woman as my friend, I, poorest of creatures, was yet a possible man.
