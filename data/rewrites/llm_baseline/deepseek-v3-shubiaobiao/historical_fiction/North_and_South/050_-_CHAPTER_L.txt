Milton throbbed like a restless beast, its chimneys belching smoke, its factories roaring with the ceaseless pulse of machinery. Iron and steam labored without thought, yet their relentless rhythm was matched only by the tireless workers—men driven by purpose, though what they sought remained uncertain. The streets bore no idlers; every face was etched with haste or worry. News was snatched up with a hunger near-violent, and men jostled in the Exchange as they did in life, each guarding his own interest.

A shadow hung over the town. Buyers were few, and sellers eyed them with suspicion, for trust had grown brittle. The tremors of financial ruin in distant ports had reached Milton, and though no house had yet fallen, whispers coiled through the streets like smoke—*Who next? How deep will the wound cut?*

"Thornton stands firm," some murmured. "His mind is shrewd, his risks calculated."

Yet in hushed corners, dissent crept in. "Aye, but his capital is sunk in new machines, in cotton bought dear. A man may stretch too far."

John Thornton himself felt the vise tightening. Pride had been his armor—pride in the name he had built from nothing, in the belief that commerce rewarded the bold and upright. His mother’s voice echoed in memory: *"Her merchants be like princes."* But now, the world he had commanded seemed to slip from his grasp.

Strange, how perspective shifted. Once, he had dreamed of influence in far-flung markets, his word as good as gold in foreign ports. Yet now, it was the faces of his own workers—men like Higgins—that haunted him. The strike had been a wound, but from it had come something unexpected: recognition. Master and man had glimpsed the humanity in one another, and Thornton could no longer dismiss their struggles as abstract.

But sentiment did not pay debts. Orders had dwindled; his stock’s value halved. Bills loomed, money grew scarce, and creditors circled. Still, he refused despair. By day, he moved through the mill with grim focus; by night, he pored over ledgers, seeking escape from the snare.

Higgins watched him. "Th’ measter’s burdened," he observed one evening, hearing Thornton’s sharp question to an overlooker, the weary sigh that followed. Later, Higgins and another stayed unseen, mending what had been neglected. Let the master think it the overlooker’s work. Small kindnesses cost little.

Yet kindness could not stem the tide. When Thornton confessed his straits to his mother, her face hardened—not at the loss of wealth, but at the injustice. "My son, just and true, yet fortune turns her back," she said, her voice trembling. "Where is the right in it?"

He shook his head. "I must bear what comes."

The final blow fell softly. His brother-in-law, Watson, had gambled on a risky venture—one Thornton had refused—and won spectacularly. Now Watson’s name rang through the Exchange, while Thornton faced the unthinkable: surrendering his mill, his home, the life he had built.

"Will you take a partner’s offer?" his mother pressed.

"No." His jaw set. "I will not kneel to a man who sees only profit."

And so the fall came. The machinery stilled; the great doors of Marlborough Mills closed. Thornton walked away, his head high, his heart heavy. The world had turned, and he with it—down, down, downee.

Yet in the wreckage, one truth remained: he would not risk another’s livelihood to save himself. Let others chase the specter of wealth. He would find his own path, though it led through shadow.

And somewhere, far from Milton, Margaret Hale moved through her own life, untouched by his ruin. The thought of her—unmarried still, as Higgins had revealed—was a fragile comfort, a golden thread in the dark.

But threads, however bright, could not mend what was broken.
