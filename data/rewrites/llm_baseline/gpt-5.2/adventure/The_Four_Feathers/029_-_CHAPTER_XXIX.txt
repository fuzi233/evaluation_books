“Three more days.”

They had said it like a prayer, like the last crumb saved in a palm-leaf packet, and sleep took them with the words still warm on their tongues. But when the dawn’s first thin light slid into the zareeba, Trench woke with his skin burning and his mouth dry as old flour. By noon his mind was running ahead of itself, stumbling over shadows, and Feversham—who had leaned on Trench’s strength for so long—found his own hands suddenly enlisted in the homely, unheroic labor of keeping a man alive.

It was not chance that struck Trench down at the very moment the world had begun to open. The House of Stone had seasoned him for three years the way heat seasons a pot—hardening, cracking, leaving a taste you cannot scrub out. Yet it was joy, the sharp, unlooked-for surge of it, that finished what hunger and vermin had begun. The prospect of escape was the last stress on a body already worn as thin as parchment.

“In a few days he will be well,” Feversham insisted, as though saying it could make it true. “It is nothing.”

Ibrahim shook his head, and the beads of his worry clicked softly as he moved—one of the few clean sounds in that place. “It is Umm Sabbah,” he said. The name went through the enclosure like a fly’s buzz: the typhus that rose from foul stone and stale breath, and took men on the seventh day.

Feversham would not believe it. He repeated, “It is nothing,” with a stubbornness that tasted of panic. But another thought, sharp as vinegar, would not leave him: Will the men with the camels wait?

Each day he went down to the Nile and saw Abou Fatma posted as always in his blue robe, the folds hanging as neat as a cook’s apron despite dust and heat. Each day Abou Fatma made his sign. Each day Feversham gave none, and walked back as though he had not seen him. The river’s small, constant noises—the lap of water against mud, the creak of a rope on wood, the low talk of men—felt like a world with its own laws, indifferent to fever.

With Ibrahim’s help he nursed Trench. The boy who brought food came daily, and Feversham sent him out to buy tamarinds, dates, bitter roots—anything Ibrahim said would cool the blood. Ibrahim worked like a careful kitchen man, crushing and soaking and straining, mixing soot-black water with tart pulp until the draughts were thin and sour and lifesaving. They carried Trench from shade to shade as the sun moved across the zareeba, as though shifting a pot away from a too-hot fire.

By giving a little more help to Idris’s starving family, Feversham got the forty-pound chains removed from Trench. It was bargaining of the plainest kind, the sort that happens over grain and salt: a little mercy purchased, not granted. Trench was fed vegetable marrow soaked in salt water; butter was packed into his mouth to keep it from cracking. His body was anointed—oil rubbed in as one rubs fat into a dry skin—and wrapped close in camel-cloths until he lay swaddled like stored food against spoilage.

The fever took its course. On the seventh day Ibrahim said, in a voice as flat as a chopping block, “This is the last. To-night he will die.”

“No,” Feversham answered at once. “That is impossible.” In his mind words rose that did not belong to this place at all—beneath the trees he knew—like a tune remembered in a foreign street. “Not here. No.”

He was not thinking now of Abou Fatma waiting by the river, nor of camels, nor of the third feather and the fourth and all the reckonings beyond Omdurman. The fear that Trench would die in that enclosure had pushed everything else aside. He remembered instead the first night, the awkward words he had spoken to Trench in the corner under the open sky, when the two of them had been strangers forced into brotherhood by chains.

“No,” he said again. “He must not die here.”

Through that day and the night that followed, he watched the long hard battle between life and death. Sometimes the three years of the House of Stone seemed to be winning; sometimes Trench’s wiry frame and stubborn constitution rose up, like yeast in dough, refusing to lie flat. That night, at least, they held. The seventh day passed. The struggle was prolonged.

Even Ibrahim began to hope. On the thirteenth day Trench slept and did not ramble; and when he woke his eyes were clear. He found himself alone, wrapped so tightly in camel-cloths he could not stir. The day’s fiercest heat had gone, and the shadow of the House of Stone lay black upon the sand like a lid.

He lay wondering, lazily, how long he had been ill. While he wondered, noises swelled outside: the gaolers shouting, prisoners crying, a commotion running toward the river like a sudden wind through reeds. The gate opened; prisoners flocked in, jostling and panting. Feversham was among them. He came straight to Trench’s corner and dropped down beside him with the heaviness of a man who has carried sacks all day.

“Thank God!” he said. “I would not have left you, but I was compelled. We have been unloading boats all day.”

“How long have I lain ill?” Trench asked.

“Thirteen days.”

Trench’s gaze drifted for a moment as though watching smoke. “It will be a month before I can travel. You must go, Feversham. You must leave me here and go while you still can. Perhaps when you come to Assouan you can do something for me. I could not move at present. You will go to-morrow?”

“No,” Feversham said. “I should not go without you in any case.” He hesitated, then let the truth fall. “As it is, it is too late.”

“Too late?” The words took time to settle. Trench wished only to lie quiet in the cool of sunset, as though stillness could be kept like water in a jar. Then the meaning came, slow and heavy. “Too late? Then the man in the blue gown has gone?”

“Yes. He spoke to me yesterday by the river. The camel men would wait no longer. They were afraid of detection, and meant to return whether we went with them or not.”

“You should have gone with them,” Trench said, without bitterness. In that moment he did not care whether he lived and died in prison, so long as he was allowed to lie still. “So our one chance is lost.”

“No, deferred,” Feversham answered. “The man who watched by the river brought me paper, a pen, and some wood-soot mixed with water. He dropped them by my side as I lay on the ground. I hid them beneath my jibbeh, and last night—there was a moon last night—I wrote to a Greek merchant who keeps a café at Wadi Halfa. I gave him the letter this afternoon, and he has gone. He will deliver it and receive money. In six months, in a year at the latest, he will be back in Omdurman.”

“Very likely,” Trench muttered. “He will ask for another letter, so that he may receive more money, and again he will say that in six months or a year he will be back in Omdurman. I know these people.”

“You do not know Abou Fatma,” Feversham said, and for once his voice had the ring of certainty. “He was Gordon’s servant before Khartum fell; he has been mine since. He came with me to Obak and waited there while I went down to Berber. He risked his life in coming to Omdurman at all. Within six months he will be back.”

Trench let it pass. His eyes wandered around the enclosure and fixed at last on a pile of newly turned earth in one corner. The smell of it—damp beneath the crust—reached even where they lay.

“What are they digging?” he asked.

“A well,” Feversham replied.

“A well? So close to the Nile!” Trench’s voice fretted. “Why? What’s the object?”

“I don’t know,” Feversham said. But fear sat in his stomach like bad grease. He suspected well enough and would not say it while Trench was weak.

Within a few days the suspicion stood up in daylight. It was announced openly that a high wall was to be built about the House of Stone. Too many prisoners had escaped in their fetters along the Nile bank. Henceforward they were to be kept from year’s beginning to year’s end within the wall.

The prisoners built it themselves of mud-bricks dried in the sun—earth mixed, pressed, baked by heat, like loaves made without kindness. Feversham worked. As soon as Trench could stand, he worked too. Day after day they lifted, set, mortised. The wall rose a foot each day. With their own hands they closed themselves in.

Twelve feet high it stood at last—smooth, strong, no projection for a toe, no seam that could be worried open in a night. Trench and Feversham looked at it until their eyes ached. Even the palm trees of Khartum were hidden now. Their world had become a square of bright blue by day, a square of dark blue by night, jewelled with points of silver and flashing gold.

Trench covered his face. “I daren’t look at it,” he said, his voice breaking. “We have been building our own coffin, Feversham—that’s the truth of it.”

Then he flung up his arms and cried aloud, as if his words might carry over mud and sand and river and reach England itself. “Will they never come up the Nile, the gunboats and the soldiers? Have they forgotten us in England? Good God—have they forgotten us?”

“Hush,” Feversham said. “We shall find a way of escape. We must wait six months. Well, we have both waited years. Six months—what are they?”

He spoke stoutly for Trench, but his own heart sank all the same. The months that followed did not deserve a chronicle; they deserved only endurance. In that pestilent enclosure only the vermin thrived. No news filtered in. Thoughts became their food, and a lizard on the wall an occasion for excitement. Scorpions stung them at night. Gaolers flogged them at times by day. They lived at the whim of Idris-es-Saier, and at the whim of Nebbi Khiddr, who always seemed to report against them to the Khalifa at the very moment Idris most needed money for his family.

Religious men came to convert them, and the long theological disputations became, strangely, events—because they broke the day with human voices that were not only commands and curses. Sometimes they were freed from heavier shackles and allowed to sleep in the open; sometimes, without reason, those privileges were withdrawn, and they struggled for air within the House of Stone, as though the place itself were a sealed jar.

Six months ended. The seventh began; a fortnight passed. The boy who brought Feversham food never came with the word they lived on.

“He will never come,” Trench said at last, despair made plain.

“Surely he will—if he is alive,” Feversham answered. Then the doubt he had fenced away crept in. “But is he alive?”

The seventh month passed. One morning early in the eighth, two of the Khalifa’s bodyguard came to the prison and spoke with Idris. Idris advanced to the two prisoners with an oily show of pleasure.

“Verily God is good to you, you men from the bad world,” he said. “You are to look upon the countenance of the Khalifa. How happy you should be!”

Trench and Feversham got to their feet with no happiness in them. The same clear questions rose in both minds: What does he want with us? Is this the end?

They were led out into the street and up toward the Khalifa’s house.

“Does it mean death?” Feversham asked quietly.

Trench shrugged and gave a sour laugh. “It is on the cards that Nebbi Khiddr has suggested something of the kind.”

They were taken into the great parade-ground before the mosque, then into the Khalifa’s house. On the threshold sat another white man in attendance, silent and watchful. Inside, the Khalifa sat on an angareb. Beside him stood a grey-bearded Greek.

The Khalifa told them they were to be employed in the manufacture of gunpowder, with which the armies of the Turks would shortly be overwhelmed.

Feversham was about to protest that he knew nothing of the process, but Trench spoke first, in fluent Arabic, with the boldness of a man who has decided to gamble on words.

“There is nothing connected with gunpowder which I do not know about,” he declared.

So both were ordered to work at the powder factory under the supervision of the Greek. That Greek they would remember with gratitude to their dying day. Out of sheer pity—knowing them to be herded in the House of Stone—he had suggested their employment. The same pity taught him to cover what they did not know, to guide their hands without betraying their ignorance.

“I know nothing whatever about the making of gunpowder except that crystals are used,” Trench confessed later, when they were alone.

“But we shall leave the prison each day,” Feversham said. “That is something, though we return each night. Who knows when a chance of escape may come?”

The powder factory lay in the northern part of the town, on the Nile’s bank just beyond the limits of the great mud wall, behind the slave market. Each morning they were let out from the prison door. They tramped along the river-bank on the outside of the town wall and came into the factory past the storehouses of the Khalifa’s bodyguard. Each evening they returned by the same road to the House of Stone. No guard went with them. Flight seemed impossible.

Each journey they scanned the dusk for the blue robe. Months passed. May came with summer heat that pressed on the ear as well as the skin, making every drumbeat and shout inside the town wall feel like an insult to quiet.

“Something has happened to Abou Fatma,” Feversham said. “He has been caught at Berber perhaps. In some way delayed.”

“He will not come,” Trench replied.

Feversham could no longer pretend. He did not know that Abou Fatma had been recognised at Berber by an old gaoler and had escaped with a sword-thrust in the thigh—a cheap price for his life, dear enough to slow him for months in the hospital at Assouan. Feversham heard nothing of that. But near the end of May, news came—thin as a strip of paper, yet heavy as bread in a starving hand—that someone else was working for their escape.

One evening, as they passed in the dusk between the storehouses and the town wall, a man in the shadow of a narrow alley whispered to them to stop. Trench knelt and examined his foot as though a stone had cut it. While he knelt, the man walked past and dropped a slip of paper at their feet.

A Suakin merchant—one who kept a booth in the grain market of Omdurman.

Trench picked up the paper, hid it in his hand, and limped on with Feversham beside him. Once they had left the houses behind and had only the wall on their right and the Nile on their left, Trench sat again. There was a crowd at the water’s edge; men moved up and down, their voices overlapping like water over pebbles. Trench took his foot into his lap and examined the sole, but in the hollow of his hand he unfolded the paper and read aloud.

His voice trembled so he could barely sound the words. Feversham could barely hear; the blood sang in his ears, louder than the river.

“A man will bring to you a box of matches. When he comes trust him.—Sutch.”

“Who is Sutch?” Trench asked.

“A great friend of mine,” Feversham said. “He is in Egypt, then. Does he say where?”

“No. But since Mohammed Ali, the grain merchant, dropped the paper, we may be sure he is at Suakin. A man with a box of matches—think, we may meet him to-night!”

They did not. A month passed. Then, one evening on the river-bank, an Arab pushed past them and murmured, “I am the man with the matches. To-morrow by the storehouse at this hour,” and as he went he dropped a box of coloured matches to the ground.

Feversham stooped at once.

“Don’t touch them,” Trench said sharply, and pressed the box into the earth with his foot before walking on.

“Sutch,” Feversham breathed. “So he comes to our help. How did he know I was here?”

Trench shook as he walked. He did not speak of the hope that had lit in him; he dared not. He tried even to pretend to himself that no message had come at all. He feared what hope could do to the mind—how it could pull and stretch until something snapped.

Both slept brokenly that night. Each time Feversham woke, a dim certainty hung in him that something great had shifted. Lying on his back beneath the stars, he had a sudden fancy he had fallen asleep in the garden of Broad Place on the Surrey hills, that he had only to raise his head to see dark pines on either side, and only to look back to see the gables against the sky. The fancy did not comfort him so much as it hurt—like a remembered word in a language you may never speak again.

Toward dawn he slept, and within an hour Trench shook him awake violently. Trench’s face was grey with fear.

“Suppose they keep us in the prison to-day,” he whispered, plucking at Feversham. “It has just occurred to me. Suppose they did that!”

“Why should they?” Feversham answered. But the fear caught him too. They sat and listened for Idris’s footsteps as though listening could change what they would hear.

Idris crossed the yard and unbolted the prison door without looking at them.

The captives fought and screamed and jammed together in the entrance, dragged back, thrust forward. One man ran out foaming at the mouth and dashed his head against the wall.

“He is mad,” Trench said, and because he was unmanned that morning he began to speak rapidly, near incoherence. “That’s what I have feared, Feversham—that I should go mad. To die, even here, one could put up with that. But to go mad!” He shivered. “If this man with the matches proves false—God, I shall be near to it. A man one day, a raving, foaming idiot the next—a thing to be put away out of sight, out of hearing. That’s horrible.”

He dropped his head between his hands, and did not look up until Idris bade them go about their work.

What work they did that day neither could later tell. The hours moved with extraordinary slowness, as if time itself were chained. Evening came at last.

“Among the storehouses,” Trench said, and they dived into the first alley they passed. Turning a corner, they saw the man who had brought the matches.

“I am Abdul Kader,” he began at once. “I have come to arrange for your escape. But at present flight is impossible.”

Trench swayed as though struck.

“Impossible?” Feversham asked.

“Yes. I brought three camels to Omdurman. Two have died. The Effendi at Suakin gave me money, but not enough. I could not arrange for relays. But if you will give me a letter to the Effendi telling him to give me two hundred pounds, I will have everything ready and come again within three months.”

Trench turned his back so Feversham would not see his face. Something in him sagged and emptied, like a bag split open. The truth stood appallingly clear: Abdul Kader would not risk his life. He would be a shuttle running back and forth between Omdurman and Suakin as long as letters could be written and money paid—but the shuttle would weave nothing.

“I have nothing with which to write,” Feversham said.

Abdul Kader produced paper and pen at once. “Be quick,” he urged. “Write quickly, lest we be discovered.”

Feversham wrote as Abdul suggested, but the futility of it was as plain to him as to Trench. When the letter was done he handed it over without ceremony, took Trench by the arm, and walked away.

They came out of the alley to the long mud wall. Sunset lay on the Nile in shifting colours—olive, then pink, then a green like glass. Above them stars were beginning to show; in the east dusk had already settled. Behind them, in the town, drums began to beat—monotonous, barbaric, the kind of sound that fills space and leaves no room for thought. Feversham, who had once found solace in quiet voices and remembered landscapes, felt the pounding as an assault on the mind’s last thin shelter.

Both men walked with chins sunk on their breasts, eyes on the ground. Their fetters seemed to have grown ten times heavier. They did not speak. They were so struck down by despair that an Arab fell into step beside them and neither noticed.

After a few moments the Arab spoke.

“The camels are ready in the desert, ten miles to the west.”

He spoke low; they were too absorbed to hear. He repeated the words. Feversham looked up.

Slowly, as though waking from a drugged sleep, meaning broke through. Slowly he recognised the voice, the shape of the man.

“Abou Fatma,” he said.

“Hoosh,” Abou Fatma whispered. “The camels are ready.”

“Now?” Feversham asked.

“Now.”

Trench leaned against the wall, eyes closed, with the face of a sick man. He looked as if he might swoon. Feversham held him by the arm.

“Is it true?” Trench asked faintly.

Before Feversham could answer, Abou Fatma went on in a swift undertone, like a cook giving instructions when the fire is at its worst and there is no time to repeat.

“Walk forwards very slowly. Before you reach the end of the wall it will be dusk. Draw your cloaks over your heads. Wrap these rags about your chains so they do not rattle. Then turn and come back. Go close to the water beyond the storehouses. I will be there with a man to remove your chains. Keep your faces covered and do not stop. He will think you slaves.”

Holding his hands behind his back, he passed rags to them while they stood close, as if they were merely bartering scraps. Then he turned and hurried away.

Very slowly Trench and Feversham walked forward toward the prison. Dusk crept across the river, mounted the long slope of sand, and enveloped them. They sat down and wrapped the rags around the iron, binding them tight until the metal’s clink was muffled, as though they were wrapping a knife so it would not betray them by shining. The sunset’s colours faded from the west. Darkness gathered quickly.

They turned and walked back along the road. The drums were more numerous now, the sound thick and crowding. Above the wall rose a glare of light. By the time they reached the water’s edge opposite the storehouses, it was fully dark.

Abou Fatma waited with a blacksmith. No words were wasted. The iron was knocked off, the last ring of it swallowed by river-noise and drumbeat.

“Come,” Abou Fatma said. “There will be no moon to-night.”

“How long before they discover you are gone?” Feversham asked.

“Who knows?” Abou Fatma answered. “Perhaps already Idris has missed you. Perhaps he will not till morning. There are many prisoners.”

They ran up the slope of sand, between the quarters of the tribes, across the narrow width of the city, through the cemetery where the ground lay uneven and the air seemed to hold its breath. Beyond the cemetery stood a disused house. A man rose in the doorway as they approached and went in.

“Wait here,” Abou Fatma whispered, and he too went into the house.

In a moment both men came back, each leading a camel and making it kneel. The animals smelled of warm hide and dry grass, a clean, living scent after stone and sweat and sickness.

“Mount,” Abou Fatma said. “Bring its head round and hold it as you mount.”

“I know the trick,” Trench said hoarsely, and swung himself up. Feversham climbed behind him. The two Arabs mounted the second camel.

“Ten miles to the west,” Abou Fatma said, and struck the camel on the flanks.

Behind them the glare of lights dwindled. The tapping of the drums diminished, until at last the night’s own sounds—hoof and sand, breath and leather—began to take back the world.
