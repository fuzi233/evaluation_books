Catherine’s heart grew heavy when she realized Gerard would soon return—within a month, perhaps even a week—and she would have to face him with the bitter truth: she had failed to watch over his betrothed. Worse still was the gnawing uncertainty of Margaret’s fate. At times, the dread took a darker shape.

“Oh, Kate,” she moaned, clutching her daughter’s arm, “what if the poor lass has thrown herself into the river?”

“Mother, surely she would never be so desperate!”

“Child, you don’t know the reckless hearts of young women left alone in the world. One day they’ll drown themselves for love of some fool who’d be forgotten in a week if only another man crossed their path. I’ve known them leap into the water bold as brass, only to scream for rescue the next instant—so fickle even in their despair! And then there are times when their bodies ache in ways no living soul should suffer, twisting their minds until patience snaps like a frayed thread. Then—into the depths they go, and men are to blame for it.”

“Mother!”

“Aye, monsters, the lot of them! Digging canals at every turn, tempting poor women to their doom. They know we won’t slit our own throats—too squeamish for blood, valuing our skins more than our lives. And hanging? By the time a lass has tied the noose, she’s had time to think twice. But a canal—ah, that’s swift as slipping into bed. The water does the rest, willing or not.” She shuddered. “Why, even I once stood at the edge, ready to jump.”

Kate gasped. “You?”

“Aye. Early in my marriage, when your father and I first learned each other’s rough edges. We quarreled, and I stormed out, weeping, wanting only to spite him. That cursed canal glittered at me, whispering how sweet it would be to make him grieve. I nearly stepped in—then I remembered my new gown, half-sewn, and my fine shoes, just bought from old John Bush. So I turned back instead. And what did your father say? *‘Let the quarrel lie, my lass. Not a word I spoke in anger was true, save this: I love thee.’*” Her voice softened. “I threw my arms around him then, thinking of the water I’d nearly chosen over his embrace. And that was better—far better—than rotting at the bottom of some ditch, my wedding clothes ruined. But my grief was nothing to Margaret’s.”

Kate offered a frail hope: perhaps Margaret loved her father too dearly to abandon him in his old age.

“Nay,” Catherine sighed. “Despair blots out all else—even love. The sky itself turns black. No, I put my faith not in the old man, but in the child she carries. A babe tugs at a mother’s heart, pleading for life even as it feeds on her very blood. That may stay her hand.”

“Then keep hope, Mother,” Kate urged. “Perhaps our fears are for nothing.” She hesitated before adding, “And for mercy’s sake, stop saying the babe will be a boy. It’s ill luck to name the child’s sex before birth—as if girls weren’t born as often as boys!”

Catherine rounded on her, indignant. “Threaten me with a girl, would you? What use is another lass when I have you? A boy—ah, a boy I could dandle on my knee and see my Gerard’s face again! It’s settled in my mind: she carries a son. And if I’m wrong, let disappointment come then—not from your tongue stealing my joy beforehand!”
