Thirteen years had elapsed, and the sun of June returned to the capital with a familiar, feverish intensity, yet Harry Feversham's vitality had been restored in a manner far more subdued, attended by a circle of intimacy significantly reduced. They convened within a chamber perched precariously high upon a monolithic edifice that loomed over Westminster like a grim sentinel, its silhouette frowning against the twilight. To a solitary wanderer traversing St. James's Park southward, pausing beneath the iron arches of the suspension bridge at night, the sudden revelation of tiered, luminous windows ascending to such dizzying heights might induce a vertigo of wonder—a sensation that in the heart of London, amidst the mundane sprawl of commerce, a mountain had risen, and that tiny, industrious gnomes were busily carving their existence into its granite flanks. Upon the tenth floor of this architectural colossus, Harry had secured a flat during his annual furlough from the Indian campaign; and it was within the dining room of this elevated sanctuary that the evening's solemn rite unfolded.

The space was dressed in tones of somber repose, a deliberate rejection of the season's deceptive warmth. Though the calendar proclaimed summer, a biting chill persisted, necessitating a roaring fire that cast dancing shadows against the walls. A bay window, its blinds drawn tight against the encroaching dark, framed the sprawling expanse of the city. Around the dinner table sat four men, the smoke of cigars mingling with the scent of roasted meat. Harry Feversham remained largely unchanged, save for a new growth of fair moustache that starkly contrasted with his dark hair, marking the inevitable passage of time. Now a man of medium stature, long-limbed and sculpted with the dense musculature of an athlete, his countenance retained the arresting clarity of the night Lieutenant Sutch had once scrutinized it so closely.

Two of his companions were fellow officers on leave, acquired that afternoon at the club. Captain Trench was a diminutive figure, his crown surrendering to baldness, possessing a visage of sharp angles and resourceful cunning, animated by black eyes that darted with relentless energy. Beside him sat Lieutenant Willoughby, a study in contradictory contradictions. A broad, rounded forehead sat above a thick, snub nose, framing a pair of vacant, protruding orbs that lent him an air of invincible obtuseness. He spoke rarely, and when he did, his words drifted aimlessly toward points long abandoned, only to circle back with labored precision. He constantly twisted a moustache whose tips curled upward with a ridiculous, almost violent ferocity. He was a man easily dismissed as inconsequential upon first glance, yet demanding reconsideration upon reflection; for he was born as stubborn as he was foolish, and the damage wrought by his ignorance was compounded by his refusal to admit error. Persuasion was a futile exercise; he did not hear arguments, but rather spun his own crippled logic in the vacuum behind his eyes until he achieved a self-satisfied equilibrium.

The fourth guest was Durrance, a lieutenant of the East Surrey Regiment and Feversham's oldest confidant, summoned specifically by telegram. It was June, 1882, a month when the civilian populace viewed Egypt with trepidation, while soldiers regarded it with a hungry anticipation. Arabi Pasha, defying every threat, methodically reinforced the fortifications of Alexandria, while further south, a darker storm swelled ominously like a gathering thunderhead. A year prior, a slender, tall youth from Dongola, Mohammed Ahmed, had marched through the villages of the White Nile, preaching with the fervor of a revivalist the advent of a Savior. The oppressed, weary under the yoke of Turkish taxation, had listened, hearing the promise echoed in the whisper of the wind through withered grass, finding holy names etched even upon the eggs they collected. By 1882, Mohammed had claimed this title and secured his initial victories against the Ottomans.

"There will be trouble," observed Trench, and this prophecy became the scripture upon which three of the four men dwelt. Yet, in a rare interlude of silence, the fourth, Harry Feversham, ventured forth on a different topic. "I am exceedingly gratified that you were all able to join me tonight," he explained to Durrance. "I also sent a dispatch to Castleton, an officer of our regiment, but he is dining with a senior official at the War Office and departs for Scotland shortly, precluding his attendance. However, I possess news of a peculiar nature."

The three men leaned forward, their minds still tethered to the looming specter of war. Yet, it was not of conflict that Harry Feversham bore tidings. "I arrived in London this morning from Dublin," he admitted, a faint flush of embarrassment coloring his cheeks. "I have spent several weeks there."

Durrance lifted his gaze from the tablecloth, meeting his friend's eyes with steady calm. "Ah?" he queried simply.

"I have returned engaged to be married."

Durrance raised his glass to his lips. "Well, here's to your fortune, Harry," he remarked, and that was the extent of his response. The sentiment, delivered with almost brusque brevity, carried no lack of weight in Feversham's estimation. The bond between these two men was devoid of effusive declarations; neither required such flourishes. Both possessed a secure consciousness of their connection, valuing it at its true, formidable worth. It was an instrument forged for hardship, designed for a lifetime of utility, yet never explicitly named between them. Both felt profound gratitude for this rare, undeserved gift, aware simultaneously that it entailed a potential debt of sacrifice. Should necessity arise, such sacrifices would be rendered silently, unmentioned. Perhaps it was precisely this awareness of their friendship's unbreakable strength that compelled them to a specific reticence in their discourse.

"Thank you, Jack!" Feversham replied. "I appreciate your kind wishes. It was you who introduced me to Ethne; I shall never forget it."

Durrance set his glass down without haste. A moment of silence stretched between them, filled only by the crackle of the fire. He sat with his eyes fixed upon the tablecloth, his hands resting lightly on the table's edge. "Yes," he said in a level tone. "I did you a good turn then." He appeared poised to elaborate, uncertain of the phrasing, but Captain Trench's sharp, pragmatic voice intervened, saving him the effort.

"Will this alter anything?" Trench asked.

Feversham replaced his cigar between his lips. "Do you mean, shall I resign my commission?" he inquired slowly.

"I don't know," Durrance responded, seizing the opportunity to rise and move to the window, presenting his back to his companions.

Feversham interpreted this abrupt departure as a rebuke and addressed Durrance's retreating form rather than Trench. "I don't know," he repeated. "It requires contemplation. There are many factors. On one side, naturally, stands my father, my career, such as it is. On the other, there is her father, Dermod Eustace."

"He wishes you to abandon your commission?" Willoughby interjected.

"He undoubtedly harbors the typical Irish aversion to constituted authority," Trench laughed. "Yet, does he require your subscription to it, Feversham?"

"It is not merely that." Still addressing Durrance's back, he continued his explanation. "Dermod is aging, his estates teeter on the brink of ruin, and there are other matters. You know, Jack?"

The direct appeal hung in the air, yet Durrance answered absently. "Yes, I know," he murmured, as if reciting a familiar mantra. "Should you require any whiskey, tap twice on the floor with your foot. The servants understand."

"Precisely," Feversham acknowledged. He proceeded, carefully weighing his words, his gaze intent upon the shoulders of his companions as he looked toward his friend. "Furthermore, there is Ethne herself. Dermod performed an apt act in naming her so. For she is of her country, and more specifically, of her county. She carries the love of it in her very bones. I doubt she could find happiness in India, or indeed anywhere beyond the reach of Donegal—the scent of its peat, its streams, and the brown, welcoming embrace of its hills. These considerations must weigh heavily."

He awaited a response, receiving none, and pressed on. Durrance, however, harbored no spirit of reproach. He knew Feversham was speaking; indeed, he wished fervently that his friend would continue, yet he paid no heed to the content of his speech. Standing transfixed before the window, he faced the glare of Pall Mall shooting upward into the sky, witnessing the chains of light stacked one above another as the city ascended northward, accompanied by the phantom rumble of a million carriages. At his feet, far below, lay St. James's Park, silent and black, a placid pool of darkness amidst the glitter and clamor. Durrance felt a profound urge to flee this room and retreat into its secrets, yet he dared not depart without notice. Consequently, he maintained his back to his companion, pressing his forehead against the cool glass, hoping his friend would persist in talking. He confronted one of the necessary sacrifices that must remain unspoken, betrayed by no sign.
