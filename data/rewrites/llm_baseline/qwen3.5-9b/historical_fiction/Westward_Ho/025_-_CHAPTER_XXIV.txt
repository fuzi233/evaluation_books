"Let us alone. What pleasure can we have / To war with evil? Is there any peace / In always climbing up the climbing wave? / All things have rest, and ripen toward the grave / In silence; ripen, fall, and cease: / Give us long rest or death, dark death, or dreamful ease."

Alexander von Humboldt, in one of his more curious passages, observed a wretched group of Indians squatting dully around their fires, smeared with grease and paint, subsisting on ants and clay. With a naive candor, he remarked that were it not for science—which teaches us that such crude material constitutes the foundation of humanity and represents the state from which we all evolved—he would have been tempted to view these hapless creatures as the final, degraded remnants of a fallen and dying race. One wishes the great traveler had possessed the courage to succumb to that temptation, accepting his own reason and common sense as the true explanation of the tragic sight, rather than adhering to the dogmas of a so-called science lacking factual basis. His own good sense, it appears, aligned instinctively with Biblical doctrine: that man in a state of nature is a fallen being, doomed to death—a grim perspective, perhaps, yet one more honorable to humanity than the theory that we descended from ape-like ancestors. It is surely more hopeful to believe that the poor Otomacs or Guahibas were simply failing to meet their potential, rather than believing they were too foolish to improve their condition over millennia.

However, our voyagers, like their contemporaries, troubled their minds with no such philosophical inquiries. Accepting the Biblical narrative as written, they sided with Humboldt's personal judgment rather than his scientific conclusions—or, more accurately, they trusted Humboldt's intuition over the shallow anthropological theories popular fifty years prior. Consequently, their new hosts were viewed as immortal souls akin to themselves, "captivated by the devil at his will," currently lost in the pathless forests and destined to remain so eternally.

Facts seemed to corroborate these old-fashioned theories, though these Indians had not sunk quite as low as the Guahibas encountered earlier on the lower reaches of the river. Upon landing, the explorers witnessed a scattered village of palm-leaf sheds, from which hammocks were suspended between trees. Here and there, amidst the forest openings, patches of cassava and indigo grew, lending the settlement a neatness and comfort surpassing the regional average. Yet, ominous signs of the evil spirit were immediately apparent.

No benevolent force could have inspired the music emanating from the clearing. As Cary noted, if Apollo and Mercury ever visited America, they had played a shabby trick on the natives, providing them with inferior instruments and worse taste. On either side of the landing site stood four or five sturdy figures, each wielding a tall drum or a long earthen trumpet. These instruments, swelling along their lengths into several hollow spheres, erupted into a deafening cacophony of howls, groans, and thumps the moment the strangers stepped ashore. This sound fully justified Yeo's observation: "They are calling upon their devil, sir." Cary retorted with a semblance of logic, "They are the less likely to be disappointed, for none but Sir Urian would ever come to listen to such a noise."

"And you mark, sirs," Yeo added, "there's some feast or sacrifice in preparation. I'm not entirely confident in them yet."

"Nonsense!" Amyas declared. "We could slaughter every soul of them in half an hour, and they know it as well as we do."

Nevertheless, a grand demonstration was clearly underway. The children of the forest lined up in two rows flanking the open space, men in the front and women behind, all adorned to the best of their ability with annatto, indigo, and feathers. Suddenly, with a hideous yell, a figure leaped into the center of the clearing—a personage who certainly would not complain if anyone mistook him for the devil himself. Dressed meticulously for the occasion, he wore a jaguar-skin with a long tail, grinning teeth, a pair of horns, a plume of black and yellow feathers, and carried a massive rattle.

"Here's the Piache, the rascal," Amyas identified.

"Aye," Yeo agreed, "in Satan's livery, and I have no doubt his deeds match his attire."

"Don't be frightened, Jack," Cary reassured, stepping forward to support Brimblecombe. "It's your business to confront him. Go at him boldly, and he will run."

The men laughed, and the Piache, realizing his intended solemn impression had failed due to his customary reliance on impudence, quickly recovered. He advanced, struck one of the musicians over the head with his rattle to enforce silence, and began a harangue. Amyas listened patiently, a cigar dangling from his lips.

"What's it all about, boy?" Amyas asked.

"He wants to know if you have seen Amalivaca on the other shore of the great water?" the messenger reported.

Amyas, familiar with this query regarding the mythical civilizer of the forest Indians—who carved the mysterious cliff sculptures before returning beyond the ocean—responded as usual by extolling the virtues of Queen Elizabeth.

To this, the Piache replied that she must be one of Amalivaca's seven daughters; some he had brought back with him, while he had broken the legs of the others to prevent their escape, leaving them to populate the forests.

Amyas countered that his queen's legs were certainly intact, describing her as a model of grace and activity, the finest dancer in her dominions. More importantly, he sought to know if the tribe would provide cassava bread and allow them to rest peacefully on the island before proceeding to fight the clothed men—the Spaniards—beyond the mountains.

Amused by this exchange, the Piache capered and somersaulted with much howling, then beckoned Amyas and his party to follow. Seeing the Indians were unarmed and in high spirits, the English complied. Leading them to the door of a carefully sealed hut, the Piache crawled up to it on all fours in an abject manner, beginning to whine at someone inside.

"Ask what he is doing, boy," Amyas instructed.

The lad queried the old cacique who had accompanied them, receiving the reply that he was consulting the Daughter of the Sun.

"Here is our mare's nest at last," Cary muttered as the Piache escalated from whines to screams, gesticulating wildly until he fell into violent convulsions, foaming at the mouth and rolling his eyes, before collapsing as if dead.

"As good as a stage play," someone remarked.

"The devil has played his part," Jack observed. "And now, by the rules of all plays, Vice should enter."

"And a very fair Vice it will be, I suspect; a right sweet Iniquity, my Jack! Listen."

From within the hut emerged a low, sweet song. The simple Indians bowed their heads in reverence, while the English stood hushed in astonishment. The voice was neither shrill nor guttural, typical of an Indian, but round, clear, and rich, reminiscent of a European. As it swelled, revealing a compass and power extraordinary for any setting—and many in the party, trained in musical traditions of old England, were adept judges of such matters—it soared and dipped, seemingly inarticulate yet perfect in melody, like the song of a bird on a branch. The wild wanderers were captivated by this new delight, marveling as the Indians bowed and welcomed the notes as messages from a higher realm. Finally, a triumphant burst rang out so shrilly that all ears echoed, followed by absolute silence.

The Piache, instantly restored to life, jumped upright and resumed his preaching to Amyas.

"Tell the howling villain to make short work of it, lad! His tune won't suffice after that finale."

Grinning, the lad informed Amyas that the Piache signaled the Daughter of the Sun's acceptance of them as friends; her allies were now theirs, and her enemies theirs. The Indians erupted in delighted screams. Amyas, rolling another tobacco leaf in a strip of plantain, replied, "Then let her give us some cassava," and lit a fresh cigar.

The hut door swung open, and the Indians prostrated themselves as the same fair apparition emerged—one they had previously encountered on the island, now decked in feather-robes and plumes of every conceivable hue. Walking slowly and stateliness, accustomed to command, she approached Amyas, glancing proudly at her prostrate adores. Pointing gracefully to the trees, gardens, and huts, her expressive gestures conveyed that everything was at his disposal. Then, taking his hand, she lifted it gently to her forehead.

At this sign of submission, a shout of rapture rose from the crowd. As the mysterious maiden retreated to her hut, the English were surrounded, caressed, and admired. They pointed with equal surprise at their swords, their Indian bows and blowguns, and the trophies of wild beasts adorning their clothing. Women hurried off to bring fruit, flowers, cassava, and—to Amyas's great anxiety—calabashes of intoxicating drink. Ultimately, the English sat beneath the trees, feasting merrily while the drums and trumpets produced hideous music, and lithe young girls and boys performed uncouth dances that scandalized both Brimblecombe and Yeo.
