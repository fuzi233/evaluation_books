Paul Oleron regarded the world with a benevolence that the world rarely returned, though at forty-four, he rarely bothered to calculate the deficit in his ledger. To weigh the scales would not change the outcome; it would only depress him further. He had walked this narrow path, chosen in the fevered, idealistic days of his youth when he believed a life devoid of nobility and selflessness was unworthy of existence. Now, the illusion was thinning, revealing a starker reality, yet he refused to anticipate the inevitable twilight of his powers where he might regret his rigid, lofty standards.

For now, his displacement into the old house, marked by insurance stamps embedded in the brick, served only as a brief interruption to *Romilly Bishop*. At fifteen chapters, the novel lay dormant, waiting for the dust to settle. As the tall man with the ascetic, monk-like face rearranged his new domain, he projected a spinster-like precision. Having lived twenty years in a dozen shifting accommodations—from garrets to flats—he had learned that method saved both time and temper. He had arranged with Mrs. Barrett, a stout Welshwoman whose voice retained the falsetto and heavy Merionethshire accent despite her London residency, to manage his breakfast and the Saturday cleaning. Indeed, he welcomed domestic labor as a necessary respite from the strain of creation.

His kitchen, adjacent to a strip containing a modern bath, overlooked a narrow alley. At one end stood a large closet with a square sliding hatch near the top—a former powder chamber where an elaborately dressed head had once received the click of a pistol. Puzzling over this relic, Oleron smiled faintly, moved by a sentiment he could not name. The space would need a new function, likely serving as his larder. While rummaging on an upper shelf, deep within the wall, he uncovered two mushroom-shaped wooden wig stands. Their origin was a mystery; perhaps the painters had unearthed them. With such limited storage, these finds offered a sliver of ingenuity for housing his linens and the accumulated, fragile treasures of his papers.

It was early spring when Oleron took possession, driven by an anxiety to publish *Romilly* before the following autumn. Yet, he resisted the urge to force the hand of his art. He sensed the book's crucial importance in his evolution and trusted it to dictate its own tempo. In his previous workroom, the narrative had begun to breathe on its own; he hoped the distraction of moving would soon fade, allowing the story to resume its march. On a crisp March morning, he stepped out, returning laden with two great bunches of yellow daffodils. He placed one on the mantelpiece beside a pair of Sheffield sticks and the other on his desk, then opened the half-finished manuscript.

Before diving into the words, however, he consulted his cheque-book and pass-book in a small rosewood cabinet. The arithmetic brought a thoughtful furrow to his brow. His installation costs had exceeded his estimates, leaving a balance of just under fifty pounds with no immediate influx.

"Hm! I'd forgotten rugs and chintz curtains," he murmured, recalling the expenses. "A pity to ruin the place for want of ten pounds... Well, *Romilly* simply *must* be out by autumn. So, here goes."

He drew his papers forward, but the ink remained dry. The square outside possessed a clamorous life that threatened to drown his focus. Hawkers cried from their carts; at noon, a troop of schoolchildren swung on his gate; later, an itinerant musician strummed a mandolin beneath his window. Oleron pushed up the sash and tossed a penny, hoping for silence, but the noise persisted. He returned to his table, only to find himself drifting. Moments passed in which he stared at the room, reconstructing its former life: a buttercup satin settee? A crystal chandelier hanging from the high molding? Where had the tambour-frame stood? The mental fatigue was useless. Frank idleness, he realized, was preferable to this futile exhaustion. He planned a walk but succumbed to a doze in his chair.

"Waking at half-past four is no triumph," he yawned upon stirring. "I must do better tomorrow." A wave of delicious laziness washed over him, tempting him to break an evening appointment.

The next morning, however, he sat down without answering any of his three letters—two bills and a note from Miss Bengough. The air was bright with white and blue skies, a noisy wind carrying a subtle shift in the colors of growing things. Light flooded the room in fitful bursts as clouds rolled northeast, reflecting off the polished table and worn floor. The street noises resumed their rhythm.

Oleron dotted patterns on his page, then stopped to align his jar of daffodils precisely against a creamy panel. He wrote a sentence that flowed for two lines before fragmenting into notes and jottings. For a moment, he convinced himself this was work, then paced the room. An idea struck him: the space needed color. It was currently too pale, mild and sweet like a kindly old face, yet devitalized and wan. It required a robust note, warmer flowers, perhaps gay fabrics for the window seats.

"Of course, I really can't afford it," he muttered as he measured the width of a window recess with a two-foot ruler.

Suddenly, his posture shifted to intense curiosity. He rose, rubbing his hands with gentle glee. "Oho, oho! These look like window boxes, nailed up. We must investigate! Yes, this is an adventure!"

Two windows faced the wall, and beyond the open bedroom door, another existed on the same plane. The seats had been repainted repeatedly, yet Oleron's probing finger detected faint traces of old nail heads beneath the fresh layers. Below the ledge, an old keyhole had been puttied over. Pulling out his penknife, he worked carefully for five minutes, then retreated to the kitchen for a hammer and chisel.

Driving the chisel under the seat, he prised the lid loose. Using the knife to sever the hinge and wedges, he positioned a wooden block and struck it gently.

"Now for our little mystery—"

The impact of the mallet sounded strangely brutal in the sweet, pale apartment. The panelling rang and vibrated like a sounding board. Echoes seemed to awaken from the cellar to the garrets above, creeping along his nerves. Pausing to dampen the noise with a duster, he pried the lid open. The paint flaked, the rusty nails squeaked, and the lid revealed the hollow space beneath. Inside, save for a few inches of scurf, mold, and ancient cobwebs, there was nothing.

"No treasure there," Oleron noted, amused at his fleeting fantasy. "*Romilly* will still have to see the autumn. Let us inspect the others."

He turned to the second window. Raising the remaining seats occupied most of the afternoon. Like the first, the bedroom seat yielded nothing. But from the second seat in the sitting room, he extracted a yielding, folded mass, thick with dust. Carrying it to the kitchen, he brushed away the grime and spread it out.

It was a large bag of ancient, frieze-like fabric, shaped like an irregular triangle with wide flaps and remnants of leather straps. The top layer was a faded yellowish-brown, while the underside revealed rich, varying shades of crimson depending on how the folds lay.

"Now, whatever could that have been?" Oleron mused, staring at the enigma. "I give it up. Whatever it is, it has settled my work for today..."

He folded the object carelessly and shoved it into a corner of the kitchen. Taking pans, brushes, and an old knife, he returned to the sitting room to clean the newly discovered compartments, lining them with paper. Once finished, he transferred his spare boots, books, and papers into the boxes, closing the lids. Amused by his little adventure, yet slightly anxious for the return to serious labor, he stood amidst the quiet of the room, the mystery of the crimson bag lingering like a shadow over his thoughts.
