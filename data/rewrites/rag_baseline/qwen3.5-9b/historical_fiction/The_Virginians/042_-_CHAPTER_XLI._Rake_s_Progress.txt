The air in the Royal Library was stagnant, thick with the dust of dead arguments. I sat at a wooden desk, cataloging the lies of the past to construct a slightly different sequence of lies for the future. It was a mechanical task, the quiet rearrangement of history until it sang a flat, circular tune. One afternoon, a clerk brought me a volume. *Memoirs of M. d'Artagnan*. Printed in Amsterdam by Pierre Rouge, a man who understood that inconvenient honesty led to the Bastille, and so the author had fled to write in the safety of exile. Now, both the author and his jailers were equally forgotten.

I took the book to my rented room. I turned the pages. There were portraits of the period within it—kings, queens, cardinals, the interchangeable courtiers. They did not interest me. They were the usual statues, rigid and silent. But in the margins of this official history, the author described a mundane waiting room. It was the antechamber of M. de Tréville, a captain of the Musketeers. Here, d'Artagnan encountered three young men in uniform.

Athos. Porthos. Aramis.

The names were unnatural. Mythological remnants ending in *os* and *is*. They were obviously false. They were pseudonyms assumed by men who had likely donned a uniform out of financial ruin, social disgrace, or a profound exhaustion with their own given identities. They had erased themselves to become functions of the state. I felt a sudden, inexplicable kinship with this erasure.

I began to search for them. I spent weeks requesting catalogs, folios, and registers. I compiled lists of nothing. The effort was immense and entirely pointless. I was on the verge of abandoning the inquiry, ready to accept that these men were merely the inventions of a mediocre soldier, when, prompted by the idle suggestion of an acquaintance named Paulin Paris, I requested another manuscript.

Consciousness returned as a sequence of dull, rhythmic aches. The floorboards of the Jolly Miller smelled of stale wine and centuries of trampled mud. Above him, the innkeeper's mouth moved. The words were a ledger of inconveniences: a disturbance had been caused, a departure was required. The world, functioning on a brutal economy of space and quiet, had no use for a bleeding boy.

D'Artagnan assembled his limbs. It was a mechanical effort, the mere function of bone and bruised tissue. He dragged himself toward the light of the doorway. Outside, the gray geometry of the courtyard framed a heavy carriage. Two Norman horses stood breathing steam into the cold air. Inside the carriage sat a woman—pale, blonde, possessing a beautiful and terrifying blankness. Beside her stood the man in the violet doublet.

Observing them, D'Artagnan felt the sudden, exhausting obligation to perform his identity. He had been taught that he was a gentleman; therefore, he was required to be outraged. He pushed himself over the threshold. He formed a shout, a hollow sound against the vast, indifferent sky.

The man in violet did not draw his sword. He merely looked. It was a gaze of absolute detachment, a look that reduced D'Artagnan to a minor atmospheric disturbance. The carriage departed. The man mounted his horse and rode away. D'Artagnan tried to pursue, but the physical reality of his damaged body intervened. He collapsed into the dirt, muttering words of vengeance that dissolved immediately into the dust. He fainted, slipping back into the void.

When he woke again, the inventory of his existence had diminished. Eleven crowns remained in a velvet purse. He sat in the dim kitchen and mixed oil, wine, and rosemary. The recipe was his mother's, a domestic ritual applied to the senseless violence of the world. He rubbed the paste into his skin. The flesh sealed, but the underlying structure remained exactly as fragile as before.
