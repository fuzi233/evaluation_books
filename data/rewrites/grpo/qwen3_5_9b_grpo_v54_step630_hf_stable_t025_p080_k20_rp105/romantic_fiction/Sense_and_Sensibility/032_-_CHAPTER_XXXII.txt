When Elinor recounted the details of her conversation to her sister, the impact was not the one she had anticipated. Marianne did not question the truth of a single word; she listened with a steady, submissive stillness, offering no defense for Willoughby and shedding tears that spoke of a conviction she found impossible to shake. Yet, while Elinor saw the signs of this realization—Marianne no longer avoiding Colonel Brandon, addressing him with a newfound, compassionate respect, and her volatile temper settling into a quieter despair—she saw no lightening of her sister's wretchedness. Marianne's mind had become fixed, but it was anchored in a gloomy desolation. The loss of Willoughby's character weighed heavier on her than the loss of his affection. The memory of Miss Williams' misery, coupled with the terrifying uncertainty of what his intentions toward her once might have been, preyed upon her so relentlessly that she could not bear to speak of her pain even to Elinor. In her silent brooding, she inflicted a deeper sorrow upon her sister than any open confession could have caused.

Mrs. Dashwood's reaction to Elinor's letter mirrored the anguish of her daughters, though her indignation burned hotter. Long letters followed swiftly, pouring out her suffering and her desperate pleas for Marianne to summon fortitude. How cruel, Elinor thought, for a mother to speak of strength when her own heart was so bruised; how humiliating for Marianne to be urged to suppress the very regrets her mother wished she could feel.

Despite the pain, Mrs. Dashwood made a difficult decision. She believed it better for Marianne to be anywhere but at Barton, where every sight and sound would resurrect the past, constantly presenting Willoughby as she had always known him. She urged her daughters to extend their stay in London, hoping that the variety of city life might, however briefly, distract Marianne from her own misery. To Mrs. Dashwood, the crowded anonymity of London offered safety; Willoughby's reputation was now so ruined that no friend would seek him out, and the sheer chaos of the city made a chance encounter unlikely compared to the quiet isolation of Devonshire. Furthermore, a letter from her son-in-law indicated that the Ferrars family would be in town by mid-February, making it prudent for the sisters to remain until they could greet their brother.

Marianne, though convinced her mother's plan was misguided and that staying in London denied her the only true solace—her mother's presence—submitted without argument. Elinor, however, found a strange comfort in the arrangement. While the delay threatened her own happiness, she reasoned that it was necessary for Marianne's sake. Moreover, Elinor suspected she could not entirely avoid Edward Ferrars; thus, the extended stay, though painful, might be preferable to an immediate return to the countryside.

Elinor's efforts to shield Marianne from the name of Willoughby proved successful. Neither Mrs. Jennings, Sir John, nor Mrs. Palmer ever mentioned him before her daughter. Elinor, however, could not enjoy this silence. She was forced to endure the clamorous indignation of her circle daily. Sir John raged against the "scoundrel," vowing never to speak to him again, even if Willoughby stood beside him at Barton Covert. Mrs. Palmer, equally furious, declared she would never mention his name again and resolved to label him a "good-for-nothing" to anyone who would listen. They delighted in sharing gossip about his new carriage, his portraitist, and the location of Miss Grey's wardrobe, treating the tragedy as a source of communal entertainment.

In contrast, Lady Middleton's calm, polite indifference was a balm to Elinor's spirit. It was a profound relief to know that at least one person in their circle felt no need to pry into particulars or worry about Marianne's health. Lady Middleton's daily, gentle exclamation of "It is very shocking!" served as a perfect vent, allowing her to maintain her dignity while condemning the wrong, before turning her attention back to her own social obligations. She even planned to call on Mrs. Willoughby immediately upon her marriage, a decision that amused Sir John but pleased Lady Middleton.

Colonel Brandon's inquiries remained a source of genuine comfort for Elinor. Having earned the right to discuss Marianne's grief through his zealous efforts to soothe it, they shared their burdens with confidence. Marianne's occasional pitying glances and soft voice when addressing the Colonel assured him of his growing influence, giving Elinor hope for the future. Unaware of this subtle shift, Mrs. Jennings grew increasingly skeptical of the match. Seeing the Colonel remain grave and unable to propose, she began to doubt whether the union would happen at all, eventually ceasing to think of Mrs. Ferrars entirely, convinced the Colonel and Elinor were destined for each other.

By early February, less than two weeks after receiving Willoughby's letter, Elinor delivered the final blow: he was married. She had ensured Marianne learned of it privately, sparing her the shock of reading it in the newspapers. Marianne received the news with a resolute composure that lasted only a moment before breaking into tears, leaving her in a state of pitiable distress for the rest of the day.

With the Willoughbys gone, Elinor hoped to encourage Marianne to venture out again. Around this time, the Miss Steeles returned to London, greeted with warmth by their relatives. Only Elinor felt pain at their arrival, particularly at Lucy's delight in finding her still in town. Lucy's teasing was relentless, mocking Elinor for not leaving sooner and boasting of her own travels with Dr. Davies, whom she coyly denied being a "beau" despite everyone's laughter. When Lucy turned her attention to Elinor's sister, feigning concern for Marianne's health to invite her to visit, Elinor politely declined, citing Marianne's nervous headaches. When Miss Steele insisted on going to see Marianne herself, Elinor's temper nearly flared, but Lucy's sharp reprimand saved her the trouble, silencing her cousin's impertinence.
