At the time arranged the previous day, Margaret and her father stepped into the street, their footsteps falling into the ancient, cyclical rhythm of those who walk toward mourning. The air of Milton carried its usual layered olfactory history: the sharp, sulfurous bite of coal smoke woven tightly with the ghostly, warm scent of roasted chicory and damp oat-flour from a distant baker’s oven. For weeks, their own home had been enveloped in the muffled acoustics of grief, and the sudden immersion into the industrial city’s rattling soundscape made them draw close to one another in unspoken, sympathetic alignment.

Nicholas Higgins was sitting by the fire-side in his accustomed corner, but the deeply resonant, earthy aroma of his burning tobacco was noticeably absent. Instead, the room smelled of cold ash and the stale, sour perspiration of a body long deprived of proper sustenance. He was leaning his head upon his hand, his unshaven jaw pale, his jacket fraying—a man visibly eroding. 

“Sit ye down, sit ye down. Fire’s welly out,” said he, grasping the iron poker and striking the coals. The harsh, metallic clatter served as a sonic shield, deflecting attention from his own decay. 

“We thought we should have a good chance of finding you, just after dinner-time,” said Margaret, acutely aware of the empty hearth where a pot of hearty, slow-simmered stew ought to have been hanging. 

“Ay, ay. Sorrows is more plentiful than dinners just now,” Nicholas replied, his voice carrying the rough, untranslatable timbre of Lancashire stoicism. “I reckon, my dinner hour stretches all o’er the day; yo’re pretty sure of finding me.” 

“Are you out of work?” asked Margaret.

“Ay,” he replied shortly. After a silence that hung heavy in the room’s acoustic space, he added, “I’m not wanting brass. Dunno yo’ think it. Bess, poor lass, had a little stock under her pillow, ready to slip into my hand, last moment, and Mary is fustian-cutting. But I’m out of work a’ the same.”

“We owe Mary some money,” said Mr. Hale, whose polished, southern lexicon often failed to grasp the delicate power dynamics of the local dialect, before Margaret’s sharp pressure on his arm could arrest the words. 

“If hoo takes it, I’ll turn her out o’ doors. I’ll bide inside these four walls, and she’ll bide out. That’s a’.” Higgins’s pride was absolute, a cultural memory of self-reliance refusing to be overwritten by charity. 

“But we owe her many thanks for her kind service,” began Mr. Hale again. 

“I ne’er thanken your daughter theer for her deeds o’ love to my poor wench. I ne’er could find th’ words. I’se have to begin to try now, if yo’ start making an ado about what little Mary could sarve yo’.” 

“Is it because of the strike you’re out of work?” asked Margaret gently. 

“Strike’s ended. It’s o’er for this time. I’m out o’ work because I ne’er asked for it. And I ne’er asked for it, because good words is scarce, and bad words is plentiful.” He spoke with a surly, rhythmic cadence, highlighting the lexical power dynamics at play. The masters owned the mills, but they also dictated the vocabulary of survival. Margaret, parsing the rich dialect, asked for his explanation. 

“Asking for work,” Higgins said. “I reckon them’s almost the best words that men can say. ‘Gi’ me work’ means ‘and I’ll do it like a man.’ Them’s good words.” 

“And bad words are refusing you work when you ask for it.” 

“Ay. Bad words is saying ‘Aha, my fine chap! Yo’ve been true to yo’r order, and I’ll be true to mine... There’s no work for yo’ here.’ Them’s bad words.” 

Mr. Hale suggested asking his old master for another chance. Higgins tittered a low, bitter laugh, a sound that grated against the room’s quiet. He explained the new dictates of the masters—how they forced men to sign pledges abandoning the Union, twisting language to breed hypocrites. “I’ve been a turn-out, and known what it was to clem,” Higgins said. The word *clem* hung in the air, a visceral, regional term for starvation that held centuries of agrarian suffering; it tasted of copper in the mouth, of stomachs hollowed out and gnawing on their own lining, far removed from the utilitarian idea of merely missing a meal. 

Margaret hesitated, her own voice dropping to a soft, hesitant timber so as not to disrupt the precarious acoustic ecology of the room. “Do you remember poor Boucher saying that the Union was a tyrant?” 

Higgins leaned heavily over the grate. When he finally spoke, he reached for an ancestral, agrarian metaphor—a mythic memory of the land before the deafening looms took over. “I ha’ read a bit o’ poetry about a plough going o’er a daisy... Th’ Union’s the plough, making ready the land for harvest-time. Such as Boucher... he’s liker a weed lounging over the ground—mun just made up their mind to be put out o’ the way.” 

He recounted Boucher’s desperate, mealy-mouthed begging at Hampers’ mill, how the man had tried to trade his loyalty for the mere promise of sustenance, only to be turned away. “Why he’d a clemmed to death, if th’ Union had na helped him in his pinch... though folk standing by, says the traitor cried like a babby!” 

“Oh! how shocking! how pitiful!” exclaimed Margaret. “You have made him what he is!” 

Before Higgins could answer, a shift in the street’s sonic environment forced itself upon their attention. It was a hollow, measured sound, a slow, mythic drumbeat echoing the ancient tread of pallbearers returning a fallen sacrifice to the earth. The chaotic, commercial noise of Milton hushed into a low, breathless murmur. The rhythmic, labored tramp of heavy boots cut a clear acoustic path through the sulfurous air. 

Drawn by an irresistible impulse, they moved to the door. Six men, including three policemen, walked in the middle of the road. Upon their shoulders rested a wooden door, taken from its hinges, bearing a heavy, wet burden. Constant droppings of water hit the cobblestones with a rhythmic, sickening *splat*. 

“We found him i’ th’ brook in the field beyond there,” a bearer muttered to the questioning crowd. “He was a determined chap. He lay with his face downwards. He was sick enough o’ living.” 

They lowered the door. The olfactory shock hit Margaret instantly—the natural, damp-earth scent of a drowned man was entirely eclipsed by the sharp, chemical reek of industrial purple dye. The brook, choked with the mill’s toxic runoff, had stained his swollen, agonizingly distorted face. Every lock of his thin hair was a conduit for the purple-tinted water, a gruesome marriage of a man to the polluted machinery that had starved him. It was John Boucher. 

Margaret, acting on a flash of ancient, pious instinct, stepped forward and draped her handkerchief over his glassy eyes. Nicholas Higgins stood rooted, his breath catching in a weak, piping wheeze. 

“Higgins, thou knowed him!” a man said. “Thou mun go tell the wife. Do it gently, man.” 

“I canna go,” Higgins whispered, the trauma robbing him of his native dialect’s strength. “I’m welly felled wi’ seeing him. We wasn’t friends; and now he’s dead.” 

The responsibility fell to Margaret. She approached the Boucher home and knocked, but the sound was instantly swallowed by a cacophony of ill-ordered children. The chaotic, untuned shrieks pierced the acoustic space, a sonic manifestation of severe psychological distress and utter neglect. The room smelled of sour milk and damp rot. 

Mrs. Boucher rocked by an ill-redd-up fireplace, clutching a year-old urchin who was crying for “daddy” and “butty.” There was no bread to give him, no thick slices of buttered loaf that might offer the deep, sensory comfort of a mother’s kitchen. 

“He’s his father’s darling,” Mrs. Boucher muttered in a low, growling tone, her dialect thickening as her wildness grew. It was a grief completely untranslatable to the polished English of the southern gentility. “Why dunno yo’ speak? Why dun yo’ stare at me wi’ your great pitiful eyes? Where’s John?” 

When Margaret’s tearful silence delivered the truth, Mrs. Boucher sank back with a cry that ripped through the room’s fragile air. “Oh, my God!” 

The other children, their brains dull and languid from chronic malnutrition, slowly deciphered the mystery. They set up a collective wail of despair, a raw, grating sound of absolute loss that Margaret scarcely knew how to endure. Mrs. Boucher quivered, sliding into a faint, overwhelmed by the sensory assault of her shattered world. 

A neighbor woman, trailing the procession, stepped inside. With a profound understanding of domestic rhythm, she recognized the immediate need to restore both acoustic and physical order. 

“I bid ’em begone, and each take a child with ’em,” the neighbor told Margaret, clearing the room of idle gazers. “The childer are sure of a bellyful to-day, and of kindness too.” 

When Mrs. Boucher roused, she wept feebly at the rough probing of her sorrow, her native tongue articulating the agonizing inadequacy of her situation. “He has left me alone wi’ a’ these children!” 

“Best comfort now would be the feel of a child at her heart,” the neighbor advised gently. “I’m sorry they took the babby.” 

Margaret went to fetch little Johnnie. She found him with his face richly smeared with grease and crumbs, a half-eaten crust of heavily buttered oatcake in his fist. He was anchored to the earth by the primal, grounding act of mastication, finding warmth and sustenance even as his familial line fractured. She carried him back and placed him in his mother’s arms, leaving the two to merge their cries in a shared, untranslatable dialect of comfort. 

As Margaret and her father walked slowly back up the street, they paused at Higgins’s closed door. They tried the latch; it was bolted. Inside, a heavy book fell to the floor with a dull thud. 

“Nicholas!” Margaret called softly. “Won’t you let us come in?” 

“No,” his voice came through the thick wood, muffled and definitive. “I spoke as plain as I could, ’bout using words, when I bolted th’ door. Let me be, this day.” 

He had retreated into the only sanctuary left to him—an absolute, unyielding silence, turning his back on a lexicon that could no longer articulate the depths of their collective starvation.
