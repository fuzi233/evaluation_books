"By the soft green light in the woody glade, / On the banks of moss where thy childhood played..."

Margaret Hale stood once more in her morning dress, traveling quietly home with her father, who had journeyed north to assist at the wedding. Her mother, however, had been detained at Helstone by a multitude of vague excuses, none of which anyone truly understood save Mr. Hale himself. He knew perfectly well that all his arguments in favor of a grey satin gown—one that sat uncomfortably halfway between antiquity and novelty—had proven futile. Lacking the funds to outfit his wife anew from top to bottom, Mrs. Hale simply would not appear at the wedding of her only sister's only child. Had Mrs. Shaw divined the true reason for her sister-in-law's absence, she likely would have showered gowns upon her; but it had been nearly twenty years since Mrs. Shaw was the poor, pretty Miss Beresford, and she had genuinely forgotten all grievances save one: the unhappiness born of disparity in age within marriage, a topic on which she could discourse for half an hour at a stretch. "Dearest Maria," she would muse, reflecting on her sister's lot, "married for love, what can she possibly lack in this world?"

If Mrs. Hale spoke truthfully, her answer might have been a ready-made list: *a silver-grey glacé silk, a white chip bonnet, oh! dozens of things for the wedding, and hundreds for the house.* Yet, Margaret merely knew that her mother had deemed it inconvenient to travel. She was not sorry that their reunion would occur at Helstone Parsonage rather than amidst the chaotic whirlwind of the previous few days in Harley Street, where she had been forced to play the role of Figaro, summoned everywhere simultaneously. Her mind and body ached with the recollection of everything she had done and said within the last forty-eight hours. Amongst the hurried farewells, the memory of those she had lived with so long pressed heavily upon her, bringing a sad regret for times that were irrevocably gone. It mattered not what those times had been; they were vanished forever.

As they traveled toward her beloved home—the place and life she had yearned for for years—Margaret felt a heaviness in her heart that she had never anticipated. Just before sleep dulls the sharp edges of sensation, the longing is most acute. She tore her mind away from the past to contemplate the bright, serene future. Her gaze shifted from visions of what had been to the reality before her: her dear father leaning back, asleep in the railway carriage. His blue-black hair was now grey, lying thinly over his brow. The bones of his face were starkly visible—not disfiguring, for his features were finely cut, lending them a certain grace, though lacking the comeliness of youth. His face was in repose, yet it bore the marks of rest following weariness rather than the serene calm of a placid existence.

Margaret was painfully struck by this worn, anxious expression. She traced the lines that spoke so plainly of habitual distress and depression back to the known circumstances of her father's life. "Poor Frederick!" she sighed inwardly. "Oh, if Frederick had been a clergyman instead of joining the navy, and thus lost to us all! I wish I knew the whole truth. I never understood it from Aunt Shaw; I only knew he could not return to England because of that terrible affair. Poor dear papa! How sad he looks! I am so glad I am going home, to be at hand to comfort him and mamma."

She prepared a bright smile, devoid of any trace of fatigue, to greet her father upon awakening. He smiled back, but faintly, as if the effort were unusual. His face settled back into its familiar lines of anxiety. He possessed a habit of half-opening his mouth as if to speak, a gesture that constantly unsettled the form of his lips, giving his visage an air of indecision. Yet, he retained the same large, soft eyes as his daughter—eyes that moved slowly and almost grandly within their orbits, veiled by transparent white eyelids. Margaret was more like him than like her mother. Occasionally, people marveled that parents so handsome could produce a daughter described as "not beautiful at all." Her mouth was wide, not a tight rosebud capable of uttering only "yes" or "no," but a soft curve of rich red lips. Her skin, though not porcelain pale, possessed an ivory smoothness and delicacy. While her general demeanor was perhaps too dignified and reserved for one so young, speaking to her father transformed her; she became bright as the morning, full of dimples and glances radiating childish gladness and boundless hope.

It was the latter part of July when Margaret returned home. The forest trees stood in a dark, full, dusky green; the fern beneath them caught the slanting sunbeams. The weather was sultry and broodingly still. Margaret loved to tramp alongside her father, crushing the fern with a cruel glee as it yielded under her light foot, releasing its peculiar fragrance. Out on the broad commons, bathed in warm, scented light, she saw multitudes of wild, free creatures reveling in the sunshine and the herbs it nourished. This life—at least these walks—realized all her anticipations. She took a profound pride in her forest; its inhabitants were her people. She made hearty friends with them, learned and delighted in their peculiar dialect, embraced their freedom, nursed their babies, and conversed or read aloud to their elders with slow distinctness. She carried dainties to the sick and resolved soon to teach at the school her father attended daily, viewing it as an appointed task. Yet, she was continually tempted away to visit individual friends—men, women, or children—in the green shade of the cottages scattered throughout the woods. Her outdoor life was perfect.

Her indoor life, however, held its drawbacks. With the healthy shame of a child, she blamed herself for her keen insight into the fact that all was not as it should be within the vicarage walls. Her mother, always so kind and tender, seemed intermittently discontented with their situation. She believed the Bishop strangely neglected his episcopal duties by failing to grant Mr. Hale a better living, and she almost reproached her husband for his inability to declare his wish to leave the parish and accept a larger charge. Mr. Hale would sigh deeply in response, stating that if he could do what he ought in little Helstone, he should be thankful, yet every day he appeared more overwhelmed; the world seemed increasingly bewildering. At each renewed urging from his wife to seek preferment, Margaret observed her father shrinking further into himself. During these moments, she strove to reconcile her mother to Helstone.

Mrs. Hale claimed that the proximity of so many trees affected her health. Margaret would attempt to lure her onto the beautiful, broad, upland common, streaked with sunlight and shadow, convinced that her mother had grown too accustomed to an indoor existence, rarely venturing beyond the church, the school, and neighboring cottages. This remedy offered temporary relief, but as autumn advanced and the weather grew changeable, her mother's conviction of the place's unhealthiness intensified. She complained more frequently that her husband—who was more learned than Mr. Hume and a better parish priest than Mr. Houldsworth—should not have secured the preferment those two former neighbors had achieved. This erosion of domestic peace, driven by long hours of discontent, was something Margaret was wholly unprepared for.

She had known, and indeed reveled in the notion, that returning home meant relinquishing many luxuries that had previously been mere troubles and trammels to her freedom in Harley Street. Her keen enjoyment of every sensuous pleasure was finely balanced, if not outweighed, by her conscious pride in her ability to dispense with them entirely if necessary. However, clouds rarely approach from the direction where one expects them. There had been slight complaints and passing regrets from her mother regarding trifles connected to Helstone and her father's position during Margaret's earlier holiday visits, but amidst the general happiness of recalling those times, she had overlooked the small, unpleasant details.

In the latter half of September, autumnal rains and storms descended, forcing Margaret to remain indoors more than she had previously done. Helstone lay at a considerable distance from neighbors sharing their standard of cultivation. "It is undoubtedly one of the most out-of-the-way places in England," Mrs. Hale remarked in one of her plaintive moods. "I cannot help constantly regretting that papa has no one to associate with here; he is so thrown away, seeing nothing but farmers and laborers from week's end to week's end. If we only lived on the other side of the parish, it would be different; there we should be almost within walking distance of the Stansfields; certainly the Gormons would be reachable on foot."

"Gormons?" Margaret asked. "Are those the Gormons who made their fortunes in trade at Southampton? Oh! I'm glad we don't visit them. I don't like shopkeepers. I believe we are far better off, knowing only cottagers and laborers, and people without pretense."

"You must not be so fastidious, Margaret, dear!" her mother replied, secretly thinking of a young and handsome Mr. Gormon she had once encountered at Mr. Hume's.

"No! I consider my tastes very comprehensive. I like all people whose occupations involve the land; I like soldiers and sailors, and the three learned professions, as they call them. I am sure you do not expect me to admire butchers, bakers, and candlestick-makers, do you, Mamma?"
