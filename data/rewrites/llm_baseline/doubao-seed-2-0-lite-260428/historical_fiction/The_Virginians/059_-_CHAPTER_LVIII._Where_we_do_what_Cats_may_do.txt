It has already been noted that our two Virginian brothers, with that peculiar brand of earnest, ill-informed wisdom common to spirited youth, had adopted staunch Jacobite opinions and professed an almost absurd devotion to the exiled Stuart royal line. The banished prince had formally recognized Madam Esmond’s father as the rightful Marquis of Esmond, and she could not bring herself to bear ill will toward an unfortunate royal house that had been so eager to acknowledge the merits of her own family. As for the petty, scurrilous gossip linking her sister Madame de Bernstein to the Old Chevalier, she tossed the memory of that odious tale aside with complete scorn, asserting quite truthfully that the first two Hanoverian monarchs were no more moral in their private lives than any Stuart king that ever sat on the British throne.

That said, the reigning de facto king was still the king, duly anointed and crowned in church, and the man who had soundly defeated the Stuart claimant outright when the two factions met on the battlefield to fight for the crown. Madam’s unshakable opinion was, then, that her sons owed it to themselves as well as to their sovereign to make their proper appearance at his royal court. If His Majesty saw fit to bestow a well-paid sinecure, or a red or blue ribbon of honour on either of the lads, she would not have been the least bit surprised. She had full faith the King knew the Virginian Esmond name as well as he knew the names of half the oldest peers of his realm. So the boys received strict orders to present themselves at court, and one can only imagine how cross their mother would have been to learn that George had borrowed Harry’s finest laced embroidered coat the very day he went to make his bow to the sovereign.

A hundred years ago, the King’s drawing room was open almost every day to the nobility and gentry of the realm, and loyal subjects – especially now that war was raging across the colonies and the high seas – could indulge their eagerness to glimpse the august sovereign a dozen times a month. The king’s council had wisely decided that risking the royal person to capture by French warships while crossing the Channel to visit his beloved native Hanover would be an inestimable loss to the British Isles, so the monarch was obliged to forgo those long-dreamed-of trips to Herrenhausen and remain, very reluctantly, among his adoring British subjects. A Hanoverian countess whose tender friendship had endeared her to the prince did her best to console him for the forced separation from his home, and from the lips of the Countess of Walmoden (whom the Crown had graciously raised to high British peerage) the Defender of the Faith could still hear the soft accents of his native German.

To this beloved sovereign, George Warrington asked his assiduous courtier uncle Sir Miles to present him. As General Lambert also had business at Kensington that day, to thank the King for his recent promotion, the two men made the journey together in a hired hackney-coach, now that Lord Wrotham had returned to his London townhouse and reclaimed the use of his own private carriage. They alighted at Kensington Palace Gate, where the sentries on duty knew and saluted the old general, and proceeded on foot at a modest pace toward the summer residence of the King.

Walking under the palace’s grand stone portico, they entered the long gallery that led to the famous black marble staircase, richly decorated and frescoed by the artist Mr. Kent, then passed through a succession of chambers hung with fine tapestries, lined with master paintings and marble busts, until they reached the King’s great drawing room. Here hung Titian’s famous *Venus*, and other works including Rubens’ *St. Francis Adoring the Infant Saviour*. Along with the rest of the waiting courtiers, the two gentlemen lingered there until His Majesty emerged from his private apartments, where he had been in conference with what the newspapers of the day referred to discreetly as his M-j-ty’s M-n-st-rs.

George Warrington, who had never stepped foot in a royal palace before, had plenty of time to admire the grand surroundings and observe the crowds around him. He saw great works of fine art for the first time in his life, lingering delightedly over Van Dyck’s magnificent portrait of King Charles the First, his queen and their children, and Tintoret’s grand canvas of Esther before Ahasuerus, where all the figures were clad in sumptuous period Venetian dress. He was so enraptured by the paintings that he barely heard General Lambert whispering the names of the notables scattered around the room in his ear.

“Yonder are two lords of the Admiralty,” Lambert murmured. “Mr. Gilbert Elliot, and Admiral Boscawen – the man whose fleet fired the first shot in your Virginia waters two years back. That stout gentleman fairly plastered in gold lace is Mr. Fox, who was once Minister, and is now content to serve as Paymaster with a princely salary.”
“His waistcoat is stitched like it was mined straight from Potosi,” George laughed.
“Aye, he loves to gather wealth and spend it freely,” Lambert went on. “Yonder is my Lord Chief Justice Willes, talking to my Lord of Salisbury, Doctor Headley – if he serves God half as faithfully as he serves the King, he’ll rise to very high honours in Heaven. He dates back to your grandfather’s time, loved by Dick Steele and hated by Dean Swift. With them is the Bishop of London, the learned Doctor Sherlock. The lawn-sleeved lords of the church don’t get half the deference they used to these days. I can remember when I was a little boy, my mother made me kneel down to the Bishop of Rochester – the one who fled over the water to serve that unnamed Stuart pretender, that Perkin Warbeck of a bishop. That handsome fair-haired man is Admiral Tom Smith, they call him Tom of Ten Thousand in the fleet. He presided over poor Byng’s court martial, and fought in vain to save him from the firing squad. The French ambassador had him broken of his rank as a lieutenant for daring to make a French man-o’-war lower its topsails to him – and our King made him a full captain the very next day. That tall, haughty man is Lord George Sackville. Now that I’m a Major-General myself, I’ll see he treats me better than a footman. I wish our stout old Blakeney was here – he’s the darling of every common soldier, and as kind and brave as that poker of a nobleman is arrogant and – Your lordship, your very humble servant. This is a young gentleman just fresh from America, who was there at that sad Braddock expedition two years ago.”
“Oh indeed?” drawled the haughty lord, looking down his nose. “I have the honour of addressing Mr. —?”
“Major-General Lambert, at your lordship’s service, who served in His Majesty’s forces long before you held your commission. Mr. Warrington, that man over yonder is Mr. Speaker Onslow, the first commoner in all of England. Where is your uncle? If Sir Miles delays much longer, I shall present you to the King myself.”
As he spoke, the old general turned his full attention back to George, paying the arrogant noble no further notice, and the man stalked off looking stunned, as if he could not believe anyone would dare treat him with such lack of deference – for a hundred years ago, a lord was a lord, and expected universal awe as a matter of right.

Presently Sir Miles’s scarlet waistcoat came bobbing through the crowd, and the three men greeted each other warmly. The general had quit Lord Wrotham’s house now that his lordship had returned to town, and Sir Miles gushed that all the neighbourhood missed him terribly. “My daughters were quite half in love with your two lovely girls, upon my word they were,” he cried. “My Lady Warrington and the girls have been scheming for weeks to find a proper way to make your acquaintance. We feel like we are old friends already! We love you, sir, for all your kindness to our dear nephews – my own flesh and blood, my poor dear brother’s boys. I confess we were a little jealous, watching them trudge off to your house so often. I’ve said to my Lady Warrington a dozen times, ‘My dear, why do we not invite the General and his family over for a plain family dinner, no fuss at all?’ Pray carry my sincerest respects to Mrs. Lambert, and thank her for all she has done for our lads.” He brushed a hand over his eyes, quite overcome with affectionate emotion, and George had to struggle mightily to keep a straight face.

Just then the doors to the King’s private apartments swung open, and the pages stepped out, preceding His Majesty. He was followed by his burly, corpulent son the Duke of Cumberland, all clad in blazing scarlet coat and matching scarlet face, and behind them came a train of officers and ministers – among whom George immediately spotted the famous Secretary Pitt, tall as a colossus, with his eagle beak of a nose and his grave, majestic bearing. Even at a distance of a hundred years, the writer feels a genuine awe looking back at that solemn figure, and an urge to doff his hat. We do not tremble at the sight of George the Second, nor are our eyes dazzled by the Duke of Culloden and Fontenoy’s portentous bulk – but the Great Commoner, the terrible Cornet of Horse, strides across the narrow map of 18th century Britain like a Colossus, and we fall quiet as he passes by in his gouty shoes, his thunderbolt hand wrapped in soft flannel. No doubt he has just come from a long, stormy interview with his royal master, boring the sharp little old king for hours with grand, thunderous speeches full of noble rhetoric even on the most trivial topics, rhetoric the thrifty, plain-spoken king heartily detests. The sublime minister passes solemnly through the crowd, and every courtier lines up respectfully against the walls.

The King walks the circle of guests, his royal son lingering a little behind to chat with favoured individuals. He is a small, sharp, bright-complexioned old man with very prominent eyes, dressed in plain old-fashioned snuff-coloured coat and brown stockings, his only ornament the blue sash of the Order of the Garter. He speaks English with a thick German accent, but with great shrewdness and simplicity, stopping to speak to those he wishes to notice, and passing others with a brief nod. He knew General Lambert very well, who had served under him at Dettingen and fought for his son in the Scottish rebellion, and he congratulated him heartily on his promotion.
“It is not always easy to do as one likes,” the King said kindly, “but I was very glad to grant that favour in your case, General – there is no better officer in my entire army.” The old veteran blushed bright red and bowed, completely gratified.

Meanwhile the King’s eyes fell on Sir Miles Warrington, whom he knew perfectly as the man who begged every minister for every available favour, and then on the young man standing at his side. “Who is this?” he asked, pointing directly at George, who stood clad in poor Harry’s best embroidered coat, holding himself in a respectful attitude. Sir Miles bowed so low he nearly bent double, and informed His Majesty that this was his Virginian nephew, Mr. George Warrington, who had come to pay his humble duty to the crown.
“Ah, so this is the other brother?” the King said dryly. “Good thing he made it over in time, or the first brother would have spent all the family fortune. My Lord Bishop of Salisbury, what are you doing out in this bitter weather? You ought to be at home in your warm bed!” With that, the little king wandered on to greet the rest of the courtiers.

Sir Miles was almost beside himself with delight at the royal condescension. He clapped George on the shoulder. “God bless you, my boy!” he cried. “Did I not tell you you would see the greatest monarch and finest gentleman alive? Is he not the very pattern of a king, my Lord Bishop?”
“That he is!” the bishop cried, clasping his hands and rolling his eyes up to the ceiling. “The best of princes and of men!”
“Yonder young man surrounded by the crowd is Master Louis, Lady Yarmouth’s favourite nephew,” Lambert pointed out to George, and before long the stout Duke of Cumberland made his way over to their little group. He clapped Lambert on the hand. “Congratulate you on your promotion, Lambert,” he said.
“Sir, I owe it entirely to your Royal Highness’s good offices,” the general said gratefully.
“Not at all – you deserved it long ago. Always been a good officer. No doubt we’ll find some active work for you soon. Is this the young gentleman James Wolfe wrote to me about?”
“His brother, sir.”
“Ah, the famous Fortunate Youth! You were with poor Ned Braddock in America, were you? Taken prisoner, and lucky enough to escape. Come and see me at my house in Pall Mall, lad. Bring him to my next levee, Lambert.” And with that, the broad back of the royal prince turned away.

“It’s pouring rain!” Sir Miles cried. “You two came on foot? No, no, you will ride home with me in my coach. I will hear no denial!” he insisted, and bundled George and Lambert back to his Hill Street townhouse. He presented the general to Lady Warrington and his two daughters Flora and Dora, and insisted they stay for a light collation, as they must be cold and hungry after their time at court.
“What, only cold mutton? An old soldier can eat cold mutton well enough,” Lambert laughed.
“And a glass of my Lady Warrington’s own spiced cordial, made by her own hand, to chase that cold palace chill away,” Sir Miles added heartily. “Capital mutton, six years old, straight off our country estate! The Warringtons have loved good mutton ever since the Conquest, you know. We live very plainly here – can’t afford to feed our rascally servants on seven-year-old mutton, ha!”

Sir Miles proceeded to tell his wife and daughters a wildly exaggerated, glowing account of George’s reception at court, so full of fulsome praise that George could barely recognize the quiet half-snub he had actually gotten from the king, and could not bring himself to contradict his uncle in front of his whole family. General Lambert sat by staring in complete disbelief – he had been at the same court that day, and seen none of the grand, flattering audiences Sir Miles described.
“We are all so proud of you, dear George,” the whole family chorused. “We love you not for your great estates or your ancient name, but for the courage you showed fighting for your country and your king.”
A little piping voice came from small Master Miles, the youngest child: “I still like Harry best.”
Sir Miles shooed the little boy off to his tutor, then turned back to Lambert, insisting he and his wife and daughters must come for a proper formal dinner at their house very soon, no refusals allowed. The ladies dragged George off to their drawing room, insisting he stay and tell them every last gory detail of the Braddock battle and his dramatic escape from the Indians, and Tom Claypool wandered in to hear the end of the tale, while Flora dabbed at her eyes with her handkerchief over the story.

When General Lambert finally got home, he could not help regaling his wife with a full account of the absurd new family they had met. The new fashionable slang word “humbug” had just come into vogue, and that was exactly the word he used to describe the entire Warrington household. He told her of the father’s over-the-top forced hospitality, the mother’s pompous flattery, the girls’ obvious coquetry, the mutton so tough it nearly broke his teeth, and the cordial that tasted like spiced turpentine. Mrs. Lambert of course contrasted their current fawning over rich, newly respectable George with the way they had snubbed poor Harry when he was visiting them earlier, before he had any fortune to make them polite.
“Is that Miss Warrington really so handsome?” Mrs. Lambert asked.
“Extremely handsome,” Lambert laughed, “and the most shameless little flirt I have ever set eyes on in my life.”
“The horrible hypocrites! I have no patience for people like that!” Mrs. Lambert huffed.
To that, her husband only answered with a silly monosyllable: “Bo!”
“Why on earth did you say ‘Bo!’ Martin?” his wife asked, confused.
“I say ‘Bo!’ to a goose, my dear,” he grinned.
Mrs. Lambert protested that she had no idea what he was talking about, what silly secret thought he was hiding, and Lambert only smiled wider and said: “Of course you don’t.”
