The dinner concluded without a trace of the earlier storm. Madame de Bernstein's face was smooth, her manner a practiced cordiality that extended even to Harry. She praised the cook, declared the fricassee superior to anything the Castlewood moats could produce, and dismissed the idea of opening a new bottle for an elderly woman. When the vintage was poured, she raised her glass to Madam Esmond Warrington, hoping Harry had secured his mother's blessing to bring an English bride home. Harry did not recall his grandmother; he recalled only the Baroness's mother, a figure of legendary beauty whose marriage to the older Colonel Esmond served as proof that disparity was a vanity.

My Lady Castlewood and her children remained silent observers while the Baroness spun tales of her lineage, her happiness, and the universal admiration of her union. The atmosphere shifted, the tension dissolving into a collective performance of reconciliation. Lord Castlewood watched with a puzzled stillness, masking any suspicion of his aunt's sudden transformation. The Baroness's good humor was infectious; no guest escaped a gracious word.

When the chaplain noted the change, the Baroness confessed her sins with theatrical humility. She recounted how, as a child in this very room, she had once been wayward, scolding her sister and boxing Harry Warrington's ears simply because he refused to accompany her to Tunbridge Wells. "I was just as wayward then," she declared, "at seven years old, as I am today at seventy." She begged forgiveness for her weariness, for telling Maria that her company was burdensome.

"I absolve your ladyship!" the chaplain cried.

"But you do not know how cross I was," she insisted, detailing her cruelty. "Maria, I ask your pardon."

"Sure, madam, you have done me no wrong," Maria replied softly.

"Indeed I have," the Baroness countered, her voice rising. "Because I was weary of myself, I told you your presence would be wearisome. Yet you offered to come. I beg, I pray, I command that you come."

Lord Castlewood filled his glass, his expression unreadable. The comedy was complete.

"You must come and stay with me, my dear," Maria said, forcing a smile. "Will you spare your sister to me?"

"Lady Maria Esmond is of age to decide," Lord Castlewood bowed. "If any of us can be of use..."

The sentence hung in the air, interpreted by the household as a dismissal of the old woman's interference. "Oh, Tunbridge will be delightful!" Maria sighed.

Harry drew patterns on the tablecloth with his finger. His mind raced through a catalogue of anticipated pleasures: walks, rides, endless conversation, secluded summer houses, moonlight, and the soft sounds of affection. The day was approaching. Everyone else was departing—the ladies to the waters, the lords to their friends—and he was to remain alone with his divine charmer. The thought was maddening. These people were leaving him to enjoy a heaven that felt too real to be true.

"I knew it couldn't be," Harry thought. "Something would happen to take her from me."

"But you will ride with us to Tunbridge, nephew Warrington?" Madame de Bernstein asked. "To keep us from the highwaymen?"

Harry felt the heat rise in his cheeks. He forced his voice steady. Yes, he would ride. He would ensure their safety. Danger appealed to him; he would slay a thousand highwaymen if they threatened his mistress's coach. Even if he could not speak to her, he would be near. He would press her hand at the inn, feel it rest in his as he guided her to the carriage. Two days to Tunbridge, perhaps a night or two at the Wells. Was the condemned man at Newgate not grateful for a brief reprieve?

They had not chosen to describe the depth of Harry's imbecility, only to indicate it. Some suffer from love gently; others are consumed by the fever until death or old age carries the scars. It was unfair to record his ravings, especially since he loved a woman twice his age. Had not a young gentleman committed suicide for Mademoiselle Ninon de l'Enclos, only to discover she was his grandmother? Perhaps Harry was making an ass of himself. Let the honest fellows kick and abuse him; they recognized a brother-donkey.

"You will stay with us for a day or two at the Wells," the Baroness continued. "Then you can return to Castlewood and the partridge-shooting."

"A whole week of heaven!" Harry agreed.

"And as there is sure to be plenty of company," the lady added graciously, "I shall be able to present you."

"Company! Ah! I shan't need company," Harry sighed.

"I mean that I shall be quite contented in the company of you two ladies," he corrected eagerly.

That evening, as the ladies retired and Lord Castlewood withdrew to his chambers, the three gentlemen assembled in Harry's small room before the punch-bowl. Will produced fresh decks of cards, proposing to settle Harry's debts by doubling or acquitting them. The chaplain possessed no ready cash, nor did Lord Castlewood's younger brother. Harry desired neither victory nor defeat; he feared giving pain to the brother of his adored Maria or appearing ungenerous to her kin. He was willing to grant them their revenge.

Until midnight, he would play whatever stakes they named.

The dice rattled, the cards shuffled. Likely, Harry paid no attention to the game itself. While the cards flew, his mind was elsewhere. He pictured his beloved sitting with her golden hair spread beneath her maid's fingers. He saw her kneeling in prayer, then sinking to rest behind her damask curtains. "Bless, bless her!"

"You double us all round?" Will shouted. "I'll take a card on each of mine. Ten... Queen... Two natural vingt-et-un! Since you doubled us, you owe me—"

Volleys of oaths erupted from Will and the chaplain at the Virginian's fortune. He won because he did not wish to win. Fortune, that coquettish jade, favored him because his thoughts dwelt on another nymph, perhaps as fickle as herself. Will and the chaplain played against him, assuming the wealthy Virginian wished to let them recover their losses. But this was not Harry's intent.

He had played hundreds of such games at home, keeping the details from his mother, learning to play fair and expect fairness in return.

"The luck seems to be with me, cousin," Harry said calmly, ignoring Will's curses. "I do not want to press it, but I am not a fool to fling it away. I have quite a heap of your promises on paper. If we continue, let us have the dollars on the table, or their worth."

"Always the way with you rich men," Will grumbled. "Never lend except on security—always win because you are rich."

"Faith, cousin, you have been flinging my riches in my face lately," the chaplain groaned. "What have we to play against our conqueror? My new gown? Set me five pieces against it? Or my Chrysostom, Foxe's Martyrs, Baker's Chronicle, and a cow with her calf?"

"I will bet one of cousin Will's notes for twenty pounds," Harry cried, producing a document. "Or my brown mare, backed red against your notes of hand."

"I have my horse. Fifty guineas," Will bellowed.

Harry accepted both offers. Within ten minutes, the horse and the mare had changed hands. Will swore more fiercely than before; the parson threw his wig to the ground, matching his pupil in volume. Harry remained calm, not elated by his triumph. They had asked him to play, and he had played. He knew he would win.

O beloved slumbering angel, he thought, am I not sure of victory when you are kind to me?

He looked out his window toward the casement on the opposite side of the court, the one he knew belonged to her. He had forgotten his victims and their groans, their bad luck crossing the courtyard. Under the brilliant flickering star, behind that lit window, lay his joy, his heart, his treasure.
