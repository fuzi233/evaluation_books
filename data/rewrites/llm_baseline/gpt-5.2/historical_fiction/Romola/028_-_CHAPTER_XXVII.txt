Tito had crossed the Ponte Rubaconte with his newest purchase hidden beneath his mantle, the metal hugging his ribs like a second, colder flesh. The Arno below ran thick and whispering, carrying the day’s uproar away in broken ripples; but the bridge stones still held the trampling echoes of hoof and boot, and the air tasted of wet leather, trodden rose-leaves, and the faint sourness that follows a crowd.

At home, Romola paced the old library with the unwearied step of one who has learned to move in a narrow world and yet keep it from stifling her. The shelves, the tables, the ink-horns and pens—all those familiar instruments of her father’s life—stood about her like silent witnesses. She had refused the day’s spectacle. Almost every window in Florence had flowered with faces to watch the French king and his nobles, but Romola’s face had not been among them. Since her father’s death, the very notion of festivity felt like a dish set before an empty chair.

He had died three months ago—swiftly, in the way sudden endings sometimes come to those who have long imagined their own slow decline. Even now she could taste the hour again as one tastes something bitter that will not be washed away by sweetness.

The bell had sounded long past the accustomed evening, and Bardo’s head, bent in his chair, had lifted with a listening impatience that Romola knew too well.

“Is not Tito coming to write?” he had said, as if he were naming a necessity of nature, like bread at table.

“No, father; he had to go to a supper at the cardinal’s,” Romola answered, turning excuse into gentleness as women will, as if softness could make up for absence. “You know he is wanted so much by every one.”

“Ah! then perhaps he will bring some positive word about the library,” Bardo said, catching at hope the way an old man catches at warmth. “The cardinal promised last week.”

She had watched his face settle—pacified, for a moment, by that thin promise. Then, after silence, a sudden flush had risen, the blood protesting against resignation.

“I must go on without him, Romola. Get the pen. He has brought me no new text to comment on; but I must say what I want to say about the New Platonists. I shall die and nothing will have been done. Make haste, my Romola.”

She had risen at once—docile, trained by years to spring at the sound of that need—and in the next minute stood ready, pen poised, the ink smelling sharp and alive.

“I am ready, father.”

But the room had fallen into a stillness that was not one of his pauses for thought. She had waited, accustomed to those long gaps where his mind wandered and returned with a phrase that had been ripened in silence. She had not looked up at once.

When she did, the posture had not changed; only life had gone out of it. She had spoken again—too late—“I am quite ready, father!” and that second readiness had been answered by the unanswering.

In the first violence of grief there had come, like a stealthy sweetness in a draught one meant to drink only for medicine, a thought that shamed her: Perhaps my life with Tito will be more perfect now. She had remembered it afterwards with a sting of indignation against herself, as if she had found in her own heart a foreign coin, bright and false.

For the dream she had carried—of three lives fitted together without friction, father and husband and herself as one happiness—had never come to full fruition. The shower of bridal sweets had dropped thorns among the sugared almonds. The crowned Ariadne under the snowing roses had felt more and more the hidden pricks in the garland.

And still, she told herself, it was not Tito’s fault. He had been gentle; he had never ceased to be gentle. Even to her father he had been all smiles and soft answers, laying phrases as deftly as a cook lays herbs over bitterness so that a man may swallow what he would otherwise reject. Yet the daily weight of Bardo’s demands—monotonous, exacting, unrelenting as hunger—could not be borne by any one but herself, month after month, year after year. She could see that now with the clear sight that comes when a burden is finally set down. She alone had the patience that sprang from blood and reverence and long habit; even she had sometimes been inwardly rebellious, her spirit cramped like a limb held too long in one posture.

Before their marriage, and even for some time after, Tito had seemed unwearying, more ready than she; but novelty is a sauce that sharpens appetite, and we lift loads readily when we believe ourselves strong. Pressure grows irksome by degrees, until the desire for relief becomes like thirst.

Romola told herself she had been foolish in girlhood, ignorant in the way of marriage. She would not make unfair demands on the man to whom she had given her deepest love and worship. If sadness had breathed on their lot while she watched her father sink, month after month, from elation into disappointment as Tito gave him less time and more excuses—if she had felt a lonely heartache when Tito’s share in the joint work dwindled to bland refusals—that sadness, she insisted, was destiny’s doing, not Tito’s. If he stayed away, it was because they were never alone; if he went to other engagements when her timid pleading might have kept him, he excused himself with such charming gaiety, and lingered with such fond playfulness that her complaint dissolved even while it rose. The heartache remained, but it was swallowed as women swallow the bitter edge of reality for love’s sake.

Her imagination worked incessantly to reconcile what she felt with what she believed. How could Tito be as good as she had thought him, and yet find it impossible to sacrifice those pleasures that society offers—pleasures more vivid to a bright creature like him than to graver natures? She herself, had she been free, would have liked more gaiety, more admiration; but she had given that up for her father, and she would have given up much more at Tito’s lightest wish. Perhaps their difference was only the old difference between man and woman: her affection absorbed her wholly, while his overflowed into the world. If there were inferiority, she insisted it must be hers. Tito was kinder than she, better-tempered, less proud; he had no angry retorts. He met complaints with sweetness and evaded unpleasantness as quietly as a cat slips from a noisy courtyard.

And Romola—large-natured enough to distrust herself—doubted her own impressions the more because love and pride both demanded a story she could live with. Disappointment—there was no softer word that told the truth—might be the common portion of wives; she did not know their hearts. Yet her case had its own sharpness: her father’s claims had asked unusual sacrifice from her husband, and Tito’s love had not been great enough to make it easy. She would not resent a self-delusion. Better any endurance than the admission, even to herself, that Tito acted unworthily. To cease to love would be to stand on the edge of a world that breaks away beneath one’s feet.

And now her father was gone. Even in the first moments when his chair stood empty, hope had crept in: there would be no more importunate claim dividing her from Tito. Their young lives would flow in one current; the true marriage would begin.

But guilt shadowed that hope. It made her clutch with the more fervour at the one thing she could still do for her father: fulfil his supreme wish. Piety to his memory would be her atonement for the joy that had dared to rise from his death.

Bardo’s life had been like a long seed-time without harvest: laborious, simple, untainted by vulgar ambitions, embittered by frustrated hopes, ending in darkness. What remained of it—besides the tablet in Santa Croce and an unfinished commentary begun on Tito’s text—was the collection he had scraped together with half a century’s frugality: manuscripts, coins, antique fragments, the very bones and organs of dead thought. To Romola, the fulfilment of his ambition about the library was no mere duty; it was sacramental, like keeping a lamp burning by a grave.

The collection was safe from creditors. When the deficit had been ascertained, Bernardo del Nero—no rich man by Florentine standards, yet generous in his old-fashioned way—had advanced the sum, accepting the lien as security.

“The State will repay me,” he had said, making light of a service that cost him inconvenience. “If the cardinal finds a building, as he seems to say he will, our Signoria may consent to do the rest. I have no children; I can afford the risk.”

But in the last ten days hope in the Medici had gone out like a candle in a draught. Even the famed Medicean treasures in the Via Larga were in danger of being scattered. French agents, sniffing through Florence as men sniff through a pantry, had begun to say—boldly, with that smooth greed that calls itself right—that Lorenzo’s antique gems belonged by nature to the first nation in Europe. And the Florentine State, with war for Pisa hanging like a hungry mouth and subsidies to the French king sure as taxes, might well prefer coin to manuscripts. Money was the only language some councils listened to, and it was a loud one.

Romola had been reared in learned seclusion, where the past was more vivid than the present. She could weigh the Pnyx and the Forum in her mind, and find them nobler than the councils of living Florentines. Public affairs had been, for her, faint and distant like drums heard across fields. Yet now the expulsion of the Medici meant, above all else, the extinction of her best hope for Bardo’s library. The uneasy times, the suspicion among shopkeepers and rabble, the danger for Medicean friends like her godfather and Tito—these she noted, but like a woman hearing of storms at sea while she is thinking of bread in the oven. Her new sharp attention to wars, visits, changes, was kindled solely by love and duty to her father’s memory.

Her ardour had always poured itself into affection. The learned pursuits she shared with Bardo had been toil borne for his sake, not nourishment sought for her own hunger. Tito’s brilliance delighted her only because it was his, because it flashed out of the face she loved. The larger possibilities of her nature lay folded like wings that had never been tried; sometimes they made a vague ache, nothing more.

But this personal stake in public events made her want at last to understand Fra Girolamo’s influence—this preaching that was drawing Florence as a fisherman draws with a strong line. Tito, as secretary and useful talent, moved in the heart of business, yet what he told her only made her long to hear the voice that commanded the city’s attention, to learn for herself what promise or threat the monk offered concerning the French.

That morning, for the first time, she had gone to an Advent sermon in the Duomo.

When Tito left her, she had resolved suddenly; she went first to Santa Croce and stood where her father lay, as one stands before a hearth where the fire has gone out and yet the smell of smoke lingers in the stones. Then she walked on to the Duomo. The earlier memory of Dino—of his words and his presence—was still there in her as a half-healed mark, but it had been covered by the daily wear of marriage, quieted by the subjection that comes to a wife who loves with full reliance. She expected Savonarola’s voice might move her in spite of his narrow monkishness, because she remembered the power it had had before.

The sermon deepened rather than altered her impression: this preacher of tribulations, fanatical though he seemed, was still a man she might revere. His denunciations and exhortations held her attention; they did not pierce her with terror or remorse. It was like distant thunder—grand, rolling, shaking the air but not the earth beneath her feet.

Yet when he invoked martyrdom, she sobbed with the rest. A new sensation passed through her—a strange sympathy with something that lay outside the definable interests of her life. It was like the thrill stirred by rare heroic touches in history and poetry; and yet the difference was like that between recalling music and being seized by living harmonies vibrating through bone and breath. Still, that emotion seemed to fall outside the inner sanctuary of her existence. It did not rearrange her loyalties; it only left a lingering echo, as a great bell leaves the ear full after the sound has ceased.

Now, in the library, she was not thinking of Fra Girolamo. She listened for Tito’s step with the anxious patience of one who has counted too many hours by absence.

Through these three months of double solitude she had thought of each day as an epoch in which their union might at last become more complete. She knew she had sometimes been too sad, too urgent about her father’s memory—too critical, or too coldly silent, when Tito narrated the talk and bustle of his world—too hasty in suggesting they should live as simply as Bardo had done, to scrape together enough to repay Bernardo and ease the difficulties about the library. She saw that she asked much: Tito could not feel as she felt; it was hard to ask him to relinquish the luxuries he laboured to obtain.

Next time he came home, she would suppress every prompting that isolated her from him. She laboured, as loving women do, to shape herself to the beloved. She strangled each rising impulse of suspicion, pride, resentment, with a desperate resolution; she felt equal to any self-infliction that would save her from ceasing to love. The thought of not loving was like a nightmare in which the world breaks away all round, leaving one’s feet over darkness.

She waited long. Tito had not come straight home after leaving Niccolò Caparra. The hours thickened; the city’s noises altered—shouts fading into scattered calls, then into the steady domestic sounds of doors barred, pots set down, water poured, the distant bark of a dog. Florence had its own acoustic climate, shifting with the day like wind over grain. When at last, more than two hours after the time she had imagined him crossing the bridge, she heard the great door of the court turn on its hinges, the sound went through her like a hand on the heart.

She hurried to the head of the stone steps. A lamp hung there, and its steady flame made the stairwell smell of oil and warmed dust. They saw each other clearly as he ascended.

Eighteen months had written more change on Romola’s face than on Tito’s. Her expression was subdued now, less cold, more beseeching; and as a pink flush spread over her cheeks with the sudden joy of his return, she looked lovelier than on the first day he had seen her. Then, any on-looker would have said Romola was born to command and Tito to bend; now her mouth trembled slightly and there was timidity in her gaze.

He made an effort to smile when she spoke.

“My Tito, you are tired; it has been a fatiguing day: is it not true?”

Maso was near; nothing more was said until they had crossed the antechamber and closed the library door behind them. The wood burned bright on the great dogs, giving out a scent of resin and smoke that should have promised comfort. Romola’s gentle voice was another welcome. She took off his mantle; he turned and kissed her—briefly—and then went to the high-backed chair set near the fire, flung himself down, tossed away his cap, and said, not peevishly but with a weary remonstrance, and a slight shudder as if cold had reached him beneath his clothes—

“Romola, I wish you would give up sitting in this library. Surely our own rooms are pleasanter in this chill weather.”

The words hurt her with their indifference. She had never seen him so unlike himself; he was usually all lively attention. She had built up his return in her mind through the long waiting, as one builds up a dish in hope—measuring, imagining the savour—and now the first taste was flat.

He must be very weary, she told herself quickly, hunting for excuses in his face as if bodily fatigue could translate moral chill into mere weakness.

“I wonder you have forgotten, Tito,” she said, gently, yet with anxiety. “You know I am making the catalogue on the new plan that my father wished for; you have not time to help me, so I must work at it closely.”

Instead of meeting her glance, Tito closed his eyes and rubbed his hands over his face and hair, as if he would wipe off something clinging. He knew he was behaving unlike himself; he promised himself amends to-morrow. But the day had raised again, like something long buried and suddenly breaking the crust, his secret fears—fears which, if Romola knew them, would estrange her for ever. Feeling already in danger from her mind, he felt an alienation already begun between them, and with it a repulsion he had not chosen. He was vexed with himself, yet could not summon warmth on command; he could only offer an excuse.

“I am not well, Romola; you must not be surprised if I am peevish.”

“Ah, you have had so much to tire you to-day,” Romola said, and knelt close beside him. She laid her arm across his chest and pushed his hair back with a caress, as she might smooth down a child’s hair after fright. The fire made the metal dogs glow red at the edges; the crackle of resinous wood rose and fell like breathing.

Then, under her forearm, she felt something hard, unyielding—no bone, no belt buckle—something that gave no warmth back. She started, drew her arm away, and looked at him with alarm.

“What have you got under your tunic, Tito? Something as hard as iron.”

“It is iron—it is chain-armour,” he said at once. He had been prepared for the discovery and spoke quietly, as of a thing not to be hurriedly explained.

“There was some unexpected danger to-day, then?” Romola said, seeking a reasonable cause. “You had it lent to you for the procession?”

“No; it is my own. I shall be obliged to wear it constantly, for some time.”

“What is it that threatens you, my Tito?” Her voice rose with terror, and she clung to him again, as if her arms could ward off knives.

“Every one is threatened in these times, who is not a rabid enemy of the Medici. Don’t look distressed, my Romola—this armour will make me safe against covert attacks.” He put his hand on her neck and smiled, and for a moment the habit of kindness flowed back, as warm broth returns to the lips after a draught of cold water.

“But my godfather, then,” Romola said, fear rushing outward to all she loved, “is not he, too, in danger? And he takes no precautions—ought he not? since he must surely be in more danger than you, who have so little influence compared with him.”

“It is just because I am less important that I am in more danger,” Tito answered readily. “I am suspected constantly of being an envoy. Men like Messer Bernardo are protected by their position and extensive family connections, which spread among all parties; while I am a Greek that nobody would avenge.”

“But, Tito—is it a fear of some particular person, or only a vague sense of danger, that has made you think of wearing this?” She could not keep down the thought that this might be something meaner than prudence; her anxiety had an undertaste of shame, as if she feared to find cowardice in the man she worshipped.

“I have had special threats,” Tito said. “But I must beg you to be silent on the subject, my Romola. I shall consider that you have broken my confidence, if you mention it to your godfather.”

“Assuredly I will not mention it,” she said quickly, blushing at having seemed to question him. Then, after a pause, her love found words that were almost a lament. “But, dearest Tito—it will make you very wretched.”

“What will make me wretched?” His face changed almost imperceptibly, as if a small dart had touched him.

“This fear—this heavy armour.” She shuddered, feeling the chill of it under her palm. “I can’t help shuddering as I feel it under my arm. I could fancy it a story of enchantment—that some malignant fiend had changed your sensitive human skin into a hard shell. It seems so unlike my bright, light-hearted Tito.”

“Then you would rather have your husband exposed to danger, when he leaves you?” Tito said, smiling, turning her tenderness into a playful challenge. “If you don’t mind my being poniarded or shot, why need I mind? I will give up the armour—shall I?”

“No, Tito, no. I am fanciful. Do not heed what I have said.” She shook her head, ashamed of her imagination. “But such crimes are surely not common in Florence? I have always heard my father and godfather say so. Have they become frequent lately?”

“It is not unlikely they will become frequent,” Tito said, “with the bitter hatreds that are being bred continually.”

She fell silent. The chain under his tunic seemed to have made a sound in her mind, like a muffled clink that would not cease. She shrank from insisting further, as if pressing the subject might press also on some hidden sore in him. She tried to shake it off, to pour warmth back into the moment.

“Tell me what has happened to-day,” she said, forcing cheerfulness as one stirs honey into a draught. “Has all gone off well?”

“Excellently well,” Tito answered. “First of all, the rain came and put an end to Luca Corsini’s oration, which nobody wanted to hear; and a ready-tongued personage—some say it was Gaddi, some say it was Melema, but really it was done so quickly no one knows who it was—had the honour of giving the Cristianissimo the briefest possible welcome in bad French.”

“Tito, it was you, I know,” Romola said, brightening at once, and kissed him. “How is it you never care about claiming anything? And after that?”

“Oh! after that, there was a shower of armour and jewels and trappings, such as you saw at the last Florentine giostra, only a great deal more of them. There was strutting, and prancing, and confusion, and scrambling; and the people shouted, and the Cristianissimo smiled from ear to ear. After that there was a great deal of flattery, and eating, and play. I was at Tornabuoni’s. I will tell you about it to-morrow.”

The mention of eating and play brought with it, for Romola, not a lively curiosity but a faint sense of distance. She thought of tables spread, spices crushed under knife, capons turning, wine poured into bright cups—rich smells rising, laughter mingling with music—while in this house the fire was more often for warmth than for feasting, and her own days had been filled with ink and lists and the dry dust of parchment. Yet she checked the thought at once; she would not let it sour her voice.

“Yes, dearest, never mind now.” She leaned closer, searching his face. “But is there any more hope that things will end peaceably for Florence—that the Republic will not get into fresh troubles?”

Tito shrugged. “Florence will have no peace but what it pays well for; that is clear.”

Her face clouded, and she felt again how all these great matters touched her chiefly through one narrow point: the library, the debt, the chance of keeping her father’s treasure from dispersal. She forced herself back into lightness.

“You would not guess where I went to-day, Tito. I went to the Duomo, to hear Fra Girolamo.”

Tito looked startled. For an instant his mind leapt—like a rat darting in a wall—to that earlier terror of his own, the thought of a face in the crowd, of a man he feared; and the sound of the Duomo’s vast interior seemed to strike him again—the roar of voices, the hollow swell of chanting, the echoing footfalls under the dome.

Romola read his look differently.

“You are surprised, are you not? It was a sudden thought. I want to know all about the public affairs now, and I determined to hear for myself what the Frate promised the people about this French invasion.”

“Well,” Tito said, recovering himself, and taking up playfulness like a shield, “and what did you think of the prophet?”

“He certainly has a very mysterious power, that man,” Romola answered. “A great deal of his sermon was what I expected; but once I was strangely moved—I sobbed with the rest.”

“Take care, Romola,” Tito said lightly, relieved that her words had not gone where his fear had darted. “You have a touch of fanaticism in you. I shall have you seeing visions, like your brother.”

“No; it was the same with every one else,” she said. “He carried them all with him—unless it were that gross Dolfo Spini, whom I saw there making grimaces.” She paused, then added, the picture coming back with the vividness of something odd in the midst of solemnity. “There was even a wretched-looking man, with a rope round his neck—an escaped prisoner, I should think, who had run in for shelter—a very wild-eyed old man: I saw him with great tears rolling down his cheeks, as he looked and listened quite eagerly.”

A slight silence fell, as if the fire had suddenly sunk. Tito’s face altered again—so small a change that a stranger might not have noticed, but Romola felt it in the air between them, as one feels a draft in a room.

“I saw the man,” Tito said at last. “The prisoner. I was outside the Duomo with Lorenzo Tornabuoni when he ran in. He had escaped from a French soldier. Did you see him when you came out?”

“No,” Romola answered. “He went out with our good old Piero di Cosimo. I saw Piero come in and cut off his rope, and take him out of the church.” Her eyes returned anxiously to Tito. “But you want rest, Tito? You feel ill?”

“Yes,” Tito said, rising.

And as he rose, the chain-armour moved beneath his tunic with the faintest rustle, like dry leaves underfoot—a sound Romola scarcely heard, but he did. To him it was the perpetual reminder that he must live in continual dread: not only of knives in the street, but of what that wild-eyed old man might have said or done, somewhere in Florence, where every whisper found an echo.
