In that sweltering month of May, Durrance cast his gaze upward from Wadi Halfa, his heart turning eagerly toward home. Unbeknownst to him, five hundred miles to the south, separated by the vast expanse of the Nubian Desert and the treacherous Belly of Stones, events of profound consequence were unfolding.

On the abandoned track linking Berber and Suakin, the Wells of Obak lay buried deep among shifting dunes. To the east, a strip of trees marked the boundary between the sands and a hard, stony plain crowned with granite hills; to the west, the desert stretched fifty-eight waterless miles toward Mahobey and Berber on the Nile. It was a landscape of such utter flatness that a mere tuft of grass, knee-high, could appear from a mile away as a tree offering shade for a midday rest, while a pile of stones no larger than one might find beside a road under repair seemed to rise like a formidable hill. This specific May offered no spot more desolate than the Wells of Obak. The sun hammered down from dawn until sunset with unbearable intensity, while all night long a biting wind swept across the land, playing havoc with the sand—building pyramids high as houses only to level them, carving valleys, and silting up slopes until the very face of the country shifted constantly. Vultures and sand-grouse held dominion over this wasteland in a perpetual lease. Scattered here and there were the bleached bones and skeletons of camels, silent testimony that caravans had once crossed and halted here; remnants of a hut constructed from branches bent into hoops indicated that Arabs had once herded goats and called this place home.

Now, the sun rose and set over an empty circle of honey-colored earth. Silence hung heavy, like night over water, making the absolute stillness a breeding ground for mystery and expectation. Yet, in this month of May, one man sojourned secretly at the wells. Every morning at sunrise, he drove two camels—swift riding-mares of the pure Bisharin breed—from the belt of trees. He watered them, then sat by the well-mouth for exactly three hours. Afterward, he drove them back into the tree line, feeding them delicately with *dhoura* (millet) spread on a cloth. For the remainder of the day, he vanished completely.

For five consecutive mornings, he emerged from his hiding place, gazing toward the sand-dunes and Berber, but no one approached him. On the sixth morning, just as he prepared to return to his shelter, he spotted the silhouette of a man and a donkey outlined sharply against the sky atop a sand ridge. The Arab sitting by the well glanced first at the donkey, noting its grey color, and half-risen to his feet. As he straightened, however, he looked at the driver and saw that while the man's *jellaba* was pulled forward to shield his face from the glare, his bare legs gleamed with an ebony blackness against the pale sand. The driver was a Negro.

The Arab sat back down, waiting with an air of total indifference for the stranger to approach. He did not flinch or turn when he heard the soft tread of the Negro's feet behind him.

"Salam aleikum," the Negro said as he stopped. He carried a long spear, a short one, and a hide shield, which he laid on the ground before sitting beside the Arab. The Arab bowed his head and returned the greeting. "Aleikum es salam," he replied, waiting silently.

"Is this Abou Fatma?" the Negro asked.

The Arab nodded.

"Two days ago," the other continued, "a man of the Bisharin named Moussa Fedil stopped me in the marketplace of Berber. Seeing that I was hungry, he gave me food. Once I had eaten, he commanded me to drive this donkey to Abou Fatma at the Wells of Obak."

Abou Fatma glanced casually at the donkey as if noticing it for the first time. "Tayeeb," he said, equally nonchalantly. "The donkey is mine." He sat motionless, appearing ready to dismiss the matter entirely.

However, the Negro held his ground. "I am to meet Moussa Fedil again on the third morning from now, in the marketplace of Berber. Give me a token to carry back, so that he knows I have fulfilled my charge and can claim his reward."

Abou Fatma drew his knife from the small of his back, picked up a stick from the ground, and notched it thrice at each end. "This shall be your sign to Moussa Fedil," he said, handing the stick to his companion. The Negro secured it tightly into a corner of his wrap, loosened the water-skin from the donkey's back, filled it at the well, and slung it over his shoulders. Picking up his spears and shield, he prepared to leave.

Abou Fatma watched him labor up the loose sand slope and vanish around the crest. Then, in his turn, he rose hurriedly. Six days prior, when Harry Feversham had departed from Obak to cross the fifty-eight miles of barren desert to the Nile, this same grey donkey had borne his water-skins and provisions.

Abou Fatma drove the donkey down among the trees and fastened it to a palm trunk. Examining the animal's shoulders closely, he found a tiny incision in the left shoulder, the skin neatly stitched closed with fine thread. With practiced hands, he cut the stitches and pressed the wound open, forcing out a tiny package no larger than a postage stamp. It was a goat's bladder, enclosing a note written in Arabic and folded minuscule.

Abou Fatma had served as Gordon's body-servant for a reason; he had learned to read during those years. Unfolding the note, he read:

*"The houses that were once Berber are destroyed, and a new town of wide streets is being built. There is no longer any sign by which I may distinguish the ruins of Yusef's house from the ruins of a hundred others; nor does Yusef any longer sell rock-salt in the bazaar. Yet wait for me another week."*

The author of this letter was Harry Feversham. Clad in the patched *jubbeh* of the Dervishes over his stained skin, his hair frizzed on top and falling in matted, seaweed-like locks down the nape of his neck, he navigated the wide streets of New Berber, searching for Yusef amidst the gaping pits of reconstruction. To the south, separated by a mile of desert, lay the old town—a warren of ruined houses facing narrow, winding alleys. Front walls had been torn down, roofs carried away, leaving only bare inner walls standing. When Feversham wandered among them at night, it seemed he had entered long lanes of five courts, crumbling into decay, each indistinguishable from its neighbor save by varying degrees of ruin. Foxes had already begun digging burrows beneath the walls.

He had calculated that one night should suffice for his stay in Berber. His plan was to creep through the gate at dusk and face the journey to Obak before the grey light quenched the stars. Instead, he was forced to move steadily forward among the crowds like a man with urgent business, dreading conversation lest his tongue betray him, listening intently for the name "Yusef" to strike his ear.

Despair visited him intermittently, fear was constant. From these sharp pains sprang a kind of madness—a frenzied obstinacy, a fanaticism akin to the dark religion of those around him—that convinced him he could not now fail, that the universe contained no injustice great enough to burden the one man least fit to bear it and then callously destroy him for trying.

Fear gripped him on that morning, three days after leaving Abou Fatma at the wells. Climbing a slope, he first saw the sand stretch like a lagoon up to the dark brown walls of the town, with the overshadowing foliage of great date palms rising on the Nile bank beyond. Within those walls dwelt the crowded Dervishes. It seemed sheer madness to imagine escaping detection there, even for an hour. Was it right, he wondered increasingly, for a man to even attempt this?

As he stood longer, the low mud walls grew sinister; the welcoming green of the waving palms, after so many arid days of sun and stone, became an ironic invitation to death. He began to question whether he had already done enough for honor by venturing so near. The sun beat down relentlessly; his strength ebbed as though his veins were opened. If he were caught—as surely he would be—he imagined the fanatical faces crowding fiercely about him... practicing mutilations? He shivered despite the intense heat, struck by the great loneliness of the place until his knees shook.

He turned and began to run, leaping in a panic across the naked desert under the sun, unchased and alone. Feeble cries broke inarticulately from his throat. Yet, he ran only a few yards before the very violence of his flight brought him to a halt.

Were four years of anticipation worth nothing? Had he schooled himself in the language, lived in the bazaars, for no purpose? Was he still the craven who had surrendered his papers? Was the quiet confidence with which he had revealed his plan to Lieutenant Sutch in the Criterion grill-room merely the vainglory of a self-deceiver? And Ethne?

He collapsed onto the ground
