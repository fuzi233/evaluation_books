They encountered the innkeeper in the hallway.

"Welcome, gentlemen," he said, lifting his cap with a low bow.

"Come, we are not in Germany," Gerard remarked.

In the main hall, they found the mistress, a robust woman of about forty. She curtseyed to them and offered a warm smile. "Take the trouble to sit down, fair sir," she said to Gerard, dusting two chairs with her apron—though they hardly needed it.

"Thank you, dame," Gerard replied. *Well,* he thought, *this is a polite nation: the trouble of sitting down? I shall endure it with singular patience; and shortly thereafter, the labor of eating, the toil of digestion, and finally, by Hercules' aid, the strain of retiring to bed, and the struggle of sinking fast asleep.*

"Why, Denys, what are you doing? Ordering supper for only two?"

"Why not?"

"What? Can we dine without waiting for forty others? Burgundy forever!"

"Aha! Courage, comrade. Le dia—"

"C'est convenu."

It seemed the Salic Law had not yet penetrated French inns. In this establishment at least, the wimple and kirtle reigned supreme; doublets and hose were few in number and lacked vigor. The landlord himself wandered aimlessly, eternally tipping his hat to people out of sheer habit; and the women, passing him in turn, shoved him quietly aside without looking at him, as one removes a live twig while rushing through a forest.

A maid brought in the supper, and the mistress followed her, empty-handed.

"Fell to, my masters," she said cheerfully. "You have but one enemy here; and he lies beneath your knife." (I shrewdly suspect this is merely a formula.)

They began to eat. The mistress pulled her chair slightly closer to the table and provided companionship as well as food, chatting genially with them like old friends. Once this initial exchange was finished, the busy dame departed quickly, sending in her daughter instead—a beautiful young woman of about twenty, who took the vacant seat. She was not quite so broad and genial as the elder, but gentle and cheerful, showing a tender concern for Gerard upon learning the distance the poor boy had traveled and the journey he still faced. She remained nearly half an hour, and when she left, Gerard remarked, "Is this an inn? Why, it feels like home."

"Qui fit François, il fit courtois," said Denys, bursting with gratified pride.

"Courteous? Nay, Christian; to welcome us like home-guests and old friends, mere vagrants here today and gone tomorrow. But truly, who deserves pity and kindness more than the weary traveler far from his kin? Hola! Here comes another."

The newcomer was the chambermaid, a woman of about twenty-five, with a prominent nose, a large laughing mouth, and a sparkling black eye, alongside a bare arm that was stout but not particularly shapely.

The moment she entered, one of the travelers passed a somewhat bold jest regarding her; instantly, the entire company erupted in laughter at his expense, thanks to her practiced tongue. Just as, in a fencing match between a novice and a master, foils clash—the novice gets pinked—so too did another, and then another, attempt to break a lance with her. But Marion planted her great arms upon her hips and held the whole room in play. This country girl possessed in perfection that rough, ready humor which may appear mean and vulgar on paper but carries all before it when spoken aloud: not the rapier of wit, but its bludgeon. Nature had done much for her in this regard, and daily practice in an inn completed the rest.

Yet, I shall not photograph her, but indicate her feebly; for it was just four hundred years ago, the raillery was coarse, she returned every stroke in kind, and though a virtuous woman, she said things without blinking that no decent man of our day would utter even among men.

Gerard sat gaping in astonishment. This was almost a new variety of *homo* to him. He whispered, "Denys, now I see why you Frenchmen say 'a woman's tongue is her sword,'" just as she leveled another assault against an opponent. Meanwhile, the chivalrous Denys, to console and support "the weaker vessel"—the iron kettle among the clay pots—administered his counter-stroke: "Courage, ma mie, le—" etc.

She turned on him immediately. "How can he be dead as long as there is an archer left alive?" (General laughter at her ally's expense.)

"It is 'washing day,' my masters," she declared with sudden gravity.

"Afterward? We travelers cannot strip and go bare while you wash our clothes," objected a peevish old fellow by the fireside, who had kept mum during the raillery but crept out into the sunshine of common sense.

"I aimed not your way, ancient man," replied Marion superciliously. "But since you ask me" (she scanned him slowly from head to foot), "I trow you might take a turn in the tub, clothes and all, and no harm done" (laughter). "But what I spoke for, I thought this young sire might like his beard starched."

Poor Gerard's turn had come; his chin crop was thin and silky.

The loudest of all the laughers this time was the traitor Denys, whose beard was of good length and singularly stiff and bristly; so that Shakespeare, though he never saw him, hit him in the bull's-eye.

"Full of strange oaths, and bearded like the pard." —*As You Like It*.

Gerard bore the Amazonian satire with remarkable calm. He possessed little personal vanity. "Nay, 'chambermaid,'" he said with a smile, "mine is all unworthy of your pains; take you this fair growth in hand!" pointing to Denys's vegetable.

"Oh, time for that, when I starch the besoms."

While they all shouted over this palpable hit, the mistress returned, and in no more time than it took her to cross the threshold, our Amazon transformed into a seeming Madonna, meek and mild.

Mistresses are wonderful subjugators. Their like, I think, breathes not on the globe. Housemaids, decide! It was a waste of histrionic ability, however, for the landlady had heard and did not at heart disapprove the peals of laughter.

"Ah, Marion, lass," she said good-naturedly, "if you laid me an egg every time you cackle, 'Les Trois Poissons' would never lack an omelet."

"Now, dame," said Gerard, "what is to pay?"

"For what?"

"Our supper."

"Where is the hurry? Cannot you be content to pay when you go? Lose the guest, find the money—that is the rule of 'The Three Fish.'"

"But, dame, outside 'The Three Fish' it is written—'Ici-on ne loge'"

"Bah! Let that flea stick on the wall! Look hither," and she pointed to the smoky ceiling, which was covered with hieroglyphics. These were accounts, vulgo scores; intelligible to this dame and her daughter, who wrote them at need by simply mounting a low stool and scratching with a knife so as to show lines of ceiling through the deposit of smoke. The dame explained that the writing on the wall was placed there to frighten moneyless folk away entirely, or to be acted upon at odd times when a non-paying face should enter and insist on being served. "We cannot refuse them outright, you know. The law forbids us."

"And how do you know mine is not such a face?"

"Out fie! It is the best face that has entered 'The Three Fish' this autumn."

"And mine, dame?" said Denys; "do you see no knavery here?"

She eyed him calmly. "Not such a good one as the lad's; nor ever will be. But it is the face of a true man. For all that," she added drily, "if I were ten years younger, I'd as lief not meet that face on a dark night too far from home."

Gerard stared. Denys laughed. "Why, dame, I would but sip the night dew off the flower; and you needn't take ten years off, nor ten days, to be worth risking a scratched face for."

"There, our mistress," said Marion, who had just come in, "did I not tell you the other day you could make a fool of them still, if you were properly minded?"

"I dare say you did; it sounds like some daft wench's speech."

"Dame," said Gerard, "this is wonderful."

"What? Oh, no, no, that is no wonder at all. Why, I have been here all my life; and reading faces is the first thing a girl picks up in an inn."

Marion. "And frying eggs the second; no, telling lies; frying eggs is the third, though."

The Mistress. "And holding her tongue the last, and modesty the day after—never at all."
