By the time the Fieldhead party returned to Briarfield, Caroline was nearly recovered. Miss Keeldar, having received word by post of her friend’s improvement, wasted no time—she arrived at home and immediately made her way to the rectory.

A gentle but steady rain fell upon the late-blooming flowers and the russet-hued autumn shrubs when the garden gate creaked open, and Shirley’s familiar figure appeared in the window. As she stepped inside, her emotions showed themselves in her usual quiet way. When deeply stirred by sorrow or joy, she rarely spoke at length. Her feelings did not spill over into words, nor did her eyes betray them easily—only flickering glances, fleeting and guarded. She embraced Caroline, gave her a single look, a single kiss, and said simply, “You are better.”

Then, just a moment later: “I see you’re safe now—but take care. May God spare your health from any further shock.”

She began speaking animatedly about the journey, yet all the while her gaze kept returning to Caroline. In her eyes there was deep concern, a quiet unease, and something like wonder.

“She may be on the mend,” her expression seemed to say, “but how frail she still is! What a trial she has endured!”

Suddenly, her glance shifted to Mrs. Pryor—and pierced straight through her.

“When will my governess return to me?” she asked.

“May I tell her everything?” Caroline whispered to her mother. A nod granted permission, and soon Shirley learned what had transpired during her absence.

“Very good,” she replied coolly. “Very good. But it’s no surprise to me.”

“What? You knew?”

“I guessed long ago. I’ve heard bits of Mrs. Pryor’s story—not from her, but from others. I know every detail of Mr. James Helstone’s life and character. An afternoon with Miss Mann taught me much. And he’s one of Mrs. Yorke’s cautionary tales—one of the crimson warnings she displays to frighten young women away from marriage. I might have doubted the truth of such accounts—both these women delight in showing only the darkest side of life—but I asked Mr. Yorke himself. He said, ‘Shirley, my dear, if you want to know anything about that James Helstone, I can only say he was a man-tiger. Handsome, reckless, soft, treacherous, polite, cruel—’ Don’t cry, Cary. Let’s not speak of it more.”

“I’m not crying, Shirley—or if I am, it doesn’t matter. Go on. You’re not a true friend if you hide the truth from me. I hate this pretense of smoothing over, hiding, distorting reality.”

“Then I’ll say no more than I already have—except that your uncle himself confirmed Mr. Yorke’s words. He hates lies, and never uses those hollow excuses that are worse than falsehoods.”

“But Papa is dead. They should let him rest.”

“They should—and we _will_ let him rest. Cry, Cary. It will do you good. It’s wrong to suppress natural tears. Besides, I like to share the idea I see glowing in your mother’s eyes as she looks at you. Every tear washes away a sin. Weep! Your tears hold a power the rivers of Damascus never had. Like Jordan, they can cleanse even a leprous memory.”

Then, turning to Mrs. Pryor, she said, “Madam, did you think I could spend days watching you and your daughter together—seeing your remarkable likeness in so many ways, noticing (excuse me) your unguarded emotions when your child is near or gone—and not form my own guesses? I formed them, and they are exactly right. I begin to think myself rather clever.”

“And you said nothing?” Caroline asked, regaining her composure.

“Nothing. I had no right to speak. It wasn’t my place. I chose not to make it so.”

“You guessed such a deep secret—and didn’t hint at it?”

“Is that so hard?”

“It isn’t like you.”

“How do you know?”

“You’re not reserved. You’re open, honest.”

“I may be open—but I know where to draw the line. I may show my treasure, but I keep a few gems hidden—a rare, unclaimed stone, an amulet whose glow I rarely allow myself to glimpse. Goodbye.”

Caroline found herself seeing Shirley in a new light. Soon, that view would deepen.

No sooner had Caroline regained enough strength to enjoy a change of scenery, a little social warmth, than Miss Keeldar began asking daily for her presence at Fieldhead. Whether Shirley had grown weary of her relatives is unknown—she never said so. Yet she claimed Caroline with such eagerness that it was clear her company was not unwelcome.

The Sympsons were devout churchgoers. Naturally, the rector’s niece was welcomed with courtesy. Mr. Sympson was a man of impeccable respectability—strict in temper, pious in belief, worldly in outlook. His wife was kind, patient, well-bred. She had been raised on narrow views, fed on a few bitter prejudices, a handful of rigid preferences soaked till their flavor vanished, with no seasoning added. She had some noble principles, wrapped in a stiff crust of bigotry that made them hard to digest. Too obedient to question her upbringing, she accepted her diet without complaint.

Their daughters were models of propriety. Tall, each with a Roman nose, they had been educated perfectly. Everything they did was done with precision. History and solid books had shaped their minds. Their principles were fixed, beyond improvement. Their lives, feelings, manners, habits—all were strictly regulated. They knew by heart the rules of young-ladies’ conduct—on speech, bearing, behavior—and never deviated. They viewed any departure from these rules with silent horror. The Abomination of Desolation was no mystery to them; they had identified it in what others called Originality. Quick to spot its signs—whether in speech, gesture, or thought—they recoiled. Danger loomed above them, peril surrounded them. What was this strange thing? Since it was incomprehensible, it must be evil. It must be condemned and locked away.

Henry Sympson, the youngest and only son, was fifteen. He usually stayed with his tutor. When free, he sought out his cousin Shirley. Unlike his sisters, Henry was small, lame, pale. His large eyes shone dimly in a wan face—usually dull, but capable of sudden brilliance. Inner emotion could flush his cheeks and give purpose to his crippled movements. His mother loved him dearly; she saw his differences as marks of divine favor. He was not like other children, she admitted. She believed him reborn—a new Samuel—called by God from birth. He was meant to be a clergyman. Mr. and the Misses Sympson, unable to understand him, left him mostly alone. Shirley made him her favorite; he made her his companion.

In the midst of this orderly family, or rather apart from it, moved the tutor—the satellite.

Yes, Louis Moore was a satellite of the Sympson household—connected, yet distant; ever present, always removed. Each member treated him with proper dignity. The father was coldly civil, sometimes irritable; the mother, kind-hearted though formal; the daughters saw him as an abstraction, not a person. By their manner, it seemed their brother’s tutor did not truly live among them. They were learned—he was too—but not for them. They were accomplished—he had talents, invisible to their senses. A vivid sketch from his hand was blank to their eyes; a bold observation from his lips went unheard. Nothing surpassed their propriety.

Or so it seemed—until Caroline discovered something astonishing. Her cousin Shirley had no real friend at Fieldhead. To Miss Keeldar, Louis Moore was no more than a teacher—no gentleman, no man—just as he was to the respectable Misses Sympson.

What had happened to the compassionate Shirley? Why was she so indifferent to the loneliness of a fellow human being thus isolated under her roof? She wasn’t cruel, perhaps—but she never noticed him. He came and went, spoke or remained silent, and she rarely acknowledged his presence.

As for Louis Moore himself, he bore it with quiet resignation. He seemed used to such life, accepting it as temporary. His mind felt shut within him—silent, uncomplaining. He never laughed; rarely smiled. He fulfilled his duties with exactness. His pupil loved him. He asked nothing more from the world than basic civility—and seemed content to accept no more, especially in that house. When Caroline offered gentle friendship, he did not encourage it. He avoided her more than sought her. One living creature besides his frail student he cherished: the rough, sullen Tartar dog. Though surly to others, Tartar had a special bond with Moore. Once, when Moore entered the room uninvited and sat down quietly, Tartar rose from Shirley’s feet and settled beside him. Shirley noticed, extended her white hand, and softly tried to lure him back. Tartar looked, drooled, sighed—but refused, settling firmly at Moore’s side. The tutor gently pulled the dog’s black-muzzled head onto his knee, patted him, and smiled—just once, to himself.

Later that evening, an observer might have seen that after Tartar returned to Shirley’s side, Moore, with a single word and gesture, drew him back again. The dog pricked his ears, stood up, and came forward with affectionate submission. As Moore petted him, the same quiet smile crossed his face.

* * * * *

One day, as Caroline and Shirley sat alone in the summerhouse, Caroline said, “Shirley, did you know that my cousin Louis was tutor in your uncle’s family before the Sympsons came here?”

Shirley hesitated longer than usual before replying, “Yes—of course. I knew it well.”

“I thought you must have known.”

“Well, then?”

“It puzzles me why you never mentioned it to me.”

“Why should it puzzle you?”

“It seems odd. You talk so freely—how could this never come up?”

“Because it didn’t,” Shirley said, laughing.

“You’re a strange creature!” Caroline said. “I thought I knew you. Now I feel I don’t. You were silent about Mrs. Pryor—and now this. But why keep secrets? That’s the mystery.”

“I never made it a secret. I had no reason to. If you’d asked who Henry’s tutor was, I’d have told you. Besides, I thought you knew.”

“I’m puzzled by more than one thing. You don’t like poor Louis. Why? Are you annoyed by his position—what you see as servile? Do you wish Robert’s brother were higher in station?”

“Robert’s brother, indeed!” Shirley snapped, her voice sharp with scorn. With a proud gesture, she plucked a rose from a branch outside.

“Yes,” Caroline repeated, gently firm. “Robert’s brother. He _is_ closely related to Gérard Moore of the Hollow—though nature gave him neither the handsome features nor noble bearing of his kinsman. But his blood is as noble, and he is as much a gentleman, if he were free.”

“Wise, humble, pious Caroline!” Shirley said ironically. “Hear her! Men and angels, listen! We shouldn’t despise plain faces or honest labor, should we? Look at the man you praise—he’s there in the garden,” she continued, pointing through the vines. And indeed, Louis Moore was walking slowly along the path.

“He’s not ugly, Shirley,” Caroline insisted. “Not ignoble. He’s quiet—his silence seals his thoughts. But I believe he’s intelligent. And remember, if he weren’t admirable in character, Mr. Hall wouldn’t seek his company.”

Shirley laughed—twice, each time with a faint edge of mockery. “Well, well,” she said. “On the grounds that he’s Cyril Hall’s friend and Robert Moore’s brother, we’ll just tolerate his existence, won’t we, Cary? You think he’s intelligent? Not quite an idiot, eh? Something commendable in his nature—meaning, not a brute. Very well. Your arguments weigh with me. So if he comes this way, I’ll speak to him.”

He approached the summerhouse. Unaware it was occupied, he sat on the step. Tartar, his constant companion, followed and lay across his feet.

“Old boy,” Louis said, pulling the dog’s tawny ear—the remnant of an organ torn in countless fights—“the autumn sun shines as warmly on us as on the richest. This garden isn’t ours, but we enjoy its greenness and scent, don’t we?”

He sat in silence, stroking Tartar, who licked him with great affection. A faint chirping began in the trees. Something fluttered down, light as leaves—small birds, landing shyly on the grass, hopping expectantly.

“The little brown elves actually remember I fed them yesterday,” Louis murmured. “They want more biscuit. Today I forgot to save a piece. Eager little spirits—I have no crumb for you.”

He reached into his pocket and pulled it out empty.

“A simple need,” whispered Miss Keeldar.

She took a sweet cake from her reticule—always stocked with something for birds or animals—and crumbled it. Bending over his shoulder, she placed the crumbs in his hand.

“There,” she said. “There’s a providence for the improvident.”

“This September afternoon is pleasant,” Louis said, calmly scattering the crumbs.

“Even for you?”

“As pleasant for me as for any king.”

“You take a kind of harsh, solitary pleasure in finding joy in nature and creatures below you.”

“Solitary, but not harsh. With animals, I feel I am Adam’s son—the heir of him who ruled over every living thing. Your dog follows me. When I enter the yard, the pigeons flutter at my feet. Your mare knows me as well as you, and obeys me better.”

“And your roses smell sweet to you, and your trees give you shade.”

“And,” he continued, “no whim can take these joys from me. They are _mine_.”

He walked off. Tartar followed, as if bound by duty and love. Shirley remained on the step, staring after him. Caroline saw her face—pale, as if her pride were bleeding inwardly.

“You see,” Caroline said gently, “he’s often hurt. That makes him moody.”

“You see,” Shirley replied sharply, “we’ll quarrel over him if we talk about him again. Drop it—forever.”

Caroline thought to herself: “He must have behaved this way before—that’s why she keeps distance. Yet why can’t she forgive his character and circumstances? Why don’t his modesty, honesty, sincerity move her? She’s rarely so unkind, so impatient.”

* * * * *

Two friends of Caroline’s confirmed her favorable view of Louis. William Farren, who had visited him with Mr. Hall, called him “a real gentleman”—there was none like him in Briarfield. “I’d do anything for that man. And to see how the children adore him, how the wife liked him from the first sight. He never steps into a house but the kids swarm around him. Those little ones seem to sense people’s true nature better than grown-ups.”

Mr. Hall, when asked by Miss Helstone what he thought of Louis Moore, replied quickly: “The best fellow I’ve met since Cambridge.”

“But he’s so serious,” Caroline objected.

“Serious? The finest company in the world! Full of quiet, unusual humor. Never enjoyed an outing more than the one I took with him to the Lakes. His mind and tastes are so refined—it does a man good to be near them. And his temper, his nature? I call them fine.”

“At Fieldhead he seems gloomy. I think he’s seen as misanthropic.”

“Oh, I suspect he’s just out of place—misplaced. The Sympsons are excellent people, but they don’t understand him. They care too much about form and ceremony—things entirely foreign to Louis.”

“I don’t think Miss Keeldar likes him.”

“She doesn’t know him—doesn’t know him. Otherwise, she has enough sense to recognize his worth.”

“Well, I suppose she doesn’t know him,” Caroline mused. She tried to excuse Shirley’s prejudice with that thought. But this explanation didn’t last long. She soon had to reject it.

One day, she was in the schoolroom with Henry Sympson, whose gentle, loving nature had quickly won her affection. The boy was working on a mechanical project—his lameness made him prefer sitting tasks. He searched his tutor’s desk for wax or twine. Moore was absent—Mr. Hall had taken him for a walk. Henry couldn’t find what he needed. He rummaged through compartment after compartment, and finally opened an inner drawer. There, instead of cord or beeswax, was a bundle of small, marble-colored notebooks tied with tape.

“What rubbish Mr. Moore keeps in his desk!” Henry said. “I hope he won’t keep my old exercises so carefully.”

“What is it?”

“Old copy-books.”

He tossed the bundle to Caroline. The packet looked neat—her curiosity sparked.

“If they’re just copy-books, I suppose I can open them?”

“Oh yes, freely. Mr. Moore lets me keep things in his desk. I give you permission.”

She examined them. They were French compositions, written in a compact, clean hand—exquisitely clear. The handwriting was unmistakable. She barely needed the names at the end of each page to confirm whose they were. Yet the name startled her: “Shirley Keeldar, Sympson Grove, ----shire” (a southern county), dated four years prior.

She tied the bundle back up, holding it tightly, lost in thought. She felt almost guilty—like she had intruded on a private memory.

“They’re Shirley’s,” Henry said casually.

“Did _you_ give them to Mr. Moore? She wrote them with Mrs. Pryor, I suppose?”

“She wrote them in my schoolroom at Sympson Grove, when she lived there. Mr. Moore taught her French—it’s his native language.”

“I know. Was she a good pupil, Henry?”

“She was wild and laughing, but delightful. She made lessons charming. She learned fast—you couldn’t tell when or how. French was nothing to her. She spoke it as quickly as Mr. Moore himself.”

“Was she obedient? Did she cause trouble?”

“She caused plenty—her own way. She was giddy, but I liked her. I’m desperately fond of Shirley.”

“Desperately fond—you silly boy! You don’t know what you’re saying.”

“I _am_ desperately fond of her. She’s the light of my eyes. I told Mr. Moore that last night.”

“He’d scold you for exaggerating.”

“He didn’t. He never lectures like governesses do. He was reading, and just smiled into his book. Said if Miss Keeldar was no more than that, she was less than he thought her. Because I’m a dim-eyed, short-sighted little chap. I’m afraid I’m a poor unfortunate, Miss Caroline Helstone. I’m a cripple, you know.”

“Never mind, Henry. You’re a lovely little fellow. And if God didn’t give you strength, He gave you a good heart and a keen mind.”

“I’ll be despised. I sometimes think both Shirley and you do.”

“Listen, Henry. I usually dislike schoolboys. I find them rude, cruel—killing birds, insects, kittens—taking joy in hurting the weak. But you’re different. I’m fond of you. You’re almost as wise as a man—far wiser, I think, than many men.”

“I _do_ like reading. I know I have sense. I know I have feeling.”

At that moment, Miss Keeldar entered.

“Henry,” she said, “I brought your lunch. I’ll prepare it myself.”

She placed a glass of fresh milk, a plate of something resembling leather, and a tool like a toasting-fork on the table.

“What are you two doing, ransacking Mr. Moore’s desk?” she asked.

“Looking at your old copy-books,” Caroline replied.

“My old copy-books?”

“French exercise-books. Look—these must be precious. They’re kept so carefully.”

She held up the bundle. Shirley snatched it. “I didn’t know one existed,” she said. “I thought they’d all burned in the kitchen fire or curled the maid’s hair at Sympson Grove. Why did you keep them, Henry?”

“It wasn’t me. I never thought they mattered. Mr. Moore put them in the inner drawer. Maybe he forgot.”

“C’est cela. He forgot, no doubt,” Shirley echoed. “They’re beautifully written,” she added, sounding pleased.
