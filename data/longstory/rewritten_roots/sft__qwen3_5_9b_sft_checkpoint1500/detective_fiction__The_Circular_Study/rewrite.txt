Detective Inspector William Gryce felt the familiar, heavy weight of age settling upon his shoulders. He had reached that particular epoch in a man's life when the natural spirits begin to flag and enthusiasm requires a relentless, artificial spur. Lately, the streets of New York had offered little in the way of special excitement, leaving him feeling dull, superannuated, and distinctly bored. In fact, he was seriously contemplating resigning his commission, trading the chaos of the force for the quiet solitude of the little farm he had purchased in Westchester. Such a retirement, however, failed to lift his gloom; action was a biological necessity for Gryce, and the exercise of his mental faculties provided a vitality that any material advantage could never hope to match.

Yet, destiny had other plans. Just as his secret dissatisfaction peaked, a telephone message crackled through Headquarters, jolting the old man back to a vigor reminiscent of his younger days. It was a dispatch from Carter's famous drugstore, reporting that a young boy had been sent inside by a mysterious woman. She claimed a bizarre crime had been committed in the mansion located on the corner of ---- Street. The boy, trembling and visibly shaken, admitted he did not know the lady, though it was obvious she had paid him handsomely. He was too frightened to speak freely until he realized the police were listening, at which point he agreed to wait at the drugstore for instructions.

*"Strange"* crime. The adjective hung in the air, striking Gryce with a quiet, persistent insistence that made him forget his advancing years in a desperate desire to understand its meaning. Around him, the uniformed officers exchanged hushed remarks about the house, a landmark of the previous century pressed tight between modern clubs and restaurants. It was a spot notorious among the locals for its history and traditions, regardless of whether anyone actually knew them. Now, a crime had occurred within those walls.

Gryce caught the eye of the inspector in charge, receiving immediate authorization to investigate. Without hesitation, he moved out, heading first to the drugstore where the boy was waiting. He took the lad with him, marching straight to the indicated address. En route, Gryce prodded the poor waif for details, but the boy's narrative remained static, offering no new data beyond the initial report. He insisted that a "lady"—a term he used carefully, avoiding "woman"—approached him while he gazed at toys in a shop window. She handed him a coin, dragged him down the street, and eventually produced another piece of money at the drugstore counter. Her promise was clear: if he delivered a specific message to the telephone operator regarding a strange crime in the old house, she would pay him. Frightened by the gravity of her words, the boy had scrambled away, only to be shaken back to his senses by the woman's grip before fleeing to deliver the news.

There was a certain candor in the boy's tone, and Gryce was inclined to believe him. However, when asked to describe the lady, the boy's observational skills proved woefully inadequate, typical of his class. All he could muster was that she was a "stunner," wearing shiny clothes and jewels. Recognizing these limitations just as he stood before the ominous building, Gryce ceased questioning the child and focused entirely on the architecture ahead.

To the naked eye, nothing suggested crime or disturbance. A closed door, a pristine stoop, and windows draped in heavy curtains hinted at inner tranquility and domestic respectability. The calm brick façade, trimmed in brownstone, stood in stark, pleasing contrast to the chaotic, sign-laden buildings jutting out on either side.

"Some mistake," Gryce murmured to himself as the perfect silence of the establishment struck him anew. Before he could conclude he was the victim of a hoax, a figure emerged from beneath the stoop—an officer whose countenance betrayed sufficient perplexity to prompt Gryce's urgent inquiry.

"Anything wrong? Bloodshed? Everything appears quiet here."

The officer saluted respectfully. "I cannot gain entry. I've rung every bell. If I hadn't seen what looked like a stir in an upper window, I'd assume the house is empty. Should I attempt to breach the rear yard via the lower windows of Knapp & Co., next door?"

"Yes," Gryce commanded, pointing toward the boy. "Take this lad with you. Secure him in one of their offices, then find a way into this house from the backyard. It should be straightforward."

The officer nodded, seized the boy by the arm, and vanished instantly into the neighboring store. Gryce remained in the area square, soon besieged by a throng of curious passersby eager to add their gossip to the growing trouble. Their clamor was cut short by the sudden opening of the front door, allowing Gryce to slip inside before the crowd could swarm.

Within, the atmosphere was one of absolute stillness. He descended into a basement hall leading to a kitchen that appeared cleaner and more orderly than usual for a household engaged in significant activity, yet he saw no evidence of tragedy or a disruption of daily routine. Satisfied that the basement held no clues, Gryce ascended the stairs alongside the officer.

As they emerged onto the parlor floor, the officer whispered a crucial detail: "Mr. Raffner, from the firm next door, tells me the occupant is a peculiar sort—a 'bookworm,' they say. He has lived here for six months, yet scarcely a soul other than himself and a strange, uncommunicative old servant has ever been seen upon the premises."

"I know," Gryce muttered softly. Everyone knew. This house, formerly the seat of one of New York's most aristocratic families, was currently inhabited by Mr. Adams. Adams was renowned for his formidable personal charm, his immense wealth, and above all, his uncongenial temperament, which strictly precluded social interaction. It was precisely this knowledge that lent zest to the investigation. Entering the home of such a man was an event in itself; entering it on an errand of life and death was something else entirely. Under the inspiration of such rare opportunities, life was reawakened in the old veins of a sagacious, albeit octogenarian, detective.

They traversed a wide, old-fashioned hall sparsely furnished in an ancient style. Two doors stood open, inviting exploration. They entered the nearest, nearly opposite the staircase, and found themselves in a room barren as a physician's outer office. There was nothing of note worthy of attention, and they would have departed as unceremoniously as they arrived had it not been for a glimpse of richness peeking through the half-drawn folds of a portière at the far end of the room.

Advancing through the indicated doorway, they paused. A single look about them rendered them speechless, appalled. Nothing in their vast experiences—few things in life prepare one for the thrilling, solemn nature of what lay before them—could have prepared them for the scene they were now summoned to contemplate.

Should I attempt its description?

It was a room of small, circular proportions, hung with strange tapestries relieved here and there by priceless curios. Although it was still daylight, the space was illuminated not by the sun, but by a jet of rose-colored light concentrated with unnatural intensity. This light did not fall upon the endless rows of books lining the lower walls, nor did it touch the great painting which, in another era, might have captured the eye. Instead, the crimson glow bathed the upturned face of a man lying upon a bearskin rug.

In his heart, a dagger pierced deep. Upon his breast lay a cross, its golden lines sharply outlined against the long, dark, swathing garment covering his body. The illumination gave him the appearance of a saint prepared in some holy sanctuary for burial, save for two fatal discrepancies: the dagger spoke of a violent end, and his face bore an anguish for which Mr. Gryce, despite a lifetime of solving mysteries, found no name. It was an expression that answered to neither fear, pain, nor surprise, nor any emotion typically visible on the countenances of victims struck down by assassins.

Mysteries.

A heartbeat of paralyzed awe held Mr. Gryce rooted before he summoned the steel of his profession back to his gaze. The chamber was a tableau of dissonance: the gloom, the hushed silence, and the air of a forgotten romance clashing violently with the grim reality of the prostrate form. Lying there, stretched out like a saint awaiting interment, was the master of the house, the sacred emblem pinned to his chest serving as a grotesque counterpoint to the earthly fury that had driven the blade home. Such contradictions, however, were enough to stagger the mind of a veteran, yet they confused Mr. Gryce only for a fleeting second.

Before Styles could voice his own bewilderment, the older officer stepped forward, taking his station beside the fallen man. The evidence was plain: life had long departed the body. Yet, duty demanded verification. Gryce laid a heavy hand upon the wrist, feeling the final, fading warmth, and peered into the eyes, which seemed to hold a silent, reproachful judgment. It took every ounce of his practiced composure to meet that gaze without flinching.

The body was still warm, but undeniably cold in spirit. Conviction settled over him, and he refrained from removing the dagger, instead studying it with a craftsman's scrutiny. It was no common instrument, but a curiosity plucked from some Eastern bazaar—a slender, ornate thing. Its presence hinted at suicide, yet the angle of entry and the posture of the wound spoke of an external hand, a stranger's violence.

The scene itself told a story of sudden, ferocious attack, yet the apartment remained a sanctuary of order. No signs of struggle marred the well-appointed room. Save for a scattering of rose petals, the air was thick with the perfume of peace and quiet luxury. Even the massive central table, where the victim had likely stood moments before his fall, bore no marks of chaos. At first glance, the articles atop it—pipes, pens, portraits, weights, measures, Roman lamps, Venetian glass, rare porcelains, medals, rough metalwork, manuscripts, a scroll of music, a pot of blooming flowers—stood undisturbed, as if the shock of the tragedy had failed to ripple through their orderly arrangement.

But a closer inspection revealed the truth. Amidst these trappings of complex taste and attainments lay a row of electric buttons. Gryce reached out, and the moment his finger brushed one, the soft red glow of the lantern overhead flickered and died, replaced instantly by a sickly, greenish glare. The room was bathed in a ghastly tint, transforming the shadows into something spectral. In haste, Gryce pressed another button, grateful as the light shifted to a mild, white radiance, banishing the pallor that threatened to overwhelm the solemnity of the spot.

"Childish toys for a man of his station," Gryce muttered, his brow furrowing. Yet, as his eyes drifted to the upturned face beneath him, the doubt lingered. Could a man with features so sharply cut, so commanding, truly possess an instinct as frivolous as a switchboard? The face itself exerted a magnetic pull, disturbing the flow of his thoughts whenever it intruded upon his focus. To attribute madness or folly to a visage of such strength and purpose was to admit one's own poverty of judgment. Thus, the lamp, with its shifting hues and scientific connections, acquired a deeper meaning, one that seemed tied to the evidence surrounding the man—the books, the apparatus, the air of a scholar's mind.

Shifting his attention to the walls, draped in tapestries and curios, Gryce found little aid for his investigation. Then, his eye caught it: a bent nail, twisted from its socket. It was the nail that had held the wooden cross resting now upon the dead man's heart. The cord suspending the cross still clung to it, tangled with the crimson threads of the garment that had received the fatal wound. Who had torn it down? Not the victim; a man bleeding from the chest could not reach the wall, nor move across the circumscribed distance to where the nail lay. Another hand had pulled it free, placing it directly in the path of the dying man's breath. Was it an act of desperate religious hope, or mad remorse? Gryce dismissed the symbolism with a passing thought, too engrossed by the golden, isolated moments of the crime scene to dwell on it further.

Beyond the table, half-hidden against the wall, hung a portrait of striking power. It depicted a young girl of extreme, pathetic beauty, dressed in the fashion of the Civil War's twilight. Her face shared a haunting, intellectual charm with the man slumped on the floor. Though fair-haired she was, and dark-he haired he, the resemblance argued a close kinship. Beneath the canvas rested a sword, a pair of epaulettes, and a medal awarded for valor.

"Mementoes," Gryce mused, tracing the line of the frame.

His search continued until he encountered a narrow curtain, its dark hue blending seamlessly with the drapery on the adjacent walls, rendering it invisible until now. It was not a window pane; the windows of this unique apartment sat high, nearly lost in the ceiling. Instead, this fabric veiled an entrance to another chamber. Pushing it aside, Gryce entered a narrow closet housing a bed, a dresser, and a small table. The bed was a narrow cot, the dresser a testament to luxurious habits, both immaculate save for a silver-backed comb missing from its surface. Gryce spotted the comb lying on the floor at the far end of the room. Nearby, on a small stand, rested a pearl-handled parasol. A woman had been here recently.

Confirmation came swiftly. On the threshold separating this room from the study lay a tiny black spangle, known to milliners as a sequin. As Gryce stooped to examine its sparkle, his eye caught others trailing along the floor, leading inevitably to the large center table. The dainty trail of these bright, falling drops affected him strangely. He knew the mechanics: the sequins were strung on a thread, and once broken, they fell one by one, allowing a detective to trace the path of a woman's steps by the debris she shed. Whether it was the delicacy of the clue or the irony of fate using vanity as a trap, Gryce smiled, a knowing, eloquent expression playing on his lips.

Before inspecting the table further, he sought the break in the string. Had it snagged on a chair leg or a doorframe? The chairs were cushioned; the floor was smooth. Then, his gaze landed on the cross again. Its base featured intricate fretwork of gold. Could the filigree have caught in the hem of her dress as she tore the symbol from the wall, severing the thread and releasing this exquisite trail?

Hastening to where the cross had hung, Gryce searched the floor, finding nothing until he reached the rug beneath the prostrate man. Amidst the long hairs of the bearskin lay a solitary spangle. He knew then that the woman in shiny attire had stooped there, right before him, leaving her mark.

Satisfied, he returned to the table for a thorough, minute examination. The result proved fruitful. After drawing his finger across a specific spot near the inkstand, a faint smile graced his lips. He lifted the inkstand, examined the underside, and replaced it with precise care. Had he expected to find something hidden? A detective's face rarely yields its secrets, but the satisfaction was evident.

Lost in contemplation before the table, Gryce turned sharply at the sound of a quick step. Styles, the officer, had returned from scouring the rest of the house and stood before him, poised to speak.

"What is it?" Gryce asked, moving toward him.

Styles pointed toward the staircase visible through the antechamber door. "Go up!" he indicated.

Gryce hesitated, glancing around the room, which held his interest deeply. Styles broke his silence, his tone urgent. "Come! I've found the guilty party. He's in a room upstairs."

"He?" Gryce was startled by the pronoun.

"Yes, no doubt about it. When you see him..." Styles trailed off, his eyes widening. "What is that? Is he coming down? I'm sure there's no one else in the house. Don't you hear footsteps, sir?"

Gryce nodded. Footsteps were indeed descending.

"Let us retreat," Styles suggested. "Not because he's dangerous, but because you must see him before he sees you. He's a strange-acting man. If he enters here, he'll surely do something to implicate himself. Where can we hide?"

Gryce recalled the small closet he had just vacated and signaled Styles to follow. They slipped inside, pulling the curtain shut until only a narrow loophole remained. The steps grew louder, advancing steadily. Soon, they heard the intruder's breathing, quick and labored.

"Does he know someone has entered?" Gryce whispered. "Did he see you when you arrived?"

Styles shook his head and pointed eagerly toward the opposite door. The awaited figure had just lifted the portière and stood fully revealed, just inside the threshold.

Both men stared. Was this the murderer? This pale, lean servant holding a tray with a single glass of water? Gryce looked to Styles for explanation, but the officer hid his own surprise. Simultaneously, the individual who had just mimicked the recent tragedy with terrifying precision rose to his feet. A profound daze replaced his earlier ferocity; he bent to retrieve the shattered glass he had dropped and turned to exit the study.

"Wait, sir!" Mr. Gryce's voice cut through the silence. "You cannot remove that evidence until we understand how your master met his end."

The command, delivered with authority, failed to register any tremor in the man's composure. He stepped past Styles, who reached out to intercept him.

"Stop!" Styles barked, seizing the servant's arm. "Do not try to slip away like smoke! Do you not hear the officer speaking to you?"

For the first time, the appeal pierced the fog of the man's mind. The glass slipped from his grasp, striking the floor with a final, shattering *clink* that mingled with a sound neither quite a scream nor a groan—a jagged, animalistic noise. As his gaze landed on the detectives restraining him, his features twisted into a mask of raw terror. Realization dawned with horrific speed; the realization of his trapped situation sent him lunging for the door. Styles, however, pinned him down, dragging the flailing body back to the center of the room, where Mr. Gryce stood over the lifeless form of the master sprawled upon the bearskin rug.

In an instant, the transformation was complete. At the sight of the corpse, a cold, hellish joy ignited the butler's vacant stare, warping his mouth into a grotesque grin. Seeking no concealment for his triumph, he let loose a low, chilling laugh that echoed through the silent study like a ghost's whisper.

Mr. Gryce felt a surge of revulsion at the irresponsible madness before him. He waited until the last reverberations of that diabolical laughter faded into the high corners of the ceiling. Then, locking his piercing gaze onto the servant's wild eyes, he commanded, "Speak! Who killed this man? You lived in this house; you must know."

The butler's lips parted, and a stream of unintelligible guttural clicks erupted from his throat. With his free hand, he frantically touched his ears, then his mouth, shaking his head violently in denial.

"It was obvious, then," Gryce muttered, the pieces falling quickly. "He is both deaf and mute."

The revelation knocked the wind out of Styles. His grip loosened instantly. Freed from restraint, the man shuffled back to his corner, where he began to reenact the murder and the subsequent grief with renewed vigor, a performance that now seemed less a confession and more a ritual of mockery. Standing amidst the stillness of death, the gleam of infernal satisfaction in the servant's restless eyes made his presence feel like a sacrilege.

Styles could endure it no longer. "Can't you speak?" he roared, frustration mounting. "Can't you hear me?"

The man merely wore a slow, ghoulish smile. Gryce intervened, cutting the silence short. "Remove him," Gryce ordered. "Search him thoroughly for bloodstains. I am going upstairs to where you first found him. If he is truly innocent, he will bear no trace of this crime."

Yet, for once, the seasoned detective faced a blank wall. The search yielded nothing: no blood on the old servant, no fingerprints, no fibers linking him to the scene. Disappointed, Gryce retreated to the study, turning his attention back to the two rooms, hoping to find a clue hidden in plain sight among the overturned furniture and scattered papers.

His efforts were fruitless. Lost in contemplation of the deceased's frozen face, which seemed to hold a story of unspoken resolve, a voice suddenly rang out from the shadows above.

"Remember Evelyn!"

The cry, sharp and clear, echoed down the stairwell. Evelyn? In a house stripped bare of every living soul, who was Evelyn, and who spoke? Gryce looked upward. Swinging from a cage suspended near the apex of a window, an English starling tilted its head, recognizing the focus of attention. With a sudden, fierce squawk, the bird repeated the phrase: "Remember Evelyn!"

It was the final, uncanny thread in a tapestry of nightmares. An odd sense of dread settled over Gryce as he leaned forward to get a better look at the bird. Suddenly, without warning, the stark white illumination flooding the study shifted, swirling into a sickly emerald green. Gryce realized with a jolt that he had inadvertently pressed a switch on the complex electrical fixture above him, altering the hue of the light.

Irritated by this trivial puzzle that interrupted his investigation, Gryce stepped back toward the door. Just then, Styles's voice drifted in from the antechamber, where he was keeping watch over the mute servant.

"Shall I release him, Mr. Gryce? He appears quite agitated, though not threatening. He looks as though he has forgotten a duty or recalled a terrible omission."

"Yes, let him go," Gryce replied swiftly. "But keep close behind him. Watch every step. Unconscious movements may yield us vital leads."

As Gryce pushed open the curtain to step out, the cry from above rang out once more—"Remember Evelyn!"—so imperative and strange that he felt compelled to glance back upward. Was the appeal meant for him alone, or perhaps for the man below, whose ears remained forever sealed? Whether trivial or central to the mystery, the detective resolved to ignore it for the moment. The mute servant emerged from the hall, returning to his station with the stiff, respectful posture of a trained employee. Carrying his master's hat in one hand and the umbrella in the other, he handed them to Gryce before retreating silently to his chair, sinking almost immediately back into his catatonic stupor.

"Hmph!" Gryce grumbled, placing the items on the hall stand. "A confused mind, unreliable memory, worthless testimony. Has the tragedy driven him mad, or does his insanity precede the crime?" He might have pondered further if the persistent ringing of the front door bell—the signal of the growing crowd outside—had not been abruptly silenced by the heavy, authoritative knock of an official visitor.

"The coroner, or some other weighty figure," Gryce noted as the door swung wide, ending his solitary golden hour.

Mr. Gryce felt a profound sense of isolation in his pursuit of the truth surrounding this death, a disadvantage far exceeding those of any previous case. The victim, a bachelor, resided alone save for a silent, mute manservant. His location in the city was remote, devoid of neighbors, and his circle of acquaintances appeared surprisingly thin despite his visible presence in the district for months. Yet, the nature of his life—eccentric and detached—meant he likely possessed no conventional social network. Hours after news of the murder spread, the silence was absolute; no curious neighbors, no concerned friends had knocked at the door. This vacuum of local knowledge, rather than diminishing the intrigue, sharpened it. Consequently, Mr. Gryce welcomed the departure of the swarm of reporters, medical examiners, and officials who had gathered since the coroner's arrival, granting him a brief, albeit heavy, solitude over the scene.

Before succumbing to exhaustion, however, two critical matters demanded resolution. First, he needed to interrogate the boy, Jake, regarding the woman who had intercepted him earlier that day. Passing a chair where the child sat dozing, Gryce roused him gently. "Come, Jake, it's time to wake up. I need you to take me to the exact spot where that lady stopped you."

Jake, groggy and searching for his hat, hesitated. "I'd like to see my mother first," he mumbled. "She'll be frantic. I've never been away so long."

"Your mother knows your whereabouts," Gryce reassured him smoothly. "I sent word hours ago. She reported you as an obedient lad who never lies."

"She's a good mother," Jake insisted, his tone warming. "If I didn't treat her right, I'd be just like Father." He pulled his cap down low. "I'm ready."

Leading the way into the cool night air, Gryce guided the boy through the sleeping streets until they reached a bustling thoroughfare. They halted before a dimly lit shop window where Jake pointed to two stuffed frogs locked in a miniature sword duel—the very display that had captured his attention when the mysterious woman approached. Gryce watched the boy intently, sensing the weight of the testimony more than the whimsical toys.

"Was she a pretty lady?" Gryce asked quietly.

Jake scratched his head, looking confused. "She scared me," he admitted. "Her clothes... oh, they were gorgeous!"

"And she was young, carrying flowers, looking troubled?" Gryce pressed, recalling his initial impression. "What? Not young, no flowers, and not trembling?"

Jake shook his head, bewildered. "Well, she looked troubled, I suppose. But she wasn't crying. When she spoke, her grip on my arm held more emotion than her voice. She just dragged me to the drugstore. If she hadn't paid me first, I'd have wriggled free, sir. I like money; I don't earn much of it."

As Gryce moved forward, muttering to himself, "Not young," his suspicion shifted. An older mistress of Mr. Adams was often far more perilous than a youthful paramour. At the drugstore, Gryce stopped Jake again. "Show me exactly where she waited."

The boy indicated the precise spot with eager precision. "And were you standing there when you returned?"

"No, sir. She vanished while I was inside."

"Do you remember if she headed up or down the street?"

"I kept one eye on her," Jake explained, his voice dropping. "I feared she'd follow me back in, and my arm hurt too much for another squeeze. As she started to move, I stepped closer. I saw her walk to the curb and raise her hand. But there were no cars for quite a while."

Gryce's brow furrowed less. "So she hailed a carriage or a hack. Did you see any empty vehicles?"

The boy shook his head. His observations ended there. Recognizing the limit of the child's memory, Gryce handed Jake to a waiting assistant and returned to the ominous house where the tragedy unfolded.

At the door, Styles informed him, "Yes, sir, a young man is waiting. He calls himself Hines; he's an electrician."

"That is the man I require," Gryce said briskly. "Where is he?"

"In the parlor, sir. Anyone upstairs?"

"No, everyone else has departed. You go up; I'll guard the entrance."

After a brief exchange, Gryce led him into Mr. Adams's study, where the body lay temporarily removed. A single candle, placed per Mr. Gryce's orders, cast a gentle light near the door. However, Gryce immediately moved to the central table, pressing a button. Hines complied, extinguishing the candle. Hines began his inspection. Aside from the evident amateur craftsmanship, he found nothing defective, save for the flawless operation of the device. Gryce expressed his dismay. "He constructed it himself?"

"Undoubtedly, or someone with equal ignorance of modern wiring techniques," Hines replied.

Gryce gestured toward a bookshelf. "Check these volumes. Could an amateur with adequate knowledge assemble such a system using only these texts?"

Hines glanced at the spines without opening them. "A man with a deft hand and a scientific bent could replicate this, and likely exceed it, given the resources here. It requires pure aptitude."

Perched high, inaccessible without a ladder, it clearly relies on some contrivance for vertical movement. Locate the mechanism."

After a brief, fruitless search, Hines discovered a second button concealed within the wall tapestries. Upon pressing it, the cage descended slowly until it rested within Gryce's reach.

The bird, seemingly aware of the gravity of the moment, fluttered nervously before settling into a quiet slumber, perhaps unnerved by the intense scrutiny of the elderly gentleman holding it. Gryce placed the cage on the floor and, idly testing the room's functions, pressed a third button he had previously overlooked.

However, the expected shift in lighting did not occur. Instead, a harsh, bilious yellow glare persisted. The recent manipulation of the switches had evidently disrupted the circuit. Disappointed, Gryce prepared to leave, but halted abruptly, pointing to a new development that had escaped their notice. The heavy panel sealing the doorway to the antechamber—the only exit from these chambers—was beginning to contract.

It isn't wood, as a sensible door should be, but..."

"Steel," Gryce finished, his tone grim. "This is the most bizarre element yet. It increasingly suggests that Mr. Adams was deluded regarding electrical applications."

"Naturally, there is another switch for that," Gryce dismissed lightly.

They searched for five minutes in vain.

Thomas Cadwalader proposed his narrative rather than dictating it. Thomas Cadwalader—known to the world as Adams—never knew his mother, who perished in his early infancy. *After leaving the train station, I bypassed all thoughts of comfort and headed straight for the cemetery.* Before settling into town, I sought Evelyn's grave to renew the oath my father had made eighteen years prior when he held a four-year-old me aloft, pointing toward John Poindexter's estate. Emerging from the shadows advanced a young girl, bathed in a nimbus of rosy light that seemed to elevate her above the earth, lending an ethereal quality to her delicate frame and strangely illuminated countenance. Clutched in her arms was a massive bouquet of snow-white lilies. Observing that her gaze was fixed not on me, but on the grave beside which I stood, I slipped into the shadow of nearby bushes and watched. She began to scatter these flowers—symbols of innocence—over the mound I had just visited.

*What did this signify? Who was this young woman honoring the grave of my long-lost sister with such tender reverence?* As she rose from her task, I could no longer suppress the emotion and curiosity swirling within me. Stepping forward, I approached with the utmost respect, noting that her face was even more radiant when engaged in speech than when bowed in solemnity. Adopting the indifferent tone of a stranger, I asked, "Who rests beneath this stone, and why do you, a mere girl, lay flowers upon a grave whose moss suggests it was dug decades before your birth?"

*Her response sent a shock through me, equal to the startling experiences of recent days.* "I strew flowers here," she replied softly, "because the girl buried under this stone shares my birthday. I never saw her, it is true, but she perished in my father's home when she was no older than I am today. Having realized what it means to die young, I have made it a custom to share my birthday blooms with her. She was a lily, they say, in both appearance and character, hence I bring her lilies."

***

*I have shaken hands with John Poindexter.* To win the favor of the daughter, I must please the father, or at least attract his notice. I believe I have succeeded in this regard.

*I no longer feel like a man in the traditional sense.* John Poindexter appears cold, his manner rigid, his opinions unyielding. Yet, he fails to inspire the abhorrence I anticipated, nor does he awaken the singular thought due to the memory of my sister. Is it because he is Eva's father? Has the daughter's loveliness cast a halo over the parent? If so, Felix has every right to execrate me and curse my father for...

***

*The introductions you procured have opened doors everywhere. There is considerable wealth here, many magnificent estates. Consequently, I find myself immersed in a congenial society, of which she is the undisputed star.* Did I not say he was, as of old, the chief man of the town?

*She is beautiful.* She possesses the daintiness of a lily and the vibrant flush of a rose. Yet, it is not her beauty that moves me; it is the strange sweetness of her nature. This sweetness, however, knows no weakness; on the contrary, it possesses a peculiar strength, becoming instantly apparent when duty calls. Could Felix have imagined a Poindexter like this? I cannot reconcile such loveliness with the execrable sin that demands vengeance upon this household. I cannot even dwell upon my past life. Under her gaze, all that is dark, threatening, secret, and revengeful slips away. I dream only of what is pure, true, satisfying, and ennobling. And this transformation is wrought by the influence of her smile, rather than her words. Have I been given an angel to degrade? Or am I so blinded as to see a saint where others—let us say, Felix—would perceive merely a pretty woman with unexpected attractions?

***

*Rides, dances, trivialities. My interest in this young lady is beginning to be publicly recognized. She alone seems unaware of it.* Sometimes I wonder if our scheme will fail due to her impassivity and her more-than-conventional innocence. I fear she may never learn to love me. Nevertheless, I have exerted every effort to please her. Indeed, I could not have exerted myself more. Today, I rode twenty-five miles on horseback to procure a trifling item she fancied.

*Not all will unfold as easily as Felix imagines.* Eva Poindexter may be a country girl, but she has standards, and mere grace and attainment are insufficient to win her. Do I possess the other qualities she demands? That remains to be seen. I possess one quality she never dreams of. Will its shadow overwhelm the rest, causing her naturally pure spirit to recoil from me just as I think her mine? I cannot tell, and the doubt creates a hell within me. Something deeper, stronger, more imperious than my revenge compels me to view winning her heart as a necessity. I have forgotten my purpose in this desire. I have forgotten everything save that she is the one woman of my life, and that I cannot rest until her heart is wholly mine. Good God! Have I become a slave where I hoped to be a master? Have I, Thomas Cadwalader, surrendered my soul to the keeping of this innocent girl? I do not even pause to inquire. To win her—that is all for which I now live.

***

*She may not care for me, yet she is interested in no one else. Of this, I am assured by John Poindexter, who seems eager to aid me in winning his daughter's heart. Hard-won, closely bound. If she ever comes to love me, it will be with the force of a very strong nature. The pale blonde has a heart.*

*If it were merely passion, I might harbor some hope of restraining it.* But it is something more, something deeper, something that constrains me to look with her eyes, hear with her ears, and throb with her

While the world turned outward, a silent, internal war raged within the depths of a nature hardened against all human sympathy. By temperament, Felix Cadwalader was impervious; by circumstance, he was isolated. I can only hint at the tragedy brewing here, a sorrow so profound it defies ordinary comprehension. We know its shape only through fragments—shredded letters, addressed to no recipient, surviving only because the handwriting betrayed their author. Discovered long after the official account of Felix's demise, these confessions lay hidden beneath the floorboards of Bartow's chamber. How Felix acquired them, or why, remains lost to history, yet they speak volumes of a mind breaking under the weight of its own contradictions.

***

*I have seen Eva Poindexter again, and heaven and hell have contended for me ever since. Eva! Eva! The girl I viewed merely as prey, whom I had sacrificed to my brother. She is far too beautiful for him—a creature too radiant for any man save one untouched by female allure, unaccustomed to voices that stir the blood or faces that ignite the heart. Is this love? Can Felix, whose soul has known but a single thought, maintain a fortress of iron strong enough to exclude a woman, let alone his sister-in-law, from a corner of his breast? Never! I am either the victim of frenzy or the plaything of demons.*

*Yet, when I close my eyes, the vision lingers: those half-lidded eyes, those lips parted in innocence, that guileless bearing, and that divine tenderness tempered with steel, commanding admiration and awakening a fire I cannot extinguish. I must flee. I must never look upon her face again. Thomas's resolve is unyielding; he must never witness mine wavering like an idol struck by lightning.*

*'If Thomas had not been raised in Paris, he too...'* But I am the sole weak link. Curses on my nature!

*'Did I vow to run?' I cannot. Not yet. I require one final glimpse, a mere confirmation that my turmoil stems from her superior divinity rather than my own frailty. If I am merely startled by finding celestial beauty where I expected pale mediocrity, perhaps I should acknowledge my folly before it consumes me.*

*'My pride is restored.' I have seen them together again—he and she. True love burned in his gaze. How could I deny it? And I managed to mask my agony, imposing his duty upon him with a straight face. She loves him—or believes she does—and the design advances. But I shall not linger to watch its completion. No, no.*

*I confessed my tale tonight, disguised as a distant memory. Oh, the devils must laugh at us men! They surely do. At times, I wonder if Father, in the clarity of his newfound vision, joins them in their mockery.*

*'Home, to my wretched secret.' Back to where nothing distracts me from gnawing griefs and thoughts nearly unbearable. I pace the floors. I scream her name aloud, even beneath the portrait of Evelyn. Moments come when I am tempted to write to Thomas—to forbid him...*

*'Eva! Eva! Eva!' Every fiber of this miserable body screams the one word. But no man shall ever know. Thomas must never learn how her image haunts my days and nights, turning life into torment and the future into ash.*

*I await his letters (sparse, cold) like a condemned prisoner awaiting the axe. Does she truly love him? Or will that exquisite, soulful nature demand a stronger mate, a more concentrated spirit, a...?*

*'What have I declared regarding secrecy?' Bartow knows. In his silence, his deafness, he has uncovered my secret, revealing himself through piercing glances, dissatisfied mannerisms, and a jealous rage at which I would laugh in madness if torment did not choke the sound. A mute servant, plucked from obscurity, jealous of my failing for John Poindexter's daughter! He was never jealous of my feelings for Evelyn. Yet, until the day I risked fate by seeking her sight a second time, her name echoed through these halls, and my eyes dwelt on but one spot—the woeful beauty of her portrait, now my living reproach.*

*I have received a shock. The starling, trained to murmur Evelyn's name, today shrieked, 'Eva! Eva!' My first instinct was to crush its throat; my second, to tear it from its cage and press it to my chest. I did neither. I am still a man.*

*Bartow will crush that bird if I do not. This morning I caught his hand on the cage, a murderous light burning in his eye, easily understood. Yet he cannot hear the word the wretched bird whispers. To him, it is merely a syllable, a name to be suppressed. Should I hoist the cage high, beyond this old fool's reach? His deafness, his inability to converse, the precision with which he obeys commands conveyed via my colored slides, his animal-like devotion to my person—these necessities sustain me now. As for the bird—Ah! There it goes again, 'Eva! Eva!'*

*'Is it hate or love? Abhorrence or passion?' Love seeks salvation, yet I harbor no thought of saving her, having acknowledged her affection for Thomas, and believing him worthy. It is not for Evelyn's sake I plan revenge, but for my own! These endless nights and days of torture—the revelation of my own nature—the coerced consent to a union promising them bliss and me eternal anguish—all cry out for vengeance. They shall not escape. These two.*

*I have woven my plans deeply, accounting for every contingency. Time, thought, and gold have been spent. The result is perfect. If they step across the threshold of my Circular Study, they must submit to my will or perish within, dragging me down with them. Oh, they shall never know happiness! Thomas need not suspect; John Poindexter need not guess! I may have forgotten the oath sworn on Father's crossed arms, but I shall never forget the immeasurable sorrows of these past months, nor the humiliation they wrought. My own weakness—the unheard-of, intolerable weakness of it—must be avenged.*

The remainder of this harrowing sequence must be recounted in Thomas's own voice, for it constitutes the core of the confession he delivered to the detectives.

In strict accordance with my pledge, I escorted my young wife to Felix's residence on the appointed day and hour. We traveled on foot, as the estate lay but a short distance from our current hotel. Upon arrival, we were admitted by an elderly servant—a mute and deaf guardian, as I had been warned—who guided us through a hallway suffused with the aristocratic gloom of antiquity. At the threshold, Evelyn paled visibly, casting a questioning glance at me. I offered a reassuring smile, masking the profound dread that churned within me, and placed my arm around her waist. Following the old man past the open door of the drawing-room, we entered a chamber of stark, unadorned simplicity that caused me to frown. However, at the far end, Bartow—known to me subsequently as Felix—parted heavy drapes to reveal a study that, despite its initial austerity, transformed instantly into something akin to a fairyland. Or perhaps it always was, had it not been for the immediate, overwhelming force of Felix's personality, which seized the room and, by extension, our attention, with a magnetic intensity.

Evelyn, to whom I had revealed little of this brother, certainly nothing to prepare her for a suitor of such striking handsomeness or mental composure, appeared initially terrified and awe-stricken. Yet, she advanced toward him with a brave step. When I introduced us, she smiled with an inviting grace that I privately hoped might disarm him.

Perhaps it did. In the yellow, flickering light of the peculiar lantern suspended overhead, his already pale features turned waxy. The hand he raised in a mechanical greeting fell heavily, and he could barely stammer a welcome. Yet, his gaze, locked upon her face, betrayed a depth of emotion he clearly struggled to conceal. She, oblivious to the nature of his feelings, likely unaware that her entire future—and indeed her life, for happiness *is* life—hung precariously in the balance beneath the weight of his indomitable will.

Knowing, or believing I knew, the gravity of the situation, I cast him an imploring glance. "I have explanations to make," I said, "but might Mrs. Adams not rest here while we speak apart?"

He regarded me with a strange expression. Did he sense the revolt in my tone? Did he grasp, as clearly afterward as then, the nature of my compliance? I shall never know. I only recall that he ceased fiddling with a small object on the table, bowed with a sarcastic elegance that made me feel inferior to him even in stature, and led the way to a small, previously unnoticed door.

"Your wife will find it more comfortable here," he observed, his speech halting with pauses that indicated great, though repressed, agitation. He opened the door to reveal a small, elegant sleeping apartment. "What we have to say cannot take long. Mrs. Adams will not find the wait tedious."

"No," she replied, a natural laugh escaping her lips—one born, I dared hope, of perfect happiness. Yet, the strangeness of the procedure must have registered in her mind, as did the unusual demeanor of both her groom and his brother.

"I will call you—" I began, but the vision of her dimpled face rising above the great cluster of roses she clutched silenced me. I forgot the sentence, the door clicked shut, and I found myself standing alone, face to face with Felix.

His breathing had eased, and his manner seemed more natural now that we were isolated. Yet, he remained silent, casting a strange, searching glance around the room—the most bizarre apartment I have ever known. Satisfied, though I could not discern why, he gestured toward the large central table, which from the outset had exuded an uncanny magnetism over him.

"Come further away," he commanded. "I need air, breathing space in this stifling room, and so must you. Besides, why should she hear what we have to say? She will know the worst soon enough. She seems a gentle-hearted woman."

"An angel!" I started, but he cut me off with an imperious gesture.

"We shall not discuss your wife—or Mrs. Adams," he protested sharply. "Where is John Poindexter?"

"At the hotel," I answered. "Or perhaps he has returned home. I no longer concern myself with his existence. Felix, I shall never abandon my wife. I would rather prove recreant to the oath I swore before I truly grasped the value of the woman whose happiness I vowed to destroy. That is why I am here. Make it easy for me, Felix. You are a man who has loved and suffered. Let us bury the past; let us—"

Did I harbor any hope of swaying him? Perhaps a childish delusion influenced me up to this point. But as these words escaped my lips, indeed before I could stumble through them, I perceived by the set, bronze-like rigidity of his features that I might falter, but he stood immovable, firmer than ever, and far less susceptible to entreaty.

Yet, what of that? Even in the worst-case scenario, what did I stand to lose? A struggle that might plunge Evelyn into bitterness and cost me a fortune I had begun to view as my own? These were trivial considerations. Evelyn must eventually learn my true identity and the story of her father's guilt. Why not now? And if we were to begin life impoverished, it was still life; whereas a separation from her...

Before I could finish this thought, Felix spoke, in language I was least prepared to hear.

"I anticipated this. From the moment you pleaded for the privilege of marrying her, I foresaw this outcome and prepared accordingly. Weakness on the part of a bridegroom was to be expected; therefore, I have steeled myself to meet the emergency. Your oath must be kept!"

Crushed by the sheer power in his voice, a tone that promised victory over any ordinary resistance, I glanced around the room, mimicking his earlier survey. Nothing visible suggested treachery, yet a deep-seated distrust—the first I had genuinely felt—had surfaced alongside the strange look he had thrown upward and around the mosque-like interior of his study. Was it his calm confidence, or the weirdness of finding myself amidst Oriental splendor and night effects in broad daylight, surrounded by the clamor of streetcars and daily traffic? It was difficult to say. But from this moment, I was gripped by a vague, superstitious affright—not for myself, but for her, whose voice we could plainly hear humming a gay tune in the adjacent apartment. I resolved to suppress all signs of unease, even smiling, though I felt the imagined eyes of Evelyn's pictured countenance burning into me. I had never lost sight of that portrait since entering the room, yet I had not directly looked at it, and now I stood with my back to it.

Felix, facing it but refusing to raise his eyes, waited for a response. Finding my words stalled, he repeated, "That oath must be kept!"

This time, I found words to answer. "Impossible!" I burst out, flinging doubt, fear, hesitation, and everything I had trembled at to the winds. "It was in my nature to take it, wrought upon by family affection, the awfulness of our father's impending death, and a thousand uncanny influences carefully measured for this end. But it is not in my nature to keep it after four months of living in the companionship of a man thirty years removed from his guilt, and of his guileless, wholly innocent daughter. And you cannot drive me to it, Felix. No man can force another to abandon his own wife because of a wicked oath taken long before he knew her. If you think your money..."

"Money?" he cried, with a contempt that honored both my feigned disinterest and his own arrogance. "I had forgotten I possessed it. No, Thomas, I should never weigh money against the happiness of living with such a woman as your wife appears to be. But her life... I might. Carry out your threat; forget to pay John Poindexter the debt we owe him, and the matter will assume a seriousness for which you are doubtless poorly prepared. A daughter dead in her honeymoon will be almost as great a grief to him as a dishonored one. And either dead or dishonored, he must find her when he returns here in search of the child he cannot long forget. Which shall it be? Speak!"

Was I dreaming? Was this Felix? Was this myself? Were these words actually pouring into my ears?

With a spring, I reached his side where he stood close against the table and groaned rather than shrieked the words: "You would not kill her! You do not meditate a crime of blood—here—on her—the innocent—the good—"

"No," he said, "it will be you who will do that. You who will not wish to see her languish, suffer, go mad. Thomas, I am not the raving being you take me for. I am merely a keeper of oaths. Nay, I am more. I possess talents, skill. This house, in which you find yourself, is proof. Silence, heavy with unspoken dread, hung over the room after Thomas Adams's desperate plea. While the listeners murmured among themselves and Thomas turned toward his wife with a mixture of love and torment, Inspector Dyer returned the memorandum to Mr. Gryce. The document, a list of twelve questions born from the investigation, stood starkly divided: ten marked "Answered," one labeled "Not Answered," and the twelfth remaining open.

The answers filled the gaps where logic met the bizarre facts of the night. They explained the shift from a calm woman arranging her hair to a frenzied killer; they decoded the dying words, *"I return you your daughter,"* and clarified why the pronoun "I" was used despite the groom's status. They revealed why the victim clutched the paper in his mouth until death, why his face showed sorrow rather than rage, and why the crimson signal light remained dark—signaling *"Nothing more required"* even as murder took place. The massive steel plate found wedged in the doorframe was accounted for, and the sudden arrival of Old Poindexter, who cried out, *"It is Amos' son, not Amos!"*, was traced to a shock that surpassed the identity of the corpse itself. Most critically, the mystery of the left-handed thrust was solved: though Mrs. Adams was right-handed, she had struck with her left because Thomas had instinctively seized her right wrist mid-lunge. A convulsive grip, released only by the horror of the deed and her subsequent fainting, forced the fatal angle. With this revelation, the puzzling posture Bartow had mimicked in his pantomime fell into perfect alignment.

Yet, two shadows lingered. Question Eight—the nature of Bartow's madness and the specific joy he felt at the tragedy—remained unanswered, hinting at a depth of obsession that the current evidence could not fully penetrate. Furthermore, the lamp's color codes, particularly the red light signaling *"Stay away,"* still defied easy explanation within the framework of Thomas Adams's testimony.

Gryce, relieved by the solution to the ninth point, picked up a blue pencil. With a smile, he crossed out the lingering doubt regarding Bartow's gestures, marking the paragraph "Answered." The mechanics of the crime were clear, the physical evidence aligned, and the immediate riddles of the night dissolved under the weight of truth. Only the silent, swirling depths of Bartow's mind and the unexplained silence of the red light continued to whisper warnings of mysteries yet to unfold.

A short while later, Mr. Gryce stood alone in the antechamber, his gaze sweeping over the scattered group of individuals with a look of intense scrutiny. A lingering question plagued him, an enigma he hoped the assembly might finally solve. His hope was not misplaced. In a secluded corner of the room, he spotted Miss Butterworth, her demeanor calm and composed, seemingly content to wait out the aftermath of a significant intervention—one in which she had undoubtedly played a pivotal role.

Gryce moved toward her with a warm smile, gently accusing her of orchestrating the scene. She immediately rejected the notion, her voice rising with feigned indignation.

"I was merely present!" she protested. "When the climax arrived—when this young lady discovered her husband had vanished abruptly for New York accompanied by two strangers—it became undeniably clear that a woman, someone who truly grasped her plight and the gravity of her situation, must stand beside her. And I stepped forward to fill that void."

"You are always the woman who steps forward," Gryce remarked gallantly, interpreting her words as a testament to impeccable timing. "So, you are already familiar with Mrs. Adams's account?"

"Yes," Butterworth affirmed, her expression softening. "Her initial frantic ramblings revealed that she was the hand that wielded the knife against Mr. Adams. Subsequently, she was able to articulate the terrifying rationale behind what we initially perceived as a heinous murder. Upon hearing her narrative, Mr. Gryce, I ceased to hesitate regarding either our moral obligations or legal necessities. Pray, do you believe she will be summoned to face justice? Is a trial, and a conviction, inevitable?"

"Madam, let me assure you," Gryce said firmly, "there are not twelve men in this entire city so lacking in common sense as to classify an act of such obvious self-defense as 'crime.' There will be no true bill against young Mrs. Adams. You need have no further anxiety."

The shadow of dread instantly vanished from Miss Butterworth's eyes.

"At last, I may return home in tranquility!" she exclaimed, her composure returning. "Those past five hours have been a harrowing ordeal, and I feel thoroughly rattled. Would you honor me by seeing me to my carriage?"

To Gryce, Miss Butterworth did not appear remotely rattled. On the contrary, she radiated a serenity and comfort that bordered on the unnatural. Yet, as the arbiter of her own state, her judgment was final. Satisfied that her charge was recovering under her husband's attentive care, she accepted his offered arm and glided toward the vehicle waiting outside.

As he helped her inside, he seized the opportunity to inquire about Mr. Poindexter.

"Where is Mrs. Adams's father? Did he permit his daughter to embark on such a perilous mission without offering to accompany her?"

The response was curt and sharp.

"Mr. Poindexter is a man devoid of sentiment," Butterworth declared. "He traveled with us to New York, yet he refused to venture to the Police Headquarters. Sir, you will find that the united fervor of three hearts consumed by righteous wrath, fueled by a vengeance I have rarely encountered, has been squandered on a man utterly incapable of suffering. Do not speak of old John Poindexter to me again. Now, if you would be so kind, instruct the driver to take me directly to my residence in Gramercy Park. I have inserted myself into the machinery of law enforcement for the very last time, Mr. Gryce—positively the last time."

She collapsed onto the carriage cushions with an air of inexorable resolve. Beneath this stern facade, however, lay a subtle, unspoken satisfaction—a quiet pride that suggested she was neither wholly displeased with herself nor with the outcome of her meddling in affairs that typically clashed with the refined sensibilities of a gentlewoman.

Mr. Gryce, stifling a smile, bowed with a courtesy deeper than usual. Under the canopy of this profound bow, the carriage wheels turned away. Walking slowly back along the pavement, he let out a long sigh, lost in thought: *Will a case of comparable intrigue and human complexity ever draw us together in counsel again?*
