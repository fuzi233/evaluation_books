When the last spear had fallen and the shouting ebbed into that stunned, after-storm hush, they carried Sir Henry and Good into Twala’s great hut as though they were sacks of meal—too precious to leave outside, too limp to help themselves. I followed, my own feet dragging as if the soles had been traded for lead. Weariness has its own taste: coppery on the tongue, sour in the throat, and sharp at the back of the nose where pain begins to bloom. Mine came with familiar companions—the old lion-wound waking like an ill-tempered dog, and a hammering skull from the morning’s blow that had sent me into darkness.

We were a wretched trio, and we knew it; yet the one comfort that could still be chewed and swallowed was this: misery is a proof of breath. Thousands that had risen with the sun full-chested and laughing now lay stiff on the plain, and would not taste anything again—neither broth nor smoke nor the clean, cold air.

Foulata was there, moving with that swift domestic certainty that turns a battlefield’s aftermath into a sick-room. Since we had helped tear her from Twala’s grip, she had made herself our handmaiden—most especially Good’s—and now she set to work on us with the grave authority of a woman who understands what bodies need when men’s pride has spent itself.

The chain shirts came off with difficulty; each link had done its duty, and each had left its mark. Underneath, the flesh was beaten purple and black as bruised fruit. Steel had kept the points out, but it could not stop the blunt cruelty of impact; Sir Henry and Good were scored and blotched everywhere, and I was not spared.

Foulata brought a poultice—green leaves pounded to a wet paste on some flat stone, the way an old country cook grinds herbs for a stew, pressing until the fibres give up their bitterness and their scent. The smell rose at once: aromatic and sharp, like crushed wild mint mixed with something resinous. She spread it on with warm fingers, firm but gentle, and the relief was immediate, the skin cooling as though a shaded stream had been laid across fire.

Bruises, though, were only a nuisance beside the true dangers. Good’s “beautiful white leg”—a phrase he would have preened at if he’d been capable of vanity just then—had a clean hole right through the fleshy part, and he had bled freely. Sir Henry had other hurts, but the worst was the deep cut along the jaw where Twala’s battle-axe had found him. It would make eating an awkward business and laughter—if any man could have laughed that night—quite impossible.

Good, to his credit, was a decent surgeon even when half undone. When his little medicine-box was brought, he worked by the trembling glow of the Kukuana lamp—primitive oil and wick, throwing a smell of warm fat into the hut. He cleaned the wounds with care, his hands steady in a way his face was not. First he stitched Sir Henry, drawing skin together with quick, practiced motions; then, with the stubbornness of sailors, he set to his own leg and did the same. Afterwards he smeared both cuts with antiseptic ointment from a small pot—pungent, medicinal, a foreign smell amid native smoke—and we bound the places up with what remained of one of our pocket-handkerchiefs.

Foulata, meanwhile, set a pot to the fire and made broth strong enough to stand a spoon in—meat simmered down until it gave up its marrow and salt, a dark, sustaining liquid that didn’t ask for chewing. We were too tired to eat, so we drank. The warmth went into us like a small promise, and when we could no longer keep our eyes open we dropped onto the piles of karrosses—heavy fur rugs that held the day’s heat and smelt of animal life and woodsmoke.

Fate, with its dry sense of humour, arranged Sir Henry on Twala’s own couch, wrapped in Twala’s own particular karross—the very bed of the man he had slain. He slept there, if the word can be used for what we did. After such a day sleep comes in fragments, and each fragment carries you back into the teeth of what you’ve done.

All night the air was thick with sound—sound more than anything, as if the land itself would not let the ear rest. From every direction came the wailing of women: keening cries that rose, broke, and rose again, each one shaped by a different throat yet part of one immense grief. It rolled over the huts like wind in long grass. In that acoustic tide I felt, for the first time fully, the horror of ambition paid in human breath. Over twelve thousand men—nearly a fifth of their army—had been destroyed. Numbers mean little until you lie in the dark and hear what they turn into: voices searching for voices that will never answer.

Toward midnight the crying thinned, not from comfort but from exhaustion, until silence came in uneasy stretches. Then, every few minutes, a long piercing howl cut through—one single note, drawn out like a blade across bone. It came from a hut behind ours. Later I learned it was Gagool, “keening” over Twala, her grief (or her theatre) as thin and sharp as her body.

I slept at last, but in jolts. Dreams returned me to the mountain-top where a warrior I had sent to his last account charged again and again; then I stood once more within that glorious ring of Greys holding the mound against the rush of Twala’s regiments; then Twala’s plumed head rolled past my feet, bloody, gnashing, eyes glaring with a life that should have been gone. Each time I woke, the real sounds—the dying echoes of mourning, the soft shift of hides, the lamp’s faint hiss—seemed no less haunted.

When dawn came we looked at one another like men who had been boiled and poured back into their skins. Good was worse. Fever had him hard; his skin burned, his eyes were too bright, and soon, to my alarm, he began to spit blood—likely from some internal hurt gained when a Kukuana had tried with desperate force to drive a spear through the mail. Sir Henry, in spite of the cut on his face, seemed comparatively fresh, though he was stiff and sore enough to move like an old man.

At about eight, Infadoos came in. The tough old warrior looked little the worse for battle; he told us he had been up all night. He greeted us warmly, grieved to see Good’s state, and shook hands with an earnestness that made his wrist-bangles click. Yet I noticed a change: when he addressed Sir Henry there was reverence in it, as though he spoke to something not quite human. And, as we learned later, throughout Kukuanaland Sir Henry had become a creature of story—no man, they said, could have fought as he fought and then, at the end of such a day, slain Twala—king, and believed strongest warrior in the land—shearing through his bull-neck at a stroke. That blow became a proverb; any feat of extraordinary strength was thereafter called “Incubu’s blow.”

Infadoos told us the regiments had submitted to Ignosi, and messages of submission were coming in from chiefs in the outlying country. With Twala dead by Sir Henry’s hand and Scragga already gone, there was no legitimate rival left alive to stir trouble. I remarked that Ignosi had swum to power through blood. Infadoos only shrugged, the way an elder shrugs at rain.

“Yes,” he said, “but the Kukuana people can only be kept cool by letting their blood flow sometimes. Many are killed, indeed, but the women are left, and others must soon grow up to take the places of the fallen. After this the land would be quiet for a while.”

Later that morning Ignosi himself came, and on his brow the royal diadem was bound. He advanced with a new weight in his step—kingly dignity laid over the memory of the tall Zulu who had appeared in Durban months before asking to enter our service. The wheel of fortune had turned; a man’s name had changed his posture.

“Hail, O king!” I said, rising.

“Yes, Macumazahn. King at last, by the might of your three right hands,” he answered readily.

All, he said, was going well. In two weeks he meant to hold a great feast to show himself to the people. The word “feast” made my mind run, absurdly, to pots and smoke and the peaceable labour of feeding mouths—how strange that a land could be scoured by spears one day and measured by cattle and beer the next, as though history could be stirred into the same calabash as porridge.

I asked what he meant to do with Gagool.

“She is the evil genius of the land,” he said, and there was iron in his voice. “I shall kill her, and all the witch-doctors with her. She has lived so long that none can remember when she was not very old; she trains the witch-hunters and makes the land wicked in the sight of the heavens above.”

“Yet she knows much,” I replied. “It is easier to destroy knowledge, Ignosi, than to gather it.”

He paused, thoughtful. “That is so. She—and she only—knows the secret of the ‘Three Witches’ yonder, whither the great road runs, where the kings are buried, and the Silent Ones sit.”

“Yes,” I said, “and the diamonds are. Forget not thy promise, Ignosi. Thou must lead us to the mines, even if thou hast to spare Gagool alive to show the way.”

“I will not forget, Macumazahn,” he answered. “I will think on what thou sayest.”

After he left, I went to Good. He was wholly delirious now; the fever had seized him and would not let go, complicated by that hidden injury. For four or five days his condition was critical, and I believe with all my heart that without Foulata he would have died.

Women are women, the world over, whatever their colour; but it was still a curious thing to watch this dusky beauty turn Twala’s hut into a place of tending. Her movements were quiet, economical, always attentive to the sound-world around the sickbed. She understood, instinctively, that noise is a kind of violence to a fevered mind. She kept voices low, steps soft, the lamp set where it would not flare and startle. At her insistence, by the king’s order, the huts for three hundred yards behind Twala’s chief hut were cleared—so that no chatter, no laughter, no clatter of domestic life would drift in and jar the sufferer’s ears. In that wide hush you could hear the small truths: the lamp’s faint crackle, the fly’s hungry buzz, Good’s rasping breath.

For the first night or two I tried to help; Sir Henry did as well when he could move. But Foulata bore our interference with impatience and at last drove us away, saying our coming and going made Good restless. She was right. She had the manner of a trained nurse without ever having seen a hospital: swift with water, careful with cloths, unceasing in her watch.

His only medicine, beyond what Good’s box could offer, was a native cooling drink she prepared: milk warmed and then tempered, infused with the juice from the bulb of a tulip-like plant. She mashed the bulb until it bled its pale bitterness, then stirred it through the milk, letting it steep until the scent changed—floral at first, then medicinal, as if the plant’s cold heart had entered the liquid. She fed it to him a little at a time, as one feeds broth to a child, and she kept the flies from his lips and eyes with endless patience.

Night after night I saw the scene by lamplight: Good’s face drawn and emaciated, eyes shining too large in his skull, his mouth running nonsense by the yard; and Foulata sitting on the ground beside him, back against the hut wall, her shapely arms making a sheltering circle of attention. Her face was weary with vigil, yet lit by a look of infinite compassion—or something nearer and more dangerous than compassion.

For two days we thought he must die. Sir Henry and I moved about with heavy hearts. Only Foulata would not accept it.

“He will live,” she said, and her certainty had the blunt force of an elder’s proverb.

On the fifth night of the illness, I went across as usual to see how he fared before taking a few hours of sleep myself. I entered carefully. The lamp on the floor made a small island of light. Good lay quite still.

So it had come at last, I thought. A sob rose in me, bitter and involuntary.

“Hush—h—h!” came a whisper from the darkness behind Good’s head.

I crept closer and saw: he was not dead. He was sleeping—sleeping soundly, the first true sleep of days—and Foulata’s narrow fingers were clasped tight in his poor white hand. The crisis had passed. He would live.

He slept like that for eighteen hours. And I scarcely like to write it, for fear I should not be believed, but through the whole of that time Foulata did not move. She feared that if she shifted, if she drew away her hand, he would wake and fall back into fever. What she suffered—cramp twisting her limbs, hunger gnawing, exhaustion settling into bone—no one can measure. When at last he woke, she had to be carried away; her legs were so stiff she could not stand.

Once the turn was taken, Good’s recovery was rapid and complete. When he was nearly well, Sir Henry told him what he owed Foulata; and at the part about the eighteen hours, the honest sailor’s eyes filled with tears. He rose at once and went straight to the hut where Foulata was preparing the midday meal—our quarters being back to normal now—taking me along to interpret in case his meaning stumbled.

The smell in the hut was comforting after days of sickness: smoke clinging to the thatch, fat warming, something starchy steaming in a pot. Foulata worked as though feeding were a kind of prayer, her hands sure, her attention divided between fire and food with the ease of long practice.

“Tell her,” said Good, “that I owe her my life, and that I will never forget her kindness to my dying day.”

I interpreted. Under her dark skin she seemed to blush. Turning to him with one of those swift, graceful motions that always reminded me of a wild bird’s flight, she answered softly, glancing at him from beneath her lashes with those large brown eyes:

“Nay, my lord; my lord forgets. Did he not save my life, and am I not my lord’s handmaiden?”

It will be observed that she had entirely forgotten the share Sir Henry and I had taken in saving her from Twala. But that is the way of women. My dear wife was much the same. I withdrew from the little scene sad at heart, for I did not like Miss Foulata’s soft glances. I knew too well the fatal amorous propensities of sailors in general and of Good in particular. There are two things, I have found, which cannot be prevented: you cannot keep a Zulu from fighting, and you cannot keep a sailor from falling in love upon the slightest provocation.

A few days after, Ignosi held his great indaba, and was formally recognised as king by the indunas of Kukuanaland. It was an imposing spectacle, with a grand review of troops. The remaining fragments of the Greys were paraded and thanked before the whole army for their splendid stand. Ignosi gave each man a large present of cattle and promoted them all to be officers in the new corps of Greys then being formed. An order went out across the land that while we honoured the country with our presence, the three of us were to be greeted with the royal salute and treated with the same ceremony and respect accorded by custom to the king. Publicly, too, the power of life and death was conferred upon us.

Ignosi reaffirmed, before his people, the promises he had made: no man’s blood should be shed without trial, and witch-hunting would cease. Words, of course, are only sound until they are made into habit; and in any country the dominant tongue can turn justice into a slogan as easily as it can turn cruelty into law. Still, spoken aloud in that open place, with the crowd’s listening silence pressing on the syllables, the promises had a weight.

When the ceremony ended we waited on Ignosi and told him we were now anxious to pursue the mystery of the mines to which Solomon’s Road ran. Had he discovered anything?

“My friends,” he said, “I have discovered this. It is there that the three great figures sit, who here are called the Silent Ones, and to whom Twala would have offered the girl Foulata as a sacrifice. It is there, too, in a great cave deep in the mountain, that the kings of the land are buried; there ye shall find Twala’s body sitting with those who went before him. There also is a deep pit, which, at some time, long-dead men dug out—mayhap for the stones ye speak of, such as I have heard men in Natal tell of at Kimberley. There, too, in the Place of Death is a secret chamber, known to none but the king and Gagool. But Twala, who knew it, is dead, and I know it not, nor know I what is in it.

“Yet there is a legend in the land that once, many generations gone, a white man crossed the mountains, and was led by a woman to the secret chamber and shown the wealth hidden in it. But before he could take it she betrayed him, and he was driven by the king of that day back to the mountains, and since then no man has entered the place.”

“The story is surely true,” I said, “for on the mountains we found the white man.”

“Yes,” he answered. “We found him. And now I have promised you that if ye can come to that chamber, and the stones are there—”

“The gem upon thy forehead proves that they are there,” I put in, pointing to the great diamond I had taken from Twala’s dead brows.

“Mayhap,” he said. “If they are there, ye shall have as many as ye can take hence—if indeed ye would leave me, my brothers.”

“First we must find the chamber,” I said.

“There is but one who can show it to thee,” Ignosi replied. “Gagool.”

“And if she will not?”

“Then she must die,” he said sternly. “I have saved her alive but for this. Stay—she shall choose.”

He called a messenger and ordered Gagool brought before him. In a few minutes she was dragged in by two guards, cursing them as she came. When Ignosi told the guards to leave her, she collapsed at once—no more than a withered bundle of rags with two bright, wicked eyes gleaming out of it like a snake’s.

“What will ye with me, Ignosi?” she piped. “Ye dare not touch me. If ye touch me I will slay you as ye sit. Beware of my magic.”

“Thy magic could not save Twala, old she-wolf, and it cannot hurt me,” Ignosi answered. “Listen. I will this of thee: that thou reveal to us the chamber where are the shining stones.”

“Ha! ha!” she piped. “None know its secret but I, and I will never tell thee. The white devils shall go hence empty-handed.”

“Thou shalt tell me. I will make thee tell me.”

“How, O king? Thou art great, but can thy power wring the truth from a woman?”

“It is difficult,” he said, “yet will I do so.”

“How, O king?”

“Nay, thus: if thou tellest not thou shalt slowly die.”

“Die!” she shrieked, terror and fury twisting her voice into something that seemed too loud for her small body. “Ye dare not touch me—man, ye know not who I am. How old think ye am I? I knew your fathers, and your fathers’ fathers’ fathers. When the country was young I was here; when the country grows old I shall still be here. I cannot die unless I be killed by chance, for none dare slay me.”

“Yet will I slay thee,” Ignosi said. “See, Gagool, mother of evil—thou art so old that thou canst no longer love thy life. What can life be to such a hag as thou, who hast no shape, nor form, nor hair, nor teeth—hast naught save wickedness and evil eyes? It will be mercy to make an end of thee.”

“Thou fool!” she shrieked. “Deemest thou that life is sweet only to the young? It is not so, and naught thou knowest of the heart of man to think it. To the young, death is sometimes welcome, for the young can feel. They love and suffer, and it wrings them to see their beloved pass to the land of shadows. But the old feel not, they love not, and—ha! ha!—they laugh to see another go out into the dark. Ha! ha! They laugh to see the evil that is done under the stars. All they love is life—the warm, warm sun and the sweet, sweet air. They are afraid of the cold, afraid of the cold and the dark—ha! ha! ha!”

She writhed on the ground in ghastly merriment, and the sound of it—thin laughter scraping along the ear—made the skin crawl more than any spear-point could have done. There are voices that carry old power; not always good power, but the kind that survives because people cannot forget it.

“Cease thine evil talk and answer me,” Ignosi said angrily. “Wilt thou show the place where the stones are, or wilt thou not? If thou wilt not thou diest, even now.”

He seized a spear and held it over her.

“I will not show it,” she hissed. “Thou darest not kill me—darest not! He who slays me will be accursed for ever.”

Slowly Ignosi brought the spear down until its point pricked the prostrate heap of rags. With a wild yell Gagool sprang up, then fell again and rolled upon the floor.

“Nay!” she screamed. “I will show thee. Only let me live—let me sit in the sun and have a bit of meat to suck, and I will show thee.”

“It is well,” Ignosi said. “I thought that I should find a way to reason with thee. To-morrow shalt thou go with Infadoos and my white brothers to the place. Beware how thou failest; for if thou showest it not, then thou shalt slowly die. I have spoken.”

“I will not fail, Ignosi,” she said, and her eyes glinted as though she tasted some private relish. “I always keep my word—ha! ha! ha! Once before a woman showed the chamber to a white man, and behold—evil befell him. Her name was Gagool also. Perchance I was that woman.”

“Thou liest,” I said. “That was ten generations gone.”

“Mayhap, mayhap,” she answered. “When one lives long one forgets. Perhaps it was my mother’s mother who told me; surely her name was Gagool also. But mark: ye will find in the place where the bright things are a bag of hide full of stones. The man filled that bag, but he never took it away. Evil befell him, I say—evil befell him! Perhaps it was my mother’s mother who told me.

“It will be a merry journey—we can see the bodies of those who died in the battle as we go. Their eyes will be gone by now, and their ribs will be hollow. Ha! ha! ha!”
