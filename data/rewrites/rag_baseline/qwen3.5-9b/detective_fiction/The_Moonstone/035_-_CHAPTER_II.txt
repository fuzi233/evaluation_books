To reconstruct the architecture of the mystery surrounding the Moonstone, one must first examine the shadowy foundations laid by the so-called "Indian Plot." This narrative thread, though sparse, carries the weight of impending catastrophe, binding itself tightly to the events yet to unfold. Approximately a week following Miss Verinder's departure, the quiet order of my professional life was shattered when a clerk entered my private chambers, bearing a card and announcing a gentleman below who sought an audience.

I examined the card. A foreign name, now faded from my memory, stood at the top, followed by a line in English that burned itself into my consciousness: *"Recommended by Mr. Septimus Luker."* The sheer audacity of a man in Mr. Luker's precarious position to presume the authority to recommend anyone to *me* seized me with such paralyzing surprise that I sat motionless, questioning the reliability of my own senses. The clerk, perceiving my bewilderment, offered his assessment of the stranger waiting in the antechamber. "He is a remarkable specimen, sir," he noted. "His complexion is so dark that we immediately classified him as an Indian, or someone of similar extraction."

Connecting the clerk's observation with the inscription on the card, a chilling hypothesis took root: the Moonstone itself was likely the catalyst for both Mr. Luker's reckless endorsement and the stranger's intrusion. Against the grain of professional decorum, driven by a singular, consuming curiosity, I resolved to grant the interview. Permit me to justify this breach of protocol: no living soul in England claims a more intimate, blood-stained connection to the romance of the Indian Diamond than I do. I was entrusted with the secret of Colonel Herncastle's escape plans; I received his periodic dispatches confirming his survival; I drafted his will, bequeathing the jewel to Miss Verinder; I convinced his executor to proceed, hoping for familial gain; and, most critically, I silenced the scruples of Mr. Franklin Blake, inducing him to transport the diamond to Lady Verinder's home. If anyone possesses a prescriptive right to the Moonstone and its entanglements, it is undeniably me.

When the mysterious client was ushered in, an immediate, visceral certainty gripped me: I stood before one of the three Indians—likely the chief. Though clad in tailored European attire, his swarthy skin, lithe physique, and a gravity of manner that transcended cultural boundaries betrayed his Oriental origins to any discerning eye. I gestured to a chair and requested the nature of his business. First, he apologized—an apology rendered in flawless, elegant English—for the liberty of disturbing me. Then, he unveiled a small parcel wrapped in cloth of gold. Beneath layers of silken fabric lay a casket, exquisitely inlaid with jewels upon an ebony base, resting on my table like a offering from a forgotten deity.

"I have come, sir," he intoned, "to request a loan. And I leave this as assurance that my debt shall be repaid."

I indicated the card. "You seek me," I replied, "at Mr. Luker's recommendation?"

The Indian bowed. "May I inquire why Mr. Luker himself did not advance the funds you require?"

"He informed me, sir, that he possessed no capital to lend."

"And thus, he directed you to me?" The Indian pointed back to the card. "It is written there."

Brief, precise, and utterly functional. Should the Moonstone have rested in my possession, I knew without hesitation that this Oriental gentleman would have murdered me instantly. Yet, barring that lethal caveat, I must testify that he represented the ideal client. He lacked reverence for my life, but he honored my time—a virtue absent in all my dealings with compatriots.

"I regret," I said, "the trouble of your journey. Mr. Luker is mistaken in sending you here. While I am trusted with funds to lend, as any practitioner of my trade, I neither lend to strangers nor accept such tenuous securities."

Far from attempting to manipulate my principles, the Indian merely bowed again and rewrapped his casket without a murmur of protest. He rose—the very embodiment of the assassin I feared—and prepared to depart the moment I had spoken.

"Will your condescension allow me one final question before I withdraw?" he asked.

I inclined my head. One question at parting? The average in my experience was fifty.

"Supposing, sir, it had been possible—and customary—for *you* to lend me the sum," he posed, "in what timeframe would it have been possible—and customary—for *me* to repay it?"

"According to the standard practices of this nation," I answered, "you would be entitled to repay, if you chose, within one year from the date of advancement."

The Indian executed a final, profound bow and vanished from the room. It was done in a heartbeat, a noiseless, supple movement akin to a cat slipping through shadows, which momentarily startled me. Once composure returned, a singular conclusion crystallized regarding this enigmatic visitor. His face, voice, and demeanor during our interaction were controlled with such perfection that they defied scrutiny. Yet, he had granted me a sliver of insight beneath the polished exterior. He showed no sign of retaining my words until I revealed the customary term for loan repayment. Upon delivering that information, he locked eyes with me, staring directly into my soul for the first time.

The inference was undeniable: his final query was not casual; it held a specific purpose, demanding a specific answer. The more I dissected our exchange, the more I suspected the casket and the plea for a loan were mere theatrical props, designed solely to pave the way for that critical interrogation. I had confirmed this deduction and sought to pierce deeper into his motives when a letter arrived—from none other than Mr. Septimus Luker himself.

He implored my pardon in tones of sickening servility, promising an explanation satisfactory to my judgment if I would consent to a personal meeting. Driven by the same unprofessional curiosity that had summoned the Indian, I scheduled an appointment for the following day.

Mr. Luker, in every regard, was a creature of inferior stature compared to the Indian—vulgar, grotesque, cringing, and tedious—rendering him unworthy of detailed chronicle. However, the substance of his confession illuminated the darkness. The day prior to the Indian's visit, Mr. Luker had been confronted by the accomplished stranger. Despite the European disguise, Luker instantly recognized the visitor as the chief of the three Indians who had previously plagued his household, forcing him to consult a magistrate. This revelation sent him into a frenzy of terror; he assumed he was in the company of the very men who had blinded, gagged, and robbed him of his banker's receipt. Paralyzed by the belief that his last hour had arrived, he adopted the persona of a terrified victim.

On his end, the Indian maintained the guise of a complete stranger. He presented the casket and made the identical application he later used with me. Seeking the quickest method to dispel the threat, Mr. Luker immediately declared his inability to lend money. The Indian then inquired about the safest recourse for securing a loan. In his panic, Luker named me as the premier choice, citing my reputation as a respectable solicitor—the first name that sprang to his mind in extremis.

"The sweat poured off me like rain, sir," the wretched man confessed, his voice trembling. "I spoke without knowing what I did. I pray you review my actions, Mr. Bruff, considering I was truly frightened out of my wits."

I excused him graciously, eager to release myself from his presence. Before he departed, I detained him for one inquiry: had the Indian uttered anything notable upon leaving Luker's residence?

Yes. The Indian had posed the exact same question to Luker that he had asked me, receiving, of course, the identical response.

What did it signify? Luker's explanation offered no clarity. My own ingenuity, unassisted, proved insufficient to unravel the knot. That evening, burdened by a dinner engagement and a mind in no genial state, I ascended the stairs, little suspecting that the path to my dressing room and the path to discovery were, on this fateful night, one and the same.
