Sally surveyed the long table with a satisfied smile, feeling a genuine happiness settle over her. The party, which had stumbled to a start with an air of nervous hesitation, was now roaring into the success she had envisioned. The initial stiffness, largely caused by her brother Fillmore's glaring white evening waistcoat, had evaporated. The transient and permanent residents of Mrs. Meecher's boarding house were once again their boisterous selves, chatting and laughing freely.

At the far end, the conversation had inevitably drifted to the great topic of the evening: Sally's inheritance. There is nothing quite as intoxicating as dictating the spending of other people's money, and her guests were enthusiastically drafting a budget for her. With rumors placing the sum at a staggering figure, their suggestions were grandiose.

"Let me tell you," said Augustus Bartlett, cutting in with the brisk, incisive style of a Wall Street broker who knows the ropes. "If I were you, I'd sink two hundred thousand into a safe bond issue—Kahn, Morris and Brown just released one you should consider—and play with the rest. By 'play,' I mean a flutter on anything promising. Multiple Steel is looking hot; they say it'll hit one hundred and fifty by Saturday."

Elsa Doland, the pretty girl with the large eyes sitting to Bartlett's left, disagreed.

"Buy a theatre, Sally, and produce some real hits."

"And lose every bean you've got," countered a mild young man with a deep voice across the table. "If I had a few hundred thousand, I'd bet it all on Benny Whistler for the heavyweight title. I have inside info that Battling Tuke has been talked into lying down in the seventh..."

"Say, listen," another voice chimed in. "Let me tell you what I'd do with four hundred thousand..."

"If I had four hundred thousand," Elsa Doland declared, "the first thing I'd do is pay my bill from last week, which is due this morning."

Sally sprang up, flitted down the table, and wrapped her arm around her friend's shoulder. "Elsa darling, are you really broke? If you are, I'll..."

Elsa laughed. "You're an angel, Sally. There's no one like you. You'd give your last cent to anyone. Of course, I'm not broke. I just returned from the road and saved a fortune. I said that only to draw you out."

Relieved, Sally returned to her seat. The company had now split into two camps. The conservative faction, led by Augustus Bartlett, was firmly investing three hundred thousand in Liberty Bonds and safe real estate. The sporting section, swayed by the mild young man's insider tips, had placed Sally's money on Benny Whistler, carefully dividing the stakes to avoid spooking the market. The mild young man was confident that, handled with cunning, odds of three to one were obtainable.

"It seems to me," Sally interjected, "that you're working with the wrong figures. I only have twenty-five thousand dollars."

The announcement sent a chill through the room. To these masters of half-million-dollar schemes, twenty-five thousand seemed laughably small, merely enough for an heiress's car fare. They adjusted mentally, however. Even a pittance could be invested.

"If I had twenty-five thousand," Augustus Bartlett rallied, "I'd buy Amalgamated..."

"If I had twenty-five thousand..." Elsa Doland began.

"If I'd had twenty-five thousand in 1900," observed a gloomy man with spectacles, "I could have started a revolution in Paraguay."

He brooded somberly on alternate histories.

"Well, I'll tell you exactly what I'm doing," Sally said. "I'm starting with a trip to Europe, specifically France. Once I get my passport, I'll loaf there for a few weeks, then return to find a cozy little business to invest in and keep me in luxury. Any complaints?"

"Even a couple of thousand on Benny Whistler..." the mild young man suggested.

"I don't want your Benny Whistler," Sally replied firmly. "I wouldn't have him even if you gave him to me. If I want to lose money, I'll go to Monte Carlo and do it properly."

"Monte Carlo!" the gloomy man brightened. "I was there in '97. If I'd had another fifty dollars..."

At the far end of the table, a cough and the screech of a chair signaled the arrival of Mr. Maxwell Faucitt, the boarding house's oldest inhabitant. Rising with the easy grace of an actor of the old school, he bowed.

"Ladies," Faucitt said, "and... gentlemen. I feel I cannot allow this occasion to pass without a few words."

His audience was unsurprised. Life in New York was always prolific of incidents, but Faucitt rarely missed a chance to speak. Settling themselves with equanimity, aided by the genial feeling of repletion from Sally's hospitality, they listened. A movement by the Marvellous Murphys—the new arrivals from the vaudeville circuit—to heckle the speaker collapsed under a cold look from their hostess. Both lissom young men admired Sally immensely, and their admiration was well-placed.

She was a small, trim wisp of a girl with tiny hands and feet, a friendly smile, and a dimple that danced in her rounded chin. Her eyes, bright hazel, vanished when she laughed, which was often. She possessed an air of distinction lacking in most of Mrs. Meecher's guests and carried youth like a banner.

"I have been asked," Faucitt proceeded, "though I am aware that others here are far worthier of such a task—Brutuses compared with whom I, like Marc Antony, am no orator—to propose the health of our charming hostess, coupled with the name of her brother, our old friend Fillmore Nicholas."

The gentleman referred to, seated at the speaker's end, acknowledged the tribute with a brief, condescending nod. He was conscious of being surrounded by social inferiors yet tried his best to be kind. Sally, seeing it, debated for an instant whether to throw an orange at him. There was one ready to her hand, and his glistening shirt-front offered a perfect target. But she restrained herself. If a hostess yielded to primitive impulses, chaos would ensue. She had just frowned down the rebellious Murphys; throwing fruit would only weaken her influence.

She leaned back with a sigh. Pomposity was a quality she thoroughly disliked. Since affluence had descended upon him, her brother Fillmore had become insufferably pompous. He regarded himself as a Man of Destiny, receiving visitors like a stand-offish monarch. It had taken Sally an hour to persuade him to leave his Riverside Drive apartment for this occasion. When he arrived, his faultless evening dress made the rest of the party look like a gathering of tramp-cyclists. His white waistcoat alone was a silent reproach to honest poverty, causing an awkward constraint throughout the soup and fish courses. Most present had known Fillmore as an impecunious young man who could stretch a tweed suit to the limit; they had called him "Fill" and lent him money in lean times. Tonight, they eyed the waistcoat dumbly and shrank back abashed.

"Speaking," Faucitt said, "as an Englishman... I may say that the two factors in American life which have always made the profoundest impression upon me are the lavishness of American hospitality and the charm of the American girl. To-night we have witnessed the American girl as hostess, and I commit myself to the statement that this has been a night none of us will ever forget. Miss Nicholas has given us a banquet. I repeat, a banquet. There has been alcoholic refreshment. I do not know where it came from, nor do I ask how it was procured, but we have had it. Miss Nicholas..."

Faucitt paused to puff at his cigar. Fillmore suppressed a yawn and glanced at his watch. Sally leaned forward raptly. She knew how happy it made the old gentleman to deliver a formal speech; though she wished the subject were different, she was prepared to listen indefinitely.

"Miss Nicholas," Faucitt resumed, lowering his cigar, "... But why do I call her Miss Nicholas?"

"Because it's her name," hazarded the taller Murphy.

Faucitt eyed him with disfavour. He disapproved of the Marvellous Murphys generally, considering these transients lowered the tone of the boarding house, but particularly because the one who had spoken had addressed him as "grandpa" on his first evening.

"Yes, sir," he said severely. "It is her name. But she has another name, sweeter to those who love her, those who worship her, those who have watched her with the eye of sedulous affection through the three years she has spent beneath this roof. That name," Faucitt said, lowering his tone to what might almost be termed personalities, "may not be familiar to a couple of dud acrobats who have only been here a week-end, thank heaven, and are off to-morrow to infest some other city. That name," he soared once more, "is Sally. Our Sally. For three years our Sally has flitted about this establishment like—a ray of sunshine. For three years she has made life for us a brighter, sweeter thing. And now a sudden access of worldly wealth, happily synchronizing with her twenty-first birthday, is to remove her from our midst. From our midst, ladies and gentlemen, but not from our hearts. And I think I may venture to prognosticate that, whatever lofty sphere she may adorn in the future, to whatever heights in the social world she may soar, she will still continue to hold a corner in her own golden heart for the comrades of her Bohemian days. Ladies and gentlemen, I give you our hostess, Miss Sally Nicholas, coupled with the name of our old friend, her brother Fillmore."

Sally watched her brother heave himself to his feet as the cheers died away, her heart beating faster with anticipation. Fillmore was a fluent young man, once a power in his college debating society, which was why she had insisted on his presence. She had guessed Faucitt would say delightful things and mistrusted her ability to reply fittingly. It was imperative that someone replied with eloquence, or Faucitt's evening would be spoiled.

Fillmore smoothed a wrinkle from his white waistcoat, resting one podgy hand on the tablecloth and the thumb of the other in his pocket. He glanced down the table with haughtily drooping eyes. Sally's fingers closed automatically about an orange as she wondered if it might be a good thing...

Nature's law held true: the most attractive girls often have the least attractive brothers. Fillmore Nicholas had not worn well. At seven, he had been extraordinarily beautiful, but after that he had gone to pieces. Now, at twenty-five, he was something of a mess. For three years prior to his twenty-fifth birthday, restricted means and hard work had kept his figure in check; but with money came an ever-increasing sleekness. He looked as if he fed too often and too well.

Sally was prepared to forgive him, however, if he would make a good speech. She saw Faucitt leaning back, all courteous attention. Rolling periods were meat and drink to the old gentleman.

Fillmore spoke.

"I'm sure," he said, "you don't want a speech... Very good of you to drink our health. Thank you."

He sat down.

The effect on the company was marked, though not identical. For the majority, the emotion was one of unmixed relief. Fillmore's attitude had been so menacing and practiced that the gloomier-minded had given him twenty minutes, and optimists reckoned ten was lucky. To the bulk of the guests, however, it was the ideal after-dinner speech.

Far different was the reaction of Mr. Maxwell Faucitt. The poor old man wore an expression of surprise and dismay as if someone had pulled the chair from under him. He felt the sick shock of treading on a non-existent last stair. Sally, catching sight of his face, uttered a sharp wordless exclamation as if she had seen a child fall in the street. The next moment she ran round the table, standing behind him with her arms around his neck. She spoke across him with a sob in her voice.

"My brother," she stammered, directing a malevolent look at the immaculate Fillmore, who avoided her gaze and smoothed another wrinkle from his waistcoat, "has not said quite—all I hoped he was going to say. I can't make a speech, but..." Sally gulped, "...but, I love you all and of course I shall never forget you, and... and..."

Here Sally kissed Mr. Faucitt and burst into tears.

"There, there," said Faucitt, soothingly. The kindest critic could not claim Sally had been eloquent; nevertheless, Mr. Maxwell Faucitt was conscious of no sense of anti-climax.

Outside the boarding house, Sally had just finished telling her brother Fillmore what a pig he was. The lecture occurred immediately after the festivities, when Fillmore, furtively collecting his hat and overcoat, had stolen forth into the night only to be overtaken by his justly indignant sister. Her remarks, punctuated by bleating sounds from the accused, lasted ten minutes.

As she paused for breath, Fillmore seemed to expand like an indiarubber ball that had been sat upon. Dignified as he was to the world, he had never been able to prevent himself from being intimidated by Sally in one of these moods. He regretted this, for it hurt his self-esteem, but he saw no way to alter it. Sally had always been like that. Even their uncle, a grim man who had become their guardian after their parents' death, had never successfully coped with her. In the last hectic scene three years ago, which ended in them leaving for the world together like a second Adam and Eve, the verbal victory had been hers. It was also Sally who had triumphed in the one battle Mrs. Meecher always fought with each patron in their first week. A sweet-tempered girl, Sally possessed cyclonic potentialities.
