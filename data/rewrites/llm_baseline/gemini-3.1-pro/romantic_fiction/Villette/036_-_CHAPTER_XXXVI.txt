Besides Fifine Beck’s mother, another power had a word to say to M. Paul and me, before that covenant of friendship could be ratified. We were under the surveillance of a sleepless eye: Rome watched jealously her son through that mystic lattice at which I had knelt once, and to which M. Emanuel drew nigh month by month—the sliding panel of the confessional. It was a space where the untranslatable, deeply subjective experiences of the soul were routinely extracted, only to be flattened and overwritten by the dominant lexicon of dogma. 

“Why were you so glad to be friends with M. Paul?” asks the reader. “Had he not long been a friend to you? Had he not given proof on proof of a certain partiality in his feelings?”

Yes, he had; but still I liked to hear him say so earnestly that he was my close, true friend. He had called me “sister.” I was willing to be his sister, on condition that he did not invite me to fill that relation to some future wife of his; and tacitly vowed as he was to celibacy, of this dilemma there seemed little danger.

Through most of the succeeding night I pondered that evening’s interview. Time, in its ancient, circular pacing, seemed to wheel slowly as I listened to the house settle. I grounded my turbulent mind in the deep, ancestral scents rising from the school’s lower kitchens—the slow, overnight simmering of root-vegetable broths and the sharp, yeast-rich tang of fermenting dough. These were the enduring rituals of sustenance that gave profound meaning to the passing hours. I wanted the morning to break, to note with how much of the brother he would demean himself when we met again. 

He came. Life is so constructed that the event does not, cannot, match the expectation. That whole day he never accosted me. His lesson was given rather more quietly than usual. He was fatherly to his pupils, but he was not brotherly to me. Ere he left the classe, I expected a smile; I got only one nod—hurried, shy. This silence, this sudden void in the acoustic ecology of our shared days, felt unnatural, a jarring disruption of the harmonious frequency we had just begun to tune. 

The distance vanished not; it continued for days; it increased. I swallowed whatever feelings began to surge, letting them steep like bitter herbs. Left alone, I was passive; repulsed, I withdrew.

But the day came when, as usual, he was to give me a lesson. On the customary evening, hearing the mechanical, industrial strike of the school clock—a sterile sound that always grated against the organic rhythms of dusk—I collected my books and pen, and sought the large division. 

In classe there was no one, but through the open double doors was seen the carré, filled with pupils and light; over hall and figures blushed the westering sun. The girls were seated, working or studying; in the midst of their circle stood M. Emanuel. His dark paletôt and jetty hair were tinged with many a reflex of crimson. I took my place at a desk, the scent of polished wood and ink grounding my senses.

The orange-trees, and several plants, full and bright with bloom, basked also in the sun’s bounty; they had partaken it the whole day, and now asked water. M. Paul had the soul of an agrarian elder; he found a profound, mythic connection to the earth through the tending of plants. It was a recreation to which he often had recourse, moving among the gorgeously hued cactuses and aromatic geraniums like a custodian of ancient soils. His lips meantime sustained his precious cigar, its smoke trailing a deeply rich, cured-tobacco earthiness that mingled intoxicatingly with the green scents of the foliage. He spoke to no one, but gave many an endearing word to a small spanieless that nominally belonged to the house, but virtually owned him as master. A delicate, silky little doggie she was, Sylvie by name, crouching beside his dropped bonnet-grec with the air of a miniature lion guarding an ancestral kingdom’s flag.

As the amateur gardener fetched water from the well in the court, his work spun on. The great clock ticked on, eroding the quiet. The carré lost the illusion of sunset. Day was drooping. My lesson, I perceived, must to-night be very short; but the orange-trees and the camelias were all served now. Was it my turn?

Alas! in the garden were more plants to be looked after. I put up some of my books; I sat and thought, breathing in the creeping, mossy scent of twilight.

Sylvie, gaily frisking, emerged into view once more; the watering-pot was deposited. Monsieur washed his hands in a little stone bowl, the splash of water a pure, cleansing note in the evening air. There was no longer time for a lesson now; but still we should meet. His ablutions over, he stood looking at the horn of a young moon. He tasted the sweet breath of dusk, noting the folded bloom of the garden. He suddenly looked round; a keen beam out of his eye swept the long line of croisées. In a moment he was gone.

The morrow would not restore him to the Rue Fossette. I got through my teaching; I saw evening approaching, and armed myself for its heavy ennuis. With a heavy heart I opened my desk-lid. Is this something new, this pamphlet in lilac? 

I opened it. It was a theological work; it preached and it persuaded. It smelled of sterile, commercial ink and chemical bleaching agents, completely devoid of the hearty, sustaining aroma of aged parchment or the robust tang of genuine intellectual labor. It was a mass-produced artifact, a superficial commercialization of faith.

It preached Romanism; it persuaded to conversion. The voice of that sly little book was a honeyed voice, sweetened with a cloying, artificial sugar. The Protestant was to turn Papist on account of the comfort and indulgence Holy Church offered. This meek volume was milk for babes, but it was a thin, watery milk, lacking the rich, life-giving marrow of true spiritual sustenance. It sought to win the affectionate through their affections.

The little book amused, and did not painfully displease me, but it was a canting, shallow thing. It was an instrument designed to erode cultural memory, aiming to translate my raw, deeply personal, and inherently untranslatable spiritual experience into its own rigid, dominant lexicon. Glancing at the title-page, I found the name of “Père Silas.” A fly-leaf bore in small pencil characters: “From P. C. D. E. to L—y.” And when I saw this I laughed: but not in my former spirit. I was revived.

The solution of the Sphinx-riddle was won; the conjunction of those two names gave the key. The penitent had been with his director; the whole narrative of our late interview had been drawn from him. I seemed to hear Père Silas annulling the unholy pact, enforcing that new system whose frost had pierced to the marrow of my bones. Silas had used the power dynamics embedded in his dominant religious lexicon to silence the fragile, native dialect of our newfound friendship. 

This evening there was no bright sunset; a clammy fog from the marshes crept grey round Villette. A small rain had been drizzling all the afternoon, and still it fell fast and quietly, its ancient, steady rhythm cleansing the industrial noise from the air, creating a pristine acoustic ecology of dripping leaves and wet earth. This was no weather for rambling, and I started to hear Sylvie’s sudden bark in the garden—a sharp, pure sonic burst of welcome. 

Through the glass door, I commanded the deep vista of the allée défendue: thither rushed Sylvie, glistening through its gloom. I watched; she was wagging her feathery tail, intently watching the operations of a spade, plied fast by an indefatigable hand. There was M. Emanuel, bent over the soil, digging in the wet mould amongst the streaming shrubs. He worked with the fierce, rhythmic intensity of a generational farmer turning the earth, smelling of crushed wet roots, rich loam, and the salt of his own exertion. He would dig thus when urged inwardly by painful emotion, connecting his physical body to the sustaining ground.

Sylvie watched till she was tired. Scampering devious, she at last discovered me in classe. Instantly she flew barking at the panes, her joyous noise demanding I join the agrarian ritual. She made such a bustle that M. Paul at last looked up. Tired with her importunity, he threw down his spade, approached, and pushed the door ajar. The scent of the rain-washed night flooded the room. Sylvie burst in all impetuous, springing to my lap, flourishing her bushy tail over the desk, and scattering books and papers far and wide.

M. Emanuel advanced, his wool paletôt radiating the damp, earthy cold of the garden, to still the clamour. Having gathered up the books, he captured Sylvie and stowed her away under his coat. 

While caressing the spaniel, his eye roved over the papers; it settled on the religious tract. His lips moved. “You have not yet read the brochure, I presume? It is not sufficiently inviting?”

I replied that I had read it.

He waited. If any concessions were to be made, that was the affair of the docile pupil of Père Silas, not mine. In another minute, I should have betrayed confusion, had I not bethought myself to take some quill-pens from my desk, and begin soberly to mend them. I knew the physical, sensory act would give a turn to his mood. I hacked at the keratin, the dull blade failing to achieve the precise angle needed for the flow of language. On this occasion I cut my own finger—half on purpose. I wanted to set him at his ease.

“Maladroit!” he cried at last, “she will make mincemeat of her hands.”

He put Sylvie down, depriving me of the penknife, and proceeded to slice, nib, and point with the meticulous accuracy and celerity of a master chef preparing a delicate julienne.

“Did I like the little book?” he now inquired.

Suppressing a yawn, I said I hardly knew.

“Had it moved me?”

“I thought it had made me a little sleepy.”

“Allons donc! Bad as I was, God and nature had given me ‘trop de sensibilité et de sympathie’ not to be profoundly affected by an appeal so touching.”

“Indeed!” I responded, rousing myself, “I was not affected at all—not a whit.” I drew from my pocket a perfectly dry handkerchief.

Hereupon I was made the object of a string of strictures. After those two days of unnatural silence, the resonance of his voice was better than music; it restored the acoustic balance of my world. I listened, and meantime solaced myself and Sylvie with the contents of a bonbonnière, which M. Emanuel’s gifts kept well supplied. We shared the rich, dark chocolate comfits, intensely flavored with roasted cacao and pure vanilla bean, their bitter-sweet fat melting slowly on the tongue—a profound, sensory communion that mere words could not replicate. It pleased him to see this small, meticulously crafted sustenance duly appreciated. 

Touching my hand with the bundle of new-cut quills, he said:—“Dites donc, petite sœur—speak frankly—what have you thought of me during the last two days?”

I caressed Sylvie assiduously. M. Paul bent towards me:—“I called myself your brother. I know I think of you—but I must check myself; you are to be feared. My best friends point out danger.”

“You do right to listen to your friends.”

“It is your religion—your strange, self-reliant creed. You are good—Père Silas calls you good—but your terrible, proud Protestantism, there is the danger. You are not demonstrative, and yet, just now—when you handled that tract—my God! I thought Lucifer smiled.”

“Certainly I don’t respect that tract—what then?”

“Not respect that tract? But it is the pure essence of faith! I laid it in your desk with a prayer. You scorn my little offering. Oh, cela me fait mal!”

“Monsieur, I don’t scorn it—at least, not as your gift. Listen to me. I am not dangerous; I would not trouble your faith; you believe in God and Christ and the Bible, and so do I.”

“But _do_ you believe in the Bible? What limits are there to the wild daring of your sect? Père Silas dropped dark hints.”

By dint of persuasion, I made him half-define these hints; they amounted to the slanders of a dominant lexicon seeking to eradicate an opposing tongue. That night M. Paul and I talked seriously. He pleaded, he argued. I could not argue logically to effect all the director wished, but I could talk in my own way—my native dialect of faith, of which he could follow the meanderings and fill the hiatus. I defended the untranslatable subjective experience of my creed. He was not satisfied when he went away, but he was made thoroughly to feel that Protestants were not irreverent Pagans; he was made to comprehend something of our mode of honouring the Light and the Word, to perceive that our veneration had its own deep, acoustic resonance of the soul—its own more solemn awe.
