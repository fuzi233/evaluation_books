Here, I must pause the narrative for a brief moment to gather the facts. By cross-referencing my own memory with Penelope's journal, it becomes evident that the period following Mr. Franklin Blake's arrival until Miss Rachel's birthday offers little of consequence save for the passage of time. For those days, the house drifted in a quiet hum, bringing forth no events worthy of chronicling. Therefore, with your permission and aided by Penelope, I shall skip forward, noting only specific dates. I reserve the detailed, day-by-day account for the later era, when the Moonstone itself became the sole obsession of everyone within our walls.

Resuming our thread, we return to the incident involving the bottle of sweet-smelling ink discovered on the gravel walk.

On the twenty-sixth, I presented this piece of "jugglery" to Mr. Franklin, recounting what I had previously shared. His assessment was immediate and sharp: he concluded not only that the Indians were stalking the house seeking the Diamond but also that they possessed a delusion of their own magic. He referred to the practices of marking a boy's forehead and placing ink in his palm, believing these acts endowed them with visions beyond mortal sight. "In both East and West," Mr. Franklin observed, "there exist individuals who practice this sort of hocus-pocus, though usually without the ink. They call it 'clairvoyance,' implying a brightness of vision." He reasoned that the Indians, convinced we held the Diamond at Frizinghall Bank, had summoned their seer to guide them if they managed to breach our defenses.

"Do you anticipate further attempts?" I asked.

"It hinges entirely on the boy's capabilities," Mr. Franklin replied. "If he possesses genuine sight capable of piercing the iron safe at the bank, we shall be spared further disturbances for some time. If not, however, we shall likely intercept them in the shrubbery before many more nights have passed."

I awaited the latter scenario with confidence, yet strangely, it never materialized. Whether the jugglers had learned of Mr. Franklin's sighting at the bank and altered their strategy, or if the boy truly glimpsed the Diamond in its hiding place—a possibility I vehemently dispute—or if it was merely a stroke of luck, remains uncertain. This much is plain fact: not a single Indian approached the house during the weeks preceding Miss Rachel's birthday. The tricksters continued their trade in town, while Mr. Franklin and I remained vigilant, determined not to tip our hand regarding our suspicions prematurely. Thus concludes my report on the Indians for the present.

By the twenty-ninth, Miss Rachel and Mr. Franklin devised a new pastime to fill the lingering silence of their days. It is crucial to examine this occupation, for it bears significant weight on future events.

It is a peculiar trait of gentlefolk, especially those of intellectual inclination, to drift blindly into pursuits that are ultimately destructive. Nine times out of ten, driven by a desire to improve their minds, they resort to torturing or ruining something, firmly convinced they are cultivating their intellect while, in truth, they are merely creating a mess. I have witnessed ladies and gentlemen alike embarking on expeditions with empty pillboxes, returning with captured newts, beetles, spiders, and frogs, only to dissect them with pin-pricked cruelty or slice them into fragments without a pang of remorse. One sees young masters peering into spider guts with magnifying glasses, or young mistresses encountering frog-headed corpses descending the stairs. When questioned on this cruel filth, the excuse is invariably a passion for natural history. Sometimes, they spend hours dismantling beautiful flowers with pointed instruments, driven by a foolish curiosity to understand their composition, unaware that knowing how a flower is built does not enhance its beauty or scent.

These activities stem from a singular root: a vacant mind and idle hands. Just as children once dabbled in muddy pies, adults indulge in "nasty science," dissecting insects and ruining blooms because they lack anything else substantial to occupy their thoughts. The result is stained canvases, fetid jars of tadpoles, and grit dropped into victuals. While laborers may curse the grind of earning a living, compare that honest toil to the idleness that splits flowers and probes spider stomachs, and one should be grateful for a purpose that fills the brain and directs the hands.

Fortunately, Mr. Franklin and Miss Rachel avoided torture. Their only sin was making a mess, specifically of the door paneling.

Mr. Franklin, a man of universal genius, dabbled in what he termed "decorative painting." He claimed to have invented a novel liquid to thin paints, a "vehicle" of unknown composition. Its function, however, was simple and olfactory: it stank. Eager to try the process, Miss Rachel received the materials from London. Mr. Franklin mixed them, creating an aroma so potent it caused the dogs to sneeze upon entering the room. Donning an apron and bib over her gown, Miss Rachel began decorating her boudoir, guided by Mr. Franklin. They started with the interior of the door. He scraped away the original varnish with pumice-stone to prepare the surface, and she covered it with patterns and devices—griffins, birds, flowers, and cupids—copied from designs by a famous Italian painter (whose name escapes me, though he is known for his Virgin Marys and a baker's sweetheart). The work was tedious and dirty, yet neither seemed to tire. When not riding, visiting, dining, or singing, they were united in their bee-like industry, spoiling the door. If a poet ever asked, "Does Satan find some mischief still for idle hands to do?" he could have looked upon this scene and confirmed it with absolute truth.

The next notable date was Sunday, the fourth of June.

That evening, the servants' hall buzzed with speculation on a domestic matter that, like the door decoration, foreshadowed future developments. Observing the evident affection between Mr. Franklin and Miss Rachel, and recognizing their compatibility, we naturally wondered if their union extended beyond art. Some predicted a wedding before summer's end. Others, including myself, conceded Miss Rachel might marry but doubted whether Mr. Franklin Blake was the intended groom, for reasons that would soon become clear.

Mr. Franklin's affections were undeniably directed toward her. The challenge lay in deciphering Miss Rachel's sentiments. Allow me to introduce her fully, for understanding her is key to solving this puzzle.

Her eighteenth birthday, approaching on the twenty-first, marked a milestone. If you favor dark-complexioned women of slender, perfect proportions, you will find Miss Rachel a vision. Her hair was the deepest black I have ever seen, matching eyes of similar intensity. Her nose was slightly small, but her mouth and chin, as Mr. Franklin noted, were "morsels for the gods." Her complexion radiated a warmth akin to the sun itself, yet maintained a flawless order. She carried her head with the upright spirit of a dart, possessing a clear, resonant voice and a smile that first lit her eyes before reaching her lips.

Yet, despite her charm, she harbored flaws. Possessing a host of attractions, she had one distinct defect: she was stubbornly independent. Unlike most girls of her age, she formed her own ideas and defied fashion if it conflicted with her views. This independence served her well in trifles but, in matters of gravity, it led her astray. She judged for herself, rarely sought advice, and seldom revealed her intentions beforehand. She confided in no one, not even her mother, separating herself completely in joy and sorrow. As my lady often remarked, "Rachel's best friend and her worst enemy are, one and the other, Rachel herself."

However, beneath this self-will lay an absolute integrity. I cannot recall a single instance where she broke her word or said "no" while meaning "yes." In childhood, she frequently took the blame for a playmate's fault, suffering punishment silently rather than confessing. Even when charged, she would shake her head defiantly and declare, "I won't tell you!" Only later, when punished for her refusal, would she admit regret, yet she never revealed the truth. She was devilishly self-willed, perhaps contradictory, but certainly the finest creature I have ever encountered.

This brings us to the question of her matrimonial prospects.

On June the twelfth, my mistress invited a gentleman from London to assist in celebrating Miss Rachel's birthday. I believed this suitor held her heart privately. Like Mr. Franklin, he was a cousin. His name was Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite.

Context requires a brief digression into family affairs. My mistress's second sister, having suffered a romantic disappointment, subsequently entered a "misalliance"—a marriage deemed unequal due to the husband's lower station. She wed plain Mr. Ablewhite, a wealthy banker at Frizinghall. While initially controversial, time and social progress vindicated the union. The Ablewhites were respectable, prosperous, and highly regarded, residing near Frizinghall. Though we shall encounter them sparingly, their eldest son, Mr. Godfrey, warrants attention for Miss Rachel's sake.

Despite Mr. Franklin's brilliance, his chances of surpassing Mr. Godfrey appeared slim. Firstly, in terms of stature, Mr. Godfrey was superior, standing over six feet tall with a handsome countenance and flowing flaxen hair. Secondly, his reputation as a barrister and a "ladies' man" was formidable. A public character and accomplished philanthropist, he presided over countless charitable committees, managing funds and soothing the tempers of ladies' councils. He was a tear-jerker at public meetings, beloved by the destitute and the benevolent alike.
