Behind the oldest stretch of Holborn, where a few gabled houses still leaned forward like tired witnesses—still peering, as if for the vanished Bourne whose name they kept after its water had gone—there opened, by a narrow turning, a pocket of quiet called Staple Inn. You stepped into it as into a pantry off a roaring kitchen: the street’s iron clatter dulled at once, as if someone had laid soft cloth over your ears. Even the air changed its temper. A few smoky sparrows, lodged in a few smoky trees, chirped with a thin, stubborn cheerfulness that pretended at hedgerows and stubble-fields; and the little squares of mould and gravel beneath them were their whole countryside, scratched and prized with a courage that made the small place feel almost rural.

Yet it was a legal nook too, with its little Hall and its little lantern in the roof—one of those architectural trifles that look like they were built to delay business, and paid for by people who did not suspect they were paying.

In years when provincial England could take mortal offence at the mere rumour of a railway line in the distance, no tall modern structure had yet risen to tower over Staple Inn. The westering sun could once have reached it cleanly; the south-west wind could once have wandered in without being sifted through brick and smoke. But on a December afternoon, towards six o’clock, neither sun nor wind did it any service. Fog had found its way into every angle of the place and sat there, thick and taciturn; candles in the occupied windows shone as if behind greasy paper, their light blurred to a jaundiced smear.

One set of chambers, in a corner house of the inner quadrangle, showed above its ill-favoured doorway an inscription, black on white, with the air of a cipher:

P J T 1747

In that room sat Mr. Grewgious, writing by the fire, and never troubling himself much about what the letters meant—except on odd occasions, when he glanced up and let his mind toy with “Perhaps John Thomas,” or “Perhaps Joe Tyler,” as a man idly tastes a word he does not mean to swallow.

What had Mr. Grewgious been in youth—ambitious or thwarted? His face would not tell. He had been bred to the Bar and aimed at chamber work: deeds, settlements, conveyancing—the dry craft of making property change hands without seeming to move. But Conveyancing and he had never properly met; it was as though he had courted a lady who would not even come to the door. They had separated by tacit consent—if separation can be said where there was no union. Then Arbitration, blown to him on some lucky wind, found in him an obstinate passion for right; after that a Receivership—fatter, and traceable to more ordinary sources—settled into his pocket. Thus by chance he found his groove, and sank into it with relief. He became Receiver and Agent to two rich estates, passed their law-business downstairs to solicitors worth paying, and put away whatever spark might once have been called ambition. If he had ever struck a light, he had long since snuffed it.

His room was a neat anatomy of trust: account-books, files of correspondence, strong boxes—each in its place with the severe tenderness of a man who could not bear that, after his death, a single figure should limp or a single fact look doubtful. The very thought of dying suddenly and leaving a matter half-verified would have turned him rigid on the spot. Fidelity was his circulating blood. It did not run quick or warm; but it ran clean.

There was no luxury. Comfort, yes—dry walls, a good fire, and a snug hearth, though the furniture was faded as an old coat. The room’s private life gathered itself about the fire: an easy-chair in the corner, and a small old-fashioned round table which, after business hours, was drawn out upon the rug from a corner where it stood turned up on its edge like a mahogany shield. Behind that defensive posture was a closet that usually held something good to drink. Beyond was the clerk’s room; Mr. Grewgious slept across the common stair; and he had cellarage below that stair which was not empty. Most days in the year he crossed to a hotel in Furnival’s Inn for dinner, and returned, to make the best of these austerities until morning brought business back again, and P. J. T., date seventeen-forty-seven, resumed its quiet rule.

As Mr. Grewgious wrote, his clerk wrote too, at a fire of his own in the outer room. This clerk was named Bazzard: a pale, puffy-faced, dark-haired man of thirty, with large dark eyes that seemed to have forgotten how to shine, and a dissatisfied, doughy complexion that suggested it wanted sending to the baker’s. He was mysterious; and, more mysterious still, he had some strange hold upon Mr. Grewgious. Like a Familiar called up by spell and never dismissed, he clung to his master’s stool when any ordinary comfort would have kicked him away. His tangled locks and his general air belonged to some gloomy shade where lies ripen luxuriantly; and yet Mr. Grewgious treated him with an unaccountable consideration.

Toward the end of the afternoon, as Mr. Grewgious arranged his papers for the night, Bazzard entered.

“Now, Bazzard,” said Mr. Grewgious, looking up across the twin flames of his office candles, “what is in the wind besides fog?”

“Mr. Drood,” said Bazzard.

“What of him?”

“Has called,” said Bazzard.

“You might have shown him in.”

“I am doing it,” returned Bazzard, and withdrew with a gloom that was almost resentful of obedience.

The visitor came in at once.

“Dear me!” said Mr. Grewgious, peering round the candles. “I thought you had called and merely left your name and gone. How do you do, Mr. Edwin? Dear me, you’re choking!”

“It’s this fog,” Edwin Drood answered, rubbing at his eyes. “It makes them smart like Cayenne pepper.”

“Is it really so bad as that? Pray undo your wrappers. It’s fortunate I have so good a fire; but Mr. Bazzard has taken care of me.”

From the doorway came Bazzard’s voice, sour as cold tea. “No I haven’t.”

“Ah! then it follows,” said Mr. Grewgious, “that I must have taken care of myself without observing it. Pray be seated in my chair. No. I beg! Coming out of such an atmosphere, in my chair.”

Edwin, smiling, yielded and sank into the easy-chair in the corner. The fog he carried in—clinging to coat and neck-shawl like damp flour to a cook’s sleeve—began at once to be licked away by the fire, which took it with a greedy hiss.

“I look,” Edwin said, “as if I had come to stop.”

“By the bye,” cried Mr. Grewgious, as if a thought had leapt out of the coals, “excuse my interrupting you; do stop. The fog may clear in an hour or two. We can have dinner in from just across Holborn. You had better take your Cayenne pepper here than outside; pray stop and dine.”

“You are very kind,” Edwin said, glancing around the bare, orderly room with the curious relish a young man has for an unaccustomed domestic arrangement—like being invited to a strange, makeshift feast.

“Not at all,” returned Mr. Grewgious. “You are very kind to join issue with a bachelor in chambers, and take pot-luck. And I’ll ask”—he dropped his voice, and his eye twinkled with a bright, secret prudence—“I’ll ask Bazzard. He mightn’t like it else.—Bazzard!”

Bazzard reappeared, as if summoned from behind a curtain of discontent.

“Dine presently with Mr. Drood and me.”

“If I am ordered to dine, of course I will, sir,” said Bazzard, with the air of a man required to eat punishment.

“Save the man!” cried Mr. Grewgious. “You’re not ordered; you’re invited.”

“Thank you, sir,” said Bazzard. “In that case I don’t care if I do.”

“That’s arranged. And perhaps you wouldn’t mind stepping over to the hotel in Furnival’s, and asking them to send in materials for laying the cloth. For dinner we’ll have a tureen of the hottest and strongest soup available, and we’ll have the best made-dish that can be recommended, and we’ll have a joint (such as a haunch of mutton), and we’ll have a goose, or a turkey, or any little stuffed thing of that sort that may happen to be in the bill of fare—in short, we’ll have whatever there is on hand.”

He issued these instructions with the same uninflected manner as if he were reading an inventory, not ordering food. Yet the list itself conjured the warmth of a kitchen against a hostile night: soup that would bite back at the fog; a made-dish—some careful composition of minced meat, gravy, herbs, and perhaps a sharp edge of wine; a solid joint with fat that would melt glossy under the knife; and a stuffed bird, its belly packed with bread-crumbs, sage, onion, and pepper, steaming like a domestic hearth made edible.

Bazzard drew out the round table and withdrew to execute the foraging.

“I was a little delicate, you see,” said Mr. Grewgious, lowering his voice when they were alone again, “about employing him in the commissariat department. Because he mightn’t like it.”

“He seems to have his own way, sir,” Edwin observed.

“His own way?” Mr. Grewgious repeated, in genuine surprise. “O dear no! Poor fellow, you quite mistake him. If he had his own way, he wouldn’t be here.”

Edwin wondered—where then would he be? But he kept the thought to himself, for Mr. Grewgious had moved to the hearth, set his shoulder-blades against the chimneypiece, and gathered his skirts as if he were bracing himself to talk at ease.

“I take it,” said Mr. Grewgious, “without having the gift of prophecy, that you have done me the favour of looking in to mention that you are going down yonder—where I can tell you, you are expected—and to offer to execute any little commission from me to my charming ward, and perhaps to sharpen me up a bit in any proceedings? Eh, Mr. Edwin?”

“I called, sir, before going down, as an act of attention.”

“Of attention!” repeated Mr. Grewgious. “Ah! of course, not of impatience?”

“Impatience, sir?”

Mr. Grewgious had meant to be arch. The intention, however, did not survive long in his face. It collapsed before Edwin’s composed manner, leaving nothing but the heat of the fire; and he shifted, rubbing himself as if he had stood too close and scorched the joke.

“I have lately been down yonder,” said Mr. Grewgious, rearranging his skirts. “And that was what I referred to when I said I could tell you you are expected.”

“Indeed, sir! Yes; I knew that Pussy was looking out for me.”

“Do you keep a cat down there?” asked Mr. Grewgious.

Edwin coloured. “I call Rosa Pussy.”

“O, really,” said Mr. Grewgious, smoothing down his head, as if he were ironing a crease out of himself. “That’s very affable.”

Edwin glanced at him, uncertain whether the word was rebuke or neutrality. It might as well have been a glance at a clock-face: the hands told the time, and nothing else.

“A pet name, sir,” Edwin explained, as if defending a seasoning that another palate did not understand.

“Umps,” said Mr. Grewgious, nodding—but with such a compromise between assent and dissent that Edwin felt oddly checked.

“Did R—” Edwin began, trying to recover his footing.

“PRosa?” Mr. Grewgious repeated.

“I was going to say Pussy, and changed my mind,” Edwin admitted. “Did she tell you anything about the Landlesses?”

“No,” said Mr. Grewgious. “What is the Landlesses? An estate? A villa? A farm?”

“A brother and sister. The sister is at the Nuns’ House, and has become a great friend of P—”

“PRosa’s,” struck in Mr. Grewgious, with his fixed face.

“She is a strikingly handsome girl, sir,” Edwin went on, “and I thought she might have been described to you, or presented to you perhaps?”

“Neither,” said Mr. Grewgious. “But here is Bazzard.”

Bazzard returned, ushering in two waiters and a fresh gust of fog that made the fire roar anew. One waiter was immovable: he brought nothing but authority and complaint. The other was flying: he carried everything on his shoulders, quick as a kitchen-boy with too many plates and not enough breath. The flying waiter whisked the cloth over the table and set it true with swift, practised hands; the immovable waiter found fault with the straightness. The flying waiter then polished the glasses to a bright transparency; the immovable waiter held them up and looked through them, as if searching for defects in the world.

Then began the flights across Holborn: out for soup, back with a tureen that breathed a peppery, meaty heat into the room; out again for the made-dish; back with a covered entrée sending up a rich scent of browned crust and gravy; out again for the joint and the poultry; back with weight and steam and the promise of fat rendered to sweetness. And between those errands, more frantic dashes for forgotten requisites—salt, mustard, bread, perhaps a relish—because each time it was “discovered” that the immovable waiter had forgotten everything at once. No matter how the flying waiter cleft the foggy air, he was reproached on every return for bringing fog with him and being out of breath, as if he ought to have left his lungs somewhere on the threshold.

When the meal was done and the flying waiter stood blown and damp at the edges like pastry that has met too much steam, the immovable waiter gathered the tablecloth under his arm with a grand air, watched the other set the clean glasses about, and then directed towards Mr. Grewgious a final look which said, as plainly as a stamped seal: Understand that the reward is mine, and Nil the claim of this slave. So, pushing the flying waiter before him, he marched out.

It was a miniature portrait—highly finished—of offices where the man who does nothing claims all credit and the man who does all is blamed for the weather. If it had been painted on ivory, it might have hung, edifyingly, in a national gallery of administrative vices.

If fog had been the cause of the feast, it served also as its sauce. Outside, the clerks in the Inn-yard could be heard sneezing and wheezing, stamping their feet on gravel in an effort to keep themselves alive; the sound sharpened the relish of warmth within. To shiver and bid the unfortunate flying waiter shut the door before he had properly opened it gave a spice beyond any bottled condiment. Even the rhythm of the door—tray first, leg after, the leg lingering like the last reluctant note in a phrase—became part of the evening’s texture: a domestic theatre of thresholds, in which cold and noise were the villains.

Mr. Grewgious went below to the cellar and brought up bottles whose colours alone seemed like remembered seasons: ruby, straw, and gold. They were wines ripened long ago in lands where air does not coagulate into fog, and then left slumbering in the dark. Once roused, they tingled as if impatient with their own age, pushing against their corks to help the corkscrew, and when freed they sprang out gaily, like prisoners turning riot into holiday. If P. J. T. in seventeen-forty-seven drank such wine—or any year of his period—then P. J. T. must indeed have been Pretty Jolly Too.

Yet the wines, though they warmed the tongue and carried their bright, sharp fruit through the mouth, made no visible change in Mr. Grewgious. They might have been poured over him and run off to waste; his face kept its high-dried, snuff-coloured composure. But he watched Edwin in his wooden, observant way; and when the dinner ended, and he motioned Edwin back into the easy-chair by the fire, his eyes followed him between the smoothing fingers with which he continually adjusted his own head and face, as if polishing himself into readiness.

“Bazzard!” said Mr. Grewgious, turning suddenly.

“I follow you, sir,” returned Bazzard, who had eaten and drunk with workmanlike gravity, mostly in silence.

“I drink to you, Bazzard; Mr. Edwin, success to Mr. Bazzard!”

“Success to Mr. Bazzard!” echoed Edwin, with an enthusiasm he did not feel, adding silently: Success in what, I wonder?

“And May!” pursued Mr. Grewgious, hesitating in a way that suggested he was trying to open a stiff lock with the wrong key. “I am not at liberty to be definite—May!—my conversational powers are so very limited that I know I shall not come well out of this—May!—it ought to be put imaginatively, but I have no imagination—May!—the thorn of anxiety is as nearly the mark as I am likely to get—May it come out at last!”

Bazzard frowned a smile at the fire and put a hand into his tangled hair, as if feeling for the thorn there; then into his waistcoat; then into his pockets. Edwin watched each motion, half expecting some literal thorn to appear and prick, but nothing emerged. Bazzard only said, “I follow you, sir, and I thank you.”

“I am going,” said Mr. Grewgious, jingling his glass softly on the table, and bending aside under cover of his other hand to whisper to Edwin, “to drink to my ward. But I put Bazzard first. He mightn’t like it else.”

He attempted a wink—so slow that it barely completed itself. Edwin, out of politeness and confusion, winked back without the faintest notion what he meant.

“And now,” said Mr. Grewgious, “I devote a bumper to the fair and fascinating Miss Rosa. Bazzard, the fair and fascinating Miss Rosa!”

“I follow you, sir,” said Bazzard. “And I pledge you!”

“And so do I!” cried Edwin.

Blank silence followed, as if some small ceremony had to cool before speech could be ventured again. Why such pauses fall upon people after they have done a simple social act—done it without any sorrowful intention—is one of those mysteries of company that no law-book explains. The only steady sound was the fire, and beyond it, muffled by fog and distance, the softened world: a hush that made every clink of glass seem louder, and every breath a little more private.

“Lord bless me,” said Mr. Grewgious at last, as though he had been compelled to speak by the weight of his own observation. “I am a particularly Angular man, and yet I fancy—if I may use the word, not having a morsel of fancy—that I could draw a picture of a true lover’s state of mind, to-night.”

“Let us follow you, sir,” said Bazzard, “and have the picture.”

“Mr. Edwin will correct it where it’s wrong,” resumed Mr. Grewgious, “and will throw in a few touches from the life. I dare say it is wrong in many particulars, and wants many touches from the life, for I was born a Chip, and have neither soft sympathies nor soft experiences. Well! I hazard the guess that the true lover’s mind is completely permeated by the beloved object of his affections. I hazard the guess that her dear name is precious to him, cannot be heard or repeated without emotion, and is preserved sacred. If he has any distinguishing appellation of fondness for her, it is reserved for her, and is not for common ears. A name that it would be a privilege to call her by, being alone with her own bright self, it would be a liberty, a coldness, an insensibility, almost a breach of good faith, to flaunt elsewhere.”

He sat bolt upright, hands on knees, chopping the sentences out as if repeating a lesson learnt by heart. He showed no corresponding feeling, unless in a slight tingling at the tip of his nose. Yet the meaning, spoken in that dry tone, fell with a sharper edge—like salt in plain broth.

Edwin, as certain points found him, turned red and then white. He stared into the fire and bit his lip.

“My picture,” Mr. Grewgious went on, “goes on to represent—under correction from you, Mr. Edwin—the true lover as ever impatient to be in the presence or vicinity of the beloved object of his affections; as caring very little for his case in any other society; and as constantly seeking that. If I was to say seeking that, as a bird seeks its nest, I should make an ass of myself, because that would trench upon what I understand to be poetry; and I am so far from trenching upon poetry at any time, that I never, to my knowledge, got within ten thousand miles of it. And I am besides totally unacquainted with the habits of birds, except the birds of Staple Inn, who seek their nests on ledges, and in gutter-pipes and chimneypots, not constructed for them by the beneficent hand of Nature. I beg, therefore, to be understood as foregoing the bird’s-nest. But my picture does represent the true lover as having no existence separable from that of the beloved object of his affections, and as living at once a doubled life and a halved life. And if I do not clearly express what I mean by that, it is either for the reason that having no conversational powers, I cannot express what I mean, or that having no meaning, I do not mean what I fail to express. Which, to the best of my belief, is not the case.”

Abrupt as a snapped string, he concluded and jerked his question at Edwin.

“I figure to myself,” he said, “that there can be no coolness, no lassitude, no doubt, no indifference, no half fire and half smoke state of mind, in a real lover. Pray am I at all near the mark in my picture?”

“I should say, sir,” Edwin stammered, “as you refer the question to me—”

“Yes,” said Mr. Grewgious. “I refer it to you, as an authority.”

“I should say, then, sir,” Edwin went on, visibly hampered, “that the picture you have drawn is generally correct; but I submit that perhaps you may be rather hard upon the unlucky lover.”

“Likely so,” returned Mr. Grewgious. “Likely so. I am a hard man in the grain.”

“He may not show,” Edwin said, “all he feels; or he may not—”

He stopped, hunting for words. And at once Mr. Grewgious struck in, in a way that made the gap yawn wider.

“No to be sure; he may not!”

After that they all sat silent again. Bazzard’s silence, however, had slipped into sleep; his head inclined, his gloom at rest at last.

“His responsibility is very great, though,” said Mr. Grewgious, after a time, his eyes fixed on the coals as if reading there.

Edwin nodded, eyes fixed likewise, as if the fire held the only language he could trust.

“And let him be sure that he trifles with no one,” pursued Mr. Grewgious; “neither with himself, nor with any other.”

Edwin bit his lip again.

“He must not make a plaything of a treasure. Woe betide him if he does! Let him take that well to heart,” said Mr. Grewgious.

He spoke in short sentences, like a charity boy repeating Proverbs; yet there was something almost dreamy—strange in so literal a man—in the way he shook his right forefinger at the live coals, as if warning the fire itself, and then sank into quiet.

Not for long. He rapped his knees, as if a carved idol had come out of reverie, and said briskly: “We must finish this bottle, Mr. Edwin. Let me help you. I’ll help Bazzard too, though he is asleep. He mightn’t like it else.”

He filled their glasses, filled his own, and drained his with decision. Then he set it bottom upward on the table as if he had trapped a bluebottle.

“And now, Mr. Edwin,” he said, wiping mouth and hands with his handkerchief, “to a little piece of business. You received from me, the other day, a certified copy of Miss Rosa’s father’s will. You knew its contents before, but you received it from me as a matter of business. I should have sent it to Mr. Jasper, but for Miss Rosa’s wishing it to come straight to you, in preference. You received it?”

“Quite safely, sir.”

“You should have acknowledged its receipt,” said Mr. Grewgious. “Business being business all the world over. However, you did not.”

“I meant to have acknowledged it when I first came in this evening, sir.”
