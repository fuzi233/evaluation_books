The new pope favored the Dominican order. A message arrived from the Vatican, summoning a learned friar to teach at the University of Basle. Clement was the perfect choice—well-versed in languages and once a student under Guarini the younger. His journey to England was delayed, though not abandoned; instead, he was sent to Basle. But since he would not be needed there for three months, he was instructed to preach along the way.

He left through the northern gate, eyes lowered, his entire being wrapped in quiet devotion. If only one could paint a soul’s history as vividly as a fresco on a wall—what a living portrait this barefoot friar would have been! Within twelve short months, he had known joy and love, loss and despair, rebellion and sin, suicide and remorse, spiritual desolation, repentance, renunciation of the world, and surrender to God. Now he walked again—yet everything had changed. No longer was he hunted by danger or temptation. Even thieves and outlaws bowed before him, not to rob, but to offer stolen coin and beg for prayers.

His journey offered few dramatic scenes. Yet I know some readers who care less for spectacle than for the inner life of the soul. For them, silence can speak louder than thunder, and stillness reveal more than storm. Such moments are often the truest markers of transformation.

On the first Sunday of his travels, Clement did something he had never done before: he prayed for Margaret’s soul. Not because he doubted her salvation—no, her eternal peace meant more to him than anything on earth—but because of his own humility. When he heard of her death, his grief had burst forth in blasphemies that shocked even well-meaning hearts. But they horrified him far more than anyone else. During his novitiate, he had long wrestled with religious despair, convinced he had committed the unforgivable sin against the Holy Spirit. Gradually, thanks to Anselmo’s gentle guidance, that darkness lifted. Yet deep self-loathing remained. He felt unworthy of grace himself, and how then could he dare pray for a soul so pure, so innocent compared to his own stained nature? So he had always asked others—good Anselm, another kind monk—to intercede for her. They never refused, nor did they half-heartedly fulfill the request. The older monks, especially the good ones among them, held their younger brothers with a tenderness that seemed beyond this world.

Now, after preaching in a small Italian town on Sunday morning, Clement felt an overwhelming surge of spiritual strength. That day, a warm sense of divine forgiveness washed over him. And for the first time, he prayed directly for Margaret’s soul. From that moment, it became his daily practice—the one pure thread binding his heart to earth. His family, once so close, now seemed like shadows from another life. The Church claimed all his allegiance. Yet even the Church could not fully extinguish lesser loves—only transform them.

For most of the journey, Clement felt little within him stir, except when he stood in the pulpit. Another rare spark came when he helped others.

Once, he passed a village where a young man lay in agony, bitten by a tarantula—or perhaps only imagining it. For two days, he had not slept, writhing, leaping, striking the walls. Village musicians had worsened it with their frantic tunes. Exhaustion and death loomed. Clement stopped, learned what had happened, and fetched a psaltery. He played soothing melodies, but they only made the youth moan and writhe. Then Clement noticed: the man’s lips moved, even in unconsciousness. He leaned closer and heard—whispered beneath the pain—a tune unlike any he’d ever heard, original, strange, born of madness or mind. He learned it and played it back. Instantly, the patient’s face softened. He danced lightly around the room, enchanted. When Clement’s fingers grew numb from repeating the melody, he saw the youth sink into peaceful sleep, cradled by the lullaby of his own imagination. It saved his life. Clement taught the tune to another, just in case, and continued on, his heart warmed.

Another time, he came upon a mob dragging a man through the streets. The man struggled, shouting in a language Clement didn’t recognize. He had entered the town proudly, waving a mulberry branch above his head. The villagers stared, then pounced. They dragged him before the podesta. Clement followed, but on the way, he quietly approached the prisoner and spoke to him in Italian—no reply. Then French, German, Dutch—still nothing. Finally, the man tried Latin, with a sharp accent. He said he was English, and had simply taken a branch to shield himself from the sun. “In my country,” he declared, “anyone may take what grows by the roadside. Confound these fools—I’m ready to pay. But here, all Italy rises up over a twig and leaves!”

The stubborn podesta wanted to throw him in jail. Clement stepped in, patiently explaining that silkworms—and thus mulberry trees—were sacred, protected by the sovereign, whose income depended on them. He urged that ignorance of such laws was natural for someone from a distant land where the tree might not even exist.

The Englishman, still irked, pulled out a purse and repeated his point: “This is a matter of commerce. What harm have I done? Say it, and I’ll pay at once.”

The podesta sniffed the gold. A ducat was levied—for the duke. About the value of the whole tree. He pocketed the coin.

The Englishman laughed as soon as he was free. “You’re too good for this hole of a country, father,” he said. “Come to England! That’s the only place in the world. I was a fool to leave it, wandering among mulberries and their fools. I’m a Kentish squire, educated at Cambridge. My name is Rolfe, and my estate is Betshanger. Man and house are yours. Come stay till Doomsday. We sit forty at dinner every day. One more or one less won’t be noticed. You can end your days with me and my heirs. Come now! An Englishman means what he says.” He gripped Clement’s hand firmly.

“I will visit you someday, my son,” Clement replied, “but not to burden your hospitality.”

Then the Englishman begged to be shriven. “I don’t know what will become of my soul,” he confessed. “I’ve lived like a heathen since leaving England.”

Clement consented gladly. Soon the islander knelt by the roadside, confessing sins from the past month. Impressed by his piety, Clement revealed he was indeed going to England. The man asked if it was true that the country teemed with Lollards and Wickliffites.

He flushed slightly. “There are black sheep everywhere,” he said. Then, after thought, added gravely: “Holy father, hear the truth about these heretics. None are more loyal to the Church than we English. But we are ourselves—free, independent. We love our ways, above all our own tongue. The Normans conquered our billhooks, but not our speech. They tried hard for centuries, by law and decree. Our foreign priests speak God’s word in Latin or French or Italian—like sheep bleating. Then came Wickliff and his followers, reading Scripture aloud in plain English. Who can resist that? God forgive me, I believe the English would turn deaf ears to St. Peter himself if he spoke not in the tongue their mothers planted in their hearts with kisses.”

He added quickly: “Not that I mean this for myself—I was bred at Cambridge, and good Latin words suit me well. But for the people. The key to English hearts is the mother tongue.”

“My son,” said Clement, “blessed be the hour I met you. Your words are sober and wise. But how shall I learn your English tongue? I have no book.”

“I would give you my Book of Hours, father—it’s in English and Latin side by side. But then—what would become of my soul, without my hours in a strange land?”

“Wait,” Clement said, smiling. “You are honest, and I am holy. Let us make a bargain: you pray for me every day for two months, and I’ll give you my Book of Hours. Here it is. What say you?”

His eyes sparkled with mercantile fire.

Clement gently smiled. Then he reached into his girdle and drew out a manuscript—written in Latin and Italian. “See, my son,” he said, “Heaven has provided for both our needs. Let us exchange books. And I will give you my prayers—not sell them. I do not trade in religion.”

The Englishman was delighted. “So I’ll learn Italian without risking my soul! Near is my purse, but nearer is my soul.”

He pressed money into Clement’s hands. The friar refused, saying it went against his vow to carry no more than necessary.

“Lay it out for the Church and my soul,” insisted the Englishman. “I don’t ask you to keep it—take it, you must.”

They clasped hands again. Clement kissed him on the brow, blessed him, and they parted.

About a mile later, Clement found two weary travelers resting under a great chestnut tree beside the road. A crude cart held a printing press—clumsy as a vinepress. A jaded mule was harnessed to it. And there, face to face, stood Clement and his old enemy.

As he looked at the press and the honest, blue-eyed Germans, he recalled the bitterness he once felt toward this machine. Now, he looked down kindly and whispered: “Sweynheim!”

The men jumped to their feet. “Pannartz!”

They fled into the woods.

Clement stood amazed. Then a face peered from behind a tree.

“What fear you?” he asked.

A trembling voice replied: “Say rather—how can a stranger know our names? I’ve never seen you before.”

“Oh, superstition!” Clement said. “I know you by your work—as all good craftsmen are known. Come forward, and I’ll tell you.”

They crept out cautiously, each watching the other.

“My children,” Clement said, “I saw a Lactantius in Rome—printed by Sweynheim and Pannartz, disciples of Fust.”

“Did you hear that, Pannartz? Our work has reached Rome!”

“By your hair and eyes, I knew you were Germans,” Clement said. “And the press speaks for itself. Who else could you be but Fust’s disciples?”

The men were astonished they had suspected magic.

“The good father has his wits about him,” said Pannartz.

“Ay,” said Sweynheim, “and with those wits, he could tell us how to get this tired beast to the next town.”

“And where to find money to feed it and us when we arrive.”

“I will try,” said Clement. “Free the mule from the cart and harness—leave only the halter.”

They obeyed. The animal immediately lay down and rolled on its back in the dust, like a kitten.

“This,” Clement said, “is how God teaches animals to refresh themselves. Horses do not know this art.”

As for money, he told them a worthy Englishman had entrusted him with funds for charity. “To whom better give a stranger’s money than to strangers?” he said. “Take it. Use it to help another traveler in need. May all nations one day learn to love one another.”

Tears filled the men’s eyes. They accepted the gift with heartfelt thanks.

“It is your nation we thank—if we knew it,” one said.

“My nation is the Church.”

Clement prepared to leave, but the men begged him to wait. They had no silver or gold—but they had something else.

They took the press from the cart. While Clement fed the mule, they worked—half in shade, half in sun—setting type and pulling a quarto sheet of eight pages already prepared. They lacked enough type to print two sheets at once.

When the page emerged, Clement was stunned.

“Are these words truly fixed upon the paper?” he asked. “Will they not fly away as swiftly as they came? And you thought me a magician!”

“It is Augustine de civitate Dei,” one said.

“My sons,” Clement breathed, “you carry the wings of knowledge. Oh, never misuse this craft! Print no evil books. They would spread like locusts and devour men’s souls.”

They swore they would rather put their hands under the screw than abuse their art.

And so they parted.

In this world, there is only meeting and parting.

At a town in Tuscany, Clement met the past in a sudden, strange encounter. He joined a motley group of pilgrims—nobles and commoners, piety and vice mingled together. Such gatherings were well painted by others; I need not describe them.

Wearied and dusty, Clement sat in a corner of the inn’s barn, studying the Englishman’s Book of Hours, deciphering it by Dutch and Latin. A servant brought a bucket half-full of water and placed it at his feet. A female servant followed with towels. Then a woman approached, crossed herself, knelt silently, removed her sleeves, and motioned for him to place his feet in the water.

It was a noblewoman doing penance. She wore a narrow mask, yet it was effective. Her touch was gentle—more delicate than those born to such service.

Such acts were not uncommon. Though Clement doubted the efficacy of such contrition, he received her service calmly.

But then she sighed deeply, her head bowed low. Her sorrow seemed to crush her. He pitied her.

“Can I do anything for your soul, my daughter?” he asked softly.

She shook her head, sobbing faintly. “Nothing, holy father. Only to confess to a sinless man how vile I am. It is part of my penance.”

“Speak, my daughter.”

“Father,” she whispered, bending lower, “these hands look white—but they are stained with blood. The blood of the man I loved.”

Clement withdrew his foot.

“Ah!” she cried. “What shall I do? All holy things recoil from me.”

“Culpa mea! Culpa mea!” Clement exclaimed. “My daughter, I was moved by earthly weakness. I shall do penance. Do not judge the Church by its weakest servants. Not her foot, but her bosom is open to you. Take courage. Purge your conscience.”

She trembled, then confessed in a whisper, cringing as if afraid the Church would strike her.

“He was a stranger, base-born, but beautiful as spring, wise beyond his years. I loved him. I could not hide it. Nobles courted me. I never thought a man of humble birth would reject me. I showed him my heart—oh, shame of my sex! He drew back. Yet he admired me—innocently. He loved another, and was constant. I used a woman’s tricks. They failed. I borrowed the wickedness of men—threatened his life, lied to his lover, accused him of faithlessness.”

“Ah!” she gasped. “You shrink. Am I not a monster?”

He wept, pleaded for mercy. Then my good angel helped me—I told him to leave Rome.

“Gerard, Gerard—why didn’t you obey me? I thought he was gone. But two months later, I met him again. Never shall I forget it. I was descending the Tiber in my galley. He came up with a gay company—and at his side, a woman as beautiful as an angel, but bold and bad. She claimed me as her rival. Traitor! Hypocrite! He had exposed me to her, to all the loose tongues in Rome. In terror and rage, I hired a bravo. When he left on his bloody errand, I wavered—too late. The dagger struck. He never returned. He was dead.”

“Alas! Perhaps he was not so much to blame. No one has ever spoken ill of his name. His body is not found. Or I would kiss his wounds—and die upon them. Around his name, silence falls, as if the grave had swallowed it.”

(Clement’s eye caught her movement. He recognized her arm, her hand.)

“And oh—he was so young to die. A poor, thoughtless boy, victim of that wicked woman’s arts. She made him reveal everything. Monster of cruelty—what penance can save me? Oh, holy father, what shall I do?”

Clement’s lips moved in prayer, but he was silent. He could not see clearly.

Then she took his feet, began drying them. She rested his foot on her soft arm, pressing gently—so tenderly she seemed incapable of harming a fly. Yet her lips had just told a terrible truth.

While he prayed for wisdom, a tear fell upon his foot.

It decided him.

“My daughter,” he said, “I too have been a great sinner.”

“You, father?”

“I—quite as great a sinner as you, though not in the same way. The devil sets traps, not only snares. But penitence softened my heart. Gratitude remade it. Seeing you penitent, I hope you can be grateful to Him who has been more merciful to you than you to your fellow.”

She took his feet again, dried them carefully, wrapped one in a towel, placed the other on dry cloth, then bowed her head.

“Listen,” Clement said. “Fix your eyes on this crucifix. I will speak.”

“Yes, father. But let me finish—wet feet are uncomfortable. And you are the holiest man whose feet I have washed. I know it by your voice.”

“Woman, I am not. As for your feet, they may wait. Obey me.”

“Yes, father,” she said humbly, yet with a woman’s subtle defiance.

She bowed over the crucifix. Low, solemn tones fell upon her ear. His words stirred her body—first with sorrow, then with shock, then with awe.

“My daughter,” he began, “the man you murdered—by intent—was Gerard, a Hollander. He loved a creature no man should love but his Redeemer and His Church. Heaven punished him. A letter came to Rome. She was dead.”

“Poor Gerard! Poor Margaret!” she moaned.

Clement faltered—then regained composure.

“His weak nature broke—body and soul. He fell ill with fever. He recovered only to rebel against Heaven. He said: ‘There is no God.’”

“Poor, poor Gerard!”

“Poor Gerard? You foolish, feeble woman! No—wicked, impious Gerard. He plunged into vice, defiled his eternal jewel. Those you saw with him were his companions—but know, rash creature, that the woman you thought his lover was a boy named Andrea, dressed as a woman to mock others. What Andrea said to you, I do not know. But be sure neither he nor any layman knows your folly.”

“This Gerard—rebel against Heaven—was no traitor to you, unworthy.”

She groaned as if in physical pain. The crucifix trembled in her hands.

“Courage,” Clement said. “Comfort is near.”

“From crime he fell into despair. One night, he stood by the Tiber, resolved to kill himself. He saw someone watching him. It was a bravo.”

“Holy saints!”

“He begged the bravo to kill him. Offered all his money—to destroy body and soul. The bravo refused. Then, unsoftened even by that refusal, he threw himself into the river.”

“Ah!”

“And the assassin saved his life. You chose Lodovico, husband of Teresa—whom Gerard had saved at sea, her and her infant child.”

“He lives! He lives! He lives! I am faint.”

Clement took the crucifix from her hands, fearing it would fall. A flood of tears released her.

Then he continued calmly: “Yes, he lives—thanks to you and your wickedness, guided by an almighty and merciful hand. You are his greatest earthly benefactor.”

“Where is he? Where? Where?”

“What is that to you?”

“Only to see him alive. To beg his forgiveness on my knees. I swear I will never presume again—how could I? He knows all. Oh, shame! Father, does he know?”

“All.”

“Then I shall never meet his eye. I would sink into the earth. But I would repair my crime. I would watch his life unseen. He shall rise in the world—where I nearly cast him. The Caesare, my family, are powerful in Rome. I am near their head.”

“My daughter,” Clement said coldly, “the man you call Gerard needs nothing man can do. Saved by a miracle from double death, he has left the world and found refuge in the Church.”

“A priest?”

“A priest. A friar.”

“A friar? Then you are not his confessor? Yet you know all. That gentle voice…”

She raised her head slowly, peered through her mask.

The next moment—she gave a faint shriek and collapsed, her brow resting on his bare feet.
