The air inside Castlewood held the weight of a settled grievance, a static charge that Will Esmond carried like a second coat. He had sworn vengeance upon the news of his brother's engagement to the colonial heiress, fleeing the house with the fervor of a man excommunicating his own blood. Yet, oaths are merely the currency of temporary exits; no one, not even the speaker, believes them to be permanent laws. Within a day or two, the prodigal returned. The fumes of the marriage feast—the smell of roast meat and clinking silver—proved too potent a lure for his stomach to resist. He came back to drink and eat at the Castlewood table, performing his revenge through the act of consumption. He raised a bumper to toast the young bride, whispering perdition beneath his breath, offering smothered maledictions as her father handed her over in the chapel.

He was not alone in his resentment. Mr. Warrington heard the abuse flow from every corner of the estate. The old gentleman's City friends, his legal adviser, the Dissenting clergyman who had welcomed them to England, and even poor Jack Lambert, the orthodox young divine, were bitter. Each claimed to have been enslaved, encouraged, and then jilted by the American girl. The lawyer, who possessed an exhaustive inventory of her properties, estates, and slaves, declared the entire account a fabrication—that New York and Virginia were myths, that Mr. Van den Bosch owned no land, and that the Guinea trade was a fiction invented by a wily old planter. The Dissenting pastor mourned his lost flock, calling the girl a monster disguised as a lamb. Jack Lambert wept over a lock of black hair, bedewing it with tears and speaking in language unfit for a cleric. And Will Esmond, indulging in his customary shrieks and curses even under his sister-in-law's nose, invoked Acheron to testify that no such artful devil existed as Miss Lydia. He labeled her an infernal Cerberus, calling down wrath upon his swindling brother who had filched his prize with fair words.

"Why," Mr. Warrington asked when Will expatiated on these grievances, "if the girl is such a she-devil, you are all the better for losing her. If she intends to poison her husband, how lucky you are not to be the victim! You should thank the gods for robbing you of such a fury; you cannot be better revenged on Castlewood than by allowing him her sole possession."

"All this was very well," Will replied, noting that his brother was still a scoundrel for cheating him out of the fortune he had risked his life to win.

George Warrington struggled to understand how his cousin had risked his existence on such a venture. Will explained with candor how the little Cerbera had orchestrated their meeting, how she had provoked George by claiming he was a coward for sneering at Will, and how Will had doubled down on his taunts until George ran away and nearly died of fright in Braddock's expedition. "Deuce take me," Will said, "I was never more surprised than when you stood so coolly in Tottenham Court Fields, for me and my second offered to wager you would never come!"

Mr. Warrington laughed, thanking Will for this assessment. "Though," he added, "it was lucky the constables arrived, or you would have whipped your sword through my body. Didn't you see how clumsy I was standing before you? You actually turned white and shook with anger!"

"Yes, curse me," Will said, turning red, "that is my way of showing rage. I was confoundedly angry with you, cousin! But now it is my brother I hate, and that little devil of a Countess—a countess! A pretty countess, indeed!" With another rumbling cannonade of oaths, he saluted the reigning member of his family.

"Well, cousin," George said, looking him queerly in the face, "you let me off easily. I dare say I owe my life to you, or at least a whole waistcoat. I admire your forbearance. What a pity such courage is wasted as a mere court usher! You are a loss to His Majesty's army."

"I never know whether you are joking or serious," Will growled.

"Few gentlemen would dare joke with you if they valued their lives or ears!" cried Mr. Warrington, enjoying the sight of his noble kinsman choking back curses, grinding his teeth, and smothering his cowardly anger. "And you should moderate your expressions regarding the dear Countess and my lord your brother. Of you, they speak most tenderly. Her ladyship has told me everything."

"What everything?" Will cried, aghast.

"As much as women ever do tell," Warrington replied. "She admitted she thought you had been a little enamored with her. What woman can help liking a man who has admired her?"

"She hates you, and says you were wild about her!" Will insisted.

"Spretae injuria formae, cousin!" Warrington quoted.

"For me? What's for me?"

"I never cared for her, and hence, perhaps, she does not love me. Don't you remember the case of the wife of the Captain of the Guard?"

"Which Guard?"

"My Lord Potiphar," Warrington said.

"Lord Who? My Lord Falmouth is Captain of the Yeomen of the Guard..." Will began, listing titles until Warrington clarified he meant the Captain of the Guard of the King of Egypt, whose wife had persecuted Joseph for not returning her affection. Will dismissed Egypt as a confounded long way off and concluded that if Lord What-d'ye-call's wife lied, it was simply the nature of her sex, which he supposed was the same everywhere.

The truth, however, was that when Warrington visited Castlewood, the little Countess had shared her version of the differences between Will and herself. Her tale differed from Will's ingenuous narrative, though Warrington suspected neither was wholly authentic. The lady was grieved to think she had been deceived in her brother-in-law. She feared his life at court and town had injured the high principles inherent to the Esmonds. She suggested Will's words were not to be trusted, that a loose life and pecuniary difficulties had made him mercenary, blunting his honor.

"We Esmonds, cousin," the little lady said, tossing her head, "must always possess... leastways, you and me, and my lord, and my cousin Harry have it, I know!" She confessed that she had doubted George's courage, without which a man of ancient family was unworthy to be called a man. She forgave George, stating she could afford to despise the slanders Will had spoken. She noted that Will had paid her court, as others had, and might have prospered better had he known her lord's qualities. But she found Will deficient in courage. "No man who lacks that can ever have the esteem of Lydia, Countess of Castlewood," she declared. She accused Will of sending for constables to prevent the match between George and himself, calling him a "mean skunk."

"I say, noblesse oblige," she continued, snapping her little fingers. "A man of our family who hasn't got courage, I don't care not this pinch of snuff for him." She gestured to the ancestral effigies painted by Lely and Kneller lining the walls of her drawing-room. "Haven't the Esmonds always fought for their country and king? If my eldest son showed the white feather, 'My Lord Esmond!' I would say to him, 'I disown your lordship!'"

Over the apartment, the domain, and the village, the new Countess soon ruled with unlimited sway. Surprisingly quick to learn the ways of command, she invented regulations for herself and made others submit. Bred a Dissenter and unfamiliar with the Established Church service, she initially made blunders regarding the direction of prayer, but by Warrington's second visit, she sailed into chapel on his arm, her footmen bearing her great Prayer-book behind her with demure precision. She seemed to patronize not only the new chaplain but the service and the church itself, as if she had never heard a Ranter in a barn.

She elevated the oldest established families, grave baronets and their wives, to the status of humble squires who rode over to pay homage, knowing their distance and giving her the pass. She acquired an old heraldry book and tutored herself with a learned old maiden lady from Winton, mastering terms like *gules*, *sables*, *bends*, and *saltires* with wonderful volubility. She made little progresses in her gilt coach-and-six or village chair, asserting a quasi-regal right of homage from her tenants. She lectured the parson on divinity, the bailiff on farming, and instructed the astonished housekeeper in preservation. She attempted to teach the great London footmen to jump behind the carriage, though the height proved too great for her to mount. She gave the village gossips instructions on nursing children long before she had one herself. In matters of physic, Madam Esmond in Virginia was not more resolute about her pills and draughts than Miss Lydia, the Earl's new bride. One wondered how such a prodigious genius could be corked down into such a little bottle as her body.
