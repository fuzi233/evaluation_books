The air grew heavy with the promise of rain, a dark and threatening cloak settling over Florence. As twilight deepened, Romola returned from the Hospital of San Matteo, accompanied by Maso and a lantern that cast a wavering circle of light around them. Just as they emerged from the gloom of the streets, her husband, Tito, stepped forth from the Monastery of San Marco. He had barely spent the day in her company, having left shortly after arriving in the Via de' Bardi. Without hesitation, he dismissed Maso, whose measured, hurried steps irritated Tito, and offered to escort Romola home.

To Tito, this attentiveness was merely an official duty, performed only when clearly expected. Between them, however, lay a chasm too wide ever to bridge. Their relationship was not one of friction or argument, but of a terrible, static alienation. They navigated life like ships passing in the night, adhering strictly to a pre-arranged script. If Tito desired supper in the old library, now transformed into a banquet hall, Romola arranged it with perfect efficiency; in return, he supplied the necessities for her charitable works, though he never asked how they were used. Yet, despite this functional harmony, they avoided eye contact. Tito feigned a belief that she preferred solitude, playfully attributing to her a nature that shunned his company, all the while keeping his distance from her chambers.

In the fervor of her early reconciliation, after deciding against flight, Romola had tried to restore a bond through open speech. She sought a moral understanding, a foundation of honesty. To Tito, however, salvation from estrangement required something else: a return to the effusive tenderness she had long ago lost. He desired a wife who loved blindly, forgetting their differences, but for Romola, tenderness was impossible where trust was absent. She could remain submissive, gentle, masking any trace of repulsion, but to fake love was a betrayal of her conscience. She was acutely aware of the cost: her husband was a stranger, alienated by the silence between them.

This distance forced her into a position of vigilance regarding secrets he would never willingly share. Regarding politics, Tito assured her that the Medici cause was hopeless and that he served the popular government with genuine conviction—a stance she now shared. Yet, subtle, odor-like impressions gnawed at her peace of mind concerning his dealings with San Marco. She was torn between the fear of confirming her suspicions and the urgent impulse to watch, lest some hidden harm unfold that she might otherwise have prevented.

As they walked, Tito broke the silence. "The business of the day is not finished," he said, his voice smooth. "I will walk you to the door, my Romola, and then I must fulfill another commission. It will take an hour, perhaps longer, before I can return to rest, as I desperately need it."

He chatted lightly of his visit to Pisa, guiding her toward a quiet loggia where a lamp burned before a painting of the Virgin. Suddenly, the street, previously empty, filled with the murmur of many feet and confused voices.

"We shall not reach home dry if we stay in the open," Tito said quickly, hastening her up the steps of the loggia with a start that betrayed his alertness. "Shelter awaits."

"Surely it is pointless to wait for such a drizzle," Romola remarked, surprised.

"No," Tito replied, his eyes fixed on the flickering lamplight. "I felt it growing heavier. Wait a moment."

His mind, trained by constant caution, had spotted the danger before the crowd fully materialized. Through the glow of the lamp, he saw the leader of the approaching group wore a red feather and a sword-hilt that glittered dangerously. This man was Dolfo Spini, the captain of the *Compagnacci*, the very last person Tito wished to meet alone with Romola. Earlier that day, Tito had met Spini, and now, following a premeditated plan, he was to meet him again.

Tito had delivered letters to Savonarola—forged documents, meticulously crafted. One bore the signature and seal of the Cardinal of Naples, a powerful influence in Rome favoring the friar. The letters claimed the Cardinal, unwilling to enter Florence due to strong reasons, wished to consult Savonarola at San Casciano, ten miles from the city, before riding out the next morning in humble priestly garb to meet him on the road. The trap was laid: Spini and his band were to ambush the friar at a lonely spot five miles from the gates, seize him along with his companion monk, and hand him over to Milanese cavalry waiting near San Casciano to transport him to Roman territory.

Tito calculated the odds. The Friar was sharp, often guarded, and unlikely to fall for a scheme requiring him to travel alone with only one monk. Yet, the Friar's mind was consumed with the hope of convening a General Council and securing papal support against the Pope. The lure of immediate service from the Cardinal, combined with his desperation, might overcome his natural caution in the brief window allowed for decision.

Tito himself had already interviewed Savonarola, declining to show the letters to anyone else. With great skill, he had hinted that they referred to a rendezvous outside the gates, urging the Friar to seek an armed guard from the Signoria and offering his own services to carry the request discreetly. Savonarola had refused, stating that an armed guard destroyed privacy. His eyes flashed with intensity, and Tito was certain the Friar intended to accept the risk.

Tito cared little for the outcome. Whether the Friar fell or rose, Tito would profit. Diamonds and papal patronage awaited if the Friar failed; if he succeeded, Tito's agency would strengthen his standing with both the Friar and Spini, making him invaluable to the Mediceans.

However, Spini was a dangerous partner. Cunning enough to delight in plots, he lacked the self-command necessary for secrecy. A drinker, prone to sudden outbursts of boisterous affection, Spini might betray the conspiracy through a single slip of the tongue or a fraternal embrace in the dark. Tito's greatest hope was that Spini had not yet seen him. But shadows played tricks; Tito, illuminated briefly by the lamp, had been seen by Spini, who, in turn, had caught a glimpse of Tito. Romola, cloaked in black, had remained unseen, standing behind her husband's shoulder in the corner of the loggia.

Hope vanished instantly.

"Hah! My carrier-pigeon!" Spini's harsh voice grated out, though he pretended to speak softly, his hand clamping onto Tito's shoulder. "Why did you run to hide? You didn't know it was comrades coming. Well, I found you; it saves time. How goes the chase tomorrow? Will the bald game rise? Are the falcons ready?"

A surge of rage threatened Tito, burning hot against this bull-faced accomplice, fit for neither leadership nor execution. His lips turned white, his mind racing for a safe exit. If he hushed Spini, it would raise alarms in Romola's mind; she was not easily silenced. If he repelled him angrily, the drunkard might become savage. Tito chose a third path, proving that Romola still held power over him: the power of dread.

He pressed Romola's hand, pretending to offer her a signal, and turned to Spini with a good-humoured smile. "Yes, my Dolfo, you may prepare in security. But take no trumpets with you."

"Don't be afraid," Spini retorted, slightly piqued. "No need to play Ser Saccente with me. I know where the devil keeps his tail as well as you do." He lowered his voice, blundering with a false sense of secrecy. "What! Did he swallow the bait whole? Didn't the prophetic nose smell the hook?"

"The brute won't be satisfied until he has emptied the bag," Tito thought. Aloud, he smiled. "Swallowed it all as easily as you swallow a cup of Trebbiano. Ha! I see torches. There must be a dead body coming. The pestilence has spread, I hear."

"Santiddio! I hate the sight of those biers. Good-night," Spini muttered, hurrying away.

The torches indeed approached, led by a church dignitary, the Vicario dell'Arcivescovo, whom Tito bowed to respectfully. But the mention of death and plague was Tito's shield, designed to dismiss Spini without warning him to leave. The moment Spini departed, Tito turned to Romola.

"Do not let *bestia*'s words alarm you, my Romola," he said quietly, his voice steady. "We will proceed. I think the rain has not worsened."

Romola trembled with indignant resolve. Her trust in his casual demeanor crumbled. "I will not go on," she declared firmly. "I will not move closer to home until I have security that this treachery will not be carried out."

"At least wait until these torches pass," Tito suggested, his self-command unbroken, though a new dislike for a wife who might thwart him arose within him.

The torches passed, followed by the dignitary. Outwardly, everything appeared normal, but inwardly, Romola felt a terrifying division. If stopping this plot required her to throw herself from the loggia with him, she felt she could do it. The discipline of two years seemed nullified; she felt nothing but the abyss separating them.

Darkness returned, obscuring their faces.
