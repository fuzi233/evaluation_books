Neville Landless had been on the road since the stars were still sharp as salt. He walked as if he could grind down distance by the sheer heat of his resentment, and when Cloisterham’s bells began their morning calling—each note flung out into the thin winter air and caught again by the stone, the river, the bare trees—he was already eight miles away, with his stomach hollow and sour from having started on nothing but a crust.

He turned in at a roadside house that called itself, in paint too cheerful for the weather, The Tilted Wagon.

The first thing that met him was sound: the slack clop of hoofs somewhere behind, the wet suction of boots in the yard, and in the bar a landlady’s voice, sharp enough to cut bread, scolding and smacking at a damp baby whose one red sock made a brighter protest than its cries. Above and beneath that, the building itself had its own small misery of noises—dripping, wheezing, the faint spit of green wood in the grate that would not properly catch. A place that served beasts readily enough—water-trough and hay to hand—yet treated a man asking for breakfast as if he had requested a miracle.

Neville sat in a sanded parlour while they laboured, and in that waiting his mind kept sliding back, as if the road were not under his feet but a track laid over some older track, one he had trodden before without knowing it: the same bells, the same fog-smell off river mud, the same clamped-down words in other men’s mouths. Outside, the wind ran over the hilltop and tugged at the signboard so it creaked in a weary rhythm, like a bad fiddle. He watched the grudging fire and wondered—idly, bitterly—how long after he had gone the sneezy heat of damp fagots would begin to warm someone else.

When breakfast came it was a mean, delayed thing: tea thin as apology, toast that smelled more of smoke than wheat, bacon hacked rather than carved, and a cheese laid out like a stranded wheel, sweating beside a mouldy cloth and a green-handled knife that had seen too many hands and not enough washing. Even the bread looked pallid, shedding crumbs as if it were tired of holding itself together. Neville ate because hunger is not proud, and because the day’s work—whatever it was—lay ahead.

He left sooner than they were ready to take his coins and later than he wished, and put the inn behind him with relief, though the stink of stale grease and wet straw clung to his coat as if it meant to travel.

A quarter of a mile on, the road bent towards the house he sought. He slowed where a cart track cut off between two high hedgerows, rutted deep and rising across the slope of a heath. The shortcut promised wind and open ground and a return to the main road further on. He hesitated—then chose it, as he had chosen other hard ways in his life because they were his choice, not someone else’s.

The track was steep, the ruts like trenches. He leaned into the climb, hearing his own breath loud in his ears. Then, behind him, other footfalls: a knot of men moving quicker than he was.

He stepped aside against the bank to let them pass.

Four went by. Not hurriedly, not politely—curiously. And as they passed, another four slackened and loitered behind him with the lazy patience of men who mean to stay near. The rest—half a dozen perhaps—wheeled about and ran back the way they had come.

Neville’s skin tightened. He looked at the four in front. He looked at the four behind. Their eyes met his as if the business were already agreed upon.

He went on.

The four ahead kept glancing back; the four behind closed in. When the narrowness of the hedged track gave way to the heath’s open slope, and still that order held—no matter how he shifted left or right—there was no longer any honest explanation to cling to. The wind on the heath was clean and biting, carrying the dry whisper of last year’s heather and the distant mutter of carts on the high road; and in that open air, the men’s silence sounded louder than speech.

Neville stopped, as a last test.

All of them stopped.

“Why do you attend upon me in this way?” he demanded, turning so he might take them as a body. “Are you a pack of thieves?”

“Don’t answer him,” said a voice from somewhere among them. He could not tell whose. “Better be quiet.”

“Better be quiet?” Neville repeated. His temper, always near the surface, flared as if the wind had fanned it. “Who said so?”

No one owned the words.

“It’s good advice,” he went on, voice rising, “whichever of you skulkers gave it. But I will not submit to be penned in between four men there and four men here. I wish to pass, and I mean to pass, those four in front.”

They stood, watching. He felt their watching as a pressure at his back and in his chest.

“If eight men—or four—or two—set upon one,” he said, the old fierce schooling of his life returning with a familiar taste of iron, “the one has no chance but to set his mark upon some of them. And by the Lord, I’ll do it, if I am interrupted any farther!”

He shouldered his heavy stick and sprang forward to break their arrangement, aiming to slip past the four ahead.

The largest of them moved with quick, practised certainty. He shifted into Neville’s path and closed with him; they went down together on the wiry grass. Neville’s stick came down hard once—wood on bone—before the man’s weight mastered him.

“Let him be!” the man said, low and controlled, as they struggled. “Fair play! His is the build of a girl to mine, and he’s got a weight strapped to his back besides. Let him alone. I’ll manage him.”

They rolled in a close scuffle, breath and cloth and grit, until both faces were smeared with blood. Neville tasted his own. The wind cooled it at once, a sting like salt. The man rose first, taking his knee from Neville’s chest.

“There!” he said. “Now take him arm-in-arm, any two of you!”

Two hands clamped on Neville—each arm locked in a grip that pretended to be companionable and was nothing of the sort. He could have wrenched free perhaps, but he knew the arithmetic: eight, and more beyond.

“As to our being a pack of thieves, Mr. Landless,” the big man said, spitting blood into the grass and wiping more from his cheek with the back of his hand, “you know better than that at midday. We wouldn’t have touched you if you hadn’t forced us. We’re going to take you round to the high road, anyhow, and you’ll find help enough against thieves there, if you want it. Wipe his face, somebody; see how it’s a-trickling down him!”

Someone dabbed at Neville’s face with a rag that smelled of old beer and rough soap. As the blood was cleared from his eyes, recognition struck through his confusion: the big man was Joe, the driver of the Cloisterham omnibus—seen once, only once, on the day Neville had arrived. That made the business stranger, not simpler.

“And what I recommend you for the present,” Joe went on, “is, don’t talk, Mr. Landless. You’ll find a friend waiting for you at the high road—gone ahead by the other way when we split into two parties—and you had much better say nothing till you come up with him. Bring that stick along, somebody else, and let’s be moving!”

Bewilderment pinned Neville’s tongue. Between the two men holding him, he walked like a man in a dream—his boots breaking the frozen crust of puddles, his ears catching snatches of their breathing, the far-off steadying roar of the high road growing louder.

They came out upon it at last, into the midst of a small gathering. The men who had run back were there. And at the centre—like the fixed point about which everything now turned—stood Mr. Jasper and Mr. Crisparkle.

Neville’s conductors brought him up to the Minor Canon and released him, as if the sight of that gentleman made them suddenly mindful of manners.

“What is all this, sir?” Neville cried, the circle closing about him. His heart knocked at his ribs as if trying to escape. “What is the matter? I feel as if I had lost my senses!”

“Where is my nephew?” Mr. Jasper demanded. The question came out of him wild, as if torn loose.

“Where is your nephew?” Neville repeated, stunned. “Why do you ask me?”

“I ask you,” Jasper flung back, “because you were the last person in his company, and he is not to be found.”

“Not to be found!” Neville echoed. The words made the morning tilt. The bells of Cloisterham seemed suddenly farther off, as if the sound itself had been stolen. “What do you mean—not to be found?”

“Stay, stay,” Mr. Crisparkle said quickly, stepping between them with the quiet authority that made men listen even when they disliked it. “Permit me, Jasper. Mr. Neville, you are confounded; collect your thoughts. It is of great importance that you should collect your thoughts. Attend to me.”

“I will try, sir,” Neville said. “But I seem mad.”

“You left Mr. Jasper last night with Edwin Drood?”

“Yes.”

“At what hour?”

Neville pressed a hand to his head, as if he could keep memory from slipping. “Was it at twelve o’clock?” he asked, looking helplessly to Jasper, and hating himself for it.

“Quite right,” said Mr. Crisparkle. “The hour Mr. Jasper has already named to me. You went down to the river together?”

“Undoubtedly. To see the action of the wind there.”

“What followed? How long did you stay there?”

“About ten minutes. Not more. We then walked together to your house, and he took leave of me at the door.”

“Did he say that he was going down to the river again?”

“No. He said that he was going straight back.”

A murmur ran through the bystanders, and the sound of it—like flies in a jar—set Neville’s teeth on edge. Mr. Jasper, who had been staring at him with an intensity that felt like hands, spoke in a low, distinct voice sharpened with suspicion.

“What are those stains upon his dress?”

All eyes swung down to Neville’s clothes. The blood from the scuffle—his and Joe’s—had dried dark in the cold air, marking him as if the morning itself accused him.

“And here are the same stains upon this stick!” Jasper added, taking it from the man who held it. He balanced it a moment as if weighing more than wood. “I know the stick to be his, and he carried it last night. What does this mean?”

“In the name of God, say what it means, Neville!” Mr. Crisparkle urged, pain and urgency together in his voice.

“That man and I had a struggle for the stick just now,” Neville said hoarsely, pointing to Joe. “You may see the same marks on him, sir. What was I to suppose, when I found myself molested by eight people? Could I dream of the true reason when they would give me none at all?”

The men admitted—reluctantly, shifting under the weight of so many eyes—that they had thought it discreet to keep silence, and that the struggle had taken place. Yet even those who had seen it looked darkly at the dried smears, as if blood upon a man’s sleeve were proof enough of any story the town already wanted to believe.

“We must return, Neville,” Mr. Crisparkle said, with a steadiness meant to anchor him. “Of course you will be glad to come back to clear yourself?”

“Of course, sir,” Neville answered. It was the only answer possible, and it tasted like submission.

“Mr. Landless will walk at my side,” the Minor Canon continued, looking round as if daring anyone to dispute it. “Come, Neville!”

They set out for Cloisterham again. The little company straggled after at different distances, one exception only: Jasper kept his place on Neville’s other side and never left it. He said nothing. Mr. Crisparkle asked again what he had asked before; Neville repeated what he had answered before; they hazarded conjectures—river, accident, some mad flight. Jasper’s silence sat among them like an additional person. Mr. Crisparkle’s manner appealed directly to him to speak, to reason, to help; and nothing could move that fixed face.

The nearer they drew to the city, the more Neville heard Cloisterham’s own soundscape rise to meet them: the hollow echo of their steps under arches, the distant hammer of a smith, the murmur of morning voices that dipped when the group passed, the church’s stone-lipped hush swallowing and returning every noise. It was as if the town listened, and listening judged.

Mr. Crisparkle suggested they call upon the Mayor at once. Jasper assented with a stern nod.

Mr. Sapsea received them in his parlour. He was informed—by Mr. Crisparkle, who stated their desire to make a voluntary statement—of what had passed and what was feared. Jasper at last broke his silence, and the careful manner of his words was more alarming than fury would have been.

He declared that he placed his whole reliance, humanly speaking, on Mr. Sapsea’s penetration. There was no conceivable reason why his nephew should have suddenly absconded, unless Mr. Sapsea could suggest one, and then he would defer. There was no intelligible likelihood of Edwin’s having returned to the river and been accidentally drowned in the dark, unless it should appear likely to Mr. Sapsea, and then again he would defer. He washed his hands—so he said—as clean as he could of horrible suspicions, unless it should appear to Mr. Sapsea that such suspicions were inseparable from Edwin’s last companion before his disappearance, a companion not previously on good terms with him; and then, once more, he would defer. His own state of mind, he being distracted and full of apprehension, was not safely to be trusted; but Mr. Sapsea’s was.

Mr. Sapsea, swelling with the importance Jasper poured into him, expressed his opinion that the case had a dark look—in short (and here his eyes settled full upon Neville’s face), an Un-English complexion. Having made that grand point, he wandered into a thicker fog of talk than even a Mayor might reasonably expect to lose himself in, and emerged with the discovery that to take a fellow-creature’s life was to take something that did not belong to you.

He wavered on the brink of issuing his warrant then and there for Neville Landless’s committal to jail under circumstances of grave suspicion; and he might have done it, had not Mr. Crisparkle protested indignantly and taken upon himself the responsibility. Neville would remain in the Minor Canon’s own house, under his own eye, and be produced by his own hand whenever demanded.

Jasper then understood Mr. Sapsea to suggest—though the Mayor said very little of it plainly—that the river should be dragged, its banks rigidly examined, particulars of the disappearance sent to outlying places and to London, and placards and advertisements widely circulated imploring Edwin Drood, if for any unknown reason he had withdrawn himself from his uncle’s home and society, to take pity on that loving kinsman’s distress and somehow send word that he yet lived.

Mr. Sapsea was perfectly understood, for this was exactly his meaning, though it was like him to believe the thought was as good as the speech. Measures were taken towards all these ends immediately.

It would have been hard to say which man was more bowed beneath horror and amazement: Neville Landless, made passive by suspicion, or John Jasper, forced active by his role. Each looked broken. Each carried, in his own way, a silence that other people filled with their guesses.

With the earliest light of the next morning men were at the river; other men—many of them volunteers, eager as if a search were a kind of public performance—spread along the banks. The river had its own voice, always, but now that voice was smothered and interrupted: poles knocking boat-sides, chains clinking, nets dragging with a wet rasp, shouted instructions snatched away by wind. All day the work went on: barge and boat, drag and net; ashore, jack-boots sucking in mud, hatchet biting at tangled growth, spade and rope, dogs nosing and whining at reeds. Even at night the water was specked with lanterns and lurid with fires. Far-off creeks where the tide changed had knots of watchers, listening to the lapping stream and staring into it as if the dark might give up what it had taken. Remote shingly causeways near the sea, lonely points off which there was a race of water, showed unwonted flares and rough-coated figures when the next day dawned again.

But no trace of Edwin Drood revisited the light of the sun.

Again the search went on through that day: now afloat, now among osiers, now tramping low places where stakes and jagged stones waited like traps, and solitary watermarks and strange signals stood up in the grey like spectres. John Jasper worked and toiled among them, his authority making room for him everywhere; and the town noted it—how he set his shoulder to ropes, how he leaned over gunwales, how he stared at mud as if it might speak. The river’s sound was in his clothes, and the smoke of the cressets in his hair.

All to no purpose. Still no trace of Edwin Drood revisited the light of the sun.

That night, after setting watchers again to keep vigilant eyes upon every change of tide, Jasper went home exhausted. He was unkempt and disordered, bedaubed with mud dried stiff upon him, his clothing torn and hanging in rags where briars and stakes had caught him. He had only just dropped into his easy-chair, head lolling with sheer depletion, when Mr. Grewgious stood before him.

“This is strange news,” said Mr. Grewgious.

“Strange and fearful news,” Jasper answered, lifting heavy eyes for an instant and dropping them again, as if even looking cost him pain.

Mr. Grewgious smoothed his head and face with that habitual gesture of his, and stood looking at the fire. The room was thick with the smell of wet earth drying on cloth, of river-mud and smoke, and beneath it the ordinary domestic scent of a hearth that has been kept whether joy is in the house or not. Outside, Cloisterham’s night noises—distant footsteps, a faint creak of signboards, wind shifting in chimneys—seemed to press close, as if the town itself waited to overhear.

“How is your ward?” Jasper asked at last, voice faint, fatigued.

“Poor little thing! You may imagine her condition.”

“Have you seen his sister?” Jasper asked, in the same worn tone.

“Whose?” Mr. Grewgious countered, curtly; and as he asked it, he moved his eyes from the fire to Jasper’s face with a cool slowness that might have been exasperating at any other time.

“The suspected young man’s,” Jasper said.

“Do you suspect him?” Mr. Grewgious asked.

“I don’t know what to think. I cannot make up my mind.”

“Nor I,” said Mr. Grewgious. “But as you spoke of him as the suspected young man, I thought you had made up your mind. I have just left Miss Landless.”

“What is her state?”

“Defiance of all suspicion,” said Mr. Grewgious, “and unbounded faith in her brother.”

“Poor thing!”

“However,” pursued Mr. Grewgious, “it is not of her that I came to speak. It is of my ward. I have a communication to make that will surprise you. At least, it has surprised me.”

Jasper turned in his chair with a groaning sigh, as though the movement pulled at bruises.

“Shall I put it off till to-morrow?” Mr. Grewgious asked. “Mind, I warn you, that I think it will surprise you!”

Something tightened in Jasper. His eyes, dulled by exhaustion, found a sharper focus. He saw Mr. Grewgious smoothing his head again and again looking at the fire, but now with his mouth set as if he had resolved not to be coaxed out of his own deliberate pace.

“What is it?” Jasper demanded, forcing himself upright.

“To be sure,” said Mr. Grewgious, provokingly slow, keeping his gaze on the fire, “I might have known it sooner. She gave me the opening; but I am such an exceedingly Angular man, that it never occurred to me. I took all for granted.”

“What is it?” Jasper insisted, harsher.

Mr. Grewgious warmed the palms of his hands, opening and shutting them before the grate. He kept looking at Jasper sideways without changing his look or his action.

“This young couple,” he said at last, “the lost youth and Miss Rosa, my ward, though so long betrothed, and so long recognising their betrothal, and so near being married—”

Jasper’s face went white in a way that made it seem suddenly not his own. His lips quivered, colourless. His muddy hands gripped the chair’s sides as if it might slide away from under him.

“—This young couple came gradually to the discovery,” Mr. Grewgious continued, evenly, “made on both sides pretty equally, I think, that they would be happier and better, both in their present and their future lives, as affectionate friends—say rather as brother and sister—than as husband and wife.”

The white of Jasper’s face changed to a leaden colour, and on its surface broke dreadful starting drops, like cold metal sweating.

“This young couple formed at length,” Mr. Grewgious went on, “the healthy resolution of interchanging their discoveries openly, sensibly, and tenderly. They met for that purpose. After some innocent and generous talk, they agreed to dissolve their existing, and their intended, relations, for ever and ever.”

Jasper rose—ghastly, open-mouthed—from the easy-chair and lifted his outspread hands to his head as if to keep it from splitting.

“One of this young couple,” Mr. Grewgious proceeded, not altering his tone, “and that one your nephew, fearful that in the tenderness of your affection for him you would be bitterly disappointed by so wide a departure from his projected life, forbore to tell you the secret for a few days, and left it to be disclosed by me, when I should come down to speak to you, and he would be gone. I speak to you, and he is gone.”

Jasper threw back his head, clutched his hair with both hands, and turned from Mr. Grewgious with a writhing motion, as if the words had become physical blows.

“I have now said all I have to say,” Mr. Grewgious added, “except that this young couple parted, firmly, though not without tears and sorrow, on the evening when you last saw them together.”

A terrible shriek tore through the room—an inhuman sound, like a living thing suddenly flung into fire. Then Jasper was no longer standing or sitting. There was only a heap of torn, miry clothes upon the floor.

Mr. Grewgious, still at the fire, opened and shut the palms of his hands as he warmed them, and looked down at the fallen figure without changing his action, as if the steady domestic ritual were the only shape he trusted in a world where words could destroy.
