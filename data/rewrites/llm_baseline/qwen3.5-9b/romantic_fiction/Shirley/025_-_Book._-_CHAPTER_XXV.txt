Not everyone who dares to fight a divine battle comes out victorious. Night after night, the sweat of agony may break forth upon the brow; the suppliant may cry out for mercy with that silent voice the soul uses when appealing to the Invisible. "Spare my beloved," it may plead. "Heal my life. Do not tear from me what long affection has woven into my very nature. God of heaven, bend, hear, be merciful!" And yet, after this struggle and crying, the sun may rise to find him defeated.

On that particular morning, which had once greeted him with the whispers of zephyrs and the songs of skylarks, the first sounds breathed from the beloved lips—whose color and warmth had faded—"Oh! I have had a terrible night. This morning I feel worse. I tried to rise, but I cannot. Unusual dreams have troubled me." Then the watcher approached the patient's pillow, observing a new and strange molding of the familiar features. He felt instantly that the unbearable moment was drawing near, knowing it was God's will that his idol should be broken. He bowed his head, submitting his soul to a sentence he could neither prevent nor bear lightly.

Happy Mrs. Pryor! She was still praying, unaware that the summer sun hung high above the hills, when her child softly woke in her arms. There was no pitiful, unconscious moaning—the sound that so drains our strength that even a vow of firmness is swept away by a rush of uncontrollable tears. Nor was there a period of deafening apathy. The first words spoken were not those of one drifting away from this world, already permitted to wander into realms foreign to the living. Caroline clearly remembered what had transpired.

"Mamma, I have slept *so* well. I only dreamed and woke twice."

Mrs. Pryor rose with a start, hiding the joyful tears summoned in her eyes by that affectionate word "mamma" and the reassuring news that followed. For many days, the mother dared to rejoice only with trembling. That initial recovery seemed like the flicker of a dying lamp; if the flame streamed brightly for a moment, the next it sank dim within the socket. Exhaustion followed closely upon excitement. There was always a touching effort to *appear* better, yet too often the body refused to obey the will; too often the attempt to hold up failed. The efforts to eat, to talk, to look cheerful proved unsuccessful. Many hours passed during which Mrs. Pryor feared the chords of life could never again be strengthened, though the time of their snapping might be postponed.

During this period, the mother and daughter seemed left almost alone in the neighborhood. It was late August; the weather was fine—that is to say, extremely dry and dusty, for an arid wind had blown from the east for a month past. The sky was very cloudless, yet a pale haze, stationary in the atmosphere, seemed to rob the blue of heaven of all depth, the verdure of earth of all freshness, and the light of day of all glow. Almost every family in Briarfield was away on an excursion. Miss Keeldar and her friends were at the seaside, as was Mrs. Yorke's household. Mr. Hall and Louis Moore, between whom a spontaneous intimacy seemed to have arisen—likely the result of shared views and temperament—had gone "north" on a pedestrian trip to the Lakes. Even Hortense, who would gladly have stayed home to aid Mrs. Pryor in nursing Caroline, had been so earnestly entreated by Miss Mann to accompany her once more to Wormwood Wells, hoping to alleviate sufferings greatly worsened by the unhealthy weather, that she felt compelled to comply. Indeed, it was not in her nature to refuse a request that appealed to her goodness of heart and, by acknowledging her need, flattered her sense of self-worth. As for Robert, from Birmingham he had traveled on to London, where he remained.

As long as the breath of Asiatic deserts parched Caroline's lips and fevered her veins, her physical convalescence could not keep pace with her returning mental tranquility. But finally, there came a day when the wind ceased to sob against the eastern gable of the rectory and the oriel window of the church. A small cloud, shaped like a man's hand, rose in the west; gusts from the same direction drove it forward and spread it wide. Wet and stormy weather prevailed for a while. When that passed, the sun broke out cheerfully; heaven regained its azure, and earth its green. The livid tint of cholera had vanished from the face of nature; the hills rose clear around the horizon, freed from that pale malaria haze.

Caroline's youth could now serve her, as could her mother's upbringing. Both, crowned by God's blessing, sent in the pure west wind blowing soft as fresh through the ever-open chamber lattice, rekindled her long-languishing energies. Finally, Mrs. Pryor saw that it was permitted to hope: a genuine, material convalescence had begun. It was not merely Caroline's smile that became brighter or her spirits cheered, but a certain look had passed from her face and eyes—a look dreadful and indescribable, yet easily recalled by those who have watched the bed of a dangerously ill person. Long before the emaciated outlines of her aspect began to fill out or her lost color returned, a more subtle change occurred; everything grew softer and warmer. Instead of a marble mask and glassy eyes, Mrs. Pryor saw lying on the pillow a face pale and wasted enough, perhaps more haggard than the previous appearance, but less awful; for it was a sick, living girl, not a mere white mold or rigid statue.

Now, too, she was not constantly begging to drink. The words, "I am *so* thirsty," ceased to be her complaint. Sometimes, after swallowing a morsel, she would say it had revived her. All descriptions of food were no longer equally distasteful; she could occasionally be induced to indicate a preference. With what trembling pleasure and anxious care did not her nurse prepare what was chosen! How she watched her as she ate! Nourishment brought strength. She could sit up. Then she longed to breathe the fresh air, to revisit her flowers, to see how the fruit had ripened. Her uncle, always generous, had purchased a garden chair for her exclusive use. He carried her down in his own arms and placed her in it himself, while William Farren was there to wheel her around the walks, showing her what he had done among her plants and taking her directions for further work. William and she found plenty to discuss. They shared a dozen topics in common—interesting to them, unimportant to the rest of the world. They took a similar interest in animals, birds, insects, and plants; they held similar doctrines regarding humanity toward the lower creation, and possessed a similar turn for minute observation on points of natural history. The nesting and activities of certain ground-bees, which had burrowed in the turf beneath an old cherry tree, were one subject of interest; the haunts of certain hedge sparrows, and the welfare of certain pearly eggs and young fledglings, another. Had *Chambers's Journal* existed in those days, it would certainly have formed Miss Helstone's and Farren's favorite publication. She would have subscribed for it, lending each number to him with implicit faith, finding great delight in its marvelous anecdotes of animal sagacity.

This is a digression, but it explains why Caroline would accept no other hand to guide her chair than William's, and why his company and conversation sufficed to give interest to her garden strolls. Mrs. Pryor, walking nearby, wondered how her daughter could be so at ease with a "man of the people." *She* found it impossible to speak to him anything but stiffly. She felt as if a great chasm lay between her caste and his, believing that crossing it or meeting him halfway would be to degrade herself. She gently asked Caroline, "Are you not afraid, my dear, to converse with that person so freely? He may presume and become troublesomely garrulous."

"William presume, mamma? You don't know him. He never presumes. He is altogether too proud and sensitive to do so. William has very fine feelings."

And Mrs. Pryor smiled skeptically at the naive notion of that rough-handed, rough-headed fellow in fustian having "fine feelings." Farren, for his part, showed Mrs. Pryor only a very sullen brow. He knew when he was misjudged and was prone to become unmanageable with those who failed to give him his due.

The evening restored Caroline entirely to her mother, and Mrs. Pryor liked the evenings; for then, alone with her daughter, no human shadow came between her and what she loved. During the day, she maintained her stiff demeanor and cool moments, as was her habit. Between her and Mr. Helstone, a very respectful but most rigidly ceremonial interaction was kept up. Anything resembling familiarity would have bred contempt immediately in one or both of them; but by means of strict civility and well-maintained distance, they managed very smoothly. Toward the servants, Mrs. Pryor's bearing was not uncourteous, but shy, freezing, and ungenial. Perhaps it was diffidence rather than pride that made her appear so haughty; but, as was to be expected, Fanny and Eliza failed to distinguish, and she was consequently unpopular with them. She felt the effect of this; it made her dissatisfied with herself for faults she could not avoid, and with everything else dejected, cold, and taciturn.
