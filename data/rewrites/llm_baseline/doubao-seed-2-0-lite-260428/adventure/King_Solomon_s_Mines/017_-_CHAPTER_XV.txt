The dust of the great battle had barely settled when Sir Henry Curtis and Captain Good were half-carried into the dead king Twala’s private hut, where I stumbled in after them. We were all three utterly broken: the day’s unbroken fighting, near-misses, and heavy blood loss had drained every last scrap of strength out of us. I’m wiry enough, built for long treks and harsh exertion, but that night my old lion-maul wound throbbed so bad I could barely sit, and the gash on my skull from the blow that knocked me out that morning sent stabbing pains behind my eyes. We were a miserable trio, no mistake—but the only thought that kept us from despair was the fact we were even alive to hurt, unlike the twelve thousand Kukuana men strewn across the plain outside, strong and well at dawn, cold before dark.

With the help of Foulata, the beautiful young woman we’d saved from Twala’s sacrifice block days earlier, we peeled off our linked steel chain shirts—the armour that had more than once saved our lives that afternoon. As I expected, the skin under the links was crisscrossed with brutal deep bruises: the steel had stopped spears and axe blades from piercing our flesh, but it had done nothing to soften the crushing force of the blows. Sir Henry and Good were covered head to toe in dark contusions, and I was far from unmarked. Foulata brought us poultices of pounded, sweet-scented green native leaves that drew the fire out of the bruises almost instantly, but the deep wounds we’d suffered still worried us far more. Good had a clean spear hole straight through the fleshy part of his well-known white calf, and he’d lost a frightening amount of blood. Sir Henry bore a ragged, gash across his jaw from Twala’s own battle axe.

Luckily, Good was a more than competent amateur surgeon. Once we dug out his tiny medicine chest, he cleaned both wounds thoroughly in the dim, guttering glow of the Kukuana hut’s crude oil lamp, stitched up Sir Henry’s jaw neatly, then did the same for his own leg. He slathered both cuts in antiseptic ointment, and we wrapped them in the last clean scraps of our tattered pocket handkerchiefs. Foulata had meanwhile brewed us thick, hot game broth, rich enough to sustain us even when we were too worn out to chew solid food. We downed it, then collapsed onto the piles of lavish fur karross rugs strewn across the hut floor. By the bitter irony of fate, the man who had beheaded Twala himself, Sir Henry Curtis, fell asleep that night curled up on Twala’s own personal couch, wrapped in the dead king’s own favourite fur blanket.

Sleep was almost impossible, even after that brutal day. The air hummed with the endless wailing of thousands of Kukuana women, mourning the husbands, sons, and brothers who had fallen in the fight. The sound clawed at my chest, and drove home just how much pointless blood had been spilled to put Ignosi on the throne. Past midnight, the wails died down to scattered whimpers, and the only remaining sound was a long, piercing, unearthly keen coming from the hut directly behind ours. I later learned that was Gagool, the ancient witch, keening over the corpse of her dead king Twala.

I drifted into fitful, nightmare-ridden sleep, jerking awake over and over replaying the day’s horrors: the warrior I’d cut down on the mountain ridge, the last immortal stand of the Greys regiment on the little mound surrounded by thousands of Twala’s troops, Twala’s plumed, gory head rolling past my boots, teeth gnashing and eyes still glaring. Dawn found my companions no better rested than I was. Good was burning up with a raging fever, delirious, and to my horror, he began to spit blood—no doubt the result of a hidden internal bruise from the warrior who’d smashed his full force spear against Good’s chainmail for minutes on end the day before. Sir Henry, sore and stiff enough that he could barely move, was otherwise steady, though his jaw wound made eating a chore and laughing completely impossible.

Around eight o’clock, Infadoos paid us a visit. The tough old warrior barely looked worn out after fighting all day and pulling an all-nighter securing the kingdom, though he admitted he hadn’t slept at all. He was overjoyed to see us, but grieved deeply at Good’s condition, and I noticed he treated Sir Henry with an almost religious reverence. Word had already spread across every corner of Kukuanaland that no mere man could have fought as Sir Henry did, or cut through Twala’s bull neck in a single blow of his axe in single combat. That feat became a proverb across the kingdom, and any impossible display of strength would be called “Incubu’s blow” from that day forward. Infadoos told us all of Twala’s remaining regiments had sworn full fealty to Ignosi, and that outlying tribal chiefs were already sending messengers to submit. With Twala dead and his brutal heir Scragga long slain, there was no rival claimant left to contest the throne.

Later that morning, Ignosi himself arrived, the royal Kukuana diadem bound to his brow, a guard of honour trailing behind him, every inch a king. I could barely reconcile the stately, powerful ruler before me with the tall Zulu man who’d turned up begging for work in Durban months earlier. We greeted him formally, and he laughed, saying he owed his crown entirely to the strength of our three right hands. We brought up our long-promised quest for Solomon’s diamond mines, and he told us he’d planned to execute Gagool—the evil genius who’d run the kingdom’s cruel witch-hunts for generations—but she was the only living soul who knew the secret path to the royal burial caves of the Silent Ones, where the mines lay hidden. I convinced him to spare her life long enough to lead us there, and reminded him of his promise to show us the stones.

Good’s fever raged for four more days, and we all thought he was a dead man. If it hadn’t been for Foulata’s tireless, unceasing nursing, I have no doubt he would have perished. The dusky, soft-eyed Kukuana girl shooed me and Sir Henry away when we tried to help, saying our clumsy movements made the delirious Good restless, and she tended him day and night without a break, cooling his fever with a native drink of milk infused with tulip bulb juice, swatting flies away from his burning face. The fifth night of his illness, I crept into the hut to check on him, and for a second I thought he was gone—he lay completely still, no more tossing and raving. But he was sleeping peacefully, and Foulata was seated on the floor beside him, her small dark fingers locked tight in his, too scared to move even an inch for fear she’d wake him. She sat like that for eighteen full straight hours, no food, no rest, until he finally stirred awake. We had to carry her away from his side afterwards: her legs had gone completely stiff, no circulation left after so long sitting motionless.

Good cried when we told him what she’d done for him, and he dragged me along as translator to thank her. When he told her he owed her his life and would never forget her kindness, she blushed darkly under her skin, and smiled softly, saying he’d already saved hers—completely forgetting that Sir Henry and I had helped rescue her from Twala’s sacrifice block too. I sighed, because I knew the way of the world: there’s no stopping a sailor from falling in love at the slightest provocation, no matter what the colour of the girl’s skin.

A few days later, Ignosi held the great royal indaba, the formal council where all the kingdom’s head chiefs crowned him king in a vast, imposing ceremony that included a full military review. The tattered surviving remnants of the Greys regiment were paraded before the whole army, honoured for their legendary stand, and every single man was given a large herd of cattle and promoted to officer rank in a new, expanded Greys corps being formed. Ignosi issued a royal decree that all three of us were to be greeted with the same royal salute given to him, granted full power of life and death across Kukuanaland, and he formally banned all the cruel old witch-hunting rituals Gagool had overseen for decades.

As soon as the ceremony ended, we pressed him again for access to the diamond mines. He confirmed the old legend: deep in the mountain, in a hidden chamber only Gagool knew the way to, there was a trove of the shining stones we sought. Generations earlier, a white man had found the place, filled a hide bag full of diamonds, but Gagool of that era betrayed him, and he was chased back over the mountains, never to return.

Ignosi called for Gagool to be brought in. The shriveled old witch was half-carried by guards, cackling and cursing the whole way, bragging that no man dared kill her—she’d outlived a dozen generations of kings, no one had the power to slay her. But when Ignosi pressed the sharp tip of his spear to her withered throat, she broke immediately, wailing and begging for her life, swearing she would lead us straight to the hidden chamber. She cackled madly on the floor, her snake-like eyes glinting, saying we’d find that old white man’s forgotten hide bag still full of unclaimed stones, that evil always befell anyone greedy enough to steal the treasure of the mountain. Her wild, shrill laughter hung in the thick hut air, a cold warning before we even stepped foot on the path to Solomon’s mines.
