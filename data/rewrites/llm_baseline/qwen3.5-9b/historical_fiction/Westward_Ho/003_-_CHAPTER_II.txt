— An Old Epigram on Drake.

Five years have passed and vanished. It is nine o'clock on a still, bright November morning, yet the bells of Bideford church ring for the daily service two hours later than usual. They do not toll soberly as is customary; rather, they break forth every five minutes into a jocular peal, tumbling head-over-heels in ecstatic joy. The streets of Bideford resemble a garden blooming in every hue, swarming with sailors, burghers, wives, and daughters, all clad in festive attire. Garlands span the thoroughfares, and tapestries adorn every window. The ships anchored in the harbor fly every conceivable flag, unleashing a tumultuous roar of cannonades of all calibers. Every stable is packed with horses, and Sir Richard Grenville's house resembles a bustling tavern, filled with feasting, drinking, unsaddling, and the frantic activity of grooms and servants.

Along the small churchyard, densely packed with women, flows the gentle blood of North Devon—tall, stately men and fair ladies worthy of an era when the English gentry led the people through personal prowess and beauty as much as through intellect and education. Leading the procession is the Countess of Bath, escorted by Sir Richard Grenville with his cap in hand, for her lord, Earl Bourchier, remains in London with the Queen. Following them are the Bassetts from Umberleigh, the Carys from Clovelly, the Fortescues of Wear, Buckland, and various other quarters, alongside the Coles of Slade, Stukelys of Affton, St. Legers of Annery, Coffins of Portledge, and even the Coplestones traveling all the way from Eggesford, thirty miles distant. Last, but certainly not least, comes Sir John Chichester of Raleigh, trailed in single file, in the old patriarchal style, by his eight daughters and three of his five renowned sons—one currently fighting valiantly in Ireland to avenge a murdered brother, destined soon to rule wisely as Lord Deputy and Baron of Belfast.

At the gate, Sir John meets his cousin of Arlington, followed by a retinue of four daughters and nineteen sons. The last has not yet passed the town hall, while the first stands at the Lychgate; both laugh as they yield the way to the elder, albeit shorter, branch of that prolific family tree. Thus, they proceed into the church, seated according to their rank—or as near as possible. This arrangement provokes several sour looks, shoves, and whispers among the high-born matrons until the churchwardens and sidesmen, overwhelmed by such a distinguished assembly, hustle frantically, turning red-faced and frantic. Desperate to avoid setting half the fine ladies of North Devon against one another in deadly feud, they implore the aid of the great Sir Richard himself to identify everyone, distinguish the elder from the younger branches, determine who bears eight quarterings in their coat of arms versus four, and thereby prevent civil strife. The elderly gentlemen are safely tucked away in the corporation pews, while the youth eagerly seek vantage points to gaze upon the ladies.

Finally, silence falls. Eyes turn toward the door, and distant music rises—a braying, screaming, thunderous melody of flutes, hautboys, drums, and trumpets—that reaches the very church doors before ceasing abruptly. The churchwardens rush to the entrance, rods in hand, amidst a general whisper and rustle accompanied by glad tears and blessings from many women and some men. The wonder of the day arrives, and the rector does not begin the morning service, but rather the ancient thanksgiving for a naval victory.

What has driven old Bideford wild with this "goodly joy and pious mirth"? Why are all eyes fixed with greedy admiration upon those four weather-beaten mariners, decorated with knots and ribbons by loving hands? Even more captivated are the gazes drawn to the gigantic figure walking before them: a beardless boy possessing the frame and stature of Hercules, towering like Saul of old, a head and shoulders above the entire congregation, with golden locks cascading over his shoulders. As the five men instinctively ascend to the altar and kneel before the rails, all eyes shift to the pew where Mrs. Leigh of Burrough hides her face between her hands, her hood rustling and shaking with joyful sobs.

These are Devon men, natives of Bideford: Amyas Leigh of Burrough, John Staveley, Michael Heard, Jonas Marshall of Bideford, and Thomas Braund of Clovelly. They are the first of all English mariners to sail the globe with Francis Drake, returning now to give thanks to God.

The tale is long. To understand how this came to pass, we must rewind slightly, almost to the point where we began in the previous chapter.

More than a year after Mr. Oxenham's departure, young Amyas lived quietly, adhering to his promises, aside from occasional bursts of fierce temper common to all vigorous young males, particularly boys of strong character. His formal scholarship advanced no faster than before, yet his home education flourished healthily. He rapidly became an expert archer, horseman, and swordsman in the traditional style of buckler practice. However, fate intervened when his father traveled to the Exeter Assizes on business. As was tragically common in those days, he contracted gaol fever from the prisoners, succumbed in the very courtroom, and died within a week.

Thus, Mrs. Leigh was left to God and her own soul, tasked with raising this young lion-cub for both this life and the next. She had loved her husband fervently and holily. He had often been peevish and melancholy—a disappointed man whose estate had been impoverished by his father's folly and his own youthful ambitions. Those ambitions had led him to Court, causing him to waste his heart and purse chasing a vain shadow. He possessed almost every talent save the ability to utilize them effectively. Though a scholar, courtier, and soldier, he found himself past forty without settled employment or purpose, hindered by a certain shyness, pride, or delicate honor that prevented him from playing a winning game in the very world he coveted. Consequently, he compensated with constant grumbling.

Ultimately, by sheer good fortune, he encountered a fair young Miss Foljambe of Derbyshire, then attending Queen Elizabeth's Court. Both weary of the world's sins, though she had witnessed fewer, they delighted in one another. Although the Queen grumbled at the lady for marrying and the gentleman for worshipping anyone but Her Majesty, they received permission to vanish from the little Babylon of Whitehall and settle peacefully at Burrough. In her, he found a treasure, realizing finally what he had sought.

Mrs. Leigh was, and remained from her youth, one of those noble old English churchwomen—free of superstition and devoid of severity—who stand as the finest features of that heroic age. Yet, a melancholy shadow clung to her. Recollections of her childhood transported her back to terrifying times when being a Protestant was an awful crime. She remembered, twenty-five years prior, the burning of poor blind Joan Waste at Derby and Mistress Joyce Lewis, a lady born like herself. Sometimes, even now, her mother's bitter cries echoed in her dreams, pleading with God to spare her fiery torment or grant her the strength to endure it, as her beloved mother had done before. That mother, of good Yorkshire stock, had served in Queen Catherine's bedchamber and been the bosom friend and disciple of Anne Askew. Mrs. Leigh had sat in Smithfield, her blood curdled with horror, watching the hapless court beauty—crippled by the rack—being carried in a chair to her fiery doom beside her fellow-courtier, Mr. Lascelles. The heavens seemed to speak their wrath and grief in solemn thunder and heavy rain falling upon the crackling pyre.

A profound sadness hung over her entire life, deepening during the reign of Queen Mary. As a notorious Protestant and heretic, she had been forced to hide among the hills and caverns of the Peak, surviving only through the love of her husband's tenants and his bold declaration that, despite being a devout Catholic, he would run through the bodies of any constable, justice, priest, bishop, or cardinal daring to execute the Queen's warrant upon his wife.

She escaped, but that darkness never fully lifted. Its hem fell upon the young girl who had shared her wanderings in the lonely hills. After marriage, she gave herself utterly to God. In surrendering to God, she surrendered to her husband, her children, and the poor of Northam Town. She remained deeply respected by the Grenvilles, Fortescues, Chichesters, and other gentle families who honored her husband's talents and enjoyed his wit. She practiced austerities that often drew kindly rebukes from her husband, yet she did so without superstitious thoughts of appeasing God's wrath or seeking His pleasure through pain. Her spirit had been trained in the freest and loftiest doctrines of Luther's school. That little mystic "German Theology"—to which the Great Reformer credited more than any book save the Bible and St. Augustine—was her counselor and comforter day and night.
