For a heartbeat, I stood paralyzed, watching the horde descend. But their initial fury broke my stance, shoving me backward. My boots crunched on stone that instantly vanished beneath me; I had stumbled straight into the yawning chasm where Issus had perished. For a fleeting second, I teetered on the precipice, the boy Xodar clinging desperately to my chest. Then gravity took its toll, and together we plummeted into the suffocating blackness.

We slammed onto a smooth, glass-like chute. With a magical snap, the aperture above sealed shut, cutting off the light. Gravity accelerated our fall, carrying us unscathed into a cavernous apartment far beneath the arena floor.

Gaining my footing, the first sight that greeted me was the vengeful visage of Issus, staring through the thick iron bars of a grate. She looked less like a goddess and more like a vulture waiting to feast.

"Rash mortal!" she shrieked, her voice echoing like grinding stones. "You shall pay the terrible price for your blasphemy! You will rot away in this secret cell, alone in eternal night, beside the decaying corpse of your accomplice, until madness and hunger drive you to devour the maggots that crawl upon your friend!"

With that, she vanished. The faint glow of the lanterns flickered and died, plunging us into an absolute, Cimmerian void.

"Pleasant old lady," a voice murmured beside me.

"Who speaks?" I asked, squinting into the gloom.

"'Tis I, your companion," the voice continued. "Honored today to fight shoulder-to-shoulder with the greatest warrior clad in metal upon Barsoom."

"I thank the Gods you live," I breathed, touching the gash in his hairline. "I feared that ugly cut on your head."

"It was but a stun," he replied lightly. "A mere scratch."

"Perhaps it were kinder if it had been fatal," I muttered, despair creeping in. "Now we seem doomed to starve and thirst in this tomb."

"Where are we?"

"Beneath the arena," I answered. "We fell down the same shaft that swallowed Issus right before she could kill us."

A low chuckle of relief vibrated through the dark. Reaching out blindly, he grabbed my shoulder and pulled me close, his lips brushing my ear.

"There are secrets buried deeper than Issus herself dreams," he whispered. "Secrets within the secrets."

"What do you mean?"

"A year ago, I labored alongside other slaves remodeling these subterranean galleries. Back then, we discovered something sealed away for eons: an ancient network of corridors and chambers running deep beneath the gardens and the temple. The overseers let a few of us explore. I know every twist and turn of this labyrinth.

"Miles of tunnels honeycomb the earth under the temple, leading down to passages connecting with the lower regions near the water shaft that feeds Omean. If we can slip into the submarine unnoticed, we might reach the sea. There are islands where the blacks never venture. Perhaps we can survive there, biding our time until fate offers an escape."

He spoke in a hushed tone, clearly paranoid enough to fear spies in the dark, so I mirrored his caution.

"Lead back to Shador, my friend," I urged. "Xodar is there. Our pact was to flee together; I cannot abandon him."

"No," the boy insisted. "To desert a friend is worse than death. Better to be recaptured together."

He began to grope the floor, searching for the hidden trapdoor. After a moment, he signaled me with a soft "S-s-t." I crawled toward the sound, finding him kneeling at the edge of a hole in the floor.

"A drop of ten feet," he whispered. "If you hang by your hands, you'll land safely on a bed of soft sand."

Carefully, I lowered myself from the abyss above into the darkness below. It was a void so complete I couldn't see my own hands if I held them inches from my nose. Never in my life had I known such total absence of light as in the pits of Issus.

For a suspended moment, I dangled in mid-air. There is a primal terror in standing over nothingness, feet hovering over empty air, the depth obscured by shadows—a panic that screams *let go*. Though the boy claimed it was only ten feet, I felt the vertigo of a bottomless pit. Then I released my grip. Four feet later, I landed on a cushion of sand.

Xodar followed.

"Hold me up to your shoulders," he instructed, "and I will reset the trap."

Once I lifted him, he took my hand. He guided me slowly, pausing frequently to ensure I hadn't wandered into a dead end. Soon, we began descending a steep, sloping tunnel.

"We will see light soon," he promised. "Down here, we encounter the same layer of phosphorescent rock that glows around Omean."

I never forgot that journey through the heart of Issus. Devoid of action, it was nonetheless charged with a strange, electric thrill. The silence of the forgotten corridors allowed my imagination to run wild, conjuring images of the ancient peoples of this dying world—their labors, their treacheries, their desperate struggles against the swarming hordes of the submerged depths that had pushed them to the highest peaks, where superstition now kept them safe.

Beyond the green savages, Mars once hosted three higher races: the Blacks, the Whites, and the Yellow Men. As the planet's oceans receded, resources dwindled, turning survival into a brutal war. The three superior races easily crushed the aquatic greens, but as their coastal cities became untenable, they were forced into nomadic life, fracturing into smaller tribes. This isolation left them vulnerable to the relentless attacks of the green hordes. The eventual result was a partial fusion of Black, White, and Yellow bloodlines, birthing the magnificent Red Men who rule today.

I had always believed all traces of the original races were extinct, yet in the span of four days, I had encountered vast numbers of Blacks and Whites. A question gnawed at me: Could a fragment of the ancient Yellow Men still hide in some forgotten corner of the planet?

My thoughts shattered when the boy gasped softly.

"At last, the lit path!" he cried.

Ahead, a dim radiance pulsed through the darkness. As we approached, the glow intensified until we burst into a well-lit corridor. We moved swiftly, emerging abruptly at the terminus of the tunnel, which opened directly onto the ledge surrounding the submarine pool.

The vessel lay at her moorings, her hatch wide open. Raising a finger to his lips, then tapping his sword with a sharp tap, the boy crept silently toward the ship. I trailed close behind.

Sliding down to the deserted deck, we crawled on hands and knees toward the hatch. A quick peek below confirmed no guards. Moving with the fluid grace of cats, we slipped inside the main cabin. Still, there was no sign of life. We quickly covered and secured the hatch.

Next, Xodon stepped into the pilot house, pressed a button, and the boat sank, disappearing into the swirling waters toward the bottom of the shaft. Miraculously, no frantic footsteps echoed through the halls. While the boy stayed to steer, I slipped from cabin to cabin, searching in vain for a single soul. The craft was utterly deserted. Such luck bordered on the impossible.

Returning to the pilot house, the boy handed me a sheet of paper.

"This explains why the crew is missing," he said.

It was a wireless transmission to the submarine's commander:

*"The slaves have risen. Bring any men you can gather; aid from Omean is too late. They are massacring everyone in the amphitheater. Issus is threatened. Haste."*

Signed, **ZITHAD**.

"Zithad is the Dator of Issus's guards," the boy explained. "We gave them quite a fright—they won't forget soon."

"Let us hope it marks the beginning of the end for Issus," I mused.

"Our first ancestor alone knows," he replied enigmatically.

We reached the pool in Omean without further trouble. Here, we debated whether to sink the submarine before departing. Deciding it offered no advantage, we concluded it was safer to leave it floating. While plenty of Blacks inhabited Omean to stop us if caught, reinforcements arriving from Issus's temples would likely increase rather than decrease our odds.

Now faced with the dilemma of crossing the patrols guarding the island, I devised a scheme.

"What is the rank or name of the officer commanding these guards?" I asked.

"A fellow named Torith was on duty this morning," the boy replied.

"And the commander of the submarine?"

"Yersted."

I found a dispatch blank in the cabin and penned a forged order:

"That is the simplest way home," I smiled, handing the note to the boy. "Come, let us see if it works."

"But our swords!" he protested. "How will we explain them?"

"Since we cannot explain them, we must leave them behind," I said firmly.

"Is it not sheer folly to place ourselves again, unarmed, in the power of the First Born?"
