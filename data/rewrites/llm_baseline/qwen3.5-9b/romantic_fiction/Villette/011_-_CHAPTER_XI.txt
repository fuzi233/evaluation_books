It was the height of summer, stiflingly hot. Georgette, Madame Beck's youngest daughter, had contracted a fever. Désirée, miraculously cured of her previous ailments, along with Fifine, had been hurried off to the countryside with Bonne-Maman as a precautionary measure against contagion. Real medical assistance was now desperately required. Although Madame had previously ignored the return of Dr. Pillule—who had visited a week prior—she now summoned her English rival to resume his rounds.

A few of the boarders began complaining of headaches and showed signs of sharing Georgette's malaise. "At last," I thought, "Dr. Pillule must be recalled; the prudent Directress would never dare allow such a young man to attend to the pupils." Madame was indeed very prudent, yet she possessed a terrifying capacity for boldness. She actually introduced Dr. John into the school section of the building, establishing him as the attending physician for the proud and handsome Blanche de Melcy and her vain, flirtatious friend, Angélique.

Dr. John, I observed, seemed gratified by this mark of confidence. If discretion alone could justify the move, he would have done so abundantly. However, in a land dominated by convents and confessionals, such a presence could not be tolerated with impunity in a *pensionnat de demoiselles*. The school gossip mill churned, whispers flew from the kitchen, rumors spread through the town, and parents wrote angry letters and paid visits of remonstrance. Had Madame been weak, she would have been ruined instantly; a dozen rival educational institutions were poised to exploit this faux pas. But Madame was not weak. Though a little Jesuit in her methods, I clapped my hands and shouted "Brava!" watching her capable demeanor, her skilled management, and her unshakeable temper.

She met the alarmed parents with a good-humored, easy grace unmatched by anyone else—a certain *rondeur et franchise* (roundness and frankness) of a good woman that achieved results where severe gravity would have failed. "Poor Doctor Jean!" she would chuckle, rubbing her fat, white hands with joyful affection. "My dear young man! The best creature in the world!" She would explain how she employed him for her own children, who screamed at the thought of any other doctor; how, trusting him implicitly for her family, it was natural to extend that trust to others; and how, besides, it was merely a temporary expedient. Blanche and Angélique had migraines; Dr. John had prescribed something; *voilà tout!* The parents' objections dissolved. Blanche and Angélique saved her from further trouble by loudly praising their physician in duet; the other pupils echoed them, unanimously declaring that if they fell ill again, they wanted only Dr. John. Madame laughed, and the parents laughed too.

The inhabitants of La Bassesse Couri must possess a vast organ of philoprogenitiveness; the indulgence of offspring seems to carry them to excessive lengths, with the children's will being the supreme law of the household. Madame now earned credit for acting with maternal partiality; she emerged victorious, and people liked her as a Directress better than ever. To this day, I never fully understood why she risked her reputation for the sake of Dr. John. Of course, everyone knew what they said: the entire house—pupils, teachers, and servants alike—affirmed she was going to marry him. They had decided it; the difference in age posed no obstacle in their eyes. It was simply to be so.

Appearances did not entirely contradict this notion. Madame seemed bent on retaining his services, seemingly oblivious to her former protégé, Pillule. She made a point of receiving his visits personally, appearing unfailingly cheerful, blithe, and benevolent toward him. Furthermore, around this time, she paid marked attention to her attire. The morning disarray, nightcaps, and shawls were discarded. Every morning visit found Dr. John greeted by Madame with neatly arranged auburn braids, a silk dress trimly fitted, and neat laced brodequins instead of slippers—in short, a complete toilette modeled on perfection, fresh as a flower. I scarcely think her intention went further than showing a very handsome man that she was not quite a plain woman; and plain she was not. Lacking conventional beauty of feature or elegance of form, she nonetheless pleased. Devoid of youthful gaiety, she cheered. One never grew tired of seeing her; she was never monotonous, insipid, colorless, or flat. Her unfaded hair, her eye with its temperate blue light, her cheek with its wholesome, fruit-like bloom—these qualities pleased in moderation but with constancy.

Did she harbor visions of adopting Dr. John as a husband, taking him to her well-furnished home, endowing him with her savings—which were rumored to amount to a moderate competency—and ensuring his comfort for the rest of his days? Did Dr. John suspect such dreams? I have seen him emerge from her presence with a mischievous half-smile playing about his lips and a look in his eyes suggesting masculine vanity, elated and tickled. With all his good looks and good nature, he was not perfect; he must have been very imperfect if he roguishly encouraged aims he never intended to succeed. But did he not intend them to succeed? People claimed he had no money and was wholly dependent on his profession. Madame, though perhaps fourteen years his senior, was the sort of woman who never aged, never withered, never broke down. They were certainly on excellent terms. Perhaps *he* was not in love; but how many people in this world truly love, or even marry for love? We waited for the outcome. For what *he* waited, I do not know, nor what he watched for; but the peculiarity of his manner—an expectant, vigilant, absorbed, eager gaze—never faded; it only intensified. He had never been quite within the reach of my understanding, and I felt he ranged farther and farther beyond it.

One morning, little Georgette was more feverish and consequently more peevish. She was crying and refused to be soothed. I suspected a particular draught ordered by the doctor disagreed with her and wondered if it should be continued. I waited impatiently for the physician's arrival to consult him. The doorbell rang; he was admitted. I felt certain of this, for I heard his voice addressing the portress. It was his custom to climb straight to the nursery, taking three steps at a time, arriving like a cheerful surprise. Five minutes passed—then ten—and I saw and heard nothing of him. What could he be doing? Perhaps waiting in the corridor below. Little Georgette continued her plaintive wailing, appealing to me with her familiar cry, "Minnie, Minnie, I'm very poorly!" until my heart ached.

I descended to investigate why he had not arrived. The corridor was empty. Where had he vanished? Was he with Madame in the dining room? Impossible; I had left her only moments ago, dressing in her own chamber. I listened. Three pupils were practicing instruments in three adjacent rooms—the dining room and the greater and lesser drawing rooms, connected to the corridor only by the portress's cabinet, originally intended as a boudoir. Further off, in the oratory, a class of twelve or more was taking a singing lesson, currently joining in a "barcarole" (so they called it), whose words I still recall: *"Fraîche," "Brisée,"* and *"Venise."* Under these circumstances, what could I possibly hear? Much, certainly, if it were relevant. Yes, I heard a giddy treble laugh issuing from the aforementioned small cabinet, right next to the door where I stood—the door standing half-open. A man's voice, soft, deep, and pleading, uttered several words, of which I caught only the adjuration, "For God's sake!"

Then, after a brief pause, Dr. John emerged. His eyes were shining, but not with joy or triumph; his fair English cheeks were flushed high. There was a baffled, tortured, anxious, yet tender expression on his brow. The open door served as a screen for me, but had I been directly in his path, I believe he would have walked past without noticing me. Some mortification, some intense vexation gripped his soul—or rather, writing my impressions now as I received them at the time, I would say some sorrow, some sense of injustice. I did not feel his pride was hurt so much as that his affections had been cruelly wounded. But who was the torturer? What being in that house held him so completely in her power? I believed Madame to be in her chamber; the room he had exited was dedicated solely to the portress's use. And she, Rosine Matou, an unprincipled though pretty little French grisette—airy, fickle, dressy, vain, and mercenary—it was surely not to her hand he owed the ordeal through which he seemed to have passed?

But while I pondered, her voice, clear though somewhat sharp, broke out in a lively French song, trilling through the still-partially-open door. I glanced inside, doubting my senses. There, seated at the table in a smart dress of pink jaconas, trimming a tiny blond cap, was Rosine. Not a living soul remained in the room except herself, aside from some goldfish in a glass globe, a few potted flowers, and a broad beam of July sunlight. This was a puzzle, but I had to go upstairs to inquire about the medicine.
