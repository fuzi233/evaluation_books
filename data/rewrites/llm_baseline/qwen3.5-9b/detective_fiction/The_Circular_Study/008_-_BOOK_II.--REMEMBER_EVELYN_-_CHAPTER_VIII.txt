The following morning, Mr. Gryce received a brief missive from Miss Butterworth, timed almost precisely to coincide with the reply he had sent her earlier. Her letter was succinct: *"You were quite correct. So far as appears, I was the sole individual to lean over Mr. Adams's study table following his tragic demise. I have been compelled to trim the frayed ends of my boa."* His response matched hers in brevity: *"My compliments, madam! Mr. Adams's jaws have been forcibly pried open. A fragment of paper was discovered clamped between his teeth. This document has been retrieved and will be examined at the inquest. However, perhaps a select few may be granted the privilege of perusing it beforehand, notably yourself."*

Of the two correspondences, the latter naturally provoked far greater agitation in its recipient. Miss Butterworth's composure was absolute, a demeanor born of knowledge she could not share with others, thereby fueling intense speculation throughout the household regarding the source of her confidence. Meanwhile, at Police Headquarters, numerous officers were occupied listening to the rambling accounts of a throng of eager informants. While the initial day yielded no leads, the second proved fruitful with a flood of testimony. Countless individuals of varying ages and stations offered observations of people leaving Mr. Adams's residence on the afternoon of his murder. Yet, when pressed for specific descriptions, these witnesses dissolved into vague generalities that were as tedious as they were unhelpful.

One chatty elderly woman claimed to have seen a lady of refined bearing admit an older gentleman in a heavy coat; another, a fashionably attired young woman, rushed in panting to describe a collision with a youth accompanied by a terribly pale young lady sporting a magnificent gray feather in her hat. She admitted uncertainty regarding the young man's identity and could not distinguish the girl's hair color, save for noting her pallor and the striking plume. Similar anecdotes abounded, confirming known facts without advancing the investigation significantly. Night fell, and the formidable mystery remained largely unsolved.

The subsequent day witnessed Mr. Adams's funeral. Devoid of relatives or intimate friends, the rites were conducted by his landlord, with his banker serving as the chief mourner. As the coffin was borne from the house, a contingent of detectives blended into the crowd blocking the street, yet their surveillance yielded nothing, neither in the thoroughfare nor at the hastily arranged cemetery. Confronted with a lack of fresh evidence, the police were forced to rely entirely on existing materials. Recognizing this limitation, Mr. Gryce excused himself from headquarters one evening. Proceeding alone and on foot, he approached the dark, seemingly shuttered house where the tragedy unfolded.

Unlocking the entrance with a key, he ventured inside and illuminated the entire premises. Upon inspecting the study, he noted that the cross had been restored to the wall, the birdcage rehung beneath the ceiling, and everything else returned to its customary order, save for the shattered casings framing the doorway to the study, which remained gaping in disarray. Although the steel plate had been slid back into its designated recess, the glimpse of its blue surface visible through the breach in the antechamber wall served as a stark, unappealing focal point. Demonstrating the instinct and dexterity of a seasoned housekeeper, Mr. Gryce pulled two rugs from the parlor floor and draped them across the openings. Consulting his watch, he noted it was shortly before nine o'clock. Taking his position behind the parlor window curtains, he awaited the anticipated arrival.

Almost immediately, a carriage halted, and a lady descended. Mr. Gryce hastened to the front door to greet her.

"Madam, your punctuality rivals my own," he remarked. "Have you instructed your coachman to depart?"

"Only as far as the corner," she replied, following him down the hall. "He awaits your signal."

"Nothing could be more perfect. Dare you remain alone for a moment while I observe from the window the arrival of our other contacts? Currently, the house contains no one but ourselves."

"If I possessed a susceptibility to fear in matters of this gravity, I would not be here. Furthermore, the house is brightly lit. Observe the crimson illumination casting over the study."

"Because a crimson light burned when Mr. Adams met his end," Gryce explained.

Suddenly, a voice echoed from the study: *"Remember Evelyn!"*

"Oh, you have summoned the bird!" Miss Butterworth exclaimed. "Yet that is not the cry that greeted me previously. Before, it was 'Eva! Lovely Eva!' Do you suppose Eva and Evelyn are one and the same?"

"Madam, amidst the myriad riddles confronting us, let us postpone this inquiry for the present. I anticipate Mr. Adams's valet arriving momentarily."

"Sir, you alleviate an immense burden. I feared the privilege of witnessing the test you intend to conduct would be denied to me."

"Miss Butterworth, you have earned a seat at this experiment. Bartow possesses a key and will enter as of old, free to act as he pleases. Our task is merely observation."

"In this room, sir? I harbor doubts I shall enjoy facing this madman directly."

"You shall not be required to confront him. We seek not to alarm him with vigilant scrutiny. Being irresponsible, he must be permitted to roam undisturbed, allowing his impulses to reveal clues regarding his habits and the peculiar dynamics of this household. I propose positioning you where the likelihood of detection is minimal—in this parlor, madam, which we have good reason to believe was rarely opened during Mr. Adams's life."

"You must extinguish the gas, then; the unfamiliar light might attract his notice."

"I shall not only extinguish the gas but draw the portières tight, creating a small aperture for your eye and another for mine. A common expedient, madam, yet highly serviceable."

As Miss Butterworth found herself drawn into the darkness behind the curtain, sharing confinement with this plausible yet enigmatic elder, a sharp intake of breath betrayed her complex and somewhat bewildered feelings. Lacking a spirited curiosity—she herself had admitted as much—she might have derived amusement from the situation. Instead, driven purely by a sense of duty, she likely blushed behind her screen, mortified by her position in the name of truth and justice, had the sudden creak of the front door not signaled the critical moment's arrival and the introduction of the deaf-and-dumb valet.

A faint "Hush!" from Mr. Gryce confirmed her suspicion. Straining every faculty to listen, she waited for the expected figure in the antechamber. He appeared even sooner than anticipated. Setting his hat upon a table near the doorway, he moved with his characteristic busyness toward the portière he undoubtedly lifted dozens of times daily. Yet, this time, he barely grazed it. Some sight—or absence thereof—halted his entry. Was it the memory of his previous visit, or the rugs hanging in an unusual configuration beside the damaged frames? Neither explanation held water, for he simply turned away with a meek, mechanical expression, reclaimed his hat, exited the antechamber, and ascended the stairs softly.

"I shall follow him," Mr. Gryce whispered. "Fear not, ma'am. This whistle will summon a man from the street instantly."

"I am not afraid. I would feel ashamed—"

But it was futile to complete her protest. Mr. Gryce was already in the hall. He returned swiftly, declaring the experiment likely a failure since the old man had retired to his chamber to prepare for bed. Leading the way into the study, he idly pressed a button on the tabletop, bathing the scene in a new radiance. For Miss Butterworth, this shift in lighting was novel; she observed the effect of the violet glow cast upon the portraits and furnishings when her admiration—and Mr. Gryce's interrupted comment—were silenced by the faint sound of the valet's descending footsteps. Hardly had they retreated to their hiding spots behind the parlor drapes when Bartow burst into the hall, his usual frantic energy unchecked.

Crossing to the study, he paused for an infinitesimal instant on the threshold, seemingly aware of something amiss, before proceeding into the room beyond. Without glancing at the rugs carefully laid over the spot where his master had fallen, he commenced his nightly preparations. Retrieving a bottle of wine from the cupboard, he placed it on the table alongside a meticulously wiped glass. Fetching his master's dressing gown and slippers, he arranged them nearby and entered the bedroom.

By this juncture, the observers had slipped from concealment close enough to witness his actions within the bedroom. He bent over the comb Mr. Gryce had left on the floor. This small object in such a location seemed to surprise him. He picked it up, shook his head, and returned it to the dresser. Turning down the master's bed, he murmured, "Poor fool!" as he and his companion crept back to their vantage point behind the parlor curtains.

"We shall gain nothing from this man," Miss Butterworth concluded abruptly.

Then, silence fell. Both froze. Bartow stood mid-study, eyes fixed on his master's empty chair with an inquiring gaze that spoke volumes. Suddenly, he turned and stared intently at the rug where he had last seen his master lying outstretched and breathless. Awakening to the reality of the event, he erupted into a frenzy, mechanically reproducing the sequence of actions they now understood he had previously witnessed. Afterward, he departed silently, creeping upstairs.

Mr. Gryce and Miss Butterworth emerged into the light, surveying one another with expressions of marked discouragement.
