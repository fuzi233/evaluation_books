When Archer walked down the sandy main street of St. Augustine toward the house designated as Mr. Welland's residence, he stopped beneath a towering magnolia tree. There, bathed in sunlight that seemed to halo her hair, stood May Welland. In that instant, a singular thought struck him: *Why had he waited so long?* Before him lay the truth, the raw texture of reality, and the life that was rightfully his. Yet, he—a man who prided himself on despising arbitrary social constraints—had hesitated to leave his desk, paralyzed by the fear of what others might think of him taking a holiday.

Her first greeting was sharp with anxiety: "Newland—has anything happened?" It crossed his mind that it would have been far more "feminine" if she had immediately deciphered the answer in his gaze. But when he replied, "Yes—I found I had to see you," her happy blush dispelled the chill of her surprise. He saw clearly then how easily he would be forgiven, how swiftly even Mr. Letterblair's mild disapproval could be melted away by the warmth of a tolerant family.

Though early, the main street offered no privacy save for formal nods. Archer longed to escape with May, to pour out his pent-up tenderness and impatience. Breakfast time had not yet arrived, and rather than inviting him inside, she suggested they stroll to an ancient orange grove beyond the town. She had just returned from a row on the river; the sun, netting the small waves with gold, seemed to have caught her within its shimmering meshes. Against the warm brown of her cheeks, her dark hair glittered like spun silver, and her eyes held a lighter, almost pale luminosity born of youth. Walking beside him with her long, swinging stride, her face bore the vacant serenity of a young marble statue. To Archer's frayed nerves, this vision was as soothing as the sight of the azure sky and the lazy river.

They sat on a bench beneath the orange trees. He draped his arm around her and kissed her. It felt like drinking from a cold spring warmed by the sun. However, his embrace may have been more forceful than intended; the blood rushed to her face, and she drew back as if startled. "What is it?" he asked, smiling. She looked at him with genuine surprise and simply answered, "Nothing." A wave of slight embarrassment washed over them, and her hand slipped free of his. It was the only time he had kissed her on the lips outside of their fleeting embrace in the Beaufort conservatory, and he noted how she was shaken, losing her cool, boyish composure.

"Tell me what you do all day," he said, crossing his arms beneath his tilted head and pulling his hat forward to shield his eyes from the glare. Letting her speak of mundane, simple things allowed his own mind to wander freely. He listened to her chronicle of swimming, sailing, and riding, punctuated occasionally by dances at a rustic inn whenever a man-of-war docked. Several pleasant visitors from Philadelphia and Baltimore were picnicking nearby; the Selfridge Merrys had arrived for three weeks following Kate Merry's bout with bronchitis. They planned to install a lawn tennis court on the sands, though only Kate and May possessed racquets, and most guests hadn't even heard of the sport.

All this activity kept May occupied. She had barely had time to glance at the vellum-bound book Archer had sent her the previous week—"Sonnets from the Portuguese"—though she was memorizing "How they brought the Good News from Ghent to Aix," a poem he had once read to her. It amused her to inform him that Kate Merry had never even heard of Robert Browning.

Soon, she exclaimed that they were running late for breakfast, and they hurried back to the dilapidated house. Its porch served no purpose, and its hedges of plumbago and pink geraniums were unpruned. This was where the Wellands had established their winter quarters. Mr. Welland's hypersensitivity regarding domestic comfort led him to reject the sloppiness of the local hotels. At immense expense and despite nearly insurmountable logistical hurdles, Mrs. Welland was forced annually to assemble a household from a mix of disgruntled New York servants and local African laborers.

"The doctors insist my husband feels he is in his own home," she explained to sympathetic visitors from Philadelphia and Baltimore, repeating the sentiment year after year. "Otherwise, he would be so wretched that the climate would offer him no benefit." Inside, Mr. Welland beamed across a breakfast table miraculously stocked with delicate fare, telling Archer, "You see, my dear fellow, we camp—we literally camp. I tell my wife and May I wish to teach them how to rough it."

Mr. and Mrs. Welland were as surprised as their daughter by the young man's sudden arrival. Archer had conveniently claimed to be on the verge of a severe cold, a justification Mr. Welland accepted as wholly sufficient to abandon any professional duties. "One cannot be too careful, especially in spring," the elder Welland remarked, piling his plate with straw-colored griddle cakes drowned in golden syrup. "Had I been as prudent at your age, May would be dancing at the Assemblies now, instead of spending her winters in a wilderness with an old invalid."

"Oh, but I love it here, Papa; you know I do. If only Newland could stay, I should prefer it a thousand times to New York," May interjected.

"Newland must remain until he has fully recovered," Mrs. Welland said indulgently. The young man laughed, feigning concern for his profession. In truth, he had arranged via telegram to extend his illness for a week. This irony illuminated the situation: Mr. Letterblair's leniency stemmed partly from Archer's brilliant resolution of the troublesome Olenski divorce case. The senior partner had informed Mrs. Welland that Archer had rendered an "invaluable service" to the entire family, noting specifically that Old Mrs. Manson Mingott had been delighted.

Later, during a carriage ride with her father—the only conveyance available in the area—Mrs. Welland broached a subject she usually avoided in May's presence. "I fear Ellen's ideas differ greatly from ours. She was barely eighteen when Medora Manson took her back to Europe. Do you recall the scandal when she appeared in black at her debutante ball? It was one of Medora's fads—and truly, this time, it proved almost prophetic! That was at least twelve years ago, and since then, Ellen has never returned to America. No wonder she is so thoroughly Europeanized."

"But European society does not condone divorce," May countered gently. "Countess Olenska believed she was adhering to American ideals by seeking her freedom."

It was the first time Archer had spoken her name since leaving Skuytercliff, and he felt heat rise to his cheeks. Mrs. Welland smiled with pity. "That is typical of the extraordinary fabrications foreigners invent about us. They assume we dine at two o'clock and tolerate divorce! That is precisely why I find it foolish to host them in New York. They accept our hospitality, then return home to repeat these silly tales."

Archer remained silent as she continued, "Yet, we deeply appreciate your success in persuading Ellen to abandon that notion. Her grandmother and Uncle Lovell could achieve nothing; both wrote that her change of heart was entirely due to your influence—in fact, she told her grandmother as much. She holds an unbounded admiration for you. Poor Ellen... she was always a wayward child. I wonder what her fate awaits?"

*What we have all conspired to create,* he thought, wishing he could speak the words. *If you would all rather she become Beaufort's mistress than a respectable woman's wife, you have certainly chosen correctly.* He imagined the sudden collapse of Mrs. Welland's composed facade, the artificial authority built upon a lifetime of mastering trivialities. Faint traces of fresh beauty lingered on her face, similar to May's. He wondered if May's visage was destined to thicken into the same middle-aged image of invincible innocence. *Ah, no,* he realized. He did not desire that kind of innocence—the blindness that seals the mind against imagination and the heart against experience.

"I verily believe," Mrs. Welland resumed, "that if the dreadful affair had surfaced in the newspapers, it would have been my husband's death blow. I know no details; indeed, I requested not to learn them, as I told poor Ellen when she attempted to discuss it. Caring for an invalid requires keeping one's mind bright and cheerful. But Mr. Welland was terribly distressed. He ran a slight fever every morning while we awaited the verdict. It was the horror of realizing his daughter could encounter such realities—but of course, dear Newland, you felt that too. We all knew you were thinking of May."

"I am always thinking of May," the young man replied, rising to truncate the conversation. He had hoped to use this private moment to urge Mrs. Welland to expedite their wedding plans, but he found no arguments capable of moving her. Relief washed over him as he saw Mr. Welland and May approaching the door.

His sole remaining hope lay in appealing directly to May. On the eve of his departure, he walked with her through the ruined gardens of the Spanish Mission. The crumbling architecture lent itself perfectly to allusions of European landscapes. May, wearing a wide-brimmed hat that cast a mysterious shadow over her too-clear eyes, became eager as he spoke of Granada and the Alhambra.
