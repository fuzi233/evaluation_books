The petty altercation between George and his cousin proved to be the catalyst for a significant shift in social dynamics. George largely ceased his visits to Bloomsbury, finding Mr. Will's attentions increasingly assiduous and his behavior so outrageously conciliatory that George struggled merely to restrain his hands from striking his cousin. Now that both parties were legally bound to maintain the peace, Mr. Will's eagerness to avoid further conflict was palpable.

Miss Lydia, however, possessed a peculiar manner of receiving her friends. Six weeks prior, she had been madly jealous of George's apparent preference for another woman; now, she seized every opportunity to compliment Theo. "Miss Theo is such a quiet, gentle creature," Lyddy remarked with evident satisfaction. "I am certain George is simply the perfect husband for her. How fortunate that horrid quarrel was prevented!" She praised the timely intervention of the constables, noting the absurdity of hearing Mr. Esmond curse and swear about his thwarted duel. "But the arrival of the constables saved your valuable life, dear Mr. George," she said with a soft smile. "I am sure Miss Theo ought to bless them forever."

When George hesitated to attend dinner with Mr. Esmond, Lyddy offered kind, albeit tactless, reassurances. "You won't stop and meet Mr. Esmond today? You don't like his company? He can't do you any harm, and I am sure you will do him none." Such speeches, delivered by a young girl to a gentleman with such casual inadvertence in the presence of others, were unlikely to make Mr. Warrington eager for the society of the American lady.

News of George's intended duel with Mr. Esmond did not reach Dean Street immediately, as George wished to spare his hosts the disturbance. However, once the ladies became aware of the plot, a tremendous commotion ensued.

"You were actually going to take a fellow-creature's life, and you came to see us without a word!" Theo exclaimed, horrified. "Oh, George, it was shocking!"

"My dear," George pleaded, "he had insulted me and my brother. Could I allow him to call us both cowards while I sat by and said, 'Thank you'?"

The General sat nearby, looking grave. "You know you consider it a wicked, un-Christian practice, Papa," Theo reminded him, echoing her father's frequent admonitions that gentlemen should possess the courage to refuse duels.

"To refuse? Yes," Mr. Lambert agreed, still appearing glum. "It requires a prodigious strength of mind," added Jack Lambert, looking equally somber. "And if any man were to call me a coward, I suppose I should be apt to forget my orders."

"You see, brother Jack is with me!" cried George. "I must not oppose you, Mr. Warrington," Jack declared.

"Mr. Warrington!" George shouted, flushing crimson. "Would you, a clergyman, urge George to break the Commandments and commit murder, John?" Theo asked, aghast.

"I am the son of a soldier, sister," the young divine replied dryly. "Besides, Mr. Warrington has committed no murder yet. We shall soon be hearing from Canada; the great question of the supremacy of the two races must be settled there ere long!" Turning his back on George, he spoke with finality, leaving the younger man to stare at him in wonder.

Hetty, looking pale at Jack's startling remark, was excused from the room under a pretext arranged by her sister. George rose and followed the retreating girls to the door. Returning moments later, he exclaimed, "Great powers, gentlemen! On my honor, you seem to be granting me the credit of shirking this affair with Mr. Esmond!"

The clergyman and his father exchanged glances. "A man's nearest and dearest are always the first to insult him," George flashed out.

"You mean to say, 'Not guilty'?" the General cried, blessing his son. "I told thee so, Jack." He rubbed his eyes, blushed, and shook George's hand vigorously.

"Not guilty of what, in heaven's name?" Mr. Warrington demanded.

"Nay," the General said, dismissing the topic. "Mr. Jack here brought the story. Let him tell it. I believe 'tis a damned lie, with all my heart." Uttering this profanity, the General strode out of the room.

The Reverend J. Lambert looked remarkably foolish. "And what is this—this damned lie, sir—that someone has been telling about me?" George grinned at the young clergyman.

"To question the courage of any man is always an offense to him," Mr. Lambert stated solemnly. "I rejoice that yours has been belied."

"Who told the falsehood, sir, which you repeated?" Mr. Warrington bellowed. "I insist on the man's name!"

"You forget you are bound over to keep the peace," Jack warned.

"Curse the peace, sir! We can go and fight in Holland. Tell me the man's name, I say!"

"Fair and softly, Mr. Warrington!" the young parson interjected calmly. "My hearing is perfectly good. It was not a man who told me the story which I confessed to imparting to my father."

"What?" George asked, the truth suddenly dawning on him. "Was it that artful, wicked little vixen in Bloomsbury Square?"

"Vixen is not the word to apply to any young lady, George Warrington!" Lambert exclaimed indignantly. "Much less to the charming Miss Lydia. She is artful—the most innocent of Heaven's creatures! She is wicked—that angel! With unfeigned delight that the quarrel should be over, and with devout gratitude that blood of kinship should not be shed, she spoke in terms of the highest praise for your decision to decline this quarrel, expressing deep sympathy for your choice of the painful but necessary method to avert it."

"What method?" George demanded, stamping his foot.

"Why, laying an information, to be sure!" Jack said. Upon hearing this, George erupted in language far too violent to repeat here and highly uncomplimentary toward Miss Lydia.

"Don't utter such words, sir!" the parson cried, now taking his turn to be angry. "Do not insult, in my hearing, the most charming, the most innocent of her sex! If she has been mistaken in her information regarding you, doubting your willingness to commit what, after all, is a crime—for homicide is a crime of the most awful description—you, sir, have no right to blacken that angel's character with foul words. Innocent yourself, you should respect the most innocent, especially as she is the most lovely of women! Oh, George, are you to be my brother?"

"I hope to have that honor," George answered, smiling. He began to grasp the other's drift. "What, then, what—if 'tis too much bliss to be hoped for by sinful man—what if she should one day be your sister? Who could behold her charms without being subjugated? I own that I am a slave. I admit that those Latin sapphics in the September issue of the *Gentleman's Magazine*, beginning *Lydicae quondam cecinit venustae* (with an English translation by my friend Hickson of Corpus), were mine. I have told my mother what has passed between us, and Mrs. Lambert also believes that the most lovely of her sex has deigned to look favorably upon me. I have composed a letter; she has another. She intends to visit Miss Lydia's grandfather today to deliver it, bringing me the answer that will make me either the happiest or the most wretched of men!"

"It was during the unrestrained interchange of family conversation that I chanced to impart to my father the sentiments my dear girl expressed. Perhaps I spoke slightly of your courage, which I do not doubt—by Heaven, I do not doubt it. It may be that she has erred regarding you as well. It may be that the fiend of jealousy has been gnawing at my bosom, and—horrible suspicion!—that I thought my sister's lover found too much favor with her. I would have all my own. Ah, dear George, who knows his faults? I am as one distracted with passion. Confound it, sir! What right have you to laugh at me? I would have you to know that *risu inepto*..."

"What, have you two boys made it up?" the General cried, entering amidst a roar of laughter from George.

"I was giving my opinion to Mr. Warrington upon laughter, and upon his laughter in particular," Jack Lambert said, still fuming.

"George is bound over to keep the peace, Jack! Thou cannot fight him for two years; and between now and then, let us trust you will have made up your quarrel. Here is dinner, boys! We will drink absent friends, an end to the war, and no fighting outside the profession!"

George pleaded an engagement as an excuse to leave early. Jack soon followed suit. When George returned to his lodgings after handling some brief business, he found the young parson already in conference with a servant at Mr. Van den Bosch's door in Bloomsbury Square.

"His master and mistress left town yesterday," the servant reported.

"Poor Jack! And you had the decisive letter in your pocket?" George asked his future brother-in-law.
