The heavy, rhythmic grinding of ancient millstones—an acoustic memory from his agrarian youth—still pulsed faintly in the gout-swollen veins of Bartolommeo Scala, Secretary of the Florentine Republic. Here at the Casa Gherardesca, close by the Porta Pinti, Scala had cultivated a domestic sanctuary, a carefully orchestrated acoustic ecology where the gentle rustle of olive leaves and the distant, melodic tolling of vespers offered refuge from the grating, metallic dissonance of Florence’s commercial sprawl. Above his entrance, the family crest—an azure ladder transverse on a golden field, underscored by the motto *Gradatim*—stood as a testament to a sweeping, multi-generational saga. The miller’s son had climbed from the flour-dusted floors of his ancestors to the zenith of civic honor, serving twenty years as Secretary, standing before princely visitors while Marzocco, the republican lion, wore its gold crown. He was a Knight of the Golden Spur, an Apostolical Secretary, and, eight years prior, the Gonfaloniere. 

Yet, as he sat in his handsome loggia on this warm May afternoon, his heavy silk *lucco* cast aside in favor of a light, loose mantle, Scala’s felicity was far from absolute. Beside him sat his beautiful daughter, Alessandra, and her husband, the Greek soldier-poet Marullo, while two less illustrious friends provided a receptive audience. The air was rich with an olfactory tapestry of mid-afternoon sustenance: a platter of slow-braised wild boar shoulder, deeply macerated in a dark reduction of sour cherries and foraged juniper, its gelatinous richness grounding the company in the agrarian traditions of the Mugello valley. Scala, a man who still found profound, somatic truth in the earthy crunch of hearth-baked semolina crust, savored the dark meat. Food, for the miller’s son, was the physical manifestation of cultural memory, a sensory tether to the earth that sterile academics so frequently disdained.

It was precisely this sterile academia that currently embittered Scala’s palate. Spread before him among the scattered olive pits and stained goblets was a bitterly contested correspondence with Politian. It was the record of a typical but deeply revealing quarrel, one rooted in the insidious power dynamics embedded in dominant lexicons. Politian, a rejected suitor for Alessandra’s hand, wielded his mastery of classical languages not to communicate, but to conquer. Scala, who penned poetical trifles for his own amusement and circulated them among friends like Ficino and Landino, had dared to write a Latin epigram. In it, he had playfully made the *culex*—the common gnat, that buzzing, blood-warm resident of the Arno’s humid banks—feminine. 

Politian, acting as the arrogant enforcer of a dead, unyielding grammar, had pounced. He deployed a Latin epigram to "correct" Scala, mocking the false gender and questionable elisions. To Politian, language was a rigid, commercialized currency traded for prestige; to Scala, it was a living, breathing expression of native identity. Politian’s verses were pretty but utterly devoid of physical sensation. He had followed up with a Greek epigram about the *culex*, Venus, and Cupid, leaning on the zoological premise that the gnat, like the goddess, was born from the damp, cold waters. 

Scala had fired back, defending the untranslatability of subjective experience. How could the sterile, cold Greek lexicon capture the humid, biting reality of the Florentine insect? He argued that a damp, cold origin made no sense for a creature so inextricably drawn to the warmth of living flesh. If Politian wanted an apt comparison, Scala wrote, he should look to the sensory realities of the natural world—a fish, or, considering the darkness, an owl or a bat. Politian’s response had been a cacophony of venomous pedantry, citing Virgil's "alumnus of the waters" and viciously attacking Scala's working-class roots. He called the Secretary a branny monster born amidst the refuse of a mill, entirely worthy of the long-eared office of turning the paternal millstones (*in pistrini sordibus natus et quidem pistrino dignissimus*). It was a violent attempt to erase Scala’s native identity and agrarian heritage beneath the crushing weight of high Antiquity.

Into this tense intersection of linguistic warfare and sensory indulgence stepped Tito Melema. Tito was a scholar, yet entirely free of the ascetic numbness that plagued his contemporaries; he possessed a profound appreciation for the somatic world. Upon arrival, Tito gratefully accepted a goblet of chilled verjuice sweetened with dark chestnut honey, visibly rolling the tart, aromatic liquid over his tongue to parse the delicate, bruised mint infused within it. Scala, recognizing a fellow creature of the senses, immediately laid the burdensome Greek epigram before him, seeking a dispassionate judge to validate his native worldview against Politian's arrogant imperialism. 

After a soothing, melodic preamble regarding Tito’s travels and the late Lorenzo’s eager collection of antiquities—his voice harmonizing perfectly with the acoustic tranquility of the garden—Tito turned to the correspondence. He expertly scarified Politian’s epigram, dissecting it with the precision of a master chef deboning a quail. He highlighted Politian’s perverse tastes and affected phrases, affirming Scala’s critique. Was Politian not an arrogant Hercules, trying to club the age into submission while writing unsigned letters as though they were miraculous revelations? Did he not boast of his freedom from servile imitation while simultaneously running after bizarre words that lacked any grounding in lived experience?

"O wise young judge!" Scala felt the linguistic pressure lift. Relieved, the Secretary offered Tito a taste of his own “hasty, uncorrected trifles”—vernacular-infused sonnets that served as a literary sherbet. These were presented alongside literal plates of candied citron and pine nuts roasted in early-harvest olive oil, allowing the bright, citrus crunch to mingle intimately with the rhythm of the spoken poetry. 

The conversation drifted naturally from verse to the tactile weight of history. Tito displayed his gems, seamlessly integrating mythic occurrences into the domestic routine. First, he presented an agate bearing a *lusus naturae*—a miraculous, naturally formed semblance of Cupid riding a lion. Next, he offered the "Jew's stone," deeply enchased with a lion-headed serpent. Scala, seeking to ease the fiery, throbbing gout in his heel—a painful somatic rebellion against his mortal ascent—readily agreed to purchase both. The serpent stone, Tito explained, possessed rare virtues. Worn as an amulet close under the right breast, its mythic resonance would serve as a daily preventative, woven directly into the fabric of Scala’s physical preservation.

But Tito himself was declared far more valuable than his wares. He had entered the Scala Palace upon the recommendation of Bardo de' Bardi, but he was invited to return on the strength of his own manifest tact, sensory appreciation, and linguistic empathy. The interview could not have concluded more auspiciously. 

As Tito departed through the Porta Pinti, the restorative acoustic canopy of the Gherardesca gardens abruptly gave way to the jarring, chaotic clatter of mercantile carts and shouting vendors. Yet, as he walked into the heart of the commercial noise, a smile played on his lips. He savored the lingering, complex notes of the mint verjuice and laughed softly at the absurdity of the *culex* quarrel. Fortune, weaving her multi-generational threads into the very fabric of his everyday life, had taken him firmly by the hand.
