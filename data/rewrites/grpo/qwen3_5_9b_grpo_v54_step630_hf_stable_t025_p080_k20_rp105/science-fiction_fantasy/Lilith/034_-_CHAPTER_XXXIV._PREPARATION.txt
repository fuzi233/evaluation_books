With victory secured, the woman of Bulika turned her gaze to the prize itself. She spoke of the city's fragility, of the wickedness festering within its walls, and of the cowardice that paralyzed its people. Within days, the chatter of the children shifted entirely to Bulika, though they possessed no true concept of what a city entailed. It was then that I discerned the design of the woman, though the motive remained veiled.

The notion of seizing the place appealed profoundly to Lona—and to me. The children were evolving with startling rapidity; I saw no insurmountable barrier to our enterprise. I knew the terrible Lilith, whether woman or leopardess, possessed only one vulnerable point: her doom lay through her daughter, and the ancient prophecy held sway over the citizens' minds. Surely, whatever risk the venture entailed was justified! If successful—who could doubt our triumph?—the Little Ones, rising from a crowd of children, would swiftly become a youthful people. Their government and influence would be rooted in righteousness. Could they not rule the wicked with a rod of iron, becoming the redemption of the nation?

Yet, I must confess, I harbored views of personal advantage, not devoid of ambition. It seemed just that Lona should reclaim the throne her mother had occupied, and natural that she should make me her consort and minister. I envisioned spending my life in her service; between us, with the Little Ones as our core, what might we not achieve in building a noble state?

I also confess to a foolish dream of establishing a gem trade between the two worlds—a project happily impossible, for it would have served only to harm both realms.

Recalling Adam's plea, I suggested to Lona that finding water might accelerate the growth of the Little Ones. She deemed it prudent to postpone this, fearing unknown consequences, noting that eventually, they would likely face a new necessity regardless.

"They are what they are without it!" she declared. "When we possess the city, we shall search for water!"

Thus, we commenced our preparations, constantly reviewing the merry troops. Lona attended chiefly to the commissariat, while I drilled the little soldiers, teaching them to throw stones and wield various weapons, striving to forge them into warriors. Our greatest challenge was rallying them instantly to the flag at the sound of the call. Most were armed with slings; the older boys carried bows and arrows. The older girls bore aloe-spikes, strong as steel and sharp as needles, mounted on long shafts—formidable indeed. Their sole duty was to guard those too small to fight.

Lona had grown significantly, yet she seemed unaware, remaining as tall as ever. Her hair lengthened, and she had become almost a woman, yet she retained none of the fleeting beauties of childhood. When we reunited after our long separation, she laid down her infant, embraced me silently, her face glowing with joy, while the child whimpered. She sprang to him, holding him instantly to her bosom. To see her with any thoughtless, obstinate, or irritable child was to witness a tender grandmother. I seemed to have known her for ages—from before time began! I hardly remembered my mother, but in my mind's eye, she now resembled Lona; if I imagined a sister or child, invariably she wore Lona's face. My every imagination flew to her; she was my heart's wife! She rarely sought me, yet was almost always within hearing distance. Whatever I did or thought, I referred to her, rejoicing that while working in absolute independence, she felt most at home by my side. She never neglected a single child, and my love only quickened my sense of duty. To love her and to fulfill my duty seemed inseparable. She might suggest tasks for me or ask counsel, but she never seemed to suppose that either of us cared for anything other than what must be done. Her love overflowed upon me—not in caresses, but in a closeness of recognition akin to the devotion of a divine animal.

I never told her anything of her mother.

The forest teemed with birds, their plumage so splendid it seemed to compensate for the lack of flowers, which apparently could not grow without water. Their glorious feathers scattered everywhere, prompting me to fashion a garment for Lona from them. As I gathered and bound them in overlapping rows, she watched with evident appreciation, never asking what I crafted but waiting expectantly for the result. Within a week or two, it was finished: a long, loose mantle to fasten at the throat and waist, with openings for the arms.

I rose and placed it upon her. She rose, removed it, and laid it at my feet—I suspect from a sense of propriety. I placed it again on her shoulders, showing her how to insert her arms. She smiled, glanced at the feathers, stroked them, then removed it and set it aside. When she departed, she carried it, and I saw it no more for several days. Finally, one morning, she approached me wearing it, carrying another garment she had fashioned similarly from the dried leaves of a tough evergreen. These leaves possessed the strength of leather and the appearance of scale armor. I donned mine immediately, and thereafter we wore these garments whenever we rode.

For, on the forest's outskirts, a troop of full-grown horses had appeared one day. Unalarmed by creatures of such different shape, I quickly befriended them, training two of the finest for Lona and myself. Already accustomed to riding a small steed, her delight was immense when she first looked down from the back of a giant beast, which seemed proud of its burden. We exercised them daily until they obeyed instantly and feared nothing; thereafter, we rode them at parade and on the march.

The undertaking sometimes appeared foolhardy, yet the woman of Bulika's confidence—real or simulated—always overcame my hesitation. The princess's magic, she insisted, would prove powerless against the children; regarding any force she might muster, our animal allies alone assured our superiority. She claimed she was ready, with a simple stick, to face any two men of Bulika. She confessed a fear of the leopardess, but I was prepared for her. Still, I shrank from carrying *all* the children with us.

"Would it not be better," I asked, "that you remain in the forest with your baby and the smallest of the Little Ones?"

She replied that she relied heavily on the impression their sight would make on the women, especially the mothers.

"When they see the darlings," she said, "their hearts will be taken by storm! I must be there encouraging them to make a stand! If there remains any hardihood in the place, it will be found among the women!"

"You must not encumber yourself," I urged Lona, "with any of the children; you will be needed everywhere!"

There were two babies besides the woman's, and even on horseback, she almost always held one in her arms.

"I do not remember ever being without a child to care for," she answered, "but when we reach the city, it shall be as you wish!"

Her confidence in one who had failed so unworthily shamed me. Yet neither had I initiated the movement, nor did I have grounds to oppose it. I had no choice but to lend my best aid. For myself, I was ready to live or die with Lona. Her humility and trust humbled me, and I gave myself heartily to her purposes.

Our path lay across a grassy plain, requiring no food for the horses or the two cows accompanying us for the infants; however, the elephants required provisions. Though the grass suited them, it was short, and with their one-fingered trunks, they could not gather enough for a single meal. We therefore set the entire colony to gathering grass and making hay, which the elephants could carry in quantities lasting several days, supplemented by fresh gathering at each halt. For the bears, we stored nuts, and for ourselves, we dried abundant fruit. Having caught and tamed several more large horses, we loaded them and the elephants with these provisions, ready to depart.

Then Lona and I held a general review, and I addressed them. I began by stating that I had learned much about them and knew their origins. "We did not come from anywhere," they cried, interrupting me. "We are here!"

I told them that every one had a mother, like the mother of the last baby; that I believed they had all been brought from Bulika when so small they could not remember; that the wicked princess there feared babies so greatly and sought to destroy them that their mothers had carried them away to safety; and that now we went to Bulika to find their mothers and deliver them from the bad giantess.

"But I must warn you," I continued, "there is danger ahead, for we may have to fight hard to take the city."

"We can fight! We are ready!" cried the boys.

"Yes, you can," I returned, "and I know you will: mothers are worth fighting for! Only mind, you must all stay together."

"Yes, yes; we'll take care of each other," they answered. "Nobody shall touch one of us but his own mother!"

"You must heed every officer's command immediately!"

"We will, we will!--Now we're quite ready! Let us go!"

"One more thing," I added. "When you strike, make it a downright swinging blow; when you shoot an arrow, draw it to the head; when you sling a stone, sling it strong and straight."

"That we will!" they shouted with jubilant, fearless cries.

"Perhaps you will be hurt!"

"We don't mind that!--Do we, boys?"

"Not a bit!"

"Some of you may very possibly be killed!" I said.

"I don't mind being killed!" cried one of the finest smaller boys, riding a beautiful little bull that galloped and leaped like a horse.

"I don't either! I don't either!" echoed from all sides.

Then Lona, queen and mother and sister to them all, spoke from her great horse beside me:

"I would give my life," she said, "to have my mother! She might kill me if she liked! I should just kiss her and die!"

"Come along, boys!" cried a girl. "We're going to our mothers!"

A pang pierced my heart.—But I could not draw back; it would be moral ruin for the Little Ones!
