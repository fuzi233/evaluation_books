Jorian fastened his doublet as he prepared to visit Peter’s tomb, his words tumbling out in a steady stream.

“They called him a magician out Sevenbergen way, you know. And folk say he left them with a parting glimpse of his craft—told them he saw Margaret’s lad returning down the Rhine, clad in fine clothes and flush with coin, but his face scarred by foreign steel, and maybe missing a limb or two. But ah, nothing came of it. Margaret still pines for her boy, and Peter lies quiet as the rest beneath his slab. You’ll see for yourself.”

He braced his hands on the edge of the grave to hoist himself out, but the friar clutched his shoulder, fingers trembling. His voice was a hushed, strained thing.

“How long has Peter Brandt been dead?”

“Two months or so. Why?”

“And his daughter buried him, you say?”

“Nay, *I* buried him. She paid for the stone and the rites.”

“Then—he had but one daughter? Margaret?”

“Aye, none other that he owned to.”

The friar’s breath hitched. “Then you believe Margaret lives?”

“Believe? I’d be dead if she didn’t. Riddle me that.”

“God help me, how should I? You love her!”

Jorian snorted. “Not beyond sense. I’m a wedded man with four stout lads of my own. No, the truth is she saved my life scarce six weeks past. Dead women don’t pull living men from the grave. I was rotting away—couldn’t keep a bite down, nor the leeches mend me. My Joan said, ‘’Tis time to buy thy shroud.’ ‘Aye,’ said I, ‘but try borrowing one first.’ Then in comes Margaret—this same lass you’d have dead three years—flings open the windows, shifts my bed, and cures me quick as blinking. With what? A sorry weed from my own garden—sweet feverfew! A weed, mind you, till it mended me. Now I bow to the blasted thing whenever I pass. But—hold, father! You’ve gone pale as milk, now red, now white again. What in Heaven’s name ails you?”

“The shock—the joy—the terror—” Clement gasped. “What is she to you? Are you kin to Margaret Brandt?”

“Nay. But I knew one who loved her near to death. Her passing near broke him, body and soul. And yet you say she lives. And I believe you.”

Jorian fell silent, then spoke gravely. “Father, you’ve asked me much, and I’ve answered true. Now, for Our Lady’s sake, answer me two things: Did you truly know one who loved this lass? And where?”

Clement nearly spoke his name—then bit back the word, remembering Jerome’s warning. “In Italy,” he said.

Jorian’s eyes narrowed. “If you knew him, name him.”

“Gerard Eliassoen.”

Jorian stiffened. “Strange. Wait—who told you Margaret was dead?”

“I was with Gerard when a letter came from Margaret Van Eyck. It said she he loved was dead and buried.” Clement swayed. “Let me sit—my strength fails. Foul play, Jorian. Foul play.”

“Father,” said Jorian, helping him down, “I thank Heaven you came to me. Sit—you’re white as a shroud. Fasting robs a man’s strength. My gut twists with this. I think I see the knot of it now. And if I’m right, there are two knaves in this town whose skulls I’d crack like clods.” He snatched up his spade and drove it furiously into the earth.

“Foul play? Aye, and if you know where Gerard is now, waste no time—warn him of the trap. I’m no quick wit, but even a slow hound picks the scent. I think you and I hold two ends of the same vile rope.”

Then, after the meandering prelude common to his kind, Jorian laid it bare. He’d been hired by Ghysbrecht, burgomaster of Tergou, to mend an upper floor. Nearing the end, he’d returned for his tools and heard raised voices below. Curiosity pinned him flat. The burgomaster spoke of a letter Cornelis and Sybrandt meant to swap for one Hans Memling carried to Gerard.

“Seemed they’d the will but not the spine,” Jorian said. “So the old fox lured them—showed a drawer full of silver. Do the deed, he said, and each could scoop all the coin one hand could hold. Well, father, I thought little of it then—save the bargain itself, which kept me awake half the night. But next dawn, who should I spy but Cornelis and Sybrandt slinking out, each with a black eye. ‘Ah,’ thought I, ‘Hans left his mark on ye. Hope that’s all you got for your pains.’ Then off they crept to Ghysbrecht’s. I followed, hid, and watched.”

Clement’s nostrils flared, his fingers twitching.

“The burgomaster had a letter in hand—I’m no scholar, but my eyes are sharp. The drawer opened. Those two rats plunged in their claws, hauling out fistfuls of silver—greedy as crows! Sybrandt smeared his hand with glue, stole extra coins stuck to his knuckles—the fool! So, you see, the Devil got his due. They sold their souls for a handful of silver. There, father—it’s off my chest at last. I’ve longed to tell someone, but not the women. Margaret’d have lost every friend had I spoken. Those two vipers are the darlings—always so. See their fine new shop in Hoog Straet? The sign’s a gilded sheep and lamb. Wolves at the teat were fitter. And today the whole clan feasts there. Oh, it’s a fair world.”

Clement sat like stone.

“Not a word?” Jorian growled. “Not even a curse for those heartless whoresons? The lad was alone in a strange land—wasn’t that grief enough? Must his own blood steal the one comfort left him? And think of her—sitting at her window year after year, watching for Gerard! No woman ever loved truer, my Joan says. Not a day passes but she weeps. And but for those lying thieves, that sweet lad—whose life *I* saved—might be with her now, the happiest man in Holland. And she, the happiest lass, not the saddest! By the blood of Christ, I thirst to see their—”

Clement erupted.

He shot to his feet, face contorted, a silent storm of fury. Then he was gone—charging down the street like a madman, fists clenched high.

Jorian gaped after him, his own rage dwarfed by the friar’s tempest.

Then—a small hand clutched his arm.

Margaret Brandt stood there, pale as mist. She had waited by Peter’s tomb, but the friar never came. Searching the church, she’d seen his shadow loom monstrously, then vanish. Now she and Jorian stared at each other, then at the distant, frenzied figure.

“What did he say to you?” she whispered.

Jorian swallowed. “He asked where you were buried.”

Her fingers dug into his sleeve. “And?”

“I told him nowhere—you live. Then he spoke of knowing Gerard in Italy—of a letter saying you were dead. It broke Gerard’s heart. The letter was from Margaret Van Eyck—or so he thought. But I told him the truth: Ghysbrecht and those two snakes swapped the real letter for a lie. And now—ah, lass, I fear I’ve loosed a storm.”

Her knees buckled. “He *lives*.” The words were a prayer. Then terror seized her. “Jorian—you don’t know him as I do. That blow he gave Ghysbrecht—the sound of it! Quick, we must stop him!”

“What? That friar—Gerard?”

“Yes! He’s come back to me at last. Oh, hurry! Before blood is spilled!”

They ran, her legs trembling, his jerkin half-fastened. As they neared Hoog Straet, a roar echoed from within. The friar burst out, white-faced, fists shaking at the sky.

Margaret screamed, then sagged against Jorian. He bellowed after the friar, but the man was deaf with rage, already vanishing down the street.

“Help me inside,” Margaret whispered, suddenly calm. “Let me face what’s done.”

The house was silent—unnaturally so. The door stood ajar. Jorian pushed it open.

What they saw inside would change everything.
