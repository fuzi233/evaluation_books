Our whole ragtag cast is a full month older than we last left them, and by some wacky unwritten small-town story law, nearly every single one of them has piled back to the dinky little shire town where we first made their acquaintance.

Frederic Lightfoot, formerly head waiter and majordomo to Sir Francis Clavering, baronet of Clavering Park, has taken out a full-page ad in the local paper to inform the nobility, gentry, and random drunk farmers of ———shire that he’s taken over the well-worn, famously cozy Clavering Arms hotel. He promises he’s spruced the whole place up to peak comfort, there’s perfect stabling for the Dumplingbeare hounds’ riders, a brand new billiard room, and cellars stocked with the fanciest wines and spirits money could buy, no expense spared. Commercial travelers are promised fair prices and no silly hidden surcharges, per his manifesto that swears he’s fully aligned with the “economical spirit of the times.”

And honestly? The old pub does look shockingly lively these days. The Clavering family crest over the gateway’s been repainted so bright it practically glows, the coffee room windows are all shiny and new draped in Christmas holly, and the local magistrates have been loitering in the old assembly room for petty sessions. The farmers’ weekly dinner is bigger than ever, everyone losing their minds over Mrs. Lightfoot’s cooking — her curries and mulligatawny soup are so famous that Major Stokes, Captain Glanders, and half the local gentry have been coming back for seconds (and thirds) at private dinners and the big Clavering Institute gala that opened the town’s new reading room. The whole shebang was chaired by Sir Francis Clavering himself, with the respected Dr. Portman at his side, while the vice chair spot was filled ably by Mr. Barker, the ribbon factory magnate who’s also running the new Clavering and Chatteris Great Western Railway branch that’s set to open next year, construction crews clattering away on the tracks as we speak.

The local paper — the *Chatteris Champion, Clavering Agriculturist, and Baymouth Fisherman*, famed for its unshakable principles, fierce loyalty to British oak trees, and very reasonable ad rates — dropped a bombshell scoop no one saw coming: “Our talented townsman Arthur Pendennis has dropped his plans to run for local parliament, on account of a very interesting upcoming personal milestone. Rumour also whispers that if Sir Francis Clavering’s ailing health forces him to step down from his parliamentary seat, he’ll hand the spot over to a young aristocrat of colossal fortune, soon to wed a lovely accomplished lady very closely related to the Clavering Park household. Lady Clavering and Miss Amory have arrived at the Park for Christmas, and a swarm of top-shelf nobility are set to visit for very special New Year’s festivities.”

If you’re a half-awake reader, you’ve already pieced together what went down in our little narrative break. Lady Rockminster grumbled a little when she heard Laura Bell had picked Arthur Pendennis over her absurdly rich “Bluebeard” candidate, but anyone who knows the old lady’s soft spots knew she was secretly thrilled Laura was about to do the correct young lady thing and marry. She told her maid Beck the news that very night, and Beck — who’d already been getting full daily updates from Martha at Fairoaks for weeks — faked the most convincing shocked delighted gasp you ever heard. The two did the math on Pen and Laura’s combined incomes: the railway was paying out a fat sum for a chunk of Pen’s family land, Laura had her own tidy little fortune, they’d be perfectly comfortable. Then Lady Rockminster hatched a plan: she’d nudge her nephew, who ran the Tape and Sealing Wax Office, to give Pen a nice cushy government sinecure, and she’d pop over to visit Major Pendennis the next day to make sure he got on board with the whole match, and agreed to leave his fortune to Pen and Laura’s kids. “That stubborn old bachelor won’t dare say no to me,” she muttered to herself over her bedtime tea.

At one o’clock sharp, the countess turned up at the Major’s rooms, and you can bet he bowed so low he nearly toppled over to receive such a distinguished guest. He wasn’t fully surprised by the news she brought — Pen had woken up that very day, remembered his uncle existed for the first time in weeks, and dashed off a quick note to fill him in:
> Dear Uncle,
> Any silly little fight we had is totally over now. I popped over to Tunbridge Wells yesterday and found the prize we were both arguing over had already been snatched up. Miss Amory dumped me without a single pang for Harry Foker, and his whopping 15 thousand a year. I walked in on them mid-snog, for crying out loud, and left him to it.
> Also, Tatham wrote to say the railway company bought three of my fields at Fairoaks for an absurdly high price. I’ll tell you all the rest when we meet.
> Your affectionate A.P.

“I think I already have an inkling of what you came to tell me, your ladyship,” the Major said, all courtly smiles. “How kind of you to go out of your way to bring me the news! It was a silly little arrangement with Miss Amory, all things considered — I wanted it once, but now I can’t say I’m sorry it fell through.”
“Good,” Lady Rockminster said, very matter-of-fact. “Now we need to get him a proper wife. That Laura Bell girl, of course.”
It took the Major all of two seconds to catch on, and the negotiation was over before he even had time to put up a token fight. Who was he to argue when a countess swore Laura Bell was a paragon, too good for the likes of Pen by a thousand miles, fit to marry a king? The second she finished that speech he decided he’d been a lifelong admirer of Laura Bell too, no notes. Ten minutes later, Pen and Laura turned up at his door, Laura blushing so bright she looked like a Christmas berry hanging off Pen’s arm, and the old Major shook their hands with so much genuine emotion he nearly spilled his sherry all over his waistcoat.

Back at Clavering Park, Lady Clavering was practically bouncing off the walls with glee. Blanche was no longer mooning over Pen and moping about her prospects, she was engaged to Harry Foker, who was so smitten he was begging to cut the engagement as short as possible. The whole household was in a perfect good mood: Sir Francis got back in his wife’s good graces, came back to live at the Park, and was suddenly flush enough to hang out at the billiard rooms and gambling halls like he was 20 again. When Pen and the Major turned up for dinner at their London house one evening, who should greet them with a perfectly straight face, offering them their choice of sweet or dry champagne, but the very same James Morgan we all know and side-eye? Chevalier Strong was there too, regaling the table with wild stories about his recent travels abroad.

He pulled Pen aside as soon as he could. “Lady Clavering invited me, but that Morgan bloke looked like he was about to turn black with rage when I walked in. He’s up to no good, mark my words. I’ll slip out first, wait for you and the Major by Hyde Park Gate.”
When the Major was leaving, Morgan helped him on with his coat, muttering something about taking a temporary job with the Clavering family.
“I’ve got a very interesting document of yours in my files, Mr. Morgan,” the Major said dryly.
“Feel free to show it to Sir Francis, I don’t care,” Morgan said, eyes down, voice cold. “I owe you for all your ‘kindness’, Major, and I’ll pay you back for it one day.”
Pen caught that line, and the look of pure hatred on Morgan’s face, and immediately yelled that he’d forgotten his handkerchief, sprinting back up the stairs to the drawing room. Foker was still lingering by Blanche, totally smitten, so Pen shot Blanche a very pointed look that she immediately understood. She ran after him when he turned to leave, shutting the drawing room door behind her.
“Have you told Harry?” Pen hissed. “Morgan knows all of her father’s old secrets.”
“I know,” she said, her usual bright smile faltering.
“Have you told Harry?”
“No, no,” she said, panicking. “You won’t tell him, will you?”
“Morgan will, if you don’t get there first,” Pen said.
“No he won’t,” Blanche said, pale all of a sudden. “I made a deal with him. Just wait until after the wedding — just wait a few weeks, I’m so wretched.”
Pen begged her over and over to tell Foker that very night, that he’d forgive absolutely anything, but Blanche just pressed a little note into his hand for Laura, told him to tell her to meet her at Madame Crinoline’s dress shop at 3pm the next day so they could drive in the park, then sashayed back into the drawing room singing, playing a bright brilliant trill on her piano just as Morgan came padding up the carpeted stairs.

Pen told his uncle everything when he got back outside.
“Begad, what’s to be done?” the Major said, shuddering. “Nothing, that’s what. We’re out of this mess, thank the good lord, leave it to the people it concerns. Don’t you dare meddle, Arthur — that girl plays her own cards, and you’d be a fool to get tangled up in it again.”
They found Strong lurking a few doors away, smoking a cigar. He laughed when he heard the whole story. “I’m an old soldier, I know when to keep my mouth shut. That Morgan villain’s been snooping around our rooms for two months, pumping old Costigan for information, getting him drunk to fish out where Altamont’s hiding. He bribed the inn porter to tell him when we got back, then waltzed his way into the Claverings’ service using all that blackmail material. He’s going to make a fortune off it, the rat.”
“Where’s Altamont now?” Pen asked.
“Boulogne,” Strong said. “I left him there, warned him not to come back. We had a massive fight, I thrashed him within an inch of his life at Baden after he tried to run off and marry that fake baroness widow, the *Barone de la Cruche-Cassee* — translates to the Cracked Jug, for crying out loud. He got so insolent I had no choice but to hit him. He never even challenged me to a duel, apologized the next day, said I was right to wallop him. I pawned every single thing I owned to pay off his debts, then went to the roulette tables with my last ten florins. I won big, thank god — and that’s it, no more gambling for me. I’m going into the wine business now! My wife’s relations live in Cadiz, I’m importing sherry and acorn-fed Toboso hams. Here’s my card, Strong and Co., sole agents for the Duke of Garbanzos’ famous Diamond Manzanilla. Come over and taste them, I guarantee you’ll love it. I’m going to make a fortune this time, no mistakes.”

By the time we circle back to Clavering, the Foker and Blanche wedding is only days away. The brand new Foker family carriage is parked in the Clavering Arms coach house, the coachman handing out free pints to every local who stops by to gawk at it. The dressmaker Madame Fribsby is sewing matching gowns for all the local tenant daughters who are going to act as a little chorus of bridesmaids, and the whole town is talking about the massive feasts, the ox roasting, the bell peals, and the grand party planned at Clavering Park for the big day.
Harry Foker himself is holding court at the Clavering Arms bar, handing out free drinks to everyone who wanders in, deliriously happy. “The whole tenantry’s going to turn up, we’ll slaughter the ox, drain every tankard, my father in law will cry and give us his blessing on the porch, it’s going to be perfect! Huxter, my man, you and your new wife are invited, all drinks on me — Lightfoot, give him everything your cellar has to offer!”
The only problem? Young Mr. Lightfoot, the new innkeeper, has full run of the fancy new cellar, and no self control. He’s been tippling from dawn to dusk, reeling around the yard sloshed, and his poor wife is already miserable, watching him waste their expensive new stock on drunk local farmers.
Morgan keeps popping over from the Park to hang around the bar, sneering at poor tipsy Lightfoot like he’s enjoying every second of the mess. Foker, for all his good humor, can’t shake the creeps the butler gives him. “I don’t trust that bloke for a second,” he confides to Mrs. Lightfoot. “He looks like he’s measuring me for a coffin every time he glances over. Blanche says I have to hire him as my personal head servant, but I don’t know — there’s something off about the guy. No I do not like thee, Dr. Fell, and I can’t tell you why!”
And under all the bright tinsel, the holly, the wedding plans, and the free flowing booze, every single one of them has a little bit of secret trouble hiding under the surface — even the gleefully happy Harry Foker, who has no idea what chaos Morgan is about to unleash right before his very perfect wedding day.
