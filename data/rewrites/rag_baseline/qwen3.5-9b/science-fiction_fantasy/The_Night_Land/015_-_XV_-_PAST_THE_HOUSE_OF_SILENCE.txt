The raft drifted with a deceptive ease, balanced perfectly by the rhythm of our strokes. I held the rear paddle; she held the front. We moved with a steady, synchronized push, covering ground faster than a hurried walk across the solid earth. By the twelfth hour, we stopped to eat and drink, resuming our labor with a motion so fluid it felt less like rowing and more like rocking gently on our heels. Hours bled into one another, marked only by the soft cadence of our conversation and her frequent glances backward, lips parted in a silent invitation that she always shook her head to deny. She refused to abandon her post, unwilling to trade the rhythm of the water for the uncertainty of walking alone.

At the eighteenth hour, we drew our blades inward. She spread her cloak beneath us, creating a makeshift bed, and we ate, drank, and fell asleep instantly, sinking into a deep, unbroken rest. Eight hours later, we awakened simultaneously, disoriented for a fleeting moment before realizing we were untouched by dreams or danger. A shared laugh erupted between us—a pure joy in simply existing awake together. We washed briefly in the sea water, refreshed, and resumed our journey along the coast, moving peacefully and contentedly for the remainder of the day.

This voyage lasted four full days, measured in twenty-four-hour cycles. We made no haste, preserving our strength. Nothing occurred to disturb us except a singular sighting: a massive creature emerging clumsily from the sea onto the shore, browsing on vegetation. From our distance, its nature remained ambiguous, yet it elicited no terror, only relief that it lay far behind us. To us, it seemed a natural occurrence, devoid of the monstrous odor that usually plagues the imagination. Such creatures defined that country; perhaps the Early World resembled it too. Extreme conditions breed extremes, a principle evident in the convergence of ancient laws and current realities. Whether driven solely by circumstance or a deeper spiritual force remained unknowable, yet the idea held no offense to reason. Even if humanity had once been a fish, or existed in a brutish state akin to the Humpt Men, the core essence—the Spirit of Man—remained constant. Development alters the vessel, refining the flesh and awakening latent spiritual capacities, but it does not change the fundamental architecture of the human soul. We move swiftly between states, capable of regression or ascent, yet we remain, ultimately, human.

Beyond this philosophical drift, two other phenomena marked our passage. First, the great beast; second, a towering volcanic peak rising directly from the ocean. The sea boiled around its base, punctuated by dozens of great jets roaring upward, accompanied by strange grunts emanating from the depths. These signs testified to the raw, waking energy of the Deep Country. We lingered in awe, staring back at the Hill of Fire and Force long after passing it. Beyond this, the geography shifted as the Great Sea fractured into smaller, interconnected channels, guiding us forward.

She showed me the specific spots where I had slept during my initial arrival, her curiosity piqued by the geography of my solitude. As the fourth day concluded, we reached the shore at a flat expanse sloping upward toward the mouth of the First Gorge. It was the tenth hour, mirroring the start of our journey. The water trip had been serene, a sanctuary of safety, but we knew the peril ahead awaited us. Our resolve hardened; if we conquered the gorge and reached the Mighty Home safely, the reward would be a lifetime of shared loveliness, a glorious repayment for our stress.

We beached the raft, wondering if anyone would ever see it again. She severed a small fragment of wood, a token for future remembrance. Gathering our gear, she helped me re-don my armor. The scrip and pouch settled against my back, the diskos gripped in my hand. She adjusted her belt, ensuring her knife was accessible, her bundle now reduced to essentials. Then, kneeling, she kissed the raft. Memories pooled around her; this was a final severance from the first phase of her life. Her heart stirred with a complex mixture of sorrow and gratitude. I knelt gently, taking her hands, and led her away from the floating timber, toward the dark maw of the Great Gorge.

To our right stood a grim, colossal mountain, crowned by the four fire-hills we had seen earlier. Below them ascended great ridges of ash, deposited over eons. She gazed upon this scene with endless wonder, a sight that defied comprehension for any human eye. We climbed into the high entrance of the gorge, stepping briefly into the gloom before pausing to turn one last time toward the Country of Seas. It was a solemn moment, knowing we might be the last of the old humans to witness that land, hidden forever in the world's eternal night. I wondered if the Humpt Men would eventually evolve to match the spiritual sweetness inherent in humanity, a thought that felt both odd and inevitable. We listened to the distant mutter of the fire-mountains and the ambient noise of life on that deep continent, standing mere paces from the silence of the gorge that would lead us into the Eternal Strangeness of the Night Land. She clung tightly to my arm as we looked back at the red glow of the dying world, where we had nearly died. Tears streamed silently down her face—not purely sorrow, but a poignant blend of grief and happiness, mourning the island where she nursed me back to health, while anticipating the stories she would tell her children of a place they would never see.

We rounded the mighty corner of the gorge and stepped forward into the gloom, stumbling slightly. For sixteen hours, we walked steadily, disturbed only by the encroaching darkness. It had been twenty-six hours since our last sleep, a foolish endurance that threatened to exhaust me before we reached the Night Land. Her voice urged caution. Eventually, we found a safe spot to rest. While eating and drinking, we calculated our progress, deciding strictly to limit our daily travel within the gorge to sixteen hours, followed by eight hours of uninterrupted sleep. We adhered to this discipline for five days until we emerged from the gorge.

Once in the lighted sections, morale lifted, though we were frequently stifled by the horrid gases rising from the fissures. My strength grew consistent with every step. She never allowed herself to be carried, moving instead with surprising lightness and agility, acclimated to the constant wandering. Periodically, I paused to show her the exact locations of my previous slumbers. She insisted on standing precisely where I had lain alone during my desperate searches, her tenderness palpable, her speech failing under the weight of her emotions.

Her questions became frequent: when would we reach the Night Land? How far was it? Excitement built within her, mirrored closely by my own. I pondered the Mighty Redoubt and the strange, monstrous landscape awaiting us. Most profoundly, I was shaken to my core by the urgency of getting her to safety, fearing that despite our progress, disaster might strike. This anxiety made racing impossible; we could not exceed our established hours. Instead, we relied on wisdom, resting faithfully after the sixteenth hour, trusting that patience was our strongest weapon.
