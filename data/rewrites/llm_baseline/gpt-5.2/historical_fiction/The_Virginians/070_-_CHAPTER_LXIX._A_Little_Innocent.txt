George Warrington had said in his letter—half laughing at it, half leaning on it like a man who puts his hand to a railing he pretends not to need—that though my Lord Castlewood had once played very sharply with Harry, he and George still met as cousins should, with a smoothness that, in London, can pass for affection. If George wanted a frank, an introduction where a door was guarded by lace and precedent, a seat to hear the thunder of a debate from the red benches, Castlewood was ever ready—so long as the helping of another man’s fortunes did not so much as scratch his own. He could be pleasant; he could be brilliant; he could even be serviceable. He had that gift, so ruinous in the end, of seeming to mean precisely what the listener most wished to hear.

Now he went further, and promised—promised with a gravity that made the promise taste substantial—to do what he could with those in power for Mr. George Warrington. George, daily less inclined to turn his face westward, seized on the offer as a hungry man seizes on bread.

For it was not only sentiment that tethered him to England. London’s life—its talk, its theatres, its books, its perpetual clatter of coaches and bells, its nightly blaze of candles and cards—pleased him infinitely more than the broad, languid stretches of plantation day. A planter’s ease might have suited him, could he have taken it with independence; but in Virginia he was, as he thought, the first and worst-treated of his mother’s subjects. He could already feel, like a strap across his shoulders, the old maternal governance tightening; he could already hear the plantation’s thick, monotonous soundscape—the drones of overseers, the creak of wagons, the same phrases repeated until they ceased to be words and became commands. And he dreaded, more than for himself, for the young bride he meant to bring there: the narrowing of her world to a routine, the petty subordination, the jealousies, the bickerings that would begin at breakfast and sour the day, the certainty of differences of opinion at every household decision, as inevitable as the noon heat. Better freedom and poverty in England, with a hope—however faint—of distinction, than that slow domestic siege.

So Castlewood’s patronage, though it came from a hand not famously clean, was eagerly accepted. My lord had not provided well for his own brother, it is true; and his own position, peer as he was, had more bitterness in it than splendour. But we believe what we wish to believe; and George chose to put weight on the promise, as if it were coin.

Unlike the Warringtons, who had received George’s engagement to Miss Lambert with varying degrees of chill and complaint, Lord Castlewood behaved as if the match were an excellent joke told at a good table. He waited upon her parents; he praised George to them with a cousinly warmth; he praised the young lady to George with a gallantry that was almost respectful; and he made himself so prodigiously agreeable in their company that these charitable folk forgot—no, rather, they were persuaded to mistrust—the bad repute of the man. It must be a wicked and scandalous world, they thought, to speak ill of one who could be so lively without insolence, so witty without cruelty, so attentive without appetite.

He said, indeed, that he was improved in their society, as every man must be who came into it; and for the time he stayed in that quiet house, it was as if he left his worldliness with his cloak in the hall, and only took it up again when he stepped into his chair and the street’s coarse roar met him. There are such little harbours, even for the wildest sailors—some domestic haven where the noises soften, where the very crackle of the fire and the clink of a spoon in a teacup rebuke the brazen shout of the gaming table. In such a haven Castlewood looked, if not wholly new, at least less battered.

Nor did the Lambert elders think the worse of him because he openly proclaimed his admiration for Miss Theo. It was genuine enough; he did not pretend it was deep.

“It don’t affect my sleep,” he said coolly; “and I am not going to break my heart because Miss Lambert prefers somebody else. Only I wish, when I was a young man, madam, I had had the good fortune to meet with somebody so innocent and good as your daughter. I might have been kept out of a deal of harm’s way; but innocent and good young women did not fall into mine—or they would have made me better than I am.”

“Sure, my lord, it is not too late!” Mrs. Lambert answered, very softly—soft as a spoon laid down on a saucer, as if loudness might jar the fragile possibility she was offering him.

Castlewood started back, misunderstanding the direction of her pity.

“Not too late, madam?” he asked.

She blushed, and steadied herself with the little domestic dignity of a mother among her own curtains. “It is too late to court my dear daughter, my lord; but not too late to repent. We read, ’tis never too late to do that. If others have been received at the eleventh hour, is there any reason why you should give up hope?”

“Perhaps I know my own heart better than you,” he said, with a plaintive humour that made his despair almost graceful. “I can speak French and German very well; and why? because I was taught both in the nursery. A man who learns them late can never get the practice of them on his tongue. And so ’tis with goodness: I can’t learn it at my age. I can only see others practise it, and admire them. When I am on—on the side opposite to Lazarus, will Miss Theo give me a drop of water? Don’t frown! I know I shall be there, Mrs. Lambert. Some folks are doomed so; and I think some of our family are amongst these. Some people are vacillating, and one hardly knows which way the scale will turn. Whereas some are predestined angels, and fly Heavenwards naturally, and do what they will.”

“Oh, my lord, and why should you not be of the predestined? Whilst there is a day left—whilst there is an hour—there is hope!” cried the fond matron, with that physician’s persistence good women have when they take a sinner for a patient.

“I know what is passing in your mind—nay, I read your prayers in your looks,” Lord Castlewood said, and his voice lowered, as though even words had to be kept from making too much noise in that decent room. “But how can they avail? You don’t know all, my good lady. You don’t know what a life ours is of the world; how early it began; how selfish Nature, and then necessity and education, have made us. It is Fate holds the reins of the chariot, and we can’t escape our doom. I know better: I see better people: I go my own way. My own? No, not mine—Fate’s: and it is not altogether without pity for us, since it allows us, from time to time, to see such people as you.”

And he took her hand, looked full in her face, and bowed with a melancholy grace. Every word might be true, and yet the truth itself might be part of the man’s indulgence—like tears that refresh a libertine as wine refreshes a drunkard. Mrs. Lambert, who heard sincerity in any tone not openly coarse, knew little of what was passing behind his fine phrases (how should she?), and so prayed for him with a gentle obstinacy. He was much better—yes, much better than the world said. He was interesting. There were hopes—why should there not be the most precious hopes for him still?

It remains to be seen which of the two judged him rightly. Meanwhile, even if the gentleman’s estimate of himself was correct, the lady’s tenderness could do him no harm. Such kind-souled doctors watch a perilous case with a zeal that is almost personal; and when the patient recovers, their interest slips away to the next bed in the ward.

But while the malady was under treatment, Mrs. Lambert could not see too much of her “sick man.” An intimacy sprang up between my Lord Castlewood and the Lamberts. It is not impossible that a worldly view or two slid, like a mouse into a pantry, even into good Mrs. Lambert’s spiritual plans; for who knows into what guarded Eden worldliness will not creep? Her son was about to take orders. My Lord Castlewood, who feared that his chaplain Mr. Sampson’s careless life and heterodox talk might lead him to resign, hinted that in such a case the modest cure would be vacant, and might suit a young divine of good principles and good manners—content with a small stipend and a small but friendly congregation.

So the acquaintance settled into habit. The ladies of Castlewood, always on their best behaviour, came more than once to curtsey in Mrs. Lambert’s drawing-room. They were civil to the parents and the young ladies; and my Lady Castlewood’s card assemblies were open to Mrs. Lambert and her household. There was play, certainly—everybody played: his Majesty, bishops, peers and peeresses. But nobody need play who did not like; and surely nobody need have scruples, when such august and venerable personages daily abetted the practice. More than once Mrs. Lambert appeared at her ladyship’s routs, grateful for her welcome, and pleased—honestly pleased, as a mother is when her daughters are admired—at the murmur of approbation the girls excited in that bright, dangerous crowd: a soft sea of voices, fans whispering like dry leaves, cards snapping, chairs scraping, a noise that could intoxicate as readily as wine.

Mention has been made of an American family, Dutch by extraction, who came to England strongly recommended by Madam Esmond, their Virginian neighbour, to her sons in Europe. Mrs. Lambert, match-maker by instinct as other people are cooks by instinct, read Madam Esmond’s intentions as clearly as if they had been written in large letters across the paper. But George was already engaged; and Harry—poor Hetty’s flame—was away on service, which circumstance Hetty’s mother perhaps did not wholly regret.

She told George, laughingly, that he ought to obey his mamma’s injunctions, break off his engagement with Theo, and make up to Miss Lydia, who was ten times—ten times! a hundred times!—as rich as her poor girl, and certainly much handsomer.

“Yes, indeed,” said George, “that I own: she is handsomer, and she is richer, and perhaps even cleverer.” Mrs. Lambert liked those praises only by halves. “But say she is all these? So is Mr. Johnson much cleverer than I am; so is Mr. Hagan the actor much taller and handsomer; so is Sir James Lowther much richer: yet pray, ma’am, do you suppose I am going to be jealous of any one of these three, or think my Theo would jilt me for their sakes? Why should I not allow that Miss Lydia is handsomer then? and richer, and clever too, and lively, and well bred, if you insist on it, and an angel if you will have it so? Theo is not afraid: art thou, child?”

“No, George,” Theo answered; and the honest look of her eyes—clear as water in a clean glass—would have shamed any jealousy. And if, after this, mamma took occasion to leave the room for a minute to fetch her scissors, or her thimble, or a bootjack and slippers, or the cross and ball on the top of St. Paul’s, or a pocket-handkerchief forgotten in the parlour—if Mrs. Lambert departed on any errand, natural or preposterous—I shall not be surprised that, upon returning, she found George in near proximity to Theo, who had a heightened colour, and whose hand George was just dropping. I shall not pretend to know what they had been doing. Have you, madam? Have you no remembrance of what used to happen when Mr. Grundy came a-courting? Are you, who were not in the room, going to cry fie? Then fie upon you, Mrs. Grundy.

Harry being away, and Theo and George irrevocably engaged, Madam Esmond’s little plans could not bite. Why should not Mrs. Lambert have plans of her own? If a rich, handsome, beautiful little wife should fall into Jack Lambert’s Oxford path, why should she not be gathered up? So thought mamma, who was always thinking of marrying and giving in marriage; and she prattled to General Lambert, who, as usual, called her a goose for her pains. Beauty and riches, she argued, are no objection. Madam Esmond desired the family should be entertained. It was not Mrs. Lambert’s fault Harry was gone to Canada. Would the General wish him to come back, leave the army and his reputation, perhaps—yes, and come to England and marry this American and break poor Hetty’s heart—would her father wish that? Let us spare further argument, and not be so rude as to hint that Mr. Lambert had some reason when he called his fond wife by the name of that absurd splay-footed bird sacrificed at St. Michael’s.

In those days, court and city drew their distinctions as sharply as a knife draws them through crust. Mr. Van den Bosch, when he first came to London, scarcely associated with any but city folk. He lodged near his agent, and his pleasures were the sober pleasures of trade: an airing to Islington or Highgate; a promenade in the Artillery Ground in Bunhill Fields; Sunday at the Baptist meeting-house in Finsbury Fields. And yet—because even the most pious household has its little contraband delights—they went on the sly once or twice to see Mr. Garrick, or that rogue Mr. Foote at the Little Theatre. A Lord Mayor’s feast was a treat to the old gentleman; and to dance with a young mercer at Hampstead Assembly gave the utmost delight to the young lady.

When George first went to wait upon his mother’s friends, he found our old acquaintance, Mr. Draper of the Temple, sedulous in attentions to Miss Lydia; and the lawyer—who was married—told George to look out, as the young lady had a plumb to her fortune. Mr. Drabshaw, a young Quaker, nephew to Mr. Trail, Madam Esmond’s Bristol agent, was also in constant attendance upon the girl, and in dreadful suspicion when Mr. Warrington appeared.

Wishing to do honour to his mother’s neighbours, George invited them to an entertainment at his own apartments; and who should so naturally meet them there as his friends from Soho? Not one of them could deny little Miss Lydia’s beauty. She had the foot of a fairy; the arms, neck, and flashing eyes of a little brown huntress of Diana. She had brought with her from home a plaintive accent—one of those sounds that carry a whole country in the mouth; and I, moi qui vous parle, have heard a hundred gross Cockney imitations of it, and watched as many absurd disguises, until one is tempted to think the very effort to “speak properly” is a kind of tyranny, levelling all the world’s living voices into one thin, fashionable note. Who sets up to say No, forsooth? You dear Miss Whittington, with whose h’s Fate has dealt so unkindly? You lovely Miss Nicol Jarvie, with your northern burr? You beautiful Miss Molony, with your Dame Street warble? All accents are pretty from pretty lips, and who shall set the standard up—rose, thistle, shamrock, or star and stripe? If Miss Lydia’s accent had been odious, Mr. Warrington, as a man of taste, had certainly disapproved; and her schoolmistress at Kensington had not done her duty by her pupil.

After the six months were over—during which, according to her father’s calculation, she was to learn all the accomplishments procurable at the Kensington Academy—Miss Lydia returned, nothing loth, to her grandfather and took her place in the world. At first it was a narrow world: Monument Yard, respectable dinners, city gallantries, Sabbaths of proper sound and silence. But she was a resolute little person, and resolved to enlarge her sphere; and whither she chose to lead, the obedient grandfather followed. He had been thwarted in youth, he said, and little good had come of the severity he underwent. He had thwarted his own son, who had turned out ill. As for little Lyddy, he was determined she should have as pleasant a life as possible. Did not Mr. George think he was right? It was said in Virginia—he did not know with what reason—that the young gentlemen of Castlewood had been happier if Madam Esmond had allowed them a little of their own way. George could not deny the rumour, nor would he try to alter the benevolent old man’s plans. As for the Lamperts, how could they do other than welcome the kind old merchant, so tender a parent, Madam Esmond’s good friend?

When Miss came from school, grandpapa removed from Monument Yard to an elegant house in Bloomsbury; and their city friends followed at first—merchants, worthy tradesmen with whom the old gentleman dealt, their ladies and sons and daughters, all gracious to Miss Lyddy. But it would be a long task to tell how these dropped away one by one, as crumbs are brushed from a cloth when company improves. There were no more junketings at Belsize, no more trips to Highgate, no more Saturday jaunts to Deputy Higgs’s villa at Highbury, no more country-dances at honest Mr. Lutestring’s at Hackney. Even their Sunday practice changed; and, oh abomination of abominations! Mr. Van den Bosch left Bethesda Chapel in Bunhill Row and actually took a pew in Queen Square Church.

Queen Square Church—and Mr. George Warrington lived hard by in Southampton Row. It was easy to see at whom Miss Lyddy was setting her cap. And Mr. Draper, who had once been full of her and her grandfather’s praises, now warned George, and gave him reports of Mr. Van den Bosch very different from the first.

Mr. Van den Bosch, for all his brag of Dutch parentage, came from Albany and was nobody’s son at all. He had made his money by land speculation, or by privateering (uncommonly like piracy), and by the Guinea trade. His son had married—if marriage it could be called, which was doubtful—an assigned servant; had been cut off by his father; had taken to bad courses; and had died, luckily for himself, in his own bed.

“Mr. Draper has told you bad tales about me,” said the placid old gentleman to George when they spoke of it; and his tone had the weary evenness of a man who has heard his life translated into other men’s words and knows how much is always lost or poisoned in the translation. “Very likely we are all sinners, and some evil may be truly said of all of us, with a great deal more that is untrue. Did he tell you my son was unhappy with me? I told you so too. Did he bring you wicked stories about my family? He liked it so well he wanted to marry my Lyddy to his brother. Heaven bless her! I have had a many offers for her. And you are the young gentleman I should have chose for her, and I like you none the worse because you prefer somebody else; though what you can see in your Miss, as compared to my Lyddy, begging your honour’s pardon, I am at a loss to understand.”

“There is no accounting for tastes, my good sir,” George said, putting on his most superb air, as if taste were a throne and he sat in it.

“No, sir; ’tis a wonder of nature, and daily happens,” the old man pursued, good-humoured and blunt. “When I kept store to Albany, there was one of your tiptop gentry there that might have married my dear daughter that was alive then, and with a pretty piece of money; whereby—for her father and I had quarrelled—Miss Lyddy would have been a pauper, you see. And in place of my beautiful Bella, my gentleman chooses a little homely creature, no prettier than your Miss, and without a dollar to her fortune. The more fool he, saving your presence, Mr. George.”

“Pray don’t save my presence, my good sir,” George cried, laughing. “I suppose the gentleman’s word was given to the other lady, and he had seen her first, and hence was indifferent to your charming daughter.”

“I suppose when a young fellow gives his word to perform a cursed piece of folly, he always sticks to it,” said the elder; then checked himself with a rueful shake of the head. “But Lord, Lord, what am I speaking of? I am aspeaking of twenty year ago. I was well-to-do then, but I may say Heaven has blessed my store, and I am three times as well off now. Ask my agents how much they will give for Joseph Van den Bosch’s bill at six months on New York—or at sight maybe for forty thousand pound? I warrant they will discount the paper.”

“Happy he who has the bill, sir!” said George, bowing, amused at the candour and at the merchant’s innocent vanity in the sound of his own wealth.

“Lord, Lord, how mercenary you young men are!” cried the old gentleman, simply. “Always thinking about money nowadays! Happy he who has the girl, I should say—the money ain’t the question, my dear sir, when it goes along with such a lovely young thing as that—though I humbly say it, who oughtn’t, and who am her fond silly old grandfather. We were talking about you, Lyddy darling—come, give me a kiss, my blessing! We were talking about you, and Mr. George said he wouldn’t take you with all the money your poor old grandfather can give you.”

“Nay, sir—” George began.

“Well, you are right to say nay, for I didn’t say all, that’s the truth. My Blessing will have a deal more than that trifle I spoke of, when it shall please Heaven to remove me out of this world to a better—when poor old Gappy is gone, Lyddy will be a rich little Lyddy, that she will. But she don’t wish me to go yet, does she?”

“Oh, you darling dear grandpapa!” Lydia cried, with a sweetness that sounded practised and yet was warm as breath on a cold pane.

“This young gentleman won’t have you.” Lydia gave him an arch look from her brown eyes—“Thank you, sir,” without saying it. “But at any rate he is honest, and that is more than we can say of some folks in this wicked London. Oh, Lord, Lord, how mercenary they are! Do you know that yonder, in Monument Yard, they were all at my poor little Blessing for her money? There was Tom Lutestring; there was Mr. Draper, your precious lawyer; there was actually Mr. Tubbs, of Bethesda Chapel; and they must all come buzzing like flies round the honey-pot. That is why we came out of the quarter where my brother-tradesmen live.”

“To avoid the flies,—to be sure!” said Miss Lydia, tossing up her little head.

“Where my brother-tradesmen live,” continued the old gentleman, as if he liked the honest weight of those words, though he was leaving them behind. “Else who am I to think of consorting with your grandees and fine folk? I don’t care for the fashions, Mr. George; I don’t care for plays and poetry, begging your honour’s pardon; I never went to a play in my life, but to please this little minx.”

“Oh, sir, ’twas lovely! and I cried so, didn’t I, grandpapa?” Lydia cried; and one could almost hear in her voice the theatre itself—the rising sighs, the smothered sobs, the applause like rain on a roof.

“At what, my dear?” asked Gappy, truly puzzled.

“At—at Mr. Warrington’s play, grandpapa.”

“Did you, my dear? I dare say; I dare say!” The old man chuckled, half ashamed. “It was mail day; and my letters had come in; and my ship the Lovely Lyddy had just come into Falmouth; and Captain Joyce reported how he had mercifully escaped a French privateer; and my head was so full of thanks for that escape—which saved me a deal of money, Mr. George—for the rate at which ships is underwrote this war-time is so scandalous that I often prefer to venture than to insure—that I confess I didn’t listen much to the play, sir, and only went to please this little Lyddy.”

“And you did please me, dearest Gappy!” cried the young lady, and pressed herself close to him as if the squeezing could seal the affection in place.

“Bless you! then it’s all I want. What does a man want more here below than to please his children, Mr. George? especially me, who knew what was to be unhappy when I was young, and to repent of having treated this darling’s father too hard.”

“Oh, grandpapa!” Lydia cried, and redoubled her caresses, as if to drown the old remorse before it could find words.

“Yes, I was too hard with him, dear; and that’s why I spoil my little Lydkin so!”

More kisses followed. The little creature flung her polished arms round the old man’s neck, pressed her dark red lips to his withered cheek, and beat a halo of powder from his wig with the violence of her tenderness. All the while she watched Mr. George, as much as to say—There, sir! should you not like me to do as much for you?

We confess—though do we confess all? George told Theo the tale of his interview with Lyddy and Gappy, and the old man’s news regarding his granddaughter’s wealth; but I do not think he told everything. Had he done so, Theo would scarcely have been so much interested, or so entirely amused and good-humoured, when next the two young ladies met.
