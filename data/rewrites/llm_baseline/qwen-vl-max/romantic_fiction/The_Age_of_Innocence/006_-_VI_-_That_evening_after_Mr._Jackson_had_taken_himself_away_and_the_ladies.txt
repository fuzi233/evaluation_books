VI. That evening, after Mr. Jackson had departed and the ladies had retired to their softly curtained bedroom, Newland Archer climbed quietly to his study. As always, a careful hand had kept the fire glowing and the lamp trimmed, casting a warm light over the room—its shelves lined with books, its mantelpiece adorned with bronze and steel figures of *The Fencers*, and its surfaces dotted with photographs of famous paintings. It was a place of comfort and quiet dignity, a sanctuary where thought could unfold without interruption.

He sank into his armchair beside the hearth, and his gaze fell upon a large photograph of May Welland, placed prominently on the table. She had given it to him in the early days of their courtship, and now it stood alone among the other portraits, as if claiming exclusive possession of his attention. He looked at her—the clear forehead, the thoughtful eyes, the bright, unguarded smile—and felt a new kind of reverence. This young woman, so full of life yet untouched by experience, was the one whose soul he would soon be entrusted to guard. And yet, looking at her face, he saw not just May, but a symbol: the product of a world that shaped women to know nothing, expect everything, and never question the rules.

It struck him again—marriage wasn’t the safe harbor he’d once believed it to be. It was more like setting sail into unknown waters. The recent stir around Countess Olenska had shaken long-held beliefs, stirring doubts he hadn’t known he carried. His own words—"Women should be free, as free as we are"—now echoed in his mind, not as a bold declaration, but as a challenge to the very fabric of the society he lived in. In his circle, such sentiments were permitted only in conversation, never in practice. "Nice" women, even when wronged, would never demand the freedom he meant. And men like himself, who spoke generously, did so not out of conviction but because it flattered their sense of chivalry. These words were mere gestures, masks for the rigid conventions that bound everyone to tradition.

But here he was, pledged to defend the actions of May’s cousin—actions that, if committed by his own wife, would bring down every social and moral condemnation he knew. Of course, this was hypothetical; he wasn’t a Polish nobleman, nor would he ever be. Yet his imagination refused to let go. What if, in his own marriage, the bond grew strained—not from scandal, but from something quieter, subtler? How much could he truly know of May, when it was his duty to hide parts of himself, and hers to have nothing to hide? What if they grew tired of each other, misunderstood, or simply drifted apart?

He recalled the marriages of his friends—those deemed happy—and found none that matched the deep, tender companionship he dreamed of sharing with May. That dream, he realized, required qualities she had been deliberately denied: independence, depth of experience, the freedom to think and feel without restraint. And with a pang of dread, he saw his future marriage taking shape—not as a union of souls, but as a partnership of appearances, sustained by ignorance and pretense.

Larry Lefferts came to mind. He was the epitome of such a marriage—his wife molded perfectly to his needs, blissfully unaware of his affairs, even laughing at the idea that Julius Beaufort might have “another establishment.” She blushed at the mention of it, as if scandal were a foreign concept. Archer tried to reassure himself that he wasn’t quite so shallow, nor May so naive. But the difference was only one of intelligence, not principle. They all lived in a world of symbols, where truth was hidden behind polite lies. Mrs. Welland, though she knew exactly why Archer had pushed for the engagement announcement at the Beaufort ball, still pretended reluctance—just as primitive brides were dragged screaming from their homes in the stories people read.

And so, the girl at the heart of this elaborate charade remained mysterious, not because she was guarded, but because she was utterly sincere. She was honest because she had nothing to hide, confident because she had no reason to fear. And yet, with this innocence, she was about to step into what was called “the facts of life”—a world she was ill-prepared to face.

Archer loved her deeply, but calmly. He admired her beauty, her vitality, her skill at riding and games, and the gentle curiosity she showed toward books and ideas under his influence. (She had joined him in mocking Tennyson’s *Idyls of the King*, but hadn’t yet grasped the magic of *Ulysses and the Lotus Eaters*.) She was loyal, brave, and possessed a quiet humor—mostly demonstrated by laughing at his jokes. And beneath her calm gaze, he sensed a warmth of feeling, waiting to be awakened.

Yet, despite all this, he felt a growing unease. Was this purity real—or merely manufactured? He believed that untrained human nature wasn’t naturally innocent or frank; it was instinctively guarded, twisted by self-protection. And now, he was surrounded by a creation of artificial virtue—crafted by generations of mothers, aunts, and grandmothers who had shaped girls into perfect wives, not because they were naturally pure, but because society demanded it.

He felt oppressed by it. This ideal of innocence wasn’t freedom—it was performance. And he feared it was designed not to protect, but to allow men like himself to enjoy the thrill of breaking it, like shattering a snow sculpture.

These thoughts were familiar—common enough among young men on the brink of marriage. But usually they came with guilt, with regret. Archer felt none of that. He couldn’t mourn the fact that he didn’t offer his bride a blank page in exchange for hers. If he had been raised as she was, he wouldn’t have been any better equipped to navigate life than a child lost in the woods. And no matter how hard he thought, he couldn *t* find an honest reason—beyond vanity or fleeting desire—why May shouldn’t have the same freedom he claimed for himself.

These questions lingered, sharpened by the arrival of Ellen Olenska. At the moment of his betrothal—a time meant for joy and clarity—he was thrust into a scandal that forced him to confront truths he’d hoped to avoid.

“Damn Ellen Olenska,” he muttered, covering the fire and beginning to undress. He couldn’t see how her fate affected him. Yet deep down, he sensed that he had only just begun to grasp the weight of the role he had accepted.

A few days later, the storm broke. The Lovell Mingotts sent out invitations for a formal dinner—three extra footmen, two dishes per course, Roman punch at the center—and added, “To meet the Countess Olenska,” following the American custom of treating strangers like royalty.

The guest list was carefully chosen, a blend of old families and fashionable young couples—Selfridge Merrys, Beauforts, Sillerton Jackson and his sister Sophy, Lawrence Lefferts, Mrs. Lefferts Rushworth, Harry Thorley, Reggie Chivers, and Morris Dagonet and his wife, a van der Luyden. It was a perfect mix—everyone who moved in the tight-knit inner circle of high society.

But forty-eight hours later, the unthinkable happened: every invitation was declined—except those of the Beauforts and old Mr. Jackson with his sister. Even the Chiverses, part of the Mingott family, refused. And every refusal was worded identically: “Regretted unable to accept,” with no mention of prior engagements. In a society where everyone knew everyone else’s schedule, this was a deliberate, cutting snub.

The Mingotts took it with grace. Mrs. Lovell Mingott confided in Mrs. Welland, who passed it on to Archer. He reacted with outrage, appealing passionately to his mother. After a period of hesitation, she yielded—as she always did—and immediately declared, “I’ll go and see Louisa van der Luyden.”

New York in Archer’s time was a small, slippery pyramid. At its base were the “plain people”—respectable families elevated by marriage to the elite, like the Spicers, Leffertses, and Jacksons. Mrs. Archer often said, “People aren’t as particular as they used to be.” With Catherine Spicer ruling one end of Fifth Avenue and Julius Beaufort the other, the old traditions were fading fast.

Above them was the dominant group—the Mingotts, Newlands, Chiverses, Mansons—who seemed to be at the top. But Mrs. Archer knew better. To the genealogists, only a handful of families truly held aristocratic status: the Dagonets of Washington Square, descendants of English nobility linked to Pitt and Fox; the Lannings, connected to Count de Grasse; and the van der Luydens, direct heirs of Manhattan’s first Dutch governor, tied by marriage to French and British aristocracy.

The Lannings were represented only by two elderly sisters, living among ancestral portraits. The Dagonets were numerous, allied with the best names in Baltimore and Philadelphia. But the van der Luydens stood above all—nearly mythical, existing in a twilight realm from which only two figures emerged: Mr. and Mrs. Henry van der Luyden.

Mrs. van der Luyden had been Louisa Dagonet, granddaughter of Colonel du Lac, a Channel Islander who fought under Cornwallis and settled in Maryland with Lady Angelica Trevenna, daughter of the Earl of St. Austrey. The ties between these families remained strong. The van der Luydens had visited the Duke of St. Austrey in Cornwall and Gloucestershire, and he had promised to return their visit—without the Duchess, who feared the Atlantic.

They divided their time between Trevenna in Maryland, their estate in Skuytercliff on the Hudson—once a colonial grant from the Dutch government—and their rare, solemn house on Madison Avenue, opened only for intimate friends.

“I wish you’d come with me, Newland,” his mother said, pausing at the door of the Brown coupe. “Louisa likes you. And of course, it’s for dear May I’m doing this—and because, if we don’t stand together, there won’t be any Society left.”
