CHAPTER XXXI. “A wild dedication of yourselves
To unpath’d waters, undreamed shores.” —SHAKESPEARE.

The morning Gwendolen Harleth became Mrs. Grandcourt was sharp and clear, the sun low in the sky, casting a pale gold over the frost-kissed leaves. The bridal party moved through Pennicote like a vision—bright, solemn, and stirring enough to draw every soul from their doors. Half the village lined the path to the church, eyes wide with admiration, breath held for the spectacle. The rector, acting as father-in-law by proxy, added dignity to the procession, while an old friend officiated at the marriage rites. Only two faces carried shadows: Mrs. Davilow’s, soft and pink around the eyes as if tears had been her companion through the night, and Anna’s, whose quiet sorrow puzzled those who saw her in such a radiant bridesmaid’s gown.

Everyone else seemed wrapped in the joy of the occasion—especially the bride. She stood tall and proud, a figure that could have belonged to any noble house. Her carriage was regal, her presence commanding. If her complexion lacked the bloom of youth, few minded—Grandcourt himself was not flushed with health, his pallor matching hers in quiet harmony. As the miller’s wife remarked, “He’s much of her husband’s complexion,” and so the union seemed complete, even inevitable.

“Surely he must love her deeply,” people whispered. “And may he never remind her she once thought of becoming a governess—or that her mother lived at Sawyer’s Cottage.” Such talk had stirred the village, but today it was buried beneath the glitter of silk and satin.

The miller’s daughter, only fourteen, couldn’t imagine how high-born men might mistreat their wives. But her mother, seasoned by years of gossip and experience, leaned in and said, “Oh, child, men are men—gentle or plain, they’re all much the same. I’ve heard my own mother say Squire Pelton used to bring his dogs and a long whip into his wife’s chamber, just to frighten her. And I was there, serving as lady’s-maid.”

“That’s ill luck for a wedding day,” the tailor interjected. “A quarrel starts with words, not whips. And women? They’ve got the tongue.”

“The Lord gave it to them,” Mrs. Girdle replied, “so they should use it. He never meant you to have it all your way.”

“And from what the groomsmen at Offendene tell me,” the tailor added, “Mr. Grandcourt speaks little. Everything seems to happen without him saying a word.”

“Then he’s the more dangerous,” Mrs. Girdle muttered. “She’s got tongue enough, I’ll wager. Look—here they come!”

“See how long her eyelashes are!” the tailor murmured. “She makes you feel odd when she looks at you.”

Indeed, Gwendolen had never looked more alive—her eyes bright, her step light, her spirit electric. It wasn’t pain she felt now, though her conscience had stirred in recent weeks. No, this was something else: a fierce, exhilarating rush, like the moment before losing at cards when Deronda watched her, breathless. She ignored the unease within, treating it like a feverish ache one endures for pleasure. Ambition, vanity, the hunger for luxury—they were too strong to be poisoned by guilt.

This morning, she could not truthfully say she regretted marrying Grandcourt. Fear did not cloud her joy—not yet. The scene around her was dazzling, and she was its center. That she was doing something wrong—that punishment might follow—that Lydia Glasher, the woman she had promised to wait for, was suffering in silence—that Deronda likely despised her for this choice—these thoughts had haunted her during the weeks of engagement. But now? Now they were buried under defiance, swept aside by the intoxication of being the heroine of her own grand story.

She walked into the future not with trembling, but with fire in her veins. She was entering a world of power—marriage, title, wealth—where she would command, not submit. The egoism that had once shaken her in shame had returned, stronger than before, fed by triumph and expectation.

She did not look like a weeping bride. Some had called her too worldly, too forward. But now, with her head high and her steps springing, she walked among illusions—and yet, deep down, she knew she was slightly drunk on her own glory.

“Thank God you bear it so well, my darling,” Mrs. Davilow whispered, helping her remove the bridal veil and slip into her traveling dress.

All the tremors had been hers—the mother’s hands shook as she touched her daughter’s shoulders. Gwendolen, sensing her distress, pushed back the sorrow with laughter and warmth.

“If I’d been going to Mrs. Mompert’s, you dear, sad, incorrigible mamma, you’d have said the same thing!” she teased, brushing her fingers gently over her mother’s cheeks. Then, stepping back with arms outstretched, she declared, “Here I am—Mrs. Grandcourt! What else could I be? You know you nearly died of grief when you thought I wouldn’t become her.”

“Hush, hush, my child,” Mrs. Davilow breathed, almost inaudibly. “How can I help feeling it? I’m parting from you. But if you’re happy, I can bear anything.”

“No, mamma,” Gwendolen smiled, shaking her head. “You’d bear it willingly—but always with sorrow. Sorrow is your spice; you taste nothing without it.”

She clasped her mother’s shoulders, showering kisses on each cheek between words. “And you’ll sorrow over me having everything at my command—everything glorious—houses, horses, diamonds—I shall have diamonds—going to court—being Lady Certainly, Lady Perhaps—grand here, tantivy there—and always loving you better than anyone in the world.”

“My sweet child,” Mrs. Davilow whispered. “But I won’t be jealous if you love your husband more. He will expect to be first.”

Gwendolen pouted playfully. “A ridiculous expectation. But I won’t treat him ill… unless he deserves it.”

They clung together, and then, despite herself, Gwendolen sobbed softly. “I wish you were coming with me, mamma.”

A tear traced her lashes—but only made her more radiant as she offered her hand to Grandcourt, ready to step into the carriage.

The rector came to bid farewell. “Good-bye, God bless you. We’ll see you again soon.” Then, turning to Mrs. Davilow, he said, half smiling, half solemn, “Let us be thankful, Fanny. She is where she belongs—beyond what I ever dared hope. Few women are chosen so entirely for themselves. You should be a happy mother.”

The journey to Ryelands took fifty miles by rail. By the time they arrived, twilight had settled over the land. Still, Gwendolen, gazing from the carriage window, caught glimpses of the estate’s grandeur—the winding drive flanked by evergreens, towering oaks, open lawns dotted with dark trees, and finally, the white mansion rising against a forested hillside, its terrace curving like a wave.

She had chattered all the way, ignoring the shift in their roles, as if yesterday’s vows were no more than a dream. Grandcourt remained quiet, content, his hand gently clasping hers—until she turned it into a tight grip, pulling him closer, restless and vibrant, like a kitten refusing to sit still.

Her excitement was growing febrile. The new surroundings, the shifting light, the nearness of fulfillment—it all sent her heart racing. Was it just the thrill of becoming someone important? Of walking through her own corridors, under ceilings high beyond reach, where painted gods and nymphs watched her pass? Of servants bowing to her whims, of being the star of a drama without the effort of acting?

Or was it something deeper—a whisper of dread beneath the triumph? A sense that this moment, so perfect, might also be the beginning of ruin?

Her voice fell silent as the gates loomed. When Grandcourt said, “Here we are at home!” and kissed her lips for the first time, she barely registered it—only absorbed the spectacle of arrival.

After the whirlwind of the day, a numbness settled over her. Yet inside, the world was blazing.

Inside the hall, warmth greeted her—carpets, portraits, statues, servants moving with precision. Not many staff—just a few from Diplow, and her own maid, now being shown to her duties by the housekeeper.

Grandcourt led her through scented corridors into a boudoir glowing with light and color. “These are our dens,” he said. “You may rest here until dinner. We dine early.”

He pressed her hand to his lips, then stepped back—more in love than he had ever believed possible.

Gwendolen removed her hat and cloak, sank into a chair beside the hearth, and stared at her reflection in the glass panels—herself repeated in soft green satin, framed by flickering flames.

The housekeeper lingered, perhaps curious about the new mistress. But Gwendolen, eager for solitude, said sharply, “Tell Hudson to leave everything after she removes my dress. I won’t need her again—unless I ring.”

The housekeeper approached. “Madam, here is a packet. I was instructed to give it only to you, when you were alone. The messenger said it was a gift from Mr. Grandcourt—something he wished you to wear before he knew of it. I felt it right to obey orders.”

Gwendolen took the package and let it rest on her lap until the door closed behind the woman.

A thought struck her—diamonds. Grandcourt had spoken of them, stored away, to be given on their wedding day. This must be it.

She peeled back the layers—paper, box, then a jewel case. Yes—diamonds. But as she opened the case, her breath caught.

Lying atop the gems was a letter.

She recognized the handwriting.

It was as if a snake had coiled itself upon the jewels.

Her heart leapt—then failed. Her hands shook as she unfolded the thin paper.

The words were clear, sharp as ice:

*These diamonds were once given with ardent love to Lydia Glasher. She passes them on to you. You broke your word to her, so you might possess what was hers. Perhaps you think you’ll be happy, as she once was, and have beautiful children who will push hers aside. But God is too just for that. The man you married has a withered heart. His best love was mine. You could not take that from me, even when you took the rest. It is dead. But I am the grave where your chance of happiness lies buried—as mine is. You had your warning. You chose to hurt me and my children. He meant to marry me. He would have, if you had not broken your word. You will be punished. I desire it with all my soul.*

Will you give him this letter to turn him against me? Will you stand before him with these diamonds on your neck, knowing these words haunt both of you? Will he believe you have the right to complain when he makes you miserable? You took him with your eyes open. The wrong you’ve done me will be your curse.

At first, Gwendolen’s eyes were fixed, reading the words over and over—like a sentence of doom. Then, a sudden terror seized her. She lunged forward, reaching toward the fire, desperate to destroy the proof before it could be seen.

The letter flew from her trembling fingers, caught in the draft, and vanished into the flames.

As she moved, the casket slipped from her lap. Diamonds scattered across the floor.

She collapsed back into the chair, motionless, helpless.

She could not see her reflections now—only ghostly images, frozen white. But close up, you might have seen her lips quiver, her hands tremble.

She sat like that—long minutes passing—knowing only that she was ill, and that those words echoed in her mind.

Poisoned gems. And the poison had entered her.

Then—knock at the door.

Grandcourt entered, dressed for dinner.

The sight of him sent another shock through her. She screamed—again and again—hysterical, raw.

He had expected a smiling wife, ready to descend. Instead, he found her pale, shrieking, jewels strewn at her feet.

Was it madness?

In some form or other, the furies had crossed his threshold.
