A familiar figure, dressed not in mourning but in the bright hues of red, green, and white, made her way toward the Piazza that morning to witness the Carnival. She came from a different quarter now—Tessa no longer lived on the hill of San Giorgio. After the events that had unfolded there with Baldassarre, Tito had deemed it wise, for both practical and emotional reasons, to move her to a quieter, more airy part of the city—a house near the wide garden grounds north of Porta Santa Croce. Though she was not permitted to go out without permission, Tessa had waited until the evening before to speak her wish. That night, Tito had visited her, and she held back her plea until she saw him relaxed and joyful, one arm around sturdy little Lillo, the other gently resting on her shoulder as she tried to steady the wobbly Ninna on her feet. Only then did she feel certain that the weariness he’d brought home with him had truly vanished from his face and voice.

Tessa had learned over time how to navigate the delicate balance between obedience and desire. She could not read, but she had become an expert in reading expressions—the subtle shifts in Tito’s eyes, the warmth of his smile, the softness of his tone. And in this moment, she saw again the same gentle, radiant man who had once awakened beneath the Loggia de’ Cerchi on a Lenten morning, untouched by deceit or suspicion. That spirit, though dimmed by years, flickered brightly in Naldo, seated comfortably in the carved armchair he had brought for his visits. Tito himself was surprised by the quiet relief he felt in these moments—no need for pretense, no fear of exposure. Tessa was too innocent, too trusting, to suspect anything. The children’s voices calling him “Babbo” were music to his ears, fleeting but sweet. He would never leave Florence without them. These round-cheeked, wide-eyed creatures clung to him, knowing no harm, and their affection, pure and unburdened, reminded him that love could bloom even in the most barren soil.

Poor Romola, with all her selfless devotion, seemed only to harden Tito’s heart—her coldness, once unthinkable in him, now a growing presence. But Tessa kept the springs of kindness open, untouched by bitterness.

“Ninna is very good now,” Tessa began, her request rising like a tide in her throat. She let the child sit on the floor. “I can leave her with Monna Lisa anytime. If she cries in the cradle, Lillo knows just what to do—he goes and thumps Monna Lisa.”

Lillo, whose dark eyes seemed deeper against his light-brown curls like his mother’s, jumped down from Babbo’s knee and immediately proved his wisdom by marching over to deliver a firm thump to Monna Lisa, who sat spinning at the far end of the room, shaking her head slowly.

“A wonderful boy!” Tito laughed.

“Isn’t he?” Tessa said eagerly, drawing closer. “And might I go see the Carnival tomorrow—just for an hour or two?”

“Oh, you wicked pigeon!” Tito teased, pinching her cheek. “That’s what you’re longing for? What do you need with carnivals now you’re an old woman with two children?”

“But old women like to see things,” Tessa replied, her lower lip trembling slightly. “Monna Lisa said she’d like to go—but she’s so deaf, she can’t hear what’s behind her, and she thinks we couldn’t manage both children.”

“No, indeed, Tessa,” Tito said, his tone turning serious. “You mustn’t think of taking the children into the crowded streets. I’d be angry if you did.”

“But I’ve never gone into the Piazza without leave since Holy Saturday,” Tessa pleaded, her voice small. “And I think Nofri is dead—his poor _madre_ died, didn’t she? I’ll never forget the Carnival I saw once—it was so beautiful: roses, a king and queen under them, singing. I liked it better than San Giovanni.”

“But there’s nothing like that now,” Tito said gently. “They’re going to burn a bonfire in the Piazza—that’s all. And I can’t let you go out alone in the evening.”

“Oh no, no! I don’t want to go in the evening. Just to see the procession by daylight. There *will* be a procession, won’t there?”

“Yes, after a sort,” Tito said, “as lively as a flight of cranes. Don’t expect roses or glittering kings and queens, my Tessa. Still, any string of people might please your blue eyes. They’ve raised something in the Piazza de’ Signori for the bonfire—you may like to see that. But come home early, and look like a grave little old woman. And if you see men with feathers and swords, keep away from them—they’re fierce and like to cut old women’s heads off.”

“Santa Madonna! Where do they come from?” Tessa gasped. “Ah! You’re laughing. It’s not so bad. But I’ll stay clear. Only,” she whispered, pressing her lips close to Naldo’s ear, “if I might take Lillo with me! He’s very sensible.”

“But who will thump Monna Lisa then?” Tito asked, struggling not to laugh but keeping a stern expression. “No, Tessa—you couldn’t care for Lillo in a crowd, and he’s too heavy for you to carry.”

“It’s true,” Tessa said, sadly. “He likes to run away. I forgot. Then I’ll go alone. But now—look at Ninna! You haven’t looked at her enough.”

Ninna, a blue-eyed child at the age of tottering and falling, was a solid little creature—like a loaded die, always landing the same way. Tessa picked her up, and while Babbo admired her new teeth and other wonders, she whispered, “And shall I buy some confetti for the children?”

Tito pulled coins from his scarsella and placed them in her palm.

“That will buy no end,” Tessa said, delighted. “I won’t mind going without Lillo so much if I bring him something.”

So Tessa set out in the morning toward the great Piazza where the bonfire would be lit. The February breeze felt warm enough to her; she wore only her green wool dress. A mantle would have hidden her new silver necklace and clasp—gifts from Tito, the only ornaments he had ever given her. She wasn’t thinking of showing off her figure—no one had ever told her she was pretty—but she was certain these trinkets were finer than anything worn by the richest peasant woman. She arranged her white hood carefully so the front of the necklace would catch the light.

She walked cheerfully through the sunlit streets, her thoughts full of sweets for the children, unaware that anyone watched her. Yet her descent from her upper room had been observed. She was being followed by someone who had long hoped to see her—someone who had trailed Tito many times before. Baldassarre carried a bundle of yarn, earning his meager living while nursing a fire of vengeance. He had come out of his way again, drawn to the house where Tito had once led him. His imprisonment had deepened his fears and twisted his belief that some dark fate protected Tito. He dared not approach openly—only in crowds or darkness, lest Tito’s gaze fall upon him and drag him once more into disgrace, stripping him of his knife and casting him into prison. His rage had turned stealthy, like a serpent waiting for its one fatal strike. Justice was weak, and the voice that once thundered from the Duomo promising vengeance had fallen silent, drowned by cruelty and cunning.

For a time, Baldassarre’s only goal was to confirm whether Tito still wore the armor—now, even death would satisfy his thirst. But he would never risk his knife again. Waiting had worn him thin. The thought of winning one of Tito’s wives to aid him haunted him—then slipped away. The wife on the hill was gone. If he could find her again, perhaps he could weave a plan. This morning, he had succeeded. He now knew where she lived. As he walked, bent under his burden, he kept his eyes on the green-and-white figure ahead, his mind tracing her life like a blind man reading lines of light.

Tessa passed through long streets, seeing only groups of country folk in their best clothes, chatting and lingering—the usual signs of a holiday before the spectacle began. Then, disappointed in her search for marvels, she noticed a man with a pedlar’s basket selling nothing but small red crosses.

A cross would be lovely above her bed—protection, strength for Ninna. But four white quattrini might be too much. She crossed the street to ask the price, afraid of spending too much on sweets.

The pedlar had turned his back, but when she approached, she recognized Bratti Ferravecchi, a familiar face from the Mercato. Accustomed to avoiding old friends, she turned away.

But Bratti’s eye was sharp. He tapped her arm with a red cross.

“Young woman,” he said, “you come from some distant castello, else you wouldn’t walk about this blessed Carnival without a cross in hand. Santa Madonna! Four white quattrini is a small price for your soul—prices rise in purgatory.”

“Oh, I’d like one,” Tessa said quickly, “but I can’t spare four quattrini.”

Bratti, who had first seen her only as a customer, now exclaimed, “By the head of San Giovanni, it’s the little Tessa—looking as fresh as a ripe apple! Did you escape Nofri? You were right—he’s on crutches now, and a crabbed man with crutches is dangerous. He can reach across the house and beat a woman sitting.”

“I’m married,” Tessa said demurely, remembering Naldo’s warning to behave gravely. “My husband takes great care of me.”

“Ah! You’ve fallen on your feet! Nofri called you vermin—but what does that matter? An ass may bray long before he shakes the stars down. I always said you did well to run. And now you’ve got a husband and plenty of money?”

“I’ve never wanted for anything,” Tessa said, still cautious.

“Then you can afford a cross. I had a Padre bless them. You get blessing and all for four quattrini. Not for profit—I barely earn a danaro. But they’re holy wares, and the path to Paradise grows harder. Even the Carnival feels like Holy Week. The least you can do is buy a cross. God guard you—think of the Devil’s tooth! Have you seen him biting the man in San Giovanni?”

Tessa grew uneasy. “Oh, Bratti,” she said, distressed, “I want to buy confetti for Lillo and Ninna. Sweet things cost so much. And they won’t like the cross, though I know it’s good.”

“Come then,” Bratti said, fond of imagining extortions and then renouncing them. “Since you’re an old acquaintance, I’ll give it to you for two quattrini. A gift, with the blessing.”

Tessa reached for her coins, trembling, when Bratti stopped her. “Wait. Where do you live?”

“Oh, far off,” she answered, distracted. “Beyond San Ambrogio, in the Via Piccola, at the top of the house where the wood is stacked below.”

“Very good,” Bratti said patronizingly. “I’ll let you have it on trust. I’ll call later. You live inside the gates? Well, I’ll pass by.”

“No, no!” Tessa insisted, fearing Naldo’s anger. “I can pay now.”

“No,” Bratti said firmly. “I’m not a hard-hearted pedlar. I’ll come and see if you have rags to trade. Here’s your cross. And Pippo’s shop is just behind you—go fill your basket. _Addio, piccina._”

Bratti walked on. Tessa, eager to spend her money before trouble struck, entered Pippo’s shop, her heart fluttering. She had let Bratti know more than she should. There were dangers in leaving home—and she hadn’t noticed the man with the yarn bundle.

But the joy of having treats for the children lifted her spirits. As she stepped into the Via de’ Libraj, her face glowed with childlike contentment. Now, she thought, the procession must be coming—she saw white robes and a banner. Her heart quickened.

She stood aside, but in the narrow street, she had to look closely. The banner showed the Holy Mother with the Babe—Tessa had grown to believe in her love ever since her own babies were born. The figures wore green wreaths and small red crosses—much like her own. They looked like angels on clouds, and to Tessa, everything in life seemed wrapped in cloud. How they came, she didn’t care. But some carried baskets—what for?

Then they turned toward her. She trembled, as if Saint Michael had shaken his head at her. All she felt was terror—until she saw a round boyish face and heard a high voice:

“Sister, you carry the Anathema. Yield it to the blessed Gesu, and He will adorn you with His grace.”

Tessa panicked. Was it her basket? Oh dear! She looked down.

“No, sister,” said a taller youth, pointing to her necklace and belt clasp. “It is those vanities—the Anathema. Take them off. Let them burn in the Bonfire of Vanities, and save yourself from burning.”

“It is the truth,” said another, taller still, clearly the leader. “Listen to divine voices. You already have a red cross—let that be your only adornment. Give up your necklace and belt, and you shall obtain grace.”

Tessa could not say no. Tears welled up. Her lips quivered. A single drop fell. She saw nothing but horror.

Suddenly, a gentle hand touched her arm. A soft, angelic voice said, “Do not be afraid. No one shall harm you.”

Tessa looked up and saw a lady in black, with a young, serene face and kind hazel eyes. She had never seen anyone like her. Under other circumstances, she might have feared such beauty—but now, only safety mattered. The tears flowed faster as she looked up, clutching her necklace.

“I can’t give them to be burned,” she sobbed. “My husband bought them for me. They’re so pretty. And Ninna—oh, I wish I’d never come!”

“Do not ask her,” Romola said softly to the boys. “It serves no purpose to take things against will. Fra Girolamo would not approve. Such things should be given freely.”

The boys obeyed. They moved on quickly, as if drawn to something else. Tessa breathed deeply—safe. Her treasures were still hers.

“Oh, I’ll go back,” she said, still shaken. “I’ll go nowhere else. But if I meet them again, and you’re not here?”

“Stay a little,” Romola said. “Come under this doorway. We’ll hide your necklace and clasp. Then you’ll be safe.”

She led Tessa under an archway and said, “Can we fit them in your basket? Ah—your basket is full of crisp things. Be careful. Lay the necklace beneath.”

To Tessa, it felt like waking from a nightmare into a dream of peace. This lady—so lovely, so strong, so gentle—was protecting her. She let Romola unfasten her necklace and clasp, watching silently.

“These are sweets for Lillo and Ninna,” she whispered.

“Those are your children?” Romola smiled.

“Would you rather go home than see more of the Carnival? Or would you like to see the bonfire pile in the Piazza de’ Signori?”

“No, oh no!” Tessa said eagerly. “I’ll never like bonfires again. I’m going home.”

“You live at some castello, doubtless,” Romola said, not waiting for an answer. “Which gate do you go toward?”

“Por’ Santa Croce.”

“Then come,” Romola said, taking her hand and leading her to a nearby corner. “If you go down this street, you’ll soon be on a straight road. I must leave now—someone waits for me. You will not be frightened. Your things are safe. Addio.”

“Addio, Madonna,” Tessa whispered, not knowing what else to say. In a flash, the heavenly lady was gone.

Tessa turned to watch—but only saw the tall figure vanish around the stone corner. She walked on, lost in wonder, longing only to return to Monna Lisa and her children. Forever, she decided, she would avoid carnivals.

Baldassarre had watched Tessa until she parted with Romola. Then he left, his yarn bundle in hand. For the first time, he felt a clue—something tangible. He had seen the two wives together. The vision had sharpened his thoughts. The tall one—Romola—was noble, rightful. She had blood that could be stirred to wrath. She understood learning, and might grasp the truth locked within Tito’s silence, like treasure buried by earthquake. She could believe him. She would *want* to believe him—if he could prove Tito’s betrayal. Women cared about that. They would take vengeance. If Romola loved Tito, her pain would be a weapon—another will, another mind to shape plans.

Both wives had been kind to him, and their kindness lingered in his memory. But pain was not a check for him—it was the natural order. Only the hard and base escaped suffering. If anyone was innocent, if anyone was noble, where could their greatest joy lie? Not in happiness—but in hatred, in triumph over injustice.

Yet he must be cautious. He would watch this wife in the Via de’ Bardi. Learn more. For even here, failure was possible. His only power now was patience.
