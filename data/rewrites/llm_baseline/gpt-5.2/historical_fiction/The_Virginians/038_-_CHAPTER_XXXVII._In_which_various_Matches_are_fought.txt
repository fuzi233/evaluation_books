The London Advertiser lay beside Mr. Warrington’s breakfast like a second plate set for temptation. The coffee—dark as walnut shell and sharp with a faint tang of burnt sugar—sent up its steam in little banners; the rolls were hot enough to tear with the fingers; a dish of kidneys swam in gravy peppered to a Londoner’s liking. Gumbo had contrived, out of the inn’s indifferent butter and some shred of herb begged from the cook, to make the bread taste less like yesterday’s loaf and more like morning. The room, for all its snugness, could not keep out the city’s ceaseless undertone—wheels worrying the stones, hawkers calling as if their throats were brass instruments, and the thin quarrel of a distant fiddle sawing at nothing in particular.

Between mouthfuls, Harry read an invitation to all lovers of manly British sport to witness the trial of Sutton and Figg. It was printed boldly, with that London assurance which makes even bloodshed sound like a public benefit. By the time his cup was drained and the last salt of the kidneys lingered on his tongue, the decision was made; the page had done its work as thoroughly as any sermon.

He drove himself to Marybone Fields with the pair of horses he had bought only yesterday—spirited brutes, quick to take the bit, and needing a hand steadier than his impatience. He did not know the road. London was not like Virginia, where a lane kept its name by being the only lane for miles and where one might follow a creek’s voice or the smell of turned earth and come right at last. Here the streets tacked and veered as though the town had been built by men in a hurry, and then mended by other men who hated the first. He found himself behind Mr. Whitfield’s round Tabernacle on the Tottenham Road, where the preaching-house sat in its green as solemnly as a fat goose in a yard; he passed fields with a few cows cropping, and the Middlesex Hospital standing in the midst like a white warning, and more than once he had to pull up to ask a ragged boy, who answered as if language were a thing to be sold by the syllable.

At length the Wooden House showed its flag and its crowd. The place was a kind of shrine to an English appetite that asked for blows as other men asked for pudding. Around the doors surged London blackguards, their voices rising and falling like a kennel of hounds; with them were a few carriages of fashion, lacquer and crest glinting among the mud-splashed hacks. Beggars and cripples worked the edges of the press; shoeblack-boys flung themselves upon one another for the honour of his boots; nosegay-women offered damp bunches whose crushed leaves gave out a green, bruised scent; flying fruiterers darted through with apples and oranges, their baskets brushing coats and dripping juice. Piemen cried their wares, the pastry smelling of suet and pepper; ale steamed from open mugs; and every sound—horn, drum, shout, oath—seemed to argue with every other, as if the city could not bear silence for fear it might remember something.

A manager, accompanied by a drummer and a horn-blower, repeatedly issued upon the little stage out front to proclaim that the noble English sports were just about to begin. The horn’s brazen note made the ears ache; Harry, who had grown with the rustle of tobacco leaves and the low talk of slaves in the quarters, felt how a man’s spirit is pressed and shaped by the noises that surround him. Here the very air seemed battered.

He paid his money and was placed in a gallery where he could command the platform. Gumbo sat below in the amphitheatre until the crush and the heat offended him; then he would go out into the outer world, drink a pot of beer, and fall in with his brother-lacqueys and the coachmen perched on boxes outside. London, Harry observed, had a prodigious quantity of those liveried creatures. They were everywhere—at chapel-doors, in theatre galleries, on landings, swarming in anterooms—powdered, buckled, nosegayed, melancholy in their finery as if they were the last of a tribe. Their talk was of vails and cards and small tyrannies; their laughter was loud and empty. One could not help thinking of other peoples being pushed aside—how a tongue and a way of living can be made to look quaint, then useless, then vanish, leaving only a few specimens for the curious to stare at. In Virginia, Gumbo’s speech carried the cadence of home and captivity together—no Londoner could hear in it what Harry heard, and what could not be translated: the salt of tidewater, the lash remembered, the kitchen-fire at night. Here, voices were made to conform; the city’s lexicon ruled like a magistrate.

Before the principal combatants appeared, minor warriors exhibited themselves. A boxing-match came off; the men feinted and hugged and panted, but neither had much stomach for punishment. The crowd grumbled as if served stale bread. Then came cudgel-playing; a few heads were tapped, a few trickles of blood shown, but the wounds were so trifling that even the women selling fruit looked disappointed. Hisses rose. “The masters! the masters!” the people bawled, demanding the true dish.

At last Sutton strode up the steps, sword in hand, in his shirt with his head shaven clean. The scars on his scalp looked like old seams in leather. He saluted the company with his weapon, and made an especial bow to a private box where sat a stout gentleman, hands folded, expression as calm as if he were at table. Presently Figg followed—also bare to the waist, his head clean-shorn and marked by former fights. Each wore on his burly sword-arm an armiger of his colour, ribbons like cockades, the way soldiers wear signs to be known even when their faces are blown to pieces.

They shook hands. The crowd’s roar rolled up to the roof. Then the word was given, and the air on the platform seemed to harden.

Figg opened with a blow so tremendous that, had it met Sutton’s honest head, it would have lopped it off as neatly as the cook’s carving-knife takes the top from a carrot—Harry had just watched such a knife at breakfast, and the comparison came to him with a sick exactness. Sutton caught the blade on his own sword; Figg’s stroke was delivered so mightily that his weapon broke in his hands. There was a shout as when a dish is dropped in a kitchen and everyone turns at once.

Other swords were brought. The first blood drawn spouted from Figg’s side; Sutton’s supporters yelled as if they had won a campaign. But Figg turned, panting, and appealed to his audience—especially to the stout personage in the private gallery—showing that the broken sword in the previous encounter had caused the wound. There was parley, cries, gestures; and while the dispute buzzed, Harry saw a gentleman in a riding-frock and plain scratch-wig enter the stout man’s box. It was his acquaintance from Tunbridge Wells, my Lord March and Ruglen.

Lord March was no great dealer in politeness; yet here he showed singular deference. Harry watched him bend profoundly as the stout gentleman produced bank-bills from a pocket-book and handed them over. The exchange was as neat as a serving of coin with dessert—no talk, no flourish, only the quiet transfer of power. Then March’s eye caught Harry; his business finished, he crossed over and greeted the young Virginian with warmth that seemed almost good-humoured.

They sat together while the combat renewed, the swords clashing with a sound like cleavers on bone. Skill and valour were on both sides, and the crowd drank it in, shouting and stamping so that the whole building rang. When the swords had done their work, the gladiators took up the quarter-staff. The long staves whistled through the air; the blows landed with a dull, meal-sack thud that made Harry’s stomach turn, though his eyes would not look away. It ended, after a long and satisfying battle to the audience, with victory to Figg, ancient champion.

All the while a thunderstorm had gathered and broken over the Wooden House. Rain drummed on the roof, and the thunder rolled like cannon over the fields. For a moment, beneath that natural tumult, the human clamour seemed thin—mere shouting against the sky’s deeper voice. Harry breathed more freely, as if the storm’s great sound cleared the air of some of its foulness.

When the fight was done, Lord March offered him a seat in his chariot. Harry gladly accepted, leaving his own phaeton to be driven home by his groom. The rain made the streets smell of wet earth and horse, of chimney soot washed down, of raw wood and damp leather. Harry, warmed with wine-less excitement, declared the sight he had witnessed to be sport indeed—better than any he had seen since coming to England. As always, any pleasure brought with it the impulse to share it with the dear companion of his boyhood. He drew breath and began, “I wish—” and then stopped as though the words had bitten him.

“No, I don’t,” he said, and stared out at the streaming windows.

“What do you wish, and what don’t you wish?” asked March, lolling among his cushions, watching him with that lazy curiosity which can be sharper than attention.

“I was thinking of my elder brother,” Harry answered, the taste of iron and rain in his mouth. “I wished he had been with me. We promised to have our sport together at home. We talked of it many’s the time. But he wouldn’t have liked this rough sort. He didn’t care for fighting—though he was the bravest lad alive.”

“Oh, he was the bravest lad alive, was he?” March drawled, eyes narrowing with amusement.

Harry, carried on by feeling, spoke of a quarrel George had had with a very gallant officer—an absurd affair, but hard to keep George off him. “I never saw a fellow so cool,” Harry said, “nor more savage and determined, God help me. For the honour of the country, I wish he could have come here instead of me, and shown you a real Virginian gentleman.”

“Nay, sir, you’ll do very well,” said March. “What is this I hear of Lady Yarmouth taking you into favour?”

“I will do as well as another,” Harry replied, stubbornly, then softened. “I can ride, and I think I can shoot better than George. But my brother had the head, sir—the head!” He tapped his own skull with a rueful candour. “Why, upon my word, he had read almost every book that was ever written; could play both on the fiddle and harpsichord; could compose poetry and sermons most elegant. What can I do? I’m only good to ride and play at cards, and drink Burgundy.”

He hung his head, as if shamed by the smallness of his own list. Yet even that list was full of sensations—horseflesh under him, the click of cards, the dark velvet warmth of wine—and he could not help adding, with a sudden rally, “But them I can do as well as most fellows. In fact, my lord, I’ll back myself.”

March laughed, relishing the naivete as a jaded voluptuary still relishes a wholesome mutton-chop. “By Gad, Mr. Warrington, you ought to be taken to Exeter ’Change and put in a show.”

“And for why?”

“A gentleman from Virginia who has lost his elder brother and absolutely regrets him. The breed ain’t known in this country. Upon my honour and conscience, I believe you would like to have him back again.”

“Believe!” Harry cried, flushing, his throat tightening. The word felt like an insult because it questioned what, to him, had no need of proof.

“That is, you believe you believe you would like him back again,” March continued, untroubled, turning his gaze out to the new houses they passed. “But depend on it you wouldn’t. ’Tis not in human nature, sir; not as I read it, at least.” He pointed as they rolled by: Sir Richard Littleton’s at the corner, my Lord Bingley’s great house beyond; Cavendish Square fenced with unsightly boards, a waste of space; Montagu House turned into a confounded museum with books and stuffed birds and rhinoceroses; the cursed New Road cut behind Bedford House Gardens. His complaints about the town’s growth came like the rattle of a cook who hates new fashions in the kitchen—too much slicing and rearranging, not enough respect for the old stock that once made everything taste right.

“Shall we go down Tyburn Road and the Park,” he asked, “or through Swallow Street and into the habitable quarter? We can dine at Pall Mall, or, if you like, with you; and we can spend the evening as you like—with the Queen of Spades, or—”

“With the Queen of Spades, if your lordship pleases,” Harry said, blushing at the name that was half woman and half card-table.

So the chariot drew up at his hotel in Covent Garden. The landlord came forward with his usual obsequiousness; recognising my Lord March and Ruglen, he bowed his wig almost to my lord’s shoes, as though humility were an art he practised for tips. In the dining-room a dinner was ordered that London called the best: soups rich with long-simmered bones; fish with a sharp sauce; roast meat with its fat crisped; vegetables boiled into submission; and a pudding heavy enough to silence talk for a minute. Yet it was the wine that truly made March attentive—Burgundy of a quality to satisfy even an epicurean earl, its scent rising when poured like warmed cherries and old oak.

Over wine there was talk of fireworks at Vauxhall or else of cards. Harry, who had never seen a firework beyond a dozen squibs let off at Williamsburg on the fifth of November—an exhibition he had thought sublime—would have liked Vauxhall for the novelty: lights over water, the crackle and bloom of fire against the night. But he yielded to March’s preference for piquet. The cards were brought; the table cleared; the world narrowed to pasteboard, fingers, and calculation.

Harry began by winning, as usual, his luck coming briskly like a good wind. Then, within half an hour, it turned and favoured March, whose skill belonged to the clubs and whose face hardly altered whether he gained or lost. Mr. Draper, Harry’s man of business, came bowing into the room. Harry, in his way, always asked everybody to sit and drink and partake of his best. He would share a crust, a haunch, a jug of water, a bottle of Burgundy, with the same open-handedness; and there was in it a country welcome that had not yet withered in him under London’s smoke.

Draper did not care much for wine, but he delighted in great company. He protested he liked nothing better than to see piquet played by two consummate players and men of fashion. He sat down, undismayed by March’s sidelong scowls, and watched. The gentlemen said they played for shillings; yet they counted gains and losses in a manner too quiet for shillings to be the whole matter. Their voices dropped; their faces stayed grave; courtesy sat between them like a third player.

Then came the first sign of trouble: a scuffling without, a whispering, an outcry like a woman in tears. The door flew open and a female burst in, producing from March an explosion of language fit to sour a cask of wine.

“I wish your women would take some other time for coming, confound ’em,” he said, flinging his cards down.

“What—Mrs. Betty!” Harry started up. It was indeed Lady Maria’s maid; and behind her stood Gumbo, his fine countenance wet and shining with tears, his chest heaving as if he had run miles.

“What has happened?” Harry asked, the room suddenly tasting of cold ashes. “The Baroness is well?”

“Help! help, sir—your honour!” Betty cried, and dropped to her knees as though the floor were a church. “Help whom?”

A howl came from Gumbo—one long sound dragged out of him, half grief and half outrage, and in it Harry heard the helplessness that no gentleman’s English can properly name.

“Gumbo! you scoundrel!” Harry demanded, startled into anger by that sound. “Has anything happened between Mrs. Betty and you?”

Gumbo stepped back with a wounded dignity, laying his hand on his heart. “No, sir; nothing hab happened ’twix’ this lady and me.”

“It’s my mistress, sir,” Betty sobbed. “Help! help! here’s the letter she have wrote, sir! They have gone and took her, sir!”

“Is it only that old Molly Esmond?” March said, taking up his cards again as if misfortunes were flies to be brushed away. “She’s known to be over head and heels in debt. Dry your eyes in the next room, Mrs. Betty, and let me and Mr. Warrington go on with our game.”

“Help! help her!” Betty cried again, and the old name, Molly, sounded in her mouth not like a jest but like a prayer. “Oh, Mr. Harry! you won’t be a-going on with your cards, when my lady calls out to you to come and help her! Your honour used to come quick enough when my lady used to send me to fetch you at Castlewood!”

March swore at her to hold her tongue, but she would not cease weeping; and the noise of her grief, in that close room, drove the game from the table as surely as a gust snuffs a candle. Harry rose, went to the bell, and said, with a steadiness that surprised even himself, “My dear lord, the game must be over for to-night. My relative writes to me in great distress, and I am bound to go to her.”

“Curse her! Why couldn’t she wait till to-morrow?” March cried, testily, as if a woman in trouble were no more than a creditor tapping at the door of his amusement.

Harry ordered a postchaise instantly. His own horses, he said, would take him to Bromley. March, who could turn any circumstance into a wager as some men turn any scraps into broth, began instantly: “Bet you you don’t do it within the hour! Bet you you don’t do it within five quarters of an hour! Bet you four to one—or I’ll take your bet, which you please—that you’re not robbed on Blackheath! Bet you you are not at Tunbridge Wells before midnight!”

“Done,” Harry said, his face hot, his mind already on the road and Lady Maria’s peril. March, with careful relish, noted down the terms of the four wagers in his pocket-book, as though writing a recipe he meant to use again.

Lady Maria’s letter was thrust into Harry’s hand. The paper was crumpled, the ink uneven, the spelling that of a woman whose tongue and schooling were at war. He read:

“MY DEAR COUSIN—  
I am fell into a trapp, which I perceive the machinations of villians. I am a prisner. Betty will tell you all. Ah, my Henrico! come to the resque of your  
MOLLY.”

The words, with their misshapen certainty, struck him harder than any cudgel blow he had seen that day. “Trapp.” “Machinations.” “Prisner.” The very letters seemed caught and cramped, as if her language itself had been seized along with her person—an old woman’s voice forced to plead in the dominant tongue of lawyers and bailiffs, where every term carries a hook.

Within half an hour of receiving the missive, Mr. Warrington was in his postchaise, cloaked against the rain, galloping over Westminster Bridge to the road that would lead him to Bromley and beyond, to succour his kinswoman. Behind him London roared on—cards shuffled, ale poured, drums beat—heedless as ever; but ahead, through the wet dark, the wheels hummed their urgent song on the road, and the horses’ breathing made the only music he could bear.
