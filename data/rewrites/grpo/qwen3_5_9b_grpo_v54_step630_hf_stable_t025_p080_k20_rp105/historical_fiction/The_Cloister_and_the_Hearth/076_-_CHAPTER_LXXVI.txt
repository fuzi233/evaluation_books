When little Gerard was barely three months old, a messenger arrived, breathless and hot from Tergou, bearing news for Catherine.

"Go back," she commanded sharply, "and tell them I cannot come, and I will not. They have already taken Kate." With that, the messenger departed, and Catherine resumed her sentence. "There, child, I must go. They are in a state of complete confusion; this is the third time they have asked. Tomorrow, my man would come himself and drag me home by the ear, with a flea in his ear." She then recounted her experiences with infants, instructing Margaret on how to handle every conceivable emergency, and pressed money upon her. Margaret declined with gratitude, but Catherine insisted, her tone turning angry. Margaret offered excuses so logically sound that Catherine dismissed them with cold contempt; to her mind, they lacked the necessary feminine spirit.

"Come, out with your heart," Catherine said, "as we part. Perhaps we shall never see one another's face again."

"Oh! Mother, do not say that."

"Alas, girl, I have seen it so often. It comes to my mind now at every parting. When I was your age, I never had such thoughts. Nay, we all believed we would live forever then. So, get it out."

"Well, then, Mother—I would rather not have told you—Cornelis must say to me, 'So you have come to share with us, eh, Mistress?' Those were his words. I told him I would be very sorry."

"Beshrew his ill tongue! What does it signify? He will never know."

"Most likely he would sooner or later. But whether or not, I will take no begrudging bounty from any family; unless I saw my child starving, and—Heaven only knows what I might do. Nay, Mother, give me but your love. I prize that above silver, and they do not grudge me that, by all I can find—for not a single stiver of money will I take from your house."

"You are a foolish lass. Were it me, I'd take it just to spite him."

"No, you would not. You and I are apples from the same tree."

Catherine yielded with good grace. When the actual moment of parting arrived, embraces and tears burst forth on both sides.

Once she was gone, the child cried profusely. All attempts to pacify him failing, Margaret suspected a pin and searched between his clothes and his skin, finding a gold angel uncomfortably lodged against his backbone.

"There, now, Gerard," she said to the babe. "I thought Granny gave in rather suddenly."

She took the coin, wrapped it in a piece of linen, and laid it at the bottom of her box, bidding the infant observe that she could be as resolute as Granny herself.

Catherine later told Eli of Margaret's foolish pride and how she had baffled it. Eli replied that Margaret was right and Catherine was wrong. Catherine tossed her head in disagreement; Eli pondered the matter in silence.

Margaret was not without domestic anxieties. She still had two men to feed and could not work as hard as she had done. She had enough to do to keep the house, care for the child, and cook for them all. Yet she had a little money saved, and she used to tell her child that his father would be home to help them before it was spent. With these bright hopes and that treasury of bliss, her son, she spent some happy months.

Time wore on; no Gerard came. Stranger still, there was no news of him.

Then her mind grew disquieted. Contrary to her practical nature, she was often lost in sad reverie, sighing in silence. While her heart was troubled, her money melted away. It came to pass that one day she found the cupboard empty and looked into her dependents' faces. At the sight of them, her bosom filled with pity. She appealed to the baby whether she could let Grandfather and poor old Martin go hungry, then went to retrieve Catherine's angel. As she unfolded the linen, a tear of gentle mortification fell upon it. She sent Martin out to exchange it. While he was gone, a Frenchman arrived with one of the dealers in illuminated works, who had previously offered her a very poor price. He told her he was employed by his sovereign to collect masterpieces for her Book of Hours. She showed him her two finest pieces, and he was charmed by one of them—the flowers, raspberries, and creeping things shaded by Margaret Van Eyck. He offered an unheard-of price.

"Nay, flout not my need, good stranger," she said. "There are three mouths in this house, and none to fill them but me."

Curious arithmetic! Number one was left out.

"I would outdo thee not, fair mistress. My princess charged me strictly: 'Seek the best craftsmen.' But I make no hard bargains; make them content with me, and me with them."

In the next minute, Margaret was on her knees, kissing little Gerard in the cradle and showering four gold pieces upon him again and again, relating the whole occurrence to him in very broken Dutch.

"And oh, what a good princess! Wasn't she? We will pray for her, won't we, my lambkin, when we are old enough?"

Martin returned, furious. "They will not change it. I trow they think I stole it."

"I am beholden to thee," Margaret said hastily, almost snatching the coin from Martin, wrapping it up again, and restoring it to its hiding place.

Before these unexpected funds were spent, she returned to her ironing and starching. In the midst of this work, Martin sickened and died after an illness of nine days.

Nearly all her money went to bury him decently.

He was gone, leaving an empty chair by her fireside. For he had preferred the hearth to the sun as soon as the Busy Body had left. Margaret would not allow anyone to sit in this chair now. Yet whenever she let her eye dwell too long on it vacant, it was sure to cost her a tear.

Now there was no one to carry her linen home. To do it herself, she must leave little Gerard in the charge of a neighbor, but she dared not trust such a treasure to mortal hands; besides, she could not bear him out of her sight for hours on end. So she set inquiries on foot for a boy to carry her basket on Saturdays and Mondays.

A plump, fresh-colored youth named Luke Peterson, who looked fifteen but was actually eighteen, came in. Blushing and twiddling his bonnet, he asked if a man would not serve her turn as well as a boy.

Before he spoke, she was saying to herself, "This boy will just do."

But she took the cue and said, "Nay; but a man may seek more than I can well pay."

"Not I," said Luke warmly. "Why, Mistress Margaret, I am your neighbor, and I do very well at coopering. I can carry your basket for you before or after my day's work, and welcome. You have no need to pay me anything. 'Tisn't as if we were strangers, ye know."

"Why, Master Luke, I know your face, for that matter; but I cannot call to mind that ever a word passed between us."

"Oh yes, you did, Mistress Margaret. What, have you forgotten? One day you were trying to carry your baby and your pitcher full of water. And I said, 'Give me the baby to carry.' 'Nay,' says you, 'I'll give you the pitcher, and keep the bairn myself.' And I carried the pitcher home, and you took it from me at this door, and you said to me, 'I am muckle obliged to you, young man,' with such a sweet voice—not like the folk in this street speak to a body."

"I do mind now, Master Luke; and methinks it was the least I could say."

"Well, Mistress Margaret, if you will say as much every time I carry your basket, I care not how often I bear it, nor how far."

"Nay, nay," said Margaret, coloring faintly. "I would not put upon good nature. You are young, Master Luke, and kindly. Say I give you your supper on Saturday night, when you bring the linen home, and your dawn-meal on Monday; would that make us anyways even?"

"As you please; only say not I sought a couple of diets for such a trifle as yon."

With chubby-faced Luke's timely assistance and the health and strength Heaven gave this poor young woman to balance her many ills, the house went pretty smoothly for a while. But her heart became more and more troubled by Gerard's long, and now most mysterious, silence.

And then that mental torturer, Suspense, began to tear her heavy heart with his hot pincers, until she cried often and vehemently, "Oh, that I could know the worst."

While she was in this state, one day she heard a heavy step mounting the stairs. She started and trembled. "That is no step that I know. Ill tidings?"

The door opened, and an unexpected visitor, Eli, came in, looking grave and kind.

Margaret eyed him in silence, with increasing agitation.

"Girl," he said, "the skipper is come back."

"One word," gasped Margaret. "Is he alive?"

"Surely I hope so. No one has seen him dead."

"Then they must have seen him alive."

"No, girl; neither dead nor alive hath he been seen these many months in Rome. My daughter Kate thinks he has gone to some other city. She bade me tell you her thought."

"Ay, like enough," said Margaret gloomily. "Like enough. My poor babe!"

The old man, in a faintish voice, asked her for a morsel to eat; he had come fasting.

The poor thing pitied him with the surface of her agitated mind and cooked a meal for him, trembling, scarce knowing what she was doing.

Ere he went, he laid his hand upon her head and said, "Be he alive, or be he dead, I look on thee as my daughter. Can I do nought for thee this day? Bethink thee now?"

"Ay, old man. Pray for him; and for me!"

Eli sighed and went sadly and heavily down the stairs.

She listened half stupidly to his retiring footsteps until they ceased. Then she sank moaning down by the cradle and drew little Gerard tight to her bosom. "Oh, my poor fatherless boy; my fatherless boy!"
