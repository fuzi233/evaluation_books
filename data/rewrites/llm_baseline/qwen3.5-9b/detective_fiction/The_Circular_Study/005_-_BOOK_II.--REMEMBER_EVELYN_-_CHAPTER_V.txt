Such an ordeal could not fail to sharpen Mr. Gryce's resolve to unravel the mysteries of the case. Upon returning to Headquarters—where his prolonged absence had undoubtedly sparked anxiety among those unaware of his mission—his first inquiry concerned the butler, Bartow. Had the man recovered his faculties during the night? The reply was disheartening. Bartow remained unchanged, and the specialist consulted regarding his mental state offered a grim prognosis for immediate recovery.

Mr. Gryce grew somber. Summoning the officer who had overseen the arrest, he asked how the mute servant had reacted to his confinement. The officer's response was brief but telling. "Surprised, sir," he reported. "Shook his head, made peculiar gestures, then resumed his pantomime. It's quite a spectacle, sir. Poor fellow, he keeps holding his right hand back as if trying to signal something to free him from custody."

Gryce noted the gesture; it mirrored the one Bartow had made upon realizing he was being observed. The meaning remained obscure. Since there was no indication the mute was left-handed, the repeated motion suggested an attempt to highlight a specific fact that might justify his release.

"Does he brood? Does he look fearful or angry?" Gryce asked.

"It fluctuates, sir. One moment he appears ready to drop off to sleep; the next, he erupts in fury, shaking his head and battering the walls. It's not a pleasant sight. He requires constant vigilance."

"Leave him be, but monitor every shift in his behavior. While his testimony may lack validity, there is significance in every movement he makes. Tomorrow, I shall visit him personally."

As the officer departed, Gryce sat in silence, contemplating the situation. He retrieved a small packet from his pocket and emptied five tiny spangles onto his desk. He studied them intently. It was a habit of his to fixate on small, seemingly trivial objects to sharpen his focus. After a moment of doubtful scrutiny, he swept the sequins back into the envelope, glanced at his watch, and moved swiftly into the outer office, pausing before a queue of waiting men.

Beckoning to a young man who had tracked his movements with keen interest—a trait not lost on the veteran observer of human nature—Gryce led him back to his private room.

"You wish to assist in this matter?" Gryce asked, his tone interrogative once the door clicked shut.

"Oh, sir—" the young man began, his enthusiasm lighting up his plain features. "I do not presume..."

"Enough!" Gryce interrupted. "You have spent six months here with no chance to demonstrate exceptional capability. Today, I intend to test your skills with something truly difficult. Can you rise to the challenge, Sweetwater? Do you know this city well enough to find a needle in this vast haystack?"

"I would dearly like to try," Sweetwater replied eagerly. "Success here would be a greater feat than living in New York itself. I have been longing for such an opportunity. Let me assure you, I will approach this judiciously, partly out of gratitude."

"We shall see," the old detective murmured. "If you crave difficulty, you will have plenty. In fact, I am asking for the impossible. We are looking for a woman. We know only that she wore a dress heavily encrusted with black spangles and carried a parasol during her visit to Mr. Adams, prior to the murder. I can provide you with a glimpse of that parasol before you depart. She vanished from somewhere unknown to us, and finding her is your objective. You are not alone in this pursuit; the task is nearly hopeless unless she identifies herself. I would despair entirely were it not for one crucial detail I will now share as my sole confidential lead."

Gryce leaned forward. "Just before disappearing from the single watcher's gaze, she approached the curbstone outside Hudson's Fruit Store on Fourteenth Street and raised her right hand exactly like this." He mimicked the gesture. "It is a slender clue, but it is all I possess aside from these five spangles recovered from her dress and my firm belief that she does not belong to the class of women frequenting police precincts. However, do not let this assumption constrain you. Preconceptions are often obstacles rather than aids."

Sweetwater, who might otherwise have been tempted by siren songs, listened with rapt delight. With a frank smile, he declared, "I'll do my best, sir. But please, do not show me the parasol; merely describe it. I dislike having colleagues mock me if I fail; I prefer to work quietly without raising false hopes."

"One of those delicate, frivolous affairs made of gray chiffon," Gryce explained. "Pearl handle, bows of pink ribbon. I suspect it was never used before. Given the value women place on such trifles, it could only have been abandoned under extreme duress or terror. There was no name on it."

"Not even the maker's mark?"

Gryce anticipated the question and was relieved by the negative answer. "No; that would have solved the puzzle too easily."

"And the time she was seen at Hudson's?"

"Four-thirty—the precise moment the warning telegram arrived."

"Understood, sir. This is the toughest assignment I've ever faced, but I accept it. When shall I report back?"

"When you have news. Ah, pause. My suspicion is that her first name is Evelyn. But remember, it is merely a hunch."

"As you wish, sir," Sweetwater said confidently, before vanishing.

Gryce, however, showed no trace of Sweetwater's optimism. The fog surrounding the case remained thick. Unlike the eager youth, he was not twenty-three years old with only victories behind him. His next hope rested on the press coverage of the crime, which was already spreading nationwide. A man of Mr. Adams's wealth and refinement surely had acquaintances who would surface upon hearing of his sudden death, especially since his assets were substantial and widely known. Yet, contrary to expectation, the day yielded no claimants to the fortune and no friends willing to lift the curtain on the stranger's life.

His banker and lawyer appeared briefly, confirming that Mr. Adams's finances were sound and that he had no known relatives. Even the landlord contributed little. He recalled meeting Mr. Adams through a deceased Philadelphia attorney who vouched for his client's respectability. The landlord remembered the tenant's ascetic appearance, his reserved demeanor, and his quiet intellect. Despite paying rent punctually, Mr. Adams had discouraged social interaction, responding to advances only with cold politeness. The landlord viewed him as a recline scholar, absorbed in studies aided solely by his deaf-mute servant. When questioned about the servant, he admitted knowing nothing of his character or loyalty, having seen him only twice. Thus, that avenue was closed.

Upon touring the study where Mr. Adams had died, Gryce examined the massive steel plate blocking the entrance and the high windows fitted with lath and plaster, reducing daylight to mere streaks. The original windows had extended to the wainscoting, but the modifications served no obvious purpose. The peculiar lamp, casting shifting shadows, added to the mystery. Most notably, the starling—the talkative bird—had been removed to headquarters for Gryce's inspection.

Further inquiries proved futile. The pension office and local G.A.R. posts offered no leads regarding the insignia found on the wall, nor did anyone recognize the artist who painted the large portrait dominating the study. Without identifying the painter, tracing Mr. Adams's background remained elusive. Every drawer and container had been searched; no will or business papers existed. It seemed the man had deliberately erased his identity, or perhaps transcended interest in the world beyond his self-imposed prison.

Late in the afternoon, reports trickled in from merchants. They unanimously described Mr. Adams and his servant as hermits who denied themselves nothing else. The master never ventured out; the servant conducted all purchases using expressive gestures that were surprisingly articulate. He demanded the best quality, refusing to tolerate deceit. While his sanity was unquestioned until the tragedy, several witnesses noted a recent change: a subdued agitation, irritability, and a malevolent glint in his eyes that suggested a grievance he desperately wished to vent. These observations, however, lacked the legal weight required for prosecution.

Just as Gryce prepared to concede defeat for the day, Sweetwater burst back into the office, his countenance revitalizing the atmosphere.

"What!" Gryce exclaimed. "You haven't found her?"

Sweetwater smiled. "Do not ask me yet, sir. I have returned to borrow the parasol for an hour. I need to conduct a specific test."

"What test? Explain!"

"Please, sir, I have little time before respectable businesses close. The test must occur in a proper establishment."

"Then you have permission. Wait here; I will fetch the parasol. Return it promptly, boy. I no longer possess the patience I once had."

"An hour, sir. Give me an hour, and then—"

The slamming of the door cut off his sentence. What followed was a torturous hour for Gryce, mercifully ended by Sweetwater's frantic return. The young man clutched the parasol.

"My experiment failed," Sweetwater admitted, "yet the parasol brought me luck nonetheless. I have located the lady, sir, and—" He paused for breath. "She is exactly as I predicted: a respectable citizen in a respectable home. Not young, but—"

"Her name, boy. Is it Evelyn?"
