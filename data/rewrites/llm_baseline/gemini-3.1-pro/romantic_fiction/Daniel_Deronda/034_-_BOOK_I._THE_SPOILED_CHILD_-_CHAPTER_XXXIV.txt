When Deronda arrived at five o’clock, the harsh, metallic din of London’s industrial streets—the relentless grinding of iron wheels and the sterile, commercial clamor of the pavement—was abruptly severed. The Christian servant closed the shop door behind him, sealing away the noise pollution of the modern era. As she ushered him into the room behind the shop, Deronda stepped into an acoustic and olfactory sanctuary, surprised by the profound, mythic warmth that suddenly enveloped him. 

The old house extended deeply at the back, its heavy walls absorbing the city’s chaotic frequencies. What might have been a gloomy cavern by daylight was now a vibrant, resonant space, illuminated by the gentle, rhythmic hiss of a fine brass lamp with seven oil-lights. The lamps cast a Venetian glow over a snow-white cloth spread upon the central table. The smoky ceiling and dark surroundings threw the human figures into striking relief, revealing a multi-generational tableau that seemed to exist outside the linear rush of time. 

The grandmother, arrayed in yellowish brown with a heavy gold chain resting where a necklace might sit, possessed the quiet dignity of a traditional agrarian elder. Her yellowed face, framed by a roll of gray hair and heavily marked eyebrows, was a living archive of cultural memory, commanding the room with picturesque authority. Young Mrs. Cohen stood clad in red and black, a string of large artificial pearls wound repeatedly around her neck, while the baby slept beneath a scarlet counterpane in its cradle. Adelaide Rebekah shone in braided amber, and Jacob Alexander strutted in black velveteen and scarlet stockings. As four pairs of black eyes glistened a welcome, Deronda felt a sudden shame for the supercilious distaste these happy, vibrant creatures had stirred in him under the harsh light of day. 

The hospitality they offered was deeply cordial. Both mother and grandmother seemed to expand in dignity upon their private hearth, meticulously presiding over the domestic ritual. Deronda’s eyes wandered to the old furniture—the oaken bureau and high side-table—recognizing them as relics of chance and economy rather than deliberate aesthetic curation. Yet, set upon the side-table was a large dish of blue and yellow ware, flanking two ancient silver vessels. Before them lay a heavy volume in darkened vellum with a deep-ribbed back, a silent testament to a lineage of preserved texts. In the corner, an open door spilled light from an inner room.

Deronda absorbed these details through parenthetic glances while attending to Jacob’s pressing, tactile solicitude regarding the knife. He had sourced precisely what the boy demanded: a piece with a white handle and the requisite hook. 

“Is that the sort of thing you want, Jacob?” Deronda asked, producing it. 

The boy subjected the tool to a severe, sensory scrutiny. The metallic *snick* of the blades and hook opening cut sharply through the room’s ambient hum. Jacob drew forth his own cork-screw knife to compare the weights and mechanics.

“Why do you like a hook better than a cork-screw?” Deronda asked.

“’Caush I can get hold of things with a hook. A cork-screw won’t go into anything but corks. But it’s better for you, you can draw corks.”

“You agree to change, then?” Deronda noted the grandmother listening to the negotiation with visible, silent delight. 

“What else have you got in your pockets?” Jacob asked with deliberative seriousness. 

“Hush, hush, Jacob, love,” the grandmother murmured softly.

Mindful of the room's gentle discipline, Deronda answered, “I think I must not tell you that. Our business was with the knives.” 

Jacob scanned his face, his dark eyes calculating, before he gravely handed over the cork-screw knife. “I’ll shwop.” Deronda pocketed it with equal gravity. Immediately, the small son of Shem darted into the illuminated inner room, his rapid, high-pitched chatter blending with a deeper voice within. 

When Mr. Cohen entered, the boy ran back, seizing a velveteen hat from a chair and placing it on his head before approaching his father. Cohen, keeping his own hat on, bypassed the visitor entirely. He stood perfectly still as his two children rushed forward to clasp his knees. Laying his hands upon each child’s head in turn, Cohen uttered his Hebrew benediction. The ancient syllables—guttural, breath-heavy, and resonant—vibrated through the room, a sudden, mythic acoustic shift that defied the superficial English lexicon of the pawnshop out front. The wife then lifted the sleeping baby from its scarlet bedding and held it beneath her husband’s outstretched hands to receive the blessing. For that suspended moment, Deronda realized this pawnbroker was not utterly prosaic; his domestic routines were inextricably woven with the profound, circular pacing of ancestral time.

“Well, sir, you found your welcome in my family, I think,” said Cohen, putting down his hat and instantly snapping back into the brisk, assimilative cadence of his commercial self. “And you’ve been punctual. Nothing like a little stress here,” he added, tapping his side pocket as he sat. “It’s good for us all in our turn. I’ve felt it when I’ve had to make up payments. I began to fit every sort of box. It’s bracing to the mind. Now then! let us see, let us see.”

“That is the ring I spoke of,” Deronda said, withdrawing it. “I believe it cost a hundred pounds. It will be a sufficient pledge to you for fifty, I think. I shall probably redeem it in a month or so.”

Cohen’s glistening eyes pinched closer together. He met the ingenuous look of this crude young gentleman who seemed to naively believe that redemption was a matter of satisfaction to a pawnbroker. He examined the ring, felt its weight, and returned it with calculated indifference. “Good, good. We’ll talk of it after our meal. Perhaps you’ll join us, if you’ve no objection. Me and my wife’ll feel honored, and so will mother; won’t you, mother?”

The invitation was doubly echoed by the women, and Deronda gladly accepted. The family gathered around the table. At present, the only visible food was a large dish concealed beneath a linen napkin, while Mrs. Cohen placed a china bowl near her husband for the washing of hands. But after donning his hat once more, Cohen paused and projected his voice toward the inner room: “Mordecai!”

Deronda wondered if this invocation was a further step in the religious ceremony, unfamiliar as he was with the ancient heroes of the faith. But a quiet “Yes” echoed from the back, and looking toward the open door, Deronda was astonished to see the enigmatic Jew he had encountered that morning in the book-shop. 

Their eyes met. The surprise was entirely mutual, yet neither made the slightest gesture of recognition. As Mordecai took his seat at the far end of the table, he offered Deronda only a cold, distant tilt of his head, as though the bitter disappointment of their morning exchange lingered like a sour note in the room’s otherwise harmonious chord.

Cohen washed his hands, pronouncing Hebrew words whose rhythmic cadence washed over the table. He then drew back the napkin. Beneath it lay two long, flat loaves of challah, their egg-washed crusts baked to a deep mahogany and thickly besprinkled with ancient seeds—a tangible, gustatory memorial of the manna that once fed their wandering forefathers. Cohen broke the bread, the crust shattering with an audible crunch that released a cloud of warm, yeasty steam laced with the rich, earthy tang of long-fermented wheat. He distributed small morsels to the family. Adelaide Rebekah stood upon her chair, her amber garment pooling around her, stretching her little Jewish nose in a solemn effort to compress her lips and appear suitable for the blessing. 

After another Hebrew benediction, the male heads were uncovered, and the meal commenced. Though Deronda was fiercely preoccupied with a desire to steer the conversation toward a leading question, and though he and Mordecai frequently exchanged fascinated, half-furtive glances, his palate could not ignore the meticulous culinary history laid before them. The centerpiece was a platter of fried fish, a masterclass in ancestral preservation and technique. The grandmother’s quiet labor was evident in the firm, translucent flakes of the whitefish, enveloped in a perfectly brittle, golden-hued crust that carried the elemental tang of cold-pressed oil and the faint brine of distant seas. It was a dish that transcended utilitarian fuel, offering profound meaning through sustenance. Deronda noted with interest that the thin, crisped tails of the fish were explicitly portioned to Mordecai—a silent, prehistoric practice of assigning a specific share to the poor relation, an ancient courtesy surviving quietly amidst modern London.

Mordecai possessed no handsome Sabbath garment. Instead of the rusty black coat from the morning, he wore a light drab paletot, shrunk from rigorous washing. The faded cloth deeply accentuated his eager, dark-haired face, lending him the visceral, unpolished air of the prophet Ezekiel—a man entirely out of step with the modish aesthetics of contemporary commerce. 

Mr. Cohen, meanwhile, dominated the sonic environment with lively chatter, steering the subjects toward safe, assimilative tastes. He boasted loudly of the Queen, the Royal Family, and the Emperor and Empress of the French, a conversation into which both the wife and the grandmother eagerly entered. Young Mrs. Cohen vivaciously demonstrated an accurate memory for distinguished birthdays, while the elder Mrs. Cohen aided her son in detailing the royal visit to the city ten years prior. 

“I dare say you know all about it better than we do, sir,” Cohen repeated as a preface to his own full information, the trio maintaining a persistent, boisterous soundscape.

“Our baby is named _Eu_genie Esther,” young Mrs. Cohen declared brightly.

“It’s wonderful how the Emperor’s like a cousin of mine in the face,” the grandmother added, her voice carrying the warm, agrarian resonance of her history. “It struck me like lightning when I caught sight of him. I couldn’t have thought it.”

“Mother and me went to see the Emperor and Empress at the Crystal Palace,” said Mr. Cohen, his dominant English lexicon striving for worldly relevance. “I had a fine piece of work to take care of, mother; she might have been squeezed flat—though she was pretty near as lusty then as she is now. I said if I had a hundred mothers I’d never take one of ’em to see the Emperor and Empress at the Crystal Palace again; and you may think a man can’t afford it when he’s got but one mother—not if he’d ever so big an insurance on her.” He stroked his mother’s shoulder affectionately, chuckling at his own humor.

“Your mother has been a widow a long while, perhaps,” Deronda interjected, seizing the acoustic space. “That has made your care for her the more needful.”

“Ay, ay, it’s a good many _yore-zeit_ since I had to manage for her and myself,” Cohen replied quickly. The sudden intrusion of the Yiddish term—a fragment of a fading native dialect—slipped through his polished veneer, a brief exposure of cultural memory. “I went early to it. It’s that makes you a sharp knife.”

“What does—what makes a sharp knife, father?” asked Jacob, his cheek bulging heavily with a piece of sweet-cake. The rich scent of toasted almonds and honey spiced with clove hovered around the boy. 

The father winked at Deronda. “Having your nose put on the grindstone.”

Jacob slipped from his chair, his spiced cake in hand, and marched to the end of the table where Mordecai had sat in total silence. “What does that mean—putting my nose to the grindstone?”

Mordecai turned his benign eyes upon the small face. His voice, when he finally spoke, possessed a deep, resonant acoustic clarity. “It means that you are to bear being hurt without making a noise.”

Jacob, in an act of sudden, tactile generosity, pressed the corner of his sweet-cake against Mordecai’s mouth as an invitation to bite, stating, “I shan’t though,” while keeping his eyes locked on the pastry to measure precisely how much was taken. Mordecai took a gentle bite, the shared sustenance bringing a soft smile to his emaciated face. The simple, sensory exchange made them both instantly more lovable.

Deronda, however, felt a pang of vexation that his initial probe had yielded little. “I fancy that is the right quarter for learning,” he said, carrying the thread forward to excuse his turning directly to the scholar. “You have been a great student, I imagine?”

“I have studied,” came the quiet, measured answer. 

“And you?—You know German by the book you were buying.”

“Yes, I have studied in Germany. Are you generally engaged in bookselling?” Deronda asked. 

“No; I only go to Mr. Ram’s shop every day to keep it while he goes to meals,” Mordecai replied. He was looking at Deronda now with a revival of his intense morning interest. It was as if some latent, unspoken vibration in Deronda’s face neutralized the earlier disappointment. After a heavy, pregnant pause, Mordecai asked, “Perhaps you know Hebrew?”

“I am sorry to say, not at all.”

The tragedy of language death fell over the table. Mordecai’s countenance plummeted. He cast down his eyelids, staring at his thin hands crossed before him, and retreated into silence. The profound untranslatability of his subjective experience hung in the air; without the native dialect, the ancient bridge between them was utterly severed. In the quiet that followed, Deronda sharply noticed the subtle, wet rasp in Mordecai’s breathing—a delicate sonic rhythm indicative of consumption.

“I’ve had something else to do than to get book-learning,” Mr. Cohen interrupted, his voice intentionally breaking the fragile silence. “I’ve had to make myself knowing about useful things. I know stones well.” He pointed to Deronda’s ring. “I’m not afraid of taking that ring of yours at my own valuation. But now,” he added, his tone dropping into a lower, familiar nasal drawl—the subtle power dynamic of the dominant commercial lexicon asserting itself—“what do you want for it?”

“Fifty or sixty pounds,” Deronda answered, a fraction too carelessly.

Cohen paused, thrust his hands into his pockets, and fixed Deronda with glistening eyes that suggested a miraculous guinea-pig. “Couldn’t do you that. Happy to oblige, but couldn’t go that lengths. Forty pound—say forty—I’ll let you have forty on it.”

Deronda, highly attuned to the room, saw Mordecai look up at the mention of the monetary affair, examining him anew. “Very well,” Deronda said softly. “I shall redeem it in a month or so.”

“Good. I’ll make you out the ticket by-and-by,” Cohen replied indifferently, holding up a finger to signal that worldly conversation must now be suspended. 

He, Mordecai, and Jacob donned their hats once more. Cohen opened a thanksgiving, carried out at first in patterned responses, until Mordecai took the lead. Delivering himself entirely to the ritual, Mordecai chanted at length. His chin was slightly uplifted, his thin hands clasped easily before him. The solemn, undulating tone of his chant was a masterpiece of acoustic ecology—a pure, ancient vibration that resonated against the smoky walls, entirely free from the self-conscious, performative commerciality of the outer world. It was a staggering juxtaposition: here, amidst common, prosperous shopkeeping types, sat a man in an emaciated, threadbare condition whose voice imposed a profound, mythic awe upon Deronda.

No sooner had the devotional strain concluded than Mordecai rose, offered a slight bend of his head to the stranger, walked back into his room, and shut the door. 

“That seems to be rather a remarkable man,” Deronda murmured to Cohen. 

The pawnbroker immediately hiked his shoulders, pushed his tongue slightly out, and tapped his own brow, making it explicitly clear that Mordecai did not meet the standard of sanity prescribed by modern industry. 

“Does he belong to your family?” Deronda pressed.

The idea struck both the ladies and Cohen as intensely ludicrous, and a ripple of amusement disrupted the lingering acoustics of the chant. 

“No, no,” Cohen said. “Charity! charity! he worked for me, and when he got weaker and weaker I took him in. He’s an incumbrance; but he brings a blessing down, and he teaches the boy. Besides, he does the repairing at the watches and jewelry.”

Deronda barely suppressed a smile at this intricate knot of authentic kindliness bound tightly with utilitarian calculation. Yet his desire to unearth more of Mordecai’s history—made only more enigmatically striking by these revelations—was baffled. Cohen decisively dismissed the subject, reverting to the commercial accommodation. He wrote out the ticket, counted forty pounds, and presented both in exchange for the diamond ring. 

Sensing that it would be indelicate to protract his stay beyond the settlement of the pretext that brought him here, Deronda took his leave. He stepped back out into the cold, abrasive noise of London, his breast-pocket holding the pawn-ticket and the forty pounds. As he walked, his mind was already weaving the timeline of his return. He resolved that after Christmas, he would journey back to this acoustic sanctuary to unearth the hidden history of the emaciated scholar, and perhaps to gently peel back the silence surrounding the elder Mrs. Cohen, discovering why it was forbidden to ask the agrarian matriarch whether she had a daughter.
