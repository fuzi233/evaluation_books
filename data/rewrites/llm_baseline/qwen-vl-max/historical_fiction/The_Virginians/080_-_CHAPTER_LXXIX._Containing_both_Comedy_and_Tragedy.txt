We, who had played our parts in the morning’s secret drama, felt a deep and bitter guilt when we saw how deeply it had wounded the man we loved and revered most. The blow we had dealt—though not meant in malice—had struck him with a force that left us trembling with remorse. His beloved daughter had deceived him; his entire family had turned against him in silent conspiracy. Oh, dear friend and father! We know now—though then we could not see—that you forgave us, even as you stood among the pure spirits of heaven, where love and mercy are the only laws. For you, forgiveness was natural, as easy as breathing. To wound you was to strike at innocence itself, to lash a child or harm one who nurtured others. And so, after the deed was done, we all knelt in shame before the man we had wronged.

I will not linger on the scenes of reconciliation, of prayer together, of quiet farewell as he departed for his government, the ship sailing away beneath a sky still heavy with sorrow. We stood upon the shore, hand in hand, silent, abashed, guilty beyond words. My wife did not come to me until he was gone. Between the wedding ceremony and his departure, she stayed at home, by her father’s side, sharing her sister’s bed. He remained kind as ever, but the women—my aunt Lambert especially—were cold, restless, unkind. Little Hetty, feverish and distant, whispered over and over, “I wish we were gone. I wish we were gone.”

Though forgiven, I kept myself apart during those last days. I saw my wife only briefly, in passing, never truly hers until the ship had vanished from sight. Then we went to Winchester and Hampton for what might be called our honeymoon. It was not joyous, but rather a quiet retreat, haunted by absence. For a time, we felt utterly alone, as though we had buried our father—or driven him into exile through our disobedience.

I sent Sampson to announce our marriage in the papers. (My wife later told me she blushed every time she saw him.) I brought Mrs. Warrington back to my old lodgings in Bloomsbury, where space was generous and life modest. There, our married life began.

I wrote to my mother in Virginia, telling her nothing of the turmoil, only that Mr. Lambert was departing for his post abroad, and that I felt bound by honor to fulfill my promise to his daughter. I added that I intended to complete my legal studies, qualify for the Bar, and eventually serve in Virginia or another colony. My good Mrs. Mountain replied, at Madam Esmond’s request, saying that for peace’s sake, correspondence should pass through her.

When I returned to London, I found my relatives in a fury—amusing, indeed, to watch. At my uncle’s house in Hill Street, the butler’s face when he said, “Not at home,” was a mask of icy disdain—worthy of Garrick in *Macbeth*. My little wife walked beside me, laughing at the fellow’s stiff welcome, when suddenly we met Lady Warrington in her carriage, stopped in the street.

I doffed my hat and bowed low. “I affectionately ask after my dear cousins,” I said.

“Do you dare look me in the face?” she gasped.

“Nay, don’t deprive me of that precious privilege!” I replied. “Move on, Peter,” she screamed to her coachman.

“You would not impale your own husband’s flesh and blood,” I said gently.

She slammed up the window of her chair in rage. I kissed my hand and bowed again—my finest bow yet.

Later, walking in Hyde Park, I met my cousin Miles riding with a groom behind him. As soon as he saw us, he galloped forward, the groom shouting, “Stop, Master Miles, stop!”

“I’m not to speak to my cousin,” said Miles, “but sending love to Harry isn’t speaking, is it? Is that my new cousin? I’m Miles, son of Sir Miles Warrington, and you’re very pretty!”

“Now, duee now, Master Miles,” said the groom, touching his hat. The boy trotted off, laughing, glancing back at us.

“You see how they treat me,” I said to Theo.

“As if you married me for their sake!” she answered, her eyes bright with love.

Ah, how happy we were! How brisk and warm the winter seemed! How cozy the kettle by the fire—where the abashed Sampson sometimes came to make punch—and how delightful the nights at the theatre, where friends gave us tickets, and we waited each day for our new play, *Pocahontas*, to outshine all past tragedies.

The fickle old aunt in Clarges Street, who had scorned me on my first arrival with my wife, soon softened. Once she met Theo—whom she’d once called a plain country girl—she fell instantly in love with her. She invited us daily to tea and supper when no other guests came. “As for company,” she would say, “I don’t ask you. You’re no longer *du monde*. Your marriage has put that entirely out of the question.” So she welcomed us as entertainment, letting us enter by the back stairs.

My wife took it all in good humor. And truthfully, I must say that her servants treated us with more respect than any duke and duchess might expect. Madame de Bernstein found my tale of Lady Warrington and the chair endlessly amusing. I acted it out for her, and regaled her with stories of the pious Baronet’s lady and daughters, which delighted the lively, mischievous old woman.

The Dowager Countess of Castlewood, now settled in Kensington, offered us the polite welcome reserved for poorer relatives. We visited her once or twice at her gatherings, but quickly gave them up—losing too much at cards, spending too much on coaches. We were soon forgotten, like others swept from fashion by death, debt, or circumstance. But Theo didn’t mind at all. She had appeared once at one of these parties, wearing ornaments left by her mother—gems the old lady cherished—but I thought her bare white neck far more beautiful than any sparkle. And there were dowagers whose wrinkled faces blazed with rubies and diamonds—jewels they’d gladly trade for Theo’s simple grace.

No one spoke to her—except Beau Lothair, a friend of Mr. Will’s, who later prowled around Bloomsbury and even sent my wife a note. I met him in Covent Garden shortly after and threatened to break his face if I ever saw him near my lodgings. After that, Madam Theo was left in peace.

Only one relative came to visit: our dear Maria, with her husband, Mr. Hagan. They often shared our humble dinners. We also had friend Spencer from the Temple, who admired our quiet happiness and asked for sympathy in his own romantic disappointments. Twice or thrice, the famous Doctor Johnson dropped by for a cup of Theo’s tea.

A cup? A pailful! “And a pail the best thing to feed him, sar!” exclaimed Mr. Gumbo indignantly—for the Doctor’s appearance was not pleasant, nor his linen clean. He snorted, flushed, sputtered, flung food about, and bellowed contradictions. He annoyed Theo, whom he professed to admire, by declaring every time he saw her, “Madam, you do not love me. I see it in your manner. Though I admire you, and come here for your sake. Here is my friend Mr. Reynolds—he shall paint you. No ceruse in his box is as brilliant as your complexion.”

So Mr. Reynolds, a gentleman of perfect manners, would have painted my wife. But I knew his price and chose not to pay it. I wish I had now—for the children’s sake, to see what she looked like thirty-five years ago. To me, madam, she remains the same. And you, my lady, are always young!

What troubled Mrs. Warrington most about Dr. Johnson wasn’t his sputtering or dirty nails, but his harsh judgment of my new tragedy. Hagan once suggested he read some scenes after tea.

“Nay, sir,” said the Doctor, “conversation is better. I can read for myself or hear you at the theatre. I’d rather hear Mrs. Warrington’s artless talk than your declamation of Mr. Warrington’s decasyllables. Tell us about your household affairs, madam. Is your father well? Did you make the pudding and butter sauce? The butter sauce was delicious!” (He had preserved a large portion in the bosom of a very dingy shirt.)

“You made it as though you loved me,” he declared. “You helped me as though you loved me—though you don’t.”

“Faith, sir,” said Hagan, spiritedly, “you’re taking some of the present away with you in your waistcoat.”

“Sir, you are rude!” roared the Doctor. “You are ignorant of the first principles of politeness—courtesy before ladies. Having received an university education, I am surprised you haven’t learned such basics. I respect Mrs. Warrington. I would never insult her guests before her!”

“Then why,” said Hagan fiercely, “did you speak of my theatre?”

“Sir, you are saucy!” thundered the Doctor.

“De te fabula,” said the actor.

“I think it’s your waistcoat that’s saucy. Madam, shall I make some lemonade? My papa has sent us fresh limes. May we send some to the Temple?”

“Madam,” said the Doctor, “if they stay in your house, they’ll lose their quality and turn sweet.”

“Mr. Hagan,” he bellowed, “you are a young sauce-box! Ho! ho! It is I who have been wrong.”

“Oh, my lord, my Polidore!” cried Lady Maria, alone in my wife’s drawing-room. “‘Oh, I could hear thee talk for ever thus, Eternally admiring,--fix and gaze On those dear eyes, for every glance they send Darts through my soul, and fills my heart with rapture!’”

“You know not, my Theo,” she sighed, “what a pearl and paragon of a man my Castalio is—my Chamont, my—oh, dear me, child, what a pity it is that in your husband’s tragedy he must take the horrid name of Captain Smith!”

This tragedy was not just my literary hope—it was the foundation of my financial future. My brother’s debts paid, my mother’s drafts honored, my own expenses covered—nearly all my inheritance had been spent. This was the moment I chose to marry. I could raise money on my estate, though costly. My mother would not leave her eldest son without support, despite our quarrels. I had health, strength, wit, friends, reputation—and above all, this famous tragedy, promised by the manager to perform. I counted on its success to sustain me.

But what becomes of youth’s arithmetic? How do we reckon a hundred pounds as a fortune, a thousand as a fortune? How could I dare gamble so boldly with Fortune?

I managed to convince my dear General, who left home believing his son-in-law had at least two hundred pounds ready. He and Molly had begun life with less, and the ravens had fed them. As for the women, poverty was a romantic idea. Aunt Lambert declared it wicked to doubt God’s provision. “Was the righteous ever forsaken? Did the just ever beg bread? I know better! No, no, my dears—I won’t fear for you. Look at me and my General!”

Theo believed me—because she wished to believe.

So we began life on a capital of Five Acts and about three hundred pounds in cash.

Soon, the day of the tragedy’s premiere drew near. My friends canvassed the town to gather supporters. I am ill at begging favors from the great—but when Lord Wrotham came to London, I went with Theo to call on him. He received us kindly, out of regard for her father—though he good-naturedly wagged a finger at me (and my wife hung her head) for having stolen a march on the General. Still, he promised to help his old friend’s daughter. He hoped for success, praised the play, and secured places for himself and friends.

But that was all I had. “Mon cher,” said the Baroness, “at my age, I should bore myself to death at a tragedy—but I’ll do my best. I’ll send my people to the boxes. Case in his best black looks like a nobleman; and Brett in one of my gowns has a *faux air de moi* that is quite distinguished. Put down my name for two front boxes. Good-bye, my dear. Bonne chance!”

The Dowager Countess sent compliments (on the back of the nine of clubs), held a card party that night, and regretted she and Fanny couldn’t attend.

As for my uncle and Lady Warrington—they were out of the question. After the sedan-chair incident, asking them to Drury Lane might as well have been asking Queen Elizabeth to go to the theater.

These were all my allies—the aristocratic connections poor Sampson had bragged about. On their strength, the manager had given Mr. Hagan his engagement.

“Where was Lord Bute?” the manager snapped, taking snuff—how different from the cheerful, eager man of six months past. “Had I not promised his lordship would come?”

“Yes,” said Mr. Garrick, “and her Royal Highness the Princess of Wales, and his Majesty too.”

Poor Sampson confessed he had, in his delusion, promised the presence of these august figures.

The next day, at rehearsal, matters grew worse. The manager was furious.

“Great heavens, sir!” he shouted. “Into what a trap have you led me? Read this letter!”

It read:

I have seen his lordship and conveyed Mr. Warrington’s request that he honor *Pocahontas* with his presence. His lordship is a patron of the drama and a generous friend to the arts. But he desires me to say he cannot consider attending himself, much less invite his Gracious Master to witness a performance in which a principal role is played by an actor who has secretly married a daughter of one of His Majesty’s nobility.
—Your well-wisher,
Mr. D. Garrick, Theatre Royal, Drury Lane.

My poor Theo had a nice dinner waiting for me after rehearsal. I blamed fatigue for my pallor. I dared not tell her the dreadful news.
