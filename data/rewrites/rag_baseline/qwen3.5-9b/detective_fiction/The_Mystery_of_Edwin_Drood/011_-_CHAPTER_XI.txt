The air inside Staple Inn tasted of wet wool and suspended dust. It was a place designed to dampen the noise of the world, a legal nook where the pavement seemed to soften beneath one's boots, and the sparrows in the smoky trees played a game of pretend-country that required no understanding of borders or maps. In the corner of the inner quadrangle, behind a portal marked *P J T 1747*, the fog pressed against the glass, blurring the candlelight into a murky, indistinct glow.

Inside, Mr. Grewgious sat by the fire. He was a man carved from the same dry wood as the furniture around him, a conveyancer who had married the law only to divorce it shortly after. Ambition had been a spark he had extinguished years ago, replaced by the steady, smokeless heat of receiverships and the careful management of other people's debts. His life was a series of precise movements: filing papers, counting coins, ensuring that every fact remained complete and every figure accurate. To leave even a single digit obscure was a death sentence for his conscience.

Across the room, sitting in the shadows, was Bazzard. He was a creature of low luminescence, a man whose complexion suggested a constant hunger and whose hair grew in tangled knots like vines choking a wall. He possessed a strange gravitational pull toward Grewgious, sticking to his side like a familiar summoned by a spell that refused to break. Grewgious tolerated him with a patience that bordered on the miraculous, treating the gloom of the clerk as if it were a necessary component of the office's atmosphere.

"Mr. Drood," Bazzard announced, his voice flat against the silence.

Grewgious looked up, arranging his papers with the deliberate slowness of a man measuring time. "He has called?"

"He has called," Bazzard confirmed.

Grewgious gestured vaguely toward the door. "Show him in."

The door opened, admitting Edwin Drood. He entered carrying the weight of the fog outside, his eyes stinging as if ground with cayenne pepper. Grewgious watched him, noting the discomfort, the slight hunch of the shoulders.

"You are choking," Grewgious observed, his tone devoid of pity but full of practical concern. "Undo your wraps. Come, sit."

Edwin hesitated, then accepted the invitation to the easy-chair in the corner. The fire roared, licking at the moisture he brought in, trying to consume the chill.

"I look as if I have come to stay," Edwin smiled, though the expression did not reach his eyes.

"We can have dinner," Grewgious said, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper that somehow carried perfectly in the small room. "Just across Holborn. Take your pepper here rather than outside. Sit."

Edwin glanced around, considering the prospect of a meal shared with a bachelor in chambers. "You are very kind."

"Not at all," Grewgious replied, his eyes twinkling with a sudden, bright idea. "You are kind to join issue with a man alone. And I shall ask Bazzard. He might object otherwise."

"Bazzard!" Grewgious called out.

"If I am ordered to dine, I will," Bazzard stated, his voice lacking any inflection of desire.

"You are not ordered," Grewgious corrected sharply. "You are invited."

"In that case," Bazzard conceded, "I do not care."

"Then it is settled," Grewgious said, issuing instructions with the rhythm of someone reciting a catechism. "Go to Furnival's Inn. Ask for the strongest soup, the best made-dish, a haunch of mutton, and whatever poultry is available. Whatever remains on the bill of fare."

Bazzard bowed stiffly and retreated to execute the command. Once the clerk was gone, Grewgious lowered his voice further. "I was delicate about employing him in the commissariat. He might dislike it."

"He seems to have his own way," Edwin noted.

"No," Grewgious said, shaking his head slowly. "If he had his own way, he would not be here."

Edwin considered this, wondering where Bazzard would be if he chose freedom, but Grewgious interrupted his thoughts. The lawyer stood up, adjusting his skirts, his back to the fire, creating a barrier between himself and the window.

"I assume you visited to inform me that you are proceeding downstairs, where I can assure you you are expected. And perhaps to discuss commissions regarding my ward? Or simply to sharpen my wits?"

"I called as an act of attention," Edwin replied.

"Attention," Grewgious repeated, leaning closer to the fire as if to burn the word into his own skin. "Not impatience?"

"Impatience?"

Grewgious straightened, realizing his attempt at archness had evaporated before it could take root, leaving only the stark reality of the situation. "I have been down there recently," he said, smoothing his coat. "That is why I said you are expected."

"Indeed," Edwin said, relaxing slightly. "Yes, I knew Pussy was watching for me."

"Do you keep a cat there?" Grewgious asked, genuinely puzzled.

Edwin flushed. "I call her Rosa Pussy."

"Pussy," Grewgious tested the word, his face unreadable. "Affable."

"A pet name," Edwin clarified, sensing a resistance he could not decipher.

"Umps," Grewgious nodded, offering a response that was simultaneously an agreement and a dismissal, leaving Edwin disoriented.

"Did she—" Edwin began, correcting himself mid-sentence. "Did Rosa tell you anything about the Landlesses?"

"The Landlesses?" Grewgious frowned. "An estate? A villa?"

"A brother and sister. The sister is at the Nuns' House and has become a close friend of..."

"Pussy's," Grewgious interjected, his face suddenly rigid.

"She is a strikingly handsome girl, sir. Surely you have heard of her?"

"Neither," Grewgious said firmly. "But here comes Bazzard."

The clerk returned, flanked by two waiters. One moved with the inertia of a statue, the other with the frantic energy of a bird caught in a storm. Together, they brought in enough fog to reignite the fire. The flying waiter moved with dizzying speed, polishing glasses, fetching soup, retrieving dishes, and returning with breathless urgency, while the immovable waiter stood still, critiquing every movement with a silent, stern gaze. Between flights, the flying waiter was reproached for bringing the weather indoors; the immovable waiter was blamed for forgetting items. Finally, the immovable waiter folded the tablecloth under his arm, delivering a look that screamed *the reward is mine*, and shoved the exhausted flyer out into the hallway.

The scene was a grotesque parody of bureaucratic efficiency, a living caricature of the Circumlocution Office itself. The fog served as the sauce for the meal, thickening the air with every sneeze and shiver.

Grewgious descended to the cellar and returned with bottles of wine that had slept in lands untouched by London's grey mist. They popped their corks with a cheerful defiance, dancing on the table as if eager to escape their long imprisonment. Yet, Grewgious remained unmoved. The alcohol did not loosen his features; instead, it seemed to highlight the dry lines etched into his face. He did not drink deeply, pouring the liquid over his own high-dried nature as if it were snuff.

When the meal concluded, Grewgious signaled Edwin to return to the corner. The lawyer turned his chair toward the fire, smoothing his hands over his face, studying Edwin with an intensity that felt like a physical pressure.

"Bazzard!"

"Yes, sir," the clerk responded, having consumed his share of food and silence with mechanical precision.

"Greetings, Bazzard. Mr. Edwin, success to Mr. Bazzard."

"Success to Mr. Bazzard," Edwin echoed, his enthusiasm sounding entirely manufactured, masking a question he dared not speak aloud.
