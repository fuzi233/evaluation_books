Mrs. Palmer’s constitution had sufficiently mended over a fortnight, her vitality restored by deeply nourishing, long-simmered bone broths and heritage capon. Thus, Mrs. Jennings relinquished her constant vigil, returning to the deeply rooted domestic rhythms of Berkeley Street. The Miss Dashwoods seamlessly resumed their places within this matriarchal sanctuary, eager to re-immerse themselves in the cyclical, mythic routines of the household, where the meticulous preparation of sustenance grounded their daily existence.

On the fourth morning of this restored harmony, Elinor sat at the heavy oak table, meticulously peeling and coring golden quince for a multi-generational preserving recipe. Her paring knife moved in a quiet, rhythmic *shhh-shhh* that honored the tranquil acoustic ecology of the room, while the bright, floral tartness of the fruit permeated the olfactory landscape. Suddenly, Mrs. Jennings shattered this peaceful sonic environment, her hurried footsteps vibrating the floorboards like an approaching tempest. She carried an air of such bursting importance that it seemed she might boil over.

“Lord! my dear Miss Dashwood! have you heard the news?”

Elinor paused, resting her knife against the pale flesh of the fruit. “No, ma’am. What is it?”

“Something so strange! But you shall hear it all. When I got to Mr. Palmer’s, I found Charlotte quite in a fuss over the child. It cried, and fretted, and was all over pimples. I looked at it directly, and told her it was nothing in the world but the red gum—a natural blooming of the skin. Mr. Donavan was sent for, and stepping into the nursery, he confirmed the very same. But just as he was departing, standing there amidst the aroma of my simmering morning oats, it came into my head to ask if there was any news. He smirked, looked grave, and whispered—lowering his voice so as not to disturb the quiet—‘For fear any unpleasant report should reach the young ladies as to their sister’s indisposition, I think it advisable to say I hope Mrs. Dashwood will do very well.’”

“What! is Fanny ill?”

“Exactly what I said, my dear! ‘Lord!’ says I, ‘is Mrs. Dashwood ill?’ And then the whole fermented secret spilled out. Mr. Edward Ferrars, the very young man I used to joke with you about, has been engaged above this twelvemonth to my cousin Lucy! Not a creature knowing a syllable of the matter, except Nancy! I never happened to see them together, or I am sure my senses would have detected the flavor of it directly. Well, this was kept a great secret, for fear of Mrs. Ferrars, until this very morning, when poor Nancy, who lacks the palate for discretion, casually served it all up. Away she went to your sister, who was sitting all alone at her carpet-work, little suspecting the bitter dish to come. Fanny had just been saying she thought to make a match between Edward and some Lord’s daughter. You may think what a blow it was to her superficial pride. She fell into violent hysterics immediately.”

Mrs. Jennings paused, shaking her head at the memory of the reported noise. “Such screams as reached your brother’s ears! A violent, piercing dissonance that ruined the acoustic peace of the entire house, reaching him down stairs where he sat writing to his steward—calculating sterile profits, no doubt. Up he flew, and a terrible scene took place. Lucy arrived, little dreaming what was brewing. Fanny scolded like a fury, her dominant lexicon of wealth and status driving poor Lucy into a fainting fit. Nancy cried bitterly; your brother walked about the room, utterly useless. Mrs. Dashwood declared they should not stay a minute longer, demanding their immediate expulsion. The carriage was brought round, and they were cast out—Lucy hardly able to walk. I declare, I have no patience with your sister. To use his love so scornfully! I have no notion of people making such a to-do about commercial greatness. There is no reason on earth why Mr. Edward and Lucy should not marry. Though Lucy has next to nothing herself, she is a traditional steward of the hearth; she knows better than anybody how to make the most of every ingredient. If Mrs. Ferrars would only allow him five hundred a-year, Lucy would cultivate a table as rich and sustaining as anybody else would with eight. Lord! how snug they might live, putting up their own preserves and living in acoustic harmony, with a maid and a man to tend a small garden.”

Elinor had time enough during this deluge to collect her thoughts. Happy to find her own internal landscape unsuspected, she was able to speak of the affair without the tremor of embarrassment. She felt much compassion for Edward’s untranslatable predicament, but for Lucy, she felt very little—and it cost her considerable emotional labor to procure even that. 

As Mrs. Jennings could talk of no other subject, Elinor saw the necessity of preparing Marianne. No time was to be lost in breaking this bitter news, lest Marianne encounter it through the harsh, unfeeling dialect of public gossip. 

Elinor’s office was a painful one. She was going to remove her sister’s chief consolation and force Marianne to taste her own disappointment all over again. Finding Marianne in the drawing-room, enveloped by the scent of the dried lavender she was sorting, Elinor delivered her narration with clear, simple precision. It was not accompanied by violent agitation; she maintained the meticulous emotional control of a master food historian handling fragile, ancient texts. The impetuous grief belonged entirely to Marianne, whose acoustic expressions of horror and excessive weeping filled the room.

Marianne could not digest it. Edward seemed a second Willoughby; the betrayal was sour on her tongue. As for Lucy Steele, she considered her so utterly lacking in sensory appreciation, so incapable of true passion, that she could not believe Edward had ever harbored an appetite for her company. 

“How long has this been known to you, Elinor?” Marianne asked, her voice trembling. “Has he written to you?”

“I have known it these four months. When Lucy first came to Barton Park last November, she told me in confidence of her engagement.”

Marianne’s eyes expressed an astonishment her lips could not immediately translate. After a long pause, she exclaimed, “Four months! Have you known of this four months?”

Elinor confirmed it.

“What! While attending me in all my misery, has this been weighing on your heart? And I have reproached you for being happy!”

“It was not fit that you should then know how much I was the reverse.”

“Four months!” cried Marianne again, grappling with this profound untranslatability of subjective experience. “So calm! So cheerful! How have you been supported?”

“By feeling that I was doing my duty. My promise to Lucy obliged me to be secret. I owed it to her, therefore, to suppress my native dialect of grief; and I owed it to my family not to create in them a hunger for answers I could not provide.”

Marianne seemed deeply struck by the quiet, sustaining power of her sister’s endurance. 

“I have very often wished to undeceive you and my mother,” Elinor added. “But without betraying my trust, I never could have convinced you.”

“Four months! And yet you loved him!”

“Yes. But my love is not merely a solitary, consuming hunger. While the comfort of others was dear to me, I was glad to spare them from knowing how much I felt. Now, I can speak of it with little emotion. I acquit Edward of essential misconduct. Lucy does not want sense, which is a foundation upon which a domestic life may be built. Edward will marry her, and time and the cyclical rhythms of habit will teach him to forget that he ever thought another superior.”

“If such is your way of thinking,” said Marianne, her worldview shifting, “if the loss of what is most valued is so easily replaced, your self-command is perhaps less to be wondered at.”

“I understand you,” Elinor replied, her voice firming as she challenged Marianne's assumption. “You do not suppose that I have ever felt much. For four months, Marianne, I have had this fermenting in my mind, unable to speak a word of it. It was forced upon me by the very person whose prior engagement ruined all my prospects, and told to me with triumph. I have had to listen to her exultation again and again, tasting the ash of it each time. I have had to contend against the unkindness of his sister and the insolence of his mother, suffering the punishment of an attachment without enjoying its sustaining warmth. The composure of mind with which I now consider the matter is the effect of constant, painful, meticulous exertion. If I had not been bound to silence, perhaps nothing could have kept me from openly showing that I was *very* unhappy.”

Marianne was entirely subdued, the erosion of her own self-centered perspective complete. “Oh! Elinor,” she cried, “you make me hate myself. How barbarous I have been to you—you, who have been my only comfort, who have borne with me in my misery! Because your profound depth of feeling cried out against my own shallow perceptions, I have been trying to deny it.”

Tender caresses followed, smelling faintly of the lavender crushed beneath their hands. In this restorative frame of mind, Marianne promised never to speak of the affair with bitterness, and to meet Lucy and Edward, should chance bring them together, with unbroken cordiality. 

She performed her promise to admiration, listening to Mrs. Jennings' endless stew of gossip with an unchanging complexion. Such advances towards heroism in her sister made Elinor feel equal to anything.

The next morning brought a farther trial: a visit from their brother. John Dashwood entered bringing with him the sterile, industrial chill of modern commerce, his presence a jarring note in the room’s quiet acoustic harmony.

“You have heard, I suppose,” he said with great solemnity, viewing them as an audience to his tragedy, “of the very shocking discovery under our roof yesterday.”

They assented silently. 

“Your sister has suffered dreadfully. Poor Fanny was in hysterics all yesterday. But her constitution is a good one. She has borne it with the fortitude of an angel! She says she shall never think well of anybody again, after meeting with such ingratitude. It was purely out of her benevolence that she invited those harmless, well-behaved girls to her house.” 

He stopped to be thanked for Fanny's supposed grace, which being done, he proceeded to outline the absolute dominance of the Ferrars' wealth. 

“What poor Mrs. Ferrars suffered is not to be described. While she had been planning a most eligible connection for him, to suspect he was engaged elsewhere! We consulted together and sent for Edward. All that Mrs. Ferrars could say to make him sever the tie was of no avail. Duty, affection—everything was disregarded. His mother explained to him her liberal designs if he married Miss Morton. She told him she would settle on him the Norfolk estate, which, clear of land-tax, brings in a good thousand a-year.” 

John spoke of the agrarian landscape not as a source of generational sustenance or rich, loamy earth, but purely as abstract, utilitarian fuel: numbers on a ledger. “She offered to make it twelve hundred! And in opposition to this, she represented the certain penury that must attend this low connection. She protested she would never see him again and would prevent his advancement in any profession.”

Marianne, in an ecstasy of indignation at this attempt to starve a soul into submission, clapped her hands together. “Gracious God! Can this be possible!” She was going to retort against such a barren, commercial worldview, but remembered her promises and forbore.

“Edward said very little,” John continued, oblivious to the quiet strength of Edward's resistance. “But he was determined. He would stand to his engagement, cost him what it might.”

“Then,” cried Mrs. Jennings with blunt sincerity, “he has acted like an honest man! I beg your pardon, Mr. Dashwood, but if he had done otherwise, I should have thought him a rascal. Lucy Steele is my cousin, and there is not a better kind of girl to manage a household and keep a hearth bright.”

John Dashwood was astonished, but his nature was too meticulously calibrated to wealth to take offense at a woman of good fortune. “I would by no means speak disrespectfully of your relation, Madam. But a secret engagement with the son of a woman of such large fortune is extraordinary. Mrs. Ferrars’s conduct has been dignified and liberal. Edward has drawn his own lot, and I fear it will be a bad one.”

“Well, sir,” said Mrs. Jennings, “and how did it end?”

“In a most unhappy rupture. Edward is dismissed for ever. Born to the prospect of such affluence! I cannot conceive a situation more deplorable. The interest of two thousand pounds—how can a man live on it? When he might have been in the receipt of two thousand, five hundred a-year, for Miss Morton has thirty thousand pounds! I cannot picture a more wretched condition.” John’s inability to conceptualize a life sustained by anything other than vast, sterile capital laid bare the absolute poverty of his own worldview. 

“Poor young man!” cried Mrs. Jennings. “I am sure he should be very welcome to bed and board at my house. It is not fit that he should be living at his own charge at taverns, eating utilitarian fodder.”

Elinor’s heart thanked her for recognizing his need for true, sensory sustenance, though she smiled at the blunt form of the invitation.

“If he had done as well by himself as his friends were disposed to,” John said, “he would have wanted for nothing. But there is one thing more preparing against him: his mother has determined to settle *that* estate upon Robert immediately. I left her this morning with her lawyer, talking over the business.”

“Well!” said Mrs. Jennings, “that is her revenge. But I don’t think mine would be, to make one son independent because another had plagued me.”

Marianne got up and paced the room, the rhythm of her steps a restless critique of the power dynamics at play.

“Can anything be more galling to the spirit of a man,” concluded John, entirely missing the point of Edward's sacrifice, “than to see his younger brother in possession of an estate which might have been his own?”
