A change fell on Margaret Brandt like a frost that comes in the night and will not melt at noon. She moved through the hosier’s house as though the rooms were full of smoke and she must feel her way by touch alone. Her hands still did their work—kneading, rinsing, sweeping—yet without their old brisk certainty; she would pause with the dough half turned, as if the living warmth under her palms accused her.

If Peter but spoke a little sharp, the way a man will when accounts go awry or a strap will not buckle, she started so violently the pot-lids on the shelf would chatter. Then she would stare at him with wide, frightened eyes, and the sound of his voice seemed to ring too loud in her ears, as if every syllable were a hammer on the house’s thin peace.

She went less often to her warm old friend, Dame Margaret Van Eyck; and when she did go, she was ill at ease there. The dame’s caresses—those motherly embraces that had once drawn a smile—were now received with a passive trembling, and sometimes Margaret shrank from them as if love itself might burn.

But the strangest thing of all was this: she would not go outside her own door in daylight. If she went to Tergou, it was after dusk, when the street-sounds softened and faces blurred into shadow; and she would return before daybreak, while only the earliest birds and the far-off creak of a cart-wheel spoke in the dark. She would not even go to matins. The bell’s clean call that used to wash the town’s sleep away seemed now too public, too piercing, a sound that drew eyes as well as ears.

At last Peter—slow to observe anything that did not touch his trade—marked it and questioned her.

“Why ever will you not show your face by day?”

Margaret’s fingers tightened on the cloth she held. “Methinks the folk all look at me.”

Another day Dame Van Eyck, with that shrewd kindness which sees beneath excuses, asked her outright what ailed her. A scared look, and then a flood of tears, were all the answer. The old lady expostulated gently.

“What, sweetheart—afraid to confide your sorrows to me?”

“I have no sorrows, madam,” Margaret whispered, “but of my own making. I am kinder treated than I deserve—especially in this house.”

“Then why not come oftener, my dear?”

“I come oftener than I deserve,” said Margaret, and sighed so deep it seemed to draw the warmth out of the room.

“There—Reicht is bawling for you,” Dame Van Eyck said, easing the moment with homely impatience. “Go, child!—what on earth can it be?”

Left alone, the dame turned the matter over as women do when their hands are busy and their minds will not rest: perhaps the girl was mortified by the contempt with which she was treated by Gerard’s kin. The thought stung her. If that were it, she would take them to task—at least the women.

The very next day she put on hood and cloak, and with Reicht trotting after her, went to the hosier’s house. Catherine received her with much respect, and thanked her with tears for her kindness to Gerard; but when, encouraged by this, Dame Van Eyck turned the talk toward Margaret Brandt, Catherine’s eyes dried as suddenly as if a shutter had been drawn over them. Her lips pinched, and her face set into that look which belongs to obstinate, ignorant women—faces that, once settled thus, might as well be a brick wall for all the softening words in the world.

Dame Van Eyck tried. She tried as one tries to coax a sour leaven into sweetness—patiently at first, then more sharply when patience fails. All in vain. Not being used to be thwarted, she grew provoked; and at last, with an abrupt, half-broken curtsey that was more dismissal than farewell, she went out. Catherine returned it with an air of defiance rather than obeisance.

Outside the door Reicht stood talking with a pale girl on crutches. Dame Van Eyck, cheeks hot, was sweeping past them with a scornful toss meant for the whole household when suddenly a little delicate hand slid into hers—timid as a bird lighting. She looked round and met two dove-like eyes, wet with tears, that sought hers with gratitude and with entreaty in one.

The old woman read that look, complex as it was; and her anger dropped away, as if some unseen hand had turned down the flame.

“I see,” said she softly. Then she kissed the girl’s brow. “Mind, then—I leave it to you.”

When she returned home she said, half to herself, half to the room that listened, “I have been to a house to-day where I have seen a very common thing and a very uncommon thing. I have seen a stupid, obstinate woman; and I have seen an angel in the flesh, with a face—if I had it here, I’d take down my brushes once more and try and paint it.”

Little Kate did not belie that hasty good opinion. She watched, waited, and chose her hour. Then she told her mother what she had learned from Reicht Heynes: how Margaret Brandt had shed her very blood for Gerard in the wood.

“See, mother, how she loves him.”

“Who would not love him?” Catherine answered, as if that settled all.

“Oh, mother—think of it! Poor thing.”

“Ay, wench. She has her own trouble, no doubt, as well as we ours. I can’t abide the sight of blood—let alone my own.”

That was a gain: the first crack in a hardened crust. But when Kate tried to press further, she was stopped short; her mother’s temper was like a pot that will not simmer—either cold or boiling.

About a month after this, a soldier of the Dalgetty breed—one of those lean, roving blades who come and go with war as if war were weather—returned from service in Burgundy and brought a letter one evening to the hosier’s house. Peter was away on business; the rest sat at supper. The table held their plain fare: coarse bread, a bit of salted meat, and a pot that sent up a homely steam smelling of cabbage and onions browned in dripping. The spoons clicked; the hearth snapped; outside, the wind worried at the eaves.

The soldier laid the letter by Catherine’s hand, refused all guerdon for bringing it, and went off toward Sevenbergen as if he carried news for no one, as if messages were nothing but another road.

The letter lay unfolded and spread out. Curiously enough, though not one of them could read, they all knew it was Gerard’s hand. The strokes, the spacing, even the way the ink thinned at the end of a line—these were as familiar as his tread on the stair.

“And your father must be away,” cried Catherine, half anger, half shame. “Are ye not ashamed of yourselves—not one that can read your brother’s letter?”

Yet though the words were to them what strange runes are to a ploughman, there was something on the page they could read. There is an art that speaks without words, and needs no priest nor scholar to interpret it; it steals through the eye into the heart, learned and unlearned alike, and crosses frontiers without losing a syllable, because it has none to lose. It is at the mercy of no translator; it writes an universal tongue.

Between two short paragraphs Gerard had drawn, in pencil, two hands clasped together.

They read that. It went straight to the marrow. As they stared, Gerard seemed present—his very manner in every turn and line—and bidding them farewell. The women wept until tears blurred the simple sketch. Giles said, “Poor Gerard!” and his voice came out lower than seemed to belong to him. Even Cornelis and Sybrandt felt a momentary remorse and sat silent, gloomy, as if the hearth had dimmed.

But how to get the words read? Pride held them. They were loth to show both ignorance and grief to a stranger.

“The Dame Van Eyck?” Kate ventured timidly.

“And so I will,” said Catherine. “She has a good heart. She loves Gerard, too. She will be glad to hear of him. I was short with her when she came here; but I will make my submission, and then she will tell me what my poor child says to me.”

She wrapped herself and went at once. The night air had that winter bite that makes every sound seem sharper: her footsteps on frozen ground, the distant bark of a dog, the thin, far bell of some late hour. When she reached Dame Van Eyck’s, Reicht took her into a room and said, “Bide a minute; she is at her orisons.”

A young woman sat pensively by the stove. She rose at Catherine’s entrance and courteously made way. The stove breathed a steady warmth; it carried scents of peat and dried herbs, and in that warmth Catherine thawed her fingers, grateful as a starved body is grateful to broth.

“Thank you, young lady,” said Catherine. “The winter nights are cold, and your stove is a treat.”

While warming her hands she inspected the other furtively from head to foot, as women will. The young person wore a common wimple, but her gown was trimmed with fur—almost a sign, in those days, of superior rank or wealth. Yet what struck Catherine most was not the fur, but the face: so candid, so modest, it promised sympathy the way fresh bread promises comfort before ever it is cut.

Catherine’s tongue loosened with hope. “Now, what think you brings me here, young lady? It is a letter! a letter from my poor boy that is far away in some savage part or other. And I take shame to say that none of us can read it. I wonder whether you can read?”

“Yes,” said the young woman.

“Can ye, now? It is much to your credit, my dear. I dare say she won’t be long; but every minute is an hour to a poor longing mother.”

“I will read it to you.”

“Bless you, my dear; bless you!” Catherine cried, and in her unfeigned eagerness she never noticed the strange, suppressed eagerness with which the other slowly put out her hand to take the letter—nor the tremor that ran through the fingers as they closed upon it, as though the paper were a living thing.

“Come, then,” said Catherine. “Read it to me, prithee. I am wearying for it.”

The young woman’s voice began steady, but too careful, like one who walks on ice.

“The first words are—‘To my honoured parents.’”

“Ay! and he always did honour us, poor soul,” Catherine answered, and clutched her apron as if it were his sleeve.

“‘God and the saints have you in His holy keeping, and bless you by night and by day. Your one harsh deed is forgotten; your years of love remembered.’”

Catherine’s hand flew to her bosom. She sank back with one long sob, as if the words had struck and soothed her at once—salt and honey in the same mouthful.

“Then comes this, madam,” the reader went on. “It doth speak for itself: ‘a long farewell.’”

“Ay—go on,” Catherine pleaded. “Bless you, girl; you give me sorry comfort. Still ’tis comfort.”

“‘To my brothers Cornelis and Sybrandt—Be content; you will see me no more!’”

“What does that mean?” Catherine whispered; and then the meaning, like a knife’s cold edge, found its way in. “Ah!”

“‘To my sister Kate—Little angel of my father’s house. Be kind to her—’”

“Ah!” Catherine cried again, the sound dragged out of her as if by force.

“That is Margaret Brandt, my dear—his sweetheart, poor soul,” she broke in, as the thought rushed upon her. “I’ve not been kind to her, my dear. Forgive me, Gerard!”

The young woman read on, but the words seemed to cost her breath.

“‘—for poor Gerard’s sake: since grief to her is death to me—Ah!’”

That last cry—half in the letter, half in the reader’s own throat—broke what she had been holding shut. Nature, resenting the struggle for unnatural composure, suddenly had its way. The young woman swayed, slipped from her chair, and fell insensible, the letter still in her hand, her head coming to rest on Catherine’s knees like a child laid down after weeping itself to sleep.
