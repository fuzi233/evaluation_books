Some of my dear readers undoubtedly enjoy visiting the renowned gardens of Regent's Park, where so many of our finned, feathered, and four-legged companions are housed in exchange for their display to our instruction and amusement. While gazing at the aquatic life within the aquarium, observing how a man's business and private thoughts inevitably mingle with the surrounding nature, I found myself once again lost in contemplation of our friends, the Virginians.

One of the most magnificent creatures I have ever witnessed, slicing through his green bath in harmonious curves—alternately flashing his black, glistening back and revealing his fair, white chest—is none other than our humble acquaintance, the flounder. We have often devoured these creatures from their watery depths at Greenwich, entirely unaware of their inherent beauty. Yet, as is the case with mankind, so it is with flounders. View them in their proper element, and they appear agile, robust, and comely; remove them from it, and their beauty vanishes instantly. Their movements become grotesque as they flap their tails uselessly against the bottom, gasping out their feeble lives. Were one to lift such a specimen gently and return it to the Thames, it might recover—but alas, there is a proverb regarding "fish out of water," and surely other naturalists have noted this before me.

Consider Harry Warrington. For a long time, he had been floundering, utterly removed from his proper element. But the moment he discovered his calling, health, strength, spirits, and energy surged back into him. With the simple tap of an epaulet upon his shoulder, he transformed into a new being. He embraced his new profession with delight, mastering every detail with eager quickness. Should I possess the skills of my friend Lorrequer, I would eagerly follow Harry into camp—to witness him marching, dining at the mess, and parading on the drill ground. I would share his carouses, sleep beneath his tent, and discuss the intricacies of warfare with him. Since I cannot do so, I leave it to the reader's imagination to flesh out this picture based on the faint outlines I provide. Simply conjure the image of Mr. Harry Warrington in his scarlet coat with yellow facings, radiating joy in bearing the King's colors and eager to fulfill the duties of his trade.

As each young man admired the virtues of the other, recognizing his brother's superior qualities, George was naturally proud of Harry's success and rejoiced in his restored fortunes. He penned an affectionate letter to his mother in Virginia, detailing the high praise Harry received—a modesty that prevented Harry from repeating it himself. George recounted how Harry had secured his first commission and explained that he intended to request permission from his mother to share the cost of purchasing a higher rank for his brother. Nothing brought George greater delight than the ability to assist Harry, especially since Harry's sudden return to active life had inadvertently depleted funds that George had rightfully considered his own. Having labored under the misconception that he was the sole heir, Harry had incurred expenses he would never have dared as a younger son. Thus, George viewed his contribution not merely as aid, but as a fitting repayment for his own liberation from financial burden.

Having concluded his account of Harry's affairs, George turned his attention to matters of his own heart, addressing a topic of deep personal concern to his honored mother. She knew that the finest friends George and Harry possessed in England were the esteemed Mr. and Mrs. Lambert—the latter being Madam Esmond's schoolmate from their youth. Where their biological relatives had proven worldly and indifferent, these true friends had remained consistently generous and kind. The General was held in high respect throughout the army, and Mrs. Lambert's maternal affection for Madam Esmond's children was unparalleled. In his letter, Mr. George confessed that he had fallen deeply in love with the elder Miss Lambert, believing his entire future happiness rested upon securing her hand. Though he had made no formal proposal, he assured his mother that his sentiments were clear, and that both the young lady and her parents appeared favorable toward him. Describing her as a model of perfection, George expressed his conviction that his destiny hung in the balance of winning or losing her favor.

Why, one might ask, does the editor withhold this correspondence? Is it not cruel to silence the secrets of a young man's love? To deny the reader the chance to overhear incoherent vows, wild raptures, and the raw follies of passion? Would we not prefer to act as eavesdroppers at twilight windows, counting sighs and handshakes, bottling hot tears, and listening to the throbbing hearts of delicate maidens? I protest, for love is sacred. Whenever we encounter it—whether shooting suddenly from two pairs of eyes, glancing sadly from one, descending from a mother to her child, or passing from a father watching his daughter whirl around the room with her captain—we must salute it. It is not only wrong to "kiss and tell," but to speak of kisses at all. Let those initiated into the mystery keep quiet. Beware the gossip who prints private conversations; as sure as they sail with you, they will cast you adrift.

While Harry's love for battle drove him to seek out powder and rush toward reluctant dragons, carrying wounded comrades from the fire, George pursued a far more peaceful and delightful diversion. He penned sonnets to his mistress's brow, paced the darkness beneath her window watching her chamber lamp, and invented endless pretexts to send notes requiring no immediate reply. He gathered verses from his favorite poets and flowers from Covent Garden for her adornment. He attended services at St. James's Church, singing along from the Prayer Book while ignoring the sermon, completely absorbed by other thoughts. He was excessively affectionate toward Miss Theo's relations: her siblings at school, her brother at college, her witty companion Miss Hetty, and even her mother, whom Martin Lambert claimed was already half in love with George himself. Indeed, if fathers grow sulky at the sight of a destined son-in-law, it is a universal truth that mothers become sentimental, loving their own loves anew.

Gumbo and Sady were constantly shuttling between Southampton Row and Dean Street. During the summer months, numerous outings were planned, with countless invitations extended to Ranelagh, Hampstead, Vauxhall, and Marylebone Gardens. George desired that Miss Theo copy a famous tragedy for the stage, marveling at her exquisite Italian handwriting. Sheets passed back and forth, accompanied by little notes of thanks, inquiry, and admiration. Consider the packet, marked in Warrington's neat script: *"T's Letters, 1758–9."* Shall we open it and expose these tender secrets to public scrutiny? Those virgin words were whispered for one ear alone. Years later, the husband undoubtedly read them with sweet pangs of memory, recalling the fond lines addressed to his lover. It would be a sacrilege to display them publicly; kindly readers need only accept our assurance that the young lady's letters were modest and pure, and the gentleman's most respectful and tender.

In essence, we have revealed very little, yet in these final months, Mr. George Warrington had decided he had found the woman of women. She may not be the most beautiful—why look further than Cousin Flora, Coelia, or Ardelia, who are undeniably more striking? Yet her sweet face pleased him more than any other. She may lack supreme intellect, but her voice was the dearest and most pleasant to hear. In her company, he felt clever, eloquent, and wise; he possessed fine thoughts and spoke with such grace that it was no wonder he delighted in her presence. Regarding the young lady, we may assume—with gratitude that heaven has not taught me to despise women—that she possesses every attribute necessary to endow her young hero: wit, wisdom, beauty, valor, and virtue.

When George's letter arrived home, it created quite a stir in the circle gathered around Madam Esmond's fireside. He was in love and wished to marry! It was natural, and would keep him safe from harm. If he intended to unite with a well-bred Christian woman, Madam saw no objection.

"I knew they would be setting their caps at him," observed Mountain. "They imagine his wealth equals his estate. He hasn't mentioned whether the young lady has money. I fear she does not."

"People would certainly set their caps at him here," retorted Madam Esmond, casting a grim glance at her dependent. "And try to catch Mr. Esmond Warrington for their own daughters, who are no richer than Miss Lambert may be."

"Suppose your ladyship means me!" exclaimed Mountain. "My Fanny is poor, as you say; and 'tis kind of you to remind me of her poverty!"

"I said people would set their caps at him. If the cap fits you, *tant pis!* as my papa used to say."

"You think, madam, I am scheming to keep George for my daughter?"

"I thank you, on my word! A good opinion you seem to have of us after the years we have lived together!"

"My dear Mountain, I know you much better than to suppose you could ever fancy your daughter would be a suitable match for a gentleman of Mr. Esmond's rank and station," Madam replied with dignity. "Fanny Parker was as good as Molly Benson at school, and Mr. Mountain's daughter is as good as Mr. Lambert's!"

Mrs. Mountain cried out, "Then you did think of marrying her to my son!"
