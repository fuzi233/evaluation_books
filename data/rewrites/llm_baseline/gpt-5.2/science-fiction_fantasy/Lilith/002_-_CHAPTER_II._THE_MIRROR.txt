For some days nothing more disturbed the ordinary order of my life. The week went by with its small homely repetitions—tea that grew cold beside my elbow while I read, the soft complaint of a settling board in the panelled wall, the hush that lived in the library as if it were a creature that fed on dust and paper. Yet the thought of that torn scrap of manuscript kept returning, like a remembered flavour that will not leave the palate. More than once I went back to it, trying with thumb-nail and patience to discover how it had been caught, what invisible tack or glue of time held it so obstinately; but it would not yield to any gentleness, and I would not tear it.

Meanwhile the closet of books began to oppress me. The air in there had the sour, shut-up smell of things long unopened—dry rot and old leather, a faint mouldiness like bread forgotten in a drawer, and that bitter, powdery tang which rises when you disturb paper that has slept too many seasons. I told myself, as one does, that it was mere fastidiousness; but it was a bodily uneasiness, and it grew until, one morning, intention hardened into resolve.

I had pushed my chair back and was in the act of rising to begin the overhauling when movement caught at the edge of my sight. Across the room, coming from the door of the closet, went the old librarian.

Or rather—so little did he declare himself to the full eye—that I can only say: a shadow crossed the light, and in that shadow I received the impression of a slight, stooping man in a shabby dress-coat that fell almost to his heels. The long tails parted a little as he went, and I saw thin legs in black stockings and large feet in wide shoes like slippers, made for silent pacing among shelves. There was something in his going that reminded me of a moth moving along a wall, close to it, never quite alighting, never quite flying.

At once I followed him. I might have been following nothing but my own uneasiness, but I never doubted I was following something.

He slipped out of the library into the hall. The soundscape of the house changed with the threshold: the library held its own deadened hush, as though the books drank sound; the hall returned every noise with a faint hollowness—my footfall in particular, which seemed too loud, too modern, too much an intrusion. Ahead of me the shadow moved without any audible answer, as if he belonged to an older acoustics.

He went straight across to the foot of the great staircase and began to ascend. I went after him, listening against my will to the little protests of the house: the first stair gave a short groan, the next a sharper creak, and then another, in different pitches, like an ill-tuned instrument remembering its age. Yet from the steps before me—where I was sure he must be—there came no sound at all. The contrast made my skin tighten, as if the silence of his passage were a kind of power.

On the first floor, where the chief rooms lay, he continued past them without pause. I, following close, had a sense of being drawn along the same old route the house itself must have known for generations: the corridor’s draught carried a cold smell from unused chambers, and somewhere, far off, a window-frame ticked with a minute, restless rhythm. We came to the foot of a narrower stair that led to the second storey, and up that also he went.

When I reached the top, a strange thing happened: I found myself in a part of the house almost unknown to me.

I had never had brother or sister to drive me, in childhood, into those rollicking explorations that teach the body every turning and nook. I had been taken away while I was still small; and until a month ago—when I returned to take possession—I had not set eyes on the place again. Now, following a figure that seemed less and less to belong to my time, I moved through passage after passage as if through a family memory whose language I had half forgotten. Doors stood with their handles dulled by disuse; the air tasted thinner here, like flour sifted too long. The house spoke in small noises—wood contracting, a distant drip in a pipe, the occasional sigh of a draught—but it spoke without words I could translate into comfort.

At last we came to a door at the bottom of a winding wooden stair. The stair rose tightly, as if it had been threaded into the house like a twist of rope. We ascended. Every step cried out under my weight—one with a long rasp, another with a sharp chirp—each board with its own complaint, as if the staircase had its own dialect of pain. Still I heard nothing from the footfall of my guide, and this, more than the darkness, made the climb uncanny: the house acknowledged me, named me loudly with every creak; it did not acknowledge him at all.

Somewhere about the middle of the winding ascent I lost sight of him. One moment the shadow was ahead, the next it had been swallowed. I hurried the last steps, and when I reached the top, the shadowy shape was nowhere visible. I could not even persuade myself that I saw him. The place was full of shadows—but he was not one of them.

I stood in the main garret, under huge beams and rafters, with great spaces around me and doors set here and there like mouths in a dim face. Long vistas opened into gloom, thinned only by a few cobwebbed windows crouching low and small skylights stained with dust. The air had the dry, sweet staleness of old straw and stored timber; it held the faintest scent of mice and the powder of years. I breathed it and felt, with a strange mingling of awe and pleasure, that this wide expanse—unvisited, unmeasured—was mine.

In the middle of the garret stood an unpainted enclosure of rough planks, its door ajar. The boards were raw and pale, as if they had been put up in haste and never meant to be seen. Thinking Mr. Raven might be there—thinking anything that would give the pursuit a human explanation—I pushed the door and entered.

The small chamber inside was full of light; but it was a light like that which dwells in deserted rooms, having no work to do. It lay upon things without warming them, as if it regretted having come. Dust swam thickly; when I moved, it rose in a soft cloud, and a few rather dim sunrays drew visible paths through the motes, making them look like drifting meal in a shaft of bakery light. That old powdery smell deepened—dry wood, stale cloth, and the faint metallic tang of cold iron.

Against one wall stood a tall mirror with a face dulled by dust, old-fashioned and rather narrow, such as might have reflected many morning faces and candle-flames before it was banished to this upper wilderness. Its frame was ebony, black as char. On the top sat a black eagle with outstretched wings; in his beak he held a golden chain, and from the chain’s end hung a black ball that looked heavier than it ought, like a fruit of darkness.

At first I had been looking at the mirror rather than into it, noting the grime on its surface and the delicate cracks in the varnish of the frame. Then, with the suddenness of a changed note in music, I became aware that it reflected neither the chamber nor my own person.

For an instant I had the impression of the wall melting away. The room’s pale light seemed to withdraw, as if ashamed, and the dust in the sunbeam stopped being dust and became something else in the eye. Could I have mistaken for a mirror the glass that protected a wonderful picture?

Before me lay a wild country—broken, heathy, and open to wind. Desolate hills of no great height rose in the middle distance, yet they looked strange, as if their very shapes belonged to a land with different names for stone. Along the far horizon stretched the tops of a mountain range, blue with distance. Nearest to me lay a tract of moorland, flat and melancholy, the ground coloured like ash and old heather, with scattered stones that seemed to have been waiting a long time.

Being short-sighted, I stepped closer to examine the texture of a stone in the immediate foreground. It was speckled and rough, and the urge to see it clearly took me as naturally as hunger. In that act I espied, hopping toward me with a solemn, measured air, a large and ancient raven. His purply black was here and there softened with gray, as if age had salted him. He came as though looking for worms, pausing and tilting his head, and I felt no astonishment at the appearance of a live creature in a picture—only a sharpening curiosity, like catching a scent you cannot name but must follow.

I took another step forward to see him better, stumbled over something—doubtless the frame of the mirror—and in the same moment found myself nose to beak with the bird.

The air struck my face with the clean chill of open space.

I was no longer in the garret chamber.

I was out of doors, on a houseless heath.
