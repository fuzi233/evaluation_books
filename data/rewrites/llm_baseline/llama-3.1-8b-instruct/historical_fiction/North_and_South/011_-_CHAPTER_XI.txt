As the evening drew to a close, Margaret sat beside her father in the warm glow of the fire, their conversation still swirling with the echoes of John Thornton's words. Mr. Hale couldn't help but steal glances at his daughter, searching for a glimpse of her thoughts. He had known the story of the young Milton manufacturer's past, gleaned from his friend Mr. Bell, but he had expected her to react with more indignation, not quiet admiration.

"Papa, please," Margaret said, her voice low and gentle, "don't look at me like that. I liked his account of himself, not the boasts about Milton, nor the condescending lectures on thriftiness. When he spoke of his mother, I saw something there, something genuine, that made me want to listen. And when he revealed his past, the way he paid off his father's debts, silently and without fanfare...it was almost beautiful."

Her mother's eyes widened in surprise. "Margaret, you're usually so quick to judge those from the wrong side of the tracks. I'm not sure Mr. Hale, you've done us a service by introducing him without warning us. His father's death, in those wretched circumstances...it's a wonder he didn't succumb to the same despair." Her voice trembled slightly. "Why, it might have been the workhouse, and we wouldn't have known the difference."

Mr. Hale nodded thoughtfully. "Perhaps it would have been a lesser evil, my dear. I've heard more of his story from Mr. Bell. His father's wild speculations, his downfall, and the shame that followed. His friends shunned him, and no one came to their aid. The boy and his sister were left to fend for themselves, living on water-porridge for years. Yet, when the time came, he returned to Milton, paid off every debt, and earned a measure of redemption. It's a remarkable tale, and one that speaks to the strength of character within him."

Margaret's eyes sparkled with understanding. "But, papa, that's just it – he sees the world through the lens of wealth and industry. He believes that those who are poor are so because they lack the iron in their veins, the drive to succeed. The poor men around him are beyond his sympathy, seen only as failures, as if their circumstances were a reflection of their character rather than the cruel hand of fate."
