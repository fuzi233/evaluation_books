My Dear Molloy,—I am writing this in case I miss you at your office. I have found out who killed Manderson, as the enclosed dispatch shows. That was my business; yours is to decide what use to make of it. It names a person nobody suspects, and it goes near to accusing him of the murder; so you will not print it before an arrest, and I take it you cannot lawfully print it after an arrest until he has been tried and convicted. When that time comes you can do as you like; and before it comes you may contrive to make something of the facts. That is for you. Meanwhile, will you put Scotland Yard in possession of what I have written? I have done with the Manderson mystery, and I wish to God I had never touched it. Here follows my dispatch. P.T.

Marlstone, June 16th.

I begin this, my third—and likely my last—dispatch to the Record upon the Manderson murder with two contrary sensations, like smelling at once the clean breath of a bakery at dawn and, under it, the sour reek from a drain. Relief first: in my earlier reports I was forced, for plain reasons of justice, to keep back facts I had already learned—facts which, if printed, would have warned a certain person and might well have put him beyond reach; for he is a man of uncommon boldness and resource. Those facts I can now set down. But I do not pretend to enjoy the tale. There is a taint upon it, an aftertaste that clings to the palate; not merely the ugliness of death, but the uglier cunning that can counterfeit the look and sound of domestic life—bedrooms, slippers by a door, a word spoken through a half-open communicating passage—as neatly as one can counterfeit a dish by following its recipe.

It will be remembered that in my first dispatch I described the situation as I found it early on Tuesday morning: how the body was discovered, what state it was in, and how complete was the mystery; I noted one or two local theories; gave some account of the dead man’s domestic surroundings; and followed, in some detail, his movements on the Sunday evening. I mentioned too what may have seemed irrelevant at the time: that a quantity of whisky far beyond Manderson’s nightly habit had vanished from his private decanter since the last hour when he was certainly alive. On the following day—the day of the inquest—I wired little more than an abstract of the proceedings; a verbatim report, at my request, was made by other Record men in court. That day is not yet over as I write; and yet I have already carried through an investigation that leads me directly to the man who must now clear himself of Manderson’s death.

There were, apart from the central enigma of Manderson’s rising long before his usual hour and going out to meet his end, two minor oddities that must have struck thousands who have read the accounts. They were apparent from the first.

The first was this: the body lay not thirty yards from the house, and yet every soul within declared they heard no cry, no struggle, no sound. Manderson had not been gagged. The marks on his wrists spoke of a grapple. And there had been at least one pistol-shot. (I say at least one; for where a firearm is used at close quarters, and hands are clutching and wrenching, the murderer very commonly fires wide once.) The silence puzzled me the more when I learned that Martin the butler was a bad sleeper with an ear as quick as a bat’s, and that his bedroom, with the window open, looked almost straight toward the shed where the body was found. Houses have their own night-music—wind in shrubs, boards contracting, the tick of a distant clock—and a man like Martin learns every note of it; a new sound strikes him as sharply as a wrong word spoken in a dialect you know by heart. Yet he heard nothing.

The second oddity was Manderson’s leaving his dental plate by the bedside. He had risen and dressed himself completely—necktie, watch and chain—and gone out without putting in the plate that carried all the visible upper teeth, a thing he had worn daily for years. It was not as if he had dashed out half-clad. Anyone who lives with such a contrivance knows that putting it in is as habitual as swallowing one’s first mouthful of tea. Speaking, eating—mere human appearance—hang upon it.

At the time neither detail seemed to lead anywhere. They only made me feel, as one sometimes does when a kitchen is too quiet, that something is going on where you cannot see it: a hand working in the scullery, a pan set on the hob without a word spoken, an intention moving soundlessly behind a closed door.

With that preamble I come to the discovery which, within the first few hours of my inquiry, set me on the track that much ingenuity had tried to cover.

I have already described Manderson’s bedroom: its severe bareness of furniture, and the strange contrast between that austerity and the profusion of clothes and shoes, and the way it communicated with Mrs. Manderson’s room. On the upper of the two long shelves where the shoes stood ranged, I found—exactly where I had been told they would be—the patent leather pair Manderson wore on the last evening he was seen alive. I had glanced along the row without any thought of clues; it happens I am something of a judge of shoe-leather, and these were all of first-rate make. But that particular pair caught my eye at once.

They were the light, thin-soled lace-up kind, without toe-caps, very beautifully made. They were old and well worn; but they had been tended, polished, kept on their trees; they looked respectable still. What I noticed was a slight splitting of the leather at the vamp—the place where strain tells when a tight shoe is forced on. Ordinarily a strong stitching guards that point; in both shoes the stitching had parted and the leather below had given. The rent was tiny—scarcely an eighth of an inch; the torn edges, released, had come together again, so that only a connoisseur of worn leather would have remarked it. Even less visible—indeed not visible at all unless you hunted for it—was a slight dragging of the stitches that bind upper to sole. At toe and outer side in each shoe the join showed the telltale tug.

There is only one meaning: the shoes had been worn by someone for whom they were too small.

Manderson’s care for his small, narrow feet was obvious from a glance at his boots and dress shoes; he was well shod, perhaps vainly, but always exactly. None of the other shoes showed such marks. This pair had been strained by a larger foot—and recently; the torn edges were fresh.

The notion that anyone had worn them since Manderson’s death was not worth the breath to utter; the body had been found only some twenty-six hours when I examined them, and in any case why should anyone borrow a dead man’s patent leathers? The possibility that someone had borrowed them in Manderson’s lifetime and spoiled them was almost as remote; with other shoes to choose from, why force these on? Besides, the only other men about were the butler and the two secretaries. I do not say I weighed those possibilities as long as I ought; my mind had begun to run, and in a business like this it is often wisdom to let it run—provided you keep a grip on the lead at the end.

Call it fancy, if you will; call it the familiar psychology of a knot of circumstances suddenly finding its right thread. When chance or labour gives you the key-fact, all your scattered observations fly into a new arrangement around it before you have consciously tasted its whole significance. I had barely formed the thought, “Somebody who was not Manderson has been wearing these shoes,” when a whole flock of remembered details rose, like scents released when a hot pan meets cold wine.

Manderson did not drink much whisky at night—yet whisky had vanished. Manderson was tidy in dress—yet the body, when found, had cuffs dragged up inside the sleeves and the shoes unevenly laced. Manderson was methodical—yet he had apparently risen without washing and had put on the previous evening’s shirt and collar and underclothing. Manderson was particular—yet his watch lay in the wrong waistcoat pocket, the pocket that lacked the leather lining meant to save the case. (I had mentioned these points in my first dispatch; none of us saw their bearing then.) Manderson was uncommunicative to his wife—yet at bedtime he had spoken to her of his doings, and with a certain unnecessary detail. And, strangest of all, Manderson had gone out without his teeth.

All that came upon me in far less time than it takes to set it on paper. Yet when the definite idea sprang up starkly before me—It was not Manderson who was in the house that night—it looked at first like lunacy. Manderson had certainly dined there. People had seen him at close quarters. He had gone out with Marlowe in the car. But—was it he who returned at ten? The question felt ridiculous and yet I could not dismiss it. A faint light began to creep, as over a field at dawn; you do not see the sun yet, but you know it is coming.

I set myself to consider, one by one, those details now newly flavoured by the shoe-leather.

Why would a man force his feet into Manderson’s narrow shoes? The answer came easily. The police understand footprints. But it was not only that the man wished to leave no prints of his own; he wished, if there were prints, that they should be Manderson’s. His whole design, if my guess was right, aimed at producing the belief that Manderson was in the place that night. And he did not merely mean to leave footprints; he meant to leave the shoes themselves. He did so. The housemaid found them outside the bedroom door, where Manderson always left his shoes. She polished them, and later—after the body was found—put them back upon the shelves.

Then the false teeth, which had seemed the wildest touch in the whole affair, fell into place with a click. A dental plate is separable from its owner. If my guess was right, the unknown had brought the denture with him and left it in the bedroom, for the same reason as he left the shoes: to make it impossible for anyone to doubt that Manderson had been there and had gone to bed there. From that followed at once the inference that Manderson was dead before the false Manderson came to the house. Other things agreed with it.

Consider the clothing. If my guess was right, the unknown in Manderson’s shoes had certainly possessed Manderson’s trousers, waistcoat, and shooting jacket. They were in the bedroom; and Martin had seen the unmistakable jacket—one that no one who knew Manderson could confuse—on the man crouched at the telephone in the library. The jacket, I now saw, was a cardinal ingredient in the masquerade. It would make Martin accept, at a glance, what he expected to see.

And then a point struck me so hard that I wondered I had not seen it earlier. So strong is the spell of an assumption that it blinds even trained eyes. Martin had not seen the man’s face—nor had Mrs. Manderson.

Mrs. Manderson, from her evidence at the inquest (I had the Record stenographers take it down verbatim), did not see the man at all. Indeed, as I shall show, she scarcely could have. She merely spoke with him in a drowsy voice through the half-open door, resuming a conversation she had had with her living husband an hour before. Martin could only have seen the man’s back as he crouched over the telephone; he had his hat on—Manderson’s broad-brimmed hat. There is character in the back of a head, in the set of shoulders, in the habitual bend; and a man of roughly the same build, with the right clothes and the nerve to hold still, needs no other disguise than good mimicry and the common human reluctance to look twice at what ought to be.

I stopped there and contemplated the coolness of it. Provided the mimicry held and the nerve held, the thing was safe—almost easy. It was like slipping into a familiar kitchen at night, moving by memory among cupboards and hooks and hearthstones, imitating the cook’s way of setting a saucepan down without a clatter: only some freak accident, some unexpected creak or sudden light, would betray you.

To return to the man’s entrance by the window rather than by the front door. The reason will be obvious. Had he come through the hall he would almost certainly have been heard by Martin in his pantry across the passage; he might have met him face to face, and then no hat and jacket could have saved him.

The whisky now took on a sharper meaning. I had not valued it highly—whisky can vanish in a house as mysteriously as cold meat after Sunday supper—but Martin had been plainly staggered by it. It seemed to me now that many a man, fresh from a bloody business—from stripping a corpse, from carrying a dead weight, from the sickly mingling of gunpowder taint and warm human smell—would turn to the decanter as to an ally. No doubt he drank before he rang for Martin; after pulling off the telephone trick, he likely drank again. But he knew when to stop.

The worst part was still before him: the business—clearly vital to him, for reasons I had yet to nail down—of shutting himself in Manderson’s room and arranging a convincing tableau of occupancy. All this under the slight but nerve-racking risk of the woman on the other side of the half-open door waking and in some way discovering him. Yet even that was calculated. From Mrs. Manderson’s bed, with its head to the wall a little beyond the door, nothing was visible through the doorway but one of the cupboards by Manderson’s bed-head. For her to see more she would have had to rise, go to the door, and look. And this man knew the household ways. He would count on her being asleep; and he would count also on the estrangement between husband and wife—an estrangement they tried to mask by keeping their rooms connected, but which was known to every servant and acquaintance—as making her less likely to take notice of her “husband’s” presence.

So I followed him in imagination up to the bedroom and watched him set about his work. And then, with a tightening at my own throat, I thought of the moment when he must have heard, like a spoon dropped on stone, the sound he dreaded most: Mrs. Manderson’s drowsy voice.

What she actually said she could not recall at the inquest. She thinks she asked whether he had had a good run in the car. Now observe what the unknown did. Here lies, I believe, a point of supreme significance. Not only did he answer her in Manderson’s voice; he volunteered an explanation. He said that, on a sudden inspiration, he had sent Marlowe in the car to Southampton—to get some important information from a man sailing for Paris by the morning boat. Why those details, from a man who had long ceased to confide in his wife, and upon a matter scarcely likely to interest her? Why, above all, those details about Marlowe?

Having carried the story so far, I now put forward definite propositions, based on the hypothesis the shoes forced upon me: that between a time somewhere about ten—when the car started—and a time somewhere about eleven, Manderson was shot, probably at some distance from the house, since no shot was heard; that the body was brought back, laid by the shed, and stripped of its outer clothing; that at some time around eleven a man who was not Manderson, wearing Manderson’s shoes, hat, and jacket, entered the library by the garden window; that he had with him Manderson’s black trousers, waistcoat, and motor-coat, the denture taken from Manderson’s mouth, and the weapon with which he had been murdered; that he hid these, rang for Martin, and sat down at the telephone with his hat on and his back to the door; that he kept the telephone between Martin and his face the whole time Martin was in the room; that when he went upstairs he entered Marlowe’s room quietly and placed the revolver with which the crime had been committed—Marlowe’s own revolver—back into the case on the mantelpiece from which it had been taken; and that he then went into Manderson’s room, put Manderson’s shoes outside the door, threw Manderson’s garments on a chair, laid the denture in the bowl by the bedside, and selected a suit of clothes, a pair of shoes, and a tie from those kept there.

Here I pause to ask the question for which the ground is now ready.

Who was the false Manderson?

Reviewing what I knew—or could with near certainty infer—about that man, I wrote down five conclusions.

(1) He had been in close relations with the dead man. In acting before Martin and speaking to Mrs. Manderson he made no slip.

(2) He was built not unlike Manderson, especially in height and breadth of shoulder, which chiefly determine the look of a seated figure seen from behind when the head is hidden and the body loosely clothed. But his feet were larger, though not enormously larger.

(3) He possessed an aptitude for mimicry and acting—likely some experience.

(4) He had minute acquaintance with the household’s habits.

(5) He was under a vital necessity to create the belief that Manderson was alive and in that house until some time after midnight on Sunday night.

So much I took as certain, or close upon it. It was as far as I could see; and it was far enough.

I now give, in the same order, the relevant facts I was able to learn about Mr. John Marlowe, from himself and from other sources.

(1) He had been Manderson’s private secretary, on terms of great intimacy, for nearly four years.

(2) The two men were nearly of the same height—about five feet eleven. Both were strongly built and heavy-shouldered. Marlowe, being younger by some twenty years, was a trifle slighter through the body, though Manderson was in good physical condition. Marlowe’s shoes (I examined several pairs) were about one shoemaker’s size longer and broader than Manderson’s.

(3) On the afternoon of the first day of my inquiry, after I had reached the results already detailed, I telegraphed a personal friend—an Oxford Fellow I knew to be interested in theatrical matters—in these terms:

Please wire John Marlowe’s record in connection with acting at Oxford some time past decade very urgent and confidential.

He replied next morning (the morning of the inquest):

Marlowe was member O.U.D.S for three years and president 19— played Bardolph Cleon and Mercutio excelled in character acting and imitations in great demand at smokers was hero of some historic hoaxes.

I had been led to send that telegram by seeing on the mantel-shelf in Marlowe’s bedroom a photograph of himself and two others in the costume of Falstaff’s three followers, with an inscription from The Merry Wives, and the imprint of an Oxford photographer.

(4) During his connection with Manderson, Marlowe had lived as one of the family. Apart from the servants no one else had his opportunity of learning, in detail, the domestic life of the Manderson house—its doors, its hours, its habits of speech, even its night-sounds and where they would be masked.

(5) I ascertained beyond doubt that Marlowe arrived at a hotel in Southampton on Monday morning at 6.30, and there proceeded to carry out the commission which, according to his story—and according to the statement made to Mrs. Manderson in the bedroom by the false Manderson—had been entrusted to him by his employer. He then returned by car to Marlstone and displayed great amazement and horror on hearing of the murder.

These are the relevant facts about Marlowe. We must now examine fact number five in connection with conclusion number five about the false Manderson.
