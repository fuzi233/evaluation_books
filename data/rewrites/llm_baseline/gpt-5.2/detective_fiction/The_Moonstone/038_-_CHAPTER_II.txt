“Betteredge,” I said, touching the book on his knee, “has Robinson Crusoe been prophesying again to-night? Has he told you to expect Franklin Blake?”

“By the Lord Harry, Mr. Franklin!” the old man cried. “That’s exactly what he’s done!”

With my hand under his elbow I helped him up from the beehive chair; and for an instant he stood wavering between his amazement at me and his awe of the volume, like a man whose faith has been rewarded too abruptly. The verdict went, as it always went with Betteredge, in favour of print. He held the book open in both hands and stared at it with a hungry, superstitious delight—as if the paper itself might steam and crackle, and the castaway step out smelling of salt and ship’s biscuit.

“Here’s the bit, Mr. Franklin!” he broke out, finding his tongue again. “As I live by bread, sir—here’s the very line I had under my eye the moment before you came in. Page one hundred and fifty-six: ‘I stood like one Thunderstruck, or as if I had seen an Apparition.’ If that isn’t plain English for ‘Expect Mr. Franklin Blake popping up of a sudden’—then there’s no meaning in the language!” He shut the book with a clap that sounded through the deserted hall, and at last gave me the hand I offered.

I was ready for questions. Betteredge had none—not at first. The old domestic instinct sprang up in him, prompt as the striking of a match in a dark kitchen: feed the visitor; make him warm; set the house in order round him, if only for an hour.

“Walk in, Mr. Franklin,” he said, with his quaint bow, holding the door behind him. “I’ll ask what brings you afterwards. I must make you comfortable first. There have been sad changes since you went away. The house is shut up, the servants are gone. Never mind! I’ll cook your dinner; and the gardener’s wife will make your bed—and if there’s a bottle of our famous Latour claret left in the cellar, down your throat, sir, that bottle shall go. Welcome! Heartily welcome!”

It cut me to the quick to balk him. But this was Rachel’s house now. After London—after what had been said and left unsaid there—I could not take meat under her roof, nor sleep between her walls, and keep the smallest self-respect alive. I took Betteredge by the arm and led him out into the garden, where the evening air smelt of damp earth and box and the day’s last warmth lingering in the stone.

There, with the long hush of the grounds round us—the distant rooks settling, the soft stir of leaves, and, beyond that, the faintest edge of road-noise like an ill-mannered whisper—I told him the truth.

Between his loyalty to Rachel and his affection for me, the old man was torn, and his distress showed itself in the very way he set his jaw. When he spoke, he spoke as he always did—plain, homely, and seasoned with that stubborn philosophy which had been brewed in kitchens and pantries rather than in books.

“Miss Rachel has her faults—I’ve never denied it,” he began. “And riding the high horse, now and then, is one of them. She’s been trying to ride over you—and you’ve put up with it. Lord, Mr. Franklin, don’t you know women by this time better than that? You’ve heard me talk of the late Mrs. Betteredge?”

I had heard of the late Mrs. Betteredge often enough—produced, like a sour ingredient, to explain the whole cookery of the sex. He brought her out now with the same certainty, as if her memory lived in the kitchen spice-box.

“Different women have different ways of riding,” he went on. “The late Mrs. Betteredge took her exercise on that animal whenever I denied her anything she had set her heart on. So sure as I came home from my work on those occasions, so sure was my wife to call to me up the kitchen stairs—and say, after my brutal treatment of her, she hadn’t the heart to cook me my dinner.

“I put up with it for a time—just as you’re putting up with it now from Miss Rachel. At last my patience wore out. I went down, and I took Mrs. Betteredge—affectionately, mind—up in my arms, and carried her, holus-bolus, into the best parlour where she received her company. ‘That’s the right place for you, my dear,’ says I; and back I goes to the kitchen. I locked myself in, took off my coat, turned up my shirt-sleeves, and cooked my own dinner. When it was done, I served it up in my best manner—and enjoyed it most heartily. I had my pipe and my drop of grog afterwards; and then I cleared the table, washed the crockery, cleaned the knives and forks, put the things away, and swept up the hearth. When it was as bright and clean again as bright and clean could be, I opened the door and let Mrs. Betteredge in. ‘I’ve had my dinner, my dear,’ says I; ‘and I hope you’ll find I’ve left the kitchen all that your fondest wishes can desire.’ For the rest of that woman’s life, Mr. Franklin, I never had to cook my dinner again!

“Moral: you’ve put up with Miss Rachel in London; don’t put up with her in Yorkshire. Come back to the house!”

It was unanswerable—in its own iron way. I could only tell him that even he, with all his honest tyranny of affection, could not persuade me.

“It’s a lovely evening,” I said. “I shall walk to Frizinghall and stay at the hotel. Come to me to-morrow morning and breakfast with me. I have something to say to you.”

He shook his head slowly.

“I am heartily sorry,” he answered. “I had hoped to hear things were smooth and pleasant again between you and Miss Rachel. If you must have your own way, sir—there’s no need to go to Frizinghall for a bed. It’s to be had nearer than that. There’s Hotherstone’s Farm—barely two miles. You can hardly object to that on Miss Rachel’s account,” he added, with a sly lift at the corner of his mouth. “Hotherstone lives on his own freehold.”

I remembered it directly: a farm-house tucked into a sheltered valley, with a bright stream running near it—the sort of place where the air seems to come strained and sweet from grass and water. The farmer let a spare parlour and bedroom now and again to anglers and sketchers and wanderers. It was, in truth, exactly the refuge I wanted—near enough to the house for business, far enough for pride.

“Are the rooms to let?” I asked.

“Mrs. Hotherstone herself begged my good word for them yesterday,” he said.

“Then I’ll take them with the greatest pleasure.”

We went back into the yard for my travelling-bag. Betteredge put a stick through its handle and swung it over his shoulder; and with the burden came back his first bewilderment. He looked at the house—as if expecting it to speak—then wheeled and looked at me more incredulously still.

“I’ve lived a goodish long time in the world,” said the best old servant breathing, “but the like of this I never did expect to see. There stands the house, and here stands Mr. Franklin Blake—and, Damme, if one of them isn’t turning his back on the other, and going to sleep in a lodging!”

He started off, wagging his head and growling as if he were scolding the very evening for permitting such a thing.

“There’s only one more miracle that can happen,” he flung back at me over his shoulder. “The next thing you’ll do, Mr. Franklin, will be to pay me back that seven-and-sixpence you borrowed when you were a boy.”

The sarcasm comforted him; it put the world straight in his mouth, if nowhere else. We passed through the lodge gates. Once we were outside the grounds, hospitality—by Betteredge’s code—ended, and curiosity began. He dropped back till we walked level, and spoke as if we had met by accident on the road.

“Fine evening for a walk, Mr. Franklin. Supposing you had gone to the hotel at Frizinghall, sir?”

“Yes?”

“I should have had the honour of breakfasting with you to-morrow morning.”

“Then come and breakfast with me at Hotherstone’s Farm instead.”

“Much obliged to you, sir. But it wasn’t exactly breakfast I was driving at.” He gave up his winding approach and came straight out. “If it’s no secret, I’m burning to know what’s brought you down here in this sudden way.”

“What brought me here before?” I asked.

“The Moonstone, Mr. Franklin. But what brings you now, sir?”

“The Moonstone again, Betteredge.”

He stopped dead in the grey thickening twilight and stared as if the words had been shouted wrong by my lips.

“If that’s a joke, sir,” he said slowly, “I’m afraid I’m getting a little dull. I don’t take it.”

“It’s no joke. I have come to take up the inquiry that was dropped when I left England. I have come to do what nobody has done yet—to find out who took the Diamond.”

“Let the Diamond be!” he burst out. “Take my advice and let it be. That cursed Indian jewel has misguided everybody that’s come near it. Don’t waste your money and your temper—in the fine spring-time of your life—by meddling with the Moonstone. How can you hope to succeed, saving your presence, when Sergeant Cuff himself made a mess of it? Sergeant Cuff!” He lifted a stern forefinger, as if the name were a scripture. “The greatest policeman in England!”

“My mind is made up,” I said. “Even Sergeant Cuff doesn’t daunt me. By-the-by, I may want to speak with him sooner or later. Have you heard anything of him?”

“The Sergeant won’t help you, Mr. Franklin.”

“Why not?”

“There’s been an event in police-circles since you went away. The great Cuff has retired. He’s got a little cottage at Dorking; and he’s up to his eyes in the growing of roses. I have it in his own handwriting. He has grown the white moss rose without budding it on the dog-rose first. And Mr. Begbie the gardener is to go to Dorking and own the Sergeant has beaten him at last.”

“It doesn’t much matter,” I answered. “I must do without Sergeant Cuff. And I must trust to you, at starting.”

I spoke, perhaps, too carelessly. Betteredge’s mouth tightened; and there was a small disturbance in his manner—as if something in him had shifted and settled again, like a dish moved in a dark cupboard.

“You might trust to worse than me,” he retorted, a little sharp.

It struck me then that he had something in reserve and was fighting with himself about giving it up.

“I expect you to help me,” I said, “in picking up the fragments of evidence Sergeant Cuff has left behind. I know you can do that. Can you do no more?”

“What more can you expect from me?” he asked, putting on his humblest face as if it were an apron he could tie at will.

“I expect more—from what you said just now.”

“Mere boasting,” he returned stubbornly. “Some people are born boasters, and never get over it. I’m one.”

There was only one way to reach him. I went to the heart—Rachel.

“Betteredge, would you be glad to hear that Rachel and I were good friends again?”

“I’ve served your family to mighty little purpose if you doubt it!”

“Do you remember how Rachel treated me before I left England?”

“As well as if it was yesterday. My lady herself wrote you a letter about it; and you were so good as to show it to me. It said Miss Rachel was mortally offended with you for the part you had taken in trying to recover her jewel. And neither my lady, nor you, nor anybody else could guess why.”

“True. And I come back and find her mortally offended still. I knew the Diamond was at the bottom of it last year, and I know it is at the bottom of it now. I have tried to see her—she won’t see me. I have tried to write—she won’t answer. How am I to clear it up? The chance of searching into the loss of the Moonstone is the one chance of inquiry that Rachel herself has left me.”

He listened; and the quiet around us deepened—the stream-sound, far off, like someone gently stirring broth; the last birds calling themselves to rest. The world had room for plain words there.

“There is no ill-feeling in this, Mr. Franklin, on your side—is there?” he asked at last.

“There was some anger when I left London,” I said. “But it’s worn out. I want Rachel to come to an understanding with me—and I want nothing more.”

He hesitated, then spoke with jealous tenderness for his young mistress, as if even her name needed guarding.

“You don’t feel any fear, sir—supposing you make discoveries—in regard to what you may find out about Miss Rachel?”

“I am as certain of her as you are,” I answered. “The fullest disclosure of her secret will reveal nothing that can alter her place in your estimation—or in mine.”

That swept away what was left of his scruple. He threw up his hands, as if surrendering to fate and affection together.

“If I am doing wrong to help you,” he said, “all I can say is—I’m as innocent of seeing it as the babe unborn! I can put you on the road to discovery, if you can only go on by yourself. You remember that poor girl of ours—Rosanna Spearman?”

“Of course.”

“You always thought she had some sort of confession about this matter of the Moonstone that she wanted to make to you?”

“I couldn’t account for her strange conduct in any other way.”

“You may set that doubt at rest whenever you please.”

It was my turn to stop. The twilight had thickened; his face was lost to me, but I could hear, in the set of his voice, that he meant every syllable.

“What do you mean?” I asked, more impatiently than I intended.

“Steady, sir,” he said. “I mean what I say. Rosanna Spearman left a sealed letter behind her—a letter addressed to you.”

“Where is it?”

“In the possession of a friend of hers at Cobb’s Hole. You must have heard tell of Limping Lucy—a lame girl with a crutch.”

“The fisherman’s daughter?”

“The same.”

“Why wasn’t the letter forwarded to me?”

“Limping Lucy has a will of her own. She wouldn’t give it into any hands but yours. And you had left England before I could write.”

“Then let’s go back and get it at once.”

“Too late to-night. They’re great savers of candles along our coast; and they go to bed early at Cobb’s Hole.”

“Nonsense! We might get there in half an hour.”

“You might, sir. And when you did, you’d find the door locked.”

He pointed down the slope. A small light glimmered below us; and in the same moment the bubbling of a stream rose clearer in the still evening air—pure country sound, undrowned by wheels or engines, as if the valley kept its own old language of water and stone while the louder world forgot how to listen.

“There’s the Farm, Mr. Franklin. Make yourself comfortable for to-night, and come to me to-morrow morning—if you’ll be so kind?”

“You will go with me to the fisherman’s cottage?”

“Yes, sir.”

“Early?”

“As early as you like.”

We went down the path that led to Hotherstone’s Farm.
