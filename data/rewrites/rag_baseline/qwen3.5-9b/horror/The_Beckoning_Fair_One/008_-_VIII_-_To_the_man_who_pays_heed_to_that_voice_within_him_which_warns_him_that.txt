To the man who listens to the whisper inside him—the warning voice that signals twilight and peril settling over his soul—terror appears as an absolute, monolithic wall against which the heart must brace itself in a heartbeat, lest the entire architecture of his nature collapse. Fortunately, such a man rarely needs to search far for defenses. Against the immediate, mundane, and fleeting terrors of existence, he constructs fortresses out of usage, observation, convention, and the rigid codes of society. He is even willing to categorize joy alongside fear as absolute entities, useful tools for survival, until the final act of treason occurs: the dismantling of these arbitrary boundaries, an act that threatens not just one individual, but the collective welfare of every soul.

In Oleron, this treason had begun. It started subtly, with an admission of the inexplicable and the horrific into a growing intimacy. He moved insensibly, unconsciously, treating it as an insult for Elsie Bengough to dictate the rules anymore. Only two months prior, the label "haunted house" applied to his beloved dwelling would have sent ice through his marrow; now, his sensory scale depressed, he could casually ask, "Haunted by what?" remaining blissfully unaware that horror, once proven relative, loses its sting. He was erasing the landmarks. Mists and confusion wrapped around him, yet he felt nothing so sharply as a voracious, gnawing curiosity. He needed *to know*. Resolution hardened in his chest; nothing short of truth would suffice. Craftily, he searched for a method to unlock the mystery.

He need not have searched. The path was effortless. Just as in his youth, when his thoughts rose spontaneously to form words that refused alteration, so now the questions he posed seemed to answer themselves the instant they were spoken. There was a thrilling exhilaration in this swift, frictionless process, a joy in his own agency he had not felt since his writing was once a daily delight. It felt as though the course of action was being dictated directly to him. Naturally, the first step was definition. He framed the problem mathematically: Given that he lacked privacy, given that the old house had captured his spirit, and given that this unknown co-tenant shared a common denominator with him, what was the variable? Clearly, the nature of the other numerator had to be identified. How? Usually, this would seem complex, but to Oleron, it was pellucid. The key lay in his unfinished novels—or rather, in both *Romillys*, the old and the proposed new one.

A short while ago, Oleron would have deemed such a theory madness; now, he accepted the dizzying hypothesis without a tremor. He opened the first *Romilly* and the second. Instantly, the investigation leaped forward. He recalled vividly finding the heroine insufficient on the very first morning he worked in the new place. Memories of his aversion surged to confirm his obscure probe. There had been the night he nearly hurled the manuscript into the fire, followed by the next morning's feverish planning of the new *Romilly*. It was then that Mrs. Barrett, overhearing him hum a phrase suggested by a dripping tap, informed him he was singing an air he had never heard: "The Beckoning Fair One."

"The Beckoning Fair One!" he murmured, the title echoing in his mind. The first *Romilly* had been definitively discarded; the second had instantly fastened itself upon him, clamoring for birth in his brain. He even fancied, looking back, that there had been a passion, a hatred, in the supplanting—that stray thoughts given to his discarded creation had offended the usurper. Astonishingly, Oleron found the almost unthinkable idea credible: that a malignancy almost homicidal could extend to his fiction's poor mortal prototype.

Despite his acclimatization to a scale where the horrible was a thing to be handled and turned over, a "Good God!" escaped Oleron. The intrusion of the first *Romilly*'s prototype into his thoughts terminated his inquiry; the mere thought of Elsie was fatal to abstraction. Furthermore, he could not recall Barrett's letter or the scene that followed without a rising flush and a tightening brow. Wisely or not, he had argued the point immediately. Striding across the square the next morning, he had confronted Barrett on his own doorstep. Returning minutes later, he felt he had only exacerbated the situation. The man was vagueness incarnate, incapable of yielding anything more definite than a muttering farrago repeating "certain things," "Mrs. Barrett," "respectable house," "if the cap fits," and "nameless proceedings."

"Not that I make any charge—" Barrett had concluded.

"Charge!" Oleron had cried. "I have my ideas of things, as I don't doubt you have yours—"

"Ideas—mine!" Oleron had shouted wrathfully, immediately dropping his voice as heads appeared at the windows of the square. "Listen here, my man; you have an unwholesome mind, which you probably cannot help, but a tongue which you can control, and shall! If there is a breath of this repeated..."

"I'll not be talked to on my own doorstep like this by anybody..." Barrett had blustered.

"You shall, and I am doing it..."

"Don't you forget there's a God above all, Who 'as said..."

"You're a low scandalmonger!..."

And so it went, a poorly begun fight continuing badly. Oleron returned wrathfully to his home, and thereafter, gazing from his window, he saw Barrett's face at odd intervals, lifting blinds or peering around curtains, as if seeking evidence for some celestial tribunal. The incident necessitated minor changes in Oleron's domestic arrangements. Barrett's tongue had already been busy; he was viewed askance by the neighbors. Judging it wiser to avoid local scrutiny until he could secure other assistance, Oleron shifted his provision shopping to distant markets. Otherwise, housekeeping was familiar territory, and he would resume his bachelor habits. Moreover, he was deep in abstruse investigations requiring solitude.

One midday, weary and unwell, glad that he need not venture outdoors, Oleron looked from his window and saw Elsie Bengough crossing the square toward his house. The weather had broken; it was a raw, gusty day. She fought the wind, forcing her way through as it billowed her ample skirts around her opulent figure and spun her veil in a chaotic stream behind her. Oleron acted swiftly, instinctively. Seizing his hat, he sprinted to the door and thundered down the stairs. Panic seized him. She must be prevented from setting foot in the place. Running along the alley, he felt his eyes drawn upward to the eaves, as if something invisible pulled them; he did not realize a slate might fall.

He met her at the gate and spoke with curious volubility. "This is really too bad, Elsie! Just as I'm urgently called away! I'm afraid it can't be helped, though, and that you'll have to think me an inhospitable beast." He poured out excuses as they formed. She asked if he was going to town.

"Yes, yes—to town," he replied. "I've got to call on—on Chambers. You know Chambers, don't you? No, I remember you don't; a big man you once saw me with.... I ought to have gone yesterday, and—"this felt like a brilliant improvisation—"and he's leaving town this afternoon. To Brighton. I received a letter from him this morning."

He took her arm and led her up the square. She reminded him that the route to town lay the opposite direction.

"Of course—how stupid of me!" he laughed loudly. "I'm so used to going the other way with you—of course; it's the other way to the bus. Will you come along with me? I am so awfully sorry it's happened like this...."

They walked to the bus terminus. This time, Elshowed no sign of internal struggle. If she detected the unusual tension in his manner, she made no comment, and he, seeing her calm, began speaking less recklessly through the silences. By the time they reached the terminus, no observer would have guessed that the pale man without an overcoat and the girl in the large skirt were each ready to sink to their knees in gratitude for saving the other from a wildly unthinkable danger.

They climbed to the top of the bus. Oleron protested forgetting his coat and claimed the day felt oppressively hot. They settled on a front seat. Now that this encounter was forced upon him, he had another demand on his tact. It had been weighing on his mind, peculiarly difficult to articulate. Revolving it for minutes, he remembered the success of his sudden-call-to-town story and cut the knot of difficulty with another lie.

"I'm thinking of going away for a little while, Elsie," he said.

She merely replied, "Oh?"

"Somewhere for a change. I need a change. I think I shall go tomorrow, or the day after. Yes, tomorrow, I think."

"Yes," she replied.

"I don't quite know how long I shall be," he continued. "I shall have to let you know when I am back."

"Yes, let me know," she replied in a tone that was, for her, suspiciously even.

He felt a flicker of unease. "You don't ask me where I'm going," he said, making a clumsy effort to rally her.

She stared straight ahead, past the driver.

"I know," she said.
