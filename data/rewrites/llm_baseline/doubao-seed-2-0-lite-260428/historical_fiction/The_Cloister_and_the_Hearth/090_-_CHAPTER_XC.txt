The day after that shattering, terrible quarrel, the small cottage on Hoog Straet lay as still and silent as a fresh grave. Even Catherine, who was by nature the sprightliest, busiest soul in the parish, moved through the rooms listless and hollow-eyed, her cheeks streaked with the traces of hours of weeping. Once the dinner dishes were cleared, she slipped off to the convent, determined at the very least to speak to Gerard, to lay the first small stone that might one day mend the rift between them. It took her a long while to make the doddery old porter understand exactly which brother she was inquiring after; when he did, he broke the news gently that Gerard had left the convent grounds late the night before, and no one knew when he would return.

Sighing heavy, she trundled off to bring the news to Margaret, and found her sunk in a chair, her hands idle, as if life had lost all its salt for her. She clutched her little boy so tight to her chest it almost hurt, as if he was the last scrap of everything she had left, and half-convinced someone would creep in and tear him away too. Catherine begged her to come back to the Hoog Straet house, but Margaret only drew a weary breath. “What for?” she sighed. “You must all think, in your hearts, that I am the cause of every last bit of this ruin.”
“Nay, nay,” Catherine hurried to reassure her, “we are not so hard-hearted. Eli dotes on you, you know; maybe you will be the one to soften his temper enough to make things right.”
“If you think I can do any good at all, I’ll come,” Margaret said, and rose slowly, no fight left in her limbs.

When they stepped over the threshold of the house, they found Eli standing beside a carpenter, nailing a new name plaque over the spot where Cornelis and Sybrandt’s had once hung. The name carved into the wood, plain and bold, was Margaret Brandt’s. For all her love for the girl, the sight drove a knife straight through Catherine’s heart. “One man’s bane is another’s meat, that’s the truth of it,” she murmured.
“You cannot force me to spend that coin unjustly,” Margaret said, cold and steady.
“You are a good soul,” Catherine conceded. “And best to make peace with it, sith Eli is master of this house, after all.”

The next day, Giles dropped by for a visit. Catherine poured out the whole sorry tale, framing the two wayward sons as poor, cursed creatures cast out onto the wide world by their own father, and begged him to take pity on them. But Giles’s sympathies ran entirely the other way: he heard her out, then said bluntly the two knaves had got off far lighter than they deserved, and ought to have been hanged right outside Margaret’s door for all the harm they had done. He dismissed them with a snort of contempt, then brightened, crowing with delight at the thought of his favourite Gerard soon coming home. “I’ll show him,” he swore, “what it is to have a brother in court who has the heart to serve his friend, and the wits to point him straight to the best possible place.”
“Bless thee, Giles,” Margaret whispered soft.
“Thou wast ever his truest friend, dear Giles,” little Kate added, “but alack, I cannot see what thou canst do for him now, after all that has befallen.”

Once Giles had left and the house settled back into its heavy, sad quiet, a well-dressed man pushed the door open softly, and asked if Margaret Brandt lived there. Catherine perked right up, her old gossipy, curious self peeking through her gloom immediately. “D’ye hear, lass? You’ve a visitor,” she called.
“Well, mother,” Margaret said listlessly, “here I am.”
Shuffling feet sounded at the threshold, and a colourless, gaunt old man was helped into the room. It was Ghysbrecht Van Swieten. Catherine shrieked, and threw her apron over her head. Margaret shuddered so hard her whole body jolted, and twisted her head sharply away so she would not have to look at the man.
A thin, wobbly voice drifted from his lips. “Good people, a dying man has come to beg your forgiveness.”
“Come to gloat over the work of your own hands, you mean,” Catherine said, yanking her apron down and bursting into sobs. “There, look – she’s fainting! Eli, quick, tend to her.”
“Nay,” Margaret said, her voice faint, “the sight of him gave me a turn, that is all. Prithee, let him say his piece and go. For he is the man who murdered every good thing in me and mine.”
“Alas,” Ghysbrecht breathed, “I am far too feeble to say all this standing, and not one of you has bidden me sit.”
Eli, who had followed him in, spoke up half sullen, half ashamed. “Well, burgomaster, it is not our custom to leave a guest standing while we sit. But man, man – you have wrought us too much ill.” His honest voice shook with the rage he fought hard to contain, for this was his own hearth.

Then little Kate, the quiet one whose words seldom fell on deaf ears in that family, spoke. “Father, mother, I owe you all duty, but this is not right. Death squares every debt. And can you not see the reaper standing at his shoulder? I shall not live long, good friends – and his time is shorter than mine.”
Eli hurried to drag a chair over for their dying enemy with his own two hands. Ghysbrecht’s attendants settled him into it, then carried in two heavy oak boxes and withdrew, leaving their master alone with the family he had tormented for so long. Every eye was fixed on him save Margaret’s. His fingers trembled as he undid the latches, and pulled from the first box the full set of title deeds to every house and acre of land in Tergou that had once belonged to Floris Brandt.
“This land was yours by right, as his granddaughter,” he rasped. “I seized it over a debt that was paid off, long ago, with interest. I kept it out of greed, but I restore it now, with penitent tears.” He opened the second box, and it glinted with three hundred and forty golden angels, stacked neat. “This is all the rent and fines I collected from that land, every penny over and above the debt Floris owed me. I kept count all these years, always telling myself I would make it right one day – but Avarice held me back. I was not born a thief, as you all know.”
“Well, will you look at that!” Catherine gasped. “And you the burgomaster, who whipped more thieves in the town square than I can count! Still, better late than never. Margaret, what ails you, lass? The man has brought you back what is yours. You are a rich woman now – alack, what a mountain of gold!”
“Bid him keep the land and the gold,” Margaret said, still not turning her head, “and give me back my Gerard, that he stole from me with his lies and his treason.”
“Alas,” Ghysbrecht sighed, “would that I could. All that was in my power to do, I have done. I dragged myself from my deathbed to bring this myself, lest some clerk or greedy kin steal it from you before it reaches your hands.”
“Old man,” Margaret said, “since you, who worshipped pelf all your life, have done this, I pray God and the saints will forgive you. But I am no saint, no angel – only a poor woman whose heart you broke. Speak for me, Kate. I feel half dead.”
Kate’s soft, silvery voice fell over the room like cool rain. “My poor sister has a grief no amount of gold can mend. Give her time, Ghysbrecht. It is not in nature for her to forgive you all at once. Her boy is fatherless, and she is neither maid, wife, nor widow – the wound was torn open only two days past, and it bleeds still. Give her time. Ere you die, she will forgive you all, even to please me – for I shall not be long behind you, Ghysbrecht. The rest of us, whose wounds are sore but not half so deep, forgive you, a penitent dying man. I, for one, will pray for you from this hour. Go in peace.”
Eli even invited him to stay to break a crust of manchet bread and drink a stoup of spiced wine to strengthen him for the journey home, but Ghysbrecht declined. “All that land I clung to for decades was nothing but a bed of nettles to me,” he said. “Now it is gone, and I feel lighter, livelier, than I have in years.” His attendants lifted him into his litter, and he was gone.

Catherine immediately fell to poring over the golden coins, she had never seen so much wealth piled in one place in her life, and grew almost cross with Margaret for sitting there like a wooden statue. She gushed on and on about all the good things they could do with the money, nagging Margaret until she finally consented to come and look at it.
“Better leave her be, mother,” Kate pleaded. “How can she relish gold, when her heart in her bosom is liker lead?”
But Catherine would not be put off. Margaret looked down at all her glinting new wealth, her eyes wide and uncomprehending. Then suddenly she wrung her hands, and a piercing cry tore out of her that echoed off the cottage walls:
She collapsed into ragged, heaving hysterics, sobbing over the fortune that had arrived far too late to share with the man she loved. A fraction of that gold, a single plot of that land, even a year or two prior – and Gerard would have never left her side, never run off for Italy chasing fortune.
Too late. Too late.
