“That doubt and trouble, fear and pain,
And anguish, all, are shadows vain,
That death itself shall not remain;
That weary deserts we may tread,
A dreary labyrinth may thread.
Thro’ dark ways underground be led;
Yet, if we will one Guide obey,
The dreariest path, the darkest way
Shall issue out in heavenly day;
And we, on divers shores now cast,
Shall meet, our perilous voyage past,
All in our Father’s house at last!”

The moment their last visitor stepped off the Hale threshold, Margaret flew up the stairs, her skirts swishing around her ankles, to yank on her bonnet and wool shawl, desperate to reach the Higgins cottage before dinner and sit a while by Bessy’s side. As she navigated the narrow, thrumming, smoke-hazed streets of Milton, she realized how much more meaning those grim, row-lined lanes held now that she cared for one of the people who called them home—they were no longer just a strange, uninviting backdrop to her family’s displacement, but spaces where lives of quiet struggle unfolded.

Mary Higgins, Bessy’s slatternly but well-meaning younger sister, had done her best to tidy the small cottage in anticipation of Margaret’s visit. She’d scrubbed a rough patch of clean stone in the center of the floor, though the flagstones pressed under the chairs and tables, and those running along the walls, still bore their dark, unwashed patina of years of factory soot. Though the day hung heavy and hot, a great roaring fire blazed in the grate, turning the tiny room into a sweltering oven. Margaret did not guess that Mary’s lavish pile of coal was a deliberate gesture of welcoming hospitality, and assumed the oppressive heat was being kept up for Bessy’s comfort. The sick girl herself lay stretched on a short squab sofa tucked under the only window, far frailer than she had been the day before, worn out from straining to lift herself at every passing footstep, craning to see if Margaret had finally arrived. Now that she was here, Bessy sank back against the cushions, content to gaze at Margaret’s face and brush her fingers over the fine weave of her visitor’s dress, with the soft, unselfconscious admiration of a child.

“I never understood why folk in the Bible made such a fuss over soft raiment before,” she murmured, her voice thin. “But it must be lovely to go dressed like you do. It’s nothing like the rough common wear I’ve always known. Most fine folks’ bright gaudy colours tire my eyes right out, but yours, somehow, rest them. Where did you get that gown?”

“In London,” Margaret laughed softly, settling her chair closer to the squab.

“London? You’ve lived in London?”
“Yes. We were there for years, before we moved to Helstone—our old country home, deep in the middle of a forest.”
“Tell me about it,” Bessy said, leaning back, closing her eyes, and lacing her hands over her breast as if to cradle every word Margaret offered.

Margaret had barely spoken of Helstone since they’d left it, save for offhand mentions when the place slipped unbidden into her thoughts; she dreamed of it more vividly than her waking life, and her memory wandered its sun-dappled paths every night before she fell asleep. But her heart opened fully to this sick girl at her side. “Oh, Bessy, I loved that home more than I can say. I wish you could see it. I can’t tell you half its beauty. Great old trees stand all around it, their branches stretching long and level to make a deep, restful shade even at noon. And though every leaf looks perfectly still, there’s a soft, rushing sound of wind moving through them, always, far enough away that it never crowds you. The turf there is sometimes as soft and fine as velvet, and sometimes thick and lush with the constant trickle of a little hidden brook that tinkles just out of sight. Then there are wide stretches of rolling ferns, some deep in the green shadow, others streaked with long swathes of golden sunlight—they look just like the sea.”

“I’ve never seen the sea,” Bessy whispered. “Go on.”

“And then there are wide commons, high up, so that you stand above the very tops of all the trees—”
“I’m glad of that. I always feel smothered, down below. When I get a rare hour off work, I run as high as I can climb, just so I can see far away, and take a deep breath of that full, open air. I get smothered enough in Milton. That soft noise of the trees you talk of, going on forever and ever? It would never make my head ache the way the mill’s roar does. I guess on those commons there’s hardly any noise at all?”
“Nothing but a lark, high up in the sky sometimes,” Margaret assured her. “Once in a while I’d hear a farmer shouting sharp and loud at his servants, but it was so far away it just felt nice, like a little reminder that other people were hard at work somewhere distant, while I sat on the heather and did nothing at all.”

“I used to think, once, that if I could just have one whole day of doing nothing, just to rest—one day in a quiet place like that—maybe it would heal me right up. But now I’ve had weeks and weeks of idleness, and I’m just as tired of them as I was of my work. Sometimes I’m so worn out I think I can’t even enjoy heaven unless I get a proper piece of rest first. I’m half afraid to go straight there without a good long sleep in the grave to set me up right.”

“Don’t be afraid, Bessy,” Margaret said, laying her hand over the girl’s. “God can give you a more perfect rest than any idle day on earth, or even the deep sleep of the grave, ever could.”

Bessy shifted restlessly on the cushions. “I wish father wouldn’t talk to me the way he does. He means well, I’ve told you that before, and I’ll say it a hundred times over. But you see, even if I don’t believe a word of what he says by daylight, when the fever’s on me at night, half asleep and half awake, all those dark thoughts come crashing back on me—oh, so bad! I think, what if this is all there is? What if the whole point of me being born is just to work my heart and my life away, to sicken in this dreary place, with the mill’s noise ringing in my ears forever, until I want to scream just to make it stop and let me have one little bit of quiet—with the cotton fluff filling my lungs until I’m thirsting to death for one long deep breath of that clear air you talk about? And my mother’s gone, and I never got to tell her how much I loved her, never got to tell her half my troubles. If this life is all there is, and there’s no God to wipe all tears away—you wench, you!” she cried, sitting bolt upright and clutching Margaret’s hand tight, almost fiercely, “I could go mad, I could kill you, I swear I could.” She fell back then, completely spent by the force of her outburst.

Margaret knelt down beside the squab. “Bessy—we have a Father in Heaven.”

“I know it! I know it,” Bessy moaned, turning her head from side to side restlessly. “I’m so wicked. I spoke so wickedly. Oh, don’t be frightened of me, and never come back again. I wouldn’t harm a hair on your head. And,” she added, opening her eyes to look straight and earnestly at Margaret, “maybe I even believe more of what’s coming than you do. I’ve read the Book of Revelations so many times I know it off by heart, and I never doubt, when I’m awake and in my right senses, all the glory I’m going to see someday.”

“Let’s not talk about the fancies that come to you when you’re feverish,” Margaret said gently. “I’d much rather hear about what you used to do when you were well.”

“I think I was still well when mother died, but I never was rightly strong after that. I went to work in the carding room not long after, and the fluff got into my lungs and poisoned me.”

“Fluff?” Margaret asked, confused.

“Fluff,” Bessy repeated. “Little tiny bits that fly off the cotton when they’re combing it out, fill the whole room until the air looks like fine white dust. They say it wraps itself around your lungs and tightens them up. Either way, there’s dozens and dozens of folks who work in the carding rooms that waste away, coughing and spitting blood, poisoned right by that fluff.”

“But can’t anything be done to stop it?” Margaret asked, aghast.

“I don’t know. Some masters have a great big wheel at one end of the carding room that makes a draught, and pulls all the dust out of the air. But that wheel costs a fortune—five or six hundred pounds, maybe, and it don’t make any profit. So barely a handful of masters will put them up. And I’ve heard of men who didn’t want to work in the mills that had them, said that after years of swallowing fluff, going without it made them ravenously hungry, and that their wages ought to be raised if they had to work in places with those wheels. So between the masters not wanting to spend the money, and the men not wanting to work the new way, the wheels never get put up. But I wish to God our factory had one.”

“Didn’t your father know all this?” Margaret asked.

“He did, and he was sorry. But our mill was one of the better ones, all in all, with a steady set of workers. Father was scared to let me go work at some strange place, and though you’d never guess to look at me now, everyone used to call me a proper, fine lass back then. And I didn’t want to be thought soft and useless. And mother said Mary’s schooling had to be paid for, and father was always buying books, going to lectures of one kind and another—all of that costs money. So I just kept on working, until I’ll never get that whirr of the mill out of my ears, or the fluff out of my throat, not in this world. That’s all.”

“How old are you, Bessy?”
“Nineteen, come July.”
“I’m nineteen too.” Margaret’s throat tightened, her heart heavy with the terrible, unfair contrast between their two lives. She couldn’t speak for a moment, fighting down the swell of tears.

“About Mary,” Bessy said, after a pause. “I wanted to ask you to be a friend to her. She’s seventeen, the last of us, and I don’t want her going to work in the mill. But I don’t know what else she’s fit for.”

Margaret glanced unconsciously at the un scrubbed corners of the small room. “She could hardly take on a servant’s position just yet, could she? We have an old faithful servant, almost a member of the family, who’s looking for an assistant, but she’s very particular about how the house is kept. It wouldn’t be fair to her to send Mary over untrained, and make her lives harder for both of them.”

“No, I see you’re right. Our Mary’s a good girl, but who’s ever been there to teach her how to keep a house? No mother, and me at the mill until I was too sick for anything but snapping at her for doing badly the things I never even knew how to do right myself. But I still wish she could live with you, all the same.”

“But even if she’s not quite ready to come work for us as a servant right now—and I don’t know what may change in time—I will always try to be a friend to her, for your sake, Bessy. I have to go now, but I’ll come back as soon as I can. If I don’t make it tomorrow, or the next day, or even for a whole week or a fortnight, don’t think I’ve forgotten you. I might be busy with things at home.”

“I know you won’t forget me. I won’t mistrust you ever again. But remember—by the time a week or a fortnight passes, I might be dead and buried.”

“I’ll come back as soon as I can, Bessy,” Margaret said, squeezing her hand tight. “And you’ll send someone to let me know if you get any worse, won’t you?”

“Ay, that I will,” Bessy said, pressing her hand back weakly.

From that day forward, Mrs. Hale grew more and more of a suffering invalid. The anniversary of Edith’s marriage was drawing near, and looking back over the whole heap of troubles the past year had piled at her feet, Margaret wondered how they had all managed to bear it. If she could have foreseen every last one of them, she would have shrunk away and hidden herself from the coming time in terror. But day by day, each one had been bearable on its own; small, bright, little sparks of quiet joy had sparkled right in the middle of all her sorrows. A year before, back when they first arrived in Milton, and Margaret had first grown quietly aware of the new fretfulness in her mother’s temper, she would have groaned bitterly at the thought of her mother bearing a long, painful illness in a strange, grim, noisy busy place, with every possible comfort of their old Helstone home stripped away. But now, as her mother’s real pain grew worse, a new, gentle patience had risen up in her. She was softer and quieter through even her worst bouts of bodily suffering, almost in exact proportion to how restless and fretful she had been in the months before, when there had been no real cause for grief at all.

Mr. Hale, for his part, was in exactly that stage of anxious denial that takes hold of men of his gentle, thoughtful temperament: deliberate, willful blindness. He grew more irritated than Margaret had ever seen him when she voiced her worries about her mother.

“Margaret, you are getting far too fanciful! Heaven knows I would be the very first to sound the alarm if your mother was truly unwell. Back in Helstone, we always knew the moment she had one of her headaches, even before she told us. Now look at her—she has a bright, healthy flush in her cheeks, just like she did when I first met her all those years ago.”

“But Papa,” Margaret said, hesitating, “I think that bright colour is the flush of pain.”

“Nonsense, Margaret. I tell you, you’re the one who’s not well. I’ll send for the doctor tomorrow for you, and then, if it will ease your silly mind, he can look in on your mother while he’s here.”

“Thank you, dear Papa. That will make me so much happier.” She stepped forward to kiss his cheek, but he pushed her away—gently, but firmly, as if her worries were unpleasant ideas he wanted to banish as quickly as he wanted her out of the room. He paced restlessly up and down the parlor floor. “Poor Maria,” he muttered half to himself. “I wish one could do what is right without hurting the people you love. I will hate this town, and hate myself too, if she—” He broke off, turning back to Margaret. “Tell me, does your mother often talk to you of the old places? Of Helstone, I mean?”

“No, Papa,” Margaret said, her voice sad.

“Then there, you see! She can’t be fretting for it, can she? It’s always been a comfort to me that your mother is so simple and open, that I know every little grievance she has the moment it troubles her. She would never hide anything serious that was hurting her health from me, would she, Margaret? I am quite sure she would never do such a thing. So don’t let me hear any more of these foolish, morbid ideas. Come, give me a kiss, and run off to bed.”

Margaret kissed him, and climbed the stairs to her room, but long after she had finished her slow, tired undressing, long after she had settled under her covers, she could still hear him pacing back and forth across the parlor carpet, his heavy, unsteady steps sounding through the quiet house, as restless as the unspoken worries he was doing his best to outrun.
