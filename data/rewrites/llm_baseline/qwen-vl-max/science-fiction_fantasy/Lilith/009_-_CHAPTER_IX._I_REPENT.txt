I placed the manuscript down, comforted by the thought that my father had glimpsed the hidden world beyond ordinary sight—and that he had known Mr. Raven. But then it struck me: I had never learned how my father died. The silence around his passing now seemed ominous. Had he, too, followed Mr. Raven into that quiet realm and never returned? The idea filled me with shame for my own flight.

What wonders might I have uncovered by now—about life, death, and the vast unseen territories beyond perception? The Ravens were not cruel or deceitful; they were simply different. One possessed a rare gift of insight, the other an ethereal beauty. And yet I had doubted them, dismissed them as threats, refused their hospitality! My pride had blinded me to their kindness, and now I was consumed with regret.

Why had I feared the dead? To share in their sacred stillness should have been an honor—one I had proven myself unworthy of. What harm could the sleeping king or the lady with the wound in her palm have done me? I longed for the calm dignity of their faces, the peace of their presence. Overcome, I wept.

Weeping, I collapsed onto a couch and fell into a deep sleep. I woke abruptly, as if summoned by a voice. The house was silent, like an empty church. A blackbird sang on the lawn.

“I must go to them,” I whispered. “I must tell them I am sorry. I will do whatever they ask.”

I rose and climbed the stairs to the garret. The wooden chamber was unchanged—the mirror dimly reflecting the room. It was nearly noon, and the sun would be higher than when I first arrived. I needed to adjust the mirrors. If only I’d seen Mr. Raven do it!

I pulled the chains, letting light fall upon the first mirror. Then I turned to the second. There—faint, trembling, like a landscape seen through rippling water—were the shapes from my earlier vision. I touched the glass. It was solid, impenetrable.

Suspecting polarization, I shifted the mirrors again and again, adjusting their angles until, by chance, clarity emerged. The mountains stood blue and steady, sharp and real.

I stepped forward—and my feet touched heather.

I remembered only vaguely the path to the cottage: through a pine forest. I wandered through thickets and small fir woods, each time thinking I recognized something familiar—but no forest appeared. The sun sank low, and the air grew cold with the approach of winter. Then, joyfully, I saw a dark shape moving toward me. It was the raven.

I rushed to meet him.

“I beg your pardon, sir,” I said, breathless. “For my rudeness last night. Will you take me with you now? I confess—I don’t deserve it.”

He looked up, then paused. “Ah,” he said softly. “My wife does not expect you tonight. She regrets we ever encouraged your stay last week.”

“Let me see her!” I pleaded. “Let me tell her how sorry I am.”

“It is of no use,” he replied. “Your night had not come then—or you would not have left us. It has not come now. I cannot show you the way.”

He paused. “The dead rejoiced beneath their daisies—they all rest among the roots of heaven’s flowers—at the thought of your joy when winter ends and morning returns with its birds. When you left, they shivered in their beds. When the spring of the universe arrives… but that is not for ages. How many ages? I do not know. Nor do I care.”

“Tell me one thing,” I begged. “Is my father with you? Have you seen him since he left this world?”

“Yes,” the raven said. “He is with us. Asleep. That was him—arm on the coverlet, hand half-closed.”

“Why didn’t you tell me?” I cried. “I was so close to him and didn’t even know!”

“And turn your back on him?” he corrected gently.

“I would have lain down at once if I had known!”

“I doubt it,” he said. “Had you been ready to sleep, you would have recognized him.” He continued, “Old Sir Up’ard and your twice-great-grandfather are already gone. Your great-grandfather has been with us for many years. I think he will soon stir. You saw him last night. Though, of course, you did not know him.”

“Why *of course*?” I asked.

“Because he is much nearer waking than you are. No one who refuses to sleep can ever truly wake.”

“I don’t understand,” I said.

“You turned away,” he replied. “And chose not to understand.”

I remained silent. But if I didn’t speak, he would leave.

“And my grandfather—” I ventured—“is he with you?”

“No,” the raven said. “He is still in the Evil Wood, fighting the dead.”

“Where is the Evil Wood?” I asked. “How can I find him?”

“You will not find him,” he said. “But you will hardly miss the wood. It is where those who refuse to sleep awaken at night—to kill their dead and bury them.”

“I still don’t understand,” I insisted.

“Naturally not,” he replied. “Neither do I understand you. I cannot read your heart or your face. When my wife and I fail to understand our children, it is because there is not enough of them to be understood. Only God can comprehend foolishness.”

Then, feeling exposed and utterly insignificant, I said, “Will you at least show me the nearest way home? I know there are many paths—I’ve taken two already.”

“There are indeed many ways,” he said.

“How do I recognize the nearest?”

“I cannot,” he answered. “You and I use the same words, but they mean different things. We often cannot tell people what they *need* to know, because they *want* to know something else—and so would only misunderstand. Home is always so far away in the palm of your hand. And telling you how to get there would be useless. But you *will* get there. You *must*. Everyone who is not at home must go home. You thought you were at home when I found you—if that had been true, you could not have left. No one can leave home. And no one has ever been or will be at home without having gone there.”

“Enigma upon enigma!” I exclaimed. “I didn’t come here to solve riddles!”

“No,” he said. “But you came—and found the riddles waiting. In truth, you are the only riddle. What you call riddles are truths—truths that seem puzzling because you are not true yourself.”

“Worse and worse!” I cried.

“And you *must* answer them,” he continued. “They will keep asking until you understand yourself. The universe is a riddle trying to escape—and you are holding your door shut against it.”

“Then please—tell me what I must do. Where must I go?”

“How should I know your to-do, or the way to it?”

“If I can’t go home, at least direct me to someone like me.”

“I do not know of any,” he said. “The beings most like you are over there.” He pointed with his beak.

All I saw was the setting sun, blinding me.

“Well,” I said bitterly, “I feel terribly treated—taken from my home, abandoned in a strange world, and given no guidance on where to go or what to do!”

“You forget,” the raven said quietly, “that when I brought you, you refused my hospitality—and yet you reached what you call home safely. Now you’ve come of your own accord.”

He turned and walked slowly away, head bowed.

I stood stunned. True, I had come on my own. But wasn’t it with intent to make amends?

My heart ached. My mind held no purpose, no hope, no desire. I watched the raven vanish into the dusk.

Suddenly, he pounced—throwing his full weight onto the ground, digging fiercely with his beak. Then, flapping his wings, he threw back his head—and something shot from his bill, soaring high into the air.

That moment, the sun set. Darkness fell swiftly. But the thing he cast began to glow—a soft radiance, pulsing like a firefly, though larger and brighter, golden-yellow. It drifted toward me.

I turned and followed.

Here, I pause to say that this account is a constant struggle. I strive to describe what cannot be described—not because I lack words, but because the events and beings involved exist beyond the reach of this world’s language. I can only convey how these things affected me—not their essence, but the feelings they stirred within me.

Even this limited expression feels incomplete. I can present only one layer of meaning, one circle of truth, while the reality holds countless depths. A single object might seem to be many things, its core shifting, uncertain—its identity changing with every glance.

I often record only a clumsy approximation of what I felt, knowing no medium in this world can carry such strangeness with clarity or certainty. Even to someone more familiar with this place, I could not guarantee the truth of my experience.

Yet I know—without doubt—that I was witnessing activity. And yet, simultaneously, I was reading a metaphysical argument. The world blurred between action and idea, sensation and symbol.

And so I follow the glowing thing, into the gathering dark, guided not by reason, but by a fragile, trembling hope.
