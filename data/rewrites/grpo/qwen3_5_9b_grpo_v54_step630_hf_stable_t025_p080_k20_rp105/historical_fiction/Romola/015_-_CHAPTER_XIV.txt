The roar of the crowd and the chaotic symphony of sounds that greeted Tito at the Piazza's entrance reminded him of Nello's warnings regarding the Fierucoloni. With a strange sense of relief, he thrust himself into the throng, enjoying the jostling and the raucous noise that filled the void. It dulled the bleak calculations of his future—the terrifying prospect of wandering alone in search of fortune—and momentarily extinguished the thought of seeking out Baldassarre.

Through the opposing archways, people surged into the square, illuminated by paper lanterns waved on sticks. A monotonous chant formed a steady thread beneath a cacophony of screams, whistles, mocking songs from boys, drumbeats, and tinkling bells. Occasionally, a lantern shattered with a crash as a stone flew blindly through the air, followed by shrieks and renewed shouting. Yet, on the periphery of this whirlwind, groups observed the Nativity of the Virgin with a more orderly devotion. Ragged men darted sharp glances around while their tongues ran on, inviting country folk to games; two figures on stilts, having snatched lanterns from the crowd, swung the lights like meteors as they paraded; a shrewd merchant sold hot *berlingozzi* from a small stall; another man, leaning against a pillar of the Foundling Hospital, hawked miracle pills guaranteed to cure toothache and drowning; a tumbler performed on a platform nearby; and a handful of apprentices, bored by the casual stone-throwing, engaged in a fierce, concentrated match of their favorite Florentine sport at the narrow entrance of the Via de' Febbrai.

Tito, forced to weave through random gaps in the crowd, found himself beside a procession of barefoot peasant women. Their sun-baked, bronzed faces and fragmentary, dirt-stained garments of obsolete fashion made them appear to the city dwellers as a weary ancestry returning from a pilgrimage begun a century prior. These were the hardy women from the Pistoia mountains, carrying a year's labor in a modest bundle of yarn on their backs. In their hearts lay a meager hope and a vast, dim fear, both entrusted to the Blessed Virgin, whose miraculous image was to be unveiled on this Eve of her Nativity, allowing her power to flow unobstructed.

At another moment, Tito was pushed toward the edge of the square, where peddlers stationed themselves safely behind pillars. There he recognized Bratti, who stood with his back to the stone, lips pursed in disdainful silence. He eyed every approacher with a cold superiority, clutching a serge cloth that hid the contents of his basket. Surprised by such unusual composure in an anxious trader, Tito stepped closer. Two women approached the basket with curiosity, prompting Bratti to pull the cloth tighter and look away. Provoked, one woman asked what was inside.

"Before I answer, Monna, I must know if you intend to buy," Bratti replied sharply. "I cannot display such wares to every fly that settles and pays nothing. My goods are too choice. Besides, I have only two left, and I have no mind to soil them. With the rumors of pestilence, they might be worth their weight in gold. No, no: *andate con Dio*."

The women exchanged glances. "And what is the price?" asked the second.

"Not within what you likely carry, buona donna," Bratti said in a tone of compassionate condescension. "Trust in Messer Domeneddio and the saints; poor people can do no better."

"Not so poor!" the second woman retorted indignantly, producing her money-bag. "Come, what do you say to a *grosso*?"

"I say you may get twenty-one *quattrini* for it," Bratti replied coolly, "but not from me, for I lack such small change."

"Two, then?" the woman demanded, growing exasperated, while her companion looked on with envy. "It won't be more than two, I think."

After further haggling and mercantile coquetry, Bratti feigned concession. "Since you are set on it," he said slowly, lifting the cover, "I would be loath to cause you harm. Maestro Gabbadeo used to say that when a woman desires something and fails to obtain it, she falls into greater danger of the pestilence. *Ecco!* I have but two left. Let me tell you, the companion to these is on the finger of Maestro Gabbadeo, who has gone to Bologna—a wise doctor indeed."

The objects were two clumsy iron rings, beaten into the shape of ancient Roman rings sometimes unearthed. Their rust and the hidden nature of their supposed potency were so convincing that the *grossi* were paid without complaint. The first woman, lacking such coins, succeeded after much feigned reluctance in trading some of her yarn for the remaining ring, carrying it off in triumph. Bratti covered his basket, now filled with miscellaneous items likely acquired under similar circumstances, and moved from his pillar, suddenly confronting Tito, who would have preferred to avoid recognition.

"By the head of San Giovanni," Bratti said, pulling Tito back to the pillar, "what luck! I was speaking of you this morning, Messer Greco. I said he has risen among the gentlemen—and I am glad, for I was at the bottom of his fortune—but I rarely catch him lying on the stones now. But it is your luck, not mine, Messer Greco, save for a small trifle to satisfy me for my trouble."

"You speak in riddles, Bratti," Tito said. "Remember, I do not sharpen my wits by haggling over iron rings; you must be plain."

"By the Holy Gospels! It was an easy bargain I gave them. If a Hebrew gets thirty-two percent, I hope a Christian may get a little more. Without a conscience, I would have doubled the money and the yarn. But speaking of rings, it is your ring—that very ring on your finger—that I could find a buyer for. Yes, a buyer with a deep purse."

"Truly?" Tito asked, examining his ring.

"A Genoese heading straight to Hungary. He came to my shop searching for old things I didn't know the price of; I wager he thought I had a pumpkin on my shoulders. He had rummaged every shop in Florence. He wore a ring—not quite like yours, but of the same fashion. As we spoke of rings, I mentioned a fine young man, a particular friend of mine, who owned such a ring. He asked, 'Who is he? Tell him I'll give him his price.' I thought of coming to Nello's tomorrow. In my opinion, Messer Greco, you are not the type to watch the Arno turn to broth and stand idle."

Tito had listened intently, though his mind raced. Why keep the ring? It had been a mere sentiment preventing him from selling it with the other gems. Had he sold it, perhaps he would have avoided Fra Luca's identification. True, it had been taken from Baldassarre's finger and placed on his own as his hand grew, but there was no valid good in such superstitious scruples regarding inanimate objects. The ring had facilitated his recognition, and Tito had begun to dislike recognition—a claim from the past. This foreigner's offer, if genuine, was an opportunity to dispose of the ring without the trouble of finding a buyer.

"You speak with your usual wisdom, Bratti," Tito said. "I have no objection to hearing what your Genoese offers. But when and where shall I meet him?"

"Tomorrow, three hours after sunrise, at my shop. If your wits are as sharp as I believe, Messer Greco, you will ask for a heavy price; he cares little for money. I believe he buys for someone else, perhaps a great *signor*."

"It is well," Tito agreed. "I will be at your shop, if nothing hinders."

"And you will doubtless deal nobly by me for old acquaintance's sake," Bratti added, refusing to fix the small fee for his service. "It seems a thousand years since I left the piazza; a fair is dull, not to say wicked, when one has no goods to sell."

Tito made a hasty sign of assent and moved away, once again swept toward the center of the square and back again, unable to determine his own course. In this zigzag motion, he was carried to the end of the piazza opposite the church, where an entertainment drew a deep crowd. Loud bursts of laughter interrupted a monologue that shifted from slow oratory to rattling buffoonery. A girl was pushed into an inner circle with apparent reluctance, while a laughing minx found her way with her elbows. A strange light bathed the square: pale stars above, dim waving lanterns below, rendering objects indistinct except when caught by the fitful glow. But in a recess, a stronger light cast the heads of spectators in dark relief as Tito was pushed toward them. Above them rose the head of a man wearing a white mitre with yellow cabalistic figures.

"Behold, my children!" the speaker cried. "Behold your opportunity! Neglect not the holy sacrament of matrimony when available for the small sum of a white *quattrino*—the cheapest marriage ever offered, dissolved by special bull at every man's will. Behold the bull!" He held up a parchment with huge seals. "Behold the indulgence granted by His Holiness Alexander the Sixth, newly elected for his piety, intending to reform the Church by abolishing the priestly abuse that hoards matrimony for the clergy. Spit once, my sons, and pay a white *quattrino*! This is the whole price. The *quattrino* is the only difference the Holy Father allows between us and the clergy—who spit and pay nothing."

Tito recognized the sharp ring of the voice, though the face remained shadowed. Stepping closer, he saw within the circle an altar-like table draped in red fabric stitched with yellow cabalistic figures. Half a dozen thin tapers burned behind it, alongside a conjuring apparatus, a large open book, and a monkey fastened by a cord to a small ring, holding a taper that flickered in its restless movements. An impish boy in a white surplice cuffed the monkey and adjusted the taper. The man in the mitre wore a surplice and a chasuble with rudely marked zodiac signs. Tito was certain now; it was Maestro Vaiano, the mountebank from whom he had rescued Tessa. *Pretty little Tessa!* Perhaps she was among the peasant women.

"Come, my maidens!" the conjuror continued. "This is the time for the pretty who have many chances, and the ill-favored who have few. Matrimony to be had—hot, eaten, and done with as easily as *berlingozzi*! And see!" He held up a cluster of tiny bags. "To every bride I give a *Breve* with a secret inside—the secret alone worth the money you pay. The secret how to... no, no, I will not tell you, making it a double secret. Hang it round your neck if you like; I don't say that won't be best, for then you will see unexpected things: though if you open it you may break your leg, *è vero*, but you will know a secret! Something nobody knows but me! Mark—I give you the *Breve*, I do not sell it. The *quattrino* is for the matrimony, and the *Breve* is free. *Orsù, giovanetti*, come like dutiful sons of the Church and buy the Indulgence of His Holiness Alexander the Sixth."

This buffoonery suited the audience perfectly. The *fierucola* was a minor occasion, so the townsfolk were content with jokes less indecent than those at carnival, rhymed by the Magnifico and his satellites. The women, however, truly began to crave the *Brevi*. Several couples had already undergone the ceremony, where the conjuror's solemn gibberish, grimaces over the open book, the monkey's antics, and the preliminary spitting had elicited peals of laughter. Now, a merry-eyed youth of seventeen in a loose tunic and red cap pushed forward, holding the hand of a plump brunette whose ragged dress revealed her round arms and legs.

"Fetter us without delay, Maestro!" the youth said. "I must take my bride home and paint her under a lantern's light."

"Ha! Mariotto, my son, I commend your pious observance..." The conjuror began, when a loud chattering warned him of a crisis with his monkey.

The temper of the imperfect acolyte was tested by his colleague's over-active discipline. A sudden cuff, delivered as the taper fell horizontally, caused the boy to leap back with violence, snapping the slackened knot of his cord. His first leap threw him to the other end of the table, his remonstrances so threatening that the imp in the surplice brandished a wand. The monkey leaped onto the head of a tall woman in the foreground, dropping his taper and chattering emphatically from that height. Screaming and confusion ensued; many spectators feared the Maestro's monkey, suspecting it of hidden mischief beyond mere teeth and claws. The conjuror himself was alarmed lest harm befall his familiar. In the scuffle to seize the monkey's string, Tito slipped out of the circle and, unwilling to contend for his place, allowed himself to be pushed toward the church of the Nunziata, joining the worshippers.

The brilliant illumination within pressed upon his eyes with palpable force after the pale, scattered lights of the piazza. For the first minute or two, he saw nothing distinct. That yellow splendor was supernatural and heavenly to many peasant women, accustomed to sleeping in twilight with mountains hiding half the sky; the uninterrupted chant from the choir was repose to the ear after the hellish hubbub outside. Gradually, the scene cleared, though a thin yellow haze from incense mingled with the breath of the multitude. In a chapel on the left, wreathed with silver lamps, the miraculous fresco of the Annunciation stood unveiled, appearing dark with the excess of surrounding light.
