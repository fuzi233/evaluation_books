> *Far, far from hence
The Adriatic breaks in a warm bay
Among the green Illyrian hills, and there
The sunshine in the happy glens is fair,
And by the sea and in the brakes
The grass is cool, the sea-side air
Buoyant and fresh, the mountain flowers
More virginal and sweet than ours.*
— Matthew Arnold

The glens cut into the high rolling tableland straddling Devon and Cornwall are exactly like the verses above describe, save that each of their gorges opens not on the Adriatic, but out toward the endless, storm-kissed Western Ocean. No other English scenery matches them: each combe has steep inland walls draped in rich ancient oak, nearer the sea banks thick with dark golden furze, then smooth sheep-cropped turf, then jagged black ironstone cliffs that jutt far out into the deep Atlantic, shaped like spires, broken castles, and sharp ragged fins. Tucked in each hollow is a narrow strip of lush watermeadow, a crystal trout stream winding back and forth between the feet of the hills, a weathered grey stone mill with spray sparkling and humming around its dripping waterwheel, dark rock pools above the tide line where salmon trout rest after swimming up from their Atlantic wanderings each autumn flood, a ridge of blown sand bright with golden trefoil and crimson lady’s finger bloom, and a bank of smooth polished pebbles down which the stream rattles on its last rush to the sea.

At the mouth of every cove lies a stretch of jagged, shark-tooth sharp rock that paves the whole bay, streaked here and there with thin lines of pink shell sand, crisscrossed with white foam from the never-resting surf. Primeval earthquakes tilted the rock strata on edge, stacked at wild, crooked angles that stretch straight out west into the water: one single rasp of that sharp stone jaw could grind the stoutest ship’s timbers to splinters in seconds. To landward, all is softness, richness, quiet peace. To seaward, it is a wild, howling wilderness of rock and rolling swell, barren to the fisherman and a death sentence for any shipwrecked mariner.

Only one of these coves, called Marsland Mouth, has a patch of protected landing ground, guarded by a long natural rock sea-wall that breaks the force of the Atlantic rollers. This is the home of Lucy Passmore, known across the shire as the White Witch — and as Sir Richard Grenville rightly guessed, this was exactly where the fleeing Jesuit priests had made for.

But the Jesuits had not arrived first. Two figures stood on the lonely shingle beach under the bright harvest October moon: Rose Salterne, and the White Witch herself. Fevered by curiosity, steeped in the old country superstitions, and drawn on by the very wildness and danger of the magic rite she’d come to perform, Rose had kept their midnight appointment, and stood shivering on the grey pebbles minutes before the clock would strike twelve.

“You’re quite safe here tonight, miss,” Lucy assured her. “My old man’s snoring sound abed, and no other soul sets foot here after dark, save the odd mermaid passing through. Good Lord, where’s our boat? It should be pulled up high on the pebbles, not down there!”

Rose pointed to a strip of sand forty yards closer to the waves, where the small craft lay half beached.
“That lazy good-for-nothing!” Lucy huffed. “He’s been out after pollock round the rocks this evening, and never bothered to haul it up high. I’ll trounce him good when I get home. I only hope he tied it fast, that’s all! He’s more trouble than all the money in the world will ever be worth. Oh deary me!”

She bustled down the shingle toward the boat, Rose trailing after her.
“Aye, it’s tied fast, sure enough, oars and all! Well I never! Oh the lazy thief, leaving the oars out where someone could steal them! I’ll just sit here in the boat and keep watch, dear, while you go down to the tide line. You have to be all alone, you know, or the spell won’t work. Here’s the magic mirror. Go on now, dip your head three times in the water, mind you don’t look back to land or toward the open sea until you’ve said the words and looked into the glass. Hurry now, it’s almost midnight.”

Lucy coiled herself comfortably in the stern of the boat, while Rose faltered the twenty yards further down the sand, slipped off her outer clothes, and stood trembling for a moment before stepping into the cold dark water. She was framed between two walls of rock: the one on her left, twenty feet high, hid her in deepest shadow, while the lower rock on her right caught the full blaze of the midnight moon, draped in thick festoons of purple living seaweed that hung over dark crevices that looked like lairs for every sea goblin in the old tales. The peaks of the left-hand rock loomed ghastly black overhead, while the high down above her right glowed pale and cold under the moon.

The breeze had died entirely away. Not even a small swell broke the perfect stillness of the cove. The gulls were all asleep on their ledges. It was that deep autumn silence so thick you could almost hear it hum. Rose stood awed, listening for any small sound that might prove there was another living thing beside her in the world.

A faint bleat, like that of a newborn lamb, drifted down from high above her head. She started and looked up. Then a wail echoed from the cliffs, like a crying hurt child, answered by a second wail from the opposite rock face. They were only passing snipe and an otter calling her cubs, but to Rose’s overwrought mind they were mysterious supernatural beings, come to answer her spell. They only quickened her anticipation, though: Lucy had told her no harm would touch her if she performed the rite correctly. She could hear her own heart hammering as she stepped deeper into the cold water, mirror held tight in her hand, then stopped aghast.

A ring of lambent, pale blue flame circled her waist. Every inch of her submerged limbs glowed with soft light. All the tiny countless life of the autumn sea, stirred by her wading steps, had flashed into sudden glowing glory:
> And around her the lamps of the sea nymphs,
Myriad fiery globes, swam heaving and panting, and rainbows,
Crimson and azure and emerald, were broken in star-showers, lighting
Far through the wine-dark depths of the crystal, the gardens of Nereus,
Coral and sea-fan and tangle, the blooms and the palms of the ocean.

She could see every small shell crawling on the white sand under her feet, every little rock fish darting in and out of crevices to stare at her with wide bright eyes, while the great brown palm-like oarweeds waving in the half-lit water below her seemed to reach up with long soft hands to beckon her down to a cold grave among their fronds. She almost turned to flee, but it was too late to back out. She dipped her head three times in haste, waded quickly back to the tide line, pushed dripping hair out of her eyes, and spoke the incantation:
> A maiden pure, here I stand,
Neither on sea, nor yet on land;
Angels watch me on either hand.
If you be landsman, come down the strand;
If you be sailor, come up the sand;
If you be angel, come from the sky,
Look in my glass, and pass me by;
Look in my glass, and go from the shore;
Leave me, but love me for evermore.

The words had barely left her lips, and her eyes were straining into the mirror (which so far only showed the sparkle of water drops dripping from her own hair) when she heard the fast clatter of hooves and the scuff of men’s boots on the pebbles. She darted into a narrow crevice cave in the high rock and dressed herself in frantic haste. The footsteps came straight for the boat. Peeping out, half-dead with terror, she saw four men: two had just leaped off their horses and set them loose, while the other two helped drag the boat down toward the sea.

Then Lucy Passmore’s portly figure rose from the stern sheets like an angry ghost, and shrieked at the top of her shrill voice:
“Eh! You villains, you rogues! What do you think you’re doing stealing a poor widow’s boat in the dead of night?”

The whole party recoiled in terror, and one man turned to run up the beach yelling at the top of his lungs: “It’s a mermaid! A mermaid asleep in Willy Passmore’s boat!”
“I wish to God that were true,” a familiar grumbling voice groaned. “It’s my wife, oh dear!” Willy Passmore cowered down, already bracing for the hearty cuff he duly received when Lucy leaped out of the boat, daring any of them to lay a hand on it, and thundered at her husband to get back home to bed.

The wily old woman was dragging the fight out for two reasons: first, to give Rose all the time she needed to hide safely, and second, because she had already spotted the suspicious Welsh gentleman leading the party, the same Popish priest she’d foretold would turn up on her doorstep long ago. She was a fiercely loyal subject to Queen Elizabeth, and hated the idea of her husband sneaking out to help these “Popish skulkers” escape the law — unless, of course, they offered her a very handsome sum of money for the trouble.

Parsons the priest thundered at her to stand aside, Campian the other Jesuit entreated her politely, Mr. Leigh’s groom swore wildly, and Willy Passmore danced around in a perfect agony of mingled fear and greed.
“No!” Lucy roared. “As I am an honest, loyal woman! This is what you left the boat down on the tide line for, is it, you old traitor? To help noxious Popish spies escape her Majesty’s judges? Stand back, cowards! D’you dare strike a defenceless woman?”

That last line was only a warning that she intended to strike them. She grabbed one of the oars, swung it round her head in great circles, and cracked Father Parsons such a hard blow across the shins that he retreated yelping.
“Lucy! Lucy!” her husband shrieked in the shrillest Devon falsetto, “Are you mad? Are you daft, lass? They promised me two gold nobles just to lend them the boat!”
“Two nobles?” Lucy shrieked back, her tone thick with ineffable scorn. “And you call yourself a man?”
“Two nobles! Two nobles!” Willy hopped about, just out of oar’s reach. “Ten? Ten nobles?”
“Ten?” Lucy jeered. “You wouldn’t sell your soul for less than twenty, you daft old thing!”

“Oh, for heaven’s sake!” poor Campian cried. “Give her ten, brother Pars — Morgan, I mean! Watch your shins, you brute — Oh, virago! What fury a woman can wield! She must be a Lamia, a Gorgon, some sort of —”
“Take that for calling an honest woman a Gorgon!” Lucy swung the oar again, and knocked poor thin Campian’s legs right out from under him. She straddled his chest exactly like the famous virago in the old ballad of Hudibras, and roared “Ten nobles, or I’m keeping you all here till the Queen’s men turn up at dawn!” The ten gold nobles were immediately pressed into her hand.

Now their dragon guardian pacified, the men dragged the boat down toward the sea, right past the narrow cave where Rose was squeezed into the darkest, furthest corner, pressed against wet seaweed and rough barnacles, holding her breath as they passed. Lucy trailed right behind them for her own reasons, spotted the dark cave mouth right at the tide line, and planted her broad person square across its opening to block any chance of the strangers noticing Rose, while she grumbled at her husband, at the priests, and most of all at Mr. Leigh’s groom, whom she prophesied very plainly would end up in Launceston Gaol and hang before the month was out. The poor serving man, who would as soon have dared leap off the top of Welcombe Cliff as talk back to the White Witch, begged for her mercy and tried every trick to hide behind one of the other men, terrified her famous evil eye would “overlook” him and curse him to ruin.

But the night’s adventures were far from over. Just as the boat was being pushed into the water, a faint halloo echoed down the beach. A minute later, a horseman came thundering down the shingle, pulled his horse up hard on its haunches right beside the terrified group, and half-fell, half-jumped out of the saddle.

The groom, who would never dare fight a witch, knew full well how to face a swordsman. He drew his blade and lunged at the newcomer, then recoiled:
“God forgive me, it’s Mr. Eustace! Oh dear sir, I thought you were one of Sir Richard Grenville’s men! Oh sir, you’re hurt!”
“Just a scratch, nothing more,” Eustace almost moaned. “Help me into the boat, Jack. Gentlemen, I have to go with you.”
“Surely not, my dear son,” kind-hearted Campian said, shocked. “We are nothing but wandering outcasts with no home to our name.”
“With you forever. Everything is lost for me here. Wherever God and the cause lead, I go,” Eustace staggered toward the boat.

As he passed the cave, Rose saw his ghastly, blood-soaked face, half-bound with a handkerchief that could not hide the convulsions of rage, shame, and despair twisting his once-handsome features. His wild, glaring eyes swept round the cove once, and locked directly on hers through the crack in the rock. His gaze was so sharp, so intense, so dreadful that Rose forgot she was hidden completely, and almost shrieked out loud.
“He’s overlooked me!” she shuddered to herself, remembering his threat from the day before.
“Who wounded you?” Campian asked.
“My cousin Amyas. He attacked me, and stole the secret letter I was carrying!” Parsons stamped up and down the sand in a fury. “The devil take him, then!”
“Aye, curse him if you want — I dare not. He spared my life, he sent me here,” Eustace groaned, making a lurch for the boat.
“Oh my dear young gentleman,” Lucy’s soft woman’s heart burst out at the sight of his pain, “you can’t sail away with a great wound like that. Let me bind it up for you, just a minute now —” She stepped forward.
Eustace thrust her roughly back. “No! Better to bear it. I deserve it. Devils, I deserve it! Push off, or we shall all be lost! William Cary is right behind me!”

At that news the boat was shoved into the water faster than it had ever moved before, and they made their escape just in time. They were barely round the edge of the headland and out of sight when the rattle of Cary’s horse hooves echoed loud down the beach.
“That rascal groom of Mr. Leigh’s is for it now, the Popish villain!” Lucy called out loud. “You stay still there, dear heart, and settle your nerves. You’re safer than a rabbit in its burrow. I’ll climb up this rock ledge and see what happens, if I die for it.”

She squeezed through a narrow cleft up to a high viewing ledge, and peered down.
“There he is, in the meadow, trying to catch the loose horses! There comes Mr. Cary! Good Lord, how he rides! He’s over the stone wall already! Run Jack, run! You’ll make the river! No, he won’t! Goodness gracious! Mr. Cary’s caught him! He’s down, he’s down!”
“Is he dead?” Rose called out, shuddering.
“Aye, as dead as a nit! And Mr. Cary’s off his horse, standing over him. No, he’s not dead! He’s getting up now. Looks like Cary hit him with the flat of his sword. What’s he doing now? Talking to him for a minute. Oh dear, oh dear!”
“Has he killed him?” poor Rose cried.
“No, not at all! He’s kicking him as hard as you’d kick a football! Good Lord, who ever saw the like! He’s kicked him straight into the river, got on his horse, and ridden off!”

Lucy climbed back down to the cave.
“Now then, my dear, we’d best head home and get you something warm to drink. You must be mortal cold by now. I was so scared for you, but I kept those rascals off clever, didn’t I?”
“I wish I’d never seen Mr. Leigh’s face,” Rose whispered.
“Aye, dreadful, weren’t it, poor soul. A terrible night for his poor mother.”
“Lucy, I can’t get that face out of my head. I’m sure he overlooked me, cursed me.”
“Oh, nonsense,” Lucy laughed. “When young gentlemen overlook young ladies, it’s almost never a curse, I’ll warrant. Don’t think on it.”
“But I can’t help it,” Rose said. “Wait — should we head home yet? Where’s that servant?”
“Never mind him, he won’t see us under the lee of the hill. I’m far more worried about my old man. I’ve got a funny feeling in my gut, the kind I get before something bad happens, like I’ll never see him again, I swear. Well, he was a biddable old soul, all said and done. Oh — all this while we’ve forgotten the whole point of this night! Who did you see in the mirror?”
“Only that face,” Rose shuddered.
“Not in the glass, maid? Tell me you didn’t see it in the glass?”
“Would to Heaven I hadn’t! Lucy — what if he’s the man I was fated to marry?”
“Him? Why, he’s a Popish priest, he can’t marry even if he wanted to, poor wretch!”
“He is not, and I have cause enough to know it!” Rose poured out the whole story of her meeting with Eustace the day before, to the only confidant she had.
“He’s a fine wooer, isn’t he?”
