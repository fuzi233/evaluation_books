The night passed without incident, leaving the weary bird of the night refreshed by dawn. By ten o'clock, Mr. Crisparkle arrived at the office, bursting through the door as if he had leaped straight from the river at Cloisterham.

"Miss Twinkleton was in a state of such agitation, Miss Rosa," he explained breathlessly, "that she followed your note to my home. To calm her, I volunteered to come immediately on the first train. I confess, at the time, I wished you had come to me; but now, I believe it was best that you stayed with your guardian."

"I considered coming to you," Rosa replied, "but Minor Canon Corner was so close to him—"

"I understand. It was entirely natural."

"I have informed Mr. Crisparkle of everything you told me last night, my dear," Mr. Grewgious interjected smoothly. "Naturally, I intended to write to him instantly, but his arrival was fortuitous. It was particularly kind of him to come, considering he had just departed."

"Have you decided," Rosa asked, turning to both men, "what course of action to take regarding Helena and her brother?"

"Why, truly," Mr. Crisparkle admitted, looking perplexed, "I am at a loss. Even Mr. Grewgious, whose intellect surpasses mine and who has been contemplating this matter all night, remains undecided. What hope do I have?"

Just then, The Unlimited appeared in the doorway, having knocked and received permission to enter. She announced that a gentleman wished to speak with Mr. Crisparkle, if such a person were present. If not, she offered her apologies for the mistake.

"Such a gentleman is here," Mr. Crisparkle confirmed, "though he is currently occupied."

"Is he a dark gentleman?" Rosa asked, stepping back toward Mr. Grewgious.

"No, Miss. More of a brown complexion."

"Are you certain he does not have black hair?" Rosa ventured, gathering courage.

"Absolutely certain, Miss. Brown hair and blue eyes."

"Perhaps," Mr. Grewgious suggested with his characteristic caution, "it would be wise to admit him, Reverend Sir, if you do not object. When one faces difficulty, one never knows from which direction a solution might emerge. My business principle is never to close off any avenue, but to observe every path that presents itself. I could recount an anecdote relevant to this, but it would be premature."

"If Miss Rosa permits, then? Let the gentleman enter," Mr. Crisparkle agreed.

The man entered, apologizing with a frank yet modest grace for not finding Mr. Crisparkle alone. He turned to Mr. Crisparkle and, with a smile, posed the unexpected question: "Who am I?"

"You are the gentleman I saw smoking beneath the trees at Staple Inn a few moments ago."

"Indeed. There I saw you. But who else am I?"

Mr. Crisparkle focused his gaze on a handsome, sunburned face, and a faint, ghostly image of a departed boy seemed to materialize in the room. The visitor noticed the flicker of memory in the Minor Canon's features and smiled again. "What shall we have for breakfast this morning? You appear to be out of jam."

"Wait!" cried Mr. Crisparkle, raising his hand. "Give me a moment! Tartar!"

The two men embraced with immense warmth, going so far—as Englishmen rarely do—as to place their hands on each other's shoulders and gaze joyfully into one another's eyes.

"My old fag!" Mr. Crisparkle exclaimed.

"My old master!" Mr. Tartar responded.

"You saved me from drowning!" Mr. Crisparkle declared.

"After which you took to swimming yourself, you know!" Mr. Tartar retorted.

"God bless my soul!" Mr. Crisparkle gasped.

"Amen!" Mr. Tartar answered.

They resumed shaking hands vigorously.

"Imagine," Mr. Crisparkle cried, his eyes shining, "Miss Rosa Bud and Mr. Grewgious, imagine Mr. Tartar, when he was the smallest of juniors, diving for me, seizing me—a large, heavy senior—by the hair of my head, and striking out for the shore with me like a water-giant!"

"I can imagine my not letting him sink, as I was his fag," Mr. Tartar said. "But the truth is, he was my best protector and friend, doing me more good than all my masters combined. An irrational impulse seized me to pick him up, or go down with him."

"Hem! Permit me, sir, to offer my congratulations," Mr. Grewgious said, advancing with an outstretched hand. "It is an honor I truly esteem. I hope you did not catch a chill. I hope you were not inconvenienced by swallowing too much water. How have you been since?"

It was unclear whether Mr. Grewgious actually knew what he was saying, though it was evident he intended to convey high friendship and appreciation.

"If Heaven had bestowed such courage and skill upon my poor mother's aid!" Rosa thought. "And he was so slight and young then!"

"I do not wish to be complimented upon it, thank you," Mr. Grewgious announced after pacing a few steps across the room in a manner so unexpected and unaccountable that everyone stared, unsure if he was choking or had the cramp. "I _think_ I have an idea. I believe I have seen Mr. Tartar's name listed as a tenant in the top set of the house next to the top set in the corner?"

"Yes, sir," Mr. Tartar confirmed. "You are correct so far."

"I am correct so far," Mr. Grewgious continued, ticking the fact off mentally with his thumb against his palm. "Might you happen to know the name of your neighbor in the top set on the other side of the party wall?" He leaned in close to Mr. Tartar, ensuring he missed nothing of his expression, despite his short-sightedness.

"Landless."

"Tick that off," Mr. Grewgious said, pacing again before returning. "Do you have any personal knowledge of him, sir?"

"Slight, but some."

"Tick that off," Mr. Grewgious noted, pacing once more. "What is the nature of this knowledge, Mr. Tartar?"

"I thought he seemed to be a young fellow in a poor way. I asked his leave—only within a day or so—to share my flowers up there with him; that is to say, to extend my flower-garden to his windows."

"Would you have the kindness to take seats?" Mr. Grewgious requested. "_I_ have an idea!"

They complied; Mr. Tartar did so readily, being somewhat disoriented, while Mr. Grewgious sat in the center with his hands on his knees. Thus, he stated his idea with his usual memorized delivery.

"I cannot yet decide whether it is prudent to maintain open communication under current circumstances, specifically between the fair member of this company and either Mr. Neville or Miss Helena. I have reason to believe a local friend of ours—one upon whom I beg to bestow a passing but hearty malediction, with the kind permission of my reverend friend—sneaks to and fro, dodging up and down. When not doing so himself, he may have an informant skulking about, perhaps a watchman, porter, or similar hanger-on of Staple. On the other hand, Miss Rosa naturally wishes to see her friend Miss Helena, and it seems vital that at least Miss Helena (if not her brother too, through her) should privately learn from Miss Rosa's lips what has occurred and what has been threatened. Am I agreed with generally in these views?"

"I entirely coincide with them," Mr. Crisparkle said, having listened attentively.

"As I have no doubt I should," added Mr. Tartar, smiling, "if I understood them."

"Fair and softly, sir," Mr. Grewgious cautioned. "We shall fully confide in you directly if you will grant us your permission. Now, if our local friend has any informant on the spot, it is reasonably clear that such an informant can only be tasked with watching the chambers occupied by Mr. Neville. Reporting to our local friend, who moves in and out there, our local friend would deduce the identities of the parties from his own previous knowledge. No one can be set to watch all of Staple, or to concern themselves with comers and goers to other sets of chambers: unless, indeed, mine."

"I begin to understand to what you tend," Mr. Crisparkle said, "and I highly approve of your caution."

"I need not repeat that I know nothing yet of the why and wherefore," Mr. Tartar added. "But I also understand to what you tend, so let me say at once that my chambers are freely at your disposal."

"There!" Mr. Grewgious cried, smoothing his head triumphantly. "Now we all have the idea. You have it, my dear?"

"I think I have," Rosa said, blushing slightly as Mr. Tartar glanced quickly toward her.

"You see," Mr. Grewgious explained, "you go over to Staple with Mr. Crisparkle and Mr. Tartar. I shall go in and out, and out and in, in my usual manner. You go up with those gentlemen to Mr. Tartar's rooms; you inspect Mr. Tartar's flower-garden; you wait for Miss Helena's appearance there, or signal to her that you are nearby; and you communicate with her freely, and no spy can be the wiser."

"I am very much afraid I shall be—"

"Be what, my dear?" Mr. Grewgious asked as she hesitated. "Not frightened?"

"No, not that," Rosa said shyly. "In Mr. Tartar's way. We seem to be appropriating Mr. Tartar's residence so very coolly."

"I protest to you," the gentleman returned, "that I shall think the better of it for evermore if your voice sounds in it only once."

Rosa, unsure what to say to that, cast down her eyes and turned to Mr. Grewgious, dutifully asking if she should put on her hat. Mr. Grewgious being of the opinion that she could do no better, she withdrew for that purpose. Mr. Crisparkle took the opportunity to give Mr. Tartar a summary of the distresses facing Neville and his sister; the delay was quite sufficient, as the hat required a little extra fitting.

Mr. Tartar offered his arm to Rosa, while Mr. Crisparkle walked ahead, detached.

"Poor, poor Eddy!" Rosa thought as they walked along.

Mr. Tartar waved his right hand as he bent his head down over Rosa, speaking animatedly.

"It was not so powerful or so sun-browned when it saved Mr. Crisparkle," Rosa thought, glancing at it. "But it must have been very steady and determined even then."

Mr. Tartar revealed he had been a sailor, roaming everywhere for years.

"When are you going to sea again?" Rosa asked.

"Never!"

Rosa wondered what the girls would say if they could see her crossing the wide street on the sailor's arm. She fancied that passers-by must think her very small and helpless, contrasted with the strong figure capable of lifting her up and carrying her out of any danger, miles and miles without resting.

She reflected further that his far-seeing blue eyes seemed accustomed to watching danger from afar, observing it without flinching, drawing nearer and nearer. When she raised her own eyes, she found he seemed to be thinking about _them_.

This slightly confused Rosebud and may explain why she never afterwards quite knew how she ascended (with his help) to his garden in the air, seeming to enter a marvelous country that bloomed suddenly like the land atop the magic beanstalk. May it flourish forever!
