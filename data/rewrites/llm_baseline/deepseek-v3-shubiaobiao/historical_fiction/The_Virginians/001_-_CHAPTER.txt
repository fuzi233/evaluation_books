The golden light of a Virginia afternoon draped the rolling hills of the estate in warmth as young Harry Warrington rode up the long drive to his family’s home. The journey had been long, the sea unforgiving, and England—though grand—had not been kind. Now, the sight of the familiar red-brick house, its white columns standing tall against the blue sky, filled him with a relief so profound it near choked him.

The servants rushed out before his horse had fully halted, their faces alight with recognition and joy. "Master Harry! Master Harry's home!" The cry spread like wildfire, and soon the porch was crowded with well-wishers, old and young. Even the hounds, who had drowsed in the shade, sprang up barking, tails wagging furiously as if they, too, remembered him.

His brother George was not among them.

Harry dismounted, his boots hitting the dust of the drive with a thud that felt heavier than it ought. He had written ahead—had George not received the letter? Or had he chosen not to meet him? The thought stung, though he brushed it aside. There would be time enough for reunions and explanations. For now, he allowed himself to be swept inside, where the scent of beeswax and aged wood welcomed him like an old friend.

His mother stood in the hall, her back straight, her expression unreadable. For a heartbeat, neither moved. Then her arms opened, and Harry stepped into them, the years of absence dissolving in that embrace.

"You’ve grown thin," she murmured, pulling back to study him. "England did not feed you well."

Harry laughed, though it came out rougher than he intended. "It fed me enough. But no dish there tastes like home."

She smiled then, a rare softening. "We’ll see to that."

But as the household buzzed around him, Harry’s gaze drifted to the staircase, to the closed door of the study where his brother often sequestered himself. The war had changed them both—perhaps in ways neither could yet name.

And so Harry Warrington returned, not as the boy who had left, but as a man carrying the weight of battles fought and debts unpaid. Home, he would soon learn, was not quite as he remembered it.
