The autumn air had finally cooled the feverish warmth of Tunbridge, and Madame de Bernstein's circle was restless, eager to trade the quiet countryside for the glittering chaos of Bath. Harry, with a reluctance that tasted like bile, promised to escort Lady Maria and the chaplain to Castlewood. Their route took them past Oakhurst, the village where Harry had once found genuine kindness, but the memory was quickly soured by Lady Maria's relentless commentary. She mocked the local young women, claiming they set their caps at Harry, and ridiculed the mother's obvious desire to secure him as a husband. Irritated and feeling the weight of her condescension, Harry declared he would pass their door without a glance. That evening, at the inn, the atmosphere was thick with tension. Lady Maria's forced smiles failed to thaw Harry's icy demeanor; her manufactured tears elicited nothing but a surly grunt from him. When she pressed for a private conversation, the chaplain, seemingly by some unseen decree, refused to leave the room, forcing her to retire with a heavy sigh. Harry bowed her out with a stiff, obstinate politeness, leaving her to the innkeeper's care.

Ten minutes after Lady Maria retired, a horse galloped out of the yard, its rider vanishing into the night. Later, when the maid Mrs. Betty entered to fetch smelling salts, she found the chaplain alone, smoking a pipe in the dim light. Harry was gone—perhaps for a moonlit walk, perhaps elsewhere. The truth, however, lay in the hills three miles from Oakhurst. There, leaning heavily on Harry's arm, Lady Maria had poured out a torrent of sentimental vows, calling herself his "Molinda" and promising eternal faithfulness. But as the sun warmed her face, revealing the fine lines of her twenty years, Harry felt the burden of her affection as an intolerable drag. He cursed the moonlight, the wine that had loosened his tongue, and the fatal pledge he had made. Her praises grated on his nerves; her claims to his image and hair, which Madame Bernstein had actually burned, struck him as absurd. It was then, as she leaned on him, that Harry suddenly jerked his arm away. In that instant of movement, Lady Maria felt the sharp pang of realization: she had lied, and she had been found out.

At Castlewood, the mood remained grim. Lord March was away, the ladies absent, and only Mr. Will, returning from partridge shooting, greeted them. He turned pale upon seeing his cousin, receiving a cold scowl in return. Yet, they met at supper, maintaining a veneer of civility. The conversation drifted to racing until Harry abruptly demanded to know about "my horse." Will, confused, asked what horse. Harry, his voice curt, specified the brown horse he had wagered against Will and won the night before Will departed. Will denied knowing anything of it. Harry summoned the chaplain to act as umpire. The chaplain confirmed Harry's claim. Will, grinning wryly, noted that Harry had sold the horse for a mere thirty shillings. Harry dismissed the insult as a trivial bet, offering chicken to Lady Maria, but the moment she rose, he followed her to the door, locking it behind her and pocketing the key.

Inside, the pretense shattered. Will, gripping a knife, asked what Harry meant by his behavior. Harry, feigning a bland smile, suggested a quarrel was inevitable and that servants should not witness disputes between gentlemen. He challenged Will regarding the fifty-guinea wager on the horse. Will denied it, laughing nervously. Harry accused him of lying as easily as cheating, stepping closer. "Hands off, Mr. Chaplain, and see fair play!" Harry shouted, his temper flaring. Before the insult could fully register, Will hurled a wine bottle at Harry's head. Harry ducked; the bottle smashed against the wainscot, shattering a portrait of an Esmond ancestor and splashing port wine over the chaplain's wig.

Gentlemen were not inclined to heed church commands. Seizing a large silver-handled knife, Will charged. Harry, recalling boxing techniques from Marybone, caught Will's wrist and delivered a rapid blow to his nose, sending him reeling against the oak panels. Will dropped his knife, which Harry's opponent kicked under the table. Undeterred, Will dashed forward like a battering ram, intending to drive his head into Harry's stomach. Harry, familiar with such brutal maneuvers from both the boxing ring and the plantations, sidestepped and struck Will's right ear with tremendous force. Will collapsed, his head striking the heavy oak table, and fell lifeless.

Harry, still trembling with adrenaline, declared the fight fair and called for the servants. They burst in to find two victims: the chaplain drenched in wine and Will sprawled in his own blood. Harry ordered his neckcloth undone and demanded a fleam to bleed him, but Will soon regained consciousness, his single functional eye rolling wildly before he began to swear and curse with articulate fury. Harry, adopting a stern humor, asked if he required more assistance. Will, recovering his strength, spat insults at the black servant Gumbo, who had tried to offer him water, before skulking off to bed, leaving a trail of curses in his wake.

The next morning, Harry's wit returned. He sent a note to Will, inviting him to ride to London or the Chocolate House, a jest that Will received with violent displeasure. Meanwhile, a simpering maid delivered a letter from Molinda, filled with declarations of undying love and pleas for reconciliation. Harry read it with a sigh, wondering where the golden tablets of his youth had faded. His dignity, however, forbade him to remain at Castlewood after such a scandal. He wrote a formal letter to Lord March, detailing the altercation and his decision to depart, with the chaplain supervising the document to ensure its propriety. Lady Maria wept at his departure, lamenting that he would find no one in London to love him as she did. Harry, however, was already cheering up, his spirits soaring as he crossed Castlewood Bridge, eager to return to the delights of London society.

Back in London, Harry's forty-four pounds from Tunbridge had vanished. Undeterred, he drew upon the five thousand pounds held in his and his brother's names, intending to replenish his funds through luck at the gaming tables. He settled into Bond Street, discarded his mourning attire for laced waistcoats and powdered wigs, and made his entrance into high society. Jack Morris introduced him to the fashionable crowd, and Harry, greedy for wine and pleasure, threw himself into the city's vibrant life.

Amidst this revelry, a letter arrived from the Honorable H.S. Conway, recounting a chaotic night at Vauxhall. Conway described Harry as a "young savage" making noise, winning money, and behaving with the enthusiasm of a child. He recounted how Harry had confronted him over rumors regarding the Esmond family and the Bernstein lineage, only to be pacified with punch and apologies. Conway noted Harry's presence at the club, alongside Lord March and himself, predicting that the three young men would soon fleece one another financially. The letter painted a picture of Harry as a whirlwind of energy and expense, a prodigal son enjoying the fruits of his inheritance while his mother prayed in the west and a tender heart beat in Oakhurst, unaware of the storm he had unleashed.
