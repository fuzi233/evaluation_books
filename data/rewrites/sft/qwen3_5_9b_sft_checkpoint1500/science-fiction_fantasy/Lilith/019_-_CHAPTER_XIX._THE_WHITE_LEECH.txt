I awoke from a deep, dreamless slumber with a searing agony in my right hand. The palm was grotesquely swollen, marked by a triangular puncture that looked exactly as if a leech had torn through the flesh. By midday, the inflammation receded; by evening, the wound had almost vanished entirely. I combed through the cavern, flipping over every rock larger than a fist, searching for anything that could have inflicted such damage, but found nothing.

Weeks dragged by in silence. The figure remained motionless, eyes sealed shut. Yet it could not be dead. There was no scent of rot, no miasma of decay clinging to the air around it. More subtly, the harsh angles of the skeletal frame beneath the skin seemed to soften, rounding out as if flesh were knitting itself back together. The parchment-like texture of the skin appeared to lose its translucence, gaining a fuller, living quality. If any transformation truly occurred—if the tide that had fled toward infinity began to turn back—then life must reside within! The thought sent a jolt of electric joy through me: surely the gentle waves of existence were reclaiming the beautiful shape that had nearly dissolved! Twice daily I scanned for proof of awakening, twice I faced doubt, sometimes despair. Yet whenever I closed my eyes and visualized the moment I first discovered her, hope surged anew.

For several weeks, this cycle continued until one night, after hours of restless wakefulness, I rose to seek the cooling breeze of the open world. Though the stream running through the cave kept the air fresh, the internal heat often grew stifling. Outside, the moon hung full and bright, casting a shadowy clarity within the grotto. Before leaving, I lingered one last time upon my "treasure." "Eternal bliss!" I whispered, the words trembling in the silence. "Do I see her eyes?" Two great spheres, black as if carved from the void of a starless night, seemed to glow with an inner luminescence born of absolute darkness, standing out against the pallid whiteness of her countenance. I crept closer, my heart hammering so violently I feared the sound would startle her. I leaned down. Alas, her lids were firmly closed! Hope and imagination had conspired to create a false dawn! My heart's desire remained unfulfilled! I turned away, collapsed onto the cave floor, and wept bitterly. Then a thought struck me: her eyes had been slightly parted when I found her, and now the terrifying slit through which nothingness peered was gone. Perhaps she had blinked, only to fall back asleep! Or perhaps she was awake, keeping them tightly shut! In either scenario, life, however faint, must have forced them closed. This realization soothed me, and I sank immediately into a heavy sleep.

That night, the bite returned. I awoke gasping with a parched throat, consumed by a burning thirst.

Morning brought another futile search. The wound bore the same signature, healing swiftly by dusk as before. I convinced myself that some giant leech occasionally ventured from the scalding stream. "If blood is its goal," I murmured to the empty air, "so long as I remain here, I need fear nothing for my prize."

It was then, that same morning, as I peeled a grape and removed the seeds to place it gently in her mouth, that her lips twitched—a faint, tentative motion of acceptance. And in that instant, I *knew*: she lived!

My hope, now fortified, turned toward practicality. She needed clothing; she must be able to rise at her own command. Venturing into the forest, I examined the flora for suitable materials. Hardly had I begun before fibrous skeletons, reminiscent of the spiny leaves of the prickly pear, suggested themselves as ideal. I gathered a cache of them, dried them in the sun, separated the netted layers, and quickly wove three thick layers of the tissue together using the sharp tip of an aloe leaf and various strands.

For the following week, the only change was that she accepted the grapes with increasing regularity. The signs grew undeniable: her form was becoming plumper, her complexion fairer. Yet her eyes remained sealed. A terrible dread would seize me periodically, fearing that her growth was some monstrous fungal aberration, the handful of fruit utterly insufficient to fuel such expansion.

Again, the bite came. Whatever entity it was, it had established a routine, visiting every three days. It favored the neck or the arm, delivering a single, precise strike, invariably during my sleep, and never during daylight. Hours I lay wide-eyed, waiting, but I heard no approach, saw no shadow. I likely never felt the actual sting. Eventually, resigned to capturing nothing, I ceased both the daytime searches and the nocturnal watches. I knew from my dwindling strength that I was bleeding at a perilous rate, yet I cared little. In my sight, death yielded to life; a soul gathered force to rescue me from solitude. We would depart together, and I would heal.

The garments were finally complete. Looking at my handiwork with quiet pride, I began matting the fibers into sandals.

One night, I woke abruptly, breathless and faint, craving the air. Rising to crawl from the cave, a soft rustle in the foliage of our makeshift bed froze me in place.

"I caught the vile beast," a weak voice croaked, spoken in my mother tongue. "I caught it in the act!"

She was alive! She spoke! I held back my overwhelming transport, terrified that my sudden joy would terrify her.

"What creature?" I breathed.

"The creature," she replied, "that bit you."

"What was it?"

"A great white leech."

"How large?" I pressed, forcing calm.

"Nearly six feet, I think," she answered.

"You may have saved my life... but how could you touch such horror? How brave!" I cried.

"I did!" was her sole response, though I sensed a shiver run through her.

"Where is it? What did you do with the monster?"

"I threw it into the river."

"It will return, I fear!"

"I doubt I could have killed it, even if I'd known how. I heard you groaning, rose to check, saw the dreadful thing at your throat, and pulled it free. I couldn't hold it back, barely managed to toss it away. I only heard it splash into the water!"

"We'll kill it next time!" I declared, then turned pale, stumbled into the open air, and collapsed.

When consciousness returned, the sun was high. The lady stood a short distance away. Even in the crude attire I had crafted, she possessed a grandeur and grace that transcended the rough fabric. I *had* seen those magnificent eyes! They had burned through the night! Dark as primordial night, they now outshone the daylight. She stood upright as a pillar, regarding me. Her pale cheek offered no emotion, only a silent query. I rose.

"We must depart!" I said. "The white leech..."

I halted. A strange smile had flickered across her beautiful face.

"Did you locate me there?" she asked, gesturing to the cavern.

"No; I brought you here," I replied.

"You brought me?"

"Yes."

"From where?"

"From the forest."

"What happened to my clothes—and my jewels?"

"You had none when I found you."

"Then why did you not abandon me?"

"Because I hoped you were not dead."

"Why should you care?"

"Because I was terribly lonely, and I wanted you to live."

"You would have kept me enchained merely for my beauty!" she accused, her voice laced with scornful pride.

Her words and gaze ignited a surge of indignation within me.

"There was no beauty left in you," I countered.

"Then, again, why did you not leave me alone?"

"Because you were of my own kind."

"Of *your* kind?" she shrieked, her tone dripping with utter contempt.

"I believed so, but I was mistaken!"

"Doubtless you pitied me!"

"Never has a woman claimed more pity, nor deserved any other feeling less!"

With an expression blending pain, humiliation, and an unutterable anger, she turned from me and stood in silence. Within the abyssal pools of her eyes, a starless night lay profound; the hatred for her rescuer had extinguished their former splendor. The light of life had faded from them.

"Had you failed to rouse me, what would you have done?" she asked suddenly, without moving.

"I would have buried it."

"It! What? Would you have buried *this*?" she exploded, wheeling around in a white fury, her arms flung wide, her eyes shooting bolts of cold lightning.

"Nay! I saw nothing of the sort! These weary weeks of vigilance and tending have restored you," I answered plainly, knowing that with such a woman, diplomacy was useless. "Had I detected the slightest sign of decay, I would have interred you instantly."

"Dog of a fool!" she screamed. "I was merely in a trance—Samoil! What a fate! Go and summon the savage woman from whom you borrowed this loathsome disguise!"

"I made it for you. It is ugly, but I did my best."

She straightened to her towering height.

"How long have I been insensible?" she demanded. "A woman could not weave such a gown in a single day!"
