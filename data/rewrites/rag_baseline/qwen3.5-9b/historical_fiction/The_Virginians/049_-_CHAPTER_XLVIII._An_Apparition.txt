The anger left Mr. Draper's throat, but the silence that followed was heavier than the words he had swallowed. Behind him, Harry had risen. The young prisoner's voice cut through the air, a sharp, half-formed exclamation meant to recall the lawyer. Pride, however, acted as a dam. Harry held his tongue; Draper chose not to break. To humble oneself before a man trapped by poverty felt like a violation of the natural order.

"An hour hence will do," Harry thought, sinking back onto the straw mattress. The movement was sluggish, defeated. He did not care for Maria Esmond. The name tasted like ash. He was ashamed of the trap, the way his boyish ardour had been harvested by a wily woman who played the game as her brother played cards. They were flesh and blood, yet they had treated him as prey, using his vulnerability to feed their own selfish ends. They had violated the sanctity of hospitality, turning the young kinsman who sought refuge into a victim. His heart was a wound that refused to clot. He leaned his head back against the rough pillow, and bitter tears soaked the fabric.

"If they had come to Virginia," he whispered to the gloom, "I would have offered a different welcome."

This desolation was interrupted by the grin of Gumbo at the door. "A lady is come to see Master Harry," the servant announced. Following him entered the lady in the capuchin.

Harry sat up, his face pale and haggard, illuminated by the flickering candlelight. The lady, overcome with a sob that broke instantly, crossed the room. Before the servant could retreat, she threw her arms around the young prisoner's neck. It was a gesture of raw, maternal tenderness. She buried her face in his pale cheek, kissing it amidst a torrent of tears.

"Oh, my Harry!" she cried. "Did I ever, ever think to see thee here?"

He recoiled, startled by the intimacy of her presence, but she sank to her knees beside the bed, seizing his feverish hand and clutching his legs. Her regard was genuine, her pity absolute. The wretchedness of his surroundings, the decay of his appearance, struck her heart with the force of a physical blow.

"I—I thought none of you would come!" Harry groaned, his voice cracking.

More tears, more kisses on his hot, young hand, more desperate clasping. This was her reply for a moment.

"Oh, my dear! My dear! I cannot bear to think of thee in misery," she sobbed.

Even hardened hearts possess cracks where light enters. That heart was not all marble; that life was not all desert. His mother could not have loved him more, nor spoken with greater tenderness than this kinswoman now kneeling at his feet.

"Some of the debts, I fear, were owing to my extravagance!" she admitted, and the statement carried the weight of truth. "You bought trinkets and jewels to give me pleasure. Oh, how I hate them now! I little thought I ever could! I have brought them all with me, and more trinkets—here! and here! and all the money I have in the world!"

She poured a cascade of brooches, rings, a watch, and a score of guineas into Harry's lap.

The sight agitated him, stirring something deep and ancient within him.

"Dearest, kindest cousin!" he sobbed. Words failed him, yet his expression conveyed the depth of his gratitude, his affection, his overwhelming emotion. Soon, the despair lifted. He smiled, putting away some of the gifts intended for Maria, recounting the dangers he had faced selling goods on credit and the insult the lawyer had just delivered regarding his finances. He would not accept her money—he claimed he had enough—but he valued her twenty guineas as if they were twenty thousand. He vowed never to forget her love and kindness. By all that was sacred, he would not! He must tell his mother of her goodness. It had buoyed him when he stood on the brink of total collapse under the weight of disgrace. Might Heaven bless her for it!

There is no need to prolong the cousins' conversation. After Maria's visit, the dark day seemed brighter to Harry; the imprisonment felt less like a tomb. The world was not entirely selfish and cold. Here was a creature who truly loved him. Even Castlewood was not as terrible as he had imagined.

He expressed his deepest grief at his inability to assist his kinsman. He was hopelessly in debt. Every shilling he had won from Harry was lost the next day to others. Yet, anything within his power, he would do. He promised to visit Mr. Warrington soon; the man was in waiting today, imprisoned alongside Harry.

Thus, the pair talked cheerfully and affectionately until the shadows lengthened across the room. Then, with a sigh, Maria bade Harry farewell.

The door had barely closed when it swung open again to admit Draper.

"Your humble servant, sir," the attorney said.

His voice grated on Harry's nerves; his presence was an offense.

"I had expected you some hours ago, sir," Harry replied curtly.

"A lawyer's time is not always his own, sir," Draper retorted, fresh from a consultation with a bottle of port at the Grecian. "Never mind, I'm at your orders now. Presume it's all right, Mr. Warrington. Packed your trunk? Why, now there you are in your bedgown still. Let me go down and settle whilst you call in your black man and tidbit yourself. I've a coach at the door, and we'll be off and dine with the old lady."

"Are you going to dine with the Baroness de Bernstein, pray?"

"Not me—no such honour. Had my dinner already. It's you are a-going to dine with your aunt, I suppose?"

"Mr. Draper, you suppose a great deal more than you know," Mr. Warrington said, folding his brocade dressing-gown around himself, looking fierce and tall.

"Great goodness, sir, what do you mean?" Draper asked.

"I mean, sir, that I have considered, and, having given my word to a faithful and honourable lady, it does not become me to withdraw it."

"Confound it, sir!" shrieked the lawyer. "I tell you she has lost the paper. There's nothing to bind you—nothing. Why she's old enough to be—"

"Enough, sir," Mr. Warrington stamped his foot. "You seem to think you are talking to some other pettifogger. I take it, Mr. Draper, you are not accustomed to have dealings with men of honour."

"Pettifogger, indeed!" Draper cried in a fury. "Men of honour, indeed! I'd have you to know, Mr. Warrington, that I'm as good a man of honour as you. I don't know so many gamblers and horse-jockeys, perhaps. I haven't gambled away my patrimony, and lived as if I was a nobleman on two hundred a year. I haven't bought watches on credit, and pawned—touch me if you dare, sir," and the lawyer sprang toward the door.

"That is the way out, sir. You can't go through the window, because it is barred," Mr. Warrington said calmly.

"And the answer I take to my client is No, then!" Draper screamed.

Harry stepped forward, his hands clenched. "If you utter another word," he said, "I'll—"

The door slammed shut. The sentence died unfinished. Draper departed, furious, heading to Madame de Bernstein. Though he gave her the best version of his story, he received fiercer language from her than he had from Mr. Warrington himself.

"What? Shall she trust me, and I desert her?" Harry stalked up and down his room, his brocade rustling like dry leaves. "Dear, faithful, generous woman! If I lie in prison for years, I'll be true to her."

With his lawyer dismissed after a stormy interview, the desolate old woman sat down to the meal she had hoped to share with her nephew. The chair remained empty before her, the glasses shining beside the silver. Dish after dish was laid before her by the silent major-domo, tasted, and pushed away. Finally, the man pressed his mistress.

"It is eight o'clock," he said. "You have had nothing all day. It is good for you to eat."

She could not eat. She wanted her coffee. Let Case go get it. The lacquies cleared the table, leaving their mistress sitting alone before the vacant chair.

Presently, the old servant re-entered without the coffee, his face etched with a strange, terrified expression. "MR. WARRINGTON!"

The old woman gasped, rising from her armchair only to sink back down, trembling violently.

"So you are come, sir, are you?" she said, her voice a shaky tremolo. "Bring back the—Ah!" Here she screamed, "Gracious God, who is it?"

Her eyes widened, staring wildly. Her white face looked ghastly beneath the rouge. Clinging to the arms of her chair for support, she watched as the visitor approached.

A gentleman whose face and figure exactly mirrored Harry Warrington, whose voice possessed strangely similar tones, had followed the servant into the room. He bowed toward the Baroness.

"You expected my brother, madam?" he said.
