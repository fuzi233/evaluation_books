Martin had laid his plans with care, weaving a scheme for his own amusement—but Fate, that relentless spinner, unraveled them with a single tug. Moore’s stubborn will proved stronger than any design. Each day, he defied Mrs. Horsfall’s expectations, reclaiming his independence piece by piece. First, he dismissed her from tending him; then he refused her coffee, insisting on breakfasting with the family. At last, he banished her from his chamber entirely.

The women of Briarfield gasped when he stepped outside, the crisp air stinging his hollow cheeks. The following morning, he followed Mr. Yorke to his counting-house and demanded a chaise. He would return to Hollow’s Cottage that very afternoon.

Mrs. Yorke protested, but her husband only laughed and sent for the carriage. Moore paid his debts in silence, pressing coins into the hands of servants and even Mrs. Horsfall, whose wounded pride melted at the chime of silver.

Then came the hardest farewell—the mistress of Briarfield herself, sunk in wounded dignity. He bent over her, his pallid face softened by a smile.

"Goodbye," he murmured, the word trembling slightly.

"And why must you leave us?" she demanded. "Stay, and we will tend you as you deserve."

"Goodbye," he repeated. "You have been a mother to me. Let your wayward son have one embrace."

Like a foreigner, he offered first one cheek, then the other. She kissed him.

"What a burden I’ve been," he muttered.

"You are the worst burden now—a headstrong boy!" she retorted. "Who will nurse you at the cottage? Hortense knows less of sickness than a child."

"Thank God for that. I’ve had enough nursing to last a lifetime."

The little girls wept as he kissed them farewell. The boys watched the chaise in silence, but Moore spared them no parting words. To Yorke, he only said, "You’re well rid of me. That unlucky shot turned Briarfield into a hospital. Visit me soon."

The carriage rolled away. Within the hour, he stood at his own garden gate, leaning against it, breath frosting in the cold twilight.

"Six months ago, I left this place a proud, angry man," he mused. "Now I return wiser, weaker, but no longer afraid. Ruin may come—I see it clearly—but it no longer feels like disgrace. If the worst arrives, I can work as Joe Scott does, earn my bread honestly. And if by some miracle trade revives..." He shook his head. "No. Better to face the truth. Ruin will come. I’ll take a sapling, cross the sea, plant it in American soil. Louis will go with me. But will *she*?"

He stepped inside.

The cottage glowed in the firelight, scrubbed to perfection in his absence. Hortense fussed over him, pride in her eyes.

"Brother, the house is clean—spotless!"

"It is," he agreed, glancing around. "Bright, somehow. Almost cheerful."

"I had it thoroughly cleaned for your return."

"You ought to celebrate—invite someone to tea. Let them see how well you’ve kept it."

"Miss Mann, perhaps?"

"Too late to disturb her. The cold would trouble her."

"Miss Ainley, then?"

"Too far. Send Harry Scott to the rectory—ask Caroline Helstone to join us."

"Tomorrow might be better—"

"Tonight," he insisted. "Let her see it like this—your handiwork."

He wrote a note, dispatched it, and within the hour, Caroline arrived. She moved with quiet grace, her cheeks rosier than he remembered, her curls smooth, her dress immaculate. She lingered in the kitchen, admiring the new kitten, soothing the startled canary, before gliding into the parlor.

The greetings were calm, cousinly—yet warmth seeped into the room like sunlight. Over tea, Hortense chattered, but soon excused herself, drawn irresistibly to the neglected depths of her drawers.

Left alone, Caroline’s voice took on a new richness, her words flowing with an ease Moore had never heard before.

"You look as if you’ve had good news," he observed.

"Do I?"

"I sent for you for comfort, but you’ve given more than I expected."

She laughed. "Is that so?"

"You’re brighter, lighter. There’s sunshine about you."

"It’s pleasant to be here."

"Pleasant for me as well. But what’s the source of this change? What hope has lit you up?"

She hesitated, then spoke softly. "Mamma. I’ve learned to love her deeply—and she loves me in return. I nurse her now, sew for her, keep her from growing old-fashioned. She’s wise, Robert—wiser than I ever knew. Every day, I love her more."

"Enough to make a man jealous," he teased.

She smiled. "The other reason I’m glad—is you."

"Me?"

"That you’re better. That we’re friends."

"Friends," he echoed. "Once, I thought we never would be."

"Caroline," he said after a pause, "one day I’ll tell you something unflattering about myself. It won’t please you."

"Don’t. I can’t bear to think ill of you."

"And I can’t bear your thinking better of me than I deserve."

She fell silent, her fingers tracing the edge of her work-bag.

"Does it concern anyone besides me?" he pressed.

She flushed.

"Tell me," he urged. "Whisper it."

She leaned close, her breath warm against his ear. A name passed between them—halting, uncertain.

His eyes darkened. "You’re sure?"

"I saw her heart, Robert. In the dead of night, she let me see it—holy as a shrine, pure as snow, warm as flame, strong as death."

"Can she love?"

"She does."

"Who?"

She whispered again.

He stiffened, then laughed—a short, sharp sound. "You’re certain?"

"Certain as I am of my own soul."

For a moment, he was silent. Then, softly: "Must I call her noble now?"

"If you speak truth—yes."

"Must I forgive her?"

"Forgive her? Was *she* wrong, or were you?"

His voice dropped lower. "Must I love her, Caroline?"

She looked up sharply, caught between affection and reproach. "Don’t you dare."

He smiled. "Then I won’t. Not unless you command it."

Before she could reply, Hortense returned, and the moment slipped away like mist. But as Caroline left, Moore stood at the stairfoot, his gaze following her.

"Shirley," he murmured to himself. "So it’s *you*."

And for the first time in months, the future did not seem so bleak.
