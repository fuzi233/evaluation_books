Eight months after the family arrived at Offendene—that is, by the end of the following June—a rumor swept through the neighborhood that sparked excitement among many. While unrelated to the outcome of the American War, this news resonated deeply across all social strata within a specific radius around Wanchester. Corn factors, brewers, horse dealers, and saddlers alike hailed it as a praiseworthy development, celebrating it on abstract grounds as proof of the value of an aristocracy in a free nation like England. Even the blacksmith in the hamlet of Diplow felt a sense of impending prosperity; the wives of laborers dreamed that their agile sons of ten or twelve would soon find employment serving gentlemen in livery; and the local farmers, though tinged with bitterness and reservation, admitted that a man might finally find an easier market for a rick of old hay or a wagon-load of straw.

If these were the aspirations of those outside society, it follows that those "betters" possessed even stronger reasons for satisfaction, driven perhaps more by the pleasures of life than its business. Yet, marriage falls under both categories. Just as the announcement of a royal visit ignites dreams of knighthood or a baronetcy among common folk, this news conjured a vague, floating vision of matrimony in the minds of several well-bred individuals.

The news was that Diplow Hall—the estate of Sir Hugo Mallinger, which for two years had stared its white, shuttered windows in a painfully cross-eyed fashion at its fine elms, beeches, lilied pools, and deer-speckled acres—was being prepared for a new tenant. It was destined to be inhabited in proper style throughout the remainder of the summer and the hunting season. However, not by Sir Hugo himself, but by his nephew, Mr. Mallinger Grandcourt, the presumptive heir to the baronetcy, since his uncle's marriage had yielded only daughters.

Nor was this the only stroke of fortune favoring young Grandcourt, as he was pleasantly called. While his claim to the baronetcy stemmed from his father, his mother had bestowed a baronial streak upon his bloodline. Should certain intermediaries in the distant background pass away, he would ascend to become a Baron and Peer of the Realm. Nature's uneven allotment grants the tuft only to the male bird, yet we need not blindly follow hasty philosophers who urge us to replicate nature in all its quirks. If Mr. Mallinger Grandcourt secured a baronetcy or a peerage, his wife would share the title. Combined with his existing fortune, this prospect made the choice of a spouse particularly significant; indeed, being currently unmarried, he was already the subject of sympathetic interest from many who viewed him as a woman destined for security.

Some readers might deem it incredulous that people would build matrimonial prospects solely on the report of a wealthy bachelor approaching maturity, dismissing such thoughts as mere gall. They would argue that neither they nor their kin possess such unbridled minds, asserting that human nature knows better than to speculate on fallacies. However, let it be noted that this narrative does not concern human nature in general, but rather a select group in a corner of Wessex—people of impeccable reputation, all of whom maintained visiting ties with persons of rank.

Consider the Arrowpoints, residing in their beautiful home at Quetcham. No one could accuse them of sordid motives regarding their daughter Catherine's marriage, given that they could leave her an inheritance of at least half a million. Yet, harboring affectionate anxieties about her position—especially after she had resolutely rejected Lord Slogan, an unexceptionable Irish peer whose estate lacked both drainage and population—they wondered, perhaps motivated by more than charity, whether Mr. Grandcourt was good-looking, of sound constitution, virtuous, or at least reformed. Were he a liberal conservative, or perhaps not *too* liberal-conservative, his succession to the title would be an event eagerly desired.

If the Arrowpoints engaged in such rumination, it is less surprising that Mr. Gascoigne, a clergyman nonetheless subject to parental and guardian anxieties, felt similarly stirred. We have witnessed how both he and Mrs. Gascoigne began to feel overwhelmed by the management of young creatures who seemed impossible to hold with bit or bridle, or any metaphor suggesting judicious advice.

Naturally, people did not openly discuss their true feelings regarding Mr. Grandcourt's arrival. On no subject is such candor prudently practiced—not even concerning the generation of acids or the destination of fixed stars. One's contemporary might find one's ideas ingenious and preemptively apply them, or conversely, hold opposing views and judge accordingly. Mr. Gascoigne did not inquire of Mr. Arrowpoint for trustworthy sources on Grandcourt as a potential husband for a charming girl; nor did Mrs. Arrowpoint whisper to Mrs. Davilow that if the prospective peer sought a wife locally, the only logical candidate was Catherine, who, however, would refuse unless he proved capable of securing her happiness.

Indeed, even to his wife, the rector remained silent about the possibility of a matrimonial match, anticipating that Mr. Grandcourt might appear at the next Archery Meeting alongside Gwendolen. Though Mrs. Gascoigne's mind was undoubtedly more active in this direction, she had only interjected casually to her sister, "It would be a mercy, Fanny, if that girl were well married!" Mrs. Davilow, sensing a critique of her darling in the fervor of that wish, offered no audible reply, though inwardly she thought, "You will not get her to marry for your pleasure"; the mild mother growing somewhat saucy when identifying herself with her daughter.

To her husband, Mrs. Gascoigne remarked, "I hear Mr. Grandcourt has two places of his own, but he comes to Diplow for the hunt. It is to be hoped he sets a good example in the neighborhood. Have you heard what sort of a young man he is, Henry?"

Mr. Gascoigne claimed ignorance; at least, if his male acquaintances had gossiped in his hearing, he was unwilling to repeat it or emphasize it in his own mind. He deemed it futile, even if appropriate, to show curiosity about the past of a young man whose birth, wealth, and leisure rendered many habits venial under other circumstances. Whatever Grandcourt had done, he had not ruined himself. It is well known that in gambling, whether professional or recreational, a man with the strength of mind to stop before ruining others is considered reformed. This was merely an illustration: Mr. Gascoigne had not heard that Grandcourt was a gambler. We can hardly label him singular for feeling that a landed proprietor with noble blood was not an object for suspicious inquiry like a reformed character offering himself as a butler or footman. Where a man can afford to dispense with reformation, it is rarely anything but genuine. Furthermore, there was no evidence suggesting Mr. Grandcourt needed reform more than other youths of twenty-five, and ultimately, the significance of his past must be judged by his present reality.

Mrs. Davilow, though outwardly unmoved by her sister's suggestive comment, could not remain indifferent internally to an advent promising a brilliant fate for Gwendolen. Speculation on "what may be" arises naturally, inevitably forming images when strangers are mentioned. First, Mr. Grandcourt's name evoked in Mrs. Davilow's mind the picture of a handsome, accomplished, excellent young man, perfectly suited as a husband. Then came the further question: Would Gwendolen be satisfied with him? Who knew what would appeal to that girl's taste or touch her affections? It might be something other than mere excellence. Thus, the image of the perfect suitor gave way to a fluctuating blend of qualities imagined to win Gwendolen's heart.

In struggling to identify the precise combination ensuring success, the mother told herself, "It would not signify about her being in love, if she would only accept the right person." For whatever marriage had been for herself, how could she desire less for her daughter? The difference lay in her own misfortunes; she never dared dwell extensively on the desirability of marriage with Gwendolen, fearing an answer akin to that of the future Madame Roland. When her gentle mother urged the acceptance of a suitor, saying, "Tu seras heureuse, ma chère," the response had been, "Oui, maman, comme toi." Regarding the problematic Mr. Grandcourt, Mrs. Davilow would least of all willingly drop hints of the airy castle-building she felt ashamed of, for such a hint might tilt Gwendolen's thoughts adversely, causing her to detest the desirable husband beforehand.

Since the scene following poor Rex's farewell visit, the mother had felt a renewed sense of peril in touching the mystery of her child's feelings and rashly determining her welfare; she could conceive of welfare only in the shape of marriage. Discussions about Gwendolen's attire for the Archery Meeting provided a relevant topic. Once it was decided that, as a touch of color on her white cashmere, nothing compared to pale green—a feather she had been testing in her hat before the mirror—Mrs. Davilow felt her ears tingle when Gwendolen suddenly threw herself into the pose of drawing her bow, saying with a look of comic delight, "How I pity all the other girls at the Archery Meeting—all thinking of Mr. Grandcourt! And they have not a shadow of a chance."
