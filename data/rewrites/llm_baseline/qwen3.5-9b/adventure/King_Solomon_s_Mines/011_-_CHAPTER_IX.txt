There is no need for me to recount in tedious detail the events of our march toward Loo. Our journey along Solomon's Great Road lasted two full days, a path that cuts a straight line right into the heart of Kukuanaland. As we traveled, the landscape seemed to grow increasingly wealthy and prosperous. The kraals multiplied, each surrounded by vast belts of cultivated fields, all constructed according to the same impressive design as the first camp we had encountered. Every settlement was heavily guarded by substantial military forces. Indeed, in Kukuanaland—as among the Germans, the Zulus, and the Maasai—every able-bodied man is a soldier. Consequently, the entire fighting strength of the nation stands ready for either offensive campaigns or defensive wars. During our passage, we were constantly overtaken by thousands of warriors rushing toward Loo to participate in the great annual review and festival. I had never witnessed troops more splendid.

At sunset on the second day, we halted to rest atop a ridge overlooking the route. Below us, spread across a beautiful and fertile plain, lay the town of Loo itself. For a native settlement, it was an enormous place, roughly five miles in circumference, with outlying kraals extending outward to serve as regimental cantonments during major ceremonies. To the north, about two miles away, rose a peculiar horseshoe-shaped hill—a feature we were destined to encounter more intimately later. The location was picturesque; a river flowed through the center of the kraal, dividing it into two sections, and appeared to be bridged at several points, exactly as we had observed from the slopes of Sheba's Breasts earlier. Far in the distance, sixty or seventy miles away, three massive snow-capped mountains emerged from the flat plain, positioned at the vertices of a triangle. Their formation differed markedly from Sheba's Breasts; rather than smooth and rounded, these peaks were sheer and precipitous.

Infadoos noticed us staring at them and offered a comment.

"The road ends there," he said, gesturing toward the mountains, which the Kukuanas called the "Three Witches."

"Why does it end?" I asked.

"Who knows?" he replied with a shrug. "The mountains are filled with caves, and there lies a great pit between them. Long ago, the wise men traveled there to obtain whatever they sought for this country. Today, it is there that our kings are interred in the Place of Death."

"What did they seek?" I asked eagerly.

"Nay, I do not know. Only the Lords who have fallen from the Stars possess that knowledge," he answered with a sharp glance. Clearly, he withheld information he deemed inappropriate to share.

"Yes," I continued, "you are correct; we learn many things from the Stars. I have heard, for instance, that the ancient wise men climbed these mountains seeking bright stones, delightful playthings, and yellow iron."

"My lord is wise," he responded coldly. "I am merely a child and cannot converse with my lord on such profound matters. My lord must speak with Gagool the Elder at the king's residence; she is as wise as you." With that, he departed.

As soon as he was gone, I turned to the others and indicated the mountains. "Those are Solomon's diamond mines," I stated.

Umbopa, standing nearby and seemingly lost in one of his characteristic trances of abstraction, caught my words.

"Yes, Macumazahn," he interjected in Zulu, "the diamonds are certainly there, and you shall have them, since you white men are so fond of toys and money."

"How do you know that, Umbopa?" I asked sharply, wary of his enigmatic behavior.

He laughed. "I dreamed it in the night, white men;" then he too turned abruptly and walked away.

"Now what," queried Sir Henry, "is our black friend implying? It is evident he knows more than he chooses to reveal. By the way, Quatermain, has he mentioned anything regarding—my brother?"

"Nothing," I replied. "He has questioned everyone he befriended, yet they all insist that no white man has ever been seen in this country before."

"Do you suppose he actually arrived here?" suggested Good. "We reached this place only by a miracle; is it probable he could have gotten here without the map?"

"I don't know," said Sir Henry gloomily, "but somehow I feel certain I shall find him."

Slowly the sun descended, and suddenly darkness swept down upon the land like a physical entity. There was no transitional period, no gentle fading; in these latitudes, twilight does not exist. The shift from day to night was as abrupt and absolute as the transition from life to death. The sun vanished, and the world was instantly draped in shadows. Yet this darkness did not endure for long. In the west, a glow appeared, followed by rays of silver light, until finally, the full and glorious moon illuminated the plain, shooting its gleaming beams far and wide, bathing the earth in a faint radiance.

We stood watching this magnificent sight, observing how the stars paled before this chastened majesty, feeling our spirits lift in the presence of a beauty beyond description. My life has been rugged, yet there are a few things for which I am grateful, chief among them being the privilege of witnessing this moon shine over Kukuanaland.

Our reverie was interrupted by our courteous companion, Infadoos.

"If my lords are rested, we shall proceed to Loo, where a hut awaits your arrival tonight. The moon is now bright, ensuring we shall not stumble in the dark."

We agreed, and within an hour we reached the outskirts of the town. Its expanse, marked by thousands of campfires, appeared utterly endless. Indeed, Good, ever prone to a jest, dubbed it "Unlimited Loo." Soon we approached a moat protected by a drawbridge, where we were greeted by the clashing of weapons and the hoarse challenge of a sentinel. Infadoos supplied a password I could not discern, which was acknowledged with a salute, allowing us to pass through the central thoroughfare of this great grass city. After nearly half an hour of walking past countless rows of huts, Infadoos finally stopped before the gate of a small cluster of dwellings surrounding a courtyard paved with powdered limestone. He informed us that these were designated as our "humble" quarters.

We entered and discovered that a hut had been assigned to each of us. These structures surpassed any we had previously seen, containing remarkably comfortable beds made of tanned skins laid upon mattresses of fragrant grass. Food was already prepared; shortly after washing ourselves with water drawn from earthenware jars, several young women of striking beauty brought us roasted meats and corn cobs, elegantly arranged on wooden platters, presenting them with deep bows.

We ate and drank, and then, at our request—which prompted smiles from the obliging young ladies—all the beds were moved into a single hut. Exhausted by our long journey, we threw ourselves onto them to sleep.

Upon waking, the sun was high overhead. The female attendants, showing no signs of modesty, were already inside the hut, having been summoned to assist us in getting dressed.

"Get dressed, indeed!" grumbled Good. "With nothing but a flannel shirt and boots, that takes little time. I wish you would ask them for my trousers, Quatermain."

I requested them, only to be informed that these sacred items had already been taken to the king, who intended to receive us in the morning.

To their surprise and disappointment, after asking the young ladies to leave, we proceeded to dress as best we could under the circumstances. Good even went so far as to shave the right side of his face again; we advised him strictly against touching the left side, where a respectable crop of whiskers had grown. As for myself and Sir Henry, we were content with a thorough wash and grooming our hair. Sir Henry's golden locks had nearly reached his shoulders, making him resemble an ancient Dane more than ever, while my own scruffy beard had grown to a full inch, exceeding the half-inch limit I generally preferred.

By the time we had finished breakfast and smoked a pipe, a messenger arrived—not less a personage than Infadoos himself—to announce that King Twala was ready to receive us, provided we wished to come.

We replied that we preferred to wait until the sun rose slightly higher, citing our lingering fatigue from the journey. It is always prudent, when dealing with uncivilized peoples, not to appear overly eager; they often mistake haste for disrespect or lack of awe. Thus, although we were as keen to meet Twala as he was to see us, we sat and waited an hour, utilizing the time to prepare gifts from our limited supplies: the Winchester rifle formerly used by poor Ventvogel and some beads. We decided to present the rifle and ammunition to His Royal Highness, saving the beads for his wives and courtiers. We had already distributed a few to Infadoos and Scragga, finding them delighted, as they had never seen such objects before. Finally declaring ourselves ready, we set off for the audience, with Umbopa carrying the rifle and beads.

After walking a few hundred yards, we arrived at an enclosure resembling the one housing our lodgings, but fifty times larger, covering at least six or seven acres. Surrounding the perimeter fence stood a row of huts, inhabited by the king's wives. Directly opposite the gateway, on the far side of the open space, stood a very large hut, built separately, where his majesty resided.
