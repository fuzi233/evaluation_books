My stay at La Terrasse extended beyond the end of the vacation, thanks to Mrs. Bretton’s thoughtful care. Her son had declared one day that “Lucy is not yet strong enough to return to that dreadful pensionnat,” and without delay, she drove to the Rue Fossette, met with the directress, and secured my continued rest under the pretense that full recovery required both quiet and change. But this kindness brought an unexpected visit—Madame Beck herself came calling.

One bright afternoon, she arrived in a fiacre, drawn all the way to the château. I suppose she wanted to see for herself what sort of place Dr. John inhabited. The grounds were lovely, the house neat and inviting—more than she expected, clearly. She praised everything: the blue salon was “une pièce magnifique”; she congratulated me warmly on having found such admirable friends—“tellement dignes, aimables, et respectables.” She paid me a compliment too, though it was delivered with her usual flourish. When Dr. John entered, she rushed toward him with uncontainable energy, launching into a torrent of French—brimming with praise for his home, his mother (“la digne châtelaine”), and his own appearance, which, indeed, looked robust and well-kept. He listened with a good-natured smile, amused but patient, as she poured out her admiration. That day, Madame Beck was radiant—like a catherine-wheel spinning with light and cheer. But when I followed her to the carriage, just before she climbed in, I saw a startling shift: the sparkle vanished. She sat rigid, grave, almost severe. A strange little woman.

I teased Dr. John afterward about her devotion to him. He laughed heartily—his eyes lit up as he mimicked her dramatic speeches. He had a keen sense of humor and made the most delightful companion—when he wasn’t thinking of Miss Fanshawe.

It’s said that sitting in gentle sunlight helps the weak regain strength. When little Georgette Beck recovered from illness, I would carry her in my arms and walk slowly through the garden beneath a wall heavy with ripening grapes. The sun warmed her frail body just as it ripened the fruit.

There are people whose presence brings comfort like sunlight does to the body. Dr. Bretton and his mother were among them. They didn’t seek to impress; they simply gave joy naturally—without effort or show. Every day during my stay, some small plan emerged that brought pleasure and meaning. Though Dr. John’s schedule was full, he always managed to join us on our outings. I never understood how he balanced so much—so many duties, yet always time left for us. I often saw him busy, but never overwhelmed, never flustered or irritable. His work flowed with ease, done with quiet strength and cheerful energy. Under his guidance, I saw more of Villette—its streets, its outskirts, its people—in two weeks than I had in eight months before.

He took me to places I’d never heard of, shared insights with genuine interest. Talking with him was never a chore—nor was listening. He avoided generalities and dry facts. Instead, he spoke with attention to detail, observing character with depth, not surface. This gave his words weight. And because he drew from his own experience—not from books or borrowed phrases—his thoughts felt fresh, alive, rare.

In those days, I began to see another side of him—one that bloomed like dawn. While his mother had deep kindness, Dr. John possessed something greater—a wider generosity. When we visited the Basse-Ville, the poorer part of town, I realized his visits weren’t only medical—they were acts of compassion. He moved among the destitute not as a duty, but as a man who simply couldn’t ignore suffering. The poor loved him; hospital patients greeted him with warmth, even reverence.

But let me pause. I must not drift into blind praise. I know well that Dr. John was not perfect—no more than I am. Human weakness touched every part of him. There was no moment when he didn’t reveal something less than divine. A god could not have borne his occasional cruelty, his vanity, or his fleeting indulgence in the present. He did not waste himself on excess, but he fed his self-love with quiet pride—taking delight in attention, in being admired, without regard for cost or consequence.

The reader may notice a contradiction: the public Dr. Bretton—the modest, tireless physician—and the private man—aware of his worth, enjoying praise, sometimes recklessly seeking it. Both are true.

It was hard to serve him quietly. You thought you’d made something small for him unnoticed—only for him to smile and say he’d watched every step. He liked knowing he was cared for, and he let it show in his eyes, in his voice.

And yet, he had a stubborn way of repaying kindness. If his mother worked for him, he returned it with laughter, teasing affection, and liveliness. If I helped him, he planned some quiet pleasure in return.

His knowledge of Villette amazed me—deep, thorough, intimate. Not just the streets, but hidden halls, museums, sacred spaces—he seemed to hold the key to every door. I knew nothing of science, but art stirred me. I loved visiting galleries, especially alone. In company, I felt drained—forced to pretend interest, to keep up conversation while half my mind shut down. No well-bred child or educated adult ever failed to shame me in such moments. But Dr. Bretton understood. He’d take me early, before crowds came, leave me there for hours, then return when his own duties were done. Alone, I could breathe. I didn’t always admire—I questioned, studied, judged. At first, there was conflict: my will demanded I praise what was supposed to be great, while my instinct refused. The harder I tried, the more I resisted. Eventually, I gave up. Why force myself? I settled into stillness, finding peace before ninety-nine out of a hundred paintings.

To me, a truly original painting was as rare as a great book. Standing before famous works, I dared to think: “This isn’t nature. Nature’s light never looked like this—never so murky, so unnatural.” The sky was indigo, but not sky-blue; the trees were thick, shapeless blobs. Some fat women painted with care struck me as anything but goddesses. Many Flemish scenes—exquisite in detail—were better suited to fashion plates than art. Yet here and there, truth broke through: a storm on a mountain, a southern sunlit day, a face that revealed genius, a portrait that caught a soul. These fragments I treasured—like friends.

One morning, nearly alone in a gallery, I found myself before a massive painting, placed in prime light, protected by a rope, with a cushioned bench before it—for those who wished to worship it long enough to need a seat. It seemed to rule the room.

It depicted a woman—larger than life, I thought. If weighed, she’d surely tip the scales between fourteen and sixteen stone. She was immensely fed—meat, bread, vegetables, liquids—must have gone into her frame. She lay half-reclined on a couch. But why? Daylight shone around her. She looked healthy, strong enough to work twice over. She had no excuse for lounging. She should have stood—or at least sat upright. And her clothes? Out of twenty-seven yards of fabric, she’d made something utterly inadequate. Then, the mess around her—pots, vases, goblets, flowers tangled together, curtains piled haphazardly—was unbearable. Checking the catalogue, I learned the name: “Cleopatra.”

I sat down—why not? The bench was there. I found the painting mostly absurd, though some details—roses, gold cups, jewels—were finely done. But overall, it was grandiose nonsense. As I studied it, the room filled. I didn’t mind. Soon I grew bored and turned to nearby still lifes—wildflowers, mossy nests, eggs gleaming like pearls beneath green water—all hanging humbly beneath that monstrous canvas.

Then a tap on my shoulder. I turned. A frowning face leaned close.

“Que faites-vous ici?” a voice asked.

“Mais, Monsieur, je m'amuse.”

“You amuse yourself? And how, pray? First, please rise. Take my arm—we’ll go to the other side.”

I obeyed. M. Paul Emanuel had returned from Rome, now a seasoned traveler, and no less strict than before.

“Let me escort you to your party,” he said, leading me across the room.

“I have no party.”

“You’re not alone?”

“Yes, Monsieur.”

“Did you come alone?”

“No. Dr. Bretton brought me.”

“Dr. Bretton and his mother, of course?”

“No—just Dr. Bretton.”

“And he told you to look at *that* picture?”

“Not at all. I discovered it myself.”

M. Paul’s hair was cut short—like raven feathers. I began to guess his intent and enjoyed keeping calm, watching him grow agitated.

“Astonishing English boldness!” he exclaimed. “Such strange women, these English!”

“What’s wrong, Monsieur?”

“Wrong? How dare you, a young lady, sit calmly like a boy, staring at *that* painting?”

“It’s ugly, but why shouldn’t I look?”

“Bon! bon! Let’s speak no more of it. But you must not be here alone.”

“If I have no society—no *party*, as you call it—what difference does it make? No one interferes with me.”

“Taisez-vous, et asseyez-vous là—là!” He set a chair firmly in a dull corner, before four particularly dreary frames.

“Mais, Monsieur?”

“Mais, Mademoiselle, sit and do not move—entendez-vous?—until someone comes for you or I give permission.”

“Quel triste coin!” I said. “Et quelles laids tableaux!”

Indeed, they were. Four paintings titled “La vie d’une femme.” Painted flat, pale, lifeless. The first: a “Jeune Fille” leaving church, missal in hand, dress prim, eyes down, mouth tight—a caricature of prudish hypocrisy. The second: a “Mariée,” kneeling, hands pressed together, eyes wide, showing the whites—annoyingly devout. The third: a “Jeune Mère,” gazing sadly at a swollen, unhealthy baby. The fourth: a “Veuve,” black-clad, holding a child, both studying a fancy tombstone. All were cold, ghostly, stiff. What women to live with—insincere, joyless, empty beings. As bad as the idle Cleopatra in her absurd splendor.

I couldn’t focus long on them. Slowly, I turned back to the gallery.

A crowd gathered around the Cleopatra—half of them ladies. Later, M. Paul explained: these were “des dames,” allowed to view what no “demoiselle” should glance at. I told him plainly I disagreed. He responded with his usual authority—demanding silence and accusing me of rashness and ignorance. Never had I seen a man so domineering in a professor’s role. Yet I noticed he stared at the painting himself with ease, lingering long. Occasionally, he glanced my way—checking if I obeyed.

After a while, he approached again.

“Had you been ill?” he asked. “I heard you were.”

“Yes—but I’m well now.”

“Where did you spend the vacation?”

“Mainly at the Rue Fossette—partly with Madame Bretton.”

“Heard you were left alone there—is that true?”

“Not entirely. Marie Broc was with me.”

He shrugged. His face shifted—anger, pity, disdain, sorrow—flickered across it. Marie Broc was known to him. Each lesson in the lowest class brought a battle within him: irritation at her appearance, her manners, her unpredictability—yet also pity for her suffering. His nature pulled him in opposite directions. He rarely yielded to anger, but when he did, it was terrifying. His passions were fierce, his attachments and aversions equally intense. Holding them back didn’t lessen their power—it only made them more visible. Such men often inspired fear, but that fear was misplaced. Nothing unsettled him more than dread in others. Nothing calmed him like trust, tempered with gentleness. But understanding that required insight—and few understood him.

“How did you manage with Marie Broc?” he asked after a pause.

“Monsieur, I did my best—but it was terrible to be alone with her.”

“So you lack courage. Perhaps charity too. You don’t have the qualities of a Sister of Mercy.”

[He was religious in his way—the self-sacrificing ideals of Catholicism deeply moved him.]

“I don’t know. I cared for her as well as I could. But when her aunt came to take her away, I was relieved.”

“Ah! You’re selfish. Women have nursed entire hospitals of such cases. Could you not have done the same?”

“Could Monsieur do it himself?”

“Women worthy of the name surpass our flawed, indulgent sex in such virtues.”

“I washed her, kept her clean, fed her, tried to entertain her. But she made faces instead of speaking.”

“You think you’ve done great things?”

“No—only what I could.”

“Then your powers are limited. Tending one idiot made you sick.”

“Not from that, Monsieur. I had a nervous fever—my mind was ill.”

“Vraiment! Vous valez peu de chose. You’re not heroic. Your courage fails in solitude—it only gives you the nerve to stare coldly at Cleopatra.”

I could have shown anger. But I hadn’t been angry with him before, and I didn’t feel like starting now.

“Cleopatra,” I repeated quietly. “Monsieur has been looking at Cleopatra too. What does he think of her?”

“Cela ne vaut rien,” he replied. “A magnificent woman—imperial height, Juno’s form—but one I’d never want as wife, daughter, or sister. So you will not look at her again.”

“But I’ve been looking—while you’ve spoken. I can see her clearly from here.”

“Turn to the wall and study your four pictures of a woman’s life.”

“Excuse me, M. Paul—they’re too hideous. If you admire them, I’ll leave you to them.”

“Mademoiselle,” he said, forcing a grimace that might have been a smile—though it was more like a twitch. “You Protestants astonish me. You Englishwomen walk through red-hot ploughshares without burning. I believe if some of you were thrown into Nebuchadnezzar’s furnace, you’d emerge untouched by fire.”

“Will Monsieur move an inch to the side?”

“How? What are you looking at now? Are you recognizing someone in that group of young men?”

“I think so—yes. There’s someone I know.”
