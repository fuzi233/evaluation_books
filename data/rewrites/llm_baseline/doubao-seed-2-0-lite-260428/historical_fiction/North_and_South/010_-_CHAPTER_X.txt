“We are the trees whom shaking fastens more.” — GEORGE HERBERT

Mr. Thornton left his home without stepping back into the dining room, already running late as he walked at a rapid pace toward Crampton. He had no wish to slight his new friend with disrespectful unpunctuality, and the parish church clock struck half-past seven just as he knocked at the Hales’ door, waiting patiently while Dixon dragged her feet—always doubly tardy when forced to stoop to the indignity of answering a visitor’s bell.

He was ushered straight into the tiny drawing room, where Mr. Hale greeted him warmly before leading him over to his wife: pale, swathed in a soft wool shawl, her bearing a silent, gentle excuse for the languid coldness of her greeting. Margaret was lighting the central lamp as dusk gathered, the soft golden glow spilling out across the dusky edges of the room, where the Hales kept their windows open to the night sky and the cool dark air, out of long-held country habit.

The little space stood in stark contrast to the dining room he had just left back at his own house: heavy, handsomely furnished, with no trace of soft domestic warmth save the spot where his mother sat, and no purpose for the room other than eating and drinking. It was true his mother preferred to spend her evenings there, and her will was unchallenged household law, but his drawing room back home was nothing like this. Twenty times the size, a hundred times finer, it did not hold a quarter of this room’s comfort. There were no gilded mirrors here, not even a small scrap of glass to catch and reflect the light the way a pond catches the sun in a wild landscape—only a warm, soft breadth of muted colour, offset by the faded, beloved Helstone chintz that draped the curtains and chair seats. An open writing desk stood under the window opposite the door, while the other window held a stand with a tall white china vase spilling over with English ivy, pale green birch fronds, and the rich copper hue of autumn beech leaves. Neat work baskets sat tucked in corners, and books stacked casually on a side table, left half-read and set down only moments before. Behind the door, a second table was laid for tea under a crisp white cloth, piled high with crumbly coconut cakes, a basket of bright oranges, and ruddy American apples heaped on fresh green leaves. It struck Mr. Thornton that all these small, graceful, thoughtful touches were second nature to the Hales, and perfectly in keeping with the quiet grace of Margaret.

She stood by the tea table in a pale muslin gown streaked soft pink, seeming not to attend to the conversation at all, her whole focus on arranging the tea cups, her round ivory hands moving over them with quiet, noiseless daintiness. A thin bracelet on her taper arm kept slipping down over her round wrist, and Mr. Thornton watched her push the troublesome ornament back up over her soft flesh, far more absorbed by that small movement than anything her father was saying. He could barely stop himself from exclaiming out loud when it slipped down her wrist again. There was so little left to do to finish preparing tea by the time he arrived, that he was almost sorry the ritual of eating and drinking broke his chance to keep watching her. She handed him his cup of tea with the proud air of an unwilling queen tending to a guest, but her eyes caught the exact second he was ready for a refill without him asking. He almost longed to watch her tend to him the way he watched her tend to her father, who playfully took her small finger and thumb between his own larger, rougher hands to use them as makeshift sugar tongs. Margaret’s beautiful eyes lifted to meet her father’s then, bright with equal parts laughter and soft love, the small private moment entirely unobserved by anyone else, or so the pair thought.

Margaret’s head still ached, her pale face and quiet bearing testimony to that fact, but she was resolved to step in to fill any awkward long pause before their guest could feel himself neglected. But the conversation flowed easily, and once the tea things were cleared away, she slipped into a corner next to her mother with her needlework, relieved she could let her mind wander back to her old Helstone home without fear of being called on to speak. Mr. Thornton and Mr. Hale were both fully absorbed picking up the thread of a discussion they had started at their last meeting.

A soft remark from her mother pulled Margaret back to the present, and she looked up from her work to suddenly notice the sharp contrast between the two men’s faces, a physical marker for how entirely different their natures were. Her father was slight of build, appearing taller than he really was until he stood next to Thornton’s broad, tall, sturdy frame. The lines on Mr. Hale’s face were soft and undulating, a faint tremor passing over them whenever his emotions shifted, every fleeting feeling written clearly on his features. His eyelids were large and arched, giving his eyes a dreamy, languid beauty almost feminine in its softness, his fine arched brows set high above those heavy lids. On Thornton’s face, by contrast, straight dark brows fell low over clear, deep-set, earnest eyes that were never unkindly sharp, but focused enough to pierce straight through to the very core of whatever he was looking at. His face bore few lines, firm as if carved out of pale marble, gathered mostly around his lips, which were slightly compressed over a set of perfect, gleaming teeth. When his rare, bright smile broke out in an instant, it flooded his whole face with light, shifting that severe, determined expression of a man ready to dare anything into a moment of unguarded, honest joy— the kind of open, unfiltered feeling you almost only ever see in small children. Margaret found she liked that smile; it was the first thing she had ever admired about her father’s new friend, and the clear contrast in their characters made perfect sense of the obvious warm attraction the two men felt for each other.

She rearranged the tangled skein of her mother’s worsted wool, and drifted back into half-thought, so completely forgotten by Mr. Thornton as he explained the magnificent, terrible power and delicate precision of the new steam hammer that she might as well have not been in the room at all. He described the way the machine could stretch from earth to sky one minute, filling the whole horizon with its force, and then shrink small enough to crack a walnut without breaking its shell the next, so obedient it might have been one of the genii from the Arabian Nights. “That great leap of imagination, that practical realisation of a gigantic thought, came from the brain of a man right here in our Milton,” Thornton said. “And that same man will keep climbing, step by step, from one wonder to the next, and there are dozens of men in this town who could step straight into his place if he fell, carrying on the work that makes all brute material power bow to science.”

“Your boast reminds me of the old ballad lines,” Mr. Hale laughed, “‘I’ve a hundred captains in England,’ he said, ‘As good as ever was he.’”

Margaret looked up in sudden bewilderment at the quotation—how on earth had they jumped from cog wheels and steam hammers to Chevy Chase?

“It is no boast,” Thornton replied, “it is plain, unvarnished fact. I will not deny I am proud to belong to a district whose very necessities give birth to that kind of grand, uncompromising imagination. I would far rather toil here, suffer, even fail and fall short, than spend a soft, dull, prosperous life in the worn old grooves of what you call the aristocratic South, where days slip by slow and careless, and people get clogged thick with honey so they can never rise and fly.”

“You are wrong,” Margaret burst out, stung to vehement defense of the Hampshire home she loved so well. Colour flooded her cheeks, angry tears pricked the corners of her eyes. “You do not know anything of the South. If there is less of the gambing, restless trade spirit that forces out these brilliant inventions, and less rapid progress, there is far less suffering too. I see men on the streets of Milton ground down by pinching care, who are not just suffering, but bitter, full of hate. In the South we have our poor, but you never see that terrible, sullen look of a man convinced the world has cheated him, that I see on every other face here. You do not know the South at all, Mr. Thornton,” she finished, collapsing into determined silence, furious at herself for speaking so much out of turn.

“Then may I say you do not know the North?” he asked, his tone soft with unmistakeable gentleness, the second he realised he had truly hurt her feelings. She stayed resolutely quiet, her heart aching for the woods and lanes of Helstone hundreds of miles away, certain her voice would shake and break if she tried to speak.

“At any rate, Mr. Thornton,” Mrs. Hale cut in smoothly, “you must admit Milton is far smokier, dirtier, than any town you will ever find in the South.”

“I will not argue that point,” he said, his quick bright smile breaking out. “Though Parliament has bidden us burn our own smoke, so I suppose we will all behave like good little children and obey the edict — sooner or later.”

“But you told me you had already altered your factory chimneys to consume all smoke, did you not?” Mr. Hale asked.

“I paid for the alterations out of my own pocket, long before Parliament ever meddled with the business. It was a steep immediate outlay, but it paid me back in saved coal, in the long run. I doubt I would have done it, if I had waited for the act to pass. I would have dragged my feet, waited to be reported, fined, and dragged my heels every step of the way before complying. All laws that rely on informers and petty fines turn inert, because the whole system is odious to everyone who has to work with it. I doubt if a single factory chimney in Milton has ever been reported for violating the smoke act, not in the last five years — even though half of them send a third of their coal bill billowing out in what we call ‘unparliamentary’ smoke.”

“All I know is that it is impossible to keep muslin blinds clean here for more than a week, when at Helstone we kept the same blinds up for a month or more, and they never looked so much as dusty at the end. And Margaret — how many times did you wash your hands before noon today? Three times, was it not?” Mrs. Hale asked.

“Yes, mamma.”

“You seem to have quite a strong objection to all acts of Parliament and government interference in the cotton trade,” Mr. Hale observed.

“I do, and a great many other masters do too, and with good reason. The whole machinery of the cotton trade is so new, is it any wonder it does not run perfectly smooth in every part all at once? Seventy years ago, what was it? Nothing. Now look at it. Rough, untrained men of exactly the same birth and station found themselves suddenly split into masters and workers, all because some of them had the sharp wit to see the enormous potential hidden in Arkwright’s clunky little spinning frame. That sudden, explosive growth gave the first generation of cotton lords almost unlimited wealth and power, over their workers, over purchasers, over the entire global market. I can show you an old newspaper advertisement from fifty years back, run by one of the half dozen calico printers in Milton at the time, that announced he would close his warehouse sharp at noon every day — and every customer had better show up before then, or be turned away. Imagine a man dictating what hours people could buy his goods! Now? I would get out of bed at midnight, hat in hand, to take an order from a good customer.”

Margaret’s lip curled a little, but she found she could not tune him out, could not drift back to her own private thoughts any longer.

“I only say this to show you the almost unlimited power the first generation of manufacturers held at the start of this century. It went to their heads. Success in trade did not mean a man was balanced or just in every other part of his life. Their sense of fairness, their simple decency, got smothered entirely under the flood of sudden wealth that poured down on them. You hear wild stories of the absurd, reckless extravagance those early cotton lords indulged in, on feast days and holidays. No one can deny how tyrannical they were over their workpeople, either. You know the old proverb, Mr. Hale: ‘Set a beggar on horseback, and he’ll ride to the devil.’ Some of those early manufacturers rode straight there in grand style, crushing human bone and flesh under their horse’s hooves without a flicker of remorse. But then the reaction came. More factories opened, more masters, more hands were needed. The power balanced out between masters and men, and now the fight is waged on fair, equal ground between us. We will not submit to an umpire we did not choose, let alone the interference of some meddler who knows nothing of the real facts of the work — even if that meddler calls itself the High Court of Parliament.”

“Must you call it a battle between the two classes?” Mr. Hale asked. “I can see from the way you use the word it is the truest description of the state of things you know.”

“It is true. It is as necessary a battle as the one that prudence and decency are always waging against ignorance and improvidence. It is one of the great beauties of our system that a working man can raise himself up to be a master by his own exertions and good conduct, that anyone who disciplines himself to decency, sobriety, and hard work can join our ranks — if not as a master, as an overlooker, a cashier, a bookkeeper, a clerk, on the side of order and authority.”

“So you consider everyone who never manages to raise themselves up in the world, no matter the reason for their failure, your enemy?” Margaret asked, her voice clear and cold.

“ Their own enemies, most certainly,” he replied, stung a little by the haughty disapproval in her tone. But in a moment, his straightforward honesty told him that was a petty, quibbling answer to what she had said. Proud as she was, scornful as she chose to be, he owed it to himself to explain exactly what he meant, as truthfully as he could. It was not easy to untangle her interpretation from his own meaning, and the simplest way to explain it was to tell them the story of his own life — personal as that felt, to tell strangers. He pushed aside the flicker of shyness that sent a faint flush up his dark cheek, and said:

“I do not speak out of turn. Sixteen years ago, my father died under terrible, miserable circumstances. I was pulled straight out of school, and had to become a man in the space of a few days. I had a mother very few people are blessed to have: a woman of immense strength, unshakable resolve. We moved to a small country town where living was cheaper than Milton, and I got a job in a draper’s shop — a very fine place, by the way, to learn the true value of goods. Week by week, the three of us lived on fifteen shillings. My mother managed our household so that I put three shillings away from that fifteen, every single week. That was the start. That taught me self denial. Now that I can afford to give my mother all the comforts her old age deserves, far more than she ever wanted for herself, I thank her silently every day for that early training. I know from my own life that it is no luck, no special merit, no rare talent that got me here — just the habits of a life that taught me to despise any indulgence I had not earned with my own work. That suffering you say you see written on the faces of Milton’s working people, Miss Hale? It is the natural consequence of chasing easy, unearned pleasure, somewhere earlier in their lives. I do not hate the self-indulgent, the lazy. I simply look on them with contempt for how small and weak their characters are.”

“But you had the rudiments of a good education,” Mr. Hale pointed out. “The eager way you are reading Homer now makes it obvious you are not meeting it for the first time — you read it as a boy, and are only recalling that old knowledge now.”

“That is true. I stumbled through Homer at school, I was even considered a halfway decent classic in those days, though my Latin and Greek have slipped away from me since. But what preparation was any of that for the life I had to lead? None at all. Utterly none. Any man who can read and write started out with just as much genuinely useful knowledge as I had back then.”

“Well, I will not agree with you,” Mr. Hale laughed. “But that is the old schoolmaster in me, I suppose, the old proverb that says ‘there is nothing like leather.’ I wonder if the memory of the heroic, simple life Homer wrote of ever gave you strength in those hard days?”

“Not a single bit!” Mr. Thornton exclaimed, laughing out loud. “I was far too busy fighting for my daily bread, pressed shoulder to shoulder with other living men fighting the same fight, to waste a single thought on long dead heroes. Now that my mother is safe, comfortable, at peace in her old age, properly rewarded for all the work she put in, I can sit back and read those old stories and enjoy them fully.”

When Mr. Thornton rose to leave after shaking hands with Mr. and Mrs. Hale, he held his hand out to Margaret to wish her good-bye the same way, the frank, familiar custom of Milton. But Margaret had not expected it, and simply bowed to him in farewell. The second she saw his half-extended hand quickly drawn back, she was sorry she had not seen his intention. Mr. Thornton, however, never guessed at her regret. He drew himself up to his full height, and walked out of the house muttering to himself:

“The proudest, most disagreeable girl I ever met. Even her great beauty vanishes from your memory, the second you see that scornful look in her eyes.”
