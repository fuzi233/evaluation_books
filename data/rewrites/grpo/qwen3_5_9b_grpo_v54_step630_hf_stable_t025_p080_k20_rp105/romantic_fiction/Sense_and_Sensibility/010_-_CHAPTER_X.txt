The following morning, Willoughby arrived at the cottage, a gesture of courtesy that Margaret Dashwood, with her characteristic blend of grace and imprecision, attributed to his role as Marianne's protector. Mrs. Dashwood welcomed him with a warmth that went beyond mere politeness, fueled by Sir John's glowing endorsement and her own profound gratitude. The conversation that ensued painted a picture of a household defined by mutual affection, elegance, and domestic harmony, leaving Willoughby thoroughly charmed. Yet, he required no further introduction to appreciate the personal charms of the sisters; their allure was evident from their first encounter.

Elinor possessed a delicate complexion, regular features, and a remarkably graceful figure. Marianne, however, surpassed her in striking presence. Though her form lacked the strict correctness of her sister's, her height gave her an imposing elegance, and her face was so radiant that calling her "beautiful" felt like a truthful tribute rather than an exaggeration. Her skin, though tanned, glowed with a brilliant transparency. Every feature was pleasing, her smile inviting, and her dark eyes burned with a vibrant spirit and eagerness that was impossible to behold without delight. Initially, Marianne kept her gaze lowered, embarrassed by the memory of Willoughby's recent aid. But as the initial awkwardness faded, replaced by an appreciation of his frankness and vivacity, her demeanor shifted entirely. When he confessed his passionate love for music and dancing, she bestowed upon him a look of such approval that he found himself speaking almost exclusively to her for the remainder of his visit.

It took little to ignite Marianne's conversation; mention of a favorite pastime was enough to silence her reserve. She spoke with uninhibited enthusiasm, lacking the usual shyness of youth. Quickly, they discovered a profound alignment in their tastes for dance and music, a shared judgment that deepened their connection. Encouraged, Marianne turned to literature, questioning him on his favorite authors. Her descriptions of her beloved writers were delivered with such rapturous fervor that any man of twenty-five, regardless of prior indifference, would instantly be won over. Their intellectual tastes were strikingly identical; they idolized the same books and passages. Where minor disagreements arose, they dissolved instantly under the force of Marianne's arguments and the captivating light in her eyes. Willoughby readily acquiesced to her views, catching her enthusiasm wholeheartedly. Before his visit ended, they conversed with the ease of old friends.

"Well, Marianne," Elinor remarked as Willoughby departed, "for a single morning, you have accomplished quite a feat. You have already gauged Mr. Willoughby's opinions on nearly every subject of consequence. You know his take on Cowper and Scott, are certain he appreciates their beauties correctly, and have received assurances that he holds Pope in moderate regard. But how long can such an acquaintance endure when every topic is exhausted with such extraordinary speed? Another meeting will merely reveal his thoughts on picturesque beauty and second marriages, after which you will have nothing left to inquire."

"Elinor!" Marianne exclaimed, indignant. "Is this fair? Is this just? Are my ideas so scanty? But I understand now. I have been too comfortable, too happy, too open. I have violated every convention of decorum; I have been sincere where I should have been reserved, spirited where I should have been dull and deceitful. Had I spoken only of the weather and the roads, limiting myself to one comment every ten minutes, this reproach would have been spared."

"My dear," her mother interjected gently, "do not take offense at Elinor's jest. I would scold her myself if she wished to dampen the joy of your conversation with our new friend." In an instant, Marianne's anger melted away.

On his part, Willoughby demonstrated every sign of delight in their growing friendship, driven by an evident desire to deepen it. He visited daily. Initially, his inquiries about Marianne's health served as his pretext, but the increasing kindness of his reception rendered such an excuse unnecessary before her full recovery allowed him to drop it entirely. Confinement to the house had never been so pleasant for her. Willoughby was a man of considerable ability, quick imagination, and lively spirits, coupled with open and affectionate manners. He was perfectly suited to capture Marianne's heart. Beyond these qualities, he possessed a natural ardor of mind that was kindled and intensified by Marianne's own example, making him irresistible to her affection.

Their society became her supreme delight. They read, talked, and sang together; his musical talents were impressive, and he read with a sensitivity and spirit that Edward unfortunately lacked. In Mrs. Dashwood's eyes, he was flawless. Elinor, however, saw only one flaw: a tendency to speak too freely on every occasion, disregarding the sensibilities of others or the propriety of circumstances. His haste in forming opinions, his sacrifice of general politeness for the sake of undivided attention when his heart was engaged, and his easy dismissal of worldly forms revealed a lack of caution that Elinor could not approve, despite Marianne's vigorous defense.

Marianne began to realize that the desperate conviction she had held at sixteen—that no man could ever satisfy her ideal of perfection—had been rash and unjustified. Willoughby embodied everything her imagination had crafted in those unhappy moments and all subsequent bright ones. His behavior declared his desires as earnest as his abilities were strong. Even her mother, who had previously harbored no speculative hopes of marriage based on financial prospects, found herself within a week hoping and expecting a union. She secretly congratulated herself on securing two such sons-in-law, Edward and Willoughby.

Colonel Brandon's partiality for Marianne, which had been noted by his friends long ago, only now became apparent to Elinor as everyone else's attention shifted to the more fortunate rival. The mockery Brandon had endured before any affection arose vanished when his genuine feelings invited the ridicule so justly due to excessive sensibility. Reluctantly, Elinor had to accept that the sentiments Mrs. Jennings had attributed to him were truly stirred by her sister. While a similarity of disposition might have accelerated Willoughby's affection, the stark contrast in their characters did not hinder Colonel Brandon's regard. Elinor viewed this with concern; what hope could a silent man of thirty-five hold against a lively one of twenty? Unable to wish him success, she heartily wished him indifference. Yet, she liked him. Despite his gravity and reserve, she saw in him an object of interest. His manners, though serious, were mild, and his reserve seemed born of sorrow rather than natural gloom. Sir John's hints of past injuries and disappointments confirmed her belief that he was an unfortunate man, earning her respect and compassion.

Perhaps she pitied and esteemed him even more because he was slighted by Willoughby and Marianne, who, prejudiced against his lack of liveliness and youth, seemed determined to undervalue his merits.

"One day," Willoughby remarked while discussing him, "Brandon is precisely the kind of man whom everyone praises and nobody cares about; whom all are delighted to see, yet nobody remembers to speak to."

"That is exactly my view of him," Marianne agreed eagerly.

"Do not boast of it," Elinor chided. "It is unjust for both of you. He is highly esteemed by the entire family at the Park, and I never fail to make an effort to converse with him."

"That he is patronized by *you*," Willoughby replied, "is certainly to his credit. But regarding the esteem of the others, it is a reproach in itself. Who would submit to the indignity of being approved by women like Lady Middleton and Mrs. Jennings if they could command the indifference of anyone else?"

"But perhaps the disdain of men like yourself and Marianne makes up for the regard of Lady Middleton and her mother. If their praise is censure, your censure may be praise, for they are no less discerning than you are prejudiced and unjust."

"In defense of your *protégé*, you can even be saucy."

"My *protégé*, as you call him, is a sensible man; and sense always holds attractions for me. Yes, Marianne, even in a man between thirty and forty. He has seen much of the world, traveled abroad, read widely, and possesses a thinking mind. I have found him capable of providing ample information on various subjects, always answering my inquiries with readiness, good breeding, and good nature."

"That is to say," Marianne sneered, "he has told you that the climate in the East Indies is hot and the mosquitoes are troublesome."

"He *would* have told me so, I doubt not, had I asked, but those were points I had already learned."

"Perhaps," Willoughby countered, "his observations extended to the existence of nabobs, gold mohurs, and palanquins."

"I may venture to say that *his* observations stretch far beyond *your* candor. But why do you dislike him?"

"I do not dislike him. On the contrary, I consider him a very respectable man, one whom everyone praises and nobody notices; who has more money than he can spend, more time than he knows how to employ, and two new coats every year."

"Add to which," Marianne cried, "that he lacks genius, taste, and spirit. His understanding has no brilliance, his feelings no ardor, and his voice no expression."

"You judge his imperfections so broadly and rely so heavily on your own imagination," Elinor replied calmly, "that the commendation I can offer is comparatively cold and insipid. I can only pronounce him to be a sensible man, well-bred, well-informed, of gentle address, and, I believe, possessing an amiable heart."

"Miss Dashwood," Willoughby protested, "you are treating me unkindly now. You are attempting to disarm me with reason and convince me against my will. It will not work. You shall find me as stubborn as you are artful. I have three unanswerable reasons for disliking Colonel Brandon: he threatened me with rain when I needed sunshine; he criticized the arrangement of my curricle; and I cannot persuade him to buy my brown mare. If it satisfies you to know that I believe his character is otherwise irreproachable, I am willing to confess it. And in return for an acknowledgment that must cause me some pain, you cannot deny me the privilege of disliking him as much as ever."
