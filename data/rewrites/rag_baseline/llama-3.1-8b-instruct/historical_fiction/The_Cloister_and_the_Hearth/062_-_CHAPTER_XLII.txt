As the darkness slowly receded, Gerard's consciousness began to stir, shrouded in a haze of desperation. On the second day, fever ravaged his mind, his thoughts splintering like fragile glass. On a nearby table, a lock of his rich auburn hair lay, its silken threads as luscious as a woman's, a poignant reminder of all that was lost.

Each day brought fresh torments: the delirium intensified, his body wracked by spasms that seemed to claw at his very soul. By the fifth day, even the leech who had been attending him surrendered, leaving Gerard to the mercy of his own frailty. As the sun dipped below the horizon on that same day, he succumbed to a deep, dreamless sleep.

Some whispered that he would awaken only to succumb to death's dark grasp, but an elderly woman, renowned for her nursing skills, dared to hope otherwise. She decreed that Gerard's youth might yet be his salvation, if he could only sleep for twelve hours uninterrupted. With a fervent prayer, she lit a wax candle to the Virgin, vowing one for each passing hour.

He slept, his chest rising and falling with a rhythmic calm. Twelve hours passed, then twenty-four, and the good woman's optimism grew. Her kindness knew no bounds; she thanked the Virgin on bended knees, her heart overflowing with gratitude.

But as Gerard continued to slumber, his nurse began to harbor doubts. Thirty hours had elapsed, and still, he showed no signs of stirring. She summoned a skilled healer, a woman of great renown, who examined Gerard with a discerning eye.

"Thirty hours," she said, her voice low and soothing. "Shall we awaken him?" Her colleague inspected him closely, her hands gentle as a summer breeze. "His breath is steady, his hand moist. I know some learned physicians would stir him, merely to gaze upon his tongue and claim ignorance; but we women possess a deeper understanding. We know that Nature's wisdom should not be trifled with. Has sleep ever harmed a troubled mind or a shattered heart?"

As Gerard slept on, the hours ticked by like grains of sand in an hourglass. When he had been asleep for forty-eight hours, a gentle breeze rustled the curtains, and the curious began to gather outside. But Fra Colonna and his giant of a friend, Fra Jerome, were granted entry, their faces somber and compassionate.
