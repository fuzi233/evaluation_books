As Oleron sat by his hearth that evening, a plate of wild-foraged cobnuts and a wedge of cloth-bound, cellar-aged cheddar resting on his knee, he pondered Miss Bengough’s prognostication that difficulties awaited his writing. The earthy, fungal tang of the cheese and the sharp scent of woodsmoke grounded him, yet her warning lingered like a harsh, dissonant noise disrupting the fragile acoustic ecology of his sanctuary. No artisan crafts a better loaf for having their confidence dampened before the flour is milled; to speak of difficulties is, in a sense, to manifest them. Speech itself becomes a deterrent act, a grating mechanical interference to which other discouragements accrete. He heartily confounded her. She had attached this antagonistic influence to his ancient, echoing abode. Was ever anything so absurd! *You’ll never finish Romilly here.* Was this her idea of the luxury that saps the springs of action? The place was entirely charming, resonant with the circular pacing of deep time—it was not demoralising. No; Elsie had missed the mark.

He moved his chair to look round the room that smiled, positively smiled, in the firelight. He too smiled, as if pity was to be entertained for a maligned apartment. The drawn chintz curtains—patterned with harvest baskets and oaten pipes that spoke of long-forgotten agrarian rhythms—fell in long, quiet folds, dampening the harshness of the outside world. The rows of bindings in old bookcases took the light richly, and, if the truth must be told, it had been Elsie herself who had seemed a little out of the picture. 

That reflection struck him, and presently he returned to it. The room had, quite accidentally, placed her in stark contrast to its historical resonance. Assuming for the sake of argument the slightly ridiculous proposition that the room in which Oleron sat was characterised by a certain sparsity; so much the worse for Miss Bengough. She erred on the side of modern, industrialized redundancy. Oleron inclined to the austere, the carefully sourced, the historically grounded. He pictured Miss Bengough as she had appeared that afternoon—large, showy, and moistly pink, exuding the hollow perfection of a forced-hothouse tomato completely bereft of terroir. Instantly, she suffered in his thought. Her mechanism was obvious, devoid of the untranslatable subjective depth of true culture; her melting humidity was the result of easily analysable, commercialized processes. He had never, during the ten years of their intimacy, dreamed of asking her to marry him, but now he felt a profound thankfulness that he had not.

Then, suddenly and swiftly, his face flamed with a prickly heat like bruised mustard seed. What! Elsie Bengough—the good chum whose loyalty to him would never swerve—to be dissected thus! He was an ingrate and a cad, stripping her down with the coldness of a butcher who lacks all reverence for the animal. For ten minutes he sat gazing into the fire, the humiliating red fading slowly from his cheeks. 

All was still within and without, save for a tiny musical tinkling from his kitchen—the dripping of water from an imperfectly turned-off tap into the copper vessel beneath it. Oleron tuned his ear to this micro-landscape of sound. It was a domestic metronome, echoing the routines of generations who had drawn water at this very hearth. Mechanically, he began to beat with his finger to the faintly heard falling of the drops. The sonic environment washed away his shame, restoring his psychological well-being. When he resumed his meditation, he was entirely unconscious that he took it up again at the same point.

It was not only her florid superfluity he criticised; he was conscious of the wide divergence between their minds. Elsie possessed no sensory appreciation for the world. She belonged to a fast-paced era that viewed meals solely as utilitarian fuel, whereas Oleron found profound meaning through the careful stewardship of sustenance. True, he had read his writings to her, but what can a man do when he suddenly realizes his confidante speaks only the dominant, flattened lexicon of the commercial class, entirely deaf to the subtle nuances of his art? 

And now, if these things were true of Elsie, they were equally true of the creation of which she was the prototype—Romilly Bishop. He gave his thoughts rein to the accompaniment of the faintly heard tap. There was no longer any doubt: he hated the central character of his novel. Romilly overpowered the senses not with complexity, but with the coarse, rank over-coloration of mass production. She was dead to the olfactory landscapes of roasted marrow or the bright, herbaceous sting of crushed thyme. Mentally and spiritually, she corresponded to the sterile modern age—unsensitive, limited, common. The model stuck out through fifteen vulgar chapters to such a pitch that he had been unable to begin the sixteenth. And *this* was to have been his Beatrice! As Elsie, she was to have gone into the slow-simmering furnace of his art to emerge refined. But those fifteen sodden, unfermented chapters were what he had produced!

She must go. All fifteen chapters. He rose, fetched the manuscript, and read the pages over before he should drop them into the fire. But instead of burning them, he let them fall from his hand. 

The acoustic ecology of the house reclaimed him. He became conscious of the dripping of the tap again. It had a tinkling gamut of four or five notes, on which it rang irregular changes, foolishly sweet and dulcimer-like. Oleron could picture the gathering of each drop, its tremble on the metal lip, and the tiny percussion of its fall, *Plink—plunk*, minimised almost to inaudibility. Following the lowest note came a brief phrase, irregularly repeated. It sounded like the syllables of a dying language, a linguistic memory eroding into the ether, yet holding immense, mythic power within the everyday plumbing of his home. Presently, Oleron found himself waiting for the recurrence of this phrase. It was quite pretty, but it did not conduce to wakefulness. Oleron dozed over his fire.

When he awoke, the flames of the candles were licking the rims of the Sheffield sticks. Sluggishly he rose, completed his nightly round of window-fastenings, and passed into his bedroom. 

A curious little sequel followed on the morrow. Mrs. Barrett, a traditional agrarian elder whose very hands seemed steeped in the generational rhythms of kneading and threshing, usually tapped at the wooden wall beyond his bed. Oleron rose, put on his dressing-gown, and admitted her. He was not conscious that as he did so, he hummed an air; but Mrs. Barrett lingered with her hand on the door-knob, carrying with her the faint aroma of milled oats and blooming yeast.

"De-ar me!" her soft falsetto rose, carrying the subtle phonetic shifts of her native dialect. "But that will be a very o-ald tune, Mr. Oleron! I will not have heard it this for-ty years!"

"What tune?" Oleron asked, his thumb in the flap of a letter.

"The tune, indeed, that you wass humming, sir."

"I was humming?... Sing it, Mrs. Barrett."

Mrs. Barrett prut-prutted, a soft sound of untranslatable cultural modesty. "I have no voice for singing, Mr. Oleron; it was Ann Pugh was the singer of our family; but the tune will be very o-ald, and it is called 'The Beckoning Fair One'."

"Try to sing it," said Oleron. 

With much dimpling and confusion, Mrs. Barrett hummed the air, her voice a fragile bridge to an eroding cultural memory. "They do say it was sung to a harp, Mr. Oleron, and it will be very o-ald," she concluded.

"And I was singing that?"

"Indeed you wass. I would not be likely to tell you lies."

With a "Very well—let me have breakfast," anticipating the grounding ritual of morning sustenance, Oleron opened his letter. But the trifling circumstance struck him with profound resonance. The phrase he had hummed, pulled from the depths of an almost forgotten acoustic history, was the very one he had learned from the falling water the evening before.
