The days stretched on, long and golden, yet they brought neither the hope Romola most cherished nor the dread she feared most deeply. No word came from Baldassarre. And though the government kept a vigilant eye, no trace of the suspected conspiracy emerged. Yet other events unfolded—events that drew her into the heart of Florence’s turmoil, transforming her quiet anxiety into active compassion. The plague spread through the city like a shadow, and Savonarola was excommunicated by the Church. These twin tragedies did not distance her from the friar; instead, they pulled her closer to him, rekindling the moral fire he had once lit within her.

Romola could not walk among the sick and dying, tending to their suffering with a soul stirred by purpose, without remembering who had awakened that purpose in her. It was Fra Girolamo who had opened her eyes to a life beyond self, to one where duty and compassion were not mere ideals but urgent realities. And now, as he stood alone against the tide of corruption and fear, she felt his strength as her own. She could not watch the silence fall upon him—the formal reading of a sentence already posted in the churches, the dimming of candles, the slow retreat of monks—without feeling her allegiance deepen. This was not just a man being condemned; it was a moral force being silenced by greed and power.

She went to the Duomo to witness the excommunication, not out of curiosity, but to strengthen herself. To see the faces of those who rejoiced in his downfall—hardened, coarse, triumphant—only sharpened her resolve. And to see the fear in the eyes of his friends, their voices trembling with confusion, only confirmed her belief: this was not justice, but tyranny dressed in holy robes.

To many Florentines, the Church was not an institution to be questioned—it was a living presence, divinely ordained, capable of blessing or cursing with invisible might. To them, Savonarola’s defiance of Rome was dangerous, almost blasphemous. But Romola had never known the Church as an inherited truth. Her faith had been shaped not by tradition, but by the man who preached against hypocrisy and lived with unwavering integrity. For her, the Church existed only insofar as it mirrored his example. His excommunication was not a spiritual crisis—it was an attack on virtue itself.

She saw clearly what others could not: on one side, a man devoted to purity and public good; on the other, a corrupt old man—Rodrigo Borgia, now Pope Alexander VI—whose lust for power and wealth had driven him to destroy a voice of conscience. The complexities of theology, the nuances of obedience and resistance, meant little to her now. What mattered was the stark contrast between right and wrong, between sacrifice and selfishness.

And so, in the face of this injustice, Romola found renewed strength. Savonarola was no longer merely a preacher challenging Florentine vices—he was now a martyr standing against the very heart of ecclesiastical corruption. He had leapt into the breach, bearing a banner not just for Florence, but for all who believed in truth. In such moments, when courage is seen in action, life seems simpler. The soul feels certain of its highest calling, and even the weakest can believe in heroism.

This fire sustained her through the sweltering summer days. Yet, despite her outward resolve, she remained silent with Tito. She had told him nothing of her meeting with Baldassarre, of the revelation that shattered her world. What would he say? Not understanding, not sorrow—but mockery, perhaps, at her sympathy for the man who had tried to kill him. Baldassarre was likely powerless now. The danger was not that he would harm Tito, but that Tito, discovering his trail, might act again—perhaps with greater cruelty.

Romola waited. She needed to see Baldassarre again, to learn the truth about the “other wife.” Was she the woman Tito had first married? If so, the wound to her pride would be unbearable. That suspicion, born of grief and doubt, mingled with her fading memories of love, eroding every tender feeling she once held for her husband. She believed in the rest of what Baldassarre had said—and that belief made Tito repulsive to her. She recoiled from him, not with anger, but with horror. Only her silence protected her from speaking words she could not control.

Tito, meanwhile, spent much of his time away from home. Like many wealthy citizens during the plague, he retreated to country villas. The Melema estate welcomed him often, and since Romola refused to leave Florence, there was no need for him to maintain a separate residence. So he drifted in and out, leaving her alone with her thoughts.

But by late July, the public crises began to ease. The plague receded. The magistrates, sympathetic to Savonarola, wrote urgent letters to Rome, pleading for the excommunication to be lifted. With less urgency pressing upon her, Romola turned inward. Her body, worn by constant labor and worry, began to falter. Languor settled over her, a natural reaction after months of strain. Yet her mind refused stillness. She could not stay indoors unless the heat pressed down like a weight.

So she walked—morning and evening—through the streets of Florence, changing her path each time, hoping against hope that she might meet Baldassarre. Perhaps illness had robbed him of memory. Perhaps he had forgotten her name, her house, even her existence. That was her faintest hope. But the deeper truth, the one she could not escape, was that he might be dead—consumed by the plague, lost to her forever.
