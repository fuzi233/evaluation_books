To reconstruct the provenance of the stolen gem, one must first anchor oneself in the lineage of the woman whose bloodline birthed the recipient of the jewel. Without the intervention of my lady—the mother of the three Herncastle sisters—the diamond could never have entered our household, nor could the heirloom have found its intended destination. Thus, the narrative arc demands we commence with her. This strategic pivot offers a distinct advantage: it establishes a temporal depth sufficient to contextualize the crime, providing the necessary distance to observe the machinery of fate without immediate bias. For those versed in the intricacies of high society, the names of the Herncastle daughters are familiar lore: Miss Adelaide, Miss Caroline, and Miss Julia. Of these triad, the youngest, Miss Julia, stands preeminent in my estimation regarding beauty and spirit—a judgment rendered not merely by hearsay, but by intimate observation during my tenure in the household.

I entered the service of the patriarch, the old lord, at the tender age of fifteen, serving as a page-boy attendant to these three noble young ladies. By divine providence, the old lord remains irrelevant to the current inquiry; his temperament was volatile, possessing a tongue of excessive length and a patience shorter than a gnat's wing, traits common among the aristocracy yet uniquely pronounced in him. My duties extended until the day Miss Julia wed the late Sir John Verinder. A man of considerable substance, Sir John required management—a necessity he embraced wholeheartedly, thriving under the governance of my lady until his final exhalation. From the moment of their nuptials until the day she gently closed his eyes, their union stood as a testament to harmonious cohabitation, a rare anomaly in a world often plagued by domestic discord. Upon the wedding day, my lady declared, "I cannot function without Gabriel Betteredge," to which Sir John replied with equal conviction, "Nor can I." Thus, my transition to his employ was seamless, driven solely by the preservation of my bond with my mistress.

Recognizing my lady's keen interest in agricultural affairs and estate management, I cultivated a parallel passion, fueled by my humble origins as the seventh son of a small farmer. Under the tutelage of the bailiff, I demonstrated diligence and competence, earning rapid promotion. Years later, following a routine Monday, my lady proposed a reorganization: "Sir John, your current bailiff is senile. Grant him a generous pension and install Gabriel Betteredge in his stead." By the subsequent Tuesday, the arrangement was finalized. Their partnership served as a beacon of marital bliss, offering a counter-narrative to the prevailing misery of arranged unions.

In this period of prosperity, I resided in a modest cottage, entrusted with the dual responsibilities of overseeing the estate's morning rounds and managing afternoon accounts, finding solace in the evening with my pipe and *Robinson Crusoe*. Contentment, I reasoned, requires little more than the solitude Adam enjoyed in Eden. However, my focus shifted to the domestic sphere, specifically to the woman who managed my household: Selina Goby. Adhering to the pragmatic wisdom of William Cobbett—that a suitable wife must chew her food thoroughly and walk with firm assurance—I found Selina perfectly aligned with these criteria. Yet, my motivation transcended mere compatibility; it was rooted in economic prudence. As a singlewoman, Selina charged a weekly stipend for her board and labor; as my wife, she provided these same services gratis. This calculation, blending fiscal acumen with affection, led me to propose the marriage to my mistress. "I have deliberated on Selina Goby," I stated dutifully, "and conclude that matrimony proves more economical than continued employment." My lady laughed, expressing shock at both my phrasing and my principles, amused by the peculiarities of a gentleman of quality. Unaware of the nuances of female sentiment, I proceeded to court her. Naturally, she accepted.

As the wedding approached, accompanied by the procurement of a ceremonial coat, a shadow of hesitation clouded my resolve. Consulting with peers revealed a universal sentiment: few men wish to proceed once the veil lifts. I went further, attempting to withdraw entirely. Recognizing the legal obligation to compensate a woman upon a man's renunciation of betrothal, I offered Selina a featherbed and fifty shillings to dissolve the engagement. To my disbelief, she refused. The attempt failed, and the wedding proceeded with minimal cost and mixed emotions. Our marriage was neither idyllic nor wretched; rather, we existed in a state of perpetual collision. When I ascended the stairs, she descended; when she sought the lower floor, I arrived above. This pattern persisted for five years until Providence intervened, removing my wife from the equation.

Left with my daughter, Penelope, shortly thereafter came the death of Sir John, leaving my lady bereft of children save for her granddaughter, Miss Rachel. Penelope, raised under my mistress's direct supervision, excelled academically and eventually assumed the role of Miss Rachel's maid. Meanwhile, I continued my duties as bailiff until Christmas 1847, when a pivotal shift occurred. My lady summoned me to her cottage for private tea, noting that since my entry as a page-boy decades prior, I had served her faithfully for over fifty years. She presented me with a magnificent woolen waistcoat, woven by her own hands, intended to shield me from the biting winter chill. Initially, I perceived this gift as a mark of honor, struggling to articulate my gratitude. However, the nature of the gesture soon revealed itself as a subtle coercion. Having discerned my advancing age, she sought to persuade me to relinquish the arduous outdoor duties of the bailiff in favor of the sedentary, indoor role of house-steward.

Resisting this indignity, I argued my case vigorously. Yet, my mistress exploited my vulnerability, framing the concession as a personal favor. Defeated, I wiped my eyes with the very garment she had gifted, agreeing to reconsider. Disturbed by this capitulation, I retreated to my usual remedies: a pipe and *Robinson Crusoe*. Within moments of reading, I encountered a passage on page 158: "Today we love, what tomorrow we hate." This epiphany resolved my dilemma; today, I desired to remain a bailiff, but tomorrow, armed with the authority of literature, I would embrace the role of steward. Thus, I slept as a farm-bailiff and awoke as a house-steward, a transformation facilitated entirely by the pages of a novel.

My daughter Penelope observes my progress and praises the authenticity of my narration, yet she identifies a critical flaw: I have chronicled my own life rather than the history of the Diamond. This deviation, inexplicable to me, mirrors the struggles of professional authors whose protagonists obstruct their narratives. Acknowledging this false start, I offer no excuses, only a resolution to maintain composure and recommence the tale from the beginning.
