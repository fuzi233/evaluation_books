When Georgette was well again, Madame sent her away to the country. I missed the child; her absence left a quiet ache in my days, a space no one else could fill. Yet I could not complain. The pensionnat brimmed with life—girls laughing, teachers bustling—but I chose solitude, though companionship was often offered.

Each teacher, in her turn, had extended some gesture of friendship, and I had tested them all. The first was honest but narrow, her thoughts as coarse as her feelings, her heart a shriveled thing. The second, a Parisian, wore refinement like a mask, but beneath lay something spoiled—no faith, no principle, only a hunger for trinkets and sweets. The third, though dull as dust, shared this greed, though hers was of a colder kind: the love of money for itself. Once, with the pride of a miser, she showed me her hoard—fifteen guineas in five-franc pieces, hidden like treasure.

The Parisienne, by contrast, was lavish in spirit, though I never saw her act on it. Once, a flicker of something darker crossed her face—a sly, serpentine glance—but it vanished as quickly as it came, hissed away by a sharp word. She loathed me after that.

Madame Beck saw through her perfectly. "She has no principles, perhaps no morals," she admitted one day, with a shrug. "But her discipline is flawless. Parents see only the surface, and so must I."

The school was a curious place, a gilded cage where every chain was draped in flowers. Romanism seeped into every rule, every indulgence—sensual pleasures permitted to balance spiritual restraint. The Church raised its children to be plump, rosy, and vacant. *Eat, drink, and leave your souls to me.*

Then came summer, and the house grew riotous with anticipation. Madame’s fête approached—a grand affair of gifts, feasting, and a play. The choice of actors had been meticulous, the rehearsals endless. M. Paul Emanuel, the professor of literature, drilled them relentlessly. From my quiet corner, I often heard his voice—sharp as a blade—lashing their feeble efforts.

*"You are dolls!"* he thundered once. *"No passion, no feeling! Must I set fire to you myself?"*

But they remained wooden, and at last, in disgust, he scrapped the tragedy and replaced it with a comedy.

The day before the fête was chaos—cleaning, decorating, a whirl of activity. I retreated to the garden, content to watch from a distance. But the next morning, all was transformed. The coiffeur arrived, turning each girl’s hair into a lacquered masterpiece. My own reflection startled me—braided, gleaming, almost foreign.

The uniform was simple: white muslin, blue sash, pale gloves. Yet it took hours to achieve. Beside them, I felt a shadow in my gray-purple dress, the color of mist on a moor. No jewels, no roses—only quietude. Madame Beck approved. *"Convenable,"* she murmured, though her eyes held no warmth.

When the others still fussed, I slipped away to the empty classrooms, cool and fragrant with flowers. I took a book, let the hum of bees lull me—until a sharp ring of the bell shattered the peace.

Footsteps followed, swift and sure, bursting into my sanctuary. M. Paul stood there, his eyes alight with impatience.

*"You will act,"* he declared, without preamble.

I stared.

A hundred protests rose—the language, the haste, the exposure. But his gaze was relentless, almost pleading beneath the storm.

*"Oui,"* I said.

His face flickered with triumph. *"Good. Now learn it."*

Before I could breathe, he swept me upstairs to an attic—hot, dusty, alive with beetles and the specter of a ghostly nun. There, atop a makeshift throne of boxes, I studied my role: a foppish villain, all vanity and lies.

M. Paul returned, critiqued, scowled. *"Again,"* he demanded.

I tried. The words felt hollow, but I shaped them as best I could. Outside, laughter drifted up from the garden. The fête had begun, and I, locked in this stifling room, was its reluctant player.
