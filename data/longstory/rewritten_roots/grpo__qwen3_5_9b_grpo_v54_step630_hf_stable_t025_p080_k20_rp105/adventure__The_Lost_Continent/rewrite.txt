The damp chill of the Grand Canary dawn seeped into our bones, a stark reminder of sleeping under the open sky. While I usually greeted such mornings with a brisk run to shake off the stiffness, the rugged terrain here offered no flat ground, only jagged lava fields. Instead, I joined Dr. Coppinger as he performed a series of eccentric dumbbell exercises with two heavy chunks of bristly rock. Coppinger, a physician by trade and a man who treats health theories with religious fervor, seemed invigorated by the exertion.

After washing in a trickling stream at the bottom of the barranca—a luxury we rarely afforded ourselves—we sat by the water's edge to ration our dwindling supplies. Our food, unfortunately, had been dampened by a local Spanish newspaper, rendering it unpalatable. "We have little left," Coppinger noted grimly, "and much harder scrambling lies ahead."

Our destination was the *fonda* at Santa Brigida, ten miles away, where we had left our traps. Before departing, however, Coppinger insisted on photographing and measuring the current cluster of caves. His ambition was boundless; having already collected two brittle, dust-ridden mummies, he remained convinced that beneath the cliff face lay the ruins of a lost Guanche university. Armed with a crowbar and a fresh supply of film, we ascended the ridge, leaving the cool stream behind for the baking sun.

Accessing the caves required a perilous descent. Thirty feet below the ridge and fifty feet above the stream bed lay the cave mouths. Laddering from below was impractical, so we employed a method of descending vertically on a thin rope, rappelling from the cliff top into each opening. Coppinger, though brave, was portly and nearing fifty, making the climb grueling. I took the lead, guiding him down, but his insistence on personally inspecting every chamber—labeling one a prison, another a pottery factory—dragged on until the heat became oppressive.

Exhausted, sweating, and with hands raw from the rope, I finally urged him to abandon the search. Yet, Coppinger's enthusiasm was infectious. "Remember," he urged, "anything you find belongs to you. I claim nothing." Reluctantly, I agreed to check the final three openings.

Scaling the fissure to the last cave proved difficult; the rock overhung, forcing me to land awkwardly on my back without letting go of the rope. Inside, the atmosphere shifted instantly. Unlike the rough, natural dens of the previous caves, this chamber was architecturally refined. The walls were smoothed by cutting tools, angles cleaned, and the structure featured interconnected rooms. There were no inscriptions, only the silence of the deep earth.

After documenting the empty chambers, I moved to a wider ledge to recharge my flashlight. As I stepped onto the stone, a sharp crackle echoed, and the floor gave way beneath me. I scrambled back just as the ledge collapsed, but my weight had triggered a shift in the cavern's stability. Peering into the newly exposed cavity, I found it sealed with a substance that was not cement. Chipping away the layers revealed sheets of material composed of two distinct parts: a shiny, talc-like base coated with a dark, toffee-colored wax bearing intricate patterns.

Ignoring Coppinger's earlier assertion that the Guanches could not write, I realized the patterns resembled script. Assuming the contents were valuable, I hacked through the bottom layers with my knife, extracting a solid block of the sheets. It measured roughly twenty by fifteen inches, surprisingly light despite its density. Lowering the artifact to Coppinger via the rope, I descended to join him.

His reaction was immediate and explosive. "The most valuable find in the Canary Islands!" he cried, wrapping the damaged sheets in his coat with trembling hands. "You've shattered the beginning and end of a priceless history, but it is yours."

"It is your fault," I retorted. "You claimed there was no writing among these ancients."

"It is not Guanche work," Coppinger snapped, examining the talc. "There is no talc in this island. This is Egyptian, or something akin to it. And that wax? It is a unique mineral, perhaps asphalt. It is a marvel."

As we made our way back to Santa Brigida, Coppinger carried the bundle himself, refusing to tie it for fear of further damage. Upon reaching the *fonda*, we feasted on the finest provisions and drank the region's vintage wine. That night, Coppinger slept little, consumed by the discovery.

The following morning, he revealed the true nature of the find. "You have stumbled upon a manuscript of unparalleled historical value," he declared, though he lamented the damage caused by my extraction. "Those top sheets likely contained a unique account of the ancient civilization of Yucatan, a lost colony of the Atlanteans."

"Atlanteans?" I asked, skeptical. "Mythical figures from Herodotus?"

"They were real," Coppinger insisted, pointing to marginal illustrations of winged crocodiles and colossal bats. "And so was Atlantis, the continent that lay north of the Canaries. This book was written by Deucalion, an Atlantean priest or general, detailing the swamping of his world. He illustrated the text fully, allowing us to reconstruct tales of extinct species and civilizations."

"But how did it get here?" I asked.

"I made a mistake regarding the origin," Coppinger admitted. "The script misled me into thinking it was Egyptian. It was Deucalion. How it reached this cave remains a mystery, likely detailed in the pages I destroyed."

Over the next year, Coppinger dedicated himself to translating the wax-on-talc manuscript. Meanwhile, I returned to England, caught a hurried boat to Liverpool, and buried myself in business. It was not until a year later, reading a newspaper report that Coppinger had donated the sheets to the British Museum with an estimated value of ten thousand pounds, that I realized the extent of his actions.

Recalling his repeated assurances that the find belonged to me by right of discovery, I wrote a pointed letter. His response was cold and legalistic, admitting my claim only to suggest that publication profits would reimburse the damage. When I replied warmly, his solicitors intervened, severing direct communication.

Though I regret the acrimony, the situation has evolved. The ancient history is now published. The credit for the translation rests with Dr. Coppinger, while all revenue generated by the text shall go to the finder of the original sheets: myself. Should this arrangement change, it will be announced publicly. For now, this stands as the agreement between the discoverer and the scholar.

The ceremony was a spectacle of hollow grandeur. The sentence had been read, the name of Phorenice, the Empress, adored with the fervor of a thousand years, and the new Viceroy installed amidst the ponderous pomp that had defined the age. Formally, I had surrendered the reins of government; formally, Tatho had seated himself upon the snake-throne, the heavy chain of gems clinking around his neck as the symbol of supreme office. Then, amidst the clamor of drums and trumpets, he rose to his feet to begin his first state progress around the gilded council chamber as Viceroy of the Province of Yucatan.

With folded arms and a bowed head, I followed him between the glittering lines of soldiers and the brilliant throng of courtiers and chiefs. The roof-beams quivered under the cries of "Long Live Tatho!" and "Flourish the Empress!" which echoed as a duty-bound refrain. The new ruler acknowledged the welcome with stately inclinations, moving in turn to the three lesser thrones of the eastern, northern, and southern governors to receive their homage. I, the man whose departure had precipitated this change, followed in his train with the prescribed meekness.

It was a hard task, yet those who hold high office learn to wear a passionless mask before the people. Twenty years prior, these same fine obeisances had been made to me. Now, the Gods had seen fit to turn the wheel of fortune. As I walked bent and humbly behind the heels of Tatho, though etiquette forbade any noisy salutation to myself, I could not suppress the warmth of kindly glances falling upon me from every soldier, courtier, and chief present in that gilded hall. It is rare for the fallen to meet such tender looks.

The custom dictates that on such great ceremonial days of changing rulers, the people may bring forward petitions, accuse their retiring head with immunity from vengeance, or propose theories for the future governance of the State. I confess to a touch of vanity in recording that not a single voice was raised against me, nor against any item of my twenty years of rule. Nor did anyone speak out for alterations in the future. Even as we circled the three prescribed times, all present offered their approval in a generous, suffocating silence.

Then, one behind the other, the new Viceroy and the old marched with formal steps over the golden tiles of the council hall beneath the pyramid. The great officers of state left their stations to join our train, and at the far wall, we arrived at the door of the private chambers that had been mine just an hour before.

Ah, well! I had no home now in any of those wondrous cities of Yucatan. A bitterness rose in my chest, though I should have been thankful enough to return to the Continent of Atlantis with my head still in its proper station.

Tatho gave his formal summons, "Open ye to the Viceroy," which the ritual commands. The slaves within sent the massive stone valves of the door gaping wide. Tatho entered, I at his heels; the others halted, sending their valedictions from the threshold, and the valves clanged shut behind us. We passed into the chamber beyond, and then, for the first time alone together and free from the forced etiquette of the courts, the new Viceroy turned with meekly folded arms and bowed low before me.

"Deucalion," he said, "believe me that I have not sought this office. It was thrust upon me. Had I not accepted, my head would have paid forfeit, and another man—your enemy—would have been sent out as Viceroy in your place. The Empress does not permit that her will shall ever be questioned."

"My friend," I answered, "my brother in all but blood, there is no man living in all Atlantis or her territories to whom I would rather hand over my government. For twenty years I have ruled this country of Yucatan and Mexico beyond, first under the old King and then as minister to this new Empress. I know my colony like a book. I am intimate with all her wonderful cities, their palaces, pyramids, and people. I have hunted the beasts and savages in the forests, built roads, and made the rivers carry shipping. I have fostered the arts and crafts like a merchant and discoursed the cult of the Gods three times daily with my own lips. Through evil and good years I have ruled here, striving only for the prosperity of the land and the strengthening of Atlantis, and I have grown to love the peoples like a father. To you I bequeath them, Tatho, with tender supplications for their interests."

"It is not I that can carry on Deucalion's work with Deucalion's power," Tatho replied softly. "But rest content, my friend, that I shall do my humble best to follow exactly in your footsteps. Believe me, I came out to this government with a thousand regrets. I would have died sooner than take your place had I known how vigorously the supplanting would trouble you."

"We are alone here," I said, stepping away from the shadows of the door. "Away from the formalities of assemblies, a man may give vent to his natural self without fear of tarnishing a ceremony. Your coming was something of the suddenest. Till an hour ago, when you demanded audience, I had thought to rule longer; and even now I do not know for what cause I am deposed."

"The proclamation said: 'We relieve our well-beloved Deucalion of his present service, because we have great need of his powers at home in our kingdom of Atlantis.'"

"A mere formality."

Tatho looked uneasily around the hangings of the chamber, drew me with him to its center, and lowered his voice.

"I do not think so," he whispered. "I believe she has need of you. There are troublous times on hand, and Phorenice wants the ablest men in the kingdom ready to her call."

"You may speak openly," I said, "and without fear of eavesdroppers. We are in the heart of the pyramid here, built in every way by a man's length of solid stone. Myself, I oversaw the laying of every course. Besides, here in Yucatan, we have not the niceties of your old-world diplomacy, and we do not listen, because we count it shame to do so."

Tatho shrugged. "I acted only according to my education. At home, a loose tongue makes a loose head, and there are those whose trade it is to carry tales. Still, what I say is this: The throne shakes, and Phorenice sees the need of sturdy props. So she has sent this proclamation."

"But why come to me? It is twenty years since I sailed to this colony, and from that day I have not returned to Atlantis once. I know little of the old country's politics. What small parcel of news drifts out to us across the ocean reads with slender interest here. Yucatan is another world, my dear Tatho, with new interests, new people, new everything. To us here, Atlantis is only a figment, a shadow far away across the waters. It is for this new world of Yucatan that I have striven through all these years."

"If Deucalion has small time to spare from his government for brooding over his fatherland, Atlantis, at least, has found leisure to admire the deeds of her brilliant son. Why, sir, over yonder at home, your name carries magic with it. When you and I were lads together, it was the custom in the colleges to teach that the men of the past were the greatest this world has ever seen; but to-day this teaching is changed. It is Deucalion who is held up as the model and example. Mothers name their sons Deucalion, as the most valuable birth-gift they can make. Deucalion is a household word. Indeed, there is only one name that is near to it in familiarity."

"You trouble me," I said, frowning. "I have tried to do my duty for its own sake and for the country's sake, not for the pettings and fondlings of the vulgar. And besides, if there are names to be in everyone's mouth, they should be the names of the Gods."

Tatho shrugged again. "The Gods? They occupy us very little these latter years. With our modern science, we have grown past the tether of the older Gods, and no new one has appeared. No, my Lord Deucalion, if it were merely the Gods who were your competitors on men's lips, your name would be a thousand times the better known."

"Of mere human names," I said, "the name of this new Empress should come first in Atlantis, our lord the old King being now dead."

"She certainly would have it so," replied Tatho, and there was something in his tone which made me see that more was meant behind the words. I drew him to one of the marble seats and bent myself familiarly towards him. "I am speaking," I said, "not to the new Viceroy of Yucatan, but to my old friend Tatho, a member of the Priests' Clan, like myself, with whom I worked side by side in a score of the smaller home governments, in hamlets, in villages, in smaller towns, in greater towns, as we gained experience in war and knowledge in the art of ruling people, and so tediously won our promotion. I am speaking in Tatho's private abode, that was mine own not two hours since, and I would have an answer with that plainness which we always then used to one another."

The new Viceroy sighed whimsically. "I almost forget how to speak in plain words now," he said. "We have grown so polished in these latter days that mere bald truth would be hissed as indelicate. But for the memory of those early years, when we expended as much law and thought over the ownership of a hay-byre as we should now over the fate of a rebellious city, I will try and speak plain to you even now, Deucalion. Tell me, old friend, what is it?"

"What of this new Empress?"

He frowned. "I might have guessed your subject," he said.

"Then speak upon it. Tell me of all the changes that have been made. What has this Phorenice done to make her throne unstable in Atlantis?"

Tatho frowned still. "If I did not know you to be as honest as our Lord the Sun, your questions would carry mischief with them. Phorenice has a short way with those who are daring enough to discuss her policies for other purpose than politely to praise them."

"You can leave me ignorant if you wish," I said with a touch of chill. This Tatho seemed to be different from the Tatho I had known at home—the Tatho my workmate, the Tatho who had read with me in the College of Priests, who had run with me in many a furious charge, who had labored with me so heavily that the peoples under us might prosper. But he was quick enough to see my change of tone.

"You force me back to my old self," he said with a half-smile, "though it is hard enough to forget the caution one has learned during the last twenty years, even when speaking with you. Still, whatever may have happened to the rest of us, it is clear to see that you at least have not changed. Old friend, I am ready to trust you with my life if you ask it. In fact, you do ask me that very thing when you tell me to speak all I know of Phorenice."

I nodded. This was more like the old times, when there was full confidence between us. "The Gods will it now that I return to Atlantis," I said. "And what happens after that the Gods alone know. But it would be of service to me if I could land on her shores with some knowledge of this Phorenice, for at present I am as ignorant concerning her as some savage from Europe or mid-Africa."

"What would you have me tell?"

"Tell all. I know only that she, a woman, reigns, whereby the ancient law of the land—a man should rule—is broken; that she is not even of the Priestly Clan from which the law says all rulers must be drawn; and that, from what you say, she has caused the throne to totter. The throne was as firm as the everlasting hills in the old King's day, Tatho."

"History has moved with pace since then, and Phorenice has spurred it. You know her origin?"

"I know only the exact little I have told you."

"She was a swineherd's daughter from the mountains, though this is never even whispered now, as she has declared herself to be a daughter of the Gods, with a miraculous birth and upbringing. As she has decreed it a sacrilege to question this parentage, and has ordered to be burnt all those that seem to recollect her more earthly origin, the fable passes current for truth. You see the faith I put in you, Deucalion, by telling you what you wish to learn."

"There has always been trust between us."

"I know; but this habit of suspicion is hard to cast off, even with you. However, let me put your good faith between me and the torture further. Zaemon, you remember, was governor of the swineherd's province, and Zaemon's wife saw Phorenice and took her away to adopt and bring up as her own. It is said that the swineherd and his woman objected; perhaps they did; anyway, I know they died; and Phorenice was taught the arts and graces, and brought up as a daughter of the Priestly Clan."

"But still she was an adopted daughter only," I objected.

"The omission of the 'adopted' was her will at an early age," said Tatho dryly. "And she learnt early to have her wishes carried into fact. It was notorious that before she had grown to fifteen years she ruled not only the women of the household, but Zaemon also, and the province that was beyond Zaemon."

"Zaemon was learned," I said, "and a devout follower

The words spoken by Tatho did not induce sleep in me that night. Instead, they ignited a cold realization within my chest: I had erred in wrapping myself so completely in the administration of Yucatan, severing my ties to the currents of events rippling through Atlantis. For years, the silence imposed by the Empress had been a thick wall, and I, consumed by my duties in the colony, had possessed neither the curiosity nor the moral imperative to tear down a veil placed by my superiors. It is against my nature to torture men into breaking orders of silence merely to satisfy my private curiosity.

Yet, the iron discipline of the Priestly Clan offered me no alternative path. My removal from office was sudden and absolute. From that moment, all authority and records passed to my successor. While courtesy might allow me to remain in the pyramid I had once commanded to watch another sunrise, the decree was clear: I must leave the territory immediately and hasten back to Atlantis.

Tatho, however, proved eager to facilitate my departure. Before dawn, he stood by my side, offering all his resources. My request was simple: "A ship to take me home, and I shall be your debtor."

His reaction was one of surprise. "You shall certainly have it if you wish," he replied. "But my fleet is foul from the long passage and requires a thorough careen. To take them would mean a slow, tedious voyage to Atlantis. Why not take your own navy? The ships are in harbor; I saw them when we arrived. They are brave vessels."

"But they are not mine," I countered. "That navy belongs to Yucatan."

"Very well, Deucalion," Tatho said, shrugging. "You are Yucatan, or rather, you were yesterday, and have been for twenty years."

I saw the implication and it displeased me. I answered stiffly that the ships belonged to private merchants or the State, and I could not claim even a single ten-slave galley.

Tatho sighed. "I suppose you know your own policies best, though to me it seems risky for a man of your stature not to maintain a personal navy. One never knows when a recall order arrives; without such precautions, a lifetime's earnings can vanish in a dozen hours."

"I have no fear for mine," I said coldly.

"Of course not, because you know me to be your friend. But had another man been appointed to this vice-royalty, you might have been sadly shorn, Deucalion. It is rare for a fellow to resist a snug hoard waiting in the very coffers they guard."

"My Lord Tatho," I replied, "it is clear we have grown apart in taste. The wealth I have amassed in this colony is coveted by few. I wear the poor clothes you see, and possess only a box of stomachic drugs and three slaves: two scribes and a sturdy European savage who cooks and bathes me. For my maintenance, I have drained the State of only a soldier's ration. If any man has extorted a single ounce of bronze from a Yucatan citizen in my name, I request you, as a final service, to hang him as a liar and a thief."

Tatho studied me with a mixture of admiration and pity. "I do not know whether to be astonished or despised. We heard tales of your uprightness in Atlantis, of your stern justice, but no soul guessed you carried your austerity this far. Money is power, Deucalion. With it, nothing can stop you; without it, you are easily trodden down."

"The Gods will choose my fate."

"Possibly. But I prefer to nourish my own. I have not come here to follow your pattern of ascetic vice-royalty. I shall govern Yucatan wisely, but I shall also govern it for the good of Tatho, the viceroy. I brought my navy of eight ships, a personal bodyguard, and my wife with her retinue. These must be provided for. If a people is to be governed, it is their privilege to pay handsomely for their prince."

"We shall not agree on this," I said. "If I thought it useful, I would supplicate you to deal with lenience when taxing these people. They have grown dear to me."

"I have disgusted you, and I am grieved. Yet, even to retain your good opinion—which I value above all—I cannot act as you did. It would be impossible, even if I wished it. Do not judge all men by your strong standard: a Tatho is no colossus like a Deucalion. Besides, I have a wife and children who must be provided for, even if I neglect myself."

"Ah," I said, "there seems to be my advantage. I have no wife to clog me."

He seized on my words quickly. "It seems you have nothing that makes life worth living. No wife, children, riches, cooks, retinue, or finery. You are plaguey ignorant of certain matters. For instance, you do not know how to dine. During our weary voyage, I promised myself that your future feasts would repay the meager sea rations. And when we arrived at the reality? A country farmer sits to more delicate fare than you offer. Your savage cook is lusty, perhaps faithful, but he is a devil-possessed chef. Gods! I have lived better on campaign."

"I eat when hunger bids me," I said coldly, "and I wear this robe until it wears out. The grossness of excessive banqueting and the effeminacy of many clothes never met my fancy. But I think we have talked long enough. There is little chance of agreement. You have changed, Tatho, with the years, and perhaps I have too. Let us part, forgetting these differences, and remembering only our friendship of twenty years ago. That, at least, always had a pleasant savour."

Tatho bowed his head. "So be it."

"And I would still charge myself upon your bounty for that ship. Dawn cannot be far off, and it is indecent for a dismissed ruler to walk the streets in daylight."

"So be it," Tatho agreed. "You shall have my poor navy. I could have wished you asked for something greater."

"Not the navy, Tatho; one small ship. Believe me, more is wasted."

"Now," said Tatho, "I shall act the tyrant. I am viceroy here, and I will have my way. You may depart naked of possessions—that I cannot help—but you shall not depart unattended."

Thus, as fate decreed, I made my transit aboard the "Bear," Tatho's private ship, escorted by the rest of his navy.

The departure was not immediate. The vessels lay moored against the stone quays, gutted of stores, their crews exhausted. To force them out instantly would have been suicide. First, the courtesies were fulfilled: the ships hauled out into the entrance basin, anchored in the swells of the fairway, and then took on wood, water, cured meat, and fish. They refitted with all possible speed.

For myself, there came, for the first time in twenty busy years, a breathing space from work. I had no further official connection with the land of my labors. By rule, I was forbidden to inspect or interfere with the working of the ship, as sea matters were the exclusive, jealously guarded property of the Mariners' Guild.

My days were spent gazing upon the quays, the harbors, the palaces, and the pyramids of the splendid city I had watched rise from its foundations. I roamed my eye over the pastures and grain lands beyond the walls, looking longingly at the dense forests behind, from which we had so tediously carved our territory.

Would Tatho continue the healthy work begun? I trusted so, despite his selfish words. At all hours, under the sun or the stars, I was free to pursue the study of higher mysteries, on which we of the Priestly Clan are trained, without aid of books or temples.

The refitting was completed with unprecedented speed. Never had ships been reprovisioned and caulked so rapidly for an over-ocean voyage. Barely a month after arriving, the fleet put out beyond the walls and began their eastward journey.

Rowing-slaves from Europe were no longer taken for such long passages, as modern humanity forbids the practice of letting them eat one another. Sails alone were an indifferent standby; instead, modern science had shown how to extract force from the Sun. This secret method drew seawater at the forepart of the vessel and ejected it with immense force at the stern, driving the ship forward even against adverse winds.

Navigation had also vastly improved. It was no longer necessary to trust wholly to a starry night. A small image, balanced in the forepart of every vessel with an arm outstretched, pointed constantly to the Southern Cross. By setting an angle, a true course could be steered. Other instruments existed for finding a true position on the ocean wastes; the newer mariner put little trust in the Gods and confided mightily in his own strength and wit.

Still, it was amusing to see these tarry fellows take their last farewell of the harbor town. Once the ship was stowed and ready, they washed, donned their finest attire, and went ashore to seek obscure temples where their gods held little sway among shore folk. There, they made sacrifices with clamor and lavish outlay, followed by a feast. They returned to the ship drunk, heavy, and evil-humored with gluttony.

Our voyage was vastly different from my previous sea-goings. We did not creep timidly along the coasts. We stood straight across the open gulf toward home, passing the band of the Carib Islands with confidence, as though they were signposts marking the sea highway. We stopped only twice to replenish wood, water, and fruit. The savages brought these commodities freely, their subjection so complete that neither stop involved even the semblance of a fight. It was a certificate of the growing power of Atlantis and her finest overseas colony.

Then boldly on we went across the vast ocean beyond, with no sacrifices to implore the Gods for direction. One might censure these rugged mariners for their impiety, yet one could not help admiring their lusty skill and confidence.

The dangers of the desolate sea are dealt out as the Gods will. We encountered storms, fought with stubborn endurance, and twice saw blazing stones from Heaven hiss into the sea beside us without injury. As unavoidable, the great beasts of the sea hunted us with their accustomed savagery. Only once did we suffer material loss, when three great sea lizards attacked the "Bear" simultaneously.

The onset occurred during the blazing midday heat, when our solar machines were drawing full force. The vessel traveled faster than a man could walk on dry land, but against the beasts, it might as well have been standing still. Three of them rose over the horizon, beating the sea into foam and waving great necks like masts. Our navy was spread in a long line; in olden days, each beast would have selected separate prey, but these creatures had learned the necessities of warfare. They hunted in packs.

They made for our ship. Tob, the captain, urged me to retreat to the after-castle for safety, citing his responsibility to Lord Tatho for my safe conduct. He warned that the beasts would seize some of the crew before being satiated, and if harm came to Lord Deucalion, Tob would have to surrender himself voluntarily to the beasts to avoid a painful death at Tatho's hands later.

However, my mind was set. A man can never have too much experience in fighting enemies, whether human or bestial. This attack was new to me, and I was eager to learn its method. I gave Tob a letter to Tatho explaining my resolve (for which the rude fellow seemed little grateful) and stayed in my chair under the awning.

The beasts surged up with champing jaws, and all the shipmen stood armed for defense. Two females flanked the ship, while the giant male approached alone. Their great heads swooped high as the yards, and the reek from them induced physical sickness.

The shipmen faced the monsters with sturdy courage. Arrows were useless against the smooth, bull-like hides. Throwing fire could not even singe them; only twenty axe blows delivered together on an attacking head could beat it back, and even then, only through sheer weight of metal, causing no real wound.

Since time immemorial, beasts have disputed with man the mastery of the earth. Only in Atlantis, Egypt, and Yucatan has man dared to hold his own, fighting with a mind strengthened by previous victories. In Europe and mid-Africa, the greater beasts hold full dominion, forcing man into crannies and tree-tops as a fugitive. Upon the great oceans, the beasts are lords, unchecked.

Still, upon this desolate sea, though the giant lizards were new to me, it was a pleasure to pit my knowledge of war against their brute strength. Since the first men did business upon the waters, they fulfilled their instincts in fighting these beasts with desperation. Hiding cowardly in the hold was useless; if the enemy could not find men above decks to glut them, they would break the ship with their paddles, slaughtering all. Thus, it was recognized that the fight must go forward as desperately as possible, ending only when the beasts had their fill and departed.

In this one-sided conflict, I found myself feeling the joy of having my thews in action. After delivering a dozen lusty blows with my axe—blows that might have been delivered against a city wall or the ark of the Mysteries—I sought a lance. With that, I made very different play.

The lizards' eyes are small and set deep in bony sockets, but I judged them vulnerable. I attacked the eyes. The decks were slippery with horrid slime, and the crew surged about, constantly offering themselves as a rampart before me due to Tob's threats. I gave a few shrewd proges with the lance to show I would not have my will overridden, and presently was given room for maneuver.

Deliberately, I placed myself in the sight of one of the lizards and offered my body to its attack.

As our fleet finally crested the horizon of Atlantis, Tob's modern instruments had guided us with a precision that bordered on the miraculous, yet the journey was far from over. We still faced ten days of retracing our path to reach the great arm of the sea where the capital lay hidden. Yet, the sight of the land itself was a miracle. The scent of earth and wild grass drifting on the breeze acted as a divine balm. Many of our crews had perished in the long crossing, and the rest were dying of scurvy, their bodies wasted by the unnatural salt diet of the open ocean. But the smell of soil and the view of the shore transformed them instantly. From helpless, broken logs who could not even lift a paddle, they became men again—lusty, active, and ready to fight for their lives and their ships.

Since my deposition in Yucatan, despite Tatho's assurances, a shadow of doubt had lingered in my mind regarding my reception in Atlantis. Would the Empress Phorenice welcome me, or sever my head the moment I stepped ashore? I had faced this uncertainty with a calm detachment, trusting that the Lord Sun ruled above Phorenice. If my head were to fall, it would be His will. Thus, I had spent the voyage studying the higher mysteries with an unloaded mind, unconcerned by the politics of the land I was approaching.

But when our navy had retraced the course sufficiently to reveal the two vast headlands marking the entrance to the inland waters, we were met not by a welcome, but by a blockade. Barely two days from the capital, we encountered another navy waiting in a sheltered bay. Their scouts had already signaled our approach, and as we rounded the headland, their ships stood out to dispute our passage.

Of our original force, only five ships remained; the rest had been lost to storms or decimated by disease. Against us were three fine ships and three heavy galleys. They were pristine, black, and gleaming, while our vessels were storm-ragged, weather-beaten, and fouled with seaweed. We flew the banners of Tatho and Deucalion openly, proud of our origin and purpose. In stark contrast, the rival navy advanced without any banner or standard, appearing as shameless scavengers hiding their true nature.

It was clear they intended to stop us by force. The seas are a lawless place where brothers often hunt one another, especially when one returns laden with plunder. Tob, with his characteristic system, immediately brought our vessel into fighting trim, and the other captains did the same, forming a compact squadron. They had no desire to surrender now that the voyage was nearing its end.

The sun shone brilliantly, seemingly encouraging the conflict, and the two navies approached with speed. However, when we were a hundred ship-lengths apart, the enemy halted. One of their galleys pulled ahead, flying green branches from her masts to signal a parley.

Given our battered state, we had no wish to invite unnecessary combat. Hoarse shouts passed between the fleets, and we too stopped. Tob hoisted a withered stick to serve as a flag of truce, signaling our readiness to talk and respect an ambassador.

The galley drew alongside, swinging round until its stern scraped our shield rail. A young, healthy man clad in fine rigging climbed aboard, sneering at our sea-tattered appearance. He looked directly at Tob and nodded with familiar confidence. "Old pot-mate," he called out, "your woman waits for you up by the quay-side in Atlantis, with four youngsters at her heels. I saw her not half a month ago."

"You didn't come out here to deliver home news," Tob replied sharply. "I've drunk enough pots with you, Dason, to know your pleasantries."

"I merely wished to remind you that your home awaits, with your wife and children ready to welcome you."

"I am not a man who forgets," Tob said grimly. "Because I keep them in my mind, I have sailed over the tops of more than one pirate. Had I been a single man, I might have accepted slavery, but I have family to protect."

"Oh, I know you're a desperate fellow," Dason admitted, his tone shifting to business. "If this comes to blows, we'll lose a few men before we sink your crippled ships and drag you home. My navy has orders. We wish to see if you will act sensibly and give us what we want, allowing you to return with your skin intact."

"You have come to the wrong bird for a plucking," Tob laughed heavily. "We took no treasure or merchandise in Yucatan. We stayed only long enough to cure our provisions and refill our stores. We sail back as we sailed out: barren ships. You won't believe me, of course, but you can search the holds. You'll find nothing but broken sailors. You can steal nothing here but blows, Dason, and we will give you those with little asking."

"I am glad you state your cargo at such a slender value," the envoy replied. "For it is that cargo I must take back if you are to earn safe conduct."

Tob frowned. "Speak plainly. I am a common sailor and do not understand fancy talk."

"It is clear," Dason said, "that you have been sent to bring Deucalion back to Atlantis as a prop for Phorenice. We find her hard enough to fight without further reinforcements. We want Deucalion in our custody to deal with as we see fit."

"And if I refuse?"

The spruce envoy glanced at our splintered ships. "Then we shall send you all to the fishes in short order. Instead of Deucalion standing before the Gods alone, he will go down with a ragged company limping at his heels."

"I doubt it," Tob said. "As for handing over my Lord Deucalion, that is out of the question. First, if I return without him, Tatho will eventually return, and I shall die by the slowest of deaths in his hands. Second, I have seen my Lord Deucalion kill a great sea lizard; he proved himself a proper man that day, and I would not give him up against his will, even to Tatho. Third, you owe me for your share in our last wine-bout ashore. I'll see you with the nether Gods before I give you anything until you settle that score."

"Well, Tob, I hope you drown easily," Dason spat. "As for that wife of yours, I've always fancied her, and I shall know how to find a use for her."

"I'll draw your neck for that, you son of a European," Tob roared. "If you don't clear off this deck, I'll draw it here myself. Go, you father of monkey children! Get away and let me fight you fairly, or by my honor, I'll stamp the inwards out of you and make your silly crew wear them as necklaces!"

With that, Dason retreated to his galley.

Instantly, Tob engaged the machine on our *Bear* and bellowed orders to the other ships. Though our crews were weak with scurvy, they were quick to obey. The five vessels surged forward, catching the sunlight to gain maximum speed. Our small navy converged, singling out one of the enemy ships. Unprepared for such a swift attack, the enemy vessel panicked, attempting to turn and flee rather than meeting us bow-on. Consequently, our five ships struck her simultaneously with their underwater beaks, tearing her planking and sinking her before we could disengage.

While we reduced their numbers to five, matching our own, the advantage did not last. The three nimble galleys formed a line, their boatswains' whips cracking as slaves bent to their oars. Soon, one of our ships was gored and sunk, her crew killed in the water without hope of rescue.

What followed was a tight-locked melee that would have warmed the heart of any warrior. The ships and galleys were forced together, grinding savagely upon the swells as if they were sentient beasts. Men shot arrows, slashed with axes, thrust with swords, and hurled fire. But the battle converged entirely upon the *Bear*. It was our flagship that bore the brunt of their spite; it was to the *Bear* that the other crews of Tatho's navy rallied as their own vessels caught fire or were sunk.

Battle is an old acquaintance to us of the Priestly Clan. Having carved out territories for new colonies, we know its frequency well enough to cloy even the most chivalrous appetite. For half my life-span, I had heard men shout "Deucalion" as a battlecry, and witnessed many lusty encounters. But this sea-fight surprised me in its savage ferocity. The bleak, unstable element surrounding us; the swaying decks; the throwing fire that burned flesh and wood alike; the great man-eating birds hovering overhead; the man-eating fish swarming from the depths to gnaw on the fallen—all combined to create circumstances fit to daunt the bravest armies.

Yet, these hardy shipmen faced it all with indomitable courage, never uttering a cry of fear. Life on the seas is so brutal, filled with savage dangers from the beasts of the deep, that Death has lost half his terror to them through sheer familiarity. They were men who would fight to a finish among themselves in taverns ashore for the pure lust of a fray. Here, in this desperate battle, the passion for killing burned within them like a firestone raging in a forest, and they took even their death-wounds laughing.

On our side, the battlecry was "Tob!" The name of this obscure ship-captain carried a confidence for our crews that many well-known commanders might have envied. The enemy had a dozen rallying cries, which confused them, but as their other commanders fell one by one, and Dason remained active with mischief, "Dason!" became the shout thrown back at us in response to our "Tob!"

I will not burden these pages with a further account of this obscure sea-fight, whose only glory was its ferocity. One by one, all ships of either side were sunk or left with all hands killed, until finally, only Dason's galley and our *Bear* remained. For a moment, we were mastered. We had a score of men left out of the entire navy that sailed from Yucatan, and the enemy had boarded us, making the decks of the *Bear* the field of battle. But they had been too busy with throwing fire. As we raged at one another, the smoke and flame from the sturdy vessel herself revealed that she was beyond salvation.

But Tob was undaunted. "They may stay here and fry if they choose," he shouted with his great boisterous laugh. "But for ourselves, the galley is good enough now. Keep a guard on Deucalion, and come with me, shipmates!"

"Tob!" our fellows shouted in their ecstasy of fighting madness. Even I could not forbear sending out a "Tob!" for my battlecry. It was a change not to be the leader, but it was a luxury to fight in the wake of Tob, despite his uncouth manner and plans. There was no stopping this new rush. Progress was slow, but Tob, with his bloody axe, cut the road ahead, and we others, filled with the lust of battle, raged like furies in his wake. Gods! but it was a fight.

Ten of us reached the galley, with flames and smoke from the doomed *Bear* spurting at our heels. We turned and stabbed madly at anyone trying to follow, hacking through the grapples that held the vessels in their embrace. The sea-swells spurned the *Bear* away.

The slaves chained to the rowing-galley's benches cared neither for the victors nor the vanquished, watching with dull concern unless a stray missile found a billet among them. But a handful of fighting men had scrambled desperately onto the galley after us, preferring any fate to a fiery death on the *Bear*, and these had to be dealt with promptly. Three, their fighting fury still red-hot, were killed wastefully to remove them from mischief. Five, who had pitched their weapons into the sea, were chained to the oar looms in place of the dead slaves. Only Dason remained to have a fate apportioned.

The fight had cooled out of him; he had thrown his arms to the sea and stood sullenly ready for whatever might befall. To him, Tob went with an exulting face.

"Ho, pot-mate Dason," he cried. "You made a lot of talk an hour ago about that woman of mine, who lives with her brats on the quay-side in Atlantis. Now, I'll give you a pleasant choice: either I take you along home, tell her what you said before the whole ship's company—who are mostly dead now, poor souls—and leave her to perform on your carcase as she sees fit by way of payment; or, as the other choice, I'll deal with you here myself."

"I thank you for the chance," Dason said, kneeling and offering his neck to the axe.

So Tob cut off his head, sticking it on the galley's beak as an advertisement of what had been done. He threw the body over the side, and one of the great man-eating birds hovering nearby picked it up and flew away with it to its nest amongst the crags.

Free at last, we feasted on the fruits and fresh meats the galley offered, while the oar-slaves pushed the vessel rushing onward toward the capital. There was a wine-skin in the after-castle, and I filled a horn, pouring some out at Tob's feet in salutation. "My man," I said, "you have shown me a fight."

"Thanks," he replied. "And I know you are a judge. 'Twas pretty whilst it lasted. Seeing that my lads were, for the most part, scurvy-rotten, I will say they fought with credit. I have lost my Lord Tatho's navy, but I think Phorenice will see me righted there. By the time the rival navy intercepted us at the mouth of the gulf, I had ceased to view myself merely as a recruit for the Empress. The ambush, the ferocity with which those ships fought solely to capture me, were omens written in fire and steel that even the blind could read: I was destined to play a central role in the nation's fate. Yet, if our arrival was watched by enemies, Phorenice had her own eyes. Scouts perched upon the mountains saw us approach and carried the news to the capital.

The sea-arm leading to the vast city of Atlantis is treacherous and winding. Where the mountains have collapsed, sending molten rock to harden the seabed, the channel narrows to a river's width before widening into a lake-like expanse. Overland, a skilled runner navigating the flats, swamps, and smoking hills could outrun any galley, provided he survived the fire-streams, floods, and beasts. Phorenice, however, spared no expense for speed. She dispatched twenty couriers to the headland overlooking the straits. Seven returned safely with their tidings; three more were known to have survived the journey.

At the time, we remained unaware of this frantic relay. The slaves rowing our galley were savages from Europe, their presence a foul odor that soured the voyage, compounded by the grit of an erupting volcano carried on the wind. We pushed forward with urgency, driven by a mixture of discomfort and a human curiosity I could not suppress despite my priestly training in stoicism. What awaited me at Atlantis? Would I be a formal prisoner awaiting trial for my actions in Yucatan? Or would I step ashore unnoticed, summoned later by the Empress? The reality remained a mystery.

As dawn broke, the peak of the sacred mountain, crowned with eternal fires, loomed ahead. The boatswains' whips cracked violently, driving the exhausted slaves to a final spurt. Against the adverse wind, we made steady progress under oars alone. The city began to rise from the water—pyramids and towers floating on the gleaming surface. Twenty years had passed since I last saw Atlantis, and my heart burned with the memory of its paving stones.

The city expanded around us as we neared. I recognized the temple of my manhood initiation, the pyramid of my secret rites, and finally, the house where I was raised. Tears blurred my vision as the memories surged.

We pulled up outside the white harbor walls, adhering to the law. The slaves slumped over their oars, panting. Usually, a port-captain's uncertainty about his own dignity leads to delays, but today, a ten-oared boat raced out from the castle, dropping the chain between the harbor gates.

"That is Dason's galley?" the captain asked.

"It was," Tob replied.

"I saw Dason's head on your bow," the captain said grimly. "You are Captain Tatho?"

"And remain so. Tatho's fleet was sunk by Dason's traitors; we took this rotting vessel to finish the voyage."

The captain scanned our deck. "Captain, I fear a dangerous reception. I see no Lord Deucalion. Has he perished under your charge? If you have let him die, the Empress will flay you alive."

"With Phorenice and Tatho both concerned for his safety," Tob interjected, "Lord Deucalion is a hazardous passenger. But I shall save my skin." He jabbed a thumb toward me. "He is here to vouch for me."

The captain stared, then bowed deeply. "I trust my Lord will pardon my ignorance of his nobility. I expected more... suitable attire."

"Pish," I said. "If I choose simplicity, I cannot object to being mistaken for a commoner. I do not wish to advertise my status with gaudy finery. End this inquiry."

The captain became instantly obsequious. "It is my honor to pilot your galley to the appointed berth."

As our slaves resumed rowing, Tob watched me with a dry smile. "I fear," he said, "that when you step among this fine gentility, Lord Deucalion will remember me only as a rude fellow."

"You think nothing of the kind," I assured him.

"Then I must prove my refinement," Tob declared, seizing my hand in his massive fist. "By the Gods, Deucalion, you may be a great prince, but I have known you as a man. You are the finest fighter of beasts and men alive today. That spear-stroke on the lizard will be sung in taverns for generations."

We entered the harbor, trumpets blaring from the castle. The port-captain had hoisted my banner to his masthead, and from the quays, the sounds of music and the glow of welcoming fires drifted across the water. It was clear the Empress felt a profound interest in a returning subject. Nothing was to be done by halves.

Our galley was moored to the royal quay, secured by rings of gold sacred to the throne.

"If Dason had foreseen this honor," Tob joked grimly, "he would have used silk rope instead of hemp. He would be frowning this very minute if the sun hadn't scorched his face stiff." He gestured to the quay. "Will you pick your way carefully over this common ship and tread the carpet they've spread for you?"

The port-captain looked horrified at Tob's rudeness. I sighed inwardly; colonial freedom had no place in Atlantis. I prepared myself for the weight of ceremony.

I consented to enter a crimson pavilion erected to shelter me until the Empress arrived, but my compliance ended there. Three gorgeously dressed chamberlains presented me with robes bedecked in every conceivable frippery, demanding I transform into a peacock.

I refused curtly. When one stammered about the Empress's tastes, I asked plainly if she had issued specific commands regarding my clothing. There were none.

"Phorenice commanded Deucalion the man to attend her," I retorted. "She sent no word on his outer casing. This dress suits my temper; it shields me from the heat and is clean. Your interference smells of impertinence."

In unison, the chamberlains drew their swords, pushing the hilts toward me.

"It would be a favor," their spokesman said, "if Lord Deucalion took his vengeance now, rather than delivering us to tormentors later."

"Poof," I dismissed them. "The matter is forgotten. You make too much of a little thing."

Their action, however, revealed a chilling truth: this was a different Atlantis, one where a man feared torture for a difference in coat cut.

Inside the pavilion, I bathed, though the water was tainted with a cheap scent. I waited with outward composure. The chamberlains, too well-bred to disturb my calm, stood silent while the Sun beat down on the scarlet roof. Outside, music blared and fires danced in the square.

It is said that the great maintain dignity by keeping lesser ones waiting. Phorenice disagreed. As soon as Tob's galley touched the royal quay, she departed the palace. Her procession, already marshalled, waited only for her. Trumpets sounded, and the advance began.

From the doorway, I watched the soldiers march straight to the square to salute, then range themselves aside. Followed by the Mariners' Guild, more soldiers, merchants, tanners, and spear-makers, all attired for a pageant. Some rode beasts tamed into servitude.

Then came the wonders. First, a huge bull mammoth emerged from the shadows of the houses. I had hunted these monsters, led raids against villages, and known their savage fury. Yet here was a giant among giants, tame as a whipped slave, bearing a golden half-castle stamped with the outstretched hand and adorned with silver snakes. Its tusks were gilded, its neck garlanded with flowers. It was a symbol of masterful strength.

Simultaneously appeared the mammoth's mistress, Empress Phorenice. While the beast drew my eye with its uncouth power, the lady on its back held my gaze immovably.

I stood as the crowd shouted, remaining in the pavilion until her steed halted in the center of the square, then I advanced across the open space, head erect, meeting her gaze.

"On your knees, my lord," whispered a chamberlain.

"At least with bent head," urged another.

I ignored them, marching forward with self-respect intact. She surveyed me, and I surveyed her. In those few seconds, I saw a woman unlike any I had imagined.

Throughout my official life, women had held no sway over me, though I had studied their influence on policy. This woman baffled me. Her body was slight, supple, and beautifully molded. Her face was a book of cleverness, fair beyond belief, with hair of lovely ruddiness cut short in the new fashion. Her eyes! Who could plumb their depths or find a trace of their heaven-made color?

She scrutinized me down to my soul, and her scrutiny seemed satisfied. She nodded as I approached. When I performed the required obeisance permitted to my rank, she spoke in a voice loud enough for all to hear.

"For others," she declared, "it is fitting to kneel, once, twice, or more, according to their station. But you are Deucalion, my lord. Though I knew you only from pictures drawn with tongues until today, I have now seen you and judged for myself. Therefore, I decree: Deucalion is above all other men in Atlantis. If one does not render him obedience, that man is an enemy of Phorenice and shall feel her anger."

A stair was brought, and I mounted to sit beside her in the golden half-castle under the canopy of royal snakes. An attendant fanned us with perfumed feathers. As the mammoth turned back toward the royal pyramid, flanked by heavily armed troops, Phorenice turned to me with a smile.

"You piqued me," she said.

"Your Majesty overwhelms me with such notice."

"You looked at my steed before you looked at me. A woman finds it hard to forgive a slight like that."

"I envied you the greatest of your conquests, and do still. I have fought mammoths and killed them, but I never dared think of taking one alive and taming it."

"You speak boldly," she smiled, "and yet you can turn a pretty compliment. Faugh! Deucalion, the way these people fawn on me gives me nausea. I am not of the same clay as they; just because I am the daughter of Gods, they must feed me on the pap of insincerity."

She was right, and the swineherd was forgotten. If she chose to maintain the fiction, it was not my place to contradict.

"I have pined long enough for stronger meat than they can offer," she continued. "I have procured my tongue-pictures of you, Deucalion. Though you do not know me, I knew you thoroughly before we met. I can admire a man with a mind great enough to forego silly gauds, excesses of feasts, or the pampering of women." She glanced down at her silks and jewels. "Women like to carry colors, but that is different. I sent for you to be my minister and bear the burden of ruling."

"There should be better men in broad Atlantis."

"There are not, my lord. I know them all by heart. They are enamored of my person, wearying me with empty phrases. Their cries of service are hollow; their own advancement and treasuries come first. So I sent for you, Deucalion, the one strong man in the world. Will you not sigh to be my lover?"

She watched for my answer.

"The Empress," I said, "is my mistress, and I will be an honest minister. With Phorenice the woman, I shall likely have little to do. Besides, I am not the sort to sport with this toy they call love."

"And yet you are personable enough," she mused. "That proves your strength, Deucalion. You will not lose your head through weak infatuation for my looks." She signaled to the attendant. "Ylga, fan not so violently."

Our conversation paused as I looked about. We passed through the chief street of the fairest city the world had ever seen. Having left it twenty years prior, I noted its growth: new temples, pyramids, palaces, and statuary. Its imperial magnificence surpassed even my memory of Yucatan's princely cities.

Yet, most striking was the poverty clinging to this splendor. Gaunt bodies and hungry faces lined the streets. Some stood naked as savages, dull to shame. Even traders, draped in tawdry gauds aping fashion, wore scared, uneasy expressions, hiding frantic hearts behind outward vauntings of prosperity.

Phorenice read my gaze.

"The season has been unhealthy," she explained lightly. "These lower people will not build fine houses to adorn my city. Living in their squalid kennels, they suffer calentures and sickness, making them disinclined to work. Trade has nearly stopped for half a year as rebels hammer at my gates."

I was startled out of my decorum. "Rebels! Who attacks Atlantis? Is the city under siege?"

"Of their condescension," she said, "they give us holiday today. Happily, my welcome to you comes undisturbed. If they were fighting, your ears would tell you. They are noisy enough. My spies say they prepare new engines for the walls, which you may break tomorrow if it amuses you. But for today, Deucalion, I have you, you have me, and there is peace. If you ask for more, I will give it."

"I did not know of this rebellion," I said. "Since you have made me your minister, I should know its scope immediately. This requires seriousness."

"Do you think I cannot take it seriously?" she retorted. She turned to Ylga. "Set loose my dress at the shoulder."

The glittering cavalcade halted before the massive stone entrance of the royal pyramid, massing in a sea of gold and silk in the great paved square. The mammoth, a beast of immense stature, was tethered before the doorway. As the trumpets blared, signaling our descent from the golden half-castle draped in the canopy of snakes, I stepped onto the ground.

It was clear we were moving from the open sky into the labyrinthine depths of the pyramid. Instinctively, a reflex of custom and reverence that had long since become part of my nature compelled me to turn toward the towering rocks of the Sacred Mountain looming above the city. I bowed low, offering the silent, prescribed prayer to the old Gods. I performed this act unthinkingly, as a matter of rote duty. Yet, as I rose, a sharp, dissonant sound cut through the air—a titter of laughter emanating from the crowd of onlookers.

I frowned, scanning the faces of the scoffers, and immediately turned to Phorenice to demand justice for their insolence. But a strange stillness gripped me. I had expected to see her mirroring my gesture, kneeling in obeisance. Instead, she stood erect, her forehead never having touched the dust. She regarded me with a look of peculiar intensity that I could not decipher.

She made no move to address the crowd's mockery. Instead, she simply said, "Come," and turned toward the doorway, crying out the secret word appointed for the day. The ponderous stone blocks barring the porch swung back on their hinges. With stately tread, she passed from the hot sunshine into the cool, shadowed gloom beyond, followed decorously by the fan-girl. A heaviness settled in my chest. I followed her inside, and the stone doors slammed shut with a sullen thud, sealing us away from the world.

We did not travel far. Phorenice halted in the Hall of Waiting. I remembered this place vividly: the red walls painted with the likenesses of kings, and the burning fountain of earth-breath that blazed from a bronze jet in the center of the floor, casting its light upon the space. Twenty years prior, the old King had welcomed me here before sending me to my vice-royalty in Yucatan. But the air had changed. Once pure and sweet, it now hung heavy with a cloying scent, making the atmosphere languid and oppressive.

"My minister," the Empress said, her voice cool. "I acquit you of intentional insult. But I believe the colonial air has rendered you a very simple man. Such an obeisance to that mountain a moment ago has not been made since I was sent to reign over this kingdom."

"Your Majesty," I replied, standing tall. "I am a member of the Priests' Clan, raised in their tenets. Before entering any house, I am taught to thank the Gods, and especially our Lord the Sun, for the air they provide. It has been my fate more than once to be chased by streams of fire and stinking vapors amidst the mountains during their boils. Therefore, I recite the prescribed prayer from the heart."

"Circumstances have changed since you left Atlantis," Phorenice countered. "And when thanks are given now, they are not thrown at those old Gods."

The implication struck me like a physical blow. If this was the new order, I would have no part in it. Serving a monarch was one thing; to palter with sacrilege, to accept a swineherd's daughter as a deity worthy of prayer, revolted my very manhood. I invited a crisis.

"Phorenice," I said, my voice steady. "I have been a priest from childhood, revering the Gods and growing intimate with their mysteries. Until I find for myself that those old things are false, I must stand by that allegiance. If there is a cost for this faithfulness, I must pay it."

She looked at me with a slow, calculating smile. "You are a strong man, Deucalion."

I bowed.

"I have heard others as stubborn," she continued, shaking out the ruddy bunches of her hair so the light of the burning earth-breath illuminated the perfection of her face. "But they were converted. I have found it as easy to convert the stubborn as to burn them. Indeed, there has been little talk of burning; they have all rushed to conversion, whether I would or no. It seems my poor looks and tongue lack charm today."

"Phorenice is Empress," I said stolidly. "I am her servant. Tomorrow, if she grants leave, I will clear away the rabble clamoring outside the walls. I must begin to prove my utility."

"I am told you are a pretty fighter," she said. "I possess some skill in arms myself and consider myself a judge. Tomorrow, we shall taste battle together. But today, I must complete the honorable reception I have planned. The feast will be ready soon, and you will wish to prepare. Chambers have been selected for your use, stocked with necessities. Ylga will show you the way."

We waited until Phorenice had vanished into the shadows of the hall's far angle, then, guided by the fan-girl who carried a lamp, we threaded our way through the narrow, twisting mazes of the pyramid.

The air was thick with perfumes, and the passages doubled back upon themselves with such cunning complexity that a stranger might spend days searching for a single chamber. These forgotten builders had ensured that kings could not be slain by vulgar assassins entering from without. Legend held that the king of the time had killed everyone who had built the pyramid or even seen the layout of its inner stones.

Yet, the fan-girl led us with practiced ease. She pushed stones to reveal passages, pressed flagstones to uncover steep stairs, and navigated the inclines with the confidence of one born to these halls. Finally, we arrived at the chamber designated for me.

"There is raiment in these chests by the walls," the girl said, gesturing to the bronze coffer filled with jewels. "These are Phorenice's first presents, a small earnest of what is to come. My Lord Deucalion can drop his simplicity and dress in finery to suit the fashion."

"Girl," I said sharply, "be more decorous with your tongue and spare me such advice."

"If my Lord Deucalion thinks this a rudeness, he can give a word to Phorenice, and I shall be whipped. If he asks, I can be stripped and scourged before him. The Empress will do much for Deucalion just now."

"You are nearer to that whipping than you think," I warned.

"I have a name," she retorted, looking at me sullenly from under black brows. "They call me Ylga. You might have heard it as we rode on the mammoth, had you not been so wrapped up in Phorenice."

I studied her curiously. "You have never seen me before, and your first words are those that could bring trouble upon yourself. There is some object in all this."

She pushed the massive stone of the chamber door and drew me away from the airshaft into the far corner. "I am the daughter of Zaemon," she whispered. "Whom you knew."

"You bring me a message from him?"

"How could I? He lives in the priests' dwellings on the Mountain you bowed to. I have not seen him in two years. But when I saw you step out from that red pavilion at the harbor, I felt a pity for you, Deucalion. I remembered you were my father's friend, and I knew what Phorenice had in store. She has been plotting this for two months."

"I cannot hear words against the Empress."

"And yet..." She stamped her sandal on the stone floor. "You must be either very blind or very daring. But I shall not interfere further—not now. Still, I shall watch. If you ever seem to want a friend, I will try to serve you."

"I thank you for your friendship."

"You take it lightly. Even now, I do not believe you know my power, nor my motive. You may be the first man in this kingdom, but let me tell you, I rank as the second lady. Remember, women stand high in Atlantis now. Believe me, my friendship is a commodity sought with frequency and industry."

"As I say, I am grateful. You seem to think little of my gratitude, Ylga, but credit me: I have never bestowed it upon a woman before. Treasure it for its rarity."

"Well, my lord, there is an education before you." She left me then, showing me how to summon slaves. For a full minute, I stood wondering at the words I had spoken. Who was the daughter of Zaemon that she should induce me to change the habits of a lifetime?

Slaves arrived eager to deck me in robes and gauds, offering a dozen perfumes and unguents. Their manner irritated me. I was clean, shaved, and my robe was unsoiled. Considering their attentions an impertinence, I ordered them to beat one another, promising that if they failed to do so thoroughly, I would hand them to the brander for stripes that would endure. It is strange, but a common menial can often surpass a rebellious general in the power to ruffle one's temper.

What shocked me most that day, however, was Phorenice's method of summoning guests to her feast. I sat in my chamber awaiting the call when, gradually, a sound grew from nothingness into a clanging din that hurt the ears. Then, the entire massive fabric of the pyramid trembled and groaned, shaking as though it were a child's wooden toy brushed by a strong hand.

This was the portent served yearly by the chiefs of the Priests' Clan on the Sacred Mountain, a sacred reminder that man was born from roaring fire and earth-tremors, and would eventually be swallowed up by them along with his sins. Here, the Empress was prostituting these solemnities into a mere call to gluttony, a signal for ribald laughter and sensuous display.

How had she acquired the authority to do this? Who was she to tamper with the dimly understood powers dwelling within the liquid heart of the earth? Had there been treachery? Had a member of the Priests' Clan betrayed the sacred vows? Or had Phorenice discovered a key to these mysteries with her own agile brain?

If the latter, I could continue to serve her with a silent conscience. She was Empress, and obedience was my duty. But if she had suborned a weaker member of the Clan, that would be a different matter. Our constitution demanded that we preserve our secrets inviolate and pursue with undying hatred both the betrayer and the recipient of his confidence.

With very undecided feelings, I obeyed the summons and allowed the slaves to lead me to the great banqueting hall. The scene was dazzling. The majestic chamber, carved with marvels, was filled with a company decked in every conceivable gaud and color. They seemed to ignore the solemn portent that had summoned them, the death stalking the city wards, or the rebels leaguered beyond the walls. They were gluttonous for the passions of the moment, their fashion dictating that they look at nothing else.

Flaming jets of earth-breath lit the hall to the brightness of midday. When I stepped onto the pavement, trumpets blared to announce my coming, yet there was no roar of welcome. "Deucalion," they lisped with mincing voices, bowing ridiculously so their ornaments jangled. When Phorenice appeared, the artificiality remained. They meant well, but heartiness had been deemed barbarism by this new culture.

Chamberlains ushered me to the dais at the far end. I was to sit on Phorenice's divan and eat from her dish.

"There is no stint to the honor the Empress puts upon me," I said as I knelt.

She gave me one of her queer, sidelong looks. "Deucalion may have more, if he asks prettily. He may have what all the men in the known world have sighed for, and what none will ever get. But I have given enough of my own accord; he must ask me warmly for those further favors."

"I ask," I said, "first, that I may sweep the boundaries clear of this rabble clamoring against the city walls."

"Pah," she frowned. "Have you appetite only for the sterner pleasures of life? My good Deucalion, they must have been rustic folk in that colony of yours. Well, you shall give me news now of the toothsome nature of this feast."

Dishes and goblets were placed before us. I had little appetite for victuals so broken and highly spiced, but the other diners appreciated the finicking cookery and luscious wines to the full. They sat like a tangle of rainbows, going into raptures over every titillation of the palate as though it were a caress of the Gods.

Phorenice, with her quick, bright eyes, looked on, occasionally tossing a word to me or calling a favored creature up to receive a scrap of viand from the royal dish. The honored ones ate with extravagant gestures or, as happened twice, tucked the food into the folds of their clothes as treasures too dear to be profaned by human lips.

To me, this flattery appeared gross and disgusting, though Phorenice, through use, seemed to take it as merely her due. There was, one had to suppose, a weakness in her somewhere, though outwardly none was apparent. Her face was strong, subtle, and wondrously comely. All the courtiers raved about her beauty, and though I was not given to such appreciation, I could see they had a groundwork for their admiration; surely the Gods had never favored a mortal woman more highly. Yet, lovely though she was, I preferred to look upon Ylga, the girl, who sat on the divan behind us as immediate attendant. There was an honesty in Ylga's face which Phorenice lacked.

These feasters did not eat to nourish their bodies but to cloy themselves, strutting forth new usages with every platter. Some manners were closely touching on disrespect.

Thus was my reception in the capital of Atlantis, and thus did I first glimpse the new policies of her who now ruled. I confess freely to my own inaction and limpness at the time; but it was all deliberate. The old ties of duty seemed lost, or at least merged into one another. Beforetime, to serve the king was to serve the Clan of the Priests, from which he had been chosen, and whose head he constituted. But Phorenice was self-made, and appeared to be a rule unto herself; if Zaemon was to be trusted, he was the mouthpiece of the Priests, and their Clan had set her at defiance. How was a mere honest man to choose on the instant between the two?

Cold argument told me that governments were set up for the good of the country at large, and I said to myself that there would be my choice. I must find out which rule promised best for Atlantis, and do my poor best to prop it into full power. And here at once there opened up another path in the maze: I had heard considerable talk of rebels; of another faction of Atlanteans who, whatever their faults might be, were strong enough to beleaguer the capital. Before coming to any final decision, it would be as well to take their claims in balance with the rest. So on the night of that very same day on which I had just re-planted my foot on the old country's shores, I set out to glean for myself tidings on the matter.

No one inside the royal pyramid gainsaid me. Phorenice glowered on the feasters for a while longer in silent fury, but saying no further word; and then her eyes turned on me, though softened somewhat.

"You may be an honest man, Deucalion," she said at length, "but you are a monstrous cold one. I wonder when you will thaw?" And here she smiled. "I think it will be soon. But for now I bid you farewell. In the morning we will take this country by the shoulders, and see it in some new order."

She left the banqueting-hall then, Ylga following; and taking precedence of my rank, I went out next, whilst all others stood and made salutation. But I halted by Tarca first and put my hand on his unclean flesh. "You are an unfortunate man," I said, "but I can admire a brave soldier. If relief can be gained for your plague, I will use interest to procure it for you."

The man's thanks came in a mumble from his wrecked mouth, and some of those near shuddered in affected disgust. I turned on them with a black brow: "Your charity, my lords, seems of as small account as your courage. You affected a fine disbelief of Zaemon's sayings and a simpering contempt for his priesthood, but when it comes to laying a hand on him, you show a discretion which, in the old days, we should have called by an ugly name. I had rather be Tarca, with all his uncleanness, than any of you now as you stand."

With which leave-taking I waited coldly till they gave me my due salutation, and then walked out of the banqueting-hall without offering a soul another glance. I took my way to the grand gate of the pyramid, called for the officer of the guard, and demanded exit. The man was obsequious enough, but he opened with some demur.

"My lord's attendants have not yet come up?"

"I have none."

"My lord knows the state of the streets?"

"I did twenty years back. I shall be able to pick my way."

"My lord must remember that the city is beleaguered," the fellow persisted. "The people are hungry. They prowl in bands after nightfall, and—I make no question that my lord would conquer in a fight against whatever odds, but—"

"Quite right. I covet no street scuffle to-night. Lend me, I pray you, a sufficiency of men. You will know best what are needed. For me, I am accustomed to a city with quiet streets."

A score of sturdy fellows were detailed off for my escort, and with them in a double file on either hand, I marched out from the close perfumed air of the pyramid into the cool moonlight of the city. It was my purpose to make a tour of the walls and to find out somewhat of the disposition of these rebels.

But the Gods saw fit to give me another education first. The city, as I saw it during that night walk, was no longer the old capital that I had known, the just accretion of the ages, the due admixture of comfort and splendour. The splendour was there, vastly increased. Whole wards had been swept away to make space for new palaces and new pyramids of the wealthy, and I could not but have an admiration for the skill and the brain which made possible such splendid monuments.

And, indeed, gazing at them there under the silver of the moonlight, I could almost understand the emotions of the Europeans and other barbarous savages which cause them to worship all such great buildings as Gods, since they deem them too wonderful and majestic to be set up by human hands unaided.

Still, if it was easy to admire, it was simple also to see plain advertisement of the cost at which these great works had been reared. From each grant of ground, where one of these stately piles earned silver under the moon, a hundred families had been evicted and left to harbour as they pleased in the open; and, as a consequence, now every niche had its quota of sleepers, and every shadow its squad of fierce wild creatures, ready to rush out and rob or slay all wayfarers of less force than their own.

Myself, I am no pamperer of the common people. I say that, if a man be left to hunger and shiver, he will work to gain him food and raiment; and if not, why then he can die, and the State is well rid of a worthless fellow. But here beside us, as we marched through many wards, were marks of blind oppression; starved dead bodies, with the bones starting through the lean skin, sprawled in the gutter; and indeed it was plain that, save for the favoured few, the people of the great capital were under a most heavy oppression.

But at this, though I might regret it abominably, I could make no strong complaint. By the ancient law of the land all the people, great and small, were the servants of the king, to be put without question to what purposes he chose; and Phorenice stood in the place of the king. So I tried to think no treason, but with a sigh passed on, keeping my eyes above the miseries and the squalors of the roadway, and sending out my thoughts to the stars which hung in the purple night above, and to the High Gods which dwelt amongst them, seeking, if it might be, for guidance for my future policies. And so in time the windings of the streets brought us to the walls, and, coursing beside these and giving fitting answer to the sentries who beat their drums as we passed, we came in time to that great gate which was a charge to the captain of the garrison.

Here it was plain there was some special commotion. A noise of laughter went up into the still night air, and with it now and again the snarl and roar of a great beast, and now and again the shriek of a hurt man. But whatever might be afoot, it was not a scene to come upon suddenly. The entrance gates of our great capital were designed by their ancient builders to be no less strong than the walls themselves. Four pairs of valves were there, each a monstrous block of stone two man-heights square and a man-height thick, and the wall was doubled to receive them, enclosing an open circus between its two parts. The four gates themselves were set one at the inner, one at the outer side of each of these walls, and a hidden machinery so connected them that of each set one could not open till the other was closed; and as for forcing them without war engines, one might as foolishly try to push down the royal pyramid with the bare hand.

My escort made outcry with the horn which hung from the wall inviting such a summons, and a warder came to an arrow-slit and did inspection of our persons and business. His survey was according to the ancient form of words, which is long, and this was made still more tedious by the noise from within, which ever and again drowned all speech between us entirely.

But at last the formalities had been duly complied with, and he shot back the massive bars and bolts of stone and threw ajar one monstrous stone valve of the door. Into the chamber within—a chamber made from the thickness of the wall between the two doors—I and my fellows crowded, and then the warder with his machines pulled to the valve which had been opened and came to me again through the press of my escort, bowing low to the ground.

"I have no vail to give you," I said abruptly. "Get on with your duty. Open me that other door."

"With respect, my lord, it would be better that I should first announce my lord's presence. There is a baiting going forward in the circus, and the tigers are as yet mere savages and no respecters of persons."

"The what?"

"The tigers, if my lord will permit them the name. They are baiting a batch of prisoners with the two great beasts which the Empress (whose name be adored) has sent here to aid us keep the gate. But if my lord will, there are the ward rooms leading off this passage and the galleries which run out from them commanding the circus, and from there my lord can see the sport undisturbed."

Now, the mere lust for killing excites only disgust in me, but I suspected the orders of the Empress in this matter and had a curiosity to see her scheme. So I stepped into the warder's lodge and on into the galleries which commanded the circus with their arrow-slits. The old builders of the place had intended these for a second line of defence, for, supposing the outer doors all forced, an enemy could be speedily shot down in the circus without being able to give a blow in return, and so would only march into a death-trap. But as a gazing-place on a spectacle they were no less useful.

The circus was bright lit by the moonlight, and the air which came in to me from it was acrid with the reek of blood. There was no sport in what was going forward: as I said, it was mere killing, and the sight disgusted me. I am no prude about this matter. Give a prisoner his weapons, put him in a pit with beasts of reasonable strength, and let him fight to a finish if you choose, and I can look on there and applaud the strokes. The war prisoner, being a prisoner, has earned death by natural law and prefers to get his last stroke in hot blood than to be knocked down by the headsman's axe. And it is any brave man's luxury either to help or watch a lusty fight. But this baiting in the circus between the gates was no fair battle like that.

To begin with, the beasts were no fair antagonists for single men. In fact, twenty men armed might well have fled from them. When the warder said tigers, I supposed he meant the great cats of the woods. But here, in the circus, I saw a pair of the most terrific of all the fur-bearing land beasts, the great tigers of the caves—huge monsters, of such ponderous strength that in hunger they will oftentimes drag down a mammoth if they can find him away from his herd.

How they had been brought captive I could not tell. Hunter of beasts though I had been for all my days, I take no shame in saying that I always approached the slaying of a cave-tiger with stratagem and infinite caution. To entrap it alive and bring it to a city on a chain was beyond my most daring schemes, and I have been accredited with more new things than one. But here it was in fact, and I saw in these captive beasts a new certificate for Phorenice's genius.

The purpose of these two cave-tigers was plain: whilst they were in the circus and loose, no living being could cross from one gate to the other. They were a new and sturdy addition to the defences of the capital. A collar of bronze was round the throat of each, and on the collar was a massive chain which led to the wall, where it could be payed out or hauled in by means of a windlass in one of the hidden galleries. So that at ordinary moments the two huge beasts could be tethered, one close to either end of the circus, as the litter of bones and other messes showed, leaving free passage-way between the two sets of doors.

But when I stood there by the arrow-slit, looking down into the moonlight of the circus, these chains were slackened (though men stood by the windlass of each), and the great striped brutes were prowling about the circus with the links clanking and chinking in their wake. Lying stark on the pavement were the bodies of some eight men, dead and uneaten; and though the cave-tigers stopped their prowlings now and again to nuzzle these and beat them about with playful paw-blows, they made no pretence at commencing a meal. It was clear that this cruel sport had grown common to them and they knew there were other victims yet to be added to the tally.

Presently, sure enough, as I watched, a valve of the farther gate swung back an arm's length and a prisoner, furiously resisting, was thrust out into the circus. He fell on his face, and after one look around him he lay resolutely still, with eyes on the ground passively awaiting his fate. Our plan was simple: I would remain hidden within the temple's sanctum until the camp fell into a heavy slumber, granting me the cover of darkness to reach the sea undetected.

Night descended wet, a drizzle soaking the earth. Through the narrow slits in the temple walls, we observed the campfires below, tended with care by men and women huddled in skins and rags. Steam rose from their bodies as the heat struggled against the chill. Folk forced into such discomfort are notoriously sharp-tongued and cruel in spirit, and Nais frowned as she watched the misery of the weather.

"A fine night," she murmured. "I would have sent my lord back to the city without a soul here being the wiser; but in this cold, people sleep sourly. We must wait until the hour drugs them sounder."

Thus we waited, sitting together on a pavement rarely touched by worshippers. Little was spoken aloud, yet there was a comfort in the silence of good company.

But as the hours stretched, the night lost its quietude. From the distance, a horn or shell began to blow a harsh, raucous note that persisted and grew louder. Voices erupted from the fires, railing against the disturber. Soon, the sound halted, and we stood up to see through the wall-slits the camp waking in unease, clustering around a figure speaking from the pedestal of a war engine.

We could not hear his words, nor did our curiosity burn brightly. Given that everyone awake seemed weary of this fellow, as Nais whispered, my escape should have been simple.

We were counting without the inevitable busybody. A dozen pairs of feet splashed through the mud to the temple's side, shouting that Nais must join the audience. She possessed a tongue of eloquence, and they feared the speaker on the war engine would sow schism unless a skilled refuter stood against him.

Now, I felt I was being dragged into a skirmish by the most unexpected chance. Yet Nais was resolved that there should be no fight if her courage could avert it. "Come out with me," she whispered urgently. "Keep distant from the light of the fires."

"But how explain my presence here?"

"There is no need to explain anything," she replied bitterly. "They will assume you are my lover. It is the custom here. But oh, why did the Gods not grant you a beard, curled as other men wear theirs? Then you could have departed safely two hours ago."

"A smooth chin pleases me better."

"So it does me," I heard her murmur as she leaned on the stone threshold and pushed it open. "Your chin."

The ragged men outside—accompanied by women—did not linger to inspect me closely. Coarse jokes flew, ones I could have met with a sword-thrust, before they stepped back into the darkness, glancing back only to ensure we followed. Everywhere else, black shadows pressed in the same direction. The rain spat noisily on the campfires as we passed, and from behind us came a tide of others. No sleepers remained; all were rushing to hear the preacher on the war engine. Had we tried to swerve, we would have been marked instantly.

We pressed on through the darkness until we were within earshot.

At first, we caught little of the preacher's words. "Who are your chiefs?" the question rang out at the climax of a fervid harangue, immediately drowning all rational discourse in uproar. "We have no chiefs!" the people shouted. "We are done with chiefs; we are all equal here. Take away your silly magic. You may kill us with magic if you choose, but rule us you shall not. Nor shall the other priests rule. Nor Phorenice. Nor anybody. We are done with rulers."

The press bore us closer to the man on the war engine. He was old, with white hair tumbling over his shoulders and a long, untrimmed white beard. Save for a wisp of rag around his loins, his body was naked, glistening in the wet. In his hand, however, he held the mark of his caste. With it, he punctuated his sentences, sometimes whirling it to bathe his wet, naked form in a halo of light. It was a wand whose tip burned with unconsuming fire, glowing and blazing like a star sent down from the high heavens. It was the Symbol of our Lord the Sun, a credential no one could forge, upon which no civilized man would cast a doubt.

Indeed, the ragged, frantic crew did not question for a moment that he belonged to the Clan of Priests, the lineage that had long ruled the land. Even in their loudest clamors, they freely acknowledged his powers. "You may kill us with your magic, if you choose," they screamed. Yet stubbornly, they refused to return to their old allegiance. "We have suffered too many things these later years," they cried. "We are done with rulers now, forever."

In my own heart, I viewed the old man with a different emotion. Here was Zaemon, father to Nais, the man I had seen yesterday seated at Phorenice's elbow, who today could denounce me as Deucalion if he chose. These rebels had expended a navy in their wish to kill me four days prior. If they knew of my proximity, even with Nais as my advocate, her cold reasoning would find little audience now. The High Gods who hold the tether of our lives hide their secrets well, but I did not think it impious to believe mine was very near the cutting edge.

The beautiful woman saw this too. She went so far as to twine her fingers in mine, pressing them as a farewell, and I pressed hers in return, sorrowful not to see her more. Still, I could not help letting my thoughts travel with a grim gloating over the fine mound of dead I would build before these ragged, unskilled rebels pulled me down. It was inevitable. For of all emotions that ferment in the human heart, the joy of strife is keenest, and none but an old fighter, face to face with what must necessarily be his final battle, can tell how deep this lust is embroidered into the foundations of his being.

For the moment, Zaemon did not see me, too wrapped in his outcry, leaving me free to listen to the burning words he spread around him and gauge their effect.

The theme he preached was not new. He declared that since the beginning of history, the Gods had set apart one Clan to rule and serve as Priests. Until the coming of Phorenice, they had fulfilled their duties with exactitude and justice. They had fought invaders, waged war against beasts, and studied earth-movements to foretell earthquakes and eruptions, spreading warnings so the people might escape devastation. They were no self-seekers; their aim was always to further the interest of Atlantis, honoring the kingdom favored by the High Gods. Under the Priestly Clan, Atlantis had reached the pinnacle of human prosperity and happiness.

"But," cried the old man, waving the Symbol until his wet body glistened in a halo of light, "the people grew fat and careless with their easy life. They began to have a conceit that their good fortune was earned by their own puny brains and thews, and was no gift from the Gods above. Presently, the cult of these Gods became neglected, and Their temples were barren of gifts and worshippers. Followed a punishment. The Gods, in Their inscrutable way, decreed that a wife of one of the Priests—a governor of no inconsiderable province—should see a woman child by the wayside and take it for adoption. That child the Gods, in their infinite wisdom, fashioned into a scourge for Atlantis. You who have felt the weight of Phorenice's hand know with what completeness the High Gods can fashion their instruments."

"Yet, even as they set up, so can they throw down, and those that shall debase Phorenice are even now appointed. The old rule is to be re-established; but not till you who have sinned are sufficiently chastened to cry to it for relief." He waved the mysterious glowing Symbol before him. "See," he cried in his high, old, quavering voice, "you know the unspeakable Power of which that is the sign, and for which I am the mouthpiece. It is for you to make decision now. Are the Gods to throw down this woman who has scorned Them and so cruelly trodden on you? Or are you to be still further purged of your pride before you are ripe for deliverance?"

The old priest broke off with a gesture, his ragged white beard sinking onto his chest. Promptly, a young man, skin-clad and carrying his weapon, elbowed up through the press of listeners and jumped onto the platform beside him. "Hear me, brethren!" he bellowed in his strong young voice. "We are done with tyrants. Death may come, and we all here have shown how little we fear it. But own rulers again we will not, and that is our final say. My lord," he said, turning to the old man with a brave face, "I know it is in your power to kill me by magic if you choose, but I have said my say, and can stand the cost if needs be."

"I can kill you, but I will not," said Zaemon. "You have said your silliness. Now go you to the ground again."

"We have free speech here. I will not go till I choose."

"Aye, but you will," said the old man, and turned on him with a sudden tightening of the brows. No blow was passed; even the Symbol, which glowed like a star against the night, was not lifted in warning. But the young man tried to retort, and finding himself smitten with sudden dumbness, turned with a spasm of fear and jumped back whence he had come. The crowd thrilled expectantly, and when no further portent was given, they began to shout that a miracle should be shown them, and then perhaps they would be persuaded back to the old allegiance.

The old man stooped and glowered at them in fury. "You dogs," he cried, "you empty-witted dogs! Do you ask that I should degrade the powers of the Higher Mysteries by dancing them out before you as though they were a mummers' show? Do you tickle yourselves that you are to be tempted back to your allegiance? It is for you to woo the Gods who are so offended. Come in humility, and I take it upon myself to declare that you will receive fitting pardon and relief. Remain stubborn, and the scourge, Phorenice, may torment you into annihilation before she in turn is made to answer for the evil she has put upon the land. There is the choice for you to pick at."

The turmoil of voices rose again into the wetness of the night, and weapons were upraised menacingly. It was clear that the party for independence had by far the greater weight, both in numbers and vigor; and those who might, from sheer weariness of strife, have been willing for surrender, withheld their word through terror of the consequence. It was a fine comment on the freedom of speech, about which these unruly fools had made their boast. With a sly malice, I could not help whispering a word on this to Nais as she stood at my elbow. But Nais clutched at my hand, imploring me for caution. "Oh, be silent, my lord," she whispered back, "or they will tear you in pieces. They are on fire for mischief now."

"Yet a few hours back you were for killing me yourself," I could not help reminding her.

She turned on me with a hot look. "A woman can change her mind, my lord. But it becomes you little to remind her of her fickleness."

A man in the press beside me wrenched round with an effort, staring at me searchingly through the darkness. "Oh!" he said. "A shaved chin. Who are you, friend, that you should cut a beard instead of curling it? I can see no wound on your face."

I answered him civilly enough that, with "freedom" for a watchword, the fashion of my chin was a matter of mere private concern. But as that did not satisfy him, and as he seemed to be one of those quarrelsome fellows that are the bane of every community, I took him suddenly by the throat and the shoulder, and bent his neck with the old, quick turn until I heard it crack. I had unhanded him before any of his neighbors had seen what had befallen. The fierce press of the crowd held him from slipping to the ground, so he stood there where he was, with his head nodded forward, as though he had fallen asleep through heaviness or fainted through the crushing of his fellows. I had no desire to begin that last fight of mine in a place like this, where there was no room to swing a weapon, nor chance to clear a battle ring.

All this time, the lean preacher from the mountains was sending forth his angry anathemas, still holding the strained attention of the people. The passage, though cunningly concealed by human artifice, was one of the ancient veins where the fiery blood of our Mother, the Earth, once coursed. Centuries had passed since lava streamed through its depths, yet the air remained warm and thick with sulfur. There was no reason to linger in such a transit. I lit a lamp from a niche and hurried along, coughing violently, wishing desperately for the simples that could soothe a catarrhal throat. Alas, the packet of medicines I had salvaged from the Vice-Royalty of Yucatan had been lost in the naval engagement with Dason's fleet. Since landing in Atlantis, there had been scant time to indulge in the refinements of medicine.

The network of subterranean channels branched wildly. Had any of the Seven Priests but one stumbled into these recesses by chance, they would have perished hopelessly in the labyrinthine windings or fallen into the pits leading to the boiling depths below. Fortunately, I carried the chart of the true course etched clearly in my mind, a memory from my initiation twenty years prior. Back then, as an appointed Viceroy, I was raised to the highest degree known to our Clan, entrusted with its secrets and working implements.

The journey was arduous; the floor was monstrously uneven, and the air foul. I knew that by the time the signs indicated I had passed beneath the city walls and entered its precincts, the day would be far spent. Here, the vow of the Seven hampered my progress: under no circumstances, regardless of stress, was egress permitted from this passage before a mortal eye. Branch after branch I tried, only to find loiterers near the exits. I had hoped to emerge via the path inside the royal pyramid, but the place hummed like a hive; there was no chance of going up unobserved. The same held true for the five subsequent outlets. The city was agog with a strange, feverish excitement.

Finally, I reached the temple of a lesser God and stood behind the image to observe. The place was empty; the dust robed the floors and the seats of the worshippers, indicating a vacancy long endured. I moved the necessary items, stepped out, and sealed the entry behind me. A broom lay unnoticed on a pew. With it, I quickly disguised all traces of my footprints and approached the temple door. It was shut. Though a priest, the secret of its opening was beyond me. Only the attendant priests of the temple could move the mechanism controlling the massive stone that filled the doorway. If everyone had gone out to witness whatever spectacle stirred the city, I was no closer to my goal than before.

There was no sound of life within the temple precincts. Decay and disuse were evident everywhere. Yet, by ancient law, there should have been at least one watchman in the priests' dwellings. Down the passages leading to them I made my way, expecting to find them deserted. It would have surprised me little. I knew the old order had changed, but I was only beginning to realize the ruthlessness with which it had been swept away and replaced by the new.

However, even in an age of general apostasy, some faithful men remained. Reaching the door of the dwelling—which lay in the roof of the temple—I gave the call. Presently, it opened. The man who peered dully through the gloom had at least remained constant to his vows, and I greeted him with respect.

His name was Ro, and I remembered him well. We had passed through the sacred college together, always known as the dullard. He possessed little capacity for the cult of the Gods, less for the arts of ruling, and least of all for the handling of arms. Appointed to a lowly office in this obscure temple, he had risen to become its second priest and one of its two custodians merely through the desertion of all his colleagues. It was not pleasant to think that a fool should remain true where cleverer men abandoned the old beliefs.

Ro performed the greater obeisance. His beard was curled in the prevailing fashion, though poorly done. His clothing was ill-fitting and unbrushed; he was always a slovenly fellow. "The temple door is shut," he said, "and I alone hold the secret of its opening. My lord comes here by the secret way, as one of the Seven. I am my lord's servant."

A motley crowd filled the street running past the front of the obscure temple, all hurrying in one direction. Given what I had been told, it required little art to guess that the great stone circle of our Lord the Sun was their destination. It grieved me to think of how many venerable centuries that great fane had stood before the weather and earth tremors without witnessing such profanation. A dark thought occurred to me: *Was our Great Lord above drawing this woman on to her destruction? Would He take some vast and final act of vengeance when she consummated her final sacrilege?*

But the crowd pressed on, thrilled and excited, thinking little (as crowds are wont) of the deeper matters beneath the bare spectacle. From one quarter of the city walls, the din of an attack from the besiegers made itself clearly heard over the houses and intervening palaces, yet no one heeded it. They had grown callous to the battering of rams and the flight of fire-darts. Their nerves, hunger, and desperation were strung to such a pitch that little short of an actual storm could stir them into new excitement over the siege.

All were armed. The naked carried weapons in the hope of overcoming someone to rob them; those with possessions walked ready to fight for their ownership. There was no security, no trust. The lesson of civilization had dropped away from these common people as mud is washed from feet by rain. In their new habits and thoughts, they had regressed to the grade from which savages like those of Europe have never yet emerged. It was a grim commentary on the success of Phorenice's rule.

The crowd merged me into their ranks without question. With them, I pressed forward down the winding streets, once clean and trim, now foul and mud-strewn. Men and women had died of hunger in these streets in recent years, rotting where they lay, and we trod their bones underfoot as we walked. Yet rising out of this squalor and misery were great pyramids and palaces, the like of which for splendour and magnificence had never been seen before. It was a jarring admixture.

In time, we came to the open space in the center of the city, the only area Phorenice had not dared to encroach upon with her ambitious building schemes. We stood on the secular ground surrounding the most ancient, the most grand, and the breast of all this world's temples.

Since the beginning of time, when man first emerged amongst the beasts, our Lord the Sun has always been his chiefest God. She had been nurtured during these years of her rule on a pap of these silly protestations, and could weigh their value with an expert's exactness.

Nor was it a case where honest confession would have served my purpose better. If I had put my position to her in plain words, it would have made relations worse. And so perforce I had to hold my tongue, and submit to be considered a clown.

"I had always heard," she said, "that you colonists in Yucatan were far ahead of those in Egypt in all the arts and graces. But you, sir, do small credit to your vice-royalty. Why, I have had gentry from the Nile come here, and you might almost think they had never left their native shores."

But here, by a lucky chance for me, an incident occurred which saved me from further baiting. The rebels outside the walls were conducting their day's attack with vigor and some intelligence. More than once during our procession, the lighter missiles from their war engines had sung up through the air, splitting against buildings and throwing splinters that wounded those who thronged the streets. Still, there had been nothing to ruffle the nerves of anyone accustomed to the haps of warfare, or to hinder our courtship. But presently, it seems, they stopped hurling stones and took to loading their engines with carcases of wood lined with throwing fire.

Against stone buildings, these did little harm, save that they scorched horribly any poor wretch within splash of them when they burst; but when they fell upon the rude wooden booths and rush shelters of the poorer folk, they set them ablaze instantly. There was no putting out these fires.

These things would have given little concern to either Phorenice or myself, as they are the trivial and common incidents of every siege; but the mammoth on which we rode had not been so properly schooled. When the first blue whiff of smoke came to us down the windings of the street, the huge red beast hoisted its trunk and began to sway its head uneasily. When the smoke drifts grew more dense, and here and there a tongue of flame showed pale beneath the sunshine, it stopped abruptly and began to trumpet.

The guards who led it tugged manfully at the chains which hung from the jagged metal collar round its neck, so that the spikes ran deep into its flesh, reminding it keenly of its bondage. But the beast's terror at the fire, which was native to its constitution, mastered all its new-bought habits of obedience. From time unknown, men have hunted the mammoth in the savage ground, and the mammoth has hunted men; and the men have always used fire as a shield, and mammoths have learned to dread fire as the most dangerous of all enemies.

I suppressed these thoughts, knowing they would only sour our ride. Phorenice, sensing my mood, matched my silence with her own. We traveled to the westernmost wall, where she led me to a solitary stone house on a tidal promontory. Here, she revealed her secret: gold collectors.

While the Sacred Mountain taught that gold existed dissolved in seawater, no one had devised a way to extract it. Phorenice had stumbled upon the trick. "I tell Atlantis only the gods possess this secret," she laughed, patting my arm. "But I am no goddess to you, Deucalion. I must have dreamed it." She explained the system: seawater sluiced through culverts over rough metal plates, trapping the yellow metal in the ridges. Her workers melted the catch into bars. "My fellows tried to cheat me," she admitted, her tone lightening, "until I towed two of them behind a galley until the cannibal fish devoured them. "More divinity," I suggested.

"I suppose so. But you too shall become a god among these silly Atlanteans if you apply your wit." Yet, the fascination of Phorenice was a powerful drug. When we left the gold collectors and encountered danger, I found myself fighting with a ferocity I rarely displayed. The cause was the pervasive unrest and poverty of the common people. We were attacked by "barbarous fishers"—desperate men driven to violence by the harshness of their trade. Starving and desperate, they sought any chance to survive, often through theft or robbery.

As we rode, Phorenice spoke of her new sumptuary laws, having confiscated gold from a smith who dared wear it. Suddenly, clumsy fishing boats paddled toward us, and savage men ran from the narrow lanes, forming threatening clusters.

The fishers pressed on, indifferent to the loss of their kin, eager only for the jewels. Two dove beneath to slit the boat's skin; I killed them. Phorenice dispatched those climbing the gunwales. The decree of Empress Phorenice moved through the city of Atlantis with the speed of a falling stone. Her philosophy was absolute: the existence of the realm served solely her will, and no price was too steep to pay for her desires. Having issued the command for the living burial of Nais, she had specified that I, Deucalion, should construct the throne of stone. Though the edict named me the builder, the unspoken understanding was that the labor would be performed by the city's hands.

Heralds proclaimed the order in every ward. Immediately, masons, laborers, stonecutters, sculptors, engineers, and architects abandoned their current tasks to converge at the rendezvous. An architect was chosen as chief, directing the lesser architects and engineers. Any material within the city walls bearing their seal was seized instantly, without payment or compensation. To accommodate the monstrous blocks of stone required, entire buildings were demolished to clear the path.

I have noted the modern obsession with erecting new palaces and pyramids. Even as an army of rebels battered the city walls with war engines, the building guilds worked steadily. Their skill, aided by Phorenice's marvelous inventions, increased constantly. While they could not move the massive blocks planted by the early Gods for the sacred circle of the Sun, they possessed rams, trucks, and cranes capable of handling amazing bulks.

The throne was to be erected in the open square before the royal pyramid. Seven tiers of stone formed the groundwork, each knee-height deep and cut in the front with three steps. In the uppermost layer lay a cavity designed to hold Nais's body, poised above which was the vast block forming the seat of the throne itself.

Throughout the night, under the light of torches, relay after relay of stonecutters, masons, and sweating laborers toiled to bring up the stone, dress it into shape, and lay it in due position. Engineers built machines for lifting, and architects verified that each stone rested in its just and perfect place. Whips cracked, and men fainted from the labor, but as soon as one became incapable, another pressed forward. No delay was brooked when Phorenice had spoken her wish.

Finally, as the square began to fill with spectators drawn to the pageant, the chippings and scaffolding were cleared away, along with the bodies of some half-score of workmen who had died from accidents or exertion. There stood the throne, splendid in its carvings and ready for completion. The lower part stood more than two man-heights above the ground; no stone of its courses weighed less than twenty men. The upper part was double the weight of any of these, carved so that a royal snake encircled the chair, its great hooded head overshadowing it. At present, however, the upper part was not on its bed, held high by lifting rams, for what purpose all men knew.

It was to face this scene that I emerged from the royal pyramid at the summons of the chamberlains in the cool of the next morning. Each great man who had come before me was accompanied by banner-bearers and trumpeters; the middle classes wore all their bravery of apparel; and even poor, squalid creatures, with ribs of hunger showing through their dusty skins, had turbans and wisps of color wrapped about their heads to mark the gaiety of the day.

One could hear the people draw a deep breath of delight as their eyes first fell upon her; it was easy to believe there was not a man in that throng who did not envy me her choice, nor was there a soul present—unless Ylga was there somewhere veiled—who could imagine that I was not overjoyed in winning so lovely a wife.

For myself, I summoned all the iron of my training to guard the expression of my face. We were here for ceremony; a ghastly enough affair throughout its acts, but still ceremonial. I was minded to show Phorenice a grand manner that would leave her nothing to cavil at. "And true. She spurns you, divorces you, and now she seeks vengeance. She knows you shared your priest's magic with Nais, keeping her in a stone coffin until you choose to wake her. Her enlightenment came from a prisoner in a neighboring cell who overheard your vows. That fool tried to bargain for his life, but Phorenice had him executed for his tardiness. Now, she intends to mutilate you—lop off your hands and feet—and drive you into the wild lands to die a slow death of hunger and beasts, all while mocking you with the knowledge that she holds Nais captive."

"No," I said firmly. "I will not be mutilated and left to starve."

"Then you must flee," Ylga urged. "I have arranged passage with Tob, the captain of Tatho's navy. He sails tonight for unknown parts, seeking to found a kingdom. He is a rough man, smelling of tar and honesty, but he needs a leader. He said you were the recruit he wanted. Go with him. It is your only chance."

We moved through the darkness, leaving the camp behind. The journey was arduous, traversing soggy ground and thorny forests. At one point, we crossed a stream of scalding water from a boil-spring. "Are you sure of your footing?" I asked, offering my hand.

"No," she replied, taking my grip. "I am not."

Together, we navigated the treacherous crossing. Once across, she slipped her hand away, sighing softly. We walked in silence for hours until we reached the estuary where Atlantis stood. Below us, the great ship anchored in the shadows waited. Tob was there, watching intently.

We boarded the vessel, and Tob greeted us with a mix of respect and practicality. He explained his plans: a voyage to the Tin Islands, a land rich in resources but distant from the reach of the city. He had gathered a crew of fifty men and eight women, escaping the tightening grip of the port authorities. As we prepared to depart, the tension of the night settled over us.

"I will never forget what you have done," I told Ylga as we stood on the deck. "Should the High Gods allow me to return to power, you shall know my gratitude."

"I do not wish to return," she said, her eyes hard. "I am sick of this life."

"But you have wealth, a palace, and status," I argued. "Phorenice will not disturb you."

"Oh, I could be a fan-girl tomorrow if I chose," she snapped. "But I do not want to go back. I can cook, shoot, and sew skins. Do not presume I am useless because you despise fine clothes."

"I have learned all I care to know about one woman," I admitted, the memory of Nais weighing heavy on my heart. "Because of her, I could not bring you."

"Kick my pride," she whispered bitterly. "I knew I was second place. Yet, no one but a boor would remind me."

"I must go alone," I said. "I suppose I shall carry only your hate."

"That is the most humiliating thing," she confessed, her voice trembling. "I cannot hate you. I seem to grow fonder of you. If I thought I could save you here, I would not have pointed you to Tob. But he waits."

Suddenly, she flung her arms around my neck and kissed me with a fierce, reckless passion. "There, that is for goodbye, dear. You see, I am reckless. I care not what I do now, knowing you cannot despise me more than you have."

She ran back into the trees, disappearing into the darkness. I watched her go, then turned to the ship. Tob signaled the crew to haul up the anchor.

"Greeting, my lord," Tob said, his tone shifting to business. "We have little force and a slow ship. The sooner we are beyond the city's range, the safer my family is. This vessel is old, rotting from the Egypt trade, but she is all I have."

"We have two months' provisions," I noted. "We will have to hunt along the coast."

"Yes," Tob agreed. "When winter comes, we will make camp and grow grain. But listen, my lord. I have a destination in mind. Islands to the north of Europe, separated by a strait. I call them the Tin Islands. They are untouched, rich in ore, and free of the old gods' influence. I intend to build a kingdom there."

"With you as king?" I asked.

"Well, I have figured it out as such," Tob grinned, though his eyes held a hint of deference. "But I know when I meet my better. I am content to serve under Deucalion. Though, I must say, a wife would have been wise to bring. The savages there are sturdy but brutish, and their customs... well, one would need a blunted taste to wish to father their children."

"I am still husband to Phorenice," I reminded him.

"The Gods give you joy of her," Tob chuckled. "But a mariner's creed says wedlock does not hold across the seas. Let that rest. The Tin Islands will delight you. Few beasts, plenty of game. It will be a desirable kingdom."

As dawn broke, the burning mountains of Atlantis loomed behind us. The crew cast fearful glances backward, expecting the navy of Phorenice to surge forth. They swore they would fight to the death rather than submit. But the tide turned, and we drifted away, the city fading into the mist.

On the third day, we stopped at a river mouth to resupply. We speared fish and hunted a young river-horse, curing the meat for the journey ahead. Tob pressed me again to join him as king, but I refused steadfastly.

"Tob," I said, standing alone on the bank as his ship prepared to leave, "I am a poor, defenseless man. What if I do not voyage to the Tin Islands? What if I stay here?"

He brightened. "You are weak, my lord. But tell me your meaning."

"It is not my wish to leave Atlantis," I said. "Put me down on any shore facing Europe, and I will commend you to the Gods. I have no money, but I offer my counsel."

Tob hesitated. The sound of kingship appealed to him, but he knew I was no sailor. "The part of Atlantis you speak of is the Dangerous Lands," he warned. "A man must be a salamander or learned in magic to survive there."

"We of the Priests' Clan have education," I countered. "My fate remains under the control of the High Gods."

He muttered something about preferring his own fate to his own right arm, but the temptation of rule was too strong. On the third day, he returned to the bank, but this time, his resolve was absolute.

"Tob," I pleaded as he stood by the ship, "this must not be."

The ship drifted away with the current, sails hoisting. Tob shouted over the roar of the water. "Aye, but it shall be! I am a strong man, and I have ordered it so. If you want the name of our Hero, wear it on yourself. Deucalion shall be God for our children!"

"This is blasphemy!" I cried. "Have a care, fool, or this impiety will sink you!"

"We will risk it!" he bellowed back. "Regard! Here is thy last horn of wine. I pour this as a libation to Deucalion, great lord that is to-day, Hero that shall be to-morrow, God that will be in time to come!"

The crew joined the acclaim, their voices rising above the crashing waves as they battled the breakers of the bar. I stood solitary at the brink of the forest, watching them vanish over the horizon. Tob, a man of low station, had forsaken the ancient gods to elevate a mortal who had momentarily caught his fancy. It was a desolating shock. If such a man could abandon the old ways, what of the mob swayed by Phorenice's rhetoric? My exile had begun with a new, profound dreariness.

Then, behind me, the rustle of a great beast signaled an attack. I had no time to mourn the loss of my homeland or the betrayal of the old faith. I had to survive the immediate threat, letting Tob and his new god-driven ship disappear into the vast, indifferent sea.

From the moment man was first shaped by the hands of the Gods, there have been corners of the earth unfit for his keeping. None are more hostile than the stretch of Atlantis facing the savage coasts of Europe and Africa. The common folk shun this place, driven by a superstition that the spirits of the wicked dead wander its daylight hours, slaughtering anyone the wilder dangers might spare. It is a grim irony that many criminals, fleeing justice, have chosen to surrender voluntarily to the tormentors rather than face the region's fabulous terrors.

To the educated, many of these tales are known to be myths; yet enough unease remains to make existence here a gauntlet of peril. The mountains are perpetually feverish, their contents boiling; earthquakes are a daily occurrence. Invisible, malevolent vapors creep with cunning speed, often choking victims before they can flee. Rivers rise with poison that leaves their color unchanged, while great fissures tear across the ground, drawing water down to the fires below only to shoot it back forth in devastating explosions. Boiling mud and molten rock are constant threats.

Yet amidst this chaos, sombre forests thrive, populated by trees of unimaginable age where fires among the herbage are rare. Beneath the canopy lies water, and the air hangs heavy with warm steam and wetness. To live in such constant, suffocating damp is mortifying to the human frame. Strength and cunning beyond the ordinary are required to survive, for these lands are the domain of lizards—the most enormous and fearsome beasts of all.

Countless families and species of these reptiles exist. With some, a man might contend with a prospect of success; with others, whose hugeness defies human force, survival is impossible. I witnessed one emerge from a lake after feeding, causing the wet land to shake and pulse with its tread. It could have swallowed Phorenice's mammoth whole, and even a charging beast could not have harmed it. Great horny plates armored its head and body, while spines along its back, tail, and limbs tore great slivers from the black trees as it moved.

Occasionally, these monsters become trapped in vast ground fissures, but rarely. Their speed is great, and their sagacity keen. They seem to sense the worst boilings of the mountains and seek safety in deeper lakes or muddy wallows. Moreover, they are better constituted than man to withstand the region's greatest danger: the heat of water harms them not. They lie peacefully in pools where steam bursts send water leaping into boiling fountains, and I have seen one run swiftly across a flow of molten rock, emerging not singed.

Thrown into such a neighborhood, my new life became perilous and hard from the outset. I came near to knowing the true nature of Fear. Only a fervent trust in the Most High Gods, and a firm belief that my life was under Their fostering care, prevented me from succumbing to that horrid knowledge. For long enough, until I learned the ways of this steaming, sweltering land, I was in as miserable a state as Phorenice could have wished to see me. My clothes rotted from my back with the constant wetness until I was as naked as a savage from Europe. My limbs were racked with agues, and I found no herbs to relieve them. For days, my only sustenance was tree-grubs and leaves; often, when I killed beasts knowing little of their qualities, I ate those that brought me pain and sickness.

But as man is born to adapt to his surroundings, so did I, as the months dragged on, learn the limitations of this new life and gather knowledge of its resources. I found a great black tree with a hollow core and a hole near its roots. There I harbored until one night a monstrous lizard, whose sheer weight made the tree rock like a sapling, endeavored to suck me forth as a bird picks a worm from a hollow log. I escaped by the will of the Gods—I could as easily have harmed a mountain as injure that horny tongue with my weapons—but I gave myself warning that such a chance must never happen again.

So I cut a ladder of footholes inside the trunk, ascending to a point ten man-heights from the ground. There I carved other notches and built a floor of tree branches to rest upon. Later, for luxury, I carved arrow-slit windows into the chamber walls and carried up sand for a hearth, allowing me to cook my victuals high above the dangers of the open below.

By degrees, I began to find how the large-scaled fish of the rivers and lesser turtles might be captured, so my ribs threatened less to start through their skin. But the lack of salads and gruels I could never overcome. All green meat was tainted with the taste of tar, a flavor I could never force my palate to accept. Of course, the peril of the greater lizards and other native dangers remained.

As the months mounted into years, and the brute part of my nature became more satisfied, other longings arose that were harder to provide for. From the ivory of a river horse's tooth, I had endeavored to carve a representative of Nais as I last saw her. Though my fingers were loving and my will good, my art was dull, and the result—despite repeated attempts—was always clumsy and pitiful. Still, in my eyes, it carried some suggestion of the original—a curve here, an outline there—and it made my old love glow anew within me as I sat and gazed upon it. Yet it did little to satisfy my longing for the woman I had lost; rather, it whetted my cravings to be with her again, or at least to know her fate.

Other men of the Priests' Clan have come out and made an abode in these Dangerous Lands, mortifying their flesh to gain intimacy with the Higher Mysteries, carrying them far past what mere human learning could teach. Some who have returned to the abodes of men have brought knowledge that earns them a reputation for magic and miracles among the vulgar, adding materially to the ardor with which common people pursue the cult of the Gods. But for myself, I could not free my mind to the necessary clearness for such abstruse studies. During my voyage home from Yucatan, I had communed with them with growing insight; but now my mind was not my own. Nais held a lien upon it, refusing to be ousted. In truth, her sweet trespass was my chief solace.

At last, my longing could no longer be denied. Through one of the arrow-slits of my tree-house, I could see far away a great mountain top whitened with perpetual snow, which our Lord the Sun dyed with blood every night of His setting. Night after night, I watched that ruddy light with wide, straining eyes. Night after night, I remembered that in days gone by, when I entered the priesthood, it had been my duty to adore our great Lord as He rose for His day behind the snows of that very mountain. Always, the thought followed: from that distant crest, I could see across the continent to the Sacred Mount, where the city lay below, and where I had buried my love alive.

So at last, I gave way and set out. A perilous journey it proved. In the heavy mists that hung always on the lower ground, my way lay blind before me, and I constantly lost it. To say I traversed three times the direct distance is a low estimate. Throughout the swamps, the great lizards hunted, and as the country was new to me, I knew no places of harbor. A hundred times I was within an ace of being spotted and devoured at a single mouthful. But the High Gods still desired me for Their own purposes, blinding the great beasts' eyes as I slunk to cover. Twice, rivers of scalding water roared boiling across my path, forcing me to delay until I could collect enough black timber to build rafts for dry ferriage.

The journey was infinitely tedious, yet to me, after those years of waiting, time passed on winged feet. I had been separated from my love until I could bear the strain no longer. Let me but see from a distance the place where she lay, feast my eyes upon it for a while, and then I could return to my abode in the tree and wait patiently for the will of the Gods.

The air grew chillier as I emerged above the region of trees onto higher ground that glares down on the rest of the world. I made buskins and a coat of woven grasses to protect my body from the keen wind. Later, where the snow lay eternally, blown into gullies and frozen into solid banks and bergs of ice, I had hard work making progress among its perilous mazes. The chill numbed me, weakening my natural strength. Overhead, following me with forbidding swoops and occasionally coming so close I had to threaten them with my weapons, were huge man-eating birds that live by pulling down any creature their instincts deem weak and likely to die soon.

But the lure ahead was strong enough to make these difficulties seem small. Though the mountain air disagreed with me, causing sickness and panting, I pressed on with all the speed I could muster toward the elusive summit. Time after time, I thought the next spur would surely bring me to the view my soul yearned for, but always there seemed another bank of snow and ice to climb.

Finally, I reached the crest and gave thanks to the Most High Gods for Their protection and favor. Far, far away, I could see the Sacred Mountain with its ring of fires burning pale under the day. Although the splendid city nesting at its foot could not be seen from where I stood, I knew its position and its plan. My soul went out to that throne of granite in the square before the royal pyramid, where once, years before, I had buried my love. Had Phorenice left the tomb unviolated?

I stood there leaning on my spear, filling my eye with the prospect, warming even to the smoke of mountains I recognized as old acquaintances. Gods! How my love burned within me for this woman. My whole being seemed gone out to meet her, leaving no room for anything beside. For a long time, a voice seemed dimly to call me, but I gave it no regard. I had come to that hoary mountain top for communion with Nais alone, wanting no others to interrupt.

But at length, the voice calling my name grew too loud to be neglected. I pulled myself out of my sweet musing with a start, realizing that here, for the first time since parting with Tob and his company, I should see another human being. I gripped my weapon and asked who called. The reply came clearly from up the slopes, and I saw a man coming toward me over the snows. He was old and feeble, his body bent, his hair and beard white as the ground beneath him. Presently, I recognized him as Zaemon. He came with incredible speed for a man of his years and feebleness, carrying in his hand the glowing Symbol of our Lord the Sun, from which holy strength would greatly augment his powers.

He came close and made the sign of the Seven, which I returned to him with due form and ceremony. Then he saluted me as a messenger appointed by the High Council of the Priests seated before the Ark of the Mysteries, and I made humble obeisance.

"In all things I will obey the orders you put before me," I said.

"Such is your duty, my brother. The command is that you return immediately to the Sacred Mountain. If human means may still prevail, you, as the most skilled general Atlantis owns within her borders, may still save the country from final wreck and punishment. The woman Phorenice persists in her infamies. The poor land groans under her heel. Now she has laid siege to our Sacred Mountain itself, swearing that not one soul shall be left alive in all Atlantis who does not bend humbly to her will."

"It is a command, and I obey it. But let me ask of another matter intimate to both of us. What of Nais?"

"Nais rests where you left her, untouched. Phorenice knows by her arts—she has stolen nearly all the ancient knowledge—that you still live. She keeps Nais unharmed beneath the granite throne in the hopes that some time she may use her as a weapon against you. Little does she know the sternness of our Priests' creed, my brother. Why, even I, that am the girl's father, would sacrifice her blithely if her death or ruin might do a tittle of good to Atlantis."

"You go beyond me with your devotion."

The old man leaned forward, his brow glowering. "What!"

"Or my old blind adherence to the ancient dogma has been sapped and weakened by events. You must buy my full obedience, Zaemon, if you want it. Promise me Nais—and your arts I know can snatch her—and I will be a true servant to the High Council of the Priests, dying in the last ditch if need be to carry out order. Zaemon looked at me bitterly. "And you are the man the High Council thought to trust as they would trust one of themselves? Truly we are in an age of weak men and faithless ones. My trek across the vast, haunted continent of Atlantis was fraught with peril, yet I need not recount every shadowed mile. There was but one halt of consequence, forced by necessity. One day, I had tracked and slain a stag in the open savannah, its breath heavy in the air. I scanned the horizon for lurking beasts, satisfied that the clearing was safe, and began to butcher the carcass, bundling the meat for the journey ahead.

Beneath the tall grass, however, waited a monstrous green worm, a many-legged horror with venom sharp enough to dissolve bone. As I moved, my heel brushed it. Like a dart of light, it struck, its tiny head snapping forward to drive fangs deep into my lower thigh. With my knife, I severed its neck; the creature writhed, twisting its hundred legs in a grotesque dance before dying. I cleaned my blade on the earth and drove it deep around the wound, forcing the blood to flow freely, washing the poison from my flesh. With the crimson stream trickling down my leg, I hoisted the meat and strode on, grateful to have escaped what could have been a gruesome end.

Yet, as I walked, a tightness seized my leg, a throbbing that grew hourly until the skin stretched taut like parchment. A sickness racked my body, violent and consuming; had a great beast found me then, I would have been devoured without a struggle. As darkness fell, I dragged myself into the crook of a tree, enduring wakefulness and agony through the night.

At dawn, when the night predators retreated to their dens, I climbed down, leaning heavily on my spear. I limped through the sombre forests, guided by the moss on the northern sides of the trees. But soon, I noticed moss growing on all sides, and my head grew light with pain and swelling. Still, I pressed on, driven by the High Council's command to secure the enlargement of Nais.

My last conscious memory was of being met in the black forest by a stranger who aided me, after which my senses fled into oblivion.

When I awoke, I lay on a bed of leaves in a rocky cleft, sheltered by stakes and crude fortifications against marauding beasts. My wound was poulticed with herbs, and squatting by a fire of sticks at the cavern's edge was a woman cooking a stew of wood-grubs and honey.

"How came I here?" I asked.

"I brought you," she replied.

"And who are you?"

"A nymph," she said. "I collect herbs, cure the sick, tell fortunes, and make predictions. In return, I accept what I can get. If they cannot pay, I curse them to wither. It is a lean existence when war and pestilence have thinned the land."

"Do you visit Atlantis?"

"Not I. Phorenice would boil me in brine if she could catch me. Our Empress tolerates no magic but her own. They say she is tearing the Priests from their Mountain now."

"Does she still reign?"

"Most vilely."

"As a maiden?"

"As the mother of twin sons. Tatho is her husband now, and has been for three years."

"Tatho! The viceroy of Yucatan?"

"There is no Yucatan," she corrected. "A vast nation of little hairy men from the West overran the country. Armed only with stone-tipped clubs, their numbers were their weapon. They desired no plunder or slaves, only the flesh of Atlanteans. They came like bees in a swarm, eating each city clean before moving on. Tatho and the few survivors sailed home, leaving their wives to be eaten by the horde. They are curious, persevering things. The Gods surely do not allow them to cross the sea to Atlantis, or they would be a plague worse than Phorenice."

I recalled tales of these creatures—beings between man and beast, held in check by the Gods, released only as a devastating army when punishment was needed. I said nothing to the nymph, deeming her a gullible commoner, though my heart bled for the fate of the Yucatan colony where I had labored so long.

"It does not suit me to have my name blazoned abroad yet," I lied, steering the conversation elsewhere. "What of Egypt?"

"In its accustomed darkness," she sighed. "Who cares for Egypt these days? Or anything but oneself? Once, folk brought offerings out of piety. Now, they only come for profit. It is a poor life for a nymph in these times; the fashion is to believe in no mysteries."

"What pestilence did you speak of?"

"I have not seen it. Thank the Gods it has not reached this way. They say it grows from the bodies Phorenice has left unburied. She slays so many that the dead lie thicker than beasts can consume them. For what sin did the Gods let her rise? I wish she and her twins were boiled alive before they crossed my path."

"She has placed images of herself in all the temples," she continued, offering me the grub-stew. "She orders prayers to her alone, declaring other gods inferior. Those insufficiently devout have their hamstrings slit by tormentors to force devotion. Will you eat? You carried meat when I found you, but now you are empty."

I stayed with the nymph for six days, her charms healing my leg. Upon recovery, I hunted a fat horse in the plains as payment and departed.

The land beyond had once thrived. Houses perched in trees or built of massive, dovetailed logs offered safety from cave-bears and tigers. Yet, not one in ten of these settlements remained. Fires had gone cold, vegetable patches reclaimed by scrub, and village gates blocked by rotting logs. Phorenice's influence spread like a blight over what was once a smiling land.

Men I encountered mostly fled into the forest at my approach. Some threatened but did not attack, sensing my lack of malice. A few offered shelter, but as I neared the city, passing through denser populations, the danger of capture grew. Watchers in towers would cry, "Here is a masterless man!" and mobs would rush to strip me of my weapons and conscript me as labor.

Filled with pity for their fallen state, I often used words to dissuade them, striking lightly only when necessary to keep moving. I avoided villages entirely, circling through the forest to escape their gaze. Similarly, I kept beyond the walls of greater towns, unwilling to risk delay under new, arbitrary laws. My progress was slow, but my pride burned hot, fueled by a fever of haste.

Finally, I found myself within a half-day's march of Atlantis itself. The Sacred Mountain loomed high, its ring of eternal fires burning beside the city. Caution became paramount. Everywhere, evidence showed the fighting men had been drained away. Women prayed for the rout of the Priests or the death of Phorenice, hoping for peace.

An army besieged the Sacred Mountain, guarding its single approach with deadly precision. After my long journey, I feared I would fail the High Council's orders. Force was useless; I would face one glorious fight against an army, ending my usefulness. No. This required guile. I hid in the outskirts of a wood, my mind churning for a plan.

Before me lay the grim city walls, temples, palaces, and pyramids. The royal pyramid's steps, overlaid with Phorenice's gold, caught my eye. But it was not the gold that moved me. In the square before the pyramid stood a granite throne carved with a snake and an outstretched hand. Beneath it slept Nais, my love, drugged and unconscious for eight years, yet alive, waiting for me. I must reach the place where Zaemon defied the Empress and announce my arrival.

I lay in the woods for a day and night, raging at my lack of a plan. Then, in the morning, a man ran toward me.

"You need not threaten me with your weapons!" he cried. "I mean no harm. You are Deucalion, though I should not know you in these rags and tangled beard. You will give me your good word. I have not loitered."

He was Ro, a lower priest I knew and little esteemed—a greedy man unworthy of trust.

"From whom do you come?" I asked.

"Zaemon commanded me. He came to my house, though how he bypassed Phorenice's blockade, I cannot tell. I told him I wished no part of his schemes. I am a peaceful man, Deucalion, with a wife who needs nourishment. I still serve the temple, though we have swept out the old gods for the Empress's image. The people are pious now, and living is easy. Offerings are plentiful. I had no wish to risk my snug post or my head."

"I can believe all that, Ro."

"But Zaemon could not be denied. He burst into a black fury. While he spoke, I felt as good as dead. You know his powers?"

"I have seen some."

It is dead and paralyzed from the wrist, a gift from Zaemon to 'collect my attention.' He spoke hard things of my apostasy, refusing my defense that the change was forced. He gave me a choice: obey his bidding exactly, or suffer a paralysis that would lead to my death in some gutter."

"With a wife depending on me, what choice did I have? I asked his pleasure. He said I must find you when you arrive from the distant lands and deliver his message."

"'Where is the meeting place?' I asked.

'There is none appointed,' he said. 'Watch and search always. When he comes, you will be guided.'"

Ro explained that he believed he had been guided to find me. "If there is gratitude, restore my hand," he pleaded. "It is awful to live like this. Zaemon could cure it from afar."

"You seem to have faith in the old priests," I noted. "But your errand is incomplete. I have received no message."

"The message is simple. Make a great blaze. Fire the grasses before this wood if you choose. On the following night, go to the flank of the Sacred Mountain where the rocks drop sheer. They will lower a rope and haul you up."

"It seems easy. I will ask Zaemon to restore your hand."

"You shall have my prayers. And look, Deucalion, it is a small matter. Better to do it now on landing. Later, you may be disturbed. Phorenice will pull you down once she sets her mind to it. She never fails. She has all the ancient learning of our Priests, plus more. She has discovered the Secret of Life these recent months—"

"She has found that?" I cried, startled. "How? Only the Three know that. It is beyond our knowledge even who are members of the Seven."

"I know nothing of her means. But she has the secret. She is as good an immortal as any of them."

Ro hurried off, asking for a small coin or link of bronze for his wife's food. I gave him nothing, not wishing to burden myself further.

Almost before Ro's heels cleared the grass, I lit the blaze. That night, under the cover of darkness, I ventured to the appointed spot. No rope dangled, but soon one descended the smooth cliff like a slender snake. I made a loop, slipped it over my leg, and pulled. Those above hauled, and I ascended the Sacred Mountain after years of toil and war. None disturbed the ascent; Phorenice's troops ignored the gaunt, seamless precipice.

The men hauling me up were old and panting, puzzling why younger priests were not assigned. I asked no questions. Among the Priest's Clan, it is assumed orders are given for the best. They took me directly to Zaemon.

The old man greeted me with the royal sign. "All hail to Deucalion," he cried, "King of Atlantis, duly called by the High Council."

"Is Phorenice dead?" I asked.

Farther and farther the great stone slid out, its slowness driving me mad. A life's education left me in that moment; I possessed no stately patience. In my puny fury, I thrust at the great block with my shoulder and head, clawing at it until my muscles rose in great ropes and knots. The High Gods must have laughed at my helplessness. Finally, when the city was numb with terror, the threatened event occurred. The capstone slid out to its balance, and the next shock threw it with a roar and a clatter to the ground. Then, a strange tremor seized me.

After all the scheming and effort, what I had prayed for had come about. Had she left Nais to lie in peace? Stolen her away to suffer indignities? Or ended her sleep with death, leaving a corpse as a grisly jest? I dared not guess. Atlantis, the great continent beyond, and all within it are doomed to unutterable destruction. Of old, it was foreseen that this wiping-out would come through the sins of men, and to this end, the Ark of the Mysteries was built under the Gods' direction. No mortal implement can scratch its surface; no wave or rock can wreck it. Inside are stored on sheets of ancient writing all the world's learning not shared by the common people, plus grain sufficient for two persons for four years, sweet water, seeds, weapons, and all other matters deemed fit."

He paused, his voice heavy with destiny. "Of all this vast country, the High Gods have decreed that two shall not perish. Two shall be chosen—a man and a woman—fit and proper to carry away the ancient learning and rear a new race to honor our Lord the Sun and the other Gods elsewhere. The woman is already within the Ark, seated in her appointed place. Though she is my daughter, the burden of her choice rests with you. As for the man, the choice has fallen upon yourself."

I was half-numb with shock. "I do not know that I care to be a survivor."

"You are not asked for your wishes," the old man said firmly. "You are given an order from the Highest Gods, who know you to be Their faithful servant."

Habit rode strong upon me. I made the required salutation and declared that I heard and would obey.

"Then it remains to raise you to the sublime degree of the Three. If your learning is so small that you cannot understand the keys to many of the Powers and the highest Mysteries when they are handed to you, that fault cannot be remedied now."

Time was short. The fight still raged at the gate, a wonder how the handful of priests held their ground. But the ocean rolled in with increasing fury, the mountains smoked and belched volleys of rock as the weight increased on their lower parts. Soon, the besiegers would see the fate threatening them and rush forward in mad fury, sweeping all obstruction before them.

Yet Zaemon and his fellow sage showed no haste. They were without temple, without sacrifice or incense, having but the sky for a roof and the Gods themselves for witnesses. Still, they performed the raising of me to their degree with all the grand, majestic form that had gathered dignity from the ages, curtailing not a single sentence. A burning mountain burst with a bellowing roar as incoming waters met its fires, but they continued gravely, reciting their sentences. Phorenice's troops broke the last resistance, pouring in a frenzied stream among the groves and temples, yet they never faltered in the ritual.

It was said that this ceremony was the grandest of our holy religion, and I found it so. Even the tremendous circumstances hemming them in could not make these frail old men forget the deference due to the highest order of the Clan.

For myself, I owned I was less rapt. Standing bareheaded in the heat, I tried to concentrate, yet was torn by a thousand foreign emotions. The awful destruction unfolding around me demanded attention; a continent was meeting its end. If Zaemon could give his mind wholly to parochial priesthood matters, I was not so stoical. Moreover, my thoughts were filled with anxieties concerning Nais. Yet I preserved a decent show of attention, making all required responses and striving to memorize the sentences that were keys to power and learning, not merely phrasings of grandeur.

It became clear that if the ceremony did not soon conclude, the further plan would fail, and I would meet my death by fighting to a finish, as I had feared. My flickering attention saw soldiers coming nearer in their frantic wanderings and the sea rolling deeper into the land.

The fires ringing half the mountain spurted to double their height, burning with an unceasing roar. Yet, despite these distractions, the two old priests continued their work as if in the quiet of an ancient temple.

At last, the ceremony ended. "Kneel," cried Zaemon, "and make obeisance to your mother the Earth, and swear by the Highest Gods that you will never make improper use of the powers over Her which you have been granted today."

When I had done, he bade me rise as a fully installed member of the Three. "You will have no opportunity to practice the workings of this degree with either of us, my brother," he said. "Presently, our other brother and I go to stand before the Gods to deliver an account of our trust. What items you remember may turn of use to you hereafter. Now we give you our farewells and promise to commend you highly to the Gods when we meet Them behind the stars. Climb into the Ark and be ready to shut the door guarding it if these raging people attempt to invade. Remember, my brother, it is the Gods' direct will that you and the woman Nais go from this place living and sound. You are expressly forbidden to accept challenge or provocation to fight on any pretext. But as long as it can be done safely, you may look out upon Atlantis in her death-throes. It is fitting that one of the only two sent hence alive should carry the full tale of what has befallen."

I climbed to the top of the Ark via the battens, then descended the internal stair to find Nais waiting in a small chamber.

"I was bidden stay here by Zaemon," she said, her voice trembling. "He forced me to this place by threats and promises that my lord would follow. He is very ungentle, that father of mine, but I think he has a kindness for us both. Is there no chance to save him from what is going to happen?"

"He will not come into this Ark, for I asked him," I replied. "It has been ordained since the Ark's construction that when its purpose comes, only one woman and one man shall be its tenants. They are here already. Zaemon's will cannot be twisted. He has a message to deliver to the Gods, and (if I know him at all), he grudges every minute lost."

I left her and climbed back up to the roof. On the mountain top, men still ran distractedly, but they were gradually converging on where the Ark rested. For the moment, I passed them lightly. The drowning of the great continent spread out below filled my vision. The ocean roared in with furious waves; the plains and level lands were foaming lakes. The great city of Atlantis had vanished eternally. Only the mountains kept their heads above the flood, spewing rocks, steam, and boiling stone, or bursting when waters reached them, creating whirlpools of surging sea and bubbling mud.

In a few breaths, every living creature in the lower grounds was smothered, save for a few huddled in oarless galleys driven inland over what had once been black forest. Even as I watched, these were swallowed by the horrid turmoil. None but those who found standing ground on the upper slopes of the Sacred Mountain survived, and their span was short as the great mass of the continent sank deeper beneath the boiling inrush of the seas.

Though the soldiery were dazed and maddened, some possessed a valiant strength of mind. Presently, a compact body of people emerged from the houses and temples, heading directly toward the Ark. Marching on the outside were Phorenice's personal guards, weapons newly blooded. They had been forced to fight their way through their own fellow soldiers. The poor demented creatures had thought it every man for himself, until these guards, by their mistress's order, proved that Phorenice still came first.

In the middle of them, borne in a litter of gold and ivory by grotesque European slaves, rode the Empress. She was still calm, still lovely, seemingly divided between contempt and amusement. Her two children lay at her feet. On her right marched Tatho, gorgeously apparelled with a beard curled and plaited into a thousand ringlets. On the other side, walking with unruffled defense, was Ylga, once again a fan-girl and second lady in this dwindling kingdom.

The party halted half a score of paces from the Ark by Phorenice's order. "Do not go nearer to those unclean old men. They carry a rank odour, and for the moment we are short of essences to sweeten the air of their neighborhood." She lifted her eyebrows and looked up at me. The Ark was a vessel of silence and surrender. Stripped of rudders, oars, and engines, it drifted solely at the whim of the High Gods. Inside, darkness pressed against the hull, thick with the scent of antiquity and the low, rhythmic groans of the survivors. I could not locate the fire-box to reignite our lamps, so we were forced to navigate the void in blindness, accepting whatever fate the waves might deal. The sea tossed us in a merciless game, yet I clung tightly to Nais, keeping her anchored to the bed. Words were scarce between us, but in our shared grief and our heavy silence, there existed a profound companionship.

When Atlantis finally succumbed to the depths, she left behind great whirlpools and spouts of drowned fire—a fleeting testament to the Sea Gods. I believe, though the black belly of the Ark hid such sights from view, that a vast hurricane followed immediately after, ensuring no fragment of the desolation remained untouched. This dreadful turmoil raged for seven nights and seven days, as later confirmed by the hour-reckoner hanging within the Ark. Then, the howling wind ceased, leaving only the roll of a long, still swell. It was a regular, oily motion, distinct from the chaos before. Only then did I dare ascend the stairs to open the hatch in the roof.

Sweet air gushed down, washing away the foulness within. As the Ark rode dryly over the seas, I went below to bring Nais up, seeking refreshment from the curing rays of our Lord, the Sun. Together, we bowed in adoration, giving thanks for His mercy in granting another day of light. We lay down to doze, seeking the easy rest we so desperately needed.

Yet, exhaustion did not bring sleep. For a long time, I stared wearily at the oily, sunlit waters. No blur of land met my gaze; the ring of ocean was unbroken on every side, and the vault of heaven remained unchanged. The bosom of the deep was littered with the wreckage of Atlantis, serving as a grim reminder that this was fact, not a nightmare. Trees, squared timber, a smashed hide-boat, and here and there the rounded corpse of a man or beast shouldered over the swells, keeping convoy with our Ark as it drifted under the guidance of the Gods and the current.

Finally, sleep claimed me. I dropped into unconsciousness, holding Nais's hand, and when I awoke, she was open-eyed, watching me with tender concern. Both of us were finely rested; rest brings a strange complacency. We were now more ready to accept the station the High Gods had ordained for us without repining. We descended once more into the Ark's belly to eat and drink, fortifying ourselves for the new life awaiting us.

Now, able to examine it at leisure, the Ark revealed itself as a wonder. Though it was the first time in its centuries of existence that it swam freely upon the waters, it showed not a single leak or crack. Inside, even the floor was bone-dry. While the grain indicated it was built of wood, no suture or joint was visible. The living timbers had been placed and then grown together by an art we have since lost, known only to the Ancients with perfection. Subsequently, a treatment—another secret of those forgotten builders—had rendered the wood as hard as metal and impervious to the weather.

In the gloomy cave of its belly, provisions were stored. At one end, in great tanks flanking a central alley, lay a prodigious store of grain. Sweet water filled tanks at the opposite end. Elsewhere were drugs, samples, and essences of beast-life, all intended for use while the Ark roamed under divine guidance. Carved into every wall, partition, and tank in the rude art of bygone times were representations of every beast that lived in Atlantis. I studied them with a hunter's interest, noting creatures strange to me that had perished along with the men who had preserved them in stone. A good store of weapons and handicraft tools also lined the walls.

For many weeks, our life endured in this Ark as the Gods drove it across the face of the waters. We possessed no government to direct our course; we could not increase our speed by so much as a hair's breadth. The High Gods, who had chosen the two of us as the sole survivors of Atlantis, held sole control of our fate. Into Their hands, we cheerfully resigned our future.

Of the land we eventually reached, where we made our abiding place and where our children were born, I shall speak in its proper time. But since this chronicle must proceed in the exact order of events, I must first narrate how we came by the sheets upon which it is written.

In a great coffer at the center of the Ark's floor, the Mysteries learned over ages were set down in accurate writing. I read through portions of them during the passing days, and the awfulness of the Powers they described appalled me. I had witnessed some of these Powers unleashed in Atlantis, bearing witness to her destruction. But here were Powers far higher still; here was the Great Secret of Life and Death which Phorenice had discovered and for which she had been destroyed. There were other things of which I cannot even attempt to scribe.

The thought of being custodian to these writings became more than I could endure. The longer the matter rested in my mind, the more intolerable the burden grew. Finally, I took hot irons and seared the wax on the sheets until every letter of the old writings was obliterated. If I acted wrongly, the High Gods, in Their infinite justice, will punish me; if it is well that these great secrets should endure on earth, They, in Their infinite power, will dictate them afresh to some fitting scribes. Yet, I destroyed them there as the Ark swayed over the waves. Later, when we came to land, I rewrote upon the sheets only the matters leading to the great Atlantis being dragged to her death-throes.
