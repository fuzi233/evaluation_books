Outside the cavern mouth we stopped short in the hard, clean glare, and for a moment the four of us stood blinking like men who have forgotten how to be ashamed.

“I am going back,” Sir Henry said abruptly.

Good gave him a sharp look. “Back? For what?”

Sir Henry’s voice thickened with a thought he had tried not to feed. “Because it has struck me that—what we saw—may be my brother.”

That fear, once spoken, took hold of us all. We turned and went in again.

The cave swallowed the sunlight at a step. After the dazzle of snow our eyes were useless; the dark lay on us like a hand over the face, and the cold in there had a different smell from the cold outside—old, shut-up, airless; a frost that had never been warmed by breath or flame. We edged forward, hearing only the faint rasp of our own boots and, beneath it, the deep stillness that seemed to have weight.

Presently shapes came back to our sight, and we stood again before the dead man.

Sir Henry went down on his knee, close, close, as if the truth would fly from him unless he pinned it with his eyes. He studied the sunken face, the hollowed temples, the stiff yellow skin stretched drum-tight over bone.

“Thank God,” he said, and the breath left him in a shuddering sigh, “it is not my brother.”

I crouched and looked also. The corpse was that of a tall man in middle age, aquiline nose, grizzled hair, a long black moustache like a frozen brush. The skin had the waxy yellow of old tallow; it clung to the cheekbones and jaw as if it had been glued there by the cold. The man’s clothes had been stripped away, save for what might once have been woollen hose; his body lay naked and stark. Round the stiff neck hung a yellow ivory crucifix.

“Who on earth can it be?” I muttered, though my own voice sounded out of place, like speech in church.

Good’s answer came quick, almost impatient, as though the obvious had been keeping him waiting. “Can’t you guess? Why, the old Dom—José da Silvestra. Who else?”

“Impossible,” I said; my tongue felt thick. “He died three hundred years ago.”

Good gave a short, bitter laugh that steamed once and vanished. “And what is there to prevent him lasting three thousand years in such a larder as this? If the temperature’s low enough, flesh keeps like mutton hung in a freezing-room, and Heaven knows no sun ever bakes this place. No jackal comes to worry it, no worm to take a share. His slave, that he speaks of, must have stripped him and left him. One man couldn’t dig a grave in this rock.”

He stooped and picked up a queerly-shaped bone, scraped and sharpened at the end into a point. “Look. Here’s the cleft bone he used to draw the map.”

For a moment we only stared. Hunger and cold and fear slid aside, and awe stepped into their place, as if some old tale had pushed open a door into the present. Sir Henry pointed to a small wound on the Dom’s left arm, the skin split and darkened there.

“And this is where he got his ink from,” he said. “Did ever man see such a thing?”

There was no doubt now. The proof sat stiff in the half-dark, crowned with its own dreadful certainty. The directions we had followed—scratched with that bone pen, stained with a dying man’s blood—had led us to the very author of them, kept as if in a glass case by the merciless cold. The crucifix that had touched his lips still lay upon his breast. The thought of it appalled me. It was like finding the hand that wrote your name in a book, still gripping the pen.

In my mind the last scene rebuilt itself without my bidding: the traveller alone beyond all help, cold gnawing him from the outside and hunger from within; the slow dimming of strength; and yet the stubborn drive to leave a message—directions, warning, temptation—for men unborn. There was a loneliness in that death that no words could cover. For an instant, too, I fancied I saw a likeness between those rigid features and the face of his descendant—my poor friend, dead these twenty years—though perhaps that was only my mind grasping for familiar ground in a place where time itself felt broken.

“Let us go,” Sir Henry said at last, very low. Then, after a pause: “Stay. We will give him a companion.”

He lifted poor Ventvögel’s body—so light now, so pitifully easy to move—and laid it near the Dom, the proud white man of a past age and the poor Hottentot, side by side, as if the mountain had demanded its due from both. Sir Henry then stooped over the Dom and with a jerk snapped the rotten string of the crucifix—his fingers were too numb to unfasten it properly. He took it. I, for my part, took the bone pen. Even now it lies before me when I write; sometimes I use it to sign my name, and the scratch it makes seems to come from colder air than any ink.

We left them there, keeping their eternal vigil in the heart of the snows, and crept out into the sun again, wondering in our hearts how many hours it would be before we lay as they lay.

After about half a mile we reached the edge of the plateau. From the desert side the nipple of the mountain had looked as though it rose in the centre; in truth it did not, and now the land fell away before us. At first we could see nothing, for the morning fog lay in thick billows, filling the hollows like milk poured into a basin. Then the upper layers thinned and tore, and through a gap we saw, far below at the end of a long slope of snow, a patch of green grass—real green, the colour of life—perhaps five hundred yards beneath us. A stream ran through it, flashing like a knife blade.

By the stream stood and lay a group of great antelopes, ten or fifteen of them, basking in the sun as if they owned it. At that distance we could not tell their kind, only the bulk and the curve of horns.

The sight struck us with a joy so sharp it was almost pain. Food—food with warmth in it, blood in it, strength enough to lift a foot and set it down again.

But how to get it was another matter. They were six hundred yards away at least; a long shot at the best of times, and we were half-dead men with shaking hands and eyes tired from snow-glare. We spoke quickly, weighing the chance of stalking. The wind was wrong, and there was the blinding white slope between us; even crawling we would show like beetles on a sheet.

“We must try from here,” Sir Henry said. “Which, Quatermain—the repeaters or the expresses?”

It was a nasty choice. The Winchesters were sighted to a thousand yards, but they struck with less authority; the expresses, though sighted only to three hundred and fifty, carried those expanding bullets that, if they hit, would drop a beast as if it had been pole-axed. Life hung on that “if.”

“We risk the expresses,” I decided. “Each take the buck opposite him. Aim high at the point of the shoulder. Umbopa—give the word. We fire together.”

We lay into our aim. There was a silence so complete that the little sounds of our own breathing seemed loud and foolish in it, and beyond us the snow held its own hush, like a great ear listening.

“Fire,” Umbopa said in Zulu.

The three rifles spoke almost as one. The reports cracked across the stillness and went rolling away in a hundred echoes. Smoke drifted and hung in grey sheets. When it cleared, one of the great bucks was on its back, legs beating the air in furious spasms, the last stubborn refusal of life.

We yelled—an ugly, broken sound, half triumph, half sob—and then, weak as we were, ran down the snow towards it. Ten minutes later its heart and liver lay before us, steaming faintly in the clean cold.

And then we stopped, struck dumb by a fresh misery. No firewood. Nothing but snow and rock. We could not cook.

Good looked at the bloody prize, then at us. “Starving men should not be fanciful,” he said shortly. “We must eat raw meat.”

There was no arguing with hunger. We took the heart and liver and buried them a few minutes in a patch of snow, letting the cold tighten them so they would slice cleaner. We washed them in the stream—ice water that made the hands ache to the bone—and then we ate.

The taste was not what horror imagines. There was iron and salt, a clean wild sweetness, and a warmth that seemed to spread outward as the first mouthful went down. We tore and chewed greedily, yet with some care, remembering what happens when a starved stomach is suddenly overfilled. We forced ourselves to stop while still hungry.

In a quarter of an hour we were changed men. Blood came back to the skin; the faintness lifted. It was as though the antelope’s strength had crossed into our veins, and the cold no longer sat so heavily upon the shoulders.

“Thank Heaven!” Sir Henry said. “That brute has saved our lives. What is it, Quatermain?”

I went to look. It was donkey-sized, with big curved horns and a thick coat; brown, with faint red stripes. I had never seen its like. I learned later the natives call it an inco, rare, only found at great height where other game will not live. The bullet had struck high in the shoulder. Whose shot did the work we could not tell; Good, I suspect, privately entered it in the account-book of his own marksmanship, and nobody was cruel enough to dispute him.

Only then did we properly look about us. While Umbopa cut away the best meat we could carry, we lifted our eyes to the world below. The mist had been sucked up by the sun—it was about eight o’clock—and the land lay open like a revealed secret.

Behind us and above, Sheba’s Breasts towered, their snowy domes coldly splendid. Before us, five thousand feet beneath, spread league on league of the loveliest champaign country. Forests lay in deep patches, dark as spilled ink; a great river wound through, silver where it caught the light. To the left stretched rolling grasslands with what might have been herds—game or cattle we could not tell at that distance—ringed by a wall of far mountains. To the right the country rose and fell in solitary hills, and between them we could see cultivated strips and groups of dome-shaped huts.

Everything glittered under the glad sunlight—rivers like bright snakes, peaks like teeth with twisted wreaths of snow. And over all hung the living breath of the place: not only its look, but its sound. Even from up there we could sense the difference from the desert’s dead hush. Water spoke everywhere—thin and quick where it ran over stones, deeper where it gathered. Wind moved through grass and trees in a soft, continuous rustle like a long-held note. Birds flashed and, though we could not yet pick their calls clearly at that height, the very air seemed tuned. After the brutal silence of snow and the mind-killing sameness of the desert wind, this layered music felt like health itself.

Two things struck us. First, this country lay far higher than the desert we had crossed—three thousand feet at least. Second, all the rivers ran from south to north. On the southern face there had been no water; on the northern, streams everywhere, most of them seeming to feed that great river.

We sat and stared, our mouths closed, as if speech would break the spell.

At last Sir Henry said, “Isn’t there something on the map about Solomon’s Great Road?”

I nodded, still gazing.

“Well—look. There it is.” He pointed a little to our right.

We looked, and there, winding down toward the plain, lay a broad road, like a pale ribbon laid by a giant’s hand. At first we had missed it because it turned behind broken country on reaching the level.

Oddly, we did not exclaim much. Wonder was becoming a tired sense, like a muscle overused. We accepted the road the way one accepts a new rule of weather in a strange climate.

“It must be quite near if we cut to the right,” Good said. “Hadn’t we better start?”

We agreed. After washing our faces and hands in the stream—we lingered on that simple domestic act as if it were a ritual restoring us to ourselves—we set off. For a mile or more we crossed boulders and patches of snow, then topped a little rise and found the road at our feet.

It was magnificent—cut out of solid rock, fifty feet wide at least, and seemingly kept in order. The strangest part was that it appeared to begin there. A hundred paces behind us, toward Sheba’s Breasts, it vanished entirely; the mountain surface lay strewn with boulders and snow, as though the road had been erased.

“What do you make of this, Quatermain?” Sir Henry asked.

I could only shake my head.

“I have it,” Good said. “It must once have run right over the range and across the desert, but the sand has buried it on that side, and up here it’s been obliterated by some volcanic eruption—molten lava.”

We seized on the explanation, not because it satisfied the mind, but because it allowed the feet to keep moving. And so we went down.

With full stomachs, travelling downhill on that splendid pathway was another world from our desperate climb. The air softened by the mile, losing its knife edge, taking on a balm that smelled faintly of leaf and damp earth. The beauty before us grew more luminous with every turn. Only the thought of Ventvögel, and that grim cave where he now lay with da Silvestra, kept cheerfulness from rising too high.

As an engineering work the road was astonishing. No difficulty had stopped the old builders. At one place a ravine three hundred feet across and a hundred deep had been filled in with massive dressed blocks, arches pierced through at the base to let water pass, so that the road strode over the gulf as if it were nothing. At another point it was cut in zigzags along the face of a precipice five hundred feet deep. In a third it tunneled through the base of a ridge for thirty yards or more.

In the tunnel we saw the sides covered with quaint sculptures—mailed figures in chariots, and, in one panel of extraordinary beauty, a battle scene with captives being marched away in the distance.

Sir Henry examined it and then said, “They call it Solomon’s Road, but in my humble opinion Egyptians were here before Solomon’s people ever set foot on it. If this isn’t Egyptian or Phoenician handiwork, it’s very like it.”

By midday we had descended far enough to find brushwood. First scattered bushes, then more and more, and at last the road wound through a grove of silver trees like those on Table Mountain at the Cape—shining leaves that caught the light as if each one were rubbed with metal. I had never seen them elsewhere and their presence here startled me.

Good’s whole face brightened at the sight—not only for the warmth of fire, but for the civilised comfort it promised. “Ah! Here’s plenty of wood. Let’s stop and cook dinner. I’ve about digested that raw heart.”

Nobody argued. We left the road and went to a stream babbling close by. Soon we had a fire going—dry boughs snapping, the smell of resin and heated sap rising deliciously, smoke curling into blue air. Umbopa had cut generous hunks of inco flesh; we skewered them on sharpened sticks and toasted them in the Kafir fashion, turning them patiently so the outside browned and crisped while the inside stayed juicy. Fat hissed as it fell into the coals; the scent was rich, meaty, and almost sweet, and it seemed to fill the hollow places in the mind as much as in the belly. We ate with relish, letting the hot juice run on the tongue, feeling the strength of it settle in the limbs.

Afterward we lit our pipes. The simple routine—fire, food, tobacco—felt like the rebuilding of a home in miniature, a domestic island in a wild sea.

The brook’s banks were clothed in dense masses of gigantic maidenhair fern, and among it grew feathery tufts of wild asparagus. The water sang as it went, not loudly but cheerfully, its voice broken into bright little notes by stones. Soft air murmured through the silver leaves overhead. Doves cooed somewhere close, their calls round and steady, and bright-winged birds flashed from bough to bough like living gems, making quick, thin sounds that stitched the silence into something gentle. After the savage, dead acoustics of the snowfields—the way sound had cracked and fled away—this layered, living soundscape worked on the nerves like medicine. One could almost believe that a place which sounded so right could not mean to kill us.

The magic of it, combined with the relief of danger apparently behind, charmed us into a kind of quiet. Sir Henry and Umbopa spoke together in low voices, half broken English and Kitchen Zulu, earnest and intent. I lay on the fern bed, its scent green and clean beneath my cheek, and watched them through half-closed eyes.

Then I missed Good.

I raised my head and found him down by the stream. He had been bathing and now wore nothing but his flannel shirt and boots. His neatness—always lurking in him like an indestructible instinct—had risen again as soon as he had water and a moment’s peace. He had washed his gutta-percha collar, shaken out his trousers, coat, and waistcoat, and was folding them as neatly as though he were packing for a railway journey. He frowned at the rents and tears our journey had made. Then he took his boots, scrubbed them with fern, and finally rubbed them over with a piece of fat he had carefully saved from the inco, until they looked, comparatively speaking, respectable. He inspected them through his eye-glass as gravely as a man judging a regiment on parade.

From a little bag he produced a pocket-comb with a tiny looking-glass fixed to it and began arranging his hair with meticulous care. He paused, studied the effect, felt his chin, where ten days’ beard bristled like scrub.

Surely, I thought, he is not going to shave.

But he was. He washed the bit of fat thoroughly in the stream, drew out a small guarded razor, and rubbed his face with the fat, working it in like some miserable substitute for soap. Then he began to shave.

It was a painful business. He groaned and muttered, wrestling the blade over stubble that was half iron in that cold. Watching him there—half naked, half lathered in melted fat, solemnly keeping up the habits of a dressing-room in the heart of a strange land—I was shaking with silent laughter, when something bright flashed past his head.

Good sprang up with a violent oath—if it had not been a safety razor he would surely have cut his throat—and I started up too.

Not twenty paces from me, and perhaps ten from him, stood a group of men: very tall, copper-coloured, some wearing great plumes of black feathers and short cloaks of leopard skin. In front of them was a youth of about seventeen, his hand still raised, body bent forward in the poised attitude of a spear-thrower. The flash had been from the weapon he had hurled.

An older man, soldier-like in bearing, stepped out, caught the youth by the arm, and spoke sharply to him. Then the whole party advanced on us.

Sir Henry, Good, and Umbopa snatched up their rifles, lifting them threateningly. The men came on as though the guns were sticks. It struck me they did not know what firearms were, or they would not have walked so boldly into their mouths.

“Put down your guns!” I shouted, because our only chance lay in not turning the first meeting into a slaughter. They obeyed. I stepped forward and addressed the older man.

“Greeting,” I said in Zulu, not knowing what other tongue to try.

To my surprise he understood. “Greeting,” he answered—not in Zulu proper, but in a dialect so near it that Umbopa and I could follow without trouble. Later we learned it was an old-fashioned form of Zulu, as Chaucer’s English is to modern speech: familiar in shape, strange in turn and cadence, heavy with older ways of naming the world.

He looked from my face to Sir Henry’s and Good’s and back again, weighing us with eyes that missed nothing.

“Whence come you?” he asked. “Who are you? Why are the faces of three of you white, and the face of the fourth as the face of our mother’s sons?” He pointed at Umbopa.

I glanced at Umbopa, and the truth of it hit me with a chill: his face and great build matched these men’s, as though he had been cut from the same timber. But there was no time to follow that thought.

“We are strangers,” I said slowly, choosing words he could grasp, “and we come in peace. This man is our servant.”

“You lie,” he answered flatly. “No strangers can cross the mountains where all things perish. But what matter your lies? If you are strangers you must die. No strangers may live in the land of the Kukuanas. It is the king’s law. Prepare, then, to die, O strangers!”

As he spoke I saw hands drift down toward the heavy knives at their sides.

Good leaned toward me. “What does that beggar say?”

“He says we’re going to be killed,” I answered.

“Oh, Lord!” Good groaned. And then, in that odd little habit of his when perplexed, he put his hand to his false teeth, pulled the top set down, and let them spring back with a snap.

It saved our lives.

The Kukuanas gave one simultaneous yell of horror and bolted backward several yards, as though he had turned his mouth into a trap.

“What’s wrong?” I whispered.

“It’s his teeth,” Sir Henry hissed. “He moved them. Take them out, Good, take them out!”

Good, bewildered but obedient, slipped the set into the sleeve of his flannel shirt.

Curiosity quickly overcame fear. The men crept forward again, eyes fixed on Good as if he were a cooking-pot that had begun to speak.

“How is it,” the old man demanded solemnly, “that this fat man—whose body is clothed and whose legs are bare; who grows hair on one side of his sickly face and not on the other; who wears one shining, transparent eye—how is it, I ask, that he has teeth which move of themselves, coming away from the jaws and returning of their own will?”

“Open your mouth,” I said to Good.

Good curled his lip and grinned like an angry dog, showing two thin red lines of gum as innocent of teeth as a newborn elephant’s. The men gasped and cried out.

“Where are his teeth? With our eyes we saw them!”

Good turned his head slowly and, with a gesture of perfect contempt, swept his hand across his mouth. Then he grinned again—and there were two rows of excellent white teeth.

The youth who had thrown the knife flung himself down and howled in sheer terror. The older man’s knees knocked together.

“I see that you are spirits,” he stammered. “Did ever man born of woman have hair on one side of his face and not on the other, or a round transparent eye, or teeth that moved and melted away and grew again? Pardon us, O my lords.”

It was luck—wild, ridiculous luck—and I seized it before it could slip.

“It is granted,” I said, putting into my voice all the authority I could counterfeit. “You shall know the truth. We come from another world, though we are men like you. We come from the biggest star that shines at night.”

A groan went through them like wind through dry grass.

“Oh! oh!” they murmured, staring at us as though the very words had altered the air.
