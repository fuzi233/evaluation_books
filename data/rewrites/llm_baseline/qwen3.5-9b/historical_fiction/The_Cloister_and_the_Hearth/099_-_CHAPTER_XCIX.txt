Jerome remained as stern and gloomy as ever in his general disposition, yet he had grown considerably softer toward Gerard. Throughout his stay in England, he had missed his brother far more than he realized until now, frequently wondering where he had gone. The news he received in Gouda elevated his opinion of the younger monk; particularly the report that Gerard had stood firm against the Pope and the Minorites regarding "the infernal heresy of the Immaculate Conception," as Jerome termed it. However, when a young novice recounted the cause of Gerard's illness with tears streaming down his face, Jerome's contempt flared anew. "Dying for a woman?" he muttered. Determined to prevent such a scandal, Jerome visited Clement twice daily in his cell, deploying all his former influence and eloquence to urge him to abandon this unspiritual despondency. He argued that the Church could not afford to lose its piety and eloquence at such a critical juncture.

Gerard listened, acknowledged the logic, admired Jerome's strength, and confessed his own frailty, though he visibly continued to fade into the realm of the dead. One day, Jerome revealed that he had heard Gerard's full story and admitted hearing it with pride. "But now," Jerome declared, "you ruin everything. This is the triumph of earthly passion. It would have been better to yield and repent than to resist while she lived and finally succumb now, destroying both body and soul."

"Dear Jerome," Clement replied, his sweetness stripping the rebuke of its edge, "I believe you misunderstand me. There is no passion here, only a profound affection for which I need not blush, nor shall I blush for it in heaven. Consider, Jerome: does the poor dog who dies of grief beside his master's grave commit sin through passion? Neither do I. Passion would have saved my life but cost me my soul. She was my guardian angel; she upheld me in my duties and charity. Her face encouraged me in the pulpit; her lips soothed me under ingratitude. She became woven into every good thing in my life, and after leaning on her so long, I could not walk alone. And, dear Jerome, believe me, I am no rebel against Heaven. It is God's will to release me. When they threw the earth upon her poor coffin, something snapped within my chest—a break that cannot be mended. From that moment, Jerome, no food tasted of anything. Even with my eyes bandaged, I could not distinguish bread from flesh by taste."

"Holy saints!" Jerome exclaimed.

"And from that same hour," Clement continued, "my deep depression vanished, and I began to smile again. Why? I read it thus: He who holds the keys of life and death gave me that minute the great summons; a cord of life snapped within me. He is merciful. I would have lived unhappily, but He said, 'No; enough is done, enough is suffered. Poor, feeble, loving servant, your faults are forgiven, your sorrows mark your end; come to your rest.' I come, Lord, I come!"

Jerome groaned. "The Church has always had her holy yet feeble servants," he lamented. "Now I would give ten years of my life to save yours. But I see it cannot be. Die in peace."

Thus, within a few days, Gerard lay dying in a state of such holy happiness that several aged saints seemed present to gather his final words. That evening, he had seen Giles, begging him not to let poor Jack starve, ensuring the trustees fulfilled their duties, kissing his parents for him, and sending Denys to his friends in Burgundy: "Poor thing, he will feel so strange here without his comrade." Following this, he had a private audience with Jerome alone. What transpired between them remains unclear, but it must have been extraordinary, for Jerome left the room with his hands clasped over his breast, his proud head bowed low, sighing heavily as he walked away.

The two monks who kept vigil with him until Matins reported that throughout the night, Gerard periodically burst forth in pious ejaculations, praises, and thanksgivings. Once, however, they noted he wandered, seemingly seeing her walking in green meadows among other spirits clad in white, beckoning him; and they, too, smiled and waved back. Both monks claimed—though perhaps fancifully—that just before dawn, three light taps echoed against the wall, one after another, very slowly. The dying man heard them and whispered, "I come, love, I come."

It is certain that Gerard uttered these words and prepared for his departure. He summoned all the monks keeping vigil. They gathered around him like gentle spirits, speaking holy words. Some prayed silently with faces touching the ground; others tenderly supported his head. When one mentioned his life of self-denial and charity, Gerard stopped him. Addressing them all, he said, "My dear brethren, take note: he who dies here so happy does not hold these new-fangled doctrines of human merit. Oh, what a miserable hour it would have been for me if I did! Nay, I hold, with the Apostles and their successors in the Church, the ancient Fathers, that we are justified not by our own wisdom, piety, or works performed in holiness of heart, but by faith."

Silence fell, and the monks exchanged significant glances. "If you please, sweep the floor," the dying Christian commanded, his voice suddenly clear and powerful, seeming supernaturally restored. They instantly obeyed, moved by awe and curiosity. "Make a great cross with wood ashes." They scattered the ashes on the floor in the shape of a large Cross. "Now lay me down upon it, for thus I shall die."

They gently lifted him from his bed and placed him upon the ash-cross. "Shall we spread out your arms, dear brother?" someone asked.

"Now God forbid! Am I worthy of that?" he replied. He lay silent, eyes raised in ecstasy. Soon, he spoke half to them, half to himself, "Oh," he murmured with subdued but intense rapture, "I feel it buoyant. It lifts me, floating in the sky, whereas my merits had sunk me like lead."

Daybreak revealed his face cast upward in silent rapture, his hands joined, resembling Margaret's. Just about the hour she died, he spoke his last word in this world. "Jesu!" And with that word, he fell asleep.

They laid him out for burial. Beneath his linen, they discovered a horsehair shirt. "Ah!" cried the young monks, "Behold a saint!" Underneath the haircloth, they found a long, thick tress of auburn hair. They recoiled, horrified, and a babble of voices erupted, some condemning, some excusing. Amidst the commotion, Jerome entered. Hearing the dispute, he turned to an ardent young monk named Basil, who was shouting scandal most loudly.

"Basil," Jerome said, "Is she alive or dead that owns this hair?"

"How can I know, Father?"

"Then, for all you know, it may be the relic of a saint?"

"Certainly it may," Basil replied skeptically.

"You have then broken our Rule, which says, 'Do not make evil constructions on any act done by a brother which can be construed innocently.' Who are you to judge such a man? Go to your cell and do not leave for a week as penance."

He then removed the lock of hair. When the coffin was to be sealed, Jerome cleared the cell and placed the tress upon the dead man's bosom. "There, Clement," he said to the lifeless face. He set himself a penance for the act and nailed the coffin shut himself.

The following day, Gerard was buried in the Gouda churchyard. The monks followed him in procession from the convent. Jerome, clearly executing the deceased's wishes, read the service. The grave was deep, containing a leaden coffin at the bottom. Poor Gerard's body, light as a feather due to his wasting, was lowered and placed beside it. After the service, Jerome addressed the crowd of parishioners who had come for a final look at their beloved friend. As he spoke of the departed's virtues, loud wailing and weeping burst forth, and tears fell upon the coffin like rain.

The monks returned home. Jerome gathered them in the refectory and spoke thus: "We have today laid a saint in the earth. The convent will keep his trentals, but we shall feast, not fast; for our good brother is freed from the burden of the flesh; his labors are over, and he has entered into his joyful rest. I alone shall fast and do penance; for to my shame I confess, I was unjust to him and knew not his worth until it was too late. And you, young monks, do not be curious to inquire whether the lock he bore on his bosom was a token of pure affection or the relic of a saint; but remember the heart he wore beneath. Most of all, fix your eyes upon his life and conversation, and follow them if you may: for he was a holy man."

So, after life's fitful fever, these true lovers found peace. The grave, kinder to them than the Church, united them forever. Now, a man of another age and nation, touched by their fate, has labored to build their tombstone and rescue them from long and undeserved oblivion. He asks for your sympathy, but not your pity. No, put this story to a wholesome use. Fiction often presents false views of life and death. Here, constrained by history, it offers true ones
