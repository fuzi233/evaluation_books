It is easy to imagine that the separation between the brothers has been finalized, that George has stepped into his role within Mr. Braddock's command, and Harry has returned to Castlewood to fulfill his duties. Yet, his spirit remains tethered to the army; life at home offers him no solace. He cannot bear to voice the depth of his sorrow at being confined beneath the somber, quiet roof of Castlewood, now rendered more desolate by George's absence. Harry avoids passing through his brother's empty chambers, turns his face away, and takes George's seat at the head of the table, sighing deeply as he lifts his silver tankard.

Madam Warrington raises a hearty "To the King" daily, and on Sundays, as Harry reads the service and prays for travelers by land and sea, she intones, "We beseech Thee to hear us," with a gravity that unsettles the room. She insists on speaking of George constantly, yet with a cheerful, unshakable optimism, as if his return were a certainty written in stone. She strides into his vacant study, posture erect, masking any outward sign of emotion. She observes that his books, linens, and papers remain meticulously arranged, speaks of him with profound reverence, and appeals frequently to the elderly servants regarding tasks meant to be completed "when Mr. George returns." Mrs. Mountain trembles whenever George's name is spoken, causing Harry to wear a look of ghastly alarm, while his mother maintains a stoic, sedate composure. Though prone to blunders at piquet and backgammon, she rises before dawn, dressed and ready. Having summoned Mr. Dempster back to reside at Castlewood, she treats everyone with gentleness rather than her former severity, often drifting into tales of her father's campaigns—where he emerged unscathed—and expressing hope that her eldest son will share that fortune.

George sends frequent letters home, and now, with the army on the move, he has begun compiling a rough journal, forwarding it as opportunity allows. These documents are devoured with delight by Harry, who reads them aloud during the long summer evenings when the household gathers. Madam Esmond sits upright at her tea-table, refusing to lean back; little Fanny Mountain sews diligently beside her; Mr. Dempster and Mrs. Mountain play their cards; and the ancient, silent servants of the house move quietly through the gloaming, listening intently to the words of their young master.

Hearken to Harry Warrington reading his brother's letter! As we gaze at the slender script on the yellow pages, carefully preserved, we might almost believe the writer and reader were truly alive. And yet, alas, they seem as though they never existed—their likenesses faint ghosts in frames of tarnished gold. Did they truly live and breathe? Did they love one another as faithful brothers and loyal gentlemen? Can we still hear their voices from the past? I am certain of Harry's voice, sitting there in the warm summer air, reading his younger brother's simple chronicle:

"It must be admitted that the colonies treat His Majesty, King George II., with scant courtesy, and his representative here burns with indignation. Virginia is difficult, Maryland is poor, but Pennsylvania is the worst of all. We begged them to send troops from home to fight the French, promising to support them upon arrival. Not only did we break this promise, failing to provide adequate provisions, but our populace demands exorbitant prices for cattle and stores, effectively cheating the soldiers who have come to fight *their* battles. No wonder the General swears, and the troops are sullen. Delays have been interminable. Due to the failure of the provinces to supply promised stores and transport, weeks and months have passed. During this time, undoubtedly, the French have fortified themselves on our frontiers and reclaimed the forts we lost.

"There will be no further suit concerning the quarrel at Benson's Tavern, nor any rematch between Colonel Washington and the anonymous young gentleman. Captain Waring sought to pursue the matter upon entering camp, delivering a message from Captain Grace, which your friend—who is as bold as Hector—was eager to accept, employing Brother Aide-de-Camp Colonel Wingfield on his side. However, when Wingfield learned the circumstances—that the altercation arose from Grace's drunkenness, fanned by Waring's own tipsiness, and involved two regulars insulting a militia officer—he swore that Colonel Washington would not engage the 44th men. Instead, he would carry the matter directly to His Excellency, who would convene a court-martial for the captains for brawling with the militia, drunkenness, and indecent conduct. Thus, the captains were forced to lower their toasting irons and swallow their wrath. They were sufficiently good-natured outside their cups, eating their humble pie with relish at a reconciliation dinner hosted by Colonel Washington, where he conducted himself with a perfection akin to Prince Prettyman. Hang him! He has no faults, and that is precisely why I dislike him. When he marries that widow—ah me! what a dreary existence awaits her."

"I wonder at the palate of some men and the audacity of some women!" Madam Esmond exclaimed, setting down her teacup. "I marvel at any woman who, having been married once, forgets herself enough to wed again! Do you not agree, Mountain?"

"Monstrous!" cried Mountain, with a peculiar expression.

Mr. Dempster kept his gaze steadily fixed on his glass of punch. Harry appeared as though he were choking with laughter or some other concealed emotion, until his mother interjected, "Continue, Harry! Proceed with your brother's journal. He writes well; but, ah, will he ever be able to write like my father?"

Harry resumed: "We maintain the strictest discipline here in camp. Orders against drunkenness and misconduct among the men are severe. The roll of each company is called at morning, noon, and night, with a return of absentees and disorderly elements submitted to the regimental commander for punishment. The men are duly punished, and the drummers are constantly at work. Oh, Harry, it made one sick to behold the first blood drawn from a great, strong, white back, and to hear the pitiful cry of the poor fellow."

"Oh, horrid!" gasped Madam Esmond.

"I believe I would have murdered Ward if he had flogged me. Thank heavens he escaped with nothing but a crack of the ruler! The men are cared for adequately, I say. I wish the officers were treated similarly. The Indians have just broken camp and retreated in anger, for the young officers were constantly drinking with the squaws—and... and... hum... ha." Here, Mr. Harry paused, unwilling to continue the narrative in the presence of little Fanny, who sat primly by her mother's side, stitching her sampler.

"Skip the mention of those odious tipsy creatures," commanded Madam. And Harry began anew, in a louder tone, with a far more satisfactory account: "Each regiment performs Divine Service at the head of its colors every Sunday. The General does everything in the power of mortal man to prevent plundering and encourages the surrounding populace to bring in provisions. He has decreed that any soldier daring to interrupt or molest market-goers shall be shot. He has ordered the price of provisions raised by a penny a pound and lent money from his own pocket to sustain the camp. Altogether, he is a strange composite, this General. He flogs his men without mercy, yet gives freely without stint. He swears terrible oaths in conversation and tells stories that would shock Mrs. Mountain..."

"Why me?" asked Mountain. "What business do I have with the General's foolish stories?"

"Never mind the stories, and continue, Harry," cried the mistress of the house.

"...would shock Mrs. Mountain after dinner, but he never misses service. He adores his Great Duke, whose name is constantly on his lips. Our two regiments both served in Scotland, where I dare say Mr. Dempster knows the color of their facings."

"We saw the tails of their coats, as well as their facings," grumbled the little Jacobite tutor.

"Colonel Washington has caught the fever sharply and has barely been well enough to keep pace with the march. Would it not be better for him to return home and be nursed by his widow? When either of us falls ill, we are almost as close as ever. But I feel somehow as if I cannot forgive him for having slighted me. Good Powers! How I have hated him for these past months! Oh, Harry! I was in a fury at the tavern the other day because Mountain arrived so promptly, ending our disagreement. We ought to have burned a small quantity of gunpowder between us and cleared the air. But though I do not love him as you do, I know he is a good soldier, a good officer, and a brave, honest man; and, at any rate, I shall love him none the less for not wishing to be our stepfather."

"A stepfather, indeed!" cried Harry's mother. "Jealousy and prejudice have completely unhinged the poor child! Do you suppose the Marquis of Esmond's daughter and heiress could not have found other stepfathers for her sons than a mere provincial surveyor? If there are any further allusions to such follies and blunders in George's journal, I implore you to skip them, Harry, my dear. Enough talk has already occurred regarding this piece of folly."
