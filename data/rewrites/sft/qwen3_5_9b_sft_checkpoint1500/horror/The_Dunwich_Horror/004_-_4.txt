For ten long years, the Whateley name sank silently into the muck of Dunwich's collective psyche, indistinguishable from the town's own decaying morals. The villagers, hardened by a lifetime of witnessing the family's grotesque rituals—their May Eve and All-Hallows orgies—had learned to look away. Yet, beneath the surface of this morbid acceptance, the house remained a festering wound. Twice annually, fires blazed atop Sentinel Hill, triggering earth-shaking rumblings that grew more violent with passing time, while within the lonely farmhouse, the air thickened with portents. Callers claimed to hear the hollow echoes of the sealed upper stories even when every soul below was accounted for, whispering theories about the speed at which livestock met their ends. Rumors swirled of complaints to the Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals, but these never materialized; Dunwichites possessed a stubborn, insular pride that refused to invite the scrutiny of the wider world.

By 1923, when Wilbur Whateley was merely ten years old, his physical presence already betrayed a terrifying maturity. With a beard, a deep voice, and a stature that defied childhood, he appeared as an ancient sage. During this period, a second siege of construction descended upon the old house, confined entirely to the sealed upper floors. Observers pieced together the chaos from discarded timber, concluding that Wilbur and his grandfather had obliterated every partition and stripped the attic floor, carving a single, vast, cavernous void between the ground floor and the peaked roof. They had demolished the massive central chimney, replacing it with a flimsy tin stovepipe that clattered ominously in the wind.

In the spring following this transformation, Old Whateley became obsessed with the proliferation of whippoorwills emerging from Cold Spring Glen to chorus beneath his window. To the old man, their arrival signaled a profound, inevitable shift. At Osborn's saloon, he confided in the local loafers, his eyes clouded with a feverish certainty.

"They whistle jest in tune with my breathin' naow," he rasped, leaning forward as if sharing a sacred secret. "An' I guess they're gittin' ready to ketch my soul. They know it's a-goin' aout, an' dun't calc'late to miss it. Yew'll know, boys, arter I'm gone, whether they git me er not. Ef they dew, they'll keep up a-singin' an' laffin' till break o' day. Ef they dun't, they'll kinder quiet daown like. I expeck them an' the souls they hunts fer hev some pretty tough tussles sometimes."

Then came Lammas Night, 1924. Dr. Houghton of Aylesbury was dragged into the dark, summoned urgently by Wilbur, who had lashed his solitary horse through the gloom and dialed Osborn's from the village. Inside, Old Whateley lay in a cataleptic trance, his breathing stertorous and ragged, marking an end imminent in the cosmic scale. Beside the bed stood the shapeless albino daughter and her oddly bearded grandson. Above them, from the yawning abyss of the newly created void, emanated a disquieting, rhythmic lapping sound, like water washing over unseen shores on a desolate beach. But it was the cacophony without that truly unsettled the doctor: a seemingly infinite legion of whippoorwills crying an endless, repetitive dirge, perfectly synchronized with the dying man's wheezing gasps. It was uncanny, unnatural—a suffocating excess that mirrored the hellish nature of the region itself.

Around one o'clock, Old Whateley broke through his stupor, choking out fragmented words for his grandson.

"More space, Willy, more space soon," he gasped, his vision likely failing. "Yew grows--an' *that* grows faster. It'll be ready to sarve ye soon, boy. Open up the gates to Yog-Sothoth with the long chant that ye'll find on page 751 *of the complete edition*, an' *then* put a match to the prison. Fire from airth can't burn it nohaow!"

His mind was clearly unhinged, fractured by visions only he could see. After a pause, during which the flock of birds outside shifted their cries to match the altered tempo of the room, he added a final, chilling warning.

"Feed it reg'lar, Willy, an' mind the quantity; but dun't let it grow too fast fer the place, fer ef it busts quarters or gits aout afore ye opens to Yog-Sothoth, it's all over an' no use. Only them from beyont kin make it multiply an' work.... Only them, the old uns as wants to come back...."

Speech dissolved back into ragged gasps, and Lavinia screamed, her terror amplified by the frantic flight of the birds tracking the man's suffering. For over an hour, this agonizing cycle continued until the final, throaty rattle silenced the old man. As Dr. Houghton drew shrunken lids over the glazing gray eyes, the tumult of birds faded imperceptibly into a silence so absolute it felt heavy with dread. Lavinia sobbed, broken, but Wilbur only chuckled, a low, resonant sound that vibrated through the room as the strange hill noises rumbled faintly in the distance.

"They didn't git him," Wilbur muttered, his heavy bass voice devoid of remorse.

By then, Wilbur Whateley had evolved into a scholar of truly terrible erudition, known by correspondence to librarians across the globe who guarded rare and forbidden tomes. Yet, around Dunwich, he was both reviled and feared, particularly due to the mysterious disappearances of youths that suspicion inevitably linked to his door. Despite this, inquiries were stifled by a paralyzing fear or by the simple, brutal reality of the Whateley gold, which flowed steadily and generously for cattle purchases, just as it had in Old Man Whateley's day.

Physically, Wilbur was a monstrous anomaly. Having reached the normal adult limit, his height seemed poised to exceed it further. By 1925, when a correspondent from Miskatonic University visited and departed pale and bewildered, Wilbur stood fully six and three-quarters feet tall. Throughout these years, he had treated his half-deformed albino mother with a contempt that bordered on hatred, eventually forbidding her from joining him on the hills for May Eve and Hallowmass. By 1926, Lavinia, trembling with a primal fear, confessed to Mamie Bishop that there was more about her son than she could comprehend.

"They's more abaout him as I knows than I kin tell ye, Mamie," she whispered, tears streaming down her pale face. "An' naowadays they's more nor what I know myself. I vaow afur Gawd, I dun't know what he wants nor what he's a-tryin' to dew."

That Hallowe'en, the hill noises roared louder than ever, and fires burned on Sentinel Hill as tradition dictated. But the villagers turned their gaze from the flames to the farmhouse, where vast flocks of unnaturally belated whippoorwills gathered in a rhythmic, screaming assembly. After midnight, their shrill notes erupted into a pandemonium of laughter and shrieks that filled the countryside, a sound so alien it seemed to tear at the fabric of the night. Not until dawn did they finally fall silent, vanishing in a hurry southward, a full month overdue for their migration. No one could say what this meant, though the implication hung heavy in the air: none of the countryfolk had perished, yet poor Lavinia Whateley, the twisted albino, was never seen again.

In the summer of 1927, Wilbur repaired two sheds in the farmyard and began transferring his books and effects to them. Shortly thereafter, Earl Sawyer noted to the regulars at Osborn's that more carpentry was underway at the Whateley home. Wilbur was sealing off every door and window on the ground floor, simultaneously removing internal partitions in a manner eerily similar to the work done four years prior. He resided in one of the sheds, appearing unusually agitated and tremulous. The townspeople generally suspected he knew something about his mother's fate, and few dared approach his neighborhood. His height had swollen past seven feet, showing no sign of stopping, as if the earth itself were stretching to accommodate his impossible growth.
