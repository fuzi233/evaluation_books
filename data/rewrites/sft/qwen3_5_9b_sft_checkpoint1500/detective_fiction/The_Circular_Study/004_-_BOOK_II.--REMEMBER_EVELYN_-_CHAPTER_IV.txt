Mr. Gryce felt a profound sense of isolation in his pursuit of the truth surrounding this death, a disadvantage far exceeding those of any previous case. The victim, a bachelor, resided alone save for a silent, mute manservant. His location in the city was remote, devoid of neighbors, and his circle of acquaintances appeared surprisingly thin despite his visible presence in the district for months. Yet, the nature of his life—eccentric and detached—meant he likely possessed no conventional social network. Hours after news of the murder spread, the silence was absolute; no curious neighbors, no concerned friends had knocked at the door. This vacuum of local knowledge, rather than diminishing the intrigue, sharpened it. Consequently, Mr. Gryce welcomed the departure of the swarm of reporters, medical examiners, and officials who had gathered since the coroner's arrival, granting him a brief, albeit heavy, solitude over the scene.

Before succumbing to exhaustion, however, two critical matters demanded resolution. First, he needed to interrogate the boy, Jake, regarding the woman who had intercepted him earlier that day. Passing a chair where the child sat dozing, Gryce roused him gently. "Come, Jake, it's time to wake up. I need you to take me to the exact spot where that lady stopped you."

Jake, groggy and searching for his hat, hesitated. "I'd like to see my mother first," he mumbled. "She'll be frantic. I've never been away so long."

"Your mother knows your whereabouts," Gryce reassured him smoothly. "I sent word hours ago. She reported you as an obedient lad who never lies."

"She's a good mother," Jake insisted, his tone warming. "If I didn't treat her right, I'd be just like Father." He pulled his cap down low. "I'm ready."

Leading the way into the cool night air, Gryce guided the boy through the sleeping streets until they reached a bustling thoroughfare. They halted before a dimly lit shop window where Jake pointed to two stuffed frogs locked in a miniature sword duel—the very display that had captured his attention when the mysterious woman approached. Gryce watched the boy intently, sensing the weight of the testimony more than the whimsical toys.

"Was she a pretty lady?" Gryce asked quietly.

Jake scratched his head, looking confused. "She scared me," he admitted. "Her clothes... oh, they were gorgeous!"

"And she was young, carrying flowers, looking troubled?" Gryce pressed, recalling his initial impression. "What? Not young, no flowers, and not trembling?"

Jake shook his head, bewildered. "Well, she looked troubled, I suppose. But she wasn't crying. When she spoke, her grip on my arm held more emotion than her voice. She just dragged me to the drugstore. If she hadn't paid me first, I'd have wriggled free, sir. I like money; I don't earn much of it."

As Gryce moved forward, muttering to himself, "Not young," his suspicion shifted. An older mistress of Mr. Adams was often far more perilous than a youthful paramour. At the drugstore, Gryce stopped Jake again. "Show me exactly where she waited."

The boy indicated the precise spot with eager precision. "And were you standing there when you returned?"

"No, sir. She vanished while I was inside."

"Do you remember if she headed up or down the street?"

"I kept one eye on her," Jake explained, his voice dropping. "I feared she'd follow me back in, and my arm hurt too much for another squeeze. As she started to move, I stepped closer. I saw her walk to the curb and raise her hand. But there were no cars for quite a while."

Gryce's brow furrowed less. "So she hailed a carriage or a hack. Did you see any empty vehicles?"

The boy shook his head. His observations ended there. Recognizing the limit of the child's memory, Gryce handed Jake to a waiting assistant and returned to the ominous house where the tragedy unfolded.

At the door, Styles informed him, "Yes, sir, a young man is waiting. He calls himself Hines; he's an electrician."

"That is the man I require," Gryce said briskly. "Where is he?"

"In the parlor, sir. Anyone upstairs?"

"No, everyone else has departed. You go up; I'll guard the entrance."

Styles ascended the stairs, leaving Gryce alone in the parlor. Soon, a dapper young man with an intelligent gaze rose to greet him. "You requested me," Hines stated. After a brief exchange, Gryce led him into Mr. Adams's study, where the body lay temporarily removed. A single candle, placed per Mr. Gryce's orders, cast a gentle light near the door. However, Gryce immediately moved to the central table, pressing a button. Hines complied, extinguishing the candle. Suddenly, the room plunged into darkness, only to be illuminated instantly by a brilliant, deep violet glow emanating from the table itself. Both men stared, stunned by the theatricality and the inexplicable mechanism.

"That is merely one filter," Gryce noted calmly. "Press another, and the hue shifts to pink. This yields green, this white, and this a sickly yellow—I daresay it suits neither of us. Examine the wiring; is there anything unusual?"

Hines began his inspection. Aside from the evident amateur craftsmanship, he found nothing defective, save for the flawless operation of the device. Gryce expressed his dismay. "He constructed it himself?"

"Undoubtedly, or someone with equal ignorance of modern wiring techniques," Hines replied.

Gryce gestured toward a bookshelf. "Check these volumes. Could an amateur with adequate knowledge assemble such a system using only these texts?"

Hines glanced at the spines without opening them. "A man with a deft hand and a scientific bent could replicate this, and likely exceed it, given the resources here. It requires pure aptitude."

"Glad to hear it," Gryce said dryly, his voice betraying a hint of resignation. "A man obsessed with mechanical gadgets often squanders fortune on frivolities. Observe this birdcage. Perched high, inaccessible without a ladder, it clearly relies on some contrivance for vertical movement. Locate the mechanism."

After a brief, fruitless search, Hines discovered a second button concealed within the wall tapestries. Upon pressing it, the cage descended slowly until it rested within Gryce's reach.

"We shall not release this poor fellow again," Hines murmured, lifting the cage. "It appears to be an English starling, quite rare here. Listen—"

The bird, seemingly aware of the gravity of the moment, fluttered nervously before settling into a quiet slumber, perhaps unnerved by the intense scrutiny of the elderly gentleman holding it. Gryce placed the cage on the floor and, idly testing the room's functions, pressed a third button he had previously overlooked.

However, the expected shift in lighting did not occur. Instead, a harsh, bilious yellow glare persisted. The recent manipulation of the switches had evidently disrupted the circuit. Disappointed, Gryce prepared to leave, but halted abruptly, pointing to a new development that had escaped their notice. The heavy panel sealing the doorway to the antechamber—the only exit from these chambers—was beginning to contract.

From a span of three yards, it shrank to a foot; from a foot, to an inch; from an inch, to mere millimeters.

"Well," Hines chuckled nervously, "that is indeed the invention of a man with plenty of leisure. Seated comfortably, he can seal himself in without shouting at a deaf servant or tripping over rugs. I suppose I admire the efficiency, though..." He trailed off, catching the grave look on Gryce's face. "...though the mechanism seems oddly constructed. It isn't wood, as a sensible door should be, but..."

"Steel," Gryce finished, his tone grim. "This is the most bizarre element yet. It increasingly suggests that Mr. Adams was deluded regarding electrical applications."

"And it implies we are trapped here," Hines added, his smile vanishing. "I see no handle to retract this slide."

"Naturally, there is another switch for that," Gryce dismissed lightly.

They searched for five minutes in vain.

"If you have no objection," Gryce suggested after a pause, "I shall restore a brighter atmosphere. Yellow is ill-suited to our current plight."

"Rose, please," Hines urged. "Unless there is someone on the other side of this steel slab, we risk remaining here until dawn."

"Theoretically, the man upstairs may hear us, but what does this contrivance signify? To me, it carries a sinister weight, especially considering how every window in these two rooms has been bricked up nearly to the ceiling."

"Precisely. A peculiar arrangement. Before we speculate further, I require fresh air. Confinement stifles my faculties."

While Hines voiced his frustration, Gryce examined the massive steel barrier. It had been custom-fitted with meticulous care to match the curvature of the walls—a costly endeavor. This detail deepened Gryce's unease; he regarded the smooth surface with growing distrust.
