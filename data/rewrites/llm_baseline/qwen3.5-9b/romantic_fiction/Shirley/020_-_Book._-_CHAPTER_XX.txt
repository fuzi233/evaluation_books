The two girls encountered no living soul on their journey back to the rectory. They slipped inside without making a sound, ascending the stairs unheard, guided only by the faint grey light of the breaking dawn. Shirley hastened straight to her bed. Although the room was unfamiliar—she had never slept at the rectory before—and although the events of the previous night constituted a spectacle of excitement and terror unlike anything she had ever witnessed, scarcely had her head touched the pillow when a deep, restorative sleep claimed her, calming her senses instantly.

Perfect health was Shirley's enviable fortune. Warm-hearted and deeply sympathetic though she was, she possessed no nervous constitution; powerful emotions could stir and sway her without draining her spirit. The storm troubled and shook her while it raged, but it left her elasticity unbroken and her freshness untouched. As every day brought new stimulation, so every night offered renewing rest. Caroline watched her sleeping form, reading the serenity of her mind reflected in the beauty of her peaceful face.

For herself, possessing a different temperament, Caroline could not sleep. Even the mundane excitement of the tea-drinking and school gathering would have been enough to keep her restless all night; the impact of the terrible drama she had just witnessed was unlikely to fade from her mind for days. It was futile even to attempt lying down; she remained seated beside Shirley, counting the slow passing minutes and watching the June sun climb higher in the sky.

Life wasted quickly during such vigils, which Caroline had lately kept far too often. During these watches, the mind, finding no pleasant sustenance, no manna of hope, and no honey of joyful memories, tried to survive on the meager diet of wishes. Finding neither delight nor support therein, and feeling itself ready to perish from craving, it turned to philosophy, to resolution, to resignation. It called upon these gods for aid, but in vain—it was unheard, unhelped, and languished.

Caroline was a Christian; therefore, in her trouble, she framed prayers according to the Christian creed, offering them with deep earnestness, begging for patience, strength, and relief. Yet, as everyone knows, this world is the scene of trial and probation. Since no favorable results had yet followed her petitions, it seemed to her that they were ignored and rejected. Sometimes she believed God had turned His face away from her. At moments she felt like a Calvinist, sinking into the abyss of religious despair, seeing the doom of reprobation darken over her.

Most people experience periods in their lives when they feel thus forsaken—when, having long hoped against hope, they see the day of fulfillment endlessly deferred, causing their hearts to truly sicken. This is a terrible hour, yet it is often the darkest point preceding the rising of the day—the turning of the year when the icy January wind sweeps across the waste, carrying simultaneously the dirge of departing winter and the prophecy of coming spring. The perishing birds, however, cannot understand the blast before which they shiver; nor can the suffering soul, in the climax of its affliction, recognize the dawn of its deliverance. Yet, let anyone who grieves still cling fast to love and faith in God. God will never deceive, nor finally desert Him. "Whom He loveth, He chasteneth." These words are true and should not be forgotten.

The household eventually came alive; the servants were up, and the shutters below were thrown open. As Caroline left the couch—which had felt like a thorny seat to her—she felt that revival of spirits that the return of day and action grants to all but the wholly despairing or actually dying. She dressed herself as usual with care, arranging her hair and attire so meticulously that no trace of the desolation she felt inwardly would be visible outwardly. When both were dressed, she looked as fresh as Shirley, though Miss Keeldar's eyes were lively, whereas Miss Helstone's were languid.

"To-day I shall have much to say to Moore," were Shirley's first words. One could see in her face that life was filled with interest, expectation, and occupation for her. "He will have to undergo cross-examination," she added. "I dare say he thinks he has outwitted me cleverly. And this is the way men deal with women—still concealing danger from them, thinking, I suppose, to spare them pain. They imagined we knew little of our whereabouts last night. We *know* they little conjectured where we were. Men, I believe, fancy women's minds are something like those of children. Now, that is a mistake."

She said this while standing before the glass, training her naturally waved hair into curls by twining it around her fingers. She picked up the theme again five minutes later as Caroline fastened her dress and clasped her girdle.

"If men could see us as we really are, they would be a little amazed; but the cleverest, the acutest men are often under an illusion regarding women. They do not read them in a true light; they misapprehend them, both for good and evil. Their ideal woman is a queer thing, half doll, half angel; their wicked woman is almost always a fiend. Then to hear them fall into ecstasies over each other's creations—worshipping the heroine of such a poem, novel, or drama—thinking it fine and divine! Fine and divine it may be, but often quite artificial—false as the rose in my best bonnet there. If I spoke all I think on this point, if I gave my real opinion of some first-rate female characters in first-rate works, where should I be? Dead under a cairn of avenging stones in half an hour."

"Shirley, you chatter so I can't fasten you. Be still. And, after all, authors' heroines are almost as good as authoresses' heroes."

"Not at all. Women read men more truly than men read women. I'll prove that in a magazine article someday when I have time; only it will never be inserted. It will be 'declined with thanks,' and left for me at the publisher's."

"To be sure. You could not write cleverly enough. You don't know enough. You are not learned, Shirley."

"God knows I can't contradict you, Cary; I'm as ignorant as a stone. There's one comfort, however: you are not much better."

They descended to breakfast.

"I wonder how Mrs. Pryor and Hortense Moore have passed the night," said Caroline as she prepared the coffee. "Selfish being that I am, I never thought of either of them until just now. They will have heard all the tumult; Fieldhead and the cottage are so near. And Hortense is timid in such matters—so, no doubt, is Mrs. Pryor."

"Take my word for it, Lina; Moore will have contrived to get his sister out of the way. She went home with Miss Mann. He will have quartered her there for the night. As to Mrs. Pryor, I admit I am uneasy about her; but in another half-hour we will be with her."

By this time, the news of what had occurred at the Hollow had spread throughout the neighborhood. Fanny, who had gone to Fieldhead for milk, returned in panting haste with tidings that there had been a battle in the night at Mr. Moore's mill, and that some claimed twenty men were killed. Eliza, during Fanny's absence, had been informed by the butcher's boy that the mill had been burned to the ground. Both women rushed into the parlor to announce these terrible facts to the ladies, concluding their clear and accurate narrative with the assertion that they were certain the master must have been involved all along. He and Thomas, the clerk, they were confident, must have gone last night to join Mr. Moore and the soldiers. Mr. Malone, too, had not been heard of at his lodgings since yesterday afternoon, and Joe Scott's wife and family were in the greatest distress, wondering what had become of their head.

Scarcely had this information been imparted when a knock at the kitchen door announced the Fieldhead errand-boy, arriving in hot haste with a note from Mrs. Pryor. It was hurriedly written and urged Miss Keeldar to return immediately, as the neighborhood and the house seemed likely to be in total confusion, requiring orders that only the mistress of the hall could regulate. In a postscript, it was entreated that Miss Helstone not be left alone at the rectory; she was advised instead to accompany Miss Keeldar.

"There are not two opinions on that head," said Shirley as she tied on her own bonnet, then ran to fetch Caroline's.

"But what will Fanny and Eliza do? And if my uncle returns?"

"Your uncle will not return yet; he has other fish to fry. He will be galloping backwards and forwards from Briarfield to Stilbro' all day, rousing the magistrates in the court-house and the officers at the barracks. Fanny and Eliza can have Joe Scott's and the clerk's wives to bear them company. Besides, of course, there is no real danger to be apprehended now. Weeks will elapse before the rioters can again rally or plan any other attempt; and I am much mistaken if Moore and Mr. Helstone will not take advantage of last night's outbreak to quell them altogether. They will frighten the authorities of Stilbro' into energetic measures. I only hope they will not be too severe—not pursue the discomfited too relentlessly."
