Time, that relentless healer, even mends the self-inflicted wounds of shy young men doomed by nature to solitude. Winter evenings arrived, and with them, work—a fine drowning pool for private sorrows. My "apartment," now warmed by a handful of fire and lit by two candles, became almost inviting. I stocked up on paper, pens, and ink. What more can a writer do, after all? He’s merely the would-be vessel—will the spirits of inspiration use him or toss him aside?

London sprawled before me, a city of a million characters, each with their own dramas, mysteries, tragedies, and jokes. The raw material was everywhere, begging only for observation and a halfway truthful report. Yet the notion of writing about what I actually *knew* never crossed my mind. No, my tales were of rosy-cheeked milkmaids and sturdy yokels. My scenes played out in windmills, on misty moors, or in crumbling granges. I suspect this phase of artistic delusion is common among young scribblers.

A gentle melancholy draped my stories. Sentiment was all the rage back then, and like any novice, I followed fashion slavishly. To be a heroine in one of my tales was, more often than not, a death sentence. No matter how vigorously her curls bounced, how merrily her laughter rang, or how fawn-like her agility, she was doomed to an early grave. Snowdrops and primroses (botany hastily skimmed from *Our Garden Favourites, Illustrated*) sprouted around her tombstone as if in a hothouse. If the setting was coastal, the sea breeze would, with suspicious enthusiasm, make her churchyard its favorite playground. Years later, a white-haired man would visit, leading small children by the hand, and regale them with the dismal tale—a thoroughly depressing bedtime story.

Occasionally, for variety, I’d kill off the gentleman instead. His final soliloquy would murmur something about it being "better this way." Snowdrops were beyond his reach, of course—his grave usually lay in some godforsaken patch of African veldt or scorching desert. Describing such exotic locales required trips to the British Museum reading room, where I’d squint at maps and hope no one noticed my frantic note-taking.

Pitiful little fledglings, these stories of mine! And yet, no matter how often I shoved them from the nest, they fluttered back to me—creased, coffee-stained, and smelling of rejection. Only one person ever admired them, wept over them: myself.

I tried every strategy. Sometimes I’d send them off with a curt note: *Take it or leave it.* Other times, I’d attach a groveling plea for consideration. Occasionally, I’d name a price—five guineas, cheque crossed—or dangle the bait of a free sample (*first contribution gratis!*). Once, in a fit of sarcasm, I preemptively included my own rejection letter, penned in brutal mock-editorial scrawl. Another time, I played the humble novice, begging for criticism.

Not a single one found a home. A month, a week, sometimes mere days later, they’d return. I never lost a manuscript. I *wished* I had—just for variety.

I despised the little housemaid who’d burst in, joyfully waving my returned envelopes between thumb and apron-covered finger. Her chronic sniff struck me as contemptuous. If I intercepted the postman myself, I swore he smirked. Tearing open the envelopes, I’d curse the stories, call them trash, sometimes fling them into the fire—only to snatch them out half-scorched, smooth their pages, and reread them. The editors couldn’t *possibly* have seen them. No, some jealous underling must have intercepted them. Or they’d arrived at the wrong moment. Their triumph was coming. Rewriting the first and last pages, I’d send them out again, hope rekindled.

Meanwhile, recognizing that a would-be literary warrior must also shine in society, I set about polishing my social graces. Smoking and drinking proved the hardest hurdles. Not out of moral superiority—my body simply rebelled. At school, I’d occasionally attempted a cigarette, only to turn green and abandon the effort, cloaking my failure in sanctimonious mutterings about *bad for growing boys*. Deep down, I knew the truth: I was a coward hiding behind virtue’s skirts.

Likewise, I’d sipped my mother’s claret and wondered why anyone voluntarily consumed such stuff. Watching grown men wince at it had been reassuring—clearly, a taste for liquor wasn’t innate. But now, a gentleman adrift in the world, my education could no longer be postponed.

Fashion then demanded that artists—especially literary ones—smoke and drink to excess. A teetotal genius would’ve been dismissed as a fraud. I might not yet be sure of the *genius* part, but the smoking and drinking? Those, at least, I could fake.

I approached the task methodically. Smoking was best practiced on Saturday nights, leaving Sunday to walk off the effects. Persistence paid off: I learned to puff a cigarette convincingly. But mediocrity wasn’t enough. The cigarette conquered, I advanced to cigars. My first remains etched in memory—as does every man’s. It was at a smoking concert in the Islington Drill Hall, courtesy of Minikin. Dizziness struck. Unsure whether the cigar or the stifling room was to blame, I slipped outside into a courtyard. A low wall divided it from a neighboring garden. The cigar was clearly the culprit. My instinct was to fling it away, but duty demanded perseverance.

Climbing onto the wall, I resolved to walk its length. Success would prove my mastery; failure would excuse me from further torment. Two yards in, I found myself draped over the wall like a discarded coat, legs dangling one side, head lolling the other. Inclination whispered vows of eternal abstinence. Pride hissed back: *What others can do, so can you. Victory comes through suffering.*

Liquor proved even harder. Whiskey tasted like hair oil (which, once accidentally swallowed, I’d found *less* revolting). Twice a week, I forced down a glass of beer, standing over myself like a stern parent until the last bitter drop was gone. As reward, I’d suck on chocolates, assuring myself that one day I’d *enjoy* this ordeal and thank my past self for the discipline.

In saner pursuits, I made better progress. Shyness ebbed—slowly. The trick, I found, was not to fight it but to laugh at it. The world’s indifference sanded down my sharper edges. People became less terrifying; I became less terrified of them.

Fate, in guiding me to this particular house in Nelson Square, had done me a favor. We were a curious menagerie under its leaky roof. Mrs. Peedles, our landlady (who shared the basement with the slavey), had once trod the boards with Charles Keane’s company at the old Princess’s Theatre. True, London had never fully appreciated her—actor-managers with wives were *complicated*, she hinted. But in the provinces? Ah, there she’d been Juliet, Amy Robsart, Mrs. Haller in *The Stranger*—the whole grand parade of *legitimate* heroines.

Doubters were silenced with yellowed handbills: *"On Tuesday Evening!!! By Special Desire!!! Blessington’s Theatre!!!"* If pressed, she’d burst into Lady Macbeth’s sleepwalking scene or Ophelia’s mad ravings.

Now, she was a faded, wheezing little woman, prone to sudden descents onto the nearest chair for tearful recitals of her woes. Unaware of this habit, she fancied herself unusually cheerful.

*"But there, fretting’s no good. We must grin and bear things in this world,"* she’d declare, wiping her eyes. *"Better to laugh than cry, I always say."* And then, to prove it, she’d laugh—a sound like a door hinge in need of oil.

Years of stair-climbing had left her perpetually breathless. *"You don’t know ’ow to breathe,"* our second-floor front—a kindly soul—once told her. *"You gargle it. Swallow it proper, like it’s medicine."* He’d pressed a handkerchief to her mouth, but to no avail. *"There ain’t room inside me,"* she’d wheezed.

Bells ruled her life. Conversation was filler between their rings.

One Christmas, a whiskey bottle fell into my hands—a gift from a commercial traveler. Not liking the stuff myself, I reserved it for Mrs. Peedles’ moments of collapse. Her face would brighten. *"Ah, well, I don’t mind if I do. It’s a poor heart that never rejoices."* Thus fortified, she’d recount tales of my predecessors—young men who’d occupied my room before meeting grim fates. One, a poet, now languished in Portland for forgery. Another, a brilliant scholar, had ended in Hanwell Lunatic Asylum.

*"Always the deserving ones,"* she’d sigh. *"I don’t know why."*

I was relieved when that whiskey bottle emptied. A second might’ve driven me to suicide.

Mr. Peedles existed—just not for Mrs. Peedles. He’d been "general utility" at the Princess’s—a fine figure of a man, easily led astray (especially by minxes). Mrs. Peedles spoke of "general utilities" with bitterness.

On weekdays, she wore a single black dress and cap. But Sundays transformed her. She’d preserved her stage wardrobe—Hermia’s flowing robes, Lady Teazle’s brocade, even paste shoes and tinsel jewels. Beneath a mackintosh, she’d sneak to visit fellow has-beens, her cheeks flushed with excitement, her eyebrows (ordinarily faint) suddenly bold. Only her thin grey hair and work-worn hands remained for identification.

Next door lived a law-writer and his wife—old, poor, and worn down by relentless bad luck. Illness, debt, a friend’s betrayal—their history was a dull recital of misfortune without moral or meaning. Now they worked listlessly, indifferent to fate, their sole luxury an occasional glass of beer. Nights, I’d hear his pen scratching through the thin wall.

He became my nightmare vision of the future. I’d tiptoe past his door, dreading encounters. Hadn’t *he* once dreamed? Might I, too, end a shabby clerk in a four-room Brixton house, with a fading wife and squalling children?

Our second-floor front was Jarman—Young London incarnate. Noisy, brash, generous, cunning, and utterly shameless. He woke like an explosion, bathed like a whale, and whistled through breakfast. His comings and goings were a symphony of slammed doors and shouted meal instructions. His absence left the house eerily silent.

Sometimes he’d march into my room with a supper tray, ignoring hints that I was busy. *"You fire away, Shakespeare Redivivus,"* he’d say. *"I’ll be quiet."* His idea of quiet was muttering melodramas in the corner: *"Ah, I ’ave killed ’er father! Stolen ’er fortune! Yet she loves me not!"*

Finding work impossible, I’d listen instead. His chief topic? Love affairs—numerous and disastrous. *"Girls,"* he’d declare, *"are like shop goods. Bright and shiny till you buy ’em, then the paint peels. Not that there ain’t good ’uns—but you’ve got to look past the advertising."*

What he sought was *"a first-class article,"* not something that would crumble at the first wear. *"They’re out there,"* he’d say cheerfully. *"Just not the ones shouting about it."*
