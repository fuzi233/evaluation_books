Margaret began to suspect that offers, in whatever house they were made, had a cruel likeness: they came unbidden, struck like a sudden draught through a door flung wide, and left the air altered long after the speaker had gone. She tried, as women do when the mind is bruised, to arrange her feelings as one might set in order a cupboard after some careless hand has overturned it—putting back what belonged together, wiping away what had spilled, finding solace in the familiar labour of naming and placing. Yet the comparison she made—between Henry Lennox and John Thornton—refused to lie neatly.

With Mr. Lennox, it had been as if a friend had stepped, by accident and for a moment, across a chalk-line neither of them had meant to cross; regret had come quickly on the trespass, and she could pity him even while she shrank. With Mr. Thornton there had been no such gentle prelude. Their acquaintance had been all collision—argument upon argument, her words flung back from him as from a rock face; and what troubled her now was not merely that he had spoken of love, but that he had done so as if love were another form of mastery. He had not seemed to care for her opinions because they were hers; only because they resisted him. And then, in that strange, wild way—passion bare as a blade—he had told her that he loved her, had loved her, would love her still.

It had crossed her mind at first—like the first sourness that rises when milk is near turning—that his offer might have been goaded out of him by what she had done the day before; that he, like others, might misread a woman’s act of mercy and courage into some foolish confession. But even before he left the room, and certainly within five minutes of his leaving, conviction rose clear and hard: he did love her. Not a fancy, not a whim, not any gentle-growing attachment that might be turned aside by prudence; it was fixed, deliberate, enduring. And Margaret shrank as if a great hand had come down upon her life—strong, alien, and repugnant to all she had been taught to call peace.

She tried to hide from the thought of him, but it followed, importunate as a scent that clings to clothes after fire: you may step out into fresh air, yet the smoke is in your hair, in the folds at the throat, in the cuffs. His voice seemed still to hang in the chamber, and, worse, the remembrance of his eyes—deep, intent, passionate—made hers fall again within her mind, as they had fallen before him.

How dared he say he would love her still, even if she shook him off with contempt? How dared he threaten her with constancy—as if her refusal were a small thing, a surface thing, that could not alter the rock beneath? Sharp speeches came to her now—keen, decisive, such as would have struck and ended the matter—only they came too late, when there was no ear to receive them. The impression of the interview lay upon her like a horror in a dream that remains after waking: you rub your eyes, you force a stiff smile, you busy your hands—yet in some corner of the room the thing crouches, listening, and you dare not name it aloud.

She rose at last from where she had stood as if rooted. She went to the window and flung it open. The air outside was hot, thick with the town’s summer heaviness; yet it relieved her like water dashed upon a burning skin. The house was hushed in the noonday stillness kept for an invalid’s stolen sleep—no cheerful clatter, no bright domestic sounds, only that strained quiet which makes a footstep seem an intrusion. Margaret opened the door with an impulse as sudden as her need: she would not be alone.

Then came, like the click of a latch within her mind, the remembrance of the message sent the night before. Bessy Higgins. Of course. The thought gave her something plain to do, and she took her bonnet and went quickly.

Bessy lay on the settle drawn close to the fire, though the day outside was sultry and the air in the room already close. The hearth had that faint, stale smell of yesterday’s coal—dusty, iron-edged—mixed with the more human odours of sickness: camphor, damp linen, and the sour-sweet taint of breath that has laboured too long. The poor girl was stretched flat, as if the least lift of her chest were an effort, her face turned a little away, her eyelids heavy.

Margaret, taking in at once how the posture cramped her breathing, did not waste words. She slid her arm behind Bessy’s shoulders and raised her gently, arranging the pillows with the quiet practised care of a woman who has nursed: one pillow under the shoulder-blades so the lungs might open; another to keep the head from falling back; the thin quilt drawn so it warmed without pressing. It was homely work—like trussing a fowl so it roasts evenly, or setting a dough to rise where the air is kindest—small adjustments that decide whether there will be ease or pain.

“I thought I should na’ ha’ seen yo’ again,” Bessy said at last, turning her large wistful gaze on Margaret’s face.

“I’m afraid you’re much worse,” Margaret answered softly. “But I could not come yesterday—my mother was so ill—for many reasons.” She coloured as she spoke, the blood rising with the memory she would not taste again.

Bessy watched her, and the corner of her mouth twitched, not unkindly. “Yo’d m’appen think I went beyond my place in sending Mary for yo’. But th’ wraglin’ and th’ loud voices had just torn me to pieces, and I thought when father left—oh! if I could just hear her voice, reading me some words o’ peace and promise, I could die away into th’ silence and rest o’ God, just as a baby’s hushed up to sleep by its mother’s lullaby.”

The room, for all its poverty, held its own acoustics—thin walls, a boarded floor that answered every shift of weight, the small tick of a clock that seemed loud in the pauses. Outside, far off, lay Milton’s ceaseless roar—mills, carts, iron on stone—like the sea heard from a sick-bed. Here, within, every sound was intimate: Margaret’s breath, the faint crackle of the fire, the rasp of Bessy’s lungs. Margaret felt, with a sudden tenderness, how much this girl craved not only words, but the very hush and cadence of a human voice laid upon the din.

“Shall I read you a chapter now?” she asked.

“Ay, do,” said Bessy. “M’appen I shan’t listen to th’ sense at first; it’ll seem far away. But when yo’ come to words I like—to th’ comforting texts—it’ll seem close in my ear, and go through me, as it were.”

Margaret opened the Bible, and began. Her voice was low and even, like water poured from a steady hand. Yet Bessy tossed to and fro. Each moment of attention seemed to wrench her into double restlessness after; her fingers plucked at the edge of the quilt as if it were a thread she could pull to unpick her pain.

At last she broke out, the words coming harsh and breathy. “Don’t go on reading. It’s no use. I’m blaspheming all th’ time in my mind, wi’ thinking angrily on what canna be helped.—Yo’d hear of th’ riot, m’appen, yesterday at Marlborough Mills? Thornton’s factory, yo’ know.”

Margaret’s heart gave a hard beat. “Your father was not there, was he?” she asked, and felt the heat leap into her cheeks.

“Not he. He’d ha’ given his right hand if it had never come to pass. It’s that that’s fretting me. He’s fairly knocked down in his mind by it. It’s no use telling him, fools will always break out o’ bounds. Yo’ never saw a man so downhearted as he is.”

“But why?” Margaret said. “I don’t understand.”

Bessy spoke with pauses, as if each sentence cost breath. “Why, yo’ see, he’s a committee-man on this special strike. Th’ Union appointed him because—though I say it as shouldn’t say it—he’s reckoned a deep chap, and true to th’ back-bone. And he and t’other committee-men laid their plans. They were to hou’d together through thick and thin; what th’ major part thought, t’others were to think, whether they would or no. And above all there was to be no going again th’ law of th’ land.

“Folk would go with ’em if they saw ’em striving and starving wi’ dumb patience; but if there was once any noise o’ fighting and struggling—even wi’ knobsticks—all was up, as they know by th’ experience of many and many a time before. They would try and get speech o’ th’ knobsticks, and coax ’em, and reason wi’ ’em, and m’appen warn ’em off; but whatever came, th’ Committee charged all members o’ th’ Union to lie down and die, if need were, without striking a blow; and then they reckoned they were sure o’ carrying th’ public with ’em.

“And besides all that, Committee knew they were right in their demand, and they didn’t want to have right all mixed up wi’ wrong, till folk can’t separate it—no more nor I can th’ physic-powder from th’ jelly yo’ gave me to mix it in; jelly’s much th’ biggest, but powder tastes it all through.”

Even in her weakness, Bessy’s mind flew to the mouth—to taste as truth. Margaret remembered the jelly—sweet, trembling, made to carry bitterness without protest—and felt how apt the image was. Wrong, once stirred in, flavoured the whole.

Bessy’s voice thinned. “Well, I’ve told yo’ at length about this’n, but I am tired out. Yo’ just think for yoursel’ what it mun be for father to have a’ his work undone, and by such a fool as Boucher, who must needs go right again th’ orders o’ Committee, and ruin th’ strike, just as bad as if he meant to be a Judas. Eh! but father giv’d it him last night! He went so far as to say he’d go and tell police where they might find th’ ringleader o’ th’ riot; he’d give him up to th’ mill-owners to do what they would wi’ him. He’d show th’ world that th’ real leaders o’ th’ strike were not such as Boucher, but steady thoughtful men; good hands, and good citizens, friendly to law and judgment, who’d uphold order; who only wanted their right wage, and wouldn’t work, even though they starved, till they got ’em; but who would ne’er injure property or life. For—” she dropped her voice, as if the very walls might carry scandal—“they do say, that Boucher threw a stone at Thornton’s sister, that welly killed her.”

“That’s not true,” Margaret said quickly. The words were out before she could school them, as if her tongue had leapt to defend what she had seen. “It was not Boucher that threw the stone.” Her face went first red, then white.

“Yo’d be there then, were yo’?” Bessy asked languidly, too worn for surprise.

“Yes. Never mind.” Margaret forced herself quieter. “Go on. Only—it was not Boucher.”

Bessy’s eyes closed again. “He did na’ speak words. He were all in such a tremble wi’ spent passion, I could na’ bear to look at him. I heard his breath coming quick, and at one time I thought he were sobbing. But when father said he’d give him up to police, he gave a great cry, and struck father on th’ face wi’ his closed fist, and he off like lightning.

“Father were stunned wi’ th’ blow at first—for all Boucher were weak wi’ passion and wi’ clemming. He sat down a bit, and put his hand afore his eyes; and then made for th’ door. I dunno where I got strength, but I threw mysel’ o’er th’ settle and clung to him. ‘Father, father!’ said I. ‘Thou’ll never go peach on that poor clemmed man. I’ll never leave go on thee, till thou sayst thou wunnot.’

“‘Dunnot be a fool,’ says he. ‘Words come readier than deeds to most men. I never thought o’ telling th’ police on him; though by G—he deserves it, and I should na’ ha’ minded if some one else had done th’ dirty work, and got him clapped up. But now he has strucken me, I could do it less nor ever, for it would be getting other men to take up my quarrel. But if ever he gets well o’er this clemming, and is in good condition, he and I’ll have an up and down fight, purring an’ a’, and I’ll see what I can do for him.’”

Bessy’s breath came short with the telling; the words were like hard crusts she had to chew and swallow. “And so father shook me off—for indeed I was low and faint enough—and his face were all clay-white where it weren’t bloody, and turned me sick to look at. And I know not if I slept or waked, or were in a dead swoon, till Mary come in; and I telled her to fetch yo’ to me.

“And now dunnot talk to me, but just read out th’ chapter. I’m easier in my mind for having spit it out; but I want some thoughts o’ th’ world that’s far away to take th’ weary taste of it out o’ my mouth. Read me not a sermon chapter, but a story chapter; they’ve pictures in ’em, which I see when my eyes are shut. Read about th’ New Heavens, and th’ New Earth; and m’appen I’ll forget this.”

Margaret read again, softly. The familiar cadences fell into the small room like a hand laid upon a fevered brow. Though Bessy’s eyes stayed shut, she listened for a while; tears gathered on her lashes and stood there, heavy as dew. At last sleep took her—broken, startled, with muttered pleadings that rose and fell like a frightened child’s talk in dreams. Margaret drew the covering up, tucking it round the thin shoulders, and sat a moment longer, her own mind too full for prayer.

When she rose to go, she felt the pull of two duties—the dying girl and the sick mother at home—like a cord tightened at both ends. The streets outside brought their own assault: the clank and rumble, the shrill cries, the harsh beat of labour. The very air seemed to ring. She hurried back, craving the comparative quiet of her own rooms.

Mrs. Hale was in the drawing-room when Margaret returned. It was one of her better days, and her voice had that weak brightness which illness sometimes borrows from temporary ease. She praised the water-bed warmly; it had been more like the beds at Sir John Beresford’s than anything she had slept on since they left the south. There was, she declared, a lost art in bed-making. Feathers might still be had, certainly; yet somehow beds were not what they had been in her youth.

Mr. Hale suggested gently that something of the merit of former beds might be due to the activity of youth, which gives a relish to rest. Mrs. Hale received this with the affronted air of a woman whose suffering is being explained away.

“No indeed, Mr. Hale, it was those beds at Sir John’s. Now, Margaret, you’re young enough, and go about i’ th’ day; are the beds comfortable? I appeal to you. Do they give you a feeling of perfect repose when you lie down upon them; or rather, don’t you toss about, and try in vain to find an easy position, and wake in the morning as tired as when you went to bed?”

Margaret laughed in spite of herself. “To tell the truth, mamma, I’ve never thought about my bed at all, what kind it is. I’m so sleepy at night that if I only lie down anywhere, I fall off directly. So I don’t think I’m a competent witness. Besides—you know I never had the opportunity of trying Sir John Beresford’s beds. I never was at Oxenham.”

“Were you not? Oh, no—to be sure.” Mrs. Hale’s face softened, and her voice took on the colour of remembrance. “It was poor darling Fred I took with me, I remember. I only went to Oxenham once after I was married—to your Aunt Shaw’s wedding; and poor little Fred was the baby then. And I know Dixon did not like changing from lady’s maid to nurse, and I was afraid that if I took her near her old home, and amongst her own people, she might want to leave me. But poor baby was taken ill at Oxenham, with his teething; and, what with my being a great deal with Anna just before her marriage, and not being very strong myself, Dixon had more of the charge of him than she ever had before; and it made her so fond of him—and she was so proud when he would turn away from every one and cling to her—that I don’t believe she ever thought of leaving me again; though it was very different from what she’d been accustomed to.

“Poor Fred! Everybody loved him. He was born with the gift of winning hearts. It makes me think very badly of Captain Reid when I know that he disliked my own dear boy. I think it a certain proof he had a bad heart. Ah! Your poor father, Margaret. He has left the room. He can’t bear to hear Fred spoken of.”

“I love to hear about him, mamma,” Margaret said, and the words were true; they were like bread in a house grown hungry. “Tell me all you like; you never can tell me too much. Tell me what he was like as a baby.”

Mrs. Hale smiled through the beginning of tears. “Why, Margaret, you must not be hurt, but he was much prettier than you were. I remember, when I first saw you in Dixon’s arms, I said, ‘Dear, what an ugly little thing!’ And she said, ‘It’s not every child that’s like Master Fred, bless him!’ Dear! how well I remember it. Then I could have had Fred in my arms every minute of the day, and his cot was close by my bed; and now—now, Margaret—I don’t know where my boy is, and sometimes I think I shall never see him again.”

Margaret sat on a low stool by the sofa, took her mother’s hand, and kissed it, stroking it as one might soothe a child. Mrs. Hale wept openly, without shame. The room held the sound of it—soft, persistent—until at last she drew herself upright, stiff with effort, and turned on Margaret with tearful solemnity.

“Margaret, if I can get better—if God lets me have a chance of recovery—it must be through seeing my son Frederick once more. It will waken up all the poor springs of health left in me.”

She paused, gathering breath, as if what remained were a step into a place she feared.

“And, Margaret, if I am to die—if I am one of those appointed to die before many weeks are over—I must see my child first. I cannot think how it must be managed; but I charge you, Margaret, as you yourself hope for comfort in your last illness, bring him to me that I may bless him. Only for five minutes, Margaret. There could be no danger in five minutes. Oh, Margaret, let me see him before I die!”

Margaret did not weigh the reasonableness; reason has no dominion over the pleadings of the dying. Memory stung her with all the neglected chances of kindness that can never be redeemed. And here the wish was so natural—so just—that it seemed to her almost a sin to hesitate. Frederick’s face, as she remembered it in childhood—sun-browned, laughing—rose like a picture in a long-closed book; it hurt to think of him wandering far away, cut off by law and shame and fear, while his mother’s life ebbed daily in this smoke-dark town.

She rose and stood opposite her mother, striving to let steadiness show in her face, so that the sick woman might drink confidence from it.

“Mamma, I will write to-night, and tell Frederick what you say. I am as sure that he will come directly to us as I am sure of my life. Be easy, mamma; you shall see him—as far as anything earthly can be promised.”

“You will write to-night?” Mrs. Hale’s eagerness quickened, fever-bright. “Oh, Margaret! the post goes out at five—you will write by it, won’t you? I have so few hours left—I feel, dear, as if I should not recover, though sometimes your father over-persuades me into hoping; you will write directly, won’t you? Don’t lose a single post; for just by that very post I may miss him.”

“But, mamma—papa is out.”

“Papa is out! and what then?” Mrs. Hale cried, and the injustice born of suffering flashed out. “Do you mean that he would deny me this last wish, Margaret? Why, I should not be ill—be dying—if he had not taken me away from Helstone, to this unhealthy, smoky, sunless place.”

“Oh, mamma!” Margaret said, wounded, yet patient.

“Yes; it is so indeed. He knows it himself; he has said so many a time. He would do anything for me; you don’t mean he would refuse me this last wish—prayer, if you will. And indeed, Margaret, the longing to see Frederick stands between me and God. I cannot pray till I have this one thing; indeed I cannot. Don’t lose time, dear, dear Margaret. Write by this very next post. Then he may be here—here in twenty-two days! For he is sure to come. No cords or chains can keep him. In twenty-two days I shall see my boy.”

She fell back, exhausted, and for a little while took no heed of Margaret sitting motionless, her hand shading her eyes.

“You are not writing!” Mrs. Hale said at last, the impatience of fear rising again. “Bring me some pens and paper; I will try and write myself.” She struggled upright, trembling with the effort.

Margaret took her hand down and looked at her mother, sad with the knowledge of how little time there might be for consent or planning. “Only wait till papa comes in. Let us ask him how best to do it.”

“You promised, Margaret—not a quarter of an hour ago—you said he should come.”

“And so he shall, mamma,” Margaret answered, voice breaking. “Don’t cry, my own dear mother. I’ll write here—now—you shall see me write—and it shall go by this very post; and if papa thinks fit he can write again when he comes in. It is only a day’s delay. Oh, mamma, don’t cry so pitifully—it cuts me to the heart.”

But Mrs. Hale did not check herself. Her tears came hysterically, and she made no effort to restrain them; rather she fed them with pictures—the happy past, and a future where she lay dead and Frederick bent over her too late, she unconscious of the sight for which she had starved her prayers. The sobbing wore her down into a spent calm at last. Then, with greedy eyes, she watched Margaret set paper on a small table, dip the pen, and begin.

Margaret wrote swiftly, the sentences urgent with entreaty. She told Frederick of their mother’s state, of her passionate longing, of the one hope she clung to. She sealed the letter quickly, for fear her mother should ask to see it and find, in Margaret’s words, either too much alarm or too much command. And then, to make the security sure, she took it herself to the post-office, as Mrs. Hale bade her—walking fast, as if speed could shorten distance and bring a ship across the ocean.

She was on her way home when her father overtook her in the street.

“And where have you been, my pretty maid?” he asked, trying for lightness.

“To the post-office—with a letter. A letter to Frederick.” The confession came out with the suddenness of truth that has been held back too long. “Oh, papa, perhaps I have done wrong; but mamma was seized with such a passionate yearning to see him—she said it would make her well again—and then she said she must see him before she died—I cannot tell you how urgent she was. Did I do wrong?”

Mr. Hale did not answer at once. The noise of Milton—its grinding wheels and iron cries—seemed to press upon the silence between them, as if the town itself listened. At length he said, quietly, “You should have waited till I came in, Margaret.”

“I tried to persuade her—” Margaret began, and then stopped; the rest was in her face.

“I don’t know,” Mr. Hale said after a pause. “She ought to see him, if she wishes it so much; for I believe it would do her much more good than all the doctor’s medicine—and perhaps set her up altogether. But the danger to him, I’m afraid, is very great.”

“All these years since the mutiny, papa?”

“Yes.” Mr. Hale’s voice had a grave steadiness, the tone of one reciting what he hates to remember. “It is necessary, of course, for government to take very stringent measures for the repression of offences against authority—more particularly in the navy, where a commanding officer needs to be surrounded, in his men’s eyes, with a vivid consciousness of all the power there is at home to back him, and take up his cause, and avenge any injuries offered to him, if need be. Ah! it’s no matter to them how far their authorities have tyrannised—galled hasty tempers to madness—or if that can be any excuse afterwards; it is never allowed for in the first instance. They spare no expense; they send out ships; they scour the seas to lay hold of the offenders. The lapse of years does not wash out the memory of the offence; it is a fresh and vivid crime on the Admiralty books till it is blotted out by blood.”

Margaret shivered as if the words had opened a cold door. Blotted out by blood. She saw—too vividly—her brother drawn home by their mother’s cry, only to be seized, to be made an example. The thought turned her sick with remorse, as if she had seasoned a family meal with poison by mistake.

“Oh, papa,” she whispered. “What have I done! And yet—it seemed so right at the time. I’m sure Frederick himself would run the risk.”

“So he would; so he should!” Mr. Hale’s voice softened, and his eyes filled with that mingling of fear and tenderness which belongs to fathers. “Nay, Margaret—I’m glad it is done, though I durst not have done it myself. I’m thankful it is as it is; I should have hesitated till, perhaps, it might have been too late to do any good. Dear Margaret, you have done what is right about it; and the end is beyond our control.”

It was comfort, and yet it was not. She heard, beneath his encouragement, the deeper current of his anxiety. She slipped her hand through his arm, and they walked home together—slowly now, as if each step carried a weight—through the harsh Milton soundscape that never rested, the town’s loud life beating on while, within their narrow circle, love and law drew tight like cords about a single desired five minutes.
