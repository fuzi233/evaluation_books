It had been three weeks since Tito handed over his florins to Cennini, and on this day of San Giovanni, he walked toward the Via de’ Bardi with a light step and a clear mind. He carried no burden, neither in thought nor in spirit. The world around him flowed easily into his nature—there was no friction between what he saw and what he felt. His joyousness was untroubled by shadows; the distant future held no fears for him, and the hidden corners of life never stirred unease. As he stepped from the bright sun into the cool shade of a narrow street, he removed his black cloth berretta—the simple cap with its upturned brim—and pushed back his brown curls, letting his head toss freely in the breeze. There was nothing deceitful in his face, nor any unmistakable mark of innocence either. It was simply a young man’s brow—strongly formed, smooth, and square—with no trace of anxiety or pretense. His eyes, wide and dark-brown, scanned the upper windows of the houses with a gaze that belonged to youth: open, undimmed, and full of quiet curiosity. They were not cunning, nor were they naive—they were simply alive.

Was it possible that Tito’s face meant different things to different people? That one could read more than one truth in its lines? His presence spoke only of ease—a calm absence of vanity, ambition, or restlessness. And so, as he passed among the holiday crowds, admiration followed him not out of obligation, but willingly, like sunlight falling where there is no shadow.

The Festa had already spread beyond the main thoroughfares. The throng that had once gathered along the procession route now fanned out in every direction, chasing new diversions. Such moments—when the crowd grows restless, when energy seeks an outlet—are often the most volatile. Where amusement is expected and denied, tempers flare, and a single person may become a target for the collective mood.

Near the church of San Martino, the crowd thickened. At the hostelry known as the *Bertucce*, or “Baboons,” a knot had formed. A commotion drew Tito’s attention. Amidst laughter, he heard a small voice cry out, trembling: “Let me go! Holy Virgin, help me!” Without hesitation, he pushed through the circle of onlookers.

He saw at once—a young peasant girl, her white hood fallen off during a struggle, her hands caught in the grip of a man dressed in the motley colors of a *cerretano*, a conjuror. The man laughed, trying to soothe her, clearly enjoying the scene. The spectators chattered, calling her names—*tonta*, *bambina*, *povera sciocca*—as if her resistance were foolishness to be corrected.

At first, the girl’s face was turned away. Tito saw only her light-brown hair, braided and pinned with a long silver clasp. Then, in the next breath, she turned—and he recognized her. It was Tessa. Her babyish features were streaked with tears, her lower lip quivering. And then, through her misted eyes, she saw *him*. A sudden spark of hope lit her face, like a child finding shelter in the arms of someone familiar.

In a flash, Tito broke through the crowd, grasped Tessa’s waist, and said firmly, “Release this child. What right do you have to hold her against her will?”

The conjuror—a man whose sharp angles of eye, nose, and jaw all seemed to point upward—grinned, showing neat teeth. He released her hands, shrugged, and backed away slightly, half-apologetic, half-defiant.

“I mean her no harm, Messere,” he said. “Ask these good folk. I only meant to show my tricks—she’d make a fine assistant with that sweet face, which would assure everyone of honesty. I promised her confetti as a reward. But perhaps you’ve better treats in your pocket? She knows it.”

A ripple of laughter went through the crowd. Tessa clung tightly to Tito’s arm, her relief plain. To her, the laughter was just noise—like the roar of beasts behind her. But Tito knew better. He sensed the crowd’s irritation at being deprived of their entertainment. He moved quickly, pulling her away before their mockery could turn sharper.

“Here’s your hood,” the conjuror called after them, tossing the white cloth over her head. “_Orsù_, don’t bear me ill will. Come back when you can spare her.”

“Oh, Maestro Vaiano,” one man shouted, watching Tessa flinch, “she’ll come back soon enough—just like the toad said to the harrow.”

Tito hurried down a side street, annoyed at the delay. He wanted only to reach the Via de’ Bardi. But Tessa, clinging to him, looked frightened again.

“No!” she whispered. “I’ve lost my mother and my father-in-law. They’ll be angry—he’ll beat me. It happened near San Pulinari—someone pushed me, and I couldn’t stop. I don’t know where they are. Please, don’t leave me!”

Her voice cracked. Tears welled again, and she sobbed quietly.

Tito walked faster. The Church of the Badia was close. He could take her inside—perhaps leave her there. But the church was closed. Instead, they paused under the cloister arches, where the air was still and shaded.

“Do you have a cousin or friend in Florence?” he asked gently. “Someone you could find? Or are you afraid to walk alone now?”

“Oh, I’m afraid,” she said, her voice trembling. “He was the devil—I know he was. And I don’t know where to go. No one—no one at all. My mother meant to eat somewhere, but I don’t know where. Holy Madonna! I’ll be beaten.”

Her little chest rose and fell, beads glinting above her green serge dress. A sob escaped, then another. Tito felt helpless. Leaving her now would be cruel. His nature could not endure such sorrow.

As she lifted her apron to catch her tears, he touched it too, rubbed her cheek, and kissed her soft round face.

“My poor little Tessa,” he murmured. “Don’t cry. We’ll find a way. Where do you live?”

She didn’t answer at first, but the sobs slowed.

“Come,” he said, “if you tell me where you’re going, I’ll take you partway.”

The apron dropped. Her face softened, glowing with trust. The conjuror, the fear, the beating—all faded into distance.

“I think I’ll go home,” she whispered, looking up at him with wide blue eyes, something sweeter than a smile on her lips. “If you’ll take me.”

“Then let’s go,” Tito said, slipping her arm through his. “Which way?”

“Beyond Peretola—where the big pear tree stands.”

“Peretola? Out which gate, pazzarella? I’m a stranger here.”

“The Por del Prato,” she said, holding his arm tightly.

He didn’t know the roads well enough to avoid crowds, but he thought: maybe among more people, he’d run into Monna Ghita. So he headed straight for Porta Rossa, then Ognissanti. Along the way, people joked about the handsome young nobleman walking with his barefooted country girl—apprentices laughed, women watched, beggars held out caps mockingly, gamblers jeered. The streets of Florence in those days were rarely orderly, and Tessa’s fear was not misplaced.

When they reached the Piazza d’Ognissanti, Tito slowed. They were both warm from walking. He led her to a stone bench against the wall.

“Holy Virgin,” Tessa breathed, “I’m glad we’re away from those boys and women. But I wasn’t really scared—you’d protect me.”

“Pretty little Tessa,” Tito smiled. “Why do you feel so safe with me?”

“Because you’re beautiful—like the saints going into Paradise. They’re all good.”

“You haven’t eaten since breakfast,” he said, seeing nearby stalls selling fruit and sweets. “Are you hungry?”

“Yes,” she said softly. “If you want some too.”

He bought apricots, cakes, and comfits, and placed them in her apron.

“Let’s walk to the Prato,” he said. “Then you might not be afraid to go alone.”

“But you’ll eat some,” she insisted, gathering her apron like a bag.

“We’ll see,” he replied aloud, but to himself: *This little contadina could inspire a better idyll than Lorenzo’s ‘Nencia da Barberino.’ If only I were Theocritus—or had time to write poetry instead of walking in the sun!* Still, it was done. He was late already. Another half-hour wouldn’t matter. *Pretty little pigeon.*

“We have a garden,” Tessa said, “and plenty of pears. Two cows, and mules. I love them all. But my father-in-law is harsh. I wish my mother hadn’t married him. I think he’s wicked—he’s very ugly.”

“And does your mother let him beat you, poverina?” Tito asked. “You said you were afraid.”

“Oh, my mother scolds me too. She loves my younger sister more. She thinks I don’t work enough. No one speaks kindly to me—except the Pievano, when I go to confession. The men in the Mercato laugh at me. Nobody ever kissed me… not like you do. You talk to me like I talk to my little black-faced kid—because I love it so much.”

It seemed Tessa had not noticed how different Tito looked now—how polished, how noble. He had not changed in her eyes. He was simply a voice and a face from another world—something kind, something pure, that had come to her from heaven. She spoke without restraint, as if he were a dream born of her own heart and the sunlight.

They reached the Prato—a large open field within the city walls, where Florentine youths played *Calcio*, a rough game of football. At midday, it was empty except near the gates, where a tent stood ready for the race.

Tito stopped at the edge of the meadow.

“Now, Tessa,” he said, “you won’t be afraid if I leave you. Addio. Shall I buy milk from you tomorrow morning, to see you’re safe?”

He said it gently, seeing her eyes widen with sorrow, her mouth droop.

She didn’t speak at first. Only opened her apron and stared at the apricots. Then she looked up.

“I thought you’d eat with me,” she said, her voice small. “We could sit under a tree outside the gate and share them.”

“Tessa, Tessa,” Tito laughed, kissing her cheeks. “You’re a little siren—you’d ruin me. I should have been in the Via de’ Bardi hours ago. No—I must go. You’re safe. Here—take an apricot. Addio!”

He took two steps away. But Tessa stood frozen, pale, a great sob rising. She turned slowly, as if accepting defeat, and began walking forward—her apron forgotten, apricots rolling onto the grass.

Tito couldn’t help it. He ran after her, picked up the fruit, and returned.

“See, silly one,” he said, handing them back. “Come, stop crying. I’ll walk with you. We’ll sit under the tree. But I must go eventually.”

So they found a great plane-tree just beyond the gate. They sat beneath it—Tessa leaning against the trunk, Tito stretched opposite, elbows resting on the mossy ground. Sunlight filtered through the leaves, dancing around them like living sparks. Tessa’s face was peaceful again. The taste of the apricots and sweets seemed delicious.

“You pretty bird,” Tito said, watching her examine the remains, debating whether to save them. “To think anyone would scold you! What sins do you confess, Tessa?”

“Oh, many,” she said. “I’m often naughty. I hate work, though I know I’ll be beaten. I give the mules the best fodder when no one sees. Then when my mother is angry, I say I didn’t do it—that makes me afraid of the devil. I think the conjuror was the devil. I’m less afraid after confession. Look—I have a _Breve_.” She pulled from her bosom a tiny bag, carefully tied. “A holy father blessed it at Easter. And I think the Madonna will protect me—she looks like she would. Maybe if I weren’t idle, she wouldn’t let me be beaten.”

“If they treat you so cruelly,” Tito said, “wouldn’t you like to leave? To live with a kind lady, if she’d take you as a servant?”

Tessa held her breath. Then whispered, “I don’t know.”

“Then would you like to be my little servant?” Tito asked, smiling. “Live with me?”

A flush of joy lit her face. “Will you take me now? Ah, I wouldn’t go home and be beaten.” She hesitated. “But I’d like to fetch my black-faced kid.”

“You must go back for your kid,” Tito said, standing. “And I must go the other way.”

“By Jupiter!” he muttered, stepping from the shade. “It’s not a pleasant walk to the Via de’ Bardi. I’d rather sleep here.”

And so he did. Tito had an instinctive aversion to discomfort—even when duty waited. He had risen early, walked in the sun, seen sights. He was tired. And Tessa made the air softer. He lay down again, placing his cap under his head. It wasn’t comfortable. So he shifted, and asked gently, “May I rest my head on your lap?”

Tessa sat still as a dove on its nest. When he slept, she dared to touch his dark curls—soft, falling backward from his ear. She was too happy to sleep, too happy to imagine he would wake and leave her. For her, this moment was everything—this shade, this silence, this breathing warmth beside her. A tiny fish needs only a small pool to feel infinite peace.

Time passed unnoticed. Then, slowly, Tito stirred. His eyes opened—first with surprise, then a smile, which faded as thought returned. He had dreamed he was in the Via de’ Bardi, explaining his lateness. The dream woke him sharply. He sat up, shook his cap, and said:

“Tessa, little one, you let me sleep too long. The sun has traveled far. I must go. Addio.”

He patted her cheek, adjusted his cap.

She said nothing. But her face told him everything. He spoke again—more seriously, almost sternly.

“Now, Tessa, don’t cry. I’ll be angry. I won’t love you if you cry. Go home to your kid. Or watch the horses start. But I can’t stay longer. And if you cry, I’ll think you’re troublesome.”

Her tears froze. She turned pale, silent, her blue eyes wide with fear.

“Look,” Tito said, soothingly, opening his wallet. “Here’s a charm I’ve kept since Sicily—a long way off.”

He emptied the contents onto her lap—coins, trinkets, scraps. Among them was his onyx ring.

“Ah, my ring,” he said, slipping it on. “I forgot it this morning. Strange—I never noticed it was missing.”

Then he found what he sought—a small piece of red coral, pointed like a horn.

“See,” he said, holding it up. “Like your goat’s horn, isn’t it? There’s a hole—so you can tie it to your cord, beside your _Breve_. It’s a ‘buona fortuna.’ If evil spirits come—dark shapes in the corner—point this coral horn at them. They’ll run away. It will keep you safe when I’m not near. Now, undo your cord.”

Tessa obeyed, her hands steady. The coral hung beside the tiny bag with the sacred parchment. She felt stronger.

“And now,” Tito said, closing his wallet, “you’ll give me a kiss. And look happy—like a good girl—and then—”

But she had already leaned forward, pressing her lips to his cheek.

“Oh, you pretty pigeon!” he laughed, cradling her face in his hands, crushing her cheeks together in a broad, gentle kiss.

Then he stood and walked away, not looking back until ten yards off—then gave a final wave.

Tessa watched him go. He could see she wasn’t crying. That was enough. He needed no sorrow in his sight.

“I wonder,” he thought, walking on, “when Romola will kiss me like that?”

The journey seemed longer now. He almost wished he hadn’t lingered. But no excuse was needed for Bardo or Romola. He could simply say he’d been delayed. Their pride would accept it. He reached Ognissanti, ate quickly, crossed the Arno by Ponte alla Carraja, and hastened toward the Via de’ Bardi—his thoughts already turning ahead, yet still carrying the echo of a child’s kiss beneath a plane-tree.
