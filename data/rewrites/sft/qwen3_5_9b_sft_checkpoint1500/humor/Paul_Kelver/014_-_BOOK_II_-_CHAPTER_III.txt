GOOD FRIENDS SHOW PAUL THE ROAD TO FREEDOM. BUT BEFORE SETTING OUT, HE WILL GO A-VISITING.

The sunlight poured into my window like a cheerful salesman trying to convince me of the day's potential, but I was already awake, listening to the street's roar. It told me plainly that the world had kicked off without me. Reaching for my watch, I found it missing from its usual spot on the rickety dressing-table. My clothes were scattered on the floor like evidence at a crime scene, and one boot sat alone in the chair by the fireplace, looking quite depressed. Where was the other?

During the night, my head seemed to have inflated with helium. I wondered idly if I hadn't accidentally put on Minikin's hat—or rather, his shoes. If I had, I'd trade them back in a heartbeat. The pair I was wearing was a top-heavy disaster, currently screaming complaints in agony.

Suddenly, the memory of last night crashed into me like a wet blanket. From a nearby steeple, the bells chimed. I counted carefully. Eleven o'clock. I sprang out of bed and immediately collapsed onto the floor.

Remembering how I'd held onto the bedpost while the room performed a wild waltz, I realized it hadn't fully stopped spinning. It was still wobbling, staggering feebly, as if exhausted from an all-night party. I crawled to the wash-stand, managed to get my head inside the basin after a few clumsy attempts, and then pulled on my trousers with difficulty. Sitting in the easy-chair, I tried to review the situation, starting from now and working backward.

"I am feeling terribly ill," I whispered. "Something has disagreed with me."

"That strong cigar," I muttered to myself. "I shouldn't have touched it. And then that tiny room stuffed with people. Plus, I've been working my tail off lately. I really need exercise."

It gave me a small thrill to realize that, despite being a shuffling coward, I wasn't easily tricked.

"Nonsense!" I told myself harshly. "Stop deceiving me. You were drunk."

"Not drunk!" I pleaded. "Please, don't use that crude word. Some folks can't handle sweet champagne, I've heard. It upset my liver. Let's call it a liver issue, please."

"Drunk," I insisted relentlessly. "Hopelessly, vulgarily drunk—drunk like any 'Arry after a Bank Holiday."

"It's the first time," I murmured.

"It was your first opportunity," I countered.

"Never again," I promised solemnly.

"The stock phrase," I noted.

"How old are you?"

"Nineteen."

"So you haven't even the excuse of youth. How do you know it won't grow on you? That you won't slide lower and lower until you're a confirmed sot?"

My heavy head dropped into my hands, and I groaned. Temperance tales read on Sunday afternoons flooded back. In my imagination, I saw myself rushing toward a drunkard's grave, sometimes fighting heroically, sometimes yielding weakly, the craving tightening its grip. In the misty air around me, I saw my father's pale face and my mother's sorrowful eyes. I thought of Barbara, and the scorn that could dance around her bewitching mouth; and Hal, with his massive disdain for all weaknesses. The shame of today and the terror of tomorrow tore at my mind.

"It shall be never again!" I cried aloud. "By God, it shall!" (One is apt to be dramatic at nineteen.) "I will leave this house at once," I continued, "escape this unhealthy atmosphere. I will scrub it from my mind, everything connected with it. I will make a fresh start. I will—"

Something I had vaguely sensed at the back of my brain stepped forward: the flabby figure of Miss Rosina Sellars. What was she doing here? What right did she have to block my path to regeneration?

"The rights of your affianced bride," my inner voice explained, with a grim smile.

"Did I really go that far?"

"We won't go into details," I replied. "I don't want to dwell on them. That was the result."

"I was—I wasn't quite myself. I didn't know what I was doing."

"As a rule, we don't when we do foolish things; but we have to pay the price. Unfortunately, it happened in front of witnesses, and she isn't the type of lady to be easily dismissed. You'll marry her and settle down in two small rooms. Her family becomes your family. You'll get to know them quickly. Among them, she is known as 'the lady,' which tells you everything about them. A lovely start to your career, isn't it, my ambitious young friend? A nice mess you've made!"

"What am I to do?" I asked.

"Honestly, I don't know," I answered.

I spent a miserable day. Too ashamed to face Mrs. Peedles or even the maid, I stayed in my room with the door locked. By dusk, feeling slightly less terrible—or perhaps just less hopeless—I sneaked out and ate a simple meal at a nearby coffee-house: tea without sugar and a kippered herring. Another gentleman sat opposite, ordering hot buttered toast, so I fled hastily.

At eight, Minikin called from the office to ask what had happened. Seeking refuge in shame, I confessed the truth.

"Thought as much," he replied. "Looks like an A1 from the way you look."

"I'm glad it's over, now," I said. "It'll be a lesson I never forget."

"I know," Minikin said. "Nothing beats a fair and square drunk for making you feel real good; better than a sermon."

In my distress, I needed advice, and Minikin, though younger, was far more worldly-wise than I.

"That's not the worst," I confided. "What do you think I've done?"

"Killed a policeman?" Minikin suggested.

"Got myself engaged."

"No one likes quiet fellows like you for going crazy when they start," Minikin commented. "Nice girl?"

"I don't know," I answered. "All I know is I don't want her. How do I get out of it?"

Minikin removed his left eye and polished it on his handkerchief, a habit he had when confused. From staring into it, he seemed to gain inspiration.

"Take-her-own-part sort of girl?"

I indicated he had diagnosed Miss Rosina Sellars correctly.

"Know how much you earn?"

"She knows I live up here in this attic and cook for myself," I said.

Minikin glanced around. "Must be fond of you."

"She thinks I'm clever," I explained, "and that I'll make my way."

"And she's willing to wait?"

I nodded.

"Well, I'd let her wait," Minikin replied, putting his eye back. "Plenty of time before you."

"But she's a barmaid, and she expects me to walk with her, take her out on Sundays, visit her friends. I can't do that. Besides, she's right: I mean to succeed. Then she'll stick to me. It's awful!"

"How did it happen?" Minikin asked.

"I don't know," I replied. "I didn't know I'd done it until it was over."

"Anyone present?"

"Half a dozen of them," I groaned.

The door opened, and Jarman entered; he never bothered to knock. Instead of his usual noisy greeting, he crossed silently and shook my hand gravely.

"Friend of yours?" he asked, pointing to Minikin.

I introduced them.

"Proud to meet you," said Jarman.

"Glad to hear it," said Minikin. "Don't look as if you've got much else to be stuck up about."

"Don't mind him," I explained to Jarman. "He was born like it."

"Wonderful gift," Jarman replied. "Do you know what I'd do if I had it?" He didn't wait for Minikin's answer. "'Ire myself out to break up evening parties. Ever thought of it seriously?"

Minikin said he'd consider the idea.

"Make your fortune touring the suburbs," Jarman assured him. "Pity you weren't here last night," he continued. "Might've saved our young friend a heap of trouble. Has he told you the news?"

I explained I'd already briefed Minikin.

"Now you've got a good, steady eye," Jarman said, referring to Minikin, who had fixed his gaze on Jarman's glass orb. "'Ow d'ye think 'e is looking?"

"As well as can be expected under the circumstances," Minikin answered.

"Does 'e know the circumstances? Has 'e seen the girl?" Jarman asked.

I said I hadn't yet had that pleasure. "Then 'e don't know the worst," Jarman said. "A hundred and sixty pounds of 'er, and still growing! Bit of a load for 'im, ain't it?"

"Some of 'em do have luck," Minikin rejoined. Jarman leaned forward, inspecting his new acquaintance.
