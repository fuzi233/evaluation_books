The air was crisp and alive with a spring breeze that kicked up clouds of fine dust. Inside the church, the atmosphere was heavy with tradition; the elderly matrons of both families had donned their faded sable coats and yellowing ermines, creating a scent of aged camphor from the front pews that nearly suffocated the faint, sweet aroma of the lilies lining the altar.

Newland Archer stepped out of the vestry onto the chancel steps of Grace Church, standing beside his best man. This position marked the arrival of the bride and her father in the brougham below. While the bridesmaids hovered in the lobby like a cluster of Easter flowers, adjusting final details, the protocol demanded that the groom stand exposed to the congregation's gaze during this brief interlude. Archer endured this formal humiliation with the same resigned grace he applied to every other ritual of a nineteenth-century New York wedding—a ceremony that felt less like a modern event and more like a relic from the dawn of history. To him, the path ahead was equally easy or equally painful, depending on one's perspective. Having guided countless other couples through this very maze years ago, he now played his part with pious obedience.

He had meticulously checked every box: the bridesmaids' bouquets of white lilac and lily-of-the-valley arrived on time; the gold and sapphire cufflinks for the ushers and the cat's-eye pin for the best man were secured; he had spent half the night refining his thank-you speeches for gifts from male friends and former lovers; the fees for the clergy were safe in his best man's pocket; and his luggage, including his travel attire, waited at Mrs. Manson Mingott's for the post-wedding breakfast. Even the railway compartment for their honeymoon was booked, adhering to the sacred taboo of concealing their destination until the very last moment.

"You got the ring, right?" whispered Young Van der Luyden, the nervous best man, acutely aware of his heavy responsibilities.

Archer nodded, performing the universal gesture of the groom: he reached into the breast pocket of his dark grey waistcoat with his ungloved right hand, confirming the presence of the small gold band engraved inside with *Newland to May, April ———, 187—.*** With his left hand clutching his tall hat and pearl-grey gloves, he resumed his vigil at the church door. Above them, Handel's majestic march swelled through the vaulted ceiling, echoing the faded memories of dozens of previous weddings where he had stood on this exact step, watching other brides ascend the aisle with cheerful indifference.

*How much like a premiere at the Opera,* he mused, recognizing the familiar faces in the boxes—and pews alike. He wondered if Mrs. Selfridge Merry would appear with her towering ostrich plumes, or Mrs. Beaufort with her diamonds and signature smile, perhaps awaiting suitable seats in the next world. His thoughts drifted to the rows of guests: the women, sharp with anticipation; the men, grumpy about changing into frock coats before lunch and fighting for food at the reception. *Too bad the breakfast is at Old Catherine's,* he imagined Reggie Chivers complaining, though he knew Lovell Mingott insisted on his own chef, promising a feast fit for kings. Sillerton Jackson likely added his authoritative note about the trendy "small table" service.

His gaze lingered on the left-hand pew, where his mother sat weeping softly beneath her Chantilly veil, holding her grandmother's ermine muff. *Poor Janey,* he thought, watching his sister strain to see past the crowd of dowdy Newlands and Dagonets in the front row. Across the aisle, separated by a white ribbon marking the family section, Beaufort loomed tall and flushed, scanning the women with arrogant scrutiny. Beside him sat his wife, radiant in silvery chinchilla and violets. On the far side, Lawrence Lefferts's polished head seemed to guard the invisible idol of "Good Form." Archer wondered how many flaws Lefferts's keen eyes would find in the ritual, then realized with a jolt that he, too, had once cared deeply about such minutiae. Now, these concerns seemed like a nursery parody of life, akin to medieval scholastics arguing over metaphysical terms no one truly understood. The recent stormy debate over whether wedding gifts should be displayed had agitated everyone, yet Mrs. Welland had firmly vetoed it with indignant tears, declaring she would sooner let reporters roam her house. Archer reflected on how aggressively he used to champion such social codes, convinced they held world-wide significance, while ignoring the reality that real lives were unfolding elsewhere.

"There they come!" the best man gasped.

Archer knew better. The initial creak of the church door merely signaled Mr. Brown, the livery stable keeper acting as sexton, conducting a preliminary survey. Soon, however, the door swung open majestically, and a murmur rippled through the congregation: "The family!"

Mrs. Welland led the procession, her large pink face set in appropriate solemnity. Her plum-colored satin gown with pale blue panels and matching bonnet received general approval. Before she settled into the pew opposite Mrs. Archer's, the crowd craned their necks for the next arrivals. Rumors had circulated wildly that Mrs. Manson Mingott, despite her frailty, intended to attend. Bets had been placed at the clubs on her ability to navigate the nave, and her family had seriously considered dismantling the front pew panel or wheeling her in a massive Bath chair—an idea so shocking to their sensibilities that they recoiled at the thought of exposing her to the press outside. Ultimately, the plan collapsed when someone noted the chair wouldn't fit under the church awning, and the fear of photographs in the newspapers caused the entire clan to shudder. Mrs. Welland had reluctantly agreed to host the wedding breakfast at her home instead, a compromise that cooled the room's temperature significantly when it became clear her mother-in-law would not be present.

In her place sat Mrs. Lovell Mingott, glowing with the high color and glassy stare typical of her age group after struggling into a new dress. Once the disappointment of her mother-in-law's absence faded, her black Chantilly lace over lilac satin, topped with a bonnet of Parma violets, created a striking contrast to Mrs. Welland's plum and blue ensemble.

Then came the third figure: a gaunt, mincing lady trailing behind Mr. Mingott, dressed in a chaotic swirl of stripes, fringes, and floating scarves. As this eccentric apparition glided into view, Archer's heart seized and stopped. He had assumed the Marchioness Manson was still in Washington with her niece, Madame Olenska, escaping the persuasive rhetoric of Dr. Agathon Carver. No one expected them to return for the wedding. For a heartbeat, Archer froze, staring at Medora's fantastical silhouette, hoping to see who followed. But the procession ended; the lesser relatives took their seats, and the eight ushers slipped silently into the lobby.

"Newland—she's here!" the best man whispered urgently.

Archer snapped out of his trance. Time seemed to have stretched infinitely since his heart had paused. The white and rose-hued procession was halfway up the nave; the Bishop, Rector, and assistants hovered near the flower-adorned altar as the first chords of the Spohr Symphony floated through the air. Archer blinked, unsure if he had actually closed his eyes, and felt his pulse begin to race again. The music, the scent of lilies, the drifting cloud of tulle and orange blossoms, his mother's convulsed face, the Rector's blessing, the synchronized movements of the bridesmaids and ushers—all these familiar elements merged into something strangely alien in his new role as a husband.

*God,* he thought desperately, *do I have the ring?* He repeated the frantic check of his pocket.

Suddenly, May appeared beside him, radiating such brilliance that it warmed his numbness. He straightened, smiling into her eyes.

"Dearly beloved, we are gathered here..." the Rector began.

The ring slid onto her finger, the Bishop offered his benediction, and the organ prepared to launch into the Mendelssohn March, essential for any New York debut.

"Your arm—I say, give her your arm!" Young Van der Luyden hissed nervously.

Once again, Archer felt untethered, drifting into the unknown. What had driven him there? Perhaps it was the fleeting glimpse of a dark coil of hair under a hat in the transept, belonging to a stranger with a long nose, so unlike the memory he had conjured that he feared madness. Now, walking slowly down the nave on the gentle ripple of the music, the spring day beckoned through the open doors, while Mrs. Welland's chestnuts, adorned with white favors, pranced at the end of the canvas tunnel. A footman draped May's white cloak around her shoulders, and Archer joined her in the brougham. She turned with a triumphant smile, their hands clasped beneath her veil.

"Darling!" he said. Suddenly, a black abyss seemed to yawn before him, pulling him deep within, yet his voice remained smooth and cheerful. "Yes, of course I thought I'd lost the ring; no wedding is complete without the poor devil of a bridegroom going through that. But you did keep me waiting, you know! I had time to think of every horror that might happen."
