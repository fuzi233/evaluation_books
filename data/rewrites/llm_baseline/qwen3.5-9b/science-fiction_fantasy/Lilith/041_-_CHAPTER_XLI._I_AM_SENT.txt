I turned back to Eve and spoke, "Mother, there is an empty couch beside Lona's. Though I feel unworthy, may I not spend this night among the dead in your chamber? Will you not forgive both my cowardice and my arrogance, and take me in? I surrender myself. I am sick of who I am, and I crave only sleep!"

"The couch beside Lona is already prepared for you," she replied. "Yet, something remains undone before you can rest."

"I am ready," I said.

"How do you know you possess the strength?" she asked, smiling.

"Because you require it," I answered.

"What is it?"

She turned to Adam. "Husband, is he forgiven?"

"From my heart," he responded.

"Then tell him what he must do."

Adam turned to his daughter. "Mara, give me your hand." She extended it gently from her lap, and he grasped it tenderly.

"Let us go to the cottage," he told me. "There, I shall instruct you."

As we moved, a sudden, violent gust struck us, accompanied by a great flapping overhead, only to die away once more into a deep, mournful sigh. Once the door of the death-chamber closed behind us, Adam sat while I stood before him.

"You must remember," he began, "that after leaving my daughter's house, you arrived at a dry rock marked by the scars of an ancient waterfall. You climbed it and found a sandy desert. Go to that same rock now. From its summit, walk deep into the desert. Do not take many steps before lying down and placing your head upon the sand. Listen. If you hear the murmur of water beneath, proceed a little further and listen again. If the sound persists, you are moving in the right direction. Every few yards, you must stop, lie flat, and hearken. Should you ever cease hearing the sound of water, you have strayed; you must listen in every direction until the flow returns. Following the sound—carefully ensuring you do not retrace your steps—you will soon hear it grow louder. That crescendo leads you to the source. This is the spot you seek.

"There, use the spade I will give you. Dig until you reach moisture. Place the hand within it, burying it flush with the desert surface, and return home. But heed these warnings carefully: carry the hand with utmost care. Never set it down in any place that seems safe; let nothing touch it. Do not stop or turn aside for anything attempting to block your path. Never look behind you. Speak to no one, answer no one, and walk straight ahead.

"It is still dark, and the morning is distant, yet you must depart immediately."

He handed me the hand and produced a spade. "This is my gardening spade," he said. "With it, I have brought forth many beautiful things into the sun."

I accepted the tools and stepped out into the night. The air was biting cold, and absolute darkness enveloped everything. Falling would be terrifying, and the terrain ahead was treacherous even in daylight. Yet, having undertaken this task, I realized instantly that I was not left to mere chance. At every step, a pale light erupted from the ground, illuminating exactly where to place my next foot. Through the heather and low-lying rocks, I walked without a single stumble.

I found the badger's burrow perfectly still; not a ripple rose, not a head emerged as I crossed it. A moon rose, casting its own silver guidance across the landscape. By morning, I was nearly past the dry channels of the river's first branch, judging I was not far from Mara's cottage.

The moon hung low, the sun not yet risen, when I saw standing in the path—a figure veiled from head to toe in moonlit mist, narrowing the road. I pressed onward as though seeing nothing. The figure tossed aside its veil.

"Have you forgotten me already?" the voice asked—the Princess, or whatever remained of her.

I neither hesitated nor answered. I walked straight forward.

"You intended then to leave me in that horrible tomb! Do you not yet comprehend that wherever I choose to be, I am? Take my hand; I am alive just as you are!"

I was on the verge of saying, "Give me your left hand," but caught myself, holding my silence, and advancing steadily.

"Give me my hand!" she shrieked suddenly. "Or I will tear you apart! You are mine!"

She lunged at me. I shuddered but did not falter. Nothing touched me, and I saw her no more.

With measured strides, filling the path for some distance, came a phalanx of armed men. I walked through them—I knew not if they parted for me or were insubstantial specters. They turned and followed; I heard and felt their march at my very heels, but I cast no glance backward. The sound of their steps and the clashing of armor faded into the void.

A short distance further, with the moon dipping near the horizon and the path swallowed in deep shadow, I spotted a woman seated where the trail narrowed so tightly I could not pass her. Her face was muffled.

"Ah," she said. "You have come at last! I have waited here an hour or more! You have done well! Your trial is concluded. My father sent me to meet you so you might rest briefly on your journey. Give me your burden, and lay your head in my lap; I will guard both of you until the sun rises fully. I am not always bitterness, nor am I bitter toward all men!"

Her words were a terrible temptation, for I was exhausted. What else could be more plausible? Had I, through slavish adherence to the letter of the command and a lack of pure insight, trampled underfoot the very person of the Lady of Sorrow? My heart grew faint at the thought, then hammered as if bursting from my chest.

Nevertheless, my will hardened against my heart, and my step did not waver. I bit my tongue to prevent an accidental reply and continued forward. If Adam had sent her, he could not complain that I refused to heed her! Nor would the Lady of Sorrow love me less for the fact that even she could not turn me aside!

Just as I approached the phantom, she pulled the covering from her face. Great indeed was her beauty, yet those were not Mara's eyes! No lie could truly or permanently mimic them! I advanced as though the creature were not there, and my foot found empty space.

I had almost reached the other side when a Shadow blocked my way—I believe it was *The Shadow*. He seemed to wear a helmet, but as I drew nearer, I realized it was merely a distorted human head, barely resembling humanity. A cold, dank, sickening wind struck me—repulsive as the air of a charnel house. Firmness abandoned my joints, and my limbs trembled as if ready to collapse into a helpless heap.

I seemed to pass through him, though perhaps it was he passing through me. For a fleeting moment, I was one of the damned. Then, a soft wind, like the first breath of a newborn spring, greeted me, and before me, the dawn arose.

My path now led past the door of Mara's cottage. It stood wide open. Upon the table inside, I saw a loaf of bread and a pitcher of water. Within or around the cottage, there was no howl, no wail.

I climbed the precipice that bore witness to the vanished river and proceeded deeper into the desert. Finally, after much listening in every direction, I pinpointed the location where the hidden water sounded loudest. I hung Lilith's hand around my neck and began to dig.

It was arduous labor, for the loose sand required a large excavation. Eventually, however, I threw up a damp spadeful. I cast the tool onto the edge and laid the hand down. Water began to ooze slowly from beneath its fingers.

I sprang up and hastened to fill the grave. Utterly fatigued, I dropped beside it and fell asleep.
