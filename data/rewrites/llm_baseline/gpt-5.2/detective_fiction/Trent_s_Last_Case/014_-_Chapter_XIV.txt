“If you insist,” Trent said, surrendering with the air of a man being marched to the block, “I suppose you will have your way. But I had much rather write it when I am not with you. However, if I must—bring me paper whiter than a star, unblemished by your address. A clean sheet. Don’t underrate the sacrifice. I never felt less like correspondence in my life.”

She rewarded him with a look that had the warmth of a well-tended hearth.

“What shall I say?” he asked, pen poised like a fork above a first course. “Shall I compare him to a summer’s day? What shall I say?”

“Say what you want to say,” she answered, practical as a woman making a sauce that must not split.

He shook his head. “What I want to say—what I have been wanting, for the past twenty-four hours, to cry out to every man, woman, and child I met—is: ‘Mabel and I are betrothed, and all is gas and gaiters.’ But it wouldn’t serve as the opening of a letter of strictly formal—one might almost say sinister—character. I have got as far as ‘Dear Mr. Marlowe.’ What comes next?”

“I am sending you a manuscript,” she prompted, “which I thought you might like to see.”

He stared at her as if she had proposed to feed a guest on plain bread when there were spices in the cupboard. “Do you realize,” he said, “that in that sentence there are only two words of more than one syllable? This letter is meant to impress, not to put him at his ease. We must have long words.”

“I don’t see why,” she said. “I know it is usual—but why? I have had letters from lawyers and business people, and they always begin, ‘with reference to our communication,’ and go on like that to the end. Yet when I see them they don’t talk like that. It seems ridiculous.”

“It is not at all ridiculous to them.” Trent laid down the pen with the relief of a cook laying aside a knife that has been sharpening his conscience instead of his appetite, and rose. “Let me explain. A people like our own, not very fond of using its mind, gets on in ordinary life with a small, simple vocabulary. Long words are abnormal; and like everything abnormal they are either very funny or tremendously solemn. Take ‘intelligent anticipation.’ In any other European country it would slip by unnoticed. With us it becomes a proverb; we grin when we hear it in a speech or read it in a leading article; we think it’s one of the finest things ever said. Why? Because it consists of two long words. The idea itself is as commonplace as cold mutton. And there’s ‘terminological inexactitude’—how we roared, and still roar. The whole joke is that the words are long. It’s the same when we wish to be very serious; we mark it by turning to long words. When a solicitor begins a sentence with ‘pursuant to the instructions communicated to our representative’—or such gibberish—he feels he is earning his six-and-eightpence. Don’t laugh! It’s perfectly true. Now Continentals haven’t got that feeling. They are forever bothering about ideas, and the result is that every shopkeeper and peasant has a vocabulary in daily use that is simply Greek to the vast majority of Britons. I remember dining with a friend of mine who is a Paris cabman. We ate in a dirty little restaurant opposite the central post office, where all the clients were cabmen and porters. Conversation was general, and it struck me that a London cabman would have felt out of his depth. Words like ‘functionary’ and ‘unforgettable’ and ‘exterminate’ and ‘independence’ hurtled across the table every instant—ordinary, vulgar, jolly, red-faced cabmen—”

“Ah, no, no, no!” Mrs. Manderson cried. “I don’t know anything at the moment except that your talking must be stopped somehow if we are to get any further with that letter. You shall not get out of it. Come!”

She crossed the room, took up his pen as neatly as if it were an implement she meant to put to honest use, and placed it back in his hand. Trent looked at it with distaste.

“I warn you not to discourage my talking,” he said gloomily. “Believe me, men who don’t talk are even worse to live with than men who do. Have a care of natures that are mute. I confess I’m shirking writing this thing. It is almost an indecency. It mixes two moods—to write the sort of letter I mean to write, and to sit in the same room with you.”

She led him back to the chair at the escritoire and pressed him down with the gentle authority of a hostess who will not let a guest leave before he has tasted what must be tasted.

“Well—but please try. I want to see what you write, and I want it to go to him at once. You see, I would be content to leave things as they are; but you say you must get at the truth, and if you must, I want it as soon as possible. Do it now—you know you can if you will—and I’ll send it off the moment it’s ready. Don’t you ever feel that—the longing to get the worrying letter into the post and off your hands, so that you can’t recall it if you would, and it’s no use fussing any more about it?”

“I will do as you wish,” he said, and turned to the paper, dating it from his hotel.

Mrs. Manderson looked down at his bent head with a light in her eyes that was neither pity nor triumph but something steadier—like the glow under a lid when the fire has been banked for the night. She made as if to smooth his rather untidy hair. But she did not touch it. Instead she went silently to the piano and began to play very softly.

The room held itself still around that sound. It was not the brassy insistence of the street, nor the coarse clatter of modern haste, but a small sheltered world of measured vibration—notes that did not shout their meanings, because they did not need to. They were domestic, and yet in them there was the feeling of old things remembered: a language of the fingers that survived when spoken assurances failed.

Ten minutes passed before Trent spoke.

“If he chooses to reply that he will say nothing?”

Mrs. Manderson looked over her shoulder. “Of course he dare not take that line. He will speak to prevent you from denouncing him.”

“But I’m not going to do that anyhow. You wouldn’t allow it—you said so; besides, I won’t if you would. The thing’s too doubtful now.”

“But,” she laughed softly, “poor Mr. Marlowe doesn’t know you won’t, does he?”

Trent sighed, his pen going again, as though he were forced to season a dish he had no desire to eat. “What extraordinary things codes of honour are!” he remarked, half to the paper. “I know there are things I should do, and never think twice about, which would make you feel disgraced if you did them—such as giving any one who grossly insulted me a black eye, or swearing violently when I barked my shin in a dark room. And now you are calmly recommending me to bluff Marlowe by means of a tacit threat which I don’t mean; a thing which hell’s most abandoned fiend did never, in the drunkenness of guilt—well, anyhow, I won’t do it.”

He bent to his writing again. Mrs. Manderson, indulgent, returned to her quiet playing; the notes moved through the twilight like the remembered calls of birds over a field that has not yet been surrendered to engines.

In a few minutes more Trent said, “At last I am his faithfully. Do you want to see it?”

She ran across the room and turned on the reading lamp beside the desk. The sudden circle of light fell on the paper like a spotlight on a confession. Then, leaning on his shoulder, close enough for him to feel the faint perfume of her hair—something clean and intimate, like linen stored with lavender—she read:

DEAR MR. MARLOWE,—You will perhaps remember that we met, under unhappy circumstances, in June of last year at Marlstone.

On that occasion it was my duty, as representing a newspaper, to make an independent investigation of the circumstances of the death of the late Sigsbee Manderson. I did so, and I arrived at certain conclusions. You may learn from the enclosed manuscript, which was originally written as a dispatch for my newspaper, what those conclusions were. For reasons which it is not necessary to state I decided at the last moment not to make them public, or to communicate them to you, and they are known to only two persons beside myself.

At that phrase her eyes lifted sharply from the page; her brows drew together.

“Two persons?” she said, and there was a note in it—small, but distinct—as when a cook tastes a broth and hears, in the mouth, one wrong note.

“Your uncle is the other,” Trent replied. “I sought him out last night and told him the whole story. Have you anything against it? I always felt uneasy at keeping it from him, because I had led him to expect I should tell him all I discovered, and my silence looked like mystery-making. Now it is to be cleared up finally, and there is no question of shielding you, I wanted him to know everything. He is a very shrewd adviser, too, in a way of his own; and I should like to have him with me when I see Marlowe. I have a feeling that two heads will be better than one on my side of the interview.”

She sighed, and the sigh seemed to make a hollow place in the music behind them. “Yes, of course, uncle ought to know the truth. I hope there is nobody else at all.” She pressed his hand. “I so much want all that horror buried—buried deep. I am very happy now, dear, but I shall be happier still when you have satisfied that curious mind of yours and found out everything, and stamped down the earth upon it all.”

She read on:

Quite recently, however, facts have come to my knowledge which have led me to change my decision. I do not mean that I shall publish what I discovered, but that I have determined to approach you and ask you for a private statement. If you have anything to say which would place the matter in another light, I can imagine no reason why you should withhold it.

I expect, then, to hear from you when and where I may call upon you; unless you prefer the interview to take place at my hotel. In either case I desire that Mr. Cupples whom you will remember, and who has read the enclosed document, should be present also.—Faithfully yours,

Philip Trent.

“What a very stiff letter!” she said when she had finished. “Now I am sure you couldn’t have made it any stiffer in your own rooms.”

Trent slipped the letter and its enclosure into a long envelope with the careful fingers of a man sealing up a smell he dislikes but cannot deny. “Yes,” he said, “I think it will make him sit up suddenly. Now this thing mustn’t run any risk of going wrong. It would be best to send a special messenger with orders to deliver it into his own hands. If he’s away it oughtn’t to be left.”

She nodded. “I can arrange that. Wait here for a little.”

When she returned, he had risen and was hunting through the music cabinet, as though the room’s stored voices might offer a safer language than ink.

She sank down beside him on the carpet, a wave of dark brown skirts pooling about her, and looked up.

“Tell me something, Philip,” she said.

“If it is among the few things that I know.”

“When you saw uncle last night, did you tell him about—about us?”

“I did not,” he answered. “I remembered you had said nothing about telling any one. It is for you—isn’t it?—to decide whether we take the world into our confidence at once or later on.”

“Then will you tell him?” Her eyes dropped to her clasped hands. “I wish you to tell him. Perhaps if you think you will guess why.... There! that is settled.”

She lifted her gaze again, and for a time there was no speech between them—only the faint after-sound of the piano in the room’s corners, and beneath that the quiet, human rustle of a house that still kept its own old dialect of creaks and breaths.

At last Trent leaned back in the deep chair.

“What a world!” he said. “Mabel, will you play something on the piano that expresses mere joy—the genuine article, nothing feverish or like thorns under a pot—but joy that has decided in favour of the universe? It’s a mood that can’t last altogether, so we had better get all we can out of it.”

She went to the instrument and struck a few chords while she thought, testing them like ingredients between finger and tongue—this one too sharp, that one too heavy. Then she began to work with all her soul at the theme in the last movement of the Ninth Symphony, which is like the sound of the opening of the gates of Paradise.
