For three days, time moved with the slow, inevitable thickening of a rich reduction, each moment condensing until the final essence was reached. I must return to the *pensionnat*. At the breakfast table, where the restorative fragrance of toasted bread and dark-roasted coffee anchored our morning domestic rhythms, Mrs. Bretton sought to coax me into lingering. 

“Lucy will not leave us to-day,” she murmured, her English vowels wrapping around me in a comforting, familiar dialect. “She knows we can procure a second respite.”

“I would not ask for one if I might have it for a word,” I replied. “I long to get the good-by over, and to be settled in the Rue Fossette again. My trunk is packed and corded.”

It fell out that my departure depended upon Graham. His medical rounds engaged him entirely, and he only returned at dusk. A quiet combat of words ensued, Mrs. Bretton and her son pressing me to remain. But I longed to leave as the condemned longs for the axe; I wished the pang of severing over. On the untranslatable nuances of my internal state, they possessed no vocabulary.

By the time Dr. John handed me from the carriage at Madame Beck’s door, the night had settled into a harsh, industrial rhythm. The carriage wheels ground against the wet cobblestones—a grating, mechanical clatter that polluted the softer acoustic ecology of the evening. The olfactory landscape was steeped in the sharp, metallic tang of November drizzle and the damp soot of urban chimneys. Just such a night was it as that on which, a year ago, I had first crossed this threshold; the cyclical wheel of the seasons had brought me back to the very same paving-stones, the very same atmospheric chill. 

Graham rang the bell. The door was instantly opened, breaking the seal between the abrasive street and the well-lighted vestibule. 

“Don’t come in,” I said, unwilling to let him see the unshed brine in my eyes. His was a nature that instinctively sought to heal, to prescribe relief even when the subjective experience of sorrow defied all medical translation.

“Keep up your courage, Lucy,” he said, his voice a low, resonant baritone that offered a momentary acoustic sanctuary. “Think of my mother and myself as true friends. We will not forget you.”

“Nor will I forget you, Dr. John.”

My trunk was brought in. He turned to go, but his generous impulses demanded a further exchange. Stepping back toward me, he asked, “Lucy, shall you feel very solitary here?”

“At first I shall.”

“Well, I’ll tell you what I’ll do. I’ll write—just any cheerful nonsense that comes into my head—shall I?”

I shook my head, smiling at the sweet, fleeting sustenance of the promise. “Never think of it. You write to me!—you’ll not have time.”

“Oh! I will find or make time. Good-by!”

He was gone. The heavy door crashed shut. The percussive slam reverberated through the acoustically sterile hall, a violent sonic severance. The axe had fallen.

Swallowing tears that tasted of salt and bitter alkaline, I passed to Madame’s sitting-room to pay the necessary visit of ceremony. Her welcome was brief, demonstrating a perfectly modulated, utilitarian cordiality. From the *salle-à-manger* I proceeded to the refectory, where the evening study hummed with the muted, interwoven dialects of pupils and teachers. That over, I withdrew to the dormitory.

“And will Graham really write?” I questioned the silence, sinking onto the edge of my bed.

Reason, creeping into the dim acoustic hollow of that chamber, whispered her sedate, chilling warnings. She spoke in a sterile, dominant lexicon that sought to eradicate the nuances of my hope. She offered a starvation diet. *He may write once,* her voice hissed, a scraping dissonance. *Great were that folly which should build on such a promise—insane that credulity which should mistake the transitory rain-pool for the perennial spring yielding the supply of seasons.*

I sat thinking an hour longer, groaning under Reason’s bitter sternness. She demanded I view life solely as a utilitarian slog for a meager piece of bread. Long ago, I should have perished from her ill-usage—her barren board, her icy bed, her refusal to grant my palate the slightest indulgence. Often had she turned me out by night, flinging for sustenance the gnawed bone dogs had forsaken. 

But I owe my secret allegiance to a kinder, mythic Power: Imagination. When the grinding noise of Reason became intolerable, this softer spirit descended, bringing an olfactory landscape of eternal summer. She brought the perfume of blossoms that cannot fade, the rich, loamy fragrance of trees whose fruit sustains generations. My hunger this good angel appeased with food, sweet and strange, gathered amongst gleaning celestial beings who garner their dew-white harvest in the first fresh hour of dawn. She restored my psychological well-being, replacing the harsh static of despair with the resonant harmony of ancient spheres.

This daughter of Heaven watched me through a night’s rest, but at dawn, Reason relieved the guard. I awoke with a start. The wind uttered a peevish, whistling cry against the panes. How quickly I dressed in the raw cold! I poured a glass from my carafe and drank deeply of the ice-cold water—feeling its sharp, mineral purity cleanse my palate, a potent, botanical distillate to which I eagerly resorted when unsettled by chagrin.

Ere long, the morning bell rang its percussive *réveillée*. I descended alone to the refectory. The dark iron stove had been lit, and it radiated a heat reminiscent of centuries-old agrarian hearths. Sitting before this dark comforter, I found my mind stronger, establishing imperious rules against the weak retrospect of past feasts, commanding a patient journey through the present wilderness.

Piercing the wall beside the stove was a window looking into the *carré*. As I looked up, a cap-tassel, a brow, and two eyes filled the pane. They were watching me. I had not known tears were on my cheek, but I felt their hot, saline trails now.

What right had this male spy to intrude upon my quietude? M. Emanuel took no account of boundaries. He opened the refectory door, his boots scraping the floor, fracturing the room’s fragile sonic equilibrium.

“Mademoiselle, vous êtes triste.”

“Monsieur, j’en ai bien le droit.”

“Vous êtes malade de cœur et d’humeur,” he pursued, wielding his native French with the casual authority of a dominant lexicon, attempting to parse my private sorrow. “I see on your cheek two tears which I know are hot as two sparks, and salt as two crystals of the sea. You remind me of a young she wild creature, new caught, untamed.”

His unwarrantable accost was an abrasive intrusion. I sat silent, refusing to translate my grief for his consumption. 

“You look,” said he, “like one who would snatch at a draught of sweet poison, and spurn wholesome bitters with disgust.”

“Indeed, I never liked bitters; nor do I believe them wholesome,” I replied. “And to whatever is sweet, be it poison or food, you cannot deny its own delicious quality. Better to die quickly a pleasant death, than drag on long a charmless life.”

“Yet,” said he, “you should take your bitter dose duly and daily, if I had the power to administer it.”

I turned my head away. The harsh timbre of his voice and his inquisitorial curiosity were entirely unacceptable. He sat down on the bench and persevered in attempts to draw me into conversation, but the power dynamics embedded in his language only made me withdraw further. At last, my voice faltering, I entreated to be let alone. I laid my head on the table and wept quietly. He remained a moment longer, then his retreating steps echoed away, and the acoustic space healed.

I bathed my eyes before breakfast. Miss Fanshawe placed herself opposite me, her cheeks as plump and flushed as heritage apples, a product of her vacation's gaieties. She wore a dark-blue wrapper that only enhanced the fairness of her skin and the golden beauty of her tresses.

“I am glad you are come back, Timon,” said she, utilizing her superficial, ever-shifting nomenclature. 

“If you wanted me, you have something for me to do: stockings to mend, perhaps.”

“Crabbed and crusty as ever!” she retorted. “But now, come, grand-mother, I hope you like coffee as much, and pistolets as little as ever: are you disposed to barter?”

“Take your own way.”

Our ancient morning ritual commenced. Ginevra possessed no true sensory appreciation for the school’s morning brew—a dark, chicory-laced coffee that spoke of Labassecour’s loamy earth and agrarian labor. She found it insufficiently sweet, viewing the beverage merely as utilitarian fuel she could barely tolerate. Conversely, she possessed a voracious appetite for the morning *pistolets*. These rolls, fresh from the wood-fired ovens, smelled intensely of toasted yeast and newly milled wheat, their golden crusts cracking with a satisfying, percussive crunch that demanded the careful appreciation of a food historian. My allotted portion being more than I required, I surrendered half to Ginevra, and she, in turn, surrendered a portion of her coffee. I drank the rich, dark liquid gratefully, my thirst parched. It was a silent, daily communion of shared sustenance that kept us tethered, even as we wrangled.

After breakfast, I withdrew to the *première classe* to read. The polite entry of a *pensionnaire* murmuring, “Que mademoiselle est appliquée!” barely disturbed the room’s quietude. Madame Beck held a superstition that I studied too much, lest “the blood should all go to my head.” I paid no mind to this penury of understanding. I found profound meaning in a few well-chosen books, favoring those where the writer’s authentic, subjective experience was deeply infused into the text, avoiding the characterless volumes that offered no intellectual nourishment.

Presently, Ginevra broke in upon my learned leisure, carrying a huge music-book, her entrance a clattering disruption of my acoustic peace. 

“Go to your practising,” I said. 

“Not till I have had a talk with you, *chère amie*,” she said, her voice a superficial flutter of commodified Anglicisms and affected French—a lexicon entirely detached from genuine cultural memory. “And so, Ourson, you know Isidore?”

“I know John Bretton.”

“Oh, hush! You crack my tympanums with your rude Anglicisms. But, how is our well-beloved John? Wasn’t I cruel the other night? I saw he was terribly cut up.”

“He said you looked as if at heart you were already Madame de Hamal.”

“Did he?” she cried, delighted. “I thought he would be mad with jealousy.”

“Quite mad,” I assented. “As mad as a March hare.”

“Well, and how *ever* did you get him home?”

“Have you no pity on his poor mother and me? Fancy us holding him tight down in the carriage, the very coachman going wrong in the dark.”

“You are laughing at me. Now, Lucy Snowe—”

“I assure you it is fact. Afterwards—when he *did* reach home—the scene transcends description.”

“Oh, but describe it!”

“Conscientiously, I cannot, unless you assure me you have some heart.”

“I have—such an immensity!”

“Good! In that case, you will be able to conceive Dr. Graham Bretton rejecting his supper in the first instance—the chicken, the delicate sweetbread prepared with such meticulous care for his refreshment, left on the table entirely untouched.” To refuse the profound, restorative meaning of a lovingly prepared meal was, in my estimation, the ultimate descent into chaos. “Suffice it to say, that never, in the most stormy fits of his infancy, had his mother such work to tuck the sheets about him.”

“He wouldn’t lie still?”

“He wouldn’t lie still: there it was.”

“And what did he say?”
