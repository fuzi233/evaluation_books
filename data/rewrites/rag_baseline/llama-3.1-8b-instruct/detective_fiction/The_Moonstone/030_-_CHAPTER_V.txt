As I stood frozen behind the curtain, my heart pounded in a fervent rhythm, driven by an overwhelming sisterly concern for Mr. Godfrey. The memory of his words still lingered in my mind like a specter: he would do it today. The phrase echoed through my thoughts like a dire prophecy, leaving me aghast and bewildered. What, oh what, would he do? Would he betray the faith that had once shone brightly in his eyes? Would he abandon us at the very foundations of the Mothers' Small-Clothes? I was a whirlwind of anxiety, my imagination conjuring up all manner of calamitous scenarios involving this enigmatic figure.

Just as I teetered on the brink of bursting forth from my hiding place to implore him to explain himself, a jarring voice pierced the air. It was loud, bold, and lacking in the gentle charm that usually accompanied the ladies of our social circle. Rachel Verinder had entered the room, her presence like a dash of cold water on a summer's day. "Why have you come up here, Godfrey?" she asked, her tone a mixture of curiosity and annoyance. "Why didn't you go into the library?"

Mr. Godfrey's soft laughter carried through the curtains, and I caught the faintest hint of amusement in his voice as he replied, "Miss Clack is in the library." Rachel's response was immediate: "Clack in the library!" She settled onto the ottoman in the back drawing room, her movements as fluid as a summer breeze. "You are quite right, Godfrey. We had much better stop here."

My own emotions seesawed between a burning fever of anxiety and a chill of fear. I made a silent decision to remain hidden, yet visible, behind the curtain. My eyes locked onto the scene unfolding before me, and I steeled myself for the trials that lay ahead. As I watched, Rachel Verinder began to speak, her voice like a gentle brook babbling over smooth stones.

"Don't sit on the ottoman," she said, her words laced with a playful authority. "Bring a chair, Godfrey. I like people to be opposite to me when I talk to them." Mr. Godfrey rose from his seat, his long frame towering above the low chair as he moved to fetch a stool. I had never seen him look so awkward, his legs appearing stilted and gangly in the dim light.
