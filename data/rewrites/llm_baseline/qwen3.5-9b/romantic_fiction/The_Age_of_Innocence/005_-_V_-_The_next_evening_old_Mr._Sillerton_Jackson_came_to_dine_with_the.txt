The following evening, Old Mr. Sillerton Jackson arrived to dine with the Archers. Mrs. Archer was a woman of quiet reserve, naturally shrinking from the clamor of society, yet she possessed an insatiable hunger to remain abreast of its movements. Mr. Sillerton Jackson approached the study of his neighbors' lives with the meticulous patience of a collector and the rigorous classification methods of a naturalist. His sister, Miss Sophy Jackson, who resided with him and was courted by everyone else deemed unworthy of securing her highly prized brother, supplemented his findings with fragments of minor gossip that neatly filled the gaps in his narrative. Consequently, whenever Mrs. Archer desired information on current events, she extended an invitation to Mr. Jackson. Since she honored very few with such requests, and since she and her daughter, Janey, constituted an ideal audience, Mr. Jackson invariably attended personally rather than dispatching his sister. Had he the power to dictate terms, he would have selected evenings when Newland was absent—not because the young man was disagreeable to him (they were fast friends at the club)—but because the old storyteller occasionally sensed in Newland a propensity to scrutinize his anecdotes with a critical weightiness that the ladies of the household rarely displayed. Furthermore, if perfection were achievable on Earth, Mr. Jackson would have requested that Mrs. Archer's culinary offerings be somewhat superior. However, New York, as far back as human history could trace, had bifurcated into two fundamental factions: the Mingotts and Mansons and their clans, obsessed with gastronomy, fashion, and finance; and the Archer-Newland-van der Luyden tribe, devoted to travel, horticulture, and high literature, who regarded coarse pleasures with disdain. One cannot have everything, after all. Dining with the Lovell Mingotts promised canvas-back ducks, terrapin, and vintage wines; conversing at Adeline Archer's offered discussions of Alpine vistas and *The Marble Faun*; fortunately, the Archer Madeira had circumnavigated the Cape. Thus, when summoned by Mrs. Archer, Mr. Jackson, a true eclectics, would typically remark to his sister, "I've suffered a bit of gout since my last repast at the Lovell Mingotts'; it will do me good to diet at Adeline's."

Mrs. Archer, a widow for many years, resided with her son and daughter in West Twenty-Eighth Street. An upper floor was exclusively dedicated to Newland, while the two women compressed themselves into narrower quarters below. In a harmonious alignment of tastes and interests, they cultivated ferns within Wardian cases, crafted macramé lace and wool embroidery on linen, amassed collections of American Revolutionary glazed ware, subscribed to *Good Words*, and devoured Ouida's novels for the atmospheric quality of their Italian settings. (They favored tales of peasant life for their scenic descriptions and wholesome sentiments, though generally preferring stories of society where motives and habits were transparent; they criticized Dickens harshly for failing to capture the essence of a gentleman, and viewed Thackeray as less comfortable in the grand world than Bulwer—though Bulwer himself was beginning to seem antiquated.) Both Mrs. and Miss Archer were ardent admirers of scenery, the primary pursuit of their occasional European excursions, regarding architecture and painting as pursuits reserved for men, particularly scholars versed in Ruskin.

Mrs. Archer was born a Newland, and mother and daughter, resembling each other as sisters, were universally acknowledged as "true Newlands": tall, pale, and slightly stooped, possessing long noses, sweet smiles, and a specific drooping dignity reminiscent of certain faded Reynolds portraits. Their physical similarity would have been absolute had not an elderly robustness strained Mrs. Archer's black brocade, whereas Miss Archer's brown and purple poplins grew increasingly loose upon her youthful frame as the years progressed. Mentally, however, the resemblance was less complete than their identical mannerisms suggested. Years of living in mutually dependent intimacy had gifted them with the same vocabulary and the shared habit of prefacing opinions with "Mother thinks" or "Janey thinks," depending on which party wished to assert dominance. Yet, in truth, while Mrs. Archer's serene unimaginativeness found comfort in the accepted and familiar, Janey was prone to sudden flights of fancy springing from wells of suppressed romance. Mother and daughter adored one another and revered their son and brother; Archer loved them with a tenderness rendered compunctious and uncritical by their exaggerated admiration, bolstered by his secret satisfaction in it. Ultimately, he believed it beneficial for a man to have his authority respected within his own home, even if his sense of humor occasionally led him to question the strength of his command.

On this occasion, the young man was quite certain that Mr. Jackson would have preferred to dine elsewhere, yet he had his own reasons for remaining. Naturally, old Jackson wished to discuss Ellen Olenska, and naturally, Mrs. Archer and Janey craved his insights. All three would feel a degree of embarrassment with Newland present, now that his prospective alliance with the Mingott clan had become public knowledge; the young man awaited the interaction with amused curiosity to observe how they navigated this awkwardness. They commenced, indirectly, by discussing Mrs. Lemuel Struthers.

"It is a pity the Beauforts invited her," Mrs. Archer observed gently. "Though Regina always follows his lead; and *Beaufort*..."

"Certain nuances escape Beaufort," Mr. Jackson replied cautiously, inspecting the broiled shad while wondering for the thousandth time why Mrs. Archer's cook invariably burned the roe to a crisp. (Newland, who had long shared this observation, could always detect it in the older man's expression of melancholy disapproval.)

"Oh, necessarily; Beaufort is a vulgar man," Mrs. Archer stated. "My grandfather Newland always used to tell my mother: 'Whatever you do, do not introduce that fellow Beaufort to the girls.' But at least he has the advantage of associating with gentlemen; in England, they say. It is all very mysterious—" She glanced at Janey and paused. She and Janey knew every detail of the Beaufort mystery, but in public, Mrs. Archer maintained the pretense that the subject was inappropriate for the unmarried.

"But this Mrs. Struthers," Mrs. Archer continued, "what did you say *she* was, Sillerton?"

"From a mine: or rather, from the saloon at the head of the pit. Then with Living Wax-Works, touring New England. After the police dismantled *that* establishment, they claim she lived—" Mr. Jackson glanced at Janey, whose eyes began to widen beneath her prominent lids. There remained significant blanks in her understanding of Mrs. Struthers's past.

"Then," Mr. Jackson proceeded (and Archer noted he was wondering why no one had instructed the butler never to slice cucumbers with a steel knife), "then Lemuel Struthers appeared. They say his advertiser utilized the girl's head for shoe-polish posters; her hair is intensely black, you know—the Egyptian style. Anyway, he—eventually—married her." There were volumes of innuendo in the spacing of the word "eventually," with each syllable receiving its due emphasis.

"Oh, well—at the stage we have reached nowadays, it doesn't matter," Mrs. Archer remarked indifferently. The ladies were not genuinely interested in Mrs. Struthers at that moment; the topic of Ellen Olenska was too fresh and absorbing. Indeed, Mrs. Struthers's name had been introduced solely to allow Mrs. Archer to pivot immediately: "And Newland's new cousin—the Countess Olenska? Was *she* at the ball as well?" There was a faint edge of sarcasm in the reference to her son, which Archer recognized and had anticipated. Even Mrs. Archer, who was seldom overly pleased with human events, had been entirely gratified by her son's engagement. ("Especially after that silly affair with Mrs. Rushworth," she had remarked to Janey, alluding to what once seemed to Newland a tragedy bearing a permanent scar on his soul.) There was no finer match in New York than May Welland; consider the situation from any angle. Of course, such a union was precisely what Newland deserved; but young men are so foolish and incalculable—and some women so ensnaring and unscrupulous—that it was nothing short of a miracle to see one's only son safely past the Siren Isle and into the harbor of blameless domesticity. All this Mrs. Archer felt, and her son knew she felt; but he also knew that she had been disturbed by the premature announcement of his engagement, or rather by its cause. It was for this reason—that on the whole he was a tender and indulgent master—that he had stayed home that evening.

"It's not that I don't approve of the Mingotts' *esprit de corps*," Mrs. Archer grumbled to Janey, the sole witness to her slight deviations from perfect sweetness. "But I fail to see why Newland's engagement should be entangled with that Olenska woman's comings and goings." She had behaved beautifully—and in beautiful behavior she was unsurpassed—during the call on Mrs. Welland; yet Newland knew (and his betrothed likely guessed) that throughout the visit, she and Janey had been nervously scanning for Madame Olenska's potential intrusion. When they departed the house together, she had permitted herself to say to her son, "I am thankful that Augusta Welland received us alone."
