If you’re driving north through central Massachusetts, distracted by the hum of your tires and the stretch of Aylesbury pike ahead, you may not even notice the moment you take the wrong fork past Dean’s Corners—until the world softens into something that no longer fits the map.

The ground rises slow, almost imperceptibly, and brier-choked fieldstone walls creep closer and closer until they brush the rutted dust of the curving road, their lichen crusted thick as old dried bruise. The trees lining the endless woodlots are too big, trunks twisted like gnarled, knuckled fists, their leaves hanging so still no breeze from the settled counties behind you seems to reach them. Wild weeds, brambles, and thick unnameable grasses choke every empty patch of dirt, lush to a fevered, almost greedy degree you never see in farmed, lived-in parts of the state. The few tilled fields you pass are barren, stunted with yellowed unthrifty crops, and the scattered houses dotting the slopes all wear the same heavy, uniform cloak of age, squalor, and rot. You never think to stop and ask directions of the ragged, solitary figures you catch glimpses of on crumbling doorsteps or in the rock-strewn meadows below. They never wave, never call out, their heads tilted at a wrong, unhuman angle, their eyes tracking you all the way down the road without a single shift of their shoulders. To speak to them feels like reaching for something you know is forbidden, something that will cling to your skin long after you walk away.

When the road crests a small rise, the high hills loom over the treeline, and that faint, crawling unease curdles in your gut. Their summits are too rounded, too perfectly symmetrical, no craggy outcrops breaking their smooth curve, as if something massive pushed up through the earth eons ago and wore the peaks down with the weight of its own body. Silhouetted sharp against the pale sky, you can make out the queer, regular circles of tall rough stone pillars that crown almost every one of them, standing straight as sentinels at attention. Gorges of unguessable depth cut across the path, and the crude wooden bridges strung across their dark mouths creak and sway under your weight like they are breathing, not built to hold anything that does not belong here. When the road dips low again, you hit stretches of marshland that make your skin prickle, that you recoil from instinctively—worse at dusk, when unseen whippoorwills chatter in overlapping, unearthly tones, fireflies swarm so thick they blur the dark, and stridently piping bullfrogs croak in a harsh, steady rhythm that sounds like counting, slow and unrelenting. The thin, glinting upper reach of the Miskatonic winds coiled at the feet of those domed hills, glinting like the scaled back of some great sleeping serpent.

You do not look at the stone-crowned hilltops then. You stare instead at their dark, precipitous wooded sides, which loom higher and higher, pressing in on you until you ache to put distance between you and them—only to find there is no turnoff, no escape. You cross a rickety covered bridge, and spot the village huddled tight between the river and the sheer slope of Round Mountain, a clump of rotting gambrel roofs that look centuries older than any architecture for thirty miles. It is no comfort to see, as you draw nearer, that most of the houses are long abandoned, crumbling in on themselves, and the old church with its broken, half-collapsed steeple now houses the only slovenly general store in the whole hamlet. You dread driving into the pitch-black tunnel of the covered bridge, but there is no way past it. Once you emerge on the other side, you can not shake the faint, heavy malign odor clinging to the dirt street: the stench of centuries of mold, of rot that no sun has ever fully burned away. It is a physical relief to peel out of the village, follow the narrow road looping around the base of the hills, and hit open level country until you rejoin the familiar stretch of the Aylesbury pike. Later, a local at the next town’s gas station will blink at you and ask how the hell you ended up in Dunwich.

Outsiders visit Dunwich as rarely as possible. After the spate of unspeakable events of 1928, all the road signs pointing toward it were torn down and burned. By every ordinary standard, the rolling wooded hills and river valleys around it are stunningly beautiful, but no landscape artists or summer tourists ever venture that way. Two hundred years ago, folks did not laugh at talk of witch blood, Satan worship, and strange presences in the woods, so they had clear, sharp reasons to avoid the place. In our supposedly sensible modern age, the full facts of the 1928 horror were hushed up by everyone who claimed to have the town’s and the world’s best interests at heart—so now people shun it without being able to say exactly why.

One obvious reason, for anyone who has spent time nearby, is that the Dunwich natives are repellently decayed, generations deep down that path of inbred retrogression common to isolated New England backwaters. They have formed a distinct, unrecognizable race of their own, marked with clear, unmistakable stigmata of degeneracy: slack faces, sallow skin, jerky uncoordinated movements, an average intelligence woefully lower than any other community in the state. Their recorded history reeks of open viciousness, half-hidden murders, incest, and acts of unnamable violence and perversity no local paper will ever print. The old gentry, the two or three armigerous families that fled Salem in 1692, have stayed slightly above the general decay—though many of their branches have sunk so far into the sordid populace that only their old family names remain to mark the origin they disgrace. Some of the Whateleys and Bishops still send their eldest sons to Harvard and Miskatonic University, but those sons almost never return to the rotting gambrel roofs they were born under. Most die drunk, screaming in city bars about the things that live under the hills.

No one, not even the handful of people who hold the full facts of the 1928 horror, can ever fully explain what is fundamentally wrong with Dunwich. Old legends speak of unhallowed indigenous rites, where the Pocumtucks called forbidden shadow-shapes up out of the rounded hills, chanting wild orgiastic prayers that were answered with deep rumbling cracks and thunder from beneath the earth. In 1747, newly arrived Congregational minister Reverend Abijah Hoadley preached a sermon no one who heard it ever forgot, in which he thundered: “It must be allow'd that these Blasphemies of an infernall Train of Dæmons are Matters of too common Knowledge to be deny'd; the cursed Voices of Azazel and Buzrael, of Beelzebub and Belial, being heard from under Ground by above a Score of credible Witnesses now living. I myself did not more than a Fortnight ago catch a very plain Discourse of evill Powers in the Hill behind my House; wherein there were a Rattling and Rolling, Groaning, Screeching, and Hissing, such as no Things of this Earth cou'd raise up, and which must needs have come from those Caves that only black Magick can discover, and only the Divell unlock.” Hoadley vanished three days after that sermon. His printed sermon, published in Springfield, still survives. His body was never found.

Reports of unaccountable noises in the hills have been logged every single year since, a puzzle no conventional geologist or physiographer has ever been able to explain. Other local traditions tell of foul sulfurous odors clinging to the hilltop stone pillar circles, of rushing, unseen airy presences you can hear faintly at certain hours at the bottom of the deepest ravines. The most well-known local landmark is the Devil’s Hop Yard: a bleak, blasted hillside where no tree, shrub, or single blade of grass will ever grow, no matter how much fertilizer or seed you dump on its burnt black soil. The natives are mortally afraid of the swarms of whippoorwills that cry out on every warm night. They swear the birds are psychopomps, lying in wait for the souls of the dying, their eerie cries timed perfectly to match the heave and gasp of a dying person’s last breaths. If they snatch the fleeing soul the moment it leaves the body, they flutter away chittering in demoniac laughter. If they fail to catch it? They fall silent, disappointed, before they start to creep closer to the house, looking for another way in.

Folks write all these tales off as obsolete, ridiculous old superstitions. But Dunwich is not like any other old New England town. It is far older than any community within thirty miles. South of the village, you can still spot the crumbing cellar walls and chimney of the old Bishop house, built before 1700. The ruins of the 1806 mill at the falls are the most modern piece of architecture standing. The 19th century factory movement died there within two years, no one ever figured out why. Oldest of all are the great rings of rough-hewn stone columns on the hilltops, which most locals attribute to no human settler at all. Caches of skulls and bones found inside the circles and around the flat table-like boulder on Sentinel Hill are widely said to be Pocumtuck burial remains—though any ethnologist who gets close enough to look will admit, off the record, that those bones do not belong to any human race that was ever supposed to exist on this continent.
