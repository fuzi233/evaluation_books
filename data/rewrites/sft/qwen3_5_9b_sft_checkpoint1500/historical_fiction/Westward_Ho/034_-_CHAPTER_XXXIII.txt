The sky, a canvas of soft, dappled grays, stretched from east to west, a modest canopy over a land weary of blood and storm. Upon the water, the *Vengeance* moved silently, her hull scraping softly against the bar as if ashamed to disturb the sleeping sands of Appledore. Her anchor dropped heavily, and her flag hung limp and heavy at half-mast—a silent testament to the loss aboard. For Salvation Yeo, the giant of the seas, lay cold and still within her hold.

From the ship, a small boat pulled away, rowing swiftly toward the western strand where the shore was steep. On land, Cary and Brimblecombe waited, their faces grim with grief. They helped lift Amyas Leigh from the deck, supporting his weight as they guided him slowly up the hill toward his home. Behind them trailed a throng of villagers, a river of humanity driven by a mixture of awe, pity, and love. Women wept openly, clutching their shawls to their mouths, while the men cheered in a murmur that sounded less like joy and more like a prayer. All in Appledore and Bideford knew the truth: the great captain was broken.

"Spare me, my good friends," Amyas whispered, his voice rough but steady despite his blindness. "I have landed here only to pass quietly into my house. Do not make me a spectacle. Think not of me, nor speak of me aloud. Follow me as Christian men would follow a brother to his grave, but leave me alone."

As he spoke, another boat glided close, carrying the coffin of Salvation Yeo, draped in the deep red of St. George's flag. The people accepted Amyas's wish with reverence. A runner was dispatched to Burrough to warn Mrs. Leigh, while the mourners formed a solemn line behind the coffin. Amyas and his two loyal friends took their places at the rear, their shoulders bowed under the weight of sorrow, as the crowd swelled behind them, filling the narrow lanes until the procession numbered nearly five hundred souls before they even reached the churchyard.

By then, the sexton had already tilled the earth, digging a grave at the west end of the church, near the towering shadow of the gray steeple that watched the sea. The parson stood ready at the gate, his robes white against the somber crowd. Inside, Mrs. Leigh sat in her bay-window, a book of prayers open before her. Beside her knelt Ayacanora, her face pale, her eyes fixed on the dust outside. Though her heart ached for the old sailor who had risked everything to save her from the jaws of beasts, she found herself thinking not of the dead, but of the blind man standing at the edge of the grave.

"They are burying Master Yeo," Mrs. Leigh murmured, reading the burial service with a gentle, hollow voice. "He loved you, Ayacanora, and sought you across the world to bring you home. Are you not sorry?"

Ayacanora did not answer immediately. She hung her head, her cheeks flushing a deep crimson. Her mind was a whirlwind of images—Amyas's voice, the feel of his hand, the terrible silence of his new world. She thought only of him.

When the final blessing was spoken and the parson stepped back, the crowd lingered, crowding around the fresh mound of earth. Amyas stood motionless at the head of the grave, his figure silhouetted against the light. The villagers watched him with an instinctive dread, sensing that something profound was about to happen. Suddenly, Amyas straightened his spine to its full, towering height. He raised his hand, not in command, but in a gesture of majestic farewell, as if addressing the spirits of the sea.

"Good people, and ye who were my comrades in the fight!" he called out, his voice echoing strangely in the quiet square. "To you, who raised me from the waves, I return today, blind and helpless, to dwell with you until the end. Here lies the flower of our mariners, a friend to the brave and a terror to the wicked. Unchanging in purpose, swift in action, he lived a life of sober triumph and terrible endurance. He humbled the pride of Spain at Rio de la Hacha and Nombre, and first dared sail those southern seas that God intends to be free for English ships. Purged by strange torments and the cruelty of the Papists, he learned to fear only God. Now, he rests, exalted like Elijah to a chariot of fire. May his mantle fall upon you, leaving these shores never without brave and pious sailors, who count their lives as cheap for the sake of their Country, their Bible, and their Queen."

With that, he turned. Feeling for Cary and Brimblecombe, he stumbled slightly before regaining his balance, and they supported him as they walked slowly away from the churchyard, down the village street, and up the lane to Burrough Gate. The crowd parted before him, moving in a silent, reverent wave, as if he were a king returning from battle, though his kingdom was now locked inside his own chest.

Amyas knew exactly when they reached the gate. With a deftness that belied his blindness, he located the lock, his fingers finding the mechanism with practiced ease. He opened it and stepped forward onto the gravel path, his boots crunching softly. Cary and Brimblecombe followed, their steps hesitant, their hearts fluttering like trapped birds. They expected a storm of emotion, a collapse of the mighty man, but Amyas moved with a strange, stoic calm. He reached the front door, felt for the handle, and pushed it open.

"Mother!" he called, his voice quiet but clear.

Instantly, Mrs. Leigh was there, rushing to him. She sobbed silently, her eyes dry with a grief too deep for tears, as Amyas wrapped his great arms around her, holding her as if she were the only solid thing in a shifting world.

"Mother," he said after a long pause, his tone gentle and self-deprecating. "I am home, because I must. Will you take me in? Will you look after this useless carcase? I promise not to be much trouble." He looked down at her, a faint, sad smile touching his lips, and pressed a kiss to her brow.

She said nothing, merely passing her arm gently around his waist and leading him inside. "Be careful, dear child," she warned softly, guiding him past the threshold. "The doors are low."

Inside, Amyas paused, then turned to call for his companions. "Will! Jack!"

But they were gone, having slipped out the back door with surprising speed. Outside, Cary leaned against a tree, wiping sweat from his brow.

"I am glad we are away," Cary muttered. "Another minute, and I would have been a fool, staring at that angel of a woman. Look at her face working, trying to keep composure!"

"And there goes a brave servant of the Queen," Jack sighed, looking toward the house. "Cut down before his work was done. Heigho! I must go home to my old father. And then..."

"And then home with me," Cary insisted, shaking his head. "We have pulled in the same boat too long, Jack. You cannot go out spending your prize money on riotous living. I must see after you ashore, or you will treat half the town to a week of tavern brawling."

"Oh, Mr. Cary!" Jack protested, scandalized.

"Come home with me," Cary declared firmly. "We'll poison the parson, and your old father can give you the rectory."

"Oh, Mr. Cary!" Jack groaned.

Together, the two friends departed for Clovelly, leaving Amyas alone in the hall.

His mother had stepped out briefly to settle the seamen who had brought his luggage, giving them bread and ale. Amyas remained seated in the old bay-window where he had spent countless hours as a boy, reading tales of King Arthur and the cruelties of the Spaniards. He reached out, his hand brushing the leather bindings of the books lying beside each other, just as they had twenty years prior. The window stood open, and the cool autumn air drifted in, carrying the sweet scents of roses, rosemary, and gilliflowers. On the table before him sat a dish of apples. He recognized them instantly by their familiar, earthy perfume—the same fruit he had climbed trees to gather as a lad.

He extended his hand, taking an apple, rolling it between his palms. He played with it, tracing its skin, as if the intervening decades had never existed. In that moment, the floodgates of memory opened. As the text speaks of a drowning man seeing his entire life in a single flash, so Amyas beheld every place he had known, every word ever spoken to him. The visions swirled rapidly until he arrived at the fairy island of Meta, hearing the thunderous roar of the cataract once more. He saw the green tops of the palm trees sleeping in the sunlight above the spray, stepping across smooth trunks to leap down to the gravel beach beside the pool. And rising from the fern-covered rocks, beautiful and radiant, appeared the vision of Ayacanora.
