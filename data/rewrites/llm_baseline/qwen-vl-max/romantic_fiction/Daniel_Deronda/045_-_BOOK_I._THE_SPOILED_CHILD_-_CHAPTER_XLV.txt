CHAPTER XLV. Behold my lady’s carriage stop the way. With powdered lacquey and with charming bay; She sweeps the matting, treads the crimson stair. Her arduous function solely “to be there.” Like Sirius rising o’er the silent sea. She hides her heart in lustre loftily.

The Grandcourts arrived at Grosvenor Square just in time to receive an invitation to Lady Mallinger’s musical gathering—a social event Sir Hugo had anticipated with some interest, knowing full well that his estranged nephew, Daniel Deronda, would be among the guests. It was only the third evening since their arrival in London, and though Gwendolen had been introduced to the grandeur of her new home—its white walls, gold trimmings, and pale crimson draperies—her mind remained elsewhere. She wandered through the rooms half-absent, her thoughts fixed not on the opulence around her, but on the prospect of seeing Deronda again, and of meeting Mirah Lapidoth, the young Jewish woman whose story had stirred him so deeply.

Gwendolen remembered every word Deronda had spoken about Mirah, especially the phrase: “capable of submitting to anything in the form of duty.” That sentence echoed in her mind now, a quiet reproach. She could not help comparing it to her own life—her submission, she knew, was not born of duty, but of compulsion, of shame, of a yoke laid upon her by choices she regretted. If anyone had told her she lacked true sacrifice, she might have argued fiercely—but deep down, she understood the truth: her obedience was not noble, but self-serving, driven by fear and pride.

The drawing-rooms of Park Lane were tastefully arranged and lightly filled when the Grandcourts entered. Soft instrumental music had just ended, giving way to murmured conversation and gentle movement. Klesmer was already seated at the piano, preparing for Mirah’s performance. He had chosen Leo’s *O patria mia* for her—a piece he believed best revealed her voice’s depth and character. As Mirah stood beside the instrument, poised and still, Gwendolen entered, resplendent in pale green velvet and diamonds that seemed almost too bright for such a somber night. She was ushered to a seat near the front, where she could see both Mirah and Deronda clearly.

With her sharp eyes and practiced composure, Gwendolen quickly scanned the room. She caught sight of Mirah, then nodded politely to Klesmer as she passed. The brief exchange sparked a sudden memory—the morning when she had dreamed of standing where Mirah now stood, admired not for beauty or birth, but for talent. Now, she was merely another ornament in silk and jewels, one of many who came to applaud or critique. A flicker of bitterness crossed her mind: *He thinks I am in the right road now.*

She did not see Deronda at first. While making polite conversation with Sir Hugo, she glanced around carefully, afraid that any obvious search for him might seem vulgar or unseemly. But even the most discreet glance can lead to unexpected encounters. Among those who met her gaze was Mr. Lush—the man once banished from her thoughts, now standing close to her husband. Sir Hugo turned away slightly, pretending to listen to Lord Pentreath. Yet Gwendolen felt a jolt—an icy sensation—like a secret reawakening. For the first time, it occurred to her: this man knew things about Sir Hugo’s life. Things that made her wretched. He had vanished from her world, hidden behind other concerns, but now he stood here, at her husband’s side, and suddenly, the past surged forward.

She forced herself to look away, pretending her attention was drawn to a painting on the wall. Then, finally, she saw Deronda—not far from the door, speaking quietly with Hans Meyrick. He looked anxious, perhaps even tense. They were both concerned for Mirah’s performance, worried that she might not be heard properly. Deronda, in particular, felt something stir within him—images of Mordecai, of the future, of the brother he would soon reveal to Mirah. He had just escaped Lady Pentreath, who had remarked, in her low, mocking tone, “Well, your Jewess is pretty—there’s no denying that. But where is her Jewish impudence? She looks as demure as a nun.”

That comment stung. Deronda felt, as he had once felt for himself, a fierce protectiveness. He resented the casual way Mirah was treated—as if she were a mere curiosity, a novelty for the fashionable crowd to examine and discard. And he knew Mordecai would feel the same pain at being labeled “Jewess” as if it were a mark of inferiority.

In that moment, as the Grandcourts entered, Deronda felt a fleeting return of his old repulsion toward Gwendolen. Not because of her beauty, but because of what she represented—wealth, indifference, a world that could not recognize the worth of someone like Mirah. It was a kind of class resentment, born of affection for what others failed to see.

Hans, admiring Gwendolen with his usual exaggeration, said, “That Vandyke duchess of a beauty!” Deronda replied, with a dryness that masked his discomfort, “I thought you could admire no style of woman but your Berenice.”
“Berenice is the style I worship,” Hans answered. “Other women I might make myself wicked for. But for Berenice, I could make myself—well, pretty good. Which is harder.”
“Hush,” said Deronda, as the music began.

He was not pleased with the reply, and was relieved when Hans moved closer to the piano.

Mirah sang *O patria mia*. Deronda listened intently. He knew Leopardi’s poem well—the image of Italy as a mother in chains, weeping in silence. The few words of the song carried the weight of the whole poem, breathing sorrow and defiance. To Deronda, Mirah’s voice seemed to echo Mordecai’s spirit—his courage, his loneliness, his love for a people he could never fight for. Certain lines not in the song rang in his mind: *Do none of thy children defend thee? Arms! bring me arms! alone I will fight, alone I will fall.* These were not words of futility, but of sacred sacrifice.

Mirah sang beautifully. When the applause rose, Klesmer leaned over and whispered, “Good, good—the crescendo better than before.” But Mirah cared less for praise than for Deronda’s approval. She looked toward him, hoping he saw her. He did, but stayed where he was, watching as admirers gathered around her. Then, the crowd parted—and Gwendolen approached, escorted by Mrs. Klesmer.

Now, standing near Mirah, Gwendolen felt a strange shift in her feelings. She no longer saw Mirah as a rival, but as someone fragile, gifted, and strangely vulnerable. She offered her thanks: “You must let me say how much I am obliged to you. I had heard from Mr. Deronda that I should have a great treat in your singing, but I was too ignorant to imagine how great.”

Mirah, absorbed in Gwendolen’s presence, replied simply: “You are very good to say so.” She found herself fascinated by the grand lady—her jewels, her bearing, the aura of a world beyond her own. It felt like a dream.

“We shall all want to learn from you—I, at least,” said Gwendolen. “I sing very badly, as Herr Klesmer will tell you,” she added, glancing up at him with a playful archness. “But I’ve been rebuked for not liking to be middling, since I can be nothing more. Is that a different doctrine from yours?”

Klesmer smiled. “Not if it means you’d study further—and Miss Lapidoth would enjoy teaching you.” Then he stepped away.

Mirah, sincere and earnest, said, “If you think I could teach you, I shall be very glad. I am anxious to teach, but I have only just begun. If I do it well, it must be by remembering how my master taught me.”

Gwendolen, taken aback by the simplicity, tried to change the subject. “You haven’t been long in London, I think? Were you introduced to Mr. Deronda abroad?”

“No,” Mirah said softly. “I never saw him before I came to England.”

“But he has seen you often, hasn’t he?” Gwendolen pressed, eager to hear more about Deronda. “He spoke of you with the highest praise. He seemed to know you quite well.”

“Oh,” Mirah said, her voice softening with gratitude. “I was poor and needed help. Mr. Deronda gave me the best friends in the world. That is how he came to know me—because he was sorry for me. I had no friends when I came. I was in distress. I owe everything to him.”

Gwendolen, who had once dreamed of struggling artists and creative freedom, couldn’t help feeling a pang. The way Mirah spoke—so humble, so grateful—was a contrast to the pride she herself had once worn. Yet she also sensed that Mirah was protecting Deronda, refusing to let anyone assume they were equals. There was grace in that.

As the music resumed, Gwendolen chose to sit farther from the piano, placing herself on a settee with only one neighbor—closer to Deronda than before. It was natural, then, that he should come over to shake hands before the next piece began. After a moment, he sat beside her, as if the music itself had drawn them together.

But when the music ended, and Gwendolen hoped to speak freely, she noticed Mr. Lush nearby, leaning against the wall. Anger flared in her cheeks, but she forced calm. “Miss Lapidoth is everything you described her to be,” she said.

“You’ve been quick to discover that,” Deronda replied, with irony.

“I haven’t found all your excellencies,” she said quickly. “But I think her singing is charming, and she is lovely—such a complete little person. I think she’ll be a great success.”

Deronda frowned. He didn’t answer. She knew he was displeased. The presence of Lush made her restless. She wanted to say something meaningful, but feared it would be overheard. So she stayed silent.

The silence stretched. Neither looked at the other until Lush finally moved away.

Then Gwendolen said, “You despise me for talking artificially.”

“No,” Deronda said, looking at her calmly. “That’s excusable sometimes. But I didn’t think what you just said was entirely artificial.”

“There was something that displeased you,” she said. “What was it?”

“It’s impossible to explain,” he said. “One cannot communicate nuances of feeling about words and manner.”

“You think I’m shut out from understanding them,” she said, her voice trembling slightly. “Have I shown myself so dense to everything you’ve said?”

Her eyes held his—glistening with unshed tears.

“Not at all,” he said, his voice softening. “But experience differs. We don’t all wince at the same things. I’ve had proof you’re not dense.”

He smiled gently.

“But one may feel things and still not know how to act,” she said, not returning the smile. “I begin to think we can only grow better by having people around us who raise good feelings. You must not be surprised at anything in me. I think it’s too late for me to change. I don’t know how to become wise, as you told me to be.”

“I seldom find I do any good by preaching,” Deronda said, thinking sadly of the necklace incident, and wondering if his efforts had only hardened her further. “I might as well have kept from meddling.”
