Beyond the walls of Madame Beck's establishment lay a garden, surprisingly spacious for the heart of a city. To my memory, it remains a place of gentle charm, though time, much like distance, possesses a softening power that transforms stone and pavement into something precious. Legend whispered that this site once housed a convent, a religious retreat swallowed by the expanding city centuries ago. It was said that a tragedy occurred there, leaving behind an inheritance of fear: a ghost story of a black-robed nun, a phantom seen wandering the vicinage on certain nights. Though the surrounding houses now choked the ancient grounds, relics of the past remained—ancient fruit trees, particularly a massive, dying pear-tree known as a "Methuselah." Beneath its gnarled roots, where moss clung to the earth, lay a glimpse of a smooth, black slab. The unconfirmed tale persisted that this was the portal to a vault, imprisoning the bones of a girl buried alive for a breach of her vows. Her shadow, it was said, haunted the moonlit thickets, her black veil mocking the timid eyes of those who dared to walk there.

Yet, stripped of such romantic rubbish, the garden possessed its own undeniable allure. On summer mornings, I rose early to claim it in solitude; on summer evenings, I lingered to keep tryst with the rising moon or to taste the cool kiss of the evening breeze. The turf was emerald green, the gravel paths stark white, and sun-bright nasturtiums clustered beautifully around the roots of the ancient orchard giants. There was a large *berceau* shaded by an acacia, and a smaller, more secluded bower nestled in vines climbing a high grey wall, where jasmine and ivy intertwined in a knot of beauty.

No doubt, at high noon, when Madame Beck's school was in full swing and the externes and pensionnaires exercised their lungs and limbs alongside the boys' college, the garden was merely a trite, trodden-down space. But at sunset, or during the hour of *salut*, when the externes had departed and the boarders were quiet at their studies, it was a paradise to stray down the peaceful alleys, listening to the sweet, exalted bells of St. Jean Baptiste.

One evening, I walked thus, detained longer than usual by the deepening calm, the mellow coolness, and the fragrant breathing of flowers answering the dew. I saw a light in the oratory window, indicating that the Catholic household was gathered for evening prayer—a rite from which I, as a Protestant, occasionally exempted myself.

"One moment longer," whispered solitude and the summer moon, "stay with us. All is truly quiet; for another quarter of an hour your presence will not be missed. The day's heat has tired you; enjoy these precious minutes."

The windowless backs of the houses bordering the garden, particularly one entire side, were skirted by the rear of the neighbouring college's boarding premises. This rear was mostly blank stone, save for attic loopholes for the servants and one casement in a lower story marking a master's study. Yet, despite this security, an alley running parallel to the high wall was forbidden to the pupils. Known as "l'allée défendue," any girl stepping there faced severe penalties under Madame Beck's strict rules. Teachers might enter with impunity, but the walk was narrow, flanked by neglected shrubs that wove a dense roof of branch and leaf, allowing sunlight to penetrate only in rare, dappled patches. After dusk, it was carefully shunned.

From the start, I was tempted to break this rule of avoidance. The seclusion and gloom of the path attracted me. For a long time, the fear of appearing singular held me back, but gradually, as people became accustomed to my habits and the peculiar shades of my nature—traits ingrained in me and inseparable from my identity—I became a frequent visitor. I tended the tintless flowers growing between the shrubs, cleared away the relics of past autumns, and cleaned a rustic seat at the far end. Madame Beck saw me working and smiled with apparent approval.

"Voyez-vous," she cried, "how clean this young lady Lucie is! You love this alley, do you not, mesdemoiselles?"

"Yes," I replied, "it is quiet and shady."

"C'est juste," she exclaimed with an air of kindness, recommending that I confine myself to it as much as I pleased, noting that since I was not charged with surveillance, I need not walk with the pupils. She added that I might allow her children to come there to speak English with me.

That night, I sat on the reclaimed seat, listening to the distant sounds of the city. In truth, they were not far; the school lay in the city's center, mere minutes from the park and palatial buildings. Wide streets nearby were brightly lit and teeming with life; carriages rolled through them bound for balls or the opera. The same hour that tolled curfew for our convent, extinguishing lamps and drawing curtains, rang out a summons to festal enjoyment for the gay city around us. I did not dwell on this contrast; my nature possessed few gay instincts. I had never seen a ball or an opera, and though I had heard them described and wished to see them, it was not a yearning to attain or taste, but merely a calm desire to observe a new thing.

A young crescent moon hung in the sky, visible through a space in the overhead boughs. She and the stars beside her were no strangers to me, for my childhood knew them well. I had seen that golden sign with the dark globe in its curve leaning back on azure beside an old thorn in Old England, just as it now leaned beside a stately spire in this continental capital.

Oh, my childhood! I had feelings. Passive as I lived, little as I spoke, and cold as I appeared, when I thought of past days, I could feel deeply. About the present, it was better to be stoical; about the future—such a future as mine—it was better to be dead. And in a state of catalepsy and dead trance, I studiously held the quick of my nature.

I remembered vividly whatever could excite me. Certain accidents of the weather were almost dreaded, for they woke the being I was always lulling, stirring up a craving cry I could not satisfy. One night, a thunderstorm broke; a hurricane shook us in our beds, and the Catholics rose in panic to pray to their saints. As for me, the tempest took hold with tyranny. I was roughly roused and obliged to live. I crept outside my casement, sat on its ledge with my feet on the roof of a lower building. It was wet, wild, and pitch-dark. Within the dormitory, they gathered round the night-lamp in consternation, praying aloud. I could not go in; the delight of staying with the wild hour, black and full of thunder, pealing out an ode language could never deliver, was too terribly glorious to resist.

I had longed, achingly, for something to fetch me out of that existence and lead me upwards and onwards. This longing, and all of a similar kind, had to be knocked on the head, which I did figuratively, after the manner of Jael to Sisera, driving a nail through their temples. Unlike Sisera, they did not die; they were but transiently stunned, turning on the nail with a rebellious wrench at intervals, causing the temples to bleed and the brain to thrill to its core.

That night, however, I was not so mutinous, nor so miserable. My Sisera lay quiet in the tent, slumbering. If his pain ached through his slumbers, something like an angel—the ideal—knelt near, dropping balm on the soothed temples, holding before the sealed eyes a magic glass of sweet, solemn visions, and shedding a reflex from her moonlight wings and robe over the transfixed sleeper, over the tent threshold, and over all the landscape without. Jael, the stern woman, sat apart, relenting somewhat over her captive, but more prone to dwell on the faithful expectation of Heber coming home. By these words, I meant that the cool peace and dewy sweetness of the night filled me with a mood of hope—not hope on any definite point, but a general sense of encouragement and heart-ease.

Should not such a mood, so sweet, so tranquil, so unwonted, have been the harbinger of good? Alas, no good came of it! Presently, the rude Real burst coarsely in—all evil grovelling and repellent as she too often is.

Amid the intense stillness of the stone pile overlooking the walk, the trees, and the high wall, I heard a sound. A casement creaked. Before I had time to look up and mark where, in which story, or by whom it was unclosed, a tree overhead shook as if struck by a missile, and some object dropped prone at my feet.

The clock of St. Jean Baptiste struck nine. Day was fading, though not dark; the crescent moon aided little, but the deep gilding of the point in heaven where the sun had beamed last, and the crystalline clearness of the wide space above, sustained the summer twilight. Even in my dark walk, by approaching an opening, I could have managed to read print of a small type. It was easy to see that the missile was a box, a small casket of white and coloured ivory. Its loose lid opened in my hand; violets lay within, smothering a closely folded bit of pink paper, a note superscribed, "Pour la robe grise." I wore indeed a dress of French grey.

Good. Was this a *billet-doux*? A thing I had heard of but hitherto had not had the honour of seeing or handling. Was it this sort of commodity I held between my finger and thumb?

Scarcely. I did not dream it for a moment. Suitor or admirer were thoughts my mind had not conceived. All the teachers dreamed of some lover; one, naturally of a credulous turn, believed in a future husband. All the pupils above fourteen knew of some prospective bridegroom; two or three were already affianced by their parents. But into the realm of feelings and hopes such prospects open, my speculations, far less my presumptions, had never once had warrant to intrude. If the other teachers went into town, took walks on the boulevards, or attended mass, they were very certain, according to the accounts brought back, to meet with some individual of the "opposite sex," whose rapt, earnest gaze assured them of their power to strike and attract. I cannot say that my experience tallied with theirs. I went to church and took walks, and am very well convinced that nobody minded me. There was not a girl or woman in the Rue Fossette who could not testify to having received an admiring beam from our young doctor's blue eyes at one time or other. I am obliged, however humbling it may sound, to except myself: as far as I was concerned, those blue eyes were guiltless, and calm as the sky, to whose tint theirs seemed akin.

So it came to pass that I heard the others talk, wondered often at their gaiety, security, and self-satisfaction, but did not trouble myself to look up and gaze along the path they seemed so certain of treading. This then was no *billet-doux*; and it was in settled conviction to the contrary that I quietly opened it. Thus it ran—I translate:—

"Angel of my dreams! A thousand, thousand thanks for the promise kept; scarcely did I venture to hope its fulfilment. I believed you, indeed, to be half in jest; and then you seemed to think the enterprise beset with such danger—the hour so untimely, the alley so strictly secluded—often, you said, haunted by that dragon, the English teacher—une véritable bégueule Britannique à ce que vous dites—espèce de monstre, brusque et rude comme un vieux caporal de grenadiers, et revêche comme une religieuse" (the reader will excuse my modesty in allowing this flattering sketch of my amiable self to retain the slight veil of the original tongue). "You are aware," went on this precious effusion, "that little Gustave, on account of his illness, has been removed to a master's chamber—that favoured chamber, whose lattice overlooks your prison-ground. There, I, the best uncle in the world, am admitted to visit him. How tremblingly I approached the window and glanced into your Eden—an Eden for me, though a desert for you!—how I feared to behold vacancy, or the dragon aforesaid! How my heart palpitated with delight when, through apertures in the envious boughs, I at once caught the gleam of your graceful straw-hat, and the waving of your grey dress—dress that I should recognise amongst a thousand. But why, my angel, will you not look up? Cruel, to deny me one ray of those adorable eyes!—how a single glance would have revived me! I write this in fiery haste; while the physician examines Gustave, I snatch an opportunity to enclose it in a small casket, together with a bouquet of flowers, the sweetest that blow—yet less sweet than thee, my Peri—my all-charming! ever thine-thou well knowest whom!"

"I wish I did know whom," was my comment; and the wish bore even closer reference to the person addressed in this choice document than to the writer thereof. Perhaps it was from the fiancé of one of the engaged pupils; and, in that case, there was no great harm done or intended—only a small irregularity. Several of the girls, the majority indeed, had brothers or cousins at the neighbouring college. But "la robe grise, le chapeau de paille," here surely was a clue—a very confusing one. The straw-hat was an ordinary garden head-screen, common to a score besides myself. The grey dress hardly gave more definite indication. Madame Beck herself ordinarily wore a grey dress just now; another teacher, and three of the pensionnaires, had had grey dresses purchased of the same shade and fabric as mine: it was a sort of everyday wear which happened at that time to be in vogue.
