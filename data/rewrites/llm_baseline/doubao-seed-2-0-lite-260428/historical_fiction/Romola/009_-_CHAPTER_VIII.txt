Northern folk think nothing of rising at dawn on Midsummer morning, tracing dew on the grassy edges of dusty lanes, spotting fresh green shoots among the dark mature foliage of oak and fir in the coppice, and leaning over a gate to gaze out at the newly shorn meadow, without once pausing to mark that the day is the Nativity of Saint John the Baptist.

That could never be true of a Florentine—least of all a 15th-century Florentine. For him, that sunrise gilding the Arno held a peculiar, unmistakeable warmth; the peal of the city’s bells was no empty clanging, but a voice proclaiming the greatest summer festival of Florence, the feast day of San Giovanni.

San Giovanni had been Florence’s patron saint for at least eight hundred years, ever since Lombard Queen Theodolinda commanded her subjects to venerate him above all others. Old chronicler Villani swore he could trace that honour back even further, to the reign of Constantine the Great and Pope Sylvester: the Florentines of that era cast down their old idol Mars, but dared not treat him with disrespect. They consecrated their grand, noble temple to God and the “Blessed Lord Saint John,” yet respectfully placed the old war god on a high tower overlooking the Arno, having found ancient records stating he had been chosen as the city’s tutelar deity under such powerful astral influence that breaking or insulting him would bring unthinkable ruin and chaos down on Florence. By the 15th century, that old cautious regard for the pagan god of spear and shield had long faded: no frowning Mars loomed over the Arno’s banks anymore, and the Republic’s defences were held to lie not in weapons, but in its craft and its coffers. Mercenaries could be hired for gold florins, and every single gold florin bore the image of San Giovanni.

Florence had won no small measure of wealth and glory in the long centuries between that old religious shift and the present day. There had been strife and bloodshed, of course: fighting between Guelf and Ghibelline, Black and White factions, orthodox sons of the Church and heretic Paterini, not to mention floods, famine, and plague. But the city had also conquered walled cities once far mightier than itself, most of all its hated rival Pisa, whose marble palaces were too tall and beautiful, whose merchant ships were too well known across the Greek and Italian coasts. Florence’s name had grown prouder by the year, recognised across every court in Europe and even deep into Africa, famed for its purest gold coinage, its finest dyes and textiles, its peerless scholarship and poetic genius, and its sharp, practical wits for statesmanship and banking. A Pope with a taste for epigrams had once dubbed Florentines “the fifth element,” so omnipresent was their influence. All that high destiny, even if it owed something to the stars and the Madonna dell’Impruneta, was widely credited to San Giovanni, whose face was stamped on every one of those fair gold florins.

It was only fitting, then, that the feast of San Giovanni—an ancient Church holiday already venerable in the days of Saint Augustine—should be Florence’s most beloved celebration. The vigil the night before was kept in strict old Florentine fashion, full of street dancing, raucous jesting, a little harmless stone-throwing and broken window panes, and spectacular street pageants only a city that employed the ingenious engineer and architect Cecca could produce. Cecca designed floats that looked exactly like the saintly figures seen in Perugino’s paintings: surrounded by their almond-shaped halos, floating on clouds of woven cloth, flanked by winged cherubs, they seemed to bring a piece of heaven itself down into the narrow streets of Florence. Even more marvellous were the giant saint figures, moving slowly and mysteriously through the crowds like colossi stepped down from the city’s domes and tribunes: men on stilts, steady of foot, enlarged by huge sacred masks and stuffed shoulders, escorted by ranks of attendant angels. To think only of the mechanics behind the magic was miserably unimaginative, even impious—for was there not a spark of sacred virtue in the images of holy things? Once the saints had processed past, honouring the patron saint outside his baptistery gates, came a troupe of playful devils in black costumes, fitted out with claws and whips, putting on impromptu farces of mock beatings and torn clothing, to keep the vigil in their own raucous way. The Tuscan mind slipped as easily from devout reverence to burlesque fun as water slips around a sharp corner.

On the feast day itself, all the tributary tributes from Florence’s dependent cities, districts, and villages—conquered, protected, or long subject to her rule—were brought in grand procession to the shrine of San Giovanni in the old octagonal baptistery, where every Florentine had once been marked with the sign of the cross in holy chrism on his brow. The whole city, from white-haired elders to lisping toddlers, dressed in their finest to see the great sights, and when the sun slanted low enough to cool the streets, the famous Corso race began: unshod horses draped in rich trappings thundered all the way across the city from the Porta al Prato in the northwest, through the Mercato Vecchio, to the Porta Santa Croce in the southeast, where the most magnificent palio, a banner of velvet and brocade lined with silk and trimmed with gold fringe fit for the city that half-clothed the whole wealthy world, waited on a triumphal car for the winning horse’s owner. The rest of the day was given over to more dancing, feasting, and celebration; an early 15th century chronicler wrote that there were so many weddings, gatherings, piping, song, and fine ornaments that the day made earth seem indistinguishable from Paradise.

In the year 1492, that illusion was a little harder to pull off. Lorenzo the Magnificent, subtle and beloved, was dead, and his arrogant, reckless son Piero had taken his place. It was an ill turn for Florence, unless one believed that a bad rider is easier to throw from a horse, and the public regret for Lorenzo was already fading beneath murmurings of desire for broader, fairer government that could stamp out corruption and revive the good old quarrelsome, striving days when Florence built its great palaces, fielded its own citizen soldiers, and drove out would-be tyrants at sword point, proud to keep its word even at its own loss. Lorenzo was dead, Pope Innocent was dying, and a messy Neapolitan succession dispute paired with the schemes of an ambitious, intriguing Milan was set to set all of Italy at each other’s throats before long. The times were growing difficult—but that was all the more reason the Republic should keep its traditional religious festivals alive.

That Midsummer morning in 1492 was no less bright than any other year. Long before the sun climbed high, all the symbolic tribute offerings gathered for the grand procession at their starting point in the Piazza della Signoria, that famous public square where the massive, turreted Palazzo Vecchio (the ancient Palace of the People) still stands, alongside the broad open Loggia built by Orcagna, the stage for every grand state ceremony. The sky stretched over them like the fairest blue tent, and the bells swung so vigorously that any evil spirit sensible enough to fear their noise must long since have fled the city. Windows and terraced roofs brimmed over with faces, the sombre stone houses hung draped in bright textiles, the soaring tower of the Palazzo Vecchio, the older square tower of the Bargello, and the spire of the nearby Badia all stood sentinel over the scene. Down below, on the broad polygonal stone flags of the piazza, the procession was arrayed: banners flying, horses draped in rich trappings, and gigantic ceri—great tapers so tall they were widely nicknamed “towers,” the grand descendants of the small torches the early Church used to worship by in the Roman catacombs. The procession had set out at dawn, so they could traverse the wide, sunbaked squares before the summer heat grew too fierce, before the long pomp completed its pilgrimage to the Piazza di San Giovanni.

In that final piazza, the city and its ingenious engineer Cecca had built a second shelter above the crowds: the whole Piazza del Duomo, stretching from the central octagonal baptistery to the cathedral façade and the surrounding house walls, was draped at a height of forty feet in rich blue cloth, stitched through with neatly sewn yellow lilies and familiar family and guild coats of arms. Bunches of colourful banners hung at graceful angles under this manmade blue sky, dappling the waiting spectators in rainbow light. They leaned from the windows, lined the narrow edge of the pavement, and craned their necks for the procession to arrive.

One of those spectators was Tito Melema. Bright and at ease amid all the surrounding celebration, he sat at the window of the room above Nello the barber’s shop, his right elbow propped on the red drapery hanging from the window sill, his head tilted back against his right hand, his curls pushed back against his ear. His face wore that soft, lively blandness that made him equally popular among men and women: no excitability, no gloom, no trace of the unease that comes with vain insecurity or excessive high spirits, no furrow of resentment or anger marring his brow. The only change in him from the two months since he first stumbled into the city in his weather-stained old tunic and hose was that soft new glow of good fortune, like a flower that has just drunk in a whole morning of warm sun. Nello, dressed in his holiday best, stood tucked in the narrow angle between Tito’s chair and the window frame, and at his elbow was young Pietro Cennini, the scholarly proofreader, not his practical builder brother Domenico. Tito glanced down at the crowds below, then up at the cluster of friends gathered around him in the room, most of whom had come straight from watching the procession’s start in the Piazza della Signoria. The painter Piero di Cosimo had the whole group laughing at his over-dramatic complaints about the bells’ noise: no amount of stuffing in his ears could block the vibration that hummed in his skull, he swore, and he would hide in the most remote valley in Valdarno during feast days if he were not a painter, forced to lie in wait for the rare, perfect shades of colour that only floated through the air when processions of banners wound through a gathered crowd.

Tito had just turned his laughing face away from the whimsical painter to watch the small, lively dramas playing out among the spectators lining the pavement, when at the corner of the marble Duomo steps almost directly opposite Nello’s shop, he spotted a man’s face tilted up towards him, staring with a gaze far more deliberate and loaded with meaning than the casual glance of a passing stranger. It was the tonsured head of a Dominican friar, rising above the order’s standard black mantle and white tunic, a sight common enough in Florence—but the look struck Tito as strangely familiar. There was a faint, warm echo of some memory attached to it, not at all unpleasant, but he could not for the life of him recall any pleasant association he had ever had with a monk. The glance and that flicker of recognition lasted no longer than a flash of lightning.

“Nello!” Tito called out hastily, only to add a moment later in disappointment, “Ah, he’s turned away. That tall, thin friar heading up the steps. I wanted you to tell me if you know anything of him.”

“One of the Preaching Friars,” Nello said carelessly. “You don’t expect me to know the private life of every black crow that flies past, do you?”

“I swear I recognise his face,” Tito said. “It’s an unusual one.”

“What, did you think it was our Fra Girolamo? He’s far too tall. Savonarola never shows himself off like that by random chance.”

“Besides, that loud-barking ‘hound of the Lord’ isn’t even in Florence right now,” added Francesco Cei, the popular local poet. “Piero de’ Medici dropped him a hint to take his ranting prophecies on a little trip out of town for a while.” The reference was a pun on the Dominican order’s official name, Domini Canes, the Hounds of the Lord, even used by the order itself in its own frescoes.

“The friar doesn’t rant or prophesy against any man,” a middle-aged man seated at the other corner of the window put in. “He only preaches against vice. If you take that as an attack on your poems, Francesco, that’s hardly Fra Girolamo’s fault.”

“Ah, he’s gone into the Duomo now,” Tito said, his eyes fixed eagerly on the figure. “No, I wasn’t making that mistake, Nello. Your Fra Girolamo has a high nose and a heavy lower lip. I saw him once—he’s not handsome, but this man…”

“Enough of your descriptions!” Cennini cut in. “Hark! Look! The horsemen and the banners are here. That standard there, carried on the horse draped in white trappings—the one with the red eagle clutching a green dragon in its talons, and the red lily over the eagle’s head—that’s the Gonfalon of the Guelf party, and the cavaliers riding close around it are the party’s chief officers. It’s one of our proudest banners, grumble as we will. It means the triumph of the Guelfs, which means the triumph of Florentine will, which means the triumph of the common popolani.”

“Nay, go on, Cennini,” the middle-aged man at the window laughed. “Which means the triumph of the fat popolani over the lean, which in turn means the triumph of the very fattest popolano over everyone less fat than him.”

“Cronaca, you’re getting sententious,” the printer said. “Fra Girolamo’s preaching will spoil you, make you take life by the wrong handle. Trust me, your architectural cornices will lose half their beauty if you start mixing bitterness into them. That’s the harsh German maniera style you used to rail at when you came back from Rome. The next palace you build, we’ll find you trying to carve the frate’s doctrines straight into the stonework.”

“That’s a fine show of cavaliers,” Tito said, already having learned the best way to endear himself to Florentines. “But are there not strangers riding among them? I see foreign costumes.”

“Assuredly,” Cennini said. “You’re looking at ambassadors from France, Milan, and Venice, and behind them are English and German nobles. It’s tradition for all distinguished foreign visitors to pay their respects to San Giovanni by riding in the train of that gonfalon. For my part, I think our Florentine cavaliers sit their horses as well as any of those rough northern cut-and-thrust men, whose wits live in their heels and their saddles. That Venetian rider over there, I’d wager he’d feel far more comfortable perched on the back of a dolphin. We know a thing or two about horsemanship here, we outshine all of Italy in the Giostra jousting games, and the fortunes we pour into them. But you’ll see a far finer show of our leading citizens soon, Melema. My own brother is marching with the officers of the Zecca, the Mint.”

“The banners are the far better sight,” Piero di Cosimo said, forgetting the bells entirely in his delight at the winding river of colour from the approaching tributary standards. “Florentine men are nothing special; from this distance they just look like a sorry patch of sallow flesh above black clothing. But those banners, with their velvet, satin, minever, and brocade, the endless play of soft light and shadow shifting over their surfaces? Pah! All human talk and doings are a tame joke. The only true passionate life lives in form and colour.”

“Ay Piero, if Satan himself could paint, you’d sell your soul to learn his secrets,” Nello teased. “Though there’s little chance of that, seeing even the blessed angels are terrible at chiaroscuro, if we go by their masterpiece, the Madonna Nunziata.”

“There go the banners of Pisa and Arezzo,” Cennini said. “Look at that sullen-faced man carrying the Pisa standard! You might as well bring your banner to our San Giovanni with good grace, Messer Pisano. The old proverb says ‘Pisans false, Florentines blind’—the second half of that no longer holds true. Behind them come the ensigns of all our subject towns and signories, Melema. They’ll all hang in the baptistery until this day next year, when they’ll be replaced with new ones.”

“They are a beautiful sight,” Tito agreed. “San Giovanni must be as pleased with that fine Italian loom work as Minerva was with her sacred peplos, especially since he’s always content to wear so little drapery himself. But those whirling tower-like ceri would soon make me fall out this window in sympathetic vertigo.”

The “towers” Tito spoke of were the most beloved part of the procession for the Florentine populace. These giant ceri, some so large they had to be mounted on wheels, were not solid wax, but hollow frames of wood and pasteboard, gilded, carved, and painted with successive circles of figures: knights on horseback, foot soldiers with lance and shield, dancing maidens, animals, trees, and fruit, “all things that could delight the eye and the heart,” as one old chronicler put it. Men stood inside their hollow frames to whirl them continuously as they moved, creating a dizzying phantasmagoric effect that made the whole line of spinning painted circles blur into a wonderful, disorienting spectacle.

“Pestilenza!” Piero di Cosimo said, stepping back from the window. “Those whirling circles stacked one on top of the other are worse than all the bells jangling put together. Let me know when the last taper has passed.”

“Nay, you’ll want us to call you when the country folk come carrying their torches, surely,” Nello said. “You wouldn’t miss the peasants from the Mugello and the Casentino, the ones your favourite Leonardo da Vinci could draw a hundred grotesque sketches of.”

“No,” Piero said firmly. “I won’t look at a thing until the car of the Zecca comes past. I’ve seen more than enough clowns carrying lopsided tapers, cowled and un-cowled, to last me a lifetime.”

“Here it comes, then, Piero—the car of the Zecca,” Nello called out after a short interval, as the smaller, trailing ceri trundled slowly past the window.

“Fediddio!”
