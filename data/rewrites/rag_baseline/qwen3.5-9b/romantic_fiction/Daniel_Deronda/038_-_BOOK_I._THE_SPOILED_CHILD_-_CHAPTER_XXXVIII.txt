There exists a school of tragic thought that posits the truest sorrow of Prometheus was not his binding *after* he had stolen the celestial fire and wedged it into the reed (*narthex*) to convey it to mankind, but rather the torment that preceded the theft. It is the agony of being thrust by the brute forces of institutionalized power—Kratos and Bia—into a solitude of despised ideas, shackled by the throbbing paralysis of poverty and disease. It is a specific kind of exile where thousands walk past, yet none truly see.

"Second-sight" serves as a banner over contested territory, yet it remains an empirical certainty that certain souls possess yearnings and conceptions—indeed, traveled conclusions—that inevitably crystallize into images possessing a prophetic foresight. The act they intend to perform rises before them in its entirety, casting a coercive shadow; the event they hunger for or dread grows with the rapidity of a seed, fed by countless unseen impressions. These visionaries are not necessarily less capable of rigorous argument nor less sane than the common calculators of the marketplace. Sometimes, their natures offer manifold openings, reminiscent of the hundred gates of Thebes, allowing a greater and more miscellaneous influx of experience than flows through a narrow, beadle-guarded portal. Of course, there are abject specimens of the visionary, just as there is a diminutive mammal one might trap within the fold of a glove. This tiny relative of the elephant harbors no malice; yet, what great mental or social archetype is immune to producing offspring whose insignificance is both grotesque and toxic? One hesitates to consider the entire genus of "patriots"; or to imagine the elbow-shoving that might occur on Judgment Day among those who styled themselves authors, clutching volumes in their hands or stacked on carts.

This apologia for inevitable kinship serves to introduce the facts concerning Mordecai, whose silhouette had gnawed itself into Daniel Deronda's mind as a new, unanswered question. Yet, Deronda's interest was merely a vague, expectant suspension. The consumptive-looking Jew, appearing to be a fervid scholar subsisting on a quiet handicraft much like Spinoza, fit into none of Deronda's anticipations.

For Mordecai, however, the impact of their meeting was transformative. For many winters, while acutely aware of his ebbing physical vitality and a widening spiritual loneliness, his passionate desire had concentrated itself in a singular yearning: to find a young ear into which he could pour his mind as a testament. He sought a soul kindred enough to accept the spiritual product of his own brief, painful life as a mission to be executed. Remarkably, the hopefulness that often acts as a beneficent delusion for consumptive patients was in Mordecai wholly diverted from the prospect of bodily recovery and channeled entirely into this yearning for transmission. The desperate cry that had panted upward from overwhelming discouragement had grown into hope, and that hope hardened into a confident belief. Instead of being checked by his clear conception of hastening decline, this belief intensified into an expectant faith in a prophecy destined to fulfill itself in a fleeting window of time.

Several years had passed since he first began measuring men with a keen glance, searching for a possibility that evolved into a distinct conception. Initially, this clarity was achieved primarily through contrast: he sought a man who differed from himself. Tracing the reasons within his own self for the rebuffs he endured and the hindrances that besieged him, he imagined a counterpart who possessed all the elements necessary for sympathy, yet embodied them differently. This man must be a Jew, intellectually cultured and morally fervid—a nature ready to be filled from Mordecai's—but his face and frame must be beautiful and strong. He must be accustomed to the refinements of social life, his voice flowing with a full and easy current, his circumstances free from sordid need. He must glorify the possibilities of the Jew, refusing to sit and wonder as Mordecai did, bearing the stamp of his people amidst the signs of poverty and waning breath.

Sensitive to physical characteristics, Mordecai had, both abroad and in England, scrutinized pictures as well as men. In vacant hours, he would linger in the National Gallery, searching for paintings that might feed his hopefulness with grave and noble types of the human form, suitable to his own race. Yet, he returned in disappointment. Instances are scattered thinly across the galleries of Europe where the fortune or selection of even the chief masters has endowed art with a face that is simultaneously young, grand, and beautiful. Where melancholy exists, it is not a feeble passivity but enters into the foreshadowed capability of heroism.

Observers may recall his emaciated figure and dark eyes deep in their sockets as he stood before a picture that touched him to new or habitual meditation. He commonly wore a cloth cap with black fur trim, which no painter would have asked him to remove. Spectators were likely to perceive him as an odd-looking Jew, perhaps one who extracted money from pictures. Mordecai, when he looked at them, was perfectly aware of the impression he made. Experience had rendered him morbidly alive to how a man's poverty and other physical disadvantages cheapen his ideas, unless those ideas belong to a Peter the Hermit ringing a tocsin for the rabble. Yet, he was too sane and generous to attribute his spiritual banishment solely to the excusable prejudices of others; certain incapacities of his own had sealed the sentence of exclusion. Hence, his imagination constructed another man—one who would be something more ample than the "second soul" bestowed, according to Cabalist notions, to aid the insufficient first. This figure would be a blooming human life, ready to incorporate all that was worthiest in an existence whose visible, palpable part was burning itself fast away. His inward need for the conception of this expanded, prolonged self was reflected as an outward necessity. The thoughts of his heart—that ancient phrase best shadows the truth—seemed to him too precious, too closely interwoven with the growth of things to lack a further destiny. As the more beautiful, stronger, and more executive self took shape in his mind, he loved it beforehand with an affection that was half-identifying, half-contemplative and grateful.

Mordecai's mind worked so constantly in images that his coherent trains of thought often resembled the significant dreams attributed to sleepers by awake persons in their most inventive moments. Nay, they often mirrored genuine dreams in their manner of breaking off the passage from the known to the unknown. For a long while, he habitually conceived of the Being answering to his need as one distantly approaching or turning his back toward him, darkly painted against a golden sky. The reason for the golden sky lay in one of Mordecai's habits. He was keenly alive to the poetic aspects of London; a favorite resort, when strength and leisure allowed, was the bridges, particularly about sunrise or sunset. Even when bending over watch-wheels and trinkets, or seated in a small upper room overlooking dingy bricks and cracked windows, his imagination spontaneously planted him on a spot commanding a far-stretching scene. His thoughts operated in wide spaces; whenever possible, he tried to subject himself to the influences of a large sky. Leaning on the parapet of Blackfriars Bridge, gazing meditatively, the breadth and calm of the river—with its long vista half-hazy, half-luminous—the grand, dim masses of tall buildings signaling world commerce, and the oncoming of boats and barges from the distant stillness into sound and color—all entered his mood and blended indistinguishably with his thinking. It acted as a fine symphony to which we can hardly be said to listen, creating a medium that bears up our spiritual wings. Thus, the figure representing Mordecai's longing was mentally seen darkened by the excess of light in the aerial background. But in the inevitable progression of his imagination toward fuller detail, he ceased to see the figure with its back turned. It began to advance, and a face became discernible. The attributes of youth, beauty, refinement, Jewish birth, and noble gravity coalesced into a form and color that were hardly individual but typical, gathered from his memory of faces seen among the Jews of Holland and Bohemia, and from paintings that revived that memory.

Reverently, it may be said of this mature spiritual need that it was akin to the boy's or girl's picturing of a future beloved. However, the stirrings of such young desire are feeble compared with the passionate current of an ideal life straining to embody itself, made intense by resistance to imminent dissolution. The visionary form became a companion and auditor, keeping a place not only in the waking imagination but in those dreams of lighter slumber wherein it is truest to say, "I sleep, but my heart waketh"—when the disturbing trivial story of yesterday is charged with the impassioned purpose of years.

Of late, the urgency of irremediable time, measured by the gradual choking of life, had turned Mordecai's trust into an agitated watch for the fulfillment that must be at hand. Was the bell on the verge of tolling, the sentence about to be executed? The deliverer's footsteps must be near—the deliverer who was to rescue Mordecai's spiritual travail from oblivion and grant it an abiding place in the best heritage of his people. Many would count this yearning an insane exaggeration of his own value, even if his ideas were as true and precious as those of Columbus or Newton. They might dismiss it as the sublimer part for a man to declare, "If not I, then another," and hold cheap the meaning of his own life.
