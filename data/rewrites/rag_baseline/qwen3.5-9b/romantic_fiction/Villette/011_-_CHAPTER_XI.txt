It was the height of summer, and the heat pressed down upon Villette with a suffocating weight. Within the pensionnat, the atmosphere shifted abruptly; Georgette, Madame Beck's youngest charge, succumbed to a sudden fever. Simultaneously, Désirée, previously plagued by chronic ailments, experienced a miraculous recovery. Seizing the opportunity to mitigate the risk of contagion spreading through the household, Madame promptly dispatched both girls to the safety of the countryside under the care of Bonne-Maman.

With the immediate crisis requiring professional intervention, Madame, despite the recent and inconvenient return of Dr. Pillule—who had already visited the establishment for a week—summoned his English counterpart, Dr. John, to resume his duties. The situation quickly escalated. Several other pensionnaires began complaining of headaches and exhibited symptoms mirroring Georgette's condition. In my estimation, this was the definitive moment for Dr. Pillule's recall; a prudent directorship would never allow a physician of such youthful inexperience to attend directly to the students. However, Madame Beck, while undeniably prudent, possessed a capacity for audacious risk-taking that defied conventional wisdom. She not only reintroduced Dr. John to the school division but formally established him as the attending physician for Blanche de Melcy, a girl of considerable pride and beauty, and Angélique, her vain and flirtatious companion.

Dr. John appeared visibly gratified by this display of confidence. Had discretion alone dictated his conduct, he might have accepted the role with humility; yet, in this specific milieu—a realm dominated by conventual strictures and confessional secrecy—such a prominent male presence could not be tolerated without consequence within a *pensionnat de demoiselles*. Rumors spread with alarming velocity: the schoolgirls gossiped openly, whispers circulated in the kitchens, and the wider town seized upon the news. Parents responded with formal letters and visits of reprimand. If Madame had lacked fortitude, she would have been undone instantly; a dozen rival educational institutions stood ready to capitalize on this perceived lapse in judgment to secure her ruin. Fortunately, Madame was not weak. Though she operated with the mindset of a minor Jesuit, I observed her handling the crisis with remarkable composure. I applauded silently, cheering internally for her ability to maintain her temper, her managerial skill, and her firm resolve.

She faced the agitated parents with a demeanor of easy grace, a quality unmatched by anyone I knew, whether attributed to innate character or cultivated performance. This specific blend of approachability and frankness allowed her to achieve objectives where severe gravity and logical argumentation would have failed. "Poor Doctor Jean!" she would exclaim, chuckling warmly while rubbing her small, pale hands together. "My dear young man! The best creature in the world!" She proceeded to justify her actions by explaining that she employed him primarily for her own children, who were so enamored with him that they would scream in distress at the prospect of any other doctor. She framed the arrangement as a matter of personal trust extended to others, noting merely that it was a temporary expedient. Blanche and Angélique, suffering from migraines, had required a prescription from Dr. John; that was the extent of the matter. The parents' objections dissolved immediately. Blanche and Angélique reinforced Madame's position by loudly praising their physician in duet, followed by unanimous declarations from the other pupils that they desired Dr. John exclusively during future illnesses. Madame laughed, and the parents joined in.

This incident revealed a peculiar trait of the Labassecouriens: an exaggerated organ of philoprogenitiveness, wherein the will of the offspring superseded all other considerations. Consequently, Madame emerged from the ordeal with flying colors, credited with acting in a spirit of maternal partiality, thereby enhancing her reputation as a director more than ever before. To this day, I struggle to comprehend why she risked her standing solely for the sake of Dr. John. The prevailing narrative among the staff—students, teachers, and servants alike—was unequivocal: she intended to marry him. They had seemingly settled the matter; the disparity in their ages posed no obstacle in their collective imagination. It must be acknowledged that appearances lent credence to this theory. Madame seemed determined to retain his services, displaying a conspicuous disregard for her former protégé, Dr. Pillule. She insisted on receiving his visits personally, maintaining an unwavering cheerfulness, blight, and benevolence toward him. Furthermore, around this time, she marked a distinct shift in her attire. The casual morning disarray, nightcaps, and shawls were abandoned. Every visit from Dr. John found her adorned with neatly arranged auburn braids, wearing a silk dress tailored precisely, and sporting neat laced brodequins instead of slippers. Her entire toilette was presented as a flawless model, fresh as a flower.

While I suspected her intentions extended no further than demonstrating to a handsome man that she was not devoid of aesthetic appeal—and she certainly was not plain—I recognized that she possessed qualities that compensated for a lack of conventional beauty or elegant form. She pleased observers without possessing traditional youth or its frivolous graces, yet she remained endlessly engaging, never monotonous, insipid, colorless, or flat. Her unfaded hair, her eyes holding a temperate blue light, and her cheeks flushed with a wholesome, fruit-like bloom offered a constant, moderate pleasure. One wonders if she harbored visions of adopting Dr. John as a husband, inviting him to her well-appointed home, utilizing her reported savings to ensure his comfort for the remainder of his days. Did Dr. John share these suspicions? I have witnessed him emerging from her presence with a mischievous half-smile playing about his lips and a look in his eyes suggesting masculine vanity, elated and tickled. Despite his evident good looks and nature, he was not perfect; indeed, he must have been profoundly flawed if he roguishly encouraged aspirations he never intended to fulfill. Yet, did he not intend them to succeed? Rumors claimed he possessed no fortune and relied entirely on his profession. Madame, though perhaps fourteen years his senior, was the type of woman who refused to age, wither, or collapse. Their relationship was undoubtedly cordial. Perhaps he did not love her, but in this world, how many individuals truly love, or even marry for love? We awaited the conclusion. Whatever he awaited, or whatever he watched for, remained unknown to me, yet the peculiarity of his manner—an expectant, vigilant, absorbed, and eager gaze—never faded; if anything, it intensified. He had always existed beyond the reach of my understanding, and I sensed he was drifting increasingly far beyond my comprehension.

One morning, Georgette's fever worsened, rendering her irritable and inconsolable. She wept, refusing to be soothed. I suspected that a prescribed draught had disagreed with her constitution and questioned whether it should be administered. Impatient for clarification, I awaited the doctor's arrival to consult him. The doorbell rang, and he was admitted; I was certain of this, for I heard his voice addressing the portress. It was his habit to ascend directly to the nursery, climbing approximately three steps at a time, appearing like a cheerful surprise. Five minutes passed, then ten, yet I saw or heard nothing of him. Where could he be? Perhaps waiting in the corridor below. Georgette continued her plaintive wailing, appealing to me with her familiar cry, "Minnie, Minnie, me very poorly!" until my heart ached. Descending to investigate, I found the corridor empty. Had he vanished? Could he be with Madame in the dining hall? Impossible; I had left her only moments prior, observing her dressing in her private chamber. Listening intently, I noted activity elsewhere: three pupils practiced instruments in the dining room and adjoining drawing rooms, separated from the corridor only by the portress's cabinet, originally designed as a boudoir. Further away, in the oratory, a class of twelve or more engaged in a singing lesson, currently performing a "barcarole" containing the words "fraîche," "brisée," and "Venise." Given these conditions, what could possibly be audible? Much, certainly, provided the sound served a purpose. Indeed, I heard a giddy, high-pitched female laughter emanating from the aforementioned cabinet, located just behind the door where I stood, which was half-open. Accompanying this was a man's voice, speaking in a soft, deep, pleading tone, uttering words of which I caught only the adjuration, "For God's sake!" After a brief pause, Dr. John emerged. His eyes were bright, yet devoid of joy or triumph; his fair English complexion was flushed; his brow bore an expression of bafflement, torture, anxiety, and tenderness simultaneously. The open door served as a screen for me, but had I been fully visible, I believe he would have passed without noticing. A profound mortification, a strong vexation, or perhaps, as I interpreted it later, a deep sorrow and a sense of injustice, gripped his soul. Who was the tormentor? What entity within that house held such power over him? I presumed Madame was in her chamber; the room he had exited was dedicated solely to the portress, Rosine Matou—a pretty but unprincipled French grisette, airy, fickle, dressy, vain, and mercenary. Surely, the ordeal he endured was not caused by her? While I pondered, her voice, clear yet somewhat sharp, broke out in a lively French song, trilling through the partially open door.
