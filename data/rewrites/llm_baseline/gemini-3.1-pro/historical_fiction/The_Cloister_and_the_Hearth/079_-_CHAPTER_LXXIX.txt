The acoustic sanctity of the hearth had been fundamentally altered by old Martin’s passing. Where there had once been the comforting, rhythmic respiration of the agrarian elder and the soft murmurs of his antiquated dialect—a living archive of cultural memory now permanently erased—there was only a resonant, hollow silence. Margaret stood before the heavy oak table, the repetitive, percussive *thwack* of her wooden pestle against the stone mortar doing little to mask the void. She who had wept so fiercely for poor old Martin was not likely to bear his loss with stoicism; to Margaret, the preparation of sustenance was a sacred, multi-generational continuum, and she had lost her most reverent consumer. 

In vain, Catherine attempted to patch the silence with the standardized vocabulary of mourning. She deployed the dominant lexicon of the era, offering sterile platitudes that fell into the kitchen's delicate sonic environment like dropped tinware. She insisted it was a happy release, that the old tree was ripe for the felling, and, most gratingly, that there were now but two mouths to feed and one care the less. 

“Such cares are all the joys I have,” Margaret whispered, the words catching in her throat, incapable of translating the subjective abyss of her grief into Catherine’s pragmatic terms. “They fill my desolate heart, which now rings as empty as this room. Oh, empty chair, my bosom aches to look upon thee.” She paused, tracing the rim of a ceramic bowl where she had once whisked marrow-rich broths for him. “Poor old man, how could I love him by halves, I that did use to sit and watch him eat, knowing that but for my hearth, he would die of hunger? He, so wise, so learned in the old tongues erst, was got to be as helpless as my own sweet babe, and I loved him as if he had been my child instead of my father. Oh, empty chair! Oh, empty heart! Well-a-day!” 

The pious tears would not be denied, seasoning the air with the brine of true mourning. Catherine, recognizing the profound untranslatability of Margaret’s sorrow, finally allowed her hollow commonplaces to die away, leaving a respectful quiet. 

Days later, the elder woman offered a different sort of confession, her voice dropping into the raw, unpolished cadence of their native, agrarian dialect. “I speak to thee out o’ my head, and not out o’ my bosom; thou dost well to be deaf to me. Were I in thy place, I should mourn the old man all one as thou dost.” Margaret embraced her, the authentic acoustic resonance of true sympathy doing her far more good than the sterile platitudes ever could. 

But Catherine’s agrarian pragmatism soon overrode her restraint. “My poor girl, you were not born to live alone,” she said one afternoon, interrupting the rhythmic simmering of a pot of spiced quince. “I have got to look on you as my own daughter. Waste not thine youth upon my son Gerard. Either he is dead, or he is a traitor. It cuts my heart to say it, but who can help seeing it? Thy father is gone, and I cannot always be aside thee. And here is an honest lad that loves thee well this many a day. I’d take him and Comfort together. Heaven hath sent us these creatures to torment us and comfort us; we are just nothing in the world without ’em.”

Margaret looked up, utterly perplexed, a wooden spoon dripping rich, amber syrup suspended in her hand. “Why, sure you are not so blind as not to see it?” Catherine pressed.

“What? Who?”

“Who but this Luke Peterson.”

“What, our Luke? The boy that carries my market basket?” 

“Nay, he is over nineteen, and a fine healthy lad. I have made inquiries for you; they all do say he is a capable workman, and never touches a drop—which is much in a Rotterdam lad, for they are mostly half man, half sponge.”

Margaret smiled for the first time in many days, misinterpreting the dynamic through the lens of her own culinary devotion. “Luke loves my dried puddings dearly,” she explained, her voice warming with the pride of a meticulous food historian. “I make them precisely to his mind—rich suet and barley, studded with dried currants steeped in tart verjuice, bound in caul fat, and hung to cure in the sweet smoke of the chimney draft. ’Tis the puddings he comes a-courting here, honoring the old recipes.”

Then she suddenly turned red, the spoon clattering against the iron pot. “But if I thought he came after your son’s wife that is, or ought to be, I’d soon put him to the door.”

“Nay, nay; for Heaven’s sake let me not make mischief. Poor lad! Why, girl, Fancy will not be bridled. Bless you, I wormed it out of him near a twelvemonth agone.”

“Oh, mother, and you let him?”

“Well, I thought of you. I said to myself, ‘If he is fool enough to be her slave for nothing, all the better for her.’ A lone woman is lost without a man about her to fetch and carry her little matters. But now my mind is changed, and I think the best use you can put him to is to marry him.”

“So then, his own mother is against him, and would wed me to the first comer,” Margaret said softly, the betrayal tasting like ash on her tongue. “Ah, Gerard, thou hast but me. I will not believe thee dead till I see thy tomb, nor false till I see thee with another lover in thine hand. Foolish boy. I shall ne’er be civil to him again.”

Afflicted with the busybody’s protection, Luke Peterson soon met an icy reception in the kitchen where he had hitherto found warmth and aromatic sustenance. Finding himself very little spoken to, and then only sharply and irritably—a harsh, discordant shift from the previously harmonious acoustic ecology of their interactions—the great soft fellow fell to whimpering. He asked Margaret plump if he had done anything to offend her.

“Nothing. I am to blame. I am curst,” Margaret said, aggressively kneading a mound of rye dough. “If you will take my counsel, you will keep out of my way awhile.”

“It is all along of me, Luke,” Catherine interjected. 

“You, Mistress Catherine? Why, what have I done for you to set her against me?”

“Nay, I meant all for the best. I told her I saw you were looking towards her through a wedding ring. But she won’t hear of it.”

“There was no need to tell her that, wife; she knows I am courting her this twelvemonth.”

“Not I,” said Margaret, slapping the dough against the floured board with a sharp *crack*. “Or I should never have opened the street door to you.”

“Why, I come here every Saturday night,” Luke protested. “And that is how the lads in Rotterdam do court. If we sup with a lass o’ Saturdays, that’s wooing.”

Margaret froze. It was a behavioral dialect native to the city, an unspoken lexicon of romance she had entirely failed to translate. “Oh, that is Rotterdam, is it? Then next time you come, let it be Thursday or Friday. For my part, I thought you came after my puddings, boy. I thought you possessed a palate for the old ways.”

“I like your puddings well enough,” Luke said tenderly. “You make them better than mother does, with the mace and the slow curing. But I like you still better than the puddings.”

“Then you have seen the last of them,” she replied. To treat the preservation of her culinary heritage as a mere vehicle for base seduction felt like an affront to the ritual itself. “How dare you talk so to another man’s wife, and him far away?” She ended gently, but with iron firmness, “You need not trouble yourself to come here any more, Luke; I can carry my basket myself.”

“Oh, very well,” said Luke. After sitting silent and stupid for a little while, he rose and said sadly to Catherine, “Dame, I daresay I have got the sack,” and went out into the bustling street.

But the next Saturday, the ambient tranquility of the evening was pierced by a pathetic sound. Catherine found him seated on the doorstep, blubbering, his whimpers echoing jarringly against the cobblestones. He told her he had got used to coming there, to the smells of the roasting meats and the rhythmic hum of the kitchen, and every other place seemed unmoored and strange. 

Catherine went in and told Margaret. Margaret sighed, the anger leaving her as she scraped the last of the rendered fat into a crock. “Poor Luke,” she murmured. “He might come in for his share of the hearth, if he could know his place, and treat me like a married wife.”

On this being communicated to Luke through the heavy timber door, he hesitated. 

“Pshaw!” Catherine whispered to the boy, leaning into the agrarian wisdom of patient endurance. “Promises are pie-crusts—made of rendered lard and winter wheat, meant to be broken in time. Promise her all the world, sooner than sit outside like a fool, when a word will carry you inside. Now you humour her in everything, and then, if poor Gerard come not home and claim her, you will be sure to have her—in time. A lone woman is aye to be tired out, thou foolish boy.”
