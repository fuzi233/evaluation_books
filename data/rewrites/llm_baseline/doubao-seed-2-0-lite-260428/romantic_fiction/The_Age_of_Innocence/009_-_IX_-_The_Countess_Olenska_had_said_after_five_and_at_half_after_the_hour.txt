Countess Olenska had said she would be free “after five”; and when the half-hour chimed, Newland Archer pressed his thumb to the bell of the peeling stucco house far down West Twenty-third Street, its frail cast-iron balcony all but swallowed whole by a giant, tangled wisteria vine. The neighbourhood was an odd choice for a woman of her family’s standing: her nearest neighbours were tiny dressmakers, bird stuffers, and ragtag “people who wrote”, and further down the unkempt cobblestone lane Archer spotted the weathered wooden cottage his acquaintance Winsett the journalist had once pointed out to him on a late-night stroll. He’d shivered then, wondering if all the great capitals of the world housed their thinkers in such shabby, mean little shelters. Ellen’s home was only barely better than Winsett’s, its modest front redeemed by a fresh coat of pale paint around the window frames, and Archer’s chest tightened as he thought: that Polish count of hers must have stolen not just her fortune, but every last one of her illusions too.

It had been a thoroughly rotten day. He’d lunched with the Wellands, hoping to steal May away for a walk in Central Park, to tell her how stunning she’d looked the night before, how proud he was to be hers, and beg her to push their wedding date forward. But Mrs. Welland had shaken her head firmly, reminding him their round of family calls was barely half done, and when he’d mentioned moving the wedding up, she’d sighed reproachfully, “Twelve dozen of everything, all hand-embroidered, Newland, we can’t rush that.” They’d piled into the family landau and trundled from one brownstone doorstep to the next, and by the end of the afternoon Archer felt like some well-trained wild animal, trotted out for every distant cousin’s approval. He knew his anthropology books were making him over-critical of what was, after all, just a loving family’s fuss over their daughter, but the thought of waiting all the way until next autumn to marry her, of months more of this endless tedious social parade, made his spirits sink cold and damp. “Tomorrow we do the Chiverses and the Dallases!” Mrs. Welland had called after him as he left, and he realised they were working their way alphabetically through every related household in New York, and they hadn’t even gotten past the first quarter of the alphabet.

He’d meant to tell May that Ellen had asked—no, almost commanded—him to call that afternoon, but there had never been a quiet spare minute to mention it. Besides, it felt absurd to bring up: May had been the one so eager for him to be kind to her cousin, hadn’t she? That very wish was what had pushed their engagement announcement forward weeks ahead of schedule. It gave him a strange, unnameable little jolt to think that if Ellen Olenska had never come back to New York, he might still be a free man, or at least less irrevocably tied to this path. But May had wanted it this way, so he felt almost relieved of the weight of keeping her in the dark about the visit.

When the door opened to a swarthy, grinning Sicilian maid who gestured him wordlessly through a narrow hall to a low drawing room lit only by a crackling fire, curiosity was thrumming under his skin. He couldn’t puzzle out the tone of her summons; she was far less simple and straightforward than the polite, melancholy woman she’d presented as at the van der Luydens’ dinner. The maid left him standing there for so long he began to wonder if she thought he’d come to wind the visibly stopped mantel clock, until she came back with a lamp and Archer fumbled through half-remembered lines of Dante to ask after her mistress, and got the giddy reply: “La signora e fuori; ma verra subito—she’s out, but she’ll be here any minute.”

By the lamp’s glow he drank in the room, soft and shadowy and nothing at all like any New York parlour he’d ever stepped into. The little bits of “wreckage” Ellen had brought back from her life abroad were scattered everywhere: slim dark wood side tables, a tiny delicate Greek bronze on the mantel, a stretch of rich red damask nailed straight to the water-stained wallpaper behind two old, unidentifiable Italian paintings. Archer prided himself on his knowledge of Italian art, had devoured Ruskin and Pater and Symonds, but these paintings confused him, didn’t fit the “approved” Italian school works every other household hung on their walls. For a second he felt foolish, waiting alone in a strange lady’s house at dusk, and a flicker of worry shot through him: what if May turned up to call on her cousin, and found him here like they were old intimate friends? But he sank into the armchair by the fire anyway, stretched his boots out to the logs, and let the weird, warm atmosphere wrap around him. The shabby rented house, that would have held nothing but pampas grass and Rogers statuettes in Medora Manson’s care, had been transformed with a few small touches into something intimate, foreign, steeped in old romantic secrets. He tried to parse the magic: the way the furniture was grouped, the single vase holding just two Jacqueminot roses (no New York hostess ever bought less than a dozen), the faint, heady scent hanging in the air that wasn’t the usual lavender sachet on handkerchiefs, but a mix of Turkish coffee, ambergris, and dried rose petals you’d only find in some far-off bazaar.

His mind wandered to the house the Wellands had already picked out for him and May, the new build on remote East Thirty-ninth Street, faced in that ghastly greenish-yellow stone the younger architects were championing as a rebellion against all New York’s uniform cold chocolate-brown brownstone. The plumbing was perfect, they’d told him, and he could have the library lined with plain Eastlake furniture and glassless bookcases, but the rest of it stretched before him like a life sentence: every evening he’d walk through those cast-iron railings, into the Pompeian vestibule, past the varnished yellow wood wainscoting, and sit in a drawing room draped in purple satin and yellow tufting, full of sham Buhl tables and gilt vitrines crammed with modern Saxe figurines. He’d daydreamed of a long honeymoon travelling, of even wintering in Egypt, but the Wellands were insistent they needed a proper New York house to come home to, and he knew he had no real way to argue.

The maid came back to stir the fire, draw the curtains, and murmur consolingly “Verra—verra, she’s coming,” before slipping out again. Archer was just standing up, half ready to leave before he made a fool of himself, when he heard the clink of hooves on the cobblestones outside. He pulled the curtain aside and peered out: Julius Beaufort’s neat English brougham was pulled up at the curb, the banker stepping down to hand Ellen out of the carriage. They stood talking a moment, Beaufort tipping his hat to her before he climbed back into his cab, and she turned to mount the steps.

When she walked into the room, there was no trace of surprise on her face at finding him there—surprise seemed an emotion she simply never bothered to waste. “How do you like my funny little house?” she asked, untying the ribbon of her velvet bonnet and tossing it and her cloak onto a side table, her eyes soft and meditative as she looked at him. “To me, it’s heaven.”

“It’s delightful, the way you’ve arranged it,” Archer said, even as he winced at how flat the words sounded.

“Oh, it’s a tiny shabby place, all my relations despise it,” she laughed, sinking into the chair opposite his by the fire. “But at least it’s not as gloomy as the van der Luydens’.”

The words jolted him like an electric shock. No one in New York dared call the van der Luydens’ grand, stately mansion gloomy—everyone who was ever allowed inside shivered in its halls and called it “handsome”. But suddenly Archer was glad she’d said what every single one of them had only ever thought. “It’s perfect, what you’ve done in here,” he corrected himself.

“I love that it’s mine,” she said, her voice dropping so low he almost missed the words. “That I can be alone here, and not have anyone watching every move I make.”

“You like being alone that much?” he asked, awkward, before he could stop himself.

“As long as my friends don’t let me get lonely,” she smiled. She leaned back, tucking her hands behind her head, and her lids drooped as she stared into the fire. “This is the hour I like best, don’t you think?”

“I was afraid you’d forgotten I was coming,” he said, a little stiff. “Beaufort must have been keeping you very occupied.”

She looked amused. “Have you waited long? He took me around to look at other houses, because apparently no one in New York will let me stay in this one. I’ve never been in a city where everyone turns their nose up at living in a ‘fun’ neighbourhood. What does it matter where you live? They say this street is respectable, after all.”

“It’s not fashionable,” Archer told her.

“Fashionable? Do all of you care that much about it? Why can’t you make your own fashions?” She sighed, softening. “But I suppose I’ve lived too freely for too long. All I want now is to fit in here, to feel safe, like people are looking after me.”

Archer’s chest ached, just like it had the night before when she’d said she was desperate for guidance. “That’s exactly what all your friends here want for you,” he said, with a flicker of sarcasm. “New York’s the safest place in the world.”

“Isn’t it?” she said, missing the mockery entirely, her face lighting up. “It feels just like a holiday you get when you’ve been a very good little girl and finished all your lessons.”

The analogy didn’t sit quite right with him. He didn’t mind making fun of New York himself, but he hated hearing anyone else dismiss it so lightly. He wondered if she realised just how powerful the unspoken rules of the city were, how close she’d come to total social ruin before the van der Luydens had stepped in to rescue her. He suspected she still didn’t see any of it, that her view of New York was still soft and blurry, and that thought nettled him.

“Last night New York turned itself inside out to welcome you,” he said. “The van der Luydens don’t do anything by halves.”

“They were so kind! It was such a lovely party,” she said, as if she’d been at a small tea at the old Miss Lannings’ instead of the most exclusive event of the season.

“The van der Luydens are the most powerful people in New York society,” Archer explained, feeling pompous even as he said it. “Unfortunately, because of Mrs. van der Luyden’s health, they almost never host.”

She lifted her thin dark brows, looking at him thoughtfully. “Isn’t that why they’re so powerful? Because they make themselves so rare?”

Archer coloured, stared at her, and burst out laughing. She’d pricked the grand myth of the van der Luydens with one tiny, simple line, and the whole thing had deflated into nothing.

The maid Nastasia carried in the tea tray then, with handleless Japanese cups and little covered sweet dishes, and Ellen leaned forward to hand him his cup, her fingers brushing his for half a second. “You’ll explain all these little unspoken things to me, won’t you? Tell me everything I ought to know.”

“Actually, you’re the one opening my eyes,” he said. “I’ve looked at all these things my whole life, I stopped seeing them years ago.”

She unclasped a small gold cigarette case strung to her bracelet, held it out to him, and took one for herself, reaching for a long spill off the mantel to light them from the fire. “Ah, then we can help each other. But I need your help far more. You have to tell me exactly what to do.”

He’d been about to blurt out “Don’t let anyone see you driving around with Beaufort,” but the warm, foreign, faraway atmosphere of the room wrapped around him, and the warning felt absurd, like trying to tell someone haggling for attar of roses in Samarkand that they ought to pack galoshes for a New York winter. From where he sat, New York felt smaller than Samarkand, viewed through the wrong end of a telescope, tiny and distant.

“I know everyone else is lining up to tell me what to do,” she said, her voice softening. “My aunts, old Granny Mingott, all of them. They’re all cross with me for trying to make my own choices. Granny wanted me to stay locked up in her house forever, but I had to be free.” Her shoulders shook, and suddenly tears were spilling down her cheeks. “Does no one here want to know the truth, Mr. Archer? The real loneliness is living surrounded by all these kind people who only ever ask you to pretend.”

“Ellen—don’t, Ellen,” he heard himself say, jumping up to kneel beside her, wrapping his hand around hers to warm it, murmuring soft reassuring things. She pulled back a moment later, wiping her wet lashes with a little laugh, and started to straighten her loosened braids. “Does no one cry here either? I suppose you never need to cry in heaven, do you?”

He was still reeling from the fact that he’d called her Ellen, twice, and she hadn’t even blinked. Far off, at the other end of that inverted telescope, he could just make out May Welland’s soft white figure, waiting for him in New York.

Then Nastasia poked her head in, chattering something in rapid Italian, and the Duke of St Austrey trundled into the room, steering a huge woman in overflowing furs, black wig and massive red plumes atop her head. “My dear Countess! I’ve brought an old friend of mine who’s been dying to meet you—Mrs. Struthers. She wasn’t invited to the party last night, and she’s simply desperate to make your acquaintance.”

“Of course I want to meet you, my dear!” Mrs. Struthers boomed, her voice as rich and loud as her plumes. “I love everyone young and charming and interesting. The Duke tells me you adore music—you play piano yourself, don’t you? Well, you simply must come to my house tomorrow evening, to hear Sarasate play. I host every Sunday night, when all of New York hasn’t a clue what to do with itself, so I invite them over to have fun. Bring your young gentleman along too,” she added, turning to Archer and shaking his hand warmly. “I can’t place you, but I know we’ve met somewhere—Paris, London, all the diplomats come to my parties. You like music too, right? Duke, make sure you bring him.”

The Duke rumbled “Rather” deep in his beard, and Archer bowed stiffly, feeling like a self-conscious schoolboy as he took his leave. He wasn’t upset the visit had ended, only wished the interruption had come a little sooner, so he wouldn’t have had to waste all that emotion leaning in so close to Ellen Olenska, calling her name like that. When he stepped out into the cold wintry night, New York felt vast and real again, and May Welland the most beautiful woman in the whole of it. He stopped off at his usual florist to send her the daily box of lilies-of-the-valley he was mortified to realise he’d forgotten that morning.

As he waited for an envelope, his eye fell on a cluster of yellow roses, so bright and sun-gold he’d never seen anything like them before. His first thought was to send them to May instead of the lilies—but they were too rich, too fiery, too bold, nothing at all like the soft, sweet girl he was engaged to. On a sudden, unthinking rush of feeling, he gestured for the florist to tuck the roses into a second long box. He slipped an envelope addressed to Countess Olenska under it, but at the very last second, he pulled his own calling card out of the fold, leaving the envelope blank. “These will go out right away?” he asked, pointing to the roses.

The florist assured him they would.
