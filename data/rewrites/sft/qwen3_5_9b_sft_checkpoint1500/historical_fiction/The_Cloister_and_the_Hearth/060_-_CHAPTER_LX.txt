Fra Colonna held the keys to the Pope's library, a privilege that allowed him to wander the ancient streets alongside Gerard whenever the friar paused from his labors. To the happy artist, Rome was a vision of eternal beauty, a city where grandeur flowed from every ruin and palace. But Fra Colonna, the scholar, treated the splendor with a dampening skepticism.

"This place you call Rome?" the friar murmured, pointing to the subterranean remains of triumphal arches buried beneath the cobblestones. "It is merely the tomb of mighty Rome." He guided Gerard's gaze upward, revealing how modern thoroughfares trampled over the roofs of ancient palaces and the tops of shattered columns. Coupling the cramped confines of the medieval city with the colossal, fragmented ghosts of antiquity peeking through the cracks, the friar struck a striking comparison. "Consider this village, 'Rome,' as nothing more than a swallow's nest clinging to the decaying eaves of an ancient abbey."

"Old Rome must indeed have been magnificent," Gerard admitted.

"Judge for yourself, my son," the friar countered, leading him toward the great sewers—the engineering marvels of early Rome designed to outlive Mount Vesuvius—and the fragments of the Temple of Peace. "Imagine the Capitol with its twenty-five temples. Look at Monte Savello; what is it but the ruins of the ancient Theatre of Marcellus? And Testaccio, one of the highest hills today? It was once a garbage heap where the women of old Rome discarded their broken pottery. Lo and behold, a mountain formed."

*"Ex pede Herculem"*—"From the foot, judge the giant"—the friar declared.

Gerard listened with respectful deference until the holy man began to draw a moral parallel, suggesting that the pagan Romans possessed a superior morality proportional to their grand architecture. Gerard resisted firmly. "Has the world suffered a loss with the coming of Christ?" he asked, though he flushed immediately, realizing he was questioning his benefactor.

"Saints forbid!" cried the friar sharply. "'Twere heresy to suggest otherwise." Yet, having conceded this point directly, he quickly retreated via subtle circumlocutions, guiding Gerard toward a forbidden door accessed by a spiral staircase. Inside lay a church crowded with a knot of onlookers gathered around a porch where a demon was being exorcised. Fra Colonna had a peculiar habit of emitting a low, mournful sound—a curdling moan—when confronted with facts that Zeno or Epicurus would reject. This noise conveyed not only sharp dissent but a profound pity for human credulity, ignorance, and error, particularly when such blindness obscured the merits of paganism.

The friar sighed and urged departure. "Then let us withdraw."

"Nay, Father! Pray, stay! I have never witnessed a devil cast out."

Reluctantly, the friar followed Gerard inside, giving a final, heavy shrug. Within, the demoniac knelt before the altar, a scarf tightly bound around his neck, held fast like a hound on a leash by the officiating priest. Few souls were present, for rumors circulated that the last demon expelled from this church fled no further than the next burrow, like a hare driven from one hole straight into another.

When the pair arrived, the priest, sensing sufficient spectators, commenced his ritual. He faced the prostrate man, breviary in hand, beginning with the arduous task of identifying the spirit tormenting the victim.

"Come out, Ashtaroth! Oh, it is not you. Come out, Belial! Come out, Tatzi! Come out, Eza! No, he trembles not. Come out, Azymoth! Come out, Feriander! Come out, Foletho! Come out, Astyma! Come out, Nebul!"

"Aha!" the priest exclaimed, his voice rising. "Have I found thee? 'Tis thou, thou reptile, at thy old tricks! Let us pray!"

"Oh, Lord, we pray thee to drive the foul fiend Nebul from this Thy creature! From his hair, his eyes, his nose, his mouth, his ears, his gums, his teeth, his shoulders, his arms, his legs, his loins, his stomach, his bowels, his thighs, his knees, his calves, his feet, his ankles, his finger-nails, his toe-nails, and from his soul! Amen."

The priest rose, turning to the congregation with a quiet geniality. "Gentlemen, we have here a devil as obstinate as one might encounter on a summer's day." Facing the patient, he alternated between rigorous addresses to the man and the fiend. Through the same mouth, the victim now cursed the holy names the priest invoked, now whimpered complaints of internal misery.

It was the priest who first confused the victim with the culprit, physically attacking the former—he cuffed him, kicked him, spat repeatedly in his face. He lit a candle, deliberately extinguished it, burned it until his fingers singed, then dropped it swiftly. He produced the *custodia*, displaying the Holy Corpore within, before burning another candle with greater caution. Finally, speaking civilly to the demoniac in his human guise, he dismissed him and accepted the applause of the crowd.

"Good father," Gerard observed, "how you have their names committed to memory. Our northern priests possess no such exquisite knowledge of the infernal squadrons."

"Aye, young man," the friar replied, "here we know all their names and their methods; these reptiles. This Nebul is a bitter, hard one to track down."

He then shared several anecdotes with the assembly, concluding with a feat from the previous day: driving a hulking fiend from a woman by her mouth, leaving behind nails, pins, and a tuft of hair, as the creature shrieked in anguish, "It is not thou that conquerest me! Behold that stone on the windowsill! Know that the angel Gabriel, descending to earth, alighted upon it—it is that stone which performed my business!"

The friar let out another mournful sigh. "And you believed him?"

"Certainly! Who, save an infidel, would discredit a revelation so precise?"

"What? Believe the father of lies? That pushes credulity beyond endurance."

"Oh, a liar does not always lie."

"Aye? Whenever he begins with an improbable tale and produces a holy relic, he arms you against the Satanic host. If fiends existed, they would not be so simple-minded. Thou shouldst have answered him from antiquity: *Timeo Danaos et dona ferentes*—I fear the Greeks bearing gifts."

"Some blackguard chopped his wife's head off on that stone, young man! You take my word for it." And the friar hurried Gerard away.

"Alas, Father, I fear you have shamed the good priest."

"Aye, by Pollux!" chuckled the friar. "I blistered him with a single touch of 'Socratic interrogation.' What modern mind can parry the weapons of antiquity?"

Later, one afternoon, after Gerard completed his daily tasks, a fine lackey summoned him to the Palazzo Cesarini. Gerard was ushered into a noble chamber where a young girl sat working on a tapestry. She rose and departed, promising to inform her mistress. An hour passed, and Gerard began to fret, wondering if nobles valued the time of a poor craftsman. Just as he resolved to slip away, the door opened, admitting a superlative beauty followed by two maids. It was the young Princess of the House of Cesarini. Though she spoke loudly and haughtily to her attendants, her demeanor softened instantly upon seeing Gerard, lowering her voice to a feminine whisper. "Are you the writer, Messer?"

"I am, Signora."

"'Tis well."

She seated herself while Gerard and the maids remained standing. "What is your name, good youth?"

"Gerard, Signora."

"Gerard? Body of Bacchus! Is that the name of a human creature?"

"It is a Dutch name, Signora. I was born at Terghou, in Holland."

"A harsh name, girls, for so well-favored a youth," she mused, "what say you?"

The maids assented warmly.

"What did I summon you for?" the lady asked, her expression shifting to lofty languor. "Ah, I remember. Sit, Ser Gerardo, and write me a letter to Ercole Orsini, my lover—or at least, that is what he claims."

Gerard sat, taking out paper and ink, looking to the princess for instructions. She sat on a high chair, almost a throne, gazing down at him with inquisitive eyes.

"Well, Gerardo?"

"I am ready, Your Excellence."

"Write, then."

"I await the words."

"And who, pray, is to provide them?"

"Who but Your Grace, whose letter it is to be?"

"Gramercy! What, you writers, find you not the words? What avail is your art without the substance? I suspect you are an impostor, Gerardo."

"Nay, Signora, I am no impostor. I might translate your speech into grammar as readily as I commit it to parchment. But I cannot interpret your silence. Therefore, speak what is in your heart, and I will transcribe it before your eyes."
