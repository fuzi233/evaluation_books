If you've ever stepped inside the *Wheel of Fortune* public house—where Mr. James Morgan's club usually congregated, and where Sir Francis Clavering once had a rather heated chat with Major Pendennis—you'll recall the layout. On the ground floor, aside from the bar where the landlady presides, there are three guest rooms. One is a general parlour for the public; another caters to gentlemen in livery; and the third, marked "Private" on the door, belongs to the exclusive "Confidentials," a club of which Morgan and Lightfoot were proud members.

Mr. Morgan, a man of considerable stealth, had eavesdropped on the earlier conversation between Strong and Major Pendennis at the Major's lodgings, gathering enough material for private speculation. Curiosity drove him to shadow the Major to the *Wheel of Fortune*, slipping quietly into the Confidential Room while Pendennis and Clavering argued loudly in the adjacent parlour. There existed a specific vantage point within the Confidential Room offering a perfect acoustic view of the next room. Since the argument between the two gentlemen was fierce and carried on in a high register, Morgan caught nearly everything. What he heard only confirmed the suspicions swirling in his mind.

"He recognized Altamont instantly upon seeing him in Sydney, didn't he?" Morgan muttered to himself, his eyes widening. "Clavering isn't married to my Lady any more than I am! Altamont is the man. Altamont is a convict. Young Harthur gets into Parliament, and the Governor promises not to cause a scandal. By Jove, what a cunning old rogue that Governor is! No wonder he's pushing the match between Blanche and Harthur so hard. Why, she'll bring a hundred thousand pounds to the marriage—and secure a parliamentary seat for her husband in the bargain."

No one else witnessed the transformation on Mr. Morgan's face as this bombshell landed, but a skilled physiognomist would have noted the shift immediately. "For my sake, and the confounded prejudices of society," he whispered, eyeing his reflection in the mirror, "damn it, James Morgan, you might marry her yourself!" Yet, realizing he couldn't wed Miss Blanche and her fortune, Morgan calculated that possessing this secret could enrich him in ways far more lucrative. Most everyone affected by this revelation would prefer ignorance: Sir Francis Clavering, whose fortune was at stake, would want silence; Colonel Altamont, whose neck was implicated, would want to bury it; and that uppity young upstart, Mr. Harthur—who was eager to enter Parliament on the strength of this arrangement and acted as proud as a duke with half a million a year (though Morgan privately believed his employer's nephew was less than that)—would pay anything to hide that he married a convict's daughter and bought his seat through trafficking secrets.

As for Lady C., Morgan reasoned, whether she was tired of Clavering or terrified for her son's safety, she would pay. And Miss Blanche would certainly reward whoever restored her rightful inheritance. "Damn it," concluded the valet, marveling at the incredible hand of luck dealt to him, "with cards like these, James Morgan, you're a made man. It might be a regular bonanza. Everyone must subscribe. With what I've already amassed, I could retire, warn my old Governor, turn gentleman, hire my own servant, and begad, live like a lord." While entertaining these feverish calculations that threatened to shake his spirit, Mr. Morgan displayed remarkable self-control, maintaining a calm demeanor and ignoring his skyrocketing future prospects to fulfill his current duties.

Colonel Altamont, central to this drama, was currently absent from London. Knowing of Sir Francis Clavering's favorite haunt, Shepherd's Inn, Morgan walked there shortly after the Baronet and Pendennis parted ways. Alas, the bird had flown; Altamont had cashed his Derby winnings and departed for the Continent. This absence was deeply vexing to Morgan. "He'll squander that money in the gambling dens along the Rhine," Morgan fretted. "I might have pocketed a good share. It's confounded annoying he's gone and couldn't wait a few days." Whether hope was triumphant or deferred, ambition realized or disappointed, Morgan bore it all with an equally serene countenance. Until the right moment arrived, the Major's boots remained varnished, his hair perfectly curled, his morning tea delivered to his bedside, and his oaths and senile sarcasm endured with silent, obsequious fidelity. Who would guess, watching him pack trunks and assist at table in the country houses where the Major stayed, that Morgan was richer than his employer, knowing his secrets and those of others? In his profession, Morgan was highly respected, renowned for his supposed wealth and wisdom at supper tables. The younger gentlemen dismissed him as stupid, devoid of ideas, and a fogey; yet, none would refuse to join in the earnest prayers of the serious-minded: "When I die, may I cut up as well as Morgan Pendennis!"

True to the customs of a man of fashion, Major Pendennis spent the autumn moving from house to house among country friends willing to host him. If the Duke was abroad, he visited the Marquis in Scotland; if not, he stayed with Sir John or some plain squire. Truthfully, the old gentleman's reputation was fading. Many of his contemporaries had passed on, and the current holders of their titles knew him not, caring little for his tales of "the Wild Prince and Poins" or the heroes of a bygone fashion era. Walking past many London doors, it must have struck the good man with melancholy how rarely they opened for him now, compared to the banquets and welcomes he once enjoyed decades prior. He began to admit he was no longer of the present age, dimly sensing that the young men mocked him. Such melancholic reflections plague many a Pall Mall philosopher. "The men aren't what they used to be," he thought. "The old grand manner and courtly grace are gone. What is Castlewood House today compared to the magnificence of the old mansion and owner? The late Lord arrived with four post-chaises and sixteen horses; the entire North Road rushed out to gaze upon his cavalcade; even Londoners stopped to watch his procession. Today's Lord travels in a railway carriage with five porters, sneaking away from the station in a brougham while smoking a cigar. The late Lord filled Castlewood with guests drinking claret until midnight; today's man hides in a hut on a Scottish mountain, spending November in a couple of closets in an entresol in Paris, where his entertainment consists of a café dinner and a ticket to a small theater. What a contrast between Lady Lorraine of the Regency—beautiful, gorgeous, magnificent in diamonds and velvet, daring in her rouge, with the wits of the world (the old, polished gentlemen, not the rabble of today with their cab-stand slang and smoke-stained coats) bowing at her feet—and today's Lady Lorraine! A little woman in a black silk gown, looking like a governess, talking of astronomy, laboring classes, and emigration, lurking in church at eight in the morning."

Abbots-Lorraine, once the noblest house in the county, had become a monastery—a real La Trappe. They didn't drink two glasses of wine after dinner; instead, every other diner was a country curate in a white neckcloth discussing Polly Higson's school progress or Widow Watkins's lumbago. "And the other young men, those lounging guardsmen and great lazy dandies—sprawling over sofas and billiard tables, stealing off to smoke pipes in each other's bedrooms, caring for nothing, reverencing nothing, not even an old gentleman who has known their fathers and betters, not even a pretty woman—" The Major pondered the difference between these men, who poisoned turnip fields with their tobacco, and the gentlemen of his time. "The breed is gone," he thought. "There's no use for them; they're replaced by a bunch of damned cotton-spinners and utilitarians, and young parson sprigs with their hair combed down their backs." He felt old; they were surpassing him; they laughed at old boys like him. And he wasn't entirely wrong. The times and manners he cherished were nearly extinct. Gay young men teased him irreverently, while serious youth regarded him with grave pity and wonder—a pain even worse if the Major had realized its full extent. But he was rather simple; his moral examinations hadn't been profound. Perhaps it never occurred to him until recently that he was anything other than a most respectable and fortunate man. Was there no old age without reverence? Did youthful folly never mock other bald heads? For the past few years, he'd sensed his day was ending and the new generation was reigning.

After a somewhat unsuccessful autumn, during which he was faithfully trailed by Mr. Morgan (while his nephew Arthur was busy at Clavering's), Major Pendennis returned to London for a brief stay at the dismal end of October, when the fogs roll in and the lawyers descend upon the city. Who hasn't watched with interest those cab-loaded cabs, piled boxes, and crowded children rattling through the streets on dull October evenings? Stopping at dark houses to unload nurses, infants, girls, matrons, and fathers whose holidays are over? Yesterday was France and sunshine, or Broadstairs and liberty; today brings work and a yellow fog. Oh, gods! What a heap of bills lies in the Master's study!
