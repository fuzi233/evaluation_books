From dreams, I now turn to truth.

It was the final day of the 1999th year of our age. The steady patter of rain had long signaled the onset of night, and I sat beside my wife, lost in thought—reflecting on the events of the past and the possibilities ahead: the coming year, the century, the new Millennium.

[Note: When I say “sitting,” I do not mean any physical posture as you might understand it in Spaceland. We have no feet, so we cannot sit or stand in your sense. Yet we distinguish mental states—lying, sitting, standing—through subtle shifts in luminosity that reflect our will. But this is a detail I must pass over, for time is short.]

My four sons and two orphaned grandchildren had already withdrawn to their chambers. Only my wife remained with me, to witness the end of one era and the dawn of another. I was deep in contemplation, recalling words spoken by my youngest grandson—a bright, promising young Hexagon, perfectly formed and radiant in intellect. Earlier, he had been undergoing his usual lesson in Sight Recognition, as his uncles and I rotated slowly and swiftly upon our centers, testing his ability to identify our positions. His responses had been flawless, earning him a rare reward: a glimpse into the deeper logic of geometry through arithmetic.

I took nine squares—each an inch on every side—and arranged them into a larger square, three inches across. From this, I explained how, though we could never see inside a square, we could calculate its area by squaring the length of its side: thus, 3² equals 9, meaning a square of three-inch sides contains nine square inches.

The boy pondered this silently, then asked, “But you’ve taught me about raising numbers to the third power. What does 3³ mean in geometry?”

“Nothing,” I replied firmly. “Geometry has only two dimensions. There is no such thing as a third.”

Then I demonstrated: a point moving three inches forms a line—three inches long, represented by 3. A line of three inches, moved parallel to itself through three inches, creates a square—three inches on each side, represented by 3².

At this, my grandson leaned forward, eyes alight. “Then if a point makes a line, and a line makes a square… why can’t a square, moving somehow parallel to itself—though I don’t see how—create something else? Something three inches in every direction? And wouldn’t that be represented by 3³?”

I bristled at the interruption. “Go to bed,” I said sharply. “If you would stop speaking nonsense, you might remember more sense.”

And so he left, disgraced. I returned to my thoughts, trying to reflect on the year passed and the future yet to come—but still haunted by the child’s bold question.

Only a few grains of sand remained in the hourglass. Rousing myself, I turned it northward—once more—for the last time in the old millennium. As I did, I muttered aloud: “The boy is a fool.”

Instantly, I felt it—a presence. A cold breath stirred the air, sending a shiver through me.

“He is not a fool!” my wife exclaimed. “And you are breaking the Commandments by dishonoring your own grandson!”

I ignored her. I looked in every direction. Nothing was visible. Yet I *felt* it—the unseen weight, the chilling whisper returning.

I rose abruptly. “What is it?” she asked, alarmed. “There’s no draft. What are you searching for? There’s nothing.”

There was nothing. I sat again, repeating, “The boy is a fool. 3³ means nothing in geometry.”

Then—clear and distinct—a voice answered:
“The boy is not a fool. 3³ has a clear geometric meaning.”

Both my wife and I heard it. She did not comprehend, but we both lunged toward the sound.

Our horror grew when we saw—*a figure*.

At first glance, it resembled a woman seen sideways. But closer inspection revealed impossibilities: the edges blurred too quickly to belong to any female form. It seemed like a circle—but one that shifted in size unpredictably, defying all known shapes.

My wife, lacking my experience and overwhelmed by instinctive fear, leapt to conclusions. “How did this person get in?” she demanded. “You promised me—no ventilators in our new house!”

“There are none,” I said. “But why do you assume it’s a woman? By my Sight Recognition—”

“Oh, I have no patience for your Sight Recognition!” she snapped. “Feeling is believing. A straight line to the touch is worth a circle to the sight.”—proverbs common among women in Flatland.

“Well,” I said, cautious not to provoke her further, “if you insist, let us ask for an introduction.”

She stepped forward, adopting her most gracious manner. “Permit me, Madam, to feel and be felt by—” Then she recoiled suddenly. “Oh! It’s not a woman! No angles—none at all! Could I have offended a perfect Circle?”

“I am indeed, in a way, a Circle,” came the voice. “A more perfect one than any in Flatland. But more accurately—I am many circles in one.”

Then, softer: “I carry a message for your husband—one I cannot deliver in your presence. If you would allow us a moment alone—”

But my wife refused to let such a distinguished visitor inconvenience himself. Assuring the stranger that her time had long passed, and apologizing repeatedly for her earlier outburst, she withdrew to her chamber.

I glanced at the hourglass. The final grains had fallen.

The Third Millennium had begun.
