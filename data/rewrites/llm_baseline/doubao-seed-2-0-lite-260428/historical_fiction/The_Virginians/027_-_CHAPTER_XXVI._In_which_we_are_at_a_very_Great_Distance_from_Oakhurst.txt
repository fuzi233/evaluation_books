The bowling green tucked beside the White Horse Tavern’s eating-room windows glowed soft and crisp under the late Tunbridge sun, dotted with rough wooden tables where guests sipped punch and tea to work off the weight of their dinners. When the three gentlemen pushed back their chairs as their meal drew to a close, Mr. Jack Morris proposed they adjourn to the turf to tap a cool bottle of Burgundy between them.

“Jack Morris would adjourn all the way to the grave itself just to find an excuse for another drink,” Lord March drawled, earning a snort from his friend.
“Every man has his own preferred path to damnation,” Jack shrugged. “I own mine leads through a bottle, and I’ve no shame for it.”
“Lord Chesterfield’s damnation is deuce-ace,” put in Lord March’s companion, Lord March, grinning. “He can’t keep his hands off cards or dice long enough to catch his breath.”
“And you, March, have not one devil but a whole crowd of them,” Chesterfield laughed back. “You gamble, you race horses, you bet on anything that moves, you drink deep, you eat well, you hoard your coin, and you chase every pretty face that crosses your path. You’ve fallen into terrible company, Mr. Warrington, landing in his lordship’s orbit. He’d play you for every single acre you own back in Virginia, given half the chance.”
“With the greatest pleasure imaginable, Mr. Warrington!” March interposed, leaning across the table with eager glint in his eye.
“For every hogshead of tobacco, every crate of spice, every enslaved man, woman, and child, every ox and ass on your plantations, every last thing that bears your name.”
“Shall we start now?” March tossed a glance at Jack Morris. “You never travel without a dice box or a bottle screw. I’ll stake you any sum you like on the turn of a die.”
“Unfortunately my lord,” young Harry Warrington laughed, shaking his head, “none of that tobacco, those enslaved people, or that land is mine yet. I only just turned of age, and my mother, scarce twenty years older than I, has every prospect of outliving me for decades.”
“I’ll bet you she doesn’t. I’ll pay you a fair sum right now against four times the amount coming to you the day she passes. I’ll stake you a handsome purse right now against the reversion of your Virginia estate when the old lady departs. What do you call the place, by the bye?”
“Castlewood.”
“A principality, by all the gossip I hear,” March said. “Though I wager the quidnuncs in London have exaggerated its value ten times over. How did you come by the name Castlewood? You’re related to the English Castlewood family, aren’t you? Oh, of course—your mother descends from the rightful head of the house, the one who took the losing side in the ’15 rebellion. My old Duchess told me the whole story a dozen times. She knew your grandfather, the friend of Addison, Steele, Pope—all the great literary wits of the day. Pity he didn’t stay home and ship the other, grasping branch of the family out to the plantations instead.”
“I’ve just come from staying at that very Castlewood, with my cousin who holds the estate now,” Harry remarked.
“Hm. Did you gamble with him? He’s infamous for his love of cards and dice.”
“Never for anything more than sixpenny stakes, just a pool of commerce with the ladies after supper.”
“So much the better for both of you. But I’ll lay ten to one you played with Will Esmond while you were there, if he was in residence.”
Harry blushed bright, and admitted that he and his cousin had indeed played a few hands of cards on quiet evenings.
“And Tom Sampson the chaplain was the third at the table, wasn’t he?” Jack Morris crowed. “I’d wager half my income Tom joined in, and may the Lord deliver you from the pair of them working together to empty your purse!”
“Nay, the truth is I won fair and square off both of them,” Harry said, smiling over the rim of his wine glass.
“And they paid you? Well! Miracles never do cease!”
“I never said anything about miracles,” Harry chuckled.
“And you don’t go blabbing all your wins and losses around town, that volto sciolto face of yours hides more than it tells, hey Mr. Warrington?” March teased.
“I beg your pardon,” honest Harry said, apologetic. “French is the only other language besides English I know a scrap of.”
“Lord March learned all his Italian at the Opera, and it cost him a pretty penny in lessons too,” Jack cackled. “We’ll have to drag you to see the shows, won’t we, March?”
“Must we, Morris?” March replied, his tone cold enough to hint he did not care for his friend’s overfamiliarity in front of a stranger.
Both of the two English lords were dressed plainly for the country: unpowdered small scratch wigs, blue frock coats with plate buttons, buckskin breeches and heavy riding boots, narrow-laced little hats, no trace of the silks and embroidery of London drawing rooms.
“I don’t much care for the Opera, my lord,” Harry said, the Burgundy warming his blood. “But I’m wild to see Newmarket, and I’ve longed my whole life to watch a proper English hunt.”
“We’ll show you both Newmarket and the hunting fields, never fear. Can you ride well?”
“I think so. I can shoot straight enough, and jump decently too.”
“What’s your weight? I bet we’re evenly matched, or I tip the scales a little heavier. I bet Jack Morris outshoots you at five and twenty paces, any bird or mark. And I bet I outjump you on flat ground, right here on this very green.”
“I don’t know Mr. Morris’s shooting skill—I’ve never met either of you gentlemen before today—but I’ll take all your bets, my lord, for whatever stakes you name!” Harry cried, the wine rushing to his head, determined not to shame his Virginia home by backing down from a challenge.
“Ponies a side on each!” March yelled.
“Done and done!” Harry and March shouted at the same time. The young Virginian would have rather fought a duel than let anyone think his colonial pride would shrink from a fair contest.
“We can try the long jump right now, if your feet are steady,” March said, springing to his feet, stretching his long limbs as he eyed the dry, crisp turf. He pulled off his heavy riding boots, then his coat and waistcoat, buckling his belt tight around his waist and flinging his clothes in a heap on the grass.
Harry was far more careful with his own garments—it was his best velvet suit, after all. He took off his coat and waistcoat, folded them neatly, and seeing all the nearby tables were sloshed with spilled punch, carried them inside the eating room to set them on a dry table by the open window.
There he found a new guest: Mr. Wolfe, the lean young lieutenant-colonel, eating a plain chicken and salad with a modest pint of small beer, completely sober. Harry was in such high spirits he clapped the man on the shoulder, told him of his bet with Lord March, and asked if Wolfe would back him for half the stake. Wolfe laughed and said he was far too poor a soldier to gamble, but he would happily come out and judge the contest to make sure no cheating happened. He set down his glass and stepped through the open window right after Harry.
“Who is that tallow-faced carrot-haired oddity?” slurred Jack Morris, well in his cups by that point.
Harry explained the man was Lieutenant-Colonel Wolfe of the 20th Regiment.
“Your humble servants, gentlemen,” Wolfe said, giving them all a stiff, formal military bow.
“Never saw such a gawky figure in my life!” Jack blurted. “Did you, March?”
“I beg your pardon—did you say March?” Wolfe asked, looking surprised.
“I am the Earl of March, sir, at your service,” the nobleman said, bowing in return. “My friend Mr. Morris is so familiar with me that after dinner, we behave like brawling brothers, no ceremony at all.”
Jack was so delighted to hear his noble friend admit their closeness he yelled at the top of his lungs, “Two to one on my lord!”
“Done!” Harry called out immediately, and a sheepish Jack had no choice but to huff “Done!” right back.
“Take that bet for yourself, Colonel,” Harry whispered to his friend. But Wolfe shook his head, saying he could not afford to lose money, and so had no right to hope to win any.
“I see you’ve already won one of our bets before we even start,” March said, squinting at Harry. “I’m an inch or two taller than you, but you’ve got an inch more breadth across the shoulders.”
“Pooh, Will! I bet you weigh twice as much as he does!” Jack shouted.
“Done, Jack!” March laughed. “All bets are for ponies. You don’t want in on this one, Mr. Warrington?”
“No, my dear fellow, one bet on him is enough for me,” Jack said, backing down quickly.
“Very good, my dear fellow,” March grinned. “Now let’s settle the long jump wager.”
Harry had on his finest silk stockings, black satin breeches, and neat polished pumps, and saw no reason to take his shoes off, unlike his lordship, whose spurred riding boots were useless for leaping. The stretch of green turf before them was thirty yards long, plenty of room for a running start, bordered by a gravel path, beyond which stood a tavern wall hung with the pub’s sign: the rampant Hanoverian White Horse between two skittles, painted bright blue, bearing the landlord’s name and his tavern’s name.
Jack Morris laid a white handkerchief on the grass to mark the takeoff point, and Wolfe walked to the far end of the green to note exactly where each jumper landed. As Harry later wrote in a surviving letter to Mrs. Mountain back at Castlewood, Virginia:
“My Lord went first, though he offered to let me lead. But I remembered the story my dearest George used to tell of the Battle of Fontenoy, so I said ‘Monseigneur le Comte, tirez le premier, s’il vous plait.’ He took his run barefoot, and for the honour of the Old Dominion, I had the great gratification of beating him by more than two feet: exactly two feet nine inches, to be precise. I jumped twenty-one feet three inches, per the inn drawer’s measured tape, and his lordship only eighteen and six. I had already won the weight bet the second I set eyes on him, before we even stepped on the turf. So he and Mr. Jack both paid up cheerfully. My wallet, which had grown nearly empty from tipping servants at Castlewood and buying a new horse at Oakhurst, was soon stuffed full of their banknotes. I will make sure my mother does not take amiss what I did, for I bet only for the honour of Virginia, and my opponent was a peer of the highest rank, holding two earldoms and heir to a dukedom. Gambling is all the rage over here, and the young bloods and fashionables bet from dawn till dusk.
“I told them, for my mischief, that there is a gentleman back home who can jump a full foot further than me. When they asked who, I said Colonel George Washington of Mount Vernon, which is true—he is the only man in either his county or mine who can do it. Mr. Wolfe then asked me a hundred questions about Colonel Washington, and showed he had heard of him before. He talked over last year’s unhappy expedition against the French as if he knew every inch of the ground, and could name all our Virginia rivers, though he did mispronounce the Potomac as ‘Pottamac,’ and we had a good laugh over it. My Lord of March and Ruglen was not at all ill tempered about losing, and not a cross word passed between us.”
When all the feats of strength were done, the four men strolled out of the tavern garden onto the main public promenade, which was thronged with Tunbridge Wells society by that hour. Jack Morris, loud and unrestrained after his wine, made pointed, often rude remarks about every person that passed, and whenever March made a witty joke, Jack would roar out “Oh ho ho! Good Gad! My dear Earl! You’ll be the death of me!” for all the promenade to hear, as if he was determined every last stranger there knew he was on familiar, brotherly terms with a peer of the realm.
A whole procession of famous faces drifted past them: M. Poellnitz, no better dressed than he had been at dinner, grinned and swept off his plumed, tarnished laced hat in salute. Then came Lord Chesterfield himself, in a pearl-colored suit, his blue Garter ribbon and star glittering on his chest, nodding pleasantly to the young men.
“I’ll back that old man to tip his hat more gracefully than anyone in all of England, France included,” March said. “He hasn’t changed the shape of that hat in twenty years. Look over there—see that great big, awkward, pockmarked man in the snuff-coloured coat, who barely touches his clumsy beaver in reply? Confound his impudence! Do you know who that is?”
“No, curse him, who is it, March?” Jack swore.
“That’s Samuel Johnson, the dictionary maker. Lord Chesterfield wrote a whole series of fine papers patronizing the man when his dictionary was coming out. Horry Walpole says they’re excellent pieces, and he knows all about that sort of literary nonsense. The impudent old schoolmaster!”
“Hang him, he belongs in the pillory!” Jack roared.
“The fat little man he’s walking with is another writer, a printer—his name’s Richardson, he wrote Clarissa, you know.”
“Great heavens! Is that the great Richardson? The man who wrote *Clarissa*?” Colonel Wolfe and Harry Warrington both cried out at exactly the same moment.
Harry ran forward to gawk at the kind, round-faced little old man toddling along the path, surrounded by a crowd of doting admiring ladies.
“Dear, dear sir,” one young lady was gushing, “you are far too good and virtuous for this world—surely you were sent here to teach the rest of us how to be good!”
“Ah, Miss Mulso! Who shall teach the teacher?” Richardson replied, lifting his mild round face to the sky. “Even I have my faults and errors. Even my age and experience cannot stop me from stumbling—oh! Heaven bless my soul, Mr. Johnson! I beg your pardon if I trod on your corn.”
“You have done both: trod on the corn, and earned your own pardon,” Johnson rumbled, then turned away muttering verses, swaying as he walked, eyes fixed on the ground, hands behind his back, swinging his heavy walking stick so recklessly he kept nearly poking his gentle companion-author in the eye.
“His eyes are not strong, dear Miss Mulso,” Richardson apologized to the young lady, “but such as they are, I would not dare cross Mr. Johnson’s cudgel.” He bowed low to Harry, who had drifted up beside them, and Harry blushed to the roots of his hair as he returned the greeting, almost too starstruck to speak. Richardson was adored everywhere he went: spinsters scattered tea leaves around his feet like offerings, matrons sewed fancy slippers for him and kissed the toes, a whole halo of pious respect hung even around his nightcap. All Europe had wept and thrilled over the pages of his immortal little novel, and Harry walked back to his friends glowing with pride, happy just to have exchanged a bow with the great man.
“Seen him! Dammy, you can see him any day in his little print shop, can’t you?” Jack laughed.
“My brother George always said he and Mr. Fielding were the two greatest geniuses in all England,” Harry burst out, earnest as ever when he spoke of his dearest friend. “He said the very first thing he would do when he traveled to Europe was go on pilgrimage to visit Mr. Richardson.”
“Your brother spoke like a man!” Colonel Wolfe cried, his pale face flushing bright. “I would far rather be a man of genius than a peer of the realm.”
“To each his own taste, Colonel,” March said, highly amused. “Your enthusiasm is quite refreshing, on my honour it is.”
“So it is—by Gad, perfectly refreshing!” Jack echoed.
“March, which would you rather be? A fat old printer who wrote a story about a ruined girl, or a peer of Parliament with ten thousand a year?”
“March—my Lord March! Do you take me for a fool?” Jack cried, his voice thick with mock hurt. “Have I done anything to deserve that kind of insult?”
“I would rather win honour than inherit honours. I would rather make my name than be born to it, even if my father’s name, thank God, is already an honest one,” Wolfe said, bowing quickly to the group. “Pardon me, gentlemen.” He darted across the promenade toward a small party of people who had just come into view: a young lady, an older lady, and a gentleman.
“That’s the beautiful Miss Lowther,” March said, nodding. “I remember now. He’s taken her arm—everyone says they’re engaged.”
“You don’t mean to say that gawky half-pay soldier is engaged to a Lowther of the North?” Jack squawked. “Curse me, what is the world coming to, with your printers and schoolmasters and all this infernal nonsense?”
Even gruff Samuel Johnson, who had barely tipped his hat to the great Lord Chesterfield a few minutes prior, was seen to take off his beaver and bow almost all the way to the ground before a florid clerical gentleman in a flowing gown and large round hat: the Bishop of Salisbury, Prelate of the Garter, wearing his blue riband and star with immense complacency. Dr. Gilbert paid endless flattering compliments to Richardson, calling him the great supporter of virtue, the preacher of sound morals, the mainstay of all true religion—praise the honest printer accepted with perfect, unfeigned conviction.
