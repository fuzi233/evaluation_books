If you’ve ever had the misfortune to wander the grubby, sun-starved alleys tucked between Lincoln’s Inn Fields and the Temple, you’ve almost certainly dodged your way past Shepherd’s Inn—an ancient quadrangle so hidden behind smoky chimneys and black gables that the sun doesn’t even bother to drop by, probably because it owes half the vendors tuppence. The narrow passages leading to it are lined with vendors selling soggy shellfish, rock-hard peppermint drops, cheap theatrical prints for bored teen clerks, and dingy secondhand beds that look like they’ve hosted nothing but bad indigestion and terrible nightmares. The doors are strung with so many bells it sounds like a cat walked through a Christmas decoration factory every time someone pushes one open, and dirty kids mill around the doorsteps so thick you can barely see the pavement under them, which is already slathered in a permanent layer of mud that never dries, no matter how hard it tries.

Ballad singers bellow off-key anti-Whig, anti-bishop, anti-royal-relative satire down every alley, Punch’s puppet show draws a crowd of urchins fighting over halfpennies, housewives scream at their loitering brats, and husbands stumble out of the gin shop yelling about how they definitely didn’t spend the rent money. Step through one rickety arch, though, and suddenly you’re in the quiet, rickety old quadrangle of Shepherd’s Inn proper, with a sad little patch of mangy grass in the middle, a statue of its founder Shepherd fenced off so the local urchins can’t beat it to bits, and tall old chambers that used to belong to lawyers, back before all the slightly dodgier crowd moved in.

These days, you won’t find any fancy barristers hanging their shingles here. The ground floor has the offices for the Polwheedle and Tredyddlum Copper Mines (which are, for the record, still 100% underwater), the Patent Inventions Registry, and the Union of Genius and Capital Company, which no one’s ever actually seen do any business. The only name that even makes it to the official Law List is Mr. Somerset Campion, who loans money to the aristocracy out of his fancy Mayfair office, and shows up at his Shepherd’s Inn front once a week in a cab with harness covered in heraldic crests that no one’s ever heard of, his groom’s breeches so blindingly white they cast a little patch of fake sunlight on the otherwise gloomy courtyard.

Wandering the courtyard watching that fancy cab every afternoon is none other than our old friend Captain Jack Costigan, shuffling around in ratty carpet slippers and a dressing gown that’s seen at least three wars, his old hat cocked over one eye. He hangs out at the porter’s lodge half the day, petting Mrs. Bolton the porteress’s kids and gushing about “theatres and me own darlin’ Leedy Mirabel” to anyone who’ll listen. Mrs. Bolton used to dance at Sadler’s Wells back in the day, as the 13th of Mr. Serle’s 40 dancing pupils, so she humours his stories.

Costigan shares the third floor of No.4 with his pal Mr. Bows, the piano teacher—their door still has the name of the last tenant, Charley Podmore the tenor, on it, which is a running theme in Shepherd’s Inn, where half the doors have names no one living has ever met. Bows gives singing lessons to the porteress’s pretty daughter Fanny, who dreams of being on the stage, and in exchange she makes their beds and cooks their breakfast. Costigan? Fanny thinks he’s the fanciest gentleman who ever lived, all his ridiculous Irish tall tales 100% true, except for the tiny flaw that he’s permanently half-cut on whisky punch. Bows, for all his grumpiness, is secretly as fond of Fanny as if she was his own kid—old softie can’t stand not loving someone, bless him.

Costigan’s stories are so grand you’d think his daughter Lady Mirabel and her rich husband Sir Charles show up to his third floor hovel every afternoon with the entire House of Lords in tow, but the truth is their last formal visit was months ago, the couple’s visiting card still stuck in his mantelpiece mirror. At first, when Mirabel married Sir Charles, she gave her da a small pension, and invited him over for dinner every now and then. For the first couple of trips, Cos even managed to stay sober, prattling on about the Dublin Castle levees he swears he attended, begging for “jus’ a little more champeane, if ye plaze” and being so absurdly polite to the footmen waiting on table that the poor lads could barely keep a straight face.

But then caution went out the window. He showed up to Sir Charles’s dinner one night absolutely sloshed, slurring anti-royal songs and trying to kiss the butler, and they had to stuff him in a carriage and send him home, locking their front door behind him. After that, Cos hung around the Back Kitchen pub for days, crying to his mates that he was exactly like King Lear, betrayed by his ungrateful daughter, forced to drown his sorrows in punch because no one loved him.

He got even worse when his money ran out, cooking up the most absurd lies to shake money out of Mirabel. Once he sent a frantic note saying a bailiff was dragging him off to jail unless she sent three pound five shillings at once, which she did, only to later find out he’d spent the whole lot at the pub. Another time he claimed he’d crashed through a plate glass window in the Strand and owed the shop owner a fortune, the cash ending up right back at the Roscius Head tavern. The third time, he said he was on his deathbed, too sick to call a doctor, so Mirabel drove straight over to Shepherd’s Inn, ran up the three flights of rickety stairs, and found him standing there in his shirt sleeves, holding a gridiron, waiting for Mrs. Bolton to bring home mutton chops for his lunch.

Poor old Sir Charles, who’s a delicate old fellow who hates fuss more than anything, was soon driven half mad by his father-in-law. He’d spot Costigan loitering outside his club, his silly hat cocked at that ridiculous angle, and dive headfirst out of his brougham like a spy running from a pursuer, just to avoid having to stop and talk to him. He cried to Mirabel that he’d have a fatal fit if he saw Costigan one more time, and threatened to move to the countryside or flee to France entirely. Mirabel finally marched over to Shepherd’s Inn, gave her da a strict talking to threatening to cut his pension off entirely if he ever harassed her husband again, and snapped at Bows for not keeping a better eye on the old drunk, leaving the kind old piano teacher with nothing but a dressing down for all his years of looking out for Cos.

Next door, at No.3, on the second floor, live two more of our old mates: Colonel Altamont, self-appointed Agent to the Nawaub of Lucknow, and Captain Edward Strong, the Chevalier himself. Their door doesn’t have a name plate at all—Strong doesn’t want every random debtor he owes tuppence to dropping by uninvited, and Altamont’s “confidential mission” to sell the Lucknow crown jewels to private London capitalists is so hush-hush he doesn’t bother telling the East India Company where he is.

Since Sir Francis Clavering rolled into town, Strong’s chambers are surprisingly cozy. He’s a genius cook, that Strong—whipping up French fricassees, Spanish stews, perfect omelettes, and making such a racket of singing and glass-clinking at his dinner parties that poor Costigan next door would sit in his own miserable rooms, green with envy, listening to the fun through the wall. His guests were the most chaotic, delightful bunch of schemers you ever met, every single one of them carrying a foolproof get-rich-quick scheme that would definitely make them millionaires in 10 months flat: there was Jack Holt who’d fought in Don Carlos’s army and swore he could smuggle tobacco into London for 30 thousand a year if someone just lent him 15 hundred to bribe one last stubborn excise officer. Tom Diver, ex-Mexican navy, said he knew exactly where a sunken treasure ship was sitting with 380 thousand dollars and 180 thousand pounds in gold bars, and all he needed was 18 hundred quid for a diving bell and four men, and he’d come back an MP and buy back his family estate. Keightley, the manager of the still-underwater copper mine, ran a side business selling Smyrna sponges and had a quicksilver scheme he swore would make his fortune too. Filby had been everything from a dragoon corporal to a fairground actor to a missionary converting the Irish, and now owned a plot of land somewhere no one could locate that he swore made him heir to a vast fortune. Even Sir Francis Clavering hung around the lot, even though he was pretty quiet, since everyone loved having a real baronet in their crew.

The problem was Altamont. Strong had dragged him home from a terrible fleabag hotel where he’d turned up almost completely penniless, despite being supposedly entrusted with a hoard of royal jewels, and now the man wouldn’t leave. Strong was sick of him. “That bloke’s no gentleman,” he grumbled to Clavering. “Dresses like a freed slave on a bank holiday. Took him to the theatre the other night, and he started yelling at the actor playing the villain so loud the box office staff almost threw us out. Then when the brigand in the afterpiece died, he sobbed like a toddler, screaming that it was a bloody disgrace, and started a fight with a bloke who laughed at him! I had to drag him out before he got us both arrested. Who even is he, Frank? Spill the beans. You two didn’t rob a church together, did you? Spit it out.”

All Clavering said was “Hang him! I wish he was dead,” and his face went so pale and grim that Strong didn’t press him any further—though he made a mental note to dig into whatever dark secret tied the fancy baronet to this wild, loud-mouthed fake Indian agent, no matter what.
