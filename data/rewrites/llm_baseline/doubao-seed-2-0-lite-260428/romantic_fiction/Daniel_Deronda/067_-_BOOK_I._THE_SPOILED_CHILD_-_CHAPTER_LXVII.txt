There is a spark of divine goodness in every human soul that drags our bravest, noblest choices out of us even when every selfish, reluctant part of our being fights to hold back.

It was an unpleasant, sharp shock for Daniel Deronda to return from his stay at the Abbey and find the last man he ever wished to see comfortably settled in the quiet Brompton lodgings Ezra and Mirah shared. Mirah had felt she could not keep Deronda’s existence from her newly returned father, and had done her best to explain how Ezra and Daniel had forged their deep, purpose-driven bond through shared faith and devotion to their people. She glossed over every part of what Daniel had done for her, entirely omitting the night he had pulled her from the cold Thames, and framing her time sheltered with the Meyrick family as the only reason she had ever crossed paths with him. The tender, fragile tie between her and Deronda was far too precious, far too intimate, to ever let her father’s rough, unworthy breath sully it. For his own unspoken, self-serving reasons, Lapidoth did not press her for details of her flight from him or how she had arrived destitute in England. But he was instantly, greedily interested to learn his two children had won the favor of a wealthy, well-connected gentleman of high standing.

Ezra was the one to break the news to Deronda, his voice soft but steady, as if he had spent weeks training himself not to flinch at the sound of his father’s name. “I am calm now, when I look at him,” he finished. “I keep hoping that Mirah’s gentle kindness, the quiet taste of a peaceful, honorable life we are giving him, will be enough to turn him away from every old temptation. I have bidden Mirah promise me, and she has sworn it, never to give him a single coin. I have shown her plainly that any money he gets for his own use will only buy him his own destruction.”

Deronda did not visit until the third day after Lapidoth’s arrival. The older man was still waiting for the new suit he had been measured for, and refused to make an impression in his ragged old clothes, so he hid behind the window curtain to watch Deronda come and go from the street below. He was surprised to see how young Deronda was, a detail Mirah had never mentioned; he had pictured the serious, scholarly friend of the grave, almost ethereal Ezra as a middle-aged, gray-bearded man. In an instant, Lapidoth’s shrewd mind jumped to the obvious conclusion: Deronda’s real, primary motive for being so generous to the pair of siblings must be that he was in love with Mirah. That was even better than if his loyalties had been tied only to Ezra: affection for Mirah promised far more willingness to indulge her father’s whims. Lapidoth had no doubt he could charm Deronda, smooth over any harsh first impressions.

He played the amiable, reformed man perfectly. He praised every note that came from Mirah’s piano, never dared smoke in the parlor that the strict landlady Mrs. Adam forbade tobacco in, and took his German pipe out to stroll the public square with the small supply of mild tobacco Mirah had bought for him. He was far too sharp to argue with Mirah when she told him she could not give him money, that she was bound by a sacred promise to her brother. He was comfortable enough to wait.

The next time Deronda called, Lapidoth was dressed head to toe in his brand new suit, preening with pride at his own reflection as he sat across from Ezra. Ezra was forcing himself, every single minute an act of iron will, to tolerate his father’s presence in the small room where he kept his most precious family papers. The very first sight of Lapidoth sent a cold, physical revulsion curling through Deronda’s chest: this was the man who had blighted his wife’s life, who had abandoned his two children to ruin, and Deronda could barely stand to breathe the same air as him. But Lapidoth did not flinch, asked politely if he might stay to hear them sort through the old documents from their locked chest, and even proved useful puzzling out the scrawled, difficult German handwriting on one tattered manuscript. He offered to transcribe every last page, pointing out that Ezra’s young eyes had started to strain from long hours of reading, while his own were still perfectly sharp.

Deronda took the offer as a small, hopeful sign that the man was finally willing to do honest, useful work, and he saw a flicker of quiet relief cross Ezra’s face before Ezra spoke. “All of that writing must happen right here, in this room,” he said firmly. “I cannot let any of these papers leave my sight, not even for an hour, for fear of accident, or fire, or them being lost.” Poor Ezra felt exactly like a warden guarding a convict on temporary leave, not his own father. Until he saw his father’s pen moving across the page with his own two eyes, he could not bring himself to trust the man to do an honest day’s work. But the arrangement chained Ezra to his father’s company, a torment made sharper by every old, painful memory it stirred, and by Lapidoth’s restless nature. The longer he settled in, the less he feared Ezra, and he would slip out constantly to smoke, wander around the room chattering nonstop about old plays he used to adapt, old adventures from their shared past, repeating jokes and stories he thought would make Mirah laugh. Every one of those minutes was a slow, quiet torture for Ezra. When she was home, Mirah would do her best to pull her father downstairs to the parlor to give her brother a few minutes of precious peace. It is not one single grand, brave choice that makes duty hard: it is the daily, unflinching weight of swallowing irritation, of ignoring small shames, of waking up every morning knowing the whole stretch of the day ahead holds more of the same, no end in sight. Mirah already feared this quiet, painful new normal might stretch on for the rest of her life.

Worst of all, Lapidoth’s leering, unspoken assumption that Deronda was courting Mirah hung between the two of them like an invisible, smudged veil. Both of them shrank back, afraid their quiet, tender feelings for each other would be soiled by his crude inferences, and each mistook the other’s new shyness and reserve for coldness, for disinterest.

But Deronda would soon get the clarity he had not dared hope for. As soon as he got back from his brief stay at the Abbey, he headed straight for Hans Meyrick’s painting rooms, knowing he owed his oldest, dearest friend the full story of what had driven his sudden trip, and the life-altering revelation he had come home with. Hans was not there; the servant said he had gone away to the country for a few days. Deronda left a long note, then waited a full week for a reply, worrying that his impulsive, soft-hearted friend had taken some quiet hurt to heart. When no note came, he called again, and stepped into the studio to find Hans in a loose light coat, no waistcoat, his long hair still dripping wet from a cold bath, his face gaunt and drawn, nothing like the ruddy, relaxed look of a man just back from the country. He had grabbed his palette and brushes and was standing posed in front of his easel like he had thrown the whole pretense together in a hurry the second he heard Deronda knock.

“You don’t look as if you’ve been out of London at all, old fellow,” Deronda said as they shook hands. “Did you go to Cambridge, then?”

“No,” Hans said shortly, tossing his palette down like he was annoyed he had even tried to keep the lie up. He pushed a chair over for Deronda, then flopped into his own, leaning back with his hands laced behind his head. “I’ve been in No Man’s Land. A very unpleasant, gray sort of place, no roads, no sun, nothing to do but mope.”

“Surely you haven’t been drinking yourself into a stupor, Hans?” Deronda said, leaning forward to study his friend’s worn face, worried.

“Nothing nearly that pleasant. I tried smoking opium. I always meant to test it someday, see what all the fuss about bliss was, and since I was fresh out of every other kind of bliss lately, I thought it was a fine time to try. But I swear to you I’ll never touch the stuff again. It disagrees with my constitution horribly.”

“What happened?” Deronda asked. “You were in such good spirits the last time you wrote to me.”

“Oh, nothing in particular. The whole world just started to look seedy, like a cabbage patch where all the cabbages had been chopped down. It’s a malady of genius, you can be sure,” Hans said, forcing a lopsided smile. “And I was just tired of being virtuous with no reward, especially in the middle of this sweltering London summer.”

“No real trouble, nothing that’s truly hurting you?” Deronda pressed.

Hans shook his head. “I came here to hear you tell me all your new secrets, but I can’t do that if you’re going to hide all your own affairs while you grill me. I’ve got no affairs worth mentioning except a petty quarrel with a bric-a-brac dealer. Besides, this is the first time in all our years of friendship you’ve ever told me anything personal about yourself. You’ve got a very long debt to pay.”

Deronda could see Hans was putting on a false front, but he trusted his old friend’s usual frankness would come back if he laid his own heart bare first. “You laughed at how mysterious I was about that trip to Italy, Hans,” he began. “That trip touched the very roots of my happiness. I never knew anything about my parents growing up. I went to Genoa to meet my mother. My father died when I was an infant. My mother was the daughter of a great, respected Jewish scholar; my father was her cousin. I suspected the truth for a long time before I left, but I was still so glad to find it confirmed. I am glad to know I am a Jew.”

“You don’t have to look surprised that I already knew,” Hans said, shifting in his seat to pick at the heel of his slipper. “My mother heard it all from the Meyricks the day after you visited their house. Brother and sister both told her. We can’t celebrate it quite the way Ezra and Mirah do, you know. But whatever makes you happy, I’ll be happy for it too—eventually. I can’t promise exactly when that eventually will land.”

“I don’t expect you to understand how I feel about it,” Deronda said. “But I couldn’t let silence hang between us when this changes every part of my future. I’ve taken Mordecai’s hopes and ideas for our people to heart. I intend to spend the rest of my life working to make them real, as much as one man can. I’ll probably travel to the East soon, and be gone for years.”

Hans did not say a word. He stood up abruptly, grabbed his palette, and stood with his back to Deronda, pretending to work at his half-finished painting. The silence stretched out thick, and both men felt the discomfort hum in the air. “I hope you don’t mind me asking,” Hans said quietly, still not turning around. “Does Mrs. Grandcourt know all this?”

“No. And I beg you, Hans,” Deronda said, sharp with anger, “to stop making jokes about that woman. Every silly assumption you’ve ever had about me and her is the exact opposite of the truth. I have never once had, and I never will have, the position of a lover to Gwendolen Grandcourt.”

For a long minute, neither spoke. “Maybe I’ve been wrong about another assumption, too,” Hans said finally.

“What assumption?”

“That you had no wish to be a lover to another woman—one who is neither wife nor widow.”

“I don’t need to pretend I don’t know who you mean, Meyrick.” Deronda’s voice was soft now, but tight with feeling. “It hurts me that what we want clashes. Tell me, if you can, if you have any reason to think your feelings for her might be returned.”

“That’s a very superfluous question for you to ask, isn’t it?” Hans said, irritation flaring in his tone.

“Why superfluous?”

“Because you’re perfectly sure of the answer already. And you’ve had far better proof than I ever got to convince you of it.”

“I’ll be more frank with you than you’re being with me right now,” Deronda said, his own temper rising a little, even as he felt deeply sorry for Hans. “I have never had the tiniest scrap of proof that my feelings would be returned. I have almost no hope at all.”

Hans spun around to glance at his friend, then immediately turned his back on him again. “And even if there was no one else in the world to worry about,” Deronda went on, stung by the thought that Hans thought he was lying, “I can’t deliberately tell her how I feel. If she didn’t return my love, I would poison the only little peaceful, happy home she has. She and I are both tied to Ezra for the rest of our lives, we’d have to see each other every single day. If I forced that pain on her, I’d be no better than a cruel, thoughtless brute.”

“I never actually told her how I feel about her, either,” Hans said, like he was defending himself.

“Then we’re on equal ground, aren’t we? You have no reason to envy me.”

“Not a single bit,” Hans said, his voice thick with bitter irony. “You know how big my conceit is, you know I think I’m better than every one of your advantages.”

“I’m a nuisance to you, Meyrick. I hate that, but I can’t help it,” Deronda said, standing up. “After all we’ve talked about before, I owed you this explanation. I don’t see how any of my quiet hopes have hurt you. And they’re not going to do me any good, not right now, not with her father living under the same roof as her. Things could drag on for two whole years, and I’d never know how she feels about me. That’s the whole truth of it, Hans. Neither of us has wronged the other. We just have to make our peace with this quiet, silly rivalry over a hope that will probably never come to anything. Our friendship is strong enough to bear that, isn’t it?”

“No, it isn’t,” Hans burst out, throwing his brushes down hard on the table, shoving his hands in his coat pockets, and spinning around to face Deronda. Deronda stepped back, stunned by the fire in his old friend’s face. “Our friendship—my friendship—can’t bear the strain of me pretending to be an ungrateful, petty coward, grudging you the one thing that makes you happy. Because you’re the luckiest man alive. If Mirah loves anyone better than she loves her own brother, it’s you.”

Hans dropped back into his chair, staring up at Deronda, his face hard and raw with unhidden pain. A shock ran all the way through Deronda, so warm and bright it felt like sunlight breaking through a storm. “That’s a very kind lie to make up for me, Hans,” he said, his voice unsteady.

“I’m not in a kind mood at all,” Hans snapped. “I hated realizing it, when I first saw it clear as day, especially back when I thought your whole heart was tied up with that duchess. But now, blast you! You turn out to be in love exactly where you should be—with a Jew, and everything perfect, everything right.”

“Tell me what made you sure,” Deronda said, the joy he had never dared to dream of bubbling up so fast he could barely breathe around it.

“Don’t ask. My own mother saw it, plain as day. The long and short of it is that Mirah is jealous of the time you spend with Mrs. Grandcourt. You ought to tell her how you feel as soon as possible, get it all out in the open. There! I’ve said it. Now I have full permission to curse you for getting the very best luck any man could ever deserve.”

“God bless you, Hans,” Deronda said, holding out his hand. Hans took it, and wrung it hard, neither of them needing to say another word.
