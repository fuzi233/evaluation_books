Newland Archer sat at the writing desk in his library on East Thirty-ninth Street, the silence of the room heavy with the weight of a lifetime. He had just returned from the grand inauguration of the new galleries at the Metropolitan Museum, a spectacle of scientific cataloguing and fashionable throngs that had somehow triggered a rusted spring in his memory. Instantly, the polished modernity of the museum dissolved. He was no longer in his own study; he was back in the old Cesnola room, sitting alone on a hard leather divan against a radiator, watching a slight figure in a sealskin cloak fade away down the meagre vista of the past.

This vision unlocked a floodgate of associations. He looked around his library with new eyes, seeing it not merely as a room, but as the stage for the most pivotal moments of his existence. It was here, nearly twenty-six years prior, that his wife, May, had broken the news of her pregnancy with a blushing circumlocution that would have amused the women of a younger generation. It was here that their eldest son, Dallas—too delicate for the winter church—had been christened by the magnificent Bishop of New York. Dallas had first stumbled across the floor shouting "Dad," while May and the nurse laughed behind the door; it was here that Mary, so much like her mother, had announced her engagement to Reggie Chivers's most reliable son, and where Archer had kissed her through her wedding veil before they descended to the motor for their Grace Church ceremony.

In this room, he and May had mapped out the futures of their children: Dallas's studies, Mary's passion for sport and philanthropy, and the vague leanings toward art that had eventually landed Dallas in the office of a rising architect. He watched the world shift around him; the young men of his generation were breaking free from the narrow grooves of law and business, diving into archaeology, architecture, and the preservation of Georgian types, mocking the meaningless use of the word "Colonial" unless applied by suburban millionaire grocers.

But above all, it was in this library that the Governor of New York, visiting one evening, had slammed his fist on the table and declared, "Hang the professional politician! Men like you, Archer, are what the country needs." How Archer had glowed at that summons, eager to roll up his sleeves and get into the muck. Yet, looking back, he wasn't so sure that men like himself were what the nation truly required. After a single term in the State Assembly and a retreat to municipal work and reform journalism, he realized his contribution was small, yet significant—a single brick in a well-built wall. He had become a "good citizen," a name invoked whenever a new school for crippled children or a chamber music society was proposed. His days were full, decently filled.

Yet, he knew he had missed the flower of life. He thought of it now with the detached serenity of someone who knows the odds were stacked against him from the start. When he thought of Ellen Olenska, he did so abstractly, as one thinks of a beloved in a painting: she had become the composite vision of everything he had missed. That faint, tenuous image had kept him faithful to May. When May had died, carried off by pneumonia while nursing their youngest child, he had mourned her honestly. Their long years together had taught him that marriage, if it maintained the dignity of a duty, was preferable to a battle of ugly appetites. He honored his past, mourning for it, acknowledging that there was good in the old ways.

His eyes traveled over the room—Dallas's renovation with English mezzotints, Chippendale cabinets, and pleasant electric lamps—resting finally on the old Eastlake writing table and the first photograph of May. There she was, tall and willowy in starched muslin, just as he had seen her under the Mission garden's orange trees. She had remained frozen in that image: generous, faithful, unwearied, yet lacking in imagination. Her blindness to change had created a joint pretence of sameness within the family, a hypocritical harmony in which father and children had unconsciously collaborated. She had died believing the world was a good place, convinced that Newland would continue to inculcate the same principles in Dallas, who would then pass them to little Bill.

Opposite May's portrait stood Mary Chivers. She was tall and fair, yet large-waisted and slightly slouching, a physical manifestation of the altered fashion. Her athletic feats could not have been performed with the twenty-inch waist her mother had worn. The difference seemed symbolic: the mother's life had been tightly girt, while Mary, no less conventional but more intelligent, led a larger life. There was good in the new order, too.

The telephone clicked. Archer picked up the receiver. "Chicago wants you."

It was Dallas, sent to the Windy City to discuss plans for the Lakeside palace. Dallas's voice was cheerful, urging his father to sail on the *Mauretania* next Wednesday to inspect Italian gardens. "It'll be our last time together in this kind of way," Dallas had said, laughing with a joy that startled Archer. Across thousands of miles of forest, river, and indifferent millions, that laugh sounded impossibly close. Dallas was marrying Fanny Beaufort on the fifth, and he needed his father's blessing immediately.

Archer paced the room, feeling the pull of that final chance to be alone with his boy. They were born comrades, and Fanny seemed unlikely to interfere. Yet, change was change. May had disliked travel, insisting on staying in Thirty-ninth Street or Newport, and Archer had always stuck to her routine, declining a trip to Paris to join the family in Italy. Since her death, the urge to travel had been strong, urged on by Mary's confidence that seeing the galleries would do him good. But Archer had been held fast by habit, by memories, by a startling shrinking from the new.

Now, reviewing his past, he saw the depth of the rut he had sunk into. The rigid divisions of right and wrong, respectable and otherwise, had left little room for the unforeseen. He remembered Lawrence Lefferts's sneering prophecy: "If things go on at this rate, our children will be marrying Beaufort's bastards." And there it was, happening right before his eyes. Dallas was marrying Fanny Beaufort, the orphaned daughter of a failed businessman and a notorious Russian wife. Society had forgotten the scandal; Fanny was pretty, amusing, and accomplished. People were too busy with their own reforms and fads to care about the past.

Newland found himself transported to Paris, standing in his hotel room overlooking the Place Vendôme. His heart beat with the confusion and eagerness of youth, a rhythm he felt was absent in his son's cool composure. Dallas took it for granted that he would get what he wanted; Newland had always taken it for granted that he wouldn't. He wondered if the certainty of rejection could ever make one's heart beat as wildly as the possibility of desire.

On the day after their arrival, with spring sunshine holding him in his open window, Newland had stipulated that he would not stay in a newfangled palace. Dallas had cheerfully agreed to take him to the Bristol, a century-old hotel now considered quaint. Newland had often imagined returning to Paris, evoking the radiant spring, the lilacs, and the majestic river. Now, the spectacle was before him, and he felt shy, old-fashioned, and inadequate—a grey speck compared to the ruthless magnificent fellow he had dreamed of being.

Dallas's hand came down on his shoulder. "Hullo, father: this is something like, isn't it?" Then, casually, he delivered the message: the Countess Olenska expected them at half-past five. Dallas spoke of it lightly, as if inviting them to tea, mentioning that Fanny had made him swear to see her, noting that she had been kind to Fanny when she arrived from Buenos Aires.

Archer stared at his son. "You told her I was here?"

"Of course—why not?" Dallas's eyebrows went up whimsically, then he slipped his arm through his father's with a confidential pressure. "I say, father: what was she like?"

Archer felt his color rise. "Come, own up: you and she were great pals, weren't you? Wasn't she most awfully lovely?"

"Lovely? I don't know. She was different."

"Ah—there you have it! That's what it always comes to. When she comes, she's different—and one doesn't know why. It's exactly what I feel about Fanny."

His father drew back. "About Fanny? But, my dear fellow—I should hope so!"

"Dash it, Dad, don't be prehistoric! Wasn't she—once—your Fanny?"

Dallas belonged to the new generation, devoid of reserve. "What's the use of making mysteries? It only makes people want to nose 'em out." But Archer saw the filial light under his son's banter.

"My Fanny?"

"Well, the woman you'd have chucked everything for: only you didn't."

"I didn't," Archer echoed with solemnity.

"No: you date, you see, dear old boy. But mother said—the day before she died. She sent for me alone. She said she knew we were safe with you, and always would be, because once, when she asked you to, you'd given up the thing you most wanted."

Archer received this strange communication in silence, his eyes fixed on the thronged square below. At length, he said in a low voice, "She never asked me."

"No. I forgot. You never did ask each other anything, did you? And you never told each other anything. You just sat and watched each other, and guessed at what was going on underneath. A deaf-and-dumb asylum, in fact!" Dallas broke off, sensing his father's mood. "I say, Dad, you're not angry with me? If you are, let's make it up and go to lunch at Henri's."

Archer did not accompany his son to Versailles. He preferred the solitude of the afternoon, needing to deal with the packed regrets and stifled memories of an inarticulate lifetime. Soon, he did not regret Dallas's indiscretion; it seemed to take an iron band from his heart to know that someone had guessed and pitied. That it should have been his wife moved him indescribably. To the boy, the episode was merely a pathetic instance of frustration. But for Archer, it was more. He sat on a bench in the Champs Élysées, watching the stream of life roll by, wondering.

A few streets away, Ellen Olenska waited. She had never returned to her husband, and when he died, she had made no change in her way of living. There was nothing now to keep them apart.

Newland walked across the Place de la Concorde and the Tuileries to the Louvre. She had once told him she often went there, and he spent an hour wandering through the galleries, letting the pictures burst upon him in their half-forgotten splendour. After all, his life had been too starved. Suddenly, before an effulgent Titian, he found himself saying, "But I'm only fifty-seven," and then turning away. For such summer dreams, it was too late; but surely not for a quiet harvest of friendship, of comradeship, in the blessed hush of her nearness.

Returning to the hotel, they walked again across the Place de la Concorde to the bridge leading to the Chamber of Deputies. Dallas talked excitedly of Versailles, his enthusiasm tumbling over his criticism, unaware of the turmoil in his father's mind. Newland felt a growing sense of inadequacy. The boy looked at fate not as a master but as an equal, feeling equal to things, knowing his way about. He was the spokesman of a new generation that had swept away the old landmarks and their danger signals.

Suddenly, Dallas stopped short, grasping his father's arm. "Oh, by Jove."

They had reached the great tree-planted space before the Invalides. The dome of Mansart floated ethereally above the budding trees, drawing up all the rays of afternoon light, hanging there like the visible symbol of the race's glory. Archer knew that Madame Olenska lived in a square near one of the avenues radiating from the Invalides. That golden light became for him the pervading illumination in which she lived. For nearly thirty years, her life had been spent in this rich atmosphere, too dense and yet too stimulating for his lungs. He thought of the theatres she must have attended, the pictures she must have looked at, the people she must have talked with. He remembered the young Frenchman who had once said to him, "Ah, good conversation—there is nothing like it, is there?"

He had not seen M. Rivière or heard of him for nearly thirty years; that fact gave the measure of his ignorance of Madame Olenska's existence. More than half a lifetime divided them. She had spent the interval among people he did not know, in a society he but faintly guessed at. During that time, he had been living with his youthful memory of her, but she had doubtless had other, more tangible companionship. Perhaps she too had kept her memory of him as something apart, like a relic in a small dim chapel where there was not time to pray every day.

They crossed the Place des Invalides and walked down a quiet thoroughfare. The day was fading into a soft sun-shot haze, pricked by yellow electric lights. Dallas stopped, looking up at a modern, many-windowed building with cream-coloured fronts and balconies.

"It must be here," he said, slipping his arm through his father's. They stood together looking up at the house.

"I wonder which floor?" Dallas conjectured, asking the porter. "The fifth. It must be the one with the awnings."

Archer remained motionless, gazing at the upper windows as if the end of their pilgrimage had been attained.

"I say, you know, it's nearly six," his son reminded him.

The father glanced away at an empty bench under the trees. "I believe I'll sit there a moment."

"Why—aren't you well?" his son exclaimed.

"Oh, perfectly. But I should like you, please, to go up without me."
