In the quiet seclusion of a Dominican guest chamber, a solitary figure lay prostrate, his strength ebbing away after violent convulsions of sickness that had finally subsided. He was as frail and broken as Margaret had been in Holland that same night. A roaring fire crackled on the hearth, casting dancing shadows against the walls where the patient's garments hung to dry. Beside the bed sat a massive friar, his presence commanding yet serene, reading aloud from his breviary. The stranger watched him with half-lidded eyes, listening intently at times, then closing his face to the moans of pain. The monk knelt, forehead touching the stone floor in fervent prayer, before rising to bid him adieu. "Day breaks," the friar announced softly. "I must prepare for matins."

"Good Father Jerome," the stranger gasped, "before you depart, tell me—how did I come to be here?"

"By the hand of Heaven," the friar replied, his voice steady. "You cast aside God's gift; He has restored it to you. Reflect on this! You have tasted the gall of the world; now taste the peace of the Church. *Pax vobiscum*."

With those words, he departed. Gerard lay back, his mind reeling in a haze of wonder and exhaustion, until sleep claimed him.

When consciousness returned, a new guardian sat by his side. This was a layman, his eyes small and darting with restless anxiety, a stark contrast to the calm majesty of Friar Jerome.

"How do you feel, messer?" the man asked earnestly.

"Extremely weak," Gerard murmured. "Have I seen you before?"

"None the worse for my gauntlet?" the man inquired, his tone laced with concern. "I nearly struck you down, or both of us would have sunk to the bottom of the Tiber."

Gerard stared, confusion giving way to recognition. "What? Was it you who saved me? How?"

"At your service, signor," the man said. "I was on an errand by the riverbanks when you approached me, begging for a dagger stroke. Yet, even as you spoke, I recognized you—the nobleman who saved my wife and child upon the sea."

"It is Teresa's husband," Gerard whispered, horror dawning. "And an assassin?"

"Indeed. After you threw yourself into the Tiber, bidding me stay back, I hesitated. Men are my enemies; I could let them all drown or hang on a single rope. But when you sank, crying out 'Margaret!' my heart screamed 'Teresa!' How could I return home and face her if I let you die, especially since your death was the very act that saved hers? So I entered the water. Lucky for us both, I swim like a duck. You, intent on destruction, tried to seize me to end us together. But I swam around you and, with my apologies, struck the back of your neck with my steel-gloved fist. You lost consciousness. Against the strong current, I dragged your body to shore, insensible and filled with water. I carried you home, thinking Teresa would nurse you and bless me. But a holy friar, the largest I have ever seen, intercepted us. He examined you closely. 'I know this face,' he said. 'It is Gerard, a fair youth from Holland.' I confirmed it. Then he declared, 'He has friends among our brethren. Leave him with us! Charity is our office.' He noted the convent possessed better means to tend you than I did. True enough. So I negotiated to visit daily, and here you are."

As he spoke, the man cast a look upon Gerard that was strangely affectionate, tinged with a deep, twisted interest.

Gerard recoiled internally, feeling as though a serpent coiled within the room. He closed his eyes.

"Ah, you wish to sleep," the man said eagerly. "I shall withdraw." He retreated on tiptoe, promising his daily return.

Gerard remained with his eyes shut, awake in a state of profound contemplation. Saved from death by an assassin. Was this not the finger of Heaven? Of that Heaven he had once insulted, cursed, and defied. A shudder ran through him at the memory of his blasphemies. He attempted to pray. He found he could utter the words, but his spirit refused to join them.

"I am doomed eternally," he cried out in despair. "Doomed, doomed."

Suddenly, the organ of the convent church erupted in rich, solemn harmony. The voices of the choir rose to meet it, chanting a full service. Among them floated a voice that seemed to tower above the rest—a sweet boy's voice, pure and angelic. Gerard closed his eyes and listened. In those harmonious tones, the days of his own boyhood flooded back, unburdened by earthly dross or the foul, fierce passions that had once rent his soul. Peace. Sweet, balmy peace.

"Yes," he sighed, tears welling in his eyes. "The Church is peace of mind. Until I left her bosom, I knew neither sorrow nor sin."

The weary creature wept openly. Just as his grief peaked, the sweet, reverend face of one he had not expected to see again beamed upon him. It was Father Anselm. The good father had arrived at the convent only the night before. Gerard recognized him instantly and cried out, "Oh, Father Anselm! You healed my wounded body in Juliers; now cure my hurt soul in Rome!"

"Alas," Anselm replied gently, "you cannot."

He sat by the bedside, placing a soothing hand upon Gerard's head to calm his agitation. Having learned the tale of Gerard's arrival, Anselm spoke to him with kindness and solemnity, making him feel the weight of his crime and urging him toward repentance and gratitude for the Divine Power that had thwarted his will to save his soul.

"Come, my son," Anselm said. "First, purge your bosom of its burden."

"Oh, father," Gerard confessed, "in Juliers I could; but now, an impious monster that I am, I dare not confess to you."

"Why not, my son? Do you think I have not sinned against Heaven in my time, and deeply? Oh, how deeply! Come, poor laden soul, pour forth your grief, pour forth your faults, hold back nothing! Do not lie oppressed and crushed by hidden sins."

Soon, Gerard was at Father Anselm's knees, confessing every sin with sighs and groans of penitence.

"Your sins are great," Anselm acknowledged. "Your temptation was also great, terribly great. I must consult our good prior."

Kissing Gerard's brow, the good father departed to determine the appropriate penance. And lo! Gerard could pray now. He prayed with all the fervor of his heart.

The phase through which this remarkable mind now passed may be summed in a single word: Penitence. He turned with terror and aversion from the world, begging passionately to remain in the convent. To him, the cloister was like a wounded bird returning to its gentle nest. He spent his novitiate in prayer, mortification, pious reading, and meditation.

Meanwhile, the Princess Claelia's spy returned home to report that Gerard was certainly dead, though the manner of his death remained unknown. She seemed literally stunned. When she finally found breath to speak, it was to lament her lot, cursed with such ready instruments of evil. "So soon," she sighed. "See how swiftly these monsters commit ill deeds. They come to us in our hot blood, first tempting us with their venal daggers, then enacting the mortal deeds we never would have conceived but for them."

Within hours, her pity for Gerard and her hatred for his murderer rose to a fever pitch, burning with the same intensity as her own foolish rage. "Poor soul! I cannot call thee back to life. But he shall never live that traitorously slew thee."

She immediately placed armed men in ambush, keeping them on guard all day, ready to fall upon Lodovico in a certain antechamber the moment he came for his money, hacking him to pieces.

"Strike at his head," she commanded. "For he wears a privy coat of mail; and if he goes hence alive, your own heads shall answer for it."

Thus she sat weeping over her victim, pulling the strings of machines designed to shed the blood of a second man in retribution for having served as her instrument to kill the first.
