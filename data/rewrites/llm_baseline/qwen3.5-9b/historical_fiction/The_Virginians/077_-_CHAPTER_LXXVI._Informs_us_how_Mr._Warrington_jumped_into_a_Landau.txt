Once the initial shock of our reunion subsided, the little maid, Hetty, launched immediately into her tirade. "So you have finally come to inquire after Theo, and you feel remorse that your neglect has rendered her so ill? Six weeks she has languished, and not a single word have you spoken concerning her! Truly, Mr. George, you are kind!"

"Kind?" Mr. Warrington gasped, incredulous. "Do you deem it kindness to attend her day and night for a year, only to vanish without a whisper?"

"My dear fellow," I replied, attempting to smooth things over, "do you recall the promise you gave my father?"

"A promise!" Hetty scoffed, rolling her eyes. "What a magnificent pledge, to sicken my darling and then, on a sudden, bid her 'Goodbye, Theo,' and stride away forever! Gentlemen make such vows merely to break them. I would not toy with a poor child's heart and abandon her if I were a man. What crime has she committed against you but to be foolish and excessively fond? By what divine right do you snatch her from us, only to desert her because an elderly American woman disapproves of her? She was content with us until you arrived. She adored her sister—there was never such a devoted sibling—until she met you. And now, simply because your mother believes a younger gentleman might suit her better, you intend to cast her aside!"

"Heavens, child!" I cried, exasperated by her stubborn logic. "Did I withdraw? Am I not barred from your household? Did not your father demand, on my honor, that I cease all attempts to see her?"

"Honor!" she retorted. "You are the men who claim superiority; it is we who must revere you! I declare, George Warrington, you belong in your schoolroom in Virginia still, tucked into bed by a black nurse, asking permission to step outside. Oh, George! I never imagined my sister was entrusting her heart to a man lacking the spine to stand by her, fleeing at the first obstacle! When Dr. Heberden mentioned attending to you, I resolved to visit, and you truly looked ill—I am glad to see it, perhaps fearing your mother. But I shall not inform Theo of your sickness. She refuses to break her solemn engagements to roam the world as if nothing occurred! That is the province of men, our superiors in courage and wisdom; to abandon an angel—an angel ten thousand times too pure for you—is what you term honor? Do not speak to me! I despise you all! Are we to worship and serve you? I care not for your wit, your tragedies, or your verses; they often seem dreadfully dull. I will not spend my nights copying your manuscripts nor waste hours gazing from a window hoping to catch you strolling with your hat cocked! If you are leaving, and God speed, give me back my sister! Return my darling of the old days, who loved us all until she saw you. You send her away because your mother seeks a richer match for you! Oh, brave gentleman! Marry whoever your mother selects, and let my dear perish here, forsaken!"

"Great heavens, Hetty!" I exclaimed, stunned by the coherence of her distress. "Is it I who wish to depart? Did I not offer to uphold my promise? Was it not your father who refused me and compelled me to swear never to seek her sight again? All I possess is my word and my honor!"

"Honor, indeed!" she snapped. "You keep your word to him and betray hers! A pretty sort of honor! If I were a man, I would show you quickly what I thought of your code! Only I forgot—you are bound to maintain the peace and must not... Oh, George, George! Do you not perceive the agony tearing me apart? I am distraught, scarcely knowing what I utter. You must not leave my darling. They do not realize at home; they assume otherwise, but I know her best, and she will die if you abandon her. Swear you will stay! Have pity on me, Mr. Warrington, and restore my dearest to me!"

Thus, the warm-hearted, frantic creature oscillated between rage and supplication, from scorn to tears. Was my little physician correct in diagnosing her state regarding her beloved patient? Was there no cure other than what Hetty demanded? Had not others endured the cruel pain of amputation, suffered the subsequent exhaustion and fever, lain near death expecting only the end, and yet recovered, limping onward through life? Perhaps, unless love is inherently selfish, ignoring the griefs and passions of others—or unless our own affection was so intense and unique that we believed no other lover could suffer as we did—in this passionate advocate for her sister, we might have witnessed proof that a loving heart could be struck by the disease of love, endure it silently, and eventually heal.

What had transpired in Hetty's own case? My sister and I, basking in our easy triumph and intimate chatter, had frequently discussed this matter. Egotists that we were, we perhaps extracted a secret zest and sense of security from her lesser fortune. It was akin to sitting by a fireside while winter howled outside, or walking beside the *maxi magno* while watching a ship toss at sea. We clung to one another more tightly, wrapped in our own bliss, viewing others' misfortunes with complacent pity.

Be the truth as it may. Granted that we might have been severed and, after a time, survived the separation—a notion my skeptical old age might concede. Yet, at that juncture, I was eager to share Hetty's terrors and apprehensions, willingly choosing to believe that the life dearest to me in the world would be sacrificed if torn from mine. Was I wrong? I would not assert that now. I may doubt myself (or perhaps I do not doubt), but of her, never; and Hetty found in me a willing partner in her alarms.

I considered imparting some of these fears to our doctor, but the good gentleman silenced me. "Hush," he said, with a comical expression of terror. "I must hear none of this. If two acquaintances chance to meet and converse in my patients' room, I cannot control myself; but as for matchmaking and love-making, I am your humble servant! What will the General do when he returns to town? He will have me dismissed from Montagu House as surely as I am a living doctor, and alive I wish to remain, my good sir!" With that, he skipped into his carriage, leaving me there to ponder.

"And you and Miss Hetty must have no further meetings here, mind you," he had warned earlier.

Oh, certainly not! We were gentlemen of honor, and so forth; our word was our bond. Moreover, to have seen Hetty was an inestimable boon, and how could we possibly be ungrateful? Refreshed by that drop of water, I fancied I could endure indefinitely. I walked away with Hetty toward Soho, never once considering arranging a new rendezvous. But the little emissary was more thoughtful. She asked if I intended to visit the Museum.

"Oh yes, sometimes, my dear," I replied. "But I am too wretched for reading now; I cannot discern what lies upon the page. I care not for my books. Even *Pocahontas* grows wearisome to me..."

"Nonsense!" she interrupted, stamping her tiny foot. "Why, I declare, George, you are stupider than Harry!"

"How do you mean, my dear child?" I asked.

"When do you go? You depart at three o'clock. You cross directly onto the road to Tottenham Court. You traverse the village and return via Green Lane, leading back toward the new hospital. You know you do! If you walk along there for a week, it cannot harm you. Good morning, sir! Please do not follow me any farther."

She dropped a curtsey, veiled her face, and departed.

That Green Lane, lying north of the new hospital, is now densely packed with houses. In my time, during the reign of the good King George II, it was a shabby rural exit from London, so perilous that city dwellers returning from their villas and country retreats at Hampstead and the outlying villages would travel in groups at night, escorted by waiters carrying lanterns to protect themselves from footpads prowling the town's edges. Hampstead and Highgate churches, each crowning its hill, formed the backdrop of the vista visible as one turned one's back on London. For one, two, or three days, Mr. George Warrington enjoyed gazing upon this landscape, walking back toward the new hospital.

Along the lane stood several small taverns. I recall one establishment selling cakes and beer, bearing the sign of the Protestant Hero, where a decent woman smiled at me on the third or fourth day, curtsying in her clean apron as she remarked, "It seems the lady does not come, sir! Your honor had best step in and take a can of my cool beer."

Finally, as I returned through Tottenham Road on the twenty-fifth of May—a day worthy of being marked with the whitest stone—a short distance beyond Mr. Whitefield's Tabernacle, I spotted a landau ahead. Upon the box-seat beside the driver sat my young friend Charley, who waved his hat and called out, "George! George!"
