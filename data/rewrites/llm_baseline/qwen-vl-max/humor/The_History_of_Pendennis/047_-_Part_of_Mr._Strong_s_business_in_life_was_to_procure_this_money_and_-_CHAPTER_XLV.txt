Every day after the grand social events at Grosvenor Place and Greenwich—where Major Pendennis had been seen enjoying himself like a man who’d just discovered that champagne is not, in fact, a beverage reserved only for kings—the old gentleman’s affection for the Clavering family seemed to grow stronger than ever. His visits became so frequent they might as well have been scheduled by the household staff. He brought gifts, offered advice, and generally behaved as though he were the family’s unofficial but very enthusiastic uncle.

He was, after all, an old man about town—a seasoned veteran of society’s glittering trenches—and had managed to get invited to places where even Lady Clavering ought to be seen. “Would your ladyship care to attend the grand entertainment at Gaunt House?” he’d ask with the air of someone delivering divine prophecy. “Or perhaps you’d prefer the breakfast ball at Viscount Marrowfat’s? It’s said to be *very* pretty. And there’ll be a Watteau quadrille—Miss Amory would look simply divine in it!”

He was always ready to escort her, or to assist Sir Francis in any way agreeable to the latter—which, given the Baronet’s current standing, probably meant helping him avoid arrest.

Now, despite his noble lineage and decent income (though we’re not sure how decent), Clavering remained something of a pariah in polite circles. Clubs blackballed him twice. In Parliament, he conversed mostly with members whose reputations were already tarnished beyond repair—men who could only be described as “the kind of people you don’t want to meet in a dark alley.” Among them: Captain Raff, the honourable member for Epsom, who retired after the Goodwood races to accept a mission to the Levant (which, according to Mr. Hotspur, the whip of the party, was code for “being sent somewhere no one else wanted to go”); Hustingson, patriotic MP for Islington, now Governor of Coventry Island (a post which conveniently silenced his denunciations of corruption); and Bob Freeny, of the Booterstown Freenys, who was famously a dead shot—so much so that the author feels compelled to say, “We therefore wish to speak with every respect.”

And yet, among all these gentlemen, none did Mr. Hotspur despise more than Sir Francis Clavering. “If that man is needed for a division,” Hotspur declared, “ten to one he’ll be found in a hell. Educated in the Fleet, never heard the end of Newgate yet. Will muddle away the Begum’s fortune at thimble-rig, get caught picking pockets, and end up on board the hulks.”

And if Hotspur, a man of high birth and impeccable judgment, could still maintain a civil façade toward Clavering for professional reasons—why couldn’t Major Pendennis do the same?

“Because,” said the Major, “he has a very good cellar and a very good cook. As long as he keeps quiet, he’s not offensive. And he rarely speaks. If he chooses to lose his money at gambling tables to blacklegs, what’s it to me? Don’t pry into people’s affairs, Pen, my boy. Every man has a cupboard he doesn’t want you to open. Why bother peering inside when the rest of the house is wide open? And what a house it is! The women are excellent. The Begum may not be refined—she calls ‘Apollo’ ‘Apoller’—but she’s kinder than most and cleverer than most too. And as for little Blanche… well, I’ve told you my opinion. She’s sweet on you, you rogue. You know she’d marry you if you asked. But you’re growing such a great man now, I suppose you won’t settle for a Duke’s daughter—eh, sir? I recommend you try one. Ask one. Try.”

Pen, perhaps slightly inflated by his recent success in life, allowed this idea to float around his mind like a balloon tethered to a garden gate. His uncle’s constant hints didn’t hurt either. Wasn’t Miss Amory, after all, showing signs of interest in renewing their early flirtation—one that had once bloomed beside the tranquil banks of the rural Brawl? Still, Pen professed no desire for marriage. “I’m happy alone,” he said, adopting his uncle’s worldly tone. “With a wife, I’d lose my place in society. I don’t fancy retiring to the country with Mrs. Pendennis, nor taking her into lodgings to be waited on by the servant-of-all-work. My illusions are over. You cured me of my first love—foolish girl, and she’d have made a fool of a husband, and a very sulky one at that. We young fellows live fast. At twenty-five, I feel as ancient as those old bachelors in the bow-window at Bays’s. Don’t be offended—I just mean I’m bored with romance. I couldn’t stir myself to fall in love with Miss Amory again any more than I could adore Lady Mirabel all over again. I wish I could. I rather like old Mirabel for being so infatuated—he’s got the most respectable part of his life in his passion.”

“Sir Charles Mirabel was always theatrical,” the Major snapped, annoyed that his nephew should speak flippantly of someone of such rank. “He acted at Carlton House when he was Page to the Prince. He’s always been mixed up with that sort of thing. He could afford to marry whom he liked. And Lady Mirabel is a most respectable woman—received everywhere, mind you. The Duchess of Connaught receives her. Lady Rockminster receives her. Young men shouldn’t speak lightly of people in that station. There’s not a more respectable woman in England than Lady Mirabel. And those old fogies at Bays’s? They’re some of the first gentlemen in England. You youngsters should learn manners, breeding, modesty.”

The Major began to suspect Pen was becoming overly pert, too full of himself, and far too popular for his own good.

Pen found this amusing. He studied his uncle’s quirks with the delight of a scholar examining rare fossils. “I’ve been a youngster for fifteen years now,” he said smoothly. “And if you think we’re disrespectful, you should see the present generation. A protege of yours came to breakfast with me the other day. You told me to invite him, so I did. We saw the sights, dined at the club, went to the play. He said the wine at the Polyanthus wasn’t as good as Ellis’s at Richmond, smoked Warrington’s cavendish after breakfast, and when I gave him a sovereign as a farewell gift, said he had plenty, but would take it to show he wasn’t proud.”

“Did he?” the Major exclaimed, instantly appeased. “You asked young Clavering? Fine boy—rather wild, but a fine boy. Parents like that sort of attention. Can’t do better than pay it to our worthy friends at Grosvenor Place. Took him to the play and tipped him? That was right, sir—that was right!” With that, the Mentor abandoned Telemachus, deciding the youth weren’t so bad after all—and that he might yet make something of that fellow.

As Master Clavering grew taller and stronger, he became less inclined to obey his fond parents and governess. Instead, he ruled the household. With his father, he was silent and sullen, avoiding him entirely. With his mother, he roared and fought whenever she refused him anything—especially food or sweets. With his governess, he kicked her shins so hard she eventually surrendered, convinced she’d lost the battle before it began. He once tried to bully his sister Blanche too—but she, possessing immense spirit, boxed his ears so soundly that he never attempted it again. She also beat him in arguments, which was impressive considering he hadn’t yet learned to read.

Eventually, Sir Francis decided enough was enough. “The little beggar should be sent to school,” he declared.

So young Frank Clavering was dispatched to the Rev. Otto Rose’s establishment in Twickenham—where future noblemen were prepped for public schools with all the dignity and luxury one might expect from a boarding school run by a man who thought moral discipline was superior to corporal punishment. Which, frankly, was a relief for everyone involved.

At Rose’s, the boys wore varnished boots, cambric handkerchiefs, and lemon-coloured kid gloves so tiny they looked like they belonged to a doll. They dressed in shawl dressing-gowns for dinner, had fires in their bedrooms, horse-and-carriage exercise occasionally, and oil for their hair. No flogging. Just enlightenment. And progress.

They progressed rapidly—so rapidly, in fact, that by age ten, young Frank had mastered the art of drinking spirits and smoking cigars. He stole his father’s Havannahs and smoked them in the stables. He drank champagne with the confidence of a seasoned officer. At ten years old, he was already a man among men—except that he still wet the bed sometimes.

When he returned home for holidays, Major Pendennis treated him with the same laborious civility he showed the rest of the family—even though the boy mocked him behind his back, calling him “Old Wigsby” and drawing caricatures of him with exaggerated noses and wigs that looked like they’d been blown off a shipwreck.

But the Major was undeterred. He encouraged Arthur to invite the boy to his chambers, to dine at the club, to visit Madame Tussaud’s, the Tower, the theatre—and to tip him at the end of each day. Arthur, being kind-hearted and fond of children, obliged. He took the boy to breakfast at the Temple, where the lad made contemptuous remarks about the furniture, the crockery, and the tattered state of Warrington’s dressing-gown. Then he smoked a short pipe and recounted a fight between Tuffy and Long Biggings at school, greatly edifying the two hosts.

Lady Clavering was grateful—more so than the boy, who took everything as his due and likely had more sovereigns in his pocket than poor Pen, who generously gave him one from his own meagre supply.

The Major, with the sharp eyes of experience and the glasses of age, watched the boy closely. He didn’t pry, but he observed. As a neighbour, a friend, and a man of the world, he took it upon himself to learn about Lady Clavering’s finances, her capital, and what the children stood to inherit.

And soon, he had a clear picture. The daughter would get little. The son—well, the son would get everything. Sir Francis didn’t care for anyone. His wife adored him. His daughter disliked him. Simple as that.

“Ah, my dear madam,” the Major said, patting the boy’s head, “this boy may wear a baron’s coronet one day—if things are managed rightly. If Sir Francis would only play his cards well.”

Lady Clavering sighed deeply. “He plays too many of his cards already, Major. I’m afraid.”

The Major admitted he knew. He’d heard of Clavering’s unfortunate habit of gambling. He pitied her sincerely. But he spoke with such genuine sympathy that she confided in him—about her first marriage, her late husband, her regrets.

She told him how, as a child sent back to India after two years in Europe, she’d met Amory and foolishly married him. “Oh, you don’t know how miserable he made me,” she said. “Between him and my father, I lived in hell. Before I met him, I’d never seen a man except my father’s clerks and servants. We didn’t go into society in India—because of…” (“I know,” said the Major, bowing.) “I was a wild romantic child. My head was full of novels. He talked beautifully of calm nights at sea, and I thought he was the hero of my dreams. So I married him. And from that day, I was wretched. My father wasn’t a good man—neither to my mother nor to me. Amory was worse. A spendthrift, a drunkard, furious when drunk. Not a faithful husband. If he’d died in jail before the trial instead of after, he’d have saved me a lot of shame.”

“And my second husband?” she added. “I’m not happy in him. I’m not happy in my daughter. She’s not a good girl—though you wouldn’t know it. She’s cost me many tears. She sneers at me because I lack learning. How could I? I was raised among natives until twelve, then sent back to India at fourteen. Ah, Major, I should have been a good woman if I’d had a good husband. Now I must go upstairs and wipe my eyes—they’re red from crying. Lady Rockminster is coming, and we’re going for a drive in the Park.”

And when Lady Rockminster arrived, not a trace of tears remained. Lady Clavering bounced out with her usual blunders and liveliness, murdering the English language with gusto.

“Begad,” the Major thought to himself, “she’s not such a bad woman. Not refined, certainly—calls ‘Apollo’ ‘Apoller’—but she’s got heart. And a devilish amount of money. Three stars in India Stock. That young cub’s getting it all—is he?”

He wished a little of it might go to Miss Blanche. Better still, one of those stars in the name of Mr. Arthur Pendennis.

Still pursuing his schemes—whatever they might be—the old diplomat began talking to Miss Blanche in a kindly, fatherly way whenever he found her alone. He visited so often, became so familiar with the ladies, that they didn’t hesitate to argue in front of him. Lady Clavering, loud-tongued and quick-tempered, frequently quarrelled with her daughter in his presence.

Blanche’s wit usually won. Her arrows pierced her mother’s defenses, driving her away in defeat.

“I’m an old fellow,” the Major said. “I’ve nothing to do. I keep good counsel. I’m the friend of both of you. If you choose to quarrel before me, I won’t tell anyone. But you’re both good people. I intend to make peace between you. I’ve done it for others—husbands and wives, fathers and sons, daughters and mothers. I like it. I’ve nothing else to do.”

One day, he entered the drawing-room just as Lady Clavering stormed out, clearly furious. “She couldn’t speak to him now,” she muttered. “Too angry with that—that—little, wicked—” Anger choked the rest.

“My dear Miss Amory,” the Major said gently, entering. “I see what’s happened. You and your mother have disagreed. Mothers and daughters disagree even in the best families. Last week, I reconciled Lady Clapperton and her daughter Lady Claudia. Lady Lear hasn’t spoken to her eldest daughter in fourteen years. Kinder, more worthy people I’ve never known—except to each other. But they can’t live together. They shouldn’t. And I wish, my dear creature, with all my soul, that I could see you with your own establishment—no woman in London could manage one better. Your own home, your own happiness.”

“I’m not very happy here,” said the Sylphide. “And my mother’s stupidity is enough to provoke a saint.”

“Precisely,” said the Major. “You’re not suited. Your mother made one mistake—was it nature, my dear, in your case?—she shouldn’t have educated you. You were raised to be brilliant, refined, surrounded by fools. Your place is to lead, not follow. I’ve watched you, Miss Amory. You’re ambitious. Your sphere is command. You can’t shine here. I hope I’ll see you in another, happier home—mistress of it.”

Blanche shrugged. “Where is the Prince? Where is the palace, Major Pendennis? I’m ready. But there’s no romance left. No real affection.”

“No indeed,” said the Major, with a sentimental sigh.

“I don’t know anything about it,” Blanche confessed, looking down. “Only what I’ve read in novels.”

“Of course not,” said the Major. “How could you? And novels aren’t true. There’s no romance left in the world. Begad, I wish I were a young fellow like my nephew.”

“And what,” continued Miss Amory, musing, “what are the men we see at balls—dancing guardsmen, penniless clerks, bores? If I had my brother’s fortune, I might have the establishment you promise. But with my name and my little means—what am I to look to? A country parson? A barrister near Russell Square? A captain in dragoons who takes lodgings and comes home smelling of smoke like Sir Francis Clavering? That’s how we girls are destined to end life. O Major Pendennis, I’m sick of London, of balls, of young dandies with chin-tips, of great ladies who know us one day and cut us the next. I’d like to leave it all and go into a convent. I shall never find anyone who understands me. I live here as alone as if I were locked in a cell forever. I wish there were Sisters of Charity here. I’d join them, catch the plague, and die of it. I wish to quit the world. I’m not very old—but I’m tired. I’ve suffered. I’m disillusioned. I’m weary. O that the Angel of Death would come and beckon me away!”

Which, of course, was just a dramatic way of saying: *Lady Flamingo cut me last night. I couldn’t get an invitation to Lady Drum’s ball. Nobody’s asking me to dance. Dora, Flora, Leonora—all engaged. Nobody wants me.*

“You judge wisely,” the Major said kindly. “The Prince doesn’t marry nowadays unless the Princess has a doosid deal of money—or is of equal rank. The young ones marry within their class. If no fortune, they have each other’s shoulders to push forward. A girl with your fortune can’t expect a great match. But a girl with your genius, tact, and manners—*with a clever husband*—can make her mark anywhere. We’re grown doosid republican. Talent ranks with birth and wealth now, begad. A clever man with a clever wife can take any place they please.”
