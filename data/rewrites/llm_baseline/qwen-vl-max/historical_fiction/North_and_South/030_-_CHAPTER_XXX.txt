“The saddest birds a season find to sing.” SOUTHWELL.

“Never to fold the robe o’er secret pain, Never, weighed down by memory’s clouds again, To bow thy head! Thou art gone home!” MRS. HEMANS.

The next morning, Mrs. Thornton arrived at the Hale household. She found Mrs. Hale far worse than before—her condition having taken one of those sudden, unmistakable turns toward death that struck fear into even the closest of her family. In just twelve hours, her face had grown pale and sunken, as though life itself were slipping away with each breath. Mrs. Thornton, who had not seen her in weeks, was unprepared for such a sight. Though she had come only because her son had begged it as a personal favor—despite her deep-seated resentment toward the Hales—she was now confronted with something far greater than pride or prejudice.

She had always doubted the sincerity of Mrs. Hale’s illness, believing it no more than a passing whim, a temporary break from the rigid routine she expected of herself. She had told her son plainly that she regretted their ever coming near this place, that he should never have become acquainted with them, and that Latin and Greek—those dead tongues—had been invented for nothing but waste and folly. Her son had listened in silence, but when she finished her tirade, he simply repeated his request: she was to visit Mrs. Hale at the appointed hour, as it would be most convenient for the invalid.

Reluctantly, Mrs. Thornton agreed—though with every ounce of ill grace she could muster. Yet, even as she resented the act, she secretly admired her son for insisting on it. He seemed so determined to keep up the connection with the Hales, so steadfast in his kindness, that she began to view him with a new respect. And yet, she still saw Margaret Hale as an intruder, someone whose presence disturbed the order of things, someone whom she could not bring herself to like.

But none of these feelings remained when she stepped into Mrs. Hale’s room. There lay the woman—once vibrant, now still and fragile—like a mother, younger than herself, lying in a bed from which there was no return. The room was dimmed, quiet, broken only by faint whispers and long silences. Life had become monotonous, yet unbearable in its very stillness. Mrs. Thornton, strong and full of vitality, entered, and Mrs. Hale, though clearly aware of her presence, did not stir at first. Tears glistened on her lashes before she finally opened her eyes.

With a trembling hand, she reached out across the bedclothes, searching for contact—until her fingers brushed against Mrs. Thornton’s firm, large ones. Then, barely above a whisper, she spoke.

“Margaret—you have a daughter… my sister is in Italy. My child will be without a mother… in a strange land… if I die… will you…”

Her voice trailed off, her eyes fixed on Mrs. Thornton’s face with a desperate hopefulness. For a moment, Mrs. Thornton’s expression did not change—cold, rigid, unreadable. But then, something stirred within her. Not thoughts of her own children, nor even her son—but a sudden memory, sparked by the arrangement of the room: a little girl, long dead in infancy, buried beneath years of grief and silence. A flash of tenderness, unexpected and sharp, pierced through her icy reserve.

“You wish me to be a friend to Miss Hale,” said Mrs. Thornton, her voice measured, unyielding, though her heart trembled beneath the surface.

Mrs. Hale held her hand tighter, unable to speak. Mrs. Thornton sighed.

“I will be a true friend—if circumstances require it. Not a tender one. That I cannot be.” (She almost added “to her,” but stopped, seeing the anguish in the dying woman’s face.)

“It is not my nature to show affection, even where I feel it. Nor do I give advice lightly. Still, at your request—and if it brings you comfort—I promise.”

There was silence. Mrs. Thornton, too honest to make promises she couldn’t keep, knew that helping Margaret Hale—whom she disliked more than ever—would be difficult, perhaps impossible. Yet, duty called.

“I promise,” she said, gravely, “that in any difficulty Miss Hale may face, if she comes to me for help, I will assist her with all my power—as if she were my own daughter. I also promise that if I ever see her doing what I believe to be wrong—wrong not touching me or mine, so that my motives might be questioned—I will tell her plainly, honestly, as I would wish my own daughter to be told.”

A pause followed. Mrs. Hale felt the limits of the promise—its conditions, its coldness—but still, it was much. She was weak, dizzy, fading fast.

“I thank you,” she whispered. “I pray God to bless you. I shall never see you again in this world. But my last words are: I thank you for your promise to my child.”

“Not kindness,” Mrs. Thornton corrected, stiffly, refusing to soften even in farewell. But once the words were spoken, she was glad they hadn't been heard. She pressed the frail hand once more, rose, and left the house without speaking to a soul.

While Mrs. Thornton was with Mrs. Hale, Margaret and Dixon huddled together in quiet urgency, planning how to keep Frederick’s arrival a secret from everyone outside the house. A letter from him might arrive any day; he would follow swiftly after. They decided Martha must leave immediately for her holiday. Dixon would guard the front door strictly, allowing only essential visitors into Mr. Hale’s downstairs room—using Mrs. Hale’s illness as justification. If Mary Higgins was needed in the kitchen, she was to see and hear as little as possible. Frederick, if necessary, would be referred to as “Mr. Dickinson.” But Mary’s dullness and lack of curiosity made her the safest ally of all.

They resolved that Martha would leave that afternoon. Margaret wished she had gone the day before, fearing it might seem odd to give a servant time off while her mistress lay so ill.

Poor Margaret! All afternoon, she played the role of a dutiful daughter, drawing strength from her own dwindling reserves to support her father. Mr. Hale clung to hope between the fits of his wife’s suffering, believing each respite marked the beginning of recovery. But when the attacks returned—each more violent than the last—they shattered his courage anew. That afternoon, he sat in the drawing-room, unable to endure the solitude of his study. His head rested on folded arms upon the table, motionless.

Margaret watched him, her heart breaking. She wanted to comfort him, but dared not speak unless he asked. Martha had gone. Dixon stayed with Mrs. Hale during her sleep. The house was silent, darkening slowly, with no one to fetch candles.

Margaret sat by the window, staring at the street lamps, but seeing nothing—only hearing her father’s heavy sighs. She hesitated to go downstairs for lights, afraid that leaving him alone might let his grief spill over unchecked. But just as she thought of checking the kitchen fire—no one else could do it—she heard the doorbell ring, sharply muffled, jingling through the house despite its quiet tone.

She jumped up, passed her father—still unmoving—then turned back, kissed him gently. He did not react.

She went down quietly through the darkness. Dixon would have chained the door, but Margaret didn’t think of danger. A tall man stood between her and the lighted street. He turned at the latch.

“Is this Mr. Hale’s?” he asked, his voice clear, full, and oddly delicate.

Margaret trembled. At first, she said nothing. Then, in a breath:

“Frederick!” she gasped, reaching out both hands to pull him inside.

“Oh, Margaret!” he said, holding her shoulders after their embrace, as if even in the dark he could read her face. “My mother—is she alive?”

“Yes, she is alive, dear brother—alive, though as ill as she can be. Alive!”

“Thank God,” he breathed.

“Papa is crushed by this sorrow.”

“You expect me?”

“No—we’ve had no letter.”

“Then I came before it. But my mother knows I’m coming.”

“Oh—we all knew you’d come. But wait—come in. Give me your hand. What’s this? Oh—your carpet bag. Dixon has shut the shutters. This is papa’s study. I’ll take you to a chair. Just a moment—let me tell him.”

She fumbled for the taper and matches. When the small flame flickered to life, she felt suddenly shy. She saw only that her brother’s face was darker than usual, and caught the glint of long blue eyes, suddenly amused, as if both were silently inspecting each other. Their glance held a moment of understanding—no words needed. Margaret already loved him, and now sensed she would like him as a companion too. Her heart lifted. The sorrow remained, but it no longer weighed so heavily.

She ran upstairs, her spirits soaring. Her father lay prostrate, helpless. But now she had a weapon to rouse him—her brother’s presence.

“Papa,” she said, throwing her arms around his neck, lifting his head gently until it rested in hers. “Guess who’s here?”

He looked at her. A flicker of recognition passed through his eyes—then vanished, dismissed as fantasy.

He buried his face again in his arms. “I don’t know. Don’t tell me it’s Frederick—not Frederick. I can’t bear it. I’m too weak. And his mother is dying!”

He wept, childlike, broken. Margaret froze, disappointed. Then she spoke again—softer, gentler.

“Papa, it is Frederick. Think of mamma—how glad she’ll be! And for her sake, how glad we ought to be! For his sake, too—our poor, poor boy!”

Her father didn’t move, but he seemed to listen.

“Where is he?” he whispered at last.

“In your study. Alone. I lit the taper and came to tell you. He’s alone, and wondering why—”

“I’ll go to him,” he interrupted, rising slowly, leaning on her arm.

Margaret led him to the study door, but her nerves overwhelmed her. She couldn’t bear to witness the meeting. She turned away, ran upstairs, and wept freely—the first time in days. The strain had been immense. But Frederick was here. He was safe. Among them again.

She wiped her tears, opened her bedroom door. No sound. Almost afraid she had dreamed. She went downstairs, listened at the study door—heard voices. Enough. She went to the kitchen, stoked the fire, lit the house, prepared food for the traveler. How fortunate that her mother slept—she knew it from the candle-lighter slipped through her bedroom keyhole. The reunion could unfold quietly, the shock absorbed before the dying woman woke.

When all was ready, Margaret entered the study, tray in hand, like a servant. She was proud to serve him. But Frederick sprang up, took the tray from her. It was a sign—of relief, of care, of everything to come.

Together, they set the table. Few words, but hands touched, eyes met—silent language of blood and kinship. The fire had died. Margaret tried to relight it, though the evenings were chilly. Yet noise must be kept distant from Mrs. Hale’s room.

“Dixon says lighting fires is a gift, not an art,” she murmured.

“Poeta nascitur, non fit,” Mr. Hale quoted faintly. Margaret smiled—glad to hear a familiar phrase again.

“Dear old Dixon! We’ll kiss her properly,” Frederick laughed. “She used to kiss me, check my face, then start again! But Margaret—what clumsy hands you have! Never saw such awkwardness. Run and wash them. I’ll manage the fire. Lighting fires is one of my natural talents.”

So Margaret went—returned—moved about, restless, joyful. The more Frederick needed, the happier she was. He understood her instinctively. It was a joy stolen from mourning, made sharper by the knowledge of the sorrow ahead.

Then came the sound of footsteps—Dixon ascending the stairs. Mr. Hale jerked upright from his chair, watching his children as if they were actors in a play not meant for him. He stood, facing the door, his face suddenly tense—fear flashing across it. Margaret shivered. It reminded her of the new dangers they faced. She gripped Frederick’s arm tightly, brow furrowed, teeth clenched. Yet it was only Dixon’s steady tread. She heard her walk to the kitchen.

Margaret rose.

“I’ll go to her. Tell her. Ask how mamma is.” Mrs. Hale was awake. She wandered at first, but tea revived her slightly. She was not talkative. Better to wait until morning to tell her of Frederick’s return. Dr. Donaldson’s visit would bring enough emotion. He could advise how to prepare her.

Margaret could not sit still. She helped Dixon with preparations for “Master Frederick.” She felt invincible. Each glimpse of Frederick talking with her father filled her with strength. She studied him—he had delicate features, softened by a swarthy complexion and quick, intense expressions. His eyes often sparkled with humor, but sometimes flashed with sudden passion—something fierce, fleeting, like the look of a wild countryman. It startled her, but not with fear. It was not anger—it was impulse, raw energy, melting back into gentleness. She trusted him instantly.

Their conversation flowed naturally. He remembered Helstone, the New Forest, their childhood. He spoke of her as if she had never changed. She could talk freely, without fear of boring him. She had feared him before—afraid he’d be unrecognizable after years abroad. But he was kind, thoughtful, sensitive to their parents’ pain. He knew when to be bright, when to be quiet. When the mood was wrong, he became a patient nurse. And when he spoke of old times, Margaret nearly wept—his memories were vivid, warm, real.

For a few hours, Mrs. Hale rallied. She held Frederick’s hand, refused to let go—even in sleep. Margaret fed him like a child, so he wouldn’t disturb her mother. Once, Mrs. Hale woke, turned her head slowly, smiled at her children.

“I am very selfish,” she whispered. “But it won’t last long.”

Frederick bent down, kissed her hand.

That peace could not last, Dr. Donaldson warned Margaret. After he left, she slipped down to Frederick, who had been ordered to stay hidden in the back parlour—now his room.

She told him what the doctor said.

“I don’t believe it,” Frederick declared. “She’s ill—very ill, perhaps dangerously. But she wouldn’t be like this if she were dying. Margaret—she needs another doctor. A London physician. Have you never thought of that?”

“Yes,” Margaret replied. “More than once. But I don’t think it would help. And we can’t afford a great surgeon from London. Dr. Donaldson is second only to the best—perhaps even equal.”

Frederick paced the room, agitated.

“I have credit in Cadiz,” he said. “But none here—because of this wretched name change. Why did Father leave Helstone? That was the blunder.”

“It wasn’t a blunder,” Margaret said grimly. “And above all, don’t let Papa hear anything like that. You don’t know how he torments himself—believing mamma would never have fallen ill if we’d stayed at Helstone. He blames himself terribly.”

Frederick stopped pacing, stood directly before her, looking at her drooping form.

“My little Margaret,” he said, caressing her. “Let us hope as long as we can. Poor little woman—look at you, all wet with tears. I’ll hope. I’ll hope against a thousand doctors. Be brave, Margaret. Hope!”

Margaret choked, her voice low.

“I must try to trust. Oh, Frederick—mamma was beginning to love me so. And I was beginning to understand her. Now death comes to tear us apart.”

“Come, come,” he said. “Let’s go upstairs. Do something—don’t waste precious time. Thinking has made me sad many times, darling. But doing? Never. My rule is a parody of ‘Get money, my son, honestly if you can; but get money.’ Mine is: ‘Do something, my sister—do good if you can; but at any rate, do something.’”

“Not excluding mischief,” Margaret whispered, smiling through her tears.
