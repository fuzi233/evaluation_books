The candlelight flickered against the dust motes dancing in the air of the Hale parlor, casting long, wavering shadows that seemed to stretch toward the door where Mr. Thornton stood waiting. The atmosphere was thick with the unspoken tension of a storm held at bay, a pressure that made the very breath feel heavier. Margaret had not anticipated the joy of her godfather's arrival, viewing it merely as a courtesy to her father, yet the moment Mr. Bell stepped inside, the rigid formalities of the household dissolved. He claimed her with a familiarity that bordered on possessive, declaring her innate ability to command his affection an hereditary trait, a power she wielded effortlessly. In return, she praised his youthful vigor beneath his academic robes, a compliment he accepted with a grin that belied the weariness etched into his features.

"You are fresh and young in warmth and kindness," Mr. Bell declared, though his tone shifted instantly to a mock severity. "Though I must confess, your opinions are the oldest and mustiest I have encountered in this long journey."

"Hear this daughter of yours, Hale!" Mr. Bell continued, his voice rising in theatrical outrage. "Her residence in Milton has utterly corrupted her soul. She is a democrat, a red republican, a member of the Peace Society, a socialist—"

"Papa," Margaret interjected, her voice steady despite the turmoil brewing within her, "it is simply because I advocate for the progress of commerce. Mr. Bell would have preferred the trade remain stagnant, bartering wild-beast skins for acorns."

"No, no," Mr. Hale countered, shaking his head with a weary sigh. "I would till the earth and grow potatoes. I would shear the beast and weave broadcloth. Do not exaggerate, missy. But I am exhausted by this ceaseless bustle. Everyone rushing over one another, driven by a frantic hunger to accumulate wealth."

"It is not everyone who can recline comfortably in college rooms and watch their fortunes swell without lifting a finger," Mr. Hale observed sharply. "Surely, many here would envy your property increasing without their own toil."

"I do not believe they would," Mr. Bell retorted, his eyes gleaming with a cynical amusement. "It is the struggle, the very chaos of competition they crave. To sit still, to learn from the dead past, or to shape the future through faithful, prophetic labor? Why, pooh! I know not a single man in Milton capable of such stillness; it is a high art indeed."

"Miltonians," Mr. Hale mused, "likely believe Oxford scholars lack the capacity for motion. It would be a boon if the two worlds mingled more freely."

"It might benefit the Miltoners," Mr. Bell conceded, "though many things beneficial to them would be profoundly disagreeable to others."

"Are you not a Milton man yourself?" Margaret asked, tilting her head. "One would assume pride in one's hometown is a given."

"I confess, I see little cause for pride," Mr. Bell admitted, his expression darkening. "If you will come to Oxford, Margaret, I will show you a place worthy of glory."

"Well!" Mr. Hale interrupted, sensing the shift in the current. "Mr. Thornton arrives tonight for tea, and he is as proud of Milton as you are of Oxford. You two must endeavor to broaden each other's minds."

"I have no desire to become more liberal-minded, thank you," Mr. Bell replied dryly.

"Is Mr. Thornton coming to tea, Papa?" Margaret whispered, her voice barely audible.

"To tea, or shortly thereafter. He was uncertain. He instructed us not to wait."

In the adjacent study, Mr. Thornton wrestled with a conflict that tore at his composure. He had resolved not to ask his mother about her attempts to counsel Margaret regarding her conduct, fearing that her filtered account would only deepen his anguish. He knew she would color the narrative, twisting his actions through the lens of her own anxieties. He recoiled from the mere mention of Margaret's name, torn between blame and a jealousy that burned hotter than reason allowed. While he condemned her, while he cherished her in spite of himself, he dreamed of her approaching with outstretched arms, a vision of lightness and gaiety that simultaneously repelled and drew him in. This phantom image, stripped of her actual character as if possessed by a malevolent spirit, haunted his waking hours, blurring the line between the virtuous Una and the treacherous Duessa. His disdain for the latter inevitably marred his perception of the former. Yet, pride forbade him from acknowledging his vulnerability by fleeing her presence. He neither sought nor avoided her, lingering unnecessarily over trivial business matters to prove his self-mastery, forcing his movements into unnatural slowness until the clock struck eight.

Upon arriving at the Hales', he found Mr. Bell still seated by the fire, engaging in tedious discourse long after business concluded. Mr. Thornton remained silent, chafing under the weight of the conversation, while Mr. Bell secretly resented Thornton's brusqueness, judging him lacking in both intellect and manners. Finally, a sound from the floor above prompted them to ascend. They discovered Margaret engrossed in a letter, animatedly discussing its contents with her father. Upon the gentlemen's entry, she hastily concealed the paper, but Mr. Thornton's keen senses caught fragments of Mr. Hale's words directed at Mr. Bell.

"A letter from Henry Lennox. It fills Margaret with hope."

Mr. Bell nodded. When Mr. Thornton's gaze swept over Margaret, she flushed crimson. He felt an overwhelming urge to rise and depart, never to return to the house.

"We were reflecting," Mr. Hale said smoothly, bridging the gap, "that you and Mr. Thornton have followed Margaret's advice, attempting to convert one another during your prolonged stay in the study."

"And you believed we had reduced each other to mere opinions, akin to the Kilkenny cats' tails. Whose opinion, pray, do you think possesses the most obstinate vitality?"

Mr. Thornton feigned ignorance, dismissing the query with disdain. Mr. Hale, polite as ever, clarified.

"Mr. Thornton, we accused Mr. Bell this morning of an Oxonian medieval bigotry toward his native town. We—Margaret, I believe—suggested it would benefit him to associate more closely with Milton's manufacturers."

"I beg your pardon," Mr. Bell corrected, his tone sharpening. "Margaret thought it would benefit the Milton manufacturers to associate more with Oxford men. Was that not so, Margaret?"

"I believe I thought mutual acquaintance would benefit both sides; I did not realize it was my specific idea, any more than my father's."

"So you see, Mr. Thornton," Mr. Bell pressed, "we should have been improving each other downstairs rather than debating vanished families of Smiths and Harrisons. Nevertheless, I am willing to contribute my share now. I wonder when you Miltonians intend to truly live. Your entire existence seems devoted to gathering the raw materials for life."

"By 'living,' do you mean enjoyment?"

"Yes, enjoyment—I do not specify the type, for I trust we both recognize mere pleasure as a poor substitute for true fulfillment."

"I would prefer the nature of this enjoyment be defined."

"Well! Enjoyment or leisure—the enjoyment derived from the power and influence money affords. You all strive for money. What is its purpose?"

Mr. Thornton fell silent. Then, softly, "I truly do not know. But money is not what *I* strive for."

"What then?"

"It is a domestic matter. I shall have to submit to such a catechism, and I am unsure I am prepared."

"No!" Mr. Hale interjected quickly. "Let us not make this personal. Neither of you represents your respective groups; you are too individual for that."

"I am uncertain whether to accept that as a compliment. I aspire to represent Oxford, with its beauty, learning, and proud history. What say you, Margaret? Should I be flattered?"

"I know little of Oxford. But there is a distinction between representing a city and representing its inhabitants."

"True enough, Miss Margaret. Now I recall, you opposed me this morning, adopting a distinctly Miltonian and manufacturing stance."

Margaret sensed the startled glance Mr. Thornton cast her and bristled at the potential misinterpretation of Mr. Bell's words. Mr. Bell continued, oblivious to the underlying tension.

"Oh! I wish I could show you our High Street, our Radcliffe Square. I omit our colleges, just as I grant Mr. Thornton license to exclude his factories when describing Milton's charms. I have a right to critique my birthplace. Remember, I am a Milton man."

Mr. Thornton felt a surge of irritation disproportionate to the provocation. He was not in a mood for jest. Had the circumstances been different, he might have relished Mr. Bell's half-teasing condemnation of a town whose life clashed so violently with his habits. But now, he was goaded into defending a position never intended to be seriously challenged.

"I do not set up Milton as a model town."

"Not in architecture?" Mr. Bell asked slyly.

"No! We have been too preoccupied to attend to mere outward appearances."

"Do not say *mere* outward appearances," Mr. Hale urged gently. "They imprint themselves upon us from childhood onward, every day of our lives."
