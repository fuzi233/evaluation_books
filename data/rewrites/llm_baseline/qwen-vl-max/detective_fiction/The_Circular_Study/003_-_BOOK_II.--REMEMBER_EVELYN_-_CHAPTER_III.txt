The man who had just reenacted the scene of violence—his movements eerily precise, his expression contorted with grief—rose slowly from the floor. His face was blank now, eyes glazed, as though the intensity of his performance had drained him. He bent to pick up the glass he’d dropped, cradling it carefully in his hands as if it were fragile evidence. But before he could step away, Mr. Gryce’s voice cut through the silence.

“Wait,” he said sharply. “You’re not taking that glass anywhere. We need to know how your master ended up dead.”

It was a command meant to shake anyone out of their stupor. Yet this man didn’t flinch. He kept walking, moving past Styles, who lunged forward and grabbed his shoulder.

“Stop!” Styles snapped. “What do you think you’re doing? You heard him. Answer him!”

This time, the man froze. The glass slipped from his grasp, shattering on the hardwood with a sharp *crack*. At the same moment, a sound escaped him—not quite a scream, not quite a sob—but something between a moan and a gasp. He’d seen them now—the two men standing firm, their eyes locked on him—and his posture betrayed fear. His shoulders hunched, his breath came in ragged bursts.

He tried to bolt. Styles held fast, dragging him back toward the center of the room, where Mr. Gryce stood beside the bearskin rug, beneath which lay the still form of the deceased master.

And then, at the sight of the body, another transformation took place. A flicker of something dark lit his eyes—joy, cold and terrible. It twisted his lips into a grin, and from deep within his chest rose a low, chilling laugh. It echoed off the high walls, unnatural and grotesque.

Mr. Gryce recoiled slightly, though he remained motionless. Disgust warred with curiosity. This man wasn’t sane. Not fully. And yet, he was clearly connected to the crime. With an icy stare, Gryce fixed the servant with his gaze—eyes so rarely met by others, now unflinching.

“Now,” he said, voice hard. “Speak. Who killed him? You were here. You must know.”

The servant’s mouth opened. But no words came. Instead, guttural sounds tumbled out—strange, rhythmic syllables, like a language without meaning. Then, with one free hand (the other still gripped by Styles), he touched his ears, then his lips, and shook his head violently.

The truth hit them all at once: he was deaf and dumb.

Styles staggered back. The revelation overwhelmed him. He released the man, who stepped away slowly, returning to his corner. There, he began again—this time with even greater vigor—reenacting the murder and the mourning. He struck the air, fell to his knees, raised his arms in sorrow. It was a ritual, repeated with mechanical precision.

When he finished, he stood rigid, eyes darting around the room. But now, there was no pity in them—only a glint of something darker, something almost cruel. His presence felt like an intrusion, a blasphemy in the quiet horror of death.

Styles couldn’t bear it any longer. “Can’t you speak?” he roared. “Can’t you hear?”

The mute smiled—slowly, deliberately—his expression one of mockery, of satisfaction. It chilled Gryce to the bone.

“Take him away,” Gryce ordered, cutting through the tension. “Search him thoroughly. Look for blood, for traces. I’m going upstairs—to the room where you first saw him. He’s too close to this to be innocent. He must carry something with him.”

But the search yielded nothing. No bloodstains. No fingerprints. No sign of struggle. The rooms above revealed no clues linking the servant directly to the crime.

Gryce grew thoughtful. He returned to the study, scanning every inch of the space, every object, every shadow. Nothing new emerged. His mind wandered back to the lifeless face on the rug—mouth open, eyes wide, frozen in agony and resolve.

Then, from above—a cry.

Not human. But clear, piercing, deliberate.

“Remember Evelyn!”

It echoed down from the rafters, startling in its clarity. Who was Evelyn? And why did this voice—coming from nowhere—insist upon her name?

Gryce looked up. High near the top of one window, suspended in a cage, sat an English starling. Its neck stretched upward, its beak open wide. It shrieked again:

“Remember Evelyn!”

The bird seemed aware of the attention it commanded. It tilted its head, stared down, and cried once more.

It was the final surreal note in a night of impossible occurrences. Gryce leaned forward, drawn to the creature, when suddenly the light changed—white to green—without warning. He realized, with annoyance, that he’d pressed a button on the strange lamp overhead. These shifting colors were distracting, trivial. But they wouldn’t wait.

He stepped back from the table, preparing to leave. Then came Styles’s voice from the antechamber.

“Shall I let him go, Mr. Gryce? He seems agitated—not dangerous, but restless. Like he’s forgotten something. Or remembered a duty he hasn’t fulfilled.”

“Yes,” Gryce replied without hesitation. “Let him go. But watch him. Every move he makes matters. He might be giving us clues without knowing it.”

He moved toward the door, ready to observe.

At that moment, the starling called again—“Remember Evelyn!”—as Gryce passed the curtains.

He paused. Turned. Looked up.

To whom was this plea directed? To him? Or to the man lying dead below, whose ears would never hear again?

It could be meaningless. Or it could be the key to everything.

But there was no time to ponder. The old servant approached—returning from the front hall, where he’d vanished after being released. In one hand, he carried the master’s hat. In the other, his umbrella.

Gryce stopped. Watched.

The mute bowed slightly, handed over the items, and retreated silently to his seat in the antechamber. Without a word, he sank into himself, drifting once more into that vacant, dazed state.

Gryce replaced the hat and umbrella on the rack. Then he muttered under his breath:

“Humph. Mind gone. Memory unreliable. Testimony useless.”

He frowned. Was the man broken by what he’d witnessed—or had he already been broken long before? Had madness come first, or had it followed the crime?

He might have found the answer—if not for the sudden interruption.

The front door bell, ringing erratically, was silenced by a sharp, authoritative knock.

Someone important had arrived.

The coroner. Or worse.

The golden hour was over.
