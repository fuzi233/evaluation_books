"Eh? Wot I say? I speak truth when I say that Buck has two devils!" François declared the following morning, his voice rough with shock. He pointed to Buck, who stood shivering beside the fire, his coat a map of fresh, bleeding wounds. "That Spitz fought like hell," added Perrault, inspecting the deep gashes that marred Buck's flank. "And that Buck fought like two hells," François countered, a grim satisfaction lighting his face. "Now we make good time. No more Spitz, no more trouble."

As Perrault packed the gear and loaded the sled, the driver began to harness the team. Buck trotted instinctively to the vacant spot at the head, where Spitz had ruled. François, however, ignored Buck and secured Sol-leks, the remaining senior dog, to the lead. In François's view, Sol-leks was the finest leader left. Instantly, Buck sprang upon Sol-leks in a frenzy, driving him backward and seizing the coveted position.

"Eh? Eh?" François cried, slapping his thighs in delight. "Look at that Buck! He kills Spitz, he thinks to take the job."

"Go 'way, Chook!" François barked, grabbing Buck by the scruff of the neck. Though the giant dog growled with a throaty, threatening rumble, François dragged him aside and shoved Sol-leks back into command. The older dog, clearly trembling with fear, accepted the burden, but Buck refused to yield. François was stubborn; he attempted to force Buck down again, but the big hound merely circled just beyond the range of François's heavy club, snarling with bitter resentment. Now wise in the ways of man's weapons, Buck watched the club warily, ready to dodge any swing, yet he showed no sign of fleeing. He wanted the leadership; it was his by right, earned through blood and battle, and he would not settle for anything less.

Perrault joined the fray. Together, they herded Buck around the camp for nearly an hour, hurling clubs at him and cursing him and his lineage down to the remotest generation. Buck dodged every blow, answering their abuse with sharp snarls, refusing to run away but instead circling the perimeter, signaling plainly that when his wish was granted, he would return and be good.

Finally, François sat down, scratching his head in frustration. Perrault checked his watch and swore; time was slipping away, and they should have been on the trail hours ago. Seeing they were beaten, François approached Sol-leks, called to Buck, and laughed. Buck responded with a dog's chuckle but kept his distance. François unfastened Sol-leks and placed him back in the rear. The team stood harnessed in a solid line, ready for the trail, with no room for anyone else but Buck at the front. Once more François called, and once more Buck laughed and stayed away.

"T'row down de club," Perrault commanded.

François obeyed. Immediately, Buck trotted forward, laughing triumphantly, and swung into position at the head of the team. With his traces fastened, the sled was broken out, and with both men running, they dashed onto the frozen river trail.

Though François had highly valued Buck, he soon realized he had undervalued him. From the first bound, Buck assumed the duties of leadership with authority. Where judgment and quick action were needed, he proved himself superior even to Spitz. But it was in enforcing order and making his mates adhere to the law that Buck truly excelled. Dave and Sol-leks, the good-natured leaders, did not mind the shift; their business was toil, and so long as that continued without interference, they cared little for who led. However, the rest of the team, having grown unruly under Spitz, were swiftly licked into shape by Buck. Pike, who habitually loafed at Buck's heels, was shaken repeatedly for laziness until he pulled harder than ever before. That same night, Joe, the sour dog, was punished soundly—a feat Spitz had never achieved. Buck simply smothered him with superior weight, cutting him until he ceased snapping and began to whine for mercy.

The team's spirit lifted instantly, regaining its old-time solidarity. As they raced toward the Rink Rapids, two native huskies, Teek and Koona, were added, and Buck broke them in with astonishing speed. "Never saw such a dog as dat Buck!" François exclaimed, marveling at the animal's worth. "He's worth one thousand dollars, by Gar! What do you say, Perrault?" Perrault nodded. They were setting records daily. The trail was packed hard, the snow dry, and the temperature held steady at fifty degrees below zero. For fourteen straight days, averaging forty miles a day, they covered seventy miles of lakes—Marsh, Tagish, and Bennett—in a single rush, with men towing behind the sled at the end of ropes. On the final night of the second week, they topped White Pass and descended the sea slope, the lights of Skaguay and the shipping fleet glowing beneath them.

It was a record-breaking run. Back in Skaguay, the men were feted with drinks, and the team became the center of a worshipful crowd. Yet, within weeks, official orders arrived, and a Scotch half-breed took charge. The journey reversed, heading back over the weary trail to Dawson. This was no longer a race; it was heavy toil, dragging a massive mail train carrying words from the world to the gold seekers under the shadow of the Pole.

Buck did not enjoy the monotony—the machine-like regularity of waking, cooking, eating, breaking camp, and traveling—but he bore it well, taking pride in his work like Dave and Sol-leks. He ensured his mates did their fair share. By day, he maintained discipline, settling fierce fights with three swift blows to establish his mastery. By night, he loved to lie near the fire, hind legs crouched, forelegs stretched, watching the flames dreamily. Occasionally, the warmth of the fire transported him back to Judge Miller's sun-kissed estate in California, to the swimming tank and his friends Ysabel and Toots. But far more potent were the memories of the wild: the man in the red sweater, the death of Curly, the great battle with Spitz. These primal echoes stirred his blood, reviving ancient instincts that had lapsed in civilized days. Sometimes, staring into the fire, he seemed to see another world entirely. He imagined a man shorter of limb, longer of arm, with knotted muscles and matted hair, squatting by a different fire, clutching a stone-tipped stick, terrified of the dark. Around that imaginary fire, he heard the crashing of great beasts, the glint of eyes in pairs, and the haunting sounds of the wild that rose the hair on his back. Then the vision vanished, the half-breed cook shouting, "Hey, you Buck, wake up!" and reality returned.

The hardship wore them down. Short of weight and in poor condition, they reached Dawson, only to drop back down the Yukon bank loaded with mail, facing relentless snow and soft trails. Every night, the dogs were tended before the men ate, their strength sapped by eighteen hundred miles of dragging sleds. Buck stood firm, keeping discipline despite his exhaustion, but others faltered. Billee whimpered in his sleep; Joe grew sourer; Sol-leks became unapproachable.

Yet it was Dave who suffered most. Something was wrong inside him. He became morose and irritable, retreating into his nest the moment camp was pitched, refusing to rise until harness-up time. When jerked by a sudden stop, he cried out in pain. The drivers consulted, pressing and prodding him, finding no broken bones but sensing a deep, internal ailment. By Cassiar Bar, he was so weak he fell repeatedly in the traces.

The Scotch half-breed called a halt, removing Dave from the team to let him run free behind the sled. Sick unto death, Dave resented this deeply. As the traces were unfastened, he grunted and growled, whimpering broken-heartedly as Sol-leks took his place. Pride of trace and trail was his, and he could not bear another dog to do his work. When the sled started, he floundered in the soft snow alongside the beaten path, attacking Sol-leks with his teeth, rushing against him, striving to leap inside the traces and reclaim his station. He whined and yelped with grief and pain, ignoring the stinging lash of the whip. Exhausted, he finally fell, lying where he dropped, howling lugubriously as the long train churned by.

With his last remnant of strength, he staggered behind the trains until the next stop, where he floundered past the others to his own sled. Standing alongside Sol-leks, he pleaded with his eyes to remain. The driver, perplexed, listened to the old tales of dogs dying when cut from the traces, hearts breaking over the denial of their purpose. Moved by compassion, the driver harnessed Dave in again. Proudly, he pulled as of old, though he cried out involuntarily from the bite of his inner wound. Several times he fell and was dragged, and once the sled ran over him, leaving a limp in one hind leg.
