Margaret slipped back to Rotterdam long before Gerard roused from the deep, healing sleep that followed his rescue, and left their little boy behind at the Gouda parsonage. No sooner was she home than she dispatched Reicht, that stout, loyal soul she trusted with every secret, straight to Gouda, bearing the plain vicar’s grey frock and wide soft felt hat she had prepared, with word-for-word instructions on how to care for her new master.

Next she sought out Jorian Ketel, the closest-mouthed man she had ever met, the only one in Rotterdam she could count on to keep silent, and the pair hatched two sly, unspoken schemes she would carry to her grave. I shall not name them here: if a woman as frank and good as Margaret kept them hidden till her dying breath, I, a mere man, *verbum non amplius addam* — no more words will I add.

For days she refused to step foot near the Gouda parsonage. The sharp words exchanged in the heat of their last argument had rankled long after the emotion of the moment faded, and she burned at the thought that Gerard could ever doubt she would see the priest in him no less than the man she had loved since girlhood. “What did he take me for?” she had wondered, stung, and her innocent pride tangled with modesty to whisper that she ought not go back uninvited. She had left little Gerard there on purpose, after all: not to win his father over with her own words, but to let the boy’s bright, uncomplicated love anchor the man forever to the living world, far from the cold darkness of his hermit’s cave.

But the separation cost her more than she had ever guessed. She had not spent a single hour apart from her son in his whole young life, and the empty quiet of her home grew unbearable by the day. By the third night, she crept through the dark all the way to the parsonage, skulking around the premises like a thief until she spotted Reicht alone in the kitchen. She tapped softly on the window pane, and begged her, for pity’s sake, to slip the boy out to her unseen. Reicht soon found an errand to take him out into the lane, and mother and child clung to each other in giddy, tearful joy before they had to part again. They struck a deal there and then: every afternoon, at the same set hour, Reicht would walk little Gerard halfway to Rotterdam, and Margaret would meet them. After the wild, gambolling play — the pair tumbling and laughing like a young cat and her first kitten — the boy would sometimes wander off to amuse himself at their feet, and the two women would huddle close to plot over poor Luke Peterson.

“Reicht,” Margaret murmured once, “I as good as promised him I would wed Luke. Told him all he had to do was say the word.”
“Poor Luke,” Reicht sighed at once.
“Why poor Luke?”
“To be tossed back and forth between yes and no like that, no mind of his own in the matter.”
“Wait — you haven’t gone and fallen soft on the boy yourself, that you take his part?” Margaret teased.
“Me?” Reicht tossed her head so hard her cap slid askew.
“Pardon me, then. You can do me a good turn, all the same.”
“Whisht! Hush! That little darling’s staring at us, eyes big as saucers, listening to every word.”
Both women ducked their heads under the same wide wool cap to muffle their voices, two quiet schemers conspiring against one unsuspecting boy.

When these secret, stolen meetings had run five days, Margaret began to chafe at the injustice of hiding like a criminal, and was sobbing over the loneliness of it when a rickety cart rumbled up to her front door. There sat Gerard, clean as a newly minted penny, his beard freshly shaved, his pale hands soft and white as snow, a faint healthy flush back in his cheeks, clad in exactly the grey frock and broad felt hat she had sent him.

She ran straight upstairs, scrubbed the traces of tears from her face, and swapped her worn everyday cap for a crisp one she had only just pulled fresh from the drawer before hurrying back down to him. He seized both her hands and kissed them, and a hot tear fell upon her knuckles. She turned her head away fast to hide the tears that had sprung to her own eyes.
“Sweet Margaret,” he said, soft, “why do you hold aloof from the good deed you yourself did? We have waited for you at the parsonage every single day, and no Margaret came.”
“You said things,” she told him, stiff, not looking at him.
“What? Things my mad hermit self babbled, back when I was nothing but a stubborn donkey?”
“Aye, but you said them all the same. And you had no cause to think so little of me.”
“Forget every word I ranted that day. No one heeds the ravings of a maniac, and I know now I was only weeks from turning into a gibbering idiot, locked alone in that cave. Not a soul on earth but you could have dragged me away. What wit, what kindness you lavished on me! I cannot thank you properly here in this street. No — I will thank you properly in the parsonage you have given me, among my flock, the people you made me shepherd. I love them already, and there I will call you what you are: the truest friend a man ever had. So now I say to you what you once said to me: come to Gouda manse.”
“Humph. We shall see about that,” she said, pretending to be cool and unhurried, though every inch of her burned to go at once.
“Margaret, do you think I would have kept our dear boy from you all these days if I had not been certain you would come back to him any hour? He has curled right around my heartstrings, but what claim have I on him next to yours? Confess now — you have thought hard, unkind thoughts of me for keeping him from you.”
“Nay, nay, never,” she said, softening. “Ah, you are yourself again, you who always thought of everyone before yourself. I have half a mind to come back to Gouda with you, just for saying that.”
“Come then with half a mind — that is worth more than the whole mind of any other man alive.”
“Well, I dare say I will. But there is no such mighty rush,” she said, forcing her face into a neutral line. “Tell me first how you get on with your new parishioners.”
“Why, my poor folk have already taken root in my heart,” he told her, smiling. “Simple, rough, a touch superstitious, but the kindest souls alive.”
“I knew you would find a grain of good in even a bushel of trouble,” she said.
“Whisht, hush — and Margaret, two men of the parish who feuded for four years straight came to the parsonage each trying to win me over to their side. But praise God, I won them over to each other instead, and now they laugh at their own old foolish quarrel.”
“But are you truly their vicar?” she pressed him. “Answer me that.”
“Certes! Have I not gone before the bishop, taken my oaths, rung the church bell, and touched the altar, the missal, and the holy chalice right there before the churchwardens? They handed me the parish seal, look, here it is tucked in my pocket. A real, duly sworn vicar, inviting his dearest friend home to Gouda manse.”
“Then my mind is at ease. Tell me everything else.”
“Well, sweet one, the soul dearest to me in all the parish is a poor cripple my guardian angel — that is you, you know, I saw you turn your head away — put right under my roof. Sybrandt and I are closer than brothers now, we forgave each other long ago. It would make you glad, and sad too, to hear how we wept and hugged. He speaks of nothing but your goodness, and is more pious and content now in his pain than he ever was in the days he was whole and strong. He will never leave my house, I swear, until he goes straight to heaven.”
“Tell me something that happened to you, not to everyone else,” she said, laughing. “I do not need to be told what a good man Sybrandt is, I have known you since we were children.”
“Let me think. At first the sun dazzled me so bad I could barely step outside, like some silly old owl just pulled from its tree — but that is all past now. And good Reicht Heynes — humph.”
“What of her?”
“Nothing but this, that she is a diamond of a woman. Her voice cuts through me like a knife, still, after all those months of silence, every voice but yours sounds harsh and loud. Oh, and I have news you will smile at. I spoke yesterday with an old man in the parish who fancies himself a terrible ill-tempered fellow, and works hard to play the part for everyone else — but his gruff act is threadbare, and the softest, kindest heart beats under it.”
“Sounds like the whole parish is full of angels,” Margaret said, dryly ironic.
“Then why do you stay away from them?” Gerard retorted. “He told me no parish in all Holland is more plagued by the devil than Gouda. And he gave me this example: ‘We had the holiest hermit in all the country, right here — but Gouda being Gouda, the devil came for him this week, bag and baggage. Not a scrap of him left but a patch of skin as prickly as a hedgehog, and a scrap of old rusted iron.’”
Margaret smiled.
“Aye — and the strangest part is, that very cave the hermit lived in has caved in entirely. If I had been stubborn enough to resist you, it would have buried me dead right where I chose to bury myself alive. I see the hand of Providence in that, Margaret. It condemns that mad, wasted life I led, and approves the new one you gave me. What say you to that?”
“Nay, I cannot pierce such mysteries,” she said, shaking her head. “I am but a woman.”
“More than that, I think. This very tale proves you are my guardian angel, nothing less. So come home to Gouda manse with me now.”
“You go on ahead,” she said. “I will follow.”
“Nay, ride back in the cart with me.”
“Not so.”
“Why ever not?”
“Can I tell you why and wherefore, being a woman? All I know is I feel — I wish — I must come alone.”
“Be it so then. I will leave the cart for you, for as you say, you are a woman who will have her way, and I will walk ahead on my own two feet, a man again, to carry the joyful news of your coming to the whole house.”

When Margaret reached the parsonage, the first thing she saw was the two Gerards together: the little boy doing wild, giddy somersaults on the patch of lawn, the father slouched in a chair in his big broad hat, pencil in hand, trying with endless patience to sketch his wiggling, fast-moving son. He showed her his rough sketches, frustrated. “I cannot fix him on the page no matter how hard I try,” he sighed. “He is pure quicksilver. Look at how he moves, a thousand different poses, none of them ungraceful, melting from one to the next like water.” He laughed a moment later. “Woe to all the old illuminators! Looking at this little rascal, I see what lumpy, lopsided, awkward toads they used to paint in missals, and call them cherubs and seraphs!” He crumpled the paper in despair and threw it away, and Margaret quietly picked it up and tucked it into the neck of her bodice.

That night, all four of them sat around the glowing peat fire, and Gerard bade them look at how the brass candlesticks and other metal trinkets glowed soft and warm in the hearthlight. “And the linen sheets I sleep in,” he said, half laughing, half earnest. “My conscience pricks me every night. ‘Who are you,’ it says, ‘to lie in linen white as spiderweb? Dives himself never had sheets so fine.’ And to think there are folk in the world who own all these nice things and still are not content. Let any one of them spend six months locked alone in a dark hermit’s cell, never seeing another human face, and they will learn how lovely this wicked world is, how fair and good ordinary men and women are. Margaret, you were always fair, but never half so bright to my eyes as you are now.” Margaret shook her head, disbelieving. “And my mother, here,” he went on, turning to Catherine, “I never noticed how exceeding comely you are until I came out of that cave.”
“Nor I neither,” Catherine said, smiling. “A score of years ago I might have passed unremarked in a crowd, but not now. I am old.”
“Every age has its own beauty,” Gerard told her, soft. “Look at that mild grey eye of yours, that has looked on nothing but motherly love for all these years — all that love has left its shadow on your face, and that shadow is a beauty no time can touch. Look at that soft lip, those pure white teeth, that brow where youth’s comeliness melts right into reverence. You are beautiful to me, mother dear.”
“That is all I need to hear, my darling,” Catherine said, wiping a happy tear from her eye. “It is past time you were in bed. You have to preach tomorrow morning.”
Reicht and Catherine exchanged a quiet, amused glance, that said plain as day: We have an amiable maniac on our hands, who thinks every last thing on God’s green earth is beautiful.

Sunday came, and they sat in the packed church to hear him preach. The pews were crammed full of people who had only come out of curiosity to gawk at the mad former hermit who had been made their vicar — and every last one of them left devout. Never had his great gift for speaking shone more bright. His own heart was so full the first time he looked out over the flock he had neglected for so many years, he poured his whole soul into the two-hour sermon that flew by so fast it felt to the crowd like barely twenty minutes. He terrified the impenitent and the careless, comforted the grieving and the afflicted, lifted the hearts of the poor, and when he finished the whole congregation stood still, rapt, unwilling to believe the rich, divine music of his voice had fallen silent.

Two poor women huddled in the back corner, crying so hard their shoulders shook.
“Wherever did he learn all that?” Catherine whispered, holding her apron to her eyes. “By Our Lady, he did not get it from me.”
As soon as they were back in the quiet parsonage, Margaret threw her arms around Catherine’s neck and kissed her. “Mother, mother,” she sobbed, “I am not quite a fully happy woman, but oh — I am so proud.” And she knelt there on the floor, and swore a solemn vow that no word or deed of hers would ever let their old love come between this young, holy priest and his Heaven.

Have you ever stood on the seashore after a great storm, when the wind dies suddenly? The waves do not stop the moment the wind that drove them fades — for a little while they lash the shore even harder than they did in the height of the gale. But you know, deep down, that the inevitable calm has come, and it is only rocking the last of the wild waves gently to sleep. That was the life of those two tempest-tossed lovers from that night, when they walked hand in hand under the stars back from the old hermit’s cave to the warm lights of the Gouda manse. Now and then a loud, roaring wave would rise up, but it was nothing more than memory’s echo of the old storm that had swept their lives for years. The gale itself was over, and the churning, boiling waters of their old pain began to sink, slow and soft and inevitable, into quiet.

That image takes the place of all the small, tedious, trivial details of the long, gentle years that followed. What matters most is their strange, unheard-of situation — one that history, bold as it is, rarely matches for oddity. Gerard and Margaret were bound tight by old affection, by years of shared familiarity, by a marriage that had been irregular but fully legal in its day. But they were separated now by the Holy Church they both loved deeply, and by their own consciences that sided unreservedly with that Church. They lived only a few miles apart, Margaret called Gerard’s own mother her own mother, and every Sunday she brought little Gerard out to Gouda, returning only after dark. No matter when she came, the parsonage was a feast day, and she was welcomed like a little queen. Catherine and Eli grew so tired of the quiet little town of Tergou, with nothing to draw them there compared to Rotterdam and Gouda, that they left it entirely and set up their new home in the city.

The years glided by, so empty of great dramatic incidents, so free of towering hope and terrible fear, so identical to one another, that I could barely mark the passage of time without dates to guide me.

Early the next year, 1471, the Duchess of Burgundy — with the Duke’s public dissent but secret connivance — raised an army to send her deposed brother Edward IV back to England to take back his crown. Our old friend Denys the soldier enlisted, and passed through Rotterdam on his way to the ships. He heard on the road that Gerard had become a priest, and Margaret was living alone. He found her at her shop, and told her he had never been the sort of man to settle down and marry — but since his old comrade Gerard had put it out of his own power to keep the troth he had sworn to her, Denys felt bound to keep it for him. “A comrade’s honour is as dear to me as my own,” he said.
Margaret stared at him, then laughed out loud. “I would rather stay your she-comrade,” she told him. “If we were to marry, we might find we do not get on half so well.” And in that role of his loyal she-comrade, she outfitted him with a fine new sword forged in Antwerp, and a double handful of silver coins.
