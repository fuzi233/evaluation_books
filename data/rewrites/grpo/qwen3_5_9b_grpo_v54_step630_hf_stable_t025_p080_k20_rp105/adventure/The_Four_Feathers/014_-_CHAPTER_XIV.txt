The heat of July and August pressed down upon the Salcombe estuary, but it was not the sun that warmed Ethne's apprehensions; they had grown heavy and cold within her chest. One morning, standing at the open window of Mrs. Adair's cottage, she voiced the fear that had been eating at her.

"I am afraid," she said, her voice trembling slightly in the bright air.

Mrs. Adair, seated in the cool shadow of the room behind her, did not look up from her book. She offered only a quiet, knowing smile.

"Of what? That some accident befell Colonel Durrance in London yesterday?"

"No," Ethne replied slowly, turning her gaze toward the stone-flagged terrace below. "Not of that. For he is crossing the lawn towards us now."

Mrs. Adair's smile deepened, though her eyes remained fixed on the printed page. "I thought so," she murmured, her voice barely audible.

Ethne ignored the comfort of the words, her mind racing with a sharper, darker question. "Do you think he has any hope at all of recovering his sight?"

Mrs. Adair set the book aside, her curiosity piqued by the girl's intensity. "Would a man travel to London repeatedly to consult an oculist if he believed the cure impossible? Of course he hopes."

"I am afraid," Ethne insisted, stepping out onto the terrace. "Laura, haven't you noticed how quickly he has changed? How fast he learns to read your silences? He infers your unspoken thoughts, completes your sentences, and interprets your gestures as commentary. At times, I feel he sees into the very corners of my mind. He reads me like a child's lesson book."

"You are at a disadvantage," Mrs. Adair observed calmly. "You no longer have your face to shield your thoughts."

"And his eyes no longer tell me anything," Ethne added, her voice dropping.

The truth lay in both statements. Once, Durrance's blindness had forced him to rely on her expressions, but now, stripped of her visual cues, he possessed a terrifying detachment. Conversely, Ethne, who had never doubted his intentions, now found herself constantly in the dark. The blindness had reversed their dynamic, leaving her vulnerable.

Mrs. Adair, however, was less concerned with the psychological shift and more intrigued by Ethne's sudden confession. The girl, once so frank, was now agitated, shifting her weight, hiding things.

"There is something you must conceal from him?" Mrs. Adair asked softly.

"Yes."

"Something important?"

"Something I must conceal at all costs," Ethne exclaimed, though a shadow of doubt crossed her mind—had Durrance already guessed?

She walked to the edge of the terrace, looking out over the lawn toward the creek. Beyond the hedge, the fields rolled gently, and in the distance, among the trees, she saw the smoke rising from Colonel Durrance's house. A sailing-boat tacked slowly against the wind further down the water. Ethne composed herself, summoning the strength to face the man approaching steadily across the grass. His cane tapped rhythmically, switching at the blades of grass with practiced habit, his feet finding the path without sight.

As Ethne descended the steps, Mrs. Adair dropped her book and moved to the window, hidden by the curtains. Her face was drawn, her eyes burning with a fierce, hungry light. She watched the meeting with a mixture of pity and triumph.

"Something she must conceal," Mrs. Adair whispered to herself, her tone laced with contempt for Ethne's fear of a blind husband. "For my part, I am glad," she thought, relieved that blindness had disabled Durrance. If her chance ever came, this dependency would be her ultimate victory. She watched Ethne and Durrance walk side by side toward the creek, sensing the plea in the younger woman's posture.

"You saw your oculist yesterday?" Ethne asked quickly as they met.

Durrance shrugged. "One must wait. Only time can show if a cure is possible."

"But must *we* wait?" Ethne pressed.

"Surely," he replied wisely. "It would be wiser on all counts." Then, unexpectedly, he asked, "Was it Mrs. Adair who proposed this plan for me to return to Guessens while you stayed here?"

Ethne, startled, explained that she had come to London in distress and that Laura had suggested the arrangement. "I welcomed it with all my heart," she said, her voice pleading for confirmation.

"I know that very well, Ethne," Durrance said gently. "But it is hard on you, who wishes to be back in your own country. I remember a sentence Harry Feversham spoke long ago in London, just before I left for Egypt. He said, 'She is of her country and more of her county. I do not think she could be happy in any place which was not within reach of Donegal.' To keep you here, at such cost to you, seems selfish."

"I was not thinking of that," Ethne interrupted, her anxiety flaring. "That makes me out selfish. I was merely wondering why you insist on waiting. For, of course, we pray for your sight, but the fact of a cure makes no difference."

She repeated the phrase with emphasis: "It can make no difference."

Durrance started as if roused from a trance. "I beg your pardon, Ethne. I was thinking of Harry Feversham. There is something I want you to tell me. You said a long time ago at Glenalla that you might one day tell me what happened. I would like to know now. What took place that night at Lennon House that broke off your engagement and sent him away an outcast?"

Ethne fell silent. "I would rather not. It is all over and done with. I do not want you to ask me ever."

Durrance accepted her refusal cheerfully. "Very well. It might hurt you to answer, and I do not wish to cause you pain."

"It is not because I fear pain," Ethne explained earnestly. "Mr. Feversham is a man I knew well, and now he is dead."

As they walked toward the gap in the trees along the creek bank, Ethne raised her eyes. The small boat she had noticed earlier had grounded itself on the shore. The sail was lowered, and standing on the bank, staring vaguely toward the house, was a man.

"A stranger has landed," Ethne said, seizing the interruption as a lifeline. "He looks lost. I will go and help him."

She ran forward, needing the distraction. The stranger approached, a man of middle height with a short snub nose, vacant brown eyes, and a ferocious moustache. He wore a hat that he removed to reveal a balding forehead.

"I have sailed down from Kingsbridge," he said. "Can you tell me if this house is called The Pool?"

"Yes; you will find Mrs. Adair if you go up the steps," Ethne replied.

"I came to see Miss Eustace."

Ethne turned, surprised. "I am Miss Eustace."

The stranger studied her in silence. "So I thought." He twisted a moustache before speaking again. "I have had trouble finding you, Miss Eustace. I went all the way to Glenalla—for nothing. Hard on a man whose leave is short!"

"I am very sorry," Ethne smiled, though uneasily. "Why have you been put to this trouble?"

"You have forgotten my name, no doubt."

"I do not think I have ever heard it."

"Oh, yes, you have. You heard it five years ago. I am Captain Willoughby."

Ethne recoiled. Color drained from her cheeks, her lips set hard, and her eyes grew cold. She glared at him silently.

Willoughby was unfazed, speaking with the air of a man forgiving a minor breach of manners. "I can understand that you do not welcome me, Miss Eustace, but none of us foresaw that you would be present when the three white feathers came into Feversham's hands."

Ethne swept the explanation aside. "How do you know I was present?"

"Feversham told me."

"You have seen him?" The cry burst from her lips, a raw throb of the heart that startled both of them. She had tried to erase Harry Feversham from her mind, to treat him as dead, yet the reality of his existence crashed over her.

"You have actually seen him?" she repeated, her voice trembling with wonder. "You have spoken to him? When?"

"A year ago, at Suakin. Else why should I be here?"

The question struck Ethne like a blow. She did not dare speculate on the answer.

"Yes," she said slowly, reluctantly. "After all, why are you here?"

Willoughby reached into his breast pocket, withdrew a case, and with deliberate slowness produced a tiny, soiled white feather. He held it out to her.

"I have come to give you this."

Ethne did not take it; she shrank from it.

"Why?" she asked unsteadily.

"Three white feathers, three separate accusations of cowardice, were sent to Feversham by three separate men. This is one of those feathers, forwarded from his lodgings to Ramelton five years ago. I am one of the three men who sent them. I have come to tell you that I withdraw my accusation. I take my feather back."

"And you bring it to me?"

"He asked me to."

Ethne finally took the feather. It was light and fragile, yet it felt momentous in her palm. Suddenly, the trees and the garden began to whirl around her. Willoughby's voice grew distant and thin, though she desperately wanted to hear every word. She felt a profound coldness despite the August sun. Yet, the presence of Captain Willoughby, one of the three men she could never forgive, gave her the strength to command herself. She refused to show weakness before any of the detested trio. With an effort, she steadied herself.

"Come," she said. "I will hear your story. Your news was a shock. Even now, I do not quite understand."

She led him to a secluded plot of grass above the creek, enclosed by thick hedges, with a garden seat in the center.

"Now," she said, gesturing for him to sit beside her. "Take your time. Forget nothing. Even his words, if you remember them! I shall thank you for his words."

She clenched the white feather in her hand. Somehow, Harry Feversham had redeemed his honor; somehow, she had been unjust to him. She was eager to learn how. Memories sternly repressed for five years flooded her mind—the Devonshire meadows, the salt of the sea, and the spring in Dublin before the feathers arrived at Ramelton.

Willoughby began his story, and almost immediately, the English countryside dissolved into a shifting mist. In its place rose the strange, wide country of the East that Durrance had described so often. The red cliffs of the coast vanished, replaced by stunted mimosas of the desert; the blue sea became vast stretches of yellow sand; the green hills turned to sharp-peaked, pyramidal black rocks rising abruptly from the dunes.

It was the bitter irony of her position that, while physically standing in the English countryside, she was mentally transported to the trials of her lover in the East, understanding his suffering only through the confidences of the man who had once accused him.
