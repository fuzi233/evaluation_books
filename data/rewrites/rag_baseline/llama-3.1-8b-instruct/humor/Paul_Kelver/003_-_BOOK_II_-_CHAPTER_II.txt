To grasp the intricate tapestry of my childhood, one must first immerse oneself in the aromatic scents of our humble abode. At seven years old, I dwelled within a domestic sphere dominated by the presence of my parents, whose union was a delicate dance of affection and frustration. Though they were not youthful, their faces, etched with the lines of time, seemed to radiate an aura of innocence, as if they had rediscovered the joys of courtship in their advanced years. My father, a man in his late thirties, would often call out from his study, his voice laced with a mix of concern and longing, "Maggie, are you occupied with something crucial? I'd like to discuss a pressing matter." Or, "Maggie, are you alone?" The responses, always delivered with a hint of exasperation, were a testament to the ebullient connection between them.

In turn, my mother's eyes sparkled with love whenever her gaze met my father's, her smile a gentle caress that seemed to soothe his every worry. Yet, amidst the tender moments, the household was not without its peculiarities. I learned to tread carefully, lest my footsteps be mistaken for those of others, and to announce my arrival with a respectful knock, a custom my aunt, Fanny, observed with unwavering dedication. Her entrance was always preceded by a low, deliberate cough, a signal that served as both warning and greeting. The sound, akin to the dying gasps of a consumptive, seemed to reverberate through the very walls, a reminder of her presence long before she appeared. Her eyes, veiled behind a mask of kindness, seemed to hold secrets, and her gentle songs, sung in hushed tones, echoed through the corridors, a soothing melody that belied the tension that simmered beneath the surface.

One day, as my mother emerged from the kitchen, she found Fanny preparing to enter the drawing room, her entrance ritual a symphony of dropped teaspoons and measured pauses. "Fanny, don't be absurd," my mother said, her words tinged with a hint of cruelty. My aunt's response, a gentle rebuke, caught me off guard: "Why shouldn't a pair of turtle doves coo and bill, when their combined age is barely over seventy, the lovely dears?"
