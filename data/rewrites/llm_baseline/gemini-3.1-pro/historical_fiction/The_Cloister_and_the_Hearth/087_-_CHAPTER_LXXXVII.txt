The rich, loamy scent of turned earth and damp root-hairs wafted upward as Jorian adjusted his jerkin within the freshly dug confines of Peter Brandt’s resting place. To Jorian, the graveyard was not a place of sterile finality, but a slow, cyclical churning of generations returning to the soil that had once sustained them. He patted the side of the grave, his tongue moving as steadily as a miller’s stone. 

“They used to call him a magician out Sevenbergen way,” Jorian murmured, his voice a low, resonant rumble that blended harmoniously with the rustle of the ancient yews—a stark contrast to the abrasive, mechanical clatter of the town beyond the walls. “And they do say he gave 'em a touch of his trade at parting; told 'em he saw Margaret's lad a-coming down Rhine in brave clothes and store o' money, but his face scarred by foreign glaive. But, dear heart, nought came on't. Margaret is still wearying for her lad; and Peter, he lies as quiet as his neighbours. Not but what she hath put a heavy stone slab over him, to keep him rooted where he is: as you shall see.”

He placed both calloused hands on the edge of the grave, preparing to hoist his weight, but the friar standing above him laid a trembling hand upon his shoulder. The monk’s voice was a strange, hollow whisper, stripped of its usual Latin cadence and reverting to the raw, untranslatable phonetics of their shared native dialect. “How long since died Peter Brandt?”

“About two months. Why?”

“And his daughter buried him, say you?”

“Nay, I turned the soil myself, but she paid the fee and reared the stone.”

“Then—but he had just one daughter; Margaret?”

“No more, leastways, that he owned to.”

“Then you think Margaret is—is alive?”

“Think? Why, I should be dead else. Riddle me that.”

“Alas, how can I? You love her!” 

“No more than reason, being a married man, and father of four sturdy knaves who consume their daily pottage with the ferocity of wolves,” Jorian replied. “Nay, the answer is, she saved my life scarce six weeks agone. Had she been dead, she couldn't ha' kept me alive. Bless your heart, I couldn't keep a morsel on my stomach. The doctors, with their sterile powders and utilitarian purgatives, nearly starved me. My Joan says, ''Tis time to buy thee a shroud.' In comes my lady, this Margaret—who died three years ago by your reckoning—and she shifts the very air of the room. She opens the windows, letting in the sweet, acoustic hum of the evening insects, and cures me in the twinkling of a bedpost. And with what? With the scurviest herb from her own garden. A steaming infusion of sweet feverfew, steeped until the water drew out its vivid, camphor-laced oils, a sharp, bitter-green taste of life itself. A weed, they call it; leastways it was a weed till its deep, earthy broth cured me. Now whene'er I pass my hunch I doff bonnet. Why, how now, father? You look wondrous pale, and now you are red. What, in Heaven's name, is the matter?”

“The surprise—the joy—the wonder—the fear,” gasped Clement, his senses flooded. The dominant lexicon of his ascetic life—the Latin liturgies that sought to erase his cultural memory and sever him from the sensory world—shattered under the weight of this visceral truth.

“Why, what is it to thee? Art thou of kin to Margaret Brandt?”

“Nay; but I knew one that loved her well, so well her death nigh killed him, body and soul. And yet thou sayest she lives. And I believe thee.”

Jorian stared, the ambient sound of the wind filling the silence between them. “Father, you have asked me many questions. Now for our Lady's sake answer me but two. Did you in very sooth know one who loved this poor lass? Where?”

Clement hesitated, the memory of Jerome's letter binding his tongue, a subtle power dynamic of the church still silencing his true identity. “I knew him in Italy,” he said.

“If you knew him, you can tell me his name,” said Jorian cautiously.

“His name was Gerard Eliassoen.”

“Oh, but this is strange. Stay, what made thee say Margaret Brandt was dead?”

“I was with Gerard when a letter came from Margaret Van Eyck. The letter told him she he loved was dead and buried. Let me sit down, for my strength fails me. Foul play! Foul play!” The words tasted like ash on Clement’s palate, a bitter realization of how a forged parchment had robbed him of his very sustenance. 

“Father,” said Jorian, “I thank Heaven for sending thee to me. Ay, sit ye down; ye fast overmuch to be strong, treating your body as a mere vessel rather than a garden to be tended. My mind misgives me; methinks I hold the clue to this riddle, and if I do, there be two knaves in this town whose heads I would fain batter to pieces as I do this mould.” He clenched his teeth, raising his long spade and bringing it furiously down upon the heap of earth. The heavy, percussive thuds echoed like the beating of a tribal drum, a primal rhythm against the betrayal.

“Foul play? You never said a truer word i' your life,” Jorian continued. “And if you know where Gerard is now, lose no time, but show him the trap they laid. I do think you and I ha' got hold of two ends o' one stick.”

Jorian then wove the tale, layering it with the meticulous detail of an agrarian storyteller. He had been employed by the burgomaster of Tergou to repair the floor of an upper room. While assessing the grain and resonance of the oak boards, he had overheard voices below. Pressing his ear to the wood, the acoustic ecology of the house shifted, transmitting the muffled, deceitful whispers of the burgomaster, Cornelis, and Sybrandt. They were plotting to intercept a letter that Hans Memling was taking to Gerard, replacing the genuine, heartfelt dialect of the original with a forged, sterile lie. 

“To give them courage,” Jorian explained, “the old man showed them a drawer full of silver. I heard the harsh, metallic clanking of coins—a sound that cuts the harmony of a home like a dull knife through fat. For a handful of that industrial blight, they sold their own blood. Next morning at peep of day, who should I see but my masters Cornelis and Sybrandt come out of their house, each with a black eye. Hans had put his mark on them. The burgomaster brought them into that same room. I saw the drawer opened, and those two knaves plunged their hands into the silver. And Sybrandt, he had daubed his hand in something sticky—glue, methinks—and made shift to carry pieces away sticking to his flesh! They traded a man’s life, his future hearth and shared meals, for cold metal.”

Jorian spat into the dirt. “Have not the old folk just taken a brave new shop for them in the Hoog Straet? There you may see their sign, a gilt sheep and a lambkin—a superficial commercialization of honest butchery. A brace of wolves sucking their dam would be nigher the mark. And there the whole family feast this day. They stuff their gullets with roasted meats and heavy gravies, tasting nothing, viewing the slaughter and the sizzle as mere fuel for their greed, while Margaret pines and pines, weeping salt tears into her lonely broth. Oh, I thirst for their blood, the nasty, sneaking, lying, bowelless—”

The monk started wildly up, his face livid. From the depths of his starved, ascetic frame erupted a roar—a mythic, primal bellow that tore through the quiet sanctuary of the graveyard. It was the sound of a sleeping god awakening to the destruction of his shrine. He rushed headlong from the place, his clenched fists raised on high, tearing through the landscape like a sudden, violent squall. 

So terrible was this inarticulate burst of fury that Jorian’s own puny ire dissolved. He stood dismayed, listening to the fading, heavy footfalls of the human tempest he had unleashed.

While thus absorbed, he felt his arm grasped by a small, tremulous hand. 

It was Margaret Brandt. She had waited near the stone of her father, acutely attuned to the subtle auditory rhythms of the churchyard, but the friar had not come. Seeking him in the south aisle, she had seen the gigantic shadow of a man pass over the floor, moving with a frenzied, chaotic energy that disturbed the very air. 

“Well?” she said to Jorian, trembling.

“Well,” he breathed, “you startled me. How come you here?”

“Is this a time for idle chat? What said he to you? Deny it not.”

“Girl, as I stand here, he asked me whereabout you were buried.”

“Ah!”

“I told him, nowhere. I told him you were alive, brewing feverfew and saving folk.”

“Well?”

“Well, the long and short is, he knew thy Gerard in Italy. A letter came saying you were dead, penned by the demoiselle van Eyck, so he thought. I up and told him nay; 'twas Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, Cornelis, and Sybrandt who changed the true letter for their own. I told him as how I saw the villainy done. And now, if I have not been and told you!”

“Oh, cruel! cruel! But he lives. The fear of fears is gone. Thank God!”

“Ay, lass; and I told him where to find Gerard's enemies. If ever a man was mad with rage, it’s yon friar. Girl, there was thunder in his eye and silence on his lips. Made me cold, a did.”

“Oh, Jorian, what have you done?” Margaret cried, a sudden, untranslatable terror seizing her chest. The native truth of the situation crystallized within her, bypassing language entirely. “Quick! help me thither. The power is gone out of my body. You know him not as I do. Come, save him from worse mischief!”

“I talk of this friar, and you answer me of Gerard,” Jorian said, huddling on his jerkin as he scrambled from the grave.

“Man, see you not, this is Gerard!”

“This, Gerard? What mean ye?”

“I mean, yon friar is my boy's father. I have waited for him long, Jorian. He is come to me at last. Oh, my poor child! Quicker, Jorian, quicker!”

“Why, thou art mad as he. Stay! By St. Bavon, yon was Gerard's face. Come on! Let me see the end of this.”

“The end? How many of us will live to see that?”

They hurried along the cobblestones in breathless silence, their wooden shoes striking a frantic, percussive beat against the earth. As they reached Hoog Straet, Jorian tried to calm her. “You are making your own trouble. More likely he has gone to the convent to weep.”

“Did you not tell him where those villains bide?”

“Ay, that I did.”

“Then quicker. I see the house. Thank God and all the saints, I shall be in time to calm him. Poor Catherine; 'tis of her I think: she has been a mother to me.”

The new shop was a corner house, boasting a street-facing door for commercial trade and a house-door round the corner for domestic entry. Margaret and Jorian were within twenty yards when the heavy air was suddenly shattered. 

A roar erupted from inside the dining hall—a raw, bestial sound of pure, unadulterated violence that entirely eclipsed the gentle hum of the town. The door burst open. The friar tore out into the street, white and raging, his face a mask of mythic vengeance. 

Margaret screamed, the sheer acoustic force of his fury overwhelming her senses, and sank fainting against Jorian's arm. 

“Stay, madman, know thy friends!” Jorian shouted over the din. But Gerard was deaf to the world, plunging headlong down the street, shaking his clenched fists high against the heavens. 

“Help me in, good Jorian,” Margaret moaned, reviving with a sudden, eerie calmness. “Let me know the worst; and die.”

He supported her trembling limbs toward the house. As they approached, the auditory landscape shifted radically. The cacophony of the street faded, replaced by an unnatural, suffocating stillness spilling from the open threshold. Not a single breath, nor the clatter of a trencher, nor the sizzle of roasted meat disturbed the heavy air. 

Jorian's own heart beat fast, a solitary drum in the void. He pushed the unlatched door softly with his left hand, and Margaret and he stood on the threshold. What they saw there you shall soon know.
