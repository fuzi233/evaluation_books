Friday evening brought Newland Archer to the Chiverses', and Saturday unfolded with the meticulous, ritualistic precision of a weekend at Highbank. In the morning, he rode an ice-boat with his hostess and the season's hardiest guests; in the afternoon, he toured the farm with Reggie, listening to the grandiose, technical discourses on equines within the opulent stables. Following tea, he found a corner in the firelit hall to converse with a young lady who, having previously claimed a broken heart at the news of his engagement, now eagerly detailed her own matrimonial aspirations. By midnight, the festivities had devolved into absurdities: he helped deposit a goldfish into a guest's bed, costumed a nervous aunt's brother as a burglar in the bathroom, and participated in a pillow fight that sprawled from the nurseries down to the basement. Yet, on Sunday, immediately after luncheon, he borrowed a cutter and drove toward Skuytercliff.

To the uninitiated, the residence at Skuytercliff appeared an Italian villa. Its architect, Mr. van der Luyden, had constructed it in his youth following his return from the Grand Tour, envisioning it as a nuptial gift for his approaching union with Miss Louisa Dagonet. It was a substantial, square wooden edifice clad in pale green and white tongue-and-groove siding, featuring a Corinthian portico and fluted pilasters separating the windows. From its commanding elevation, terraces lined with balustrades and urns cascaded in the style of a steel engraving toward a modest, irregular lake edged in asphalt and draped by rare weeping conifers. To either side, the legendary weedless lawns, speckled with "specimen" trees of distinct varieties, rolled outward to vast stretches of grass crowned with intricate cast-iron decorations. Below, nestled in a depression, lay the four-room stone cottage erected by the first Patroon in 1612.

Against the uniform backdrop of snow and a somber winter sky, the Italian villa seemed to loom with a grim severity. Even in summer, it maintained a formidable distance, daring even the boldest flowerbeds no closer than thirty feet from its imposing facade. As Archer rang the bell, the prolonged chime resonated as if echoing through a mausoleum. When the butler eventually answered, his astonishment was palpable, bordering on the theatrical shock of someone roused from eternal slumber. Fortunately, Archer belonged to the family; despite the irregularity of his arrival, he was entitled to learn that the Countess Olenska was absent, having departed for afternoon services with Mrs. van der Luyden precisely forty-five minutes prior.

"The Master," the butler continued, "is present, sir, though I suspect he is either concluding a nap or perusing yesterday's *Evening Post*. Upon returning from church this morning, he remarked his intention to review the paper after lunch. If you prefer, sir, I might approach the library door and listen—"

Archer, offering thanks, indicated he wished to seek out the ladies. Relieved, the butler closed the heavy door with majestic finality. A groom escorted Archer's cutter to the stables, and he proceeded on foot through the park toward the main road. The village of Skuytercliff lay a mile and a half distant, yet Archer knew Mrs. van der Luyden never traversed the grounds on foot; he needed to remain on the highway to intercept the carriage. Soon, however, descending a footpath intersecting the road, he spotted a slender figure enveloped in a crimson cloak, preceded by a large dog. He hastened forward, and Madame Olenska halted abruptly, her face lighting with a welcoming smile.

"Oh, you've come!" she exclaimed, drawing her hand from her muff.

The vibrant red of her cloak rendered her lively and striking, reminiscent of the Ellen Mingott of yore. Laughing as he took her hand, Archer replied, "I came to discover what you were fleeing from."

Her expression darkened, and she answered, "Well—you shall see shortly enough."

The response baffled him. "Pray, do you imply you have been overtaken?"

She shrugged, executing a slight movement akin to Nastasia's, and resumed in a lighter vein, "Shall we continue our walk? I am quite chilled following the sermon. And what difference does it make now that you are here to shield me?"

Blood surged to Archer's temples as he seized a fold of her cloak. "Ellen—what is it? You must tell me."

"Oh, later—first let us race; my feet are frozen to the earth," she cried. Gathering the cloak, she fled across the snow, the dog bounding around her with playful, challenging barks. For a fleeting moment, Archer stood transfixed, delighted by the flash of the red meteor against the white expanse; then he launched himself after her. They converged panting and laughing at a wicket leading into the park.

She looked upward at him, smiling. "I knew you would come!"

"That proves you desired my arrival," he returned, his joy disproportionate to the triviality of their jest. The white brilliance of the trees filled the air with a mysterious luminescence, and as they trekked onward over the snow, the ground itself seemed to sing beneath their footsteps.

"Whither did you depart from?" Madame Olenska inquired.

He recounted his travels, adding, "And it was due to your note."

After a pause, she spoke with a barely perceptible chill in her voice, "May requested you to ensure my safety."

"I required no prompting."

"You mean—I am so manifestly helpless and defenseless? What a pitiful creature you must all consider me! Yet these women here appear not—to feel the necessity: no more than the blessed in paradise."

He lowered his voice. "Of what nature is this need?"

"Ah, do not inquire! I do not speak your tongue," she retorted petulantly.

The words struck him like a physical blow, leaving him standing motionless in the path, gazing down at her. "Then why did I come, if I do not share your language?"

"Oh, my friend—" She placed her hand lightly upon his arm, and he pleaded earnestly, "Ellen—why will you not reveal to me what has transpired?"

She shrugged again. "Does anything truly occur in heaven?"

Silence fell between them, and they walked several yards without exchange. Eventually, she declared, "I will tell you—but where, where, where? One cannot find solitude for a single instant in that vast seminary of a house, with every door ajar, and servants constantly delivering tea, firewood, or newspapers! Is there no corner within an American dwelling where one may exist alone? You are so shy, and yet so public. I invariably feel as though I am back in the convent—or upon a stage, before a dreadfully polite audience that never applauds."

"Oh, you dislike us!" Archer exclaimed.

They passed the dwelling of the old Patroon, characterized by squat walls and small, square windows clustered tightly around a central chimney. The shutters stood wide, and through one of the freshly washed panes, Archer discerned the glow of a fire.

"Why—the house is open!" he observed.

She paused. "Not usually, certainly. Only today, at least. I wished to inspect it, and Mr. van der Luyden had kindled the fire and opened the windows so we might halt there on our return from church this morning." She ascended the steps and tested the lock. "It remains unlatched—what fortune! Enter, and we may converse quietly. Mrs. van der Luyden has departed to visit her elderly aunts in Rhinebeck, and we shall not be missed for another hour."

He followed her into the narrow passageway. His spirits, which had plummeted at her previous words, leaped irrationally upward. The humble little cottage stood there, its paneling and brass fittings shimmering in the firelight, as if magically conjured to receive them. A massive bed of embers still glowed in the kitchen chimney beneath an iron pot suspended from an ancient crane. Rush-bottomed armchairs faced one another across the tiled hearth, and rows of Delft plates lined the wall shelves. Archer stooped, throwing a log onto the embers.

Madame Olenska, removing her cloak, seated herself in one of the chairs. Archer leaned against the chimney, regarding her.

"You are laughing now; yet when you penned your letter, you were wretched," he stated.

"Yes." She hesitated. "But I cannot feel wretched when you are present."

"I shall not remain long," he rejoined, his lips tightening with the effort to utter no more than necessary.

"No; I understand. However, I am improvident: I inhabit the moment wherein I am happy."

The words slipped through him like a seduction. To block out this allure, he stepped away from the hearth, standing to gaze out at the black tree trunks against the snow. Yet it seemed she, too, had shifted her position, and he still perceived her, situated between himself and the trees, drooping over the fire with an indolent smile. Archer's heart beat with insurrection. Might it be that she had fled from him? Had she waited to disclose this truth until they were sequestered together in this private chamber?
