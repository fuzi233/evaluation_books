The Secret Garden

Aristide Valentin, Chief of the Paris Police, was running late for dinner. His guests had begun to arrive before him, but their anxiety melted away when Ivan, his trusted servant, reassured them. Ivan was an old man with a scarred face and a grey moustache, who sat silently at a table in the entrance hall—a space lined with ancient weapons. Valentin’s home was as unusual as its owner: a grand, centuries-old house perched beside the Seine, shaded by towering poplars. But its most striking feature was that it had no exit other than through the front door, guarded by Ivan and the armory. The garden was expansive and ornate, with multiple doors leading from the house into it. Yet there was no way out from the garden—its perimeter was sealed by a high, smooth wall topped with sharp spikes. It was, perhaps, a fitting refuge for a man whose life had earned him the sworn hatred of a hundred criminals.

Ivan informed the guests that Valentin had called to say he’d be delayed ten minutes. In truth, he was finalizing grim matters—executions and other affairs that repulsed him deeply, though he handled them with cold precision. Though ruthless in tracking down lawbreakers, he was merciful in sentencing. As head of French—and much of European—police reform, he had championed lighter punishments and prison reforms. He was one of France’s great humanist freethinkers, though such men often mistake kindness for indifference, making mercy colder than justice itself.

When Valentin finally arrived, he was already dressed in formal black and wearing the red rosette—an elegant figure, his dark beard streaked with silver. He moved directly to his study, which opened onto the garden behind the house. The garden door stood open, and after locking his official box in its usual place, he paused at the threshold, gazing out. A sharp moon pierced through the tattered clouds of a storm, and Valentin watched it with a rare wistfulness—unusual for a man of such scientific rigor. Perhaps such minds carry within them a silent intuition of life’s greatest mysteries. But soon enough, he shook off the mood. He was late, and his guests were arriving.

A quick glance into the drawing room confirmed what he already suspected: his most important guest had not yet arrived. All the others were present—Lord Galloway, the English Ambassador, a choleric old man with a russet face like a ripe apple, adorned with the blue ribbon of the Garter; Lady Galloway, slim and delicate, with silver hair and a refined, sensitive expression; her daughter, Lady Margaret Graham, pale and elfin with copper-colored hair; the Duchess of Mont St. Michel, dark-eyed and regal, accompanied by her two daughters, equally striking; Dr. Simon, a typical French intellectual, with glasses, a pointed beard, and a forehead furrowed by constant skepticism; and Father Brown, a priest from Cobhole, Essex, whom Valentin had met recently in England.

But it was Commandant O’Brien, a tall man in Foreign Legion uniform, who drew Valentin’s attention more than any. O’Brien had bowed stiffly to the Galloways, receiving only cold acknowledgment. Now he approached Valentin alone, his demeanor both bold and melancholic. Clean-shaven, dark-haired, and blue-eyed, he carried himself with the swagger of a soldier who had seen too much—victories and defeats alike. Born an Irish gentleman, he had fled his homeland after financial ruin and now lived freely under foreign banners, spurs jingling and sabre at his side. When he bowed to the Ambassador’s family, Lord and Lady Galloway remained rigid, while Lady Margaret turned her gaze away.

Yet none of these figures held true significance for Valentin. None were the guest he awaited. He was expecting Julius K. Brayne, a man of global fame, whose wealth and eccentric philanthropy had made headlines across America and Europe. Brayne had funded obscure religions, funded unknown poets, and once claimed Luke P. Tanner of Paris, Pennsylvania, was “more progressive” than Walt Whitman. He believed in progress, whatever form it took—even if it meant supporting causes few could understand. He admired Valentin, calling him “progressive,” though this flattery was misplaced.

Then, as if summoned by fate, Brayne entered the room. His presence was immense—like a physical force. He was enormous, fat as he was tall, clad entirely in black evening wear, without so much as a watch chain or ring. His white hair was swept back like a German general’s, his face red and round, with a single dark tuft beneath his lip that lent his cherubic visage a sinister, almost demonic air. But his arrival wasn’t merely dramatic—it was urgent. He was late, and the dinner had already begun. He was quickly ushered into the dining room with Lady Galloway on his arm.

The Galloways were generally cordial, except for Lord Galloway, who grew increasingly irritable. He was fine during dinner, but as cigars were passed, three younger men—Simon, Brown, and O’Brien—drifted away to mingle with the ladies or smoke in the conservatory. Then the diplomat became restless. Every minute brought fresh dread: was O’Brien signaling to his daughter? How? He couldn’t imagine, but the suspicion gnawed at him. Left alone with Brayne and Valentin over coffee, he found himself trapped between two men who spoke of ideas he didn’t care to hear. Their debate on “science and religion” reached a dull climax, and Lord Galloway rose abruptly, seeking the drawing room.

He wandered through long corridors, lost for several minutes, until he heard voices—the doctor’s high-pitched lecture, then the priest’s quiet tone, followed by laughter. With a curse, he thought they must be arguing again. But when he pushed open the salon door, he saw only absence: Commandant O’Brien was gone. Lady Margaret was gone too.

Frustrated, he stormed back down the hall. His obsession with protecting his daughter from the reckless Irishman had become all-consuming. As he headed toward the back of the house, where Valentin’s study lay, he unexpectedly encountered his daughter—her face pale, her expression scornful. If she had been with O’Brien, where was he? If not, where had she been?

Driven by irrational suspicion, he pushed deeper into the shadowed corners of the mansion, eventually finding a servants’ entrance leading to the garden. The storm had cleared. The moon, sharp as a scimitar, lit every corner of the garden. A tall figure in blue—O’Brien—was walking briskly across the lawn toward the study door. Moonlight glinted off his shoulder epaulettes, confirming his identity.

He vanished through the French windows, leaving Lord Galloway furious and bewildered. The garden seemed like a stage set for romance—moonlit, serene, mocking his authority. The grace of O’Brien’s stride enraged him as if the man were a rival. He felt trapped in a dream of troubadours and fairy tales. To shake off the madness, he stepped forward—then tripped over something in the grass. Irritated, he looked down. Then, curiously, he looked again.

And suddenly, the moon and the poplars witnessed something extraordinary: an elderly English diplomat sprinting across the lawn, shouting at the top of his lungs.

His cries brought Dr. Simon to the study door. The doctor, pale and anxious, listened as Lord Galloway gasped, “A corpse in the grass—a bloodied corpse! O’Brien has gone mad!”

“We must tell Valentin at once,” Simon said, once the nobleman had described what he’d seen. “It’s fortunate he’s here.” And just then, the detective entered, drawn by the commotion. At first, he came as a host might—concerned, courteous. But when told of the body, his expression shifted instantly. This was no longer social duty; this was his work.

“Strange,” he said, as they hurried outside, “that I’ve hunted mysteries across the globe, only to find one in my own backyard. But where is the body?”

They crossed the lawn, now veiled in a light mist rising from the river. Guided by Galloway, they found the corpse half-buried in deep grass. It was a large man, broad-shouldered, lying face down. Only his back was visible—black cloth, bald head, a few strands of wet brown hair clinging like seaweed. From beneath his face, a crimson trail of blood seeped slowly into the grass.

“At least,” Simon said, voice low and solemn, “he isn’t one of us.”

“Examine him, doctor,” Valentin said sharply. “He may not be dead.”

Simon knelt. “Not quite cold,” he murmured, “but dead enough. Help me lift him.”

They raised the body slightly. The moment they did, the horror became undeniable: the head had been completely severed. The neck was cleanly cut, as if by a blade of divine precision. Even Valentin flinched. “He must have been as strong as a gorilla,” he muttered.

Simon lifted the head. The neck was slashed, but the face was intact—pale yellow, sunken yet swollen, with a hawk-like nose and heavy lids. It resembled a wicked Roman emperor, perhaps with a hint of Chinese imperial lineage. No one recognized it. Nothing else marked the man—except the faint white gleam of a shirtfront beneath the body, stained with blood. He had been dressed for a formal occasion.

Valentin dropped to his hands and knees, scanning the ground around the body. Simon helped, though clumsily. Galloway joined in, barely knowing what to do. They found nothing but broken twigs—snapped short, as if chopped. Valentin picked one up, studied it briefly, then tossed it aside.

“Twigs,” he said flatly. “Twigs. And a stranger, headless. That’s all we have.”

Silence fell. Then Galloway snapped, “Who’s that? Over by the garden wall!”

A small figure emerged from the haze—Father Brown, the priest, looking confused. “I say,” he said quietly, “there are no gates to this garden, do you know?”

Valentin frowned—his instinctive irritation flaring at the sight of the cassock. But he was too fair-minded to dismiss the point. “You’re right,” he admitted. “Before we solve how he died, we must ask how he got in.”

He turned to the group. “If possible, let’s keep this discreet. We have ladies, a foreign ambassador. If we must treat this as a crime, we will. But until then, I’ll handle it myself. I’m the head of the police—I can afford privacy. I swear I’ll clear each of you before I call my men. Gentlemen, upon your honor, none of you leave this house until noon tomorrow. There are rooms for all. Simon, go fetch Ivan—the man in the front hall. Tell him to send another servant to guard the door and come to me immediately. Lord Galloway, you’ll inform the ladies. They must stay. Father Brown and I will remain with the body.”

His command was obeyed without hesitation. Simon went to the armoury, roused Ivan, the detective’s private man. Galloway returned to the drawing room, delivering the news with tact—by the time the company gathered, the women were shocked but calm. Meanwhile, Father Brown and Valentin stood beside the corpse, silent under the moonlight—two figures representing opposing views of life and death.

Ivan burst from the house like a cannonball, racing across the lawn. His face was alive with excitement. “Sir!” he exclaimed. “May I examine the remains?”

“Yes,” Valentin said. “But briefly. We must discuss this indoors.”

Ivan lifted the head—then nearly dropped it.

“Why,” he gasped, “it’s… no, it can’t be. Do you recognize this man, sir?”

“No,” Valentin replied coolly. “Let’s go inside.”

Together, they carried the body to a sofa in the study, then moved to the drawing room.

Valentin sat at his desk, writing rapidly. His eyes were sharp, unyielding. “Is everyone here?” he asked.

“Mr. Brayne is missing,” said the Duchess.

“And Mr. O’Brien,” added Galloway, voice hoarse. “I saw him walking in the garden when the body was still warm.”

“Ivan,” Valentin ordered, “go fetch Commandant O’Brien and Mr. Brayne. Brayne is finishing a cigar in the dining room. O’Brien, I believe, is pacing the conservatory. I’m not certain.”

Ivan vanished. Before anyone could speak, Valentin continued, swift and steady.

“A dead man has been found in the garden—beheaded. Dr. Simon, you examined it. Could such a wound be made with a knife?”

“Impossible,” the doctor said. “Not even clumsily. This was clean. A battle-axe, an old headsman’s axe, or a two-handed sword.”

“But there aren’t any swords here!” the Duchess cried.

Valentin kept writing. “Could it be done with a French cavalry sabre?”

A knock came at the door—soft, yet chilling. Everyone froze.

“Possibly,” Simon whispered.

“Thank you,” Valentin said. “Come in, Ivan.”

The servant reappeared, bringing Commandant O’Brien, disheveled and defiant.

“What do you want?” O’Brien demanded.

“Sit down,” Valentin said calmly. “Why aren’t you wearing your sword?”

“I left it on the library table,” O’Brien replied, his accent thickening. “It was in the way.”

“Ivan,” Valentin said, “fetch the Commandant’s sword from the library.”

As the servant left, Valentin asked, “Lord Galloway says he saw you in the garden before discovering the body. What were you doing?”

O’Brien threw himself into a chair. “Admiring the moon. Communing with Nature, me bhoy.”

Silence. Then another knock.

Ivan returned, holding an empty steel scabbard. “This is all I found.”

“Place it on the table,” Valentin said, without looking up.

The silence was suffocating—like the quiet before a verdict. The Duchess’s sobs had ceased. Galloway’s rage had cooled. Then a voice broke the stillness.

“I can tell you,” Lady Margaret said, her voice clear and trembling with courage. “I know what O’Brien was doing in the garden. He asked me to marry him. I refused. I told him, given our circumstances, I could give him nothing but respect. He was angry. But I swear—he never touched that man. I offer him my respect now. I’ll swear it anywhere.”

Galloway stepped toward her, his voice low and threatening. “Hold your tongue, Maggie. Why defend him? Where’s his sword? Where’s—”

He stopped. Her eyes locked onto his—cold, fierce, revealing a truth far deeper than murder.

“You old fool,” she whispered. “What are you trying to prove? I tell you—he was innocent while with me. But if he wasn’t, he was still with me. If he killed someone in the garden, who saw it? Who knew? Do you hate Neil so much you’d accuse your own daughter—”

Lady Galloway screamed.

Everyone sat frozen, sensing the hidden tragedies between lovers—murdered husbands, poisoned hearts, betrayals older than memory.

Then, from the corner, a gentle voice interrupted: “Was it a very long cigar?”

They turned. Father Brown, still in his corner, had spoken.

“I mean,” he said, “the cigar Mr. Brayne is finishing. It looks nearly as long as a walking stick.”

Valentin glanced up—surprised, then thoughtful.

“Quite right,” he said. “Ivan, go check on Mr. Brayne. Bring him here at once.”

The servant left. Valentin turned to Margaret.

“Lady Margaret,” he said, “we’re grateful for your honesty. But there’s a gap. You were seen leaving the study, and Galloway found the garden minutes later. O’Brien was still walking it.”

Margaret smiled faintly. “I just refused him. We wouldn’t return arm in arm. He’s a gentleman. He lingered behind. So he’s accused.”

“In those moments,” Valentin said, “he might have—”

The knock came again.

Ivan poked his head in. “Sir, Mr. Brayne has left the house.”

“Left?” Valentin rose.

“Gone,” Ivan said, grinning. “Scooted. Evaporated. Hat and coat are gone. And here’s something to cap it all—I ran outside and found a trace. A big one.”

“What?” Valentin asked.

“I’ll show you.”

Ivan returned, brandishing a naked cavalry sabre—bloodstained near the tip and edge.

The room fell silent. The weapon gleamed like a bolt of lightning.

“I found this,” Ivan said calmly, “flung among the bushes fifty yards up the road to Paris. Right where Mr. Brayne threw it when he ran.”
