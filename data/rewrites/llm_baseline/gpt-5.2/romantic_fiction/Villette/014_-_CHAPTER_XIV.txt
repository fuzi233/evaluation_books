Georgette, once her cheeks had taken back their little roses and her cough was gone, was sent away into the country. Madame called it prudence; I felt it as loss. The child had been a warm hand slipped into mine in a house where, though there was always movement, there was little tenderness. When she left, the corridors seemed longer, the stair-steps more hollow to the foot, as if the building itself had exhaled and emptied.

I might have had companions, too. Each of the teachers, in turn, made her trial of me—an invitation, a smile held out like a spoon to see whether I would taste and approve. I did taste. One was honest enough, but she handled ideas as a farmwife handles bruised apples—sound where you bite, rotten where you don’t look; her feelings were coarse-grained, her talk salted always with herself. The second was Parisian, glazed over with a varnish of manners that shone prettily till you scraped it; beneath lay a damp, unwholesome slough. The third was a pale, characterless creature, yet she had one passion as sharp as vinegar: money. Not money to buy bread, or pay rent, or put a little meat into the pot, but money for the very look and clink of it, as if coin were spice and she lived on aroma.

Once, by way of favour, she drew me up-stairs, unlatched a secret panel, and showed me her hoard—a heap of coarse five-franc pieces, perhaps fifteen guineas’ worth, spread like dull, heavy lozenges on a board. The sight of it made her eyes take a greenish glisten, like a cat’s in the dark. She hovered over it with dotage, speaking of it with a perseverance as strange as it was unlovely in one not yet twenty-five; she loved it as a hen loves her eggs, not because they will hatch, but because they are there—warm under her, hers.

The Parisienne, Mademoiselle St. Pierre, was the opposite: prodigal in desire, though how far she ventured in deed I never knew. Once only I saw a snake-head of profligacy peep from her habits—cautious, curious, tasting the air. It rustled in the leaves of a bad novel; and when, in haste and unwisdom, I struck at it with anger, it recoiled, vanished, and hissed from hiding. From that hour she hated me with the cold, clean hatred that never troubles itself to flame.

She was always in debt. Her salary was anticipated, not merely in dress, but in perfumes that clouded a room with heavy sweetness, in cosmetics that gave her sallow skin a false bloom, in confectionery, and in condiments—little costly jars and packets that sharpened a palate already deadened. She had in her something like an epicure’s habits without an epicure’s joy: she sought sensation as one might seek a strong sauce when the meat is tainted. Thin, regular, yellowed, with perfect teeth and lips drawn fine as a thread, she looked at once craving and ungrateful. Work she hated; what she called pleasure she loved—a brainless dissipation, an insipid chewing of time.

Madame Beck knew her through and through, and spoke of her with an odd mixture of shrewdness, indifference, and dislike. When I asked why she kept such a woman, she answered, simply, that it suited her interest. For St. Pierre possessed, in almost a singular degree, the power of keeping order among a turbulent troop. She held them without noise, without passion, as a still frost can hold a stream. Knowledge she scarcely communicated; vigilance she carried like a rod that never needed to strike. “Je sais bien qu’elle n’a pas de principes,” Madame admitted, with a candour as calm as calculation; “son maintien est toujours convenable: c’est tout ce qu’il faut.” Parents looked no farther; the pupils looked no farther; therefore she looked no farther either.

It was a strange, frolicsome, noisy little world, this school, where chains were hidden under flowers. Romanism pervaded the place like incense—sweetening, stupefying, penetrating every chink. There was jealous spiritual restraint, but beside it an ample licence of the body, as if food, play, warmth, and bright shows could be used to bribe the senses into silence. I could not but mark how carefully the days were padded with comfort and diversion, lest a mind should fall still enough to hear its own questions. The house was always full of sound—the scrape and patter of feet, the chirp of girls, the sharp call of a mistress, the bell’s metallic command. Even the garden had its appointed clamour, laughter like flung pebbles, a racket of games. I learned to think of happiness there as something manufactured, not grown: a kind of music-box gaiety, wound up daily, and as mindless.

Yet at this season—summer in its ripest glow—even that manufactured gaiety took on a charm. The broad folding-doors and the two-leaved casements stood open from morning till evening. Sunshine seemed naturalized in the air; clouds, those dear grey drifts I had known at home, appeared banished to far seas. The house lived out of doors. Classes were held in the grand berceau; meals were taken there too, and I remember especially the odour of bread just broken, the warm, faint sourness of butter, the bruised sweetness of strawberries, and the green, cooling breath of cucumbers sliced thin and salted, sweating on their plates. Bees worked in the borders as steadily as any diligent pupil; the acacias, when stirred, dropped a faint almond scent; and under all the domestic bustle ran the soft acoustic fabric of summer—leaf-rustle, insect-hum, distant cart-wheels, the occasional clack of a gate.

There was, moreover, a note of preparation—holiday edging toward licence. Though the long autumn vacation was still two months away, another important day approached first: Madame’s fête.

The management of it fell chiefly on Mademoiselle St. Pierre. Madame, of course, maintained the air of disinterested ignorance; she never knew, never suspected, that a subscription was annually levied on pupils and teachers to purchase her a handsome present. One must, out of politeness, forget a brief, secret consultation on this point held in Madame’s own chamber.

“What will you have this year?” St. Pierre asked, chin already set.

“Oh, no matter—let it alone. Let the poor children keep their francs,” Madame replied, with a benign modesty worn like a lace cap.

St. Pierre’s chin protruded more. She called Madame’s displays of bonté “grimaces,” and never even pretended to respect them. “Vite!” she said. “Name the article. Jewellery or porcelain? Haberdashery or silver?”

“Eh bien! Deux ou trois cuillers, et autant de fourchettes en argent.”

And so it became a handsome case of plate worth three hundred francs—forks and spoons sleeping in velvet, to be awakened and flashed at table.

The programme of fête-day ran thus: the presentation, a collation in the garden, a dramatic performance with pupils and teachers as actors, then a dance and supper. To my eyes it looked gorgeous—flowers and white dresses, ribbons, and bright voices floating among the greenery. St. Pierre understood such arrangements; she managed them with the neatness of one who can set out a table so that every dish shines, and yet can taste nothing.

The play was the main labour. It demanded a month’s drilling: choosing actors, teaching them to speak, to hold themselves, to move; then rehearsal on rehearsal till their little parts were worn into them like a pattern into cloth. For this St. Pierre did not suffice. Another power was needed, and it came in the person of M. Paul Emanuel, professor of literature.

I was never present at his histrionic lessons; but I saw him often crossing the carré—quick, intent, as if always on his way to rebuke some error. In warm evenings, when doors stood open, I heard his voice ring through the place. His name, with anecdotes of him, went round the house like a refrain. Miss Ginevra Fanshawe, chosen for a prominent part, seasoned all her talk to me with him—as if she sprinkled a dish with pepper, not because she loved it, but because it gave her an agreeable thrill to sneeze. She called him hideously plain, and professed herself frightened into hysterics at the sound of his step; yet she watched him with a curious attention, like one who cannot resist listening to thunder.

He was a dark little man; pungent and austere. His close-shorn black head, broad sallow brow, thin cheek, wide quivering nostril, and thorough glance gave him, even at a distance, the look of a keen instrument. Irritable he was; one heard that, as he apostrophized his awkward squad. Sometimes impatience broke from him like a snapped cord. “Ecoutez!” he would cry, and his voice—clear, vehement—rang through the premises like a trumpet. Then would come the thin pipe of a Ginevra, a Mathilde, a Blanche, trying to echo him; and one understood the groan of scorn, the fierce hiss of rage, that answered their tame imitation.

“Vous n’êtes donc que des poupées!” I heard him thunder once. “Vous n’avez pas de passions. Vous ne sentez donc rien? Votre chair est de neige, votre sang de glace! Moi, je veux que tout cela s’allume, qu’il ait une vie, une âme!”

Vain resolve. When at last he found it vain, he broke the whole business down. Hitherto he had been drilling them in grand tragedy; he tore the tragedy to morsels, and came next day with a compact comic trifle—light enough for their smooth pates to carry without strain. To this they took more kindly; he knocked it into them with brisk blows of instruction.

Mademoiselle St. Pierre always presided at his lessons. I was told her polished manner, her tact and grace, pleased him greatly. She could please whom she would—for an hour. Then the feeling dried, vanished like dew.

The day before the fête was holiday too, given up to clearing, cleaning, arranging, decorating the schoolrooms. Indoors there was such bustle that a quiet person could not plant a foot without being jostled. I escaped to the garden. All day I wandered or sat there, taking warmth from the sun, shade from the trees, and a kind of company from my own thoughts. I spoke but two sentences to any living being, and did not feel lonely; I was glad to be still. Once or twice I passed through the rooms as a looker-on, saw the changes: a green-room contrived, a dressing-room planned, a little stage erected with scenery; and there, directing all with St. Pierre, was M. Paul Emanuel, while a merry band of pupils—Ginevra among them—worked under his control.

The great day arrived. The sun rose hot and unclouded, and burned so till evening. Doors and windows were flung wide, and that gave a sense of summer freedom—freedom, indeed, was the day’s proclaimed order. Teachers and pupils came down to breakfast in dressing-gowns and curl-papers, indulging forenoon slovenliness with delight, as if fasting aldermen permitted themselves a last plain crust before the feast.

About nine o’clock, the coiffeur arrived—an important functionary. Sacrilegious as it seems to confess, he fixed his head-quarters in the oratory; and there, in presence of bénitier, candle, and crucifix, he solemnised the mysteries of his art. Each girl in turn passed through his hands and emerged with hair smoothed like a shell, intersected by faultless white lines, and wreathed in Grecian plaits that shone as if lacquered. When my turn came, I hardly believed the glass. The garlandry of woven brown hair amazed me; I feared it was not all my own, and tugged at it to make sure. The artist had made the most of indifferent materials—like a cook who, with patient beating and careful seasoning, can coax a lightness out of stubborn dough.

Then the oratory closed, and the dormitory became a steaming scene of ablutions, arrayings, and bedizenings. To me it was always an enigma how so much time could be spent in doing so little. The process was intricate and prolonged; the result simple: clear white muslin, a blue sash—the Virgin’s colours—white or straw-coloured kid gloves. Three mortal hours were devoted to that uniform. Yet the effect was perfect in fit and freshness; every head dressed with compact taste—suiting the firm, full comeliness of Labassecourien outlines, though too stiff for any more flowing style.

Amid that diaphanous snowy mass, I felt myself a shadowy spot on a field of light. I had not courage to put on transparent white. Something thin I must wear—the weather and rooms were too hot for substantial fabrics—so I hunted through a dozen shops till I found a crape-like material of purple-grey, the colour of dun mist lying on a moor in bloom. My tailleuse made it as well as she could; as she wisely observed, since it was “si triste—si peu voyant,” care in fashion was more imperative. I had no flower, no jewel to relieve it; and, worse, no natural rose of complexion.

Daily routine lets us forget such deficiencies; but bright occasions force them on us, as a feast forces one to notice who has no appetite.

Still, in that gown of shadow I felt at home—an ease I should not have had in anything brilliant. Madame Beck herself kept me in countenance; her dress was almost as quiet as mine, except for a bracelet and a brooch bright with gold and fine stones. We met on the stairs; she nodded and smiled approval. Not that she thought me handsome—beauty seldom engaged her—but she deemed me convenable, décente; and Convenance and Décence were the two calm deities she worshipped. She even paused, laid her gloved hand on my shoulder—holding an embroidered, perfumed handkerchief—and whispered a sarcasm on the other teachers, whom she had just complimented aloud. “Nothing so absurd,” she said, “as for des femmes mûres to dress themselves like girls of fifteen—quant à la St. Pierre, elle a l’air d’une vieille coquette qui fait l’ingénue.”

Dressed a couple of hours before the rest, I did not go to the garden, where servants were propping long tables, placing seats, spreading cloths for the collation. The thought of all that food being arranged—the cold meats glazed, the cakes stacked, fruit washed and piled, bottles set in rows—might have tempted some; but the clamour of preparation repelled me. I went instead to the schoolrooms, now empty, cool, and clean: walls fresh stained, floors newly scoured and still scarcely dry; flowers fresh gathered in pots; draperies newly hung to soften the great windows.

I withdrew to the first classe, smaller and neater than the others, and took from the glazed bookcase—of which I kept the key—a volume whose title promised interest. The glass-door of the room opened into the large berceau. Acacia boughs caressed its panes as they stretched across to meet a rose-bush blooming by the opposite lintel; in that rose-bush bees murmured, busy and content. I began to read. The stilly hum, the embowering shade, the warm lonely calm, started to steal meaning from the page and lure me into reverie—as if the garden’s natural music were washing the harder noises of the house from my mind.

Then the street-door bell rang—sharper than any peal that much-tried instrument had yet produced—and snapped the thread of my dream.

The bell had rung all morning: workmen, servants, coiffeurs, tailleuses, coming and going. It would ring all afternoon too, as a hundred externes arrived in carriages and fiacres; and in the evening, parents and friends would gather for the play. A ring—even a sharp one—should have been nothing. Yet this peal had an accent of its own. It startled my book from my knee.

As I stooped to pick it up, I heard a step—firm, fast, straight—cutting through the house: through vestibule, along corridor, across carré, past the divisions, into the grand salle. The closed door of my little classe offered no obstacle; it burst open. A paletôt and a bonnet grec filled the space; and two eyes, first vague, then hungry, dived into me.

“C’est cela!” said a voice. “Je la connais: c’est l’Anglaise. Tant pis. Toute Anglaise, et, par conséquent, toute bégueule qu’elle soit—elle fera mon affaire, ou je saurai pourquoi.”

Then, with a stern politeness—as if he thought I had not caught his mutterings—and in a jargon of English so mangled it seemed chewed rather than spoken: “Meess—play you must: I am planted there.”

“What can I do for you, M. Paul Emanuel?” I asked; for it was he, and he was in a state of no little excitement.

“Play you must. I will not have you shrink, or frown, or make the prude. I read your skull that night you came; I see your moyens: play you can; play you must.”

“But how, M. Paul? What do you mean?”

“No time to lose,” he said now in French, brisk as a knife. “Let us thrust to the wall reluctance, excuses, minauderies. You must take a part.”

“In the vaudeville?”

“In the vaudeville. You have said it.”

I drew breath as if I had been struck. What did the little man mean to do with me—me, who would rather have been set to scrub the boards than stand in public mimicry?

“Listen,” he commanded. “The case shall be stated, and you shall answer Yes or No; and according to your answer shall I ever after estimate you.”

His cheek was lit with the glow of a nature always near ignition. His glances shot sharp as skewers; and I saw how quickly the injudicious, the hesitating, the sullen, the affected—above all, the unyielding—might make him violent and implacable. Silence was safest; I listened.

“The whole matter is going to fail,” he began. “Louise Vanderkelkov has fallen ill—at least so her ridiculous mother asserts. For my part, she might play if she would: it is only good-will that lacks. She had a rôle—whether you know it or not is equal—without that rôle the play stops. There are but a few hours to learn it. Not a girl here will accept it. It is not an interesting part, not an amiable part; their vile amour-propre—that base quality of which women have so much—revolts. Englishwomen are either the best or the worst of their sex. Dieu sait que je les déteste comme la peste, ordinairement—” this he bit out between teeth that seemed to grind the words—“but I apply to an Englishwoman to rescue me. What is her answer—Yes, or No?”

A thousand objections crowded me: the foreign tongue, the scant time, the public exposure. Inclination recoiled; ability faltered; self-respect trembled. “Non, non, non,” cried everything within. Yet when I looked up and met his vexed, fiery searching eye, I saw behind its menace something like appeal—almost need. My lips dropped the word “Oui.”

For a moment his rigid countenance relaxed, a quick quiver of content passing over it; then it bent again to business.

“Vite à l’ouvrage! Here is the book; here is your rôle: read.”

I read. He did not praise; at some passages he scowled and stamped. He gave a lesson; I imitated as diligently as I could. The part was disagreeable—a man’s part, an empty-headed fop’s. One could put into it neither heart nor soul: it was like being asked to cook with no fire, to season with no salt. I hated it. The play itself was a trifle: two rivals striving for the hand of a fair coquette. One lover was called the Ours—good, gallant, unpolished, a rough diamond; the other was a butterfly—a talker and traitor. I was to be that butterfly.

I did my best—which was bad. It provoked M. Paul; he fumed. I strained to do better than my best. Perhaps he gave me credit for good intention, for he grew partially content. “Ça ira!” he cried at last. Then, as voices began sounding from the garden and white dresses fluttered among the trees, he added, “You must withdraw. You must be alone to learn this. Come with me.”

Without time to deliberate, I was swept along as in a whirlwind—up-stairs, up two flights, indeed up three; that fiery little man moved as if the house were mapped in his blood. He bore me to a solitary lofty attic, put me in, locked me in. The key turned; he took it and vanished.

The attic was no pleasant place. In summer it was hot as Africa; in winter, they said, cold as Greenland. Boxes and lumber filled it; old dresses hung against the bare wall; cobwebs netted the ceiling. It was known to be tenanted by rats, black beetles, and cockroaches; rumour even affirmed that the ghostly Nun of the garden had once been seen there. At one end the light fell away into partial darkness, and across that gloom an old russet curtain was drawn, screening a sombre row of winter cloaks hanging each from its pin like malefactors from gibbets. From behind that curtain, they said, the Nun would issue.

I did not believe in the Nun; I believed, immediately and against my will, in the vermin. A large dark rat with a long tail glided from that squalid alcove, smooth and quick as a shadow spilled. My eye fell on black beetles dotting the floor like burnt currants scattered from a careless hand. Those sights discomposed me more than I care to confess. The dust, the heat, the stale smell of stored cloth and old wood—everything in that place oppressed the senses.

The heat would soon have been intolerable had I not opened and propped the skylight, letting in a slice of air. Under it I pushed a large empty chest, set a smaller box upon it, wiped the dust from both, gathered my dress fastidiously around me—my best, and therefore a legitimate object of care—climbed up to this makeshift throne, and sat down to learn my rôle. And while I repeated the lines—French words tasting foreign and dry on the tongue—I kept a sharp watch on beetles and cockroaches, of which, even more than of rats, I sat in mortal dread.
