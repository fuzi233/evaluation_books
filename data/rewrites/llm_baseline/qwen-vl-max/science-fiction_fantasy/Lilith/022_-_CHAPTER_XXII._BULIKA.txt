I had lost all sense of direction, wandering in a state of restless confusion, when I stumbled into the path of a leopardess—her paw submerged in a crimson tide that surged against my ankles like a small stream. The force of it startled me, and I recoiled instantly, driven not only by instinct but by a vague, chilling suspicion: whose blood was this? Yet I did not flee entirely. Instead, I followed the sound—the steady, whispering rush of liquid—upward along its course, trusting it to lead me toward Bulika.

But soon doubt crept in. No beast, however vast, could sustain such a flow unless every vein in its body were torn open and replenished from some endless source. That made no sense. I dipped a finger into the current. It was cool, clear, and utterly unlike blood. It was water—softly murmuring, flowing unchannelled across the grass as if guided by unseen hands. A river without banks, born of mystery.

Still, I dared not drink. The taste of it might be poison, or worse—some elixir of illusion. But the mere touch of moisture on my skin revived me. My steps grew lighter, my breath steadier. I walked on, drawn by the faint glow of dawn breaking through the night’s veil.

Then, just as the sky began to pale, I saw it: a city rising against the horizon—its towers ancient, its walls weathered by time itself. It stood silent, solemn, as though carved from dreams long forgotten. I looked back at the stream. It was gone. Only the bent grass marked its path, and scattered pools glistened where it had once flowed. The fountain must have ceased somewhere near—perhaps within the city’s embrace.

Around Bulika lay gardens thick with unfamiliar plants—vegetables I couldn’t name, growing in neat rows beneath a sky too still for birds. There was no water here, no flowers, no signs of animals. The gardens pressed close to the walls, separated by mounds of gravel and refuse—waste thrown from above, perhaps by those who lived within.

The nearest gate stood half-open, unguarded, unbarred. Its hinges groaned with age, fixed in place as if neither opened nor closed since the beginning of time. I stepped inside.

The street stretched before me—long, empty, silent. Not a soul stirred. No footsteps echoed. No breath broke the stillness. Had I entered a city of the dead?

I turned back, climbed over heaps of dust, crossed several roads leading to other gates. I would not return until life stirred again. What was I seeking? What did I hope to find? I didn’t know. But I needed to see her again—the woman I had awakened. Not for love, not for companionship. She had awoken something dark in me—fears, suspicions, a pull toward the unknown. And yet, her presence held a power I could not resist. To understand her—to grasp even a fragment of her nature—was to gaze into wonders beyond imagination.

But I was reckless. Man should not seek knowledge at the cost of his soul. Yet I had done something irrevocable. I had brought back a force that was meant to die. And now, I bore responsibility for what might follow. I had learned she hated children. The Little Ones—were they in danger?

That was why I came. To learn their fate. To protect them. To understand.

Eventually, movement stirred in the city. I passed through another gate, wandered down narrow alleys lined with tall, shadowed houses, until I reached a small square. There, I sat on the base of a pillar crowned by a grotesque bat-like figure, watching the world wake.

Soon, figures appeared—slow, indifferent. I spoke to one. He glared, spat a curse, and walked on. I rose and continued deeper into the maze of streets, finding more people, all moving with the same dull rhythm. No children. Not one.

Then, near a gate, I encountered a group of young men—tall, broad-shouldered, with faces like those of the giants I’d met before. They surrounded me, eyes narrowing, then shoved, laughed, threw stones. I endured it. I didn’t want conflict. I wanted only to observe, to survive.

I appealed to others—those who seemed kinder—but none stopped. I was poor. That was enough. In Bulika, poverty was a crime. Deformity, sickness—they taxed these things. And the laws of their princess, which ensured the weak served the strong, were obeyed with grim pride.

Finally, I fled. No one chased me past the gate. Except one—a slow, clumsy man sitting there, eating bread. He picked up a stone to throw. But in his clumsiness, he hurled the bread instead.

I took it. He didn’t dare come after me. Beyond the walls, they were cowards.

I walked a short distance, lay down in the grass, ate the bread, and fell asleep. The sun warmed me, renewed my strength. When I woke, it was night. The moon hung high, bright and familiar—like an old friend. I thought it was the same moon that had watched over me during my first terrifying night in this world.

A cold wind blew from the city gate, carrying with it a sour, rotten smell. But it didn’t chill me. The sun had filled me with warmth.

I crept back into the city. Those few still awake huddled in corners, shivering against the blast.

I walked slowly through the narrow streets, when suddenly—a flash of white leapt across the road ahead. A great shape, glowing under the moonlight, bounded forward and vanished into the shadows.

I turned down a side lane—so narrow it barely allowed passage—but it led me to a wider street. As I stepped out, I saw it again: the creature, following something that moved like a man.

It was a shadow—but not like any shadow I had known. It was flat, two-dimensional, yet solid. Opaque. Where it stood, nothing behind it could be seen—not because it blocked light, but because it *consumed* sight. In darkness, it was blacker than night. In moonlight, it wore its own shadow like a cloak—no separate outline, no trailing form. It was a being of pure absence.

And behind it, stalking silently, was the leopardess—white as bone, radiant in the silver light. Her shadow slid beside her, dark and smooth, like ink on paper.

They moved together. From shadow into light. The Shadow deepened, becoming almost void. The leopardess blazed, glowing with inner fire.

I stood opposite, bare feet tapping softly on stone. The leopardess never glanced my way. But the Shadow—once, it seemed to turn. I lost its profile. For a heartbeat, I saw only a sharp vertical line, a blade of darkness.

Then the wind struck me—cold, sudden, piercing. It passed *through* me. I shuddered. My heart pounded like a pebble trapped in a child’s rattle, bouncing wildly against the walls of my chest.

And in that instant, I knew: I was no longer alone. Something had seen me. Something older, darker, far more real than I had ever imagined.
