The Curates at Tea.

Caroline Helstone was eighteen—just eighteen—and at that age, life’s true story had scarcely begun. Before then, we live in a world of dreams, listening to tales that are wondrous and sometimes heartbreaking, but always tinged with fantasy. Our world is filled with heroes and half-gods, shadowy forests and glowing skies, rivers that roar like thunder and flowers that bloom with impossible sweetness. The moon we gaze upon glows with a brilliance no earthly light can match; the sun burns not just as a star, but as a divine fire, the home of gods.

At eighteen, we stand on the edge of illusion, looking toward reality. Behind us lies the enchanted land of childhood—the realm of elves and magic. Ahead, the shores of truth rise slowly into view, distant and hazy, yet promising peace and clarity. We see green meadows beneath the blue horizon, silver streams shimmering in the sunlight, and believe that if only we could reach them, we would never hunger or thirst again. But between us and that promised land lie deserts, floods, and rivers darker than death. True joy must be earned—not given. And those who have fought for great things know how deeply it costs: the heart’s blood stains the brow of the warrior before victory’s crown rests upon it.

At eighteen, we don’t know this. Hope smiles upon us, and we believe her promises without question. Love arrives like a lost angel at our door, and we welcome him instantly, embracing him without fear. We do not see his quiver, nor feel the sting of his arrows—only the warmth of a new awakening. There is no thought of poison, no dread of wounds that cannot heal. Passion, though it may one day become agony for some, feels now like pure delight. At eighteen, we step into the school of experience—but we do not yet know its lessons: humbling, crushing, grinding, yet purifying and strengthening.

Alas, Experience! No other teacher bears such a cold face, wears such a black robe, or wields such a heavy rod. None so sternly drags the novice into duty, none compels learning with such unrelenting force. Yet without her, how many stumble through life? How many trespass on forbidden ground, fall down deadly slopes?

Caroline, returned home by Robert Moore, had no wish to spend the evening with her uncle. His room was sacred territory—she rarely entered it. Tonight, she stayed away until the bell rang for prayers. In Mr. Helstone’s household, part of the evening service was read aloud from the Bible. He recited it in his usual nasal tone—clear, loud, and monotonous. When it ended, Caroline approached him as she always did.

"Good-night, uncle."

"Hey! You've been out all day—visiting, dining, whatnot!"

"Just at the cottage."

"And your lessons?"

"Yes."

"And the shirt?"

"Only half done."

"Well, that’ll do. Stick to your needle. Learn to make shirts, gowns, piecrusts—and you’ll be a clever woman someday. Now go to bed. I’m busy with a pamphlet."

Soon, Caroline was alone in her small bedroom. The door bolted, her white dressing gown draped over her shoulders, her long hair loose and flowing like waves to her waist. Pausing after combing it, she leaned her cheek on her hand, staring at the carpet. And there, rising around her, came the visions of eighteen.

Her thoughts spoke to her, gently, soothingly. She smiled as she listened. She looked lovely in quiet contemplation—but lovelier still was the spirit within her: youthful hope. According to this hopeful voice, disappointment was behind her. She had stepped into morning—a real spring dawn—and her sun would soon rise. She couldn’t imagine being deceived. Her expectations seemed grounded in truth.

"When people love," she mused, "they marry. I love Robert. I know he loves me. I’ve believed it before, but today I *felt* it. When I looked up after reading Chénier’s poem, his eyes—how handsome they are—spoke directly to my heart. Sometimes I fear speaking to him, afraid I might be too bold, too forward. I’ve regretted words said too freely, worried I’ve revealed more than he expected. But tonight… tonight I could have told him anything. He was so kind. So gentle walking up the lane. He doesn’t flatter or say silly things. His way of loving—friendship, really, though I don’t yet call him my lover—yet I hope he will be—is better than any I’ve read about. It’s original, quiet, strong, honest. I truly like him. I’d make a good wife. I’d tell him his faults—yes, he has a few—but I’d care for him, comfort him, try to make him happy. I’m sure he won’t be cold tomorrow. I think… I *know*… he’ll come here or ask me to go to him."

She resumed combing her hair—long, thick, like a mermaid’s. Turning her head, she caught her reflection in the mirror. For plain women, such sights sober the mind—seeing themselves without enchantment, knowing others see no charm. But for the fair, the image is captivating, and must be so. Caroline saw a graceful form, a face that, captured in that moment, would have been beautiful. She could not help drawing strength from the sight. With renewed joy, she went to bed.

And with the same joy, she rose the next morning. As she entered her uncle’s breakfast room, greeting him softly, even the stern old man paused, thinking for a moment that his niece was growing “a fine girl.” Usually quiet and shy with him, she was unusually talkative today. Only harmless topics were allowed between them—Mr. Helstone never discussed deeper matters with a woman. She told him about flowers blooming in the garden, asked when the gardener would trim the borders, mentioned that starlings were building nests in the church tower (Briarfield Church stood near the rectory), and wondered why the bells didn’t scare them.

“Like all fools who’ve just paired,” he replied, “they’re blind to inconvenience.”

Encouraged by her spirits, Caroline ventured a remark she’d never dared before.

“Uncle,” she said, “you always speak of marriage with scorn. Do you think people shouldn’t marry?”

“It’s wisest to stay single—especially women.”

“Are all marriages unhappy?”

“Millions are. If everyone told the truth, perhaps all are, more or less.”

“You’re always annoyed when asked to marry a couple. Why?”

“Because I don’t like being an accomplice to pure folly.”

He spoke easily, clearly pleased to share his views. Emboldened, Caroline pressed further.

“But why is it folly? If two people care for each other, why shouldn’t they choose to live together?”

“They grow tired of each other—within a month. A spouse isn’t a companion. They’re a fellow-sufferer.”

It wasn’t innocence that prompted her next words—it was discomfort, a sense of opposition to his beliefs.

“One would think you’d never been married, uncle. One would think you were an old bachelor.”

“Practically, I am.”

“But you *were* married. Why did you marry, then?”

“Every man is mad once or twice in his life.”

“So you grew tired of Aunt Helstone, and she of you? You were miserable?”

Mr. Helstone pursed his lips, wrinkled his brow, and grunted.

“Did she not suit you? Was she ill-tempered? Did you grow used to her? Were you sorry when she died?”

“Caroline,” he said, lowering his hand slowly toward the table, then slamming it down sharply, “understand this: it’s vulgar and childish to confuse general truths with individual cases. Every rule has exceptions. Your questions are stupid, infantile. Ring the bell. Breakfast is over.”

After the meal, the pair usually parted until dinner. But today, Caroline remained. She sat on the window seat, watching the garden. Mr. Helstone glanced at her uneasily—wishing she’d leave—but she paid no attention. He returned to his newspaper, which carried exciting news from the Peninsula, including long dispatches from General Lord Wellington.

Meanwhile, Caroline’s mind swirled with thoughts—thoughts stirred by their conversation, but not born of it. They buzzed like bees disturbed in a hive. These thoughts had first taken root years ago, but now they surged anew.

She reflected on her uncle’s character, his opinions—especially about marriage. Again and again, she had compared them to her own feelings, seeing the vast chasm between them. On the other side of that divide stood another figure—faint, shadowy, almost unreal—the half-remembered image of her father, James Helstone, brother to Matthewson Helstone.

Whispers had reached her about him. Old servants had hinted. She knew he was not a good man. She remembered little—only dark fragments. A time spent with him in a great city, where she had no maid, locked in a bare garret room with no carpet, a bed without curtains, scarcely any furniture. He left early every morning, often forgetting to return for her meals. When he came back at night, he was either furious, raving, or worse—stupid, helpless, senseless. She fell ill. One night, he burst into her room, shouting he’d kill her because she was a burden. Her screams brought help. From that moment, she never saw him alive again—only in his coffin.

That was her father. And she had a mother—though Mr. Helstone never spoke of her, though she could not remember ever seeing her. But she knew her mother lived. This woman was the drunkard’s wife. What had their marriage been?

Turning from the lattice, where she had watched the starlings (though unseen), Caroline broke the silence in a low, bitter voice:

“You call marriage miserable, I suppose, because of my parents. If my mother suffered as I did with Papa, she must have lived a terrible life.”

Mr. Helstone turned sharply in his chair, peering over his spectacles.

“Your father and mother? Who’s been talking to you about them?”

“No one. But I remember something of Papa. And I pity Mama. Where is she?”

The question had been on her lips for years—but never spoken.

“I hardly know,” he replied. “I barely knew her. I haven’t heard from her in years. Wherever she is, she thinks nothing of you. She never asks about you. I believe she doesn’t want to see you. Come, it’s time for school. You’re going to your cousin at ten, aren’t you? The clock has struck.”

Perhaps she would have said more—but Fanny entered, informing Mr. Helstone the churchwardens wished to speak with him in the vestry. He hurried off, and Caroline set out for the cottage.

The path from the rectory to Hollow’s Mill sloped downward. She ran most of the way. Exercise, fresh air, and the thought of seeing Robert lifted her spirits. Approaching the white house, hearing the mill’s thunder and rushing water, she saw Moore at his garden gate.

He stood there in his belted Holland blouse, a light cap on his head—a look that suited him well. He was looking down the lane, not toward her. She stopped, hiding behind a willow, studying him.

“He has no equal,” she thought. “He’s as handsome as he is wise. What a sharp eye! What clear, spirited features—thin and serious, yet graceful. I like his face. I like him—more than any of those clumsy curates—more than anyone. Bonny Robert!”

She hurried to meet him. But when she appeared, Robert seemed to shrink from her presence. He would have vanished like a ghost—if he could. But he was flesh and blood, so he endured the greeting. It was brief—cousinly, brotherly, friendly—but not romantic. The magic of last night was gone. The same man stood before her—but not the same heart beat in his chest.

Rude disappointment. Sharp pain. At first, she refused to believe it, though she felt it. It was hard to pull her hand away from his, wanting at least a warm squeeze. It was hard to turn her eyes from his, hoping for more than a cool nod.

A man in love might explain. A woman in love can say nothing. To speak would bring shame, guilt, self-betrayal. Nature would punish such weakness with the lightning strike of self-contempt. Accept what is. Ask no questions. Say nothing. That is wisdom. You expected bread—you got a stone. Break your teeth on it, don’t scream. Your mental stomach, if you have one, is stronger than an ostrich’s. The stone will digest. You held out your hand for an egg—fate gave you a scorpion. Close your fingers tightly. Let it sting. Don’t flinch. In time, your hand will swell, your arm will tremble—but the scorpion will die. And you will learn the great lesson: how to endure without a sob.

For the rest of your life—if you survive—your strength will grow, your wisdom deepen, your sensitivity fade. You may not know this now. But nature, your ally in sorrow, seals your lips, silences your voice, commands calm. At first, you wear a mask of ease. Later, it settles into sadness, paleness. Then it fades, leaving stoicism—bitter, yes, but powerful.

Bitter? Yes. Bitterness is strength. Sweetness after suffering is a myth. After torment, there may be numbness. Or energy—dangerous energy, deadly when faced with injustice.

Have you read the ballad of “Puir Mary Lee”? An old Scottish song, author unknown. Mary was wronged—perhaps made to believe a lie passed for truth. She does not complain. She sits alone in a snowstorm, and we hear her thoughts. Not heroic thoughts, but those of a peasant girl, deep in grief, fierce in resentment. Anguish has driven her from the hearth to the icy hills. Crouched under the cold drift, she recalls horrors: “the yellow-wymed ask,” “the hairy adder,” “the auld moon-bowing tyke,” “the ghaist at e’en,” “the sour bullister,” “the milk on the taed’s back.” She hates them—but worse, she hates Robin-a-Ree.

“Oh, ance I lived happily by yon bonny burn—
The warld was in love wi’ me;
But now I maun sit 'neath the cauld drift and mourn,
And curse black Robin-a-Ree!

“Then whudder awa, thou bitter biting blast,
And sough through the scrunty tree,
And smoor me up in the snaw fu' fast,
And n'er let the sun me see!

“Oh, never melt awa, thou wreath o' snaw,
That's sae kind in graving me;
But hide me frae the scorn and guffaw
O' villains like Robin-a-Ree!”

But these reflections do not belong to Caroline Helstone—or to the situation between her and Robert. Robert had done her no harm. He had told her no lie. If blame existed, it was hers. Any bitterness she felt should be poured upon herself. She had loved without being asked—a natural, sometimes inevitable risk—but one fraught with pain.

Robert had shown affection at times. Why? Because she had made herself pleasing to him. Despite his efforts, he could not suppress his feelings—even though his judgment disapproved, his will resisted. He was preparing to withdraw from close contact with her. He did not want his emotions entangled, nor to be drawn into a marriage he considered imprudent.

So what was she to do? Give in to her heart, or master it? Pursue him, or turn inward? If weak, she would chase him—losing his respect, gaining his dislike. If wise, she would govern herself, resolve to tame her emotions, to face life honestly, to learn its harsh truths, to study its difficult puzzles with seriousness and conscience.

She showed signs of wisdom. She left Robert quietly—without complaint, without tears. She returned to her studies with Hortense as usual. At dinner, she went home without lingering.

After eating, finding herself alone in the rectory drawing-room while her uncle lingered over port, she faced a new problem: “How shall I get through this day?”

Last night, she had hoped it would be like yesterday—filled with happiness, with Robert. Today, she knew her mistake. Yet she could not accept that no chance would bring her back to Hollow’s Cottage, or bring Robert into her life again.

He had walked up after tea more than once to visit her uncle. The bell would ring, his voice echo in the hall at twilight—when she least expected joy. This had happened twice after he had treated her with unusual reserve. Though he rarely spoke to her in front of her uncle, he had looked at her with softness when seated across from her. The few words he offered were comforting. His farewell was warm.

Now, perhaps, he would come tonight, whispered False Hope. She knew it was False Hope—but she listened.

She tried to read—her thoughts wandered. She tried to sew—each stitch was boredom. She opened her desk, attempted a French composition—only errors emerged.

Suddenly, the doorbell rang sharply. Her heart leapt. She rushed to the door, opened it slightly, peered out. Fanny was admitting a visitor—a gentleman—tall, exactly Robert’s height. For one second, she thought it was him. She exulted. Then the voice asked for Mr. Helstone. Irish. Not Robert. Malone, the curate.

He was led into the dining room, where, no doubt, he helped the rector empty the decanters.
