At eight o’clock the following morning, Nathaniel Burton Cupples stood on the veranda of the Marlstone hotel, contemplating breakfast with his usual deliberation. The previous day’s excitement had disrupted his appetite, leaving him unusually hungry. He decided to compensate with an extra piece of toast and an additional egg—adjustments he would balance at luncheon.

Having settled the matter, Cupples turned his attention to the view. His connoisseur’s eye traced the rugged coastline, where a pierced rock jutted from the glassy sea, and the patchwork of pastures and woodlands sloped toward distant moors. A man of orderly habits and precise thought, he took quiet pleasure in such landscapes.

Cupples was spare of frame, nearing sixty, with a wiry vigor that belied his delicate constitution. His sparse beard and sharp nose lent him a clerical air, reinforced by his somber attire. A retired banker and widower, he was a man of methodical intellect, devoid of imagination but rich in kindness. His life revolved around books and museums, his mind a repository of eclectic knowledge.

As he finished his meal, a motor car pulled up the drive. “Who is that?” Cupples asked the waiter.

“Der manager,” came the indifferent reply. “He meet a gendleman from der train.”

The car halted, and a tall, loosely built man sprang onto the veranda, flinging his hat onto a chair. His face was high-boned and animated, his tweeds disheveled.

“Cupples, by all that’s miraculous!” the newcomer exclaimed, seizing Cupples’ hand. “My luck’s in today. Second stroke already! How are you, my dear fellow? And why this ruined breakfast?”

“I half expected you, Trent,” Cupples replied warmly. “You look splendid. Join me—unless you’ve eaten?”

“An enormous breakfast,” Trent declared, vanishing inside to wash.

Cupples took the opportunity to telephone. When he returned, Trent was already seated, attacking a plate of food with gusto. “I suspect this will be a long day,” Trent said between bites. “I’ll need fortification. You’ve guessed why I’m here?”

“Undoubtedly,” Cupples said. “The murder.”

“A colorless way of putting it,” Trent grinned. “I prefer ‘avenger of blood.’ The *Record* sent me to hunt the guilty and restore society’s honor. Speaking of which—” He leaned forward. “Your manager’s a marvel. Met me at the station, whisked me straight to the corpse. It’s still in Dr. Stock’s surgery, untouched. Post-mortem’s today, so I barely made it.”

“I saw the body,” Cupples remarked. “Remarkably little disfigurement, considering. Bruised wrists, though.”

“Ah, the wrists.” Trent’s eyes gleamed. “Manderson always wore stiff cuffs, but they were pushed up when he was found. Suggestive, no? The manager thought it proof of haste—shoelaces hastily tied, teeth forgotten. But then why the meticulous hair parting? Why fully dressed, watch in the wrong pocket? Signs of agitation, yet also deliberation.”

Cupples nodded. “A contradiction. But I may assist you. Mrs. Manderson is my niece.”

Trent nearly choked. “You’re joking.”

“Not at all. Her father was my late wife’s brother. I’ve avoided speaking of her marriage—it’s been painful. Last night, her secretary, Bunner, mentioned hoping the *Record* would send you. Mabel remembered your work on the Abinger case. She loathes publicity but agreed to cooperate if you came.”

Trent absorbed this, then lit his pipe. “Cupples, is there something about the Mandersons you’re withholding?”

Cupples hesitated. “Manderson was… unloved. Even by his wife. He had a coldness, a lack of empathy. Mabel was miserable, though she’d never admit it. A week ago, she wrote to me for help.”

“Did she love him?” Trent pressed.

Cupples sighed. “I think not. But pride kept her silent. Manderson was outwardly generous, yet privately cruel. When I confronted him, he dismissed me. Said, ‘This has gone far enough.’ Next morning, he was dead.”

They walked in silence, the sea breeze ruffling their hair.

“You told me all this,” Trent mused, “because you feared I’d suspect Mabel.”

Cupples met his gaze. “Precisely. I wanted you to understand.”

Trent exhaled smoke. “Then let’s find the truth.”
