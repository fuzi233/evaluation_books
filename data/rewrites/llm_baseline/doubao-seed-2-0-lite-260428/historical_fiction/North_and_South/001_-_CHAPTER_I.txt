“Edith!” said Margaret, soft as the rustle of the white muslin draped over the sofa cushions. “Edith!”
As she half-expected, her cousin was fast asleep. Edith lay curled on the crimson damask sofa in the back Harley Street drawing room, her blue ribbons askew and her silk curls spilling over the arm, looking so soft and glowing that Titania herself, if ever caught napping in muslin and blue ribbons on a velvet sofa, might have been mistaken for her instead. Margaret’s breath caught a little at the sight. They had grown up side by side from childhood, and every acquaintance save Margaret had always gushed over Edith’s famous prettiness — but Margaret had never truly registered how much she cherished that charm until the last few days, when the looming loss of her constant companion sharpened every sweet, silly little quirk of Edith’s into something unforgettable.
They had been chattering nonstop only minutes before: of lace wedding dresses, of the ceremony, of Captain Lennox’s stories of their new posting at Corfu, of the near-impossibility of keeping a piano in tune in that salt-heavy Mediterranean air (a trial Edith had insisted was the worst fate that could befall a married woman), of the gowns she would need for their immediate post-wedding trip to Scotland. But their whispers had grown slower, sleepier, until Margaret, after pausing to watch her cousin, realized that Edith had rolled herself into a little ball of fabric and hair and drifted off into a peaceful post-dinner nap, even over the low buzz of guests in the front room.
Margaret had been just about to tell Edith of all the quiet plans she had made for her own return to the Helstone parsonage, the country home where her father was the clergyman, the place where she had spent every holiday of her childhood — even though for the last ten years, Aunt Shaw’s Harley Street house had been her official home, after her parents had decided London would give her a better education. With no one left to listen, she leaned back in her chair and brooded over the coming upheaval, soft and happy even as a faint sting of regret pricked at her at the thought of leaving her gentle aunt and dearest cousin behind for who knew how long. As she daydreamed of taking up her rightful place as the only daughter of the parsonage, snatches of conversation drifted in through the door from the front drawing room.
Aunt Shaw was talking to the half-dozen ladies who had joined them for dinner, their husbands still lingering over port in the dining room. These were their closest London acquaintances — neighbors Mrs. Shaw called friends, people who dropped in unannounced before luncheon to borrow sugar or return a book, who dined with the Shaws far more often than they did anyone else. They had all been invited to this farewell feast in honor of Edith’s upcoming nuptials, a plan Edith had hated at first, for she was expecting Captain Lennox on a late-evening train that very night. But spoiled, easygoing Edith had never been strong-willed enough to fight her mother for long, and she had caved the moment she saw Mrs. Shaw had ordered every rare, expensive seasonal delicacy supposed to soften the grief of a farewell dinner. She had contented herself with slumping in her chair, picking at her food and looking distant and grave, while the rest of the party laughed at the witticisms of Mr. Grey, the gentleman who always took the foot of Mrs. Shaw’s dinner table and always asked Edith to play for the company after the meal. Mr. Grey was in particularly fine form that night, and the gentlemen stayed downstairs far later than usual — a lucky thing, Margaret thought, as she caught the fragments of gossip drifting through the wall.
“I suffered so terribly myself, you know,” Mrs. Shaw was saying, her soft, plaintive voice carrying clear. “Not that I wasn’t happy with dear departed General Shaw, of course — but a disparity in age is such a terrible drawback. I swore Edith would never have to face that. No maternal partiality, I swear: I knew from the moment she turned sixteen she would marry very young. I always said she’d be wed before she turned nineteen. I had a perfect premonition the moment Captain Lennox —”
Her voice dropped to a whisper, and Margaret could easily fill in the rest. Edith’s romance had run far smoother than any in the acquaintance’s circle. Mrs. Shaw had leaned into that “premonition” of hers, actively pushing the wedding along even though many of Edith’s friends had thought a pretty, wealthy young heiress could aim far higher than a penniless army captain stationed half a world away. But Mrs. Shaw insisted her only child would marry for love, and sighed so heavily after the words that anyone not in on the secret might have thought her own marriage to the rich, respected General had been a grand great love match, rather than a practical arrangement she had entered into with nothing but cool respect for his position. She far more enjoyed the romance of Edith’s engagement than Edith herself did, in fact. Edith was certainly fond enough of Captain Lennox, but she would have traded all his stories of Corfu’s sunlit cliffs and impromptu barrack dances for a fine townhouse in Belgravia in a heartbeat. The same wild, gipsy-style makeshift life that made Margaret’s face glow with excitement when she heard of it, Edith pretended to shudder at — half to make her captain coax her out of her silly “dislike” with kisses and promises of grand furniture, half because she truly hated the thought of living without all the small comforts she was used to. If a duke with a grand estate and a title had come courting her, she might have wavered for a moment, but she would always have chosen Lennox in the end — though she would no doubt have pined quietly, later, that he could not be both the man she loved and the owner of a Belgravia house and a title. She was her mother’s daughter, after all: Mrs. Shaw, who had deliberately married the General for nothing warmer than respect for his character and his fortune, spent most of her quiet days softly bemoaning her terrible, unfortunate fate to be tied to a man she had never loved.
“I spared no expense on her trousseau,” were the next words Margaret caught, clear as day. “She’s got all the beautiful Indian shawls the General gave me, the ones I’ve not had the heart to wear since he died.”
“She’s a very lucky girl,” gushed Mrs. Gibson, whose daughter Helen had married only three weeks prior, and was thus far more invested in the conversation than the rest. “Helen has been pining after an Indian shawl for months, but when I saw the prices they ask for those things now I simply had to say no. She’ll be green with envy when she hears Edith has a whole set. Are they Delhi shawls? The ones with the tiny stitched borders?”
Margaret heard Aunt Shaw’s voice rise again, like she was pushing herself up from her couch to peer through the doorway into the dimmer back drawing room. “Edith! Edith!” she called, before sinking back against the cushions like the effort had tired her out completely.
Margaret stepped out of the shadow of the doorframe. “Edith’s asleep, Aunt Shaw. Is there anything I can help you with?”
All the ladies gasped “poor little dear!” at the news, and Mrs. Shaw’s tiny lapdog Tiny began yapping frantically, overexcited by the sudden burst of sympathetic noise. “Hush, you naughty little thing, you’ll wake your mistress,” Mrs. Shaw chided the dog. “I only meant to ask Edith to send Newton down with the shawls to show everyone. Would you run and do that for me, Margaret dear?”
Margaret climbed the three flights of stairs to the old top-floor nursery, where Newton, Edith’s lady’s maid, was hunched over a table mending lace for the wedding gown, grumbling under her breath about how the light up there was so bad she could barely see to stitch. Newton huffed and complained the whole time she was undoing the shawls, which had already been pulled out to show off four or five times that day, while Margaret wandered around the little room, her chest tight with nostalgia. This was the first room she had come to know in the Harley Street house nine years prior, when her father had brought her up from the New Forest, wild and sunburned and used to running barefoot through the woods, to live and learn and play alongside her cousin Edith. She remembered the dim, dark London nursery, ruled over by a strict, formal nurse who fussed endlessly over clean hands and torn frocks, her first tea eaten up there while her father and aunt dined somewhere what had felt like miles below, in the bowels of the earth as she had thought at the time. Back in Helstone, her mother’s dressing room had been her nursery, and she had always eaten every meal with her parents. She remembered the wild, bitter tears she had cried her very first night in that room, hiding under the bedclothes, the nurse telling her to be quiet so she would not wake Edith — how she had cried all the harder, quieter, until her pretty grand new aunt had come upstairs with her father to check on his little sleeping daughter. Nine-year-old Margaret had immediately hushed her sobs, pretending to be fast asleep so she would not make her father sad, so he would not think he had made a mistake leaving her here. Now, at eighteen, she loved the old dismantled nursery more than any other room in the house, and looked around it with a cat-like soft regret, knowing she would never step foot in it again three days from now.
“Ah, Newton,” she said. “I think we’ll all be sorry to leave this dear old place.”
“Not me, miss, not one bit,” Newton huffed. “My eyes aren’t what they used to be, and the light up here’s so bad I can barely mend lace unless I’m right by the window, and there’s a draught there strong enough to give you your death of cold. I’ll be right glad to get to Naples, where there’s proper warmth and proper light.”
“Well, I’m sure you will,” Margaret smiled. “Thank you, Newton, I can carry the shawls down myself. You get back to your mending.”
She trundled back down the stairs with the bundle of silk draped over her arms, breathing in their sharp, spicy Eastern scent. With Edith still fast asleep on the sofa, Aunt Shaw had Margaret stand in her place as a living mannequin, draping the long, gorgeous shawls over her tall, slender figure. Margaret was wearing her black mourning silk for a distant relative of her father’s that day, and the dark fabric made the bright, rich colors of the shawls glow far better than they ever could have on petite Edith, who would have been half-smothered under their length. She stood perfectly still under the chandelier, quiet and passive, as her aunt turned her this way and that, and once or twice she caught a glimpse of herself in the mantel mirror, smiling softly at the absurd, princess-like figure staring back. She stroked the soft silk gently, enjoying the texture and the bright colors like a small child, a quiet pleased smile playing on her lips.
Then the door opened, and the servant announced Mr. Henry Lennox.
Several of the ladies jumped back half-embarrassed, caught red-handed fawning over pretty fabric. Mrs. Shaw held out a hand to him, chattering a mile a minute, peppering him with questions about his brother the bridegroom, his sister Janet who was travelling down from Scotland to be bridesmaid, and every other distant member of the Lennox family she could think to ask after. Margaret stood still for a moment, not wanting to disturb the drapery and ruin the display, meeting Henry’s amused, bright gaze with a knowing smile, certain he would see how absurd the whole scene was. It was clear no one needed her as a shawl-bearer any longer, so she drifted to the edge of the room to keep the other guests entertained while her aunt was distracted.
Almost immediately, Edith wandered in from the back drawing room, winking and blinking in the bright gaslight, shaking her rumpled curls back from her face like Sleeping Beauty just startled awake from her enchanted nap. Even half-asleep, she had apparently sensed a Lennox in the house worth waking up for, and she immediately swarmed Henry with dozens of questions about her soon-to-be sister-in-law Janet, a woman she had never met but claimed to already adore so fiercely Margaret would have felt jealous, if she had not known full well it was all performative sentiment.
As Margaret drifted toward the far wall, out of the main circle of conversation, she saw Henry glance pointedly at the empty chair next to hers, and knew he would make his way over the moment Edith released him from her interrogation. She had not been sure he would make it that night, given his busy lawyer schedule, and she was glad to see him — he liked and disliked almost all the same things she did, and talking to him always felt like talking to someone who understood. Her face lit up into an open, unselfconscious grin when he finally sat down beside her.
“I take it you are all deep in ladies’ wedding business,” he said, laughing. “Very different from my line of work, where I spend all day drawing up dry marriage settlements. Playing dress-up with shawls is far more fun than that, I’m sure.”
“I knew you’d laugh the moment you walked in and saw me swathed in silk,” Margaret laughed back. “But truly, Indian shawls are perfect things, in their own way.”
“I don’t doubt that. Their price tags are perfectly enormous, too, nothing wanting.”
The rest of the gentlemen drifted in one by one from the dining room, and the hum of conversation swelled louder around them.
“This is your last dinner party before the wedding, isn’t it?” he asked. “No more of these before Thursday?”
“No,” Margaret said. “I think after tonight we can all finally rest. I’ve not had a quiet minute for weeks now. I can’t wait for all the hands-on work to be done, all the arrangements finished, and have a moment to just think. Edith will be glad for that, too, I’m sure.”
“I wouldn’t count on that for her, but I can very well imagine you are exhausted,” he said. “Every time I’ve seen you lately, you’ve been swept up in a whirlwind someone else made.”
“I have,” Margaret sighed, thinking of the endless stream of trivial little crises that had occupied every minute for the past month. “I wonder if every wedding has to be preceded by that kind of chaos, or if it could ever be calm and peaceful, right before the day.”
“Cinderella’s godmother ordering the trousseau, the wedding breakfast, writing all the invitations, that sort of thing?” he teased.
“But are all those troubles really necessary?” Margaret asked, looking him straight in the eye. She was tired to the bone of all the silly arrangements Edith had obsessed over for the past six weeks, all for the sake of a pretty effect, and she was craving someone to talk to about what a wedding could actually be, beyond the fuss.
“Oh, they’re necessary enough,” he said, his tone turning serious. “We have forms and ceremonies to get through, not so much for our own sakes, but to stop the rest of the world from gossiping. No one can be truly happy if the neighbors are whispering about you. But how would you arrange your perfect wedding, then?”
“Oh, I’ve not thought about it much,” Margaret said. “Only that I’d want it to be on a fine bright summer morning, and I’d want to walk to church through the shade of the trees, not drive to a stuffy London church on a paved street. No hordes of bridesmaids, no fancy wedding breakfast. I expect I’m just rebelling against all the nonsense I’ve been dealing with the last few weeks.”
“I don’t think you are,” he said. “That idea of quiet, stately simplicity fits your character perfectly.”
Margaret winced a little, not liking the compliment. He had a habit of trying to draw her into conversations about her own personality, with him taking the endlessly flattering side, and she always found it awkward. “It’s only natural I think of Helstone church, and the walk to it, rather than a London church,” she said, cutting him off. “Tell me — I’ve never properly described Helstone to you, have I? You’ll be coming to visit us once this house is shut up and 96 Harley Street is all dusty and dull. Is it a village or a town, first of all?”
“Hardly even a village, just a tiny hamlet,” Margaret said, her face lighting up. “There’s the church, and a handful of little cottages on the green, with roses climbing all over their walls.”
“Roses blooming all year round, even at Christmas, I suppose, to make the story perfect?” he teased.
Margaret frowned, a little annoyed. “I’m not making up a fairy tale, I’m describing the place exactly as it is. You shouldn’t have said that.”
“I’m sorry, I’m penitent,” he laughed. “It just sounded like something out of a storybook, not real life.”
“And it is a storybook sort of place,” Margaret said eagerly. “Every other part of England I’ve seen is so hard and prosaic, after the New Forest. Helstone is like a village out of a Tennyson poem. But I won’t describe it anymore. You’ll just laugh at me if I tell you how much I love it.”
“I promise I won’t. But since you’re determined not to tell me about the village, tell me about the parsonage — the home you’re going back to.”
“I can’t describe my home,” Margaret said softly. “It’s home. I can’t put the charm of it into words.”
“Very well, I yield,” he said. “You’re rather severe tonight, Margaret.”
“Am I?” she said, turning her big soft eyes to him in confusion. “I didn’t mean to be.”
“I made one silly joke, and now you won’t talk to me about Helstone or your house, even though I told you how much I want to hear,” he said, pretending to pout.
“But you can’t talk about a home properly unless you’ve been there,” Margaret said. “You won’t get it until you see it for yourself.”
“Then tell me what you do all day there, then,” he said. “Here in London you read, or do lessons, until midday, take a walk before lunch, drive out with your aunt in the afternoon, and go to some party or another in the evening. What does a typical day in Helstone look like? Will you ride, or drive, or walk everywhere?”
