There were hours in Shirley Keeldar’s life when she seemed to live on air—on the mere fact of being, as if existence itself, unhelped by effort, were a feast laid before her. After a morning brisk with tasks and talk, she would sometimes drift out of doors, and, choosing the foot of a tree whose shadow fell like a cool hand, stretch herself at length upon the turf. She asked for no company beyond Caroline—only that Caroline should be near enough to call; she asked for no spectacle but the blue height overhead, with its thin flocks of cloud travelling like white sails; and no sound but the field’s low music—the bee’s steady drone, the leaf’s confidential whisper, the light, pure hush that holds the heart together. In such leisure she did not read printed pages; she read memory, and that other volume, unopened yet, expectation. Her face, at moments, wore a smile as though she tasted something sweet on the tongue of thought—some morsel of the past, some promise of the future; and all seemed kindly, warmed with hope.

Yet on one of these afternoons Caroline, coming to rouse her—thinking the stillness had lasted long enough—found, to her astonishment, dew upon Shirley’s cheek that was not dew. Those brilliant eyes were full; the lashes glittered with moisture.

“Shirley! why do you cry?” Caroline asked; and the emphasis fell, despite herself, on you.

Miss Keeldar turned her head upon the grass, and smiled up—half mocking, half soft. “Because it pleases me mightily to cry,” said she. “My heart is both sad and glad. But why, you good patient child—why do you not bear me company? I weep tears, delightful and soon wiped away; you might weep gall, if you chose.”

“Why should I weep gall?”

“Mateless, solitary bird!” was all the answer she gave—an answer like a proverb in a dialect no one has written down.

“And are not you too mateless, Shirley?”

“At heart—no.”

“Oh! who nestles there, Shirley?”

Shirley only laughed, and sprang up with sudden lightness, as if she had shaken off the drop that trembled on her eyelash. “I have dreamed,” she said—“a mere day-dream—certainly bright, probably baseless!”

Time passed; and Caroline, though stripped now of the easy illusions which once made the future a plain road, still obeyed old habit and old association. Many an evening, as naturally as one goes to the hearth when the air cools, she found her feet carrying her to the field-style and the thorn above the Hollow. That place had become to her what a certain savour is to memory—one smell of crushed thyme, and whole summers return; one glimpse of a familiar stile, and whole histories wake.

The night after the incident of the note she went again, and watched, as she had watched before, for her beacon. She watched in vain. No lamp glimmered; no kindly point of light answered her. At length the rising of certain constellations—clear and cold as a clock-face—warned her how late it was, and sent her away.

Passing Fieldhead on her return, she paused in spite of herself. The house lay under moonlight with a beauty so composed it seemed enchanted into calm. The building, made pale by that serene radiance, wore a pearly gilding; around it the darker mass of trees gathered in a mellow brown gloom, as if night itself were protective. The broad pavement before the door shone like lighted stone—so bright that it might have been a strip of white shore beyond some black flood. Across it lay two sharp shadows, long and sable, cast by two figures.

At first those figures were motionless; then they moved together—step answering step—and spoke low, their voices in the same key, subdued, intimate, guarded by the night air that carried every syllable away before it could be caught.

Caroline’s gaze fixed—earnest, unwilling, yet unable to turn aside. As the pair came from behind the cedar trunk, the moon made their outlines and faces distinct.

“Is it Mrs. Pryor and Shirley?” she thought.

Shirley it certainly was. No one else wore so proud a grace even in carelessness; no one else had that lithe movement, that half-mirthful, half-musing face where tenderness and mockery could dwell together without quarrel. She had not covered her hair against the dew; her curls lay free, veiling neck and shoulder with their dark rings. A gold ornament gleamed through the folds of the scarf across her bust, and a bright gem flashed from the white hand that held it close. Yes—Shirley.

Then the companion—Mrs. Pryor?

Mrs. Pryor, if Mrs. Pryor could command six feet of height and had exchanged her widow’s sober weeds for a man’s figure and carriage. It was a man. Tall, young, stately. Robert Moore.

They spoke softly; their words could not be made out. To linger was not to listen in malice; the moon shone openly upon them as if it had been invited to witness their meeting. And Caroline—who had once walked with Moore herself through summer evenings—could not resist. She stayed.

She remembered other nights: when he had been used to go up the Hollow with his cousin after sunset, to breathe the earth’s freshness where the terrace grew thick with fragrant herbage; and below, from the ravine’s rifted darkness, came that lonely water’s voice, always the same and never the same—moaning among wet stones, between weedy banks, under alder-boughs that made a roof of shadow. That sound, a small native music of the place, had once soothed her; now it seemed to belong to a life she had lost, like an old song she could no longer sing in its original tongue.

“But I used to be closer to him,” she thought. “He felt no obligation to treat me with homage; I needed only kindness. He used to hold my hand; he does not touch hers. And yet Shirley is not proud where she loves. There is no haughtiness in her look now—only a little in her port, what is natural to her and inseparable. Robert must think, as I think, that he is looking down on a fine face; but he must think it with a man’s brain, not with mine. She has such generous, yet soft fire in her eyes. She smiles—what makes her smile so sweet? I saw that Robert felt its beauty, and he must have felt it with his man’s heart, not with my dim woman’s perceptions. They look to me like two great happy spirits. That silver pavement is like the white shore we fancy beyond the death-flood. They have reached it; they walk there united. And what am I—standing here in shadow, shrinking into concealment, my mind darker than my hiding-place? A poor doomed mortal, asking in ignorance and hopelessness wherefore she was born and to what end she lives; thinking always how she shall encounter death, and by whom be sustained through it.

“This is the worst passage I have come to yet; still I was prepared. I gave Robert up—I gave him up to Shirley—the first day I heard she was come, the first moment I saw her—rich, youthful, lovely. She has him now. He is her lover. She is his darling. She will be more his darling when they are married. The more he knows her, the more his soul will cleave to her. They will both be happy; I do not grudge them their bliss—but I groan under my own misery. Some of my suffering is very acute. Truly I ought not to have been born; they should have smothered me at the first cry.”

Here Shirley stepped aside to gather a dewy flower; then she and her companion turned into the path that lay nearer the gate. A few words of their talk floated clearer. Caroline would not stay to hear. With a noiseless step she withdrew; and the moonlight, which had held her shadow against the wall, kissed the stone again when the darkness of her presence moved away.

But the night had ears; and the reader may remain where Caroline would not, and catch what can be caught of their discourse.

“I cannot conceive why nature did not give you a bulldog’s head,” said Shirley, “for you have all a bulldog’s tenacity.”

“Not a flattering idea. Am I so ignoble?”

“And something too you have of the same animal’s silent ways of going about its work. You give no warning; you come noiselessly behind, seize fast, and hold on.”

“This is guess-work. You have witnessed no such feat on my part. In your presence I have been no bulldog.”

“Your very silence indicates your race. How little you talk, and yet how deeply you scheme! You are far-seeing; you are calculating.”

“I know the ways of these people. I have gathered information of their intentions. My note last night informed you that Barraclough’s trial had ended in his conviction and sentence to transportation. His associates will plot vengeance. I shall lay my plans so as to counteract—or at least be prepared for—theirs. That is all. Having now given you as clear an explanation as I can, am I to understand that for what I propose doing I have your approbation?”

“I shall stand by you so long as you remain on the defensive. Yes.”

“Good! Without any aid—even opposed or disapproved by you—I believe I should have acted precisely as I now intend to act, but in another spirit. I now feel satisfied. On the whole, I relish the position.”

“I dare say you do. That is evident. You relish the work which lies before you still better than you would relish the execution of a government order for army-cloth.”

“I certainly feel it congenial.”

“So would old Helstone. It is true there is a shade of difference in your motives—many shades, perhaps. Shall I speak to Mr. Helstone? I will, if you like.”

“Act as you please. Your judgment, Miss Keeldar, will guide you accurately. I could rely on it myself in a more difficult crisis. But I should inform you Mr. Helstone is somewhat prejudiced against me at present.”

“I am aware—I have heard all about your differences. Depend upon it, they will melt away. He cannot resist the temptation of an alliance under present circumstances.”

“I should be glad to have him; he is of true metal.”

“I think so also.”

“An old blade, and rusty somewhat, but the edge and temper still excellent.”

“Well, you shall have him, Mr. Moore—that is, if I can win him.”

“Whom can you not win?”

“Perhaps not the rector; but I will make the effort.”

“Effort! He will yield for a word—a smile.”

“By no means. It will cost me several cups of tea, some toast and cake, and an ample measure of remonstrances, expostulations, and persuasions. It grows rather chill.”

“I perceive you shiver. Am I acting wrongly to detain you here? Yet it is so calm—I even feel it warm—and society such as yours is a pleasure to me so rare. If you were wrapped in a thicker shawl—”

“I might stay longer, and forget how late it is, which would chagrin Mrs. Pryor. We keep early and regular hours at Fieldhead, Mr. Moore; and so, I am sure, does your sister at the cottage.”

“Yes; but Hortense and I have an understanding, the most convenient in the world, that we shall each do as we please.”

“How do you please to do?”

“Three nights in the week I sleep in the mill—but I require little rest—and when it is moonlight and mild I often haunt the Hollow till daybreak.”

“When I was a very little girl,” said Shirley, “my nurse used to tell me tales of fairies being seen in that Hollow. That was before my father built the mill, when it was a perfectly solitary ravine. You will be falling under enchantment.”

“I fear it is done,” Moore answered; and his voice sank, as if he spoke into the hush between two heartbeats.

“But there are worse things than fairies to be guarded against,” pursued Miss Keeldar.

“Things more perilous,” he agreed.

“Far more so. For instance—how would you like to meet Michael Hartley, that mad Calvinist and Jacobin weaver? They say he is addicted to poaching, and often goes abroad at night with his gun.”

“I have already had the luck to meet him. We held a long argument together one night. A strange little incident it was; I liked it.”

“Liked it? I admire your taste! Michael is not sane. Where did you meet him?”

“In the deepest, shadiest spot in the glen, where the water runs low under brushwood. We sat down near that plank-bridge. It was moonlight, but clouded, and very windy. We had a talk.”

“On politics?”

“And religion. I think the moon was at the full, and Michael was as near crazed as possible. He uttered strange blasphemy in his Antinomian fashion.”

“Excuse me, but I think you must have been nearly as mad as he, to sit listening to him.”

“There is a wild interest in his ravings. The man would be half a poet, if he were not wholly a maniac; and perhaps a prophet, if he were not a profligate. He solemnly informed me that hell was foreordained my inevitable portion; that he read the mark of the beast on my brow; that I had been an outcast from the beginning. God’s vengeance, he said, was preparing for me, and affirmed that in a vision of the night he had beheld the manner and the instrument of my doom. I wanted to know further, but he left me with these words—‘The end is not yet.’”

“Have you ever seen him since?”

“About a month afterwards, returning from market, I encountered him and Moses Barraclough, both in an advanced stage of inebriation. They were praying in frantic sort at the roadside. They accosted me as Satan, bid me avaunt, and clamoured to be delivered from temptation. Again, but a few days ago, Michael took the trouble of appearing at the counting-house door, hatless, in his shirt-sleeves—his coat and castor having been detained at the public-house in pledge. He delivered himself of the comfortable message that he could wish Mr. Moore to set his house in order, as his soul was likely shortly to be required of him.”

“Do you make light of these things?”

“The poor man had been drinking for weeks, and was in a state bordering on delirium tremens.”

“What then? He is the more likely to attempt the fulfilment of his own prophecies.”

“It would not do to permit incidents of this sort to affect one’s nerves.”

“Mr. Moore, go home!”

“So soon?”

“Pass straight down the fields, not round by the lade and plantations.”

“It is early yet.”

“It is late. For my part, I am going in. Will you promise me not to wander in the Hollow to-night?”

“If you wish it.”

“I do wish it. May I ask whether you consider life valueless?”

“By no means. On the contrary, of late I regard my life as invaluable.”

“Of late?”

“Existence is neither aimless nor hopeless to me now, and it was both three months ago. I was then drowning, and rather wished the operation over. All at once a hand was stretched to me—such a delicate hand I scarcely dared trust it; its strength, however, has rescued me from ruin.”

“Are you really rescued?”

“For the time. Your assistance has given me another chance.”

“Live to make the best of it. Don’t offer yourself as a target to Michael Hartley; and good-night!”

The next day Caroline was under promise to spend the evening at Fieldhead; and she kept her word. Between that moonlit walk and this homely visit lay hours black as a soot-cloud. Most of the interval she passed shut in her own room, only coming out to join her uncle at meals. When Fanny showed signs of curiosity, Caroline forestalled questions by saying she was altering a dress and preferred sewing upstairs, that she might not be interrupted.

She did sew. The needle ran ceaselessly; the muslin—thin, obedient—was tucked and plaited with patient fingers. Yet her brain worked faster than her hands. Again she felt, with a sharper craving, that she must have fixed occupation—something that would press upon her from morning to night and allow no leisure for thought, however harsh, however irksome. Her uncle must be entreated once more; but first she would consult Mrs. Pryor. While she stitched, the summer dress spread on the little white couch at the foot of which she sat, she planned and replanned, as if life were cloth one could cut into a new shape if only the pattern were right. Now and then a tear fell on the muslin and darkened it for an instant; then she wiped it away, re-threaded the needle, smoothed the tuck, and worked on.

Late in the afternoon she dressed and went to Fieldhead. She entered the oak parlour as tea was being brought in, and Shirley asked why she came so late.

“Because I have been making my dress,” Caroline answered. “These fine sunny days began to make me ashamed of my winter merino, so I have furbished up a lighter garment.”

“In which you look as I like to see you,” said Shirley. “You are a lady-like little person, Caroline.—Is she not, Mrs. Pryor?”

Mrs. Pryor rarely paid compliments, rarely spoke at all of faces and figures; but she drew Caroline nearer with a grave tenderness, swept the curls from her cheek, traced with her hand the oval line as if to assure herself it was still there, and said quietly, “You get somewhat thin, my love, and somewhat pale. Do you sleep well? your eyes have a languid look.” She gazed anxiously as she spoke.

“I sometimes dream melancholy dreams,” replied Caroline; “and if I lie awake for an hour or two in the night, I am continually thinking of the rectory as a dreary old place. You know it is very near the churchyard. The back part of the house is extremely ancient, and it is said that the out-kitchens there were once enclosed in the churchyard, and that there are graves under them. I rather long to leave the rectory.”

“My dear, you are surely not superstitious?”

“No, Mrs. Pryor; but I think I grow what is called nervous. I see things under a darker aspect than I used to do. I have fears I never used to have—not of ghosts, but of omens and disastrous events; and I have an inexpressible weight on my mind which I would give the world to shake off, and I cannot do it.”

“Strange!” cried Shirley. “I never feel so.” Mrs. Pryor was silent.

“Fine weather, pleasant days, pleasant scenes, are powerless to give me pleasure,” Caroline went on. “Calm evenings are not calm to me. Moonlight, which I used to think mild, now only looks mournful. Is this weakness of mind, Mrs. Pryor, or what is it? I cannot help it. I often struggle against it. I reason; but reason and effort make no difference.”

“You should take more exercise,” said Mrs. Pryor.

“Exercise! I exercise sufficiently. I exercise till I am ready to drop.”

“My dear, you should go from home.”

“Mrs. Pryor, I should like to go from home, but not on any purposeless excursion or visit. I wish to be a governess, as you have been. It would oblige me greatly if you would speak to my uncle on the subject.”

“Nonsense!” broke in Shirley. “What an idea! Be a governess! Better be a slave at once. Where is the necessity of it? Why should you dream of such a painful step?”

“My dear,” said Mrs. Pryor, “you are very young to be a governess, and not sufficiently robust. The duties a governess undertakes are often severe.”

“And I believe I want severe duties to occupy me.”

“Occupy you!” cried Shirley. “When are you idle? I never saw a more industrious girl than you. You are always at work. Come—come and sit by my side, and take some tea to refresh you. You don’t care much for my friendship, then, that you wish to leave me?”

“Indeed I do, Shirley; and I don’t wish to leave you. I shall never find another friend so dear.”

At that, Shirley’s hand went into Caroline’s with impulsive warmth; her face softened as though some proud shield had slipped aside for an instant.

“If you think so, you had better make much of me,” said she, “and not run away from me. I hate to part with those to whom I am become attached. Mrs. Pryor there sometimes talks of leaving me, and says I might make a more advantageous connection than herself. I should as soon think of exchanging an old-fashioned mother for something modish and stylish. As for you—why, I began to flatter myself we were thoroughly friends; that you liked Shirley almost as well as Shirley likes you, and she does not stint her regard.”

“I do like Shirley. I like her more and more every day. But that does not make me strong or happy.”

“And would it make you strong or happy to go and live as a dependent amongst utter strangers? It would not. And the experiment must not be tried; I tell you it would fail. It is not in your nature to bear the desolate life governesses generally lead; you would fall ill. I won’t hear of it.”

She paused, having spoken with a decision that was almost command. Then, still a little ruffled, she began again. “Why, it is my daily pleasure now to look out for the little cottage bonnet and the silk scarf glancing through the trees in the lane, and to know that my quiet, shrewd, thoughtful companion and monitress is coming back to me; that I shall have her sitting in the room to look at, to talk to, or to let alone, as she and I please. This may be a selfish sort of language—I know it is—but it is the language which naturally rises to my lips, therefore I utter it.”

“I would write to you, Shirley.”

“And what are letters? Only a sort of pis aller. Drink some tea, Caroline. Eat something—you eat nothing. Laugh and be cheerful, and stay at home.”

Caroline shook her head and sighed. She felt already how hard it would be to persuade anyone to help her to that change of life which she believed would be cure. Could she follow her own judgment, she thought she might find something harsh, perhaps, but effectual—some discipline that would leave her no room to ache. Yet that judgment rested on circumstances she could fully explain to none—least of all to Shirley. To other eyes it would look fanciful, unaccountable; and it was opposed accordingly.

There was indeed no present need of money to drive her from a comfortable home into service; and there was every likelihood her uncle would in some manner provide for her. So her friends thought, and as far as their sight reached they reasoned well. Of Caroline’s inward suffering—of racked nights and leaden days—they had no suspicion. She could not make it plain. It seemed untranslatable even to herself; it had no name in their language. To wait and endure was the only plan left. Many who wanted bread and clothing had livelier hearts and brighter prospects than hers; many, pinched by poverty, were less straitened than she.

“Now, is your mind quieted?” asked Shirley. “Will you consent to stay at home?”

“I shall not leave it against the approbation of my friends,” Caroline replied; “but I think in time they will be obliged to think as I do.”

During all this Mrs. Pryor looked far from easy. Habitual reserve held her tongue as a tight band holds back speech. Questions crowded her mind which she never ventured to put; advice formed itself silently and died there. Had she been alone with Caroline she might have spoken more directly; but Shirley’s presence—familiar as it was—sealed her lips with inexplicable scruple. Her concern showed itself only by small indirect ministries: asking whether the fire made Caroline too warm, setting a screen between her chair and the hearth, shutting a window from which she fancied a draught might come, and glancing often and restlessly at the pale face she watched as one watches a flame one fears may go out.

Shirley, however, would not let the matter die in sadness.

“Having destroyed your plan,” she said, “which I hope I have done, I shall construct a new one of my own. Every summer I make an excursion. This season I propose spending two months either at the Scotch lochs or the English lakes—that is, I shall go there provided you consent to accompany me. If you refuse, I shall not stir a foot.”

“You are very good, Shirley,” Caroline answered.
