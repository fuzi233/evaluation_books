The following day proved far livelier and busier than either of us—or, at the very least, I—had anticipated. It transpired to be the birthday of one of the young princes of Labassecour, the eldest, I believe, the Duc de Dindonneau. Consequently, a general holiday was declared throughout the schools, particularly at the principal "Athénée" or college. The students of that institution had concocted a loyal address intended for presentation; to this end, they were to assemble in the public hall where annual examinations were traditionally held and prizes distributed. Following the ceremony of presentation, an oration, or *discours*, was scheduled to be delivered by one of the professors.

Several of M. de Bassompierre's friends—the *savants*—being variously connected with the Athénée, were expected to attend, along with the respectable municipality of Villette, M. le Chevalier Staas, the burgomaster, and the parents and kin of the Athenians in general. M. de Bassompierre was persuaded by his friends to accompany them; his fair daughter, of course, would be part of the party. She sent a brief note to Ginevra and myself, inviting us to arrive early so we might join her.

As Miss Fanshawe and I dressed in the dormitory of the Rue Fossette, she suddenly burst into laughter.

"What now?" I asked, for she had paused mid-operation, suspending the arrangement of her attire to gaze at me.

"It seems so odd," she replied, with her customary blend of half-honesty and insolence, "that you and I should now be so much on a level, visiting in the same sphere, sharing the same connections."

"Why, yes," I said. "I had little respect for the connections you chiefly frequented a while ago; Mrs. Cholmondeley and her circle would never have suited me at all."

"Who *are* you, Miss Snowe?" she inquired, in a tone of such undisguised and unsophisticated curiosity that I laughed in return.

"You used to call yourself a nursery governess; when you first arrived here, you truly had the care of the children in this house. I have seen you carrying little Georgette in your arms, like a *bonne*—few governesses would have condescended so far. And now, Madame Beck treats you with more courtesy than she does the Parisienne, St. Pierre; and that proud chit, my cousin, has made you her bosom friend!"

"Wonderful!" I agreed, much amused by her mystification. "Who am I indeed? Perhaps a personage in disguise. Pity I do not look the part."

"I wonder you are not more flattered by all this," she continued. "You accept it with strange composure. If you really are the nobody I once thought you, you must possess a cool temper."

"The nobody you once thought me!" I repeated, and my face grew slightly hot. Yet, I refused to be angry. Of what consequence was a schoolgirl's crude misuse of the terms 'nobody' and 'somebody'? I confined myself to observing that I had merely received civility, and asked what she saw in civility sufficient to throw the recipient into a fever of confusion.

"One cannot help wondering at certain things," she persisted.

"Wondering at marvels of your own manufacture. Are you ready at last?"

"Yes; let me take your arm."

"I would rather not: we shall walk side by side."

When she finally took my arm, she invariably leaned upon it with her entire weight. Since I was neither a gentleman nor her lover, I disliked the gesture.

"There, again!" she cried. "I thought that by offering to take your arm, I was intimating approval of your dress and general appearance; I meant it as a compliment."

"You did? You meant, in short, to express that you are not ashamed to be seen in the street with me? That if Mrs. Cholmondeley were fondling her lapdog at some window, or Colonel de Hamal picking his teeth on a balcony, and should catch a glimpse of us, you would not quite blush for your companion?"

"Yes," she said, with that directness which was her finest trait—which lent an honest plainness even to her falsehoods, giving them a quality that served as the sole preservative of a character otherwise ill-formed to endure.

I delegated the task of commenting on this "yes" to my countenance; or rather, my lower lip voluntarily anticipated my tongue. My expression conveyed neither reverence nor solemnity.

"A scorners, sneering creature!" she exclaimed as we crossed a great square and entered the quiet, pleasant park, our nearest route to the Rue Crécy. "No one in this world has ever been such a Turk to me as you!"

"You bring it upon yourself," I retorted. "Leave me alone; have the sense to be quiet; I will leave you alone."

"As if one *could* leave you alone when you are so peculiar and so mysterious!"

"The mystery and peculiarity being entirely the invention of your own brain—mere maggots—be so good as to keep them out of my sight."

"But *are* you anybody?" she insisted, pushing her hand under my arm despite my resistance; and that arm pressed itself with inhospitable closeness against my side to ward off the intrusion.

"Yes," I said. "I am a rising character: once an old lady's companion, then a nursery-governess, now a school-teacher."

"Do—*do* tell me who you are? I promise not to repeat it," she urged, clinging with ludicrous tenacity to the wise notion of an incognito she had seized upon. She squeezed the arm of which she now had full possession, coaxing and conjuring until I was obliged to pause in the park to laugh. Throughout our walk, she rang the most fanciful changes on this theme, proving through her obstinate credulity or incredulity her inability to conceive how anyone, unsupported by birth or wealth, lacking any consciousness of name or connection, could maintain an attitude of reasonable integrity. As for me, it was sufficient for my mental tranquility that I was known where it mattered that I should be known. The rest sat lightly upon me: pedigree, social position, and obscure intellectual acquisitions occupied roughly the same space in my thoughts and interests; they were my third-class lodgers, entitled only to the small sitting-room and the little back bedroom. Even if the dining and drawing rooms stood empty, I never confessed this to them, considering minor accommodations better suited to their circumstances.

Soon, however, I learned that the world held a different estimate. And I make no doubt the world is very right in its view, yet I also believe I am not entirely wrong in mine. There are those whom a lowered position degrades morally, to whom the loss of connection entails the loss of self-respect. Are they not justified in placing the highest value on that station and association which serves as their safeguard against debasement? If a man feels that he would become contemptible in his own eyes were it generally known that his ancestry was simple rather than noble, poor rather than rich, a worker rather than a capitalist, would it be right to severely blame him for keeping these fatal facts out of sight—for starting, trembling, quailing at the chance of exposure? The longer we live, the wider our experience grows; the less prone are we to judge our neighbor's conduct or question the world's wisdom. Wherever an accumulation of small defenses is found, whether surrounding the prude's virtue or the man of the world's respectability, be assured, it is needed.

We reached the Hôtel Crécy. Paulina was ready; Mrs. Bretton was with her. Under her escort and that of M. de Bassompierre, we were soon conducted to the place of assembly and seated in good seats at a convenient distance from the Tribune. The youth of the Athénée were marshaled before us; the municipality and their burgomaster occupied places of honor; the young princes, with their tutors, held a conspicuous position; and the main body of the building was crowded with the aristocracy and leading burghers of the town.

Regarding the identity of the professor who was to deliver the *discours*, I had entertained neither care nor question. I vaguely expected a *savant* to rise and deliver a formal speech, half dogma to the Athenians, half flattery to the princes.

The Tribune was empty when we entered, but within ten minutes it was filled. Suddenly, in the span of a second, a head, chest, and arms emerged above the crimson desk. I recognized this head: its color, shape, posture, and expression were familiar to both me and Miss Fanshawe. The blackness and closeness of the cranium, the amplitude and pallor of the brow, the blue fire of the glance—details so domesticated in memory and woven with so many whimsical associations—as almost by their sudden apparition, tickled my fancy into laughter. Indeed, I confess, for my part, I laughed until I was warm; but then I bowed my head, making my handkerchief and a lowered veil the sole confidants of my mirth.
