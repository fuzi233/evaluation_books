When the legendary wanderer from Troy recounted his trials and triumphs to Queen Dido, history records that the monarch listened with rapt attention, captivated by the eloquent storyteller who spun his perils with such vivid charm. A chronicle emerged, far more poignant than any prior event in the life of Pius Aeneas, leaving the unfortunate princess to regret the moment she yielded to the persuasive tongue of that dangerous orator.

Harry Warrington possessed none of Aeneas's rhetorical mastery, nor was his elderly aunt, we may assume, quite as susceptible to sentiment as the tragic Dido. Yet, the lad's account proved deeply moving, delivered with an artless sincerity and a warm, cordial voice. More than once during his narration, Madam Bernstein found herself swept into a tenderness she rarely permitted herself. In that barren expanse of her existence, there had been few oases—few moments of genuine refreshment. It had been a prolonged solitude until this friendly voice entered her ear, stirring her heart with unfamiliar pangs of affection and empathy. She adored this boy, intoxicated by this newfound sense of compassion and regard. Except for a fleeting, faint sensation in her earliest youth, she had known no tender emotion for any human soul.

Such a woman would naturally scrutinize her own feelings with keen vigilance, surely smiling inwardly as she observed the flush rising to her cheeks whenever the boy approached. Her eyes welcomed him eagerly, tracking his movements with fond delight. "Ah, if she could have borne a son like that, how she would have cherished him!" whispered her inner monologue. Then, the dark, scoffing spirit of Conscience intervened: "Wait, Beatrix Esmond. Wait. You know you will tire of this infatuation, just as you have of everything else. You know that when this passing fancy fades, the boy may perish without a single tear from you; or worse, you may grow bored with his stories. Your destiny is loneliness—absolute, enduring loneliness."

"Then what?" countered the practical side of her mind. "If life is a desert, there are still halting places, shadows, and springs of water. Let us enjoy them for today. We know we must march toward tomorrow and trudge on toward our fate regardless."

Smiling softly as she followed the lad's tale, she recognized familiar traits in his simple stories about his mother—a striking resemblance to herself. Madam Esmond was indeed jealous? Yes, Harry admitted. Did he love Colonel Washington? He liked him, but merely as a friend, declaring a hundred times that his mother held no deeper feeling for him. Though he felt shame owning that he himself had once been absurdly jealous of the Colonel, the truth remained.

"Well," remarked Madam Beatrix, "you will see that my half-sister will never forgive him. And do not be surprised, sir, that women take a fancy to men younger than themselves. Do I not dote upon you? And do not all the people of Castlewood fret with jealousy?"

Despite the intense envy harbored by the family regarding Madame de Bernstein's new favorite, the House of Castlewood displayed no outward ill will toward their young guest and kinsman. Within two days of arriving at the ancestral home, Mr. Harry Warrington had transformed into "Cousin Harry" in the eyes of both the young and the middle-aged. Particularly in the presence of the Baroness, the Countess of Castlewood treated her kinsman with exceptional grace. Seizing numerous private moments, she lavished praise upon the young Huron, marveling at the elegance of his manners and appearance, and wondering how a child raised in a distant province could possibly acquire such refinement.

These notes of admiration and inquiry were met by the Baroness with equal composure. Speaking parenthetically, the chronicler cannot resist offering a respectful observation on the peculiar conduct of ladies toward one another—their smiles and curtsies that serve as daggers, their compliments laced with hatred, their determined patience in avoiding offense, and their innocent dexterity in poisoning the communal cup of conversation while maintaining a serene smile. One imagines my Baroness and Countess doing exactly this a century ago, exchanging greetings of "My dear," "My dear creature," and "My dear sister"—even when they were most ready to draw swords.

"You wonder, my dear Maria, that the boy should be so polite?" cried Madame de Bernstein. "His mother was nurtured by two impeccable gentlemen. Colonel Esmond possessed a certain grave courtesy and a majestic bearing, qualities I fail to find among modern gentlemen."

"Eh, my dear, we all praise our own era!" retorted the Countess. "My grandmother used to insist there was nothing comparable to Whitehall under Charles II."

"My mother witnessed King James II's court briefly," the Baroness replied. "Though not court-trained herself, as her father was a country clergyman, she was exquisitely well-bred. Her second husband, the Colonel, was a man of vast travel, experience, and learning, frequenting the finest circles of Europe. They could not abandon their good manners upon retiring to their estate, and our boy inherited them naturally."

"Nay, excuse me, my dear, for doubting your perception of your mother," the Countess countered gently. "She could not possess the perfection your filial devotion imagines. She ceased loving her daughter—you have acknowledged that—and I cannot conceive of a complete woman with a cold heart. No, no, my dear sister-in-law! Manners are certainly essential, and for a daughter of a country parson, your mother was adequate—I have seen many of the clergy who are quite respectable. Mr. Sampson, our chaplain, is very well. Dr. Young is very well. Mr. Dodd is very well. But they lack the true *air*. As how should they? I protest, I beg pardon! I forgot your lord bishop, your ladyship's first choice. But as I stated, to be a complete woman, one requires what you possess, what I thank Heaven for possessing myself—a good heart. Without affection, the entire world is vanity, my love! I swear I live, exist, eat, drink, and rest solely for my sweet, sweet children—for my wilful Willy, for my self-willed Fanny, my dear naughty loves!"

As she spoke, she rapturously kissed bracelets on each arm containing miniatures of the two young girls. "Yes, Mimi! Yes, Fanchon! You know I adore you, you dear, dear little things! And if either of you were to die, your poor mistress would follow!"

Mimi and Fanchon, two trembling Italian greyhounds, leaped into their lady's arms to kiss her hands, though respectfully avoiding her rouge-covered cheeks.

"No, my dear! For nothing do I bless Heaven more (though it subjects me to excruciating torment frequently) than for endowing me with sensibility and a feeling heart!"

"You are full of feeling, dear Anna," said the Baroness. "You are renowned for your sensibility. You must share a little of it with our American nephew—or cousin—I hardly know the precise relation."

"Nay, I am here merely as a guest in Castlewood. The house belongs to my Lord Castlewood, not to me, nor to him unless he chooses to reclaim it. What can I do for the young Virginian that hasn't already been done? He is charming. Are we even jealous of him for being so, my dear? And though we see the fancy the Baroness de Bernstein has taken to him, do your ladyship's nephews and nieces—your actual blood relatives—complain? My poor children might feel mortified, for indeed, in mere hours, this charming young man has achieved more progress than my poor darlings have managed in their entire lives: but are they angry? Willy has taken him riding. This morning, was not Maria playing the harpsichord while my Fanny taught him the minuet? 'Twas a delightful trio, I assure you, bringing tears to my eyes to behold such youthful harmony. Poor lad! We are as fond of him as you are, dear Baroness!"

It was later revealed that Madame de Bernstein had inadvertently overheard the true nature of events unfolding during this seemingly harmless scene. Lady Castlewood had entered the room where the young people amused themselves, accompanied by her son, William, who arrived fresh from the kennels in his riding boots.

"Bravi, bravi! Oh, charming!" exclaimed the Countess, clapping her hands, nodding with one of her brightest smiles at Harry Warrington, and casting a sharp glance at his partner that Lady Fanny instantly comprehended. Perhaps Lady Maria at the harpsichord understood it too, for she played with renewed vigor, shaking her waving curls over the keys.

"Infernal young Choctaw! Is he teaching Fanny the war-dance? And is Fan planning to try her tricks upon him now?" demanded Mr. William, whose temper was notoriously volatile. That was precisely the message conveyed by his mother's look to Fanny.

"Are you going to try your tricks upon him now?"

Fanny executed a very low curtsey, Harry blushed crimson, and they both halted their dancing, visibly disconcerted. Lady Maria rose from the instrument and withdrew.

"Nay, go on dancing, young people! Do not let me spoil the sport. Let me play for you," suggested the Countess, sitting down to the keyboard.

"I—I don't know how to dance," Harry murmured, hanging his head, his blush surpassing the finest carmine the Countess could apply.
