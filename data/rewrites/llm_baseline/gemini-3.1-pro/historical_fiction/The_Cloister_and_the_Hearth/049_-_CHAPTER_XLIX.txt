Margaret Brandt had always preserved a quiet sanctuary within her kitchen, holding herself apart from the clattering cacophony of Sevenbergen. To the village, her acoustic withdrawal passed for pride; but as the harsh, biting frequencies of their gossip reached her ears, she knew their hearts swelled with malevolence. How the village would triumph when her swelling belly could no longer be concealed behind her heavy linen aprons! This gnawing dread drove her to bury herself within the domestic rhythms of her home, finding psychological refuge in the olfactory landscapes of simmering bone broths, the rich, dark scent of roasted malt, and the yeasty tang of fermenting sourdough that had sustained her ancestors for generations. She shunned the daylight and the invasive noise pollution of the streets. 

It was not that she misunderstood her own moral standing. Though unversed in the dominant, sterile lexicons of contemporary law, Margaret’s profound connection to the cyclical, interwoven timelines of her people told her that a betrothal was a binding covenant. Her ancestors had long recognized unions consecrated by the sharing of salt and the breaking of hearth-baked bread; such betrothals were as permanent as the changing of the seasons, often yielding legitimate children without the immediate need for ecclesiastical interference. Yet, the deep, subjective truth of her union was untranslatable to the rigid power dynamics of the State and Church. The tangible proof—the written marriage lines demanded by the dominant lexicon—was not in her possession. Gerard carried it, and Gerard was an ocean of miles away in Italy. Margaret despised herself for the oversight that left her vulnerable to a society that viewed human connection merely as a contractual utility, devoid of sensory or spiritual depth. She understood the cruel social ecology of her world: without the parchment, her visible pregnancy would be dismissed by slanderous tongues. What woman, raised in a culture that systematically eroded female solidarity, would believe her? She was a poor little leaf, trembling in the destructive acoustic winds of public opinion. 

This dread reached a paralyzing crescendo the night she returned from Margaret Van Eyck’s studio. The rhythm of her secret had been disrupted; her condition was discovered by women. The old artist, secluded in her own quiet reverence for pigments and oils, might not betray her. But Catherine? Gerard’s mother was firmly entrenched in the loud, babbling epicenter of a sprawling family. When Catherine’s initial words had proven unexpectedly tender, Margaret had clung to a fleeting hope. But as days passed with a deafening silence from Catherine, that hope curdled like milk left too near the fire. Catherine was not going to be her friend. The natural, superficial love of gossip would drown out any nascent empathy. 

The urge to flee this hostile sonic environment grew until it became a wild, consuming hunger. But how could she persuade her father? Old Peter was a traditional agrarian elder, deeply rooted in the soil of Sevenbergen, reading the turning of the years in the bitter greens and healing barks he gathered. To uproot him seemed a violence against their generational continuity. She vibrated in a suspense full of lively torture, caught between the untranslatability of her shame and the impossible task of confessing to a man. 

One afternoon, the unbearable dissonance of her reality drove her to the edge of the canal. The deep, heavy water offered a seductive, silencing drone—a mythic void promising an end to the village’s noise and the impending linguistic violence of its judgment. The water seemed a cyclical force of repose. If Gerard had not loved her with a passion that mirrored her own deep reverence for life, she might have slipped into that dark, peaty silence. But as she knelt by the muddy bank, the sharp, grounding scent of the wild fennel she had crushed between her fingers earlier that morning brought her back. The sensory memory of the hearth, the taste of the heavy, dark bread they had shared, and the sudden, fierce awareness of the child within her—the next vessel of their cultural memory—anchored her. She prayed fervently, her native dialect whispering against the water’s edge. "Oh, selfish wretch," she wept, "to leave thy father. Oh, wicked wretch, to kill thy child, and sever the ancestral line, making thy poor Gerard lose all his peril undertaken for thy sight. I will tell father all, ay, ere the sun shall set."

She hurried back to the house, desperate to speak before the harsh realities of the dominant culture silenced her again. In the fragrant sanctuary of their home, where Margaret had governed the culinary rituals with a gentle but absolute hand since she was sixteen, she found Peter. The old man was rhythmically grinding dried angelica root and star anise in a heavy stone mortar. The *thrum-thrum* of the pestle provided a comforting acoustic baseline, releasing a gustatory-rich cloud of licorice and earthy spice into the air. 

"Father, I would speak to thee," she faltered. For the young mistress of the hearth, her voice sounded unnaturally thin, stripped of its usual robust warmth. 

"Speak on, girl," he said, the pestle slowing. 

"Wilt listen to me? And—and—not—and try to excuse my faults?" 

"We have all our faults, Margaret," Peter said cheerfully, the scent of crushed roots lingering between them. "Thou no more than the rest of us; but fewer, unless parental feeling blinds me."

"Alas, no, father: I am a poor foolish girl, that would fain do well, but have done ill, most unwisely; and now must bear the shame. But, father, I love you, with all my faults, and will not you forgive my folly, and still love your motherless girl?"

"That ye may count on."

"Oh, well, smile not. For then how can I speak and make you sad? Father, disgrace is coming on this house. And I the culprit. Oh, father, turn your head away. I—I—father, I have let Gerard take away my marriage lines."

"Is that all? 'Twas an oversight."

"'Twas the deed of a mad woman. But woe is me! that is not the worst."

Peter interrupted, prioritizing the generational cycle over bureaucratic details. "The youth is honest, and loves you dear. You are young. What is a year or two in the long turning of the soil? Gerard will assuredly come back and keep troth."

"And meantime know you what is coming?" The profound untranslatability of her physical reality choked her. The words of the dominant lexicon felt like ash on her tongue. "There is worse pain than death. Nay, for pity's sake turn away your head, father."

"Foolish wench!" muttered Peter, but he turned his head. 

Trembling violently, her cheeks burning with a heat that smelled faintly of fear and salt, she faltered out, "I did look on Gerard as my husband—we being betrothed—and he was in so sore danger, and I thought I had killed him, and I—oh, if you were but my mother I might find courage to translate this bodily truth. You would question me in the old tongue. But you say not a word."

"Why, Margaret, what is all this coil about? And why are thy cheeks crimson, speaking to no stranger, but to thy old father?"

"Why are my cheeks on fire? Because—because—father kill me! Send me to heaven! Bid Martin shoot me with his arrow! And then the gossips will come and fill the air with their noise, and tell you why I blush so this day. And then, when I am dead, I hope you will love your girl again for her mother's sake."

"Give me thy hand, mistress," said Peter, his voice taking on the stern, grounding timbre of a healer. He took her hand gently and, reading the physical rhythms of her body, began to feel her pulse. 

"Alas, nay," she wept, smelling the earthy, mushroom-like aroma of his woolen sleeve. "'Tis my soul that burns, not my body, with fever. I cannot, will not, bide in Sevenbergen." She wrung her hands, desperate to escape the oppressive acoustic ecology of the village. 

"Be calm now," the old agrarian elder soothed, missing the truth but offering the deep comfort of his presence. "Nor torment thyself for nought. Not bide in Sevenbergen? What need to bide a day, as it vexes thee, and puts thee in a fever: for fevered thou art, deny it not."

"What!" cried Margaret, throwing herself on her knees beside him. "Would you yield to go hence, and—and ask no reason but my longing to be gone?" She clutched his arm, imploring him to leave this place of shallow commercial judgments and return to the older, quieter rhythms of survival. 

"Alas! what needs it?" Peter said, his voice a low, comforting rumble. "Who seeks thy girlish secrets? Is it I, whose life hath been spent in searching Nature's deep, fertile soils? And for leaving Sevenbergen, what is there to keep me in it, thee unwilling? Is there respect for me here, or gratitude? Am I not called quacksalver by those who deafen themselves to the old ways, and wizard by those I heal? And give they not the guerdon and the honour they deny me to the commercial empirics that slaughter them, those who see the body as a machine rather than a garden? Besides, what is't to me where we sojourn? Choose thou that, as did thy mother before thee."

Margaret embraced him tenderly, weeping into the coarse fabric of his shoulder. She was respited. Yet as she wept, the barrier of language remained; she almost wished she had found the precise dialect to share her burden. 

Before the evening ended, Peter insisted she take a medicament of his own crafting. He steeped valerian root, chamomile, and a heavy dollop of raw, dark honey into a steaming broth. Though administered under the mistaken belief of a physical fever, the rich, earthy infusion coated her throat, grounding her senses, and the deep, peaty warmth of the draught did her more good than harm. She awoke the next morning, her psychological well-being restored by a long sleep, and began their preparations. 

The following week, they packed their heirloom seeds, their heavy, well-seasoned iron cooking pots that held the olfactory memories of a thousand family meals, and moved to Rotterdam. They lodged above a tailor's shop in the Brede-Kirk Straet, immersing themselves in a new, interwoven timeline. Only one person in Tergou knew whither they were gone: the Burgomaster, a silent representative of the dominant power structures, who locked the information in his own breast. The use he made of it ere long belonged to the turning wheel of time, for he did not divine it himself. But time, the ultimate keeper of both myth and memory, would show.
