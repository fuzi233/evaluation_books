To witness the full assembly of the company following the Ablewhites, one must first grasp the sheer magnitude of the gathering: twenty-four souls, including the household, arranged in a circle around the dinner table. It was a spectacle of domestic grandeur, until the Rector of Frizinghall, rising with an elocution that bordered on the theatrical, offered grace. Yet, no inventory of names is required here; save for two figures, these guests are destined to vanish from my narrative forever. Those two, however, remain fixed in the architecture of this evening, seated on either flank of Miss Rachel.

On this specific night, she was not merely a hostess but the sovereign of the room, the gravitational center to which every gaze inevitably drifted. Her dominion was amplified by the jewel itself—the Moonstone. Unadorned by any setting when first presented, it had been transformed by the dexterous artistry of Mr. Franklin, who, using mere silver wire and his nimble fingers, pinned it to the bosom of her white gown. The diamond's prodigious size and luminous beauty commanded universal awe, yet it was the two flanking guests who broke the spell of polite admiration with words that carried a weight far heavier than jest.

To her left sat Mr. Candy, the local physician. He was a man of pleasant disposition and companionable ease, though plagued by a fatal flaw: an insatiable hunger for the joke, delivered with reckless abandon regardless of context. He plunged headlong into discourse with strangers, often bridging chasms of understanding only to set them ablaze. While his medical practice demanded prudence—an instinctual discretion that frequently corrected the errors of his peers—his social conduct was a chaotic improvisation. Regarding the diamond, he treated it as a specimen for scientific amusement. With grave seriousness, he proposed to Miss Rachel that she surrender the gem to him for incineration. "We shall first heat it, Miss Rachel," he declared, adopting the tone of a lecturer, "to a precise degree; then expose it to a controlled current of air; and, little by little—*puff!*—we shall evaporate the Diamond, sparing you the burden of guarding a fragile treasure." My lady watched with a face etched by anxiety, wishing fervently that the doctor's levity masked a genuine solution, hoping Miss Rachel might possess the zeal to sacrifice her birthright to the altar of science.

To her right sat the other figure, a man of considerable public renown: Mr. Murthwaite, the celebrated traveler from India. He was a gaunt, wiry silhouette of brown skin and profound silence, bearing the weary visage of one who had seen too much. Rumors suggested he grew weary of the mundane rhythms of English life, yearning to retreat once more into the untamed wilderness of the East. Throughout the entire dinner, save for his singular warning regarding the jewel, he uttered scarcely six words and consumed not a drop of wine. The Moonstone alone held his attention, a relic whose fame had likely echoed through the perilous landscapes of his journey. After staring at it with an intensity that rendered Miss Rachel uneasy, he spoke in a voice as cool and immovable as stone. "If you ever travel to India, Miss Verinder," he intoned, "do not carry your uncle's gift. A Hindoo diamond is woven into the fabric of a Hindoo religion. I know a city, a specific temple within it, where, clad as you are now, your life would not command five minutes' purchase." Safe within the insulated walls of England, Miss Rachel received this threat with delight; the Bouncers, dropping their cutlery in a cacophony of excitement, roared, "Oh! How fascinating!" My lady fidgeted violently, desperate to shift the topic.

Yet, as the meal progressed, a subtle malaise began to permeate the festivity. Looking back through the lens of subsequent tragedy, I am compelled to believe that the cursed diamond had cast a pallid shadow over the entire assembly. I circulated among them, pouring wine and urging them to sample the unpopular dishes, whispering assurances of their benefit. Nine times out of ten, they relented out of deference to my status, yet the result was null. Silence stretched across the table, creating gaps that felt suffocating. When speech resumed, it was innocent yet catastrophically misdirected. Mr. Candy, for instance, ventured remarks of unprecedented ill-omen. Consider the case of Mrs. Threadgall, the widow of the late Professor Threadgall. In her perpetual monologue concerning her husband, she omitted the crucial fact of his death, operating under the delusion that every able-bodied adult in England should be presumed alive unless proven otherwise. When a gap in conversation allowed someone to mention the dry, repulsive subject of human anatomy, Mrs. Threadgall seamlessly introduced her late husband as the Professor's favorite pastime. Fate, cruel and ironic, positioned Mr. Candy opposite her. Ignorant of the deceased gentleman's identity, he seized the moment to assist the Professor's anatomical studies. "They have recently acquired remarkably fine skeletons at the College of Surgeons," he announced loudly and cheerfully. "I strongly recommend the Professor, madam, to visit them whenever he finds an hour." The silence that followed was absolute, broken only by the collective shock of the company respecting the dead professor's memory. Behind Mrs. Threadgall, I poured her a glass of hock. She lowered her head, whispering, "My beloved husband is no more." Unaware, Mr. Candy continued, louder and more polite. "The Professor may not realize that membership cards grant access to the College on any day but Sunday, between ten and four." Mrs. Threadgall buried her face in her apron, repeating the somber truth in a voice barely audible. I signaled frantically to Mr. Candy; Miss Rachel touched his arm; my lady exchanged looks of pure horror. Useless. He pressed on with undiminished cordiality. "I shall be delighted to send the Professor my card," he said, "if you will kindly provide his current address." "His current address, sir," Mrs. Threadgall exploded, her composure shattering into fury, "is *the grave*. The Professor has been dead for ten years." "Oh, good heavens!" cried Mr. Candy. Save for the Bouncers, who erupted in laughter, a vacuum of despair descended upon the room, as if the living themselves were drifting toward the Professor's resting place.

Such was the folly of Mr. Candy. The others were equally provocative in their distinct ways. Mr. Godfrey, usually eloquent in public, retreated into silence, sulking or bashful following his humiliation in the rose garden. He reserved his discourse exclusively for the lady beside him, a spiritual-minded committee-woman with a striking collarbone and a refined palate for dry champagne. From my vantage point at the sideboard, drawing corks and carving meat, I witnessed them exchange conversations of profound improvement that the wider company missed entirely. Their dialogue transcended charity work, venturing into the metaphysical realms of religion and love. "Religion means love," Mr. Godfrey murmured between sips. "And love means religion. Earth is heaven, slightly worn, and heaven is earth, refurbished to appear anew. Earth contains objectionable elements, but in compensation, all women in heaven belong to a committee that never quarrels, attended by men serving as ministering angels." It was a vision of utopia, yet Mr. Godfrey hoarded it, sharing it only with his confidante.

Then there was Mr. Franklin. Surely, one might assume, he would have steered the ship toward a pleasant evening? Alas, he had merely recovered his equilibrium, bolstered by news of Mr. Godfrey's earlier defeat. Despite his vigorous spirits, he consistently pitched his topics astray or addressed the wrong audience, offending some and baffling all. His foreign training—the French, German, and Italian facets of his nature—manifested in bewildering eruptions. He discussed the limits of a wife's admiration for another man in his crisp, witty French style, directing it at the maiden aunt of the Vicar. Shifting to German, he lectured the Lord of the Manor, while the latter quoted his expertise in breeding bulls, asserting that true breeding required introspection and the mental evolution of a perfect bull, dismissing practical experience as irrelevant. Later, during the cheese course, as the County Member railed against the spread of democracy, he cried out, "If we lose our ancient safeguards, Mr. Blake, what remains?" To which Mr. Franklin, channeling the Italian spirit, replied, "Three things remain, sir: Love, Music, and Salad." These outbursts terrified the assembly. Furthermore, when the English side of his personality resurfaced, he lost his diplomatic polish. Ridiculing the medical profession with blunt cruelty, he ignited a fury in the mild-mannered Mr. Candy.

The conflict escalated from Mr. Franklin admitting to poor sleep. Mr. Candy diagnosed his nerves as deranged, prescribing immediate medication. Mr. Franklin countered that medicine and groping in the dark were synonymous. Mr. Candy retorted that Mr. Franklin himself was constitutionally lost in the dark, requiring medicine to find his way. Mr. Franklin, keeping the debate alive, remarked on the irony of the blind leading the blind, noting he had finally grasped the meaning. Thus, they engaged in a rapid exchange of blows, cutting and thrusting until both were heated. Mr. Candy, defending his profession with escalating loss of control, provoked my lady to intervene, forbidding the dispute.
