The next day, Denys found a moment alone with Catherine and her daughter and told them the rest of what had happened—though he took care to excuse himself for not sharing it with Cornelis and Sybrandt. “It is not my place to speak ill of them,” he said. “They come from good stock. But Gerard does not count them among his friends in this matter—and I am Gerard’s comrade. And we soldiers have a rule: never tell the enemy anything but lies.”

Catherine sighed, but said nothing. The tale he told stirred deep sorrow within them all—a storm of grief and agitation. They wept at the thought of the friends parted so cruelly, and even now Denys could scarcely speak of it without emotion. Yet, slowly, their pain gave way to a bright hope: the joyful expectation that Gerard would return soon.

Denys shared that hope fully. But he reminded them that such confidence did not mean they should ignore Gerard’s last wishes. “Suppose he returns next week,” he said, “and Margaret is nowhere to be found—what kind of welcome will he get? What face shall I have to show him?”

Catherine, who had never before been so fond of the absent Margaret, now felt a fierce tenderness toward her. She was just as eager to find her as Denys was—to bring her back, to comfort her, to make peace before Gerard came home. But she could not agree with Denys that they should leave Sevenbergen to search for her. “She must have told someone where she was going,” she insisted. “It’s not as if they were fugitives. They owe no one a stiver here. And dear heart, Denys, you can’t hunt all of Holland for one girl.”

“Can I not?” Denys replied grimly. “We shall see.”

After a pause, he added, “We’ll divide our forces. You stay here—keep your eyes and ears open. I’ll go through every town in Holland, if need be.”

“But she won’t be far,” Catherine said. “Three people don’t travel fast or far, not like one.”

“That’s sensible,” she admitted. But she insisted he visit the demoiselle Van Eyck first. “She and Margaret were close friends. If anyone knows where she’s gone, it’s her.”

Denys was ready to go at once. So Catherine, with a quick touch of neatness, joined him. They arrived at the Van Eyck’s house, where the old lady sat in a richly furnished room. She received them graciously, and Catherine laid out their purpose.

At the mention of Margaret’s name, the tapestry slipped from the demoiselle’s hands. “Gone? Gone from Sevenbergen—and didn’t tell me? The ungrateful child.”

The visitors were stunned. “You didn’t know? When was the last time she visited?”

“Perhaps ten days ago,” the woman said. “I’d taken out my brushes after years of silence, meaning to paint her portrait. But I didn’t finish it—for reasons.”

Catherine frowned. “It’s most strange that she should leave like this—without warning, without word or reason. Just when all our hearts are warm to her! And now here is Gerard’s friend returned from the ends of the earth, bringing news and comfort from Gerard himself—and cannot find her. And Gerard himself is expected any day. What are we to do? I’m sure she didn’t leave without cause. Can you not give us a clue, my good demoiselle? Prithee, do.”

“I have none to give,” the elder woman said, slightly testy.

Then Reicht Heynes appeared in the doorway, cheeks flushed. “So you’ve been listening all along, eh?”

“What are my ears for, mistress?” Reicht retorted.

“True enough,” the demoiselle conceded. “Then throw some light on this dark matter.”

“There’s no darkness to me,” Reicht said. “And the clue? Call it a two-ply thread, and both ends are right here in this room. Oh, mistress, I wonder at you sitting there pretending.”

“Marry, come up!” the mistress snapped, her own face nearly as red as Reicht’s.

“So it was *you* who drove the foolish girl away.”

“I did my part,” Reicht said quietly. “What sort of greeting did you give her last time she came? Do you think she couldniced it, being all alone and friendless? And you said, ‘I’ve changed my mind about painting you,’ turning up your nose at her.”

“I did not turn up my nose,” the demoiselle protested. “Mine isn’t shaped like yours for looking heavenward.”

“Oh, all noses follow the heart’s bent,” Reicht said. “Poor soul. She came into the kitchen to me. ‘I am not to be painted now,’ she said—tears in her eyes. She said no more. But I knew well what she meant. I had seen you.”

“Well,” said Margaret Van Eyck, “I confess it—and I ask you to judge me, madam. These young girls do nothing on their own. They mimic their lovers’ ways. Now, your Gerard is skilled in many things, especially illuminating. Margaret is his pupil—patient and devoted. What marvel? She has a woman’s eye for color, and a lover to imitate. It’s a trick I despise: for by it, the noble art of color—free, royal, aspiring—becomes enslaved to petty crafts. It’s reduced to serving letters and books, caged like insects in gold leaf, made small to match the littleness of ink and parchment.”

“And yet,” she continued, “affection rules us all. When the poor girl brought me her thorn leaves, lilies, ivy, dewberries, ladybirds, butterfly grubs—Nature’s very scum—stuck fast in gold like wasps in honey, and her daily book showing she’d spent hundreds of hours on each page, I was furious. An immortal soul, so much labor, so much skill—wasted on trash, leaves, bugs, and barren letters! But I have feelings. I held back, dammed my better judgment, and looked kindly upon it, to see how it might be improved. I said, ‘Since Heaven has doomed us to spend our time, soul, and color on great letters and tiny beetles, rather than saints and heroes, why not present Nature as it truly is?’ I told her, ‘The grapes I see walking abroad hang in the air, not stuck in a wall. Even these insects—these creatures of Nature’s slime—do not live wedged in metal prisons like flies in glue, but crawl and hover freely, infesting the air.’”

“Ah, my dear friend,” the girl had said, “I see now where you’re going—but this ground is gold; we may not shade.”

“Who said so?” I asked.

“All teachers of this craft,” she replied. “And—Gerard himself!”

“Then let Gerard and his gang go to the devil,” I said. “Give me a brush!”

And so I chose a color false in nature, but true in relation to that glaring gold background. In five minutes, I painted a bunch of raspberries—stalk and all—so lifelike they seemed ready to fall into your mouth. A butterfly grub she’d drawn so truthfully it could turn the stoutest stomach. My lady flung her arms around my neck and cried, ‘Oh!’”

“Did she?” Denys managed to say at last.

“The little love!” he added.

Margaret Van Eyck stared, then smiled.

She went on to explain how step by step, she’d been led to promise to take up painting again—the art she’d abandoned with a sigh when her brothers died—and to paint the Madonna once more—with Margaret as model. And she revealed, almost casually, how girls became saints.

“Your hair is adorable,” she’d said.

“Why, it’s red,” Margaret replied.

“Yes,” the artist said, “but what a red! How brown! How glossy! Most hair is worthless to us painters. Yours is the artist’s very hue. But your violet eyes—earthly, yet now languid without Gerard, now fiery in hope of him—I will lift them to heaven in holy meditation. Your nose, which already turns upward (though not as piously as Reicht’s), I will soften slightly. And your chin—strong, resolute—will be weakened a bit.”

“Weakened her chin?” Catherine gasped. “Alack, what does that mean? I don’t understand.”

“It’s a resolute chin,” the demoiselle explained. “Not too strong for this world—but for a Madonna? No thank you.”

“Well, I never,” Denys muttered. “A resolute chin.”

“And now comes the rub,” the old lady continued. “When you told me she was—well, *that*—it struck me hard. I dropped my brushes. Was I really going to turn a girl who couldn’t keep her lover at bay into the Virgin Mary—at my age? I still love the poor ninny. But I adore our blessed Lady. Say you, ‘a painter mustn’t be peevish in such matters’? Well, most painters are men—and men can do anything. Their saints and virgins are no different from their mistresses. But I am not a man. I am a woman. My painting is half craft, half devotion. And so now you may read me. It was foolish, perhaps, but I couldn’t help it. Yet I regret it.”

She ended despondently, having begun in defiance.

“Well, dame,” Catherine said gently, “you must think how it would wound the poor girl’s heart—she so fond of you.”

Margaret Van Eyck only sighed.

Reicht, who had been biting her lips impatiently, turned sharply on Catherine. “Why, dame, do you think Margaret and Peter left Sevenbergen *only* because of that? Nay.”

“For what else, then?” Catherine asked.

“What else? Because Gerard’s family treats her so cruelly. Who would stay among cold-hearted folk who sent her lad to Italy—and now, with him gone, refuse to relent, refuse to come near her, even though she’s left behind?”

Reicht’s voice rose. “You said you were going to see her.”

“Oh, yes—going, and going, and going,” Reicht snapped. “You should have said less—or done more. With your words, you lifted her heart—and with your deeds, you crushed it. ‘They have never been,’ she said to me, with such a sigh. And I know how she feels—because I, too, am far from my kin. When I first came to Holland, I often cried to myself. But ten times rather would I be Reicht Heynes, with leagues between me and all my kith, than be her—surrounded by those who ought to love her, yet treat her as lonely as I.”

“Alack, Reicht,” Catherine said, “I did go yesterday—and before that. But something always came up.”

“Mistress,” Reicht said sharply, “did anything stop you from eating your dinner those days? I doubt it. And if your heart had been as kind to your own flesh and blood as it was to your flesher’s meat, nothing could have kept you from her—sitting alone, watching the road for you, her child’s child beating beneath her breast.”

Before Reicht could continue, the Van Eyck sprang from her seat. “Since you disrespect my guests, seek another place!”

“With all my heart,” Reicht said boldly.

“Nay, mistress,” Catherine interjected, “true folk speak plainly. Her tongue is a stinger.”

Tears welled in her eyes.

“But better she said it than thought it. Now it won’t rankle. And you shall not part with her. Beshrew the wench—she knows she’s a good servant and takes advantage. We poor women who keep house must pay tax for value. I once had a fine servant when I was young. Dear me, how she ground me down! In the end, by Heaven’s mercy, she married the baker—and I was my own woman again. ‘No more good servants shall come here,’ I said. ‘I’ll take fools and teach them. As soon as they learn their right hand from their left, they saucy me—then out they go, and I take another dunce.’ It’s wearisome, teaching fools one by one—but there—*I* am mistress.” She paused, then added, with a sudden twist, “And you are mistress here, I suppose.”

“No more than that stool,” the Van Eyck declared loftily. “She is neither mistress nor servant—she is *gone*. Dismissed. There’s an end of her. Did you not hear me turn the saucy baggage off?”

“Ay, ay—we all heard,” Reicht said, utterly indifferent.

“Then hear me!” Denys said solemnly.

They all turned, like dials, to fix their eyes on him.

“Ay, let us hear what the man says,” urged the hostess. “Men are fine fellows, with their great hoarse voices.”

“Mistress Reicht,” Denys began, with dignity so grand it verged on absurdity, “you are dismissed. If I may advise—since you are no longer a servant, become a mistress, a queen.”

“Easier said than done,” Reicht replied bluntly.

“Not a jot,” Denys answered. “Here is one who is a man—though only half an arbalestrier, thanks to that Englishman’s arrow. But I left a similar token in his body. That is comfort. I have twenty gold pieces” (he displayed them) “and a strong arm. In another week or so, I shall have twice as many. Marriage is not my habit—but I capitulate to virtue. You are beautiful, kind, honest, and above all—you stand by my she-comrade. Be then an arbalestriesse!”

“And what the devil is that?” Reicht asked.

“I mean—be the wife, the mistress, the queen of Denys of Burgundy here present.”

Silence fell—deep and sudden.

Then, it broke into a burst of outraged indignation.

Catherine: “Well, did you ever?”

Margaret: “Never in all my born days.”

Catherine: “Before our very faces!”

Margaret: “Of all the absurdity, and insolence of this ridiculous sex—”

Denys observed dryly, “The female to whom I spoke is silent. The others, whose eloquence is not demanded, are loud.” The voices ceased. All eyes turned to Reicht.

She glanced up at him from beneath her lashes and said, “I mean to look at any man carefully before I leap into his arms.”

Denys straightened to his full height. “Then look your fill—and leap.”

This sparked a new, unexpected development. The Van Eyck thrust out a long white finger, level with her eye, and shouted, “Stand, in the name of all the saints!”

“You are beautiful,” she cried. “You are inspired—with folly. What matters? You are inspired. I must take off your head!”

And in a flash, she was sketching him with her pencil.

“Come out, hussy!” she screamed at Reicht. “More in front of him. Keep the fool inspired and beautiful. Oh, why didn’t I have this madman for my centurion? They sent me a brute with a low forehead and a shapeless beard.”

Catherine stood, stunned, watching this pantomime. Secretly, she decided her venerable hostess had been a disguised lunatic all along—and was now revealing her true self.

Reicht, meanwhile, was miserable and cross. “I left my caldron in a precarious state,” she declared. “And duties so grave as mine leave no time to waste playing the statee and the fool.”

Her mistress replied, “It is possible to be rude and rebellious to your poor, old, affectionate, desolate mistress—without being utterly heartless and trampling on art.”

Reicht stopped. Pouted. Looked like a little basilisk at the inspired model causing her distress.

He met her gaze with unwavering admiration.

Only when the artist’s wrist cramped from disuse did the scene dissolve. By then, she had made a rough but noble sketch.

“I can work no more,” she said sadly.

“Then, mistress,” Reicht asked, “may I go to my pot?”

“Ay, ay—go to your pot! And get into it. You’ll find your soul there. Then you’ll all be together.”

“Well, but Reicht,” Catherine laughed, “she turned you off.”

“Boo, boo, boo!” Reicht scoffed. “If she wants to get rid of me, let her turn herself off and die. I’m sure she’s old enough. But take your time, mistress—if you’re not in a hurry, neither am I. When that day comes, it’ll take a man to dry my eyes. And if you’re still of the same mind then, soldier, you can say so. If not—well, it’ll be all one to Reicht Heynes.”

And she walked away.

But her words did not fall unheard. Neither Catherine nor the demoiselle could hide from themselves that this blunt, solitary girl had likely read Margaret Brandt correctly—and that she had left Sevenbergen, broken-hearted.

Catherine and Denys bid farewell to the Van Eyck, and that very afternoon, Denys set out on his quest—a wild goose chase, indeed.

His plan, like all great undertakings, was simple: he would visit a hundred towns and villages, asking in each about an old physician with a fair daughter, and an old longbow soldier. He would question burgomasters about newcomers, and stand by fountains, watching the women and girls as they drew water.

And so he went—on foot, across Holland, month after month—without finding her.

Yet, in a way, the journey was its own reward. Those who sit at home, blindfolded by pride, believe that peace and quiet are the truest joys after danger. But those who observe before they speak know that men who risk their lives constantly crave excitement between battles—they are not made for innocent tranquility.

Denys was no exception. His life had been half Sparta, half Capua—soldier and libertine, never mixed. But now, for the first time, he found himself caught between peace and duty. And he saw it as duty.

So, for months, he was a demi-Spartan—sober, vigilant, indomitable, and strangely happy, despite constant disappointment.

He flirted wildly on the road—but wasted no time on it. And during all his wanderings, he never told a single woman that “marriage was not one of his habits.”

And so we leave him—the Pilgrim of Friendship—tramping the roads of Holland, seeking not just a girl, but the honor of a vow.
