It has always struck me—though I speak humbly before the judgment of seasoned military men—that the earthworks of Breed's Hill served as the backbone of the Continental Army throughout our entire war. The slaughter inflicted upon us from behind those lines was so devastating, and the resolve of the enemy so unyielding, that the British commanders thereafter held a profound respect for American barricades. Indeed, if the foe were merely firing from behind a row of blankets, certain of our own generals hesitated to force the breach. At White Plains, when Washington's army stood on the brink of total annihilation, we later learned that our victorious troops were halted in their tracks by a mere barrier of cornstalks and straw.

Another chance was granted us, lasting through a harsh winter. During this season, the dwindling, starved, and disheartened forces of Congress lay helpless under our grasp, while the magnanimous Lord Howe left the infamous encampment of Valley Forge untouched. Instead, his great, brave, and impeccably equipped army fiddled, gambled, and feasted in the comfort of Philadelphia. By the order of his compatriots, triumphal arches were raised, medieval-style tournaments staged in mocking celebration, and beautiful ladies draped wreaths around the necks of this "clement" chief, offering fantastical mottos declaring his laurels immortal. Yet, why have my ungrateful countrymen in America never erected a statue to this general? For in their entire army, there was no officer who fought their battles better; no one who retrieved their errors with such adroitness; no one who ensured their defeats caused them so little harm. Most importantly, when fortune shifted and the stronger force inevitably gained the upper hand, who displayed such tireless tenderness, patience, and complacency in helping the defeated opponent rise again?

Ah! Recall the eighteen years prior, and the fiery young warrior England dispatched to fight her adversary across the American continent. Imagine him forever pacing the defenses behind which the enemy lay sheltered, sleepless and eager by day and night. Consumed by fierce wrath and longing, he never closed his eye, intent on watching, winding his track with an untiring scent that panted and hungered for blood and battle. He prowled through midnight forests or climbed silent precipices before dawn, until his great heart was nearly worn out, until finally the foe revealed himself. Then he sprang upon him, grappled with him, and, dying, slew him! Think of Wolfe at Quebec, and then listen to Howe's fiddles as he sat smiling among the dancers in Philadelphia.

A favorite scheme among our ministers at home and some of our American generals was to establish a communication between Canada and New York, hoping thereby to sever New England from the neighboring colonies, overpower them in detail, and force submission. Burgoyne was entrusted with this plan, setting forth from Quebec with confident promises of success. His march began in military splendor; the trumpets of his proclamations blew ahead, bidding the colonists remember the immense power of England and summoning the misguided rebels to lay down their arms. He brought a formidable English force, an army of German veterans, a dreadful band of Indian warriors, and a brilliant train of artillery. It was expected that the populace along his route would rally to the Royal cause.

Initially, the Continental force opposing him was small, and Washington's army was further weakened by the withdrawal of troops hurriedly sent to meet this Canadian invasion. This was the moment when Washington's diminished army should have been struck down; but a greater Power willed otherwise. Nor am I, for one, inclined to regret the ultimate termination of the war. Looking back over the game now, how clear the blunders appear on the losing side! From start to finish, we were perpetually arriving too late. Our supplies and reinforcements from home arrived too late. Our troops struggled, and our succor reached them too late. Our fleet appeared off Yorktown just too late, after Cornwallis had already surrendered. A way of escape was opened to Burgoyne, but he resolved upon retreat too late.

I have heard discomfited officers in later days claim infallibly how a different wind would have saved America for us; how we must have destroyed the French fleet but for a few tempests; how, once, twice, thrice, but for the fall of night, Washington and his army would have fallen into our power. Who has not, while reading history, speculated upon the "has-beens" and "might-have-beens" of the world? I take my tattered old map-book from the shelf and gaze upon the board where the great contest was played. I marvel at the curious chances that lost it. Putting aside any idle talk regarding the relative bravery of the two nations, I cannot help but see that we held the best cards, yet we lost the game.

I confess the sport possessed a considerable fascination for me, stirring my languid blood. My brother Hal, settled on his plantation in Virginia, was perfectly content with the pursuits he found there. The company of country neighbors sufficed him; he never tired of tending his crops and people, catching fish, shooting ducks, hunting in the woods, or enjoying his rubber and supper. Happy Hal, in his great barn-like house, beneath his roomy porches, with dogs lying at his feet; his friends, the Virginian Will Wimbles, staying at free quarters in his mansion; his negroes fat, lazy, and ragged; his shrewd little wife ruling over them and her husband—who always obeyed her implicitly while alive, and was quickly consoled when she died! I say "happy," though his lot would have been intolerable to me. Wife, friends, plantation, and town life in Richmond (which succeeded to the honor of being the capital when our Province became a State)—how happy is he whose foot fits the shoe Fortune provides!

My income was five times greater, my house in England larger, built of brick and faced with freestone. My wife—would I have changed her for any other woman in the world? My children—well, I am content with Lady Warrington's opinion of them. But with all these plums, peaches, and rich fruits pouring from Plenty's horn into my lap, I fear I have been but an ingrate. Hodge, my gatekeeper, who shares his bread and scrap of bacon with a family as large as my own, seems to enjoy his meal as much as I do, despite Mrs. Molly preparing her finest dishes and Mr. Gumbo uncorking the choicest bottles from the cellar. Alas! Sweetmeats have lost their flavor for me, though they may still delight my young ones in the nursery, and the perfume of claret grows stale upon old noses.

Our parson has preached many sermons to me, and perhaps I cared little for them when he first began. Do you remember, honest friend? (Sure he does, for he has retold the story over the bottle as often as he has delivered his sermons, and my Lady Warrington pretends she has never heard it.) I say, Joe Blake, you remember full well, with advantage, that October evening when we scrambled up an embrasure at Fort Clinton, and a clubbed musket would have dashed these valuable brains out, had not Joe's sword whipped my rebellious countryman through the gizzard. Joe wore a red coat in those days—the uniform of the brave Sixty-Third, whose leader, the bold Sill, fell pierced with many wounds beside him. He exchanged his red coat for black and my pulpit. His doctrines are sound, and his sermons short. We read the newspapers together over our wine. Not two months ago, we read our old friend Howe's glorious deed of the first of June. We were told how the noble Rawdon, who fought with us at Fort Clinton, had joined the Duke of York; and today, His Royal Highness is in full retreat before Pichegru, while he and my son Miles have taken Valenciennes for nothing! Ah, Parson! Would you not like to don your old Sixty-Third coat? (Though I doubt Mrs. Blake could ever make the buttons and buttonholes meet again over your large frame.)

The boys were acting a play with my militia sword. Oh, that I were young again, Mr. Blake! That I had not the gout in my toe! I would saddle Rocinante and ride back into the world, feel the pulses beat again, and play a little of life's glorious game! The last "hit" I witnessed was gallantly won by our side; though it is true that even in this party, the Americans won the rubber. Our people gained only the ground they stood on, capturing and destroying the guns, stores, and ships, while our efforts at rescue were too late to prevent the catastrophe looming over Burgoyne's unfortunate army.

After one of those inevitable delays that retarded our plans and weakened the blows our chiefs intended to deliver, an expedition was launched from New York at the close of September, 1777. Had it advanced merely a fortnight earlier, it might have saved the doomed force of Burgoyne. *Sed Dis aliter visum* (But Fate saw otherwise). The delay here was not Sir Henry Clinton's fault, who could not leave his city unprotected, but rather the winds and weather that delayed the arrival of reinforcements we had long awaited from England. The fleet bringing them carried long, fond letters from home, containing the very latest news of the children under the care of their good Aunt Hetty and their grandfather.
