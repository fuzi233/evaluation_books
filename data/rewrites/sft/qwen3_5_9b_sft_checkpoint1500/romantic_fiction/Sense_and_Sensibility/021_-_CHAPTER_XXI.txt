Sir John and his wife departed for Cleveland the following day, leaving Barton Hall to the solitude of the Dashwoods and the Middletons. Yet this brief respite from social intrusion proved fleeting. Before Elinor could fully settle the lingering questions regarding Charlotte Palmer's unexplained happiness or Mr. Palmer's unassuming demeanor, Sir John and Mrs. Jennings returned with fresh recruits for their circle of acquaintances.

During a morning excursion to Exeter, the pair had encountered two young ladies whom Mrs. Jennings promptly identified as relatives. Seizing the opportunity, Sir John immediately invited them to the Park, overriding any remaining local commitments. To Lady Middleton, this announcement sent a tremor of genuine alarm coursing through her. She had never laid eyes upon these strangers, possessed no evidence of their elegance or gentility, and relied solely on the hollow assurances of her husband and mother, which held little weight in her mind. That they were her cousins only deepened her apprehension, rendering Mrs. Jennings' counsel—that they were kin and therefore must be endured—to seem woefully inadequate. Despite her protests, however, Lady Middleton eventually acquiesced to the inevitable, resigning herself with the stoic composure of a well-born woman, though she did not fail to deliver a gentle, daily reprimand to her husband regarding his poor taste in company.

The sisters arrived shortly thereafter, and their reception was, by all accounts, gratifying. Dressed with smart precision and conducting themselves with polite civility, they expressed a delight in the house and furniture that bordered on the ecstatic. Furthermore, their evident, almost doting affection for the children won Lady Middleton's favor within the hour. To her ladyship, their enthusiasm constituted "enthusiastic admiration," and Sir John's confidence swelled accordingly. He hastened to the cottage to announce the news to the Miss Dashwoods, declaring the Miss Steeles to be the sweetest girls imaginable. However, Elinor, perceptive and grounded, recognized that the title "sweetest girl" could apply to countless women across England, regardless of their varied forms, faces, tempers, or intellects. Sir John, a benevolent and somewhat garrulous soul, insisted that the entire family accompany him to view his guests, unable to tolerate keeping even a distant cousin to himself.

"Do come now!" he urged, his voice rising in persuasion. "Pray, come! You must see them. Lucy is monstrously pretty and so good-humored! Already the children are clustering around her as if she were an old friend. They both long to see you above all else, having heard at Exeter that you are the most beautiful creatures in the world. I have confirmed their stories, adding greatly to the truth. You will be charmed, I am certain. They have filled the carriage with toys for the children. Why are you so cross? After all, you are cousins, in a manner of speaking. You are mine, and they are my wife's; surely there is blood between us."

Despite his fervor, Sir John could not secure an immediate audience. He managed only a vague promise of a visit within a day or two before departing, returning home to boast anew of the Miss Steeles' attractions, as he had done previously.

When the promised visit finally occurred, the reality of the encounter offered a starker contrast to Sir John's hyperbolic descriptions. The eldest sister, nearing thirty, possessed a countenance that was neither plain nor sensible, offering little to admire. Her younger companion, however, aged no more than twenty-two or twenty-three, displayed considerable beauty. Her features were pleasing, her eye sharp and quick, and her bearing possessed a distinct smartness that, while lacking true grace, lent her a notable presence. Their manners were notably civil, and Elinor quickly accorded them credit for a measure of sense, observing with what constant and judicious attention they strove to please Lady Middleton.

With the children, they were in a state of perpetual rapture, extolling their beauty, courting their notice, and humor every whim. Any spare moments they had were devoted to admiring Lady Middleton's activities, whether performing tasks or modeling elegant new dresses that had captivated them the previous day. For those who court through such trivialities, a fond mother, though rapacious in her pursuit of praise for her offspring, remains the most credulous of humans. Her demands are exorbitant, yet she swallows anything. Consequently, the excessive affection and endurance shown by the Miss Steeles toward Lady Middleton's children passed without the slightest suspicion. Lady Middleton viewed their impertinent encroachments and mischievous tricks with maternal complacency, interpreting their sashes being untied, hair pulled, and workbags rifled through as signs of reciprocal enjoyment. The only anomaly that struck her was the composed silence of Elinor and Marianne, who sat apart from the lively chaos.

"John is in such spirits today!" she remarked, watching Sir John toss a pocket-handkerchief from a window. "He is full of monkey-tricks."

Soon after, observing the second boy pinch a finger, she fondly exclaimed, "How playful William is!" Then, tenderly embracing a silent toddler, she cooed, "And here is my sweet little Annamaria... Never was there such a quiet little thing!"

Unfortunately, in bestowing these embraces, a pin hidden in her head-dress lightly scratched the child's neck. This minor injury triggered screams of such violence that they rivaled any noise the children usually made. Lady Middleton's consternation was profound, but it paled beside the alarm of the visiting sisters. In a frantic display of affection, the trio rushed to soothe the sufferer. The child was lifted into her mother's lap, showered with kisses, bathed with lavender water by one of the Miss Steeles, who knelt to attend her, while the other stuffed her mouth with sugar-plums. Yet, the child remained wise enough to continue weeping, kicking her brothers who dared approach her. United soothings proved ineffective until Lady Middleton recalled a similar incident involving apricot marmalade for a bruised temple. A slight intermission of screaming followed the proposal of this remedy, giving hope for acceptance. The child was carried out to seek the medicine, followed by the two boys despite her mother's entreaties, leaving the four young ladies alone in a sudden, heavy quiet.

"Poor little creatures!" sighed Miss Steele as soon as the children departed. "It might have been a very sad accident."

"And yet I hardly know how," cried Marianne, "unless it had been under totally different circumstances. But this is the usual method of heightening alarm where there is naught truly alarming."

"What a sweet woman Lady Middleton is!" observed Lucy Steele.

Marianne remained silent, for it was impossible for her to feign sentiments she did not feel, however trivial the occasion. Thus, the burden of maintaining polite falsehoods fell entirely upon Elinor. She endeavored to speak of Lady Middleton with greater warmth than she genuinely felt, though far less effusively than Miss Lucy.

"And Sir John too!" exclaimed the elder sister. "What a charming man he is!"

Again, Miss Dashwood's commendation, simple and just, lacked the éclat of her companion. She merely noted that he was perfectly good-humored and friendly.

"And what a charming little family they have! I never saw such fine children in my life. I declare I quite doat upon them already, and indeed I am always distractedly fond of children."

"I should guess so," said Elinor, smiling, "from what I have witnessed this morning."

"I have a notion," said Lucy, "that you think the little Middletons rather too much indulged; perhaps they are the outside of enough. But it is so natural in Lady Middleton. For my part, I love to see children full of life and spirits; I cannot bear them if they are tame and quiet."

"I confess," replied Elinor, "that while I am at Barton Park, I never think of tame and quiet children with any abhorrence."

A short pause ensued, broken by Miss Steele, who seemed eager for conversation. "And how do you like Devonshire, Miss Dashwood?" she asked abruptly. "I suppose you were very sorry to leave Sussex."

Startled by the familiarity of the tone, Elinor admitted that she had indeed been sorry.

"Norland is a prodigious beautiful place, is it not?" added Miss Steele.

"We have heard Sir John admire it excessively," said Lucy, seemingly feeling compelled to apologize for her sister's boldness.

"I think everyone *must* admire it," replied Elinor. "Though it is not to be supposed that anyone can estimate its beauties as we do."

"And had you a great many smart beaux there? I suppose you have not so many in this part of the world; for my part, I think they are a vast addition always."

"But why should you think," interrupted Lucy, blushing, "that there are not as many genteel young men in Devonshire as Sussex?"
