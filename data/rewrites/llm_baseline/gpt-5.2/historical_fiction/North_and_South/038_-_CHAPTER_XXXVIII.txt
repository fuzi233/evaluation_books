It was not only that Margaret Hale had been known to Mr. Thornton to have spoken falsely—though she told herself that this alone had turned his mind against her—but that the falsehood, in his thoughts, clung like a burr to one conclusion: another man.

He could not dislodge the image—fond, earnest, unmistakably intimate—of the look he had once seen pass between her and that other. The posture of trust, of familiar confidence (and, if he would be honest, something nearer to endearment). It rose before him in the clatter of the mill, in the hush of his own room, in the very rhythm of his tread across the hearthrug. And then—because misery never comes with one barb only—the setting returned with its cruel exactness: dusk thickening into twilight; a place far from home, and lonely enough to make even innocence appear like secrecy.

His better self had argued, at first, that circumstances might be accidental, innocent, justifiable. But once grant her the right to love and be beloved (and had he any ground to deny it, after the clear severity with which she had cast his own love from her?), it was easy—terribly easy—to imagine she had been drawn on to linger, to walk farther, to stay later than she had meant.

Yet always the same thought cut him deeper than the rest: that lie.

A lie implied consciousness; something wrong to be hidden; a shame crouched somewhere behind the bright candour in which he had believed she lived. And that was what made it agony rather than simple contempt. He did her the justice—though it would have relieved him to call her utterly unworthy—that falsehood was unlike her. Therefore, in his tortured reasoning, the stain became proof not of her baseness, but of the strength of her attachment: she had loved another so blindly as to violate her own truthful nature for him. The dark, slight, elegant, handsome man—while he himself was rough and stern, strongly made, a Milton manufacturer whose hands were more used to iron and cotton than to tenderness.

Jealousy lashed him as if it were a scourge. He went back, point by point, to her sharp words; to the sting of her saying she would have done as much for any man in the crowd—more readily than for him. He had tried to value the mechanical courage with which she had flung herself between him and the mob; now he mocked himself for it. That act, he told himself bitterly, had been for peace, for the crowd, for the common good. But with the other man there had been something withheld from the world: looks, words, hand-clasps, concealments—lies—all set apart and saved for him alone.

He was conscious he had never been so irritable in his life. Every question asked of him seemed to deserve a short, abrupt answer—more like a bark than speech. And the consciousness hurt his pride; he had always prided himself on self-control, and control himself he would. So he smoothed the manner into quiet deliberation; but the substance of what he said was harder, sterner than usual. At home he grew more silent still, passing his evenings in a continual pacing to and fro—an exercise that would have irritated his mother exceedingly had it been practised by anyone less dear than her son, and did little to soften her own tongue.

“Can you stop—can you sit down for a moment?” she cried at last. “I have something to say to you, if you would give up that everlasting walk, walk, walk.”

He sat down instantly, on a chair against the wall, as if obedience cost him less than motion.

“I want to speak to you about Betsy. She says she must leave us; that her lover’s death has so affected her spirits she can’t give her heart to her work.”

“Very well,” he said, his voice quiet with effort. “Other cooks are to be met with.”

“That’s so like a man. It’s not merely the cooking; it is that she knows all the ways of the house.” Mrs. Thornton’s mouth tightened. “Besides, she tells me something about your friend Miss Hale.”

“Miss Hale is no friend of mine. Mr. Hale is my friend.”

“I am glad to hear you say so,” returned his mother quickly, “for if she had been your friend, what Betsy says would have annoyed you.”

“Let me hear it,” he said, in that very composed tone he had been forcing on himself for days.

“Betsy says that the night on which her lover—I forget his name, for she always calls him ‘he’—”

“Leonards,” he answered, without looking at her.

“The night on which Leonards was last seen at the station—when he was last seen on duty, in fact—Miss Hale was there, walking about with a young man who, Betsy believes, killed Leonards by some blow or push.”

“Leonards was not killed by any blow or push.”

Mrs. Thornton’s head lifted. “How do you know?”

“Because I distinctly put the question to the surgeon of the Infirmary. He told me there was an internal disease of long standing, caused by Leonards’ habit of drinking to excess; that the fact of his becoming rapidly worse while in a state of intoxication, settled the question as to whether the last fatal attack was caused by excess of drinking, or the fall.”

“The fall!” she repeated sharply. “What fall?”

“Caused by the blow or push of which Betsy speaks.”

“Then there was a blow or push?”

“I believe so.”

“And who did it?”

“As there was no inquest,” he replied, the words clipped, “in consequence of the doctor’s opinion, I cannot tell you.”

“But Miss Hale was there?” His mother’s eyes bored into him.

He did not answer.

“And with a young man?” she persisted.

Still he said nothing. At last, as if to put up a barrier with a legal phrase, he observed: “I tell you, mother, that there was no inquest—no inquiry. No judicial inquiry, I mean.”

“Betsy says that Woolmer—some man she knows, who is in a grocer’s shop out at Crampton—can swear that Miss Hale was at the station at that hour, walking backwards and forwards with a young man.”

“I don’t see what we have to do with that,” he returned. “Miss Hale is at liberty to please herself.”

“I’m glad to hear you say so,” said Mrs. Thornton, with a kind of eager relish. “It certainly signifies very little to us—not at all to you, after what has passed! But I—I made a promise to Mrs. Hale, that I would not allow her daughter to go wrong without advising and remonstrating with her. I shall certainly let her know my opinion of such conduct.”

“I do not see any harm in what she did that evening,” he said, rising and coming nearer, as though the subject drove him from his seat. He stood by the chimney-piece with his face turned away, as if he could not endure to have his mother read his features.

“You would not have approved of Fanny’s being seen out, after dark, in rather a lonely place, walking about with a young man. I say nothing of the taste which could choose the time, when her mother lay unburied, for such a promenade. Should you have liked your sister to have been noticed by a grocer’s assistant for doing so?”

He answered without turning. “In the first place, as it is not many years since I myself was a draper’s assistant, the mere circumstance of a grocer’s assistant noticing any act does not alter the character of the act to me. And in the next place, I see a great deal of difference between Miss Hale and Fanny. I can imagine that the one may have weighty reasons, which may and ought to make her overlook any seeming impropriety in her conduct. I never knew Fanny have weighty reasons for anything. Other people must guard her. I believe Miss Hale is a guardian to herself.”

“A pretty character of your sister, indeed!” Mrs. Thornton snapped. “Really, John, one would have thought Miss Hale had done enough to make you clear-sighted. She drew you on to an offer, by a bold display of pretended regard for you,—to play you off against this very young man, I’ve no doubt. Her whole conduct is clear to me now. You believe he is her lover, I suppose—you agree to that.”

He turned. His face was grey, set like stone; and when he spoke, it was as if some part of him had been struck. “Yes, mother. I do believe he is her lover.” Then he wheeled away again, twisting as though in bodily pain. He leaned his forehead into his hand; and before she could seize on the confession, he swung back suddenly, quick as a man who cannot bear his own silence.

“Mother. He is her lover, whoever he is; but she may need help and womanly counsel;—there may be difficulties or temptations which I don’t know. I fear there are. I don’t want to know what they are; but as you have ever been a good—ay! and a tender mother to me, go to her, and gain her confidence, and tell her what is best to be done. I know that something is wrong; some dread, which must be a terrible torture to her.”

“For God’s sake, John!” His mother’s harshness gave way to a real shock. “What do you mean? What do you mean? What do you know?”

He did not reply.

“John! I don’t know what I shan’t think unless you speak. You have no right to say what you have done against her.”

“Not against her, mother! I could not speak against her.”

“Well! you have no right to say what you have done, unless you say more. These half-expressions are what ruin a woman’s character.”

“Her character! Mother, you do not dare—” He faced her, eyes lit, and for one instant all his controlled composure seemed likely to shatter. Then he drew himself up, forcing dignity over passion like a lid clamped down.

“I will not say any more than this, which is neither more nor less than the simple truth, and I am sure you believe me,—I have good reason to believe, that Miss Hale is in some strait and difficulty connected with an attachment which, of itself, from my knowledge of Miss Hale’s character, is perfectly innocent and right. What my reason is, I refuse to tell. But never let me hear any one say a word against her, implying any more serious imputation than that she now needs the counsel of some kind and gentle woman. You promised Mrs. Hale to be that woman!”

“No!” said Mrs. Thornton, drawing in a breath that seemed to sharpen her. “I am happy to say, I did not promise kindness and gentleness, for I felt at the time that it might be out of my power to render these to one of Miss Hale’s character and disposition. I promised counsel and advice, such as I would give to my own daughter; I shall speak to her as I would do to Fanny, if she had gone gallivanting with a young man in the dusk. I shall speak with relation to the circumstances I know, without being influenced either one way or another by the ‘strong reasons’ which you will not confide to me. Then I shall have fulfilled my promise, and done my duty.”

“She will never bear it,” he said, the words coming out like a groan.

“She will have to bear it, if I speak in her dead mother’s name.”

“Well!” he broke away, as if her voice had struck him. “Don’t tell me any more about it. I cannot endure to think of it. It will be better that you should speak to her any way, than that she should not be spoken to at all.” Then, as he bolted himself into his private room, the last words escaped him between his teeth, half curse, half prayer. “Oh! that look of love! And that cursed lie; which showed some terrible shame in the background, to be kept from the light in which I thought she lived perpetually! Oh, Margaret, Margaret! Mother, how you have tortured me! Oh! Margaret, could you not have loved me? I am but uncouth and hard, but I would never have led you into any falsehood for me.”

The more Mrs. Thornton brooded on what her son had pleaded—mercy for Margaret’s “indiscretion”—the more bitter her feeling grew. She took a savage satisfaction in the thought of “speaking her mind” under the clean apron of duty. She liked the fancy of showing herself untouched by the glamour she knew Margaret could cast over weaker people, as if beauty were some costly spice that made harsh meat palatable. She went to bed turning the girl’s black hair and clear skin and lucid eyes over in her thoughts with contempt, as a housekeeper might look at a showy dish—handsome to see, but badly seasoned, and unsafe to trust.

When morning came she did not wait to be softened by time. She went.

“Is Miss Hale within?” she asked, though she had already seen Margaret at the window. She had her feet in the little hall before Martha had half shaped the answer.

Margaret was alone, writing to Edith—pages filled with her mother’s last days, set down with the careful tenderness of one who tries to keep the dead near by naming every small particular. It was softening work; tears came unbidden, and she brushed them away just as Mrs. Thornton was announced.

Margaret rose to receive her with a gentle, ladylike composure that disconcerted attack. Her voice, low and rich, was softer than usual; her manner more gracious, because gratitude warmed her—gratitude for the courteous attention of this call, for remembered kindnesses that now seemed doubly precious in bereavement. She tried to find easy subjects: she praised Martha, the servant Mrs. Thornton had found for them; she spoke of Edith and asked after the little Greek air of which Miss Thornton had told her. The very domesticity of it—the familiar names, the soft rustle of ordinary talk—made Mrs. Thornton’s prepared severity feel oddly out of place, like a sharp knife laid among rose-leaves.

She sat, silent at first, because she was forcing herself back into the posture of duty. Then suspicion pricked her: what if this sweetness were a coating—sugar poured over something bitter—meant to propitiate Mr. Thornton? What if the other attachment had failed, and it now suited Miss Hale to draw back her rejected lover? Poor Margaret—there was only this truth in it, that Mrs. Thornton was mother to the one person whose esteem Margaret dreaded she had lost; and that fear, unconfessed, lent an added eagerness to her wish to please.

At last Mrs. Thornton stood, as if to go; yet the words she had come to say clung in her throat. She cleared it and began.

“Miss Hale, I have a duty to perform. I promised your poor mother that, as far as my poor judgment went, I would not allow you to act in any way wrongly, or”—here she softened, grudgingly—“inadvertently, without remonstrating; at least without offering advice, whether you took it or not.”

Margaret stood before her, blushing like a culprit. Her eyes widened, searching Mrs. Thornton’s face. She thought the reproach was to be for the lie she had told—perhaps Mr. Thornton had sent his mother to warn her how near she had come to exposure, to public contradiction. Her heart sank that he had not come himself, to hear her penitence, to restore her—if restoration were possible—to his good opinion; but humility had beaten pride too low for resistance. On that charge she would have borne blame meekly.

Mrs. Thornton continued.

“At first, when I heard from one of my servants, that you had been seen walking about with a gentleman, so far from home as the Outwood station, at such a time of the evening, I could hardly believe it. But my son, I am sorry to say, confirmed her story. It was indiscreet, to say the least; many a young woman has lost her character before now—”

The word struck Margaret like a slap. Fire leapt in her eyes. This was new; this was insult. If Mrs. Thornton had come about the falsehood—well. That would have been deserved; she would have owned it, humbled herself, taken the pain as she must. But to interfere with her conduct—to speak of her character—she, a stranger, presuming to suspect—

She would not answer. Not a word.

Mrs. Thornton saw the battle in her eyes, and it called up her own combative temper like a matched flint.

“For your mother’s sake,” she went on, “I have thought it right to warn you against such improprieties; they must degrade you in the long run in the estimation of the world, even if in fact they do not lead you to positive harm.”

“For my mother’s sake,” Margaret said, her voice shaken with tears, “I will bear much; but I cannot bear everything. She never meant me to be exposed to insult, I am sure.”

“Insult, Miss Hale!”

“Yes, madam,” Margaret answered more steadily, “it is insult. What do you know of me that should lead you to suspect—Oh!” Her strength broke; she covered her face with her hands. “I know now. Mr. Thornton has told you—”

“No, Miss Hale,” said Mrs. Thornton; and her own truthfulness, stiff and unbending, made her check the confession that trembled on Margaret’s lips, though curiosity itched to hear it. “Stop. Mr. Thornton has told me nothing. You do not know my son. You are not worthy to know him.”

She drew herself up, and her voice grew hard with pride in him.

“He said this. Listen, young lady, that you may understand, if you can, what sort of a man you rejected. This Milton manufacturer, his great tender heart scorned as it was scorned, said to me only last night, ‘Go to her. I have good reason to know that she is in some strait arising out of some attachment; and she needs womanly counsel.’ I believe those were his very words. Farther than that—beyond admitting the fact of your being at the Outwood station with a gentleman, on the evening of the twenty-sixth—he has said nothing—not one word against you. If he has knowledge of anything which should make you sob so, he keeps it to himself.”

Margaret’s face stayed hidden; the tears ran between her fingers. The mention of his “tender heart” cut her with a strange double edge—pain and gratitude together. Mrs. Thornton, seeing the tears, felt herself a little mollified in spite of herself.

“Come, Miss Hale. There may be circumstances, I’ll allow, that, if explained, may take off from the seeming impropriety.”

Margaret could not—must not—explain. If she spoke, she would betray what she had sworn to keep silent. Yet she longed, with an almost childish longing, to stand well with this stern woman, because behind her stood the man whose judgement had become so terribly important.

“I can give you no explanation,” she said at last, low. “I have done wrong, but not in the way you think or know about. I think Mr. Thornton judges me more mercifully than you;”—it took all her strength not to choke—“but, I believe, madam, you mean to do rightly.”

“Thank you,” said Mrs. Thornton, stiffening. “I was not aware that my meaning was doubted. It is the last time I shall interfere. I was unwilling to consent to do it, when your mother asked me. I had not approved of my son’s attachment to you, while I only suspected it. You did not appear to me worthy of him. But when you compromised yourself as you did at the time of the riot, and exposed yourself to the comments of servants and workpeople, I felt it was no longer right to set myself against my son’s wish of proposing to you—a wish, by the way, which he had always denied entertaining until the day of the riot.”

Margaret winced, drawing in her breath with a long, hissing sound; but Mrs. Thornton, intent on her own case, took no notice.

“He came; you had apparently changed your mind. I told my son yesterday, that I thought it possible, short as was the interval, you might have heard or learnt something of this other lover—”

That was too much. Margaret threw her head back with proud disdain, the line of her throat curving outward like a swan’s.

“What must you think of me, madam?” she asked, each word cold with scorn. “You can say nothing more, Mrs. Thornton. I decline every attempt to justify myself for anything. You must allow me to leave the room.”

And she swept out with the noiseless grace of an offended princess, leaving Mrs. Thornton standing amidst the quiet furniture and the faint lingering scent of ink and paper—witness to grief made visible in a half-written letter.

Mrs. Thornton had enough natural humour to feel, even as she gathered her shawl, the ludicrousness of being dismissed like a tiresome servant. She was not greatly angered. She cared too little for Margaret for that. Indeed, Margaret’s flash of passion had mollified her more than meekness would have done; it proved her words had struck home. As she went down the street she thought, with grim satisfaction, that the girl had pride enough to shrink from talk.

“My young lady,” she said to herself, “you’ve a pretty good temper of your own. If John and you had come together, he would have had to keep a tight hand over you, to make you know your place. But I don’t think you will go a-walking again with your beau, at such an hour of the day, in a hurry. You’ve too much pride and spirit in you for that. I like to see a girl fly out at the notion of being talked about. It shows they’re neither giddy, nor bold by nature. As for that girl, she might be bold, but she’d never be giddy. I’ll do her that justice. Now as to Fanny—she’d be giddy, and not bold. She’s no courage in her, poor thing!”

Mr. Thornton was spending his morning far less satisfactorily than his mother had spent hers. She had at least carried out her purpose; he was left with figures that would not be bent by will. He tried to understand precisely where he stood—what the strike had done him. Much of his capital was locked in new and expensive machinery; and he had bought cotton largely, counting on great orders in hand. The stoppage had thrown him behind; even with his own trained men he would have struggled to meet the engagements, and now the incompetence of the Irish hands—forced into work they had to be taught while speed was most needed—was a daily irritation, like grit in the teeth.

It was not a favourable hour for Nicholas Higgins to make a request. But he had promised Margaret to do it at any cost, and he was a man who, once pledged, held to it as if it were a knot in rope. So, though every moment of waiting added to his repugnance, his pride, and the sulky set of his mouth, he stood hour after hour leaning against the dead wall—first on one leg, then on the other—hearing the mill’s relentless music: the distant thud and whirr, the iron pulse of industry beating on the air until it seemed to press on a man’s skull. The town’s noise—wheels, voices, the harsh cries of boys—lay over him like a net.

At last the latch was sharply lifted; out came Mr. Thornton.

“I want for to speak to yo’, sir.”

“Can’t stay now, my man. I’m too late as it is.”

“Well, sir, I reckon I can wait till yo’ come back.”

Mr. Thornton was already half-way down the street. Higgins sighed; but there was no help for it. To catch “th’ measter” in the street was his only chance. If he rang the lodge bell or went to the house, he would be turned aside to the overlooker like any other hand.

So he stood again. At dinner-time the crowd poured out of the millyard; he gave a short nod here and there to the few who knew him, and scowled with all his might at the newly imported Irish “knobsticks.” The very sound of them—strange vowels, quick chatter—seemed to him part of the same force that had broken their strike: not men, but a noisy instrument brought in to grind them down.

At last Mr. Thornton returned.

“What! you there still!”

“Ay, sir. I mun speak to yo’.”

“Come in here, then. Stay—we’ll go across the yard; the men are not come back, and we shall have it to ourselves. These good people, I see, are at dinner,” he added, shutting the door of the porter’s lodge so that the street-noise dulled to a distant murmur.

He stopped to speak to the overlooker, who bent and said in a low tone, with the cautious relish of a man passing on dangerous knowledge:

“I suppose you know, sir, that that man is Higgins, one of the leaders of the Union; he that made that speech in Hurstfield.”

“No, I didn’t,” said Mr. Thornton, looking round sharply at his follower. Higgins was known to him by name—turbulent spirit, agitator, a face out of meetings.

“Come along,” he said, rougher than before; and as he led the way into the counting-house, his thoughts hardened. It was men such as this, he told himself, who interrupted commerce, injured the very town they lived in—demagogues, lovers of power at whatever cost to others.

“Well, sir! what do you want with me?” Mr. Thornton demanded, facing him as soon as they were inside.

“My name is Higgins—”

“I know that,” Mr. Thornton broke in. “What do you want, Mr. Higgins? That’s the question.”

“I want work.”

“Work! You’re a pretty chap to come asking me for work. You don’t want impudence, that’s very clear.”

“I’ve getten enemies and backbiters, like my betters; but I ne’er heerd o’ ony of them calling me o’er-modest,” Higgins answered. The words were steady, but the blood rose; not so much at the insult as at the tone that tried to stamp him down.

Mr. Thornton’s eye fell on a letter addressed to himself upon the table. He took it up, broke the seal, and read it through, his face unmoving. When he had finished, he looked up as though Higgins were an interruption he had indulged too long.

“What are you waiting for?”

“An answer to th’ question I axed.”

“I gave it you before. Don’t waste any more of your time.”

“Yo’ made a remark, sir, on my impudence,” Higgins said, the stubborn pride in him coming out like a square shoulder, “but I were taught that it was manners to say either ‘yes’ or ‘no,’ when I were axed a civil question. I should be thankfu’ to yo’ if yo’d give me work. Hamper will speak to my being a good hand.”

Mr. Thornton’s mouth tightened. “I’ve a notion you’d better not send me to Hamper to ask for a character, my man. I might hear more than you’d like.”

“I’d take th’ risk,” Higgins replied, meeting him without flinching. “Worst they could say of me is, that I did what I thought best, even to my own wrong.”
