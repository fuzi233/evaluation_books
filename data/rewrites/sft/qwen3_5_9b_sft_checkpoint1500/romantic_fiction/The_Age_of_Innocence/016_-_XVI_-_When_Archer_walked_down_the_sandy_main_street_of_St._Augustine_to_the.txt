When Archer walked down the dusty, sun-bleached avenue of St. Augustine toward the modest boarding-house designated as Mr. Welland's residence, his breath hitched. There, beneath the sprawling boughs of a magnolia tree, bathed in a halo of golden sunlight, stood May Welland. In that single, luminous instant, a crushing guilt washed over him: *Why* had he waited so long? Here, finally, was the truth—the raw, beating reality of a life that was rightfully his. Yet, the man who prided himself on scorning arbitrary rules had trembled at the thought of leaving his desk, terrified of the whispers behind closed doors, ashamed of the idea of stealing a mere holiday.

Before he could fully process the weight of his hesitation, her voice broke through the haze. "Newland—has anything happened?" The urgency in her tone stung him; surely, if she had looked into his eyes, she would have seen the answer instantly. It would have been far more natural, far more "feminine," for her intuition to bridge the gap immediately. But when he managed to choke out, "Yes—I found I had to see you," a soft blush rose on her cheeks. It melted the chill from her surprise, and a warm current flowed through Archer. He realized then how easily forgiveness could be bought, how swiftly even the mild, disapproving glances of a man like Mr. Letterblair could be dissolved by the tolerant, forgiving spirit of a family like the Wellands.

Though it was early, the main street offered no privacy save for the stiffest of formalities. Archer's restless nerves screamed for solitude, for the chance to pour out every ounce of his tenderness and his desperate impatience. Breakfast was still hours away, and instead of inviting her inside, she suggested they stroll to an ancient orange grove beyond town limits. She had just returned from a row on the river, and the sun, weaving nets of gold over the lazy water, seemed to have draped her in its warmth. Across the warm, brown canvas of her cheeks, her tousled hair glittered like spun silver wire. Her eyes, too, seemed lighter, almost translucent in their youth, holding a limpid depth that unsettled him. With her long, swinging stride, she moved beside him with the serene, statuesque grace of a young marble athlete carved by an indifferent sculptor.

To Archer, whose nerves were frayed tight, this vision was a balm, a soothing antidote to the blue skies and the sluggish river. They sat on a weathered bench beneath the orange trees, and he slid his arm around her, drawing her close enough to kiss her. It felt like drinking from a frozen spring under a blazing sun—a pure, vital refreshment. Yet, perhaps his embrace was more fervent than intended. Instantly, color flooded her face, and she pulled back as though he had startled a wild bird.

"What is it?" he asked, forcing a smile, trying to mask the panic in his chest. She stared at him, bewildered, replying simply, "Nothing."

A fragile silence settled between them, heavy with unspoken things. Her hand, previously resting near his, slipped away. It was the first time he had kissed her on the lips outside of their brief, secret embrace in the Beaufort conservatory. He saw the cool, boyish composure crack; she was disturbed, shaken, her defenses crumbling.

"Tell me what you do all day," he urged, crossing his arms beneath his tilted head and using his hat to shield his eyes from the blinding glare. Letting her speak of ordinary, mundane things was the only way to anchor his own racing thoughts, to ground himself in the present. He listened as she recounted a simple chronicle of swimming, sailing, and horseback riding, punctuated occasionally by dances at a rustic inn when a man-of-war docked. A scattering of pleasant visitors from Philadelphia and Baltimore picnicked nearby, including the Selfridge Merrys, who had arrived for three weeks due to Kate Merry's bout with bronchitis. They planned to install a lawn tennis court on the sands, yet aside from Kate and May, hardly anyone owned a racquet, and most guests hadn't even heard of the game.

These activities kept her perpetually occupied, leaving her little time to delve into the small vellum volume Archer had sent her a week prior—"Sonnets from the Portuguese." Instead, she was memorizing "How They Brought the Good News from Ghent to Aix," a poem he had once read to her. It delighted her to inform him that Kate Merry had never even heard of Robert Browning.

Soon, she jumped up, declaring they would be late for breakfast, and they hurried back to the ramshackle house with its sagging porch, unpruned hedges of plumbago, and beds of pink geraniums where the Wellands had made their winter home. Mr. Welland's acute domestic sensibilities recoiled from the squalor of the southern hotel, and despite immense expense and nearly insurmountable difficulties, Mrs. Welland was forced, year after year, to improvise a household staffed by disgruntled New Yorkers and local African laborers.

"The physicians insist that my husband feels he is in his own home," Mrs. Welland explained to sympathetic Philadelphians and Baltimoreans, season after season, "or else the climate will do him no good, and he will remain wretched." Indeed, Mr. Welland, beaming across a breakfast table miraculously stocked with exotic delicacies, often declared to Archer, "You see, my dear fellow, we camp—we literally camp. I tell my wife and May I wish to teach them how to rough it."

Both Mr. and Mrs. Welland were as surprised as May by the young man's abrupt return. Archer had preemptively claimed he was on the brink of a severe cold, a pretext that Mr. Welland accepted as wholly sufficient grounds for abandoning professional duties. "One cannot be too cautious, especially toward spring," the older gentleman chided, piling his plate with straw-colored griddle cakes drowned in syrup. "Had I been as prudent at your age, May would be dancing at the Assemblies now, rather than spending her winters in a wilderness with an invalid."

"Oh, but I love it here, Papa," May protested. "If only Newland could stay, I would prefer it a thousand times to New York."

"Newland must stay until he has completely recovered," Mrs. Welland said with indulgent patience. The young man laughed, feigning agreement, noting the necessity of his profession, though he had successfully delayed his return via a series of telegrams, ensuring his illness lasted a full week. This delay cast an ironic shadow over the situation; Mr. Letterblair's leniency stemmed partly from Archer's brilliant handling of the troublesome Olenski divorce case. Mr. Letterblair had informed Mrs. Welland that Archer had rendered an "invaluable service" to the entire family, a sentiment echoed by the formidable Old Mrs. Manson Mingott.

One afternoon, as May drove with her father in the only carriage available, Mrs. Welland seized a rare opportunity to broach a subject she usually avoided in front of her daughter. "I fear Ellen's ideas differ profoundly from ours," she began. "Medora Manson took her back to Europe when she was barely eighteen. You recall the scandal when she appeared in black at her debutante ball? It was one of Medora's fads—and remarkably prophetic, considering. It has been at least twelve years since Ellen last set foot in America. No wonder she appears so thoroughly Europeanized."

"But European society does not condone divorce," Archer interjected gently. "Countess Olenska believed she was adhering to American ideals by seeking her freedom." It was the first time he had spoken her name since leaving Skuytercliff, and a flush warmed his cheeks.

Mrs. Welland smiled with condescending pity. "That is precisely the extraordinary fabrication foreigners conjure about us. They assume we dine at two o'clock and sanction divorce! That is why I find it so foolish to host them in New York. They accept our kindness, return home, and regale their friends with these same tedious tales."

Archer held his tongue, allowing her to continue. "However, we deeply appreciate your success in persuading Ellen to abandon the notion. Neither her grandmother nor Uncle Lovell could sway her; both wrote that her change of heart was solely due to your influence—in fact, she told her grandmother as much. She holds an unbounded admiration for you. Poor Ellen; she was always a wayward child. I wonder what fate awaits her?"

"If you all truly wished it," Archer thought, the words burning on his tongue, "you would have preferred her to be Beaufort's mistress than a respectable wife." He wondered what reaction this thought would provoke. Could he envision the sudden collapse of Mrs. Welland's composed, placid features, the artificial authority built upon a lifetime of mastering trivialities? Faint traces of fresh beauty lingered on her face, mirroring May's. He asked himself if May's countenance was destined to harden into the same image of invincible, middle-aged innocence.

"No," he thought fiercely. "I do not want May to possess that kind of innocence—the innocence that locks the mind against imagination and the heart against experience!"
