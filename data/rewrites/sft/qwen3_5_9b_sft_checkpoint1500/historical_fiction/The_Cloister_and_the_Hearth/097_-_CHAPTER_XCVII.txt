In the quiet sanctum of the dying, a priest finds his soul stripped bare, reduced to its essential office. Gerard performed the final rites for the departed woman with a mechanical, detached precision, treating her as if she were the humblest of his flock, though the truth gnawed at him. Once the candles burned low and the prayers ceased, a stupor, heavy and sudden, descended upon him. He sat amidst the ashes of her life, numb to the terrible shock of loss, unable to comprehend the depth of his grief until a wave of religious fervor seized him. In a trance of gratitude, he knelt, thanking Heaven for her Christian end, as if her passing were a gift rather than a wound.

Then, a singular thought consumed him: to shield her from the gaze of strangers, to return her dust to the soil of his own churchyard. That same evening, a solitary cart, drawn by one weary horse, moved slowly toward Gouda. Inside rested a simple coffin, and beside it lay a man utterly broken, his chin propped upon the lid, his arms wrapped around it as if holding back the world.

The brief, frantic energy required for these preparations had drained Gerard completely. Now, he lay crushed, clutching the cold iron latch that secured the coffin, refusing to let go. The driver, a silent figure who had guided the beast, walked ahead in respectful silence, offering only a whisper when he halted the horse, mindful of the mute agony behind him. When they sought shelter for the night, the innkeeper and his fellow travelers, driven by kindness, urged the mourner to rest and eat. Gerard, however, repelled them with a ferocity that bordered on madness, snarling like a loyal hound guarding a treasure. He would not be parted from her, clinging to the cold metal through the long hours. Finally, the others, seeing the futility of their concern, draped a heavy cloak over him and left him alone in the dark.

By noon, the sorrow-laden cart crested the hill to the manse, where nearly thirty parishioners had gathered, murmuring over trivial matters until the absence of their pastor became apparent. Upon seeing the scene—their beloved healer so utterly stricken, leaning against the cart—a collective wail rose from the crowd. This cry shattered Gerard's lethargy. His eyes opened, focusing on the familiar faces surrounding him. "Oh, my children!" he cried, his voice cracking with raw anguish, "my children!" Yet the words died in his throat, choked by a pain too vast for speech.

Despite the gentle protests of those around him, Gerard insisted on burying her himself the following day. He read the burial service, his voice trembling only at the breaking points, tears streaming freely onto the fresh earth. When the final Amen faded, he remained standing before the open grave, a statue of stillness, while the villagers retreated, unwilling to disturb his solitude.

Time seemed to suspend around him. He stood there, lost in a waking dream, until the sexton, a man of simple wit, began to shovel the earth back into the hole without offering any warning. The sound of the spade striking the wood, followed by the first layer of dirt falling softly upon her, caused Gerard to utter a scream that pierced the air, sharp and human.

The sexton paused, his hands hovering.

Gerard swayed violently, pressing a hand to his heaving chest. The sexton stepped forward, catching the faltering priest. Neighbors hurried to assist, calling for aid as they lifted Gerard from the ground. Jorian Ketel, who had lingered nearby, grieving for the benefactress who had shown him such kindness, broke from the crowd and rushed into the yard. Together, the two men supported the weakened Gerard back to the safety of the manse.

"Ah, Jorian! Good Jorian!" Gerard gasped, collapsing into a chair, his fingers clawing at his ribs. "Something has snapped within me; I felt it, I heard it snap... Here, Jorian, right here," he whispered, pointing frantically to his heart, where the silence of the grave had finally echoed too loudly.
