It invariably happened in the same way.

On the night of her annual ball, Mrs. Julius Beaufort never failed to appear at the Opera. In fact, she deliberately scheduled her grandest evening event on an Opera night, a strategic move designed to signal her total detachment from domestic drudgery and her command of a staff capable of orchestrating such a spectacle in her absence.

The Beaufort residence was one of the few houses in New York boasting a true ballroom—a luxury that predated even the mansions of the Mingotts and the Headly Chiverses. While the city was beginning to view the practice of covering a drawing-room floor with a "crash" and moving furniture upstairs as somewhat provincial, the Beauforts maintained a dedicated space for dancing. For three hundred and sixty-four days of the year, this room remained shut away in darkness, its gilt chairs stacked in corners and its chandelier covered in a protective bag. This undeniable architectural superiority was deemed sufficient compensation for any regrets regarding the Beaufort past.

Mrs. Archer, who loved to distill her social observations into axioms, once remarked, "We all have our pet common people." Though the phrase was bold, its truth was quietly acknowledged in many exclusive hearts. Yet, the Beauforts were not merely common; some whispered they were worse. Mrs. Beaufort hailed from one of America's most revered families; she was the lovely Regina Dallas of the South Carolina branch, a penniless beauty introduced to New York society by her cousin, the imprudent Medora Manson, who was always doing the wrong thing for the right reasons. Being related to the Mansons and the Rushworths granted one a *droit de cité* in New York society, as Mr. Sillerton Jackson, a frequent visitor to the Tuileries, termed it. However, did one not forfeit that privilege by marrying Julius Beaufort?

The question remained: who was Beaufort? He passed for an Englishman; he was agreeable, handsome, ill-tempered, hospitable, and witty. He arrived in America with letters of recommendation from Old Mrs. Manson Mingott's English son-in-law, a banker, and quickly secured an influential position in the world of affairs. Yet, his habits were dissipated, his tongue bitter, and his origins mysterious. When Medora Manson announced her cousin's engagement to him, it was viewed as another act of folly in her long history of imprudence.

But folly is often justified as surely as wisdom. Two years after young Mrs. Beaufort's marriage, it was admitted that she presided over the most distinguished house in New York. No one knew exactly how this miracle was achieved. She was indolent, passive, and even the caustic called her dull. Yet, dressed like an idol and draped in pearls, she grew younger, blonder, and more beautiful with each passing year. She reigned from her heavy brownstone palace, drawing the entire world within its walls without lifting a jeweled finger. The knowing ones whispered that it was Beaufort himself who trained the servants, instructed the chef on new dishes, directed the gardeners on which hot-house flowers to cultivate for the dinner table, selected the guests, brewed the after-dinner punch, and dictated the intimate notes his wife sent to her friends. If he did these things, they were performed in secret, presenting to the public the image of a careless, hospitable millionaire strolling into his own drawing-room with the detached air of an invited guest, remarking, "My wife's gloxinias are a marvel, aren't they? I believe she gets them out from Kew."

New York was agreed that Mr. Beaufort's secret lay in his ability to carry things off. It was perfectly acceptable to whisper that he had been "helped" to leave England by the international banking house where he had worked; he dismissed that rumor as easily as the rest. Though New York's business conscience was as sensitive as its moral standards, he swept everything before him, dragging the entire city into his drawing rooms. For over twenty years, people had said they were "going to the Beauforts'" with the same sense of security as if they were visiting Mrs. Manson Mingott's, with the added satisfaction of knowing they would receive hot canvas-back ducks and vintage wines rather than tepid Veuve Clicquot without a year and warmed-up croquettes from Philadelphia.

Thus, as usual, Mrs. Beaufort appeared in her box just before the Jewel Song. And when, as was customary, she rose at the end of the third act, wrapped her opera cloak around her lovely shoulders, and vanished, New York knew that the ball would begin within the hour.

The Beaufort house was one that New Yorkers were proud to display to foreigners, particularly on the night of the annual ball. The Beauforts had been among the first in New York to own their own red velvet carpet, having it rolled down the steps by their own footmen under their own awning, rather than renting it along with the supper and ballroom chairs. They also pioneered the custom of allowing ladies to remove their cloaks in the hall, instead of shuffling up to the hostess's bedroom to recurl their hair with the aid of a gas burner; Beaufort was understood to have remarked that he supposed all his wife's friends had maids who ensured they were properly *coiffées* before leaving home.

Furthermore, the house was boldly planned with a ballroom, so that instead of squeezing through a narrow passage to reach it—as at the Chiverses—one marched solemnly down a vista of enfiladed drawing rooms (the sea-green, the crimson, and the *bouton d'or*), seeing from afar the many-candled lustres reflected in the polished parquetry, and beyond that, the depths of a conservatory where camellias and tree-ferns arched their costly foliage over seats of black and gold bamboo.

Newland Archer, fitting for a young man of his station, strolled in somewhat late. He had left his overcoat with the silk-stockinged footmen—the stockings being one of Beaufort's few eccentricities—and dawdled for a while in the library, hung with Spanish leather and furnished with Buhl and malachite, where a few men were chatting and putting on their dancing gloves. Finally, he joined the line of guests whom Mrs. Beaufort was receiving on the threshold of the crimson drawing room.

Archer was distinctly nervous. He had not returned to his club after the Opera, as the young bloods usually did, but, the night being fine, had walked a considerable distance up Fifth Avenue before turning back toward the Beauforts' house. He was deeply afraid that the Mingotts might be going too far; that, in fact, Granny Mingott's orders might have summoned the Countess Olenska to the ball.

From the tone of the conversation in the club box, he had perceived how grave a mistake that would be. Although he was more than ever determined to "see the thing through," he felt less chivalrously eager to champion his betrothed's cousin than during their brief talk at the Opera.

Wandering into the *bouton d'or* drawing room—where Beaufort had had the audacity to hang "Love Victorious," the much-discussed nude by Bouguereau—Archer found Mrs. Welland and her daughter standing near the ballroom door. Couples were already gliding over the floor beyond: the light of the wax candles fell upon revolving tulle skirts, upon girlish heads wreathed with modest blossoms, upon the dashing aigrettes and ornaments of the young married women's coiffures, and upon the glitter of highly glazed shirt-fronts and fresh *glacé* gloves.

Miss Welland, evidently about to join the dancers, hung on the threshold, holding her lilies-of-the-valley (she carried no other bouquet), her face a little pale, her eyes burning with candid excitement. A group of young men and girls were gathered about her, engaging in much hand-clasping, laughter, and pleasantry, while Mrs. Welland, standing slightly apart, shed the beam of a qualified approval. It was evident that Miss Welland was in the act of announcing her engagement, while her mother affected the air of parental reluctance deemed suitable for the occasion.

Archer paused for a moment. It was at his express wish that the announcement had been made, yet it was not how he would have wished his happiness to be known. To proclaim it in the heat and noise of a crowded ballroom was to rob it of the fine bloom of privacy which should belong to things nearest the heart. His joy was so deep that this blurring of the surface left its essence untouched; but he would have liked to keep the surface pure as well. It was a certain satisfaction to find that May Welland shared this feeling. Her eyes fled to his beseechingly, and their look said: "Remember, we're doing this because it's right."

No appeal could have found a more immediate response in Archer's breast; yet he wished that the necessity of their action had been represented by some ideal reason, and not simply by poor Ellen Olenska. The group surrounding Miss Welland made way for him with significant smiles, and after taking his share of the felicitations, he drew his betrothed into the middle of the ballroom floor and put his arm about her waist.

"Now we shan't have to talk," he said, smiling into her candid eyes as they floated away on the soft waves of the Blue Danube.

She made no answer. Her lips trembled into a smile, but the eyes remained distant and serious, as if bent on some ineffable vision. "Dear," Archer whispered, pressing her closer; it dawned on him that the first hours of being engaged, even if spent in a ballroom, held something grave and sacramental. What a new life it was going to be, with this whiteness, radiance, and goodness at one's side!

Once the dance was over, the two, as became an affianced couple, wandered into the conservatory. Sitting behind a tall screen of tree-ferns and camellias, Newland pressed her gloved hand to his lips.

"You see I did as you asked me to," she said.

"Yes; I couldn't wait," he answered, smiling. After a moment, he added, "Only I wish it hadn't had to be at a ball."

"Yes, I know." She met his glance comprehendingly. "But after all—even here we're alone together, aren't we?"

"Oh, dearest—always!" Archer cried.

Evidently, she was always going to understand; she was always going to say the right thing. The discovery made the cup of his bliss overflow, and he went on gaily, "The worst of it is that I want to kiss you and I can't." As he spoke, he took a swift glance about the conservatory, assured himself of their momentary privacy, and catching her to him, laid a fugitive pressure on her lips. To counteract the audacity of this proceeding, he led her to a bamboo sofa in a less secluded part of the conservatory. Sitting down beside her, he broke a lily-of-the-valley from her bouquet. She sat silent, and the world lay like a sunlit valley at their feet.

"Did you tell my cousin Ellen?" she asked presently, as if she spoke through a dream.

He roused himself and remembered that he had not done so. Some invincible repugnance to speak of such things to the strange foreign woman had checked the words on his lips.

"No—I hadn't the chance after all," he said, fibbing hastily.

"Ah." She looked disappointed but gently resolved on gaining her point. "You must, then, for I didn't either; and I shouldn't like her to think—"

"Of course not. But aren't you, after all, the person to do it?"

She pondered on this. "If I'd done it at the right time, yes; but now that there's been a delay, I think you must explain that I'd asked you to tell her at the Opera, before our speaking about it to everybody here. Otherwise she might think I had forgotten her. You see, she's one of the family, and she's been away so long that she's rather—sensitive."

Archer looked at her glowingly. "Dear and great angel! Of course I'll tell her." He glanced a trifle apprehensively toward the crowded ballroom. "But I haven't seen her yet. Has she come?"

"No; at the last minute she decided not to."

"At the last minute?" he echoed, betraying his surprise that she should ever have considered the alternative possible.

"Yes. She's awfully fond of dancing," the young girl answered simply. "But suddenly she made up her mind that her dress wasn't smart enough for a ball, though we thought it so lovely; and so my aunt had to take her home."

"Oh, well—" said Archer with happy indifference. Nothing about his betrothed pleased him more than her resolute determination to carry to its utmost limit that ritual of ignoring the "unpleasant" in which they had both been brought up.

"She knows as well as I do," he reflected, "the real reason of her cousin's staying away; but I shall never let her see by the least sign that I am conscious of there being a shadow of a shade on poor Ellen Olenska's reputation."
