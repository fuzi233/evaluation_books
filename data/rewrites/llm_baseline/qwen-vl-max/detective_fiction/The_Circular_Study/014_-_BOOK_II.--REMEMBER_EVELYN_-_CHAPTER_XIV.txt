“It is like and unlike what I’ve already told you,” began young Adams, his voice low and strained. “In my earlier confession, I wove truth with falsehood. To make things clear—and to help you understand why my wife did what she did—I must begin again, as though I’d never spoken a word before.”

“Wait!” Mr. Gryce interrupted sharply, raising a hand. “We’ll hear you in a moment.” Then, leaning close to the inspector, he murmured a few words, after which he pulled out a pencil and scribbled several lines on a slip of paper. He handed it over to the inspector, who read it carefully, glancing back at it repeatedly as Adams—now properly known as Cadwalader—continued.

These notes, clearly meant as a guide for the unfolding tale, were written in a steady hand, each point precise and urgent. They read:

1. Why should a woman—calm enough to pause and fix her hair during the early moments of an interview—become so consumed by fury that she kills a man she had no known grudge against? (Recall the comb found on the floor of Mr. Adams’s bedroom.)

2. What meaning lies behind these words, written just before the confrontation: *“I return you your daughter. Neither you nor she shall ever see me again. Remember Evelyn!”*?

3. Why use the pronoun *“I”*? What was Mr. Felix Adams’s relationship to this girl—Evelyn—that allowed him to speak in such terms after her marriage to his own brother?

4. If he used those words, why, when attacked, did he try to swallow the paper? And why did he die with it clenched between his teeth?

5. If he died from rage—“as monsters do,” as she herself said—why did his face bear not hatred, but sorrow? Why did his expression show resolve, rather than the wild panic one might expect after being struck down by a sudden, unexpected attacker?

6. If he had strength enough to grab the paper and bring it to his mouth, why didn’t he use that same strength to turn on a light—one that could have summoned help instead of leaving the room bathed in crimson glow? According to our understanding of the signal system, red means: *“Nothing more required. Keep away.”*

7. What was the purpose of the massive steel plate hidden within the door frame? And why did it remain motionless—silent and still—during the most critical moment of his life?

8. How did old Poindexter arrive so quickly after the murder? His muttered words—*“It is Amos’s son, not Amos!”*—suggest confusion. Did he not know who he would find here? Was the sight of the dead man—bearing a cross upon his chest and pierced by a dagger—less shocking than the identity of the victim himself?

9. Recall Bartow’s silent performance. The fatal wound was delivered by a left-handed thrust. Watch closely for any sign that the woman is left-handed. Also, consider why she held her other arm rigidly stretched behind her for so long.

10. Why does the bird—whose cry is *“Remember Evelyn!”*—sometimes shift to *“Poor Eva! Lovely Eva! Who would strike Eva?”* This variation suggests a deeper connection between Mr. Adams and his brother’s bride—one that spans years and secrets.

11. If Bartow is truly innocent—merely a witness—then why does he seem so joyful at the outcome? This may not be part of Thomas Adams’s confession, but it cannot be ignored. The deaf-and-dumb servant was driven mad by something that brought him pleasure. What could that be?

12. Study this chart, compiled through repeated tests with Bartow and the mysterious lamp in Mr. Adams’s study:
- White light: Water wanted.
- Green light: Overcoat and hat to be brought.
- Blue light: Return books to shelves.
- Violet light: Prepare the study for night.
- Yellow light: Await the next signal.
- Red light: Nothing needed; stay away.

The red light was lit during the final scene. Can this be explained by the account Mr. Adams is about to give?

With these questions in mind—each a thread in the tapestry of this crime—let us now listen to the full story. Not just of the murder, but of the events that led to it—events buried deep beneath silence, memory, and a name whispered by a bird: *Evelyn*.
