Hopeless love had thoroughly agitated Ginger. Previously, his soul had been placid. Even the financial crash that had reshaped his existence had barely bruised him; his temperament allowed him to endure life's slings and arrows with a philosophical "Right ho!" But now, everything had changed. "By gad!" Ginger thought, stopping abruptly opposite Devonshire House. "If he uses that damned shrubbery as a soup strainer tonight, I'll slosh him with a fork!"

Actors and actresses seem to share a peculiar childhood trait: just as children crumble instantly under the weight of bad news, they also bounce back with surprising speed when fortune smiles upon them. Sally found it baffling that any human being could generate such widespread euphoria simply by handing her brother Fillmore a check for twenty thousand dollars. If the Second Coming had actually occurred, the troupe of the *Primrose Way* Company couldn't have looked more united or content. The heavy fog of lethargy and discouragement, born from a week of doing absolutely nothing, evaporated off them like a wet coat left in the sun.

Suddenly promoting that lowly creature from the depths—the assistant stage manager—to the dizzying heights of "Proprietor" appealed directly to their theatrical instincts. Most of them had starred in plays where the downtrodden heroine is elevated around eleven o'clock, so this twist felt perfectly dramatic and logically sound. Furthermore, now that Miss Hobson was gone, everyone finally admitted she had been a walking blight on the production. Optimism became the air they breathed; cheerful rumors began to circulate like wildfire. Bowler-hatted Teddy claimed, straight from the lift-boy at his hotel, that the theater ban would be lifted by Tuesday at the latest. Meanwhile, the girl running the cigar stand at the Pontchartrain told the actor playing the butler that Toledo and Cleveland were set to open tomorrow. Everyone agreed the sun was breaking through the clouds and that Fate would soon give up its hopeless crusade against keeping good people down.

Fillmore was restored to his former self. Naturally, everyone has their own unique way of expressing elation. For Fillmore, it meant purchasing a brand-new waistcoat, stocking up on a hundred half-dollar cigars, and becoming obsessively particular about his lunch choices. It might have been an optical illusion, but to Sally, he seemed to have gained at least six pounds in the first twenty-four hours of this new regime. Previously, as a servant managing paper knives and props, he had been almost gaunt. Now, as a manager, he bloomed into soft, billowy curves. When he stood on the sidewalk before the theater, gazing proudly at the new posters bearing the legend *"FILLMORE NICHOLAS PRESENTS,"* the general public literally had to walk around him to avoid collision.

In this atmosphere of bubbling joy, it was difficult to understand why Sally, the fairy godmother responsible for the entire turnaround, wasn't equally ecstatic. It puzzled her deeply. Gerald was cheerful and talkative. At least, he saw no faults in life. He spoke expansively about his grand plans. "If this play gets over—and it will—I'll show 'em!" His jaw was squared, his eyes glowing as they fixed on the inviting horizon. "Just one success—that's all I need—then watch me! I haven't had my chance yet, but..." His voice rolled on confidently, but Sally had stopped listening.

It was the season when the chill of evening rushes quickly to follow the mellow warmth of the afternoon. The sun had dipped behind the trees, and a cold wind was rising from the river. She had finally realized the fundamental divide between the male and female outlooks on life. Success! How men worship it, and how little of themselves they possess to offer for anything else. Ironically, it was the very theme of Gerald's play—which she had single-handedly saved from destruction—that served as the subject matter here. I even starred her in a production once. Remember those pearls she wore? I bought them. And now she won't speak to me. Simply because I criticized her hat. I wish you could have seen that hat. You would agree with me, surely, because you are sensible and you understand hats. I don't know what to do. I come here every single night." Sally nodded; she had noticed him often, though this was the first time Lee Schoenstein, the gentlemanly master of ceremonies, had formally introduced him to her. "I come here every night and dance past her table, but she refuses to look at me. What," he asked, tears welling in his pale eyes, "would you do?"

"I don't know," Sally replied honestly.

"Nor do I. I assumed you wouldn't, given that you are a sensible, broad-minded..." He paused, correcting himself. "Nor do I. I am making one last attempt tonight, provided you can keep a secret. Will you promise not to tell anyone?" he pleaded urgently. "But I know you won't, because you are sensible... I am bringing her a small gift. It arrives tonight. A little present. Surely that will soften her heart, don't you think?"

"A bigger one would likely work better."

Mr. Cracknell kicked her shin in a gesture of dismayed surprise.

"I never considered that. Perhaps you are right. But it is too late now. Still, it might help. Or perhaps not? What do you think?"

"Yes," Sally said.

"I thought as much," Mr. Cracknell sighed.

The orchestra crashed to a halt with a thunderous thud, leaving Mr. Cracknell clapping weakly in the center of the floor. Sally slipped back to her table. Her former partner, after casting an uncertain glance around as if searching for a lost item he couldn't quite identify, zig-zagged off toward his own seat. The roar of conversation, previously drowned out by the music, erupted with renewed vigor. The air inside was hot and thick with voices. Pressing her hands over her closed eyes, Sally was reminded once again that she suffered from a pounding headache.

It had been nearly a month since Sally returned to Mr. Abrahams' employment. It had been a dull, leaden month—a monotonous sequence of lifeless days where existence felt like a bad dream. In some strange, nightmare-like fashion, she felt severed from her kind. Weeks had passed without seeing a familiar face. None of her old boarding-house companions had crossed her path. Fillmore, presumably driven by a guilty conscience, had avoided her, while Ginger was busy forging his destiny on the south shore of Long Island.

Lowering her hands, Sally opened her eyes and surveyed the room. It was packed, as usual. The Flower Garden was one of many venues that had surged in popularity riding the crest of New York's dancing craze. Doubtless because, as its proprietor claimed, it was a pleasant place managed with care, it had maintained steady prosperity unlike many of its rivals. Its advertisements proudly declared it a "supper-club for after-theatre dining and dancing," boasting of being "large and spacious, sumptuously appointed," and "one of the town's wonder-places" featuring an "incomparable dance-floor, enchanting music, cuisine, and service de luxe." From which one could safely infer that Isadore Abrahams held the place in high regard.

There had been a time when Sally enjoyed it too. During her initial period of employment, she found it diverting, stimulating, and full of entertainment. But those days were gone. Back then, she had never suffered from headaches or, worse, this dreadful listless depression that weighed her down, turning her nightly work into a burden.

"Miss Nicholas."

The orchestra, rarely silent for long at the Flower Garden, struck up again. Lee Schoenstein, the master of ceremonies, was introducing a new partner. Sally rose mechanically.

"This is the first time I've been in this place," the man said as they navigated the crowded floor. He was big and clumsy, naturally. Tonight, however, it seemed to Sally that the entire world was big and clumsy. "It's a swell place. I come from up-state. We have nothing like this where I'm from." He cleared a patch of floor, using Sally as a battering ram, and Sally, despite her recent unpleasant experience with Mr. Cracknell, began to look back at it with a touch of wistfulness. This man was undoubtedly the worst dancer in America.

"Give me li'l old New York," the man from up-state declared unpatriotically. "It's good enough for me. I've been to some swell shows since arriving. Seen this year's 'Follies'?"

"No."

"You go," he urged earnestly. "You go! Take my word for it, it's a swell show. Seen 'Myrtle Takes a Turkish Bath'?"

"I don't go to many theatres."

"You go! It's a scream. I've been to a show every night since getting here. Regularly. Swell shows all of them, except this last one. I certainly picked a lemon tonight, alright. I was taking a chance, y'know, because it was an opening. Thought it would be something to brag about when I got home—that I'd been to a New York opening. Cost me two-seventy-five, including tax, and I wish I'd gotten it in my kick right now. 'The Wild Rose,' they called it," he said satirically, as if exposing a low trick by the management. "'The Wild Rose!' It sure made me wild, alright. Two dollars seventy-five tossed away, just like that."

Something stirred in Sally's memory. Why did that title feel so familiar? Then, with a shock, she remembered. It was Gerald's new play. For some time after returning to New York, she had been haunted by the fear that stepping out of her apartment might mean encountering him stepping out of his. Then she had read a newspaper paragraph that eased her anxiety. Gerald was touring with a new play, and she was almost certain "The Wild Rose" was its name.

"Is that Gerald Foster's play?" she asked quickly.

"I don't know who wrote it," her partner replied, "but let me tell you, he's one lucky guy to get away alive. There are fellows breaking stones on the Ossining Road who've done far less to deserve a sentence. Wild Rose! I'll tell the world it made me go good and wild," said the man from up-state, an economical soul who despised waste and was used to stretching his humor to give it every chance. "Why, before the second act ended, the people were beating it for the exits, and if someone hadn't shouted 'Women and children first,' there would have been a panic."

Sally found herself back at her table without fully realizing how she had arrived there.

"Miss Nicholas."

She started to rise, only to realize this wasn't the voice of duty calling her through Mr. Schoenstein's gold teeth. The man who had spoken her name had seated himself beside her, speaking in precise, clipped accents that were oddly familiar. The haze cleared from her eyes, and she recognized Bruce Carmyle.

"I called at your place," Mr. Carmyle was saying, "and the hall porter told me you were here, so I ventured to follow. I hope you don't mind? May I smoke?"

He lit a cigarette with considerable flair. His fingers trembled slightly as he raised the match,

The crimson tide receded slowly from Sally's cheeks, and her heart, which had hammered like a trapped bird at the mere echo of his voice, settled back into a steady rhythm. Once the initial shock wore off, a surprising calm washed over her. Every time she had rehearsed this inevitable confrontation in her mind, she had felt a rising panic, a dread that tightened her chest. Yet, now that reality had crashed into her, it felt strangely manageable. The chaotic energy of the night had drained her of capacity for wild emotion.

"Hullo, Sally!" Gerald called out.

His voice was thick, slurring slightly, and a foolish, vacant smile played on his lips as he swayed against the doorframe, supporting himself with one hand. He was shirt-sleeved and collarless, the marks of a heavy night of drinking etched clearly on his appearance. His face was pale and puffy, surrounded by a halo of sodden disreputability.

Sally said nothing. Burdened previously by a numbing exhaustion, she now seemed to have entered a second phase—a sort of Indian summer of hyper-alertness born of over-tired nerves. She regarded him with quiet, cool, and entirely dispassionate eyes, as if observing a total stranger.

"Hullo!" Gerald tried again.

"What do you want?" Sally asked.

"Heard your voice. Saw the door open. Thought I'd come in."

"What do you want?"

The weak, painted-on smile vanished from Gerald's face. A single tear tracked down his cheek; his intoxication had tipped into the maudlin stage. "Sally... S-Sally... I'm very miserable." He stumbled over the difficult syllables. "Heard your voice. Saw the door open. Thought I'd come in."

Something flickered at the back of Sally's mind. *Have I been through this before?* Then memory clicked into place. This was simply Mr. Reginald Cracknell all over again.

"I think you had better go to bed, Gerald," she said steadily.

Nothing about him registered anymore—not the sight of his degradation, nor the shameless misery dripping from his pores.

"What's the use? Can't sleep. No good. Couldn't sleep. Sally, you don't know how worried I am. I see what a fool I've been."

Sally made a quick gesture, checking what she feared was about to erupt into a belated apology for his treatment of her. She didn't want to stand there listening to Gerald weep for having ruined her life. But it wasn't that weighing on his soul.

"I was a fool ever to try writing plays," he continued, his voice trembling. "Got a winner first time, but can't repeat. It's no good. Ought to have stuck to newspaper work. I'm good at that. Shall have to go back to it. Had another frost to-night. No good trying any more. Shall have to go back to the old grind, damn it."

He wept softly, full of pity for his tragic case. "Very miserable," he murmured.

He stepped forward, lurched, and retreated to the safety of the doorframe. For an instant, Sally's artificial composure was pierced by a swift stab of contempt. It passed quickly, and she was armored in indifference once more.

"Go to bed, Gerald," she said. "You'll feel better in the morning."

Perhaps a glimmer of foresight penetrated Gerald's muddled intelligence. He winced, his manner deepening into melancholy. "May not be alive in the morning," he said solemnly. "End it all!" he repeated, making a sweeping gesture that he abruptly cut off as he clutched the friendly door.

Sally was not in the mood for melodrama. "Oh, go to bed," she said impatiently.

The strange, frozen indifference that had held her began to thaw, replaced by a growing resentment—against Gerald for degrading himself, and against herself for ever finding glamour in such a man. It humiliated her to recall how utterly she had once allowed his personality to master hers. Under the sting of this humiliation, she felt hard and pitiless. Dimly, she sensed a curious change within her. Normally, the sight of any living creature in distress would stir her sympathy instantly; but Gerald mourning the prospect of returning to regular work made no appeal to her. The sufferer noted this and commented on it.

"You're very unsympathetic," he complained.

"I'm sorry," Sally replied.

She walked briskly to the door and pushed it open. Gerald, still clinging to his support, moved out into the passage, attached to the handle like a man whose world had suddenly lost its foundation. He released the handle and shuffled uncertainly toward his own door. Finding it ajar, he staggered across the threshold. Sally watched him reach his destination safely before turning into her bedroom, intending to terminate the disturbing night with sleep.

Almost immediately, she changed her mind. Sleep was impossible. A feverish restlessness seized her. She threw on a kimono and went to the kitchen to check if her supplies permitted a glass of hot milk. She had just remembered presenting the last of the milk to a sandy cat with a purposeful eye that had dropped in through the window earlier that morning, when a muffled crash interrupted her regrets.

She listened intently. The sound seemed to come from across the passage. Hurrying to the door, she opened it. Instantly, from behind the door of the apartment opposite, erupted a perfect fusillade of crashes, each one seeming to her strained ears louder and more appalling than the last.

There is something about sudden, loud noises in the stillness of the night that shatters the most rigid detachment. Only moments ago, Gerald's threat of violence had left her unmoved; now, her mind leapt back to his words, and apprehension succeeded indifference. Undoubtedly, Gerald was in an irresponsible mood, capable of almost anything under the influence. Listening in the doorway, Sally felt a momentary panic.

A brief silence followed the explosion, but as she stood hesitating, the noise broke out again. This time, so loud and compelling was it that Sally hesitated no longer. She ran across the passage and pounded on the door.

Whatever devastation had occurred inside, Gerald had survived it. Moments later, a dragging footstep sounded, and the door swung open. Gerald stood on the threshold, the weak smile back on his face.

"Hullo, Sally!"

At the sight of him—disreputable and seemingly unscathed—Sally's brief alarm died, replaced by the old feeling of impatient resentment. In addition to her other grievances, he had apparently frightened her unnecessarily.

"Whatever was all that noise?" she demanded.

"Noise?" Gerald considered the point, mouth agape.

"Yes, noise," Sally snapped.

"I've been cleaning house," Gerald said with the owl-like gravity of a man barely conscious of his lack of faculties.

Sally pushed past him. The apartment was almost an exact replica of her own, evidently decorated by Elsa Doland with painstaking, niggly feminine care. Amateur interior decoration had always been Elsa's hobby. Even in the unpromising surroundings of her bedroom at Mrs. Meecher's boarding-house, she had contrived a certain daintiness that Sally, lacking such talent, had always envied. Elsa's mind ran toward small, fragile ornaments; she was not afraid of over-furnishing. Pictures jostled one another on the walls; china of all descriptions cluttered little tables; there was a profusion of lamps with parti-colored glass shades; and plates lined a series of shelves.

One might say the plates were ranged and the pictures jostled, but it would be more accurate to say they had jostled and been ranged, for Sally could only reconstruct the scene by guesswork. She had walked in briskly but pulled up short, appalled by the majestic ruin that met her gaze. A shellburst in a sitting-room could hardly have created more havoc.

The psychology of a man of weak character under the influence of alcohol and disappointed ambition is difficult to fathom; his moods shift with bewildering rapidity. Ten minutes prior, Gerald had been in the grip of clammy self-pity. Now, however, there had manifestly occurred a brief but adequate spasm of berserk fury. What caused it, or why it expended itself so abruptly, Sally was not psychologist enough to explain; the ocular evidence was convincing enough.

A heavy niblick, flung petulantly—or remorsefully—into a corner, indicated the weapon of destruction. Bleak chaos reigned everywhere. The floor was littered with every imaginable shape and size of broken glass and china. Fragments of pictures, looking as if chewed by a prehistoric beast, lay amid heaps of shattered statuettes and vases. As Sally moved slowly into the room, china crackled beneath her feet. Surveying the stripped walls, she turned to Gerald for an explanation.

Gerald had subsided onto an occasional table, weeping softly again. It had struck him once more that he had been terribly, terribly mistreated.

"Well!" Sally gasped. "You've certainly made a good job of it!"

There was a sharp crack as the occasional table, never designed to bear heavy weights, collapsed in a splintering flurry of broken legs under the pressure of the homeowner. Sally's mood underwent an abrupt change.

Darkness was gathering with a distinct sense of apology, as though it felt bad about interrupting what had been a flawless summer day. To the west, past the tree line, a faint afterglow still clung to the horizon, while a new moon hung overhead like a polished silver sickle above the great barn.

Sally stepped out onto the porch and performed a solemn triple bow for luck. Standing on the gravel, she breathed in the sweet scents of the evening and decided that life was simply wonderful. By now, however, the darkness had shaken off its initial hesitation and set about its work with vigorous efficiency. The sky settled into a uniform, deep blue, speckled with quiet stars, until the cement road leading toward Patchogue and Babylon dissolved completely into invisibility.

Lights flickered on in the houses across the meadow. From the direction of the kennels came a single, sleepy bark. The small, white, woolly dog—Toto—who had been trotting at Sally's heels, halted abruptly and emitted a sharp, challenging squeak. The evening was so still that Ginger's footsteps, pounding home from the village where he had procured provisions, evening papers, and yarn for Sally's sweater, were audible long before he turned into the gate.

Sally couldn't see him yet, but she looked toward the sound and felt that familiar, cozy thrill of happiness that had visited her every evening for the past year.

"Ginger!" she called out.

"What ho!"

The woolly dog, issuing another important squeak, scurried down the drive to investigate, only to be greeted with icy indifference. Ginger, despite his professed love for dogs, had never managed to warm to Toto. When Sally, a month prior, had found Mrs. Meecher distraught because her pet had fallen into a terrible lethargy, she had begged Ginger to take the invalid away for some country air.

"It's wonderful what you've done for Toto, angel," Sally remarked as Ginger approached, evading the animal's enthusiastic leaps with frosty precision. "He's a different dog."

"Bit of luck for him," Ginger replied. "All through my time at Mrs. Meecher's, I never saw him move faster than a stately walk. Now he's running around all the time."

"The blighter had been overeating since birth," Ginger explained. "That was the whole problem. A little judicious dieting fixed him up."

"We'll be able to," Ginger continued, brightening, "ship him back next week."

"I shall quite miss him."

"My cave-man!" Sally murmured. "I always said you had a frightfully brutal streak in you. Ginger, what an evening!"

"Good Lord!" Ginger exclaimed suddenly as they stepped into the light of the open kitchen door. "Now what?"

He stopped and stared at her intently. "Do you know you're looking prettier than you were when I headed down to the village!"

Sally gave his arm a gentle squeeze. "Beloved!" she cooed. "Did you get the chops?"

Ginger froze in his tracks, horror washing over his face. "Oh, my aunt! I completely forgot them!"

"Oh, Ginger, you old chump," Sally laughed. "Well, you'll have to undergo a little judicious dieting yourself, just like Toto."

"I say, I'm dreadfully sorry. But I did get the wool."

"If you think I'm going to eat wool..."

"Isn't there anything else in the house?"

"Vegetables and fruit."

"Fine! Though, of course, if you're desperate for chops..."

"Not at all. I'm spiritual. Besides, people say vegetables are excellent for blood pressure or something."

"Didn't you get the mail either?"

"Absolutely not! I was on top of it like a knife. Two letters from fellows wanting Airedale puppies."

"No! Ginger, we're getting on!"

"Pretty bloated," Ginger agreed complacently. "Pretty bloated. If things keep buzzing along like this, we might even manage to get that two-seater. There was a letter for you. Here it is."

"It's from Fillmore," Sally said, examining the envelope as they moved inside the kitchen. "About time, too. Haven't heard a peep from him for months."

She sat down and opened the letter. Ginger hauled himself onto the table, wriggled into a comfortable position, and began reading his evening paper. After skimming the sports section, he lowered the paper and watched Sally's bent head with a profound sense of contentment. Despite being married for nearly a year, Ginger still wandered through a magical world in a state of dazed disbelief, unable to fully grasp that such bliss existed. In all his travels, he had seen many things that looked promising from afar, but none that held up under close inspection—except for this business of marriage. With Sally as his partner, marriage seemed to be one of the rare endeavors in life without any hidden catches. His honest eyes gleamed as he observed her.

Ginger squinted at the cryptic message with a puzzled frown. "What is it?" he asked.

"Why 'Popp'?" Ginger interrupted, voicing a question that baffled him deeply.

"Just a trade name, silly," Sally dismissed. "Gladys is a wonderful cook; she makes the pies while Fillmore goes around selling them. They did so well that they've launched a full-blown restaurant, which is also a success. Listen to this."

She giggled again and turned the page. "...sound financial footing. In fact, our success has been so instantaneous that I have decided to launch out on a truly massive scale. It is Big Ideas that lead to Big Business. I am contemplating a vast expansion of this venture. In a very short time, I shall organize branches in New York, Chicago, Detroit, and all the major cities, each managed by a dedicated manager and featuring, in addition to standard cuisine, our famous Popp's Outstanding Pork Pies. "Isn't he a little wonder!" Sally exclaimed.

Sally rose and tousled his red hair. "Poor old Ginger! I knew you'd never be able to stick to it. Come on, it's a lovely night. Let's walk to the village and feast at the inn. We're going to be millionaires before we know where we are, so we can easily afford it."
