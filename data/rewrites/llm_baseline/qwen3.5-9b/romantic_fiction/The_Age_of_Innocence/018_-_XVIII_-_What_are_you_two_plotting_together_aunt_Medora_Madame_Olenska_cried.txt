"What are you two plotting together, Aunt Medora?" Madame Olenska cried as she entered the room. She was dressed for a ball, shimmering and glimmering softly, as if her gown were woven from candlelight itself. She carried her head high, challenging a room full of rivals with the air of a beauty aware of her own allure.

"We were merely saying, my dear," Mrs. Manson replied, rising gracefully and pointing archly at the flowers, "that here was something beautiful to surprise you with."

Madame Olenska halted, her gaze fixed on the bouquet. Her complexion did not flush, but a white radiance of fury swept over her like summer lightning.

"Ah!" she exclaimed in a shrill voice that Newland Archer had never heard before. "Who is ridiculous enough to send me a bouquet? Why a bouquet? And why tonight of all nights? I am not going to a ball; I am not a girl engaged to be married. But some people are always ridiculous."

Turning back to the door, she called out, "Nastasia!"

The ubiquitous handmaiden appeared instantly. Archer heard Madame Olenska speak in Italian, pronouncing the words with deliberate slowness so that he could follow every syllable: "Here—throw this into the dustbin!"

"But no—it's not the fault of the poor flowers," she continued as Nastasia protested. "Tell the boy to carry them to the house three doors away, the residence of Mr. Winsett, the dark gentleman who dined here. His wife is ill—they may derive pleasure from them... The boy is out, you say? Then, my dear one, run yourself. Here, put my cloak over you and fly. I want this thing out of the house immediately! And, as you live, do not say they come from me!"

She draped her velvet opera cloak over the maid's shoulders, turned back into the drawing-room, and shut the door sharply. Her bosom rose high beneath her lace bodice, and for a fleeting moment Archer thought she was about to weep. Instead, she burst into laughter. Looking alternately at the Marchioness and at Archer, she asked abruptly, "And you two—have you made friends?"

"It is for Mr. Archer to decide, darling; he has waited patiently while you were dressing."

"Yes—I gave you ample time; my hair simply refused to cooperate," Madame Olenska said, lifting her hand to the piled curls of her chignon. "But that reminds me: I see Dr. Carver is gone, and you will be late at the Blenkers'. Mr. Archer, will you put my aunt in the carriage?"

She followed the Marchioness into the hall, watched her settle into a chaotic heap of overshoes, shawls, and tippets, and called from the doorway, "Mind, the carriage must return for me at ten!"

Returning to the drawing-room, Archer found her standing by the mantelpiece, scrutinizing her reflection in the mirror. It was unusual in New York society for a lady to address her parlour-maid as "my dear one" and dispatch her on an errand wrapped in her own cloak. Yet, through his deeper anxieties, Archer tasted the pleasurable excitement of inhabiting a world where action followed emotion with such Olympian swiftness.

Madame Olenska did not move when he approached from behind. For a second, their eyes met in the mirror; then she turned, threw herself into the corner of the sofa, and sighed, "There is time for a cigarette."

He handed her the box and lit a spill. As the flame flickered up into her face, she glanced at him with laughing eyes. "What do you think of me in a temper?"

Archer paused, then answered with sudden resolve: "It helps me understand what your aunt has been saying about you."

"I knew she had been talking about me. Well?"

"She said you are accustomed to all manner of things—splendors, amusements, excitations—that we could never hope to provide here."

Madame Olenska smiled faintly into the circle of smoke gathering around her lips. "Medora is incorrigibly romantic. It has compensated her for so many other deficiencies!"

Archer hesitated, then took his risk again. "Is your aunt's romanticism always consistent with accuracy?"

"You mean: does she speak the truth?" Her niece considered. "Well, I will tell you: in almost everything she says, there is something true and something untrue. But why do you ask? What has she been telling you?"

He looked away into the fire, then back at her radiant presence. His heart tightened with the realization that this was their last evening by that fireside, and that moments hence the carriage would arrive to whisk her away.

"She claims—and pretends—that Count Olenski has asked her to persuade you to return to him."

Madame Olenska made no reply. She sat motionless, holding her cigarette in a half-lifted hand. The expression on her face had not altered; Archer recalled that he had previously noted her apparent incapacity for surprise.

"You knew, then?" he broke out.

She remained silent until the ash dropped from her cigarette. She brushed it onto the floor. "She has hinted about a letter: poor darling! Medora's hints—"

"Is it at your husband's request that she has arrived here so suddenly?"

Madame Olenska seemed to weigh this question carefully. "Again, one cannot tell. She told me she received a 'spiritual summons' from Dr. Carver, whatever that signifies. I fear she intends to marry Dr. Carver... poor Medora, there is always someone she wishes to wed. Perhaps the people in Cuba simply grew weary of her! I believe she accompanied them as a sort of paid companion. Truly, I do not know why she came."

"But you do believe she possesses a letter from your husband?"

Once more, Madame Olenska brooded in silence before saying, "After all, it was to be expected."

The young man rose and leaned against the fireplace. A sudden restlessness seized him, rendering him tongue-tied by the awareness that their minutes were numbered, and that at any instant he might hear the wheels of the returning carriage.

"You know that your aunt believes you will return?"

Madame Olenska lifted her head quickly. A deep blush rose to her face, spreading over her neck and shoulders. She blushed rarely and painfully, as if the heat burned her skin.

"Many cruel things have been believed of me," she said.

"Oh, Ellen—forget me; I am a fool and a brute!" She offered a small smile. "You are terribly nervous; you have your own burdens. I know you feel the Wellands are unreasonable regarding your marriage, and naturally, I agree with you. In Europe, people do not comprehend our lengthy American engagements; I suppose they are not as calm as we are." She pronounced the "we" with a faint emphasis that lent it an ironic tone.

Archer sensed the irony but dared not pursue it. After all, she had perhaps deliberately steered the conversation away from her own affairs, and following the evident pain his previous words had caused her, he felt compelled to follow her lead. Yet the waning hour filled him with desperation; he could not endure the thought of a barrier of words falling between them once more.

"Yes," he said abruptly. "I traveled south to ask May to marry me after Easter. There is no reason why we should not wed then."

"And May adores you—and yet you could not convince her? I thought her too intelligent to be the slave of such absurd superstitions."

"She *is* too intelligent—she is not their slave."

Madame Olenska regarded him. "Well, then—I do not understand."

Archer reddened and hurried on with a rush. "We had a frank discussion—almost the first. She considers my impatience a bad sign."

"Merciful heavens—a bad sign?"

"She believes it indicates that I cannot trust myself to continue caring for her. She thinks, in short, that I wish to marry her immediately to escape someone whom I—care for more."

Madame Olenska examined this proposition curiously. "But if she thinks that—why is she not in a hurry as well?"

"Because she is not like that: she is far nobler. She insists all the more on the long engagement to grant me time—"

"Time to give her up for the other woman?"

"If I choose to."

Madame Olenska leaned toward the fire, gazing into it with fixed eyes. Down the quiet street, Archer heard the approaching trot of her horses.

"That *is* noble," she said, with a slight break in her voice.

"Yes. But it is ridiculous."

"Ridiculous? Because you do not care for anyone else?"

"Because I do not intend to marry anyone else."

"Ah." Another long interval passed. Finally, she looked up and asked, "This other woman—does she love you?"

"Oh, there is no other woman; I mean, the person May was thinking of was—never—"

"Then, why, after all, are you in such haste?"

"There is your carriage," said Archer.

She half-rised and looked about her with absent eyes. Her fan and gloves lay on the sofa beside her, and she picked them up mechanically. "Yes; I suppose I must depart."

"You are going to Mrs. Struthers's?"

"Yes." She smiled and added, "I must go where I am invited, or I shall be too lonely.
