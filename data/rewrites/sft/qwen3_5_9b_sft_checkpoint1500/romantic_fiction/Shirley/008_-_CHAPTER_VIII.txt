Before the sun had crested the horizon, Moore had already mounted his horse and ridden out to Whinbury, returning long before his sister could prepare the *café au lait* or slice the tartines for breakfast. The nature of his business dealings remained a closely guarded secret; Hortense, accustomed to silence regarding his movements, offered no inquiries, nor did he offer accounts. To Moore, commerce was a realm of complicated and often dismal mysteries, buried deep within his breast and emerging only to terrify Joe Scott or startle a foreign correspondent. A profound reserve concerning matters of importance seemed bred into his mercantile blood.

Once breakfast was concluded, Moore retired to his counting-house. Henry, Joe Scott's boy, delivered the letters and newspapers. Seated at his desk, Moore broke the seals of the documents and scanned them. They were brief, yet far from sweet; on the contrary, they tasted sour. As he laid down the final letter, a derisive, defiant sniff escaped his nostrils. Though he uttered no soliloquy, a glare in his eye seemed to summon the devil, charging him to sweep the entire enterprise into Gehenna. Choosing a pen, he stripped away the feathered top in a brief spasm of finger-fury—his face remained placid—and dashed off a batch of answers. Sealing them, he walked through the mill. Upon returning, he sat to read his newspaper.

The contents failed to hold his attention. He frequently laid the paper across his knee, folded his arms, and gazed into the fire, occasionally turning his head toward the window or checking his watch. His mind was clearly preoccupied. Perhaps he was contemplating the beauty of the weather—for it was a fine, mild morning—and wishing to be out in the fields. The door of his counting-house stood wide open. The breeze and sunshine entered freely, yet the first visitor brought no spring perfume, only an occasional puff of sulfur carried on the wings of a thick, sable column of smoke rushing from the gaunt chimney.

Suddenly, a dark-blue apparition—that of Joe Scott, freshly emerged from a dyeing vat—appeared at the threshold. Uttering, "He's comed, sir," he vanished.

Mr. Moore did not lift his eyes from the paper. A large, broad-shouldered man, clad in fustian garments and gray worsted stockings, entered. Received with a nod, he took a seat, remarking as he removed his hat—a very bad one—and stowed it beneath his chair, wiping his forehead with a spotted cotton handkerchief, that it was "raight dahn warm for Febewerry." Moore assented with a slight, inarticulate sound. The visitor then carefully deposited an official-looking staff in the corner beside him, whistled to signal his ease, and settled back.

"You have what is necessary, I suppose?" asked Moore.

"Ay, ay! all's right."

The visitor resumed his whistling while Moore returned to his reading. Soon, however, Moore rose, opened a cupboard within reach of his long arm, and retrieved a black bottle—the same he had produced for Malone's benefit—a tumbler, and a jug. Placing them on the table, he indicated to his guest the jar of water in the corner.

"Help yourself," Moore said. "There's water in that jar."

"I dunnut knaw that there's mich need," the fustian gentleman replied, rising and complying. "For all a body is dry (thirsty) in a morning."

As the visitor mixed a portion with skilled hands and tested it with a deep draught, sinking back satisfied, Moore, chary of words, offered only a negative murmur.

"Yah'd as good," the visitor continued. "It 'uld set ye up wald a sup o' this stuff. Uncommon good hollands. Ye get it fro' furrin parts, I'se think?"

"Ay!"

"Tak my advice and try a glass on't. Them lads 'at's coming 'll keep ye talking, nob'dy knows how long. Ye'll need propping."

"Have you seen Mr. Sykes this morning?" Moore inquired.

"I seed him a hauf an hour--nay, happen a quarter of an hour sin', just afore I set off," Joe Scott replied. "He said he aimed to come here, and I sudn't wonder but ye'll have old Helstone too. I seed 'em saddling his little nag as I passed at back o' t' rectory."

The speaker proved a true prophet. Five minutes later, the trot of a little nag's hoofs echoed in the yard. A well-known nasal voice cried, "Boy," presumably addressing Harry Scott, who usually hovered around the premises from nine to five.

"Take my horse and lead him into the stable."

Helstone entered, marching nimbly and erect, looking browner, keener, and livelier than usual.

"Beautiful morning, Moore. How do, my boy? Ha! whom have we here?" Turning to the man with the staff, Helstone exclaimed, "Sugden! What! you're going to work directly? On my word, you lose no time. But I come to ask explanations. Your message was delivered to me. Are you sure you are on the right scent? How do you mean to set about the business? Have you got a warrant?"

"Sugden has."

"Then you are going to seek him now? I'll accompany you."

"You will be spared that trouble, sir; he is coming to seek me. I'm just now sitting in state waiting his arrival."

"And who is it? One of my parishioners?"

Joe Scott had entered unobserved. Now standing like a sinister phantom, half of his person dyed in the deepest tint of indigo from a recent vat, he leaned on the desk. His master's answer to the rector's question was a smile. Joe took the word. Putting on a quiet but pawky look, he said, "It's a friend of yours, Mr. Helstone, a gentleman you often speak of."

"Indeed! His name, Joe? You look well this morning."

"Only the Rev. Moses Barraclough; t' tub orator you call him sometimes, I think."

"Ah!" said the rector, producing his snuff-box and administering a very long pinch. "Couldn't have supposed it. Why, the pious man never was a workman of yours, Moore. He's a tailor by trade."

"And so much the worse grudge I owe him, for interfering and setting my discarded men against me."

"And Moses was actually present at the battle of Stilbro' Moor? He went there, wooden leg and all?"

"Ay, sir," said Joe. "He went there on horseback, that his leg mightn't be noticed. He was the captain, and wore a mask. The rest only had their faces blackened."

"And how was he found out?"

"I'll tell you, sir," said Joe. "T' maister's not so fond of talking. I've no objections. He courted Sarah, Mr. Moore's servant lass, and so it seems she would have nothing to say to him; she either didn't like his wooden leg or she'd some notion about his being a hypocrite. Happen, for women is queer hands... she'd have encouraged him, in spite of his leg and his deceit, just to pass time like."

"Joe, you're insupportable," interrupted Mr. Moore. "You spin out your explanation as Moses spins out his sermons. The long and short of it is, Murgatroyd was jealous of Barraclough; and last night, as he and a friend took shelter in a barn from a shower, they heard and saw Moses conferring with some associates within. From their discourse it was plain he had been the leader, not only at Stilbro' Moor, but in the attack on Sykes's property. Moreover, they planned a deputation to wait on me this morning, which the tailor is to head, and which, in the most religious and peaceful spirit, is to entreat me to put the accursed thing out of my tent. I rode over to Whinbury this morning, got a constable and a warrant, and I am now waiting to give my friend the reception he deserves. Here, meantime, comes Sykes. Mr. Helstone, you must spirit him up. He feels timid at the thoughts of prosecuting."

A gig rolled into the yard. Mr. Sykes entered—a tall, stout man of about fifty, comely of feature but feeble of physiognomy. He looked anxious.

"Have they been? Are they gone? Have you got him? Is it over?" he asked.

"Not yet," returned Moore with phlegm. "We are waiting for them."

"They'll not come; it's near noon. Better give it up. It will excite bad feeling..."

"_You_ need not appear," said Moore. "I shall meet them in the yard when they come; _you_ can stay here."

"But my name must be seen in the law proceedings. A wife and family, Mr. Moore..."

Moore looked disgusted. "Give way, if you please," he said. "Leave me to myself. I have no objection to act alone; only be assured you will not find safety in submission. Your partner Pearson gave way, and conceded, and forbore. Well, that did not prevent them from attempting to shoot him in his own house."
