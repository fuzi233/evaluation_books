The cathedral roof offered no shelter for secrets, only the weight of stone and the passage of time. For over half a year, Mr. Crisparkle and John Jasper walked the same corridors, their footsteps syncing with the rhythm of the building, yet their minds remained in separate, silent rooms. They spoke of nothing concerning Edwin Drood. Not a word. The subject hung between them like a ghost, visible only because it was absent. Jasper, the hunter of Neville Landless, and Crisparkle, his shield, existed in a state of perpetual, unspoken opposition. Neither dared name the tension; neither could bear to look directly at the other's design.

Jasper possessed a nature that rejected pretense. He would have welcomed the topic, perhaps demanded it, if he believed it would yield fruit. But his silence was not passive; it was a fortress. He was a man carved from a single, hard idea, concentrated so intensely on his fixed purpose that he had severed the connection with everything else. He moved through the world in a mechanical harmony with others, a perfect gear fitting into a machine, yet his spirit exchanged nothing with the air around him. He had once confessed this isolation to his lost nephew, before the necessity of his current rigidity became absolute.

Jasper knew Rosa had fled. He knew the cause. Whether he believed he had terrified her into silence, or if she had whispered the truth to Crisparkle, remained a question Crisparkle could not answer. As a man of justice, Crisparkle admitted that loving Rosa was not a crime, nor was offering love above revenge. Yet, the dreadful suspicion that haunted Rosa found no harbor in Crisparkle's mind. Even Helena and Neville, if the thought touched them, never voiced it. Mr. Grewgious, with his implacable dislike of Jasper, kept his distance, avoiding any mention of that specific evening when he had warmed his hands at the gatehouse fire and stared down at a pile of torn, muddy clothes on the floor.

Cloisterham slept, a drowsy entity waking occasionally to reconsider a story older than six months, dismissed by the magistrates. The town debated: had Neville been killed by a treacherous rival? By an open struggle? Or had he simply vanished, spirited away for his own ends? The narrative shifted with the wind, lifting its head to note Jasper's devotion to discovery and revenge, then dozing off again. This was the equilibrium of the world at that hour.

The cathedral doors locked for the night. The Choir-master, granted a brief leave for a few services, turned his face toward London. He traveled by the same route Rosa had taken, arriving on a hot, dusty evening. His luggage was light enough to carry in one hand. He walked to a hybrid establishment tucked behind Aldersgate Street, near the General Post Office—a place that was hotel, boarding-house, and lodging-house depending on the traveler's mood. It advertised itself in the railway papers as a novel enterprise, timidly emerging from the shadows. It apologized for not adhering to the old constitutional model of ordering a pint of sweet blacking to drink and discard; instead, it suggested the traveler might have his boots blacked, or perhaps just his bed, breakfast, and a porter for a fixed sum. From such premises, many Britons in low spirits deduced that the times were leveling, except for the high roads, which were destined to vanish entirely.

Crisparkle ate without appetite and stepped out again. He moved eastward through the stale streets until he reached a miserable court, particularly wretched among its peers. He climbed a broken staircase, opened a door, and peered into a dark, stifling room.

"Are you alone here?"

"Alone, deary; worse luck for me, and better for you," replied a croaking voice. "Come in, come in, whoever you be: I can't see you till I light a match, yet I seem to know the sound of your speaking. I'm acquainted with you, ain't I?"

"Light your match, and try."

"So I will, deary, so I will; but my hand that shakes, as I can't lay it on a match all in a moment. And I cough so, that, put my matches where I may, I never find 'em there. They jump and start, as I cough and cough, like live things. Are you off a voyage, deary?"

"No."

"Not seafaring?"

"No."

"Well, there's land customers, and there's water customers. I'm a mother to both. Different from Jack Chinaman t'other side the court. He ain't a father to neither. It ain't in him. And he ain't got the true secret of mixing, though he charges as much as me that has, and more if he can get it. Here's a match, and now where's the candle? If my cough takes me, I shall cough out twenty matches afore I gets a light."

She found the candle and lit it, but the success seized her instantly. She sank into rocking motions, gasping in intervals: "O, my lungs is awful bad! my lungs is wore away to cabbage-nets!" until the fit subsided. While the cough consumed her, she had no sight, no power save the struggle. As it receded, she strained her eyes and, upon articulating, cried out, staring:

"Why, it's you!"

"Are you so surprised to see me?"

"I thought I never should have seen you again, deary. I thought you was dead, and gone to Heaven."

"Why?"

"I didn't suppose you could have kept away, alive, so long, from the poor old soul with the real receipt for mixing it. And you are in mourning too! Why didn't you come and have a pipe or two of comfort? Did they leave you money, perhaps, and so you didn't want comfort?"

"No."

"Who was they as died, deary?"

"A relative."

"Died of what, lovey?"

"Probably, Death."

"We are short to-night!" she cried, laughing propitiously. "Short and snappish we are! But we're out of sorts for want of a smoke. We've got the all-overs, haven't us, deary? But this is the place to cure 'em in; this is the place where the all-overs is smoked off."

"You may make ready, then," replied the visitor, "as soon as you like."

He removed his shoes, loosened his cravat, and lay across the foot of the squalid bed, his head resting on his left hand.

"Now you begin to look like yourself," the woman approved. "Now I begin to know my old customer indeed! Been trying to mix for yourself this long time, poppet?"

"I have been taking it now and then in my own way."

"Never take it your own way. It ain't good for trade, and it ain't good for you. Where's my ink-bottle, and where's my thimble, and where's my little spoon? He's going to take it in a artful form now, my deary dear!"

She began her process, bubbling and blowing at the faint spark trapped in the hollow of her hands, speaking in tones of snuffling satisfaction without pause. When he spoke, he did not look at her; his thoughts were already roaming ahead by anticipation.

"I've got a pretty many smokes ready for you, first and last, haven't I, chuckey?"

"A good many."

"When you first come, you was quite new to it; warn't ye?"

"Yes, I was easily disposed of, then."

"But you got on in the world, and was able by-and-by to take your pipe with the best of 'em, warn't ye?"

"Ah; and the worst."

"It's just ready for you. What a sweet singer you was when you first come! Used to drop your head, and sing yourself off like a bird! It's ready for you now, deary."

He took the pipe with great care, bringing the mouthpiece to his lips. She seated herself beside him, ready to refill it. After inhaling a few whiffs in silence, he accosted her doubtfully:

"Is it as potent as it used to be?"

"What do you speak of, deary?"

"What should I speak of, but what I have in my mouth?"

"It's just the same. Always the identical same."

"It doesn't taste so. And it's slower."

"You've got more used to it, you see."

"That may be the cause, certainly. Look here." He stopped, becoming dreamy, forgetting he had invited her attention. She bent over him, speaking in his ear.

"I'm attending to you. Says you just now, Look here. Says I now, I'm attending to ye. We was talking just before of your being used to it."

"I know all that. I was only thinking. Look here. Suppose you had something in your mind; something you were going to do."

"Yes, deary; something I was going to do?"

"But had not quite determined to do."

"Yes, deary."

"Might or might not do, you understand."

"Yes." With the point of a needle, she stirred the contents of the bowl.

"Should you do it in your fancy, when you were lying here doing this?"

She nodded. "Over and over again."
