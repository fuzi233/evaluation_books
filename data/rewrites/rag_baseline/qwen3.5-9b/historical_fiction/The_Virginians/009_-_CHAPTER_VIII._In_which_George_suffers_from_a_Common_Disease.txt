The house of Castlewood was dressed in splendor, a performance of silk and polish designed to mask the sadness of the occasion. Madame Esmond wore a gown of magnificence beyond her custom, striving to make the entertainment pleasant for her guest despite the weight pressing on her own spirit.

The General's new aide-de-camp arrived first. Mr. Washington. He dismounted at the steps, his servants leading the horses away. No young gentleman in the colony possessed finer mounts or greater skill on horseback than he. In the covered gallery, before the Major could retreat to remove his riding-boots, he and the hostess paced together. She spoke of her son's departure; he confirmed his appointment to General Braddock. Between them, the negro servants carried dishes past, descending the steps onto the rough lawn, moving through the shadows like silent attendants to a play they did not understand.

Washington announced the arrival of Mr. Franklin of Pennsylvania, riding in the Commander-in-Chief's coach. Madame Esmond recalled that Franklin had once been a printer's boy. Things had come to a strange pass when such men rode in coaches. Washington countered that Franklin was shrewd and sensible. Madame Esmond believed the Major was too liberal in his praise. Yet Washington insisted the printer was ingenious and meritorious.

"I am glad," Madame Esmond said in her stately manner, "that as my boy goes to make the campaign, he will not be with tradesmen, but with gentlemen, with gentlemen of honour and fashion."

Washington had seen the gentlemen of honour and fashion over their cups. Perhaps he knew their sayings did not tend to edify a young man entering life. He chose silence, stating only that Harry and George must take their share of good and bad, hearing what both sorts had to say.

"To be with a veteran officer of the finest army in the world," the widow faltered, listing the virtues: gentlemen bred in the court, friends of the Duke. "With you, dear Colonel Washington, by whom my father always set such store. You don't know how much he trusted in you. You will take care of my boy, sir?"

She leaned heavily on his arm as they entered the mansion. Here they were joined by George. He came accurately powdered and richly attired, saluting his parent and friend with low, respectful bows. Unlike the modern youth who walk into rooms with hobnailed shoes and wideawakes, puffing cigars in faces, George bowed low. Yet he held no good humour. A polite smile played around the lower part of his countenance, while watchfulness and wrath glared from the eyes above.

"What had been said or done?" nothing that could not be spoken before decent company. So why did Madame Esmond blush, and the brave Colonel look red as he shook the young friend's hand?

"Have you had good sport?" Washington asked.

"No," George said curtly.

They looked at the picture of his father hanging in the parlour. The Colonel, usually taciturn, launched into a long description of his sport. He described the woods, the king's officers, the birds shot, and the game brought down. Not a jocular man, he became animated describing General Braddock's heavy person and great boots floundering through the Virginian woods. He recounted how the General hunted with a pack of dogs gathered from various houses, accompanied by negroes barking as loudly as the dogs, actually shooting the deer when they came in sight.

"Great God, sir!" Braddock had exclaimed, puffing and blowing, "what would Sir Robert have said in Norfolk, to see a man hunting with a fowling-piece in his hand, and a pack of dogs actually laid on to a turkey!"

"Indeed, Colonel, you are vastly comical this afternoon!" Madame Esmond laughed neatly, while her son listened, looking more glum than ever.

"The General meant Norfolk at home, madam, not Norfolk in Virginia," Washington corrected gently. "Braddock was talking of a visit to Sir Robert Walpole, who lives in that county, and of the great hunts the old Minister keeps there... I should like to see a good field and a good fox-chase at home better than any sight in the world," the honest sportsman added with a sigh.

"Nevertheless, there is good sport here," young Esmond sneered.

"What sport?" the other asked, looking sharply.

"Why, sure you know," George continued, stamping his foot as if charging with foils. "Are you not the best sportsman of the countryside? Are there not all the fish of the field, and the beasts of the trees, and the fowls of the sea—and the fish of the trees, and the beasts of the sea—and the—bah! You know what I mean. I mean shad, and salmon, and rock-fish, and roe-deer, and hogs, and buffaloes, and bisons, and elephants, for what I know. I'm no sportsman."

"No, indeed," Washington replied with a look of scarcely repressed scorn.

"Yes, I understand you. I am a milksop. I have been bred at my mamma's knee. Look at these pretty apron-strings, Colonel! Who would not like to be tied to them? See of what a charming colour they are! I remember when they were black—that was for my grandfather."

"And who would not mourn for such a gentleman?" the Colonel said, as the widow, surprised, looked at her son.

"And, indeed, I wish my grandfather were here, and would resurge, as he promises to do on his tombstone; and would bring my father, the Ensign, with him."

"Ahh, Harry!" Madame Esmond burst into tears as her second son entered the room—in just such another suit, gold-corded frock, braided waistcoat, silver-hilted sword, and solitaire, as his elder brother wore.

"Oh, Harry, Harry!" she cried, flying to her younger son.

"What is it, mother?" Harry asked, taking her in his arms.

"What is the matter, Colonel?"

"Upon my life, it would puzzle me to say," the Colonel answered, biting his lips. "A mere question, Hal, about pink ribbons, which I think vastly becoming to our mother; as, no doubt, the Colonel does."

"Sir, will you please to speak for yourself?" the Colonel bustled up, then sank his voice again.

"He speaks too much for himself," the widow wept.

"I protest I don't any more know the source of these tears, than the source of the Nile," George declared. "And if the picture of my father were to begin to cry, I should almost as much wonder at the paternal tears. What have I uttered? An allusion to ribbons! Is there some poisoned pin in them, which has been struck into my mother's heart by a guilty fiend of a London mantua-maker? I professed to wish to be led in these lovely reins all my life long," and he turned a pirouette on his scarlet heels.

"George Warrington! what devil's dance are you dancing now?" Harry asked, loving his mother, loving Washington, but above all loving and admiring his brother George.

"My dear child, you do not understand dancing—you care not for the politer arts—you can get no more music out of a spinet than by pulling a dead hog by the ear. By nature you were made for a man—a man of war—I do not mean a seventy-four, Colonel George, like that hulk which brought the hulking Mr. Braddock into our river. His Excellency, too, is a man of warlike turn, a follower of the sports of the field. I am a milksop, as I have had the honour to say."

"You never showed it yet. You beat that great Maryland man was twice your size," Harry broke out.

"Under compulsion, Harry. 'Tis tuptu, my lad, or else 'tis tuptomai, as thy breech well knew when we followed school. But I am of a quiet turn, and would never lift my hand to pull a trigger, no, nor a nose, nor anything but a rose," George said, handling one of Madame Esmond's bright pink apron ribbons. "I hate sporting, which you and the Colonel love, and I want to shoot nothing alive, not a turkey, nor a titmouse, nor an ox, nor an ass, nor anything that has ears. Those curls of Mr. Washington's are prettily powdered."

The militia colonel, offended by the first part of the talk and puzzled by the last, had taken a modest draught from the great china bowl of apple-toddy standing to welcome guests in all Virginian houses. He cooled himself by pacing the balcony in a very stately manner. Almost reconciled with the elder, the appeased mother stood giving a hand to each of her sons. George put his disengaged hand on Harry's shoulder.

"I say one thing, George," Harry said with a flushing face.

"Say twenty things, Don Enrico," the other cried.

"If you are not fond of sporting and that, and don't care for killing game and hunting, being cleverer than me, why shouldst thou not stop at home and be quiet, and let me go out with Colonel George and Mr. Braddock?" Harry delivered his speech.

The widow looked eagerly from the dark-haired to the fair-haired boy. She knew not from which she would like to part.
