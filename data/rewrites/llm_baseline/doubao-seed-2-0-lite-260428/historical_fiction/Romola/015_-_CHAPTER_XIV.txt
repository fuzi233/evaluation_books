The roar of the moving crowd and the wild jumble of noise bursting off the piazza hit Tito the moment he stepped through the entrance, and he remembered Nello’s offhand story about the Fierucoloni harvest fair. He pushed his way deeper into the throng, even taking a strange quiet pleasure in the good-natured hoots and accidental elbowing, for the chaotic movement filled the empty, gnawing stretches of time that had lately been full of dreary premonitions of wandering alone across unknown lands chasing a vague fortune. He’d even caught himself half considering, in his bleaker moments, giving up and hunting down Baldassarre after all.

At every piazza inlet, crowds of people jostled their way inward, paper lanterns balanced on tall sticks bobbing and weaving uncertainly above their heads. A low, droning, monotonous peasant chant cut a clear line through the din, crisscrossed by high-pitched screams, sharp whistles, piping taunts from gangs of boys, the thud of distant drums, and the tinny jingle of tiny hand bells, all clashing in a glorious, unruly symphony. Every few minutes, one of the dim floating lanterns shattered with a soft thwack, felled by a stray stone thrown in the name of mischief, followed by a girl’s shriek and a fresh wave of whooping. On the edges of the chaos, though, groups of fairgoers were marking the vigil of the Virgin’s Nativity with far more purpose than random stone-throwing. Ragged men with sharp, shifty glances darting between passersby chattered merrily as they lured country folk to their crooked dice games, swearing the terms were fair and generous. Two stilt-walkers in half-baked masquerade costumes had snatched lanterns from the crowd, swinging the lights back and forth like meteors as they strode across the square. A shrewd vendor turned a steady profit at his small covered stall selling hot berlingozzi, the beloved chewy farinaceous street treat. A peddler stood balanced on a barrel, back braced against a pillar of the Spedale degl’ Innocenti loggia, hawking miracle pills invented by a Salerno physician, guaranteed to ward off both toothache and death by drowning. Not far off, leaning against another pillar, a tumbler showed off flips and tricks on a tiny raised platform, while a handful of apprentices, bored by the scattered chaos of random stone fights, held a concentrated private tournament of the favourite Florentine sport at the narrow mouth of the Via de’ Febbrai.

Tito, forced to slip through random gaps in the crowd, found himself for a moment right next to a slow trotting procession of barefoot, thick-calloused peasant women, their sun-bronzed faces lined with years of mountain wind, their clothes stitched from obsolete fabrics and faded old fashions, crusted with layers of inherited grime. To city eyes, they looked like a long-dead generation of ancestors wandering home from a pilgrimage they’d set out on a full century before. These were the hardy, sparsely fed women from the Pistoia mountains, each carrying a year’s worth of hand-spun yarn slung in a modest bundle over their shoulders, their hearts holding only a meagre hope of small good fortune and a wide, unnameable dim fear of harm—all of which they trusted the Virgin to tend to, for that very night, the curtain would be pulled back on her miraculous angel-painted icon, letting her holy power stream out unobstructed over all the faithful.

A moment later the crowd swept Tito toward the piazza edge, where the more stationary peddlers and performers had positioned themselves to keep their backs clear of trouble. Among them, Tito spotted his old acquaintance Bratti, the pedlar, leaning against a pillar, mouth pursed in a disdainful silence, eyeing every passerby with a cold, superior glance, one hand clamped tight over a serge cloth covering the basket slung at his chest. Surprised to see the usually frantic, haggling trader acting so aloof, Tito drew nearer and watched as two women wandered up to Bratti’s basket, curious. The pedlar only pulled the covering tighter, and pointedly looked away. Too intrigued to leave, one of the women asked what wares he was hiding.

“Before I answer that, Monna, I must know if you mean to buy,” Bratti said, snappish. “I can’t show goods as choice as mine at a fair where every fly can land on them for free. I’ve only two left, and I won’t soil them for nothing—with the plague wise men whisper is coming, these will soon be worth their weight in gold. No, no, andate con Dio.”

The two women exchanged a look. “What’s the price, then?” the second asked.

“More than you’re likely to have in your purse, buona donna,” Bratti said, his tone pitying and supercilious. “I’d recommend you put your faith in the Lord and the saints: poor folks can do no better.”

“Not *that* poor!” the woman huffed indignantly, yanking her money bag out of her skirts. “Come now—what do you say to one grosso?”

“I say you can get twenty-one quattrini in change for that,” Bratti replied coolly, “but not from me. I haven’t got that small of change.”

“Two grossi, then?” the woman snapped, exasperated, while her companion eyed her with faint envy. “It can’t possibly be worth more than two.”

After more back and forth haggling, Bratti made a great show of softening into concession. “Since you’ve set your heart on it, I’d hate to do you a mischief,” he said, slowly lifting the serge cover. “Maestro Gabbadeo always said a woman denied the thing she’s set her mind on is more at risk of the plague than ever. Ecco, I’ve only two left, and let me tell you, the matching ring is on Maestro Gabbadeo’s own finger, that wisest of doctors who’s lately gone to Bologna.”

The precious goods were two clunky iron rings, hammered to look like the antique Roman artifacts sometimes dug up from Florentine backyards, their thick rust and vaguely promised magical properties convincing enough that the two women paid the agreed sum without complaint. The first woman, who had no grossi to spare, bartered with a generous handful of her own hand-spun yarn to take the last ring, and wandered off triumphant. Bratti covered his basket—now stuffed full of random odds and ends he’d bartered for on equally favourable terms—and stepped away from his pillar, nearly bumping straight into Tito, who had been about to slip away unrecognised.

“By the head of San Giovanni, this is luck!” Bratti yelped, yanking Tito back behind the pillar. “I was talking about you this morning, Messer Greco! Said you’d climbed up among the signori now, and I’m glad of it, seeing as I was at the start of your good fortune. I never thought I’d catch you loitering on the piazza stones these days! This is your luck, though, not mine—save for a small trifle to compensate me for my trouble in the matter.”

“You speak in riddles, Bratti,” Tito said, smiling faintly. “I don’t sharpen my wits the way you do haggling over fake iron rings. Say plainly what you mean.”

“By the Holy Gospels, that bargain was generous! If a Hebrew can make thirty two per cent, a Christian deserves a little more, right? I could have charged twice the money and twice the yarn if I hadn’t a conscience. But speaking of rings—*that* ring on your finger? I can get you a buyer with a very deep money bag.”

“Truly?” Tito said, glancing down at the plain old ring Baldassarre had slipped onto his finger as soon as his young hand had grown big enough to fit it.

“A Genoese merchant heading straight for Hungary, from what I hear. He rummaged through every shop in Florence looking for old trinkets no one else knew the value of, then wandered into my stall. He had a ring on his finger not unlike yours, and when we got to talking about old rings I said I had a fine young acquaintance of mine with one far finer. He said ‘tell him I’ll meet whatever price he asks.’ I was going to stop by Nello’s shop to find you tomorrow—for I’ve always thought, Messer Greco, that you’re not the sort to stand by and watch the Arno run broth without dipping your finger in.”

Tito had not missed a word, and his mind raced the whole time. Why had he ever kept the ring, anyway? It had only been silly sentiment that had stopped him selling it alongside Baldassarre’s other gems. If he’d been sensible and sold it then, he might never have been recognised by Fra Luca, tied back to a past he was desperate to outrun. Superstitious scruples about an inanimate object were pointless. This was a perfect chance to get rid of it, no extra work required.

“You speak with your usual sharp sense, Bratti,” Tito said. “I’ve no objection to hearing what your Genoese will offer. When and where am I to meet him?”

“Tomorrow, three hours after sunrise, at my stall. If your wits are as sharp as I’ve always known them, you’ll name a heavy price—he doesn’t care about the cost. I swear he’s buying the ring for some great noble, not for himself.”

“Then I will be there, if nothing hinders me,” Tito agreed.

“Doubtless you’ll deal generously by me for old acquaintance sake, so I won’t waste time bickering over the small tip you’ll give me,” Bratti cackled. “I can’t wait to get out of this piazza—fairs are dull, not to say wicked, when you’ve no more goods left to sell!”

Tito waved a quick goodbye, and the crowd swept him away again, zigzagging across the square without him having a say in his path, until he reached the far end of the piazza opposite the church, in a deep recess where the building line jutted inward, and a huge laughing crowd had gathered. Loud bursts of laughter interrupted a monologue that shifted from slow, oratorical grandeur to fast, buffoonish patter. A strong, concentrated light burned in the recess, silhouetting the heads of the spectators as the crowd pushed Tito slowly forward, and above them rose the head of a man wearing a white mitre painted all over with yellow cabalistic symbols.

“Behold, my children!” the man boomed. “Behold your chance! Do not neglect the holy sacrament of matrimony when it costs only one white quattrino—the cheapest marriage ever offered, pre-dissolved by special papal bull at every man’s own will and pleasure! Behold the bull!” He held up a crumpled parchment strung with huge dangling seals. “Behold the indulgence granted by his Holiness Alexander VI, newly elected Pope for his great piety, who means to reform the Church and wisely start by abolishing that priestly abuse that hoards too much of this privileged matrimony for the clergy and leaves none for the laity! Spit once, my sons, pay one white quattrino, and the bargain is done! The quattrino is the only difference the Holy Father allows between us and the clergy, who spit and pay nothing at all!”

Tito recognised that sharp, ringing voice, though the man’s face was hidden in shadow cast by the torches behind him. He pushed closer, and saw that behind the speaker, a mock altar draped in red cloth stitched all over with yellow zodiac signs was raised on a small platform. Half a dozen thin tapers burned at its back, a conjurer’s props scattered across its surface, a large open book propped in the centre, and at one front corner a monkey tied by a cord to a ring, holding a tiny lit taper in one paw. An impish little boy dressed in a miniature white surplice spent most of his time cuffing the monkey and straightening the wobbly taper every time the creature fidgeted. The man in the mitre wore a surplice too, over a chasuble printed with the zodiac signs in black on yellow cloth. Tito knew that sharp, angled face under the mitre: it was Maestro Vaiano, the mountebank he had rescued Tessa from not long before.

“Come, maidens!” Vaiano whooped. “This is the hour for the pretty girls who have many chances, and the plain girls who have few! Matrimony hot, eaten, and disposed of as easily as a berlingozzi! And see!” He held up a cluster of tiny drawstring bags. “To every bride I give a breve with a secret inside—the secret alone is worth the quattrino you pay for the ceremony! The secret to—no, I won’t tell you what it’s about, which makes it double the secret! Hang it round your neck unopened if you like, and you’ll avoid all the mishaps you never expected! Though if you open it you might break your leg, è vero, but you’ll know a secret no one else in Florence knows! And mark you: I give you the breve for free, I don’t sell it, like every other greedy holy man would. The quattrino pays for the marriage, the breve is a gift. Orsù, giovanetti, come be dutiful sons of the Church and buy the indulgence of his Holiness Alexander VI!”

The crowd ate the buffoonery up. The Fierucola fair was a modest occasion, and the Florentine townsfolk were happy to settle for jokes far tamer than the obscenely rhymed carnival fare the Magnifico and his poet friends usually served up. The women, besides enjoying the laughter, soon grew eager to get their hands on the mysterious breves. Several couples had already gone through the silly ceremony, roaring with laughter at Vaiano’s gibberish Latin, his dramatic grimaces over the open book, the monkey’s antics, and the required preliminary spit. Now a merry, bright-eyed seventeen year old boy in a loose tunic and red cap pushed forward, tugging a plump dark-haired girl by the hand, her ragged clothes showing off her round arms and legs charmingly.

“Fetter us right now, Maestro!” he yelled. “I’ve got to take my bride home and paint her portrait by lantern light!”

“Ha, Mariotto, my son, I commend your pious devotion—” Vaiano began, just as the monkey chattered loudly behind him, signaling disaster. The little creature’s patience had frayed thin under the surplice boy’s constant cuffing, and when the boy smacked him hard as his taper tipped horizontal, the monkey leaped back so hard he slipped the loose knot on his cord. He bounded first to the far end of the altar table, chattering furiously, and when the surplice boy picked up a wand to threaten him, he leaped straight onto the head of a tall woman in the front row, dropping his taper as he went, and screeching triumphantly from his new perch. The crowd erupted in screams and chaos, many of them half-convinced the mountebank’s familiar could do far more hidden mischief than just bite or scratch. Vaiano panicked, afraid his monkey would get hurt in the scuffle to grab him, and in the commotion Tito slipped out of the circle, letting the crowd carry him toward the doors of the Church of the Nunziata.

The brilliant golden light inside pressed against his eyes like something tangible after the dim, shadowy chaos of the piazza, and for a minute he could barely make out anything. That warm yellow glow felt supernatural, almost heavenly, to the many mountain peasant women who spent their lives half-shrouded by peaks and went to bed at twilight, and the low uninterrupted chant from the choir felt like soft rest to ears ringing with the piazza’s racket. Slowly the scene cleared, though a thin haze of incense mixed with the breath of the packed crowd hung in the air. In a left nave chapel strung with silver lamps, the miraculous fresco of the Annunciation stood unveiled, glowing so bright from all the surrounding lights it looked dark from where Tito stood on the far right of the nave. The whole of the great church was crammed full of peasant women, some kneeling, some standing: the rough, sun-darkened skin and dingy homespun clothes of the highest mountain dwellers mingled with the softer faces and bright white or red head drapery of wealthier families from the lower valleys. Spread high across the walls and ceiling was another, far older multitude, all pressed close together to be as near as possible to the Virgin’s power. These were hundreds of votive wax effigies: great Florentine statesmen in their black silk lucco council robes, popes, emperors, kings, cardinals, famous condottieri perched on their plumed chargers, even visiting Mohammedan merchants standing in easy, tolerated companionship with Christian cavaliers. Many of the older figures had faces blackened by centuries of incense fumes and robes tattered with age, others were brand new, bright in fresh red mantles or polished steel armour. Wedged between full effigies were severed wax arms, legs, and other fragments of bodies, with only a few empty gaps where a figure had been removed for public disgrace, or fallen ominously—like Lorenzo de Medici’s effigy had done, six months before. It was a teeming resurrection swarm of long dead people and pieces of people, their varying degrees of newness and decay mirroring the dingy, bright-spotted crowd below them.

Tito’s eyes wandered over the wild mass of people, searching. He’d thought of Tessa already, and the sea of white hoods made him hope he might spot her sweet face under one of them. It was a far more welcome thought than the vision of Romola staring at him with cold, changed eyes. He searched for a long time without luck, and was about to leave the church, weary of the unvarying scene, when he caught sight of Tessa no more than two yards away, right by the doorway. She was kneeling with her back propped against the wall, behind a group of standing peasant women who were craning to find a spot closer to the miraculous icon. Her head lolled a little to the side with exhaustion, her blue eyes staring absently at an altarpiece of Archangel Michael in armour, his face young and his hair flowing, surrounded by bearded tonsured saints. Her right hand, clutching a bunch of cocoons, hung limply by her side, her round cheeks pale either from the golden light or the tiredness written all over her. Her lips were pouted softly, and every few seconds her eyelids fluttered half shut, looking like a large, sweet sleepy child.
