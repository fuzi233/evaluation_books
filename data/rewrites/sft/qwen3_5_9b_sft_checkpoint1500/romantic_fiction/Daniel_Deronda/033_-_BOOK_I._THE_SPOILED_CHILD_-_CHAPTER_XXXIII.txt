"No man," declared a Rabbi, offering an undeniable example, "shall fashion the bones of his father and mother into spoons"—assured that his listeners understood the ethical barriers to such financial prudence. Yet the market for spoons had never widened sufficiently to prompt anyone to ask, "Why not?" or to claim that human advancement relies on repurposing human remains. The sole restraint was a sentiment, a principle that holds sway only over those who believe feelings constitute the true currency of the world.

Deronda, however, chose a less fashionable mode of exercise than riding along Rotten Row. He frequently wandered through the districts of London most densely populated by common Jews. He strolled to the synagogues during services, peered into shop windows, and studied the countenances of passersby—a pursuit unlikely to yield immediate results. Why, he mused, did he not seek out an influential Rabbi or a prominent community member to inquire about a mother named Cohen, a son named Ezra, and a lost daughter named Mirah? He considered approaching someone after Christmas. Yet, despite his appreciation for poetry in everyday details, Deronda found that when a sharp personal interest was ignited, he could not, any more than others, permanently shield himself from the crushing weight of the harsh, unyielding Reality. This Actual world never consults our tastes; it offers no selection. We know that enthusiasm finds comfort among abstract ideas, tolerating the smell of garlic from medieval times and ignoring the drab uniforms of classic processions. But when ideals confront us as warm, tangible flesh, enthusiasm grows squeamish and struggles to stand upright. Lying dreamily in a boat, imagining oneself searching for a maiden's kin in Cordova amidst Jews of Ibn-Gebirol's era, one bears all physical inconveniences without shock. Even if the grim scenes of St. Mary Axe and Whitechapel were magically transported to the Rhine's banks in the late eleventh century—where the cries of Crusaders sounded like bloodhounds' bays and devilish missionaries wielded swords and torches before the stoic, heroic figure of the reviled Jew—the squalor of dingy shops and plain faces would vanish beneath the thrill of contemplative emotion. Yet the fervor we feel for a grandiose martyrdom pales beside the enthusiasm that sustains itself where there is no danger, no challenge, only the impartial noon sun falling upon commonplace, perhaps slightly repulsive, objects that are truly beloved ideas made manifest. Here lies the essence of poetic power: the imagination's strength to pierce or elevate solid fact, rather than drifting among clouds. To glory in a prophetic vision of knowledge covering the globe is an easier exercise for the believing mind than witnessing its dawn in newspaper placards staring from a bridge beyond the cornfields. For those of us, delicate souls, it might well happen that we stood in the midst of Armageddon, unaware save for the annoyance of explosive smoke and bodies scrambling on the ground around us.

It was customary for Deronda to despise the weak, fastidious sympathy that recoils from the broad sweep of human life. But now, with Mirah standing before him as a living reality, whose fate he felt compelled to safeguard, he viewed every common Jew and Jewess through the lens of comparison with her. A presentiment troubled him: a collision between her idealized notion of an unknown mother and brother and the stark, discovered facts. This foreboding burned hotter within him because of a suppressed awareness that a similar, terrifying possibility of collision might be hidden within his own destiny. Did he not harbor a secret grievance against the way others had shaped his life, lacking the open daylight of full relations and the guiding clarity of primary duties that others possessed?

He did not eagerly seek to find any specific individual. When, as was his habit, he glanced at the sign above a shop door, he was relieved it did not read "Ezra Cohen." Indeed, he wished, almost desperately, that Ezra Cohen did not own a shop. Wishes are often deemed ominous; according to the superstitious view, if one harbors an impious objection to a squint, one's offspring is more likely to be born with one, and conversely, if one desires a squint, it shall not come to pass. This despondent calculus of probability is wholly rejected by the hopeful, who take their wishes as sufficient guarantees of fulfillment.

One morning, Deronda turned into a quiet side street to escape the noise and obstructions of Holborn, and the scales tipped toward the despondent view. He was weary of the streets and paused to hail a passing cab when his eye was caught by a pair of magnificent, antique clasps in chased silver displayed in a window to his right. His first thought was that Lady Mallinger, with her staunch Protestant taste for such Catholic relics, might wish to wear them as a bracelet. Then his gaze swept over the window's contents, revealing that this was a pawnbroker's establishment specializing in jewelry, lace, and all equivocal objects introduced as *bric-à-brac*. A placard in the corner announced: *Watches and Jewelry exchanged and repaired*.

His scrutiny had not gone unnoticed. A figure emerged from the doorway, regarding him with a tone of cordial encouragement. "Good day, sir," he said. In that fleeting instant, Deronda recognized the unmistakably Jewish face of a young man, roughly thirty, who winced under the shopkeeper's persuasive gaze. Before the persuasion could take root, Deronda stepped to the other side of the street and beckoned the cabman to draw up nearby. From there, he read the name above the window: *Ezra Cohen*.

There might be a hundred Ezra Cohens inscribed above shop fronts, yet Deronda had not encountered them. Presumably, the young man engaging a potential customer was Ezra himself, an age appropriate for Mirah's brother, who had grown to manhood while she remained a child. Yet, as he drove homeward, Deronda's first endeavor was to convince himself that there was no warrantable presumption that this Ezra was Mirah's brother. Next, he reasoned that even if, against all logic, he proved to be that brother, and inquiries revealed the mother to be deceased, it was not his duty to disclose this discovery to Mirah. An inconvenient thought disturbed this conclusion: Mirah would possess a religious longing to know of her mother's death and to ascertain whether her brother lived. How far was he justified in determining another's life by his own notions? Was it not his private complaint that others had arranged his life without open daylight, denying him the full guidance of primary duties that others enjoyed?

Immediate relief from this internal turmoil came from the reflection that he had made no real discovery yet. By investigating the facts more closely, he would surely certify that no decision was demanded of him. He intended to return to the shop soon to purchase the clasps for Lady Mallinger. However, he was hindered for several days by Sir Hugo, who, preparing for an after-dinner speech on a burning topic, required Deronda to research the legal aspects of the issue. Their daily arguments invariably ended in a drawn battle. On many subjects, they held opposing views, yet Sir Hugo cared little for this and, when Deronda articulated his point well, remarked with a mixture of satisfaction and regret:

"Confound it, Dan! Why don't you seize an opportunity to speak these things in public? You're wrong, you know. You won't succeed. You've got the massive sentiment—the heavy artillery of the country—against you. But it's excellent ground for a young man to display himself. When I was your age, I would have taken it. It would throw you into greater relief. If you seized such an occasion to make an impression, you might be in Parliament in no time. And you know that would gratify me."

"I am sorry not to do what would gratify you, sir," said Deronda. "But I cannot persuade myself to look at politics as a profession."

"Why not? If a man is not born into public life by his position in the country, there is no other path but to embrace it through his own efforts. The business of the nation must be conducted—Her Majesty's Government carried on, as the old Duke said. And it never could be, my boy, if everyone regarded politics as prophecy, demanding an inspired vocation. If you are to enter Parliament, you cannot sit still waiting for a call, either from heaven or constituents."

"I do not wish to make a living out of opinions," Deronda replied. "Especially out of borrowed opinions. Not that I intend to blame other men. I dare say many better fellows than I do not mind climbing onto a platform to praise themselves, and giving their word of honor for a party."

"I'll tell you what, Dan," said Sir Hugo, "a man who sets his face against every sort of humbug is simply a three-cornered, impracticable fellow. There is a bad style of humbug, but there is also a good style—one that oils the wheels and makes progress possible. If you are to rule men, you must rule them through their own ideas. I agree with the Archbishop at Naples who organized a St. Januarius procession against the plague. It is useless to have an Order in Council against popular shallowness. No action is possible without a little acting."

"One may be obliged to yield to occasional necessities," said Deronda. "But it is one thing to say, 'In this particular case, I am forced to put on this foolscap and grin,' and another to buy a pocket foolscap and practice grinning.
