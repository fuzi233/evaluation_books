"Wooed and married and a'."

"Edith!" Margaret whispered, her voice soft and gentle. "Edith!"

But as Margaret half suspected, her cousin had already surrendered to sleep. Edith lay curled upon the crimson damask sofa in the back drawing-room of Harley Street, a vision of white muslin and blue ribbons. If the fairy Titania had ever donned such attire and drifted off upon a similar cushion, she could hardly have been distinguished from the sleeping girl. Margaret, struck anew by the radiant beauty of her cousin, felt a fresh pang of affection. They had grown up side by side since childhood, yet while the world had long remarked upon Edith's prettiness, Margaret had remained oblivious to it until recently. Now, with the impending loss of her constant companion looming, every sweet quality and charm Edith possessed seemed amplified. Their conversation had drifted from wedding dresses and ceremonies to Captain Lennox's descriptions of life at Corfu, where his regiment was stationed, and the formidable challenge of keeping a piano in tune during the Scottish visits that would follow their marriage. But the whispers had grown drowsier; after a pause, Margaret realized that despite the hum of voices in the next room, Edith had rolled herself into a soft ball of muslin, ribbon, and silken curls, drifting into a peaceful afternoon nap.

Margaret had been on the verge of sharing her own visions of the future—the quiet life awaiting her in the country parsonage where her parents resided, a place where her brightest holidays had once been spent, though for the last decade, Aunt Shaw's house in London had been her true home. Deprived of a listener, she was forced to brood silently on the coming change. It was a happy brooding, albeit tinged with regret at the indefinite separation from her gentle aunt and beloved cousin. As she contemplated the joy of becoming the only daughter in Helstone parsonage, fragments of conversation from the adjacent room drifted to her ears.

Aunt Shaw was speaking to the five or six ladies who had dined there, whose husbands remained in the dining-room. These were the familiar acquaintances of the house, neighbors whom Mrs. Shaw termed "friends" simply because she dined with them more frequently than others, and because they were willing to exchange calls before luncheon if either she or Edith needed something. These ladies and their husbands had been invited as friends to a farewell dinner honoring Edith's approaching marriage. Edith had initially objected, knowing Captain Lennox was expected by a late train that very evening; yet, being a spoiled child who lacked a strong will, she had yielded when her mother insisted on ordering the extra delicacies of the season, deemed efficacious against the grief of farewells. Edith contented herself by leaning back, playing absently with the food on her plate, looking grave and distant, while everyone else enjoyed the witty remarks of Mr. Grey, the gentleman who invariably sat at the bottom of the table and had asked Edith to provide music for the drawing-room. Mr. Grey was particularly agreeable tonight, and the gentlemen lingered downstairs longer than usual—a mercy, judging by the snippets Margaret overheard.

"I suffered too much myself," a voice murmured. "Not that I was not extremely happy with the poor dear General, but disparity of age is a drawback; one I was resolved Edith should not encounter. Without maternal partiality, I foresaw the dear child was likely to marry early; indeed, I often said I was sure she would be wed before nineteen. I had quite a prophetic feeling regarding Captain Lennox"—here the voice dropped to a whisper, but Margaret easily supplied the missing name. The course of true love in Edith's case had run remarkably smooth. Mrs. Shaw had yielded to this presentiment, urging the marriage forward, even though it fell below the expectations many held for a young, pretty heiress. Mrs. Shaw declared her only child should marry for love, sighing emphatically as if love had not been her motive for marrying the General. She seemed to enjoy the romance of the current engagement more than her daughter did. Edith was thoroughly and properly in love, yet she would certainly have preferred a grand house in Belgravia to all the picturesque hardships of life at Corfu. The very aspects that made Margaret glow with anticipation caused Edith to pretend to shiver and shudder; partly for the pleasure of being coaxed out of her dislike by her fond lover, and partly because anything resembling a gypsy or makeshift existence was truly distasteful to her. Yet, had anyone offered a fine house, a vast estate, and a noble title, Edith would have clung to Captain Lennox while the temptation lasted. Once it faded, she might well have harbored little-concealed regrets that her husband could not unite every desirable trait in his person. In this, she was merely her mother's child, who, after deliberately marrying General Shaw out of respect for his character and establishment, constantly, though quietly, lamented her hard lot in being united to one she could not love.

"I have spared no expense in her trousseau," another voice declared. "She possesses all the beautiful Indian shawls and scarves the General gave me, which I shall never wear again."

"She is a lucky girl," replied another voice, which Margaret recognized as Mrs. Gibson, a lady taking a double interest in the conversation because one of her daughters had married within the last few weeks. "Helen had set her heart upon an Indian shawl, but when I found the extravagant price, I was obliged to refuse her. She will be quite envious when she hears of Edith's shawls. What kind are they? Delhi? with the lovely little borders?"

Margaret heard her aunt's voice again, rising as if she had lifted herself from her half-recumbent position to gaze into the dimly lit back drawing-room. "Edith! Edith!" she cried, then sank back as if weary by the exertion. Margaret stepped forward.

"Edith is asleep, Aunt Shaw. Is there anything I can do?"

All the ladies exclaimed "poor child!" upon receiving this distressing news, and the minute lap-dog in Mrs. Shaw's arms began to bark, seemingly excited by the burst of pity.

"Hush, Tiny! You naughty little girl! You will wake your mistress. It was only to ask Edith if she would tell Newton to bring down her shawls; perhaps you would go, Margaret dear?"

Margaret ascended to the old nursery at the very top of the house, where Newton was busy preparing laces required for the wedding. While Newton muttered grumblings as he went to undo the shawls—which had already been displayed four or five times that day—Margaret looked around the nursery. It was the first room in that house with which she had become familiar nine years ago, when she was brought, untamed from the forest, to share the home, play, and lessons of her cousin Edith. She recalled the dark, dim atmosphere of the London nursery, presided over by an austere and ceremonious nurse who was terribly particular about clean hands and torn frocks. She remembered the first tea served there—separate from her father and aunt, who dined far below, an infinite depth of stairs; for unless she were up in the sky, the children thought, they must be deep down in the bowels of the earth. At home, before moving to Harley Street, her mother's dressing-room had been her nursery; and as they kept early hours in the country parsonage, Margaret had always eaten with her parents. Oh! How well the tall, stately girl of eighteen remembered the tears shed with such wild passion of grief by the little girl of nine, hiding her face under the bedclothes on that first night; how she was bid not to cry by the nurse lest it disturb Miss Edith; and how she had wept more quietly and bitterly until her newly seen, grand, pretty aunt arrived with Mr. Hale to show him his little sleeping daughter. Then the little Margaret had hushed her sobs, striving to lie quiet as if asleep, fearing to make her father unhappy by her grief, which she dared not express before her aunt and which she rather thought wrong to feel after the long hoping, planning, and contriving they had undertaken at home to arrange her wardrobe for her grander circumstances and to bring her father up to London, even for a few days.

Now she loved the old nursery, though it was dismantled, and she surveyed it with a kind of cat-like regret at the idea of leaving it forever in three days.

"Ah, Newton!" she said. "I think we shall all be sorry to leave this dear old room."

"Indeed, miss, I shan't for one. My eyes are not so good as they were, and the light here is so bad I can't see to mend laces except just at the window, where there's always a shocking draught—enough to give one one's death of cold."

"Well, I dare say you will have both good light and plenty of warmth at Naples. You must keep as much of your darning as you can till then. Thank you, Newton, I can take them down—you're busy."

Thus Margaret descended, laden with shawls, inhaling their spicy Eastern scent. Her aunt asked her to stand as a sort of lay figure to display them, as Edith remained asleep. No one noticed, but Margaret's tall, finely made figure, clad in a black silk dress worn as mourning for a distant relative of her father's, set off the long, beautiful folds of the gorgeous shawls that would have half-smothered Edith. Margaret stood directly under the chandelier, silent and passive, while her aunt adjusted the draperies. Occasionally, as she was turned, she caught a glimpse of herself in the mirror over the chimney-piece and smiled at her own appearance—the familiar features in the usual garb of a princess. She touched the shawls gently as they hung around her, taking pleasure in their soft feel and brilliant colors, and rather liked to be dressed in such splendor, enjoying it much as a child would, with a quiet, pleased smile on her lips.

Just then the door opened, and Mr. Henry Lennox was suddenly announced. Some of the ladies started back, half-ashamed of their feminine interest in dress. Mrs. Shaw held out her hand to the newcomer; Margaret stood perfectly still, thinking she might still be needed as a block for the shawls, but looking at Mr. Lennox with a bright, amused face, certain of his sympathy in her sense of the ludicrousness of being thus surprised.

Her aunt was so absorbed in asking Mr. Henry Lennox—who had not been able to attend dinner—all sorts of questions about his brother the bridegroom, his sister the bridesmaid (coming from Scotland with the Captain), and various other members of the Lennox family, that Margaret realized she was no longer wanted as a shawl-bearer. She devoted herself to amusing the other visitors, whom her aunt had momentarily forgotten. Almost immediately, Edith emerged from the back drawing-room, winking and blinking her eyes against the stronger light, shaking back her slightly ruffled curls, and looking altogether like Sleeping Beauty just startled from her dreams. Even in her slumber, she had instinctively felt that a Lennox was worth rousing herself for. She had a multitude of questions to ask about dear Janet, the future, unseen sister-in-law, for whom she professed such affection that, had Margaret not been very proud, she might have almost felt jealous of the mushroom rival. As Margaret sank further into the background upon her aunt joining the conversation, she saw Henry Lennox directing his gaze toward a vacant seat near her; she knew perfectly well that as soon as Edith released him from her questioning, he would take possession of that chair. She had not been quite sure, from her aunt's rather confused account of his engagements, whether he would come that night; it was almost a surprise to see him, and now she was assured of a pleasant evening. He liked and disliked nearly the same things she did. Margaret's face lightened into an honest, open brightness. Soon he arrived. She received him with a smile devoid of any tinge of shyness or self-consciousness.

"Well, I suppose you are all in the depth of business—ladies' business, I mean. Very different to my business, which is the real true law business. Playing with shawls is very different work to drawing up settlements."

"Ah, I knew how you would be amused to find us all so occupied in admiring finery. But really Indian shawls are very perfect things of their kind."

"I have no doubt they are. Their prices are very perfect, too. Nothing wanting."

The gentlemen began to drop in one by one, and the buzz and noise deepened in tone.

"This is your last dinner-party, is it not? There are no more before Thursday?"

"No. I think after this evening we shall feel at rest, which I am sure I have not done for many weeks; at least, that kind of rest when the hands have nothing more to do, and all the arrangements are complete for an event which must occupy one's head and heart. I shall be glad to have time to think, and I am sure Edith will."

"I am not so sure about her, but I can fancy that you will. Whenever I have seen you lately, you have been carried away by a whirlwind of some other person's making."

"Yes," said Margaret, rather sadly, remembering the never-ending commotion about trifles that had persisted for over a month. "I wonder if a marriage must always be preceded by what you call a whirlwind, or whether in some cases there might not rather be a calm and peaceful time just before it."

"Cinderella's godmother ordering the trousseau, the wedding-breakfast, writing the notes of invitation, for instance," said Mr. Lennox, laughing.

"But are all these quite necessary troubles?" asked Margaret, looking up straight at him for an answer. A sense of indescribable weariness regarding all the arrangements for a pretty effect, in which Edith had been busied as supreme authority for the last six weeks, oppressed her just then; and she truly wanted someone to help her find a few pleasant, quiet ideas connected with a marriage.
