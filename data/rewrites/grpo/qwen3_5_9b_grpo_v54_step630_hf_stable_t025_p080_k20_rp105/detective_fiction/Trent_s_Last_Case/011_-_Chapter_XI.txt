Hitherto Unpublished

My Dear Molloy:—Should you not be at your office when this reaches you, know that I have solved the Manderson mystery. The enclosed dispatch details the solution. My role ends here; yours begins with deciding how to wield this information. It implicates an unsuspected individual, practically accusing him of murder. Prudence dictates withholding publication until his arrest, and legality forbids it thereafter until a guilty verdict is secured. You may choose to release it then, or perhaps utilize the facts sooner. That is your prerogative. For now, contact Scotland Yard. I am done with this case; I pray I never touched it. Follows my dispatch. P.T.

Marlstone, June 16th.

I commence this third, and likely final, dispatch to the *Record* regarding the Manderson murder with a conflicted heart. Relief washes over me, for in my previous reports, justice demanded I withhold crucial facts. To reveal them then would have alerted a suspect of uncommon boldness and resourcefulness, potentially allowing him to flee. Now, I set forth those withheld truths. Yet, I confess, the tale of treachery and perverted intellect leaves a bitter taste. It reveals a revolting depth of motive beneath the crime's surface, a puzzle I believe I have finally unraveled.

Recall my first dispatch, describing the scene upon my arrival at Marlstone on Tuesday morning. I recounted the discovery of the body, the surrounding mystery, local theories, and Manderson's movements the prior evening. I noted a peculiar detail: a quantity of whisky, far exceeding his nightly habit, had vanished from his private decanter. The following day, the inquest, I reported only an abstract of the proceedings. As I write, that day is not yet over, and my investigation has concluded, pointing directly to the man who must clear himself of Manderson's death.

Beyond the central enigma of Manderson's early departure, two minor oddities stood out from the start. First, the body was found mere yards from the house, yet no one claimed to hear a cry or struggle. Manderson was not gagged; wrist marks indicated a fight; and at least one pistol shot had been fired. This silence was baffling, especially given that Martin, the butler, was a poor sleeper with keen hearing, and his bedroom window faced the shed where the body lay.

Second, Manderson had left his dental plate by the bedside. He had dressed fully—necktie, watch, chain—yet forgotten the plate he wore daily. It was not a frantic flight; anyone familiar with removable dentures knows inserting it is second nature. These anomalies hinted at a shadow lurking in the mystery.

My breakthrough came in Manderson's bedroom. Amidst the rigid simplicity of the room, I examined the shoes. On the upper shelf, where Manderson's patent leather dress shoes should have been, I found them. They were old, well-polished, and expertly made. However, a connoisseur would notice a slight split in the leather of the vamp, where the laces rise. The stitching holding the upper to the sole was strained and visible at the toe and outer side. These were not signs of Manderson's careful, narrow feet. They were the marks of someone forcing their larger feet into shoes too small.

The tears were fresh. Manderson wore his own shoes with care; none of the others bore such damage. Someone else had worn them recently. The possibility of post-mortem tampering was negligible; the body had been found only twenty-six hours prior. Nor would Manderson have allowed others to borrow and ruin his prized footwear. The only men in the house were the butler and two secretaries. Yet, I let my thoughts wander, allowing the daemon of deduction to range.

Psychologically, once a key fact is grasped, the mind rearranges itself around it. The thought "Someone other than Manderson wore these shoes" triggered a cascade of realizations. It was unlike Manderson to drink heavily, to be untidily dressed, to skip washing, or to leave his bedroom without his false teeth. It was strange he spoke so freely to his wife at bedtime, or left his dental plate behind. All these inconsistencies converged on one terrifying certainty: Manderson was not in the house that night.

If a man was impersonating Manderson, why force his feet into Manderson's narrow shoes? To create the illusion that Manderson was present. The maid had found the shoes outside the bedroom door, polished and replaced on the shelf, ensuring the house appeared occupied. Similarly, the missing dental plate was a deliberate prop. The impersonator had brought it, leaving it in the bedroom to cement the belief that Manderson had retired there.

This implied Manderson was dead before the impostor arrived. The clothing confirmed this. The trousers, waistcoat, and shooting jacket were in the bedroom. Martin had seen the jacket on the man at the telephone. The impersonator knew Martin would recognize the coat, so it was essential.

A critical oversight had blinded everyone: Martin and Mrs. Manderson had never seen the impostor's face. Mrs. Manderson, half-asleep, merely resumed a conversation with her "husband." Martin saw only the man's back as he crouched at the telephone, wearing Manderson's broad-brimmed hat. The back of a head and neck holds too much character; the impostor, matching Manderson's build, needed no disguise beyond the hat, jacket, and mimicry.

The entry via the garden window, avoiding the front door where Martin might intercept him, was logical. The missing whisky was likely a comfort for a man fresh from a bloody crime, seeking solace before executing his final act. He knew when to stop, realizing the true danger lay ahead: shutting himself in Manderson's room and staging the scene, risking detection by the woman in the adjoining room. From her bed, she could only see a cupboard; the estrangement between the couple suggested she would assume the presence of her husband was normal.

Following this hypothesis, I visualized the sequence: The impostor entered, rang for Martin, and sat at the telephone with his back to the door. While Martin was occupied, the impostor ascended to the bedroom floor, entered Marlowe's room, and placed the murder weapon—a revolver taken from Marlowe's case—onto the mantelpiece. He then moved to Manderson's room, placed the shoes outside, scattered the clothes, set the dental plate in the bowl, and selected a fresh suit.

Who was this false Manderson? Five conclusions emerged:
1. He knew Manderson intimately.
2. He matched Manderson's height and shoulder breadth but had larger feet.
3. He possessed exceptional mimicry skills.
4. He knew the household routines intimately.
5. He desperately needed to convince everyone Manderson was alive until after midnight.

Mr. John Marlowe fit this profile perfectly. He had been Manderson's private secretary for four years, sharing Manderson's height and build. Though slightly slimmer, Marlowe's shoes were notably larger. I telegraphed an Oxford friend regarding Marlowe's acting history. The reply revealed Marlowe was a former Oxford University Dramatic Society member, playing roles like Bardolph and Mercutio, renowned for character acting and imitations. A photograph in Marlowe's bedroom confirmed his theatrical past. He had lived as part of the family, knowing every domestic detail. Furthermore, Marlowe had a verified alibi: he arrived in Southampton at 6:30 AM on Monday morning to execute a commission Manderson supposedly gave him.

Yet, contradictions remained. Only Marlowe knew Manderson intended to go to Southampton. Manderson, upon returning from the drive, openly mentioned Southampton to two people, even calling a hotel there to verify Marlowe's story. This call was the one Martin was busy with. If Manderson was alive until after midnight, Marlowe could not have committed the murder. But if Manderson died by eleven, how did Marlowe appear in Southampton by 6:30 AM? He had to exit the house unseen and unheard before midnight, while Martin, instructed to stay awake until 12:30 AM, stood sentinel near the staircase.

This was the crux. I spent the day before the inquest testing my theory, finding only one weakness: Martin's vigilance. I needed proof that the impostor could have fled before Martin fell asleep. I decided to wait until the inquest proceedings at the hotel began, securing White Gables for my investigation.

Armed with a camera, I searched for evidence. I photographed two fresh, large fingerprints on the polished front of the drawer in Manderson's bedroom. I found five prints on the French window in Mrs. Manderson's room, which stood open at night, and three on the glass bowl holding the dental plate. I collected samples from Marlowe's bedroom and compared them with prints Marlowe had unknowingly made on my diary leaves earlier.

By six PM, two hours after the jury's verdict, I had matched the prints. Two of the five window prints and the three on the bowl belonged to Marlowe's left hand; the remaining three on the window and two on the drawer were his right hand. By eight PM, enlarged prints from a professional photographer confirmed the identity. Marlowe had recently been in Manderson's bedroom and Mrs. Manderson's room, places he had no business visiting.

At nine PM, back in my hotel room, I finalized the narrative. The impostor, having staged the room and spoken to Mrs. Manderson about Marlowe's trip to Southampton, switched off the light and lay in bed. Once assured she was asleep, he slipped out in stocking feet, carrying the bundle of clothes and shoes. He pushed the French window open, stepped onto the balcony railing, and dropped to the lawn below.

This escape could have taken half an hour after his entry at 11:30 PM. The rest is conjecture for the authorities. The corpse was found next morning, dressed untidily. Marlowe appeared in the car in Southampton by 6:30 AM.

I conclude this manuscript in my hotel room at Marlstone. It is four o'clock in the morning. I depart for London by the noon train from Bishopsbridge, delivering these pages immediately to you. Please convey the substance of this report to the Criminal Investigation Department.
