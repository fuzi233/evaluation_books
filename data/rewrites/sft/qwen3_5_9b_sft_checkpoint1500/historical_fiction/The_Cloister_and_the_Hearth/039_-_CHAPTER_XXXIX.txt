Travel holds a peculiar allure for the English soul, regardless of whether the wanderer seeks holy shrines or mere adventure. Even ancient friendships possess a strange fascination, akin to fossils preserved in stone. Yet, since the true heart of our tale beats within Holland, it is time to return there—to those ordinary lives and everyday struggles that have shaped humanity across the ages.

Jorian Ketel arrived at Peter's house determined to claim Margaret's promise, only to find her confined to a fever-stricken bed. Upon learning his purpose, Peter, inflamed by public sentiment, rebuffed him with sharp disdain and ordered him from the premises. Those standing nearby, swayed by the crowd's undiscriminating fury, threatened to dunk the intruder. Both father and daughter were beloved figures, and the entire town buzzed with gossip regarding their plight. Thus, Jorian departed in a sullen mood, regretting his previous kindness. Such repentance, born of anger rather than genuine remorse, is not uncommon.

Meanwhile, Dierich Brower, caught attempting to buy information about Martin Wittenhaagen from a drunken man at "The Three Kings," was seized and hurled into a horse pond. Threatened with further punishment if he ever returned, he was effectively silenced. Simultaneously, municipal jealousy flared in Sevenbergen. The burgomaster dispatched a formal letter to his counterpart in Tergou, accusing Gerard of trespassing and demanding any future complaints be lodged locally.

Ghybrecht Van Swieten, the cunning mastermind behind these machinations, suppressed his rage. He replied politely, claiming the fugitive was merely a traveler named Gerard from Tergou who had stolen town records before fleeing abroad. Having lost track of Gerard, Ghybrecht feigned closure, masking his growing unease. However, his composure was a thin veil over mounting dread. Baffled by the failure to locate Gerard in Sevenbergen, he secretly dispatched emissaries to Tergou to trace the boy's movements. To his infinite frustration, they returned empty-handed. This silence terrified him; he feared Gerard was lurking nearby, waiting for the perfect moment to strike back. Consequently, Ghybrecht transformed into a moody, irritable man, his eyes darting nervously as if expecting an unseen blow. The misery he had inflicted upon others offered him no solace.

Back at the modest household in Tergou, the atmosphere had shifted irrevocably. Though outward appearances remained unchanged, the simple happiness that once filled the home had vanished. Little Kate knew the extent of Cornelis and Sybrandt's role in banishing Gerard. While she held her tongue to prevent further discord, she often shuddered at their presence, wincing at their hypocritical expressions of regret. Catherine, exercising a mother's vigilance, observed Kate's distress without speaking aloud, silently piecing together the truth. The "black sheep" themselves, acting out of guilt, succeeded in convincing Father Giles and their father of their contrition, yet the demure satisfaction hidden beneath their feigned sorrow betrayed them to the keen feminine insight of the women.

"Their actions, though seemingly innocent, spoke volumes," Catherine reflected internally.

Mistrust and suspicion now dominated the dinner table, serving as grim substitutes for Gerard's vibrant presence, which had once illuminated the circle. The old hosier, wounded by his son's disobedience, maintained a stoic exterior, refusing to utter Gerard's name. Yet, beneath his Spartan facade, Nature pulled relentlessly at his heartstrings. His greatest anxiety was not his pride, but Gerard's safety. "If I but knew where the boy is, and that his life and health are secure, my cares would be few," he would sigh deeply, his voice trembling with unspoken love. Perhaps, if Gerard had simply walked through the door at that moment, the old man would have embraced him with tears, offering forgiveness rather than reproach.

Publicity, however, proved to be the most surprising development. Within a week of Gerard's departure, his exploits became the talk of every street corner. Popular sympathy rallied fiercely behind the lovers, condemning Gerard's "cruel" parents and the meddling burgomaster who dared interfere in matters that concerned no one.

"Mother," Kate whispered, "everyone knows Margaret is down with a severe fever. Her father is terrified."

"Margaret?" Catherine asked, feigning calm. "Which Margaret?"

"Oh, Mother!" Kate exclaimed. "Gerard's Margaret."

"Gerard's Margaret!" Catherine shrieked, the color draining from her face. "How dare you utter that name in this house? That hussy has ruined everything. She is the cause of my boy's exile, preventing him from becoming a priest among us. She has left me a desolate, broken-hearted widow. Do not weep, my child; I speak harshly because I bear this grief alone. At night, I dream of him, plagued by horrors—wild beasts tearing at him, robbers raising their cruel knives to strike his pale face. If I recall correctly, while I sit here in relative comfort, perhaps he lies dead in some savage land, all because of that girl. Her name is poison to me; I tremble whenever I hear it."

"I shall say nothing to hurt you further, Mother," Kate murmured tenderly, though a sigh escaped her lips.

Outside, the world treated Margaret with pity. Young men and maids exchanged sympathetic glances as they passed her window, whispering of the "beauty of the village dying for love." They underestimated her unyielding spirit and selflessness. Gerard lived, and she trusted implicitly in his constancy. Her father was helpless, and her love for him compelled her to fulfill her duties despite her frailty, though she performed them with a bowed head and a heavy heart. Physical exhaustion had triggered a violent fever, leaving her utterly debilitated. Though the illness eventually receded, it left her in a terrifying state of weakness, where life felt like a burdensome weight.

In moments of such fragility, love and friendship become angelic presences, bringing comfort through gentle voices and healing touches. For many days, Margaret lay alone, the hours rolling over her like leaden waves. Existence seemed a futile struggle, a shadow of what it had been. Lacking Gerard, she had no reason to strive for recovery. She would lie motionless for hours, tears streaming silently down her cheeks.

One morning, awakening from a restless sleep, she found two visitors in her chamber. One was a servant; the other, distinguished by thick fur collars and sleeves, was clearly a woman of status. A narrow strand of silvery hair escaping her coiffure revealed she was well past the age when sensible women hid their years. Their expressions were kind and warm. Margaret attempted to rise, but the older lady gently pressed a hand to stop her.

"Lie still, sweetheart. We have not come to tire you, but to comfort you, God willing. Cheer up, and tell us, who do you think we are?"

"Nay, madam," Margaret replied, her voice weak. "Though I have never met you, you are the Demoiselle Van Eyck, and this is Reicht Heynes. Gerard spoke often of you and your kindness. Madam, he has no friend like you near him now." As the thought crossed her mind, she sank back into the pillows, fresh tears filling her eyes.

The kindly Reicht Heynes began to weep for company, but her mistress chided her softly. "You are a charming companion for a sickroom," she remarked, employing innocent wit to lift the patient's spirits. With remarkable skill, the elderly woman, who had witnessed life's full spectrum of trials, soothed and engaged Margaret. Within an hour, Margaret rested her head on the stranger's shoulder, her eyes reflecting gentle gratitude.

"Alice!" the old lady exclaimed, tracing the strands of hair. "Look at this, Reicht!"

Reicht approached, admiring the locks sincerely. The poor girl, hardly able to endure flattery, blushed. "Truly, madam, I used to find it hideous. But he praised it, and ever since, I have been almost vain of it, saints forgive me. You know how foolish those who love become."

"They are greater fools than those who do not," the old lady countered sharply.

Margaret lifted her eyes, searching for the meaning behind the words. This visit marked the beginning of a series of daily calls from Margaret or Reicht, which continued until the patient fully recovered. Under their tender care, Margaret improved rapidly. Reicht credited the progress to nutritious dishes prepared in Peter's kitchen, but Margaret herself recognized the deeper healing power of the kind words and encouraging gazes that reminded her she had friends to live for.

Elsewhere, Martin Wittenhaagen marched directly to Rotterdam, intent on confronting the issue head-on. His adversary was a bipedal figure crowned with royal horns: Philip the Good, Duke of Burgundy. Finding the court currently seated at Ghent, Martin sought an audience but was initially dismissed by pages and lackeys. Undeterred, he intercepted the Duke while hunting, boldly breaking court protocol to initiate a conversation by loudly begging for mercy.

"Why, where is the peril, man?" the Duke laughed, surveying his surroundings.

"For mercy from an old soldier hunted down by burghers!" Martin cried.

Kings, like common folk, vary in temperament, yet they share a tendency to be affable toward those of low station. These subjects pose no threat of rivalry, amusing the monarchs with their bluntness and novelty, refreshing the court with a touch of raw nature rarely seen in palaces.
