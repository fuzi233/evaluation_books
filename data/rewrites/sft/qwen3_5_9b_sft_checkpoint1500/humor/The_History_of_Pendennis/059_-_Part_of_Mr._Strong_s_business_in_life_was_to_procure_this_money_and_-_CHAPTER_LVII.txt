Major Pendennis kept his word to Warrington as well as his conscience allowed, successfully convincing his poor wife Helen that the connection between her son and the detestable little gatekeeper was severed forever. Helen, in turn, could lay aside her fears of an imprudent attachment or a humiliating match for Pen. Young Arthur, having shaken off the initial blow to his vanity, breathed easier knowing that Miss Fanny was not destined to die of love for him and that no dire consequences awaited their brief, unfortunate liaison.

With these anxieties dispelled, the entire party was free to execute their planned journey to the Continent. Arthur Pendennis, a *rentier* and *voyageant*, traveled alongside Madame Pendennis and Mademoiselle Bell, accompanied by George Warrington, a *particulier* of thirty-two years, six feet tall, of ordinary features, black hair, and a black beard. They secured passports from the Consul of His Majesty the King of the Belgians at Dover and crossed the channel to Ostend. From there, they leisurely toured Bruges and Ghent on their way to Brussels and eventually the Rhine.

It is not the purpose of this narrative to chronicle this well-trodden circuit, nor to detail Laura's delight in the tranquil, ancient cities she encountered for the first time, nor Helen's wonder at the Beguine convents. Yet, the sight of black-veiled nuns kneeling with outstretched arms before illuminated altars filled her with almost terror. She observed strange pomp and ceremony in Catholic worship, barefoot friars roaming the streets, crowned images of saints and virgins before which crowds bowed in open defiance, as she believed, of the written law. Priests in gorgeous robes lurked in dark confessionals; theaters were open, and people danced on Sundays. These new sights and manners shocked and bewildered the simple country lady.

Whenever the young men returned from their evening drives or walks, they found the books of devotion laid out on the table. Upon entering, Laura would typically pause in reading the Psalms or sacred pages, which Helen above all others cherished. The recent events concerning her son had cruelly shaken her spirit. Laura watched every movement of her dearest friend with intense, hidden anxiety. Poor Pen remained constant and affectionate in his attentions to his mother, whose wounded bosom still yearned for him. Yet, a secret barrier existed between them, accompanied by an anguish bordering on rage on Helen's part. She felt dispossessed of her son's heart, sensing recesses within it that she neither dared nor could enter. She longed for the sacred days of boyhood when Arthur's heart held no secrets, when she was his all in all, when he poured his hopes, pleasures, childish griefs, vanities, and triumphs into her willing and tender embrace. Now, however, fate, selfishness, and nature had driven him forth on wayward wings to range his own flight, sing his own song, and seek his own home and mate.

Watching this devouring care and racking disappointment in her friend, Laura once remarked to Helen, "If Pen had loved me as you wished, I should have gained him, but I should have lost you, Mamma; I know I should. And I like you to love me best. Men do not know what it is to love as we do, I think." Helen, sighing, assented to this portion of the speech, though she protested against the former part. For my part, I suppose Miss Laura was right in both statements, particularly regarding the latter assertion, which is an old and received truism: love is an hour with us, but it is all night and all day with a woman. Damon has taxes, sermons, parades, tailor's bills, parliamentary duties, and the devil knows what else to contend with; Delia has to think about Damon. Damon is the oak—or the post—that stands upright, while Delia is the ivy or honeysuckle whose arms twine about him. Is it not so, Delia? Is it not your nature to creep about his feet and kiss them, to twine round his trunk and hang there, while Damon stands like a British man with his hands in his breeches pockets, tolerating the pretty fond parasite clinging around him?

Old Pendennis had merely accompanied his friends to the water's edge before leaving them aboard the boat, entrusting the chief charge of the little expedition to Warrington. Old Pendennis himself was bound for a brief visit to the residence of a great man and friend. After this sojourn, he proposed joining his sister-in-law at the German watering-place to which the party was bound. The Major felt that his long attentions to his sick family had earned him a little relaxation. Although the best of the partridges had been thinned off, the pheasants remained at Stillbrook, where the noble owner still resided. Old Pendennis betook himself to that hospitable mansion, finding great comfort within. A royal duke, some noted foreigners, illustrious statesmen, and pleasant people visited it. It did the old fellow's heart good to see his name listed in the *Morning Post* amidst the distinguished company entertained by the Marquis of Steyne at his country-house. He proved a very useful and pleasant personage in a country-house, entertaining young men with queer little anecdotes and risqué stories during shooting parties and in the smoking-room, where they laughed at him and with him. He was obsequious with the ladies in the morning rooms, walking the new arrivals about the park and gardens, showing them the *carte du pays*, pointing out the best views of the mansion and the lake, indicating where timber was to be felled, tracing the old roads before bridges were built, and recounting where old Lord Lynx discovered Sir Phelim O'Neal on his knees before her ladyship. He called lodge-keepers and gardeners by name, knew exactly how many domestics sat in the housekeeper's room and how many dined in the servants'-hall. He had a word for everybody, about everybody, and a little against everybody. Invaluable in a country-house, he richly merited and enjoyed his vacation after his labors. Perhaps, while thus deservedly enjoying himself with country friends, the Major was not displeased at transferring the command of the family expedition to Warrington, thereby keeping him in the service of the ladies—a servitude George was only too willing to undergo for his friend's sake and for the sake of a society he found increasingly delightful.

Warrington, a proficient German scholar, willingly gave Miss Laura lessons in the language, who was eager to improve, whereas Pen, for his part, was too weak or lazy to resume his studies. Warrington acted as courier and interpreter, managing baggage, money matters, and putting the little troop into marching order. He located the English church and walked with great decorum alongside Mrs. Pendennis and Miss Laura if they chose to attend. He walked beside Mrs. Pendennis's donkey on evening excursions, arranged carriages for her, procured copies of *Galignani*, and even devised comfortable seats under lime-trees for her when guests paraded after dinner near the Kursaal band, where our tired friends stopped to listen to their pleasant music. Many a fine-whiskered Prussian or French dandy, come to the baths for the game of *Trente-et-quarante*, cast longing glances toward the pretty, fresh-colored English girl who accompanied the pale widow and would have longed to dance the galop or waltz with her. But Laura rarely appeared in the ballroom, save once or twice when Pen condescended to walk with her. As for Warrington, that rough diamond lacked the polish of a dancing-master and did not know how to waltz, though he would have liked to learn had he had such a partner as Laura. Such a partner! Psha, what had a stiff bachelor to do with partners and waltzing? What was he about, dancing attendance here, drinking in sweet pleasure at a risk he knew not of what after-sadness, regret, and lonely longing? Yet, he stayed on. To the unobservant eye, one might say he was the widow's son, watching her with constant care and watchfulness; or perhaps an adventurer seeking to marry her fortune, or at least secure some great treasure or benefit from her. Very likely he did, for ours, as the reader has possibly already discovered, is a Selfish Story, and almost every person, according to his nature and the way of the world, is occupied about Number One. Thus, Warrington selfishly devoted himself to Helen, who selfishly devoted herself to Pen, who in turn selfishly devoted himself to himself at this present period, having no other personage or object to occupy him except his mother's health, which gave him serious and real disquiet. Though they sat together, they spoke little, and a cloud always hung between them.

Every day Laura looked for Warrington and received him with increasing frankness and eager welcome. He found himself talking to her as he did not know he could, performing acts of gallantry that astonished him afterward. He found himself staring blankly into the glass at the crow's-feet around his eyes, at streaks of white in his hair, and at intrusive silver bristles in his grim, blue beard. He found himself looking at the young bucks at the bath—the bland, tight-waisted Germans, the capering Frenchmen with their lacquered mustaches and trimmed varnished boots, the English dandies, including Pen, with their calm, domineering airs and insolent languor.
