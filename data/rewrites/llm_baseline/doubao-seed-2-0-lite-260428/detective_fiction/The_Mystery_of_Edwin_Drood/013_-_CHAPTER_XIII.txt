A soft, anticipatory hush was about to settle over Miss Twinkleton’s select Nuns’ House establishment: the Christmas recess was at hand. The era of casually calling the school break “the half” was long past, even for the erudite Miss Twinkleton, who now grandly referred to the expiring period as “the term,” far more elegant, far more collegiate. For days, discipline had slackened to a delightful, giddy laxity. Bedroom club suppers were held behind locked doors, where a dressed tongue was carved open with a pair of craft scissors and passed around the room using curling tongs as serving utensils. Marmalade was doled out on makeshift plates cut from curling paper, and the tiny squat glass that weakly junior student Rickitts used for her daily iron tonic was repurposed for passing around draughts of sweet cowslip wine. The housemaids had been bribed with frayed ribbons and half-worn shoes not to mention the crumbs stuffed deep in bed linens; the daring Miss Ferdinand even treated the whole party to a rousing solo on a comb wrapped in scrap paper, before two of her schoolmates smothered her under a fluffy pillow mid-verse to quiet her laughter.

These were not the only signs of dispersal. Boxes that stayed locked in storage most of the year appeared in every bedroom, and a wild, dramatic frenzy of packing ensued, entirely disproportionate to the small amount of clothing actually being folded. Leftover cold cream, pomatum, and handfuls of spare hairpins were freely handed out as gifts to the maids. In strict confidence, girls whispered to each other about the handsome “golden youths of England” who promised to call the very second they stepped through their front doors at home. Only Miss Giggles, a girl famously short on sentiment, declared she planned to greet any admirer by pulling faces at them through the window; she was roundly outvoted by an enormous, giddy majority. The night before break, a formal pact was struck that no one would sleep a wink, and that all means possible would be used to summon ghosts to haunt the halls. The compact always broke within an hour: every girl was fast asleep, and all of them were up before dawn the next day.

The closing ceremony was held at noon on departure day. Miss Twinkleton, flanked by the sharp, no-nonsense Mrs. Tisher, hosted a small reception in her parlour, where even the globes in the corner were already draped in brown holland for the winter, and glasses of sweet white wine and plates of sliced pound cake sat arranged on the side table. “Ladies,” Miss Twinkleton began, “another revolving year has brought us round to this festive season, when the first feelings of our nature swell in our—” She always drew up short of saying “bosoms” at this point, every single year, and smoothly corrected herself: “hearts. Our hearts. Hem! Once more, a revolving year, ladies, has brought us to a pause in our studies—studies we can only hope we have advanced greatly in. Like the mariner on his bark, the warrior in his tent, the captive in his dungeon, and the traveller in every kind of conveyance, we all yearn for home. Do we open this day, as Mr. Addison’s famous tragedy does, with ‘The dawn is overcast, the morning lowers, / And heavily in clouds brings on the day, / The great, th’ important day—?’ Not at all! From horizon to zenith all is couleur de rose, all redolent of our dear relations and friends. May we find them thriving as we expect, and may they find us thriving as they expect! Ladies, let us exchange farewells now, and wish each other every happiness until we meet again. And when the day comes to return to those pursuits which—” Here a wave of collective visible gloom settled over the room “—pursuits which, pursuits which—let us always remember what the Spartan General said, in words too well known to repeat, at the battle it would be superfluous to name.”

The house maids in their best starched caps passed around the trays, the girls sipped their wine and crumbled their cake, and hired coaches soon choked the entire street outside. Farewells were swift, and as Miss Twinkleton kissed each girl’s cheek, she pressed a neatly sealed envelope into her hand, addressed to her next of kin with “Miss Twinkleton’s best compliments” scrawled in the corner, presented with an air of a delightful, unexpected surprise, as if the missive could possibly be anything but the term’s bill.

Rosa had watched this annual exodus dozens of times, and had no other home to return to, so she was perfectly content to stay behind at the Nuns’ House, all the more happy to have her new closest friend Helena Landless there with her. But there was a clear, unspoken blank in their friendship that Rosa could not miss: Helena, who knew her brother Neville’s secret regarding Rosa, and who had sworn to Mr. Crisparkle never to speak of the matter, pointedly avoided ever mentioning Edwin Drood’s name. Rosa could not fathom why, but she noticed the pattern clearly. If not for that strange silence, she would have poured out all her tangled doubts and hesitations about Edwin to Helena, but with no outlet for her feelings, she could only puzzle over why the avoidance of Edwin’s name lingered, even though Helena had confirmed the two young men were supposed to reconcile the moment Edwin arrived in Cloisterham.

It would have made a lovely, soft scene: all those pretty girls crowding to kiss Rosa goodbye in the cold Nuns’ House porch, the sunlit little figure peeping out between the pillars, unaware the gargoyle faces carved into the spouts and gables above leered down at her, waving furiously at the departing coaches. The hoarse High Street rang with clear silvery voices calling “Good-bye, Rosebud darling!” and the stone effigy of Sapsea’s father set above the doorway across the street looked for all the world like he was calling out to passersby: “Gentlemen, lend me your attention to this charming little last lot left behind, and bid with a spirit worthy of the occasion!” A few sparkling, youthful ripples of laughter later, the street went quiet again, and Cloisterham settled back into its usual sedate, dull rhythm.

If Rosebud waited for Edwin Drood with an uneasy heart in the frosty little parlour of the Nuns’ House, Edwin was no less uneasy as he walked from the gatehouse towards her. He had far less force of will than the tiny, adored “fairy queen” of Miss Twinkleton’s school, but Mr. Grewgious’s firm lecture in Staple Inn had pricked his lazy, drifting conscience. The old lawyer’s unshakable, clear view of right and wrong in the matter of his engagement to Rosa could not be laughed off or brushed aside. If not for that dinner, and the small engagement ring tucked inside the breast pocket of his coat, he would have drifted all the way to his wedding day without a single real pause for thought, assuming everything would sort itself out if he just left it alone. But the reminder that he owed a duty to both the living and the dead had stopped him cold. He now knew he had to either give the ring to Rosa, or take it back. Once forced into that narrow path, he found he was seeing Rosa’s wants far more unselfishly than he ever had before, and far less sure of his own feelings than he had been all his easy, careless life.

“I’ll follow her lead, see how we get on,” he resolved, as he turned in through the Nuns’ House gate. “Whatever happens, I’ll hold Grewgious’s words in mind, and try to be true to everyone involved.”

Rosa was already dressed for a walk, waiting for him. It was a bright, sharp frosty day, and Miss Twinkleton had graciously given her permission to take fresh air. The pair slipped out together before either Miss Twinkleton or the officious Mrs. Tisher could find a reason to interfere with the rules of proper chaperonage.

“My dear Eddy,” Rosa said, once they turned off the High Street and onto the quiet riverside paths winding around the cathedral, “I need to talk to you about something very serious. I’ve been thinking about it for a very long, long time.”

“I want to be serious with you too, Rosa dear,” Edwin replied. “I mean to be earnest, no more silly jokes.”

“Thank you, Eddy. You won’t think I’m unkind for bringing it up first, will you? You won’t think I’m only speaking for myself? That wouldn’t be generous, and I know you’re generous.”

“I hope I’m never ungenerous to you, Rosa,” he said. He did not call her Pussy anymore. Not ever again.

“And there’s no chance we’ll quarrel over this, right?” Rosa added, slipping her hand through his arm. “We have so much reason to be lenient with each other.”

“Of course we do, Rosa.”

“Then be a dear brave boy, Eddy. Let’s call the engagement off, from this day forward. Let us be brother and sister, nothing more.”

“Never husband and wife?”

“Never.”

Neither spoke for a long stretch of the path, until Edwin said, with visible effort, “Of course I’ve known this has been on both our minds. And I swear to you freely, this idea did not start with you.”

“Nor with you, dear,” Rosa said, her voice soft and earnest, tears pricking her eyes. “It just… drifted into being, between us. You aren’t truly happy with the idea of marrying me, and I’m not truly happy with the idea of marrying you. I’m so sorry, so terribly sorry.”

“I’m deeply sorry too, Rosa. Deeply sorry for you.”

“And I for you, poor boy! And I for you!” That gentle, unresentful, forgiving affection between them softened the whole situation, stripped away any impression they were being silly or capricious or that the whole arrangement was a failure. Their new bond felt more honourable, more selfless, more true than the old forced romantic tie ever had.

“If we both knew, days and days ago, that the old arrangement our parents left us was never going to work,” Rosa said, dabbing at her eyes, “what better thing could we do than end it now? It’s right that we’re sad, but how much better to be sad now than to be stuck miserable together, and angry at each other for the rest of our lives.”

They sat down on the half-ruined stone steps overlooking the river, and Rosa laid out exactly how she saw their relationship: Edwin had liked her, thought she was a nice little thing, gotten used to the idea of marriage to her, accepted it as an inevitable fact of life without ever stopping to question if he loved her properly. She had slowly, gradually started to think the same thing, stuck as she was at the school with no other distractions, until Grewgious had come to visit her and gently but firmly impressed on her how careful they ought to be with their inherited engagement, and she resolved to speak to Edwin the very first chance they got to be alone. Edwin admitted Grewgious had spoken to him too, back in London. His hand drifted to the ring in his breast pocket, then paused: if he was going to give it back to Grewgious, what was the point in upsetting Rosa by showing it to her?

“The girls will be so dreadfully disappointed,” Rosa said, laughing through the last of her tears, dewdrops glistening on her eyelashes. “They’ve all been looking forward to our wedding for ages, poor pets.”

“Ah, but I fear the news will hit Jack worse than anyone,” Edwin said, suddenly jolting at the thought. “I never considered Jack!”

Rosa’s head snapped towards him, her eyes wide and intent, a look that vanished as quickly as a flash of lightning, leaving her confused, her breath coming fast. She looked down at the ground instantly, as if she would do anything to take that reaction back.

“You don’t doubt this will come as a terrible shock to Jack, do you, Rosa?”

“Why should I?” she said evasively, hurrying her words. “I never thought about it. He hardly seems to have anything to do with our engagement.”

“My dear child! How could someone as completely wrapped up in someone else as Jack is in me, Mrs. Tope’s phrasing, not be completely stunned by a full, sudden shift like this in my life?” Edwin said, thinking aloud. “How on earth am I going to break it to him? I’m dining with him tomorrow and the next day, Christmas Eve and Christmas Day, and I can’t ruin his holidays. He fusses over every little trivial thing about me, he’ll be completely upset. Wait—you said your guardian promised to come down if you wrote to ask him? Would you mind if he’s the one to tell Jack? The other trustee, that makes perfect sense. He can lay out all the details gently, far better than we could. I’m not a coward, Rosa, but to tell you the truth, I’m a little afraid of Jack’s reaction. I saw him have one of his fits once, this wild paroxysm, and I’m worried a shock like this, coming straight from me, might trigger it.”

“Edwin, you are not afraid of him!” Rosa cried, her face going white, her hands clasped tight.

“Easy there, sister Rosa, what do you see from that turret of yours?” Edwin laughed, steadying her. “I’m not literally afraid of the dear old fellow, I just don’t want to catch him off guard. Grewgious is so steady, he’ll talk Jack’s thoughts back into order in no time.”

Rosa calmed, clearly relieved that Grewgious would be the one to break the news to Jasper, acting as a buffer between the man who was her music master and this awkward, sharp secret. Edwin’s hand closed around the little ring case again, and once more he decided to say nothing of it. The beautiful stones were nothing more than a memento of broken, childish hopes, a cruel little reminder that all human plans were brittle dust. He would give it back to Grewgious when he arrived, and it would go back into its old dark cabinet, alongside old letters and abandoned vows, until the day it might one day resurface and repeat its sad old round.

The frosty day faded as they walked along the river, planning their new separate paths: Edwin would head off to his engineering work in Egypt earlier than planned, Rosa would stay on at the Nuns’ House at least as long as Helena remained. They agreed everyone would be told that they remained the very best of friends, no hard feelings at all. All the while, both held back small secrets of their own: Rosa planned to ask Grewgious to arrange for her to stop taking music lessons from Jasper immediately, and Edwin found himself idly, curiously wondering if he would ever get to know Neville Landless’s sister Helena better.

The sun dipped low and painted the old stone cathedral walls crimson as they made their way back towards the Nuns’ House. Dark seaweed washed up at their feet at the river’s edge, and rooks circled overhead, their hoarse cries like dark splashes in the fading dusk.

“I’ll tell Jack I’m leaving the country soon, once Grewgious has had the talk with him,” Edwin said quietly. “I’ll be gone before they meet, it will be better that way, don’t you think?”

“Yes.”

“We know we did the right thing, Rosa?”

“Yes.”

“We know we’re better off this way, even now?”

“And we will be far, far better off, much happier, later on.”

The old tender affection for the bond they were letting go lingered between them, so they slowed their steps, prolonging their final parting. When they reached the stand of elms near the cathedral where they had sat together on Edwin’s last visit, they stopped by unspoken consent. Rosa lifted her face to his, something she had never done when they were formally engaged.

“God bless you, dear! Good-bye!”

“God bless you, dear! Good-bye!”

They kissed each other, warm and fervent.

“Now take me home, Eddy,” Rosa said. “I want to go and be by myself for a little while.”

“Don’t look back, Rosa,” Edwin cautioned her, as he tucked her arm through his. “Didn’t you see Jack? He was standing under the trees. He watched us say goodbye. Poor fellow, he has no idea we’ve called the whole thing off. This is going to hurt him so badly.”

Rosa hurried her pace, not stopping once until they passed under the gatehouse arch and back onto the main street. “Is he following us? Can you look without making it obvious? Is he behind?”

“No. Wait—yes, he is,” Edwin said. “He just stepped out under the gateway. The dear, overprotective old fellow just wants to make sure you get in safe. I’m afraid he’s going to be bitterly disappointed when he finds out.”

Rosa grabbed the rusty old bell handle and pulled hard, the hoarse clanging echoing inside the Nuns’ House. Before the door swung open to let her in, she turned and gave Edwin one last, wide, wondering look, as if she was screaming at him to understand, to see the danger he was blind to, before she vanished inside and the heavy oak door clicked shut between them.
