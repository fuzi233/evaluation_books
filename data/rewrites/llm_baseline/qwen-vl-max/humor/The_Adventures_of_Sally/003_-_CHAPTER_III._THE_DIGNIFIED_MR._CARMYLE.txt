By six o'clock the next evening, Sally had resigned herself to the fact that Ginger Kemp would have to navigate life on his own—without the sage advice she’d been itching to deliver. She hadn’t seen him all day, and in just over an hour, she’d be boarding the 7:15 express from Roville to Paris, then on to Cherbourg and her transatlantic liner bound for New York. Still, a flicker of hope remained. At half past six, after depositing her luggage with a porter who seemed genuinely fond of her (and possibly of her suitcase), she made one last dash to the Casino Municipale.

She couldn’t bear the thought of leaving without giving Ginger a little nudge in the right direction. Like many bright, energetic girls, Sally had a talent for meddling—what her brother Fillmore called “interfering with other people’s lives.” And Ginger? He was clearly someone who needed a push. So when she walked into the casino and spotted a fiery red head glowing above the crowd near a roulette table, her heart leapt.

Roville-sur-Mer had two casinos. The one on the Promenade catered to sunbathers and casual boule players. But the real action—the kind that drew serious gamblers with serious intentions—was at the grand Casino Municipale, tucked near the railway station. And it was clear as soon as Sally entered that Ginger wasn’t just playing; he was *winning*.

He sat like a king atop a mountain of square counters, looking every inch the triumphant tycoon. As Sally watched, a wooden-faced croupier slid another pile across the table with a theatrical rake. “Epatant!” murmured a man beside her, elbowing her ribs in excitement. Sally didn’t speak French, but she got the gist: *Wow*. The whole room seemed to agree. There’s something oddly communal about watching someone else win at roulette—like a shared spiritual high. The croupier twisted his moustache with one hand and spun the wheel with the other. Silence fell.

Sally shifted to a less crowded spot and finally saw Ginger’s face—and burst out laughing.

He looked exactly like a terrier peering down a rat hole. His hair stood on end, his eyes were wide, and his entire posture screamed, “I’m about to become rich!”

Her laugh cut through the hush like a foghorn. In the quiet reverence of a lucky streak, it was akin to shouting during a funeral—or, more accurately, a revival meeting. The crowd turned, horrified. Even the croupier paused mid-spin. Ginger, startled, jumped up—and just then, the ball clattered into a red slot.

The crowd groaned. A collective shudder passed through them like a wave of grief. Poor Ginger. He’d picked the wrong color. And everyone blamed Sally.

Ginger, however, seemed unbothered. He gathered his winnings, stuffed them in his pocket, and pushed through the crowd toward her. By now, she was officially the casino’s least favorite person.

“I say,” he said, pulling her aside with surprising strength, “this is brilliant—running into you like this. I’ve been searching everywhere.”

“You didn’t find me?” she said, amused. “That’s because I’ve been looking for *you*.”

“No really?” He grinned, relieved. He led her to a quiet anteroom outside the gambling hall—a cozy corner where only a uniformed attendant stood guard, looking mildly bored but deeply intrigued by the drama unfolding.

“That was very kind of you,” Ginger said, breathless.

“I wanted to talk before my train left.”

“Your train?” he asked, suddenly alarmed. “What do you mean?”

“The puff-puff,” she explained cheerfully. “Leaving tonight.”

“Leaving?” He looked as if she’d announced the end of the world. “You’re not going away from Roville?”

“I’m afraid so.”

“But why? Where are you going?”

“Back to America. My boat leaves Cherbourg tomorrow.”

“Oh, my aunt!”

“I’m sorry,” she said, touched by his distress. She liked being appreciated. “But…”

“I say…” Ginger went bright red and stared hard at the attendant, who was now humming a love song under his breath. “Look here—will you marry me?”

Sally blinked.

She’d known Ginger was unpredictable. But *this*?

“Marry me?” she echoed.

“You know what I mean.”

“Yes, I suppose I do. You’re referring to matrimony. Yes, I understand.”

“So—how about it?”

She composed herself. Her sense of humor kicked in. She studied him carefully. He wouldn’t meet her gaze, instead fixating on the official, who had now begun swaying gently, lost in romantic fantasy.

“Isn’t this… well, slightly premature?” she asked.

“It has to be,” Ginger said, exasperated. “I thought you were staying weeks.”

“But we’re practically strangers,” she said, patting his hand. The attendant sighed dramatically.

“I see what’s happened,” she continued. “You’ve mistaken me for someone else—someone you actually know. Take a good look at me.”

“If I take a good look at you,” Ginger muttered, “I can’t guarantee the outcome.”

“And this is the man I was going to lecture on ‘Enterprise’.”

“You’re the most incredible girl I’ve ever met,” he declared, still staring at the attendant. “I don’t care how sudden it is. I fell in love the moment I saw you. That’s it.”

“But—”

“Now listen,” he said, shifting gears. “I know I’m not much. But I just won a fortune. We could start fresh. I’ve messed up everything I’ve tried, sure—but there must be something I can do. With you around to encourage me, I’d give it my best shot. I mean—”

“Have you considered I might already be engaged?”

“Oh, golly!” He finally turned to face her. And in that moment, something in his eyes—pure, unguarded sincerity—struck her. It wasn’t a joke. He was serious.

“Well,” she said slowly, “as a matter of fact, I am.”

Ginger bit his lip. Silence.

“Oh, well,” he said finally. “That’s that.”

Sally felt a strange mix of pity and amusement. It was maternal, almost. Like watching a child plead for a toy they can’t possibly afford. She wanted to hug him. She couldn’t, though. Not because he was a grown man, but because he didn’t feel like one.

“You don’t really mean it,” she said gently.

“Don’t I?” he whispered. “Oh, don’t I?”

“You can’t,” she insisted. “Love at first sight doesn’t happen in real life. Love comes when you know someone—when you’ve spent time together—”

She stopped.

Because she realized: *she* hadn’t known Gerald Foster for long either. They’d met, fallen in love, and gotten engaged in less than two weeks. What did she know about him except that she loved him?

Her argument crumbled. “It’s ridiculous,” she said weakly.

Ginger slumped into melancholy. “I knew you wouldn’t care,” he said quietly. “I’m not much of a chap.”

And there it was—the perfect opening.

“That’s exactly what I wanted to talk about,” she said, seizing the moment. “I’ve been trying to find you all day to finish what I started in the elevator last night. Mind if I talk to you like an aunt? Or maybe a sister? Honestly, the best plan would be for you to adopt me as an honorary sister. What do you think?”

Ginger didn’t seem thrilled by the idea.

“Because I *do* care about you,” she added.

He perked up. “That’s very kind.”

“I’m going to offer some wisdom,” she said, leaning forward. “Ginger, why don’t you *brace up*?”

“Brace up?”

“Yes! Stiffen your spine, stick out your chin, square your elbows. Stop flopping around like a wet rag and doing nothing while you wait for ‘the family’ to rescue you. Why do you need jobs handed to you? Why don’t you go out and grab one? Why worry about what they think? Why not make yourself independent? Sure, you lost your money. But so has everyone who’s ever done anything worthwhile. It’s part of the game. You’re not a puppy to be picked up and dropped wherever they please. A job is something you choose and fight for. You’ve got money now—use it to look around. Then *do something*. Try to remember you’re alive. And try to forget the family exists.”

She paused, catching her breath.

Ginger stared. “When you talk fast,” he said slowly, “your nose gets all wiggly. Looks great.”

Sally gasped. “You weren’t listening at all!”

“Oh, yes I was!” he protested. “I heard every word.”

“What did I say?”

“You told me to get a job.”

“Well, yes. Though I phrased it better.”

“Right,” he said, nodding solemnly. “I’ll do it.”

He looked at her with such earnest devotion that she nearly melted.

“I wish you’d let me write to you,” he said. “Letters. I think it would help.”

“You won’t have time.”

“I’ll make time. Do you have an address in America? Somewhere I can send letters?”

She gave him Mrs. Meecher’s boarding house address. He wrote it on his cuff with trembling fingers.

“Fine,” she said. “Do write. I’ll want to know how you’re doing.”

“Oh, my goodness!” she suddenly shrieked. “That clock—it’s wrong!”

“What time does your train leave?”

“Leave? It’s *gone*! Or leaving in two seconds!”

She bolted for the swing door, knocking the uniformed attendant sideways. “Goodbye, Ginger! Write to me! Remember what I said!”

Ginger, surprisingly agile, followed her out. They sprinted across the square. “Stick it!” he yelled encouragingly.

They reached the platform just as the train began to move. Ginger lunged for the nearest door, wrenched it open, scooped Sally into his arms, and hurled her inside.

She landed squarely on the toes of a man in the corner seat. Bouncing off, she rushed to the window.

Ginger ran alongside, keeping pace. “Ginger! My poor porter! Tip him! I forgot!”

“Right ho!”

“And don’t forget what I said!”

“Right ho!”

“Look after yourself—and *Death to the Family!*”

“Right ho!”

The train pulled away. Sally turned back one last time. Ginger had stopped, waving a handkerchief.

Then she turned to apologize to the man whose toes she’d crushed.

“I’m so sorry,” she panted. “I hope I didn’t hurt you.”

She found herself face-to-face with Bruce Carmyle—Ginger’s cousin, the dark, grim man from the beach.

Mr. Carmyle, usually impervious to surprises, felt momentarily stunned. This was the French girl from yesterday—the one who’d caught Lancelot’s eye. Except now she was speaking fluent English. How? And how had she gone from total stranger to sprinting with his cousin down station platforms in less than twenty-four hours?

And why did she call him “Ginger”?

Bruce Carmyle disapproved of familiarity. But since the girl was undeniably attractive—even flushed and panting—his disapproval softened into a polite smile.

“Not at all,” he said, though his big toe throbbed like a drum.

“If you don’t mind,” Sally said, sitting down, “I think I’ll breathe.”

She breathed.

The train rolled on.

“Quite a close call,” Carmyle said amiably.

“Yes. Lucky Mr. Kemp was there. He throws straight, doesn’t he?”

“Tell me,” he said, “how do you know my cousin?”

“We didn’t know each other yesterday,” she said. “We stayed at the same hotel. We got stuck in an elevator for hours. That’s when we really got to know each other.”

A waiter entered, announcing dinner in surprisingly good English.

“Would you like dinner?” Carmyle asked.

“I’m starving,” she replied.

As they walked to the dining car, Sally mentally apologized to herself for judging people by their appearance. This man, despite his stern look, was actually quite pleasant.

But once seated, Carmyle transformed. He became rigid, severe. He frowned at a stain on the tablecloth, then scowled at the menu. Sally, meanwhile, charmed the waiter—who seemed instantly smitten—into treating her like a daughter.

Carmyle, irritated, snapped his order in clipped Anglo-French.

“Boum!” the waiter exclaimed proudly, vanishing.

“Nice old man,” Sally said.

“Infernally familiar,” Carmyle muttered.

Sally decided discussion about waiters was futile. She changed the subject.

“By the way,” she said, “my name is Nicholas. Always good to start with names, isn’t it?”

“Mine is—”

“Oh, I know yours. Ginger told me.”

Carmyle stiffened.

“Indeed?” he said coldly. “So you’ve become quite intimate.”

“Why ‘apparently’?” she countered. “I told you we got intimate. You can’t spend half the night trapped in an elevator with someone and *not* get to know them. I found Mr. Kemp very pleasant.”

“Really?”

“And very interesting.”

Carmyle raised an eyebrow. “Interesting?”

“I did call him interesting.”

She was warming to the battle.

“He told me all about himself.”

“And you found that entertaining?”

“Why not?”

“Well…” A faint, icy smile touched his lips. “My cousin has many qualities—he played football well, and he’s a decent amateur boxer—but I wouldn’t call him entertaining. We find him rather dull.”

“I thought only royalty used ‘we.’”

“I meant myself—and the rest of the family.”

“Ah,” Sally said, voice dropping. “The family.”

She couldn’t stop herself from thinking rude things.

“Mr. Kemp told me about Mr. Scrymgeour,” she said.

Carmyle stared at the loaf of bread.

“Indeed?” he said. “He has a charming lack of discretion.”

The waiter returned with soup. “V’la!” he announced, grinning.

Sally’s expression froze. She’d been snubbed.

“I think Mr. Kemp had bad luck,” she said. “If you don’t mind, I’d prefer not to discuss it.”

“Of course,” Carmyle said, regaining his composure. “He was perfectly justified. Mr. Scrymgeour was beating a dog.”

“I’ve heard the details.”

“Oh? Then you must agree with me?”

“I do not. A man who gives up a good position just because—”

“Oh, well, if that’s your view, I suppose we can’t talk.”

“Quite.”

“Still,” she said, “what do you plan to do about Gin—about Mr. Kemp?”

Carmyle grew colder.

“I’m afraid I cannot discuss—”

“Oh, for goodness’ sake!” Sally snapped. “Try to be human! Don’t keep snubbing people. You remind me of those eighteenth-century portraits—wooden faces, gold frames, fishy eyes—like I’m some sort of regrettable incident.”

“Rosbif,” the waiter said cheerfully, reappearing.

Carmyle attacked his beef with grim determination.

Sally sat in silence, bristling with righteous anger.

“I’m sorry,” Carmyle said gravely. “If my eyes are fishy, I hadn’t realized.”

“I suppose you never had sisters,” she said. “They’d have told you.”

He went silent.

After coffee, she stood up.

“I think I’ll go,” she said. “I don’t want any coffee, and if I stay, I might say something rude. I thought I could help Mr. Kemp. But clearly, I can’t. Goodbye, Mr. Carmyle. Thank you for dinner.”

She walked away, leaving him staring after her—indignant, yet strangely captivated.

And deep inside, something unexpected stirred in Bruce Carmyle’s heart.
