Had your father, young folks, possessed even the commonest measure of prudence, this chapter would never have been written—nor would you yourselves have ever come into being to plague him in a hundred ways: to shout and laugh in the corridors when he seeks quiet at his books; to wake him after dinner, as any healthy country gentleman should be allowed to doze; to misplace his spectacles, steal his newspaper when he wants to read it, and burden him with tailors’ bills, mantua-makers’ bills, tutors’ fines—all of which you now inflict upon him with such cheerful regularity. You break his peace at night when you fall ill, though he would sleep undisturbed if only your silly mother could keep still for half an hour. And when Joan cannot sleep, what use is there in Darby putting on his nightcap? Every little ailment one of you has suffered has terrified her so that I confess I’ve never known true tranquility. Were I not the most patient creature alive, I’d have wished to be rid of you all.

Now, when you’ve grown past childhood, long petticoats, chicken-pox, smallpox, whooping-cough, scarlet fever, and those other delightful afflictions of youth, what must that stubborn woman propose but to turn the south rooms into a nursery for possible grandchildren, and set up Captain Miles with a wife—marry him early, just as we did! She says he’s too fond of Brookes’s and Goosetree’s when he’s in London. With perverse wisdom, she hints that while an entrée to Carlton House may be pleasant, it’s perilous for a young man. She wants Miles to live away from temptation, sow his wild oats, and marry—just as we did. Marry! My dear, we had no business to marry at all! By every rule of reason and duty, I ought to have broken off my engagement with Miss Theo—she would have married someone else—and chosen a wealthy wife instead. Your Uncle John was a parson and couldn’t fight; poor Charley was still at school; and your grandfather was too old to challenge me with sword or pistol. I say again: never was a more foolish match than ours. Our relatives were perfectly right to be angry with us. What are relations for, after all, but to find fault and quarrel?

When Hester marries, Master George, remember to argue with her if she does not take a husband of your choosing. When George secures his living—after becoming senior wrangler and fellow of his college—Miss Hester, you must lift your nose disdainfully at any young lady he fancies. As for you, my little Theo, I cannot bear to part with you. You must stay with your old father; he likes you to play Haydn for him, and peel his walnuts after dinner.

[On the blank leaf opposite this paragraph is written, in a large, girlish hand: “I never intend to go.--THEODOSIA.” “Nor I.--HESTER.” They both married, as I see by the note in the family Bible—Miss Theodosia Warrington to Joseph Clinton, son of the Rev. Joseph Blake, later Master of Rodwell Regis Grammar School; and Miss Hester Mary, in 1804, to Captain F. Handyman, R.N.—ED.]

While they enjoyed the blessing (so they thought!) of meeting daily, billing and cooing like lovers in a dream, your parents lived in a fool’s paradise, heedless of the world around them and its gossip and interference. Rinaldo was as brave a warrior as ever slew a Turk—but you know he loved lingering in Armida’s garden. Pray, my Lady Armida, what did you mean by casting your spells over me in youth, so that neither glory nor fashion, nor gaming tables, nor the society of witty men whom I met, could keep me long from your apron-strings, or out of reach of your gentle, simple talk? Pray, my dear, what did we say to each other during those endless hours together? I never slept after dinner then. Which of us was wittier? Was it I—or you? And how came our conversations to be so delightfully sweet? I remember that year I didn’t even care to go see Lord Ferrers tried and hanged, though all London was running after him. The King of Prussia’s capital had fallen; had the Austrians and Russians encamped around the Tower, there could scarcely have been greater stir in London. Yet Miss Theo and her young gentleman felt no real emotion—no pity, no indignation. What mattered to us the fate of Leipzig or Berlin? The truth is, that dear old house in Dean Street was an enchanted garden of joy. I’ve been idle since, but never so happy.

Shall we order the postchaise, my dear, leave the children to run the household, and drive up to London to see if the old lodgings are still available? And you can sit in your old window seat, waving a little handkerchief as I walk down the street. Say our actions were imprudent. Would we not do it all over again? My good friends, if Venus herself had walked into the room and challenged the apple, I was so infatuated I would have given it to your mother. And had she had the choice, she would have preferred her humble servant in a threadbare coat to my Lord Clive with all his diamonds.

Once, indeed, and for a brief time that year, I had thoughts of taking to the highway to be caught and hanged like Lord Ferrers; or joining the King of Prussia and asking some of his enemies to end my life; or enlisting for India, where I might perform some desperate feat that would end in death. Ah me! That was truly a dreadful time. Your mother dares not speak of it now, save in a whisper of fear; nor think of it—it caused her such cruel pain. She remained unhappy for years afterward on the anniversary of that day, until one of you was born on it. Suppose we had been parted—what would have become of us? What would my life have been without her? When I think of that possibility, the whole world becomes a blank.

I do not say we would be parted now. God has granted us thirty years of union. We’ve reached autumn. Our successors are ready. And whichever of us departs first knows the other will follow soon. But we *were* parted in our youth—and I shudder to think what might have happened, had not a dearest friend brought us back together.

Unknown to myself, and perhaps meaning only my good, my relatives in England wrote to Madam Esmond in Virginia, describing what they called the folly of my engagement. Every one of them sang the same tune. I saw the letters later—burned them all years after when my mother showed them to me in Virginia. Aunt Bernstein was quick to advise. A young man with no striking looks, no family, no fortune—was ever such an imprudent connection? And should it not be broken off for dear George’s sake? She had several suitable matches in mind for me. With my name and prospects, it was a shame I should throw myself away on this girl. Her sister ought to intervene—etc., etc.

My Lady Warrington also wrote—her letter adorned with scripture texts. She wrapped her worldly concerns in religious garb. She pointed out how I was living among actors and such people—whom she wouldn’t say were entirely without religion (Heaven forbid!), but who were deplorably worldly. She refrained from saying an artful woman had ensnared me for her daughter, having failed to capture my younger brother. She said nothing harmful about the young woman I had chosen, but insisted Miss L. had no fortune, and her parents might naturally wish to avoid a scandal. She had taken counsel, sought guidance, and felt bound to act. Sir Miles, a man of excellent judgment in worldly matters (though he knew and sought a higher), fully agreed with her and urged her to write, to beg her sister to interfere before the marriage took place.

And who else but the new Countess of Castlewood must insert her finger into the pie? She wrote a majestic letter to Madam Esmond, declaring that Providence had placed her at the head of the Esmond family, and thus it was her duty to warn her kinswoman to break off this marriage. I believe the three women conferred beforehand, sending packet after packet of warnings across the Atlantic.

One raw April morning, as Corydon went to pay his usual visit to Phillis, he found not his beloved with her usual smile, but Mrs. Lambert, eyes red with tears, and the General as pale as death. “Read this, George Warrington!” he said, as his wife buried her face in her hands, placing a letter before me—whose handwriting I instantly recognized. I can still hear the sobs of dear Aunt Lambert. To this day, the sound of fire irons stirring a fire above gives me a chill. I heard such noises that day in the girls’ room, where the sisters sat together. Poor, gentle child! Poor Theo!

“What can I do now, George, my poor boy?” asked the General, pacing the room with desperation etched on his face.

I did not finish reading Madam Esmond’s letter—faintness seized me. But I fear I could recite parts of it now by heart. Its tone was polite, its words measured, though they implied that Mr. and Mrs. Lambert had tricked me into the marriage; that they knew such a union was beneath me; that (as Madam E. understood) they had desired a similar match for her younger son—a plan abandoned, perhaps wisely, when it became clear Mr. Henry Warrington was not the heir to the Virginian estate. If Mr. Lambert was a man of honor, as he was reputed, Madam Esmond doubted he would persist after her representations. She would not command her son, whose temperament she knew well—but for the sake of Miss Lambert’s reputation and peace, she urged the engagement be dissolved by her family, not by Rachel Esmond Warrington of Virginia refusing to marry.

“God help us, George,” the General said, “and give us strength to bear this grief and these charges your mother has laid upon us. They are harsh, but they matter less now. What matters most is sparing my poor girl as much sorrow as we can. I know you love her deeply—you’ll help me and her mother make this blow as light as possible on that tender heart. Since she was born, she’s never hurt a soul—and it’s cruel that she should suffer.”

As he spoke, he passed a hand over his dry eyes.

“It was my fault, Martin! It was my fault!” wept the poor mother.

“Your mother gave us her word,” said the father. “And do you think I will withdraw mine?”

“I will not!” I cried, swearing a thousand frantic oaths—vows they already knew—that I was bound to Theo before Heaven, and nothing could tear me from her.

“She will demand the separation herself,” said the General. “She is a good girl, God help me! And dutiful. She won’t want her parents accused of scheming, or treated with scorn. Your mother may not have known what she was doing—but it’s done. You may see the child, and she will tell you as much. Is Theo dressed, Molly? I brought the letter home from my office last night after you left. The women had a terrible night. She knew at once by my face that news had come from America. She read the letter calmly. Said she’d like to see you and say farewell. Of course, George, you’ll give me your word of honor not to try and see her again. As soon as my duties allow, we’ll leave this place—but mother and I think we’re best together. Perhaps you should go. But give me your word, at least, that you’ll not seek her out. We must spare her pain, sir! We must spare her pain!”

The good man sank into a deep anguish, and I, though not yet overwhelmed by my own grief, felt his pain, and pitied him.

Could it be that the lips I had kissed yesterday were to speak to me only once more? We were all here—together, loving each other, sitting in the room where we met every day. My drawing lay on the table beside her little workbox. She was upstairs. She would come down soon. Who opens the door?

I see her sweet face. It looked like little Mary’s when we feared she would die of fever. Even a smile played upon her lips. She comes forward and kisses me.

“Good-bye, dear George,” she says.

Great Heaven! An old man sits in this room—my wife’s workbox across from me, and she but five minutes away. My eyes grow dim and full—I cannot see the book before me. I am twenty-three again. I relive every stage of that agony. Once, I suffered it sitting in my own postchaise, with my wife actually beside me. Who dared defile her pure love with suspicion? Who had the right to stab such a soft heart? Don’t you see my ladies sharpening their knives, and the poor child baring hers?

My wife enters. She has been serving tea or tobacco to some of her pensioners.

“What makes you look so angry, papa?” she asks.

“My love,” I say, “it is the thirteenth of April.”

A pang crosses her face, followed by a tender smile. She has endured the martyrdom—and in the midst of pain, a halo of forgiveness appears.

I cannot forgive—not until the days of dotage come, and I cease remembering anything.

“Hal will be home for Easter,” she says. “He’ll bring two or three friends from Cambridge.” And immediately she begins planning how to entertain the boys. When is she ever occupied, but with plans to make others happy?

A gentleman in spectacles, seated before an old ledger, writing down pitiful memories of his own life, is a curious and absurd sight. My corns ache, I know—but I suspect my neighbor’s shoes pinch him too. I will not howl over my own grief, nor dwell long on this one. Many another man, I dare say, has had the light of his life suddenly extinguished, joy snuffed out, and left to darkness and vague torment. I tried to read Howell’s Letters at that time—and when I reached the part about Prince Charles in Spain, the whole tragedy rose again before me. I went to Brighthelmstone, stayed at the inn, had a room facing east, watched the sun rise many mornings after nights of wakefulness, and smoked my pipe of Virginia in his face. When I’m there by chance and see the sun rising now, I shake my fist at him, thinking: O orient Phoebus, what terrible grief and savage wrath have you not seen me endure!

Though my wife is mine for so long, I remain angry—just the same. Who dared, I ask, make us suffer so?

I was forbidden to see her. I kept my promise and stayed away—from that dreadful meeting and parting. But at night, I would go and watch her window, see the lamp burning there. I’d go to the Chartreux (where I knew another boy), call for her brother, and feed him with cakes and half-crowns. I’d invite her elder brother to dine, almost kiss him when he left. I used to breakfast at a coffeehouse in Whitehall just to see Lambert go to his office; we’d salute each other sadly, pass without speaking.

Why did not the women come out? They never did. They were working on her, persuading her to forget me. Oh, the weary, weary days! Oh, the maddening time!

At last, a doctor’s carriage began appearing daily before the General’s house. Was she ill? I confess I was almost glad she was. My own suffering was so unbearable I greedily wanted her to share it. And would she not? What grief of mine has her gentle, compassionate heart not felt? What pain would she not endure to spare me even a pang?

I sought out the doctor. I had an interview with him. I told my story, laid bare my heart with a passion so sincere it moved him to sympathy. My confession helped him understand his young patient’s illness—one his medicines could not cure. I had promised not to see her or go near her—I kept my word. I had promised to leave London—I left. Twice, thrice I returned, poured out my sorrows to him. He accepted my fee now and then, always received me kindly, let me speak.

Ah, how I clung to him! I suspect he had known sorrow himself—he knew how to comfort the broken-hearted.

He did not tell me how dangerously ill she was, though he did not hide how gravely sick she had become. I told him everything—that I would marry her, defy every risk, that without her, I was utterly ruined, cared not what became of me. My mother had once consented, but now withdrew her approval—when the bond between us had, as I believed, grown stronger than filial duty.

“I think, sir,” said the doctor, “if your mother heard you and saw Miss Lambert, she might relent.”

Who was my mother to hold me in bondage? To claim the right to make me miserable? To take this angel from my arms?

“He cannot,” he said, “be a message-carrier between pining young ladies and lovers shut out from their hearts’ gates. But this much I will say: I have seen you, and I am prescribing for you too.”

Yes, he must have suffered once. I saw him, you may be sure, on the very day he kept his promise to me. He said she seemed comforted by hearing news of me.

“She bears her suffering with angelic sweetness. I prescribe Jesuit’s bark, which she takes—but I am not sure the news of you has not done more good than the medicine.”

Later, the women admitted they had never told the General about the doctor’s new patient.

I know not what wild expressions of gratitude I poured out to the good doctor for the comfort he brought me. His treatment healed two souls. ‘Twas but a drop of water—but to a man raging in torment, a drop is enough.

I loved the ground he trod, blessed the hand that held mine, the pulse he felt. I had a ring with a pretty cameo of Hercules. Too small for his finger—and he wore no ornaments. I fastened it to his watch-chain, hoping she might see it and recognize it as mine.

How I clung to Spencer at this time—my friend from the Temple, who also had an unfortunate love affair—we walked from my apartments to the Temple, and he back to Bedford Gardens. Our talk was always of our women. I doubt I told everyone my grief. My kind landlady and Betty the housemaid pitied me. My son Miles, who for once has been reading my manuscript, says, “By Jove, sir, I didn’t know you and my mother were so deeply smitten. The year I joined, I was struck hard myself—an infernal little jilt who left me for Sir Craven Oaks of our regiment. I thought I’d go mad.” And he gives a melancholy whistle and walks away.

The General had to leave London soon for a military inspection, as the doctor casually mentioned. But since I had sworn not to present myself at his house, I kept my vow—though I made use of the physician’s permission to visit him and learn of his dear patient. His reports were far from encouraging.

“She does not rally,” he said. “We must send her back to Kent, or to the sea.”

I did not know then that the poor child had begged so piteously not to be moved that her parents, sensing perhaps why she wished to stay in London, and fearing it might be dangerous not to indulge her, had yielded and agreed to remain in town.

At last, one morning I came, as usual, and took my place in the doctor’s front parlour, where patients were called in turn to the consulting room. I sat there, idly browsing the books on the table, paying no attention to anyone. Soon the room emptied—save for me and one lady, veiled.

I usually stayed till the end, for Osborn, the doctor’s man, knew my purpose: I wasn’t there for my own health.

When only I and the lady remained, she reached out two small hands and whispered, “Don’t you know me, George?”

And the next moment I had my arms around her, kissed her as passionately as I ever had—and gave way to a flood of emotion so refreshing, for my parched soul had raged and tormented for six weeks. This was a glimpse of heaven.

Who was it, children? You think it was your mother the doctor brought to me? No. It was Hetty.
