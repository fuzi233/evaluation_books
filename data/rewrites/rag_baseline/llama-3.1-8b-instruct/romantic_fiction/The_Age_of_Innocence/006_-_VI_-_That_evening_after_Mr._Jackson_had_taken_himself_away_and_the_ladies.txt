The flickering gaslight cast eerie shadows on the walls of Newland Archer's study. He settled into his armchair, surrounded by the comforting familiarity of his books and photographs. May Welland's portrait, given to him during their early romance, now dominated the table, its image seared into his memory. He gazed at the frank forehead, serious eyes, and innocent mouth, feeling an unsettling sense of detachment. Marriage, he realized, was not the sanctuary he had been led to believe – rather, it was a journey into uncharted waters, where the boundaries between reality and convention blurred.

The Countess Olenska's situation had unsettled his deeply ingrained convictions, forcing him to confront the contradictions within himself. Women, he thought, should be free – free to make their own choices, to forge their own paths. Yet, in his world, such ideas were dismissed as radical, even treasonous. The notion of "nice" women demanding freedom was anathema, and those who advocated for it were seen as romantic dreamers. But what about the women who were wronged, who were trapped in situations they couldn't escape? Shouldn't they be entitled to the same autonomy he claimed for himself?

Newland's thoughts turned to his own marriage, and the inevitable compromises it would require. He was pledged to defend May's cousin, the Countess, against the judgments of society, yet he felt a twinge of discomfort whenever he considered his own wife's limited agency. What did he truly know of May, beyond the polished exterior she presented to the world? And what of his own past, which he had carefully hidden from her? The thought sent a shiver down his spine, as he realized that their marriage might become a union of convenience, held together by ignorance and social expectations.

He thought of Lawrence Lefferts, the embodiment of the very ideal he sought to avoid. Lefferts's marriage was a masterclass in hypocrisy, a façade of respectability concealing a life of secret desires and transgressions. Newland wondered if he, too, would become like Lefferts, trapped in a loveless marriage, perpetuating the very conventions he despised. The prospect was a bleak one, and he pushed the thought aside, trying to focus on the present moment. But the unease lingered, a persistent hum in the background of his mind, a reminder that marriage was not the sanctuary he had imagined, but a complex, often suffocating institution that threatened to consume him
