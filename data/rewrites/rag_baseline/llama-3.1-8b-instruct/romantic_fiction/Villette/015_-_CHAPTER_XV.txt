As the academic year drew to a close, a period of intense focus settled over the Pensionnat Beauvais, its students and faculty alike gearing up for the impending examinations. The months leading up to this culminating event had been a time of relative relaxation, punctuated by Madame Beck's festive gathering and its attendant weeks of leisure. Now, however, the air was thick with anticipation and the weight of expectation.

For the English teacher, the upcoming examination posed a particularly daunting challenge. Ninety students, each with their own unique linguistic profile, required individualized instruction and attention to master the intricacies of the English language. The task was not for the faint of heart, and I, along with the rest of the faculty, felt the pressure mounting.

M. Paul, the school's autocratic director, had taken it upon himself to oversee the examination process, relegating his colleagues to secondary roles. His disdain for collaboration was well-known, and it seemed that even his own family members were not immune to his dictatorial tendencies. Madame Beck, for instance, had expressed a desire to lead the geography section, only to be rebuffed by her kinsman's imperious edict.

As I strolled through the garden on the eve of the examination, I encountered M. Emanuel, his eyes blazing with intensity behind his spectacles. His attire, a curious blend of elegance and informality, seemed calculated to intimidate. "Thus," he declared, his voice low and menacing, "you shall reign supreme tomorrow, alongside me. Will you not savor the sweet taste of authority? I detect in you a certain... something, a radiant ambition!" Little did he know that I found the prospect of being the center of attention decidedly unappealing, my priorities lying elsewhere. As we spoke, I couldn't help but wonder how it was that M. Paul managed to cultivate such an aura of invincibility among our students, his reputation for infallibility seemingly impervious to scrutiny.
