The jagged, dissonant clangour of conflict shattered the subterranean stillness, tearing me from the heavy depths of unconsciousness. For a disorienting heartbeat, my senses struggled against the acoustic violence—the metallic scrape of chains, the wet, guttural snarling of grim beasts, and the heavy, rhythmic exhalations of a man pushed to the brink of physical endurance. It was a harsh, industrial pollution of sound that deeply offended the natural auditory balance of the mind. 

As I pushed myself up from the cold floor of the chamber, the olfactory landscape washed over me: a stifling blend of old rust, the ammonia tang of feral sweat, and the unmistakable, rich copper aroma of fresh blood. Across the room, chained to the blank wall, rested the savage brutes and a group of prisoners. Among them stood a young red Martian woman, the one whose timely cry had just spared my life. She was a perfect specimen of her ancient, copper-skinned race, entirely unadorned, stripped of the tactile textiles and woven histories of her people. Yet, when she spoke, her voice possessed a pure, unpolluted timbre, carrying the subtle, melodic inflections of a fading regional dialect—a sonic remnant of an older, agrarian Barsoom before the Therns imposed their sterile, dominant lexicon upon the world. 

It took only seconds for the chaotic acoustics bleeding through the partition to translate into a clear image in my slowly returning faculties: Tars Tarkas was locked in a desperate struggle. I threw my weight against the secret door, but it was as unyielding as the mythic cliffs of the ancestors. I swept my hands over the cold, senseless gold, searching for a mechanism, when the young woman’s voice cut through the ambient noise. 

“Save thy sword, O Mighty Warrior,” she called out, her phrasing echoing the circular, generational storytelling of the red plains. “Shatter it not against senseless metal which yields better to the lightest finger touch of one who knows its secret.”

“Know you the secret of it then?” I asked, craving the quiet resolution of the lock. 

“Yes; release me and I will give you entrance to the other horror chamber. The keys to my fetters are upon the first dead of thy foemen.” She pointed toward the fallen custodian. “But why would you return to face the fierce banths? Why step back into the jaws of that trap?”

“Because my friend fights there alone,” I answered. 

I knelt beside the carcass of the custodian. The air around the body was stale, lacking the vibrant, earthy aromas of true life, smelling instead of cold stone and utilitarian stagnation. I retrieved an oval ring heavy with keys. The fair Martian maid’s fingers, adept and sensitive to the subtle mechanical vibrations of the metal, quickly selected the key that sprung the great lock at her waist. Freed, she moved with the silent grace of a harvester through high crops, retrieving a slender, needle-like key. She inserted it into a nearly invisible pore in the wall. 

The door swung with a heavy, oiled whisper. The floor beneath my feet pivoted, carrying me into a suffocating miasma of spilt blood, the heavy musk of adrenaline-soaked fur, and the bitter ozone of clashing steel. 

The great Thark stood with his back against an angle of the walls. Facing him in a tense, predatory semi-circle crouched a half-dozen huge monsters. Their blood-matted heads and the deep, precise lacerations across the Thark’s glossy green hide told a story of brutal, protracted violence. Sharp talons had carved his flesh into ribbons. Tars Tarkas was deeply weakened, his body depleted of the vital sustenance and fluids required to fuel his massive frame. Yet, he stood anchored, embodying the indomitable courage of a people deeply rooted in the harsh, cyclical ecology of their world. 

I raised my sharp longsword, the air tasting of iron, but a gentle hand touched my shoulder. I turned to find the young woman beside me. 

“Wait,” she whispered, her breath carrying the faint, sweet scent of a desperately empty stomach holding onto its dignity. “Leave them to me.”

Defenceless, she stepped toward the snarling banths. She did not yell, nor did she add to the violent noise pollution of the chamber. Instead, she spoke a single Barsoomian word. It was a phoneme rooted in an ancient, untranslatable agrarian tongue, a sound born of the open fields and the quiet rustle of domestic life, entirely alien to the sterile, oppressive environment of the Therns. The acoustic resonance of that single syllable was profound. The great beasts wheeled upon her, and for a terrifying second, I expected them to tear her apart. Instead, the untranslatable authority of her ancestral dialect washed over them. They slunk to her feet, whining softly, their aggressive frequencies neutralized. She murmured to them again in low, rhythmic tones, leading the six mighty monsters across the chamber. She opened the opposite panel, sent the beasts through, and turned to us with a quiet smile before rejoining us in the room.

For a long moment, the only sound was the synchronized, heavy breathing of survivors. 

“I heard the fighting,” Tars Tarkas finally rumbled, his deep voice restoring a measure of acoustic harmony. “But I did not fear for you, John Carter, until I heard the report of a revolver shot. That violent noise stripped the last vestige of hope from me, for I knew you had no such weapon. Tell me of it.”

I recounted the events, the words feeling inadequate to bridge the gap between our subjective experiences. Together, we examined the panel through which I had entered, but it defied our tactile investigations. Our path backward was sealed. 

As we turned, another panel swept silently open, and the young woman stepped back into the room. 

“Who are you?” she asked, studying us. “And what is your mission, that you have the temerity to attempt to escape the Valley Dor?”

“I am not of Barsoom,” I replied, my voice steady in the quiet room. “I have taken no voluntary pilgrimage upon the River Iss. My friend is Jeddak of all the Tharks, and I am drawing him away from the living lie that has lured him to this frightful place. I am John Carter, Prince of the House of Tardos Mors.”

She smiled, a warmth returning to her eyes. “Naught that passes in the world we have left is unknown here. I heard of you, many seasons past.”

“And who be you?” I asked. “Why are you a prisoner, yet wielding a linguistic authority over these beasts that speaks of deep, ancestral knowledge?”

“Slave I am,” she answered, a profound sorrow in her cadence. “For fifteen years, the cycle of harvests has passed without me. I have been a slave, and now that my knowledge of their ways threatens their artificial dominance, I am condemned to die the death.”

“What death?” I asked, tasting a sudden bitterness in the air. 

“The Holy Therns eat human flesh,” she said, her voice dropping into a register of profound disgust. “But only flesh that has died beneath the sucking lips of a plant man—flesh from which the vibrant, defiling blood of life has been drained. It is a sterile, utilitarian consumption. They possess no reverence for the deep history of food, no appreciation for the sensory communion of a shared meal. To them, the generational cycle of nourishment is reduced to this gruesome, mechanized harvest.”

“Were it the Holy Therns who felt my blade?” I asked. 

“No, those were lesser therns. The Holy Therns abide upon the outer slopes, in luxurious palaces connected to these caves by labyrinthine passages. This sunless world is kept to furnish their sport and their soulless sustenance.”

She spoke of the Temple of Issus, an ultimate haven the Therns believed to be a paradise of fleshly delights, where they passed after a lifespan of a thousand years. She detailed the rigid, hierarchical reincarnation they blindly trusted—from Thern to plant man to great white ape to the slimy silians of the Lost Sea—a horrific, cyclical worldview utterly devoid of the warmth of true cultural memory or the domestic joys of the living world.

“We sent several Holy Therns to the silians today, then,” Tars Tarkas noted with a grim, rumbling laugh. 

“And so your deaths will be the more terrible,” she warned. “You cannot escape.”

“One has escaped, centuries ago,” I reminded her, appealing to the deep, historical rhythms of her people. 

“It is useless,” she sighed. 

“It is our solemn duty,” I insisted, ensuring my tone carried the acoustic weight of conviction. “We know this valley is a rank deception. The Therns are moral pygmies who have commercialized and sterilized the sacred transition of life. If we shrink from this duty, we allow the true cultural memory of Barsoom to be eroded by their false, dominant lexicon. We must return to our people, to preserve the native truth of the world.”

The girl and the green warrior stood in thoughtful silence, absorbing the acoustic ecology of the chamber. 

“Never had I considered it in that light,” she murmured. “I will go with you as far as we can go.”

“To the gates of Issus, or to the snows of the north or south,” Tars Tarkas declared, his voice a steady drumbeat. “Tars Tarkas follows where John Carter leads.”

Thuvia—for that was her name—released the other red Martian captives. We armed ourselves with the swords, daggers, and revolvers stripped from the dead therns, weapons reappropriated from their sterile masters to serve the raw, passionate will to live. 

With Thuvia guiding us, we moved seamlessly through the subterranean maze, our footsteps falling into a synchronized, stealthy rhythm. We avoided the dissonant echoes of approaching patrols, seeking a distant storeroom where we might provision ourselves. 

We had been walking for an hour when the quiet was ruptured. As we entered a great chamber, a man stepped into our path. He was clad in leathern trappings, and upon his brow sat a golden circlet holding an immense, scintillating jewel. The stone radiated nine primary and unseen colors, a priceless artifact of Barsoomian history worn by those who manipulated the massive engines of the atmosphere plant. 

The thern’s eyes narrowed, his very presence an arrogant intrusion upon our shared silence. “Stop!” he commanded. “What means this, Thuvia?”

Thuvia raised her revolver. The gunshot was a deafening, industrial concussion that tore violently through the acoustic ecology of the corridor. The thern collapsed, dead before he struck the floor. The sharp, acrid smell of burnt powder immediately overpowered the stagnant air. 

“Beast!” she hissed, the word carrying years of suppressed culinary and cultural starvation. 

Then she turned, her eyes widening as she looked from the corpse to me. “O Prince,” she breathed. “Fate is kind. Notest thou not the remarkable resemblance between this Holy Thern and thyself?”

He was indeed of my exact stature and facial structure. But where I bore close-cropped black hair, a mass of flowing yellow locks spilled across the stone. 

“Do you wish me, with my black hair, to pose as a yellow-haired priest of this infernal cult?” I asked. 

Smiling, Thuvia knelt beside the body. She removed the golden circlet, and then, to my utter revulsion, lifted the entire scalp of yellow hair from the man’s head. Beneath it, the thern was entirely, unnaturally bald. 

“They are all thus from birth,” Thuvia explained, holding the lifeless wig. “They spring from a race that once possessed luxuriant golden hair, but over ages of stagnation, they have lost it entirely. The wig is but a superficial facade, a piece of manufactured apparel so deeply tied to their fragile power dynamics that appearing without it is their deepest disgrace.”

I took the wig. It smelled intensely of synthetic, perfumed oils—a sterile, commercial scent completely devoid of the rich, loamy complexity of natural life. Slipping it over my dark hair, I crowned myself with the jeweled circlet and donned the dead man's harness, becoming Sator Throg, a Holy Thern of the Tenth Cycle. 

We carried the body with us until we reached the storeroom. Thuvia’s keys worked the locks, granting us access to a chamber heavy with the metallic tang of stored munitions and ancient provisions. My body, pushed far past the limits of its reserves, ached for proper sustenance, for a meal prepared with care rather than choked down for mere survival. But exhaustion claimed supremacy over hunger. 

I sank to the floor, instructing Tars Tarkas to rest and setting two of the freed prisoners on watch. Surrounded by the rhythmic, synchronized breathing of my companions—a sound that finally restored a measure of domestic peace to the hostile dark—I fell instantly into a deep, dreamless sleep.
