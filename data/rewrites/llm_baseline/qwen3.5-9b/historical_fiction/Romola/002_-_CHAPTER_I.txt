The Loggia de' Cerchi sat nestled in the beating heart of ancient Florence, hidden away in a maze of narrow lanes behind the Badia. Today, few strangers ventured this way unless driven by a doubtful quest for a specific, austere doorway marked with the inscription: *Qui Nacque Il Divino Poeta* ("Here was Born the Divine Poet"). To Dante's ear, these same streets had once echoed with the clamor of bloody feuds between rival clans; but in the fifteenth century, they resounded only with the raucous, ahistorical squabbles and boisterous jokes of wool-carders working in the districts of San Martino and Garbo.

Beneath this loggia, on the early morning of April 9, 1492, two men locked gazes. One stood slightly stooped, peering down with the keen scrutiny of curiosity; the other lay sprawled on the cobblestones, gazing upward with the startled eyes of someone jolted awake from a vivid dream.

The standing figure spoke first. He was a gray-haired man of broad shoulders, built by nature "with the fist and polished with the pickaxe," as a Tuscan might say. Yet, the self-important gravity etched into the deep lines of his brow and mouth served to counteract any assumptions of coarseness suggested by his rugged appearance. Beside him lay a large, well-filled sack of leather, and before him hung a peddler's basket filled with trinkets: spools of thread, sewing pins, and shards of glass, likely acquired in trade for his wares.

"Young man," he said, tapping the ring on the finger of the reclining figure, "once your chin grows a stiffer beard, you'll learn better than to sleep in street corners with such a gem on your forefinger. By the holy Evangelists! If anyone else had stood over you two minutes ago, I'd have taken it. But Bratti Ferravecchi is not a thief. The cat cannot eat her mouse unless she catches it alive, and Bratti cannot enjoy a gain unless he tastes the bargain first. Why, young man, one San Giovanni, three years past, the Saint sent a corpse my way—a blind beggar with a cap lined with coins. My stomach revolted at the idea of taking money I hadn't earned, until I realized San Giovanni owed me the value of what I spent annually at the Feast. So I buried the body, paid for a Mass, and called it a fair deal. But how does a youth with the face of Messer San Michele sleep on a stone slab with the wind for a blanket?"

His deep, guttural words were barely intelligible to the newly awakened listener, yet he understood the gesture toward his ring. With a half-automatic compliance, he removed the band and shoved it inside his doublet, then rose and stretched.

"Your tunic and hose clash dreadfully with that jewel, young man," Bratti remarked deliberately. "One might say the Saints sent *you* a corpse; but if you took the jewels, I trust you buried him—and you can surely afford a couple of Masses for him to boot."

A sharp, painful thrill seemed to shoot through the stranger's frame, halting his stretch. For an instant, he turned on Bratti with a sharp frown, but quickly masked it with indifference. He pulled off the red Levantine cap that dangled like a pouch over his left ear, tossed back his long, dark-brown curls, glanced at his attire, and smiled.

"You speak truth, friend: my garments are weather-stained like an old sail, and they are not old either. Like an old sail, however, they have borne the brunt of both sea spray and rain. The fact is, I am a stranger in Florence. When I arrived footsore last night, I preferred casting myself into a corner of this hospitable porch to continuing my hunt for a hostel, which might prove to be a nest of bloodsuckers of every stripe."

"A stranger, truly," Bratti agreed. "For your words melt from your throat so perfectly that neither a Christian nor a Florentine can distinguish a hook from a hanger. Are you not from Genoa? By the cut of your clothes, you seem more likely from Venice."

"At this precise moment," the stranger replied with a smile, "where I come from matters less than where I can find breakfast. Your city casts a grim shadow here: can you direct me to a livelier quarter where I might secure a meal and lodging?"

"I certainly can," Bratti said. "And it is your good fortune, young man, that I happened to walk in from Rovezzano this morning and detoured to Mercato Vecchio to say an Ave at the Badia. That is your luck. But let us see what my profit shall be. Nothing for nothing, young man. If I guide you to Mercato Vecchio, you shall swear by your patron saint to offer me the first bid for that stained suit of yours when you acquire finer raiment—which, doubtless, you will."

"Agreed, by San Niccolò," the other laughed. "But let us depart for this said Mercato, for I feel the urgent need to line this doublet you covet."

"Coveting? Nay," Bratti grunted, hoisting his bag onto his back and starting off. Suddenly, he broke into loud, harsh tones resembling the grinding of a cart wheel: "*Chi abbaratta—baratta—b'ratta—chi abbaratta cenci e vetri—b'ratta ferri vecchi?*" ("Who wants to exchange rags, broken glass, or old iron?")

"It is worth little," he continued, returning to a conversational tone. "Hose and all, your clothes fetch a low price. Still, if you wish to equip yourself with a lute surpassing any new instrument, a sword wielded by a Ridolfi, or a paternoster of the finest fashion, I could offer a grand bargain by allowing for the cost of your current attire. Simple as I stand here, I possess the best-furnished shop in the Ferravecchi district, situated close to the Mercato. Blessed be the Virgin! It is not a pumpkin I carry on my shoulders. But I do not remain caged in my shop all day; I have a wife and a raven to mind the stock at home. *Chi abbaratta...?* Now, young man, whence do you come, and what brings you to Florence?"

"I thought you delighted in nothing without a bargain," the stranger observed somewhat scornfully. "You have offered me nothing yet in exchange for that information."

"Well, well; a Florentine does not mind paying a fair price for news, even if he gains no hose thereby. If I take you to the fairest maiden in the Mercato for a cup of milk—that shall count as a fair bargain."

"Nay; I can locate her myself if she truly resides in the Mercato, for pretty heads tend to peer out of doors and windows. No, no. Moreover, a sharp trader like you should know that he who bids for nuts and news may discover them hollow."

"Ah! Young man," Bratti said, glancing sideways with a hint of admiration, "you were not born on a Sunday—the salt shops were open when you drew breath. Are you not a Hebrew? From Spain or Naples? Let me tell you, the Minor Friars are trying to heat Florence as hot as Spain regarding those dogs of hell who seek to monopolize usury profits for themselves, leaving none for Christians. And when you stroll the Calimala with a scrap of yellow cloth in your cap, it will ruin your beauty more than a sword-cut across that smooth olive cheek. *Abbaratta, baratta...* I tell you, young man, grey cloth stands against yellow cloth. There is as much grey cloth in Florence as needed to clothe the Duomo, yet not enough yellow cloth to make hose for Saint Christopher—blessed be his name, and grant me a vision of him today! *Abbaratta, baratta, b'ratta—chi abbaratta?*"

"All that is delightful information you part with gratis," the stranger retorted, "but it concerns me not. I am no Hebrew."

"See!" Bratti exclaimed triumphantly. "I've secured a fine bargain with mere words. I've extracted something from you, young man, though you are as slippery as a lamprey. Praised be San Giovanni! A blind Florentine matches two one-eyed men. But here we are in the Mercato."

Emerging from the narrow alleys, they entered a vast square known to earlier chroniclers as the Mercato Vecchio, or Old Market. Though this plaza had served as a provisioning hub since time immemorial—and fond imagination suggested it might be the very spot where the Fiesolan ancestors descended from their high fastnesses to trade with valley villagers—it had not been entirely avoided by Florentine wealth. In the early decades of the fifteenth century, nearing its end, the Medici and other powerful families of the *popolani grassi*, or commercial nobility, maintained residences there. They apparently found the roar of mixed dialects and the butcher's stalls, which the old poet Antonio Pucci hailed as a chief glory of a market surpassing all others, insufficient to offend their ears or shock their eyes. However, the pride of mutton and veal was currently absent; Lent was not yet fully concluded.
