Dixon came back from Milton at the very hour when Margaret’s days were beginning to taste of sameness, and, with her return, the past returned too—wrapped in lint and lavender, tied up with pins, and thrust into the drawers of the present as if it belonged there.

She stepped into her old place as though she had never left it, bringing with her a whole market-basket of tidings, each laid in as carefully as eggs in straw. Milton, it appeared, had not stood still because Margaret had gone away. It had married and eaten and talked, and set a sharp edge on its tongues, and Dixon’s were the sharpest of all.

Martha, she said, had gone to live with Miss Thornton—now Mrs. Thornton by marriage—“and such a wedding breakfast, Miss! I could tell by the very descriptions what was on the table. They’d have had cold fowls in jelly, and hams glazed till you could see your face in them, and a great cake cut up as if it were a sacrament.” Dixon spoke as one who had never forgiven strangers for dining without her. “And the dresses! And the bridesmaids! Folks said it was over-grand, considering Mr. Thornton had lost money by the strike, and had to pay for contracts failing. But rich folks in Milton, Miss, they’ll spend on show as if the clatter of knives and forks could drown out bad luck.”

Then she launched into the sale of furniture—those old, beloved pieces she had once counted on as if they were an inheritance of her own. They had fetched little, which Dixon called a shame, “seeing how Milton people boast of their wealth—yet they’ll haggle like women at a butter-stall.” Mrs. Thornton had come and taken “two or three bargains,” and Mr. Thornton, coming the next day, had bid against himself—so eager, so unguarded, so unlike the cold master Dixon liked to criticise—that the bystanders had laughed outright at it.

“So as I observed, Miss, it made things even. If his mother paid too little, he paid too much.”

Margaret listened, and the very names—Milton, Thornton—were like a spice jar opened for a moment: a sudden sharpness in the air, a pricking in the memory. Dixon, full of small domestic economics and great social judgments, did not understand that Margaret took in these scraps as one takes in a scent that has been lost for months, and cannot help following it through corridors of thought.

Mr. Bell, Dixon went on, had sent endless directions about his books. There was no pleasing him. He wanted this packed so, that wrapped thus, that labelled with an exactitude Dixon thought folly.

“There’s no understanding gentlemen, Miss, when they begin to fret about their paper and print. If he’d been there himself it would have been plain enough; but letters always were and always will be more puzzling than they’re worth.”

Of the Higginses she had almost nothing; her mind slipped away from that neighbourhood of her life as if it were greasy ground she did not care to step on. Nicholas, she believed, was well. He had come more than once to ask after Miss Margaret—the only person, Dixon said, who ever did ask, “except once Mr. Thornton.”

“And Mary?” Margaret asked, though she knew Dixon’s tone before the answer came.

“Oh! Mary’s well enough. A great stout slatternly thing, Miss! And I did hear—unless it was a dream, though it would be strange if I dreamt of such people—that she’d gone to work at Mr. Thornton’s mill, because her father wished her to know how to cook. As if mill-work taught a girl the difference between a clear broth and dish-water!”

The tale had indeed the ill-jointed look of a dream, and Margaret said as much. Yet she caught at it, because it proved that in Milton even the roughest lives had begun to turn, however clumsily, towards knowledge and home; that hunger there had not been only for bread, but for the skill of making bread good.

And it was pleasant—however Dixon shrank from it—to have someone near who could speak of Milton at all. Margaret found herself drawing Dixon on, as if by making the place vivid in talk she could make it nearer to being reached again in fact. But Dixon, for all her gossip, would rather cast that part of her history into shadow; she was fonder of telling over Mr. Bell’s sayings, and of shaping them into her own firm notion that he intended to make Margaret his heiress.

She watched Margaret as she brushed out her hair, or folded her gowns, as if a fortune might drop from a curl or a hem.

But Margaret gave her no hold. She would not answer insinuating questions, however disguised as prophecy. It was not that she despised the idea of wealth; she had learned too well how money could mean warmth and medicine and peace. Yet to be spoken of as an heiress, to be weighed and measured by what might be left her, had a taste in it like tin—sharp, lingering, unwelcome.

All the while, beneath the smooth hours, Margaret had a longing that would not shape itself into a clear thought: she wanted to hear that Mr. Bell had gone to Milton.

It had been understood between them—at Helstone, under that sunny leisure which had made even grave matters seem light—that the explanation she desired should be given to Mr. Thornton only by word of mouth, and even then should not be pressed upon him. Mr. Bell had taken that task upon himself, half in kindness, half in his own careless justice; and Margaret, though she would not confess hope even to herself, found each letter of his an occasion of secret listening.

Mr. Bell was no great correspondent. He wrote when the humour took him, and whether he sent many pages or only a line, Margaret always unfolded his notes with the faint expectation that some mention of Milton would fall out like a pressed flower from between the leaves.

But he said nothing—nothing of going there, nothing of seeing Thornton, nothing of clearing away the strange fog that had settled on Margaret’s life. She would put away the letter with a small disappointment that had no right, and yet was there.

Well—she must be patient. Sooner or later the mists would lift.

Yet even his letters were altered. They were short and complaining. Sometimes there was a bitterness in them that jarred like a wrong note in familiar music. Mr. Bell did not look forward; he regretted backward. He seemed tired of the present, as if the very air of Oxford had grown close to him.

Margaret began to fear he was ill. Once she asked outright after his health; and he answered by return with a little snappish jest—that there was an old-fashioned complaint called the spleen, and that he was suffering from it, and it was for her to decide whether it lay in mind or body; but he wished, he said, to grumble without being compelled to send a bulletin each time.

After that she asked no more.

One day Edith, letting slip a fragment of talk from when Mr. Bell had last been in London, planted a new idea in Margaret’s mind so suddenly that it was like the striking of a match in a dim room: Mr. Bell had half thought of taking her to Cadiz in the autumn—to see Frederick and his new wife, and to hear from Frederick himself the truth about the mutiny.

Margaret questioned Edith until Edith, tired and half wounded, protested there was nothing more. Mr. Bell had said he always went somewhere in the long vacation; why not Spain as well as anywhere? He wanted, perhaps, to hear with his own ears what Frederick had to say, and it would be a good opportunity for Margaret to become acquainted with her sister-in-law. That was all.

But to Margaret it was not all. Spain stood in her imagination like a bright dish set at the far end of a long table—exotic, sun-warmed, full of unknown flavour; an outlet from the monotony that had begun to lie on her days like over-boiled starch. It was a mere castle in the air, a Château en Espagne indeed; yet the thought of it delighted her as if she could already hear foreign streets, and smell garlic and oranges, and taste salt air on a tongue that had known only London’s soot.

Edith did not share this quickening of spirits. Edith was in the mood that made any pleasure taken elsewhere feel like treachery.

She began to cry—because she had nothing else particular to do, and because her own feelings always seemed to her to deserve immediate ceremony—and said she knew she cared far more for Margaret than Margaret did for her.

Margaret soothed her as best she could. She did not say, “It is not you I would leave, but myself,” because Edith would have heard only the leaving. So Margaret kept her gladness shut up, as one keeps a fragrant spice from someone who would call it too strong.

Only when Dixon came to dress her for dinner did Margaret allow herself the harmless luxury of speaking it aloud.

“Dixon,” she said, “should you not like to see Master Frederick and his new wife—very much indeed?”

Dixon’s face stiffened with a pious apprehension that had always in it a relish of drama.

“She’s a Papist, Miss, isn’t she?”

Margaret hesitated the smallest moment—then, with a recollected dampness of feeling, answered, “I believe—oh yes, certainly.”

“And they live in a Popish country?”

“Yes.”

“Then I’m afraid I must say my soul is dearer to me than even Master Frederick—his own dear self. I should be in perpetual terror, Miss, lest I should be converted.”

Margaret could not help smiling a little.

“Oh. I do not know that I am going. And if I go, I am not such a fine lady as to be unable to travel without you. No, dear old Dixon—you shall have a long holiday if we go. But I’m afraid it is a long ‘if.’”

Dixon did not like the speech. Margaret’s way of saying “dear old” to those she loved was meant as warmth; but Dixon, not much past fifty and holding herself still in her prime, winced as if Margaret had dusted her with flour and called it snow. Besides, she did not enjoy being taken at her word. Under all her terror of priests and Popish mysteries there lurked a keen curiosity—about Spain, and the Inquisition, and the strange rites she had heard whispered of, as children whisper of ghosts.

Clearing her throat, as if negotiation were a kind of moral labour, she asked whether, if she took care never to see a priest, nor to enter one of their churches, there would really be so much danger of conversion. Master Frederick had “gone over” unaccountably, to be sure.

“I fancy it was love,” Margaret said, and sighed, “that first predisposed him to conversion.”

“Indeed, Miss!” Dixon replied, with a sudden solemnity, as if love were a contagion worse than Catholicism. “Well! I can preserve myself from priests and from churches; but love steals in unawares. I think it’s as well I should not go.”

Margaret laughed a little more openly then; and yet she was afraid of letting her thoughts dwell too long on Spain, for fear the bright picture should sharpen the greyness of the actual day. Still, even a fancied journey drew her mind away from that other restless desire—to have everything explained to Mr. Thornton.

Mr. Bell, for the present, seemed fixed at Oxford. He did not speak of Milton, nor appear to have any immediate purpose of going there. Something like a restraint lay upon Margaret too, preventing her from asking him directly, or even alluding again to any such visit. She did not feel free to mention what Edith had said about Spain; it might have been no more than five minutes’ fancy in Mr. Bell’s mind. He had not named it at Helstone during that long, sunlit day; perhaps it had arisen later and would disappear again.

Yet, if true—what a bright outlet it would be from the present life, which was beginning to fall upon her with a sameness almost audible.

For Margaret had come to notice the sounds of the house as one notices the ticking of a clock in sickness. In Edith’s drawing-room everything was soft-footed—carpets swallowing steps, servants trained to make themselves noiseless, curtains shutting out street-cries. The hush was meant for comfort, but it could be oppressive: a quiet so complete that every small discord—an impatient tapping, a child’s shriek—rang as if in a church.

One of the few keen pleasures she had was in Edith’s boy.

He was the pride and plaything of both father and mother so long as he was good; and when he was good, he was displayed like a new ornament—blue sash, smooth hair, a little polished creature to be admired. But he had a will of his own, and when that will burst out, it came with a stormy passion that made the room echo and the servants freeze in the doorway. Edith would sink back as if struck, exhausted before she began to strive, and sigh—

“Oh dear, what shall I do with him! Do, Margaret, please ring the bell for Hanley.”

Margaret almost liked him better then, in those red-faced declarations of self, than in his tame, ribboned moods. She would carry him away into a room apart, where the battle could be fought without an audience. There, in the thick quiet broken by sobs and stamping, she would meet his rage with a firm steadiness that did not fear noise, yet did not answer it with noise. She would hold him, speak to him, sometimes say nothing at all until the storm began to exhaust itself; and then, with sudden little charms—some swift story, some change of tone, some play that turned his attention without bribing him—she would coax him back to peace on the side of right.

At last he would rub his hot, tear-smeared face over hers, kissing and clinging as if love were a kind of surrender; and often he fell asleep in her arms or on her shoulder, his breath evening out, the small sounds of distress changing into the steady, animal assurance of rest.

Those were Margaret’s sweetest moments. In them there was a taste of something she believed would be denied her for ever—a fulness that was more than duty, a tenderness with roots.

Into this household life Mr. Henry Lennox came often, and his presence altered the air as a sharper seasoning alters a dish that has grown bland. Margaret found him colder than formerly, if more brilliant; yet there were strong intellectual tastes in him, and much knowledge, and that gave flavour to conversation that otherwise would have been thin enough.

He looked—Margaret could not help seeing it—with a slight contempt upon his brother and sister-in-law, upon their way of spending time as if it were something to be killed gently between visits and dinners. Once or twice he spoke to his brother, in Margaret’s hearing, in a tone edged like a carving-knife, asking whether Captain Lennox meant entirely to relinquish his profession.

And when the Captain answered, contentedly, that he had quite enough to live upon, Mr. Lennox’s lip curled.

“And is that all you live for?”

Yet the brothers were attached in their fashion: the cleverer always leading, the other patient enough to be led, as cattle follow a familiar hand. Mr. Lennox himself was pushing hard on in his own profession, cultivating connections with a profound calculation. He was keen-sighted, far-seeing, intelligent, sarcastic, and proud; he moved among people as if he were always measuring their value, and could not forgive those who did not measure up.

Since that one long conversation with him—about Frederick’s affairs, on the first evening when Mr. Bell had been present—Margaret had had little intercourse with Mr. Lennox beyond what their common nearness in Edith’s house brought about. Yet it was enough to wear off shyness on her side, and to smooth away, on his, any remaining look of wounded vanity.

They met continually; but Margaret thought he avoided being alone with her. Perhaps he too knew that they had drifted far from where they had once seemed moored, side by side—not only in opinions, but in taste itself, in the very instinct of what made life savoury.

And yet, when he had spoken unusually well, when some epigram of his struck the table with a clean ring like a well-placed knife, she would see his eye seek her face first—only for an instant, but unmistakably—as if he waited for her to translate his cleverness into approval. In the family talk that constantly threw them together, her opinion was the one he listened for with a deference the more complete because he concealed it, and paid it as if against his will.

So the days went on: domestic, orderly, outwardly quiet. But in Margaret’s mind the past and the possible future kept stirring like leaven—Milton with its harsh music of wheels and voices; Spain with its imagined sun and foreign speech; and, between them, the unspoken explanation meant for one man’s ear alone, held back by tenderness, restraint, and time.
