"Edith!" said Margaret, softly, "Edith!"

But as Margaret half suspected, Edith had succumbed to sleep. She lay curled upon the sofa in the back drawing-room of Harley Street, appearing exquisite in her white muslin and blue ribbons. If Titania had ever been attired in white muslin and blue ribbons, and had fallen asleep upon a crimson damask sofa in a rear chamber, Edith might easily have been mistaken for her fairy counterpart. Margaret was once again struck by her cousin's beauty. Having grown up together since childhood, Edith had long been remarked upon by everyone save Margaret for her prettiness; yet Margaret had never truly considered it until recent days, when the looming prospect of separation seemed to amplify every sweet quality and charm Edith possessed. They had been discussing wedding dresses, wedding ceremonies, Captain Lennox, and the tales he had shared regarding Edith's future life on Corfu, where his regiment was stationed. They had also debated the formidable challenge of maintaining a piano in tune—a difficulty Edith regarded as a primary threat to her marital happiness—and planned the gowns required for the Scottish visits that would follow her marriage. However, the whispered tones had gradually grown drowsier. After a pause of several minutes, Margaret realized that, despite the hum of conversation in the adjacent room, Edith had rolled herself into a soft sphere of muslin, ribbon, and silken curls, drifting off into a peaceful post-prandial slumber.

Margaret had intended to confide in her cousin some of the plans and visions she harbored concerning her future life in the country parsonage, where her parents resided. It was there, amidst her bright holidays, that she had spent her youth, although for the last decade Aunt Shaw's house had effectively served as her home. Deprived of a listener, however, she was forced to brood silently on the impending changes. It was a happy brooding, albeit tinged with regret at the indefinite separation from her gentle aunt and beloved cousin. As she contemplated the joy of fulfilling the significant role of sole daughter in Helstone parsonage, fragments of conversation drifted from the next room. Aunt Shaw was speaking to the five or six ladies who had dined there, whose husbands remained in the dining-room. These were the familiar acquaintances of the household; neighbours whom Mrs. Shaw termed "friends" simply because she dined with them more frequently than others, and because if either she or Edith required something from them—or vice versa—they did not hesitate to visit before luncheon. These ladies and their husbands had been invited specifically to consume a farewell dinner in honor of Edith's approaching nuptials. Edith had initially objected to this arrangement, as Captain Lennox was expected to arrive via a late train that very evening; yet, though she was a spoiled child, she lacked a strong will of her own due to her carelessness and idleness, and yielded when she discovered her mother had absolutely ordered those seasonal delicacies supposedly efficacious against excessive grief at farewell feasts. Contenting herself by leaning back in her chair, merely manipulating the food on her plate, Edith appeared grave and absent-minded, while those around her delighted in the witty remarks of Mr. Grey, the gentleman who invariably sat at the bottom of the table at Mrs. Shaw's gatherings and requested that Edith provide musical entertainment in the drawing-room. Mr. Grey proved particularly agreeable during this farewell dinner, causing the gentlemen to linger downstairs longer than usual. It was fortunate they did so, judging by the fragments of conversation Margaret overheard.

"I suffered too much myself; not that I was not extremely happy with the poor dear General, but still, disparity of age is a drawback; one that I was resolved Edith should not have to encounter. Of course, without any maternal partiality, I foresaw that the dear child was likely to marry early; indeed, I had often said that I was certain she would be wed before she was nineteen. I had quite a prophetic feeling when Captain Lennox"—here the voice dropped to a whisper, yet Margaret could easily fill in the blank. The course of true love in Edith's case had run remarkably smoothly. Mrs. Shaw had succumbed to the presentiment, expressing it as such, and had rather urged on the marriage, although it fell below the expectations many of Edith's acquaintances had formed for her, a young and pretty heiress. Nevertheless, Mrs. Shaw declared that her only child should marry for love—and sighed emphatically, as if love had not been her motive for marrying the General. Mrs. Shaw derived far more enjoyment from the romance of the current engagement than her daughter did. Though Edith was thoroughly and properly in love, she would certainly have preferred a substantial house in Belgravia to all the picturesque allure of the life Captain Lennox described at Corfu. Indeed, the specific aspects that caused Margaret to glow with excitement as she listened, Edith feigned shivering and shuddering at; partly for the pleasure she derived from being coaxed out of her dislike by her devoted lover, and partly because anything resembling a gypsy or makeshift existence was genuinely distasteful to her. Yet, had anyone offered a magnificent house, a vast estate, and a noble title in addition, Edith would still have clung to Captain Lennox so long as the temptation lasted. Once the allure faded, it was possible she might harbor little qualms of ill-concealed regret that Captain Lennox could not embody everything desirable in himself. In this regard, she was but her mother's child: Mrs. Shaw, after deliberately uniting with General Shaw driven solely by respect for his character and establishment, constantly, though quietly, lamented her hard lot in being joined to one whom she could not love.

"I have spared no expense in her trousseau," were the next words Margaret heard. "She possesses all the beautiful Indian shawls and scarves the General gave to me, which I shall never wear again."

"She is a lucky girl," replied another voice, which Margaret recognized as belonging to Mrs. Gibson, a lady who was taking a double interest in the conversation due to the fact that one of her daughters had been married within the last few weeks. "Helen had set her heart upon an Indian shawl, but truly, when I found what an extravagant price was demanded, I was obliged to refuse her. She will be quite envious when she hears of Edith possessing Indian shawls. What manner are they? Delhi? With the lovely little borders?"

Margaret heard her aunt's voice again, but this time it seemed as if she had risen from her half-recumbent posture and was gazing into the more dimly lit back drawing-room. "Edith! Edith!" cried she; and then she sank as if wearied by the exertion. Margaret stepped forward.

"Edith is asleep, Aunt Shaw. Is there anything I can do?"

All the ladies exclaimed "poor child!" upon receiving this distressing intelligence regarding Edith; and the tiny lap-dog in Mrs. Shaw's arms began to bark, as if agitated by the outburst of pity.

"Hush, Tiny! You naughty little girl! You will wake your mistress. It was merely to ask Edith if she would instruct Newton to bring down her shawls; perhaps you would go, Margaret dear?"

Margaret ascended into the old nursery at the very summit of the house, where Newton was diligently arranging some laces required for the wedding. While Newton departed (not without a muttered grumble) to unlock the shawls—which had already been displayed four or five times that day—Margaret surveyed the nursery. It was the first room in that house with which she had become acquainted nine years prior, when she was brought, untamed from the forest, to share the home, play, and lessons of her cousin Edith. She recalled the dark, somber atmosphere of the London nursery, presided over by an austere and ceremonious nurse who was terribly particular about clean hands and torn frocks. She recollected the first tea ceremony held there—separate from her father and aunt, who were dining somewhere far below, an infinite depth of stairs; for unless she were situated in the sky (so the child thought), they must surely be buried deep within the bowels of the earth. At home—prior to her relocation to Harley Street—her mother's dressing-room had served as her nursery; and as they maintained early hours in the country parsonage, Margaret had always taken her meals with her father and mother. Oh! How vividly the tall, stately girl of eighteen remembered the tears shed with such wild, passionate grief by the little girl of nine, as she hid her face beneath the bedclothes on that first night; and how she was bid not to cry by the nurse, lest it disturb Miss Edith; and how she had wept as bitterly, yet more quietly, until her newly arrived, grand, and pretty aunt descended softly upstairs accompanied by Mr. Hale to reveal his little sleeping daughter. Then the little Margaret had hushed her sobs and endeavored to lie quiet as if asleep, fearing to render her father unhappy through her grief, which she dared not express before her aunt, and which she rather believed it wrong to feel at all after the long hopes, plans, and schemes they had undertaken at home before her wardrobe could be arranged to suit her grander circumstances, and before Papa could abandon his parish to ascend to London, even if only for a few days.

Now she loved the old nursery, though it was but a dismantled place; and she looked about with a kind of cat-like regret at the prospect of leaving it forever in three days.
