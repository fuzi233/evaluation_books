Mrs. Jennings arrived at their chamber with the urgency of a soldier responding to a distress signal, bypassing the formalities of announcement to stride inside. Her expression was etched with genuine alarm.

"How do you do, my dear?" she asked, her voice modulated with a practiced, compassionate cadence directed at Marianne, who had turned her face away, refusing to engage.

"Is she holding up, Miss Dashwood? Poor creature! She looks critically compromised. No surprise there. Indeed, the facts are irrefutable. He is to be wed imminently—to a woman of negligible character! I possess zero tolerance for such conduct. Mrs. Taylor relayed the intelligence to me mere moments ago, sourced from a confidante of Miss Grey herself; absent that confirmation, I would have dismissed it as fabrication. I nearly collapsed upon hearing it. 'Well,' I declared, 'if this holds water, he has treated a young lady of my acquaintance with abominable cruelty. May his new wife torment his soul until the end of days.' Such sentiments shall remain my constant refrain, my dear. You may rely on it. I cannot comprehend why men persist in this behavior. Should I encounter him again, I shall administer a beating he has not endured in decades. Yet, there is solace, my dear Miss Marianne: he is not the sole eligible male in existence. With your visage, you shall never lack suitors. Alas, poor thing! I shall cease disturbing her further; she requires immediate catharsis to purge these emotions. Fortunately, the Parrys and Sandersons arrive tonight, providing necessary distraction."

She departed, moving on tiptoes, operating under the assumption that auditory stimuli would exacerbate her young friend's condition.

To Elinor's astonishment, Marianne resolved to dine with the household. Elinor counseled against it, yet Marianne insisted, "I shall descend; I can endure it, and the commotion will serve as a buffer." Satisfied that her sister was momentarily governed by such pragmatic reasoning, though doubting her ability to sustain the meal, Elinor offered no further objection. Adjusting Marianne's attire as best she could while the latter remained recumbent, she prepared to escort her to the dining hall upon summons.

Seated there, though appearing profoundly wretched, Marianne consumed more sustenance and displayed greater composure than anticipated. Had she attempted speech or registered half of Mrs. Jennings' well-intentioned but tactically flawed attentions, this equilibrium would have fractured. Instead, she remained mute; the abstraction of her thoughts shielded her from awareness of the surrounding chaos.

Elinor, acknowledging Mrs. Jennings' benevolence despite its frequent distressing and occasionally absurd manifestations, offered the requisite acknowledgments and reciprocated civilities that Marianne lacked the capacity to provide. Observing Marianne's unhappiness, Mrs. Jennings concluded that every available resource should be deployed to alleviate her state. Consequently, she treated her with the indulgent affection reserved for a favored child on the eve of departure. Marianne was assigned the prime seat by the fire, tempted with every delicacy in the house, and subjected to a barrage of current events. Had Elinor not witnessed the somber countenance of her sister acting as a brake on mirth, she might have been entertained by Mrs. Jennings' efforts to cure romantic disappointment with confections, olives, and a robust hearth. However, once the relentless repetition of these gestures forced themselves upon Marianne's consciousness, she could endure no longer. With a sharp exclamation of *Misery* and a gesture commanding her sister to desist, she rose abruptly and fled the room.

"Poor soul!" cried Mrs. Jennings immediately upon her exit. "How grievous it is to witness! I declare she departs unfinished regarding her wine and the dried cherries! Lord! Nothing appears to benefit her. Were I privy to anything she desired, I would scour the entire town for it. Well, it is peculiar that a man should treat such a lovely girl so poorly! But when capital flows freely from one side and is virtually nonexistent on the other, Lord bless you! they disregard such trifles entirely—"

"The lady in question—Miss Grey, I believe you termed her—is affluent?"

"Fifty thousand pounds, my dear. Have you encountered her? Described as smart and stylish, though lacking conventional beauty. I recall her aunt, Biddy Henshawe; she wed a man of considerable means. The entire family possesses wealth. Fifty thousand pounds! And by all accounts, it will not materialize until absolutely necessary, for they claim he is utterly depleted. No wonder! Dashing about in his curricle and hunting mounts! Well, discourse is futile. Yet, when a young man, regardless of identity, courts a pretty girl and pledges matrimony, he lacks justification for renouncing his vow solely due to financial decline and the availability of a richer prospect. Why not, in such an instance, liquidate his equine assets, lease his estate, dismiss his staff, and implement a comprehensive restructuring immediately? I wager Miss Marianne would have waited for circumstances to align. But that strategy fails in the modern era; no aspect of pleasure can be relinquished by the youth of this generation."

"Do you ascertain the nature of Miss Grey? Is she reputed amiable?"

"I have heard no malice regarding her; indeed, she is rarely discussed, save that Mrs. Taylor remarked this morning Miss Walker hinted to her that Mr. and Mrs. Ellison would welcome Miss Grey's marriage, noting their inability to coexist harmoniously."

"And who constitute the Ellisons?"

"Her guardians, my dear. Though now of age, she may elect her own path; and what a selection she has made!—What now," pausing briefly, "your afflicted sister has retired to her chambers, presumably to lament in solitude. Can we procure anything to console her? Poor dear, it seems cruel to permit isolation. Well, shortly we shall host acquaintances, offering slight amusement. What shall we play? She detests whist, I know; does she prefer any round games?"

"Dear madam, such kindness is superfluous. Marianne, I suspect, will not emerge from her room again this evening. I shall endeavor to persuade her to retire early, for she clearly requires repose."

"Aye, that proves optimal. Permit her to designate her own supper and retire. Lord! No wonder she has appeared so debilitated and dejected over the past fortnight; this matter has undoubtedly loomed over her head during that interval. And thus, the letter received today finalized the tragedy! Poor soul! I assure you, had I suspected the truth, I would not have jesting about it for all my fortune. Yet, you understand, how could I divine such a development? I assumed it was merely a commonplace love letter, knowing young people delight in being mocked regarding such affairs. Lord! How distressed Sir John and my daughters will be upon learning this! If I possessed my faculties, I might have visited Conduit Street en route home to inform them. I shall see them tomorrow."

"It would be unnecessary for you to caution Mrs. Palmer and Sir John against mentioning Mr. Willoughby or alluding to the preceding events before my sister. Their inherent benevolence will intuitively recognize the brutality of feigning knowledge of the matter in her presence. The fewer words spoken to me concerning this subject, the more my feelings will be spared, as you, my dear madam, will readily concede."

"Oh! Lord! Yes, I truly do. It must be dreadful for you to hear it discussed; and regarding your sister, I assure you, I would not utter a single word to her for the world. You observed I remained silent throughout dinner. Neither would Sir John, nor my daughters, for they are all remarkably thoughtful and considerate, particularly if I offer a hint, which I certainly shall. Personally, I believe the less discourse devoted to such matters, the better; the sooner it blows over and vanishes. What utility does talking ever serve, you know?"

"In this specific affair, it serves only harm; potentially more so than in many analogous cases, for it has been accompanied by circumstances rendering it unfit for public conversation for the sake of all involved. I must accord this justice to Mr. Willoughby—he has breached no explicit engagement with my sister."

"Law, my dear! Cease defending him. No explicit engagement indeed! After touring her extensively through Allenham House and selecting the very chambers wherein they were to reside henceforth!"

For her sister's sake, Elinor could not press the subject further, hoping it was not required for Willoughby's benefit; though Marianne might suffer greatly, he could gain little by enforcing the absolute truth. Following a brief silence, Mrs. Jennings, with her innate hilarity, erupted anew.

"Well, my dear, 'tis a veritable adage regarding an ill wind; it shall prove advantageous for Colonel Brandon. He shall secure her eventually; ay, he shall. Mark my words, if they are not united by Midsummer. Lord! How he shall chuckle over this tidings! I trust he arrives tonight. It matters not to one whether it is a superior match for your sister. Two thousand a year, free of debt or deduction—save for the minor love-child, indeed; ay, I forgot her; yet she may be apprenticed out at minimal cost, and then what significance does it hold? Delaford is a charming locale, I can attest; precisely what I term a pleasant, old-fashioned setting, brimming with comforts and conveniences; completely enclosed by substantial garden walls adorned with the finest fruit trees in the nation; and such a mulberry tree in one corner!
