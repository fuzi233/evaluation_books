A primal rhythm stirred within Buck, a restive beating against the bonds of civilized routine. The dormant, wild blood within him began to stir, waking from a long, comfortable hibernation. If Buck had possessed the capacity to read the broadsheets, he would have discerned the gathering storm: a tempest not merely threatening his own existence, but sweeping across the entire coast from Puget Sound to San Diego, targeting every robust, woolly dog of the tide waters. Men, lost in the blinding white void of the Arctic, had stumbled upon gold. The news spread like wildfire, drawing thousands northward in a frantic rush, seeking heavy-hauled beasts with powerful limbs and thick fur to endure the biting frost.

Buck, however, lived in the sun-drenched Santa Clara Valley, the domain of Judge Miller. His estate was a sprawling kingdom, nestled back from the road, half-concealed by ancient trees that framed glimpses of a wide, cool veranda encircling the mansion. Gravel drives wound through expansive lawns shaded by towering poplars, leading to a rear section that rivaled the front in grandeur. Great stables housed a dozen grooms and pages; rows of vine-covered servants' cottages stretched endlessly alongside orderly outhouses, grape arbors, verdant pastures, orchards, and berry fields. There was even a pumping plant for the artesian well and a massive cement tank where the Judge's sons cooled themselves in the sweltering heat.

Over this immense territory, Buck reigned supreme. Born there, he had spent four years as its undisputed sovereign. While other dogs existed—the populous kennel dwellers, the obscure Toobs and Ysabells who rarely ventured outdoors, or the pack of fox terriers who yelped warnings from behind glass windows—they were subjects, not rivals. Buck was neither a domestic pet nor a kennel inmate. He commanded the realm. He dove into the swimming tank, hunted alongside the Judge's sons, escorted the young ladies Mollie and Alice on twilight jaunts, and sprawled before the roaring library fires on winter nights. He bore the grandchildren on his back, rolled them in the grass, and guarded their footsteps through wild escapades down to the stable fountain and beyond into the paddocks. Among the terriers, he stalked with imperial disdain; regarding the exotic pugs and hairless Mexicans, he offered a total, regal silence. For Buck was King, lord over all living things on Judge Miller's grounds, human inhabitants included.

His father, Elmo, a colossal St. Bernard, had been the Judge's shadow, and Buck seemed destined to tread the same royal path. Yet Buck was smaller, weighing only one hundred and forty pounds, owing to his mother, Shep, a sturdy Scotch Shepherd. Still, those one hundred and forty pounds, bolstered by the dignified bearing of a life well-lived and universal respect, allowed him to move with kingly grace. For four years, he had lived as a satiated aristocrat, possessing a fine, perhaps slightly inflated, pride typical of country gentlemen insulated by wealth. However, he had avoided the fate of a mere pampered lapdog. The joys of the hunt and the invigorating dip in the cold tubs had kept his muscles lean and hard, his spirit sharp. Thus, in the autumn of 1897, amidst the global frenzy of the Klondike strike, Buck remained the picture of canine contentment, unaware of the shadows lengthening beneath his paradise.

He did not know that Manuel, a gardener's helper, was a dangerous acquaintance. Manuel suffered from a singular vice: a love for the Chinese lottery. Worse still, he possessed a fatal flaw common to gamblers—a blind faith in a "system." Such belief required capital, yet a gardener's wages barely covered the needs of a wife and numerous children.

On a memorable night, while the Judge attended a Raisin Growers' Association meeting and the boys organized an athletic club, Manuel committed his treachery. Unseen save by a solitary man, Buck and Manuel slipped through the orchard. Buck believed it a simple stroll. They emerged at the tiny flag stop of College Park. There, the stranger spoke gruffly, advising Manuel to wrap the cargo carefully before delivery. Manuel complied, wrapping a length of stout rope around Buck's neck, sliding it beneath his collar.

"Twist it tight enough, and you'll choke him plenty," Manuel advised.
The stranger nodded, a ready affirmation.

Buck accepted the rope with quiet dignity. He trusted the men he knew and assumed they possessed a wisdom surpassing his own. When the ends of the rope fell into the stranger's hands, Buck growled menacingly, his pride dictating that an intimation was sufficient to command compliance. Instead, the rope tightened instantly, strangling his breath. Rage surged through him. He lunged at the man, who intercepted him, clamped a hand around his throat, and executed a deft throw, sending Buck crashing onto his back. The rope cinched mercilessly. Consciousness returned as a dull ache in his tongue and a violent jolting motion. The shrill whistle of a locomotive signaled a crossing. Riding with the Judge was familiar, but this was different. Anger, unbridled and royal, flooded his heart. When the man lunged for his throat, Buck reacted with lightning speed, snapping jaws shut on the hand. Sensory deprivation followed until the grip loosened.

"Yes, he gets fits," the man muttered, concealing his mangled hand from the baggageman drawn by the commotion. "Taking him up to 'Frisco. A crack dog-doctor there says he can cure him."

His hand was wrapped in a bloody rag; his trousers were torn from knee to ankle. When questioned by the saloon-keeper about the accomplice, the reply was "A hundred." The keeper, calculating the profit, deemed the risk justified. The man, examining his bleeding palm, worried about rabies, but the saloon-keeper laughed, urging him to load the freight.

Dazed, suffering excruciating pain in his throat and tongue, Buck attempted to confront his tormentors. He was thrown down and choked repeatedly until they managed to file off the heavy brass collar from his neck. The rope was cut, and he was flung into a cage-like crate.

Inside, he lay through the weary night, nurturing his wrath and wounded pride. Confusion clouded his mind. Who were these strange men? Why confine him? A vague, oppressive dread of impending doom settled over him. Whenever the shed door rattled, he leaped to his feet, expecting the Judge or the boys. Instead, the sickly glow of a tallow candle revealed the saloon-keeper's bulbous face. The joyous bark trapped in Buck's throat twisted into a savage growl. The keeper, indifferent, left him alone.

At dawn, four men entered, lifting the crate. To Buck, they were more tormentors—ragged, unkempt, evil-looking. He stormed and raged at them through the bars, only to realize when they poked sticks at him that biting was the expected response. Sullenly, he lay down as the crate was hoisted into a wagon.

Thus began a journey through many hands. Express clerks, trucks, ferry steamers, railway depots, and express cars marked his path. For two days and nights, the express car rattled at the tail of shrieking locomotives. Buck ate nothing and drank nothing. His initial growls at the express messengers earned him teasing, mockery, and taunts. They mimicked dogs, mewled, and flapped their arms. Silly, he thought, yet infinitely insulting to his dignity. His anger burned hotter than his hunger. High-strung and sensitive, the mistreatment sent him into a fever, exacerbated by the inflammation of his parched, swollen throat.

He was relieved the rope was gone; it had given them an unfair edge. Now, he vowed, he would make them pay. Two days and nights of torment forged a reservoir of hatred that promised destruction to anyone who crossed him. His eyes turned bloodshot, transforming him into a raging fiend. So altered was he that the Judge himself would not recognize him. The messengers breathed a sigh of relief when they unloaded him at Seattle.

He had been ripped from the heart of civilization and dumped into a chaos where peace, rest, and safety were non-existent concepts. Buck had never witnessed dog fights like those of these wolf-like creatures until that fateful morning. It was a vicarious tragedy, for if he had not been present, he might not have survived to learn its lesson. Near the log store, Curly, a friendly soul, attempted to approach a massive husky nearly twice her size. There was no warning—only a lightning-fast leap, a clatter of teeth, and a retreat. The speed and cruelty of the slaughter stunned Buck. Before the shock of Curly's death faded, François strapped Buck into a harness. Under this harsh, combined discipline, Buck learned rapidly. "T'ree vair' good dogs," François told Perrault, the team's driver. "Dat Buck, heem pool lak hell. I tich heem queek as anyt'ing."

By afternoon, Perrault arrived with two new additions: brothers named Billee and Joe. Though sons of the same mother, they were opposites. Billee tried to appease Spitz with wags and whimpers, fleeing when that failed, crying softly as sharp teeth scored his flank. Joe, however, presented a terrifying visage. Whenever Spitz circled, Joe spun on his heels to face him, mane bristling, ears pinned back, lips writhing, jaws snapping with diabolical intensity. He was the embodiment of belligerent fear, so formidable that Spitz was forced to abandon his attack. Covering his humiliation, Spitz turned on the harmless, weeping Billee and drove him back toward camp.

Later that evening, Perrault brought in a ninth dog: Sol-leks, the Angry One. Long, lean, and gaunt, he bore the scars of battles and possessed a single eye that warned of prowess. Like Dave, he demanded nothing and expected nothing, marching into the camp with such deliberate authority that even Spitz left him alone. However, Sol-leks had a fatal flaw: he detested having anyone approach his blind side. Buck, unwittingly guilty, allowed himself to be caught on Sol-leks's vulnerable flank. The punishment was immediate—a slash from Sol-leks's claws that opened Buck's shoulder to the bone. For the rest of their journey, Buck kept a respectful distance from Sol-leks's rear, avoiding any further trouble.

That night, Buck faced the ultimate challenge of survival: sleep. The camp tent glowed warmly under a candle, but when Buck entered, Perrault and François bombarded him with curses and kitchen utensils, driving him back into the freezing cold. A biting wind chilled him, especially where his wound lay. Lying on the snow, he tried to rest, but the frost soon drove him upright. Miserable and shivering, he wandered among the tents, finding no warmth. Savage dogs lunged at him, but he bristled and snarled, honing his defensive instincts, and they retreated.

An idea struck him. Returning to the tent area, he found his teammates missing. Confused and forlorn, he circled the tent until the snow gave way beneath his paws, revealing something moving below. Another lesson learned. Buck selected a spot, dug a hole with frantic efficiency, and collapsed inside. Soon, his own body heat created a sanctuary, and he slept soundly through the long, arduous day, wrestling with nightmares in his slumber.

Morning brought a new terror. Roused by the noise of the waking camp, Buck blinked into a white world. Snow had fallen during the night, burying him completely. The walls of snow pressed in on every side, triggering a primal, ancestral fear—the fear of the wild beast trapped in a den. Muscles contracted spasmodically, fur stood on end, and with a ferocious snarl, he exploded upward, bounding free into the blinding daylight. Snow flew as he shook himself off, realizing instantly where he was and recalling the events of the previous twenty-four hours.

"Wot I say?" François shouted, spotting Buck emerging. "Dat Buck for sure learn queek as anyt'ing."

Perrault nodded gravely. As a government courier carrying vital dispatches, he needed the best possible team, and Buck's rapid adaptation pleased him immensely. Within an hour, three more huskies joined the team, bringing the total to nine. Harnessing and ready, they swung up the trail toward Dyea Canyon. Buck, relieved to be moving, found the hard labor surprisingly engaging. The entire team was animated by a fierce energy, a transformation wrought by the harness. The formation was set: Dave as wheeler, followed by Buck, then Sol-leks, with the remaining dogs strung out ahead, led by Spitz. Buck had been strategically placed between Dave and Sol-leks to receive direct instruction. Both were apt teachers, correcting his errors swiftly with sharp teeth. Dave was fair, nipping only when necessary, while Sol-leks's wrath was absolute. Once, during a halt, Buck tangled in the traces, causing a dangerous delay. Both Dave and Sol-leks flew at him, delivering a sound trouncing. The resulting tangle was severe, but Buck learned quickly to keep the lines clear. By day's end, the nagging had subsided, and Perrault even paused to examine Buck's hooves with care.

Their journey was grueling. They climbed the Canyon, traversed Sheep Camp, crossed the Scales and the timberline, and scaled glaciers and drifts hundreds of feet deep. That night, Buck made his snow-hole and slept deeply, only to be roused in the dark to re-harness for another leg of the trip.

For days, they broke their own trail, working harder and slower through the deep powder. Perrault traveled ahead, packing the snow with webbed shoes, while François guided the sled at the gee-pole. They covered forty miles on the packed trail before hitting the broken ground, where progress slowed considerably. Day after day, the cycle repeated: breaking camp in the dark, hitting the trail at dawn, pitching camp after dusk.

Thirty days after leaving Dawson, the Salt Water Mail, with Buck and his companions at the lead, stumbled into Skaguay. They were a wreck, utterly drained. Buck, once a sturdy one hundred and forty pounds, had shrunk to one hundred and fifteen. His mates, though naturally lighter, had shed proportionately more. Pike, the habitual liar, now limped with genuine injury; Sol-leks hobbled, and Dub bore a torn shoulder blade.

Every step was a labor of agony. Their feet fell heavily, jarred their bodies, and doubled the exhaustion of the day. It was not the fatigue born of sudden, frantic exertion, from which recovery takes hours. It was the slow, relentless draining of months' toil, a "dead tired" that consumed the last reserve of strength. Every muscle, fiber, and cell was spent. In less than five months, they had traversed twenty-five hundred miles, resting for only five days. Arriving at Skaguay, they appeared to be on their last legs, barely managing to keep the traces taut as they tottered down the main street.

"Mush on, poor sore feets," the driver coaxed, his voice flat. "Dis is de las'. Den we get one long res'. Eh? For sure. One bully long res'."

The drivers, having covered twelve hundred miles with merely two days' rest, believed justice and reason demanded a long stopover. Yet, the mail was clogged with Alpine congestion due to the flood of prospectors rushing north, leaving behind families who stayed home. Official orders mandated that fresh, vigorous Hudson Bay dogs replace the worn-out, worthless teams. The old dogs, counting for little against dollars, were to be sold.

Three days passed, confirming the depth of their weakness. On the fourth morning, two men from the States approached and purchased the team, harness included, for a song. One was named Charles, a middle-aged man with watery eyes and a mustache that curled fiercely upward, defying the drooping lip beneath. Both men were hopelessly out of place, mysteries that baffled understanding.

Buck observed the transaction, sensing that the Scotch half-breeds, Perrault, François, and the former drivers were fading from his life. Driven to the new owners' camp, Buck saw a scene of slovenly disorder: a tent half-stretched, unwashed dishes, and chaos everywhere. There was a woman, whom they called "Mercedes"—Charles's wife and Hal's sister.

His sister and brother-in-law supported him, but their heavy outfits and incompetence frustrated every attempt to increase travel time. They could not feed the dogs enough, yet they could not make them run faster. Worse, they did not know how to work themselves, delaying starts until daylight faded.

Charles and Hal wrangled constantly, each convinced he did more than his share, dragging in arguments about art, politics, and distant relatives. By the time Buck stumbled upon him, Thornton was already healing, though a slight limp lingered. Now, lying by the riverbank during the long, languid days of spring, watching the water flow and listening to the birdsong, Buck slowly regained his strength. As his wounds scarred over and muscle swelled beneath returning flesh, Buck grew lazy, content to loaf alongside Thornton, Skeet, and Nig, waiting for the raft that promised a journey south.

Skeet, a small Irish Setter, had been the first friend Buck made in his dying state, unable to resist her gentle advances. Older than the days he had known, Buck linked the past with the present, swaying to a mighty rhythm that pulsed through him like the tides. When Buck secured sixteen hundred dollars for John Thornton in a single afternoon, he unlocked a future that promised both freedom and adventure. With those funds, Thornton settled his debts and joined his partners, Hans and Pete, on a perilous expedition eastward in search of a legendary lost mine. This mine was a subject of ancient lore, shrouded in tragedy and mystery, marking a site where dying men had once sworn oaths backed by gold of a unique, unrecognizable grade. While few had succeeded in finding it, many had vanished forever into its treacherous embrace. Now, Thornton, accompanied by Buck and a team of loyal dogs, ventured onto an unknown trail, hoping to claim what others had failed to achieve.

Their journey took them seventy miles upstream along the Yukon, then west into the Stewart River. They navigated past the Mayo and McQuestion rapids, following the river until it narrowed into a gentle streamlet winding through towering peaks that formed the continent's spine. Thornton was a man who lived lightly in relation to both humanity and nature. Unafraid of the wilderness, armed only with a rifle and a pouch of salt, he traveled with the unhurried rhythm of an Indian hunter. If the hunt yielded no meal, he pressed on, trusting that sustenance would eventually appear. Their sled loads consisted primarily of ammunition and tools, while their provisions varied from starvation to riotous feasting depending entirely on the luck of the hunt.

For Buck, this existence was pure delight. Weeks were spent traversing strange landscapes, camping in frozen muck, and panning for dirt. They rafted across alpine lakes and paddled slender canoes carved from standing timber down unknown rivers. The seasons shifted rapidly; they crossed mountain passes in summer blizzards, endured the midnight sun on exposed ridges, and foraged for strawberries in glacier-shaded valleys. By autumn, they entered a desolate, silent realm of weird lakes where wildfowl had once dwelt but now left no sign of life, save for the chilling winds and the melancholy ripple of icy waves.

Through another winter, they tracked the obliterated paths of previous explorers. At one point, they discovered an ancient blaze leading toward what seemed like the Lost Cabin, yet the trail emerged from nowhere and vanished into nothing, preserving its mystery alongside the man who made it. Later, amidst the rotting debris of an abandoned hunting lodge, Thornton unearthed a long-barreled flint-lock—a relic of the early Hudson Bay Company era, valued then as highly as a man's height in beaver skins. Eventually, spring returned, and their wanderings concluded not with the mythical cabin, but with a shallow placer in a broad valley. Gold lay there like yellow butter at the bottom of the pan. They worked tirelessly, earning thousands daily, sacking the dust and nuggets into moose-hide bags and piling them beside their spruce-bough lodge. Now, the heavy labor ceased. Buck's duties reduced to occasional hauling of meat, leaving him ample time to muse by the fire. The vision of the "short-legged hairy man" from his past returned with renewed frequency. In those mental projections, fear was paramount. The hairy man slept restlessly, peering into the dark with terrified eyes, ready to flee at the slightest sign of danger. Whether walking a beach gathering shellfish or creeping through the forest, they moved with hyper-vigilance. The man could swing effortlessly through the canopy, a creature as at home in the trees as on the ground, often roosting high above the earth to spend nights in vigil.

Closely intertwined with these visions was the Call, a mysterious resonance emanating from the depths of the forest. It stirred a vague, sweet gladness within Buck, awakening wild yearnings for something he could not name. He began pursuing it, barking softly or defiantly, thrusting his nose into cool moss and rich soil, or crouching behind fallen logs, wide-eyed and attentive. Impulses seized him uncontrollably; he would dash from a lazy doze into the forest aisles, running for hours through the undergrowth and across open spaces where niggerheads clustered. He relished the twilight of summer midnights, listening to the forest's sleepy murmurs, deciphering signs and sounds as one reads a book, searching for the source of the summons.

From the forest came the Call, distinct and undeniable—a long, drawn howl, neither exactly like nor unlike any noise a husky dog might make. The blood-lust within him intensified. He became a killer, a predator living solely by virtue of his own strength and prowess, thriving in a hostile environment where only the strong survive.
