Here is my rewritten version of Chapter II from *The Cloister and the Hearth*, tailored for a modern reader while preserving the historical tone and charm of the original:

---

“The soup is hot,” said Gerard.

“But how are we to get it to our mouths?” sighed the old scholar, his shoulders drooping.

Margaret’s eyes sparkled with quiet amusement. “Father, the young man has brought us straws.”

“Ah, yes,” the old man murmured, “but my bones are stiff, and the fire is too fierce to kneel beside with such short reeds. By St. John the Baptist, the lad is clever!”

For while he spoke, Gerard had already solved the problem. With swift fingers, he untied the knot at his breast, removed his hat, and placed a stone in each corner. Then, wrapping his hand in the hem of his jerkin, he deftly lifted the flask from the flames, nestled it between the stones, and presented the makeshift bowl beneath the old man’s nose with a cheerful grin. Trembling, the scholar bent forward and sipped through the straw.

His face, drawn and weary, transformed before their eyes—color returning, his gaze brightening. He drew a long breath and exhaled triumphantly.

“Hippocrates and Galen!” he declared. “A *soupe au vin*—the very elixir of life! Blessed be the nation that conceived it, the woman who brewed it, and the young man who brings it to starving souls. Here, child, take your turn while I enlighten our benefactor on the virtues of this sovereign remedy.”

Margaret pressed the hat into his hands. “Father, spare us the lecture—at least until the soup is drunk!”

The old man chuckled but obliged, and soon the meal was finished. As they prepared to resume their journey, Gerard found himself fumbling with the ribbon Catherine had tied. Margaret watched his struggles with quiet amusement before offering her help.

Her fingers, deft and graceful, worked the stubborn knot, her auburn hair glinting silver in the fading light. Gerard stood motionless, a strange warmth spreading through him as her touch lingered—a sensation entirely new, as if the world had shifted beneath his feet.

When at last she stepped back to admire her handiwork, she found his gaze fixed upon her with such intensity that she faltered, cheeks flushing, and retreated to her father’s side.

They entered Rotterdam through the Schiedamze Poort, but their paths soon diverged—Gerard bound for the Hooch Straet and the Stadthouse, while the scholar and his daughter turned toward the Ooster-Waagen Straet. Their farewells were swift, and Gerard was left adrift in the bustling city, his heart heavy with sudden loneliness.

*I shall never see her again,* he thought bitterly. *What cruel fate brings beauty, wit, and kindness across my path only to snatch them away?*

Distracted, he wandered until a crowd drew him toward a meadow by the Maas, where games and revelry unfolded. Jugglers, wrestlers, and trained animals vied for attention, but Gerard’s thoughts were elsewhere. Hearing that the Duke had retired to the Stadthouse, he hastened there—only to find the gates barred by a pompous official.

Then, to his astonishment, he spotted Margaret and her father pleading for entry.

“My cousin the alderman is within,” the old man insisted, offering a written plea.

The gatekeeper sneered. “I am no messenger. Stand aside.”

Margaret turned, her voice trembling. “Good people, we have traveled far. Must we beg at the door like strangers?”

The crowd jeered.

Before she could shrink further, Gerard seized her hand.

She gasped—a sound of relief, of startled joy—and clutched his fingers tightly.

A bystander laughed. “Ho! She’s found her sweetheart!”

Margaret whirled, eyes blazing. “I have no sweetheart! But I know a true friend when I see one—which is more than you louts understand!”

The crowd fell silent, chastened.

Gerard seized the moment. “I am a competitor,” he announced.

The gatekeeper scowled but consulted his list. “Gerard Eliassoen may enter.”

“Not without my friends.”

“They are not your company.”

“Then I stay.”

A stalemate—until Gerard, with a grin, bellowed at the top of his lungs:

The gatekeeper blanched. “Madman! The Duke is within earshot!”

“Then open the gate,” Gerard said sweetly.

With a frantic wave, the porter ushered them through, and they emerged into a scene of dazzling opulence—tables laden with delicacies, guests in silks and jewels, music weaving through the air.

The Duke himself, seated high above, acknowledged them with a nod.

As they feasted—on roasted boars with gleaming tusks, sugared castles, wines like liquid rubies—Gerard found himself lost in Margaret’s presence. The world around them faded; even the food held no taste compared to the pleasure of her company.

“What do you admire most here?” she asked softly.

He did not hesitate. “You.”

She flushed, but did not pull away.

And as the minstrels played and the fountain sparkled, Gerard knew one truth with absolute certainty:

This was no world of sin and misery.

Not while she was by his side.
