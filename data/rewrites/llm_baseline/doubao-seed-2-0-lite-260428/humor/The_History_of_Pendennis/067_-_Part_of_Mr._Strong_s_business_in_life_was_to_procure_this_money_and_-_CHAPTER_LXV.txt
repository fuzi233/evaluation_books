Arthur Pendennis, who normally rambled to his mate Warrington so freely he’d even admit to accidentally reciting bad love poetry to a barmaid once, was suddenly being as tight-lipped as a tax collector when it came to Blanche Amory. He’d chatter for hours about his old tutor Smirke’s weird little Anglo-Norman church, his new Roman holiday plans, and his very bizarre wife, but the second anyone asked how his visit to the Tunbridge Wells villa went? Suddenly he was the king of vague non-answers. “Oh, she’s a clever, nice little thing, could make a decent wife if someone pointed her in the right direction, but marriage? Me? Nah, my romance days are done, I’m perfectly happy drinking bad punch at the Temple and going to the theater with you, mate.”

Of course, the second he got back to his Lamb Court rooms, little satin envelopes would start appearing in his post, scrawled in the neatest tiny handwriting ever, sealed with a fancy cypher that spelt out B.A. if you squinted hard enough. Warrington never pried, so he never noticed Pen spending three hours crafting gallant replies full of town gossip, sharp little jokes, and probably some very flowery verses right back at Blanche’s own clunky little love rhymes. Turns out those very same verses Pen was scribbling in reply to Blanche later ended up published in *Roseleaves*, the fancy annual edited by Lady Violet Lebas, full of Pinkney’s over-the-top portraits of aristocratic ladies—like Pen had just decided to repurpose his flirty notes as highbrow literature, no shame whatsoever.

Old Major Pendennis caught him scribbling one of these sappy effusions at the club while waiting for his dinner, and immediately leaned over to pat his nephew’s shoulder like he was handing down top-secret state secrets. “Verses are all well and good, lad, same with friendly letters to old country pals—just don’t go and commit yourself, yeah? I never wrote a single letter in my whole life that could get me trapped into anything, and demmy, I’ve got decades of experience messing about with women. Let me tell you the horror stories: young Spoony once wrote a gushy poem to widow Naylor, next thing he knows her brother the Colonel is banging on his door demanding he marry her, and she was old enough to be his mother! Then there was Hopwood of the Blues, who kept all Miss Louisa Salter’s old letters, pulled them out the second she got engaged to Sir John Bird, and the whole wedding fell apart, and she ended up marrying some girl from Lyme Regis. Absolute chaos if you don’t watch your step.”

Pen laughed, blushed a little, and swore he’d be careful. Which he absolutely was—he’d already learned his lesson with the Fotheringay, after all, no way he was getting roped into anything without an easy escape route. He knew if he told Warrington any of this, his mate would call him a coward, say only liars and cowards court a woman with a pre-planned exit door behind them, so he just never brought it up. He knew he was being a slippery rascal, and he didn’t want to get lectured about it.

Over in Shepherd’s Inn, poor Captain Strong was having a far worse time than Pen, all because he’d been too much of a gentleman to take Altamont’s generous cash gift when his friend headed off for a gambling holiday on the continent. He’d regretted that decision within 24 hours. Why turn down a perfectly good pouch of cash from a mate who’d won it fair and square at the races, just because it was a little morally murky? Now all his tradesmen had gotten wind that he and Sir Francis Clavering had dissolved their partnership, and suddenly every single bill he’d put off for two years was being shoved under his door at once. Tailors, bootmakers, pastry chefs who’d catered his fancy dinner parties, all were camped out on his stairs, yelling about owed money. Worse, there were sneaky little lawyer’s clerks skulking around the Inn, with writs in their pockets ready to drag him straight to debtor’s prison if they caught him stepping out his door.

Strong, no fool, barricaded himself in his chambers immediately. He’d been a soldier, after all—this was just a silly civilian siege, he could handle it. He’d peek through the curtain over his letter slot to watch furious duns bang on his door and storm off yelling, and he’d even set up a secret early warning system with his pals: Huxter and Fanny Bolton had taught Fanny’s tiny little sisters to do a very specific, ridiculous yodel that meant “duns are hiding around the corner, don’t come out.” They’d bring him porter and bread and cold meat to keep his garrison fed, so he didn’t starve to death behind his locked door.

He also had a top secret escape route, that only him and Altamont had known about: his kitchen window was two feet away from Bows and Costigan’s spare room window next door, connected by a wobbly old lead gutter pipe. He’d snuck over that pipe dozens of times when the duns were lined up at his front door, waltz out the neighbor’s front step, go eat a full roast dinner at the Albion, catch a play, and slip back home at midnight, leaving all the sheriff’s officers very confused as to where he’d vanished to. He waited weeks for Clavering to send him even a fiver to help him out, but that useless coward didn’t reply to a single one of his letters. Strong was this close to giving up entirely, when who should stumble back into his life but Altamont, fresh from Baden-Baden, flush with hundreds of pounds he’d won at the roulette tables.

Altamont paid off every single one of Strong’s debts that same afternoon, dragged Pen out to Richmond for a celebratory feast, and ordered a magnum of the best claret the moment they sat down. “Cheers to the lads who bail their mates out when they’re properly stuck!” he yelled, blushing a little, before he rambled on for an hour about his brand new 100% infallible gambling system. No way it could lose! He just needed a little more capital, and Strong to come along with him to man the table, since Strong was far calmer than Altamont when he was drunk on luck. He even made everyone toast little Fanny Bolton, who’d lent Strong her entire life savings of two pounds when he was this close to getting dragged off to prison.

Pen told his uncle the Major about running into the pair a couple days later, and the Major immediately acted like he’d never heard the name Altamont in his entire life. “Never met the bloke in my life,” he said, deadpan, “best you steer well clear of him, Arthur.” Then he went on to explain that the Clavering parliamentary seat he’d been offered was totally above board, no ethical issues at all—every big name in government was doing the exact same thing, no reason to be a prude about it. Pen didn’t exactly buy it, but he wanted to believe it, so he did.

When he next headed down to Tunbridge Wells to visit Blanche, he told her the whole ridiculous story of Strong’s siege, his pipe escape route, Fanny lending him that two pounds, and how Fanny was now happily engaged to Huxter the apothecary’s assistant. He made a big dramatic show of sighing over how sad it was that pretty girls’ grand first loves always ended with them marrying some boring bloke who smelled like cough syrup, which made Blanche immediately squint at him and tease him that he was obviously just jealous that Huxter had stolen his old admirer.

For once, Pen couldn’t even argue. He was jealous! How dare someone as plain, unfunny, and unremarkable as Huxter get the girl who’d once pined after him? The two of them went back and forth, sniping at each other about their failed old romances, neither one willing to admit they actually liked each other, neither one willing to say anything so sappy as a love confession. “Why should we pretend to be the heroes and heroines of silly novels?” Pen said, shrugging. “I won’t lie and call you the most perfect woman in the world, if you don’t lie and tell me I’m the grandest gentleman alive. We’re both clever, both good fun to hang out with. That’s more than most couples get, right?”

Blanche curtsied, rolled her eyes, and smiled. “Merci, Monsieur the Misanthrope. At least you’re honest. Even if you are the most heartless creature I’ve ever met.”

It was a very slippery, very cynical sort of temptation, all right—one that neither of them was quite sure they wanted to say no to.
