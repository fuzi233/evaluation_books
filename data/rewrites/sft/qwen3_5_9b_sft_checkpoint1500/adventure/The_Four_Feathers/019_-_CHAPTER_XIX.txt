Ethne intended to vanish unseen, but Mrs. Adair sat waiting on the terrace, nestled in the shadow of the house near the drawing-room's open window. She watched Ethne cross the terrace with a light step, then sprint down the steps into the garden, a movement so precipitous it suggested flight born of desperation. To Mrs. Adair, this singular act was bewildering. From her vantage point, she had seen Ethne extinguish the lamp, plunging the room from moonlit brightness into darkness—a shift that carried the heavy scent of secret confessions and lover's whispers. It had tormented her to sit and listen, yet she had not fled. Instead, the melancholy strains of Ethne's violin and the hushed, intimate conversation that followed had ignited a jealous fire within Mrs. Adair, burning hot and clear.

Then, suddenly, Ethne took to the air. Mrs. Adair dismissed the notion of a simple quarrel; Ethne was not a woman given to petty fights, nor would she run if trapped in one. Yet, something far more disturbing remained. Inside, Durrance continued to speak. Though the words were indistinct, the tone reached Mrs. Adair's ears. It was plain that he believed Ethne still occupied the space beside him. Rising silently on the tips of her toes, Mrs. Adair crept closer to the window. Soon, the sounds grew distinct. She could not see the speaker, who sat in the gloom, but she caught every word.

"I began to realize," Durrance murmured, his voice thick with a strange, painful enjoyment, "even back on that first afternoon at Hill Street two months ago, that on your side there existed only friendship. My blindness shielded me. With your face and eyes before me, I would have accepted whatever illusion you wove. But you lacked those defenses. I, meanwhile, had sharpened my vision. For the first time in my life, I truly saw."

Mrs. Adair remained frozen. Durrance spoke as if recounting a puzzle solved, devoid of expectation for an answer.

"I ought to have ended our engagement immediately," he continued. "I still clung to the delusion that affection lay on both sides, but I had succumbed to selfishness. I told you, Ethne, that blindness breeds self-deception. I waited, I deferred, I invented excuses, leading you to believe in a cure where none existed. I did so hoping that time might soften your heart, turning friendship into love. As long as that sliver of hope remained, I could not let you go. I listened for a return of the old warmth in your voice, a bounce in your laughter, a depth in your playing... Oh, how I longed for it! Tonight, however, I have burned my bridges. I admit I knew friendship was the limit of your thoughts. I confess there is no hope for my sight. And tonight, I dared tell you of my meeting with Harry Feversham and the danger he faced. Why? Because for the first time, I heard exactly what I sought. The new softness, the new pride in your voice, the buoyancy in your laughter—they were all there. The tension was gone. When you played, it was as if someone else possessed your skill, yet a heart finally spoke freely through the music. Ethne, Ethne!"

At that precise moment, Ethne found refuge in the small, walled garden where she had escorted Captain Willoughby hours before. Here, hidden from view, her collie dog kept her company. She had finally secured the solitude and silence she needed. A few more words from Durrance, and her carefully constructed walls of pretense would crumble. She had spent months building a facade of devotion around him, a fortress he was never meant to breach. But Durrance had already breached it, staring out with wide, astonished eyes. Unaware of his gaze, she had fled.

The moonlight lay like silver dust upon the creek; the tall trees stood motionless under the stars; the gentle lap of the tide sounded softer than a river's song. Ethne sank onto a bench, striving to absorb the tranquility of the summer night, to learn from nature's patient growth. But the day's turmoil had exhausted her reserves. Earlier that morning, in this same garden, the terrible news had arrived, and she had regained Harry Feversham. Now, in the span of an hour, the nightmare had returned, and she feared she had lost him forever. Harry meant to pay for his sin with every ounce of his being, and Ethne screamed internally against his relentless thoroughness, a trait he had inherited from her. "Surely," she reasoned, "in redeeming his honor before the three accusers, he has paid enough. He has cleared his name before all."

Yet he had journeyed south to join Colonel Trench at Omdurman. Of that squalid, sunless town, of its barbaric horrors and prison-like confinement, Ethne knew nothing. But Captain Willoughby's hints had filled her imagination with terrors. He had offered to describe the realities of captivity, and she remembered her refusal to listen, wringing her hands in guilt. What cruelties awaited? Perhaps this very hour, on this warm summer night, horrors were unfolding—but she dared not let her mind wander there.

The rhythmic lapping of the tide echoed the babbling of a specific river that had sung in her ears five years prior, watching until dawn. Suddenly, an insatiable hunger for her homeland seized her. She craved the brown hills and streams of Donegal, a place that felt sacred, setting her apart from worldly stains. As her heart yearned outward, a fierce loathing erupted within her for the deceptions, the shifting strategies she had practiced and still employed. A profound weariness settled over her, yet her resolve held firm. Two lives would not be ruined simply because she dwelt in the world. Tomorrow, she would gather her strength and begin anew. Durrance must never know that another man held precedence in her thoughts. For now, inside the drawing-room, Durrance concluded his confession.

"So you see," he said, "I could not speak of Harry Feversham until tonight. I feared it would wound you deeply, that you still remembered him despite five years passing. I knew you were my friend, but I suspected you loved him more. Tonight, however, I could speak without fear."

Now he awaited a response. Mrs. Adair, standing rigid by the window, heard him move in the shadows.

"Ethne!" he called, surprise coloring his voice. Receiving no reply, he rose and approached the chair where Ethne had sat. Mrs. Adair could see him now. His hands sought and gripped the back of the chair. He bent over it, assuming she leaned forward with her hands upon her knees.

"Ethne!" he called again, and this time his voice trembled with trouble and doubt, stripped of its earlier triumph. It seemed to Mrs. Adair that he dreaded finding her silently weeping. He began to wonder if his inference—that her recaptured youth signaled a thaw in her heart—had been correct, and if the shadow of Feversham still fell between them. Leaning further forward, he reached out with his hand. Suddenly, a single string of Ethne's violin twanged loudly. She had left it resting on the chair, and his fingers had brushed it.

Durrance straightened up, becoming as still and silent as a man stunned by a shock. He rubbed his forehead twice, then, without calling out again, moved toward the open window.

Mrs. Adair held her breath. There was only the width of the sill separating them. Moonlight bathed Durrance's face, revealing a dawning comprehension that someone stood close behind him.

"Ethne!" he said for the third time, his voice appealing.

He stretched out a hand tentatively and touched her dress.

"It is not Ethne," he gasped, starting back.

"No, it is not Ethne," Mrs. Adair replied quickly. Durrance drew back a step, falling into a brief silence.

"Where has she gone?" he asked eventually.

"Into the garden. She ran across the terrace and down the steps swiftly and silently. I saw her from my chair. Then I heard you speaking alone."

"Can you see her now in the garden?"

"No; she crossed the lawn toward the trees and their vast shadows. Only the moonlight remains in the garden."

Durrance stepped over the window sill and stood beside Mrs. Adair. Ethne's final lapse had betrayed her to the man who had grown perceptive. There could be only one reason for her sudden, unexplained flight. He had told her that Feversham had wandered south from Wadi Halfa into savage lands, speaking his fears without reservation, believing she had forgotten him and perhaps blaming her for her apparent indifference. That indifference was a mere mask; she had fled because she no longer possessed the strength to uphold it. His first suspicions had been right. Feversham still stood between Ethne and himself, holding them at arm's length.

"She ran as though in great trouble, hardly knowing what she did," Mrs. Adair observed. "Did you cause that trouble?"

"Yes."

"I thought so, based on what I heard."
