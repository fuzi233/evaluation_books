The fire in the schloss hall spat thin, blue-edged smoke that stung our eyes when the messenger rode up at dusk, his horse lathered from ten leagues of hard travel out of Gratz. He was the grave, dark-bearded son of the local picture restorer, his cart stacked heavy with two great iron-hooped oaken crates crammed full of the old family portraits we’d sent away months prior to scrape a century’s worth of soot and woodsmoke from their canvases. In our isolated mountain hold, any visitor from the tiny nearby capital was a novelty; we all clustered in the entrance hall the moment his boots clomped over the cold stone threshold, hungry for scraps of news from the world beyond our pine woods.

The crates sat heavy and silent against the wall while the servants fed the man his supper, and by the time he trundled back in with chisels, hammers, and screwdrivers, we’d all gathered around the hearth to watch the unpacking. Carmilla slouched low in her velvet armchair, half-lidded eyes gliding over the flames, listless as a cat that had napped the whole afternoon away. One by one, the restorer pried the old portraits free of their linen wrappings: stiff dark canvases almost all of them, heirlooms from my mother’s ancient Hungarian line, my father checking each off against a crumpled list in his hand. Most I’d never properly seen before, so caked with decades of grime they’d been nothing but blurry dark blobs hanging on the walls of unused corridors.

“Ah, there’s one I’ve been half-curious to see,” my father said, tapping his list. “Top left corner, faint name Marcia Karnstein, dated 1698. It was so blackened I could never make out the subject.”

I remembered that small, nearly square unframed canvas, the size of a dinner table leaf, hidden away in a lumber room for as long as I could recall. The restorer fished it out of tissue paper a moment later, holding it aloft with obvious pride.

My breath caught in my throat. It wasn’t just restored. It breathed. The paint looked almost wet, the girl in the portrait smiling soft, like she was just about to step right out of the frame. It was Carmilla. Down to the very curl of her lip, the long dark lashes brushing her cheek, the tiny brown mole half-hidden in the curve of her throat.

“Carmilla, it’s a miracle!” I blurted before I even thought. “It’s you, exactly. Look, Papa—the little mole on her throat, you can’t tell them apart!”

My father laughed, but it didn’t reach his eyes. He glanced away fast, like forcing himself to be casual, and turned back to chat with the restorer about the finer points of the paint work, never lingering on the portrait the way I couldn’t stop myself from. The longer I stared, the more my skin prickled. The girl in the portrait was dressed in 17th century velvet, her hair coiled the exact lazy, unstudied way Carmilla always wore hers.

“Can I hang this in my bedroom, Papa?” I asked.

“Of course, my dear,” he smiled. “If you think it’s that good a likeness, it must be far prettier than I remembered.”

Carmilla didn’t so much as glance at the painting. She was leaning back in her chair, her eyes fixed fully on me, a strange, dreamy rapture softening her face, like she was seeing something no one else could.

I leaned in to squint at the writing in the corner, now perfectly clear, picked out in faded gold leaf, a tiny coronet etched above it. “That’s not Marcia. It says Mircalla, Countess Karnstein, Anno Domini 1698. Mama was descended from the Karnsteins, you know.”

Carmilla’s voice was slow, honey-thick, lazy. “So am I, I think. A very old, very long line. Are there any Karnsteins left living, do you know?”

“None that bear the name, far as I know,” I told her. “The family was ruined in some old civil war centuries ago. Their castle ruins are only three miles out in the woods.”

“Fascinating,” she murmured, not sounding half as interested as she should. She nodded at the hall door, propped half open to the night. “Look at that moonlight. Let’s take a little turn around the courtyard. We can see the river and the road winding down the valley.”

“It’s exactly the same kind of night you arrived here,” I said.

She sighed, a soft, shaking little sound, and stood. We looped our arms around each other’s waists, stepping out onto the cold flagstones. We walked slow, no sound but the faint crunch of our slippers on the gravel at the courtyard edge, out to the old drawbridge where the whole moon-drenched valley spread out below us.

“You were thinking about the night I came?” she whispered, her breath warm against my ear. “Are you glad I’m here?”

“More than glad, dear Carmilla,” I said.

“You asked for that portrait that looks like me, to hang by your bed,” she sighed, her arm tightening so tight around my waist it almost hurt, and she rested her heavy, perfumed head on my shoulder. “You’re so romantic, Carmilla,” I laughed, a little nervous. “I bet all your stories are full of some great old grand romance.”

She kissed me, soft and silent, her lips colder than they should have been.

“I’m certain you’ve been in love before,” I said, my voice wobbly. “That you’ve got some great secret affair going on right now.”

“I’ve never loved anyone,” she breathed right against my neck, her hand sliding into mine, “and I never will. Unless it’s you.”

She was so beautiful in the silver moonlight, her skin pale as bone, no shadow stretching behind her on the flagstones at all. She hid her face in my hair then, heaving great, tumultuous sighs that verged on sobbing, her hand trembling hard in mine, her cheek hot as a fever against mine.

“Darling, darling,” she murmured, “I live in you. You would die for me. I love you so much.”

I jolted back from her, my heart hammering so hard I could hear it in my ears.

Her face was blank. All the light, all the fire, all the warmth was gone. Her eyes were glassy, empty, her skin as colorless as a sheet of parchment.

“Is the air… chill?” she mumbled, drowsy, like she was just waking up from a very deep, very strange dream. “I’m shivering. Did I fall asleep? Let’s go back inside. Come. Come in now.”

“You look unwell, Carmilla,” I said, already nervous, reaching to steady her. “Faint. You must have a glass of warm wine.”

“Yes. That would be nice,” she said, slow, as we trundled back toward the hall door. “I’m fine now. I’ll be myself in a minute. Wait—let’s look out a second more. It might be the last time we see moonlight like this together.”

A cold prickle of fear crawled up my spine. The villagers had been whispering about a strange wasting sickness creeping through the nearby homesteads, making young girls fade and die for no doctor could name. “Papa would be heartbroken if you were sick and we didn’t call for help right away,” I told her. “We have a very skilled physician staying nearby, he saw Papa earlier today—”

“I know how kind you all are,” she cut me off, that lazy little smile back on her face, like nothing had ever happened. “I’m not sick. Just weak, that’s all. People say I’m too languid to exert myself, I can barely walk as far as a three year old. Sometimes all my little strength gives out, and I drift off like that. I bounce right back, see?”

It was true. By the time we got back to the fireside, she was chattering animatedly, laughing at my father’s little jokes, no trace left of that strange fevered moment out by the drawbridge. The rest of the evening slipped by quiet and uneventful, no more of her odd, passionate little fits to make me squirm or frighten me.

But that very night, something happened that turned all my quiet, unspoken worries sharp and new—something that jolted even Carmilla’s endless, sleepy languor into sudden, sharp, alive energy.
