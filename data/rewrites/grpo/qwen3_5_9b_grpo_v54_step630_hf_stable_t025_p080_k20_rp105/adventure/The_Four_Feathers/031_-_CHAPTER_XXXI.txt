The August sun beat down with the relentless heat of the Sudan, having etched deep lines into Harry Feversham's face and marked the years of his exile. Yet, as he rode up the narrow streets of Ramelton, unseen and unacknowledged, he moved like a ghost among ghosts. The intervening years had faded from his mind during his long journey across Egypt and Europe, becoming mere shadows compared to the vivid, painful reality of this small Irish town. It was here he had known his greatest joy and his deepest despair; it was here he had brought comfort and inflicted ruin.

Pausing briefly before the high church on his right, he wondered if Ethne remained in Ramelton, if her father, Old Dermod, was still alive, and what reception he might expect. But the morning air, filled with the song of larks and the harsh cry of a landrail, snapped him back to the present. He realized he was alone on his horse in the quiet street, surrounded only by phantoms of laughter and shared walks that no longer existed.

His path led him to a thick grove, a broken fence, and a gateway standing open to the weeds. Ignoring the signs of abandonment, he rode through, following a neglected drive that ended before a crumbling house. The sight of the roofless rooms and tumbling walls finally broke his trance. Dismounting, he tied his horse to a tree and rushed into the house, calling out. Silence answered him. Running from room to room, then down into the garden where the grass grew wild and flowers lay tangled, he confirmed the truth: no one lived here.

Returning to Ramelton, he checked into the local inn and ordered lunch. When the landlady informed him that Lennon House had burned down five years prior, detailing the ruin of the Eustace family and the death of Dermod Eustace, Feversham remained silent. He asked where Miss Eustace now resided.

"Glenalla," she replied, eager to share gossip about the village and the prospects of Miss Eustace's marriage. "It's a poor mountain spot, but perhaps you wish to visit her?"

"Yes," Feversham said, rising to leave. "Please have my horse brought around."

The landlady, undeterred, noted that a similarly dark-complexioned gentleman had visited a fortnight prior seeking the same address. Feversham dismissed the coincidence, paid for his meal, and rode out, telling the innkeeper he would not return to Ramelton.

Four miles on, he turned onto a track leading to a small bay of the lake, riding past white cottages into a purple hollow. The village of Glenalla appeared quiet and unplanned, scattered across the landscape until he reached a solitary house and, beyond it, a small grey church perched on a tiny plateau. A path of white shell-dust led to its wooden gate, where a grey collie barked.

Feversham stopped. The dog's markings were familiar. He dismounted, tethered his horse, and stepped into the churchyard. The collie sniffed him doubtfully before bursting into an exuberant welcome, jumping and whirling around him with sharp, excited cries. Feversham laughed, patting the animal, but his amusement vanished instantly as he looked toward the open church door.

Standing in the threshold was Ethne Eustace.

She did not move or speak as he approached. When he reached her, she simply said, "Harry."

The silence between them was heavy with six years of absence. To the casual observer, little had changed, but to them, the differences were stark. Feversham was thinner, his face wasted, bearing the signs of the hard life in the House of Stone. Yet, Ethne saw more than his physical toll. She noticed the disappearance of the timid diffidence that had once plagued him; he was now quietly self-possessed, neither arrogant nor insecure. He had faced a terrible trial and emerged knowing he had not failed.

"It is not all harm which has come of these years," Ethne said softly, her face lighting up. "They were not wasted."

Feversham thought of her lonely years in this remote village, a choice she would never have made otherwise. He looked into her face, noting the deepening character lines—the mark of suffering and wisdom.

"I am sorry," he said. "I did you a great wrong six years ago, and I need not."

She held out her hand. "Will you give it me, please?"

For a moment, he misunderstood. "That fourth feather," she clarified.

Feversham reached into his coat and shook two ostrich feathers into her palm. He offered her the larger one, but she insisted on both. There was no point in keeping Castleton's feather; it had served its purpose. He handed them over, and she clasped them tightly against her breast, smiling with a pride that surprised him.

"I have the four feathers now," she declared.

"Yes," Feversham replied. "All four. What will you do with them?"

"Do with them!" she laughed in scorn. "I shall do nothing with them. I shall keep them. I am very proud to have them to keep."

Just as she had once kept his portrait, she kept the feathers. While Feversham felt they were dead things, crushed by the weight of their history, Ethne held them as living tokens of redemption.

"Colonel Trench was here a fortnight ago," she mentioned casually. "He told me you were bringing it back to me."

"But he did not know of the fourth feather," Feversham corrected. "I never told any man that I had it."

"Yes," Ethne said seriously. "You told Colonel Trench on your first night in the House of Stone at Omdurman. He told me. I no longer hate him."

Feversham was glad to hear it, acknowledging Trench as a great friend. Ethne fell silent for a moment before recalling their drive from Ramelton years ago, when they had spoken of true friends—those recognized instantly and never lost, even in death.

"I said one could always be sure of such friends," she continued, her voice trembling slightly. "And you answered that one could make mistakes. Yes, I disagreed. I believed one might seem to make mistakes for a long time, but in the end, one would be proved right. I remembered those words clearly when Captain Willoughby brought me the first feather, filled with remorse. I remembered them again today, though I have no room left for remorse. I was right, you see. I should have clung to my faith. But I did not."

Her voice shook as she admitted her youthful haste and lack of understanding. She opened her hand to gaze at the white feathers one last time, then turned and walked inside the church. Feversham followed her, the weight of the past finally laid to rest.
