Life is a fickle parent; sometimes she adorns herself in jewels and fine feathers, striding up marble staircases with her chin high, never glancing down at the servants trailing behind her. Other times, she dresses in rags, wanders through shadowy alleyways, inhales the smoke of cheap spirits, and joins in the screaming chaos of the destitute. Yet today, she appeared as a thrifty matron—neat, capable, and brimming with cheerful morning thoughts and clever schemes to find abundance within scarcity.

Mrs. Meyrick's home was quiet. The front parlor overlooked the river, while the rear faced lush gardens, allowing the window to remain open even as Mrs. Meyrick read aloud to her daughters, refreshing the air in their modest double room where a single lamp and two candles burned. The candles sat on a separate table for Kate, who was sketching illustrations for a publisher; the lamp served not only the reader but also Amy and Mab, who were embroidering satin cushions destined "for the great world."

From the outside, the house looked narrow and shabby, the bright light filtering through the holland blind highlighting the heavy, old-fashioned window frame. However, it is comforting to know that countless such cramped, fog-shrouded slices of London have historically—and still do—serve as sanctuaries for a culture remarkably free from vulgarity. In poverty, ostentation becomes irrelevant; the grand spectacles of society merely provoke petty rivalries or futile attempts at possession. The Meyrick household belonged to this rare breed. They clung to this specific row-house because its interior was anchored by objects permanently placed, holding memories of the mother's marriage for her, and serving as an unquestioned, star-like constancy for the children.

Mrs. Meyrick endured many hardships so she might preserve certain engravings her husband deeply loved. Consequently, the narrow walls displayed a condensed world history in scenes and portraits the children had memorized since infancy. Their chairs and tables were old companions preferred over anything new. Despite lacking furniture a broker would covet—save for the prints and the piano—these two small parlors offered space and tools for a life of broad horizons, elegantly curated to embrace the highest realms of music, painting, and poetry.

Even during their leanest days, before Kate began earning wages, they likely managed without a servant to tend fires or sweep floors. Yet, they remained fastidious. They refused to believe that the manners of fashionable ladies were as coarse, self-centered, and laced with slang as depicted in contemporary literary caricatures. The Meyricks possessed peculiar quirks, streaks of eccentricity inherited from both parents, their minds resembling medieval castles with unexpected recesses, hidden passages, and sudden vistas.

Yet, mother and daughters were bound by a triple knot: family love, a shared reverence for noble deeds and masterful works, and a habit of diligent industry. When Hans once offered to spend his money to make their lives more luxurious, they unanimously declined. Both parties were spared the regret of seeing his artistic ambitions crushed by the allure of secure income—a sacrifice that would eventually force him to abandon his fellowship. They could all laugh at his Gavarni-style caricatures and forgive his pursuit of a natural calling, unobstructed by their own selflessness and independence. A grand treat of opera-going in the gallery awaited whenever Hans visited, but otherwise, they continued their established rhythm.

Seeing the group as they gathered that evening, one could scarcely wish for any alteration to their existence. They were all diminutive, perfectly scaled to their miniature rooms. Mrs. Meyrick was a lively little woman, half-French, half-Scotch, possessing an articulate speech that seemed to illuminate her listeners' understanding. Though nearing fifty, her rippling hair, tucked beneath a Quaker-style net cap, was mostly gray, contrasting with her bright brown eyebrows and eyes. Her black dress, buttoned up like a priest's cassock, suited her neat, barely-five-foot figure.

Her daughters mirrored her, save for Mab, who inherited Hans's light hair and fair complexion, along with a bossy, irregular brow and other distinctive traits reminiscent of him. Everything about them was compact: from the tight coils of their hair pinned *à la Chinoise* to their gray skirts, which defiantly rejected the era's fashion demanding that four feminine forms fill every inch of the front parlor. If they were wax figures, they could easily fit into a fashionable lady's traveling trunk. Their faces seemed ready to speak, as if their minds had been shelled like horse-chestnuts, revealing a bright core within.

The only large entity in the room was Hafiz, the Persian cat, comfortably perched on the brown leather back of a chair, occasionally blinking his large eyes to ensure the smaller creatures caused no mischief.

Mrs. Meyrick had just concluded reading Erckmann-Chatrian's *Histoire d'un Conscrit*. Mab, having dropped her embroidery to stretch forward and fixate on the reader, suddenly declared, "I think that is the finest story in the world!"

"Of course, Mab!" replied Amy. "It is the last you've heard. Whatever pleases you is the best in its turn."

"It hardly counts as a story," corrected Kate. "It is a fragment of history brought close to us with a powerful telescope. We can see the soldiers' faces... no, more than that—we can hear everything. We can almost hear their hearts beating."

"I don't care what you call it," Mab interrupted, tossing her thimble aside. "Call it a chapter in Revelation. It makes me want to do something good, something grand. It fills me with sorrow for everyone. It reminds me of Schiller—I want to take the world in my arms and kiss it. Instead, I must kiss you, little mother!" She flung her arms around her mother's neck.

"When you're in that mood, Mab, your work suffers," Amy noted dryly. "Finishing your cushion without staining it would count as doing something good."

"Oh—oh—oh!" groaned Mab, stooping to retrieve her scattered supplies. "I wish I had three wounded conscripts to nurse!"

"You'd spill their beef tea while talking," Amy teased.

"Poor Mab! Don't be too hard on her," the mother interjected gently. "Hand me the embroidery, child. Continue with your enthusiasm, and I'll resume the pink and white poppies."

"Well, Ma, I think you're more caustic than Amy," Kate observed, tilting her head to inspect her drawing.

"Oh—oh—oh!" cried Mab again, rising and stretching her arms dramatically. "Something wonderful must happen! I feel like the Deluge! The waters of the great deep are breaking loose, and the windows of heaven are opening! I must sit down and play the scales!"

As Mab began opening the piano while the others laughed at her dramatic climax, a carriage halted before the house, followed immediately by a sharp rap on the knocker.

"Dear me!" exclaimed Mrs. Meyrick, jumping up. "It is past ten, and Phoebe is already asleep." She hurried out, leaving the parlor door ajar.

"Mr. Deronda!" she called out.

The girls heard their mother's exclamation from inside. Mab clasped her hands, whispering loudly, "There now! Something *is* going to happen." Kate and Amy abandoned their tasks in sheer amazement. However, Deronda's reply was so low they couldn't catch his words, and Mrs. Meyrick promptly shut the parlor door.

"I know I am placing a tremendous trust in your kindness," Deronda continued after delivering a brief summary of events. "But you can imagine how helpless I feel with a young creature like this on my hands. I cannot leave her among strangers, and given her nervous state, I dread bringing her into a house full of servants. I have relied on your mercy. Please, do not consider my actions unjustified."

"On the contrary," Mrs. Meyrick responded warmly. "You have honored me by trusting me. I understand your predicament. Please bring her in. I will go and prepare the girls."

While Deronda returned to the carriage, Mrs. Meyrick re-entered the parlor and announced, "Here is someone to replace your wounded conscripts, Mab: a poor girl attempting to drown herself in despair. Mr. Deronda found her just in time to save her. He brought her along in his boat, unsure what else to do safely, so he entrusted her to us. She appears to be a Jewess, but highly refined—he says she knows Italian and music."

The three girls, curious and expectant, moved forward, standing together in a silent unity of compassion. Mab looked slightly awestruck, as if this fulfillment of her wish were supernatural. Meanwhile, Deronda approached the carriage door where a pale face now peered out with alert observation.

"I have brought you to some of the kindest people in the world," he told the girl. "These are daughters like yourself. It is a happy home. May I take you to meet them?"

She stepped out obediently, slipping her hand into his and momentarily forgetting her hat. As Deronda led her into the warm glow of the parlor, where the four small women awaited, she became a vision capable of stirring even the coldest hearts. Initially dazed by the sudden brightness, she hadn't fully focused her gaze before Deronda guided her hand into her mother's.
