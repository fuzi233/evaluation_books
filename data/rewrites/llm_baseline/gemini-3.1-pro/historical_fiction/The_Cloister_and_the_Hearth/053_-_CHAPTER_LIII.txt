The rhythm of the hearth-fire marked the passage of a tense afternoon, the popping of seasoned ash wood serving as the only voice in the kitchen until Catherine, an agrarian elder whose hands bore the ingrained memory of countless harvests, turned from the heavy stone mortar where she had been grinding fragrant coriander and brown mustard seed.

“Nay, Richart,” Catherine murmured, her voice steeped in the weary timbre of one trying to preserve the domestic harmony of her household. “For Heaven’s sake, let not this one sorry wench set us all by the ears. Hath she not soured the blood of this family enough?”

“In very deed she hath,” Richart replied, pacing the swept floorboards. “Fear me not, good mother. Let her come and read the letter of the poor boy she hath by devilish arts bewitched, and then let her go. Give me your word to show her no countenance beyond decent and constrained civility: less we may not, being in our own house; and I will say no more.”

On this brittle accord, the family awaited the intrusion. Margaret, for her part, prepared for the encounter as a warrior donning armor, though her armaments were woven of thread and dye. Denys had brought word they would receive her rather than come to her; her lip had curled at the petty assertion of power embedded in their burgher dialect, a pride of unlettered folk clinging to the dominant lexicon of their threshold. Yet for Gerard’s sake, she swallowed her loathing. 

Her garments were chosen to parry the sharp, scrutinizing glances of her foes. She wore a kirtle of dark blue English cloth, redolent of woad and the faint, earthy sweetness of crushed lavender in which it had been stored. Her farthingale and hose were of a glossy roan, the color of young claret poured against candlelight. There was no pretentious fur, only plain, snowy linen framing her throat in a square cut, starched and curiously plaited, smelling faintly of lye and fresh river water. A tight-fitting hood of dark blue cloth, hemmed with a subtle flash of silver, covered her head. Below the roan petticoat, her red shoes set off an arched instep. Beauty knew its business, anchoring itself in the physical reality of the senses.

She kept them waiting until the sun cast long, amber shadows by the dial. When she finally set out, the crunch of her red shoes against the cobblestones formed a hesitant rhythm beside Denys’s heavy, martial tread. Halfway to the house, the acoustic landscape of the town seemed to overwhelm her, and she stopped.

“Denys!” she whispered, her voice fracturing.

“Well, she-general?”

“I must go home,” she said, the words slipping out as a piteous exhale.

“What, have ye left somewhat behind?”

“What?”

“My courage. Oh! oh! oh!”

“Nay, nay, be brave, she-general. I shall be with you.”

“Ay, but wilt keep close to me when I be there?”

Denys swore his oath, and they resumed their march, the scent of damp earth and woodsmoke heralding their arrival at Eli’s door.

Within, a stifling silence had pooled. Mortification, curiosity, panting affection, and a sharp aversion mingled like contrasting spices in a bitter broth. When Denys pushed open the door alone, his rough, guttural organ cut through the quiet: “The she-comrade is without.”

“Fetch her in,” Eli ordered, his voice carrying the immovable weight of the patriarch. “Now whisht, all of ye. None speak to her but I.”

The door opened with a slow, agonizing creak. Margaret Brandt crossed the threshold, pale but resolute, carrying the heavy scent of cold air and unspoken grief into the warm, herb-scented room. They all rose but Kate, staring in mute shock. Margaret’s condition was undeniably visible beneath the heavy drape of her claret farthingale. Cornelis and Sybrandt’s eyes narrowed in silent hatred; Richart frowned at the disruption of her maidenly form, though the women’s postures softened at the undeniable, bodily truth of the life she carried.

“Be seated, mistress,” Eli said gravely, gesturing to a solitary chair set apart, an island of isolation.

She inclined her head and crossed the room. Drawing the letter from her bosom, she pressed the parchment to her lips—a tactile, intimate communion with the absent writer—and disposed herself to read. Yet, feeling the oppressive isolation of her placement, she dropped her hand to her side and made a swift, furtive motion to Denys. He stepped to her shoulder like a sentinel, his heavy boots sounding a defiant note against the floorboards.

Then, Margaret began to read. Her voice was a revelation of acoustic ecology, a mellow, clear instrument that vibrated with a soft, thrilling earnestness. She took Gerard’s written words, foreign and distant, and breathed them back into the native, resonant dialect of his ancestors, bridging the inherent untranslatability of his suffering.

*“I do nothing doubt, my Margaret, that long ere this shall meet thy beloved eyes, Denys, my most dear friend, will have sought thee out, and told thee the manner of our unlooked-for and most tearful parting. Therefore I will e’en begin at that most doleful day. What befell him after, poor faithful soul, fain, fain would I hear, but may not. Friend more stanch and loving had not David in Jonathan, than I in him. Be good to him, for poor Gerard’s sake.”*

A profound groan escaped Denys, shattering the quiet. He leaned his head against the high back of Margaret’s chair. She turned, finding his rough hand and pressing it warmly. Together, the sweetheart and the friend held fast as the voice wove on.

*“I went forward all dizzied, like one in an ill dream; and presently a gentleman came up with his servants, all on horseback. He drew rein at the brow of the hill, and sent his armed men back to rob me. They took my purse and the last copper, and rid gaily away. I wandered stupid on, a friendless pauper.”*

A collective sigh whispered through the room, punctuated by a sharp oath from Denys.

*“Presently a strange dimness came o’er me; I lay down to sleep on the snow. ’Twas ill done, with a store of wolves hard by, their howling a terrifying echo in the frozen silence. The drowsiness that did crawl o’er me was more than nature—it was a mythic slumber, a descent into the white void. And from that sleep I ne’er had waked, but that a hand did shake me rudely. I awoke to my troubles, and there stood a servant girl in her holiday suit.* 

*“‘Are ye rich, or are ye poor?’ she speered. I gave a loud cry at the untranslatable cruelty of the question. I told her I had been rich in a friend, rich in money and the spirits of youth, but now the Bastard of Burgundy had taken my friend, and a gentleman my purse. I was the poorest of the poor. Because I recked not whither I went, I followed her to a fine house hard by, into a noble dining-hall hung with heavy black tapestries that deadened all sound.”*

Margaret’s voice carried them into the mourning hall. Gerard’s letter did not describe mere sustenance, but a profound, mythic ritual seamlessly integrated into the domestic space.

*“There was set a table with many dishes, and but one plate. ‘Fall to!’ she whispered. When I speered why, she told me that when a gentleman dies in Burgundy, they serve the dead man’s dinner up as usual, till he be in the ground, and set some poor man to it. So I behoved to sit down. This kind lass poured me a goblet of wine. The rich, strong vintage—tasting of deep, loamy terroir, crushed dark cherries, and the toasted oak of its cask—curled around my sick heart, warming my frozen blood. I tasted it and wept that Denys was not there to appreciate its complex notes. She made me eat of every dish. Nought was here but her master’s daily dinner, yet it was a feast of profound culinary memory. I partook of roasted capon rubbed with wild thyme, and root vegetables glazed in elderberry reduction, nibbling of every dish to honor the dead. Eating not merely for fuel, but to anchor my shivering soul in the rich, sensory history of the estate.”*

*“The next day, they were all most kind, and the girl proffered me money from her small wage. My stomach rose against it; I would not take a free gift. So I set her in a good light to draw her picture, preserving her image as I had preserved the taste of the mourning feast.”*

Margaret paused, handing Denys a colored drawing to pass around. The women examined it with meticulous curiosity. It was a tangible record of a foreign culture: the girl’s hair bound in a tight linen bag, a yellow half-kerchief crossing her head to form stark, geometric points over her forehead and bosom. She wore a red kirtle kilted high, revealing a green farthingale and a great red leather purse, her feet clad in low-quartered, square-toed yellow leathern shoes—a vibrant tapestry of regional identity.

Margaret continued:

*“Often I sat down on the road where two paths met, and with heavy head did think of thee, my poor sweetheart, and of the little house at Tergou, where they all loved me once; though now it is turned to hate.”*

“Alas! that he will think so,” Catherine whispered, the agrarian rhythm of her heart aching at the broken familial bond.

“Whisht, wife!” Eli commanded.

*“And me sighing so in the quiet of the woods, a jarring noise shattered the peace. One came carolling like a bird adown t’ other road. I jumped up in ire to flee his dissonant, grating mirth. Lo! the songster was a humpbacked cripple, with a bloody bandage o’er his eye, and both legs gone at the knee.”*

“He! he! he! he! he!” Sybrandt cackled, his laughter a harsh, ugly sound that violated the room’s somber acoustic ecology.

Margaret’s eyes flashed with fierce, protective fire; she began to fold the parchment.

“Nay, lass,” said Eli. “Heed him not! Thou unmannerly cur, offer’t but again and I put thee to the door.”

“Why, what was there to gibe at, Sybrandt?” Catherine remonstrated mildly. “Is not our Kate afflicted? And is she not the most content of us all, singing like a merle and weaving her sweet notes into the very life of this house? Read on, lass, and stop our gabble wi’ somewhat worth the hearkening.”

*“I took myself to task for bemoaning my health while this wreck of nature praised God. He chanted for charity with a whine as piteous as wind at a keyhole. But when I told him I was robbed and poor as he, he ceased his whining in an instant. He spoke in a big, manly voice, stripped of its artificial piety. ‘Here, youngster, pull thou this strap.’ I pulled, and down came a stout pair of legs out of his back; half his hump melted away. His sores were but a painted fiction.”*

A collective, breathy “Oh!” escaped Margaret’s listeners.

*“He laughed at my astounded face, speaking in the dominant, cynical cant of the roads. He offered me his protection, advising me to quit France and make for the great German cities, the Paradise of craftsmen. He spoke of the cul de bois and the cul de jatte, offering to teach me how to maund, chaunt, and patter—a corruption of language that eroded all honest meaning. I told him I’d liever die than shame myself with such deceptive lexicons.”*

“Good lad! good lad!” Eli murmured.

*“Seeing I would not beg, he hired me to be the fingers to his head. ‘Now, servant,’ said he, ‘we will dine.’ Out of his beggar’s wallet he brought a feast that defied his lowly station: cold roasted fowl dripping with savory fats, pastries rich with butter, a good dozen of precious spices—nutmeg, clove, and mace—lapped delicately in fine flax paper, and wine fit for a king. Ne’er feasted I better, savoring the sharp bite of black peppercorns and the sweet finish of the wine under the open sky. Yet when I praised honest Lazarus who fed on crumbs, this rogue scoffed, admitting his own sores were painted with spearwort and biting acids that no dog would lick.*

*“We took the road and came upon two little wayside inns, their armories faded. Here I saw the hollow soul of commerce. My master disguised himself as a decent tradesman and played upon the vanity of the innkeepers with fulsome flattery. He pitted ‘The Three Frogs’ against ‘The Four Hedgehogs,’ convincing each to pay me a silver franc to paint their signs brighter than the other’s. They cared not for the art, only the loud, superficial commercialization of outshining their neighbor.*

*“With our coin, Cul de Jatte bought me a psaltery. So I played a strain, a harmonious, sweet melody the good monks taught me. The resonance of the strings reminded me of my young days and our hearth, bringing the water of true cultural memory to my een. But my master’s visage twisted as if sipping foulest medicine. ‘Zounds, stop that bellyache blether,’ quoth he. ‘That will ne’er wile a stiver out o’ peasants’ purses.’ He claimed it would gar the kine jump into rivers to escape the sound. And then, he sang so blasphemous a stave, and eke so obscene, that it was as an industrial clatter, a harsh noise pollution that assaulted the mind and spirit. I drew away lest the lightning strike his ribaldry.* 

*“I bade him keep his breath to cool his broth. Never would I shame my folk by joining his profane chorus. ‘Then,’ says he sulkily, ‘the first fire we light by the wayside, clap thou on the music box! so ’twill make our pot boil for the nonce; but with your,*

*Good people, let us peak and pine,*
*Cut tristful mugs, and miaul and whine*
*Thorough our nosen chaunts divine,’*
