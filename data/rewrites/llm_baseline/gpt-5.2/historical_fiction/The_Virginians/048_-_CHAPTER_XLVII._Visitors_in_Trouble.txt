Gumbo’s kind offices—his soft-footed comings and goings, his murmured assurances, his very way of setting a cracked jug of water and a coarse napkin as though they were silver and damask—had worked upon Harry Warrington’s anger like a slow simmer upon tough meat. The second night in that sour spunging-house was not so noisome as the first; for there was, at least, one faithful creature near him who could warm a corner of the cold place with human care.

Yet the warmth went no further than that corner. Toward the rest of the world Harry’s heart stayed hard, proud, and impatient. He lay listening to the house breathe—chains clinked below, a cough worried the next room, a key turned with that metallic scrape that seemed made to rasp a man’s spirit raw—and he told himself that England was a land where friendship had been turned to trade, and trade to cruelty. Aunt Warrington with her pieties; March with his great airs; Castlewood with his easy scorn: each had been tested, and each, in Harry’s mind, had failed. Not for twenty years of prison, not for famine, not for the shame of being pointed at in the street, would he stoop to ask their aid again.

He would leave. He would go anywhere that did not have this perpetual jangling of metal and coin in its very speech. Germany, perhaps—where a king made war openly and a man might be ruined by cannon instead of by creditors. Or Virginia—where the woods kept old sounds in their branches, where the whippoorwill and the creek’s talk were honest, and a man’s name meant what it meant in his mother’s mouth. He pictured, with a hunger that was almost bodily, the clean snap of tobacco leaves curing in barns, the sweet damp of earth at dawn, a kitchen where bacon hissed and corn-cakes browned, where the dialect of the quarter and the drawl of Tidewater—those languages that carried memory in their very vowels—had not been beaten thin by London’s sharp, fashionable tongue. He would be factor and steward; he would hunt all day; he would marry Polly Broadbent or Fanny Mountain; he would become anything—planter, farmer, even a plain tobacco-grower—rather than remain among these fine gentlemen whose politeness had the sound of knives.

So he rose with an outward face made cheerful by obstinacy, and an inward temper still burning. Early in the morning Gumbo appeared, punctual as any steward in a great house, having come from Bond Street with Mr. Harry’s letters.

“I wanted to bring some more clo’es, master,” honest Gumbo said, with that look of patient shame he wore whenever a white man’s pride had been brought to beggary; “but Mr. Ruff, de landlord, he wouldn’t let me bring no more.”

Harry scarcely cared to see the papers. He broke one seal, then another, then a third: all were bills, all with that same cold, printed certainty, as if money itself had learned to write in a harsher hand than men. A fourth was from the landlord: no more goods to go out; the debt must be paid; if not, the goods should be sold; and the black man must sleep elsewhere. Ruff had almost refused Gumbo his own clothes and portmanteau.

Gumbo, however, had refuge. He said it simply, but his eyes were busy on Harry’s face. “I been stayin’ wid friends at Lord Wrotham house. Wid Colonel Lambert people,” he added, as though dropping a stone into still water to see what circles would come.

“And Miss Hetty,” Gumbo continued, lowering his voice as if sorrow must be spoken softly, “she fall down in a faint when she hear you taken up; and Mr. Lambert, he very good man, and he say to me dis mornin’, he say, ‘Gumbo, you tell your master if he want me he send to me, and I come to him.’”

At the mention of Hetty, something in Harry’s breast gave way for a moment. Whatever else England had been to him—its gaming-tables, its drawing-rooms, its scornful laughter—Hetty Lambert’s gentleness had not been a lie. He did not altogether believe in Gumbo’s fainting tale; he knew how the man embroidered kindness into legend, as if affection must be made larger to cover misfortune. But that she had been distressed—yes, he believed that. It touched him, and the touch stung.

Then Colonel Lambert’s name came, and the softness curdled at once. The colonel—who had flung back his gifts, who had insulted him with that calm superiority which is a whip without a lash. Send to Lambert? Ask the man for help? Harry’s jaw set as though he were biting iron.

“I send to Lambert,” he said to himself, grinding his teeth. “If I were starving I would not ask him for a crust.”

He dressed with the same punctilious care he would have used in his best days, as though a clean neckcloth and ordered hair could silence the dirty clatter of bolts and bars. He called for breakfast. The bread was coarse, the tea thin, the room steeped in the smell of stale spirits and damp wool; still, he made himself eat, not from appetite but from that stubborn discipline that had been beaten into him by plantation and camp. The house was full of sound—men bickering in low voices, a distant shout, a laugh that ended in coughing—each noise an insult, each echo a reminder that he was penned.

He sent Gumbo away with a brief note to Mr. Draper, attorney in the Temple, requiring that gentleman’s attendance.

The note, indeed, had the air of a command flung to a slave. Draper, who had long endured Harry’s condescension with a sour patience, read it and coloured.

“It’s all very well for a fine gentleman to give hisself airs; but for a fellow in a spunging-house! Hang him!” Draper said to himself. “I’ve a great mind not to go!”

Nevertheless he went. He came in a mood bristling with small resentments, and found the young Virginian more lordly in adversity than he had ever been in prosperity.

Harry sat upon the bed as if it were a chair of state, wrapped in a splendid brocaded dressing-gown that made a kind of mockery of the dingy room. His hair was dressed; his hands were clean. He motioned to Gumbo as though Gumbo were a footman in Clarges Street.

“Gumbo, a chair to Mr. Draper.”

“Excuse me, madam, but such haughtiness and airs I ain’t accustomed to!” Draper had exclaimed, afterwards, when he went to report proceedings to Madame de Bernstein; and the old Baroness, far from being angered, had smiled. There was a relish in her for pride—especially pride that bit when it was wounded. It amused her that the boy could still carry himself like a gentleman while the law had him by the throat.

She and Draper had arranged beforehand what line he should take. Draper was capable, even shrewd; but like many men whose living depended upon phrases and forms, he could not understand the kind of pride that is not a bargaining chip. He went in thinking he should manage Harry as he managed common clients: a push here, a threat there, a promise dangled, a paper thrust under the nose. He did not see that Harry’s very sense of language—what was binding, what was base, what words could be endured—was of another country, another upbringing, another music.

“Sit down, if you please,” Harry said, with a cold civility that made the request sound like an order. “Let us talk my business over. Much obliged to you for coming so soon in reply to my message. Had you heard of this piece of ill-luck before?”

“Bad news travel quick, Mr. Warrington,” Draper replied. “And I was eager to offer my humble services as soon as ever you should require them. Your friends, your family, will be much pained that a gentleman of your rank should be in such a position.”

“I have been very imprudent, Mr. Draper. I have lived beyond my means.”

Draper bowed—one of those bows which seem to say, I expected no better.

“I played in company with gentlemen much richer than myself. A cursed run of ill-luck has carried away all my ready money, leaving me with liabilities to the amount of five hundred pounds, and more.”

“Five hundred now in the office,” Draper said promptly.

“Well,” Harry continued, staring straight at him, “it is such a trifle that I thought by sending to one or two friends yesterday I could have paid my debt and gone home without further to do. I have been mistaken. I thank you to put me in the way of raising the money as soon as may be.”

Draper pulled a long face, as though five hundred pounds were a mountain.

“Why, sir, it can be done!” Harry said sharply, provoked by the display.

“It can be done in time,” Draper answered, enjoying the chance to look grave, “but it will require a considerable time. To touch the property in England which is yours on Mr. George Warrington’s death, we must have the event proved, the trustees released—and who is to do either? Lady Esmond Warrington in Virginia, of course, will not allow her son to remain in prison, but we must wait six months before we hear from her. Has your Bristol agent any authority to honour your drafts?”

“He is only authorised to pay me two hundred pounds a year,” Harry said.

“I suppose I have no resource, then, but to apply to my aunt, Madame de Bernstein,” Harry went on, forcing the words through his pride. “She will be my security.”

“Her ladyship will do anything for you, sir; she has said so to me, often and often,” Draper replied. “And if she gives the word at this moment you can walk out of this place.”

“Go to her, then, from me,” Harry said. “I did not want to have troubled my relations; but rather than continue in this horrible needless imprisonment, I must speak to her. Say where I am, and what has befallen me. Disguise nothing. And tell her that I confide in her affection and kindness for me to release me from this—this disgrace.”

His voice shook on the last word. For an instant he heard, under the present sounds, other remembered sounds: his mother’s voice in Virginia, calling him by his name in that slow, native music; the kitchen’s steady labour; the crackle of a fire fed with pine; words that did not cut, because they belonged.

Draper watched him, then said, with a dry relish, “Sir, I was with her ladyship yesterday, when we talked over the whole of this here most unpleasant—I won’t say, as you do, disgraceful business.”

“What do you mean, sir? Does Madame de Bernstein know of my misfortune?” Harry demanded.

“Every circumstance, sir. The pawning the watches, and all.”

Harry flushed as though he had been struck.

“It is an unfortunate business,” Draper continued, “the pawning them watches and things which you had never paid for.”

Harry sprang up from the bed. There was something in his look that made Draper feel, for a moment, the difference between a man who has been taught to command bodies and a man who has only ever commanded papers.

“It may lead to litigation and unpleasant remarks being made, in court,” Draper went on, half frightened, half delighted to be giving pain. “Them barristers respect nothing; and when they get a feller in the box—”

“Great Heaven, sir!” Harry cried. “Do you suppose a gentleman of my rank can’t take a watch upon credit without intending to cheat the tradesman?”

“Of course you meant everything that’s honourable,” Draper said, winking, “only, you see, the law mayn’t happen to think so. Your aunt says it’s the most imprudent thing ever she heard of—to call it by no worse name.”

“You call it by no worse name yourself, Mr. Draper?” Harry said, speaking each word slowly, as if he were measuring them, trying to keep mastery over his own voice.

“Heaven forbid that I should say anything as between gentleman and gentleman,” Draper answered; “but between me and my client, it’s my duty to say, ‘Sir, you are in a very unpleasant scrape,’ just as a doctor would tell his patient, ‘Sir, you are very ill.’”

“And you can’t help me to pay this debt off,” Harry said, with a bitter astonishment, “and you have come only to tell me I may be accused of roguery?”

“Of obtaining goods under false pretences? Most undoubtedly, yes. I can’t help it, sir. Don’t look as if you would knock me down. A young gentleman who has only two hundred a year from his ma’, orders diamonds and watches, and takes ’em to a pawnbroker. You ask me what people will think of such behaviour, and I tell you honestly. Don’t be angry with me, Mr. Warrington.”

“Go on, sir,” Harry said, and the words came like a groan.

Draper now felt the moment was his. “But you ask if I can’t help to pay this debt off? And I say Yes—and that here is the money in my pocket to do it now, if you like—not mine, sir; my honoured client’s; your aunt, Lady Bernstein. But she has a right to impose her conditions, and I’ve brought ’em with me.”

“Tell them, sir,” Harry said, his pride bending because he saw the door open.

“They are not hard. They are only for your own good. If you say Yes, we can call a hackney-coach, and go to Clarges Street together, which I have promised to go there whether you will or no. Mr. Warrington, I name no names, but there was a question of marriage between you and a certain party.”

“Ah!” Harry breathed; and his face, for the first time that day, showed something like relief. The very thought of being unknotted from that foolish promise was like drawing a thorn from the flesh.

“To that marriage my noble client, the Baroness, is most averse,” Draper continued. “Having other views for you, and thinking it will be your ruin to marry a party—of noble birth and title it is true; but, excuse me, not of first-rate character, and so much older than yourself. You had given an imprudent promise to that party.”

“Yes,” Harry said, “and she has it still.”

“It has been recovered,” Draper replied. “She dropped it by an accident at Tunbridge, so my client informed me; indeed her ladyship showed it me, for the matter of that. It was wrote in bl—”

“Never mind, sir!” Harry cried, turning as red as the ink he imagined, and as ashamed as though his folly had been shouted down the whole passage.

“At the same time letters, wrote to you, and compromising a noble family, were recovered,” Draper went on. “You had lost ’em. It was no fault of yours. You were away when they were found again. You may say that that noble family, that you yourself, have a friend such as few young men have. Well, sir, there’s no earthly promise to bind you—only so many idle words said over a bottle, which very likely any gentleman may forget. Say you won’t go on with this marriage—give me and my noble friend your word of honour. Cry off, I say, Mr. W.! Don’t be such a d—-fool, saving your presence, as to marry an old woman who has jilted scores of men in her time. Say the word, and I step downstairs, pay every shilling against you in the office, and put you down in my coach—either at your aunt’s, or at White’s Club, if you like—with a couple of hundred in your pocket. Say yes; and give us your hand! There’s no use in sitting grinning behind these bars all day!”

All this, in substance, was what Harry desired. His flame for Maria Esmond had long since guttered out; it had been vanity and youth, and the intoxication of being pursued by a famous woman’s notice. If she would release him, he would bless the release. Yet Draper’s tone—his coarse jokes, his familiarity—fell on Harry’s ear like the clank of the keys below: the sound of a man trampling another man’s pride for sport.

“Come! Give us your hand, and say done!” Draper pressed, with a knowing wink. “Don’t stand shilly-shallying, sir. Law bless you, Mr. W., if I had married everybody I promised, I should be like the Grand Turk, or Captain Macheath in the play!”

The attorney’s easy profanity in the presence of an oath disgusted Harry. He drew back, scarcely aware that he did so, and gathered the brocaded gown about him as if to shut out not cold but familiarity.

“Give me a little time to think of the matter, if you please, Mr. Draper,” he said. “And have the goodness to come to me again in an hour.”

“Very good, sir—very good, sir!” Draper answered, biting his lip till it whitened. “Most parties would not want an hour to consider about such an offer as I make you; but I suppose my time must be yours. I’ll come again, and see whether you are to go or to stay. Good morning, sir—good morning.”

He went down the stairs muttering. “Won’t take my hand, won’t he? Will tell me in an hour’s time! Hang his impudence! I’ll show him what an hour is!”

In his chambers he vented himself on clerks and clients. He sent a messenger to the Baroness to say he had waited on the young gentleman, who had demanded time for consideration—mere form, no doubt. Then he went to dinner at his leisure and did business as though a man’s liberty were an item to be entered when convenient. It was late afternoon before he turned toward Cursitor Street.

“He’ll be at home when I call, the haughty beast,” Draper sneered.

At the spunging-house the double doors were opened by a sheriff’s follower with a face both flushed and solemn, and an air of importance so comic that it might have been harmless, had the smell not betrayed the truth. The little passage between the doors was rich with spirituous fumes; gin and sour breath lay there like a fog.

“The Fortunate Youth in his room?” Draper asked.

“Mr. Warrington is in his apartment,” the fellow said, “but—” and here he winked and laid a finger to his nose with drunken significance.

“But what, Mr. Paddy from Cork?” Draper snapped, pushing forward.

“My name is Costigan,” the man returned, swelling and scowling. “Me familee is noble, and me neetive place is the Irish methrawpolis, Mr. Six-and-eightpence!”

“Confound you, sir, let me pass!” Draper bawled.

“I can hear you perfectly well, Six-and-eightpence,” Costigan retorted, slurring and blustering, “except your h’s, which you dthrop out of your conversation. I’ll thank ye not to call neems, me good friend, or me fingers and your nose will have to make an intimate hic-quaintance. Walk in, sir! Be polite for the future to your shupariors in birth and manners, though they may be your infariors in temporary station. Confound the kay! Walk in, sir, I say—Madam, I have the honour of saluting ye most respectfully!”

A lady came down the stairs at that moment, her face covered with a capuchin, further hidden by a handkerchief. She gave a small cry, startled to find herself confronted by a man like Draper pressing forward with bold curiosity.

Draper would have peered at her—he had that vulgar gallantry which mistakes intrusion for compliment—but Costigan thrust his leg between them, barring the way like a gate.

“Keep your own distance, if you plaise! This way, madam! I at once recognised your ladysh—”

He stopped himself and, with a sudden drunken discretion, slammed the door on Draper’s nose, leaving the attorney to find his own way upstairs, fuming and bewildered amid the sour, echoing sounds of the place.

In Clarges Street, meanwhile, the old Baroness de Bernstein paced upon her drawing-room floor, leaning on her crutch, turning her head at every rumble of wheels. The street’s noises—coach-horses, calls, the far-off clatter of a city always buying and selling—came to her like a taunt. She, who on ordinary days would have scourged a cook for five minutes’ delay, had put off dinner from hour to hour. She had ordered two covers. She had commanded extra dishes, as though to tempt a boy’s appetite back into him with rich gravies and crisped pastry, as if liberty itself might be served hot and fragrant upon a plate.

Four o’clock passed. Five. At six she went again to the window, and at last a coach did stop at her door.

“Mr. Draper,” the servant announced.

He entered bowing profoundly. The Baroness trembled upon her stick, her old eyes bright and impatient.

“Where is the boy?” she demanded. “I told you to bring him, sir! How dare you come without him?”

“It is not my fault, madam,” Draper said, with a wounded dignity. “Mr. Warrington refuses to come.”

And then, in the same room where soups and roasts were cooling for want of the one guest they were meant to warm, Draper began to give his version of the interview—each word chosen to make his own affront sound like the young man’s insolence, and each syllable carrying, under its meaning, that harsh London note which had already begun to corrode Harry Warrington’s faith in kindness.
