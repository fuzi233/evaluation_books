Silence enveloped the room like a damp shroud. Mrs. Henry van der Luyden's slender fingers rested on the arm of her chair, her eyes fixed on her cousin Mrs. Archer as she spoke. The high-ceilinged drawing-room, with its pale brocaded armchairs and gauze-draped ormolu mantelpiece, seemed to amplify every sound, rendering each word a deliberate choice. 

Mrs. van der Luyden's portrait, painted by Huntington, hung above her, a striking likeness of the woman who sat before it. Twenty years had passed since its creation, yet the painting remained a testament to her enduring elegance. Her black velvet gown, adorned with intricate Venetian point, seemed a timeless choice, one she continued to wear when receiving guests at home. The fair hair that had once been youthful now framed her face with subtle wisps, and her pale blue eyes, set apart by a straight nose, seemed frozen in a perpetual expression of gentle kindness.

Newland Archer observed her with a mixture of esteem and wariness, the latter stemming from the sense that her reserve was not so much a quality as a carefully cultivated art. Like all her family, he admired her, but found her reserved nature difficult to approach. It was as if she existed in a state of suspended animation, preserved in the airless atmosphere of her impeccable existence, like a body trapped in glacial ice.

Mrs. van der Luyden's response to her cousin's words was characteristically measured, her lips barely moving as she inclined her head in consideration. Her habitual phrase, "I shall first have to talk this over with my husband," hung in the air, a promise of deliberation and caution. Yet, as she reached for the bell-rope, a subtle shift occurred, a glimmer of surprise in her expression that hinted at a deeper consideration. "I think," she said, "I should like Henry to hear what you have told me."

The footman appeared promptly, and Mrs. van der Luyden instructed him to summon her husband from his newspaper, her tone imbued with a quiet authority that bordered on reverence. As she waited for Mr. van der Luyden to arrive, the silence in the room deepened, punctuated only by the soft ticking of a clock.
