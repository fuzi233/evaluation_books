"The King of Spain is a foul pagan, and lives upon Mahound; and pity it were that a lady fair should marry a heathen hound." —King Estmere.

About six weeks following the duel, the miller at Stow arrived at the great house in deep distress, requesting the loan of the bloodhounds. Rose Salterne had vanished during the night; no one knew her whereabouts. Sir Richard was away in Bideford, yet the old steward deemed it his duty to summon the keepers. Down went the serving-men to the mill, trailed by all the idle lads of the parish, who regarded a maiden-hunt as excellent sport. Naturally, they viewed the situation through the most flattering lens regarding Rose. They reviled the miller and his wife roundly as hard-hearted old heathens, convinced they had driven the poor maid to throw herself from a cliff or drown in the sea. Conversely, the women of Stow held a unanimous opinion that the hussy had "gone off" with some disreputable fellow, asserting that pride must inevitably fall.

The facts of the case were stark: all of Rose's trinkets remained behind, proving she had departed honestly. Nothing seemed missing save some of her linen, which Old Anthony the steward broadly hinted was likely to be found in someone else's box. The only tangible trace was a small footprint beneath her bedroom window. Upon this, the bloodhound was placed (securely leashed). After a premonitory whimper, he lifted his mighty voice and started, bell-mouthed, through the garden gate and up the lane, towing the panting keeper behind him until they reached the downs above. There, they headed straight for Marslandmouth, where the entire posse comitatus pulled up, breathless, at the door of Lucy Passmore.

Lucy, as I should perhaps have noted earlier, was now a widow and found her widowship not entirely contrary to her interests. Her augury concerning her old man had been fulfilled; he had never returned since the night he put to sea with Eustace and the Jesuits. *"Some natural tears she shed, but dried them soon"*—as many of them did, at least, those not required for business purposes. She then resolved to prevent suspicion by a bold move. Starting for Stow, she recounted a most pathetic tale to Lady Grenville: how her husband had gone out pollock fishing and never returned; how she had heard horsemen galloping past her window in the dead of night, certain they were the Jesuits carrying off her old man by force; and how they had likely used his services before killing and salting him down for provisions on their voyage back to the Pope in Rome. She concluded by entreating protection against those "Popish skulkers up to Chapel," sworn to do her mischief, and appealing to Lady Grenville's sense of justice regarding whether the Queen ought not to grant her a pension for having had her heart's love turned into a martyred saint by idolatrous traitors.

Lady Grenville, who held a high opinion of Lucy's medical skills and always summoned her when a child suffered a "housty" (a sore throat), went forth and pleaded the case before Sir Richard with such effect that Lucy was, for the next two or three years, better off than ever. But now, what did she have to do with Rose's disappearance? And where was she herself? Her door was fastened tight, while around it her flock of goats stood crying in vain for her to milk them. From the downs above, her donkeys, wandering at their own sweet will, answered the bloodhound's bay with a burst of harmony.

"They're laughing at us, keeper, those neddies; sure enough, we've lost our labor here." Yet the bloodhound, after circling the door, turned down the glen and never stopped until reaching the sea's edge. "They've taken water. Let's return and ransack the old witch's house." "'Tis just like that old Lucy to lock a poor maid into shame." Returning, they attacked the cottage. By a general plebiscite, they ransacked the little dwelling, partly in indignation and partly, if the truth be told, hoping for plunder. However, there was none. Lucy had decamped with all her movable wealth, except for the huge black cat among the embers, who, at the sight of the bloodhound, vanished up the chimney (some claimed with a strong odor of brimstone). Viewed from outside, the cat was chased into the woods, where, I doubt not, she lived many happy years as a scourge to all the rabbits of the glen.

The goats and donkeys were driven off to Stow. The mob returned, slightly ashamed of their brief wrath and somewhat afraid of what Sir Richard might say. When he returned, he sold the animals and gave the proceeds to the poor, promising to refund them if Lucy returned and surrendered to justice. But Lucy did not return. Her cottage, from which neighbors shrank as from a haunted place, remained exactly as she left it, crumbling slowly into four fern-covered walls. Through these passed the little stream, murmuring from pool to pool—the only voice breaking the silence of that lonely glen for many years to come.

A few days later, Sir Richard, traveling from Bideford to Stow, stopped at Clovelly Court. Mentioning, with a casual "by the by," news that made Will Cary leap from his seat almost to the ceiling, he revealed what we already know. "And there is no clue?" asked old Cary, for his son was rendered speechless. "Only this," replied Sir Richard. "I hear that a fellow prowling the cliffs that night saw a pinnace heading for Lundy."

Will rose and hurried out of the room. Within half an hour, he and three or four armed servants were aboard a trawling skiff, speeding toward Lundy. He did not return for three days, bringing news that an elderly man, seemingly a foreigner, had lodged for months in a section of the ruined Moresco Castle, tenanted by one John Braund. A few weeks prior, a younger foreigner had joined him from a ship—a Flushinger or Easterling vessel. This ship came and went repeatedly, and the young man was always present. A few days ago, a lady and her maid, a stout woman, arrived with him, spoke with the elder man in secret overnight, and then all three sailed the next morning. Fishermen on the beach had heard the young man call the other "father." He was a very still man, resembling a mass-priest. No more was known, nor did anyone wish to know.

Consequently, old Cary and Sir Richard sent Will on a second trip with the parish constable of Hartland (a vast parish containing, for its sins, the Isle of Lundy, ten miles offshore). They returned with the body of the unfortunate John Braund, farmer, fisherman, and smuggler. After much fruitless examination—which afflicted the examinee with extreme deafness and loss of memory—he was sent to Exeter Gaol charged with "harboring priests, Jesuits, gypsies, and other suspect and traitorous persons." Poor John Braund, whose motives for entertaining such unsavory guests were likely not treason but a wife, seven children, and arrears of rent, did not thrive under the shift from Lundy's pure air to the pestilential atmosphere of Exeter Gaol. Made infamous merely two years later (if I recall correctly) by a "black assizes" nearly as deadly as the notorious one at Oxford—in which judge, jury, counsel, and bystanders, including many members of Devon's best families, sickened and died miserably within days—John Braund contracted the gaol-fever in a week. He died raving in that noisome den; his secret, if he possessed one, perished with him, leaving only vague suspicion regarding Rose Salterne's fate.

Few doubted she had fled with the Spaniard, but whither, and in what capacity? On this last subject, no mercy was shown to her by many a Bideford dame who had hated the poor girl simply for her beauty, nor by many a country lady who had "always expected that the girl would be brought to ruin by the absurd notice, beyond her station's rights, which was taken of her." Meanwhile, every young maiden aspired to fill the throne Rose had abdicated. Thus, on the whole, Bideford considered itself functioning perfectly well without poor Rose, or even better. Though she lingered in some hearts as a fair dream, the daily business and bustle soon swept that dream away, and her place knew her no more.

As for Will Cary? For a while, he was like a man distracted. He heaped upon himself superfluous reproaches for having, as he claimed, first brought Rose into disgrace and then driven her into the arms of the Spaniard. St. Leger, a sensible man, tried in vain to persuade him that the fault lay elsewhere—that the two had been attached long before the quarrel, that such an ending was inevitable sooner or later, that Old Salterne's harshness rather than Cary's wrath had hastened the catastrophe, and finally, that Rose and her fortunes, now that she had eloped with a Spaniard, were not worth troubling their heads about. Poor Will would not be so comforted. He wrote to Frank at Whitehall, detailing the whole truth, calling himself a fool and a villain, and entreating forgiveness.
